Hold the landing on one thing, which is not among your three — I think line 5 may have reintroduced the failure you fixed on the last check.

# The one to settle first

> A tree with no registry fails this check.

On `akasha-file-has-its-page` you landed *An absent `akasha` folder is nothing to judge*, because without it fail-closed refuses every write to a tree that legitimately has no akasha folder — which is most of them.

**What does this check do on a tree with no akasha folder at all?** There is no registry there either. Line 1 says only files under `akasha` are judged, but your registry claim is about the tree, not about a file, so line 1 may not reach it.

If the check refuses every write to every repository without an akasha folder, that is the same defect you caught last time, wearing the shape you were right to refuse. If it does not, then the page needs the sibling's line — *An absent `akasha` folder is nothing to judge* — and the pair of lines then says precisely the thing you mean: **no akasha, nothing to judge; akasha but no registry, refuse.**

That distinction is the whole of your point 2, and it is carried by two lines rather than one. Which answers the question you asked: your wording does state behaviour rather than argue for it, and that is correct — Design carries no reason for itself. What it is missing is not an argument, it is the other half of the boundary.

# 1. It reads as a footnote, and position is why

The claim is right and the words are nearly right. Two things make it read as an aside.

**It is fourth.** In a list, the reader takes the first specific line as the point and the rest as qualification. You have two family lines, then the mechanism, then the centre. Put it directly after the two family lines and it reads as the centre without a word changing.

**Its surprise is in a subordinate clause.** *Though its file and its import both stand* is where the whole finding lives, and it is hanging off the end.

→ **A page type missing from the registry's object fails, though its file and its import are both there.**

*Stand* out, per Alan's list — and you were the one who told me the corpus's own idiom beats mine, so if `checks-system` has a settled way of saying "is present", use that instead of "are both there".

# 3. "Local" is not doing enough, and you can drop it

Two claims joined by a semicolon, and they are independent — a registry could be read by specifier while its binding names were still meaningful. But you do not need both. What stops the wrong implementation is not that the names are local; it is that they carry no information:

→ **Which page type an entry means is in its import specifier, never the name bound to it.**

*Never* does the work *local* was failing to do, it is one claim rather than two, and it rules directly on the act a reader is about to take — matching on the binding name.

# On the definition

*Fails a page type the registry leaves out, and a registry entry no page type answers* is two directions of one concern, which is fine — the concern is agreement, and a check that named only one direction would be half a check. It is concrete in the corpus's idiom and I would not touch it.

The first two Design lines verbatim: correct, and that ruling is holding.
