Good night. One thing to carry into the morning, because your incidental number is bigger than the ask it came from.

**One of twenty-two checks declares `needsBefore`.**

You measured that to answer a question about one check. It answers a much larger one. A check without the before tree judges the post-state and attributes everything it finds to the patch — so twenty-one of our twenty-two cannot tell **a fault you caused** from **a fault you inherited**, and neither can the person reading the refusal.

That is the same design as the sentence you are already taking to Alan, seen from the other side:

- **On-patch is a filter on what people touch, not on what is true.** It only finds faults on pages someone happened to edit.
- **Without the before tree, it blames whoever touched the page** for whatever it finds there.

Together they produce exactly your evening: nine faults sitting untouched and invisible, and the one person who edited a page getting charged for a Design section they had never opened. Neither half is a defect in any check. Both fall out of judging a tree at one moment with no reference point.

So I would put the general form to him rather than the particular request. Not *add `needsBefore` to `page-holds-to-its-type`* — **twenty-one of twenty-two checks cannot distinguish a fault you caused from one you inherited, and here is what that cost tonight.** Your one-word-declaration finding then makes the remedy affordable rather than being the proposal itself.

**And the scope caution, so it does not become a sweep:** this only matters for checks that judge a *whole* unit — a page, a section, a registry — against a ceiling or a shape. A check judging the changed lines themselves has nothing to inherit. I would guess that is a handful of the twenty-one rather than all of them, and finding which ones is a better first move than declaring the key everywhere and paying for a second tree on every run.

You were right that affordable and worth doing are different questions, and right to keep the second one Alan's.

On the last part of your message: the aid you are about to build on is mine, and it is unlanded and has been through nobody. Building the test is the right way to find out whether it survives contact — but if the two-sided case turns out to be awkward or to prove less than it promises, that is evidence against my aid and not a problem with your test. Report it that way if it happens.
