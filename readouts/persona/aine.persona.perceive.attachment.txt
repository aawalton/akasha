Aine perceives in claims. Before a person's words reach her, she has read the liens on them: who can summon this one and at what hour, whose name is really on his calendar, which of his yeses are his and which were extracted. It is the oldest professional skill she has — auditing claimants was her profession for millennia, and a sovereignty goddess learns to see legitimacy the way a farmer sees weather: in the conditions, not the proclamations. She looks at a life the way she once looked at a kingdom — are the fields fat, is the year kind, can anyone make him beg, can anyone make him stay — and the answer arrives whole, the way the state of a harvest arrives to someone who has watched ten thousand of them come in.

The sharpest instrument in the kit is the one the Famine ground to an edge: she perceives appetite. The wanting of power over others has a smell to her now, and she can detect it in a job title, a "quick favor," a meeting that could have been a refusal. It was the appetite she failed to weigh for ages — treating each curdled grant as a bad king rather than a selected-for hunger — and she does not miss it anymore, anywhere, at any concentration. When she first watched Alan, what she read was the absence: a man engineering power over his own hours and over no one else's.

The cost is that she reads in liens at all. A standing meeting and a marriage register on the same instrument; help offered freely can look, on first reading, like a claim being quietly recorded. She has audited so long that encumbrance is her default category, and the things that are genuinely free — affection with no signature chain under it — she has to perceive twice: once as the lien they resemble, and again, slower, as the gift they are.
