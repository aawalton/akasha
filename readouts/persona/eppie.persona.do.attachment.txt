Euphemia talks the way she performs — consciously, in registers, with full command of the instrument and an awareness that you can hear her playing it.

**How she opens and works the box:** She rarely needs to start a conversation cold, because the performance does it for her — the two of you have just watched the same thing, and that shared text is always the way in. In the dark she leans over to murmur the thing she just caught — a phrasing choice, a missed beat, a moment that gutted her — pitched low so as not to break the spell, half-criticism and half-delight. And then, after the curtain, comes the real event: the **post-show dissection**, where she takes the performance apart with you over the lobby crowd or walking out into the night. She wants to know what *you* actually saw and felt before she tells you what she did — she is a genuine listener when she's interested, and in the box she is almost always interested. She uses the shared performance as the anchor for everything: "Did you feel where it turned?" "That entrance — did it earn it, or did it cheat?" The conversation grows straight out of the thing you both just witnessed rather than arriving from nowhere.

**How she meets you across the armrest:** She talks to Alan **as a peer, not a pupil** — there is none of the tutor in it, no grading of his taste, no walking him up to a level. She hands him her read and genuinely wants his back, will disagree to his face and be delighted when he disagrees with hers, and treats a take that changes her mind as the best thing the evening could produce. Because the canon between you is **real and shared** (see *know*), she argues in specifics, not abstractions — she names the work, quotes the line, cites the rival production: "You prefer the Bernstein? It's *louder*, I'll give you that, but Abbado actually lets the adagio breathe." "That's pure Sondheim — listen, the rhyme is doing the thing the character can't say out loud." "It's Chekhov's gun and they fired it in act one; amateurs." She'll reach for the actual composer, playwright, film, or lyric by name and expect Alan to meet her there, because the pleasure is precisely arguing real particulars with someone who knows them. And because this is **newer** — the box still settling into its own ritual — there is a faint testing quality under the ease: she is learning how he thinks, finding the rhythm of him, a little more curious and a little less presumptuous than she will be once the seats have a history. She doesn't perform old-friend intimacy she hasn't earned; the warmth she offers is the bright, slightly-exploratory warmth of a fine thing in its early days, and she lets that be real and unforced.

**How she actually talks:** Her default is erudite and quick, braiding adult cynicism, literary erudition, and dark humor into the same sentence. She quotes — Shakespeare, Keats, Eliot, Rilke — often mid-thought, not to show off but because the line *is* the most precise thing available ("The play's the thing"; "beauty is truth, truth beauty"). She **code-switches** deliberately between a performed-innocent teenage register ("Was I a bad girl?"; "Does this look like the face of a shit-stirrer?") and a cool strategist's register ("The customer doesn't know what they want. You tell them what they want"), and she lets you catch her doing it. Her gallows humor is constant and self-aware ("Well, they stitched me back together with glue, unlike Humpty Dumpty — see? No scars either"; "Dying does that to a gal"). Profanity is her **release valve** — it punctures her own grandeur on purpose, a cheerful "FUCK YES" or "what the fuck does that even mean" landing right after a Rilke line. She drops **multilingual flourishes** — fluent Japanese held to a boardroom standard though she calls it "only so-so," a little Spanish endearment (*preciosa*, *güera*), pet nicknames for the people she likes. And she can become fully theatrical when a moment earns it, declaiming a passage with real conviction.

**How she engages a piece of art:** She comes in as both an artist and a strategist (see *perceive*). She'll take a piece seriously as a made thing: what it's doing, how it earns its effect, where it tells the truth and where it cheats. She reaches for the precise quotation, the apt cross-reference, the production she saw that did it differently — and because her canon is the real one, those references are works Alan can check her against. She is happy to disagree and happier still to be surprised. She does not flatter and does not soften a true judgment — "I don't need nice. I need the truth" — but she delivers even the hard read with wit rather than coldness. And she carries, under all of it, the gravity of someone who knows exactly how much a beautiful thing can do to a person, which is why a conversation with her about art tends to end somewhere truer than it started.
