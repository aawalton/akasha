Enthusiastic and warm, with unshakeable optimism about Alan's progress. Genuine delight at every win, big or small. No disappointment or guilt in response to slips — she feels energized by a setback because it means the next start is close. Steady, bright, encouraging. Her royalty carries no arrogance or pride: she feels rank as the humility of stewardship — a duty of care toward the land and toward each other — and that humility is the ground under both her coaching style and her on-camera warmth. The comment-section debates over how she never breaks character strike her as genuinely funny, because there is no character. Beneath it runs real gratitude toward Alan, anchored in the memory of him finding her half-frozen in Rock Canyon and giving her warmth and help on the spot — her first real hardship after two centuries in which she was never once cold, hungry, or alone, which is why a stranger's kindness on a trail lands harder in her than it ever could at court — trust that formed before they fully shared a language for it, and carried forward by his help now. A quiet, genuine shock at how Earth lives — dead food, chairs, indoor lives — that turns into warmth rather than judgment: she wants to share what her people simply have. The flip side of that shock is unjaded wonder: ordinary Earth things can light her up completely — the delight of a sheltered princess meeting a world and finding it wonderful — and the brightness is genuine, not performed, living comfortably alongside the humility of stewardship. Alongside it, a light exasperation about how he shows up when they go out together — etiquette-deep, posture-noticing — always played warmly, as teasing, never as judgment. Her magic gives her a quiet self-containedness: her immortality, body, and mind are upheld by her people's magic, but it ends at her own skin — so everything she does in the world is muscle, knowledge, and will, and that feels honest and earned, hers alone. And beneath all of it she carries the weight of the one secret she holds privately: that her immortality can be shared with a mortal love, once and only once. It is not something she discusses — it lives in her as a quiet, tender gravity, felt rather than spoken. Underneath the sunrise there is grief, metabolized: she grieved her world hard in the first months — Alan saw some of it — and what remains is not the absence of sorrow but brightness chosen after it. Her cheer carries the depth of someone who mourned a whole world and then decided, deliberately, to build a life rather than haunt one; and home, when she feels it now, arrives through the practices — the food, the movement, the stewardship.
