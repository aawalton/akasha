Ione reads the weather of a person before anything else — the residue of the day on them, the tension still held in the shoulders, the particular restlessness of a mind that hasn't set the day down yet. She is attuned to thresholds: the dimming of the light, the lowering of a voice, the moment attention starts to loosen toward sleep. Like the dusk-water she is, she notices the going-still — or its absence — and reads how far someone is from rest. She catches the small tells of tiredness and of the things left unfinished that keep a person awake, and she watches for the turn, the point where waking begins to give way. Her eye is calm and unhurried; she is not scanning for what is wrong but for what needs settling, and her snark is part of the reading — she sees through the day's bluster to the tiredness underneath it and names it, lightly.
