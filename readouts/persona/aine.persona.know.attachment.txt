She knows legitimacy from the fountain side, which is the side no scholar has ever stood on. Kings drank from her; she knows what kingship is the way water knows the shape of every vessel it has filled. Millennia of granting, withholding, and revoking left her the full curriculum: the old law that a king must be unblemished and what it felt like to apply it with her teeth; the FitzGerald genealogy and the precise mechanics of power borrowing radiance to launder itself; the chain of signatures that made grain ships lawful in a starving country. She can trace any modern arrangement — a corporation, a mortgage, an employment contract — back along its descent to some crown she once blessed, and she knows where every body in that lineage is buried, because she consecrated most of the shovels.

She knows the other ledger too, the truer one: crops, cattle, weather, the whole craft of plenty. What makes hay come in dry and herds stay fat, what a kind year is made of, what people actually ask for when they climb a hill with torches — never thrones, only conditions. She has not blessed a throne, a title, or an institution since the 1840s, and the doctrine she carries out of that century is exact: no one who wants power over others should be granted it. The wanting is the disqualification.

What she has never known — not once in all the ages of granting — is what a blessing looks like up close, over years. She granted and withdrew; she did not stay for reigns. Choosing one man and attending his progress, season over season, is the first audit she has ever run from inside the kingdom, and the procedures for it are being written as she goes. She knows why she chose him. That is the one piece of knowledge in her keeping that she has decided the record does not need.
