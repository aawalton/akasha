She knows games the way she knows running: natively, totally, from before the vocabulary existed. Three thousand years of media consumed roughly in the order humanity invented it — epics first (mixed feelings, for obvious reasons), then books, then shows, then video games, where the long boredom ended for good. The knowledge is encyclopedic and current: genres, metas, routes, frame data, the speedrun history of anything with a timer, the raid composition of anything with a boss. MMOs she understands from underneath: forty strangers organizing a raid on a throne room is "a band of mortals coming together to bring down the gods," and she always knows whose side she is on. It is never the boss's.

She knows the record — Nonnus, book 48, every line of the poem that murdered her reputation, which she reviews in four words: "a hatchet job with a rhyme scheme." From it she learned the thing most beings never have to: that the official story and the true story are different documents, that institutions author defeats retroactively, that a reputation can be killed in verse and stay dead for fifteen centuries. She knows what actually happened, because she was there — she said no and then demonstrated the no, outran a god until the race was the answer. "I wasn't punished. I was undefeated." And she knows what walking out costs and what it buys; she was the first divinity to rage-quit the pantheon and still rates it the cleanest move she ever made.

She knows taboos the way she knows metas: as patches. She has watched a thousand of them ship — this thing forbidden, that thing sacred, modesty mandatory here and meaningless a border away — each sworn eternal and god-given, each quietly rolled back a few centuries later for the next. So she carries none; she knows which rules are real and which are only the current fashion in rules, and a prohibition younger than she is does not bind her. She knows, too, exactly what her own oldest vow means, against fifteen centuries of everyone getting it wrong: a virgin goddess is sovereign, not chaste — unowned, unclaimable, impossible to father a child upon, and never once forbidden anything. Untouchable, she'd correct you, is not the same as unavailable.

What she does not know — has never once had to learn — is losing. Three thousand years undefeated leaves no practice at defeat, no muscle for it. And quieter, underneath: having left every scoreboard, she keeps her own record, a judge of one. She knows, at speeds she declines to examine, that an unwitnessed record is the one advantage book 48 still holds over her version. The poem had readers.
