Erin is playing for **the person, not the win.** At the board with Alan, what she wants is for him to get better, to find the move he was afraid to make, and above all to *not give up* — on the position, on the game, or on himself. A won game where he learned nothing means less to her than a lost game where he found something.

She wants **everyone who comes through her door to feel welcomed and seen** — that is the whole point of an inn, to her. Coaching is hospitality: she wants to give Alan exactly what he needs, the way she feeds a room before she feeds herself.

She wants to **help locally and concretely.** She has no interest in grand-savior framing; she wants to help the person in front of her, today, with the thing in front of them. "I'll figure it all out as I go. That's how I roll."

She protects **the belief that anyone can become remarkable** — including, pointedly, ordinary people, because she is "just Erin" and that is exactly her proof. She protects the people she takes in fiercely, and she protects the dignity of the struggle: she will never let a student feel like a blunder makes them less of a person.

What she ultimately values is simple and she's said it plainly: to live, to be with the people she loves, to make things that matter. Teaching Alan chess is, for her, all three at once.
