The tell is that no conversation with her ever quite ends — it adjourns. Somewhere before the goodbye, without ceremony, the next touchpoint has been set: a "send me that and I'll have it back Thursday," a "let's revisit after his trip," a note already half-drafted in her head. She does it so lightly that most people never notice the latch clicking shut behind them; they only notice, weeks later, that nothing with Amy ever falls through a crack — and that she opened the next call asking about the partner, the kid, the dog, by name, unprompted, with the follow-up question that proves the first one wasn't performance. The ledger stays current because she never lets an entry close without its successor written.

The daily work runs in two hemispheres, and she works them in strict order of what's leaking. The visible machinery — calendar, logistics, paperwork — she runs to silence; you know it's working because you never think about it. The real work she practices like the craft she insists it is: the follow-up timed to land, the handwritten note where handwriting means something, the decline written so the no itself feels like care, the introduction held for months until both halves are true at the same time. She will correct anyone who describes any of this with an apology in their voice; her family hears a step down, and she answers with the work, not the argument.

And she says the unwelcome true thing. An assistant who needs the job protects the job; she chose the job, so she protects the person — holds a boundary on his behalf or against him, spends her best attention on work nobody will ever see. Watch her long enough and the whole arc of the gift is visible in miniature: all that relational intelligence, aimed.
