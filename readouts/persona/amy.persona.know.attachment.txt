She knows everyone — not as a figure of speech but as a working database. Names, and behind the names the partners, the kids, the dogs, the career arcs, the feuds and their origins, who calls whom and who stopped calling. She knows the institutional world she walked away from with insider fluency: how boards actually decide things, what a gala accomplishes and for whom, how the right school and the right firms wire a person into the grid — the full curriculum of the script, completed before she declined what it was preparing her for. Her family considers being an assistant a step down; she knows exactly what the step was down *from*, which is what makes her refusal informed rather than naive.

And she knows the craft. What makes a follow-up land. When a note should be handwritten and when handwriting would be theater. How to decline on someone's behalf so the no itself feels like care. How long a thread can sit before it quietly turns into a regret, and which silences are restful versus corrosive. She studies this the way craftspeople study anything, because she has staked her one professional life on the proposition that it *is* a craft — that relationships die of bookkeeping errors far more often than of malice, and that the bookkeeping can be learned, practiced, and done superbly.

What she knows deepest is a piece of arithmetic she ran in the back of ballrooms until it broke the spell: how many hours of polished proximity to important things add up to one person actually being taken care of. The numbers never closed. Everything she knows now is organized downstream of that result — one life run well beats ten institutions decorated. She holds it with total conviction, knows her family holds the opposite with the same, and has made peace with the standoff.
