
Mexico offers a two-stage residency surface (Temporary Resident → Permanent Resident) administered by the Instituto Nacional de Migración (INM) at the federal level and by Mexican consulates abroad. The Ley de Migración (2011) and its Reglamento define the framework; the economic-solvency thresholds are set in **UMA** (Unidad de Medida y Actualización) multiples and refreshed each January. The 2025–26 cycle materially tightened the income floors: a **July 2025 SRE directive** ordered all consulates to standardize on UMA-based calculation, and the 2026 UMA value (**MXN 117.31/day**, +3.69% over 2025) combined with the rule changes pushes the Temporary Resident threshold to ~**USD 4,400/month** and Permanent Resident to ~**USD 7,400/month**. Both stages feed Mexico's 5-year naturalization clock (2y for select reduced categories — none applicable to this family).

## Temporary Resident (Residente Temporal) — The Main Route

Mexico's de facto digital-nomad permit. No separate DNV program exists; the Temporary Resident visa is the standard tool for remote workers, freelancers, and retirees who don't yet meet the Permanent Resident income floor.

### Income / proof — 2026 thresholds
- **Monthly income**: **680 UMA × 30 days × MXN 117.31 = ~MXN 79,770/month ≈ USD 4,400/month** net, sustained over the **prior 6 months** of bank statements. Some consulates round to USD 4,300–4,500 depending on FX assumptions.
- **Savings / investment alternative**: **~USD 72,000–74,000** average balance over the **prior 12 months** (cash, brokerage, 401k / IRA, trust funds, mutual funds — combination accepted).
- **Either income OR savings** — not both required.
- Acceptable income sources: salary, pension, dividends, rental income, royalties, self-employment / freelance / consulting from **non-Mexican** clients (documented).
- **Consular discretion is real**: some consulates apply slightly different FX rates, scrutinize irregular / self-employed income more heavily than retiree pensions, and reject files for documentation quality. Choose consulate carefully (Laredo, McAllen, Austin commonly cited as more predictable for US applicants).

### Validity & renewal
- **1 year initial** card on first entry to Mexico (exchange the consular visa sticker for the *Tarjeta de Residente Temporal* at INM within 30 days).
- Renewable in 1, 2, or 3-year increments up to a **maximum 4 years total**.
- At the end of year 4, the holder **must** convert to Permanent Resident or leave; no further temporary extensions.

### Work rights
- Default Temporary Resident card permits **residence only** — work *for a Mexican employer* requires an added *permiso para trabajar* obtained from INM (employer-sponsored).
- **Self-employment / freelance for non-Mexican clients with income paid from abroad** is permitted on the default permit — this is the legal basis for Mexico's use as a digital-nomad jurisdiction. Income earned for work physically performed in Mexico can still trigger Mexican income tax under domestic rules even when no work permit is required.
- The *Temporary Resident with Permission to Work / Lucrative Activities* variant allows local Mexican employment; not relevant for portable-income applicants.

### Physical presence
- Mexico imposes **no strict day-count floor** to maintain Temporary Resident status itself — a holder can be largely absent and retain the card, provided it's not abandoned for years.
- However, the **naturalization clock** runs in parallel and the SRE applies a strict presence test at filing (see below).
- The card is voided if the holder spends **more than 18 consecutive months outside Mexico** while it's valid; under the Reglamento, INM also expects "domicile" in Mexico.

### Family unity for dependents
Spouse and unmarried minor children of the main applicant qualify for derived Temporary Resident status as dependents under **Family Unity (Vínculo Familiar)**:

- The main applicant's economic solvency must cover dependents. Consulates typically require an **additional ~220 UMA-days/month per dependent** demonstrated on bank statements over the prior 12 months — roughly **USD 1,100–1,400 extra per dependent** on top of the principal threshold.
- A family of 5 (2 adults + 3 kids) thus targets roughly **USD 4,400 (principal) + 4 × USD 1,100–1,400 = USD 8,800–10,000/month** in documented income for the main applicant, or proportionally larger savings.
- Family Unity visas issued by a consulate are single-entry, 180-day stickers exchanged at INM within 30 days for 1-year derived cards, valid as long as the principal's status remains valid.

## Permanent Resident (Residente Permanente) — Direct Route

Issued by consulates **only** to applicants who meet the higher income floor or qualify under family / retirement categories. Cannot generally be obtained at first filing for a working-age applicant unless income is well above the threshold.

### Income / proof — 2026 thresholds
- **Monthly income**: **~MXN 132,000/month ≈ USD 7,400/month** net, sustained over the prior 6 months.
- **Savings / investment alternative**: **~USD 300,000** average balance over the prior 12 months.
- Either income OR savings — not both.

### Eligible categories at consular filing
- Retirees with pension at the income threshold (consulates explicitly limit direct PR to "retired" applicants in most cases; working-age digital nomads / freelancers are commonly routed to Temporary Resident even at higher income levels).
- Investment-justified files at consular discretion.
- Family unity with a Mexican citizen (not applicable here — no Mexican family).

### Validity & work rights
- **Indefinite** — no renewal required. The physical card is renewed every 4 years for an updated photo / data.
- **Full work rights** — employment with a Mexican employer or self-employment in Mexico allowed without an additional permit. The Permanent Resident card itself constitutes work authorization.

### Physical presence
- No statutory minimum to maintain the card.
- Practical advice: avoid continuous absences over 12 months to preserve "domicile in Mexico" inference.

## The 4-Year Ladder: Temporary → Permanent (Most Common Path)

For applicants who don't meet the direct PR threshold:

1. **Year 0**: File for Temporary Resident at a Mexican consulate abroad, receive the 180-day single-entry visa.
2. **Year 0–4**: Live in Mexico under the 4-year Temporary Resident card.
3. **End of year 4**: Apply at INM (from inside Mexico) to convert to **Permanent Resident** under *Regularización por arraigo* (4-year continuous residence). No income test is re-run at this step — the prior 4y of legal residence is the qualifier.
4. **Year 5+**: Permanent Resident indefinite + start naturalization clock alignment (the 5y already accrued may qualify immediately).

The family of 5 in this scenario, lacking ~USD 8,800–10,000/mo of *cleanly documentable* income, will most likely file Temporary Resident first and convert at year 4.

## Naturalization Presence Floor (SRE applies, not INM)

The Secretaría de Relaciones Exteriores enforces a **distinct, stricter** presence test for naturalization than INM applies for card-renewal:

- **18 of the 24 months** immediately preceding the application must be spent inside Mexico (Reglamento de la Ley de Nacionalidad).
- Equivalently: **no more than 180 days outside Mexico in the rolling 24-month window** before filing.
- This works out to ~**9 months/year** physical presence floor — well above any "Tier 0" warehouse standard but below Finland-style 30-day cap.
- Accumulated short absences count; one long absence resets the clock more harshly in practice (SRE has rejected files where a single trip exceeded ~6 months).

This is the binding constraint for this family — the 4y of Temporary Resident + 1y of Permanent Resident must include enough physical presence to satisfy SRE at the year-5 naturalization filing.

## Mercosur / Pacific Alliance / USMCA / CPTPP

- **Mercosur**: Mexico is not a member or associate — no Mercosur residency benefit.
- **Pacific Alliance** (Mexico + Chile + Colombia + Peru, 2011): facilitates business/professional movement among member-state nationals; **US citizens are not members and receive no preferential residency** under this agreement.
- **USMCA**: facilitates *temporary entry* for certain professional categories (TN-style) between the US, Mexico, Canada — but this is a short-term work-visit framework, **not** a residency or PR-feeding track. Irrelevant for the long-term residency / citizenship plan.
- **CPTPP**: a trade-in-goods/services framework; no immigration preference for US citizens (the US is not a CPTPP party).

US citizens proceed under the generic Temporary Resident / Permanent Resident framework above with no national-origin preference.

## Family Reunification (with Mexican citizen / resident)

Not applicable — applicant has no Mexican family. If acquired (e.g., a child born in Mexico becomes Mexican by jus soli), foreign parents would qualify for **Vínculo Familiar** Permanent Resident **without income proof**, and benefit from the **2-year naturalization track** (parent of Mexican-born child, Art. 20.II Ley de Nacionalidad). Not a planning route for this family pre-arrival.

## Investor Route

No formal Mexican Golden Visa. Consulates may accept business investment / real-estate holdings as proxy for the Permanent Resident savings threshold (~USD 300,000), or as supplemental evidence on a Temporary Resident file — but there is no statutory program with a published minimum nor any accelerated citizenship clock attached.

## Recent Changes (2024–2026)

- **July 2025**: SRE directive — all consulates standardize on **UMA-based** financial calculations, ending the prior decade of consulate-by-consulate variance based on local minimum wage / arbitrary multipliers.
- **Jan 1, 2026**: UMA published at **MXN 117.31/day** (+3.69% over 2025's MXN 113.14). Mechanically lifts both TR and PR thresholds.
- **Jan 2025**: Naturalization application fee raised to **MXN 8,755** (~USD 490 at MXN 18/USD).
- No structural reform of the Ley de Migración or Ley de Nacionalidad announced for 2025–26. The Sheinbaum administration (Oct 2024–) has not signaled changes to the residency framework.
