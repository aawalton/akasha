
## Jus sanguinis — citizenship by descent

### Bilineal jus sanguinis (Nationality Act Article 2(1))
- **Eligibility**: a child is Japanese at birth if either parent is a Japanese national at the time of the child's birth. Bilineal since the 1985 reform — both maternal and paternal lines transmit equally. Applies to children born inside or outside Japan with no generational limit. Post-birth paternal acknowledgment by a Japanese father can confer nationality on a child born to an unmarried foreign mother (2008 Supreme Court ruling reading down the prior in-wedlock requirement).
- **Timeline**: automatic at birth; consular birth report must be filed within 3 months of a birth abroad or the child must elect Japanese nationality by age 22 (Reservation of Nationality, Article 12).
- **Dual citizenship**: technically prohibited — Article 14 requires anyone holding a foreign nationality alongside Japanese to elect one before turning 22 (or within 2 years of acquiring the second nationality). In practice, the Ministry of Justice does not actively police election declarations, and many dual-national children born abroad keep both passports quietly. The 2023 Supreme Court ruling on Article 11 (see below) hardened the policy against adults who voluntarily naturalize elsewhere, but did not change enforcement against jus-sanguinis dual nationals from birth.

## Jus soli safeguards against statelessness (Article 2(3))

### Birth in Japan to unknown or stateless parents
- **Eligibility**: a child born in Japan is Japanese at birth if (a) both parents are unknown, or (b) both parents are stateless. A child born in Japan to identifiable but stateless parents may also be granted nationality via simplified naturalization after 3 years of residence (Article 8(4)).
- **Timeline**: automatic at birth (parents unknown) or after the 3-year residence path.
- **Dual citizenship**: not applicable — Japanese by birth.

## Naturalization (kika / 帰化)

Naturalization is fully discretionary — Article 5(1) lists conditions the Minister of Justice "may not" waive, but meeting them does not create a statutory right. Approval is a Ministry of Justice decision with no judicial review on the merits.

### Standard naturalization — Article 5
- **Statutory requirements (Article 5(1))**:
  - 5 continuous years of domicile in Japan
  - 18 years or older with legal capacity under home-country law
  - Good conduct (zenko-shōmei) — clean criminal, tax, traffic, and social-insurance record
  - Independent livelihood — financial self-sufficiency through own assets/skills or those of a household member
  - Renunciation of original citizenship (Article 5(1)(v)) — either be stateless or be willing to renounce upon acquisition
  - No history of seeking to overthrow the Constitution
- **Operational (April 2026 tightening)**: per Ministry of Justice screening-practice revisions effective 1 April 2026, applicants are now in practice expected to show **~10 years of residence history, 5 years of tax records, and 2 years of social insurance payment records** before approval. The statute still reads "5 years"; the screening bar has effectively doubled.
- **Informal requirements** (not in statute, applied in screening):
  - Japanese language at ~3rd-grade reading/writing level (interview, sometimes a written test)
  - Adoption of a Japanese-style name on the family register (no longer strictly required by policy, but still commonly suggested by case officers)
- **Timeline**: 12–24 months from filing complete documents at the local Legal Affairs Bureau to MOJ decision; typical recent cases trend toward the high end (18–24 months).
- **Dual citizenship**: must renounce the prior nationality after the MOJ approves naturalization; the **September 2023 Supreme Court ruling** (8-plaintiff Swiss/Liechtenstein case) upheld Article 11(1)'s loss-of-Japanese-nationality rule and, by extension, the renunciation regime as constitutional. For naturalizers (as opposed to jus-sanguinis dual nationals), the renunciation step is documented and enforced.

### Simplified naturalization — Article 6 (relaxed residence)
- **Eligibility**: domicile in Japan currently, plus one of:
  - Continuous domicile in Japan for 3+ years and a parent (other than an adoptive parent) who was born in Japan
  - Born in Japan and continuously domiciled for 3+ years (or a parent born in Japan)
  - Continuous domicile for 10+ years
- **Other Article 5 conditions (age, conduct, livelihood, renunciation) still apply** — "simplified" only relaxes the residence and capacity criteria.
- **Historical use**: this provision (combined with Article 8) is the route most Zainichi Koreans and Taiwanese — descendants of pre-1945 Japanese nationals who lost it under the 1952 San Francisco Peace Treaty implementation — have used to renaturalize.

### Simplified naturalization — Article 7 (spouse of Japanese national)
- **Eligibility**: either
  - Spouse of a Japanese national with 3+ years of continuous domicile in Japan and currently domiciled, OR
  - Spouse of a Japanese national married 3+ years and continuously domiciled for 1+ year
- **Article 5 livelihood/conduct/renunciation conditions still apply.**
- **Timeline**: 8–14 months — generally the fastest practical path for adult applicants with no Japanese ancestry.

### Simplified naturalization — Article 8 (close family / former-national lines)
- **Eligibility (selected)**: child (non-adopted) of a Japanese national domiciled in Japan; adopted child domiciled 1+ year who was a minor under home-country law at adoption; former Japanese national domiciled in Japan; person born in Japan, never having had any nationality (i.e., stateless from birth), domiciled 3+ years.
- **Notes**: Article 8(4) is the operative recovery route for stateless persons born in Japan.

### Extraordinary naturalization — Article 9 (distinguished service)
- **Eligibility**: foreign national who has rendered "special distinguished service" to Japan. The Minister of Justice may grant naturalization notwithstanding Article 5 conditions, **with prior approval of the National Diet**.
- **Practical status**: **never invoked.** The provision exists but has not been used in a single case since enactment.

## Loss and recovery of Japanese nationality

### Article 11 — automatic loss upon voluntary acquisition of foreign nationality
- A Japanese national who voluntarily acquires a foreign nationality loses Japanese nationality automatically by operation of law. The September 2023 Supreme Court ruling (rejecting the 8-plaintiff challenge in the Swiss/Liechtenstein case) confirmed Article 11(1) is constitutional, citing prevention of conflicts of diplomatic protection, military service, and taxation.
- This is the core reason Japan is described as banning dual citizenship: jus-sanguinis dual nationals from birth can keep both quietly, but a Japanese adult who naturalizes elsewhere loses Japanese status the moment they do.

### Reacquisition (Article 17)
- A former Japanese national who lost nationality under Article 11/12 may reacquire it by domicile in Japan plus a notification to the Minister of Justice, subject to discretionary refusal grounds. In practice, MOJ treats Article 17 narrowly; the more common route for former nationals is simplified naturalization under Article 8(2).

## Dual-citizenship reality check

- **Law**: Articles 11, 14, 15, 16 form a complete renunciation regime — voluntary foreign-acquisition triggers automatic loss; dual nationals from birth must elect one by age 22 (Article 14); failure to elect can trigger a ministerial notice and ultimately loss (Article 15).
- **Practice**: enforcement against jus-sanguinis dual nationals from birth is sparse — the MOJ rarely audits passports, and many Japanese-American/-European/-etc. citizens carry both quietly for life. For naturalizers, by contrast, the renunciation step is documented at the point of approval and **must** be completed; there is no informal tolerance.
- **Direction of policy**: the 2023 Supreme Court ruling closes off the most plausible constitutional challenge route. Diet bills to liberalize Article 11 (allowing emigrant Japanese to keep nationality while naturalizing abroad) have been introduced but not advanced.
