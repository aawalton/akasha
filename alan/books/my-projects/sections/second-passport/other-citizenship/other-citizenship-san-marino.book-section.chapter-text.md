
San Marino has roughly 33,000 citizens and is famously one of Europe's most restrictive naturalization regimes — historically requiring 30 years of residence (reduced to 20 in subsequent amendments) and 10 years for spouses. Nationality law is governed by Law no. 114 of 30 November 2000 (the "Law on Citizenship"), as amended in 2004, 2016, 2019, 2021, and 2026. The 2026 amendment is the most consequential recent change: San Marino now fully permits dual citizenship for naturalized citizens, ending the prior renunciation requirement. Each naturalization (other than statutory paths) requires a discretionary act of the Grand and General Council (Consiglio Grande e Generale) passed by two-thirds majority — there is no automatic administrative right.

## Citizenship by descent and birth

### Jus sanguinis at birth

A child born to two Sammarinese citizen parents acquires citizenship automatically at birth, regardless of place of birth. Where only one parent is Sammarinese, the child has a claim that must be confirmed by a declaration of intent — both parents declare within 12 months after the child reaches 18 that the child elects Sammarinese citizenship of the citizen parent. Failure to make the declaration in window forfeits the claim. Transmission is direct parent-to-child only; there is no Italian-style multi-generational jus sanguinis through grandparents or great-grandparents.

### Anti-statelessness jus soli

A child born in San Marino acquires citizenship at birth if both parents are stateless, or if the child is found abandoned in San Marino with both parents unknown (foundling rule). There is no general jus soli — birth on Sammarinese territory to foreign parents does not by itself confer citizenship.

## Naturalization by residence (statutory)

### Standard naturalization — 20 years residence

A foreigner holding a valid residence permit who has lived continuously in San Marino for 20 years may petition for naturalization. (The historical threshold was 30 years; subsequent amendments reduced it to 20.) The application is forwarded to the Grand and General Council, which examines petitions at least every six months and grants citizenship by special law approved by 2/3 majority. Requirements: clean record (no criminal conviction in San Marino or abroad; no prison sentence exceeding 1 year), Italian-language knowledge, demonstrated integration, stable income and financial independence. Even after 20 years and meeting all conditions, the Council's vote is discretionary — there is no automatic right to naturalization.

### Reduced naturalization for spouses — 10 years marriage and residence

The residence period drops to 10 years for the foreign spouse of a Sammarinese citizen, provided the marriage and the residence are both continuous over the period. Same other conditions (clean record, language, integration). Same Council 2/3-majority approval. Older sources mention a 15-year marriage threshold; the current threshold under the consolidated law is 10 years.

## Parliamentary grant (Consiglio Grande e Generale)

### Citizenship by special law

In practice every naturalization runs through the Grand and General Council via a special law passed by 2/3 majority. The Council reviews accumulated petitions in batches at least twice yearly. There is no separate "distinguished service" route codified as a numerical eligibility shortcut — Council discretion is the route. The Council can in principle grant citizenship outside the standard residence thresholds (e.g., to individuals of recognized merit to the Republic), but such grants are exceptional, politically negotiated, and not advertised as a public program. Petitioners cannot apply for the "merit" path; they can only have their case advanced by political sponsors.

## Loss and re-acquisition

### Voluntary renunciation

A Sammarinese citizen may renounce citizenship voluntarily, provided renunciation does not render the person stateless. The post-2026 dual-citizenship rule means most renunciations are now unnecessary — Sammarinese citizens may freely hold other nationalities without losing San Marino citizenship.

### Involuntary loss

Naturalized citizenship may be revoked if it was obtained by fraud, false representation, or concealment of material facts. Jus-sanguinis citizenship is more robust and generally cannot be stripped.

### Re-acquisition

Re-acquisition petitions follow the same procedural path — submitted to the Grand and General Council and considered alongside acquisition petitions in the twice-yearly review cycles. Specific terms are not codified as a public administrative right; the Council retains discretion.

## Dual citizenship

Until the 2026 amendment, San Marino required renunciation of all prior citizenships as a condition of naturalization, and Sammarinese citizens who voluntarily acquired a foreign nationality risked loss of San Marino citizenship. **The 2026 amendment to Law 114/2000 fully legalized dual citizenship**, both for incoming naturalized citizens (no renunciation required) and for outgoing Sammarinese citizens acquiring a second nationality. This is a recent and material change — pre-2026 guidance from older sources is now outdated. Anyone naturalizing under the 20-year or 10-year-spouse paths from 2026 onward may retain their original passport.

## Practical assessment

For US citizens with no Sammarinese ancestry and no Sammarinese spouse, the only realistic path is 20 years of continuous legal residence followed by a Grand and General Council vote that is not guaranteed. With a Sammarinese spouse, the timeline compresses to 10 years but the Council vote remains discretionary. There is no investment-based or fast-track naturalization. The 2026 dual-citizenship reform removes the renunciation barrier, materially improving the proposition for those willing to commit to long-term residence, but it does not shorten the timeline. San Marino is best understood as a residency destination (with attractive tax regimes for HNW individuals and pensioners) where citizenship is a slow, uncertain by-product rather than a planned outcome.
