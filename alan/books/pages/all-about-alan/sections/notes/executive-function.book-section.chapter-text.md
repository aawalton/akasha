
# Executive function

> Executive function — per-EF detail (11 EFs), recurring patterns across the list (compensation hides deficit, modulator-driven asymmetry, aphantasia restructures, EF budget), and compensation strategies.

Per-EF detail and Alan's specific experience. The 11 EFs are listed and defined in [adhd-as-neurotype.md](adhd-as-neurotype.book-chapter.md); definitions are not repeated here. The captures below follow the order of that list.

## General compensation strategy: the EF budget

Executive function is metabolically expensive. The general strategy across all EFs:

> Don't pay the cost when I don't have to (accept the impulse) so that I can afford to pay the cost when I need to (inappropriate impulse).

Concretely: do not fight every impulse, every distraction, every shift. Let most pass through — acknowledged but not acted on — and reserve the budget for the cases that would actually cause harm or violate the goal. The framing applies first to response inhibition, but generalizes (with adjustments) across several other EFs (see *Patterns across EFs* below).

## Per-EF detail

### Response inhibition

- **Severity.** Significant historically — caused real distress before he understood the mechanism. Now manageable via the general strategy.
- **Manifestation pattern.** A trigger (internal thought or external stimulus) generates an impulse to respond; the impulse is often not appropriate to the environment.
- **Specific intrusive form.** Intrusive impulsive thoughts of the "what would happen if I [did extreme thing]?" shape — e.g., a "what if I jumped off this bridge?" thought. Felt possible enough to be scary before the mechanism was understood. Once reframed as response-inhibition signal rather than real intent, much less distressing.
- **Standard form.** Trigger → impulse → response not appropriate to the environment, in the way most people experience response-inhibition lapses (the impulse is normal; the regulation is what is expensive).
- **Compensation.** The general strategy applies directly — accept the impulse (don't pay the inhibition cost on benign or non-actionable impulses) so the inhibition budget is available for impulses that would actually be harmful. Letting the bridge thought pass through, rather than fighting it, is itself the system.

### Working memory

- **Severity.** Hits hard. Numerical anchor: typical working-memory capacity is around 5±2 chunks; Alan ranges 1-3 typically — roughly half of normal.
- **Recognition compensates for recall externally.** People often think Alan has an excellent memory because his recognition is strong and his conceptual chaining is dense — given a reference he recognizes it and pulls a wide network of associated context. The conflation of "memory" with "recall" lets recognition strength stand in for working-memory weakness, hiding the deficit from outside observers. (See [recognition-vs-recall.md](recognition-vs-recall.book-chapter.md).)
- **Compensation.** Extensive externalization systems built up over years. Specific systems detailed in the systems notes (TBD — see threads).

### Emotional control

- **Severity.** Hard to rate apples-to-apples with the other EFs — the aphantasia + alexithymia composition makes the EF rating ambiguous (see Mechanism).
- **Mechanism — body / conscious dichotomy.** Recent self-understanding (within the last few weeks): emotions are *present at body level at stronger-than-normal force*, but *largely absent from conscious experience*. Aphantasia extends to emotional recall; alexithymia-adjacent limits constrain recognition of current emotions. The result is a dichotomy — conscious experience has almost no emotional content while physical experience is deeply emotionally affected. Canonical home for the underlying mechanism: [alexithymia.md → conscious vs. unconscious emotional architecture](alexithymia.book-chapter.md#conscious-vs-unconscious-emotional-architecture) and [aphantasia-mechanism.md](aphantasia-mechanism.book-chapter.md).
- **The Spock pattern.** Most of the time Alan appears under constant conscious control to an unusual degree. In rare circumstances the powerful body-level emotion breaks through and overrides the conscious control. From the outside, emotional control reads as exceptional; from the inside, the EF is shouldering more force than typical and is therefore more brittle when overload finally hits.

### Sustained attention

- **Severity (asymmetric).** The conventional naming is misleading. The issue is not sustained attention as such — it is *sustained attention without interest*. Alan struggles sharply on the no-interest side; on the interest side his sustained attention is exceptional.
- **Numerical anchor.** When following his interests, Alan sustains focused attention for roughly 12 hours continuously, daily. By the standard EF framing this would look like an EF *strength* rather than an impairment — but it is the same EF, just with the interest modulator on.
- **Composition (AuDHD).** Hyperfocus on interest is commonly attributed to ADHD, but it composes with autism's special-interests intensity (see [special-interests.md](special-interests.book-chapter.md)). The 12-hour figure is plausibly the AuDHD composition rather than ADHD alone.

#### The college grade inversion — executive dysfunction showing through before it had a name

The clearest early evidence of the sustained-attention asymmetry is a grade pattern that runs backwards from almost everyone else's. In his college-at-sixteen years (see [self-chosen-difficulty.md](self-chosen-difficulty.book-chapter.md)), Alan got **A's in his hard courses and B's in his easy ones**. For nearly everyone, grades fall as difficulty climbs — the hard courses are where the B's and C's land. Alan's slope is inverted.

The mechanism, in his words: *"Attention. Easy courses require executive function to keep my attention while hard courses had the interest and attention built-in. I didn't understand that I had executive dysfunction from AuDHD back then, but in retrospect, it makes perfect sense."*

The inversion is the sustained-attention modulator made visible in an outcome metric:

- **Easy courses taxed him.** With no built-in interest, the only way to hold attention on easy material was to *manufacture* it by hand — pay the executive-function cost to keep focus on something the interest channel wasn't supplying. That hand-fed attention is expensive and leaky, so the easy courses landed at B's.
- **Hard courses were free.** The difficulty supplied the interest, and the interest supplied the attention. With the modulator on, sustained attention is exceptional rather than impaired — so the hard courses landed at A's, with the attention coming for nothing.

Inverted observation: the pattern reads from the outside as "good at hard things, careless about easy ones." The mechanism is the opposite of carelessness — it is **executive dysfunction**, specifically the cost of manufacturing attention without interest, showing through in the grades years before Alan had the AuDHD diagnosis to name it. The conventional reading (grades track difficulty) assumes attention is a flat resource you spend on whatever's in front of you; for Alan attention is interest-gated, so the difficulty axis and the attention-cost axis run opposite ways. Same modulator as the 12-hour-on-interest figure above — here measured as a transcript instead of a workday.

### Task initiation

- **Severity.** Hits significantly. Standard manifestation — the wall at "start," especially on tasks that are unappealing or ambiguous.
- **Compensation — recovery rule "If I don't want to, I can't."** Active during burnout recovery (see [autism-burnout.md](autism-burnout.book-chapter.md)). When the task is not wanted, Alan does not force initiation; he waits until want exists.
- **Cost trade-off.** "Shoulding" through resistance has a massive nervous-system cost (see [nervous-system-sensitivity.md](nervous-system-sensitivity.book-chapter.md)). The rule explicitly accepts late-completion of important things as cheaper than the shoulding cost — sometimes things get done later than they should, but that cost is judged smaller than the alternative.
- **Parallel to response inhibition.** Same shape as the inhibition-budget strategy: don't pay the EF cost when you don't have to. Generalizes the EF-budget framing across initiation, not only inhibition.

### Planning / prioritization

- **Severity (raw).** Significant — Alan reports problems caused by the EF, but routed through his compensation systems rather than experienced as a raw deficit. Reframe surfaced this chunk: the EF *is* impaired; the compensation has been so robust and so habitual that it hid the deficit from his own self-model.
- **Compensation — written plans 10× more detailed than peers.** Long-habituated, so much so that it has read as a planning *strength* from the outside (and from the inside) rather than as a coping mechanism for an underlying impairment.
- **Problems still occur, routed through the compensation.** The visible cost is not the absence of planning; it is the over-robustness of the planning systems themselves. Specifics not yet captured — probable directions: time spent planning vs. doing, plan rigidity once built, over-engineering, an all-or-nothing relationship to the plan. Probe in a follow-up.

### Organization

- **Severity (raw).** Significant — same shape as planning / prioritization. Without robust organizational systems, Alan would lose track of objects, files, and information daily.
- **Compensation — robust organizational systems.** Same shape as planning: compensation has been built up to the point of carrying the EF; the underlying impairment is invisible from the outside and partly from the inside.

### Time management

- **Severity.** Hits, but qualitatively different from standard ADHD "time blindness." The aphantasia interaction restructures the EF.
- **Mechanism (root).** Sense of time appears to be primarily grounded in sensory memory. With no sensory memory (aphantasia), there is no *intrinsic* sense of time — only explicit conceptual notice via external markers (clocks, calendars). Full mechanism + hypotheses + lived consequence in [time-perception.md](time-perception.book-chapter.md).
- **Compensation.** Explicit conceptual notice. Calendars, time-blocking, deliberate "what time is it now?" check-ins. Without these, time effectively does not pass for him.
- **Lived feel.** "Old for his age" since childhood; comfortable with effectively infinite time horizons; felt ageless always. Detail in [time-perception.md](time-perception.book-chapter.md).

### Goal-directed persistence

- **Self-label: "serial quitter."** When interest drops, Alan quits. If the goal truly matters, interest returns and he restarts.
- **Reframe.** *Persistence over long time scales rather than consistency.* The conventional EF definition assumes persistence = consistent forward effort; Alan's persistence runs in quit-restart cycles, with the *truly-mattering* filter selecting which arcs come back.
- **Composition with other patterns.**
  - Interest modulator (same shape as sustained attention).
  - Recovery rule "If I don't want to, I can't" (same EF-budget logic as task initiation).
  - Effectively-infinite time horizon (see [time-perception.md](time-perception.book-chapter.md)) — long-arc persistence is only viable if subjective time supports decade-scale return cycles.

### Flexibility

- **Severity (inverted): a strength, not an impairment.** Alan is very flexible. The mechanism is aphantasia-driven — with no sensory or emotional recall, there is *no emotional momentum* tying him to the original plan or strategy. Pivoting is cheap because there is no felt pull toward the previous course.
- **Mechanism causal chain.** Most people carry an emotional / sensory inertia that resists redirection; aphantasia removes that inertia (see [aphantasia-mechanism.md](aphantasia-mechanism.book-chapter.md)). Cognitive flexibility — the EF as conventionally defined — is therefore largely a *consequence* of aphantasia for Alan, rather than a learned skill. This is the same mechanism behind his inverse-of-typical transition ease at the activity level (see [routines-scripts-systems.md](routines-scripts-systems.book-chapter.md)).
- **Caveat — capacity margin.** Alan lives with almost no margin in his capacity. Unexpected external demands can therefore be extremely damaging — but this is a *capacity* failure, not a *flexibility* failure. The pivot itself is easy; what is costly is paying for an unbudgeted demand on a system that has no slack.

### Metacognition

- **Severity (asymmetric).** Live (real-time) metacognition is really difficult; Alan usually does not succeed at noticing in the moment. Explicit *conceptual* introspection is exceptionally strong — Alan has a reputation for being incredibly introspective, but the introspection that earns that reputation is deliberate, retrospective, and analytic, not real-time.
- **Compensation hides the deficit.** Same shape as working memory, planning, and organization — strong explicit-conceptual review compensates for and externally masks the live-monitoring deficit.
- **Probable mechanism (aphantasia).** Live metacognition typically depends on a felt sense that "this isn't working" — a sensory / emotional self-monitoring signal. Without that signal (aphantasia), the only available path is explicit conceptual review, which is necessarily slower and offline. Hypothesis to verify.

## EF (Mana) was once the binding constraint — and what retired it

For a stretch, executive function was not just impaired-but-compensated — it was the **scarcest bar in the whole [four-resource model](four-resource-model.book-chapter.md)**, the [migrating bottleneck](four-resource-model.book-chapter.md#the-migrating-bottleneck-governor) governing everything else, the way Safety governs now. EF/[Mana](mana.book-chapter.md) had a real turn on the throne. Then three things retired it and the throne passed to Safety: nervous-system recovery (slowest, the one Alan expects to matter most), the harness, and Vyvanse (fastest and most noticeable, but not the largest). The full migration and the noticeable-vs-largest axis are in [four-resource-model.md → Mana was a real former bottleneck](four-resource-model.book-chapter.md#mana-was-a-real-former-bottleneck--the-migration-ran-ef--safety).

### The harness as a dated event, not only standing compensation

The [agent harness](agent-harness.book-chapter.md) is framed elsewhere as *standing compensation* — the permanent externalization of the EF Alan can't hold internally (external state-keeping, explicit valuation, action-sequence scaffolding; see [agent-harness.md → Compensation](agent-harness.book-chapter.md#compensation--what-the-harness-substitutes-for)). That framing is the steady-state role. This capture adds the other face: the harness also functioned as a **dated event** in the bottleneck history. Building it out did not merely hold EF level day to day — it **relieved the binding EF constraint**, one of the three movers that retired EF as the bottleneck. So the harness is both: a permanent prosthetic that runs every day, and a one-time relocation of the load-bearing point that helped pass the throne from EF to Safety. The standing-compensation reading and the event reading are not in tension — the same system did both.

Alan's anchor, asked whether the harness alone retired EF: *"Close, the harness helps, but general nervous system recovery also has an impact."* The harness was necessary but not sufficient — the slow nervous-system mover did the larger share.

## Patterns across EFs

The eleven per-EF captures organize more naturally by recurring pattern than by linear severity. Four patterns surfaced; they are organizing lenses, not a partition (a single EF can sit in more than one).

### Compensation hides the deficit

Working memory, planning / prioritization, organization, and metacognition share a shape. The raw EF is severely impaired; long-practiced compensation (recognition + dense chaining for working memory; 10× detailed written plans for planning; robust organizational systems; deliberate explicit-conceptual introspection for metacognition) is so effective and so habitual that the deficit is invisible to outside observers and partly to Alan himself. Editorial value for the book: the experienced phenomenology is "I do X really well"; the underlying mechanism is "severe Y impairment with effective Z compensation." Without the compensation in any of these, the EF would land near the top of the severity list.

### Modulator-driven asymmetry

Sustained attention, task initiation, and goal-directed persistence are not uniformly impaired — they are gated by an interest / want / mattering modulator. With the modulator on, the EF can be exceptional (Alan sustains roughly 12 hours of focused attention daily on his interests). With the modulator off, the EF is severely impaired. The Dawson & Guare list captures the neurotypical mechanism; the modulator-shape is what the ADHD experience actually traces.

Canonical home for the modulator's mechanism — including the two-channel structure (instinctive + conceptual), the autism × ADHD sub-mechanisms inside the instinctive channel, the multi-stream day pattern, and the starting-friction analysis — is [interest-modulator.md](interest-modulator.book-chapter.md).

### Aphantasia restructures the EF

Time management, emotional control, and flexibility are not just "more or less impaired" versions of the conventional EF — they are qualitatively different. Time perception loses its sensory-memory ground and becomes available only through explicit conceptual notice (see [time-perception.md](time-perception.book-chapter.md)). Emotional control bifurcates into a body-level layer (where emotions still hit at full or stronger force) and a conscious-access layer (where emotional content is largely absent), producing the Spock pattern. Flexibility becomes a *strength* because aphantasia removes the emotional momentum that resists pivoting in most people.

### EF budget — "don't pay the cost when you don't have to"

Response inhibition and task initiation share a compensation pattern that probably generalizes further: do not fight every demand on the EF. Let benign cases pass through (don't suppress benign impulses; don't force initiation on tasks that aren't wanted right now), reserving the budget for the cases where the EF cost is worth paying. The "If I don't want to, I can't" recovery rule is one instantiation; the let-the-bridge-thought-pass response-inhibition reframe is another. Generalizing the budget framing across the rest of the EF list is a thread for later.
