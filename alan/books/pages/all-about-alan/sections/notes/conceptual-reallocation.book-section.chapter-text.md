
# Conceptual reallocation (advantage)

- All mental process that would otherwise be occupied by sensory or emotional memory and imagination has instead been repurposed for conceptual memory and imagination, which functions.
- Effects: understands abstract concepts incredibly easily; automatically transfers mental models between domains (specifics → abstractions → transfer).
- Concrete: algebra by age 4; tutored in classes he was currently taking in college.
- He learns conceptual information faster.

## Forced abstraction buys transfer learning for free

The advantage isn't a separate gift — it falls out of the deficit. Because Alan can't store the particular ([recognition-vs-recall.md](recognition-vs-recall.book-chapter.md): no inverting a hash back to the original), he is **forced to generalize to an abstraction to encode a solution at all.** He literally can't "just remember how I solved a particular problem" — the particular doesn't persist. The only thing that survives encoding is the generalized form.

The benefit follows mechanically: encoding a solution *requires* producing the generalized abstraction, so once it's encoded he *has* the generalized abstraction — and transfer learning is free. There's nothing extra to do to make a solution transfer across domains; the abstraction is the only thing that was ever stored.

This is why he often reaches for a **conceptual bridge** where other people would use memory or imagination. The bridge is a generalized abstraction standing in for a stored particular he can't keep.

## The brain-training anchor — where direct recognition craters and a bridge rescues

The architecture shows up cleanly in a brain-training-game profile. Alan scored **98+ percentile on everything except one task.** The exception: a circle with dots moving in a specific direction is shown, then hidden for 3 seconds, then three circles appear and he has to pick the one whose dots move in the original direction.

This is a pure direct-recognition task with a 3-second store — exactly the move the one-directional hash can't make (hold a particular, invert it back at evaluation). After an hour of practice he was still at the **30th percentile**, and he only got *that* far by **translating the direction into a clock-hand position, holding the abstraction, then translating back at evaluation.** Without the clock-hand bridge he scored *below* 30th percentile; the bridge *rescued* him up to 30th — it didn't drag him down.

The clock-hand bridge was slower and more error-prone in close cases than direct recognition appears to be for other people. That's the architecture in miniature: the one task built to demand direct, non-abstractable recognition is exactly where he craters, and a conceptual bridge is a partial lifeline — better than nothing, worse than the direct faculty he doesn't have.
