
# Disputed misery

> The disputed-misery model — the marriage memory-dispute between Alan and Jen, treated rigorously. Contemporaneous daily tracking puts Alan at wishing he didn't exist on ~80% of days across ~20 years of extreme load; the dispute is not whether he was miserable but whether he was really that miserable. A five-point argument (with the formally-correct Shapley/Pearl-3 answer flagged as unavailable) attributes the misery to load, not the marriage; the contemporaneous record is structurally immune to the saliency bias alleged; Jen's doubt is read as protective rather than as an epistemic error.

The single model **most under dispute** between Alan and Jen. It is a memory-dispute about the worst stretch of Alan's life, and the two of them do not agree on what happened — not on the facts of any one day, but on the magnitude of the whole.

This note treats the dispute the way Alan treats it: as a question with data, an attribution problem with a formally-correct answer, and a counterpart whose disagreement has its own mechanism. Rendered as Alan's model, not as asserted fact about Jen.

## The claim, from contemporaneous data

Across roughly **20 years under extreme load relative to his nervous-system capacity** — including the first 18 years of his marriage to Jen — Alan hit the point of **wishing he didn't exist on about 80% of individual days.** This is the [nonexistence-ideation longing window](stress-responses.book-chapter.md) read across two decades. For Alan it is an objective fact of his existence, because it comes from **long-term contemporaneous daily tracking**, not from looking back.

The number is the load-bearing anchor. It is not "I remember it being bad." It is a count, logged day by day as the days happened, across the same [20-year measurement record](energy-accounting.book-chapter.md) that grounds the rest of the resource model.

## What is actually in dispute

The dispute is precise, and it is not the obvious thing.

- **Not in dispute:** that he was miserable. Both Alan and Jen can hold that.
- **In dispute, at the deepest level:** whether he was *really that miserable at all* — whether the 80% figure is true, or whether Alan is himself suffering a saliency bias that inflates it.

So the disagreement is not about valence (bad vs. fine) but about magnitude (this bad vs. not nearly this bad). Jen's memories of the period are **not nearly as negative** as Alan's record.

### Jen's memory is state-dependent

Jen's cognition is more emotional and anecdotal than Alan's, so his rigorous, data-anchored arguments **do not land for her** — they are the wrong currency for how she holds the period. And her memories of it are not fixed: they run **more negative when she is not doing well in the present.** This is correspondence bias / state-dependent reconstruction — the present state colors the reconstructed past, so the same 20 years read darker on her bad days and lighter on her good ones.

The contrast with Alan's architecture is sharp. His record is contemporaneous, so it does not move with his present state; her recollection is reconstructive, so it does. The [present-tense model](present-tense-model.book-chapter.md) names why Alan's own memory could not supply a stable record either — his map is [affectively flat and reconstructed on read](present-tense-model.book-chapter.md#the-map-is-affectively-flat--structure-no-charge), which is exactly why the external daily log, not recall, is what carries the claim.

## The saliency-bias rebuttal

Jen's allegation is that Alan's 80% is saliency bias — that the worst days are overweighted in how he remembers the period. The rebuttal is structural, not rhetorical: **the misery record is contemporaneous daily logging, not retrospective recall.** Saliency bias lives in retrospective reconstruction — in how a remembered period gets re-weighted on the way back up. A day-by-day log written as the days happened has no reconstruction step for the bias to act on. The mechanism Jen alleges requires a faculty the record doesn't use.

Alan separately flags that he is **not** claiming immunity from saliency bias in general. He flags one place he suspects his own may operate: his memory of **having grown up in the church** (the [faith thread](testing-and-the-seed.book-chapter.md)). That is retrospective, so the bias has a surface to act on there — and naming it is the honesty move that keeps the contemporaneous-record rebuttal clean rather than self-serving.

## Was the marriage the cause?

A separate question from the magnitude: granting the misery, did the **marriage** cause it? Alan's model: **no — the load caused the misery, and the marriage ran alongside it.**

He frames the attribution honestly as intrinsically hard. The mathematically correct, definitive answer would be the **Shapley value** of each factor's contribution — and computing a Shapley value requires **Pearl level-3 (counterfactual) information**: what Alan's misery would have been in the worlds where the marriage was absent but the load held, and vice versa. That information is not fully available here, so the exact attribution is unrecoverable. What follows is an **approximation**, explicitly not the definitive answer.

### The five-point approximation

Five independent lines, none decisive alone, jointly pointing at load rather than marriage:

1. **Temporal precedence.** The misery started **~2 years before the marriage.** The effect predates the alleged cause.
2. **Dose-response.** The misery is **quite predictable from allostatic-load and recovery measurements**, both as load rose and as it fell. A factor that tracks the dose in both directions is the better causal candidate.
3. **Not a consistent stressor.** The marriage **added** allostatic load at times and **reduced** it at others. A cause of sustained misery would push consistently in one direction; the marriage did not.
4. **Base rate.** The baseline rate of nonexistence ideation in autistic adults is **extremely high across individuals in all circumstances of life.** The 80% sits against a high anchor that has nothing to do with this particular marriage.
5. **The retirement natural experiment — the strongest point.** [Retirement](employment-cessation.book-chapter.md) was a successful intervention that **removed the misery without the marriage going away.** The load varied; the marriage held constant; the misery lifted. That is a near-clean natural experiment isolating load as the operative variable — the closest thing available to the counterfactual the Shapley value would need.

Point 5 is the strongest because it most nearly supplies the missing level-3 information: a real-world manipulation of load with the marriage held fixed, and the outcome moved with the load.

## Jen's doubt is plausibly protective, not an error

The cleanest reading of Jen's disagreement is not that she is reasoning badly. Alan finds it plausible that her doubt of his record is **protective rather than epistemic.**

The structure: if his record is true, then her husband was at the floor of not wanting to exist on most days for ~20 years, while she was right beside him — and much of that time she did not see it that way. That may be **more than she can let be fully true.** In Alan's words: *"It seems very human to believe what you need to in order to survive."*

This reframes the dispute. It is not a contest over who has the facts right; it is one partner holding a record the other partner may not be able to afford to hold. The disagreement is doing a job for Jen — and naming that job is more accurate than scoring the argument.

The next move in the arc inverts the lens: where Jen can believe what she needs to in order to survive, Alan suspects he is **missing the system that does that** — he believes whatever best fits his information even when the belief makes his life very hard. That asymmetry, and what it costs and gifts him, is [alternative-gated endurance](alternative-gated-endurance.book-chapter.md) and the [missing self-deception system](alternative-gated-endurance.book-chapter.md#the-missing-self-deception-system).
