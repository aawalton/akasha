
# Information diet

> Information-diet-as-remediation-strategy — for some information-and-media categories the highest-leverage remediation is eliminating consumption entirely rather than switching vendors. The category-level instance of the not-needing direction from alternatives.md.

For some information-and-media categories the highest-leverage remediation isn't "find a better vendor" — it's "stop having the need." Whole categories of dependency can be removed by changing consumption patterns rather than by selecting different vendors.

## The observation

The [information-and-media audit](information-and-media.book-chapter.md) surfaces a pattern the other audits don't carry as cleanly: multiple major dependency categories have already been remediated by elimination, not by vendor switching.

- **News.** No daily news source — no newspaper subscription, no news app, no scheduled exposure.
- **Podcasts.** No podcast app installed, no recurring podcast subscriptions, no scheduled podcast listening.
- **Most social platforms.** No Reddit, no HackerNews, no Discord (for information), no Mastodon, no X / Twitter as ongoing dependencies.

Each of these categories is dominated by D-tier vendors in the framework's structural reading. The household's posture isn't "find the trustworthy news source" — it's "the consumption is gone." The category-level dependency has been remediated to zero.

## The mechanism

Information consumption creates several distinct dependency surfaces, each of which is a vendor relationship even when no money changes hands:

- **Attention extraction.** Algorithmic feeds and notification cycles compete for cognitive bandwidth and shape what gets attended to. The platform is the vendor; attention is the price.
- **Outrage cycles.** News and political-content pipelines convert events into emotional spikes that compound over time. The vendor sells engagement; the cost is cognitive load and disposition shift.
- **Cognitive load.** Long-form podcasts and dense reading consume time and processing capacity. The vendor sells content; the cost is the bandwidth that would otherwise be available for something else.
- **Platform infrastructure.** Where you consume creates a dependency on top of what you consume. Even high-quality content delivered through a captured platform inherits the platform's grade.

Eliminating consumption removes all four surfaces at once for the category. Switching vendors only addresses the platform-infrastructure surface — the attention, outrage, and cognitive-load costs persist regardless of which vendor delivers the content.

## Why it works for this household

Three reinforcing reasons make the eliminate-the-category move work specifically well here:

- **AuDHD lens.** Low-stimulation lifestyle reduces overwhelm and decision fatigue. Algorithmic feeds and outrage cycles are high-stimulation surfaces by construction; removing them aligns with the broader sensory-load-conservation strategy.
- **Burnout-recovery context.** Cognitive-load conservation is itself a load-bearing strategy during burnout recovery. Reducing the information surface frees bandwidth for the recovery work directly. Information consumption is one of the easiest discretionary loads to drop.
- **Information FOMO traded for life bandwidth.** What's gained by reading 30 minutes of news daily rarely changes any decision the household actually makes. The FOMO is real but the marginal information-value-to-decisions ratio is close to zero for the categories that got eliminated.

## Generalizability

The pattern extends beyond information and media. The not-needing meta-strategy from [alternatives.md → self-reliance as not-needing](alternatives.book-chapter.md#self-reliance-as-not-needing) applies at the **category** level wherever consumption is contingent rather than essential:

- **Most social media** — eliminate the category vs. find a trusted platform.
- **Most news** — eliminate the category vs. find a trusted source.
- **Most podcasts** — eliminate the category vs. find a trusted host.
- **Some convenience purchases** — eliminate vs. find a B-tier vendor.
- **Some app subscriptions** — uninstall vs. migrate.

Each of these is a candidate for category-level elimination once the question shifts from "which vendor in this category do I trust?" to "do I need this category at all?"

## Caveats

The pattern is powerful and the caveats need to be loud, because category-level elimination has failure modes the per-vendor switching path doesn't:

- **Not every need is discretionary.** Category elimination requires confidence that the missed information wouldn't have changed any decision. For news and podcasts and most social platforms, that's empirically true for this household. For load-bearing reference material — legal and financial updates, healthcare research, technical documentation for owned infrastructure — it's false. The audit / plan judges per-category whether the consumption is contingent.
- **Counterculture cost.** The eliminate-the-category move can feel countercultural in contexts that assume baseline news literacy or social-platform presence. Social cost is a hidden tax — relationships that depend on shared current-events context lose some texture when one side has dropped the consumption.
- **Information starvation has its own cost.** Some baseline awareness is required for civic participation, professional context, and unexpected-event response. The elimination has to be calibrated — sparse-by-design rather than zero-by-default.
- **Selective re-introduction.** Categories that got eliminated may need to be selectively re-introduced when circumstances change (election cycle, professional pivot, household-health event). The not-needing posture is a current-state assessment, not a permanent commitment.
