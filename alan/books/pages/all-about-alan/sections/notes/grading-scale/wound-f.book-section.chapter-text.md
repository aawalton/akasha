
# The wound-F — stress of connection as a standing cost

> The wound-F: when the F grade is a trauma-trigger cost of being connected, not a trust verdict. The flip criterion, why intolerance runs opposite to dependency depth, and how it differs from the bad-actor F.

The two tiers have explicit, quotable definitions:

- **Google = D** — "use as little as possible, but don't hurt yourself."
- **Tesla = F** — "it's not worth using even if there are no other options."

"Not worth using even if there are no other options" means the utility could be infinite and the answer is still no. The F is a verdict on **being connected**, not on the product. The product could be fine; the *connection itself* is a net loss.

## The intolerance runs opposite to dependency depth

The thing that flips a company D→F is **not** how entangled I am with it — it runs the other way.

- **Google** I'm vastly more entangled with — it's woven through my identity layer (see [identity-recovery-anchor.md](../identity-recovery-anchor.book-chapter.md)) — and I tolerate it at D.
- **Tesla** was the lighter, optional connection — the one I could have walked away from cheapest — and it's the one I can't stand at F.

**Concrete anchor.** For a long time Tesla was the only reasonable electric vehicle. I wanted a second EV and wanted it electric. But my trust toward Tesla is broken enough that "I can't stand to be connected to the company in any way" — so I'd rather go without the want entirely than connect to Tesla to get it.

## The mechanism

Beyond a trust grade there is a **stress of being connected** that is its own standing cost. Past some point I'd rather eat the loss of the want than keep carrying that stress. That standing cost — not dependency depth — is what flips a company from D (minimize but tolerate) to F (refuse even with no alternative).

This is distinct from the [ranking criterion](../ranking-criterion.book-chapter.md), which is mechanical and folds emotional cost into switching cost. The stress-of-connection cost sits at the grade level: it changes what the grade *is*, not just where the item lands in the remediation queue.

## What earns the F — the flip criterion

The threshold is concrete, and it is narrow. In my words: *"There are very few companies that hit this level for me, and the thing they have in [common] is they have caused me immense personal pain that pushed me past my breaking point at least once. I have trauma responses and any association with them is a trigger."*

So the F is not a trust verdict that merely got worse — it is a **wound**. An F-grade company caused me immense personal pain past a breaking point at least once. After that, any association with it is a [trauma trigger](../trauma-encoding.book-chapter.md): the brand, the product, the logo read as partial cues that fire the encoded response. "Not worth using even if there are no other options" is what a trigger feels like priced as a grade — the connection itself costs more than any utility it could buy.

Two consequences follow from the criterion:

- **It is harm-driven and personal, not dependency-driven.** This is why the [intolerance runs opposite to dependency depth](#the-intolerance-runs-opposite-to-dependency-depth). Google never pushed me past a breaking point, so it stays at D no matter how entangled I am. Tesla crossed a personal line, so it lands at F even though the connection was optional and shallow. Depth of dependency does not enter the criterion; whether the company once broke me does.
- **The F is very rare.** Only a handful of companies ever reach it, because the bar is a personal-pain breaking point, not bad behavior in general. This separates the wound-F from the [bad-actor F](../grading-scale.book-chapter.md#f--actively-avoiding) (OpenAI): a company can earn the standard F on demonstrated misalignment without ever having personally injured me. The wound-F is a stricter, narrower, more personal tier that happens to share the letter.
