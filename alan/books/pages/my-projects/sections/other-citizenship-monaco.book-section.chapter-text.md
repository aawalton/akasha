
Monaco is a sovereign city-state (~38,000 residents, 2.02 km²) under the constitutional monarchy of Prince Albert II. It is **not in the EU** but sits inside the Schengen area via France under two bilateral agreements. Monégasque citizenship is one of the most restrictive in the world: there are roughly **9,000 native Monégasques** out of ~38,000 residents, and the Sovereign Prince has full discretion over every naturalization. Approximately **8–10 naturalizations are granted per year**. The governing instrument is Law No. 1.155 (1992) on Acquisition and Loss of Nationality, as amended.

## Acquisition at birth

### Jus sanguinis (automatic)
A child born **anywhere in the world** to at least one Monégasque parent — by birth, naturalization, reintegration, or option — automatically acquires Monégasque nationality at birth. Either parent qualifies; the 2011 reform equalized maternal and paternal transmission. There is no generational cap beyond the standard "parent was Monégasque at the time of the child's birth" rule.

### Jus soli (very narrow)
Monaco does **not** follow general jus soli. Birth on Monégasque soil to foreign parents confers no automatic right. Statelessness-prevention rules permit acquisition for a child born in Monaco who would otherwise be stateless, subject to declaration.

### Adoption
A minor adopted (full adoption) by a Monégasque citizen acquires nationality. Simple adoption does not transmit nationality.

## Naturalization by sovereign ordinance

Naturalization in Monaco is **never an entitlement** — Article 5 of Law No. 1.155 states the Sovereign Prince *may* grant nationality. Even applicants who perfectly meet every statutory criterion can be refused without reason, and no appeal lies against a refusal. The decision is published as an *Ordonnance Souveraine* in the Journal de Monaco.

### Standard naturalization (10-year track)
- **10 years of continuous ordinary residence** in Monaco, computed **after age 18** (years as a minor do not count).
- **Manifestation of attachment to Monaco** — demonstrated by social and economic integration, community involvement, French language proficiency at roughly **B1–B2 CEFR**, and knowledge of Monégasque institutions and history.
- **Good character** — clean criminal record from Monaco and every country of prior residence.
- **Self-sufficient means** — no reliance on public assistance.
- **Discharge of military obligations** in the country of origin — the Ordonnance Souveraine cannot be signed until the applicant is released from any conscription liability abroad.
- **Renunciation of prior nationality** — proof of loss of the original citizenship must be filed before the Ordonnance is signed. Monaco does **not** permit dual citizenship via this track.
- Sovereign discretion: even with all boxes ticked, refusal is common; the Prince may dispense with the residence requirement in exceptional cases (Article 5, second paragraph).

### Reduced naturalization for spouses (10-year marriage track)
Following the 2011 nationality-law reform that equalized the genders, a person married to a Monégasque citizen may acquire Monégasque nationality after **10 years of marriage** *and* uninterrupted cohabitation in Monaco. The spouse retains his or her original nationality — **this is the only standing exception to Monaco's no-dual-citizenship rule**. Prior to 2011 the rule was 5 years for wives of Monégasque husbands and 10 years for husbands of Monégasque wives; some older sources still cite the asymmetric figures. The reform also tightened access — children of an acquirer-by-marriage no longer automatically follow.

### Reduced naturalization — other reduced cases
Law No. 1.155 (as amended) recognizes narrow reduced or dispensation cases — for example a person whose mother or father acquired Monégasque nationality after the applicant's birth, or a former Monégasque seeking reintegration. These are handled case-by-case under sovereign discretion; there is no public statutory five-year residence track outside the marriage path.

### Exceptional naturalization for distinguished service
The Sovereign Prince may, by Ordonnance Souveraine, grant nationality without satisfying the residence requirement to a person who has rendered exceptional service to Monaco — a discretionary, statutorily-unbounded prerogative. This route accounts for a portion of the ~8–10 annual naturalizations and is not an avenue an applicant can plan against.

## Reintegration and option

### Reintegration
A former Monégasque who lost nationality (e.g. by acquiring a foreign one before the 2011 reform) may apply for reintegration. Granted by Ordonnance Souveraine on the same discretionary basis as naturalization; the prior-renunciation/military-service conditions apply.

### Option
Certain persons — for example a minor whose parent naturalizes, or a person who became Monégasque automatically but seeks to confirm by declaration upon majority — may exercise an option to retain or acquire the nationality. Strict deadlines apply (typically before age 21).

## Loss of nationality

A Monégasque may lose nationality by:
- **Voluntary acquisition of a foreign nationality** without authorization — historically automatic loss; the 2011 reform softened this for those who were Monégasque at birth (loss is no longer automatic in every case but remains the rule for naturalized citizens).
- **Declaration of renunciation** filed with the Director of Judicial Services, where the applicant already holds a foreign nationality.
- **Forfeiture (déchéance)** decreed by Ordonnance Souveraine for acts seriously contrary to the interests of Monaco — limited to naturalized citizens within 10 years of acquisition.

A native-born Monégasque (jus sanguinis) cannot ordinarily be deprived of nationality.

## Dual citizenship policy

- **Standard naturalization (10-year track):** dual citizenship **prohibited**. Applicant must renounce prior nationality before the Ordonnance is signed.
- **Naturalization by marriage (10 years):** dual citizenship **permitted** — the spouse retains the original nationality. This is the principal exception.
- **Jus sanguinis at birth:** dual citizenship **permitted in practice** — a child born to one Monégasque and one foreign parent typically holds both. Post-2011 the law tolerates this rather than forcing a choice at majority.
- **Reintegration:** generally requires renunciation of the foreign nationality acquired in the interim.

A naturalized Monégasque who later acquires a third nationality risks loss of Monégasque citizenship.

## Recent changes (2011–2026)

- **2011 reform** — Law No. 1.387 of 19 December 2011 equalized maternal and paternal jus sanguinis transmission, equalized the marriage-track residence rules across genders at 10 years, and tightened automatic transmission to children of acquirers-by-marriage.
- **2017 reform** — Law No. 1.441 of 13 July 2017 further refined transmission rules and the conditions for loss.
- **2024–2026** — no further statutory changes; naturalization throughput remains in the high single digits per year. Practitioners continue to report longer-than-statutory de facto residence expectations (12–20 years in practice for a non-spouse applicant to be granted, even though the law says 10).

## Practical assessment

For a US citizen with no Monégasque ancestry and no Monégasque spouse, Monaco citizenship is **statistically near-unobtainable** in any realistic planning horizon:
- 10 years of residence is the *floor*; 12–20 years is the empirical norm before approval.
- Renunciation of US citizenship is a precondition.
- ~8–10 grants per year against a residence base of ~30,000 non-citizens implies an annual approval probability under 0.05 % for any given resident.

The realistic Monaco strategy is **residency** (which delivers most of the practical benefit — tax residency, Schengen access, lifestyle), not citizenship. See `residency/monaco.md`.
