
## Acquisition by birth, descent, and adoption

### Citizenship by birth (conditional jus soli since 2006)
The Citizenship Amendment Act 2005 ended unrestricted birthright citizenship for births on or after 1 January 2006. A child born in New Zealand acquires citizenship at birth only if at least one parent is, at the time of birth, a New Zealand citizen, the holder of a residence-class visa, or otherwise entitled to reside indefinitely in New Zealand (or in the Cook Islands, Niue, or Tokelau). Births before 1 January 2006 remained unrestricted jus soli back to 1 January 1949.

### Citizenship by descent (one-generation rule)
A child born outside New Zealand is a citizen by descent if at least one parent is a New Zealand citizen otherwise than by descent. The rule is one-generation: a parent who is themselves a citizen by descent cannot transmit citizenship by descent to a child born abroad — those children must instead apply for citizenship by grant. Registration is required (not automatic). Stateless safety valve: a child born abroad who would otherwise be stateless can claim citizenship by descent even where the parent is a citizen by descent only.

### Adoption
A child adopted by a New Zealand citizen under a New Zealand adoption order is treated as a citizen by birth from the date of adoption. Overseas adoptions finalised before 18 November 1992 or before the child turned 14, where the foreign adoption meets the section 17 Adoption Act 1955 criteria and at least one adoptive parent is a citizen, give citizenship by descent.

## Citizenship by grant (naturalisation)

### Standard naturalisation
The default route. Requirements:
- **Residence**: hold a residence-class visa and have been *present in New Zealand on a residence-class visa* for at least 1,350 days across the five years immediately before applying, **and** at least 240 days in *each* of those five years. Time on a temporary visa does not count, even retroactively.
- **Good character**: criminal-history checks across every country of residence; serious offences (especially within the last 5–10 years) typically block approval.
- **English language**: must be able to understand and speak English at a level sufficient for everyday use; checked at interview or via an accepted qualification.
- **Intention to continue residing**: must intend to continue living in New Zealand, or to enter Crown service / international service of a NZ-based organisation, after the grant.
- **Oath / affirmation of allegiance** at a citizenship ceremony.

### Marriage / spouse path
New Zealand has no spousal fast-track. A partner of a NZ citizen must travel the same 5-year residence-on-a-residence-visa road; marriage shortens nothing on the citizenship clock. The Partner-of-a-New-Zealander Resident Visa is the residence pathway that feeds into it.

### Ministerial discretion (exceptional circumstances / distinguished service)
Section 9(1)(c) of the Citizenship Act 1977 allows the Minister of Internal Affairs to grant citizenship despite not meeting the standard requirements where there are exceptional circumstances of a humanitarian or other nature, or where the grant would be in the public interest (including distinguished service to New Zealand or the Crown). Used sparingly — a handful per year.

### Statelessness protection
The Minister may grant citizenship to a person who would otherwise be stateless. New Zealand has ratified the 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness. Combined with the descent-side stateless safety valve above, the framework closes most stateless-at-birth scenarios.

### Resumption (former citizens)
A person who renounced or lost NZ citizenship can apply to *resume* it. The Minister may grant resumption where the applicant is of good character and the grant is not contrary to the public interest. There is no residence requirement for resumption.

## Realm and historical-residual categories

### Cook Islands, Niue, Tokelau citizens
Nationals of the Cook Islands, Niue, and Tokelau are New Zealand citizens — the Realm of New Zealand is a single citizenship jurisdiction, and persons born there with the parental link meet the same birth/descent rules as if born in New Zealand proper. There is no separate "Cook Islands citizenship" that exists outside of NZ citizenship.

### Western Samoa Citizenship Act 1982 — restored 2024
The Citizenship (Western Samoa) (Restoration) Amendment Act 2024 (in force 26 November 2024) restored NZ citizenship-as-of-right to people born in Western Samoa between 13 May 1924 and 1 January 1949 (and their descendants), reversing the 1982 stripping that followed the Privy Council's *Lesa* ruling. An estimated up to 3,480 living Samoan elders qualify; application fees are refunded.

## Dual citizenship policy
New Zealand has permitted multiple citizenship since the British Nationality and New Zealand Citizenship Act 1948 came into force on 1 January 1949. No renunciation of prior nationality is required to be granted NZ citizenship; NZ citizens who acquire another nationality do not automatically lose NZ citizenship. The Minister retains a residual power to deprive a dual citizen who acts contrary to the interests of New Zealand, used very rarely.

## Processing and timelines (May 2026)

The Department of Internal Affairs (DIA) cleared most of the post-COVID backlog through 2024–2025. As of early 2026, simple complete applications process in 1–4 months; the DIA's published timeframe for 92–94% of applicants sits at 16–19 months for older filings still in the queue. The active backlog dropped from a peak of ~37,690 (April 2022) to ~11,000 in early 2026 — roughly a 70% reduction.

## Recent changes (2024–2026)

- **2024-11**: Western Samoa Citizenship Act 1982 restoration amendment passed unanimously, giving back citizenship-as-of-right to affected Samoan elders and descendants.
- **2025-09**: legislation introduced a future in-person citizenship knowledge test (20 multiple-choice questions, 75% pass) for most adult grant applicants — scheduled to take effect in late 2027, not yet live.
- **2024–2026**: DIA cleared ~70% of the application backlog via staff cross-training and process redesign.
- **No** change to the underlying 5-year / 1,350-day / 240-day-per-year residence formula; no spousal fast-track introduced.
