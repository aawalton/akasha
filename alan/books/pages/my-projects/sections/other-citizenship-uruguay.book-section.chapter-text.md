
Uruguay is one of Latin America's most stable jurisdictions: a consolidated democracy, strong rule of law, low corruption (highest TI score in LATAM), and a generally pro-immigrant tradition. Its naturalization route is among the most accessible in the region — 3 years (with family) or 5 years (single) of residence, no investment minimum required, modest physical-presence floor in practice. The catch is constitutional: Uruguay maintains a sharp two-track distinction between **natural citizens** (nationals) and **legal citizens** (naturalized), with materially different rights.

## Natural vs Legal Citizenship — The Constitutional Distinction

Uruguay's 1967 Constitution (Arts. 73–81) separates *nationality* (a sociological fact) from *citizenship* (a legal status). Natural citizens are "nationals"; legal citizens are not. This produces real differences:

- **Presidency / Vice-Presidency**: only natural citizens may hold (Art. 151). Absolute bar — no waiting period clears it.
- **Senate**: requires 7 years of legal-citizen status (Art. 98). House of Representatives: 5 years. Public employment: 3 years.
- **Loss of status**: natural citizens can never lose nationality (Art. 81) — acquiring another citizenship merely suspends "exercise of rights of citizenship," restored upon return + registry. Legal citizens *lose* Uruguayan citizenship if they naturalize elsewhere.
- **Passport (historic anomaly fixed then partially un-fixed)**: until April 2025 Uruguayan passports listed naturalized citizens' country of *birth* in the "Nacionalidad" field (an ICAO misinterpretation), making the passport useless for visa-free travel since border guards read it as the birth-country passport. The April 16, 2025 reform listed all citizens as "URY" and dropped "Lugar de nacimiento." France, Germany, and Japan rejected the new format; on July 29, 2025 Uruguay reversed and reinstated the birthplace field. Passports issued 2025-04-16 to 2025-07-29 are eligible for free replacement at DNIC.
- **Dual citizenship**: natural citizens may hold any number of other citizenships freely. Legal citizens, by the dominant constitutional reading, lose Uruguayan citizenship upon naturalizing elsewhere.

## Natural Citizenship

### Jus soli (birth on Uruguayan soil)
Any child born in Uruguayan territory is a natural citizen, regardless of parents' status (Art. 74). No exceptions for diplomats or transit births in practice.

### Jus sanguinis (children of Uruguayans born abroad)
Children of a Uruguayan father or mother, born abroad, become natural citizens upon (a) taking up residence in Uruguay (*avecinarse*) and (b) registering in the Civic Registry (Art. 74). Registration is also possible at a Uruguayan consulate abroad (Civil Register Inscription of Birth), which preserves the right to naturalize without the residence step on activation. This effectively extends one generation; grandchildren born abroad to parents who never registered are generally not eligible without a generational-gap reopening.

## Legal Citizenship (Naturalization, "Ciudadanía Legal")

Granted by the **Corte Electoral** (Electoral Court), not the immigration ministry. The Constitution sets the floor; the court evaluates.

### Family residence — 3 years
Available to applicants with **constituted family in Uruguay**: married, in a recognized concubinato (civil-union / common-law partnership registered ≥ 5 years), or with a dependent child residing in Uruguay. The spouse / partner does not need to be Uruguayan — a foreign couple both holding Uruguayan residence qualifies.

### Single residence — 5 years
Default track for unmarried applicants without dependents.

### The clock — when 3/5 years actually start
The constitutional clock runs from the date of **first arrival to initiate the residency application** (the *fecha de inicio del trámite*), not from when the residencia permanente card is issued. So a US applicant can land, file the *residencia permanente* application, and begin accruing constitutional residence the same day, even though card issuance may take 8–12 months. The Corte Electoral verifies this independently of immigration paperwork.

### Means of livelihood
Article 75 requires "medios de vida" — a stable lawful source of income or productive activity (employment, business, pension, investments, profession). No fixed monetary threshold; the court evaluates qualitatively. The same income proof used for residencia permanente (~USD 1,500/month single, ~USD 2,500 couple) generally satisfies.

### Good conduct
Clean criminal record from countries of residence over the prior 5 years, plus Uruguay. The court also weighs "buena conducta" qualitatively at the hearing.

### Spanish — informal, not a test
There is **no formal language test**. The Corte Electoral hearing is in Spanish and the applicant plus two witnesses answer a short verbal questionnaire ("Where do you live? What do you do? Are these your witnesses?"). Conversational Spanish suffices; the threshold is real but low. Applicants who cannot answer simple questions are rejected.

### The three-year wait after grant (oft-confused)
There is **no general 3-year wait** between Carta de Ciudadanía issuance and rights. The "3 years" figure refers specifically to **public-employment eligibility** under Art. 76. Voting rights for naturalized citizens activate **3 years after Carta issuance** (Art. 75) — this is the actual delay. Passport application is immediate upon Carta issuance.

### Carta de Ciudadanía
After the hearing, the Corte Electoral issues the Carta de Ciudadanía in 6–12 months (recent timelines closer to 8–10 months). With it, the applicant applies for a Uruguayan passport at DNIC (National Civil Identification Directorate) — typically issued within weeks.

### Physical presence during accrual
The Corte Electoral enforces a "genuine residence" standard. Practitioners cite 183 days/year as the working floor, with no single absence exceeding 6 consecutive months (a 6+ month absence resets the clock). Travel is permitted; absences shorter than 6 months are summed and weighed.

## Marriage
No separate fast track. Marriage to a Uruguayan does **not** by itself confer citizenship and does not reduce the 3-year floor below the family-residence baseline. It does establish "constituted family," qualifying the foreign spouse for the 3-year (vs 5-year) track from the start of their own residence.

## Distinguished Service / Parliamentary Grant
Article 75(3) authorizes the General Assembly (Parliament) to grant legal citizenship by special vote for "notable services rendered to the country" or "relevant merits." Discretionary, rare, no minimum residence. Not a planning route.

## Resumption / Re-Acquisition
- **Natural citizens** who naturalized abroad: rights of citizenship are restored automatically by returning to Uruguay (*avecinarse*) and registering with the Civic Registry. Nationality itself was never lost.
- **Legal citizens** who lost status by naturalizing abroad: must restart — no expedited re-acquisition.

## Investor Route — No CBI
Uruguay has **no citizenship-by-investment program**. Money does not buy a Carta. Investment routes (real estate, business) accelerate *residency* and *tax residency* but the 3/5-year constitutional citizenship clock applies unchanged. Uruguay is sometimes marketed as offering "citizenship by investment" — this is a residency program with a downstream naturalization path, not a CBI in the St. Kitts / Malta sense.

## Recent Changes (2024–2026)
- **Mar 1, 2025**: Yamandú Orsi (Frente Amplio) inaugurated. Coalition lacks Chamber majority (48/99), narrow Senate majority (16/30). No naturalization-law reform announced; the dominant policy strain is managing a 2025 Cuban-migrant surge and an Aug 2024 executive decree creating a special residency program for ~20,000 asylum-seekers stuck in migratory limbo.
- **Apr 16, 2025**: Passport "Nacionalidad/Ciudadanía" reform — all citizens listed as URY, birthplace field removed.
- **Jul 29, 2025**: Passport reform reversed after France / Germany / Japan rejected the format. Birthplace field reinstated. Free replacement window open for passports issued during the gap.
- **Apr 2025 (proposed)**: Government announcement that passports would *forward* list "URY" for all — partially walked back per above.
- No change to the 3 / 5 year naturalization floor.

## Voting / Passport Differences — Quick Reference
| Right | Natural citizen | Legal citizen |
|---|---|---|
| Vote in national elections | from age 18 | 3 years after Carta (Art. 75) |
| President / VP | yes (35+) | never |
| Senate | yes (30+) | 7 years after Carta + age 30 |
| House | yes (25+) | 5 years after Carta + age 25 |
| Public employment | yes | 3 years after Carta |
| Hold dual citizenship | yes (nationality unloseable) | no (loses Uruguayan on foreign naturalization) |
| Passport application | immediate at majority | immediate upon Carta issuance |
| Pass nationality to children abroad | yes (jus sanguinis applies) | not as "natural" — children acquire legal-citizen status via residence |
