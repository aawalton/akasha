
# Decision rubric

> The decision system Alan built to stand in for the future-simulation he can't run — a two-mode split (cheap decisions made empirically; expensive ones made by an iterated rubric), the rubric as externalized forward model, touring for data rather than gut feeling, and the two-way coherentist error correction between the rubric and the holistic gestalt.

The system Alan uses to make decisions without an internal forward simulator. It compensates for the [no-forward-simulator property](aphantasia-mechanism.book-chapter.md#no-forward-simulator) — total aphantasia means he cannot pre-play a possible future as experience, so the thing most people do to decide isn't available to him.

## The missing default

Most people decide by running little imagined futures — half-sensory, half-emotional "movies" of how each option would go — and picking the option that feels best. That move is foreign to Alan. There is no internal screen to run it on ([aphantasia-mechanism.md → statelessness](aphantasia-mechanism.book-chapter.md#statelessness--only-conceptual-memory-persists)). The whole system below exists because the default forward-simulation channel is closed.

In its place, Alan runs two modes, selected by how expensive the decision is to reverse.

## Mode 1 — cheap / reversible: try it and learn

If a decision isn't excessively expensive to reverse, he makes it empirically. Experience is his best data, so the cheapest way to find out is to try the thing and observe the result. No rubric, no deliberation — the world is the forward model, and a reversible decision is a cheap query against it.

This is the same empirical-collision logic that the [optimism oscillator](insight-and-restructuring.book-chapter.md#why-he-cant-calibrate-optimism) runs on: with no internal simulator and often no priors, the only calibration channel is to make the move and feel the outcome. For reversible decisions that channel is cheap enough to be the whole method.

## Mode 2 — expensive / irreversible: the iterated rubric

When a decision is expensive and hard to reverse — buying a house is his example — try-it-and-learn is too costly. So he builds a rubric instead.

The procedure:

1. **Enumerate factors.** List every factor he conceptually thinks matters.
2. **Apply to specific cases.** Score real candidate options against the rubric.
3. **Iterate to coherence.** Adjust the rubric and the scores until they are internally consistent with each other *and* externally consistent with his preferences, as far as he can determine them.
4. **Decide by the rubric.** Once it's coherent, the rubric makes the call.

The rubric is an **externalized forward model** standing in for the simulation he can't run. And the iteration-to-coherence is the [graph-collapse move](insight-and-restructuring.book-chapter.md#insight-as-graph-collapse) applied deliberately to a decision: a messy graph of uncertain factors driven to collapse into a clean, structured model — except here he forces the collapse on purpose instead of waiting for an insight to land.

## Reading his own preferences

The rubric's external-consistency check requires knowing his preferences, and those aren't fully transparent to him. To read them for a house, he and his wife physically tour the houses and check whether the rubric matches up with how it actually is to be there. He samples the real thing rather than simulating it — the same try-it-and-learn move from Mode 1, used here as an instrument inside Mode 2 to read a preference he can't introspect directly.

### It's data, not feeling

The tour isn't a gut verdict. What it actually gives him is **data he didn't have before** — sometimes data on dimensions he hadn't thought to consider. This is the characteristic move: what someone else would call "a feeling about the place," Alan parses as information. The tour expands the feature space; it doesn't deliver an oracle. (Same parse-feeling-as-data pattern as [alexithymia.md → operational consequences](alexithymia.book-chapter.md#operational-consequences), where felt states are read as proxy signals rather than verdicts.)

## How an unconsidered dimension announces itself

A missing dimension surfaces through **pairwise comparisons**. The rubric says A > B. But his intuition says B > A. The contradiction is the signal.

He defines intuition precisely: the **direct gestalt of perceived data** — a holistic parallel integration of everything perceived, including dimensions not yet named in the rubric. The gestalt is doing the integration the rubric does, but over a wider feature set, some of it pre-conceptual. So when the gestalt and the rubric disagree on a pair, the gestalt is often integrating a factor the rubric hasn't captured. He then reverse-engineers the gestalt to find what that factor is, and adds it to the rubric.

The same pairwise-rank-ordering machinery shows up in [gender-dysphoria-mechanism.md](gender-dysphoria-mechanism.book-chapter.md#narrativeperformative-measurement-80-male) — ranking attributes pairwise across positive and negative framings to extract a preference structure he couldn't state outright.

## Two-way error correction (coherentist epistemology)

Neither the rubric nor the gestalt is sovereign. Each catches a different failure mode in the other:

- **External consistency (rubric vs. gestalt) catches missing dimensions.** When the gestalt overrules the rubric on a pair, he trusts the gestalt and hunts for what the rubric is missing.
- **Internal consistency (rubric scores coherent across all cases) catches a noisy gestalt.** The gestalt can also just be wrong. Requiring the rubric to stay internally consistent across every case is what catches a misleading holistic read.

The two constraints cross-check each other. The decision is the **fixed point** where the explicit model and the holistic read agree with each other and with themselves. This is a coherentist epistemology in miniature: truth isn't read off one privileged source; it's the mutually-consistent settling point of two independent constraints.

## Three error sources, two layers

When the gestalt and the rubric disagree on a pair, there is not one possible culprit but three:

- **A missing factor.** The gestalt is integrating a dimension the rubric hasn't named — the case above.
- **A noisy gestalt.** The holistic read is just wrong; internal consistency across cases catches it.
- **A miscalibrated case-score.** The rubric has the right *factors*, but his evaluation of this specific case against an existing factor is off.

The third source means the rubric can be wrong at **two layers**: the set of factors (is the right dimension even present?) and the per-case scores plugged into those factors (is this case scored correctly on the dimensions that are present?). A disagreement can come from either layer.

The two layers calibrate at different speeds. The factor set can change in one insight — name the missing dimension and it's added. The per-case scores calibrate only **slowly, over time**, as repeated exposure corrects an individual case's read. So a persistent gestalt-vs-rubric disagreement on one case, where the factor set looks complete, points at a slow-moving miscalibrated score rather than a missing factor.

## Resolution by compactness

With three candidate culprits, Alan needs a way to pick which one is actually wrong. He resolves the ambiguity by thinking it through: he tests each possibility — missing factor, noisy gestalt, miscalibrated case-score — and keeps whichever one produces a **more compact conceptual representation.**

Compactness is the selection criterion. The resolution that lets the whole picture collapse into the cleaner, smaller model is the one he trusts. This is not a special rule for decisions; it is his general truth-test applied here on purpose. The same gauge that fires as the satisfying "click" of an insight — [truth is more compressed than falsehood](compression-epistemology.book-chapter.md#the-core-claim) — is what breaks the three-way tie. Forcing the rubric to coherence and breaking its ambiguities by compactness is the [graph-collapse move](insight-and-restructuring.book-chapter.md#insight-as-graph-collapse) run deliberately, with compactness as the explicit objective rather than a feeling he waits for.
