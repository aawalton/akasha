
# Trust criterion

> The trust criterion — duration in decades and demonstrated resistance under pressure, with recent behavior as the most diagnostic signal because every organization is currently being tested in real time.

The rule for deciding whether a large organization has earned my trust.

## Definition

An organization is trusted when it meets both conditions:

1. **Duration measured in decades.** The track record has to be long enough that profit-pressure cycles have already tested it. Years are not enough; decades are the unit. A young organization, no matter how aligned its current behavior looks, has not yet been tested.
2. **Demonstrated resistance under pressure.** The organization has been pushed toward enshittification — by activist investors, market downturns, growth-at-all-cost cycles, ownership changes — and has visibly chosen not to yield. Resistance under pressure is the signal; absence of pressure is not.

Both conditions are necessary. Decades without pressure tests prove nothing. Strong recent resistance without a long track record is suggestive but not yet sufficient.

## Recent behavior is highly diagnostic

The enshittification pressure is now universal. Every corporation is currently being tested in real time — by investor expectations, by market consolidation, by the same wave of platform-economy dynamics applied to non-platforms.

This means recent behavior carries unusually strong signal:

- An organization that resists **right now**, under maximum pressure, has an even stronger signal than one whose track record predates the current cycle. The hard part of the test is happening live; passing it is the strongest possible evidence.
- An organization that is starting to enshittify **right now** is revealing its real character. The mask is coming off across the economy at the same time. What looked like a quirky exception turns out to be a quirky exception that lasted only as long as the pressure stayed mild.

Practical consequence: don't grandfather organizations on past behavior alone. The current cycle is the most informative test the framework gets to run. Reassess.

## Compressed trust-earning timeline

The decades-duration condition is binding in normal times. But when every existing trusted organization is failing simultaneously, the framework has to admit organizations that haven't had decades yet but are demonstrating the right behavior under maximum pressure.

I don't have a clean rule for this yet — flagged for the framework to resolve. Candidate framings:

- **Hold the decades line and accept fewer alternatives.** The framework refuses to compress; gaps in the alternatives space become candidates for [self-reliance](alternatives.book-chapter.md).
- **Treat resistance-under-current-pressure as worth more years than calm-period years.** A few years of demonstrated resistance now might substitute for decades of untested calm. Pressure-weighted duration.
- **Two-tier trust.** Decades-tested organizations are "trusted"; younger-but-resisting organizations are "provisionally trusted" — usable but not insulated against re-evaluation.

Open question for a later cycle.

## What pressure looks like

Pressure is the test, so the framework needs to recognize it. Pressure points the trust criterion watches for:

- **Ownership changes** — acquisition, IPO, going private, founder departure, succession. Each is a moment when the organization's character can shift abruptly.
- **Activist investors** — explicit pressure to extract value from existing customer relationships.
- **Market consolidation** — competitors disappearing, leaving fewer constraints on enshittification.
- **Macro stress** — recessions, supply shocks, regulatory changes. Stress-tests the organization's stated values against its survival incentives.
- **Growth-at-all-cost cycles** — when the broader economy rewards scale-by-extraction over scale-by-service, the temptation lands on every organization simultaneously.

Resistance under any one of these is evidence. Resistance under several compounding at once is strong evidence.

## What demonstrated resistance looks like

Specific behaviors that count as resistance, not just stated values:

- **Holding price stable** in a category where competitors are raising prices via shrinkflation, fee creep, or quality degradation.
- **Treating workers above market** when peers are squeezing labor costs.
- **Maintaining product quality** when competitors are substituting cheaper inputs.
- **Maintaining return / refund / support policies** that favor customers when competitors are eroding theirs.
- **Refusing to monetize trust assets** — customer data, customer attention, customer loyalty — that competitors are converting to revenue.
- **Resisting growth that requires abandoning the model.** Saying no to expansion that would dilute what made the organization worth trusting.

Stated values are not evidence. Behavior under pressure is the only evidence the framework accepts.

## Stability is part of trust, not a separate axis

**If I can't trust an organization to survive, I can't trust them.** Stability concerns — the likelihood an organization continues to operate at all — are reservations on the trust dimension itself, not a separate scoring axis. A financially fragile organization fails the trust test the same way a misaligned one does: the relationship can end unilaterally and against my interest. That puts stability on equal footing with ethics or ideology reservations.

The rule's worked consequence (UCCU dropping to D on small-bank stability risk after the SVB collapse, despite member-ownership alignment), the component decomposition it sits in — financial resiliency, ethics, political ideology, ownership structure — and the grading scale that summarizes the composite trust state all live in [grading-scale.md → stability is part of trust](grading-scale.book-chapter.md#stability-is-part-of-trust-not-a-separate-axis).
