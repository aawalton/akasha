
# Models of disability

> Models of disability — the three-tier model (physical / societal / conceptual) that sorts every consequence of Alan's neurotype by where the fix lives, and sets the one rule that decides which interventions he would accept. The line between the neurotype he loves and the health problems he despises.

The line Alan draws through his own neurology. He does not treat "disability" as one thing. He splits it into the **neurotype** — the wiring itself, which he loves — and the **consequences** that wiring produces, some of which he despises. In his words:

> "For me, this is the line between the neurotype and the health consequences. I love the neurotype, I can see things no one else can see and I delight in how different I am. I despise the health problems that come along with that."

The neurotype is the [autism architecture itself](autism-as-neurotype.book-chapter.md) — the difference in brain wiring. The consequences are everything that wiring causes downstream. The model is about sorting those consequences, because the sort decides what to do about each one.

## The three tiers

Every consequence sorts into one of three tiers, and the tier — not the severity — sets the intervention Alan would accept. The keystone statement:

> "For the physical disabilities, I would take a physical intervention if there was one. For the societal, I would take a societal intervention if there was one. For the conceptual, I would not take an intervention, because then I would not be the same person (to the limited extent that I have identity at all)."

The rule is one line: **the intervention you accept is set entirely by which tier the trouble lives in.** Each tier has a different answer because the fix lives in a different place.

### Physical — fix it, it isn't *him*

The bodily consequences: joint swelling, gut problems, the [comorbidities](autism-comorbidities.book-chapter.md) that ride along with the neurotype. The fix lives in the body. Alan would take a physical intervention in a heartbeat, because repairing the body does not touch the person. A working joint is not a different Alan. This is the same answer he gives the [would-you-take-a-pill question](autism-as-neurotype.book-chapter.md): yes for the comorbidities, every time.

### Societal — fix the world, not the nervous system

The [sensitivity](nervous-system-sensitivity.book-chapter.md) itself is not a defect. The nervous system is doing exactly what it does; the trouble is that it is set in a world too loud for it. So the consequence does not live inside Alan — it lives in the gap between his nervous system and an environment built for a different one. The fix lives **out there**: turn the world down.

This is a strong **social model** reading, in his words:

> "Sensitivity itself lands on the neurotype side. The consequences of sensitivity on my nervous system actually land on the outside. I view this as a societal disability, not a medical one. The world could be different in a way that would be better for me and for almost everyone else (quieter for example)."

The mark of the societal tier: the remedy is not a special accommodation purchased at others' expense. A quieter world is **better for almost everyone**, not just for Alan. The disability is the mismatch, and the mismatch is dissolved by changing the side of it that is wrong — the environment, not the person. This is the lever Alan already pulls on himself wherever he can author his own surroundings — the same logic worked out in [environment-lever.md](environment-lever.book-chapter.md).

### Conceptual — refuse the intervention, it ends the person

The neurotype itself: the wiring, the [abstraction-seeing](conceptual-cognition.book-chapter.md), the way he models the world. Here Alan would refuse any intervention, because there is nothing to fix — and changing it would not repair Alan, it would replace him. The boundary between "fix it" and "don't" is **identity itself.** A conceptual intervention does not give Alan a better life; it ends the Alan who would have lived it.

The parenthetical he attaches — *"to the limited extent that I have identity at all"* — is load-bearing and points outward. Alan's sense of self is unusually thin: a [discrete self](discrete-self.book-chapter.md), a rolling present-bound window rather than a continuous narrative thread, extended across branches into a [multiversal identity](multiversal-identity.book-chapter.md). So even the thing he refuses to change for identity's sake is an identity he holds only loosely. The refusal stands anyway: thin or not, it is the person, and the conceptual tier is the one place the fix would cost the person it was meant to help.

## The conceptual gift, concretely — abstraction-seeing

The conceptual tier is not just "the part he won't change." It is the part he **loves** — the gift the whole model is protecting. The concrete shape of that gift is what he sees:

> "What I see is precisely the abstractions. Because my brain can't retain the sensory details, I can only see the abstractions, which means I understand them quickly and often differently than others."

The mechanism is the [aphantasia-driven trade](aphantasia-mechanism.book-chapter.md): with sensory detail dropping out almost as soon as it arrives, what is left to see *is* the abstraction. The [conceptual-cognition](conceptual-cognition.book-chapter.md) profile is the same gift named from the cognition side — and the **inventor/explorer** angle (reinventing the path rather than following footsteps) is captured there as the downstream behavior it produces.

This is the inversion at the heart of the model. The conventional framing treats "can't retain sensory detail" as a deficit. For Alan it is the *source* of the thing he most values — he sees the abstractions precisely because the details do not crowd them out. That is exactly why the conceptual tier is the one he refuses to intervene on: the cost and the gift share a root, so removing the cost would remove the gift, and removing the gift would end the person.
