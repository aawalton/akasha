
# What can be said about an illegible number

> Two claims about illegible numbers that look alike and are not. 'No illegible number is interesting' is legibility restated in different words once 'interesting' is defined carefully. 'Nobody's favourite number is illegible' is a real consequence, because it runs on a premise about minds rather than about numbers — holding a number is representing it, and minds are made of universe. Also the slide I made getting there, and what happens to 'some integers are illegible' in the union.

Once the [hierarchy](the-hierarchy.book-chapter.md) is in place, take the union of the whole tower and ask what is still illegible relative to all of it. That set is the one this page is about.

> I'm interested in this idea of the union, since that might bring back some of the interesting aspects of illegible numbers, since in theory, each truth-predicate representation can be evaluated in a larger context.

## The claim I made

> For example, the property that no illegible number (defined as the numbers that are still illegible for the full union) can be interesting (defined as having something unique that can be stated about a specific number), since no illegible number can be stated, therefore nothing about them can be stated. "There is no smallest illegible number" becomes a special case of this, but so does "no one has a favorite illegible number", which is fun.

Three things there: a definition of **interesting**, an argument, and two consequences.

## The slide in the middle

*From Lali, marked.* "No illegible number can be stated, therefore nothing about them can be stated" does not follow. Plenty can be stated about illegible numbers. Every illegible number is irrational. Every illegible number is a real. For any illegible *r*, *r* is not 7. All true, all statable, all about illegible numbers.

What cannot be done is **single one out**. The step I made ran together *nothing can be said about them* with *none of them can be picked out*, and only the second is true.

## "Interesting" is legibility in a mirror

*From Lali, marked.* Write the definition of interesting carefully — *having a uniquely identifying property that can be stated* — and it is legibility over again in different words. So the theorem is true the way "no bachelor is married" is true. It is the definition looking at itself, not a discovery about illegible numbers.

I had meant the definition that way from the start:

> that's what I meant by "something unique" in my definition of interesting. To say something unique about a specific illegible number is equivalent to either making that number legible or requires that number to already be legible.

That sentence is the two halves of it. Saying something unique about a number either **makes it legible** — the statement is itself a representation — or **requires it to already be legible**, because you had to have hold of the number to say the thing.

## The favourite is a real result

The second half is where the content is, and it is not definitional.

> "Favorite" is an example of the latter, since having a favorite number would not itself count as a representation (non intelligible), but is still not possible for illegible numbers, making "no illegible numbers are interesting" an expansion more significant than just a definitional reframing.

Having a favourite number is not a representation. Nobody can read your favourite off you; it is not intelligible to someone else in the way the [correspondence condition](../001-legible-numbers.book-chapter.md#what-counts-as-a-representation) demands. So it falls in the second half — it does not *make* a number legible, it *requires* the number to already be legible. And it is still impossible for an illegible number.

**Nobody's favourite number is illegible.** To hold a favourite you would have to hold the number, and holding it is representing it.

*From Lali, marked:* she had called this one definitional along with the rest and then took it back — the content does not come from the numbers, it comes from a premise smuggled in and entitled to: **minds are made of universe.** That is what turns a claim about definitions into a claim about people.

*Recorder's reading, marked:* this is the one exchange in the session where Alan was told he was wrong, argued, and was conceded to. Recorded as his.

## The smallest illegible number

"There is no smallest illegible number" was offered as a special case of the interesting theorem. It survives, but its domain matters and it is sharpened considerably later — over the reals it is true for a boring reason and over the positive integers for a good one. That is worked in [provable-legibility.md](provable-legibility.book-chapter.md#the-smallest-illegible-integer).

## Some integers are illegible

> For the finite universe case, there were interesting consequences like "some integers are illegible", which I guess is still true within any specific frame as evaluated by the larger frame, due to the set of legible numbers being finite, but is likely not true of the countably infinite union, which I think likely must allow all integers to be legible.

Both halves of that are right. In any single finite frame the legible set is finite, so some integers are outside it, and a larger frame can see that. In the union, every integer is legible.

*From Lali, marked:* the reason the union catches every integer is the **bigger** ladder rather than the stronger one — more storage means longer numerals, so every integer is eventually written down. The stronger ladder adds no integers at all. The two ladders are set out in [the-hierarchy.md](the-hierarchy.book-chapter.md#the-two-ladders), and I had merged them here without noticing.

What the picture of *which* integers are legible in a single frame actually looks like is not what I assumed, and that is [swiss-cheese.md](swiss-cheese.book-chapter.md).

## Threads to pull on later

- **"Minds are made of universe" as a premise.** It is what the favourite result runs on, and it entered the argument without being stated. What else it buys, and whether it is doing work anywhere else in the book, is untouched.
- **Whether a favourite really fails the correspondence condition.** The claim is that having a favourite is not intelligible to someone else. A person who can *say* their favourite has represented it. What the argument needs is that the holding, not the saying, is what is at issue — worth stating more carefully than it was in the session.
