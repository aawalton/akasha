
Mexico's naturalization framework sits in the **Ley de Nacionalidad** (1998, as amended) and its Reglamento, administered by the Secretaría de Relaciones Exteriores (SRE) through the Dirección General de Asuntos Jurídicos (DGAJ). The headline numbers are friendly: **5 years of legal residence** (Temporary or Permanent) for the default track, **dual citizenship permitted since 1998** without renunciation, **modest written history/culture exam** (10 questions, 8 to pass), no formal DELE-equivalent Spanish test. The catches are the **18-of-24-months physical presence** rule and a written history/culture exam that was meaningfully toughened in 2018.

## Dual Citizenship — US-Compatible

The 1998 constitutional reform (Art. 30, 32, 37) and the new **Ley de Nacionalidad** (Mar 23, 1998) ended Mexico's prior prohibition on dual nationality. Mexicans by birth **cannot be deprived** of their nationality (Art. 37(A)). Naturalized Mexicans can hold other citizenships in practice — the historical renunciation oath under Art. 17 has been read down so that the Secretaría cannot require the renunciation until *after* granting Mexican nationality, and the renunciation is not enforced internationally; the US does not recognize Mexican-required renunciations of US citizenship under Vance v. Terrazas / Afroyim v. Rusk. **US citizens naturalizing in Mexico keep their US passport** without action by either government.

A separate 1998 transition window (closed March 2003) allowed prior Mexicans who had lost nationality by US naturalization to recover it; ~67,000 used it. Not relevant for a never-Mexican US-citizen applicant.

## Naturalization by Residence — Default 5-Year Track

The standard path under **Art. 19 + Art. 20** of the Ley de Nacionalidad.

### Residence — 5 years (Art. 20)
**Five consecutive years** of legal residence in Mexico immediately preceding the application. **Both Temporary Resident and Permanent Resident time counts.** The 4y of Temporary Resident card + 1y of Permanent Resident (the standard ladder) qualifies; so does 5y of Permanent Resident directly if the applicant qualified for PR at consular filing.

### Physical presence — 18 of 24 months (Reglamento)
The Reglamento de la Ley de Nacionalidad imposes a strict presence test: **the applicant must have physically been in Mexico for at least 18 of the 24 months immediately preceding the application** — i.e., **no more than 180 days outside Mexico in the rolling 24-month window** before filing. SRE has rejected files where a single absence exceeded ~6 months even if total days outside the country were under 180.

This is the binding maintenance burden — well above a "warehouse permit" but below Finland-style 30-day-cap regimes.

### Means of livelihood
No fixed monetary threshold for naturalization itself. The applicant's lawful resident status (which already required income proof at the residency stage) is treated as sufficient. SRE does not re-run the income test.

### Good conduct / criminal record
- Mexican federal criminal-record check (constancia de no antecedentes penales).
- Foreign criminal-record certificate from the applicant's country of nationality (US federal + state-level FBI report), apostilled and Spanish-translated.

### Spanish — informal SRE-administered, no DELE
**No DELE or equivalent external test.** The Spanish test is administered by SRE at the naturalization interview and consists of: the applicant draws one card from a stack of image cards and verbally describes the image in **three complete grammatically correct sentences**, within 10 minutes. Latin American and Spanish nationals are exempt from this component; **US applicants are not exempt** but the bar is conversational, not academic. Conversational fluency at B1 is more than sufficient.

### Mexican history, geography, culture — 10-question written exam
The Examen de Cultura General Mexicana, redesigned in 2018 to be substantially more demanding than the earlier version:

- **10 questions** drawn from a public bank of ~200 questions published by SRE.
- **8 of 10** required to pass.
- **10 minutes** to complete.
- Coverage: history (independence, revolution, presidents), geography (states, capitals, rivers), civics (Constitution, symbols), popular culture, gastronomy.
- Applicants **60+** are exempt.
- Failure: 15-business-day waiting period before retake (and a re-paid fee).

### Good integration into Mexican national culture (Art. 19)
A qualitative requirement evaluated through the exam + interview together. Not separately tested for a working family of 5 living in Mexico full-time.

### Carta de Naturalización — issuance & timeline
After exam + interview pass, the file is forwarded from the SRE consular module / state delegation to the **DGAJ Central Office** in Mexico City, which issues the **Carta de Naturalización**. Practitioner estimates:

- File assembly + interview booking: **2–4 months** from initial appointment request.
- DGAJ review + Carta issuance: **6–12 months** after the interview, sometimes faster.
- **Total naturalization step (from filing to Carta in hand): ~12–18 months**, on top of the 5y residency clock.
- Total clock from arrival to passport: **~6.0–6.5 years** in the standard 4+1 Temporary→Permanent ladder.

### Fees
**MXN 8,755** application fee (since Jan 2025), ~USD 490 at MXN 18/USD.

## Reduced 2-Year Track (Art. 20.II)

Two years of legal residence suffices for any of:

- **Spouse of a Mexican citizen** with documented cohabitation in Mexico.
- **Parent of a Mexican-born child** (the child acquires Mexican nationality by jus soli at birth; the parent's clock resets to 2y).
- **Nationals of Latin American countries or the Iberian Peninsula** (Spain, Portugal).

**None apply to a US-citizen family with no Mexican spouse or Mexican-born child at arrival.** If a child were born in Mexico to this family, both parents would qualify for the 2y track from the child's birth date — a meaningful incidental accelerator but not a planning route this project is built around.

## Distinguished Service / Exceptional Talent (Art. 20.II discretionary)

The Executive may waive residency entirely for foreigners who have rendered **outstanding services** in cultural, scientific, technical, artistic, sports, or business matters. Rare, fully discretionary, not a planning route.

## CBI — None

Mexico has **no citizenship-by-investment program**. Capital does not buy a Carta. Real-estate or business investment can accelerate residency (by satisfying the Permanent Resident threshold directly) and counts toward the 5y clock, but the constitutional clock applies unchanged.

## Loss / Restoration

- **Naturalized Mexicans** may **lose** their nationality (Art. 37(B)) by acquiring an additional foreign nationality *after* naturalization, fraudulently asserting foreign nationality, or living abroad continuously for **5+ years**. The "additional foreign nationality" provision is a theoretical exposure for naturalized Mexicans who pick up *yet another* citizenship later; the US citizenship the applicant already holds at naturalization is grandfathered as a pre-existing nationality.
- **Mexicans by birth** are constitutionally **unloseable** (Art. 37(A)). This creates the asymmetric two-track quality also seen in Uruguay: birth-citizen rights are stronger than naturalized-citizen rights.

### Office-holding restrictions
The Constitution reserves several offices for **Mexicans by birth only**:

- **President / Vice-President**: by-birth only (Art. 82.I).
- **Federal Cabinet Secretaries**: by-birth only (Art. 91).
- **Supreme Court ministers**: by-birth only (Art. 95.I).
- **Federal Senate / Chamber of Deputies**: by-birth only (Art. 55.I, 58).
- **Governors of states**: by-birth only under most state constitutions.

Naturalized Mexicans can hold most other public-sector posts and may serve in the military with naturalization-letter dispensation. None of this is load-bearing for the user's family profile.

## Passport Strength

The Mexican passport carries **~158 visa-free / visa-on-arrival destinations** including Schengen, UK, Japan, Canada, most of South America. Stronger than most LATAM passports; weaker than EU passports. Most relevantly, Mexicans need a visa for the US — the US dual citizenship preserves that access anyway.

## Recent Changes (2024–2026)

- **Oct 1, 2024**: Claudia Sheinbaum inaugurated. No naturalization-law reform announced; the administration's immigration focus has been transit migration policy with the US, not naturalization framework.
- **Jan 2025**: Naturalization application fee raised to **MXN 8,755**.
- **Jul 2025**: SRE directive standardizing consular **UMA-based** financial calculations (affects the residency on-ramp, not the citizenship step).
- **2018** (background): Cultura General exam redesigned to be substantially harder — relevant because most expat guidance pre-2018 understates the difficulty.
- No change to the 5y residence floor, 18-of-24-month presence rule, or dual-citizenship policy.

## Comparison to Uruguay (the project's other LATAM 5y-ish path)

| Dimension | Mexico | Uruguay |
|---|---|---|
| Standard clock | 5y residence | 5y solo / 3y family |
| Reduced track for couples | None applicable | 3y "constituted family" applies to any foreign married couple |
| Total clock to passport | ~6.0–6.5y | ~3.5–4.0y family / 5.5–6.0y solo |
| Physical presence floor | 18 of 24 months (~9 mo/yr) | ~183 d/yr, no single absence >6mo |
| Spanish test | Informal SRE oral (image-card description) | Informal Corte Electoral hearing |
| Civics test | 10Q written, 2018 redesign harder | None |
| Dual citizenship | Yes (1998 reform) | Yes for legal citizens until they naturalize in a third country |
| Loss for naturalized | Yes, on 5y abroad or additional naturalization | Yes, on additional naturalization (loses Uruguayan) |
| Office restrictions | President/VP, Cabinet, Supreme Court, Congress: by-birth only | President/VP: natural only; Senate: 7y after Carta |
| CPI 2025 | **27 / 141st** (last among OECD) | **75 / 12th** (#1 in LATAM) |

Uruguay's 3y family track for foreign married couples is structurally faster *and* the rule-of-law / corruption profile is materially stronger. Mexico is competitive on cost of living and on the optionality of jus soli for any future child born there, but is dominated on the headline non-EU naturalization comparison.
