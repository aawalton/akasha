
# The attention budget

> The attention-budget model — the high-throughput road's capacity cashes out as available attention, split into a fixed baseline sensory draw and a discretionary remainder, so over- and under-stimulation are one fixed cost measured against a swinging budget.

The [high-throughput road](high-throughput-road.book-chapter.md) is capacity for something. Asked what it actually gives him when the traffic lights work, Alan's answer was one word: **attention.** "That's the core of the throughput — how much attention do I have available." When the regulation is good, the road clears, and what clears is attention he can then spend.

## Baseline draw and discretionary remainder

The attention budget splits in two:

- **Baseline sensory draw.** A fixed, always-on cost — the attention consumed just by processing ambient sensory input on a [non-habituating](non-habituation.book-chapter.md), [highly sensitive](nervous-system-sensitivity.book-chapter.md) nervous system. It's there whether or not Alan is using it for anything.
- **Discretionary remainder.** Whatever's left after the baseline draw. This is the usable budget — attention he can spend on whatever he wants in the moment.

The baseline draw is roughly a **fixed absolute cost**. What swings wildly is the total budget, and therefore the *proportion* the baseline takes.

## The economics: one fixed cost against a swinging budget

The whole over- vs. under-stimulation picture is one fixed sensory cost measured against a budget that swings by orders of magnitude with the regulation state:

- **Attention low → baseline draw is 50–100+% of the budget.** When the road is jammed and total attention has collapsed, the same fixed sensory cost now eats most or all of it. At 100+%, demand exceeds capacity: there's nothing left over, the budget overflows, and the overflow *is* the overstimulation. No discretionary attention remains.
- **Attention high → the draw is unmeasurable.** When the road is clear and total attention is large, Alan **doesn't usually notice the draw enough to measure it.** It's a rounding error against the budget. This is the "[empty road](high-throughput-road.book-chapter.md#direction-inverts-with-resources)" — there's so much discretionary attention that the baseline cost disappears, and the absence of demand reads as understimulation.

So over- and under-stimulation are not two different amounts of input. They are **one fixed sensory cost measured against a wildly swinging attention budget.** The cost never changed; the budget did. This is the same one-input-two-readings structure the [road model](high-throughput-road.book-chapter.md) gives at the hardware layer — here stated in the currency the throughput actually spends.

## Why this reframes sensory reduction

If the baseline sensory draw is a fixed cost subtracted from the budget, then cutting that draw reclaims discretionary attention directly. Minimalism and the broader sensory-reduction kit (see [sensory-regulation-tools.md](sensory-regulation-tools.book-chapter.md)) read, under this model, as **lowering the baseline draw to widen the discretionary remainder** — and that move matters most when the budget is smallest, i.e. when Alan is depleted and the draw is already eating 50–100+% of what little he has. The toolkit inventory is being mapped separately; the point here is just the mechanism that makes it pay off.

The competition has a third party: the [interoceptive channels](sensory-experience.book-chapter.md) bid in this same budget and usually lose — body signals arrive at low amplitude against external senses (sound alone ~5x louder), which is why hunger and thirst go unnoticed until the external field quiets. Cutting the draw doesn't just free discretionary attention; it lets the quiet channels through. The dial runs both directions: deliberately routing attention *to* a quiet signal raises its felt amplitude — which is how watching for loneliness amplified it (the [solitude experiment](solitude-experiment.book-chapter.md)'s iatrogenic hypothesis).
