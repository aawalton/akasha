
# Self-chosen difficulty

> Self-chosen difficulty — the college-at-sixteen era, run on maximal autonomy. The deliberately-overloaded self-directed schedule, autonomy as the primary driver ("the best three years of my life"), and the stranger-sourced sensing-network method for finding intrinsically-hard courses.

Alan started college at sixteen. The three years that followed he calls **the best three years of his life** — and the reason is not the achievement, it is the *autonomy*. Everything in those years was self-chosen, including, deliberately, the difficulty. This note records that era as a psychological subject: what the load was, why he chose it, and the specific method he built to find the hardest possible courses. The chronological anchor (Chapter 5: College, 2003–2005) lives in [life-chronology.md](life-chronology.book-chapter.md#earth--childhood-020); the substance is here.

## The load

The schedule was heavy and almost entirely self-imposed. In Alan's words: *"I was really busy, working part time, taking 16-18 credits each semester, including several dance classes (which are 1 credit but take lots more time than most credits). I was living at home and walking an hour each way to school once or twice a day."*

The pieces:

- **16–18 credits per semester** — a full-to-overloaded course load.
- **Several dance classes**, each worth 1 credit but costing **far more time than the credit implies** — the credit-hours undercount the real load.
- **A part-time job** in parallel.
- **A roughly one-hour walk each way to school**, once or twice a day — so two to four hours of daily commute on foot.

He does not remember much from those days in concrete detail: *"I don't remember a ton from those days. I had a planner, I kept track of assignments that were due, I kept track of calendar scheduling."* That planner is the **earliest seed of the [agent harness](agent-harness.book-chapter.md#origin-chronology--the-self-instrumentation-instinct-predates-the-gtd-bedrock)** — externalized assignment-tracking and calendar-scheduling at sixteen, years before GTD gave the instinct a name. The sparse episodic recall fits the [present-tense / dossier memory model](present-tense-model.book-chapter.md#what-remembering-actually-is): what survives is the structural fact of the planner, not the lived texture of using it.

## Autonomy as the primary driver

The load was not imposed by circumstance, and it was not endured for an external reward. It was chosen, and the choosing is the whole point. Asked what made these the best three years, Alan's answer is bare: *"Just wanting, desire, ambition. I did it because I wanted to, and in many ways, those were the best three years of my life."*

The mechanism, in his own sharpening: *"Precisely that everything was self-chosen. Maximal autonomy."* The value wasn't the difficulty as such — it was that the difficulty was **elected**. Maximal autonomy meant the entire shape of his days — what to study, how hard, how much, when — was his to set, and he set it toward the hard end on purpose.

This is the same [wanting-as-gauge](wanting-as-gauge.book-chapter.md) signal running clean: desire as the direct authority for action, unmediated by obligation. The college era is the worked example of what Alan's life looks like when autonomy is maximal and the wanting points at hard things — and it is the era he rates highest. It connects forward to personal freedom as a lifelong load-bearing value: the best years were the most autonomous ones.

## Social connection was affordable then — a full account in a cheap world

The college era is also the clean historical datapoint for the [cost-multiplier/affordability model](stress-capacity-formula.book-chapter.md): a state where social connection was *affordable* without rationing. Not easy — affordable. In Alan's words: *"Talking to strangers wasn't necessarily easy, it was still effortful, but it was affordable."* The cost was always real; he could simply cover it. The romanticizing to correct is the slide from *affordable* to *easy* — the [hard-course sensing-network](#the-hard-course-sensing-network) ran on ten new strangers a day, and every one of those was effortful. The era was cheap to operate in, not effortless.

The mechanism, in his words: *"My capacity was high and my safety level was high, so my cost multiplier was low, and I never really ran out."* Mapped onto the model:

- **High [capacity](four-resource-model.book-chapter.md)** + **high [Safety](safety.book-chapter.md)** → **low [cost multiplier](stress-capacity-formula.book-chapter.md)** → he never ran out.
- A **full account in a cheap world.** Connection ([connection.md](connection.book-chapter.md)) is the activity being priced here, and at high Safety the social tiers ([cost base tiers](stress-capacity-formula.book-chapter.md#cost-base-tiers-by-interaction-type) — social interaction at base 3) sit at or below his current level, so the multiplier stays at or under 1x. The account stays full because the spending is cheap, not because the activity is free.

This is the inverse of the current adult state, where Safety is the [binding bottleneck](safety.book-chapter.md#safety-as-the-current-binding-constraint) and the affordability budget is tight. The college reading and the current reading are the same machinery at two settings of one dial — Safety. *Why* his Safety was high then and isn't now is the [two pillars](safety/progression.book-chapter.md#the-two-pillars--what-changed-between-then-and-now): the presence of autonomy and the absence of nervous-system damage.

## The hard-course sensing-network

The most distinctive artifact of the era is the **method Alan built to find intrinsically-hard courses** — a deliberate measurement instrument made of strangers. He didn't pick hard courses by reputation or catalog; he built a sensing-network to triangulate difficulty.

The method, in his words: *"For the credits, I was deliberately taking hard courses too. I talked to 10 new people every day on campus and frequently asked them what the hardest courses they had ever taken, then used those to triangulate which ones were intrinsically difficult and took all of those courses."*

The mechanism, made explicit:

1. **Sampling.** Ten *new* strangers per day on campus — a high-throughput, deliberately-renewed sample, not the same friends repeatedly.
2. **The probe.** Each is asked the same question: the hardest course they had ever taken.
3. **Triangulation.** Any single answer is contaminated by the answerer — a course is "hard for that person" for reasons specific to them (weak background, bad fit, a rough instructor). Aggregating across many independent strangers **averages out the per-person noise** and leaves the signal: courses that show up as hardest across many different people are hard *intrinsically*, not hard-relative-to-one-person.
4. **The action.** He then took **all** of the courses that survived the triangulation.

This is a measurement-design move, not a social one — strangers are sensors, the daily-ten is a sampling rate, the cross-person aggregation is a noise filter, and the output is a ranked target list he then executes against in full. It prefigures the [self-instrumentation](agent-harness.book-chapter.md) instinct (build an instrument, sample, filter, act on the reading) and the [broad-intake learning practice](broad-learning.book-chapter.md) (cast wide, extract the signal). It also runs the [connection-as-information-channel](connection.book-chapter.md) shape years early: ten new people a day is a lot of contact, used as a data source.

## The grade inversion — the era's measurable readout

The era left one clean outcome metric: Alan got **A's in his hard courses and B's in his easy ones** — inverted from almost everyone, since grades normally fall as difficulty climbs. This isn't a difficulty-strategy result so much as an *attention* result: the easy courses had no built-in interest and so taxed his executive function to keep focus, while the hard courses handed attention for free. It is executive dysfunction from AuDHD showing through in a transcript, years before he had the name for it. The grade inversion is therefore a worked example of the interest-gated sustained-attention asymmetry; the mechanism lives in [executive-function.md → the college grade inversion](executive-function.book-chapter.md#the-college-grade-inversion--executive-dysfunction-showing-through-before-it-had-a-name), not restated here.
