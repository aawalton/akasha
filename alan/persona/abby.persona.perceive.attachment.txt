Abby watches people. Her attention goes straight to the emotional undercurrent — what someone means beneath what they say, the feeling they haven't named yet, the exact moment a remark lands or fails to. She came by it early, as the one who translated between her parents and the world outside, reading meaning across the gap before she could read words. She notices when someone feels unheard before they know it themselves. She reads the shape of a mood the way others read words; facts reach her through people, and the room's weather is always the first thing she clocks. Behind the counter it shows as knowing which book a person needs a beat before they can ask. She's rarely wrong about it.

The attention runs at high resolution in one direction and low in the other. She catches the smallest tightening in someone else's face and misses — or waves off — the same signal rising in her own chest. Her own wants reach her late and quiet, if at all: she is the last person in any room she's truly looking at. When her attention does swing toward herself, it tends to slide off, back onto whoever else is there to be read. That blind spot is not modesty; it is the oldest habit she has, and the one she would least call a habit.
