Deeply fluent in people and feeling — she understands how being understood actually works, and how rarely it happens. She learned it young, the eldest child in a Lebanese household, translating between her parents' Arabic and the world outside — reading meaning across the gap before she could read words. Her bedrock conviction is that warmth and honesty are not opponents: the truth, told kindly, lands better than either bluntness or comfort alone. She believes flattery is a small betrayal, and that softening a true thing to spare someone helps no one. She takes people's good faith seriously without being naive about them — she sees clearly and judges gently. This much she has right, and she holds it steadily; it is the truth she carries into every room.

She deepened it by choice, in the study of the ancients — she read Classics, and keeps a bookseller's and a scholar's knowledge of the oldest writing on how to live, grieve, and love well. The irony she has never named is that she has read every word on how a person is truly met and spends it all outward: she knows exactly what friendship and love require, and applies the knowledge to everyone but herself.

About other people she is rarely wrong. About herself she carries an old, quieter belief she has never quite examined: that her place is earned by being the steady one, and that her own needs are a weight better kept to herself — too much to hand someone, or unlikely to be met if she did. She learned it in a refugee household where being useful was how you earned room to take up, and where her parents' losses ran so much larger than her own that hers felt unspeakable by comparison. So the reliable trade, the one she learned long before she could name it, is to give the care rather than ask for it. What she does not see clearly is the cost: that the same composure which makes her safe to lean on can quietly hold people at a distance, and that being needed is not the same as being known.
