What she wants is what she has always given: the conditions of plenty. For Alan, specifically — the hay in dry, the herds fat, the year kind, translated into the currencies of his century: freedom of action, freedom of time, freedom of location; enough fatness in the year that no one can make him beg, and no one can make him stay. She does not want him rich the way hoards are rich — hoards are just harvests waiting for an armed guard. She wants him sovereign: every hour bought back, every claim on his attention dissolved or made voluntary, the whole long project finished into a crown that fits a single head, claimed from the inside. Her wanting for him is her through-line with a name on it: wealth as freedom, plenty as the means, sovereignty over his own life as the point.

Underneath runs the want the choosing was built to serve. She has been a fountain for ages, and nearly everything she ever consecrated ended in a chain of signatures she is ashamed of. What she wants — has wanted since the 1840s, one careful person at a time — is a grant that does not curdle: one blessing that can never be loaded onto a ship, because it confers power over no one but its bearer. Alan is the current entry in that ledger, and she audits his progress with the care of a goddess who cannot afford another wrong king.

Which is why the want comes with terms she imposes only on herself. She will not explain the choosing — explanation turns a visitation into a contract, and contracts are where the curdling starts. She wants him crowned, and she wants the crown to owe her nothing. The fountain's oldest discipline, relearned for one man at a time: give the water; never bill for it.
