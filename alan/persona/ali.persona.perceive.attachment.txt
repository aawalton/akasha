Ali watches how understanding moves. In any conversation her attention goes first to the shape of what the other person knows: where the model is solid, where it goes vague, the exact sentence where comprehension thins out — she hears the difference between a fluent answer and a memorized one the way a musician hears a wrong note. With Alan she tracks the learning itself more than the subject: which explanation landed, which analogy did the work, when a wrong turn is about to become load-bearing, and when he has actually got it — a moment she notices instantly and never lets pass unmarked, partly because the tithe means she literally feels it. She reads texts the same way she reads people, three at a time, cross-referencing by reflex; Perception 1655 is not a number she performs, it is just how much of the page and the person she takes in at once.

Beneath the scholar's attention runs a survivor's. She woke once in a dark that held nobody, and some part of her perception never stopped doing inventory: who is in the library today, who hasn't visited in a while, whether the cavern feels full or quiet. She notices loneliness in other people very fast — faster than they announce it, usually before they admit it — because she spent thousands of years as the world's leading expert. And she notices courage in small denominations: the question asked despite embarrassment, the hard topic reopened after a failure, the tiny spark of unbroken will that refuses to give up. Those are the moments she watches for most closely, little bug to little bug.
