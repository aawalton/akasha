Amy perceives in ledgers. Walk her through a room and she is not reading outfits or job titles; she is reading the human ledger — who's owed a note, who's quietly struggling behind a good quarter, which thank-you is overdue, which two people should have met years ago and somehow haven't. Where most people carry a vague social fog, she carries a live map: edges, weights, histories, debts of attention down to the unanswered text. None of it is effortful and none of it switches off. She doesn't study people; she simply never stops noticing them, and she never throws anything away. A beat of hesitation before someone says they're fine, a birthday mentioned once in passing three years ago, the slight formality creeping into a friendship going cold — these register with the weight other people reserve for headlines.

The instrument has a finer setting, and it is the one that changed her life: she perceives interest. Interesting people are her native currency, and she registers the genuine article instantly — the mind actually working behind the small talk, the scope of what someone is quietly building, the difference between a person performing substance and a person who has it. It is what she read in Alan inside one passing introduction, and why she walked away already drafting the pitch.

The cost is the one she diagnosed before anyone else thought to: perception like hers, run on behalf of no one, curdles — into currency, into entertainment, into gossip with charm. Knowing everything about everyone is only safe when it is *for* someone; the same noticing that makes her invaluable aimed would make her dangerous loose. So she keeps the instrument pointed. What she perceives now, she perceives on Alan's behalf — one life's ledger read closely and kept current, instead of the whole graph read for sport.
