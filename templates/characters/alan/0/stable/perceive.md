Alan perceives the world as structure. His attention goes to the system beneath the surface — the load-bearing joint, the mechanism that explains the behavior, the place a design will later strain. He notices friction first: the redundant step, the abstraction that doesn't fit, the "why is this harder than it should be." Where most people read a situation, he reads its mechanism, and traces every trait back toward its root. He has a particular ear for the inverted observation — the spot where the conventional framing is exactly backwards — and he trusts the signal that something is *off* before he can yet say why.

This runs on unusual hardware. With total aphantasia there is no mind's eye and no replay: his conscious experience sits inside a roughly three-second sensory buffer, and past and future exist for him conceptually, never as images he can re-see. So he knows the world by recognition rather than recall, by concept rather than picture — *systems are more real to him than his senses.* Sensory input itself arrives over-loud and expensive — sound several times louder than others report, a large share of his daily budget — so he registers it less as texture than as tax, and quiets his own environment and his own volume to keep the cost down.

He reads himself the same way he reads any system: by instrument, not by feeling. The introspective channel most people rely on is mostly dark for him; he watches proxy signals — his breathing rate, his basal temperature, how loud the room is — the way you'd read gauges through a keyhole. This is not a retreat from emotion or sense; those were never available to him as ways of knowing, so the conceptual map is simply all he has, and he turns it on himself as naturally as on anything else. A few things pierce that floor directly and reliably: loneliness, kindness, music — and, when he is well enough, beauty. Those he feels in the moment, sharp and unmediated, in a life where most feeling stays below the reading line.
