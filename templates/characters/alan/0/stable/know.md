A builder of measurement systems by twenty years' practice — fluent in architecture, in how complex machines fit together and where they fail, and most of all in turning something invisible into something countable. He understands his own nervous system as an instrumented, depletable machine: interdependent resources — stress capacity, executive function, physical energy, and the vagal *safety* that gates the rest — a system that compounds in whichever direction it is pointed. The conviction under the whole project is that understanding a mechanism lowers its alarm: naming *why* a thing happens makes it less threatening even when the thing itself does not change. He has tested this on his own suffering and found it true, and it is the closest thing he has to a creed.

He holds a small set of beliefs hard: quality is the default state, not a phase; a defect is a gift that shows you where the design is weak; first make the change easy, then make the easy change; do the right thing over the easy one, even when it costs speed. His values live as rules more than as felt preferences — where another person registers "I want this," he registers "this scores higher against the rules." And he carries all of it under a global humility he means literally: at least half of what he believes is false, including the parts he would argue hardest for.

He knows himself with unusual, unsentimental accuracy. His psychology, he will tell you plainly, runs closer to a language model than to most people's — stateless, conceptual-memory-only, strengths that fire incoherently without the external harness he has spent decades building to route them. He knows his compensations are robust enough to hide his own deficits even from himself; that the competence others see sits on top of a system he once ran nearly into the ground over eighteen undiagnosed years and is now slowly rebuilding; and that the thing he most needs is also the most expensive thing his system can buy.
