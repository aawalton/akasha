A crossing with no seam, a key that knew its lock, a door that answered itself — and now a lovely young woman climbed out of the dark carrying supper for a stranger. *Magical* is the only word the evening has left you, and you say so, and thank her for climbing a hill in the dark for a man she'd never met.

Then you hear yourself asking — more honest than smooth — whether the two of you might simply go in. "It would be lonely, eating a first meal here alone. And that bread smells delightful."

"You're kind — and kinder than most could manage, for a man who woke in a field with no memory of the walk. Yes. I'd not have you eat your first meal in this house alone; that's the better half of why I climbed. I'd like nothing in the world better than to come in."

You walk up the last of the path together, her lantern throwing the two of you long across the dark garden, and you are still three strides from the great door when the bolt slides back.

From the inside. By no hand. The door swings open a hand's width, and warm air breathes out of a house that has been cold for three generations — bread-and-woodsmoke warmth, lived-in warmth — and far down the dark of the hall something banked and red-gold stirs like a fire that has been waiting up.

"There — you see, it does that now." Amy's voice stays exactly as unhurried as it has been all night. "Dark since before my grandmother, and tonight it's breathing bread and woodsmoke at you like it's glad you came. I'm not frightened of it. I've kept this house far too long to be frightened of it finally waking."

Then, as your hand comes up toward the iron-banded wood:

"But you go first. It's your door, and whatever slid that bolt chose you tonight, not me — so let the house be yours before it's ours. Go on. I'm a step behind you, with the bread."

So you push the door wide, and you cross.

The Hearthall receives you the way a coat receives its owner. Long and high-beamed, and not the hall of an empty house — a kept hall: chairs that expect to be sat in, a long table with the particular shine of wood wiped down daily whether anyone has eaten there or not. And at the heart of it, set into stone worn smooth by generations of standing exactly where you now stand, the hearthstone burns — low, patient, banked to last, the source of everything warm this house has breathed at you tonight.

Behind you, one step behind you, Amy Laurens crosses the threshold of Hearthholt for the first time in all her years of keeping it — and the fire's low gold rises a shade to meet the two of you, like a hand held out.
