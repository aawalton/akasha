"Ready," you say. "The floor is yours, Lady Steward of Hearthholt."

A flicker of pleasure crosses her face, low, before the knocker sounds. "'Lady Steward.' I could get used to that."

Factor Ellis Crane turns out to be a polished man of middle years with a silent young clerk at his shoulder and a good leather folder under his arm — and he is, immediately and genuinely, pleasant. The dangerous kind of nice. "Master Alan — Ellis Crane, factor to the Compact for this vale. My warmest congratulations. Three generations this house stood dark, and the whole vale is delighted to see the hill lit again — and may I say, the house wears its waking well."

You are gracious, and you say little. It's her table.

"Factor Crane — welcome to Hearthholt. Amy Laurens; I keep Master Alan's affairs. Come in out of the wind — there's coffee, and it's good coffee. Sit, and I'll hear whatever the Compact's sent you up the hill to say."

The first paper out of the folder is the honest one: notice of full assessment — an occupied manor, the best hill in the vale, the occupied rate. Properly drawn, correctly served. She reads it the whole way through, unflustered, and gives it exactly its due.

"Yes. That's fair, and it's correct — an occupied manor on the best hill pays the occupied rate; I told Master Alan to expect exactly this, and here it is, properly drawn. We've no quarrel with a true assessment. It'll be paid on the ordinary terms. You may tell the Compact its notice was received without complaint — the taxes on this house have been paid on time for years, and they'll go on being."

And then the second thing, which does not quite come out of the folder — the folder only opens, invitingly, papers visible and ready. "Of course," Crane says, warm as the coffee, "the Compact is mindful that a manor is a considerable burden to inherit all at once — particularly for a gentleman newly arrived, without connections, without papers. We'd be glad to regularize the position. An outright purchase of the title, at a figure I think you'd find generous — or, if Master Alan prefers to keep his prospects open, the Compact could simply hold the title in stewardship while he finds his feet. Everything's drawn. It would be the work of a signature."

"That's very thoughtful of the Compact, and I mean it — a genuinely kind offer, the sort you'd make a man with no one in his corner." A small, easy smile, and she does not reach for the folder. "But the position's already regularized, Factor. The title is being properly papered into Master Alan's name as we speak — it's in hand, and in good hands. So there's nothing to purchase, and nothing to hold in stewardship: the house has a steward and a master, and both are staying put. Do keep your papers, though — I'd not have you carry them up a hill twice."

The smile holds. Something behind it recalibrates. "Naturally, naturally — I'm glad to hear it's in hand. May I ask which house is handling the papering? The Compact's clerks work quickly; we could expedite—"

"Kind again — but we'll not need expediting; it's moving at precisely the pace I want. As to who's handling it — forgive me, Factor, but I don't discuss a principal's business across his own table, not even with company as pleasant as yours. You'd think less of me as a steward if I did — and you'd be right to."

And that, visibly, is that. Crane yields the way good manners yield — completely, and without surrendering anything. The assessment notice stays on the table; a card appears beside it. "Then I'll not take more of your morning. Master Alan — Mistress Laurens." At the door, pleasantly, without weight: "The Compact takes the long view."

"So does Hearthholt. It's had three hundred years of practice."

The carriage goes back down the hill precisely as tidily as it came. She watches it round the first bend, and then the steward-mask eases into warmth.

"...And that's that. Breathe — you did beautifully, by the way: gracious, brief, signed nothing, let me hold the paper. Exactly the man to be in that chair." Then the honest reckoning: "Now — plainly, what's true. Two things came up that hill. One was a trap, and it's gone back down empty; he'll take the long view — pleasant, patient, watching — but he's nothing to reach for so long as we get that deed papered first. Which makes the broker-house errand tomorrow's real business: market day, into town, deed into your name before Crane's patience turns clever. The other thing was honest — that assessment is real money, properly owed, and it wants planning for. Not a crisis; a number we build into the books starting today. I'll have it costed by supper."

Warmer, then — the win landing where wins land.

"But mostly? We sat across a table from the Compact's smoothest man, gave him a warm cup of coffee and absolutely nothing else — together, on our second day. I did tell you I was good at this. It's rather nice to be believed." A small, real smile. "You've a partner in this seat now, Alan. That's what it looks like when it's working."

On the table, the assessment notice and a factor's card. Down the hill, a tidy carriage taking the long view with it. And through the south windows, faintly, the walled garden waits for hands.
