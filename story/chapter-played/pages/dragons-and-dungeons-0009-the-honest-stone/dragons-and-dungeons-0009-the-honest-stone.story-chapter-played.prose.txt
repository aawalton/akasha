The black-scaled girl ate apart from us, and I left her to it; I did not want to crowd a creature still deciding whether free meant safe. So I went to Tygryth instead, and sat, and asked him for the one thing no one in three hundred years had thought to.

"Friend," I said, "I know I am not yet strong enough to truly stand at your side in what is coming. You have seen me fight. You have lived long and learned much. Is there anything you could teach me, while we prepare for the next step of the road?"

Something shifted in the ancient face — surprised, in a way three centuries rarely allow. He glanced once at the freed girl finding her feet apart from us, and marked that I had given her the room, and then he settled onto the fallen piling and gestured me down across from him.

"In three centuries no short-lived thing has ever asked me to teach it," he said. "They ask me to fight for them, to fly them somewhere, to frighten someone. You ask to be more than you are." A slow nod. "Your creed. The one thing they cannot collar is the self you make. Yes. I can teach to that." He held up one hand — the same hand that had pulled the sound out of the world. "You think the hush is a dragon's trick, beyond you. It is not — not the root of it. The root is subtraction. When I take the sound from a place, first I take it from myself — my own breath, my own heart-drum, the roar of my own wanting. Only when I am quiet inside can I feel what the world is doing outside." His gold eyes held mine. "You already do a sliver of this. That hauler — you did not watch the knife. You felt where it was already going, and stepped off the line before it arrived. You read his intent in the breath before the strike. You did it by instinct. I will teach you to do it by will."

He laid out the lesson plainly: sit, breathe low into the belly, and put down the noise inside one strand at a time — the ache in the ribs, the drum of the heart, and loudest of all the wanting, the part braced for the next fight. Quiet all three, he said, and the world gets louder; intent has a texture, and it arrives a half-breath before motion does, and a half-breath is everything. "It will not come today," he said. "It comes in years. But the first sitting is the first stone, and we have hours before dusk." Across the bank Wren wrestled the blue lantern out from under the steering-bench and threw over her shoulder, dry, "Oh, good — meditation. I'll just rig the boat that sails us into a chain-maker temple all by myself, shall I?" There was no real bite in it. She left us to it.

So I sat, and I went straight past the first three steps. I had long known how to take the sound out of myself — to still all noise and rope my breath to my heartbeat; that part of his discipline I had carried since the temple. So I reached past it, outward, for the thing I had felt when he stilled not only himself but the world. He had said I could do the same. I trusted him. I tried to replicate it, even the smallest part.

And I reached for the wrong thing, and almost touched it anyway.

I could not take the sound from the world — I understood later I was never going to; the hush is wrung from a muscle no human body owns. But in that perfect inner quiet I found the place where he gripped it. My awareness brushed a seam in the world, a cold smooth edge, the handle of a door far too large for my hand. I could not pull it open. But for one heartbeat I leaned my weight on it — and the river dropped a half-tone, just around me, just for that breath, a small soft circle an arm's span wide where the reeds went faint and the water pulled back and my own silent heartbeat was the loudest thing left. Then it snapped shut, and the cost landed all at once: a bright spike behind my eyes, a thread of warmth at my nose.

When I opened my eyes, Tygryth's were wide, the molten gold of them lit with something I had not seen on him before. Not pride. Closer to alarm. "Stop. Stop there." His hand came up, gentle, halting. "You felt the seam." It was not a question. "Three hundred years, monk. It took me the better part of a century behind a collar to find the edge of that door, with a dragon's body screaming at me to. You found it in a single sitting and leaned your whole weight on it with a borrowed hand. There is blood on your lip. That is the door telling you it is too heavy. Push it the way you just tried and you will tear something in yourself that does not grow back." The alarm cooled into a teacher's care, and something graver underneath. "So hear the lesson change. You are not built to take the sound. But you have proved you can feel the seam — and feeling is the half that matters in a fight. You reached for my power and brushed my senses instead, and the senses are yours to keep, safely, for as long as you train them." A dark, slow almost-smile. "I offered to teach you a stone a year. You picked up three before the river warmed your blood. I will be honest, friend — you frighten me a little. Keep the sensing. Leave the door to me. Now wipe your face, and this time reach only to listen — not to push."

I wiped my lip, and breathed, and understood. "Thank you for the concern and the guidance," I said. "I think I follow. But it would be easier with something to sense — something to stand in for the threats I'll need to find later." I turned and called across the bank to the girl with the black scales, with a smile. "My lady — would you care to amuse yourself by throwing pebbles at me while I meditate? I would count it a favor."

She was crouched with her back to the cage she'd climbed out of, a flat river-stone already turning over and over in her scaled fingers. Her head tilted, amber eyes narrowing, and something that was not quite a smile pulled at the corner of her mouth. "You'll regret asking," she told me, low and rough. "I don't miss." The first pebble was already gone — a lazy underhand flick, no real venom in it, a test throw aimed to clip my shoulder.

I closed my eyes and let it come. And I felt it — not the stone, the intent: a small wrongness plucking at the edge of my stilled awareness, a pull off to my left a half-second before it arrived. I felt it, and could not place it fast enough; my mind reached toward it, grabbed for it, tried to hunt it down — and the stone clipped my shoulder, a clean light tap, exactly where she'd aimed. But my shoulder had already begun to turn. A finger's width. A heartbeat late, yet unmistakably moving — my body answering a warning my mind hadn't finished reading.

Tygryth made a low pleased sound. "There. You felt it — and then you chased it, and the chasing is what cost you the half-breath. This is the hardest turn for a fighting man: do less. Do not hunt the threat. Let it arrive. Sit like a still pool and let its wrongness fall into you as a ripple. Your body already trusts the sense more than your mind does. Now get your loud, clever mind out of its way."

"I nod," I said, and stilled myself again, eyes closed, and let it come.

This time I did not chase. This time I was a still pool with my eyes shut — and so the lie never reached me, because the lie was for the eyes. Two stones left her hands. To a watching man the high one would have been the whole world: slow, bright, arcing, here it comes, catch it. But I was not watching. In the quiet the high stone arrived almost weightless, a pretty thing with no venom behind it, a ripple so faint it barely stirred the pool. It carried no meaning. The low one screamed — off her knuckles, sidearm, blind-side, aimed at the seam of my ribs, a hard dark ripple of real intent, the only honest thing she threw. I felt it a half-breath early, low and to the right, and for once my clever mind stayed out of the way; my forearm was already dropping across my body, and the rib-cracker met the meat of my arm with a flat crack and skipped off into the reeds. The pretty stone sailed past my unmoved head and plopped into the river, ignored. I never opened my eyes.

For a moment there was only the river and her rough, surprised laugh hanging in the gold air. "Now we're playing," she breathed.

Tygryth took a breath before he trusted his voice. "Do you understand what you just did? She lied to you with the bright stone, the way every real enemy will. And it did nothing, because you were not watching her hand — you were feeling her intent. Here is the whole secret, and you found it yourself: a feint is a lie the eye can be told, but it is a lie the sense cannot, because the real blow still has to mean it. The killing stroke always carries weight the decoy does not. The eye sees two threats. The stillness hears only the one that means to land." He shook his head slowly. "I could lie to your eyes all day. I do not think I could lie to that." A glance at his feral kin, crouched and grinning with a third stone already in her fingers, and something rueful and warm crossed the dusk-dark face. "And it seems my blood has decided she likes you. Gods help us both."

This time I stayed still with my eyes closed, but resolved that if I caught the stone I would send it back to her. And she did something I would only understand after. She rocked up onto her knees and picked the throw clean and slow and announced — a straight true arc, no feint beneath it, no second truth hiding behind the first. "This one's true," she told me, low, and I heard that she meant more than the stone. "No catch in it." The pebble left her fingers. "See if you can tell the difference now — between the stone that wants to hurt you, and the one that just wants to be caught."

It fell into my stillness with no lie laid over it — one clean ripple, single and true, arriving exactly where the sense said it would and exactly when. The rib-cracker had screamed; this one did not scream at all. It came in soft, unhurried, almost asking, a ripple shaped less like a threat than like an open hand. My eyes stayed closed. My hand rose on its own — not a block, not the hard tuck of a forearm, but my palm turning up and open into the dusk-gold air — and the stone settled into it as if it had been thrown for no other reason. My fingers closed. Caught. It was the first thing all evening that did not feel like training. It felt like an answer.

I had meant to throw it back. I did not. I opened my eyes and found hers already on me, and instead of returning the stone I closed my hand around it and tucked it away into my pocket. "Thank you, my lady, for assisting me in this," I said, and meant every word of it. "I hope you enjoyed it as much as I did — and I will treasure your gift."

Something in her face came undone before she could stop it. She looked away fast, down at her own scaled hands, and when her voice came it was rougher than she wanted, reaching for gruffness like the only armor she had left. "It's a rock, monk. The river's full of them." But she could not make it land mean. It came out wrecked and small, and she did not take it back.

And then — because there were dead men waiting across the water who needed reminding what they had become, and I told her so as I turned toward the boat — I felt her not let me go the way the cage had taught her to. She got up. Legs steadier than they had been an hour before, blood and river-mud drying on her hands, and she fell in beside me — not behind, not at heel, but at my side, close enough that her shoulder could brush my arm. She did not ask if she was allowed. "Where you're going," she said, low, the gruff gone now, just bare and certain underneath, "there's more of them. More of the men who make cages. I'm coming." A breath, and then the truth slipped out, quieter, the first thing she had ever chosen to belong to. "Not for you. For the ones still in the dark, waiting for someone to take the cloth off. Somebody should have come for me sooner." Her jaw set. "So I'll go be the somebody." And she looked at me sidelong, the ghost of the grin coming back, feral and alive. "Try to keep up."

Tygryth had gone very still, and when he looked at her there were three centuries behind his eyes. "For three hundred years I have watched my blood be taken into the dark," he said, for her alone. "I have never once watched it walk in on its own feet to bring someone out." He inclined his head to her — not a grandfather to a child, but one dragon to another, the deepest courtesy his kind owns. "That is the thing they could never collar." Then, sidelong to me, dry as old stone, reassembling himself: "You have set something loose in my blood, monk. I have not decided whether to thank you or fear you. Likely both."

And then it was time, and the four of us went down to the water as the last gold drained out of the west.
