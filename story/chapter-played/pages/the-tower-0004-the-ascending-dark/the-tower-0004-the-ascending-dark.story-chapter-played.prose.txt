# The Ascending Dark

Then you climb — up the cleared floor, past the dead Sentinel's rubble, to the arch at the head of the stair the guardian died holding — and you go through it carefully, the Furnace-heart raised before you for its dim red light.

Beyond the arch, the floor falls away. There is no floor. You stand at the lip of a vast black shaft that climbs and drops past any light you carry — a lightless throat of the Tower with cold air sighing up out of the depths, dust and old grease on its breath — and far, far above, so high it might be a star, a single thin seam of grey: the way out. Stairs climb the wall in broken bolted flights, some fallen away into gaps; and past where the stairs give out there are SLABS — great flat stones rising and sinking through the dark on a slow, steady beat, passing each other on no machine you can see. You nudge a chip of rock off the lip. It falls, and falls, and you never hear it land. A fall here is not a hurt. It is the end.

And you are not alone in it. Somewhere up in the moving gaps between the slabs, something LIVES — you hear it before anything else: a dry shift of weight, a breath that is not the wind, circling in the black where your small light cannot reach. Then it goes silent, and strain as you might you cannot find it, cannot see it. It hunts the dark, and the dark is everything here, and your eyes were never your strength. But your mind is. Crouched at the lip with the core's glow in your fist, you read the whole shape of it cold: this floor belongs to the thing in the black, and the one thing that takes it back is LIGHT. And there — wedged in the wall at the first broken landing, within a careful reach — a guttered lantern-bracket and a sealed flask of oil, sitting just where a climber would want them. Light first. Then the moving stones and their patient beat, and the long fall under every leap.

You have not set a foot past the lip. You have only looked, and listened, and understood. The way up is a dark mouth with a hunter in it and a single lamp waiting to be lit — and you crouch at the threshold of it, a warm mace in one hand and a banked coal in the other, having learned the shape of the dark without yet stepping into it.

You decide the dark mouth itself is the place to learn. You will not climb into it — better to meet whatever lives up there here, at the lip, with footing under you, than higher where a wrong step is the end. So you plant yourself at the threshold and begin to practice.

Small. As small as you can pinch a thing of fire and still send it. You flick a bead of emberlight up into the black and it sails and pops against far stone, and for a heartbeat a patch of the shaft stands lit — then dark again. Another, smaller, steadier; another. Spot by patient spot you paint the lower throat of the shaft out of the black and hold it in your head: the first broken flights bolted to the wall and the gaps where they have fallen away; the nearest slabs rising and sinking on their slow regular beat; and there at the first landing, just where you read it from the threshold, the guttered bracket and the sealed flask of oil waiting to be lit. Your control tightens as you go — the bolts coming smaller, truer, costing you less to place exactly where you want them.

And you test the three ways of throwing them. Direct, from your own hand, they are sharpest and come the instant you will them — but each one is paid out of you. Pushed through Burning Anger they leap far and bright, the mace flinging fire across the dark like a thrown brand — but fat and crude, and you feel its bound warmth bleeding out fast under the work; it is a flare-thrower, not a candle. Pushed through the Furnace-heart they come steady and clean and cost you almost nothing — the core feeds them out of its own banked fire, cooling slowly as it pays, and you understand you could light this dark for a long, long time this way. Three tools. You file each one where you can reach it.

You are still working when the dark changes its mind about you.

All this while the thing up in the shaft has been circling, idle, a dry shift of weight at the edge of hearing. Now it stops circling. Now it comes. Whatever it is, it has felt your little suns and your warmth blooming over and over at the threshold, and it is done watching — the sound of it firms into purpose and begins, deliberately, lower and nearer, to close on the warm thing standing in its doorway. You snap a bolt toward the sound, and for one white instant the light catches it: a long pale low shape, close to the stone, eyes throwing your glare back flat and bright — and it FLINCHES, recoiling from the brightness before it pours itself back into the black and keeps coming. The light hurts it. You know that now, bought with a single glimpse.

It is almost on you. You crouch at the lip you chose to hold — the re-bound mace low in one fist and its charge near spent, the dimming core in the other, two-thirds of your focus still in you — with a hunter you cannot quite see closing the last of the dark between you, and the entrance you meant to defend at your back. You wanted to meet it here. Here it comes.

It comes. In the last of the dark it gathers and lunges for the warm thing in the doorway — and you give it exactly what you have been holding back. You do not meet it in the open; you have stepped back into the throat of the entrance, where the stone closes in around you and there is nowhere for it to slide aside, nowhere to flank. You wait the half-breath until it commits its whole long body into the gap — and then you open the Furnace-heart's last fire through your own and throw it all forward at once: one broad wash of Ember poured into the doorway with nowhere to go but through it.

Let's see how the night likes meeting the sun.

The night does not like it at all. There is no dark here for it to be — the fire fills the choke wall to wall and the creature is caught full in the open flame with no slot to pour itself into, and the light does to it what light does to a thing built for the dark. It comes apart. The wave takes it head-on and flings the ruin of it back into the shaft, the pale shape blackening and breaking in the glare, and the long body that meant to kill you out of the dark falls away from your doorway dead before a single claw ever reaches you.

Then the fire gutters down and the dark slides back in — quieter, now, than it was. Whatever you feared up here is gone; you feel it in the air, the hunted prickle lifting off the back of your neck. By the last dim coal in your fist you can make out the wreck of it slumped against the broken stair: a long lean carcass, a tough pale hide stretched over too many ribs, and where its eyes should be, two clouded lenses that seem to drink the little light you have rather than throw it back. Strange salvage, if you want it.

The core in your other hand has gone dark and cold. You spent its last in the wave — an empty stone now, no glow left to give. You have your low-charged mace, your own fire, and the dark above you still to climb.

And the Tower marks the kill.

You crouch over the wreck of the thing and take what is worth taking. The hide peels away light and tough under your knife, pale as something that has never seen a day; and from the ruin of its face you work loose the two clouded lenses that were its eyes — cold little stones that swallow your coal light instead of catching it, strange enough that you pocket them. Then you get a grip on what is left of the carcass and haul it up onto your shoulder.

Down you go, the way you climbed, back through the dead quiet of the cleared rooms — past the Sentinel's rubble, down to the low warm dark by the water where your brazier stands. You let the carcass down by the stone and straighten, and take stock of the place you have made your own these long turns: the cistern with Cold Revenge soaking in it, the heaped salvage, the iron hammer-head still wanting a haft — and the brazier itself, cold. Dead cold. You burned the last of your fuel the night you forged yourself, and there is nothing left in the basket but old ash. Whatever you mean to do here, one last time, before you leave these rooms for the long climb — the forge is dark, and the fire to do it by is not yet lit.

There is no preparing anything over a dead hearth, so you go and find fire's food. Down the cleared flights to the first floor — the bottom of all this, where you started — and you work it over slow and thorough, turning up what a hundred climbers and your own earlier passes have left: a broken stool gone to splinters, the dry guts of something wooden long collapsed, a drift of brittle debris in the corners that takes a flame. It is not a woodpile, but it is real fuel, more than you hoped to find in a place so picked-over, and you bundle the best of it up and haul it back up to the water and the cold brazier — an armful for the fire, and a little over to keep.

You build it up properly this time: the foraged wood stacked in the basket and the coil of rotted rope laid through it for tinder, and then you call a seed of your own Ember down into the heart of it the way you can now without a flint or a spark to beg from. It catches. The rope crackles and the old wood takes and the brazier wakes into real flame, throwing live orange light across the water and the stone — the first proper fire you have had since the night you forged your flesh.

Into it you set your three fire-things. The Furnace-heart, dark and spent, you bed down in the coals with a seed of essence in it; the little rivet beside it, the same. And this time the mace goes in too — for it is bound again, not the dead iron it was that cold night, and when you give it its seed the head drinks the heat and holds it, its low charge beginning to climb. All three sit in the fire now, drinking, their charges rising as the pyre burns down through your fuel. The forge is alive again, the leftover wood stacked dry at your side, and the work you came down here to do is waiting on whatever you decide it is.

Before the fire does its slow work you do yours. You sit by the pyre and take up the three points the Tower gave you for the kill, and you spend them the way you like to — one at a time, slow, feeling for where each one lands.

The first you press into your PRESENCE, an attribute you have let lie. It answers oddly — not in the flesh like strength but in the air around you: a small added weight to the way you sit here, the sense that were anyone in this dark to look, their eye would catch and hold on you a beat longer than before. You file the lesson away — that this one governs how a person fills a room and leans on the minds in it.

The second you give to LUCK, the lowest thing on your sheet, and this is the strangest of the three, because it is almost nothing you can hold. No filling, no weight — only the faintest sense, gone before you can name it, that the world's million small coin-flips might fall a hair more often your way than they did a moment ago. Fortune's thumb, set the barest touch lighter on the scale. You take it on faith more than feeling.

And the last you set where you are already strongest — your INTELLECT, building the high thing higher. This one you feel cleanly: a widening behind the eyes, more room to hold a thing and turn it, and under it the cool well of your focus going deeper, a little more of you to spend before you run dry. The points are placed. You sit sharper, weightier, and a touch luckier by the fire, your three fire-things still drinking the flame beside you and the work itself still ahead.

You will not be back, and you cannot carry it all, so you stop being careful with what you can spend and start being curious instead. The cold bar first. You lift Cold Revenge dripping from the cistern, set both hands to it, and pull — not into an item this time but into yourself, drawing its cold charge in through your skin the way you drew the rivet's fire that day by the water.

It comes in WRONG. Where the ember came home smooth, a thing your body knew and wanted, this is the opposite of welcome — a hard, biting essence that does not belong in you, that fights up the channel and tries to bleed back out the moment it is in. A lesser grip would have let it shock through you and freeze something it should not. But you have the hands for it now: you hold the cold steady, thread it slow and deliberate through the meat of you, and make it show you exactly what it does.

What it does is nothing that lasts. For as long as you push it the flesh goes numb and stiff and strange, a brittle false hardness — and the instant you stop feeding it, it is gone, draining away like water through a fist, leaving not a grain set behind. Your body will not take the cold. The fire it took because the fire was already yours, the same single note your essence has always rung; the cold is the other pole, a thing you can hold in your hand and put into iron but never into the man. Ember already hardened in you everything essence can harden. There is nothing here the cold can add. You let the last of it bleed off, shake the chill out of your fingers, and set the drained bar down — answered, and none the worse for asking. Cold Revenge is an empty cold-husk now, to refill in the water if you want it; and you know one true thing more about what you are.

The rivet has drunk its fill. You lift it from the coals where it has sat since you built the pyre, and it comes up hot and heavy and FULL, a small iron sun you can close in one fist, its bound ember pressing at the shell of it and wanting out. The pitted hammer-head waits on the stone beside it. You have forged before with open flame driven up under the metal; this time you mean to forge with the charge itself, and see what that changes.

It works at once. You set the rivet to the iron and open its binding a careful crack — not pulling the ember into yourself, not throwing it, only letting it RUN OUT into the head as working heat — and a deep even orange blooms up from the contact and races through the mass faster than any brazier ever drove it. A thing the size of a knuckle is running your forge. So: it can be done.

And it is easy on you, which is the surprise. The rivet pays for the fire; your own well barely stirs, where keeping an open channel lit always cost you to hold. All you spend is the fine will to meter the charge, to keep it from dumping itself all at once. But you feel the other edge of it too — the rivet is EMPTYING under your hand, a purse with the coins leaving it, and when it is spent the heat is simply gone. Open fire is endless while the fuel lasts. This is cheap and it is bounded, both at once.

The real thing you did not expect is what the ember does while you hammer. It does not only heat the iron and pass through. Every blow that shapes the head drives a little of that ember DOWN into it and SETS it there — the same way your own essence set into Burning Anger long ago, only here it is the smithing itself doing the binding. The head is not just getting hot. It is getting BOUND, a faint new ember-quality waking in the worked metal and taking hold. You ease off with your heart going, because you see what you have stumbled into: that the hammer and the binding are not two crafts but one, that a tool could be made ember from the inside out. The head cools half-shaped in your grip, faintly alive now with a fire that is partly its own — still a bare head, still wanting a haft, no longer only iron. The rivet in your other hand is an emptied husk, its whole charge poured into the work.

You have one thing left to spend and a use for it, so you spend it. Kneeling at the pyre with the last of your focus cool and low in you, you set to binding — pushing your own ember out of yourself and down into iron, the way you have learned to, only now you do three at once and do not stop to rest between them.

The hammer-head first, to finish what you started. The nascent ember you woke in it before is still there, half-set; you take it the rest of the way, drive the fire down into the grain and seat it, and feel the head go from iron-that-holds-some-warmth to a thing that is ember in its bones — done, bound, only wanting a haft. The brazier-stand next, the old iron basket that has held your every fire: it takes the bind easy and grateful, same as your own pole always does, and you feel it learn to keep a coal of its own and wake its own flame.

Then the cold bar — the one that was Cold Revenge, that you bit with the swarm's cold and have carried ever since. This one fights you. The old cold-bind is still in it, the other pole, and when your ember goes in the two grind against each other like wrong-cut gears. But the cold was only ever a crude grip, a thing that bled and needed water to hold at all, and your fire is HOME in iron — so you simply pour harder, and the ember does not negotiate with the cold so much as BURN IT OUT, scouring the old binding loose and taking its place clean. The bar goes from a thing that bites cold to a thing that holds your fire, and you understand a new edge of what you are: that you can walk into a crude opposite bind and evict it, make it yours. The cold is gone out of it. It is an ember rod now, like everything else in your hands.

That is the last of your focus, and you let it be. You heap what fuel you have left onto the brazier — the whole remainder, holding nothing back — and you set every ember thing you own into it: the mace, the rivet, the new-bound head and rod, the brazier-stand alight under all of it, and the Furnace-heart down in the heart of the coals. Then you sit, and you go still, and you meditate while the fire works.

Time runs out long and quiet by the water. The pyre eats its fuel and the metal drinks the fire, and one by one you feel them come up to full — a low chorus of charged ember all around you, every battery topped, the mace heavy with heat again, the Furnace-heart a small banked sun. And you fill too: the cool well of your focus rising back to its brim, the ache going out of your limbs, the last of the cold-burn from before closed over and gone. When you open your eyes the fuel is spent to ash and the fire is sinking, but you are whole and brimming and surrounded by charged iron, gathered and ready in the warm dark by the cistern — and the long black shaft of the next floor is still up there, waiting, whenever you choose to rise.

You are rested, and every piece of iron you own is brimming with fire, and you have a shape in your head you have been carrying since the forge-floor taught you what your hands could do. So you begin, and you do not stop until it is finished.

Burning Anger first — you take it apart. The mace that has killed for you since the cistern comes undone under your own ember and the hammer-head's blows: the dense steel head off, the long iron haft free in your hands. Then the haft and the ember rod that was once Cold Revenge — anger and revenge both, the two weapons you named in the dark down here — you lay them together and you weld them into one. This is where you find the white you could never reach alone: with all your charged metal feeding the heat at once, the seam goes past orange, past yellow, up into a white so bright it throws your shadow huge on the cistern wall, and the two iron lengths run together into a single shaft as if they had never been apart. The batteries pay for the fire your own affinity could not.

Then the head. You set the Furnace-heart at the center of it — the dead guardian's core, your deepest vessel — and you build the weapon's striking mass out around it, breaking down the rivet, the old brazier-stand that has held your every fire in this place, and Burning Anger's steel head, layering each one onto the core white-hot and beating it home, ring after ring, until the Furnace-heart beats at the heart of a maul like a banked sun in an iron fist. That head you weld to one end of the shaft. The forge-bound hammer-head you weld to the other. And all the while you spend yourself the way you meant to — pacing the fire out of your own core drop by drop, never dumping, never running dry, the well sinking lower and lower as the work climbs toward done, so that the last weld cools under your hands at the very moment the last of your focus goes out of you. Spent, exactly. Not a drop wasted, not a drop short.

You set the butt of it to the stone and stand back, and for a while you just look.

It is the finest thing you have ever made. Two-handed, double-ended, heavier than anything you have carried — a maul head with a sun in its heart on one end, a hammer poll on the other, the whole long shaft saturated with ember through every weld so that it seems to hold its own dim glow even quenched and cooling. Every battery you owned is in it now; every named iron you carried down here is in it now; the brazier that warmed you and the core you tore out of a guardian and the bar you bit the swarm with, all of it gathered into one weight balanced so true it feels lighter in the hands than it has any right to. It has no name yet. You will know the name when it has done something to earn one.

Then you sit, because the work is done and your well is dry, and you meditate one last time by the sinking warmth. The focus rises back in you slow and full, the ache leaves your arms, and the new weapon rests across your knees drinking the last of the ember it can hold. When you open your eyes you are whole and full and armed as you have never been — and there is nothing left down here to make, nothing left to mend, only the long black shaft of the floor above and whatever waits at the top of it, for whenever you decide to climb.

You travel light, because the Tower has taught you that what you carry should earn the weight of it. The two clouded eye-lenses and the cold river-stone go into your pockets — small strange things, kept for what they might yet have to teach you. The stalker's pale hide you shake out and tie loose around your neck, a rough cloak against whatever waits in the dark above; it is not fitted and it is not fine, but it turns a blow better than bare skin, and the dead hunter has no more use for it.

Then you take up the maul, and you stand a moment with the weight of it settling into your hands, deciding what to call this thing you have made. But the truth is you already know. It is a different weapon than the one you named at the bottom of the cistern — heavier, doubled, a guardian's heart beating fire where a plain steel head used to sit — and yet that very steel is still in it, folded into the new head, the metal that crushed the drowned thing carried up through every shape this iron has taken: bar, mace, conduit, and now this. The name has come up through all of it unchanged, because the thing at the core of it never changed — only grew. So you keep it. Burning Anger. Still. Always. You shoulder the maul, and it sits there glowing low and patient against your back like it has known its name all along.

You go back to the throat of the shaft and this time you do not stop at the lip. At the first landing you find what your scouting promised: a bracket-lantern and a flask of old oil, sealed. You fill the lantern and you do not bother with flint — you set a fingertip of your own ember to the wick and it catches at once, and for the first time the lower shaft has light that is not the far grey seam. The dark pulls back off the broken flights and the rising slabs, and the whole murderous geometry of the place lays itself out for you to read.

So you climb. The flights are bolted to the wall, solid where they hold and simply gone where they don't, and you take the gaps the way your legs have always wanted to take everything — a long step here, a driving leap there, your lower body doing what it is built for while the maul rides glowing on your back. Where the stairs give out the slabs begin, rising and sinking on the slow patient beat you watched from the lip days ago, and now that you can SEE it and you already know its rhythm in your bones, the timing is almost easy: you wait, you read the lift, you go — slab to slab, up through the moving dark, the long fall breathing cold under every jump and never once getting you. You are, it turns out, very good at this. You climb until the wall-stair is far below and a narrow stone ledge snags out of the dark ahead, and you swing onto it to breathe.

You are not the first to rest here. A man is on the ledge with you — or was; he has been dead a long time, dried and still, his back to the cold stone and a burnt-out hooded lantern gone cold in his lap. You take the lantern. It is a better tool than your open flame for what is above — it throws its light one way and keeps the glare out of your own eyes — and you wake it with a touch of ember and hood it down to a directed beam. He has no more use for it, the way the stalker had no more use for its hide.

Scratched into the wall by his shoulder, in a hand that goes ragged and then frantic toward the end, is a message:

  "To whoever climbs after — the stones do not move on their own. The thing at the top moves them. It sits in the chain and it sleeps, and the slabs are only its breathing; come near the grey light and it wakes, and there is nowhere up there to stand that is not inside its reach. I could not get past it. Three days on this ledge now and I am done climbing. If you mean to try — the weight is the whole of it, the chain and the drum it turns on and the catch that holds the—"

and there it stops, unfinished, the last stroke trailing off the stone.

You look up. Far above, past more rungs of rising slab, the grey seam of the exit is brighter now — and below it, where the moving stones seem to dock against something, the dark gathers into a mass too square and too still to be more shaft. You cannot make out what it is from here. But you know now that it is there, and that it is waiting, and that it wakes to the climb. The lantern burns steady in your fist. The slabs go up and down their slow beat. The ledge holds you, for the moment, in the middle of the dark.

You go up out of the dead man's ledge and you do not stop again. The hooded lantern throws its beam ahead of you, and the slabs come and go on the beat you have carried in your body since the lip far below; you ride them, leap to leap, patient and quiet, giving the dark nothing to hear. The wall-stair is long gone — there is only the moving stone and the long fall — and then the moving stone begins to change. The slabs slow as they near the top and dock, one after another, against a shelf of worked stone: a gantry, solid underfoot, the first still ground in the whole shaft. And there, at last, is the grey seam grown into a doorway — the exit-stair, close enough to see the steps.

Something fills the way onto it.

It sits across the only dock to the stair: an immense seated figure of fused stone and iron, taller than three of you, and the great central chain you have climbed alongside runs straight into it and winds around its middle. It does not move. It does not breathe. It is the thing the note warned of — the giant that is the weight. You hold still on the gantry's edge and you bring the lantern's beam onto it, slow, along the chain to where it wraps a great drum at the figure's waist. And there, in a pocket of shadow the open dark would never have let you find, the beam catches a single fat iron pin seated across the drum — a catch, holding the whole wound length of that chain against all its tension. The note's broken last line closes itself in your head. The catch that holds the weight. That is the whole of it. Everything this thing is hangs on that one pin.

You do not give it the chance to wake.

You set your feet on the still stone — the one thing this floor has refused you until now — and you draw the maul back, and the core wakes white in its head as you pull on it and on your own fire at once, everything you carry and everything banked in the weapon gathered into the swing. Then you bring it down on the pin with all of it: the mass of the maul, the wave tearing out through its head, your ember and the core's ember poured together onto that one seated catch.

The pin shears.

For half a breath, nothing. Then the drum lets go. The chain rips off it in a long scream of iron, all the stored tension of the whole counterweight unwinding through the colossus at once — and the figure comes apart from the waist outward, stone and iron flung wide, the freed chain whipping away into the black. The vast mass that was the counterweight drops out from under it and falls, and falls, and never once makes a sound. And below you, down the whole length of the shaft, the slabs sink one final time and stop.

The dark goes still. The beat that has tried to throw you into the deep since you entered is simply gone. You stand on a gantry that does not move, in a silence you have not heard since the arch closed behind you, the grey light of the exit open just past your shoulder. The warden lies in pieces across the stone — and among the wreck, where the chain tore loose, the heavy wound drum at its core has come to rest, and a long arm's-reach of the great chain with it.

You go back up to the headworks one last time, to the thing you broke. It is still now — truly still, in a way it never was while it slept; the great chain hangs slack off the gantry and the counterweight has settled into the dark below and will not rise again. The warden does not move when you climb over it. You take what the Tower owes you for the killing. The winding-drum — the dense iron core the whole murderous mechanism turned on — you work free of its cradle, and it is the heaviest thing you have carried out of any floor, a sullen weight that seems to keep turning a little after your hands go still. And a long length of the chain itself, struck loose at a worn link, heavy and cold and good iron.

Then you drag them up. The last rungs of slab are easier now that nothing moves them, and the grey seam of the exit widens as you climb until it is a mouth of pale light, and you haul yourself and your dead weight of salvage up and through it, and the long cold shaft — the dark, the dead climber on his ledge, the broken warden in its chain — drops away beneath your boots and is closed. Another floor under you. You do not look back down it.
