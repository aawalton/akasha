For a long stretch there was only the wingbeat and the wind, and I thought perhaps a dragon does not know how to answer such a thing. When Tygryth spoke at last, the great voice was low and stripped of all its earlier theater, and he did not look back at me, as though the wish were easier to say to the open air than to a face.

"Do you know, friend, no one has asked me that since the world was younger. They asked what I could *do*. What I could guard, or burn, or bear. Never what I wanted." A slow beat of the wings. "I had begun to believe I had stopped wanting. That they had taken even that. But you ask, and so I find it is still there." The molten eye turned to the silver river far below and followed it. "I had a brood once. A long time ago, before the chain. Three young — and the ones who took me scattered them to the corners of this world so that I would do as I was told and never fly free to find them. That was the true collar, monk. The iron was only its echo." The voice roughened. "My wish is the simplest and the largest there is. I would find my children. I would learn if any of them still live. That is all I have wanted for three hundred years, and until tonight I did not have the freedom to want it aloud."

The wings tilted, and now he did look back, and there was something almost shy in it — an ancient creature asking a favor of a friend instead of commanding a servant. "And here is the strange thing, freedom-lover. That light below, burning where no light should be, in a valley I have never flown — I feel it. The way you feel a name you have forgotten on the tip of your tongue. I cannot tell you why. But of every direction in this wide dark world, that one tugs at me. Do we trust the pull and go down, or do you, with your monk's clear eyes, judge it wiser to wait and watch and learn what burns there first?"

I thought about the lights rising off the towers, and how a hunted creature learns to mistrust exactly the things that call to it most. "Let us follow your heart," I said, "but let us do so wisely. We go down — but we go down quiet, and we look before we are seen." Then, because a half-remembered legend had been turning over in my mind since the shrine, I added: "The old stories say some dragons may take a smaller form at will. Is that within your powers? It would be easier to learn what keeps a fire in an empty valley if we did not arrive as a dragon."

A low rumble of approval moved through him. "You temper a thing without dousing it. I begin to think the chain did me a kindness, in the end, if it left me waiting for a friend like you." There was the ghost of the old amusement, returned. "And yes. The legends, for once, are not wrong. It is an old art, and freedom restores it to me — I have not worn another shape since before the iron, but a thing like that you do not forget. Watch, friend, and keep your seat one moment more."

He descended in a long silent glide and set down at the dark edge of the valley, in the black shadow of a stand of pines, well short of the light. And then I saw the strangest and most beautiful thing yet in that world. The vast dark shape *folded* — not shrinking so much as gathering, moonlight running off the scales like water, wings drawing in like a closing book — until what stepped out of the settling dark beside me was a tall figure in a traveler's dark cloak: a man, or near enough, with skin like dusk and eyes still molten gold under a fall of black hair, moving with a weight that was somehow more than a man's. "Smaller," he said, in a voice now human-pitched and wry, flexing unfamiliar fingers. "Stranger. We will not announce a dragon's arrival after all. Lead on, monk — these legs are yours to outpace, I think, for a while yet."

Together, on foot, we stole toward the light. The valley floor was soft with moss that swallowed our steps; the silver river murmured cover for our movement. As we closed, the light resolved: not a campfire, not a window, but a single lantern hung on a tall iron pole, standing entirely alone in the middle of a wide clearing. No house. No camp. No keeper. Just a lantern burning a steady, impossible blue-white flame in the empty dark, and around its base, half-sunk in the moss, a ring of standing stones, old and leaning and carved with worn marks neither of us could read from there.

Tygryth had gone very still beside me. "That flame," he murmured, and his human voice had gone thin. "Friend — I know that flame. I do not know how, but I do."

The clearing was quiet. Too quiet — no insects, no wind in that one bowl of the valley. And from somewhere near the ring, so faint I might have imagined it, came a sound that did not belong in an empty clearing: a small, single scrape of stone on stone.

I put a finger to my lips for silence. Tygryth dipped his dark head — understood — and sank into the pine-shadow behind me, still as the standing stones themselves. And I went forward alone, low, pouring myself from shadow to shadow across the moss toward that small dry scrape.

The blue-white light grew as I neared the ring, and I learned three things in quick succession.

The scrape was digging. A figure knelt just inside the circle of stones, at the base of the tall leaning monolith nearest the lantern — small, hooded, working a flat shard of stone into the earth with the careful, exhausted rhythm of someone who had been at it a long while. A satchel lay open beside them, spilling rolled vellum, a bundle of chalk, and a rubbing — a sheet pressed and shaded to copy the carvings off the stones. A scholar. A grave-robber. A pilgrim. I could not yet tell which.

The second thing: the carvings, this close, were not unreadable to my eye at all. They were pictures. A great winged shape. Three smaller winged shapes beneath it. And a line of figures with poles and chains, driving the small ones apart in three directions. I was looking at Tygryth's grief, cut into stone, older than the chain that had bound him. Someone had recorded what was done to his brood, here, in this ring.

The third thing froze me mid-step: the blue-white flame did not flicker, and it did not consume the wick, and as I watched, the digging figure paused and reached up without looking and warmed their hands at it — and the flame leaned toward them, like a thing that knew them.

Then the figure went rigid. The digging stopped. And a voice — young, hoarse, trying very hard to sound brave — spoke to the dark in my direction without turning around. "I can hear you breathing. I have a knife and I have wards and I have absolutely nothing left to lose, so you will tell me what you are before you take one more step."

I did not reach for a weapon, because I had none and wanted none. I stayed low, and I spoke openly into the dark, and I let her hear that there was no threat in it. "Hello, stranger. I am a humble seeker of truth and a breaker of chains. I mean you no harm. Would you share your knowledge with me? I would gladly share mine in return."

The words did something to the clearing. The flame flared, just once, a blue-white tongue stretching tall, and the kneeling figure flinched as though the light had reacted on her behalf. Slowly, knife still up, she turned. The hood fell back. A young woman — younger than her hoarse warning had sounded — gaunt with long travel, dark circles under fierce eyes, a smear of stone-dust and old tears on one cheek. But it was not her face that stopped my breath. It was the line of her brow, the set of her jaw, and high on each cheekbone, catching the blue light, a faint scatter of scales. Not enough to be a dragon. Far too much to be only a woman.

"...A breaker of chains," she repeated, and the knife wavered, and her voice cracked straight down the middle of the brave act. "Do you know what you are saying to me, in *this* place? No. No, you can't. No one comes here. No one has come here in years." She gestured, half-wild, at the stones — at the great winged shape and the three small ones driven apart. "My grandmother carved these. Before she died waiting. This is what they did to my line, seeker. They took the great one and they scattered his get to the three corners, and they bound us small so we would never fly home and never find each other." Her hand rose to the scales on her cheek, unconscious, ashamed and defiant at once. "I am the last of one corner. I have been copying every stone in this valley because the marks are a *map*, and I am one girl with a knife and a flame I do not understand, and I have run out of years to do this alone."

The flame leaned toward her again, patient, and her eyes brimmed. "You said you would trade knowledge. Then trade me this first, and do not lie, because the flame will know — who sent you, and how did a humble seeker come to stand in the one clearing in the world that matters to me?"

And behind me, at the tree-line, I felt rather than heard Tygryth go utterly, breathlessly still. He was hearing every word. His grief. His map. Possibly his blood, kneeling in the moss a dozen feet away.

I did not answer her question with my own voice. Some moments are not yours to stand inside. I turned to the dark at the tree-line, and I beckoned Tygryth forward — this meeting was his to bear — and I stepped back, out of the light.

He came forward slowly, the cloaked figure with dusk-dark skin and molten eyes, moving into the blue-white ring with a weight far greater than a man's. And the moment his foot crossed into the circle of stones, the flame in the lantern roared upward, a column of blue-white fire stretching toward the moon, throwing the whole clearing into sudden brilliant light.

The girl scrambled back with a cry, knife forgotten in the moss. "What —"

But Tygryth had stopped dead, staring at the lantern, his human composure coming apart at the seams. "That flame," he breathed. "I know it now. I have always known it. It is hearth-fire. It is kin-fire — a dragon kindles it for her young so they may always find their way home, and it cannot be lit by any hand but the blood of the one who made it." His molten gaze dropped, slowly, from the roaring flame to the girl crouched beneath it — to the scales on her cheek, blazing now in the blue light. "She did not light it knowing what it was. She lit it because it is *hers*. Because it is *mine*."

He sank to his knees in the moss — an ancient creature, kneeling, to be level with a frightened girl. When he spoke again his voice was barely a voice at all. "Child. Look at me. Three hundred years ago they took me in iron and scattered my three young to the corners of the world so I would never fly home to find them. I have just this night been set free by the friend standing in the dark behind you. I came to this valley because something *pulled*, and I did not let myself believe what it was." A tear ran molten-bright down a face that was only wearing the shape of a man. "Your grandmother. Tell me her name. Tell me her name, and I will tell you if she was my daughter — and if she was, then you, child, are the first of my blood I have laid eyes on in three centuries, and you have not been alone in this for one single moment that I would not have crossed the world to end."

The kin-fire blazed between them. The map-stones stood witness all around. The girl's knife lay in the moss, and her hand had risen to her mouth, and her fierce exhausted eyes were streaming, and she was staring at the kneeling dragon as though the ground had opened under everything she thought was true.

She opened her mouth to speak her grandmother's name.

And I stood back in the dark, my torn hands at my sides, having given them this with my own open hands — and I understood that the chain I had turned a key on, a world away in a ruined shrine, had reached further than I could have known. I broke a chain that night. I am still learning how far the freeing of it ran.
