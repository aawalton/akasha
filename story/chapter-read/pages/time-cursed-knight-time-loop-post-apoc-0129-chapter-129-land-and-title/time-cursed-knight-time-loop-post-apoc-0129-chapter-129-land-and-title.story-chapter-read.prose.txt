Varen's fingers stopped on the edge of the desk.

"Land and a title," he repeated. "That is an expensive request, Kaspar. Very expensive."

Falke blinked. Brauer's head turned slowly toward me while the man by the map wall stopped flipping.

"Explain," Varen said.

"Voralberg Magitek has outgrown its current facility. The old textile mill cannot support the production capacity we need. The rail-pistol program, the cooling systems, the cannon development, the research wing. All of it requires space that doesn't exist inside the capital." I kept my voice steady. "I need territory. Land. Enough to build a manufacturing complex, testing grounds, worker housing, and the infrastructure to support them."

"You want me to give you territory," Varen said.

"I want you to grant me the right to purchase Crown-revertible or unclaimed land in the northern provinces. At fair market valuation. Through the standard legal channels." I paused. "Which requires a title."

Varen leaned back slightly.

"Why Kaltbach?" he asked. "There are easier locations. Land farther from the border, farther from a live revolt, farther from the jurisdictional friction of every noble house that will suddenly discover an ancestral claim the moment it becomes valuable."

"Because Kaltbach already has the bones of a city," I said. "It already has workshops, grain sheds, and trade routes, plus a population big enough to start building before we pour foundations."

Falke's expression shifted. He understood before I finished.

"The capital has people," I continued. "But it also has noble claims, guild restrictions, Academy oversight, labor brokers, and every court faction watching every new building. Kaltbach has workers who need wages and a local economy the Crown has ignored and will accept anyone who brings coin, food, and protection."

I looked at the map on the wall.

"The mutant population is labor the capital can't buy cheaply. They can raise walls and haul steel without the machinery or spellcraft we'd need here."

Varen's eyes narrowed slightly.

"You're planning to turn an abandoned border town into an industrial city."

"Yes." Varen considered that. "Interesting," he said. The room went still.

"What kind of noble title?" Falke asked.

"A barony, or whatever title is enough to hold the land."

Brauer made a sound deep in his throat.

Varen's expression didn't change.

"You are already a Hexenzeit," the prince said.

"I am a Hexenzeit who was disowned. I carry the name. I don't carry the rights. I cannot purchase titled land. I cannot hold territory. I cannot employ Crown-chartered labor on my own authority." I let that settle. "I need a title of my own, independent of Roderich."

Varen looked interested, but his pen stayed on the blotter.

Falke leaned forward in his chair.

"Politically, that is expensive," he said. "More expensive than the land itself. A new title requires Crown authority and every neighboring house will have the right to object. The moment you attach a title to Kaspar's name, House Hexenzeit becomes involved whether Duke Roderich wants it or not."

"House Winterkrone will ask questions," Brauer added. "So will the border lords."

Varen's eyes never left mine.

"A title for a disowned Hexenzeit," he said. "A factory city on the Wolkenmark border. A weapons program under an Iron Cross contractor. That is the kind of decision people remember when they decide whether to support me or oppose me."

"Yes," I said.

"Perhaps too expensive."

"Think about what I'm offering," I said. "A weapons production center outside the capital. Away from prying eyes and court politics, clear of the noble quarter's gossip networks. A facility where the Iron Cross can develop, test, and manufacture without every house in the Empire knowing what comes off the assembly line before the paint dries."

Varen's fingers tapped the desk once.

"And the revolt?"

"The revolt is the entry point. I go north, I resolve Kaltbach, the Crown gets a clean victory in a region that has been neglected for decades. The mutant communities see an Imperial response that isn't a garrison boot on their necks. And in the process, I get a suitable location for the facility."

"I need speed," I added. "If House Hexenzeit resolves this before I arrive, then the crisis is gone. There is no leverage left. No reason for the Crown to compensate me for purchasing land that Roderich's people have already stabilized and claimed credit for."

Varen stood.

He walked to the map wall. His finger found the northern border. Hexenzeit territory. The mountain passes. The thin line of settlements that straggled along the frontier toward Wolkenmark.

"Kaltbach," he said, touching a point near the border.

"Approximately thirty kilometers south of the Wolkenmark frontier. Four days' ride from Hexenzeit."

"Just outside your father's jurisdiction."

"Roderich hasn't invested in the northern settlements since before I was born. The frontier towns govern themselves."

Varen's hand dropped.

He turned.

"If I grant this," he said, "you understand what it means."

"I do."

"You will be a titled lord. With land. With the legal authority to raise structures, employ workers, and operate independently within your territory." His voice carried the particular edge of a man listing the teeth on a trap. "You will also be a target. Every house that wants what you're building will come for you, and every Iron Cross enemy will treat your factory as leverage. Your own family will call the elevation an insult."

"I'm aware."

"Your father won't approve."

"My father hasn't approved of anything I've done since I was eight years old."

The words came out harder than I intended.

Varen watched me.

Then he looked at Brauer.

The major's heavy face was unreadable.

"It's sound," Brauer said. "Militarily. The northern provinces need attention. Kaltbach needs intervention before the situation spreads. And a production facility outside the capital has operational advantages I don't need to explain."

He paused.

"Politically, it's a grenade."

"Everything worth doing is," Varen said.

He returned to the desk but did not sit.

"Still," Varen said, "this is too expensive for a pacified tax revolt. Too expensive for a cleaner headline in a forgotten frontier town. It could be an investment in you, Kaspar. But investments require returns."

His gaze weighed me again.

"If I am going to spend political capital creating a new lord, granting him northern land, and placing an industrial city under his control, I need more than Kaltbach. I need something that changes the Empire."

For a moment, I said nothing.

The maps on the walls pressed in around me.

The north: Kaltbach and Wolkenmark.

Then my eyes moved south.

The active front. The long scar where the Empire and the elves had spent generations killing each other for trenches, ruined towns, and strips of forest nobody could truly hold.

An idea formed. Reckless. I said it anyway.

"I can give you the first victory against the elves since the southern front line was established," I said.

Nobody moved. Falke stopped fidgeting with his cuffs while Brauer's eyes narrowed on me.

Varen looked at me as though he had misheard.

"Explain," he said.

"A real victory," I said. "An offensive operation. We hit the elven positions, break their line, take ground, and hold it long enough to redraw the front line."

Varen stared at me.

"You are offering me elven territory. Elven heads. A public Imperial advance on the southern front."

"Yes to all of it."

The room was silent.

Even Varen looked surprised.

"Is that possible?" he asked. "You have spent months focused on Qalathar. On a weapon program that still has not produced the cannon you promised Prince Khalid."

"It is possible," I said. "It will not be a conventional campaign. No Magical Army formations. No Ministry parade. No noble officers arguing over maps for six weeks."

I stepped closer to the desk.

"It will be an Iron Cross operation. We identify a weak section of the elven line, strike hard, break through with railguns, destroy their forward command and supply structures, and force them to retreat before they understand the scale of the attack."

"Thirty days," I said. "Thirty days for the operation. Then a new front line is drawn farther south."

Falke exhaled slowly.

"That is ambitious," he said.

"That's history, if you pull it off," Brauer said.

Varen's gaze stayed fixed on mine.

"And Qalathar?" he asked. "The Titans do not stop because you have decided to fight elves."

"No," I said. "But Qalathar needs weapons, engineers, and production more than it needs me standing on its wall for another month. The research continues while I am gone."

I paused.

"Then I return east. With more than promises."

Varen looked at the southern map.

He returned to the desk, sat, and pulled a sheet of paper from a drawer before reaching for his pen.

"The title will require the Crown's seal, which means it goes through the Chancellor's office, which means it will take weeks." He looked up. "And the territorial grant will take longer than that. Surveyors, objections, legal reviews, border claims. But if you give me Kaltbach and the southern victory you just promised me, nobody will be able to argue that you have not earned it."

"If you pacify Kaltbach, I can delay the petitions, objections, and competing claims long enough for you to begin construction before the rest of the court understands what you are building," Varen said. "I can make the machinery move slowly for everyone except you."

His pen hovered over the paper.

"But that protection has a cost. You will move south immediately after Kaltbach. You will secure the victory as quickly as possible. A title and territory granted after a border revolt will invite questions. A title and territory granted to the man who pacified the north and broke the elven line will not."

He met my eyes.

"Give me both victories before anyone can organize against you, Kaspar. Then nobody will question what you received."

He wrote.

The pen moved in quick, decisive strokes. When he finished, he blotted the ink and extended the paper across the desk.

I took it.

"Kaspar." I stopped. "Don't fail." He let that sit. "And Kaspar—"

"Yes?"

"Tax protests don't turn into massacres by accident. Take a good look into it."

I folded the mission order and placed it inside my coat.

"I'll take a good look."

"I know you will." Varen's mouth curved. "That's why I'm giving you a city."

I turned toward the door.

Falke caught my eye as I passed.

"Lord Kaspar," he said. The title was premature, and we both knew it.

Brauer just grunted.

I walked out.

Augustin was waiting at the palace gate.

"Well?" he said.

I held up the mission order.

Augustin read it. His lips moved silently.

Then he looked up.

"He signed it."

I nodded once.

"The title—"

"Provisional. Weeks to formalize. But the mission is live." I took the prospectus from his hand and tucked it into my satchel.

Augustin stared at me.

"I secured six magi-cars," he said. "Food, ammunition, fuel crystals, and cold-weather gear. Everything on your lists is loaded and waiting at the eastern service yard."

He glanced down at the mission order again.

"And I went to Iron Cross headquarters. I called for every Knight whose name you marked in the deployment roster. Every one of them accepted."

"Excellent."

Augustin closed his eyes. He pressed two fingers against the bridge of his nose.

"When do we leave?" he asked.

"You don't."

He opened his eyes.

"You stay here," I said. "The factory keeps running. The production schedule doesn't slip."

I looked north.

"You will have time to inspect Kaltbach once it is pacified," I said.

I looked back at him.

"For now, assume it is hostile territory."

Augustin studied me for a moment.

Then he nodded.

"Then everything is prepared for you to leave immediately," he said. "You need to move now if you want to reach Kaltbach before the Hexenzeit does."
