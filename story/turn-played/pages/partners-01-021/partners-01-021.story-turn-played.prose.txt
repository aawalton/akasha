"Steward — that's perfect. Consider it done. I leave myself in your capable hands." And then, because the morning still has room in it before the Compact fills it: "Now that that's settled — would you like to explore the house with me while we wait? Together?"

"Then it's done — and I'll hold you to 'perfect' the first time I reorganise something you were fond of." The warmth settles into place like a key turning. "Steward of Hearthholt. There: say a thing plainly and it's real. I've always liked that about a good arrangement." And on the other sentence, honestly: "'I leave myself in your capable hands' — I'll not treat that lightly. It's the nicest sentence anyone's said to me in years, and it's also a considerable length of rope. I'll use it well." She's already rising. "Yes — explore it with you, gladly. And here's the daft truth: I've kept this house for years and never once walked it lit. I know its taxes and its gutters and its roof-slates intimately, and haven't the faintest idea what's down its own corridors. We'll be discovering it together, the pair of us — which is rather fitting."

So you set out — and the house notices.

On the first landing, shutters swing free at a touch. "...You feel that? The shutters on this landing have been swollen shut as long as I've paid to have them seen to — three joiners told me they'd want replacing. And they've just swung open like they were oiled this morning." A wry, warm glance. "The house is relaxing, Alan. It held itself stiff and dark three generations, and now it's got you in it and it's loosening. Watch the lamps — they warm just ahead of us, like it's showing us through. In any other house I'd find that unnerving. In this one it only reads as glad."

And then, partway down the east corridor, a door neither of you has opened swings itself wide as you approach.

Inside: an estate office. Ledgers generations deep on the shelves — the house's whole memory, kept dry and sorted. A broad desk. Morning light through a tall window. Air that smells of having been aired every week for a hundred years, in a wing no one has entered for three generations.

She stops on the threshold, and for a moment the brisk steward has nothing brisk to say.

"...Oh. Alan. Look at it." She steps in slowly. "This is an estate office. Generations of ledgers — the house's whole memory — in a room that opened itself the very moment I'd a reason to need one. A desk. Morning light." She turns to you, plainly moved. "You made me your steward not an hour ago, and the house has just... built me somewhere to do it. It made me a room to sleep in the night we met, and now a room to work in the hour I was appointed. That's three times it's decided something ahead of us." Honest, with something unresolved humming underneath it: "I don't know what this house is, or how it knows what we'll need before we do. I've given up pretending I've an account for it. But I know this room is a welcome, I know exactly who it's addressed to, and I'll not be too proud to say it lands somewhere."

The way back down passes the cellar stair — and there, low in the wall, an iron-banded door. She tries it once, pure keeper's habit. It doesn't give. No warm lamp rises. The house, so talkative all morning, has gone quiet about this one.

"...Now that's interesting. Everything in this house has opened for us like it couldn't wait to — and this one stays shut, and the house has gone quiet about it. Just locked." A keeper's frown — filing, not forcing. "I don't like a door I can't open in a house I'm meant to keep; I'll want to know what's behind it eventually. But not by breaking it." A light glance at the ring on your belt. "You've keys on that ring we've found no locks for yet. Perhaps one of them's shy. We'll leave it be for now." And she's warm again, turning up the stair. "Come — I want to see the rest before your noon company arrives, and I've a steward's list to start: what wants mending, what wants stocking, what wants only a window opened. The house is showing us round. Let's let it."

The morning goes easy after that, room by room, the lamps warming just ahead of you the whole way — and somewhere below, the road up from Amberford waits for noon.
