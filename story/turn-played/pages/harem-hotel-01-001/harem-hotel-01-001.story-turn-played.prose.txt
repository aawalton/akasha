You come up out of nothing the way a diver breaks the surface — no shore behind it, no swim you remember taking. One moment there is no you at all. Then there is: flat on your back on a floor that is cool and close-carpeted, a ceiling above you doing a competent impression of a ceiling.

You take inventory the only way you trust it, which is from the outside in. Your breath is going fast — you clock that before you clock being afraid, and read the fear off the breath rather than the other way round. Your hands are pressed flat to the carpet, holding on, as though the floor were a thing that might quit. Heartbeat loud under your own ribs. Nothing hurts. For one blank second you don't have your own name, and then you do — *Alan* — and it arrives clean and sure and fastened to absolutely nothing else. No door you came through. No before.

Then the letters.

They assemble in the air a foot off your face, pale and frameless, a pane the frost-color of a cold window, text writing itself across it in a hand that is not a hand. It does not ask. Something passes over you — through you — a cool flat attention laid down your whole length like a tailor's tape, and the pane fills as it reads.

It gives you back to yourself in a column of quiet facts, and you find you can read them untaught — where you are strong, where you are thin. Low on the pane, one line where a value should sit stays blank. Not zero, not hidden behind anything — the line simply has nothing in it. Set apart from the rest, two facts that are not about you: the name of the place — the Harem Hotel — and its floor, and the floor is the first.

Then the attention lifts off you. The pane holds another moment, indifferent as a receipt, and goes still.

You sit up into a hallway.

It runs long both ways, and it is expensively long — the corridor of a place that spent money to make you feel you had arrived somewhere. Close one way, not far, the hall ends at a wide door standing a hand's-width open on a room plainly meant for you: a bed built for more than one, low warm light, a made and waiting place. The other way the hall runs on, and narrows, and darkens toward a single shut door at the very end — and you understand without deciding to which end is the soft one and which is not.

You are still testing whether your legs intend to cooperate when the soft end of the hall does a thing walls do not do. The wall beside the open door seams without a sound and *grows* — a second doorway pressing itself out of flat plaster, molding and lintel and all, as if the corridor had recalled it wanted one more room here and simply put one in.

A woman comes out of it.

She stops in the doorway itself — one hand laid flat to the molding the wall has only just finished making, testing that it holds — and looks at you a while before she says anything, as though the looking were the part worth doing slowly.

You take her the way you took the pane — fast, off the edges first. Tall. Dressed for somewhere warmer than a basement: deep sapphire silk off the one shoulder, a thread of silver at her wrist. Silver-white hair. A scatter of pale scales high on each cheekbone catching the low light, and two dark horns swept back off her brow that your mind reaches to file under *costume* and can't. Her eyes are a pale silver-grey, round-pupiled, and already on you — have been, you would guess, a beat longer than yours have been on her.

"There you are," she says. She takes in the whole picture of you — landed on the floor of your own hallway, read but not yet risen — and her smile does not widen so much as settle in, unhurried, like someone who already knows how the evening goes and is glad of it anyway. "The first thing you do is lie there and read yourself before you will trust your own feet." A held beat. "That is a better opening than most manage on purpose."
