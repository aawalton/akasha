"Don't stop," you tell her — the words forced out against the plaster crushing your chest. Pinned as you are, one arm still caught in its grip, you drive down with everything left to you: your shoulder, your free hand, all your slight weight bearing the cracked mass low, holding its blank attention on you and off the woman at its flank. And it stays down. Above you Aria does not stop — her hand works the split in a sustained tearing, claws in the crack to the wrist, widening it past anything the plaster can draw shut, until the wound opens all the way to its center, to the hard pale core it was all poured around: the keystone, bared at last.

And there is something in it — where the crack runs deepest, half-swallowed in the core, a hard edge catches the light in a way plaster never would: worked metal, a haft, a blade seated in the keystone as though the hall had set it there and grown the guard up around it. The free hand you are bearing down with is a span away. You do not think; you close your fingers on the haft and work it loose — a knife, slim and plain and cold, no longer than your hand.

You know what the knife is for, as surely as you have known nothing else in this place. It is the one thing left you can do, and you do it — you turn it in your fist and drive it back into the keystone it came out of, all your weight behind the one true placement — not power, you have none, but the blade finds the fault and goes home. And the Doorward stops. The grinding, the shudder, the vast grip on your arm — gone, cut clean, the straining mass gone still around you. You lie in the sudden quiet inside dead plaster, the knife buried to its hilt, and it does not move again.

Then the plaster begins to move — not toward you but away, the white mass loosening and drawing back into the wall it grew from, until there is nothing on the floor but you and the knife and Aria. And the floor itself takes a breath — a long slow lift through the soles and the spine, the light along the walls sliding a shade warmer as the whole floor climbs. When it settles the cold chamber is gone, folded away behind you, and the hall runs on ahead — longer, and darker at its far end, where a new shut door has grown where the wall had run unbroken before.

And Aria is a half-step changed. Nothing you could point to, nothing so plain as taller — but she straightens up out of the wreck of the fight more easily than she should, standing into the space a shade more solidly than she had this morning, steadier on the follow-through. As if, in climbing, you had loosed something in her that had been held tight, and the same step that lifted you had lifted some part of her too. You see it take — there, and marked, and set aside. "You went into a wall with your bare hands," she says, turning the little blade a slow look, "and came out holding the one edged thing it was hiding. I could not have written you a better opening." She puts out a hand and pulls you up onto the floor that is not the one you started on.
