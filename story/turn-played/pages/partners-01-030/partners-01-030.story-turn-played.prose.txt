You follow her down to the kitchen and step in the way you decided to on the stairs: not to run it with her — to help her run it. Her kitchen, her dinner, her vision. You've come to be useful under it.

She looks up as you come in, and reads it off you instantly.

"Well. Look who's reporting for duty in his own kitchen." Warm, seeing it clearly. "And you've come in under my flag, haven't you — not to run it with me, to help me run it. You've no idea how rare that is in a man who owns the room; most would 'help' by rearranging my whole plan and calling it partnership." She's already handing you the first job, delighted. "So — you're mine for the afternoon, and I'll use you well. Apron's on that hook. You're on the rough work and the reach: I want the big copper pot down from the high shelf, the range built to a good steady heat, and every root in that miraculous larder scrubbed and on the board. And here's the vision, so you serve it and not just follow it — tonight's the first meal this house has laid for a guest in three generations, and I mean it to say one thing the moment she sits: you are welcome, you are safe, and there is enough. Warm, generous, nothing fussy. A table like arms open. Everything we cook, we cook toward that. Yes?"

Yes. You take the apron. You get the pot down, build the fire, scrub the roots — and the two of you fall into a rhythm, easy talk over busy hands.

"—Good, you've a knife hand; keep those cuts even and they cook even, that's the whole secret, there isn't a second one. Now the shape of it: our warden brings garden greens, which is a gift and a test both — she'll know at once whether we did right by them, so we do them simply and let them be the best thing on the table. That's manners: cooking someone's own gift back to them with respect. Around them, something roasted and honest from the larder, bread I'll not apologise for, and something sweet at the end — because a table with no sweet in it is a table that doesn't quite mean the welcome." A warm aside, close in the work: "You're good at this, you know. Steady. You don't crowd the person beside you — you just fit in alongside. I keep learning things about you in the hands you don't remember." A light bump of your shoulder, unhurried, new-partnered. "Mind that bread. Don't let me talk you into burning it."

A pot comes to a simmer. She wipes her hands and pulls a ledger over — the promised dull thing, done lightly, in the gaps.

"—Give that a stir now and then while I do a dull thing I promised. The factor's assessment... there. A real number, but a survivable one: the estate carries it on this year's terms without you selling so much as a candlestick, and once that deed's papered tomorrow the dormancy years can't be clawed back. I'll have it clean in the office ledger by week's end. Costing done — see, I keep my promises before supper." She sets it aside, and there's a quieter beat over the work. "Can I tell you the daft thing I keep noticing? Every single thing we've done today has been a first for this house — first steward, first meeting, first deed, first guest — and I keep being the one standing next to you for each. I told you what first would mean to me: foundation, cornerstone, all of it, and I meant every word. But there's a smaller, sillier truth under the grand one — I just like being the one next to you when the firsts happen. That's not strategy. That's only glad."

The afternoon works itself golden around the two of you — bread out and knocking hollow, the roast turned, the greens washed and waiting for the moment they're needed and not a minute before. At last she steps back and surveys it all.

"—There. Look at it. Table's laid, the greens ready the moment she's through the door, bread warm, and the light just starting to go." A satisfied steward's-eye pass over the room, and then a warmer look at you. "The house feels ready, doesn't it. Not stiff-ready — glad-ready. Same as it's been all day." She straightens a place setting that didn't need it, and turns to you, warm and certain. "Go and light the lamp in the gate when the sun's down, would you — that's the signal; that's us saying the door's open. Then come stand with me, and we'll meet whoever the house sends up the hill together. First dinner of the family, Alan. Let's open the door."

Through the tall windows the sun is just touching the hills, and down at the gate the lamp waits for your hand.
