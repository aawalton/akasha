I had stepped out of the light on purpose, and so the moment, when it came, was theirs.

The blue-white flame roared between them, and the girl's lips parted, and the name came out cracked and small and shaking, the most precious thing she owned. "Sael. Her name was Saelith. She told me her mother was a great silver-grey wyrm who sang the kin-fire alight — that she was stolen from the nest before her scales had hardened, driven west and bound small. She carved these stones from memory, so that one of us, someday, might read the way home."

The effect on Tygryth was total. The kneeling dragon made a sound I have never heard a living thing make — half a sob, half a note of the kin-song itself — and the flame bowed toward him. "Saelith. Sael. My smallest. The one who would not sleep unless I sang. They drove her west." His molten eyes lifted to the girl, streaming. "She was my daughter. Which makes you, child, my own blood — the first of my line I have beheld in three hundred years. You are not the last of a broken corner. You are the first thread of a family I am going to gather back together if it takes me another three hundred years."

He opened his arms, and the girl — who had been brave and alone in that clearing longer than anyone should be asked to bear — broke and fell into them, and the kin-fire folded its blue light down over them both like a wing. I watched a chain I had broken a valley away reach all the way here and bind something back together instead, and I thought that if I did nothing else worthwhile in this strange world, that would have been enough.

The world rarely grants more than one kindness at a time. The kin-fire guttered low, its roar dropping to a flinching ember, and every standing stone in the ring went cold and dark at once. The girl pulled back, animal-wary under the tears. "The fire only does that when something that should not be here comes into the valley."

From the dark rim of the clearing a voice carried across the moss — smooth, unhurried, far too pleased with itself. "Three hundred years, and the old worm slips his collar at last. And lights a beacon, and gathers his strays, and does our finding for us. How thoughtful." Tall robed figures stepped out of the tree-line, moving without sound, faces lost in shadow.

Tygryth rose, putting his body between the threat and the girl. "Friend. These are the chain-makers. The ones who scattered my blood. I will not run from them a second time — but I will not spend her life, or yours, on my pride. Do we stand here, in the ring, or do we take her and the map and vanish into the dark?"

I had already counted them, and counted the time. "There cannot be many," I said. "They have not had time to circle the storm-wall we passed through, so these must already have been near — a forward few, not an army. I will guard your kin, that you may fight freely." I set myself between the girl and the dark, and I let the old creature off the leash of his own caution. "Show them what it means to anger a dragon."

His answer was not words. It was a laugh — low and terrible and three hundred years overdue. "You heard the monk." And then he stopped wearing the shape of a man.

The borrowed form unfolded, moonlight pouring off scales going dusk to black to gleaming as the dragon rose to his full and proper size inside the ring, wings cracking open over the standing stones. The kin-fire roared back to its full column and threw the chain-makers' faces into harsh light. They faltered. They had planned for a chained worm and a tired girl. They had not planned for this.

I did exactly what I had said I would. I planted myself in front of the girl, my bound and bleeding hands rising into a guard — no weapon, no armor, only the one thing that can never be taken from me. "Stay at my back," I told her. "Whatever happens in front of me, you do not need to watch. Watch the map."

Tygryth hit the front rank like a falling mountain. Real dragon-fire, blue-edged — two robed figures simply ceased — and the clearing dissolved into roaring chaos. Eight of them, perhaps, not eighty, caught out of position and badly surprised. But some slipped his reach. Two peeled away and came around low and fast, robes streaming, straight for the girl behind me. They had not come to capture this time. They had come to cut the thread before it could be rewoven, and they split wide, left and right, to break a single guard.

There is no time for kindness for those who come at you with blades. I did not wait for the left one — he was a half-step ahead, so he was the one I spent myself on. I stepped inside his reach, faster than a wounded man should move, and put every ounce of myself into one rising fist with no thought at all for my own defense. My torn knuckles took his throat square. There was a sound like a green branch breaking, and he folded, the knife dropping from a hand that had forgotten it. The strike tore my raw hand wider, a hot bright line up my wrist — and I did not care, and that not-caring was exactly why it landed.

But the wolf leaves you open, and the world is fair about these things. The right attacker was already there, his blade driving into the gap I had left. I wrenched back toward the girl, too slow to be clean, and the steel scored a long shallow line across my ribs as I turned. Not deep. Paid for, though.

And then I changed gears entirely. No more wolf. The pain settled into that cold quiet place I keep for exactly this, and I read him — the load of his weight, the tell in his shoulder before the thrust, the gap he did not know he left when he reset. A blacksmith's puzzle, and I have solved this one many times before. He lunged; I slipped it. He cut; I was not there. He overcommitted — there — and I trapped the wrist and turned it the way the joint refuses to go, and his own blade fell into my waiting hand as his arm locked useless. For one held breath I had his stolen knife at the hollow of his throat and his whole startled face in the blue kin-light, waiting to learn what kind of man I was.

I am a breaker of chains. I had named myself that, and some sentences are only believed when they are kept. I looked him in the eyes. "Let all who hold chains beware," I said — quietly, with no heat in it at all — and then I finished the guard with his own knife, cleanly. He went down with the words still settling on him. I chose the message over the answers he might have given. Let the ones who sent him hear it from the survivors: the old worm is loose, the thread is guarded, and the breaker of chains does not bargain.

The ring went still. The last robed shapes were gone, swallowed into the dark, carrying that sentence back with them. The kin-fire stood tall and clean again, as though it approved.

Tygryth settled back into the ring, folding down into the dusk-skinned man so he could stand close. His molten eyes travelled from the two dead chain-makers, to the blood on my hands and the dark line across my ribs, to my face, and there was something in his look that was not quite ease. "You are a gentle creature, friend. I have watched you all night choose the kind path, the equal path, the freeing one. And I have just watched you choose that. I do not name it wrong — they came for her life, and a message kept is a shield raised. But I will say one true thing, because we trade in true things, you and I: mercy is a chain you wear by choice, and tonight you took it off. Be sure, in the nights to come, that it is you who decides when it goes back on — and not the killing that decides for you."

I took the warning the way it was given. It was a true thing, and I would carry it.

Then the weight lifted from his voice, and he went to the girl, still pressed to the cold monolith with the map clutched to her chest. "It is over, child. They are routed. You are guarded. You are not alone — not tonight, not again." He looked back at me, at the rubbings in her arms. "And now there is the matter of that map, and the two corners of my blood still scattered in the dark. But you are bleeding from three places and the dawn is not far. So I will ask as a friend, not a dragon: do we make camp here under the kin-fire and read the map and bind your wounds and rest — or is there fire still in you to press on while their trail is warm?"

"You speak truly," I said. "I am no dragon. Though my flesh has been hardened by suffering, I am still mortal and frail. We have accomplished the work of this day and more. Let us rest; what will be, will be, and we will face it tomorrow."

Tygryth inclined his dark head. "Spoken like a creature wiser than his short years." And the night, for the first time since I woke on a cold flagstone floor a whole world ago, was allowed to simply be quiet.

We made camp in the ring. The kin-fire banked low until it was only a soft blue hearth-glow, warm now instead of roaring, and under it the three of us became, briefly, an ordinary thing: travelers around a fire. The girl — whose name she finally offered in a voice gone shy without the knife in it, Wren — cleaned my torn hands with water from the silver river and bound them in strips of her own copied vellum, and I let her, because letting her tend me was its own kind of gift to give. Tygryth kept the dusk-skinned shape and sat with his back to a standing stone, and for a long while the great old creature just watched his blood breathe across the fire, as though he could not yet trust that the sight would still be there if he looked away.

When the wounds were bound, Wren unrolled the rubbings between us, and the map showed itself whole for the first time. Three corners. This western one, where the kin-fire was lit and the line nearly ended. And two more, far across the carved valley — one to the frozen north, ringed in symbols Tygryth read as deep ice, deep silence, and one to the south, over water, marked with something that made even a dragon go quiet: a sigil of a closed eye. "Two left," he murmured. "One under ice. One behind a watching dark. We will read the way truly in the morning light — but they are there, friend. After three hundred years, I know at last that two of my children may yet be there."

The fire settled. Wren fell asleep mid-sentence, the way the truly exhausted do once they finally feel safe, curled small against the warm stone, and Tygryth drew a wing-edge of shadow over her without seeming to think about it. And I lay back under a sky full of unfamiliar stars, bound and scarred and alive, a stranger to this world who had already changed the shape of it — and I let myself rest at last. North into the ice, or south behind the closed eye. That was tomorrow's question. Tonight the kin-fire kept the watch.
