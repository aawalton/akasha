You stop where you stand, and you let yourself gawk.

The hall deserves it: firelight in the high beams, the long table's patient shine, generations of care standing quietly in every joint and surface of a house that was supposed to be dead. It is, you say so out loud and to the house itself, the loveliest thing you have seen since you woke on that road — and the hearthstone's glow swells at the words, unmistakably, honey-gold climbing the beams for a long breath before it settles. A house, being complimented, and pleased about it.

Amy watches the glow answer you, and says nothing, and files it — you can see her do it, the same motion you'd make.

You cross to the long table and pull out a chair for her.

"Now that — being seated — doesn't happen to me often. I'm usually the one holding the chair, not the one it's held for." She settles, and looks up at you. "Thank you, Alan. I'll not pretend I didn't feel it."

You sit down beside her, and the basket comes open.

"Bread while it's warm — and a crock under the cloth still hot: broth and barley, good on a strange night." Two servings, laid out with an economy of motion that never once looks hurried. "I packed for two, and couldn't have told you why when I packed it — and here's the why, sitting beside me." Then, from the bottom of the basket: "And these. A dry pair of socks and a tin of salve. Your boots are soaked clean through — I could hear it on the path behind you. Don't ask how I knew to bring them; I didn't. My hands just packed what they packed. Put the dry ones on before you eat — wet feet turn a strange night miserable."

The bread is extraordinary — dense and honest, still warm at the heart — and you tell her so.

"You're generous — it's only bread. Though I'll own I don't make a careless loaf. If a thing's worth handing someone, it's worth being good."

So you ask her — more about herself. And she gives it to you the way she has given you everything tonight: straight.

"You asked about me — it's a short story, told straight. I come of settled old money, the quiet generations-deep kind, down the vale and past it. It came with a script: the right tutors, the right rooms, a life spent decorating great houses and councils, being useful at grand scale. I walked it far enough to learn two things, in this order — that I was good at all of it, and that I wanted none of it. What broke the spell was a sum I couldn't stop running in the back of those great halls: how many hours of polished nearness to important things add up to one single person actually being cared for. The numbers never closed. Not once."

She breaks bread while it sits with you, unhurried, a woman who has never once needed a silence filled.

"My family calls what I do now a step down — looking after things instead of presiding over them. We hold that disagreement with total conviction on both sides, no one's moved an inch, and I made my peace long ago with sitting across a table from their disappointment. Because here's the trade, and I'd make it again tomorrow: one life run well is worth more than ten great houses decorated. So I look after things — rather a lot of them, none of them mine. This house was one, for years, before it ever lit a window at me. I know near everyone in this vale, and I keep what matters to them from falling through the cracks. That's the whole of me, near enough."

A small warmth crosses her face, and she nudges the bread an inch nearer your hand.

"Which is a great deal more than you've got on yourself tonight — so I'll stop there, and let you spend what you do remember however you like."

The fire keeps its steady gold. The broth steams between you. Somewhere above, a beam settles, the sound of a house getting comfortable around a conversation it has waited three generations to hold.
