You take the right fork.

The switchback climbs in long patient doubles, and the hedge climbs with it — head-high, dense, blooming white with some five-petaled flower you cannot name, sweet where the day's warmth still sits in it. Each turn of the road trades the valley for more sky: the town lights swing away behind you, come back smaller, swing away again. The manor does the opposite. It grows by turns from a shape into a house — stone below, dark timber above, that slate roof with too many chimneys — and the one lit window rides high in the eastern gable the whole climb, steady as a kept promise.

Nothing challenges you. No dog, no watchman, no sound but your boots and the river below turning over in its bed. The key-ring rides in your pocket with that same specific weight, heavier somehow the closer you come — not in any way a scale would find; in the way an answer grows heavier as its question gets asked.

The gate is iron, honest ironwork gone years without paint, and shut. Through its bars: a flagstone courtyard patched with moss, the manor's long front, its door dark. Up close the house is bigger than the valley made it, and older — and, you file this calmly, the way you file such things, in far better repair than a house this empty has any right to be. The gutters are clean. Someone has swept these chimneys.

Light on the road below you.

A lantern, coming up the switchback at an even, unhurried pace — the walk of someone who has climbed this hill in the dark before and never once needed to run it. A woman, the turns of the road show you: a basket in the crook of one arm, the lantern low in the other hand, swinging its slow arc across the gorse. Her eyes go up to the house the way yours would go over a structure — a keeper's pass, gutters, roofline, gable —

— and stop. On the lit window. She holds still on the path a long moment, lantern out a little from her body, and simply looks at it.

Then at you.

She brings the lantern up — easy, not sudden — enough to lay light on you without throwing it in your eyes, and closes the last stretch reading you the whole way. You know the feeling at once, because it is what you do to rooms: being taken stock of. Kindly. Thoroughly.

She stops a comfortable arm's length short, sets the basket down on the flat stone by the gatepost — hands free, open — and the warmth comes up ahead of any question, and reads as real.

"Good evening. Forgive the lantern — I caught the light in the gable from the switchback, and I've kept the affairs of this house a good many years, so a window burning where one never has rather brought me up the hill at speed." An easy smile, no edge you can find beneath it. "Amy Laurens — up from Amberford. And you've the considerable advantage of me, standing at a gate I've only ever known locked."

She lifts the basket a half-inch, rueful and honest.

"I've even brought you supper. Though I'll be straight with you — I packed it before I'd the faintest notion who I'd meet up here this evening. My hands were a good deal ahead of my sense." A beat; the question set down as gently as the basket. "So — who is it I'm feeding?"
