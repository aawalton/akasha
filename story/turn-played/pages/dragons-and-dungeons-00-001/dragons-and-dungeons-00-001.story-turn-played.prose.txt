I woke where I had never been, and for a breath the wrongness of it pressed on me — a strange floor, a strange dark. Then the old discipline answered: what is, is. I let the confusion go and began, instead, to learn.

The breathing was the loudest fact in the room — vast, slow, patient, coming from beyond a door that stood ajar across the shrine. The most important thing, then. But a monk surveys before he commits, so first I looked to my exits. The shrine was small, four walls of weeping stone; the roof had fallen in one corner into a slope of rubble a determined man could climb, up and out through the broken ceiling into the night. Slow, noisy, but real. One way out the top. The ajar door was the other. Good to know which was which before I needed it.

Then I crossed to the door, and the years paid me back in full. Heel, arch, toe; weight poured rather than dropped; my own heartbeat the only metronome. Not a whisper of sound. At the gap I let one eye drift to the seam.

Beyond lay the shrine's old nave, moonlight pooling through a row of shattered windows. The breathing was enormous — in, a long pause, out — and on each exhale a faint warmth rolled under the door and touched my bare ankle. Then the smell: woodsmoke and hot iron, and beneath it something almost sweet, like scorched honey. In the center of the chamber, coiled in a shaft of moonlight and rising and falling with that slow rhythm, was a shape with scales. Dark ones. It had not woken.

I have learned, through great pain, that hearts are fickle, and that the friendships worth having stand on equal footing. As much as I hoped a creature so beautiful might see me as a friend, I was alone in a world I had never seen, and not ready to spend my life on a great challenge for the mere hope of a great reward. So I did not wake it. I poured myself along the chamber's edge instead, shadow to shadow, never crossing the moonlight where it slept — looking for some way to widen my options before I committed to anything.

Three things resolved out of the dark.

First: the dragon was chained. A collar of black iron at its throat, a great corroded chain running to a ringbolt sunk in the floor. Someone had put it here. It was not the master of this place — it was its prisoner. That changed the whole board.

Second: against the side wall, half-buried under a fallen tapestry, a low stone altar. On it, dulled by dust, a brass key the length of my forearm, and beside it a clay lamp with oil still pooled in the bottom — a torch, if I wanted light I controlled.

Third: behind the altar, a narrow servants' passage descending into the dark, cool air sighing up from it. A third way out, and a private one.

And on the last exhale, the great breathing hitched — just slightly. A sleeper near the surface of waking. I did not have long.

My creed decided it before my fear could argue. I value freedom and self-improvement above all else, and I could not leave such a creature in bondage, even at the risk of my life. I took the brass key from the altar, turned to the coiled dark, and spoke into it.

"O great and beautiful dragon — do you wear these chains by choice? If not, I would gladly free you from them, by key or by force. No being of such wisdom and strength should live in bondage."

The breathing stopped. Not slowed — stopped, the way a held breath stops, which told me it had never been as asleep as it let me believe. One eye opened, molten gold and slit, and found me with the unhurried certainty of something that had watched a great many small brave creatures decide what they were.

"By choice," it said, and the voice rolled through the floor into my feet. "No one wears iron by choice, little monk. They wear it because someone stronger willed it so." The head lifted, the chain drawing taut with a sound like a cracked bell, and it leaned close enough that the warmth stopped being pleasant. "You offer to free me. Bold. But you are new to this world — I can smell it on you — so let me teach you its first lesson. The one who chained me will know the instant the collar opens, and they are not far. Free me, and you make an enemy of them tonight and a debtor of me, and you do not yet know which is the worse bargain. So choose with your eyes open, freedom-lover. Will you turn the key — or will you be wise, for once, and ask me a question first?"

I did not take the bait of enemies and debts. There was only one thing I needed to know.

"There is only one question that matters here. Do you wish to be free?"

The gold eye changed. The amusement went out of it — not into anger, into something rawer, something it had not meant to show me. For a long moment there was nothing in the chamber but that look and the trembling hum of the chain.

"...Yes." Quieter than all the rest, the theater gone out of the voice. "Yes. I wish to be free. I have wished it longer than your whole short life, and no one — no one — has thought simply to ask. They came to use me, or to slay me, or to gawk. You are the first to treat the wish as mine to hold." The great head lowered until the eye was level with mine and the collar's lock turned toward me of its own offering — the throat bared, the most vulnerable thing a dragon owns, laid open to a stranger with a key. "The danger I warned you of is real; I warned you because freeing me may cost you dearly tonight. That, too, was a gift. Both are true at once. Turn it, then, freedom-lover — if your creed is more than a word."

No more words were needed. I crossed the four strides — past the edge of safety, into the reach of the jaws — and turned the key.

The lock gave with a sound like a struck anvil. The collar fell open and dropped, the chain following it to the floor in a slithering crash — and the dragon did not take my head. It went utterly still. Then the molten eye closed and a breath went out of it older than the shrine itself, the relief of a creature that had finally decided I was safe to show it to.

"Free," it said, and the word broke a little. "You crossed into the jaws for a stranger's freedom. There was no trap. There was only a creature who had forgotten what it looked like when someone meant it." The head dipped — the nearest thing to a bow a dragon gives. "You have my name when you have earned it, and my debt from this moment. I am yours, as the freed are bound to the ones who turned the key."

And then the cost arrived, exactly as promised. Far off and high, through the broken ceiling, a horn — long and brass and furious — wound across the night from the direction of towers I could not yet see. Another answered. Then a third, closer. The warmth drained out of the air. The freed head snapped toward the sound, and for the first time I saw fear move through something so vast.

"They felt the collar open," it said, all softness gone to iron. "They are coming. We have until those horns reach the third gate — not long. Climb to my back, or take your passage and run, but choose now, because we are both hunted as of this heartbeat."

It lowered a freed wing toward me like a ramp into the dark sky. Behind me, the servants' passage sighed cool. The horns wound closer.

And there I stood — the freed and the free, the hunt already loosed behind us, a wing to the sky on one side and the earth's dark road on the other — with a choice to make, and no more time to make it.
