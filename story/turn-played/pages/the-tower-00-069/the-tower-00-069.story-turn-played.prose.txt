You go back up to the headworks one last time, to the thing you broke. It is still now — truly still, in a way it never was while it slept; the great chain hangs slack off the gantry and the counterweight has settled into the dark below and will not rise again. The warden does not move when you climb over it. You take what the Tower owes you for the killing. The winding-drum — the dense iron core the whole murderous mechanism turned on — you work free of its cradle, and it is the heaviest thing you have carried out of any floor, a sullen weight that seems to keep turning a little after your hands go still. And a long length of the chain itself, struck loose at a worn link, heavy and cold and good iron.

Then you drag them up. The last rungs of slab are easier now that nothing moves them, and the grey seam of the exit widens as you climb until it is a mouth of pale light, and you haul yourself and your dead weight of salvage up and through it, and the long cold shaft — the dark, the dead climber on his ledge, the broken warden in its chain — drops away beneath your boots and is closed. Another floor under you. You do not look back down it.

You come out into warmth.

After the cold of the shaft it lands on you like a hand: a long, low, golden room, firelit and close and kind. A hearth is banked high at the far end, throwing soft light down a table laid the whole length of the room — bread, and dark meat, and bowls that steam gently in the still air — and chairs drawn ready, and at the head of the table a tall hooded figure rising to its feet as you appear, both hands opening toward you in welcome. "Come in," it says, and the voice is gentle, glad, the voice of someone who has waited a long time for a guest. "Come in, climber. You've come so far. Sit. Eat something, and rest — you're safe here. You've earned it."

You set the drum and the chain down at the threshold. And you do not step across it. Because everything the Tower has sharpened in you is, very quietly, screaming.

It is the hearth that gives it away first. You are standing near enough that a fire that size should be pressing heat against your face — and there is nothing. The flames climb and curl and the air off them is cold, and to you, now, a man whose whole body has been remade into an instrument for fire, a fire that throws no heat is as wrong as a voice in an empty room. And the moment you have caught that one lie the others come apart in your hands. The light has no honest source: the hearth and the lamps and the hooded host pour gold across the room and not one of them casts a true shadow, while your own body and the maul on your back throw theirs hard and black across the floorboards. The host's open-handed welcome — you watch it open again, and again, the exact same motion, a fraction of a loop in a thing wearing the shape of someone alive. And down the edges of that laden table, where the cloth hangs and the warm light thins, the pale shapes are not the legs of chairs. They are old, and they are bone, and there are a great many of them. And running under all of it, woven into the warmth itself, there is a pull — a soft, steady invitation to let your shoulders down, to set the heavy thing aside, to sit — and your will, tempered floor by floor, knows the exact weight of your own tiredness, and this is not it. This wanting-to-rest is being pressed into you from outside.

And it does not stop at this room. The warmth runs back past the hearth into spaces you cannot see from the threshold, and — this is the part that turns your stomach — it does not thin as it goes. It deepens. Whatever is being worked here is worked richer and kinder and more convincing the further in it reaches; this bright welcoming hall is only the shallow edge of something that runs back too far for one floor.

The host stands at the head of its table with its hands held open, patient, kind, waiting. The bread steams. The cold fire burns. The salvage sits at your feet on the threshold-stone, and behind you the way you climbed is still open to the dark.
