You take them.

"Thank you, ma'am," you say. "I appreciate your kindness. If there is anything I can do to help while I'm here, I'm eager to learn."

You pull the dress over your head and work your arms into it. The wool is worn thin at the elbows and smells of the box it was kept in. The clogs take both hands and a moment of feeling for the right way round. Then you stand up in them.

Joan nods and puts you to work.

Turnips to peel. She sets you at the bench with a knife and a bowl of them, and goes out to the well. By the time she is back you have three done, and she stands and watches you finish the fourth.

"Thinner," she says.

You take the peel thinner.

Then water to fetch, and the fire to feed, and the morning goes like that. The clogs are stiff and clumsy after a morning barefoot, and loud on the flags. Your blistered palms sting at every grip. Your feet hurt worse inside the clogs than they did out of them, a dull hot ache that doesn't let up.

You work slower than you want to, and you set down every thing where she wants it. Joan watches, and corrects you with a word or two, and the word is always right.

While the pot heats you ask about the house. Who eats where, and what is fetched from where. Joan answers in three words or fewer, and by noon you know the kitchen and the byre, the well and the woodpile under its lean-to.

At noon Aldo comes in, and Tamsin with him. The men eat out in the barn.

There is bread on the board, pease pottage in the bowls, small beer in a jug. You sit at the table with Aldo and Joan, and it is the first real food since you woke. The pottage is hot, and salty, and you eat all of it. You drink deep of the well water, and then more, and Joan fills your cup again without being asked.

Aldo eats with his head down. He doesn't speak of the count, and he doesn't speak of the stick.

When the board is cleared, Joan sets the last bowl aside and looks at you.

"If the village asks, you're a hired hand who works with her hands," she says. She wipes the board down with her forearm. "A woman who reads has been somewhere. Folk will want to know where that was."

A knock at the door.

Hild comes in with a basket on her arm, a bundle of green stuff tied in it. She is the miller's wife, and the healer here, and she has come about Bet's fever. She stops in the doorway when she sees you, and looks you over: hands, face, dress, clogs.

"New hand," she says to Joan.

"Reckoner," says Joan.

She hears her own word, and stops.

Hild sets her basket down on the board and takes you in again, slowly, from the crown of your head downwards.

"Hired for the house," Joan says.

Hild lets that sit. Then she sits down across from Joan, and the two of them talk about Bet: comfrey and yarrow to steep, and the fever watched through the night, and how the girl slept yesterday and how she slept today.

At last Hild picks up her basket and goes to the door. With her hand on it she turns back.

"Where do you come from, girl?" she says. "And whose are you?"
