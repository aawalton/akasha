"A bit of both, I'm afraid," you say. "Certainly didn't plan to end up in a ditch with only the clothes on my back." You look at the cart, the sacks, the lashed-down heap of iron. "You look like an enterprising fellow. Could you use an extra pair of hands in exchange for a meal? I know I don't look like much, but I'm good at cleaning and organizing. I might be able to help you turn some of those treasures into something people would buy?"

He hears you out, chewing the inside of his cheek. His eyes go back to your shirt.

"Both," he says. "Honest answer, anyhow."

"Treasures." He snorts. "Rags and bones, girl. Only papermaker, fuller and glue-boiler buy it."

He looks at your hands.

"Clean and sort, you say. I've had hands before. Soft ones don't last the first sack."

He climbs down off the board, stiffly, one hand pressed to the small of his back as he straightens. He goes round to the tail of the cart, takes hold of a sagging sack, and drags it off. It drops onto the verge with a wet slap.

The smell reaches you a moment later. Mould and old fat. Something inside the sack clicks, like bones knocking together.

"Linen in one heap, wool in another," he says. "Rot back in the ditch. Bones in the bucket."

He sets a dented bucket down beside the sack.

"Do that sack clean and there's bread and cheese in it, and the ride to Ashford. No coin."

He leans his back against the wheel, folds his arms, and watches.

You kneel on the wet grass and pull the neck of the sack open. The smell catches at the back of your throat. Your mouth was already dry, and the stink makes it worse.

The rags inside are sodden, matted together with muck. All of them are the same brown. You pull one free and rub it, then another. Wet through, they feel nearly alike under your fingers, one a little rougher, maybe, or maybe not.

You work fast, so he can see you working. Pull a rag loose, feel it, drop it on the left heap or the right. Pull, feel, drop. The heaps grow.

One lump won't come apart. It is packed tight, heavier than the rest. You get your fingers into it and tug hard.

It tears open.

A slurry of black rot pours out across the heap by your knee, and it is moving. Maggots, hundreds of them, pale and writhing in the muck.

You jerk back. Your heel hits the bucket and knocks it over, and bones roll out across the grass and drop into the rut.

The carter pushes off the wheel. He crouches over your heaps with a grunt, one hand going to his back again.

He lifts a rag off the heap you meant for linen, rubs it between his finger and thumb, and drops it.

"Wool."

He lifts another. "Wool." He looks at the fouled heap, the maggots working through it. "And now the both of 'em spoilt."

He picks up a bone out of the rut and drops it back in the bucket.

"Penny a sack, clean. That there's a quarter-penny of muck." He reaches for the next bone. "Ride's still yours to Ashford. I don't leave folk on the road. The bread's not."

He straightens slowly, the bucket in one hand. His eyes go to your shirt and stay there, the same way they stayed on the rags between his fingers.

"That shirt, though. Close weave, dyed even. I'll give you fifteen pennies for it."
