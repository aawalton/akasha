You take the two wooden buckets up by their rope handles and set off across the yard.

The rope bites into your greased palms. Before you reach the green, two of the blisters give. They tear open under the rope, wet and raw, and every step swings the buckets against them.

The well stands two hundred paces off across the green, a windlass over it. There are women round it: four wives and two girls, waiting their turn, talking, their pails at their feet.

The talk stops as you come up.

They look at the red hair. At the borrowed kirtle, and the clogs. One of the girls whispers something behind her hand.

You wait your turn without a word. After a while the talk starts up again, low, with glances your way.

When it's yours, you take the crank of the windlass. The rope bucket comes up slow out of the dark, dripping, and you pour it off into the first of Joan's buckets, and let it down again. Your palms are slick with grease and blood now. The crank handle slips twice in your grip.

You fill both buckets to the brim, lift them, and turn to go.

Your clog comes down on the wet stone of the well's lip and shoots out from under you.

You go down on one knee. One bucket hits the stones full, and its bottom stave splits.

The water sheets across the flags and over the feet of the nearest wife. She jumps back and shrieks.

The girls laugh outright. The broken bucket rolls away across the stones, leaking the last of it, a crack running down its side.

The wife with the wet feet looks down at her hem, and then at you. Her face is hard.

"That's Joan Reeve's bucket," she says.

Another woman, stout, with a yoke across her shoulders, looks at your bleeding palms.

"Whose runaway are you, then, that can't carry water?" she says, loud enough for the whole green to hear.
