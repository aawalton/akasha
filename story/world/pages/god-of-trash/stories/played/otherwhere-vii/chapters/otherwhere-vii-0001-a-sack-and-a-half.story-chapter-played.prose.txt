The last thing you remember is your back window. A book open on your knee, the evening going blue outside the glass, your eyes getting heavy.

Then wet. Cold water soaking through the back of your shirt, and a smell of mud and rot and something sour, like old vegetables left too long in the bin.

You open your eyes.

Sky. Pale, washed, early-morning sky, with a few thin clouds going pink at their edges. Reeds lean over you on both sides, brown and bent.

You are lying in a ditch.

You sit up fast, and the water sloshes, and your weight goes wrong. You throw out a hand to catch yourself and it lands short, in the mud. You pull it out and look at it. Small. Pale. Freckled across the back, with a narrow wrist and thin fingers, mud to the knuckles.

A heavy rope of dark red hair falls forward over your shoulder, its end trailing in the brown water.

Your shirt is still on you, the dark grey one, but it hangs loose now, soaked, the collar slipping off one shoulder. Your black tights are there too, clinging and wet. No shoes. Your feet in the ditch water are small and pale.

You say something. It comes out in a woman's voice.

The ditch is shallow, maybe knee-deep, and full of rubbish: a torn rag caught on the reeds, a broken piece of brown pot, a single cracked boot with no laces. The bank above you is only a few feet high.

You climb it. The mud sucks at your feet.

At the top there is a road. Not paved. Two deep ruts worn into packed earth, with a ridge of grass down the middle and puddles standing in the ruts. On either side, flat fields run away in long strips, some green, some brown and turned. A crow lifts off one of them, complaining.

About a mile down the road there is a village. Low houses with thick, shaggy roofs. Smoke going straight up from a few chimneys in the still air.

And beyond the village, far beyond it, a mountain.

It is huge and blue with distance, and at its very top, catching the first of the sun, is a city. White. Too white, too clean, too delicate to be real at this distance, a crown of pale towers on the peak. Some of them do not seem to touch the mountain at all.

You stare at it for a long moment.

Behind you, from the direction away from the village, you hear the creak and rattle of wheels, and the slow clop of hooves, coming up the road.

You step up off the top of the bank and onto the road, mud to the shins.

Ditch water runs out of the shirt as you move. It sheets down the backs of your legs, cold through the tights.

The body is lighter than you expect, and its balance sits lower. Your first step lands short of where you aimed it. You pitch forward, and your hands go down on the ridge of grass between the ruts, and you catch yourself there.

The ruts are cold under your bare feet, and gritty. Small stones bite into your soles. The soles are soft, and every stone finds them.

You turn toward the sound, back along the road, away from the village.

The light has gone grey-gold. A cart is coming up the road at a walk, out of the flat country that way. It has two wheels with iron rims, loud on the stones, and a grey mule between the shafts.

It is piled high and lashed down with rope: bulging sacks, broken crockery, a tangle of bent scrap iron. A crate of old bottles rides on top, and it clinks at every rut.

A man sits hunched on the front board, the reins loose in his hands. He is lean and stooped, about fifty, with a grizzled beard, a greasy leather cap, and a coat patched at both elbows.

He sees you while the cart is still some way off. He keeps the mule to its walk.

The cart draws level with you, and he pulls up.

"Whoa, Patience."

The mule stops and puts its nose down into the verge grass.

He looks you over slowly, from your bare muddy feet to your dripping red hair, the way a man counts coins. His eyes stop longest on your shirt.

When he speaks, the words aren't English. You understand every one of them anyway, without effort, as if you'd always known them.

"Robbed, are you? Or run off from somebody?"

"A bit of both, I'm afraid," you say. "Certainly didn't plan to end up in a ditch with only the clothes on my back." You look at the cart, the sacks, the lashed-down heap of iron. "You look like an enterprising fellow. Could you use an extra pair of hands in exchange for a meal? I know I don't look like much, but I'm good at cleaning and organizing. I might be able to help you turn some of those treasures into something people would buy?"

He hears you out, chewing the inside of his cheek. His eyes go back to your shirt.

"Both," he says. "Honest answer, anyhow."

"Treasures." He snorts. "Rags and bones, girl. Only papermaker, fuller and glue-boiler buy it."

He looks at your hands.

"Clean and sort, you say. I've had hands before. Soft ones don't last the first sack."

He climbs down off the board, stiffly, one hand pressed to the small of his back as he straightens. He goes round to the tail of the cart, takes hold of a sagging sack, and drags it off. It drops onto the verge with a wet slap.

The smell reaches you a moment later. Mould and old fat. Something inside the sack clicks, like bones knocking together.

"Linen in one heap, wool in another," he says. "Rot back in the ditch. Bones in the bucket."

He sets a dented bucket down beside the sack.

"Do that sack clean and there's bread and cheese in it, and the ride to Ashford. No coin."

He leans his back against the wheel, folds his arms, and watches.

You kneel on the wet grass and pull the neck of the sack open. The smell catches at the back of your throat. Your mouth was already dry, and the stink makes it worse.

The rags inside are sodden, matted together with muck. All of them are the same brown. You pull one free and rub it, then another. Wet through, they feel nearly alike under your fingers, one a little rougher, maybe, or maybe not.

You work fast, so he can see you working. Pull a rag loose, feel it, drop it on the left heap or the right. Pull, feel, drop. The heaps grow.

One lump won't come apart. It is packed tight, heavier than the rest. You get your fingers into it and tug hard.

It tears open.

A slurry of black rot pours out across the heap by your knee, and it is moving. Maggots, hundreds of them, pale and writhing in the muck.

You jerk back. Your heel hits the bucket and knocks it over, and bones roll out across the grass and drop into the rut.

The carter pushes off the wheel. He crouches over your heaps with a grunt, one hand going to his back again.

He lifts a rag off the heap you meant for linen, rubs it between his finger and thumb, and drops it.

"Wool."

He lifts another. "Wool." He looks at the fouled heap, the maggots working through it. "And now the both of 'em spoilt."

He picks up a bone out of the rut and drops it back in the bucket.

"Penny a sack, clean. That there's a quarter-penny of muck." He reaches for the next bone. "Ride's still yours to Ashford. I don't leave folk on the road. The bread's not."

He straightens slowly, the bucket in one hand. His eyes go to your shirt and stay there, the same way they stayed on the rags between his fingers.

"That shirt, though. Close weave, dyed even. I'll give you fifteen pennies for it."

"I'll take the ride," you say, "but keep the shirt, thanks."

He shrugs, as if he'd expected it. "Suit yourself." He tips the last bone into the bucket.

He takes the fouled sack by its neck and heaves it back up onto the cart, and slings the bucket up after it. Then he climbs onto the board, one hand on the rail, and jerks his head at the seat beside him.

"Up, then."

The board is high. You have to put a bare foot on the wheel hub and haul yourself up by the rail, and your arms have less in them than you expect. You get there. You sit on the plank beside him, the wet shirt stuck to your back.

He clicks his tongue. Patience leans into the traces, and the cart jolts forward up the ruts toward the village.

The seat bangs up through you at every rut. Behind you, iron clanks and the bottles chatter in their crate. The air of the cart's going runs through your wet shirt and your wet tights, and you start to shiver. Your mouth is dry and tacky now. You keep swallowing, and there is nothing there to swallow.

On both sides the fields are cut stubble, pale and bristly, some of them half turned to dark earth.

You pass a farmstead set back from the road. A dog comes tearing out and races the whole length of its fence beside you, barking.

The carter doesn't look at it. He doesn't look at you either, and for a while he doesn't say a word.

At a bend in the road stands a stone, knee-high and furred with moss. Something is carved on its face: a small flower, its head drooping on its stem.

Past the stone the road runs down to the village. Thatched houses are strung along it, and smoke rises from their roofs.

The road runs down to a slow, clear river. The ruts go straight down the bank and into the water at a ford, and climb out again on the far side, and the road goes on beyond.

Beside the ford is a green. There is a stone well on it, a pen of rough rails, and a post stuck all over with notices.

Women kneel at the river's edge, beating linen on flat stones. One of them looks up at the cart. Then her eyes go to your legs in the black tights, and she nudges the woman next to her.

The carter reins in at the edge of the green.

"Ashford."

He looks at you sideways, the first time he has looked at you since the stone.

"So. What is it you mean to do?" He nods at the village. "They're taking on hands for threshing this week, if you've the arms for it."

"Thanks for the tip," you say. "Harvest is always busy, so I'll help there if I can. Who should I talk to about that?"

"Aldo Reeve. Headman." He points his whip past the green. "Big thatch. He's threshing in his barn."

He lowers the whip.

"I'll not speak for you, mind. I've known you an hour, and half of that you spent spoiling my rags."

He jerks his head at the ground. You slide off the board and drop onto the grass.

"Twenty for that shirt, if you think better of it," he says. "I'm round the midden behind the mill till noon."

He clicks his tongue, and Patience plods off along the row of houses, the bottles clinking behind.

You cross the green toward the big thatch. The earth is packed hard, and your bare soles are tender on it. Down at the river, two of the women stop beating their linen and watch you go by.

The shirt has half dried on you, gone stiff at the hem and shoulders. The tights still hold the wet, cold against your legs.

The well stands in the middle of the green, a wooden bucket on a rope beside it. Your mouth is dry.

Past the green a farmhouse sits long and low under a deep thatch. A barn stands beside it, its big doors wide open.

From the barn comes a steady thud. Thud. Thud. Dust drifts out through the doorway into the sun.

Inside, on a floor of beaten earth, four men swing long jointed flails up and over and down onto barley spread across the floor. In the draught of the doorway two women toss grain in flat baskets, and the chaff lifts off and blows away.

A heavy man in a leather apron stands watching the flails, arms folded, a straw in his teeth.

One of the winnowing women sees you and says something to him, and the heavy man turns.

He looks at your bare feet. Then your legs, the loose shirt, the wet red hair. His face stays flat.

He comes to the doorway and stands square in it.

"Help you?"

You ask him if there's work going at the harvest.

"Flails are men's work." He tips his head toward the women at the door. "Winnowing, six pennies and your noon bread." He shifts the straw to the other side of his mouth. "But I don't take on folk off the road. I've a village to think of."

He takes the straw out of his teeth.

"Who are you, then? And who's to speak for you?"

"I'm Nala," you say. "I'm not looking for a handout, just a meal for honest work. I'm happy to do the work first, so you're not risking anything on my lack of reputation. I'm afraid I'm alone here."

He puts the straw back and chews it. He looks at you for a long moment: at your bare feet again, and then at your face.

He takes the straw out.

"Work first. That's fair spoken." He nods at the doorway. "Winnow the morning. Last till noon without wasting my barley, and there's bread in it. Waste it and you're back on the road, and no hard feelings."

He turns his head. "Wyn!"

The older of the two winnowers straightens up. She is bent even straightened, toothless, seventy if she's a day, with a kerchief tied over her hair.

"Gammer Wyn. She'll show you." Aldo jerks a thumb at a barrel standing inside the door. "Butt by the door's for the hands." Then he walks back to his flails.

Gammer Wyn looks you over, from your hair down to your toes, and sniffs.

"Well. You've arms, at least." A pause. "Just."

The other winnower, a girl of about sixteen, snorts into her basket. She doesn't look up.

Wyn puts a basket into your hands. It is wide and flat and shallow, tightly woven, and heavier than it looks. She scoops barley into it from the threshed heap: grain, husks and broken bits of straw all together.

"Up and a flick," she says. "Let the wind have the light stuff. Catch the heavy. Up and a flick."

She tosses her own basket. The grain jumps and falls back into it, and a pale cloud of chaff drifts out through the door on the draught.

You try.

Half the grain goes up with the chaff, and pours over the rim onto the floor. The dust goes straight up your nose and down into your dry throat, and you cough until your eyes run.

You try again, and again. The basket is awkward in your hands. You toss too hard, and grain flies. You toss too timidly, and the chaff sits there in it.

Inside a quarter hour your arms start to ache. Then your shoulders burn. Your stomach growls, loud enough to hear over the flails. The draught in the doorway cuts through your damp tights, and your hands go stiff with it.

"Softer, girl. Softer. You're not beating a rug." Wyn shows you again. And then again.

Past the hour, your arms are shaking. You lift a full basket, and the rim slips in your fingers.

The whole basket goes over. Clean grain pours down into the swept heap of chaff and dirt.

The girl laughs out loud.

The flails stop.

Aldo walks over and looks down at the grain lying in the dirt. The straw is back in his teeth.

"That's the one," he says quietly. "Next goes on the floor, you go on the road."

He walks back. The flails start again. Thud. Thud.

Gammer Wyn gets down on her knees, grunting, and starts picking the grain out of the chaff by the handful. She doesn't look up.

"Where'd you spring from, girl, never to have held a basket?"

"My father was a scribe," you say. "I've mostly worked with my wits and not my muscles. I can read, write, and do sums, but not sure that's needed here."

Wyn's hands stop in the chaff. She sits back on her heels and stares up at you.

"A scribe's girl!" she says, loud enough to carry over the flails. "Gentry come down, is it?"

She reaches up and takes one of your hands, holds it up and turns it over in her own. Her palm is hard as a board.

"No wonder. Hands like a new cheese." She lets it go.

Across the doorway, the girl's basket stops mid-toss. She looks at you properly for the first time, and her mouth goes thin.

"Reads," she says, to the air. "Reads, and can't hold a basket."

Wyn talks straight past her. She struggles up off her knees with grain in both fists and tips it into her basket.

"Aldo!" she calls. "Aldo Reeve! Come here and hear this."

The flails slow. Aldo looks round, frowning, and walks over, wiping his hands down his apron.

"This one's a scribe's girl. Reads and reckons, she says." Wyn jerks her chin at him, then at you. "Tell him."

Aldo looks at you. The straw goes from one side of his mouth to the other.

"Reckons," he says. He doesn't sound pleased. He sounds careful.

He looks back at the four men leaning on their flails, and at the heap of threshed barley. Then at the grain Wyn saved out of the dirt.

"Steward from the mountain comes in six days, when we're done. Weighs the grain, takes his tenth. Writes it in his book, and I make my mark under it." He takes the straw out. "Can't read a line."

He goes to a post by the door. A bundle of hazel sticks hangs there from a peg, and he pulls one free and holds it out to you.

It is a split stick, pale where the bark has been shaved off, cut all along one edge with notches. Seven deep notches sit at the thick end, and four shallow ones after them. At the thin end runs a row of nine more, each one crossed through with a slanting cut.

"That's last year's barley, by the sack. Anyone can say they reckon. What's it come to?"

You take the stick and turn it over in your hands. The split face is flat and smooth, the notched edge rough under your thumb.

"Oh! I've seen these, but only in museums," you say. "The stick is split so two people can each hold a half, and neither can change it without the marks no longer matching, right?"

At "museums" Aldo's brow creases. Wyn's lips move on the word, soundless. Across the doorway the girl frowns at it.

Aldo lets it go by. He is watching the stick in your hands.

When you get to the halves, Aldo goes very still.

"Steward has the other half," he says slowly. "We notch 'em together. Both at once."

You run your thumb along the notches. "I'm not completely sure what the marks mean on their own," you say, "but at a guess, I'd say that's seventy-four. Seven tens for the deep notches, and four ones for the shallow. And the nine crossed through are contracts complete?"

At seventy-four, he lets out a long breath through his nose.

He looks at the stick. Then he looks at you, the way he might if the stick had spoken.

"Seventy-four," Wyn says. "Hark at that. Took the miller a week of Sundays."

"Not contracts, though." Aldo reaches out and taps the thin end with a thick finger, on the crossed notches. "Them's the steward's. The mountain's tenth. A sack a notch, carted off, crossed on both halves."

You look down at the nine crossed notches.

The sum does itself. A tenth of seventy-four is seven, and a bit over. Seven and a half sacks, no more.

The steward took nine. A sack and a half more than a tenth, carted off last year.

"Oh," you say. "Oh dear. I'm afraid the steward has been cheating you, then. For a ten percent tithe, he should have taken only seven and a little less than half, not nine. He's taken more than one and a half more than he should have."

The colour goes out of Aldo's face. He takes the stick back out of your hands, and he doesn't say a word.

Behind him the flails keep on. Thud. Thud. One of the men glances over.

"I said it!" Wyn slaps her knee. "Crooked scale. Said it these ten years, and every one of you let it go by."

"Wyn." Aldo says it low, and she shuts her mouth. He turns his head to the girl. "And you. Not a word."

The girl looks from him to you. Her eyes are bright, and her lips are pressed tight together.

"He said our sacks weighed light," Aldo says at last. "His scale said so. Nine made the tenth." He turns the stick over in his hand. "I made my mark under it. What was I to say, with his mage stood by him?"

He is quiet for a long moment. His thumb runs along the crossed notches, one by one.

"Last year's gone. I'll not go shouting at the mountain's man over a sack and a half."

He looks up at you.

"But he comes again in six days, with his scale and his book. I'd sooner have someone by me this time who can read his book and reckon his tenth." He nods, once, as if to himself. "Stay through the weighing. Bed and board, and ten pennies a day. Joan'll say where you sleep."

He holds the stick out to you again.

"No more baskets. Keep your mouth shut, and keep my count."

You take the stick from him.

"I will gladly stay," you say, "but on the condition that you let me teach you. And anyone else you decide as well. You should learn enough to keep your own count with confidence, so no one can cheat you."

Aldo stares at you. Then he looks down at the floor, and his big hands work at the edge of his apron.

"Teach me," he says, low. His eyes go to the men at the flails. "Not here. Not in front of them. After supper, then. By the rushlight, in the kitchen. Nobody's business but ours."

He rubs his jaw.

"You'd do that. On top of the reckoning, for no more pennies."

He smiles. It is the first time you have seen it. It is small, and brief, and it changes his whole face.

"Tamsin first," he says, louder. "I meant to send her to Bramwick for her letters, and never did."

The girl's basket stops. Tamsin stares down at the chaff in it, her cheeks gone red.

"Don't want it."

"You'll have it," says Aldo.

Wyn cackles.

Aldo sobers. "Quiet about it, mind. A village that reckons looks like a village after trouble. The steward's not to smell it before the weighing. Not a whisper. Understood?"

He jerks his head toward the house. "Go on to Joan. Tell her I said. Wyn, take her basket."

You cross the yard to the farmhouse with the tally stick still in your hand.

A woman comes to the door, lean and quick, wiping flour off her forearms. She looks you up and down.

You tell her what Aldo said. Her eyes go to your bare feet, to the tights, to the stick in your hand, and back up to your face.

"Reckoner," Joan says flatly. "Well. He's wanted one since before we wed."

She leads you round the side of the house to the cow byre. Inside it is dim and smells of dung and warm hay, and a ladder goes up through a square hatch in the ceiling.

Up top is a low loft under the thatch. The cows' breath rises through the boards and keeps it warm. A straw tick lies on the floor.

"That's yours while you're ours," Joan says from the ladder. "Dinner's at noon, at my table. Not the barn's."

She climbs down and goes back into the house. When she comes out again she has a folded brown dress over one arm and a pair of worn wooden clogs in her hand.

"My Bet's. She'll not miss them, abed." She holds them out to you. "Borrowed, mind. You'll not sit at my table in your shift and hose."
