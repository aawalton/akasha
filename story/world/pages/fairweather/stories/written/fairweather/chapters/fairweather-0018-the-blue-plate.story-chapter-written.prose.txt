Elsie woke in the dark. The round window was still black, with only the red glow of the canal lanterns low in it, and the attic smelled faintly of yeast coming up through the boards. She couldn't tell what had woken her. She lay on her back under the sloping ceiling and found she wasn't sleepy at all. It was the hour before dawn. She knew it by the quiet. On Ash Lane Ines had held her teacup under her chin and whispered about it. "When it's still dark and very quiet and everyone's asleep. And it's all just mine."

Elsie lay and listened to it. The canal slapped softly at the wall under the window, slap, and a pause, and slap. Far off, out toward the Mere, a boat was moving. She could hear an oar knocking in its rowlock, slow, a long way out, and then she couldn't. Below her, under the floor and the stair and the dark shop, an iron door clanked. That was Mrs Merriweather at the big oven, the way she was every morning from four. The clank came up through the boards, and after it the yeast smell came up warmer.

Elsie turned on her side toward the sill, where Moonpenny's clay pot made a dark shape against the window. "Moonpenny," she whispered. "This is Ines's hour. This one, now. It's her favourite." She listened a while longer to the canal, and to where the boat had been, and to the oven door. The quiet was soft and close and all round her, like a second blanket. "It's rather nice, actually. I can see why she likes it." Moonpenny's bud stayed shut and green, and Elsie smiled at it in the dark.

On the sill beside the pot, where she had left them, the two grey gloves lay side by side. The first was whole, thumb and four fingers, lying flat with its fingers together. The second lay next to it, shorter, its cuff and hand finished and the needles still stuck through it. Out of its side stood the thumb, half grown, a little grey stub that stopped where she had stopped last night. In the dark the pair of them were the same grey as the window.

She sat up with the blanket round her shoulders and took the second glove into her lap by the round window. She found her place by feel. Round and round, plain, plain, plain. The needles ticked very softly, so as not to wake anyone, though there wasn't anyone up here to wake. The black in the window went to navy, and the navy to a thin grey. The canal came up out of the dark beneath it, pale and smooth as a ribbon. She knitted the last round, drew the wool through the top, and pulled it snug. The thumb was done.

Downstairs the shop was warm already, and dim, lit only by the open mouth of the oven at the back. Mrs Merriweather stood before it in her apron with the long wooden peel, sliding out loaves. "Good morning, Mrs Merriweather!" Mrs Merriweather nodded without turning round. Elsie looked at the window. The blue plate sat where she had set it last night, under the glass, and the honey cakes sat on it in their row. All of them were there, every one. She counted them anyway.

She went and stood over the plate. The cakes had kept most of their crackle. They had only gone a little dull, the way honey does when it has sat out all night in the air. Through the glass, along the canal walk, the red paper lantern over the door was going pale against the grey. Elsie looked at the full plate a moment longer. She thought of moving it. She thought of tipping the cakes into the tin for the birds. She left it exactly where it was, and turned round.

"Mrs Merriweather, may I buy a little crock of honey? A small one. The smallest you have." Mrs Merriweather set down the peel. She took a crock from the shelf over the flour bin, a little brown one no bigger than a fist, and filled it from the big jar, the honey going in slow and gold off the ladle. Elsie opened the purse on her belt and counted three copper pips onto the counter, one, two, three, and pushed them across. Mrs Merriweather swept them into her palm.

She cut a square of clean muslin from the roll and laid it over the mouth of the crock. She tied it down with string, twice round the neck and a knot, her floury fingers quick and sure. She was looking at the crock, not at Elsie. "Who's it for?" It was the first thing she had said all morning, and Elsie stood up a little straighter.

"A girl called Ines. She likes hot milk with honey in. Not tea. Milk. And the hour before dawn. When it's still dark and very quiet and everyone's asleep. She says it's all hers then." She looked at the window, where the grey had gone to pearl. "I was awake for it this morning. I think she's right about it."

Mrs Merriweather looked at her for a moment. She took up the string again and tied a second knot over the first, and pulled it hard, so the muslin sat tight as a drum over the honey. She pushed the crock across the counter. She went to the bread shelf, took down yesterday's loaf, cut the heel off it with one stroke of the knife, and pushed that across as well. "Breakfast." She went back to her oven.

The guild hall was quiet at seven, and still cool from the night. The tall doors stood open on Brightwater Square, the sawdust on the boards was fresh, and only a handful of adventurers were in yet, yawning at the board. At the clerk's desk, standing very straight in front of it, was Ines. She was in her black lace to the wrists and the throat, her sleek bob shining, and against the big timber desk in the big timber hall she looked smaller even than she had in her own doorway.

Elsie saw the space round her before she saw anything else. The boards round Ines were bare. There was a ring of empty floor round her, an arm's length and more on every side, swept and clean, with the morning light lying across it. It was the same as the ring round Noor's table in the corner, the same exactly. Everyone else in the hall was standing somewhere else, at the edge of it, as if a line had been chalked on the boards that only they could see.

Behind Ines, where the queue should have been, two adventurers were waiting with a notice. They stood a whole long pace back from where they ought to have stood, so far back they were nearly at the board. One of them had her hand up by her mouth. She was whispering to the other, and the other was looking at Ines's back, at the black lace, and nodding. "Necromancer." It was a whisper that carried.

Elsie went straight across the bare boards. "Good morning, Ines!" She came up beside her at the desk, right beside her, close enough that her elbow touched the black lace. Ines turned her head. Her dark eyes went wide, and then something in her face let go a little at the corners, the way it had at her door yesterday when the stiffness went out of her. "Good morning." Elsie stayed exactly where she was. Behind them the two adventurers whispered again.

Ines turned back to the clerk and folded her small hands on the edge of the desk. "Biscuit is home." The clerk leaned forward to hear her. "My cat. The notice. I. Calderón." She pointed at the board without looking round at it. "He was found on the dome of the Hall of Naming. His foot was caught. He's home now, on my bed, asleep." She tipped her head toward Elsie, the smallest tip. "By these ones. They found him. These ones."

The clerk looked at Ines and at Elsie. She took down the notice book, found the line and wrote in it. She bent and unlocked the iron box under the desk. She counted silver out onto the wood: one lantern, with its little ring, and then another, with another. Two. They lay side by side on the desk in the morning light, each with its lantern stamped on its face. The clerk stamped the notice paid, thump, and pushed the two coins across toward Elsie with one finger.

The door banged at ten past seven, and Tamsin came in with Tilly and Cora behind her, all three together, as if they had met on the steps. Tamsin had Margery on her back and her curls tied up anyhow. Cora was buttoned to the throat with her ledger under her arm, her bun as smooth and tight as a river pebble. Tilly came last. She had her fat grave book clutched under one arm and her goggles crooked, and as she came through the door she yawned enormously, with her whole face.

"I read about raising," Tilly said through the tail of the yawn, before she was halfway across the hall. "All night. All of it. Everything I've got, and two books off my landlady's shelf that turned out to be about drains." She stopped beside Ines and blinked at her, owlish, the soot on her cheek smudged wider than usual. "I've got forty questions. I counted. Forty." She yawned again, so wide her eyes watered, and hugged the book to her chest.

Elsie picked the two lanterns up off the desk. They were still cool from the iron box. She held them out on her palm so all of them could see. "I was thinking, on the way here. About these." She looked at Tamsin, and at Margery's haft over Tamsin's shoulder. "They should go to Margery's whetstone. Both of them, whole. If that's all right with everyone." She looked round at them in turn. "It was Margery that cut all those stones out of the mill race."

"Yes," Tilly said at once. "Oh, yes. Yes."

Tamsin didn't say anything. She stared at the two lanterns on Elsie's palm. After that she stared at Elsie. Her mouth opened and shut again. The red came up under her freckles, from her neck to her copper curls, until her whole face was as red as her hair and the freckles stood out pale on it. "It was a cat notice. It's the whole cat notice, Elsie. That's all of it."

Elsie took Tamsin's big hand by the wrist and turned it palm up. It was rough and warm and twice the size of her own, the knuckle wraps still on. She tipped the two lanterns into it. She folded Tamsin's fingers down over them one by one, the way Tamsin had folded Tilly's at this same desk yesterday, and held the closed fist between both her hands for a moment, firmly, so it would stay shut. "There. For Margery."

Tamsin looked down at her fist. She cleared her throat. "Margery says thank you," she told the fist, in a deep, rough voice that was meant to be Margery's. She reached up over her shoulder with her other hand and patted the axe's haft where it stood up behind her head, pat, pat, the way you'd pat a horse that had done well. Her face was still red. She put the fist with the coins in it into her pocket and kept it there.

Elsie took the little crock out of her satchel. The muslin was still tight over its mouth, and both knots still hard. She turned to Ines and held it out to her in both hands. "This is for you. For your milk. Honey. I said I'd bring you some." She smiled. "It's from the Honeycomb. Mrs Merriweather tied two knots on it, so it won't spill on the way home, even if Biscuit sits on it."

Ines took it. She took it in both hands, the way she had taken Biscuit on the leads, and held it against the black lace at her chest. She didn't speak. She looked down at the little brown crock and the muslin and the string. She looked at it the way you'd look at a bird that had come down onto your hand: very still, hardly breathing, as if it might go off again if she moved. Elsie watched the pink come up her throat into her face.

Ines tucked the crock inside her lace, against her chest, the way you'd tuck a kitten in out of the rain, and held it there with one hand flat over the top. "For tonight's milk." She looked up at Elsie, and her eyes were bright. "Tonight." She pressed her hand a little flatter over the crock.

Tilly had been hopping from foot to foot. "Ines. Ines. May I come and ask you my forty questions? Not all at once. Well, perhaps all at once. I've written them down, look, they're in the back." She held up the grave book. "I wouldn't touch anything. I wouldn't touch the birdcages. I'd sit on the floor." Ines looked at her over the crock. "Yes."

Tilly's face lit up.

Ines bent her head over the crock. "Today. If you like. Today."

Cora had stopped at the end of the desk. She stood there with her ledger shut under her arm and her other hand at her side, and she was watching Ines. Elsie saw her watching. Her face was perfectly still, the grey eyes level, and Elsie couldn't tell from it what she was thinking, not at all. It was the face she had worn that first morning at the Crooked Kettle's door, before anybody had asked her how she took her tea.

At last Cora stepped forward and stood very straight in front of Ines in her charcoal coat. "Miss Calderón. For the guild's record. Biscuit's return will be noted as a found cat." She paused. "A found cat. Only that." She didn't look away.

Ines looked at her. She looked first at Cora's coat, at the long charcoal front of it buttoned to the throat, the way she had looked at it from her doorway yesterday, a long moment. Her eyes went up to Cora's face. Her hands tightened on the crock. "Thank you, Warden."

Cora inclined her head the smallest bit, the way she did on the guild steps at night, and stepped back to the end of the desk.

Elsie took Ines's free hand. It was cold again, small and cold. "Come on. Come and meet Noor. You'll like her." She turned toward the corner and set off across the hall with Ines's hand in hers. They went over the bare boards together, past the two whispering adventurers, out of Ines's empty ring and across the swept floor between, and into Noor's. Ines came along beside her, small and quick, and her cold fingers held on the whole way.

Noor was at her table in the middle of her ring of empty tables, in her long green coat, with her coffee. She watched them come. She had watched Elsie cross this floor before, alone, board by board, with a cup in her hand. This time there were two of them, hand in hand, and Elsie saw Noor see it. Her dark eyes went from Elsie to Ines, and down to their hands, and up again. She set her coffee down on the table without drinking.

"Noor! It's tomorrow." Elsie took hold of the good chair across from Noor by its back and shook it, and rapped on the seat with her knuckles, twice, for luck. It was the same good chair as yesterday, and it knew her now, she was nearly sure. She sat down on it and smiled across the table. Ines stood at her shoulder with the crock of honey held against her lace.

Noor looked up at Ines. Ines looked down, at Noor's hands on the table, at the black gloves. Neither of them looked away. They stayed like that for what seemed to Elsie a long time, Noor's kohl-dark eyes on Ines's face and Ines's eyes on the gloves, both of them very still, as if each were reading something written small on the other. Elsie looked from one of them to the other and kept quiet, which was hard.

"Sit if you like," Noor said at last. "People cross the street from me too." She lifted one gloved finger and pointed it, without hurry, round at the empty tables. "You'll have noticed. So you needn't sit too close. Things go wrong near me. Cups. Chairs." Her eyes flicked to Elsie and back. "Ask her about chairs."

"I don't mind bad luck." Ines pulled out the chair beside Noor's, not across the table but right beside her, and sat down on it, close, so close that the black lace of her sleeve touched the green of Noor's coat. She drew the crock of honey out of her lace and set it down on the table in front of her, carefully, and folded her hands in her lap. "Things go wrong near me too. People say so."

Noor didn't look at Ines. She looked at her own cup. She watched it in its saucer, steaming, with her gloved hands flat on the table either side of it, the way you'd watch a dog that bites. Elsie watched it too. A long breath went by, and another. The cup stayed exactly where it was, the coffee flat and still inside it, and the steam went straight up. Noor frowned at it. She frowned at it as if it had broken a promise to her.

Elsie leaned her elbows on the table and put her chin in her hands. She had asked it every day since the first one. "Noor. Will you have lunch with me today?"

Noor picked up her cup by its handle. She didn't drink. She set it down again and turned it a quarter turn on the saucer with two gloved fingers, and then another quarter turn, slowly. She kept her eyes on the cup the whole while. "One o'clock. The Crooked Kettle." She gave the cup a third quarter turn.

"I won't wait." Noor still didn't look up. "If you're not there at one, I'll eat, and I'll go. I won't sit there watching the door." She straightened the cup so its handle came round to her glove. "So you'd better not be late." The tips of her ears had gone pink under the straight black hair, the same as yesterday.

Elsie squealed. It came straight out of her, high and delighted, and she clapped her hands together over the table, once, twice, three times. Ines jumped. At the third clap Noor's spoon leapt out of her saucer. It went straight up off the china with a little ring, turned over once in the air, struck the edge of the table and dropped off onto the floor, where it skittered away over the bare boards and lay still.

All three of them looked at the spoon on the floor. Noor looked at it longest. She looked at it for a long while, lying there on the boards with the light on it, and the corner of her mouth went in. It went in hard, at one side, the way it had over the honey cakes yesterday, as if she were biting the inside of her cheek to keep it where it was. She didn't get up to fetch the spoon. She left it lying there.

At ten to eight Tilly came over to the edge of the ring with her grave book. "Ines! Are you ready?" Ines picked up her crock and stood. Tilly took her by the elbow as if they had known each other for years, and the two of them went off toward the doors, Tilly talking already. "How do you raise a mouse, though, exactly? Do you start at the tail end, or the—" Her voice went out through the doors into the square. Ines looked back once over her shoulder before the doors took her.

By a quarter past eight Brightwater Square was full of sun. Tamsin went straight across it to a cutler's on the far side, a narrow shop with knives laid out in its window on green baize, and Elsie and Cora went in after her. Tamsin tried four stones in her hand, weighing each, rubbing her thumb along it, and chose a long grey one, fine-grained and smooth. She took one of the two lanterns out of her pocket and set it on the counter. The cutler took it and wrapped the stone in brown paper.

They went down to the canal steps at the corner of the square, where the stone stairs ran down into the water and the sun lay warm on them. Tamsin sat on the lowest dry step with her boots near the water and laid Margery across her knees. She wet the stone in the canal and set it to the edge. She drew it along the curve of the blade in long, slow, even strokes, from heel to toe and back, the whole length of the edge every time.

The stone rang on the steel. It made a clean high sound, a ringing hiss, over and over, and the sound went out across the water. After a while Tamsin began to hum. It was the same three notes over and over, low in her throat, going up and down with the stroke of the stone. She didn't seem to know she was doing it. Elsie listened, and smiled to herself. She hummed like that over her knitting. Tamsin had caught it off her, like a cold.

Elsie sat on the step above Tamsin with her knees up and her skirt tucked under, and took out the second glove. The thumb stood up finished from its side, neat and grey. She counted the stitches round the top of the hand and parted off the ones for the first finger onto her needles. She began it. Plain, plain, plain. Her needles clicked under the ring of Tamsin's stone, and the two sounds went along together down the steps.

Cora stood at the top of the steps. She stood very straight, with her ledger under her arm, looking out over the canal, the way she stood at the edge of everything. Elsie looked up at her and patted the step beside her. Cora didn't move. Elsie patted it again and again. She kept on patting, pat, pat, pat, looking up at her and smiling, until Cora came down the steps and sat on it, stiffly, with her knees together and her ledger on them.

Tamsin stopped. She lifted Margery off her knees by the haft and held her up to the light. The sun ran along the edge. It shone there in a single thin bright line, all the way round the curve from heel to toe, so bright it hurt to look at, with the blade dull grey on either side of it. Tamsin turned the axe a little this way and a little that way. The bright line ran up and down the edge like water.

She held out her own forearm, brown and freckled, and laid the edge against it. She drew it along very lightly, barely touching. A little patch of fine copper hairs came away on the steel, clean, as easily as anything. The skin under them was bare and smooth and quite unhurt. Tamsin looked at it. She twisted round on the step and looked up at Elsie. She grinned, wide and crooked, with the whole of her face.

"Let's go and look at the house." Tamsin wrapped Margery's edge in its leather and slung her onto her back. "Only look. We won't go in, we won't knock, we'll only walk by it." She was on her feet already. "Just to say good morning to it. It's on our way." She thought about that. "Well. It's on a way."

Willow Canal was a quiet one, with willows leaning over the water and the towpath worn bare beneath them. They came along it at half past nine. Elsie saw the blue door from two bridges off, the way she always did, as if it were the only blue thing on the whole canal. Then she saw that it was standing open. Voices were coming out of the house, out of the open door and the open front windows: people talking inside, and boots on bare boards.

In the wild garden, among the long grass and the seeding poppies, stood the letting agent from Coin Street, the plump man in the waistcoat, with his watch chain glinting across it. With him were four adventurers. He was showing them round. He was pointing out the garden to them, the wall, the old apple tree, the shed. The four of them were looking where he pointed, the way Elsie and Tamsin and Tilly had looked through the railings with their noses against them.

They were a C-rank party. Elsie could see the letter on the tags at their collars, and see it in their leather. It was good leather, dark and supple and well oiled, every buckle matching. One of them had a measuring tape. She was standing at the front window with it stretched between her two hands, measuring across the glass from one side of the frame to the other, and calling the number over her shoulder to another of them, who wrote it down.

Tamsin stopped dead at the gate. She stopped so suddenly that Elsie walked into her back. Her hand went up to Margery's strap at her shoulder and closed on it, tight, her knuckles going pale, and stayed there. She didn't speak. She stood at the gate with her hand on the strap and watched the woman with the tape at the window, and her jaw was set the way it set on the Underbloom stair.

The agent saw them and came down the path to the gate, mopping his forehead with a handkerchief. He knew them. Elsie saw it in his face, and saw his eyes go to Tamsin's hand on the strap. "Good morning. It's as I told you, my dear. Whoever brings me the four suns first." He tucked the handkerchief away and gave a small shrug. "It's not mine to choose. It never was."

Behind him, the C-rank party had come out onto the path. The tallest of them, a broad woman with a sword on her hip, called over the agent's shoulder to the garden in general. "We'll have the gold after our Midsummer job. Week after, at the outside. Keep the card in the window for us." The others laughed. The one with the tape rolled it up with a snap, and they all looked at the house as if it were already theirs.

Elsie looked up at the house. She looked at all three storeys of it, narrow and tall, leaning a little toward the canal as if to see itself in the water. She looked at the sills, one at every window, broad and empty, and thought of Mrs Pennywhistle on one, Sunny on another and Moonpenny at the top. She looked at the garden gone to seed, the poppies dropping their petals in the long grass, and the blue door, open, with its fish for a knocker.

Tamsin let go of the strap. She held up her hands and began to count on her fingers, her lips moving, her eyes fixed somewhere past the gate. She folded down one finger and the next. She got to the end of one hand and went on to the other. At last she stopped, frowned, put both hands down flat against her legs, and started again from the beginning, slower, moving her lips over every finger.

"With Tilly's purse too," Tamsin told the gate. "All three of us. Everything we've got, and the cat notice, less the stone." She let her hands drop. "We're about eight lanterns short." She looked at the woman who had rolled up the tape. "About eight."

"Eight lanterns isn't so very many," Elsie said. It came out small.

Tamsin looked round at her.

Elsie smiled at her anyway. "It isn't! It's only a notice. It's only one good notice."

They walked on along the towpath past the front of the house, past the open blue door, with the voices coming out of it. As they passed it Elsie reached out and pressed two fingers to the blue frame, quickly, the way she had the first time, for luck, and for good morning. A step behind her Tamsin reached out too. She pressed her two big fingers to the same place on the frame, and took them away, and walked on.

Elsie glanced back. Cora had stopped on the towpath. She was looking at the two of them, at Elsie and then at Tamsin, and at their hands, which had gone back to their sides. She turned her head and looked at the door. She looked at it a long moment, at the blue paint and the brass fish and the frame where their fingers had been, with her ledger held flat against her coat. At last she came on after them.

The guild hall was full and loud by a quarter past ten, all sawdust, wet coats and talk. Tamsin went straight past the clerk's desk to the E-rank board and began at the top of it, reading fast, her finger running down each notice and on to the next, her lips moving. She didn't stop to talk. Elsie followed her down the board with Cora a pace behind, and watched her read, and read the notices over her arm.

Tamsin's finger stopped. She tapped the notice under it, hard, twice. "There." Elsie leaned in. It was pinned up near the bottom, a square of good paper with a festival seal on it in red wax. Guard the Midsummer barges from river thieves, it said, in a big clear hand. And underneath, in figures you couldn't miss: twelve lanterns. Tamsin tapped it again. "There!" She turned round with her eyes shining.

Elsie read the rest of it. Dusk to dawn on the twentieth, it said. Boats to be lent by the festival, from the Lantern Steps. She read it through twice, the way she read everything that mattered, with her finger under the words, and on the second time through she stopped at the twentieth. That was the day after tomorrow.

"Twelve lanterns." Tamsin held up both hands with the fingers spread, and then two more fingers. "Twelve. Eight for the house." She folded eight fingers down and wiggled the four that were still up. "And four left over for pie. Pie for everyone. On the step. With the door open." She was grinning again, the crooked grin from the canal steps. "We'll have a step. Our own step, Elsie."

Cora had come up behind them. She read the notice over Elsie's head, and her eyes stayed on the paper. "Thieves on Midsummer night carry knives. Not cream bowls."

Elsie turned round. "We'll be careful. We'll be very careful. I promise." She looked up at Cora. "And you'll be in the boat. You're always in the boat. So we'll be safe. We're safe when you're there."

Cora looked at her. She looked down at Elsie for a long moment, from under her straight dark brows, with the notice and the noise of the hall all round them. Her lips came apart a little. They closed again. She didn't argue any more. She shifted her ledger to her other arm and stood there beside the board, and was quiet.

Elsie took the notice down and carried it to the clerk's desk. She took up the pen and signed for the party in her round careful hand: Fairweather, Reyes, Brandt. The clerk read the names, and the notice, and looked up at Cora behind Elsie's shoulder. She reached for the stamp and stamped it, thump, with the date: the twentieth. She wrote it in her book as well, and pushed the notice back across the desk.

"The whole city'll be out on the Mere," Tamsin said at Elsie's elbow. "Every boat there is. Lanterns on every one, and the fireworks off the barge." Her eyes were bright. "And Celestine Okafor sings. On the festival barge. She does every year. Mum used to row us out to hear her, me and Nell, when we were small. You can hear her right across the water, from the far shore. Everybody goes quiet for it."

Elsie had gone still. She looked down at the stamped notice in her hand. "Celestine Okafor is Amara's mother."

She thought of Amara on the barrow in the dark, with the lights coming up out of the grass round her like slow bubbles, singing to the empty downs. She thought of the hollow pressed into the grass where Amara sat. She thought of how her voice had stopped, the moment Amara knew they were there, stopped dead, as if a door had shut on it. Amara couldn't sing a note if anyone was listening. And the whole city would be out on the Mere.

She stood by the clerk's desk with the stamped notice in her hand and looked at it, at the twentieth and the red festival seal. Tamsin was still talking about pie. Elsie folded the notice in half, and in half again, along the creases, slowly, and put it into her satchel. She kept her hand on the satchel flap a moment after.

At eleven Tamsin hitched Margery up on her back. "I'm off to the docks. I'm going to write Mum. And Nell. About the barges. About guarding the barges on Midsummer, with an Enthraller and a Warden and an axe that can shave you." She grinned. "Nell will be sick with jealousy. She's wanted to be on the water at Midsummer, not just watching it." She went off through the crowd, Margery's haft bobbing over the heads, and out of the door.

At half past eleven Elsie and Cora walked through Lantern Square. It was a wide square of tall old houses with high windows, quieter than Brightwater, with plane trees round its edge. In the middle of it, where another square would have had a fountain, a field of bronze stood up out of the paving. It was a field of threads. Elsie slowed when she saw it, and then stopped, at the edge of it, with Cora beside her. In among the rows, a little way in, five rods stood together with pale candle wax pooled round their feet.

The threads were rods, hundreds of them, thin bronze rods no thicker than a finger, standing up out of the stone in long rows, each as tall as a woman. They had gone green-brown with weather. Every one of them had been cut clean through, near the top, a hand's breadth down, so that a little gap of air stood between the rod and its tip, and the tip was set back on it a fraction askew, as if it had just been snipped. On each rod, at the height of Elsie's eyes, a name was cut.

"What is it?" Elsie's voice came out soft, the way voices do in a church.

Cora stood beside her looking at the rows, not at Elsie. "The Memorial of Strings. For the war's dead. The War of Strings." She paused. "Each thread is a person. They were cut, so the threads are cut."

Cora walked into the rows. She didn't seem to decide to. She simply went, off the paving and in among the rods, along a row and down another, as if her feet knew the way without her. Elsie followed a step behind. Cora stopped. She stood before five threads that stood together, a little apart from their neighbours, in a short row of their own, and she stood there very straight with her ledger against her coat.

Elsie came up beside her and read the names. There were five of them, on five cut threads, and every one of them ended Vane. Two were older names, a man's and a woman's, and three were boys' names, one after another. Beneath them, on the paving at the foot of the five rods, there was wax. It was candle wax, five little pools of it, pale and fresh, still soft-looking at the edges, as if the candles had only burned down in the night.

"My grandmother's parents." Cora was looking straight ahead at the bronze. "And her three brothers." She lifted her hand and touched the nearest rod, the first boy's name, with two fingers, and took them away.

"She lives on this square. She has lived here since. She lights five candles every night. She brings them out here, and lights them, and sits with them until they're out. Every night. Sixty years."

Elsie didn't speak. She couldn't think of anything that would be the right size. She stood beside Cora and looked at the five names a long time, one after another, the man's, the woman's and the three boys'. She looked at the soft pale wax at their feet. She thought of Cora's grandmother as a girl, with five names to keep. She thought of five candles, every night, carried out across a square. Her eyes had filled. She let them.

She reached out and took Cora's hand. It was the bare one, hanging at Cora's side, and she took it quite simply, the way she would take anyone's, and held it. She didn't say anything. She stood there beside Cora in the rows of cut threads with Cora's hand in hers. They looked at the five names together. The pigeons walked about on the paving round their feet, and the plane trees moved overhead.

Cora didn't take her hand back. It lay in Elsie's for a while, quite still, long-fingered and cool. Slowly, so slowly Elsie hardly felt it begin, Cora's fingers closed. They came round Elsie's hand one at a time, the long fingers folding over the back of it, until they held on. They held on tight. Cora kept looking straight ahead at the bronze, and her fingers kept holding.

"I understand now," Elsie said to the names, after a while. "Why you said it. On the dome, when you'd pulled me back. Don't do that again." She looked at the five threads, cut through, all in their row. "It's because of them. Because of people getting lost. You've had so many people lost already. Before you were even born."

"That isn't why." Cora stopped.

Elsie looked up at her. Cora's face had gone very still, the way it went on ice. Her lips were apart, as if the next words were there behind them, ready. She didn't say them. She closed her mouth and kept looking at the bronze. Her fingers tightened once round Elsie's, and that was all.

Then, from somewhere above them, a voice began to sing. It came from a tall house on the north side of the square, five storeys of pale stone with long windows, from one of the highest windows, which stood open on its hinges to the warm morning. It was a girl's voice. It came down over the square and the plane trees and the bronze threads, not loud, without any words, as if whoever it was had forgotten the window was open.

It was low and warm, and it wandered. It went up, came down again and went somewhere else, wherever it liked, slow and easy, like someone walking on the downs with nowhere to be. Elsie knew it at once. She knew it the way she'd know her mother's humming. She had heard it in the dark on Bellmoor barrow, with the lights coming up round it out of the grass, before anyone knew she was listening.

Elsie looked up. She couldn't help it. Her head went up by itself, toward the high open window. And the singing stopped. It stopped dead, in the very middle of a note, as if a hand had been put over it. The note hung in the air over the square for a moment, and was gone. After that there was only the pigeons, and a cart somewhere, and the plane trees.

A face came to the window. It was there for a heartbeat, high up, small at that height: a face, and long braids hanging down past it on either side, and a hand on the frame. Elsie saw it see her. At once the curtain swung across, a pale curtain, fast, so fast it billowed out through the open window and was sucked back in again. It hung there, shut, and stirred, and was still.

Elsie let out her breath. "It's Amara." She kept looking up at the curtain. She didn't wave. Her hand had begun to come up, the way it came up for everybody, and she put it down again by her side and kept it there.

"We shouldn't stand here and listen, Cora. She wouldn't want us to. Not if she'd known." She looked down from the window. She was still holding Cora's hand, and Cora was still holding hers, and she didn't let go. She walked on, out of the rows of threads and across the square the other way from the tall house, with Cora's hand in hers, not looking back at the window once.

The Crooked Kettle was on the north side by the bridge with the cats, and Elsie and Cora came in out of the sun at five to one. It was cool and brown inside, low-beamed, smelling of tea and soup. Noor was already there. She had a corner table at the back, by herself, with her back to the wall and her long green coat still on, and the tables round her were empty. She was sitting very straight. The clock on the wall said five to one.

Cora went to the little table by the door without a word. She sat down at it with her back to the wall, where she could see the whole room, and ordered a pot of tea from the waiter, black, strong, one sugar. She opened her ledger on the table in front of her and uncapped her pen. Elsie went on to the back of the room, to the corner, and stood beside Noor's table.

Noor looked past her, at Cora by the door with her ledger open, and then up at Elsie, with one eyebrow up. "You brought your Warden."

"Cora goes everywhere." Elsie knocked on the seat twice and sat down. "She can't help it. It's her job." She leaned in over the table. "But she likes quiet. It's one of her favourite things. I wrote it down. So she won't talk. She'll only write." She smiled at Noor. "It's like having a very polite coat stand."

They had leek soup and brown bread. The soup came in two thick brown bowls, pale green and steaming, with a swirl of cream on top, and the bread came in a basket with a cloth over it. Noor didn't take off her gloves. She ate with them on, neatly, holding her spoon in black leather fingers and breaking her bread into small even pieces, and she didn't spill a drop. Elsie watched her a moment and thought she must have had years of practice.

The street door opened behind Cora and a gust came in off the canal. It went right through the tea room. It lifted Elsie's napkin up off her lap, carried it across the table like a little white bird, and dropped it flat into her soup, where it sank. Elsie looked at it. Then she laughed. She fished it out with two fingers, dripping pale green, and held it up, and laughed so much she had to put it down on her bread plate.

The waiter came past with a pot of tea on a tray. Right beside their table, on the flat boards, his foot went out from under him. He staggered. The tray went one way and the teapot the other, out over the edge, tipping. He lunged and caught it by its handle a finger's breadth above the floor, with the lid rattling. He stood up slowly, red in the face, holding the teapot, and looked at the floor where he'd tripped. The floor was perfectly flat.

Elsie felt something slide. Her flower clip had come loose. It slipped down out of her hair past her ear, quite slowly, as if it had made up its mind, and dropped off the end of her bob. It fell straight into the bread basket. It landed on a brown crust, pink and white among the bread, and lay there. Her hair swung down across her eye on that side.

Noor put down her spoon. She laid it beside her bowl, very carefully, and folded her gloved hands on the table. She looked at the napkin on the bread plate, at the waiter with his teapot, at the clip in the bread basket. "This is why I eat alone."

Elsie pushed her hair out of her eye. "It's the best lunch I've had since I came to Lanternmere." She thought of the napkin flying, the waiter's face and her clip in the bread. She thought of Noor here at the table at five to one, waiting, when she had said she wouldn't wait. "It is. Truly."

Noor looked at her across the table. She looked at her for a long time, with her hands folded, her dark kohl-lined eyes very steady on Elsie's face, as if she were looking for the joke in it and couldn't find one. At last she unfolded her hands. She reached into the bread basket with two gloved fingers and fished the clip out from among the crusts. She brushed a crumb off it with her thumb.

She leaned across the table. With her gloved fingers she took the loose hair that had fallen over Elsie's eye and smoothed it back, along the side of Elsie's head, behind her ear, the leather cool and soft against Elsie's temple. She slid the clip in over it and pressed it shut, careful, exactly, the way she broke her bread. She sat back in her chair and picked up her spoon again, and looked at her soup.

Elsie touched the clip. It was in properly, firmer than she ever managed herself. She smiled at Noor over the soup. "Noor. On Midsummer night. We're going to be out on the Mere, all of us, guarding the barges from river thieves, from dusk till dawn. We've signed for it. Will you come?" She leaned forward. "Out on the water with us. In the boat."

"We'd love a fourth. We truly would. Tamsin and Tilly and me, and you. And Cora, but Cora doesn't count, she's the Warden, she's always there." She looked at Noor's gloves on the spoon. "And you needn't sit near anyone if you'd rather not. You can have the whole back end of the boat to yourself. We'll sit up at the front. We'll wave."

Noor stared at her. She held her spoon still above the soup and stared at Elsie across the table, and her face had gone quite still. "Boats sink round me." There was nothing dry in it at all.

"Then we'll take Tilly's floating vials," Elsie said at once. "She has a kind that floats. They bob. You hold on to one, and up you come, like a cork." She counted on her fingers. "And Tamsin swims like a fish. She grew up on a barge. If the boat sinks she'll just pick us all up and swim us home."

Noor turned her cup on the table. She turned it a full turn this time, slowly, all the way round, with two gloved fingers, until the handle came back to where it had started. She looked at the cup and not at Elsie. "Ask me tomorrow," she said.

Elsie put her head back and laughed out loud, so that the old men at the next table but two looked round. "That's what you said yesterday! That's exactly what you said yesterday, at the guild, over the coffee. Ask me tomorrow." She pointed her spoon across the table at Noor, still laughing. "And look what happened."

When the bowls were empty Elsie reached for her purse. Before she had the strings undone, Noor had put her gloved hand into her coat and brought out coins, and laid them on the table by the bread basket in a neat little stack: enough for both of the soups and both of the breads. She slid the stack to the table's edge with one finger. Elsie opened her mouth.

"Don't."

At the door Elsie stopped beside Cora's table. Cora was putting the cap on her pen. Her ledger lay open on the table in front of her beside the empty teapot, and Elsie looked down at it. Under today's date, in Cora's small exact hand, there was one line. Lunch. And under that, the line Elsie always looked for. Skills used, none.

They walked back along the canals in the long afternoon, Elsie and Cora, at half past two, the slow way, because there wasn't any hurry. The sun had come round high and hot. The water was bright, and the paper lanterns hung pale and still along the walks, waiting for evening. Pigeons sat on the bridge rails with their eyes shut. Elsie walked on the canal side. Cora walked on the other, a little nearer than she used to, with her ledger under her arm.

Elsie knitted as she walked. She had the trick of it now, from the road to the mill, and she could watch her feet and the needles both. Plain, plain, plain. The first finger of the second glove grew between her needles, round and grey, over one bridge and the next. On the third bridge she stopped and held it up against her own hand to see. It had grown past the end of her own first finger already. It went on past it, a whole knuckle, and she smiled and went on knitting.

Cora was watching the needles. Elsie could feel her watching, the way she had on the road from the mill, steadily, a long time, her grey eyes on the grey wool going round. They went along a whole canal like that. At last, so low that Elsie only just caught it over the water: "Whose fingers is it measured for?" Cora's eyes stayed on the needles, and her hands held the ledger flat against her coat.

"It's still a surprise!" Elsie beamed up at her. She held the knitting close against her chest a moment, as if to hide it, and laughed. "You can't know yet. Not till it's finished." Cora didn't ask again. She looked down. She looked at her own hands, holding the ledger, at her long bare fingers lying along the black leather, pale and still. She looked at them for a long while, as they walked.

At six the guild hall shut. The porters dragged the tall doors across, one and then the other. The day's crowd came down the steps into Brightwater Square in twos and threes, in the long gold light. Cora went up to the top step, by the doors, and stopped there, where she always stopped, where she went off duty. She turned round to face Elsie on the step below.

"Fairweather." She inclined her head, the way she did every evening. Then she didn't go. She stood there on the top step in the gold light, very straight, with her ledger in both hands in front of her, and didn't turn away, and didn't go down the steps. Elsie waited on the step below with the glove in her hand and looked up at her.

"I'll be at the Lantern Steps at dusk," Cora told a point just past Elsie's shoulder. "On the twentieth. With my lantern." Her hand moved to the shuttered brass lantern hooked at her belt and touched it. "For the boats. At dusk. I'll be there before you."

"I know." Elsie came up the last step and hugged her. She did it quickly, without asking, both arms right round Cora's middle, coat, ledger, lantern and all. She squeezed. The coat smelled of ink and, faintly, of lavender. The ledger's corner dug into Elsie's ribs. She didn't mind. "I know you will," she told the charcoal wool.

Cora stood stiff in it. She stood as stiff as a post, her arms out a little from her sides, the ledger held up out of the way. At last one of her hands came down. It rested on Elsie's back, between her shoulder blades, flat, for a moment, warm through the cream halter. After that it lifted. Elsie let go and stepped back, smiling.

Cora went down the steps past her and off along the canal toward Wick Lane, straight-backed, her ledger under her arm and the low sun throwing her long shadow out over the cobbles in front of her. Elsie watched her go along the water, past the first bridge. At the second bridge Cora stopped. She turned her head and looked back over her shoulder, at the guild steps, at Elsie on them, and stood like that a moment before she went on.

At half past eight Elsie sat on her bed in the attic by the round window with the second glove in her lap. The lamplighters' boats had been along Thimble Canal. Every paper lantern was lit, and the light came up off the water and in at the window red and gold. She had finished the first finger at supper. She had started the second. Plain, plain, plain. It grew between her needles a round at a time, beside the long finished first one.

Below the window, on the canal walk, someone stopped. Elsie saw it out of the corner of her eye, a shape stopping under the red lantern over the shop door, and she looked down. It was a girl. She stood on the walk with her back to the canal and her face to the Honeycomb's window. The red lantern light lay on her, on her shoulders and on her long braids, which hung down her back past her waist.

The girl stood looking in at the honey cakes. She stood a long time, quite still, the way she had stood the other night, her hands at her sides, just looking at the blue plate through the glass. Elsie held her needles still. She didn't breathe. Down on the walk the girl looked a little longer. At last her shoulders went down a little, and she turned away from the window, toward the bridge, to go.

Elsie dropped her knitting. She dropped it on the bed, needles and all. She was off the bed, across the attic and down the stair before she had thought about it, in her stockings, as fast as the narrow stair would let her, one hand on the wall. She ran through the dark shop, past the cold counter, the loaves under their cloths and the warm black shape of the oven. She got to the door.

She pulled the bolt and opened the door, and the bell over it jangled. The canal walk was red and gold with lantern light. Amara was three steps along it, toward the bridge, with her back to the shop. At the bell she stopped. She stood quite still, and turned round, slowly, in the lantern light, with her long braids swinging, and looked at Elsie in the doorway.

"Hello, Amara." Elsie was out of breath from the stair. "They're for you. The honey cakes. They're for you." She held on to the door. "They've always been for you, every one of them, since the barrow. I put them in the window for you." She stood back and held the door wide, as wide as it would go. "Come in."

Amara laughed. It came out of her startled, as if Elsie had jumped out at her, and she put her hand up to her mouth. "I've walked past three times," she said through her fingers. "Three times now. I kept thinking I'd come in, and then I'd get to the window, and then I'd be at the bridge." She took her hand down. "Three!" She laughed again, a little less startled.

"I know. I saw you from up there." Elsie pointed up at the round window. "Wait." She went back into the shop, to the window, and lifted the blue plate out of it with both hands, the honey cakes and all. She brought it out to the step and sat down on the step with it. She patted the stone beside her, and Amara came back the three steps and sat.

Amara took a honey cake. She held it a moment and looked at it. She bit it, and shut her eyes. She ate it slowly, with her eyes shut, all of it, in small bites, the way you'd eat something you had been thinking about for days. When it was gone there was honey on her thumb. She licked it off, still with her eyes shut, and sighed through her nose.

Amara opened her eyes. "It's better than the barrow. Better than on the barrow, I mean. In the dark, out of your satchel." She looked at the plate. "Those were good. This is better." Elsie clapped. She clapped quietly, though, with her fingertips, because it was late and the windows along Thimble Canal were lit and people were going to bed behind them.

Amara took another cake and held it in both hands. She looked down at it, not at Elsie. "This morning, on Lantern Square. Did you hear me?" She turned the cake a little. "At the window. Singing." She didn't look up.

"Yes. A little. Only a little." Elsie looked at the canal. "And then you stopped, and I saw you at the window, and I walked on." She turned to Amara. "I didn't think you'd want me to stay. Not to stand there listening. So I didn't. I took Cora and we went."

Amara turned the cake in her fingers. She turned it round and round, looking down at it, not at Elsie. "You walked on," she said at last. She turned the cake once more. "Thank you."

She took a bite of the cake and chewed it a long time. "My mother wants me to sing on the barge. On Midsummer night. Beside her. The whole Mere out on the water, and every boat, and her, and me." She looked out at the canal, at the lantern light on it. "She's asked me this year. She's so pleased about it."

"I haven't told her." Amara's voice had gone very small. "That I can't. That I can't sing a note if anyone's listening." She looked down at the cake in her hands. "She doesn't know. She thinks I'm only shy, and I'll grow out of it, and Midsummer is when I'll grow out of it, all at once, in front of everyone." She laughed, and it wasn't really a laugh. "She's so pleased."

Elsie looked at her: at her hands round the cake, and the braids, and her face turned down in the lantern light. "Oh, Amara." She had a honey cake of her own, half eaten, in her lap. She put it down on the step beside her, on the bare stone, and left it there.

She put her arm round Amara's shoulders. She did it the way she always did, without asking, and she didn't take it away again. She left it there, warm, round Amara's shoulders on the step, her hand resting on the far one. Amara's shoulder was stiff under her hand. Elsie kept her arm there, and kept it warm, and looked at the canal with her.

Amara leaned. She leaned into Elsie's side by inches, slowly, a little and then a little more, as if she were letting herself down into warm water. The stiffness went out of her shoulder under Elsie's hand. At last her head came down onto Elsie's shoulder and rested there, heavy and warm, her braids lying over Elsie's arm. She let out a long breath.

"I'll be out on the Mere that night," Elsie said after a while. "On the dark water. All night, dusk till dawn, in a boat, guarding the barges from thieves. We've signed for it." She rubbed Amara's shoulder with her thumb. "So I'll be ever so busy. Looking out for thieves. I won't be listening at all."

"If you look out for a boat with a pink head in it, that'll be me." She tipped her head against Amara's so Amara could feel which head she meant. "Not listening. Not listening at all. Looking the other way for thieves, very hard, with my fingers in my ears." She thought. "Well. With one finger in one ear. I'll need the other hand for the boat."

Amara laughed into her shoulder. It was a wet sort of laugh, half a laugh and half something else. Elsie felt it more than heard it, against her arm and her side. Amara didn't lift her head. She stayed where she was, on Elsie's shoulder, with her face turned into the cream halter, for a long while. Elsie held still and let her, and watched the lanterns on the water.

When Amara sat up at last, Elsie fetched a sheet of the bakery's brown paper from the shop and wrapped the rest of the honey cakes in it, every one that was left on the blue plate, and twisted the ends shut the way Mrs Merriweather did. She put the parcel into Amara's hands and closed Amara's fingers round it. "For home. For tomorrow. For whenever."

Amara stood up with the parcel held against her chest. She stood on the canal walk in the lantern light and looked down at Elsie on the step for a moment, her braids hanging forward over her shoulders. "You're the Enthraller. Aren't you? The one with the pink hair. Everyone's talking about you."

"Yes." Elsie made a face. "It's a dreadful name, isn't it? I didn't pick it." She hugged her knees on the step. "I'd have picked one with flowers in. Something you could say at a tea table without everybody dropping their cups."

Amara laughed again. It was a proper laugh this time, out loud, and she put her hand over her mouth to keep it in, because of the late windows. "Goodnight, Elsie." She went off along the canal walk under the lanterns, toward the bridge, with the parcel against her chest. Halfway to the bridge she looked back. At the bridge she looked back again. Then the bridge took her.

Up in the attic at half past nine Elsie lit the candle and sat on the bed with the little blue notebook on her knee. She turned to Noor's page, to the raincloud, and under the favourite things she wrote, in her round hand, Lunch, yes! and underlined it twice. She turned on to Amara's page, past sticky honey cakes and the downs at night, and wrote underneath, Home. Ate three.

She turned on to a clean page and wrote MIDSUMMER at the top in capitals. Underneath she drew a boat, a little rowing boat on wavy lines of water, with a lantern on a pole at the front of it. She drew five heads in the boat in a row: one with a bob, one with wild curls, one with two braids and goggles, one with a bun, and one with long straight hair, sitting a little apart at the back, but in the boat.

She looked at it for a while, with the pencil at her lip. Very small, at the very back of the boat, behind the long straight hair, she drew a sixth head. It had braids, a lot of them, long, down its back. She looked at that for a while longer. At last she turned the pencil round and rubbed it out, carefully, with the end, until only a faint grey smudge was left at the back of the boat.

She knelt up on the bed with her elbows on the sill, by Moonpenny's pot. "There's a square full of bronze threads," she whispered, "and every one's cut through. And five of them have fresh wax under them. And somebody sang out of a high window, and then she stopped. And the blue door was standing open, and there was a woman measuring the window with a tape." She put her chin on her arms. "And Noor said yes. To lunch. She said one o'clock."

She blew out the candle. In the red light off the canal she took the second glove out of the tangle on the bed and smoothed it flat: the thumb done, one finger done, long and grey, and the second finger just begun on the needles. She laid it on the sill beside the first, palm down. She spread her own hand flat beside the two of them, and the finished finger reached a whole knuckle past her own.
