Elsie woke to the slap of water against the hull and a dark square of window above her head. For a moment she did not know where she was. She smelled coal and canal and soap. She felt the narrow mattress under her hip, the wall at her back, and something heavy and warm lying across her waist. It was Tamsin's arm. It lay over her in the quilt, slack and heavy, the hand open against Elsie's stomach through her shift, the freckled wrist turned up. Elsie looked down at it and smiled. She did not move, so as not to wake her.

But Tamsin was awake. Elsie could tell from her breathing, which was very quiet and very careful, and from how still she lay, stiller than any sleeper, her whole long body gone rigid along Elsie's side. Elsie turned her head on the pillow. Tamsin was lying on her back with her eyes open, looking straight up at the planks of the cabin roof. Her face was red to the hairline. Her arm did not move from Elsie's waist. She spoke to the ceiling, hoarse. "Morning. Good morning. Did you. Sleep. All right."

"Lovely." Elsie yawned and snuggled down.

The cabin was full of the smell of frying. Rosa stood at the black stove in her faded red shirt with her sleeves pushed up, turning slices of bread in a pan of sizzling fat with her fingers. She was singing under her breath, a slow wharf song with a lot of verses about a drowned bargeman. The lamp was out, and the grey light came in through the geraniums. Nell's shelf of a bunk over the stove was empty, the blanket thrown back. Through the open hatch Elsie heard her up on deck, whistling, coiling rope and talking to a gull as if it owed her money.

They ate fried bread at the folding table with mugs of strong tea, the four of them crammed elbow to elbow again. Rosa put an egg on everyone's plate whether they wanted one or not. Elsie was halfway through hers when she remembered. She put down her fork. "Oh. The flower cart." They all looked at her. "There's a notice on the board. At the guild. An escort, for a flower cart round the lake villages. I saw it two days ago and I meant to ask Tilly, and then—" She looked at Tamsin. "I ought to go to the guild."

Nell looked up sharply from her egg, and it was Tamsin she looked at, not Elsie. "You're going. Off to the city with her, the minute you've eaten." Tamsin opened her mouth. "Then you come back. Tonight. For supper. We're loading all day and we go through the lock in the morning, and you're not to just walk off up that lane and leave it another year. You promise me." She put down her fork and pointed it across the table at her sister. Her chin had gone stubborn and square. "Say it. Say you'll come back for supper."

Tamsin's eyes were on her plate. "I promise." She lifted her head to Nell's face. "I promise. I'll come back for supper."

Nell narrowed her green eyes at her. "Say it to Mum."

"Nell—"

"Say it to Mum. She'll hold you to it. I'm only your sister."

Tamsin turned on the bench to Rosa at the stove. Rosa turned round with the pan in her hand, and waited.

"I'll come back for supper, Mum."

Rosa nodded once, slowly, and turned back to the stove. Elsie saw her wipe her eyes on her shoulder.

Rosa came out to the plank to see them off, in her apron with her shawl round her shoulders against the early chill. She hugged Tamsin first, for a long time, without saying anything. She turned to Elsie next and hugged her, a big coal-smelling hug that lifted Elsie onto her toes and squeezed the breath out of her. "You." Rosa's mouth was in her hair. "You come back too. Any time we're in." She set Elsie down, held her off by the shoulders and looked at her. "And next time you bake that cake, you save me a slice. A big one. I'll know if it's small."

The city was white with mist. It came up off the Mere in the early mornings and lay along the canals, thick and soft and cool. The water vanished under it, the bridges rose out of it into white, and the far banks were only shapes. The paper lanterns hung along the towpaths unlit and beaded with wet. Elsie and Tamsin walked back the way they had come, over bridge after bridge, their footsteps loud on the stones. A baker's boy went by with a tray on his head. A cat sat on a bollard, damp and offended. Elsie breathed the cool air and felt full of the barge still.

Tamsin was quiet. She walked beside Elsie with her hands in her pockets and her hair springing loose from its comb in the damp. She did not say much, but it was a different quiet from yesterday's. Elsie could feel it down the Tether, and it was not cold or held. It was something more like the slow warm feel of a full stomach. On the second bridge Tamsin bumped Elsie's shoulder with hers, on purpose, then looked away over the canal as if she had not. On the fourth she did it again. Elsie bumped her back, and Tamsin's mouth twitched at one corner.

They came down off the fifth bridge. "Nell is wonderful. She's so quick. And funny. And she's got your grin, did you know? Exactly. And she loves you so much, Tamsin." Tamsin was quiet for a few steps. At last: "Yes. She is." She took one hand out of her pocket and rubbed the back of her neck. "I took the letters. The bundle. They're inside my shirt." She patted the place, over her heart. "I'm going to answer every one. All of them. Even the one about the heron. I'll be at it a month." She grinned, crooked. "She'll have to post me the next lot."

The guild hall was only just opening. A clerk was propping the great doors back with wedges. Inside, the lamps were still being lit along the beams, and the long hall smelled of floor polish and cold ashes. There were few adventurers yet: two at the tea counter, yawning, and one asleep on a bench with his hat over his face. By the F-rank end of the quest board a woman stood waiting with her arms folded. She was small, broad and sunburnt to the colour of a brick, in a straw hat and a smock with dried petals stuck to it. She was frowning at the board.

She turned as they came up and looked them over, Tamsin's axe and Elsie's satchel. Her frown eased a little. "You after a job? Because I've a job. That one." She jabbed a thick finger at a notice on the board: the flower cart, round the lake villages, five lanterns. "Two days it's hung there, untaken. Too far, too dull, not enough pay, they say." She spat to one side, neatly. "And meanwhile the reed sprites rob me blind. Every trip. My roses, my ribbons, my good red hat. I'm not asking for heroes. I'm asking for somebody to swat."

The doors banged. Tilly came in at a run, with her goggles bouncing on her head, her apron flapping and her belt of vials clinking. One braid was done and the other half done and coming loose, its tie in her teeth. She came skidding up the hall toward the board, saw Elsie, beamed round the tie, and saw Tamsin beside her, close beside her, at this hour of the morning. The two of them had come in together through the same door. Tilly stopped dead in the middle of the floor. She took the tie out of her mouth. Her bright blue eyes went from one of them to the other.

"Elsie. Where were you? Last night?" Tilly came the rest of the way slowly, holding the tie. "I came round. To the Honeycomb. I knocked. I brought a pear fizz, a fresh one, the new kind, I put the pear in later this time like I said, and I thought we could have it on your window sill and look at the canal." She pushed her loose hair back. "And you weren't there. I knocked twice. The baker came down and said you'd gone out with a cake in the morning and not come back, and looked at me, and I didn't know where you were."

"Oh, Tilly, I'm so sorry. I'd have loved a fizz. I was on Tamsin's mother's barge, on the Long Cut. We took the cake, and her mum and her sister made us stay for supper, and then it was late and her mum said to sleep aboard, so I did." Elsie smiled at the memory. "In Tamsin's bunk. It's tiny. We had to share." Tilly went very quiet. She stood with the tie in her fingers and looked at Elsie, then at Tamsin, then at the floor between her own boots. Her face did something small and careful. "Oh."

Elsie saw the half-done braid coming loose over Tilly's shoulder. She took the tie gently out of Tilly's fingers, without asking, and stepped round behind her. She undid the loose part and combed it through with her fingers, the long fair hair with its singed ends, and began to plait it again from the nape of her neck. Tilly went perfectly still. She stood in the middle of the guild hall like a statue, with her arms at her sides and her eyes fixed straight ahead on the quest board. She did not move at all while Elsie plaited, except that her ears went slowly pink.

Elsie tied off the braid and patted it. The charcoal-painted door across the hall opened. Cora came out, buttoned to the throat, with the ledger under her arm and her black hair drawn back so tight and smooth it shone under the lamps. She came straight across to them, took her place a step behind Elsie and inclined her head. "Fairweather."

Elsie turned round to her with a smile. "Good morning, Cora. Did you sleep well?"

Cora looked at her. There was a small pause. "Adequately." After another moment: "Thank you." She opened the ledger.

The carter took them to the clerk's desk, where a thin young clerk with ink on his fingers wrote out the escort in a big book: the flower cart, the lake villages, this party, and their names, one under another. Elsie signed her own in round careful letters. Tamsin scrawled hers, and Tilly wrote hers very small. The clerk blotted it and stamped it. Beside them Cora wrote in her ledger at the same time, in her small exact hand, the date, the notice and the names. Elsie, watching her pen, saw her own name come out at the top of the line.

The flower cart stood waiting at the old flower market, a donkey cart with high slatted sides, stacked with empty buckets. Between the shafts was a grey donkey with a straw hat of its own on, holes cut for its ears. The carter climbed up onto the seat. "Should've been gone at dawn. We go at dawn. Back by dusk. That's the round. We'll be tight for it today, and that's on account of waiting for you lot, so I'll thank you not to dawdle." She clicked her tongue. The donkey sighed and leaned into the collar. The cart rolled out over the cobbles, and they walked beside it.

Past the last houses the city fell away, and the shore road ran out along the Mere in the sun. The mist had burned off. On the right hand the lavender fields rolled away up the slope in long purple rows, humming with bees, and between them stood fields of poppies so red they hurt to look at. On the left hand were the reed beds, tall and green and whispering, and beyond them the flat bright water of the Mere going all the way to the sky. The road was white dust between the two. The cart's wheels squeaked, and the air smelled of lavender and warm water.

Elsie walked on the lavender side. She let her hand trail out beside her through the tops of the nearest row as she went, so that the flower heads brushed her palm and bobbed back behind her. Her fingers came away smelling sweet and sharp. She held them to her nose, then let them trail again. A bee came with her hand for a while, and went. The sun was warm on her bare shoulders. Without noticing, she began to hum, the caravan song from yesterday, and the cart squeaked along in time. Tamsin, on the reed side, looked across at her and smiled.

The carter flicked the reins at a fly on the donkey's rump. Nobody had asked. "Four villages on the round. Sweetwater first, that's roses. Then Larkford, that's poppies. Then Pennyfield, where there's the Poppy inn and we stop to eat, and then Reedby at the far end, which is all sorts. Lavender, cornflowers, sweet peas. Few miles apart, each of them." She spat again, neatly. "Sweetwater, Larkford, Pennyfield, Reedby. And back. You'll know them in your sleep by the end." Elsie said them over to herself, softly, so as to keep them: Sweetwater, Larkford, Pennyfield, Reedby.

Sweetwater was a cluster of white cottages round a green with a pump, and every garden was roses: climbing the walls, spilling over the gates, standing in rows in the fields behind in every colour from cream to near-black. The growers had the buckets waiting at the roadside, rows of them, packed with long-stemmed blooms in water. Elsie took one in both arms. It was heavier than it looked, the water slopping cold against her chest. She staggered with it to the cart and lifted it up to the carter. She went back for another, and another, with petals sticking to her arms and her halter damp.

Tilly had stopped at the first bucket and put her face in it. She went down the row of them sniffing, bucket after bucket, her eyes half-shut and her nose buried in the petals. As she went she talked, fast, mostly to herself. "Rhodinol. Lots of it. And geraniol, that's the sweet. Phenylethyl, that's the honey under it. This one's got eugenol, smell, clove, that's a damask, has to be. That's citronellol, lemony, see? This one's an old one, an old one, it's got a bit of musk." The growers stopped working and stared at her. One of them nudged another.

Elsie set down her bucket by the cart and went over to the growers. "She's an alchemist." She was beaming at them. "Tilly. She's the cleverest person I know. She can smell what anything's made of. She can make fire burn blue." The growers looked at Tilly again with different faces, and one of them nodded slowly, impressed. Tilly lifted her head out of a bucket of cream roses and heard. She stood with a rose petal stuck to the end of her nose and went pink. The pink went up and up until it reached the tips of her ears.

They went on out of Sweetwater with the cart heavy now and smelling like a summer evening. The road bent close along the reed beds. The reeds stood higher than Tamsin's head here, thick and green and whispering. As the cart came along beside them they began to rustle, though the air was still. Elsie looked. The rustling ran along the reeds beside the cart, keeping pace with it. And out of the green, from somewhere quite near, there came a giggle: a tiny, high, breathless giggle, like a girl's laugh heard through a keyhole. Another came, from a little further along.

Something darted out of the reeds. It was so quick that Elsie saw it only as a flash: a thing the length of her finger, with wings like a dragonfly's buzzing to a blur, a thin green body, and a face, a tiny pointed laughing face. It shot across the road in front of her nose and straight into the back of the cart. It came out again with a whole red rose in its arms, a rose bigger than itself, and zipped back into the reeds with it, the rose's stem trailing. The reeds closed over it. There came a whole chorus of giggling.

The carter did not turn round. "Reed sprites. There they are. Told you." She flicked the reins. "Harmless, mind. They don't bite and they don't sting. They just take. Anything bright. Flowers, ribbons, buttons, a shiny buckle, a hat with a feather in it. My good red hat went in there in the spring and I've not seen it since. They've a nest of stolen things somewhere in there the size of a haystack, I'll be bound." She jerked her chin at the rose stem lying in the road, dropped. "They'll strip the cart bare by Reedby if you let them. That's what you're for."

Tamsin took it personally. She dropped back to walk at the tail of the cart, by the roses, with her fists up and her eyes on the reeds. When the next sprite came darting out, a flash of green wings going for a white rose, she swung at it with her open hand, fast as anything. She missed. The sprite went over her hand, under her elbow and round her head once, close, so that her curls lifted in the wind of its wings. It hung in the air just out of reach in front of her face and giggled at her. It stuck out a tiny tongue, and was gone.

Tilly had been walking with her tongue between her teeth, sorting through the vials at her belt. Now she unstoppered a little brown one, sniffed it, made a face, and stoppered it again. She unstoppered a green one next and nodded. She tipped a few drops of it into her palm and flicked them, wrist and fingers, out across the road at the reeds. A sharp sour smell went up, like vinegar and crushed ants, and Elsie's eyes watered. In the reeds there was a sudden storm of tiny sneezes, dozens of them, high and furious. A frantic whirring of wings followed, going away fast in every direction.

Larkford was nearly all poppies. The fields came right down to the road on both sides, a red so bright and thick it seemed to hum. The growers there had their buckets of them waiting, the flowers like crumpled silk. They loaded them in among the roses. A stretch of straight flat road followed in the sun, and the carter let them ride. Elsie climbed up and sat on the back of the cart among the buckets with Tilly beside her. "It's the sour that does it," Tilly said. "That's the vinegar part. And the ants, they can't abide ants, I knew they couldn't, it gets right up their little noses and—" Her words came slower and slower, until her head dropped onto Elsie's shoulder and she was asleep.

Elsie kept very still. She sat with her back against a bucket and her legs hanging over the tail of the cart, swaying with it. She did not shift her shoulder, though it began after a while to ache, because Tilly's breathing had gone deep and slow against her neck. Her mouth had fallen a little open and her goggles had slid crooked. She looked very small and very tired. Elsie thought of her knocking twice at the Honeycomb last night with her fizz. Carefully, with her free hand, she took a poppy from the bucket beside her and tucked its stem into the top of Tilly's braid, where it sat like a flame.

Cora did not ride. She walked beside the cart on the reed side, as she had walked all morning, upright in her charcoal coat buttoned to the throat. Her hands were clasped behind her back, and her grey eyes moved steadily over the reeds, keeping watch for the next flash of wings. The sun was high and hot now. Elsie, swaying on the tail of the cart with Tilly asleep on her, watched her. She saw that the back of Cora's neck, between her collar and her tight black bun, had gone pink. The tip of her fine straight nose had gone pink too.

At a crossroads beyond Larkford there was a stone horse trough with a pump. When the cart stopped to water the donkey, Elsie eased Tilly's head down onto a folded sack, so gently that she slept on, and slid off the cart. A clump of dock grew by the trough. She picked the broadest leaf she could find, dipped it in the cold water and shook it. She went up behind Cora where she stood watching the reeds. "Hold still." She laid the wet leaf, cool and dripping, flat across the back of Cora's sunburnt neck, above her collar, and smoothed it down with her palm.

Cora went still. She went entirely still, the way she had gone still over the tea cup on the guild stairs, her hands still clasped behind her back, her eyes still on the reeds. Water ran from the leaf down under her collar. Elsie saw her shoulders tighten at the cold, then slowly, slowly let go. She did not turn round. She did not reach up to take the leaf off. She stood there by the trough in the sun and let it stay. After a moment Elsie saw her close her eyes, just for a breath, and open them again on the reeds.

Pennyfield was the biggest of the villages, with a stone bridge over a brook and a squat church tower. At the far end of its one street, under a painted sign of a red poppy, stood the Poppy inn. Carts were drawn up outside it in the shade of a chestnut tree, half a dozen of them, piled with hay, flour sacks and crates of hens. Their carters sat at long tables out on the cobbles with mugs in front of them. Their own carter pulled up among them and climbed down, stretching her back. "Noon. We eat." So they ate too.

They took an outside table under the chestnut. A girl from the inn brought them each a plate: a heel of brown bread, a wedge of crumbly white cheese, and a heap of dark cherries still on their stalks. It was three pips a plate. Elsie counted three copper pips out of her purse into the girl's palm for her own. Tamsin dug three out of her pocket, and Tilly, yawning, still with the poppy in her braid, found hers at the bottom of her apron among the corks. Cora had brought her own bread and cheese wrapped in a cloth. She unwrapped it neatly at the end of the bench.

Tilly ate her cherries very carefully, one at a time, laying each stone in a neat row on the edge of her plate. Halfway down the row, without looking up, in a voice that came out a little too high: "So what's it like? Tamsin's bunk?" She laid down another stone. "On a barge. I've not been on a barge. I just wondered what they're like. Bunks. On barges." She picked up another cherry and examined it closely. Cora, at the end of the bench, paused with her bread halfway to her mouth, then went on eating.

Tamsin leaned back on the bench with her long legs stretched out under the table and her hands behind her head. "Narrow." She grinned, crooked and slow, straight across the table at Elsie. "Very narrow."

Elsie laughed. "It is. We were squashed flat. Tamsin nearly fell out, and I had to pull her back in."

Tilly stabbed a cherry. She stabbed it with her fork, quite hard, right through, so that the juice spurted out red over her plate and onto her cuff. She looked at it in surprise, as if someone else had done it, and ate it.

Elsie looked round at them all: Tamsin stretched out in the sun, Tilly with cherry juice on her cuff and a poppy in her hair, Cora upright at the end of the bench with her lunch in its cloth and a damp patch on her collar where the leaf had been. A warm full feeling came up in her chest. "I wish we all lived under one roof. All of us. In one house, on a canal, with a kitchen, so we could have breakfast together every morning like on the barge." The table went quiet. Tamsin's grin stopped. Tilly stopped chewing. Cora set down her bread.

After a moment Cora spoke, looking at her bread. "That is not how the guild works. A party is a party for the length of a notice. Then it's a name on a slate again." She brushed a crumb off the cloth. "It isn't a household." She opened her ledger on the table beside her plate, uncapped her pen and wrote a line, in her small hand. Elsie watched her write it and could not see from across the table what it said.

Elsie leaned over the cherry stones. "What are you writing?" Cora finished the line. She capped the pen, blotted the page with the corner of her cloth and looked up at Elsie with her cool grey eyes, perfectly level. "Skills used: none." She shut the ledger. Elsie laughed. Tamsin snorted into her mug. Tilly laughed a beat behind them, with her hand over her mouth full of cherry, and pointed at the ledger. "The cellar!" Cora picked up her bread again. The corner of her unsmiling mouth moved, very slightly, and was still.

Past Pennyfield the road went down into a long hollow where a slow stream fed the Mere. The reeds grew thick there, thicker than anywhere yet, right up to the edge of the road on the left hand and in places across it, so that the cart's wheels crushed them. They stood far over Tamsin's head. The road was in green shade between them. The air was hot and still, and it smelled of mud. The carter sat up straight on her seat and gathered the reins short. Her voice dropped. "Here's the bad stretch. Here's where they live. Look sharp."

They came all at once. The reeds on the left hand heaved and rustled from end to end. Out of them boiled a cloud of sprites, dozens upon dozens, green wings buzzing like a swarm of bees. The air was full of giggling, high and shrill and delighted, coming from everywhere. They mobbed the cart. They poured over the buckets, pulled at the roses and tugged at the poppies, two and three to a flower. They swarmed round the donkey's hat. They went for Tamsin's knuckle wraps, Tilly's brass goggles and the shiny buttons on Cora's coat. The carter swore and flapped her hat at them.

Elsie flapped too, laughing, with both hands, at the sprites going for the roses. She felt a tiny sharp tug at her scalp, just above her ear, and a whir of wings by her cheek. She put her hand up. Her clip was gone. She turned and saw it, already a yard away and rising: her pink-and-white clip, held in the arms of a single sprite and flashing in the sun as it went. The sprite was looking back at her over the top of it with its tiny face split by a grin. It dived. It dropped like a stone into the reeds, and they closed over it.

Elsie's hand stayed in her hair where the clip had been. She felt the place, the little gap in the hair above her ear, with her fingertips, as if the clip might still be there and she had only missed it. It was not. Her eyes filled. They filled all at once and brimmed over and ran. She stood in the road among the giggling and the buzzing with her hand pressed flat to the side of her head, and could not stop them. It was only a clip. She could not have said why it mattered so much, only that it did, and that the place above her ear felt cold and bare and wrong without it.

Tamsin had seen. Elsie did not see her see it; she only heard a sound behind her, a kind of growl. She turned, and Tamsin was already gone off the road. She went into the reeds at a run, straight in where the sprite had gone, crashing through them with her arms up before her face. The reeds swallowed her to the shoulders. She was in up to her waist in black water and mud and still going, shoving the reeds aside with both arms, shouting something at the sprites that Elsie could not catch, before anyone could so much as say her name.

"Reyes!" Cora took a step to the reed edge and looked down at the black mud where Tamsin had gone in, sucking and bubbling round the broken stems. "Reed beds swallow boots. And then the legs in them." She looked down at her own black boots, and at the long skirts of her charcoal coat, buttoned to the throat. She stepped off the road into the reeds, coat and all. The mud took her to the shin at once. She waded after Tamsin with her ledger held high over her head in one hand.

Elsie wiped her face with the back of her wrist and went after them. Behind her Tilly squeaked, "Oh, oh no, oh, all right," and came too. The mud was warm. That was the surprising thing: it was warm as bathwater, thick and soft and black. It came up over Elsie's boots and over her knees at the first step and held on, so that every step was a pull and a slow sucking let-go. The reeds closed over their heads, green and dim, and buzzed with sprites. Tilly held on to the back of Elsie's belt and waded. "Sorry. Sorry. Sorry." That was to the mud.

They found it in a little clearing in the reeds where the water opened out, black and still, with the sky reflected in it. The sprite sat on the very top of a bending reed head, swaying, with Elsie's clip clutched to its chest in both arms. Three other sprites hovered round it in the air, close, admiring. One touched the pink enamel flower with a tiny finger. Another stroked the white. The first one turned the clip a little in the sun to show them how it shone. They all made soft cooing sounds over it, like doves. Tamsin stood a few yards off, mud to the ribs, panting.

Elsie waded forward until she was close. She crouched down in the mud, slowly, so as not to frighten it, until the warm black water was at her waist and her face was level with the reed head. The sprite watched her with bright black eyes like seeds. "Hello. Excuse me. I'm so sorry to bother you. That's my clip, you see. It's very pretty, I know. I like it too." She held out her muddy hand, flat, palm up, under the reed. "Please may I have it back? I'd be ever so grateful. I'll find you something else bright."

The sprite giggled. It looked at her open hand, and at her wet face, and giggled, a tiny silvery sound. It shook its head hard from side to side so that its pointed ears flapped. It hugged the clip. It wrapped both arms and both legs tight round it and pressed its cheek to the enamel flower. It shut its eyes in bliss and rocked on the swaying reed head with it as if it were a baby. The three others giggled with it. One of them patted the clip, possessively. Elsie's face crumpled. She let her hand fall into the mud.

"Wait. Wait, wait, I've got— hang on." Tilly let go of Elsie's belt and stood up straight in the mud, swaying. She pulled two vials from her belt, one clear and one a pale cloudy yellow. Her hands were shaking. She pulled the corks with her teeth and spat them into the reeds. She tipped the yellow into the clear, a little, and a little more, and swirled it, frowning hard. Then it caught. A small steady blue flame stood up out of the neck of the vial, clean and bright and still, burning without a wick. Tilly's hands went steady round it.

The sprites went silent. All four of them, and all round the clearing in the reeds the giggling and buzzing stopped too, at once, as if a door had shut on it. The sprite on the reed head opened its eyes. It stared at the blue flame. They all stared, their tiny faces turned toward it and lit blue from underneath, their wings slowing and folding, their mouths open. Elsie saw it shine in every pair of seed-black eyes, a little blue spark, over and over, all round the clearing, dozens of them, rapt and still, as if they could not look at anything else.

Tilly waded a step closer, holding the flame up, slow. She bent and set the vial down on a thick knot of reed root that stood up out of the water. She settled it there carefully so it would not tip, and stepped back. The flame burned on, small and blue and steady, reflected in the black water underneath so that there seemed to be two. The sprite on the reed head looked at it. It looked down at the clip in its arms, pink and white. It looked at the flame. Then it let go of the clip entirely, and flew straight down to the blue flame.

The clip dropped. It fell turning through the air, flashing. Elsie lunged and caught it an inch above the black water with both hands, and held it to her chest. She knelt there in the mud a moment with her eyes shut, holding it. She scrambled up, dripping to the waist, turned round and threw her arms round Tilly's neck. She kissed her on the cheek, hard and loud, smack, mud and all, so that a black smudge was left on Tilly's cheek in the shape of Elsie's mouth. "Tilly. Tilly, you're a wonder. You're a wonder."

Tilly did not move. Elsie let go of her and turned away, laughing and wiping her eyes with the back of her wrist. But Tilly stayed exactly where she was, up to her knees in the warm mud among the reeds, with one hand lifted and pressed flat against her own cheek, over the place. Her blue eyes were enormous. Her mouth was a little open. She stood and stared at the reeds, holding her cheek. When Elsie started back the way they had come and called her name, she did not follow. She seemed to have forgotten how, as if her legs belonged to somebody else.

Tamsin came wading past her. She looked at Tilly, standing there like a post in the reeds with her hand on her cheek, and rolled her eyes. Without stopping she reached out, took her by the back of her collar and towed her. Tilly came along behind her like a boat on a rope, stumbling and squelching through the mud, her boots coming out of it with loud sucking pops, her free arm flapping for balance. Her other hand stayed on her cheek the whole way back. Tamsin looked down at her once over her shoulder. Her mouth went crooked and odd, and she looked away.

On the solid ground of the road Elsie stopped and pinned the clip back into her hair. Her fingers were black with mud and shaking a little. She pushed it in above her ear, where it went, by feel. It went in crooked and caught a lock of hair wrong. Cora came up out of the reeds behind her, mud to the knee, coat black to the hem, the ledger still held high and clean. She looked at Elsie's clip. She reached over without a word and took it between her finger and thumb. She slid it out and set it in again, straight.

Cora's fingers did not leave at once. They stayed a moment at Elsie's temple, by the clip, very light, two fingertips resting against the side of her head just where the hair met the skin; and Elsie, standing still for her, could feel that they were cool, and that they were not quite steady. Cora was looking at the clip, not at Elsie's face, with her grey eyes very intent, as if she were checking her work. Then she seemed to notice where her hand was. She took it back and stepped back with it, a full step, to her place behind Elsie. She clasped her hands behind her coat.

The carter was sitting on the cart's seat where they had left her, with her hat back on and a heap of sprite-pulled petals in her lap. The sprites had gone quiet in the reeds behind her. She watched the four of them come squelching up out of the reed bed one after another onto the road: Tamsin black to the ribs, Tilly black to the thighs and holding her cheek, Elsie black to the waist with her clip on straight, and the Warden black to the knee with her ledger in her hand. The carter put back her head and laughed and laughed until she had to hold on to the seat.

Reedby, at the far end of the round, sat on a spit of land between the reeds and the open water. It grew a little of everything. The growers there had the last buckets waiting at the roadside: lavender in tight purple bundles, cornflowers bluer than the sky, and sweet peas in every soft colour, pink, mauve, white and the dark red of wine. They smelled so sweet that Elsie put her face in a bucket of them the way Tilly had done at Sweetwater, and breathed. They loaded them on top of everything else, wherever they would fit, until the cart was heaped up high.

The growers looked at the mud on them and laughed, but kindly. They took them round to the horse trough in the yard behind the last cottage and let them wash. Elsie scrubbed her arms, her knees and as much of her legs as she could reach, and the water went black. Tilly, still pink, scrubbed her cheek very lightly round the mud smudge without quite washing it off. Cora washed her boots and wrung out the hem of her coat. Tamsin took one look at the trough, bent over it and plunged her whole head in, curls and all, right up to the shoulders.

She came up streaming, gasping, laughing, with her copper curls flattened dark to her head and water pouring off her chin and down her front. Tilly was standing right beside her, wringing out a cuff. Tamsin looked at her sideways and grinned. She shook her head like a dog, hard, so that her curls whipped round and flung water out in a great glittering spray all over Tilly. Tilly shrieked. She leapt back, dripping, with her hands up and her goggles fogged. Soon she was laughing, laughing so hard she had to sit down on the edge of the trough. Tamsin laughed too, and splashed her again.

They turned the cart for home in the late afternoon. It was so heaped with flowers now that the buckets stood two deep and the donkey had to lean into the collar on every rise. The scent came off the load in waves, roses, lavender and sweet peas, so thick and sweet that the donkey walked along the shore road in a cloud of it, with bees following. Elsie's legs were stiff with drying mud. Her halter was damp. Her clip was in her hair. The sun stood well over to the west above the Mere, and the water glittered. She was very happy.

Cora walked beside her. Elsie had been thinking for some time, since the trough.

"Cora? What do you like? Besides tea."

Cora looked at her. "Like."

"Yes. Things you like. Black tea, strong, one sugar, I know that one. What else?"

Cora turned her face back to the road. She walked for a long while without answering, past the end of one lavender field and the start of another, so long that Elsie thought she had decided not to. At last, very low: "Quiet."

Elsie nodded. She did not ask anything more. She walked on beside Cora in the afternoon sun and kept her mouth closed, not humming, not talking, all the way along the next field, a long one, poppies. The cart squeaked ahead of them, the bees went by, and Tamsin and Tilly's voices were far up at the front. She let the quiet be Cora's. It was harder than she had expected. Halfway along the field Cora looked at her, a short sideways look, and away. Near the end of it she looked again. That time her look stayed a moment longer before it went back to the road.

Tilly had climbed up onto the tail of the cart, where Elsie had sat in the morning. She was riding there among the buckets of sweet peas with her back against them and her legs hanging down over the edge. Her boots were caked grey with dried mud to the ankle. She swung them, one and then the other, kicking at the dust of the road as it went by beneath her. The poppy had gone limp in her braid. She was humming something tuneless. She still had the faint grey smudge on her cheek. As Elsie came up alongside she saw Tilly touch it.

Elsie hopped up onto the tail of the cart beside her. The cart dipped and swayed, and Tilly grabbed a bucket and laughed. They sat side by side swinging their legs. After a while Tilly touched the smudge on her cheek with two fingers, looking down at her boots. "Elsie. Back there. In the reeds." She rubbed the smudge with her thumb. "Do you kiss everyone? Like that? When they get your things back? Is it a thing you do?" She laughed, a small embarrassed laugh, and swung her boots harder. "I only wondered. I didn't know where to put my feet, after."

"Oh, Tilly." Elsie thought about it for a moment, swinging her legs. "Only people I love." She looked at Tilly's cheek, the grey smudge on the one side and the clean freckled other. "And I only did the one side, didn't I? That's not fair on the other." Before Tilly could say anything she leaned over and kissed her on the clean cheek, soft and quick, with a little smack. She sat back. "There. Now they match." She smiled at Tilly, pleased with herself, and swung her legs.

Tilly went red. She went red the way a kettle comes to the boil, slowly and then all at once, from her collar up her throat, over both cheeks, the smudge and her ears, right up into her hairline under the goggles, until she was as red as the limp poppy in her braid, redder. She put both hands over her face. The carter glanced back over her shoulder from the seat and frowned at her. "You all right back there, girl? You've gone scarlet. Had too much sun, I shouldn't wonder. Get your hat on." Tilly made a muffled noise into her hands.

They came into the city at dusk. The lake had gone violet behind them. As the cart rolled in over the first bridge, the lamplighters' boats were setting out along the canals: low narrow boats with a man or a woman standing in the stern of each with a long pole and a flame at the end of it, poling from lantern to lantern. One by one the paper lanterns along the towpaths caught and glowed, red and gold and white. Their light ran out across the dark water and shook there. The donkey's ears pricked. He knew where he was. He picked up his feet for home.

At the old flower market the stall-keepers were waiting with their lamps lit. The carter drew up, and they all set to unloading. They handed the buckets down from the cart into the stalls, roses, poppies, lavender and sweet peas, until the market was a field of flowers under the lanterns and the cart stood light and empty. The carter climbed down and stood in front of the three of them, Elsie, Tamsin and Tilly. She dug in her smock and counted out five silver lanterns, one at a time, into Elsie's cupped hands. "There. Worth every pip. Good round, that. Fewest roses lost since spring."

They shared it out on the edge of a stall under a lantern. Elsie gave Tamsin one silver lantern and Tilly one, and kept one. She took the other two to the flower-seller at the next stall, who changed them for twenty copper pips out of her apron. Elsie counted six pips into Tamsin's palm, six into her own and six into Tilly's. Two copper pips were left in her hand. She looked at them, then at Tilly, and folded Tilly's fingers over them. "Those are yours. For the blue flame." Tilly looked down at the two pips in her fist, then at Elsie, and swallowed.

Cora walked them back to the guild hall, through the lamplit squares, with her coat stiff with dried mud to the knee. The clerk was just shutting the great doors as they came up. Cora stopped him with a hand and went in. Through the gap Elsie saw her at the desk under the last lamp, opening the ledger and writing the notice in, the date, the pay and the names, in her small exact hand, blotting it and shutting it. She came out again and stood on the step. The doors closed behind her with a boom. "I'm off duty. Goodnight, Fairweather."

"Wait. Wait, Cora, I saved you something." Elsie felt in her satchel and brought out a sprig of lavender, a long one. She had taken it from the very last bucket at Reedby and kept it safe all the way home between the pages of her notebook, so that it had come out flat, only a little crushed and still smelling sweet. She held it out on her palm. Cora looked at it. She took it, carefully, by the stem, and turned it once in her fingers. She slid it into the buttonhole of her charcoal coat, at the throat. She inclined her head, turned and walked away across the square.

Elsie watched the lavender go off across the square on the dark coat, a small spot of colour, until Cora turned the corner. Beside her Tamsin cleared her throat. "I'm due on the Marigold. For supper. I promised." She shifted from foot to foot and rubbed the back of her neck. "You heard me promise. Twice." She looked at Elsie sideways, with her wet-dried curls springing everywhere. "Come with me? Mum'd be glad. Nell would. There'll be stew again, probably. Mum does stew when she's glad." She grinned, but it was a hopeful grin, not a sure one. "Will you come?"

Elsie wanted to. She wanted to so much that she felt it in her chest: the low cabin, the stove, Rosa's laugh, the squeezebox, Tamsin's narrow bunk. She thought of Nell up on the cabin roof among the geraniums, with her scarred hand, saying I write her every week. She shook her head. "Not tonight. Tonight's Nell's. She's waited a year. You should go on your own, and sit with her, and let her have you all to herself, every bit." She took Tamsin's hand and squeezed it. "Go on. And tell your mum I'll save her a big slice."

Tamsin looked at her. Something came down the Tether, warm and sudden and so strong that Elsie caught her breath. Tamsin stepped forward and hugged her, hard, both arms right round her and her face pressed into Elsie's hair beside the clip, so hard that Elsie's feet came off the step. She held on a long moment. She did not say anything. She let go, stepped back, turned and ran: off across the square in the lantern light toward the Long Cut, her boots loud on the stones, her curls bouncing, her axe on her back. She did not look back once.

Tilly walked Elsie home. They went along Thimble Canal under the lanterns. Somewhere along the way, Elsie could not have said when, their hands had found each other. Tilly swung them, their joined hands, forward and back between them as they walked, high, like children, and Elsie laughed and let her. "And the sneezing, did you hear them, dozens! And the blue flame, and their faces, Elsie, all of them gone still at once. What do you think a sprite's made of? Mostly wing? I'd love to know. I'd give anything to know." Every time she ran out of breath she swung their hands higher, and Elsie swung them back.

At the Honeycomb's door, under its painted sign, Tilly stopped. She let go of Elsie's hand and stood on the step. "Goodnight. Goodnight, Elsie." She went down one step backwards and stopped. She looked up at Elsie under the lamp, with her goggles crooked, the limp poppy still in her braid and her cheek still faintly grey. "Goodnight." She laughed at herself and went pink. She turned and went off along the canal at a half run, her braids bouncing. Elsie stood in the doorway and watched her until she had gone round the bend.

Up in the attic Elsie lit the candle. She pulled off her boots, which were stiff as wood with dried mud, and stood them by the door. She poured the chipped jug into the basin. Sitting on the stool with one foot in it at a time, she scrubbed. The reed mud had dried on her legs in a grey crust from her knees down, cracked like an old plate. Under it her skin was pink and tender from the sun. She scrubbed with the cloth until the water in the basin was black. She threw it out of the round window into the canal, filled it again and scrubbed some more.

When she was clean and in her shift, she went to the crate under the round window and watered the plants, one at a time, as she did every night: Mrs Pennywhistle the fern first, then Sunny, then Doris, who wanted very little, then Captain. She crouched by the mint with the jug. "There are sprites in the reeds by the lake," she told him in a low voice, "about as long as your tallest leaf, with wings like dragonflies, and they steal anything bright. One stole my clip. Tilly got it back with a blue flame." She thought. "You'd have liked them. They giggle." Captain smelled of mint.

She sat down at the rocking little table, got out her notebook and opened it at the page of names. The lavender sprig had left a faint purple print on the paper where it had lain all the way home. She dipped her pen. Under Tilly, she wrote: blue flame, and then, after a moment, cheek. She looked at that, smiled, and left it. She went down the page to Cora, where it said black, strong, one sugar. Under that, in her round careful hand, she wrote: quiet. She sat and looked at the word for a while, and blew on the ink.

She blew out the candle and lay down on the narrow bed. Through the round window the canal lanterns made gold shapes on the sloped ceiling. They shook when a boat went by. Elsie lay on her back with her hands folded on her stomach and felt for the thread. It was there, as it was each night now, running out from under her breastbone across the dark city to the Long Cut; and from the far end of it, faint with distance but plain, came Tamsin: loud, and warm, and laughing, laughing so hard Elsie could feel it shake. Elsie laughed too, out loud, alone in the attic, without knowing why.

There were footsteps on the narrow stairs: slow, heavy, careful ones, coming up from the bakery. Elsie sat up. There was a knock, one, and the door opened. Agnes came in with a candle in one hand and a plate in the other: on the plate, on a square of brown paper, a thick slice of honey cake, golden, with the dip in the middle. Agnes set it down on the little table beside the notebook. She looked at the muddy boots by the door, then at Elsie sitting up in bed, and nodded once. She went out. The door closed, and her footsteps went slowly down.
