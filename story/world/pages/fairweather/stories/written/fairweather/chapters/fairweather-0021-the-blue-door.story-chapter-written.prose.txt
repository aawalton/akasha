Elsie woke at half past four. She knew the hour by the smell before she opened her eyes: the first bread was in downstairs, and the warm of it came up through the boards. She was lying on top of the blanket in her birch crown and her mother's shawl, just as she had lain down, and a leaf of the crown had got into her ear. On the sill, in the grey light of the round window, Moonpenny was still open.

She had half thought the flower would be shut again by morning. It sat wide on its stem, white as milk, its petals curled at the edges, and the whole attic still smelled faintly of honey and rain.

"Good morning, Moonpenny." Elsie knelt up on the bed and leaned her arms on the sill, close enough to smell the flower. "You stayed open. Thank you. I thought you might be tired after last night." She worked it out on the boards above her head. Yesterday had been Midsummer, and Midsummer was the twentieth. "So it's the twenty-first." She said the rest more quietly, to hear how it sounded out loud in the attic. "Today's the house."

She said it again, to be sure it was still true. "Today's the house." She held up one hand and counted it on her fingers, the way Tamsin did when she was planning, folding one finger down for each thing. "Pay at dawn, at the Steps." Thumb. "Coin Street at eight." First finger. "Four suns." Second finger. She looked at the two fingers still standing and couldn't think of anything to put on them, so she folded them down too, and held the fist against her chest.

She lifted the birch crown off her head. The leaves had gone soft in the night, and one of its blue ribbons had come untied. She laid it on the sill beside Moonpenny's pot, where the white flower leaned over it. The shawl she kept on, because the attic was cold before the sun. Her clip had come out on the pillow. She found it by feel and pinned it back above her ear.

The bee tin stood on the rocking little table with a cloth over it, where she had set it down at two in the morning. Elsie lifted the cloth and then the dented lid with its worn painted bee. Two honey cakes were left in the bottom from the boat, sitting in their own crumbs, a little squashed at the edges from the satchel. She looked at them a while, and at the cold grey window, and thought of Coin Street, and of a promise.

Downstairs the oven was lit and roaring, and the shop was hot already, though the street door was still barred and the blind still down. The racks were filling. Mrs Merriweather stood at the oven mouth with the long peel, sliding out the first loaves one after another, brown on top and floury underneath, and knocking each one on the bottom with her knuckle as it came. She didn't turn round. "You're up."

Elsie came and held out one of the honey cakes on her palm. "May I warm this on the ledge? Only the one. I promised it to the letting agent on Coin Street." She looked at it. "I promised it warm. And it's gone cold in the night."

Mrs Merriweather set down the peel. She took the cake off Elsie's palm and put it on the oven ledge herself, right at the back, where the brick was hottest. "Two minutes." She held up two floury fingers. "Not three."

"Not three," Elsie said, and stood by the ledge to watch it.

Mrs Merriweather went back to her loaves. "How was the boat?"

"Thieves." Elsie counted on her fingers again without meaning to. "And a bell. I rang it. And Moonpenny opened."

Mrs Merriweather grunted at the thieves, short and low, at the floor. At Moonpenny she stopped with a loaf on the peel. She looked round at Elsie and nodded. It was a slower thing than the grunt, and she held it a moment before she turned back to the oven.

At two minutes Elsie lifted the cake off the ledge. It was hot right through, and the honey crust had gone soft and shining again. She wrapped it in a clean cloth from the drawer, folded the corners in over it, and tucked it inside the shawl against her middle, under the knot. It sat there warm as a sleeping cat.

At ten to five the Lantern Steps were grey with mist. The Mere below them had gone white, all of it, as far as she could see, and the far shore had gone with it, and the festival barge, and the reeds. The paper lanterns strung along the top wall were out, every one, burnt down in the night to black wire, and the air smelled of wet stone and cold candle. Elsie pulled the shawl closer round the warm lump at her middle and went down.

The steps were strewn with burnt-out lanterns. They lay where folk had dropped them going home, red and gold and white, the paper gone limp and dark with the damp, the little painted fish on the white ones running in the wet. Gulls walked among them on their pink feet. They turned the lanterns over with their beaks and picked at the twists of nut paper for the sugar, and squabbled over it, and walked on, and the whole long flight of steps was quiet except for them.

Halfway down, alone on the grey stone, sat Ines in her black lace, with her knees together and her hands folded on them. Biscuit lay asleep on her feet, his small white bones curled tight round each other, his tail wrapped over his nose where his nose would have been. The mist had beaded on the lace of her sleeves like little seed pearls. Elsie went down and sat by her. The step was cold and wet through her skirt, and she tucked the shawl under her. Ines turned her head and looked at her, and her dark eyes were wide awake and clear, as if she had slept all night.

"This is my favourite hour," Ines whispered. "Before the sun." She looked out at the white water. "I went home after the fireworks. Then I came back for it."

They sat a while. A gull came close, looked at Biscuit, and went off sideways. Ines leaned until her mouth was near Elsie's ear. "Did the boat sink?" It was so low that Elsie barely heard it.

"No. Not even a little." Elsie shook her head. "A thief fell in, and he couldn't swim, and Noor stood up in the stern and threw him Tilly's vial. Underarm, like this." She drew the little arc in the air with her hand. "It landed right by his fingers. He held it round the middle."

Ines looked down at Biscuit on her feet and stroked the top of his skull with one finger. "He wouldn't come home last night. After you'd all gone." Her whisper went softer still. "He stayed in Noor's lap until she stood up. She sat there a long time." The finger went on stroking. "If she hadn't stood up, he'd have stayed till dawn."

Someone came down through the mist from the top of the steps, tall and straight, stepping between the dead lanterns without looking down at them. It was Cora, coat buttoned to the throat, not a hair out of her bun. She had her grey gloves on. The lavender was still at her throat, in the top buttonhole of the charcoal coat, a little crushed now from the night, its purple gone dark in the damp.

"Are your hands warm?" Elsie asked, before good morning.

Cora stopped on the step above them. She looked down at her hands. She flexed them in the grey wool, slowly, closed them and opened them. "They are."

Tamsin came next, straight down the middle of the steps, kicking a lantern out of her way so it went bouncing. Her birch crown was still on, crooked over one ear, its red ribbons limp. Her copper curls hung wet and dark with mist. There were shadows under her green eyes, and her jerkin was buckled wrong by one hole. She dropped onto the step on Elsie's other side, hard enough to jar it.

"Didn't sleep." She scrubbed her face with both hands. "Lay there all night looking at the ceiling. Rooms don't move."

Tilly came yawning, so wide that her goggles slid down her forehead and she had to push them up again. Her braids were done up anyhow, and one of them was coming loose. She had something gripped in one fist: a grey wool sock, a big one, darned at the heel in brown. It sagged at the toe like a full purse and clinked at every step she took. She held it up for them all to see. "My savings." She yawned again. "All of them."

Tamsin looked at the sock. "Why a sock?"

"My purse has a hole in it." Tilly sat down on Ines's other side and settled the sock in her lap with both hands round the lump. "The sock hasn't." She looked at the darn at the heel. "Yet."

Noor came last. She came along the waterfront out of the mist in her long green coat, with her hands in her pockets, and the mist seemed to let her through and close behind her. The few early folk on the waterfront moved to the wall as she passed. She came down the steps between the dead lanterns and sat one step above them all, a little apart, with her knees drawn up under the coat.

Biscuit woke. He lifted his skull off Ines's feet, stretched each little bone leg in turn, and climbed down off her boots. He crossed the step without hurrying, climbed up the next one, and sat down on Noor's boots instead, square on the toes. Noor looked down at him. Her feet stayed exactly where they were.

At five a bell struck somewhere over the roofs. Odile Marsh came up the steps from her boat with a small iron strongbox under one arm and her slate under the other. Her grey hair was beaded with mist. She walked as briskly as she had the evening before, though she had been out on the water all night.

She set the strongbox on a bollard at the bottom step, unlocked it with a key from round her neck, and paid the guard boats one by one, calling their numbers in the voice that had carried over the whole Mere. "One." A man came down out of the mist and held out his hands, and she counted coins into them. "Two." A pair of old women in matching hats. "Three." The guards came down and went away again with their fists shut tight. She went on through the numbers. Eleven was last.

"Eleven."

They all went down together, Tamsin and Tilly, Cora, Noor at the back. Elsie was in front. Odile looked at her and nodded at her hands, so Elsie cupped them. Odile counted the coins in one at a time, saying the numbers under her breath: twelve silver lanterns. They were cold and wet from the mist, and they clinked against each other as they landed, each with a little lantern stamped on its face. Every one was heavier than Elsie expected. By the twelfth her hands had sunk an inch.

Odile shut the strongbox. "The thief who couldn't swim is in the watch house." She tucked her chalk behind her ear. "He talked all night. He's named the other three." She tapped her slate. "I'll send the guild a chit. Eleven did well." She looked along the five of them. "I want eleven again next year."

Then she looked past them at Noor, still standing on the step above, with Biscuit sitting by her boots. "At the back again," Odile said.

Noor looked back at her. "Maybe."

Odile Marsh grunted, tucked the strongbox under her arm, and went down the steps to her boat.

Elsie held the twelve lanterns out on her two palms, so that everyone could see them, cold and shining in the grey. Tamsin bent over them at once and began to divide shares on her fingers, one hand counting the other, her lips moving and her forehead creased. "Five into twelve." She frowned. "Doesn't go." She started again, folding her fingers down. "Two each, that's ten, two over, and two over into five is..." She stopped, and tried it a third way, with her eyes shut. "Still doesn't go."

"A Warden is paid by the guild," Cora said. She said it out over the Mere, to the mist. "I take no share."

Elsie looked up at her. Cora was looking at the white water, very straight, with her grey hands folded in front of her coat.

"I said on the water," Noor said from the step above. "Mine goes to the house." She put her hands back in her pockets. "I'm not taking it back."

Tilly looked from the coins in Elsie's hands to Cora, and to Noor. "Then all twelve go to the house." She turned her sock upside down over the step and shook it. Her savings poured out on the wet stone in a heap, copper and silver together, rolling. One pip went off down the step on its edge, and Tamsin trapped it under her boot.

They piled everything together on the flat of the step. Elsie set down the twelve lanterns and emptied her own purse on top of them, shaking out the last pip from the corner. Tamsin turned out hers, a handful of coin and a bit of string and a button. Tilly's heap was underneath it all. It made a good big pile, copper and silver all mixed. It shone dully in the grey light, and the five of them looked down at it.

Tilly knelt by the pile and sorted it. She was fast. She pushed the pips into stacks of ten with one finger, click, click, click, and set the silver lanterns in rows beside them, and every stack came out the same height. Her lips moved the whole time, quick and silent, and her braids swung forward and she flicked them back without stopping. Tamsin crouched beside her and watched her hands go back and forth, back and forth, and her face went slowly blank, like someone watching a juggler.

Tilly sat back on her heels. "Four suns." She looked at the stacks. "And eight pips over." She bent over them and counted them all again, faster, touching each stack with a fingertip as she went, to be sure. She sat back again. "Eight over."

She looked up at Noor, and at Cora. "Without Noor's share," she said, all in a rush, "and Cora's, we'd have been forty pips short. Forty."

Tamsin whooped. She threw back her head and whooped at the sky. A gull went up off the step beside her in a clatter of wings, and the mist swirled after it.

Elsie went up a step behind Noor and hugged her round the neck from behind, shawl and all, with her cheek against Noor's black hair.

"The step," Noor said, into the shawl.

Elsie hugged Cora next. Cora had come down to the bottom step, and Elsie went and put both arms round her middle, round the damp charcoal coat. Cora went stiff all over, like a post. Then one grey glove came up and patted Elsie's back, once, between the shoulders.

They swept it all back into the sock, pips and lanterns together, and it went in with a long rattle and hung heavy. Tamsin knotted the top and shoved it down the front of her jerkin and did the buckle up over it. It made a lump. She looked about her at the steps and the gulls and the empty waterfront with her chin up and her hand on the lump.

"Let them try," she said. The gulls kept to their own end of the step.

Noor stood up. Biscuit slid off her boots and went back to Ines. "I'm going to bed."

"Come to Coin Street first," Elsie said. "Please. To see where your coin goes."

Noor looked at the lump in Tamsin's jerkin for a long moment.

"I'd like to see the door," she said. "Just the door."

Elsie turned to Ines, who had got up with Biscuit in her arms. "Will you come to supper tonight? At the blue door on Willow Canal." She smiled at her. "It'll be ours by then. I'm nearly sure."

Ines looked startled. Her dark eyes went very wide. Then she nodded, quickly, twice.

Ines went up the steps toward Ash Lane, small in her black lace among the dead lanterns, with Biscuit riding on her shoulder. At the top she looked back once, and the mist took her.

"Three hours till eight," Tamsin said. She patted the lump. "Breakfast."

"Eight pips," Tilly said.

Tamsin thought about it. "A loaf, then."

At the top of the steps a woman was setting out a stall, loaves in a basket under a cloth. Tamsin undid her jerkin, untied the sock, fished about in it and came up with one copper pip, and paid it over. She tied the sock again and shoved it back. "Seven over," Tilly said behind her.

They ate the loaf walking the canals as the mist lifted off the water, torn into five, still warm in the middle. Elsie walked between Tamsin and Tilly, bumping shoulders. Cora walked a little ahead, eating hers in small neat bites, and Noor came at the end of the row with her piece in her gloved hand, a step behind, keeping pace. The sun came through. The canals went from white to green, and the first shutters went back along the walks, and somebody threw a bucket of water down a step.

At a quarter past six they came along Willow Canal and stopped on the walk across the water from the blue door. All five of them stood there in a row and looked at it, and kept quiet.

The blue paint was peeling. It had come away in long curls round the hinges and along the bottom, where the wet got at it, and an older green showed underneath. The knocker was a brass fish with a ring in its mouth, gone green with age, and a little worn at the tail where hands had lifted it. Two windows went up above the door, one over the other. On the step a tabby cat lay asleep in the first of the sun, with its paws tucked under.

Noor looked at it a long time. She stood with her hands in her coat pockets and looked at the door, and up at the old brick above it, the two windows one over the other, and down at the door again. Her face didn't change.

"It's a door," she said at last. "It stays where it is."

"Yes," Elsie said.

"I'll have a room over the water," Tamsin said. She had said it every day since the eighth, and she said it now as if it had only just come to her. "Over the canal. So it's going by under me."

"The shed," Tilly said. "For the still. And the room beside Elsie's." She pointed up at the roof, as if she could see through the slates. "So we can knock goodnight through the wall."

Elsie looked up at Cora. Cora stood a little way along the walk with her hands clasped behind her back.

"I have not been asked," Cora said.

"You're being asked," Elsie said. "Now."

Cora looked across at the door. She looked at it a long moment, at the curling blue paint and the green fish and the sleeping cat. "I will consider it." One grey fingertip went up and touched the lavender at her throat.

On the step across the water the tabby cat woke. It stretched its front legs out long, and then its back legs, and got up and walked off along the walk with its tail high.

"Look at that," Tamsin said. "Even the cat's moving out for us."

At ten to eight Coin Street lay in shade, a narrow street of tall grey houses with brass plates by their doors, all polished. The sun hadn't got down into it yet, and it was cool. The letting agent's door was still shut, and his window had the blind down. The plate by his door read: H. Penney, Lettings. They stood in a bunch on the cobbles outside it. Tilly shifted from foot to foot. Tamsin kept one hand flat on the lump in her jerkin.

Elsie put her hand flat on her shawl, over the bundle. The cake was still warm. Nearly. She kept her palm pressed over it, to hold the warm in a little longer.

Four adventurers turned into the far end of the street, walking fast, two and two. They wore good mail that shone even in the shade, and long swords, and their boots rang on the cobbles all together. Even from this end Elsie could see the copper tags at their throats, and the C stamped on each one.

"That's them," Tamsin said, low. "The C-rank lot." She stood up straighter. "After Midsummer, they said." Her hand went to the lump under her jerkin. "It's after Midsummer."

Over the roofs the tower clock began to strike eight. Tilly's head came up. "That's Father's clock," she said. "He made the works." The second stroke came, and the third. Inside the agent's door a bolt drew back.

Tamsin pulled the door open and held it wide with her arm. Elsie went in first. The others crowded in behind her, Tilly and Tamsin and Cora, and Noor last, so that the little office was full all at once.

Mr Penney looked up from his desk. It was heaped with keys: keys on rings, keys with paper tags, big iron keys and little brass ones, keys in tin trays and keys loose among his papers. He had his waistcoat on, buttoned tight, and his gold watch chain across it. He looked at all five of them filling up his office, and blinked. Elsie went straight to the desk, untied the knot of her shawl, took out the cloth bundle and set it down in front of him among the keys.

"It's the honey cake," she said. "I promised."

Mr Penney looked at the bundle. He unfolded the cloth one corner at a time, carefully, as if there might be something in it that would spill. The honey cake sat in the middle, still shining, and a little steam came off it into the cool of the office. He looked at it with his hands flat on the desk on either side of it. He didn't pick it up. He looked at it the way Elsie had seen people look at a letter they had been waiting on for a long while, before they opened it.

Tamsin undid her jerkin, untied the sock, and set it on the desk beside the cake with a heavy clink. "Four suns." She cleared her throat. "In pips, mostly. Sorry."

The door went behind them, and the bell over it jangled. The C-rank leader came in, a tall woman with her hair cropped short and a fat leather purse in her hand, and her three filled the doorway behind her in their mail. "The blue door," she said, over their heads. "Willow Canal."

Mr Penney looked up at her and spread his hands. "First come, madam, I'm afraid. I did say." He nodded down at the sock on his desk.

The tall woman looked at the sock. Then she looked at Elsie. Her eyes went to Elsie's hair, the pink of it, and stayed there.

"The Enthraller's lot," she said.

Tamsin folded her arms over her jerkin. She stood up to her full height with her arms folded. "The Fairweather party."

The tall woman looked at Tamsin's arms. She shrugged. "Plenty of houses." She turned and went out, and her three went after her, and the door swung to. Tilly let out a breath like a kettle coming off the boil.

Mr Penney untied the sock and poured it out on the desk among the keys. It came out in a long rattle. He counted. He pushed the pips into heaps of ten with the end of his pencil, and the heaps into rows, slowly, with his lips moving, and now and then he went back and counted a heap again. It took a long time. Tilly watched every heap and her lips moved with his. Noor stood by the door with her back to the wall, very still, her gloved hands at her sides.

A ring of keys slid off the edge of the desk and hit the floor. Then the tray beside it tipped. Then the whole great heap of keys went over the edge after it, ring after ring, sliding and slithering, in one long jingling avalanche that went on and on and ended somewhere under his chair.

In the quiet after it, Noor said flatly, "That was me."

Mr Penney was already down on his knees on the floorboards, gathering. "Happens every day," he said from under the desk. "Every day of the week."

Elsie knelt down to help. She gathered keys in both hands from under the desk and the chair and heaped them back on the desk, and Tilly gathered the ones by the door. One key had slid further than the rest, all the way across the boards, and come to rest against the toe of Elsie's boot. It had a blue paper tag on a string.

Mr Penney climbed back into his chair and finished counting. He sat back. "Four suns," he said. "Exact." With his pencil he pushed seven pips back across the desk. Tilly scooped them up and dropped them in the sock, and pulled it tight.

He opened a big book and wrote in it, and read it out as his pen went. "Four suns received. A month's rent, and one month in hand." He blotted the line. "Two suns due on the first."

Tamsin's face fell. Elsie watched it go. "The first's ten days off." Tamsin looked at the sock in Tilly's hand, gone thin and light. "Two suns. In ten days."

"Notices," Elsie said.

Mr Penney held out his hand across the desk. Elsie looked at it, and then down at the key in her own hand, and gave him the one with the blue tag. He wrote on the tag in small letters. Then he took Elsie's hand, laid the key in her palm, and closed her fingers round it with both of his own.

Then he picked up the honey cake and bit it. He chewed. He looked at the cake in his hand. "It's warm," he said. He took another bite. "It's exactly as warm as you said it would be."

Out on Coin Street the sun had come round over the roofs at last. Elsie held the key up in it. The blue tag turned on its string, with Mr Penney's small writing on it, and the key shone. Tamsin caught her round the waist and lifted her clean off her feet and spun her, round and round in the middle of the street, until Elsie's boots flew out and she shrieked and Tilly shrieked with her.

"I am due at the hall," Cora said, when Tamsin had put her down. "I am late." She looked at the key in Elsie's fist. "I shall come this evening. To see the sills." She turned and went off up Coin Street toward Brightwater Square, fast and straight.

Noor stood with her hands in her pockets. "I've seen the door now. I'll sleep."

"Supper," Elsie said. "Tonight. Come to supper."

Noor looked at her. "Ask me tonight." She went off the other way, toward Wick Lane.

Elsie and Tamsin and Tilly ran. They ran all the way to Willow Canal, over two bridges and along the walks, dodging a man with a barrow, Tamsin in front, Elsie with the key held tight in her fist, and Tilly's sock jingling at every step. A woman on a step shouted after them to mind themselves.

At twenty to nine Elsie put the key in the blue door. Her hand was shaking a little. The key went in. It wouldn't turn. She tried it the other way, and back, and then it turned and the lock went over with a clunk, and the door stuck fast in its frame and didn't move. Tamsin put her shoulder to it and leaned. It opened with a long groan, like something waking up.

Inside was the kitchen, one long room running from the front of the house right through to the back. A long scrubbed table stood down the middle of it, its top gone pale and soft-grained with years of scrubbing. A black iron range sat in the chimney breast, with a brass rail along its front for drying cloths. Under the back window was a stone sink with a pump beside it. The air smelled of old soot and shut windows. Dust hung in the sunlight from the window, turning slowly, gold.

Six chairs stood round the table, and each was its own. One was painted green. One had arms. One had a rush seat with a hole coming through, and one was so low that whoever sat in it would have her chin on the table. Elsie went all the way round and counted them, touching the back of each one as she passed, the way she touched her plants in the morning. "One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six."

Tilly opened the back door. It stuck and then swung, and the garden came in: long grass up to her knees with red poppies standing in it, brambles heaped along a low brick wall at the bottom, and an old apple tree leaning over to one side, its trunk grey and its leaves bright. Bees were already busy in the brambles. Somewhere a blackbird was going. Tilly ran down through the long grass to the shed at the far end, braids flying, poppies going down in front of her, pulled its door open and looked in. She shrieked. "It has shelves!" Her voice came back up the garden. "Elsie! It has shelves! Three of them! And a bench!"

The back sills faced south. The sun was already on them, lying warm along the stone. Elsie put her hands flat on the kitchen sill, and she could see her plants there already, Captain and Sunny and Doris and Mrs Pennywhistle all in a row, with their leaves to the light.

Up the stairs was a parlour over the canal, with two tall windows and a green sofa. The sofa was long and old, its green worn pale on the arms, and it sagged in the middle, as though a great many people had sat in that one spot over a great many years. There was a hearth with nothing in it, and a rag rug, and the light off the canal moved on the ceiling in long wobbling lines.

Tamsin went straight past it to the window. She didn't look at the sofa or the hearth. She pushed up the sash, and it went with a squeal and a shower of old paint. The canal was right below, green and close, moving. She leaned out on her forearms on the sill. A punt went by underneath her, the pole going in and coming up, and the man poling it looked up at her and touched his cap. Tamsin stayed leaning there, looking down at the water going by under her, and didn't say anything. Elsie stood in the doorway and didn't either. She left her there and went on up.

Behind the parlour was one small bedroom with a bare bedstead in it, its ropes laced back and forth across the frame where a mattress ought to be. Its window looked out the back, over the garden and the top of the apple tree. At the top of the house were two small rooms under the slope of the roof, side by side, with one wall between them. The ceilings came down so low at the sides that Elsie could touch the boards without stretching. One room had a gable window, full of roofs and chimneys and sky, and pigeons along the gutter.

Tilly went into the other room. Elsie heard her stop. Then three knocks came through the wall, sharp and quick. Elsie, standing in the gable room, knocked back three.

"I'll have this one," Elsie called. "The gable."

"I'll have this one!" Tilly called back through the wall.

Tamsin's voice came up the stairs. "I'm having the one behind the parlour!"

Elsie went down. Tamsin was standing in the doorway of her little room, looking at the bare bedstead. "Where'd Cora go?" she asked, quieter. "If she comes."

"We'll find somewhere." Elsie looked through into the parlour at the long green sofa. "There's the sofa."

Tilly came down the stairs behind her and looked past them at the ropes. "There aren't any mattresses."

"We've slept on worse," Tamsin said.

"I haven't," Tilly said.

At half past nine they parted on the step to fetch their things: Tamsin off toward the docks for her bag, and Tilly running for Pickle Row.

Elsie walked back to Thimble Canal alone. She kept her hand in her pocket the whole way with the key in it, her fingers closed round it, and every bridge or so she squeezed it to be sure it was still there. It was. It went warm in her hand. Somewhere along Thimble Canal she caught herself humming.

Mrs Merriweather was at the counter, wrapping a loaf in paper for a woman with a basket. When the woman had gone, Elsie set the bee tin down in front of her, washed and dried and shining, with its lid on straight. "I've found a house," she said.

Then it all came out in a rush, without stopping: the Steps in the mist and the twelve lanterns, cold, and Tilly's sock, and the stacks of ten, and the C-rank lot in their good mail, and the keys going off the desk, and the blue door that stuck, and the six chairs, and the shed with shelves, and the tree. Mrs Merriweather listened with her arms folded over her apron. She didn't say a word until it was all out and Elsie had to stop for breath.

"I'll pay for the attic to the end of the week," Elsie said. "So you're not short."

"The week's paid."

Mrs Merriweather pushed the bee tin back across the counter to her. "Keep it." She tapped the dented lid with one finger. "For the honey cakes."

She took a loaf off the rack behind her, put it in the tin, and pressed the lid on. Then she grunted. It was a low grunt, lower than the honey one, lower than any Elsie had heard from her.

Elsie's eyes filled. She leaned right over the counter, flour and all, put her arms round Mrs Merriweather's neck, and hugged her hard. Mrs Merriweather smelled of bread and woodsmoke and the honey she had brushed on the cakes. She stood there and was hugged, stiff, with her floury hands held out to the sides. Then one hand came up and patted Elsie's back, twice.

"Oven's cool by four on Sundays," Mrs Merriweather said, when Elsie had let go. "If you want to bake."

"Yes," Elsie said. "Yes. Please."

"It's Willow Canal," Elsie told her. "The blue door. Mind the step." She wiped her eyes. "In case anyone asks for me."

In the attic Elsie packed the crate. She did it slowly. Captain went in first, because he was the steadiest, then Sunny with her little green lemons, then Doris, fat and prickly, then Mrs Pennywhistle with her fronds folded carefully in over herself. Elsie wedged them all in with the bits of sacking. Then she untied her mother's shawl from round her shoulders, folded it in four, and laid it over the top of them to keep them from tipping. Her shoulders felt bare without it, and cool.

Moonpenny couldn't go in the crate. Not open as she was. Elsie looked at the white flower on the sill. "I'll carry you," she told her. "In my arms. All the way there."

She stood and looked round the attic: the round window with the canal light moving in it, the narrow bed under the slope, the stool, the jug with its chip, the little mirror hung crooked on the slant of the wall. The boards with their splinters. She had come up those stairs alone, with the crate and the satchel, and lain awake the whole first night listening to the oven below. "Thank you," she told the room, out loud. "For having me."

At ten to eleven Tamsin's voice came up from the street. "Elsie!" Elsie knelt up at the round window. Tamsin stood on the walk below with her bag over one shoulder and Margery on her back, squinting up.

Tamsin came up the stairs, ducking, ducked again under the slope, and picked up the crate. Mrs Pennywhistle's fronds bobbed. She swung it up onto one shoulder and carried it down two flights like that, steady, ducking under every beam, one hand on the crate and one on the wall. Elsie came behind with Moonpenny held in both arms against her chest, watching her own feet.

At half past eleven Tilly was sitting on the step of the blue door with a carpetbag beside her and a crate full of copper pipe, bent and coiled and shining in the sun. "My landlady helped me carry the copper down," Tilly said. "She sang the whole way down the stairs." She patted the crate. "I think she was pleased to see the back of it."

Elsie put a plant on every sill. Captain went on the kitchen sill in the sun, by the sink. Sunny went on a back sill, where the lemon could have the most light, and Doris beside her, and Mrs Pennywhistle in the shade by the back door, because ferns liked it. Moonpenny went up to the parlour, on the sill over the canal. Elsie set her down in the middle of it and stood back, and the white flower sat there against the green water like a little moon.

Tilly carried her copper down the garden to the shed, an armful at a time, the pipes clanking against each other. Elsie watched her from the back door. On the last trip Tilly set the crate down inside, and stood among the empty shelves with the light coming in through the dusty little window, and laid her palm flat on the bench. She stood like that, quite still, with her hand on the old wood, for a long time. Elsie went back inside and left her there.

At noon Tamsin lit the range. She knelt in front of it with a twist of paper and some kindling from the yard, and blew on it. It caught. Then smoke came pouring out of the front of it, and out of every crack in the iron, and round the oven door and the hot plates, thick and grey and stinking, until the whole kitchen went grey and the window with it and Elsie couldn't see the table.

They ran out coughing into the garden and stood in the long grass with smoke coming out of the back door after them. Tamsin wiped her streaming eyes. "The flue."

Tilly coughed. "A sweep costs." She looked down at the sock in her hand, and weighed it. "Seven pips."

Tamsin took the yard broom from beside the back door and lashed it to a clothes pole with a bit of rope, tight, with her teeth on the knot. She went up through the house with it. A minute later Elsie, standing in the long grass, saw her climb out of the gable window onto the roof with the pole over her shoulder.

The broom went halfway down the chimney and stuck. Elsie heard it go in from the garden, a scraping. Tamsin pulled. It stayed. She pulled harder, with her boots braced on the slates, and swore at it, loud, a barge swear Elsie didn't know. It stayed stuck.

At a quarter to one they ate Mrs Merriweather's loaf in the long grass under the apple tree, torn in three, with nothing on it.

Tilly lay back in the grass. Her braids spread out round her head among the poppies, and a ladybird climbed onto one of them and she let it. "I've lived over Father's shop, and at the College, and on Pickle Row." She looked up into the branches, where the little green apples hung. "Not one of them had a tree."

Tamsin lay down on Elsie's other side, with her hands behind her head. Elsie was in the middle, in the poppies, on her back, looking up at the apple leaves moving against the blue. The sun came down through them in coins and went over her face, warm and then not, warm and then not. The grass smelled green and hot. Somewhere near her ear a bee was working the poppies.

In the grass Tilly's hand found Elsie's. Her fingers slid in between Elsie's fingers and held on, warm and a little sticky from the bread. Elsie held back and gave it a squeeze. On her other side Tamsin turned her head on the grass and saw. She looked at their two hands for a moment. Then she looked away, up at the sky, and kept looking at it.

"This is the best day," Elsie told the leaves. "Better than Midsummer, even." She thought about it, properly. "I'm sure."

She dozed off there in the sun, with Tilly's hand in hers and Tamsin's shoulder warm against her other side. The last thing she heard was the bee, and Tamsin breathing, slow, and the leaves.

At four she woke with an apple leaf on her cheek. Tilly was propped on one elbow over her, laughing. She picked the leaf off with two fingers and showed it to her.

The grass on Elsie's other side was pressed flat and empty, and still a little warm. From the house came a banging. Elsie sat up with poppy petals in her hair. Through the open back door she could see Tamsin in the kitchen, bent over the long table, scrubbing at it with a brush in long hard strokes, both hands on the brush, as if the table had done something to her.

So they scrubbed the house. Tilly took the stone sink, and pumped and scoured until the stone came up pale. Elsie did the sills, every one, upstairs and down, until her plants sat on clean stone and the water in her bucket was black. Tamsin did the floor on her knees with a bucket and a brush, all of it, from the back door to the front, and then the stairs, and didn't stop to rest.

At half past five Elsie sat at the scrubbed table with her ink and her paper and wrote to her mother. She wrote that they had a house. It had a blue door, she wrote, and a fish to knock with, and six chairs that didn't match, and a garden, and a tree. She wrote who would sleep in which room. At the bottom she wrote the new address. Then she wrote it again under that, bigger, so her mother couldn't miss it.

At six the fish knocker went, very softly, two little knocks. Ines stood on the step with Biscuit on her shoulder and a crock of honey held in both arms against her lace, the same kind of crock Elsie had carried up Ash Lane to her.

"A house should have honey," Ines whispered. She came in and set the crock down in the middle of the long table, and turned it a little until it sat just so.

Biscuit jumped down off her shoulder. He went across the clean floor to the range, which had gone cold since the smoke, sniffed at it, climbed in through the open oven door, turned round three times on the iron, and curled up in there as if he'd always had it.

At half past six Amara was on the step with a big covered basket over her arm, so heavy she had to lean the other way. Her braids were tied back with a gold ribbon. "Mrs Merriweather told me," she said. "I went in for honey cakes, and she said the blue door on Willow Canal." She smiled. "And to mind the step."

She set the basket on the table and lifted the cloth: a cold roast chicken, a raised pie with a crust shaped like leaves, a bowl of dark cherries, and half a cake with white icing gone a little soft at the edges. "From Mother's Midsummer table," Amara said. "There's always far too much. The cook was going to give it to the gulls." She looked round the long kitchen, at the sills, at the six chairs. "I thought a new house might be hungry."

Elsie hugged her. She went straight into it, the way she went into everything, with both arms round Amara's neck. Amara held on. She held on a long time, her arms tight round Elsie's back over the halter, and turned her face into Elsie's hair, and whispered into it, so close that Elsie felt the words warm against her scalp. "I sang."

"I know," Elsie said. "I wasn't listening." Her voice went up high. "I was washing a bowl. Very hard." Her eyes filled.

Amara laughed, and her eyes filled too. She stood back and wiped them with her fingers. "I looked for the pink. I looked and looked." She laughed again. "I found it when the bell rang."

"Mother cried," Amara said. "On the barge, after. When we'd done, and they were all cheering." She shook her head slowly, as if she still didn't quite believe it. "She held my face in her hands and cried. Twenty-two years, and I'd not once seen her cry."

Elsie wiped her own eyes with the back of her wrist, and laughed at herself. Amara was looking at her mouth. She looked at it while Elsie wiped, steadily, with her own eyes still wet, and then away, quick, at the six chairs round the table, and her hand went up to her braids.

At seven the knocker went again. Noor stood on the step with a black iron coffee pot in her gloved hand. "It's tonight," she said. "I was asked."

Tamsin came in from the garden, sooty from the roof, black down one side of her face and across both hands. "Range smokes," she said, before hello. She jerked her thumb at it. "Broom's stuck up the flue."

Noor went to the range to set down her pot. She stood beside it a moment with the pot in her hand, looking at the black iron.

Deep in the chimney something cracked, a sharp dry sound like a stick breaking. Then the soot came down. It came down in a great black roaring fall, a year of it, and the broom came down with it, pole and rope and all, and clattered into the hearth.

Tamsin had been crouched at the hearth, peering up. The soot billowed out over her. When it settled she was black from her curls to her belt, black eyebrows, black lips, and two green eyes blinked out of it.

"Things break near me," Noor said flatly.

Tamsin spat soot. "And unbreak."

Tilly knelt and lit the range. The flame caught and went straight up the clean flue with a long soft roar, like a breath drawn in, and the kitchen stayed clear.

At the stone sink Elsie worked the pump, wet a cloth under it, and scrubbed the soot off Tamsin's face. Tamsin had to bend down for it, with her hands braced on the edge of the sink. Elsie started at the forehead, under the curls. She went slowly, freckle by freckle, rinsing the cloth out black in the sink and wetting it again, until the gold of Tamsin's skin came up from under the black a little at a time. Every freckle she found she wiped round twice.

Tamsin held still with her eyes shut. Elsie washed her cheeks and her nose and down the side of her neck, and then round her mouth, carefully, at the corners and along the top lip and under the bottom one, with her other hand holding Tamsin's chin up to the lamp. Tamsin's mouth was soft under the cloth. Her breath stopped. Under Elsie's fingers her jaw went tight, and her ears went red, both of them, right through the soot still on them. Elsie kept washing. She wanted to get every bit.

Noor set her coffee pot on the range, at the back, where it would keep warm. She turned it so the handle faced out. She looked at it sitting there on the black iron. "So there's coffee," she said. "When I come."

At half past seven the fish knocker went three times, exactly, with the same space between each knock. "That's Cora," Elsie said, and ran for the door.

Cora stood on the step with a pot of lavender under one arm, a whole plant, grown tall and bushy and full of flower, in a clay pot. Elsie could smell it from the hall. Cora held it out to her in both grey hands. "It is for a sill."

Elsie carried it in and set it on the kitchen sill beside Captain. She stood back and looked at the two of them together in the last of the sun. "Ledger," she said. "Her name's Ledger." At the corner of Cora's mouth something twitched.

Cora looked round the kitchen. She looked at the plants on every sill and the scrubbed stone under them, at the crock of honey in the middle of the table, at Noor's black coffee pot on the range, and at Biscuit asleep in the oven with the door open. Her eyes stayed on Biscuit a while. Then they came to Elsie, at the table, and stayed there too.

Then supper: seven of them, and six chairs. Elsie counted, and counted again. She fetched her empty plant crate from the foot of the stairs, turned it up on its end at the corner of the table, and sat on it.

Tamsin was half out of her chair at once. "Have mine."

"Have mine," Tilly said, already up.

Amara half rose.

"I like the crate," Elsie said, and stayed on it, and swung her boots.

They ate Amara's basket, the cold chicken and the leaf-crust pie and the cherries and the soft white cake, with Ines's honey spooned over the cake and the bread. Noor went round the table with the coffee pot and poured for anyone who'd have it, and stood at each shoulder until the cup was full.

Cora drank Noor's coffee. She set the cup down. "It wants one sugar."

"It does not," Noor said.

Tamsin told the thieves with her arms, rowing at them across the table so that Tilly had to rescue the cherries. Tilly did the bang, with her hands flung up. In the oven Biscuit purred, a soft rattle from his ribs.

Elsie looked down the long table at them in the lamplight, all of them: Tamsin with soot still in her ears, Tilly with cherry juice on her chin, Cora upright with her cup, Noor with the pot, Ines small and quiet beside Amara, Amara laughing at something. Seven, counting her. Seven at a table that had six chairs. Her eyes filled right up. Tamsin passed her a napkin without looking round.

Cora opened her ledger on the table beside her plate, away from the honey. She uncapped her pen and wrote one line in her small, exact hand. Then she turned the book round on the table so that Elsie could read it without asking, and held it flat with two grey fingers. It read: Willow Canal. Supper. Skills used, none.

At ten Cora stood. She buttoned her coat to the throat over the lavender and tucked her ledger under her arm. "I shall walk Noor home to Wick Lane. It is on my way."

"It is," Noor said, and got up.

At the door Elsie asked her again, quietly, under the noise from the table. "The sofa. Will you? Instead of going home?"

"The Accord," Cora said, "is silent on sofas."

Then she looked at Elsie a moment. "Perhaps," she said. "Not tonight." She touched the lavender at her throat with one grey fingertip, and went out into the dark.

Noor stopped on the step. "Tomorrow, then." Elsie opened her mouth, and Noor said, "Not ask me tomorrow. Only tomorrow." She went off after Cora along the walk.

Amara and Ines went off together toward Lantern Square, Amara's empty basket on her arm and Biscuit riding on Ines's shoulder, his bones white in the lamplight. Amara waved from the bridge. Ines lifted one small hand.

The kitchen went quiet. There were the three of them, Tamsin and Tilly and Elsie, and the range ticking as it cooled, and the lamp, and the plates. Captain and Ledger stood side by side on the dark sill. The house smelled of lavender and coffee and soot.

Tilly went up first, yawning. Elsie heard her climb both flights, quick and light, and her door go, and then the bed ropes creak overhead as she lay down on them.

At twenty to eleven Elsie lay in the gable room on her blanket, spread over the bare bed ropes. They pressed little squares into her back through the wool. The room smelled of dust and warm slates. The gable window was full of stars, and the roofs below were black against them, and there was a chimney pot with a pigeon asleep on it.

Three knocks came through the wall by her head. Elsie knocked three back. Through the wall Tilly laughed.

Under her breastbone the thread to Tamsin was awake. It wasn't hot, the way it went hot when the red came. It was restless. It tapped and tapped, like fingers drumming on a table, and wouldn't settle, and Elsie lay on the ropes with her hand on her chest and felt it go.

At ten past eleven Elsie got up and went down in the dark in her stockinged feet, feeling for each stair with her toes. On the middle floor Tamsin's door stood open. The bare bedstead in there was empty, its ropes pale in the dark. A cool air came from the parlour, and the sound of water. Tamsin was in there, on the green sofa.

She was lying along it under the open window, on her back, with one arm folded under her head and her boots off, wide awake, listening to the canal. The water made small sounds against the wall below, lapping and settling. She didn't turn her head when Elsie came in. "Rooms still don't move," she said to the ceiling.

"The water does," Elsie said. She came and sat down on the sofa beside her. The middle of it gave under her at once, and sagged, and slid them both down into the dip together, Tamsin rolling against her, her hip against Elsie's hip.

Tamsin laughed, low, in her chest, where Elsie could feel it. She tried to shift over to make room, and the sofa slid her straight back into the middle. "It won't let us apart," she said. Elsie leaned on her, her head on Tamsin's shoulder. Tamsin's arm came round her, slowly, and stayed, heavy and warm, her hand on Elsie's bare arm, her thumb still.

Tilly came to the doorway with her blanket round her shoulders and her braids down, loose and crinkled from the plaiting, her feet bare. "The wall went quiet," she said.

She looked at the two of them on the sofa for a moment, in the light off the canal. Then she came in and climbed onto the other end of it, and drew her knees up, and tucked her cold feet in under Elsie's legs.

Over Elsie's head Tamsin and Tilly looked at each other. Elsie saw it, from under Tamsin's chin. Tilly lifted her chin. Tamsin looked at her a moment longer, and nodded, once.

Tilly spread her blanket over all three of them, shaking it out so it reached. The sofa sagged further and tipped them all in toward the middle, Tilly's feet under Elsie's legs and Tamsin's arm round her and the blanket over everything. All three of them stayed just where it had put them. Tamsin's breathing went slow against Elsie's ear. Tilly's feet warmed up.

Tamsin spoke into Elsie's hair, low, her breath warm on Elsie's scalp. "Mum's barge is in tomorrow." Her arm tightened a little. "She'll want to see the door."
