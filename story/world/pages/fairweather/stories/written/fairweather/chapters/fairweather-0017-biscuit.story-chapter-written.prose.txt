Elsie woke to grey light and the smell of flour. The loft was dim under its rafters, and the little window at the end had gone from black to pearl. Somewhere below, the wheel was creaking round. She lay on the lumpy sacks without moving and found out where all of her was. Her right hand was under the blanket, and there was another hand in it. It lay open in hers, palm up, long-fingered and warm now, the way it had turned over in the night. Cora's hand. Cora was still asleep.

Elsie looked at her. Cora's face was a hand's breadth away on the sacking, and it was different. All the straightness had gone out of it. Her brows weren't drawn. Her mouth, which was always shut like a ledger, had fallen a little open, soft, and her breath came out of it slow and even, warm on Elsie's chin. Her cheek was creased from the sack. One black strand had come loose from her bun in the night. It lay across her temple and down over her ear, a single dark line on her pale skin.

Elsie lay very still. She didn't want to so much as breathe hard. At the barrow Cora had sat up the whole night against the stone with her lantern, watching the wight's door, and Elsie had woken twice and found her grey eyes open both times. On the downs she'd been awake first. In the hay loft she'd been awake first. Here she was, though, with her mouth open and her hair coming down, sound asleep like anybody. Elsie felt she had been handed something to hold. She kept her hand exactly where it was and watched her sleep: the slow breath, the crease from the sack, the loose strand stirring a little each time Cora breathed out.

Behind her Tilly was wound round her back like a scarf. Her knees were tucked in behind Elsie's knees, her arm was over Elsie's waist. Her face was buried in Elsie's hair at the nape of her neck, breathing into it, slow and damp and warm, a little whistle at the end of each breath. Over by the ladder Tamsin lay face down on the sacks where she had dropped, one arm hanging off the edge and her boots still on, snoring her little kettle snore into the sacking. Elsie smiled into the half-dark.

Cora's eyes opened. They opened all at once, without any blinking or stirring first, and they were very close, grey as the window and still soft at the edges with sleep. They looked straight into Elsie's. For one breath Cora didn't move. She didn't look away. She lay there on the sacking with her lips apart and the loose strand across her cheek. She looked at Elsie, near enough that Elsie could see the black flecks in the grey, as if she had woken somewhere she didn't know yet and was finding out what it was.

Then her eyes went down, to the blanket, to where their two hands lay together under its edge, her fingers along the inside of Elsie's wrist. Elsie watched the red come. It started at her throat and went up her face like wine poured into water, right up to her hairline. Cora sat bolt upright. The rafter was lower than she was. Her head met it with a solid wooden knock that shook flour dust down onto all of them, and she made a small sound through her teeth and clapped a hand to the top of her head.

"Oh, Cora, your poor head." Elsie sat up too and reached for it, and Cora leaned away from her hand, red to the ears. Elsie let it drop. "Did you sleep? Properly, I mean."

Cora had her hand pressed flat to her crown. "Yes."

The grey smudges under her eyes had gone pale, almost gone. Her eyes were clear. Elsie clapped her hands. "You did! You actually did. It's true this time. I can see it is."

Cora didn't answer that. She found the loose strand with her fingers and began pinning it back into her bun. She did it without a mirror, the way she always did, but her fingers weren't as quick as they were at the Copper Baths. They fumbled the pin. It slid out. She caught it and tried again, and her hands weren't quite steady on it. She kept her eyes on the little window the whole time, on the grey light, on the rafter, at the sacks, anywhere but at Elsie, until the strand was gone and every hair was back where it belonged.

Elsie knelt up at the little window and rubbed a clear patch in the dust with her sleeve. The sky over the river was pink and gold, and mist lay on the water meadows in long white scarves. Below her the great wheel was going round. It turned slowly and steadily, the paddles coming up dark and dripping out of the race, pale with the dawn sky, pouring their water off in sheets and going down again. The race beneath it ran clean and fast. There was no weed in it at all. Elsie could see the stone bottom through the water.

Down on the bank, beside the race, there was a wall. It hadn't been there last night. It was made of stones, the round grey river stones they had spent all yesterday hauling out, and they had been stacked one on another into a low neat wall, each stone fitted to the next, as tidy as the courses of a cottage. It ran along the bank for the length of a cart. Beside it, coiled round and round like a rope on a quay, lay all the weed from the race in one great green heap.

The cottage door opened. Bess Hobb came out in her shawl with her grey hair down her back in a plait, yawning, floury already at the hands. She came round the end of the mill toward the wheel, the way she must have come every morning this month to see what the night had done. She stopped. Elsie saw her see it: the clear race, the turning wheel, the stacked wall of stones on the bank. Bess stood quite still in the wet grass. At last she put her floury hand over her mouth and kept it there.

They all came down the ladder, Tamsin last and yawning, and out into the cold morning. Bess hadn't moved. She was looking past the wall now, at the back step of the mill. Elsie looked too. The blue bowl sat on the stone where Bess had set it at dusk, but it had been turned upside down. It sat there on its rim, mouth to the step, the chipped place at its edge catching the light. Elsie bent and lifted it. Inside it was clean, licked clean right to the bottom, and dry.

"That's how Mam found it," Bess said, from behind her hand. "Every morning. Every single morning, upside down on the step, and she'd laugh. 'There,' she'd say. 'It's said its thank you.'" She took her hand away from her mouth. She was laughing. She was crying at the same time, both together, her square face crumpling and her shoulders going, tears running down through the flour on her cheeks in two pale lines. "Upside down. Oh, Mam. Oh, the daft old thing."

Elsie put the bowl down and went and hugged her. She put both arms round Bess's middle as far as they would go, which wasn't far. She held on, and got flour all down her front and in her hair. Bess stood stiff for a moment. At last one big hand came up and patted Elsie's back, hard, thump, thump, thump, the way you'd pat a calf you were pleased with, so hard it nearly knocked the breath out of her. Elsie laughed into the shawl and didn't let go, and Bess went on patting.

Breakfast was in the cottage at half past six, at the long scrubbed table, with the door open on the bean rows and the sun coming in across the floor. There was porridge from a great black pot. Bess came out of the dairy with a jug and poured cream over every bowl, thick yellow cream, not a drizzle of it but a whole jug, round and round until the porridge was swimming. "For once." She set the empty jug down with a bang. The younger daughter looked at the jug and at her mother and smiled down into her bowl.

Tamsin had three bowls. She ate the first as if she had been starved for a week and the second as if for a day, and took the third more slowly, with her elbow on the table, scraping the sides. Tilly had her grave book propped open against the porridge pot and read it over her spoon, her eyes going along the small print while the spoon found its own way to her mouth. Halfway through she dripped a great blob of creamy porridge right onto the woodcut of a wight. "Oh, sorry," she told it, and wiped it with her thumb.

When the bowls were empty Bess went to the dresser and came back with a scrap of brown sack paper and the stub of a pencil. She smoothed the paper flat on the table with the side of her hand. She wrote on it slowly, her tongue between her teeth, pressing hard, in big square letters like her own hands. Elsie read it upside down. Hobb Mill wheel is cleared and the job done. She signed it underneath, Bess Hobb, with a flourish on the B that went right off the edge of the scrap, and pushed it across to Elsie.

Next she went out to the mill and came back carrying a sack on her shoulder. She dropped it onto the end of the table with a thud that made the bowls jump. It was a full sack of flour, the mouth tied up with string, plump and soft and white at the seams. "That's for Agnes Merriweather," she said. "Ground this morning. Fine as you like, first grinding off the clear wheel." She patted the sack. "You tell her it's my sorry. For the grit, and the short weight, and the whole sorry month. Tell her Bess said so."

Tamsin got up. She didn't wait to be asked. She took hold of the sack by its neck and its bottom corner and swung it up onto her shoulder in one go, the way she'd swing a coal sack off a barge. She settled it there. Flour puffed out of the seams and powdered her copper curls white on that side. With her other hand she picked up Margery from where the axe leaned by the door. "Right. Ready when you are."

At the gate the younger daughter caught up with them. She came running down the path from the cottage with something in her hand wrapped in a cloth, and stopped in front of Elsie, out of breath and pink. She looked back once at the cottage, where her mother stood in the door. "Thank you," she whispered. She pushed the cloth into Elsie's hand. Inside it was a bun, round and brown and still so hot from the oven that Elsie could feel it through the cloth. The daughter ran back up the path before Elsie could say a word.

They set off north up the river road with the Wend beside them, running clear and quick and brown over its stones. The mist had gone off the meadows. The sun was already up over the willows and warm on their backs. Their shadows went in front of them on the white road, long and thin, four shadows and an axe and a sack. Tamsin walked in front with the flour. Tilly walked with her book under her arm for once, shut. Cora walked at Elsie's side with her ledger, very straight, and didn't look at her.

Elsie unwrapped the bun. It was still warm. She broke it in two, and it came apart unevenly, one piece bigger than the other, soft and steaming in the middle. She held out the bigger piece to Cora. Cora looked at it, and at the smaller piece in Elsie's other hand, and took the big one. She ate it walking, in small careful bites, looking straight ahead up the road. She ate all of it. At the end she brushed the crumbs off her coat into her palm and ate those too, every one, and then looked at the river.

Elsie got out the second glove. The cuff was done now, a good grey ring of ribbing, and she had started the hand above it on the road yesterday. She found her place and began. Plain, plain, plain. The needles clicked in time with her boots on the road. The grey grew in her hands, round and round, the hand of the glove getting longer than its cuff, until it was time to make room for the thumb. She counted under her breath, set the stitches aside on a spare bit of wool, and started the thumb.

Cora was watching the needles. Elsie could feel her watching, the way she had on the downs and yesterday, steadily, a long time, her eyes on the grey wool going round. A mile went by.

"Are they for cold hands?"

It was so low that Elsie barely heard it over the river, and Cora didn't look up from the needles. Her own hands were holding the ledger against her chest, both of them, one over the other, as if she were keeping them out of sight.

"They're a surprise." Elsie beamed at her and held the knitting up a moment to show her the thumb starting. "You can't know yet. That's how surprises work." Cora looked at the knitting. She looked down at her own hands on the ledger, bare and pale, the long fingers pressed flat to the black leather. She looked at them for a long moment. She didn't say anything. Elsie went back to her thumb, humming, and didn't see where Cora looked after that.

Up ahead Tilly had caught up with Tamsin and was walking beside her, peering round the sack. "Tamsin, does a boggart count as a grave thing? Do you think? Because it's sort of a spirit. A house spirit. And spirits are halfway to grave things, aren't they, so if I put it in the back of the book—"

"Doesn't count. Not a boggart, not a wight, not any of it, not if it drinks cream." Tamsin laughed so hard the sack slipped, and flour came out of the seam in a puff and went all over Tilly.

They came in by the south gate at half past nine, past the gate-ward yawning in his box. Inside, the city closed round them, warm and busy and loud. The streets smelled of bread from every bakery they passed and of canal water underneath, green and old. Carts rattled over the bridges. A woman was hanging sheets out of a top window across the lane to the window opposite. Elsie breathed in the bread smell and the canal smell together. After the mill, and the downs, and the loft, it felt like coming home.

The Honeycomb's bell jangled when Tamsin shouldered the door open. Mrs Merriweather was behind the counter, cutting a loaf. Tamsin walked straight up to her and swung the sack off her shoulder onto the counter, and it landed with a soft heavy whump that blew flour across the loaves. Mrs Merriweather put down her knife. She looked at the sack. She looked at it for a while, at its fat white sides and its string. At last she lifted her eyes from the sack to Elsie, standing behind Tamsin with flour still in her hair.

"The race is clear," Elsie said. "The wheel's turning. It was a boggart, and it wasn't spite, Mrs Merriweather, it missed the cream. Bess's mother used to leave it a bowl every night, and after she died it stopped, and the boggart didn't know why. So Bess put the blue bowl out on the step last night, full. And this morning the stones were all stacked up on the bank in a wall, and the bowl was licked clean and turned upside down. That's how it says thank you." She put her hand on the sack. "And that's from Bess. She says it's her sorry. For the grit and the short weight, and all the month."

Mrs Merriweather didn't answer. She untied the string at the mouth of the sack and folded the top back. The flour inside was white as milk. She put in her finger and thumb and took a pinch of it. She rubbed it between them, slowly, holding it up near her ear, the way she had rubbed the grit yesterday morning. Elsie held her breath and listened too. The flour slid soft between the floury fingers. It didn't squeak. It only whispered, dry and silky, and fell back into the sack.

"Good," said Mrs Merriweather. That was all. She tied the string back round the sack. She went to the shelf behind her and took down the seed cake, the big round one with the cracked top, and cut four thick slices off it with four strokes of the knife. She wrapped them in paper, each one by itself, and twisted the ends. She set the four little parcels on the counter in a row in front of Elsie and pushed them an inch toward her with one finger. After that she went back to her loaf.

Elsie gathered the seed cake into her satchel. As she turned to go she saw the window. The honey cakes were still there on their blue plate, in their row. They had been there two days now. They had gone dull, their crackle gone soft, and they had sunk in the middle the way cakes do when they are left, each one a little hollow in the top. Every one was there, all in their row. The plate sat under the window in the morning sun with its row of tired cakes, waiting.

She went and stood over them. She looked at them for a long moment, at the sunk tops and the faded gloss. She thought of Amara under the red lantern, out on the canal walk in the dark, looking at the plate a good while and then going off toward the bridge. "I'll bake fresh ones." She turned round from the plate to Mrs Merriweather. "Tonight. After closing, if I may. With Bess's flour. In case."

Mrs Merriweather looked at her across the counter and gave one short nod.

The guild hall was full and noisy at twenty past ten. It smelled of sawdust and ale and wet coats. Elsie went straight to the clerk's desk with the others behind her and laid Bess's scrap of sack paper down on it, flat, smoothing out the creases. The clerk picked it up. She read it, and frowned, and read it again, holding it closer, her lips moving over the big square letters. She looked at the flourish going off the edge. She looked up at Elsie, and at Cora behind her with the ledger, and put the scrap down.

The clerk unlocked the iron box under the desk and counted silver out of it onto the wood, one lantern at a time, each with a little ring. Nine of them. She stamped the notice paid. Tamsin swept the nine lanterns up into her big hand before anybody else could touch them and dealt them out like cards, into Elsie's palm and Tilly's palm and her own. "One for you, one for you, one for you. And one, and one, and one. And one, and one, and one." Three each. She closed Tilly's fingers over hers for her.

Elsie looked at her three. They sat in her palm, round and bright, each with its little lantern stamped on it, and they were warm from the clerk's box, warm as if they had been sitting in the sun. She closed her hand on them and felt them knock together. That was Bess's wheel, she thought, and the blue bowl, and the cream. She opened her purse on her belt, dropped them in one after another, clink, clink, clink, and drew the strings tight.

As the strings closed, the air in front of her opened. A window hung there, at the height of her eyes, small and clear and steady, its light falling on her hands and on the purse in them. She hadn't wished for it. It had simply come.

Ash Lane ran behind the Hall of Naming, down the back of it, where the Hall's great pale wall went up on one side like a cliff. It was narrow and shady and cool after the square. The houses on the other side were tall and thin, four storeys, five, and they leaned toward each other across the lane at the top so that the sky was only a strip. Window boxes hung out of the upper windows with geraniums in them. It was quiet. Their boots sounded loud on the cobbles.

Halfway down there was a house narrower than all the others, so narrow its door took up nearly the whole of the bottom of it. It was painted black, door and frame and window sills, all of it, a deep soft black like a crow's back. In its front window hung dried flowers, bunches of them upside down. And low down in the black door, at the bottom, where a cat would find it, a little square flap had been cut, with a leather hinge at the top.

Her eyes were dark and large. The rims of them were red, and the lids were swollen, as if she had been crying for a long time and only lately stopped.

Her eyes had gone past Elsie's shoulder, to Cora, standing a little behind in her charcoal coat with her ledger under her arm. They went to the coat, to the long dark front of it buttoned to the throat, and stayed there, and Ines held on to the door.

Only then it would stay on the board till it curled, like the others did."

"How not ordinary?" Tamsin had her arms folded, at the back.

Ines twisted the lace. "He's bones. Only bones. Little white ones." She swallowed. "He was my kitten. When I was small. He died when I was little, and I cried for a month. And years after, I was named, and I learned how." Her chin wobbled. "So I raised him again. He's my kitten. Raised again. That's all he is."

Tamsin took a step back. She took it without seeming to know she had, off the doorstep and down onto the cobbles, and her hand went up to the strap of Margery on her back. Her freckles stood out on her face. Tilly took a step forward at the same moment. She went up onto the step Tamsin had left, close to the black door, with her book clutched to her chest, and her blue eyes were shining like the blue flame in her vials.

Elsie thought about it. "Does he still purr?"

Ines stared up at her, a long stare, her red-rimmed eyes searching Elsie's face. "Yes," she whispered at last. "He purrs. He hasn't any throat to purr with. He hasn't anything. But he does." Her voice cracked a little on it. "He purrs all the time. On my lap. He purrs on my feet when I'm asleep."

"Then he's a cat," Elsie said. "He's a cat, and he's a lost one, and he's yours, and you miss him." She reached out and took Ines's hand out of the lace. It was small and very cold, cold as a stone from a stream, and the fingers were thin as twigs. Elsie folded it in both of hers to warm it. "We'll find him. We will. Don't you worry."

Ines looked down. She looked at her hand, lying in both of Elsie's, small and white between the fingerless gloves. She looked at it for a long time, as if it belonged to someone else and she was surprised to see it there. Then the pink came. It came up from the black lace at her throat and over her pale face, slowly, all of it, cheeks and nose and ears, right up into her black fringe, until her face was pinker than Elsie's hair. She still didn't take her hand back.

"He's been gone three days," Ines whispered. "Three nights. He goes out over the roofs at night, through the flap, and up the drainpipe, and along. He always has. He likes the roofs." She looked up the lane at the strip of sky between the leaning houses. "But he's back by the morning. Every time. He's never been gone past one night before." She held on to Elsie's hand. "And people see him, and they scream, and they throw things."

"Where does he like best? When he's not on the roofs. What does he like?"

Ines thought. "Warm places. He's always cold. He hasn't any fur. He likes warm laps best. And the sills, when the sun's on them, he lies along them flat. And the step by the bakery at the end of the lane, by the bread oven, where the wall's warm." She almost smiled. "He'll lie anywhere warm. He'd lie in the oven if I let him."

"We'd go faster in two halves," Tamsin said, and Elsie and Tilly both nodded. Tamsin and Tilly took the back yards, the long thin yards behind the houses with their washing lines, water butts and sheds, climbing the walls between them. Elsie, Ines and Cora took the lane itself: the gutters, the sills, the doorsteps and the drainpipes, up one side and down the other. It was noon. The sun had come round into the lane at last and lay along the cobbles in a hot bright stripe.

A washerwoman was hanging out sheets on a line strung across a yard.

"Excuse me," Elsie called. "Have you seen a small white cat?"

The washerwoman dropped a wet sheet in the basket. "White cat? White cat? I'll give you a white cat." She pointed at her yard wall, with her hand shaking. "A skeleton. Sat on that wall. Two nights back, under the moon, bold as brass, washing its face. I screamed the street up. I screamed the whole lane up."

A boy was sitting on a doorstep with a top and a whip. He was about ten, with a scab on his knee. "I seen it. Yesterday at dusk. Running along the gutter of the long roof over there. Something white, small, quick, going like a rat." He grinned. "It rattled. It rattled like dice in a cup. Clack clack clack, all along the gutter." He pointed with the whip. "That way. Toward the lake." Ines thanked him in a whisper, and he gawped at her lace.

Tamsin came back down the lane at half past one with Tilly behind her. She was pale under her freckles. There was a feather in her curls. "A pigeon," she said, before anyone could ask. "It came out at me. Out of a shed. Right in my face, all wings and noise." She pulled the feather out and looked at it. "I nearly had Margery off my back. I nearly took the shed down to the ground over a pigeon." She shook her head. "I thought it was him. I thought it was the bones."

At two they came to the end of the lane, where it opened out by the side of the Hall of Naming. Elsie stopped. Above them, over the Hall's pale wall, the great glass dome stood up into the sky. The afternoon sun was full on it. It blazed. It blazed so white and gold that she had to put up her hand against it, and even from down in the lane she could feel the heat coming off it onto her face, the way heat comes out of an oven when the door is opened.

Elsie looked up at it, squinting through her fingers. She thought about Biscuit, who had no fur, who was always cold, who liked the step by the bread oven and the sills when the sun was on them. "Ines. If I were a cat. If I were a cat made all of bones, and I was cold all the time." She pointed up at the blazing dome. "That's where I'd want to lie. Up there. On that."

Ines looked up. She shaded her eyes with one small hand and looked up at the dome, at the glass blazing in the sun, at the hot white curve of it against the sky. Elsie watched her face while she looked. It changed. Ines took her hand down from her eyes and put it over her mouth, slowly, and pressed it there. Above the hand her dark eyes had filled up, and she didn't look away from the dome.

Inside, the Hall of Naming was cool and hushed and full of coloured light from the high windows, the way it had been on Elsie's own morning. A priestess in white was crossing the floor with a taper. Elsie knew her at once: the priestess who had stood by her in the silver circle on her Naming morning. The priestess saw her and stopped dead. Her face went pale. Her eyes went past Elsie to the small figure in black lace at her elbow, and she went paler still, white as her robe, and the taper shook.

Elsie went up to her and smiled, very nicely, the way her mother smiled at a farmer's wife who had set the dog on them. "Good afternoon. I'm so sorry to trouble you. I wonder, may we go up on your roof? Only for a little while. There's a lost cat, you see, and we think he may be up on the dome." The priestess looked at her. She looked at Ines. She didn't say a word. She lifted the hand without the taper and pointed, stiffly, across the hall, at a small arched door in the wall.

Behind the door a stair went up inside the thickness of the wall, narrow and winding, stone steps worn hollow in the middle, with slits of window here and there letting in blades of sun. It went round and round and up. Tamsin had to duck. Tilly counted the steps out loud and lost count at ninety. At the top a low door opened onto the leads: a flat walkway of grey lead sheet that ran round the foot of the dome like a collar, with a little stone parapet on the outside and the great glass curve going up on the inside.

They went round the leads one behind another, Elsie first, with the dome going up beside them, hot and blinding. The whole city spread out below the parapet in roofs and canals. They went round to the far side, where the sun struck full. And there he was. Up on the glass, in the full blaze of it, lay a small white cat. He was curled nose to tail like any cat on a hearth. He was made of bones, little white bones, every one of them, and the sun shone straight through him.

He was up on the curve of the dome, above the gutter at its foot, a long arm's length out onto the glass and then some, where it began to slope up steeply. Elsie looked closer. One of his hind legs was stretched out behind him, not tucked in like the others. The little foot at the end of it was down in a crack in the lead where two of the dome's panes met, wedged in fast between the strip of lead and the glass. He couldn't pull it out.

"Biscuit," Ines whispered. She knelt on the leads by the gutter with her hands together. "Biscuit. Oh, Biscuit." The little skull lifted off the bony paws and turned toward her. He mewed. It was a thin, dry sound, like a twig snapping in a fire, and it went right into Elsie. He tried to get up. He pulled at the caught foot and slipped back on the hot glass. He lay down again and mewed again, and couldn't come to her.

Cora had come up behind them. She looked at the cat, and at the glass, and over the parapet. "No. It's too steep. Look at the curve of it." She pointed. Through the glass, past the little cat, far down, Elsie could see the floor of the Hall, small as a picture, its stones laid out in rings. "That's forty feet. Forty feet, if a hand slips, onto the stones of the hall floor. We send for the Hall's ladders. We send for a roofer."

Elsie was already sitting down on the leads and pulling off her boots. She pulled off her stockings and stuffed them in. "He's been here three days. Three days in a crack. He's frightened. And a roofer's heavy." She stood up in her bare feet. "I'm the lightest." She was. She looked at Tamsin, who was the heaviest, and at Tilly, who was nearly as small as she was and white to the lips. "So it's me." Before Cora could speak again she got down on her hands and knees and crawled out onto the glass.

She heard Cora move behind her. She heard the charcoal coat hit the lead, flat, and felt hands close round her bare ankle. Both hands. Long fingers, wrapped all the way round, gripping hard, so hard it hurt, the thumbs pressed into the hollow behind the bone. Cora had thrown herself down flat on the leads behind her, on her front, and taken hold. Elsie glanced back between her own arms. Cora was lying full length on the lead with her face white and her teeth set, her arms out straight, holding on.

The glass was hot. It was hot as a griddle under her palms and her knees, so hot she had to keep moving her hands. She crawled out on it slowly, flat, spreading her weight, the way you go out on ice. The sun pressed on the back of her neck. And under her, through the glass, far, far down, she saw the floor of the Hall of Naming, and in the middle of it the silver circle set into the stone, small as a ring on a table. She had stood in it. She had stood right there and been named.

She inched up the slope toward him, palm by palm. Somewhere along the way she began to hum. She didn't know she was doing it. It was the song her mother hummed over the fire in the caravan, low and wandering, with no words. Biscuit had been pulling at his foot again, scrabbling on the glass with his little bone claws. He stopped. He went still, and turned his skull toward her, and listened, with the empty round holes of his eyes on her face.

She reached him. Close to, his foot was tiny, a little fan of white bones no bigger than a sparrow's, caught down in the crack between the lead and the glass. Elsie worked her fingers in beside it. She went slowly. She eased the bones out one at a time, the way she'd worked the stones loose in the mill race yesterday, wiggling each one a little this way and a little that way until it came. One toe. Another. The long bone of the foot. Biscuit held still and let her, and at last the whole foot slid free.

He climbed into her arm. He didn't wait to be lifted. He got up on his bony legs and stepped straight into the crook of her elbow as if he'd been waiting three days for it. He turned round once, and settled. He hardly weighed anything. He was as light as a bundle of dry twigs, all angles, and he was hot from the glass all through. Then, against her arm, he began to purr. He hadn't anything to purr with. He purred anyway.

The purr buzzed up her arm. It went up through the bones of her elbow into her shoulder, warm and steady and rattling a little, like a kettle lid just before the boil. It was warm from the glass. It was so like any cat's purr, any cat on any lap, that Elsie laughed. She laughed out loud, right there on top of the dome in the blazing sun, flat on the hot glass with the whole city under her and a skeleton cat in her arm purring like a little engine.

"Fairweather." Cora's voice came from behind her, rough. "Stay still." Elsie stayed still. The hands round her ankle began to pull. They drew her back down the glass, slowly, steadily, one hand letting go and taking a new grip higher up her leg and then the other, hand over hand, as if she were a rope being hauled in. The glass slid hot under her stomach. The gutter came under her knees, and then the lead. At last she was on the leads again, on her side, with Biscuit in her arm.

Cora's hands let go of her leg. Cora sat back on her heels on the lead. Her face was white, whiter than at the Copper Baths when the eel had her arm, white to the lips, and her grey eyes were very wide and fixed on Elsie. She was breathing hard, fast and deep. Her shoulders went up and down under her shirt, as if she had run all the way up the stair and round the leads twice. A strand of her hair had come loose again. She didn't put it back.

Elsie sat up and held Biscuit out. Ines took him. She took him in both hands as if he were made of glass, and held him up under her chin, tight against her throat. His little skull pushed up under her jaw. He was purring. Ines shut her eyes. Her face crumpled, and she cried, standing there on the leads in the sun, her shoulders shaking and the tears running down her pale cheeks onto his white bones. She didn't make a sound the whole time.

Elsie shuffled over on the lead and sat down next to Cora. She pulled her stockings out of her boots, and her boots on. Her hands were still hot from the glass. She laced the left boot and tied it in a double bow. Beside her Cora hadn't moved off her heels. Her hands were lying on her knees. Elsie looked at them. They were shaking. They were shaking hard, both of them, the long fingers trembling against the dark cloth of her trousers, and Cora was looking down at them as if she didn't know whose they were.

Elsie took them. She took both of Cora's hands in both of hers and held them still in her lap. "I'm all right. Look. I'm all right. Not a scratch." She turned her palms up to show her, pink from the glass. "See?" Cora looked at Elsie's palms. She looked at them a long time. Her hands in Elsie's were cold again, cold in the full sun. "Don't do that again." It hardly carried, and it wasn't level.

Elsie squeezed the cold hands. "I'd do it for any of you. For Tamsin, or Tilly, or you. For a cat. I'd go out on the glass for any of you." Cora lifted her eyes from Elsie's palms to Elsie's face. She looked at her. She looked for a long moment. Her lips came apart as if she might answer. They closed again, and she didn't say anything to that at all.

On Ash Lane, outside the black door, Ines stopped with Biscuit under her chin and turned round on the step. She shifted him into the crook of one arm. She pushed the black door open behind her, all the way, and held it there wide with both hands, Biscuit tucked between her elbow and her ribs. "Would you like to come in?" It was so quiet that Elsie had to lean down to hear it, and her pale face had gone pink again under the fringe, and she kept both hands on the door.

The front room was small, smaller than Elsie's attic, and full. There were books on every wall, from the floor to the ceiling, books on the chairs and books on the floor in towers. Dried flowers hung upside down from the beams in bunches, lavender, rose and poppy heads. The room smelled of them, dry and sweet and dusty. And there were birdcages. A dozen of them, little brass and wicker cages hung from hooks in the ceiling, and every one had its door standing open.

Tamsin stayed by the door. She came in, because the others did, but she put her back against the wall right beside the door frame and stayed there, standing, with Margery still on her back and her arms folded tight. It was a small room. It was as far from Biscuit as she could get in it. Her eyes followed the little cat as Ines set him down on the hearth rug. "Please sit," Ines said, and Tamsin didn't. She stood very straight, the way Cora stood, and watched him.

Biscuit shook himself, a rattle all over like dice. He looked round the room. He trotted straight across the rug to Tamsin, quick and purposeful, as if he had picked her out from the doorstep. He put his front paws up on her boot. He climbed it, up her boot and her knee, bony claws catching in her trousers. Tamsin made a sound. She sat down on the nearest chair, hard, without meaning to, and he climbed into her lap, turned round once, and settled there in a heap of white bones, purring.

Tamsin sat rigid. She sat bolt upright on the chair with her back off the chair back and her hands held up in the air on either side of her, open, fingers spread, as if she had put her hands up to a highwayman. She didn't touch him. She looked down at the skeleton cat in her lap with her face gone the colour of whey. "Elsie." It came out very small for Tamsin. "Elsie. What do I do? What am I meant to do?"

"Stroke him. He likes it. Go on."

Tamsin looked at her. She looked at the cat. Slowly she brought one hand down out of the air. She put out one finger, just one, and touched it to the top of his little skull, and drew it down, along the knobs of his spine, very lightly. Biscuit pushed up against the finger and purred louder. Elsie watched Tamsin's face change. The horror went out of it, bit by bit. Her mouth came open, and her eyes went round and soft. She stroked him again.

Ines went to make tea. There was a little iron range in the corner and a kettle on it already warm. She filled the pot. But when she lifted the kettle to pour, her hands shook so much that water slopped out of the spout and hissed on the range, and the lid of the pot rattled. Elsie got up. She took the kettle out of Ines's hands, gently, and filled the pot herself. When it had stood she poured a cup for Ines first and put it into her hands, and then poured for everyone else.

Tilly had a hundred questions about raising. She sat cross-legged on the floor at Ines's feet with her grave book open on her knee. "Can you raise a dog? Can you raise a horse? Can you raise a person?"

Ines shook her head at each. "Birds. And mice. That's all I can raise. Anything bigger won't come." She looked at the heap of white bones in Tamsin's lap. "And Biscuit. Only ever him." Tilly wrote it all down, and Ines leaned forward a little more with every answer, her eyes beginning to shine.

While Tilly was writing an answer down, Ines turned in her chair to Elsie. She held her teacup in both hands. "You're the Enthraller. Aren't you? The one they named in the Hall. This month. With the pink hair." She looked at Elsie's hair. "The whole city talks of you. In the market. On the bridges. I heard it at the baker's at the end of the lane. The Enthraller."

"Yes, that's me." Elsie made a face. "It's a dreadful name, isn't it? I didn't pick it. I'd have picked something much nicer. Something with flowers in." She drank some tea and looked at Ines over the cup with her head on one side. "Is Necromancer a dreadful one too? To have, I mean? It sounds like it might be. It's got a lot of corners."

Ines looked into her cup. "People cross the lane. When they see me coming. They cross to the other side, and walk faster." She turned the cup in her small hands, and lifted her eyes to Elsie's face. "And in Brightwater Square they look at you the same way. I've seen them. When you walk across. They watch you over their shoulders and they step out of your way. The same way exactly."

Elsie hadn't noticed. She sat with the cup in her hands and thought about it, and it came back to her in pieces: the Hall going quiet on her Naming morning, the whole of it, all those faces, and the clerk's pen stopping in the middle of her name. She had thought they were only surprised. She looked down into her tea. She went quiet. It was a long while before she lifted her eyes again.

"They'll watch you all your life," Ines whispered. "I'm sorry. That's what it is, being one of us. They watch, and they watch, and they wait. They're waiting for you to be what the name says." She looked at Biscuit in Tamsin's lap. "Birds and mice, and one kitten. That's all I've ever raised. They still wait."

Elsie thought about that. She put her cup down on the hearth and folded her hands in her lap. "Then they'll be waiting a very long time. They'll get awfully tired, waiting. Their legs will ache." Ines looked at her. She looked at her for a long while, her dark eyes wide. At last her mouth moved at the corner. It very nearly smiled.

Cora was standing by the window. She hadn't sat down. She had her ledger open on her arm, and her pen out, and she was standing with the light from the window falling across the page. Elsie glanced at her. The pen was over the page. It stayed over the page. It didn't move. Cora's eyes weren't on the ledger. They were on Ines, and then on Elsie, and her face had the look it had when she was reading something hard in small print.

Elsie took her little blue notebook out of her satchel, and her pencil. She opened it on her knee, past Tamsin's page, Tilly's, Cora's and Noor's with its raincloud, to a clean page. She wrote INES at the top in capitals and underlined it, and licked the pencil. "Now. What are your favourite things? I write them down for everybody. So I know. Tell me three, to start with. You can have more later."

Ines looked at the notebook. She looked at her name at the top of the page in Elsie's round capitals. She thought, with her cup held under her chin. "Hot milk. With honey in it. Not tea. Milk." She thought again. "The hour before dawn. When it's still dark and very quiet and everyone's asleep, and it's all just mine." She looked at Tamsin's lap. "And Biscuit asleep on my feet. In the night. When he's heavy."

Elsie wrote them all down. Hot milk with honey. The hour before dawn. Biscuit asleep on her feet. Beside Ines's name at the top she drew a tiny cat, curled up nose to tail. She drew its ribs in, its little skull, and its tail in a row of dots like beads, so that it was a cat made all of bones. She turned the notebook round on her knee to show her. Ines leaned over and looked at it. She looked at it a long time, and didn't say anything.

"I'll go to the guild in the morning," Ines whispered. She set down her cup. "First thing. I'll tell the clerk Biscuit's home. That you found him." She looked at Elsie, and then at Cora by the window. "So your notice pays. It has to be the one who put it up that tells them. I know how it's done." She folded her hands in her lace. "I'll be there when it opens."

At the door Elsie hugged her. She did it the way she always did, without asking, arms right round, and pulled her in. Ines went stiff. She went stiff as a post in Elsie's arms, and for a moment Elsie thought she had done it wrong. Then the stiffness went out of her. It went all at once. She was still instead, quite still. Her face came down against Elsie's shoulder and stayed there, and Elsie could feel her breathing against the cream halter, small and quick.

"You may come again," Ines whispered, into her shoulder. "If you like. If you want to." She stepped back. Her face was pink again, up into her fringe. "You don't have to."

"I will. I'll come. I'll bring you honey for your milk."

Ines looked at her. The pink went darker, and she shut the black door very gently.

They walked back toward Brightwater Square through the long gold light off the lake. The sun had gone down low over the Mere, and it came along the canals sideways. It lay on the water in a burning road, on the walls of the houses and on their faces, so they all walked squinting and gold. The paper lanterns along the canals were not yet lit and hung pale in it. Swallows went screaming low over the water. Elsie's hands still felt warm from the glass.

Tamsin walked in front with her hands in her pockets. Every few steps she took one out and looked at it, at the finger, and put it back. "I had a dead cat in my lap," she told the canal at last. "A dead cat. In my lap. Made of bones. And it purred." She shook her head slowly. "It purred at me, Elsie. It picked me." She looked round at them, and her crooked grin came out, wide and amazed. "I'm going to tell my mum. I'm going to write it to Nell. They'll say I'm making it up."

Tilly was walking with her grave book open in front of her again, bumping into lamp posts. "His little skull. Did you see it? When he turned round to listen? It's beautiful. The curve of it, and the little holes for his eyes, all clean and white." She had found the very last page of the book, the blank one inside the back cover, and she was drawing on it as she walked, slowly, with her pencil, tongue out. Elsie leaned over to look. It was Biscuit, curled up, every rib, with his skull lifted to listen.

The guild hall's doors were standing half shut when they came into Brightwater Square at six, and a porter was dragging the second one across. The day's crowd was coming down the steps in twos and threes. It was shutting time. Cora went up the steps ahead of them and stopped on the top one, by the doors, where she always stopped, where she went off duty. She turned round. She didn't say Fairweather and incline her head, the way she did every evening. She stood there with her ledger in both hands.

Cora opened the ledger. She turned it round, the right way up for Elsie, and held it out in front of her, open, at the page. Elsie came up the last step to see. There it was, today's date, and under it in Cora's small exact hand: Ash Lane. Lost cat, found. And under that, the line Elsie always looked for: Skills used, none. Cora held it there, open, while Elsie read it, and didn't take it back until she had read it twice.

Elsie reached out and squeezed Cora's hand, the bare one holding the edge of the ledger, round the fingers. She stopped. Cora's hand was warm. It was warm right through, the fingers and the palm, warm as Elsie's own. "Cora!" She squeezed again to make sure. "Your hand's warm. It's actually warm. Feel." She held it up between them as if Cora might not have noticed. "It was like ice up on the dome, and now look. That's sleeping. That's what sleeping does."

"I have my report to write," Cora told the carved oak doorframe of the guild hall, a little louder than she needed to, looking hard at the grain of the wood beside Elsie's head. Her hand was still in Elsie's. The red had come up out of her collar and filled it, all the way round, right to the top button, and was going on up her neck.

She took her hand back. She went down the steps past them and off along the canal toward Wick Lane, straight-backed, her ledger under her arm, the low sun throwing her long shadow out in front of her over the cobbles. She didn't look back. Elsie watched her go along the water, past the first bridge, and the second. At the third bridge Cora stopped. She turned her head over her shoulder and looked back, at the guild steps, at Elsie on them, for one moment, before she went on.

Tamsin stretched till her back cracked, and hitched Margery up. "Docks. Supper. I'll dream about bones."

"I'm going home to read everything I've got about raising," said Tilly. "All of it. Tonight."

They went off from the bottom of the steps in opposite directions, Tamsin toward the docks and Tilly toward Pickle Row. Both of them kept turning round and waving, walking backwards, waving, until the corners took them, Tamsin's big wave and Tilly's small one. Elsie waved at both at once.

At seven the Honeycomb was shut and dark at the front, and the big oven at the back was still warm from the day. Mrs Merriweather put another log in for her, nodded, and went up to bed. Elsie tied on an apron. She took down the bowl and the scales. She untied Bess's sack and scooped the flour out of it into the bowl. It was soft and fine and white as milk. When she rubbed a pinch between her finger and thumb it didn't squeak at all. She smiled at it and started.

She baked them the way Mrs Merriweather had taught her, honey, butter, eggs and Bess's fine flour, the oven moderate and not hot. When they came out golden she brushed each one all over with the very last of the comb, thick and dark, and slid them back into the oven. She stood in front of it with her eyes shut and counted to twenty out loud. One, two, three. On twenty she took them out. They crackled. She could hear them crackling on the tray as they cooled, like a fire just catching.

She took the old plate out of the window, the two-day cakes, sunk and dull, and put them on the side for the morning birds. She washed the blue plate and dried it. She set the new honey cakes out on it in a row, one by one, shining and crackled and smelling of honey. She carried it to the window and set it down just where the old one had been. Outside, along Thimble Canal, the red paper lantern over the shop was lit, and its light came through the glass and lay on the fresh cakes.

Up in the attic at nine she lit the candle and sat on the bed with the blue notebook on her knee. She turned to Ines's page. Under Biscuit asleep on her feet she wrote, in her round hand, Biscuit: bones, white, purrs, likes warm places best. Above the little bone cat she had drawn by Ines's name, she drew the dome, a round hump of glass, and above the dome she drew a small sun with short lines coming out of it all round, shining down on him.

She took the second glove out of her satchel and sat on the bed by the round window, where the light from the canal came in red and gold. Outside the lamplighters' boats had been and gone. She found her place, on the thumb. She picked up the stitches she'd set aside on the road and worked them round on the needles, round and round. The thumb grew up out of the hand of the glove between her needles, a little grey tube, longer and longer, while the candle burned down and the canal slapped softly below.

When she had done a little of the thumb, she put down the needles and took the finished glove off the sill. She pulled it on over her own hand, the way she had the night she finished it. It went on easily. It was warm from the window. She held her hand up in the candlelight and spread her fingers, and the ends of the glove's fingers flapped, empty, a whole knuckle past the ends of hers. She wiggled them and they wagged. She thought of Cora's hand palm to palm with hers in the loft. Just right.

She took the glove off and laid it back on the sill by Moonpenny's clay pot. Beside it she laid the other: the half-glove, with its cuff and its hand and its thumb just begun, the needles still in it. She set them side by side, palm down, thumbs in, the way two hands lie when someone sets them down to rest. They weren't a pair yet. One was much shorter. But they looked like a pair now, nearly, there on the sill by Moonpenny with the canal light on them.

Elsie knelt on the bed with her elbows on the sill and her chin in her hands. "Moonpenny. Listen. Today I held a cat made all of bones, and he purred. He hasn't a throat, and he purred anyway. All up my arm." She touched the orchid's bud with one finger. "And I'm a level higher, and Cora's hands are warm." She turned and leaned over the candle, and blew. The flame lay flat and went out, and a thin thread of smoke went up past the two grey gloves on the sill.
