Elsie woke to the bread smell coming up through the boards, and lay a moment listening to the ovens knock below, and then remembered and turned her head on the pillow to look. The parcels were there by the door where she had left them, in their little pile in the grey light: three brown paper parcels tied with string, and the one on top with its name turned toward the bed. Cora. She smiled at it. The paper had gone soft at the corners overnight from the honey, and the whole attic smelled faintly of buns.

She sat up and reached for the little blue notebook on the stool and opened it on her knees. Under Tamsin, in yesterday's pencil: ankle, check it. Under Tilly: her father. Clocks. Gear Street. She read it the way she read her mother's letters, slowly, hearing it. Tamsin's ankle had been bitten clean through the trouser, and she had walked off down the dock steps on it as if it were nothing. Elsie meant to look at it before anyone could tell her it was fine. She tapped the word with her finger, twice, to fix it, and shut the book.

She knelt by the crate under the window with the water jug and went down the row. Mrs Pennywhistle got her drink first, because she was oldest. "We're going after a goat today," Elsie told her, tipping the jug carefully into the fern's pot. "A lost one. In a real dungeon, under the old flower market. Tamsin's very pleased. I don't think Tilly is." The fern's fronds stirred in the draught from the window and then hung quite still, long and grave and green, as if taking the matter under proper consideration. "I know," Elsie said. "I'll be careful."

She washed and dressed by the window. She laced the cream halter snug up the front and tied it off under her breasts, stepped into the sage skirt, buckled the belt, and pinned the pink-and-white clip above her ear. Then she packed the satchel on the bed. The three parcels went in first, upright, so the buns would not squash. The pot of salve went in beside them, and two clean rolls of bandage, and the notebook last, flat against the side. She lifted the satchel to test it. It smelled of honey through the canvas.

Downstairs the baker was pulling the first trays, red-faced in the heat, and the bakery was loud with the oven doors. Elsie waited by the counter until she turned round, and then held out the brown paper from the day before, smoothed flat and folded in four, with the string wound round it in a neat little hank. "Your paper," she said. "I said I'd bring it back." The baker looked at the folded square, and at Elsie, and took it and put it under the counter with the others. She nodded, once, and turned back to her trays.

The guild hall was half empty at half past seven. The long tables were bare and the great room echoed, and the morning light came down in long dusty slants from the high windows onto the boards. A sweeper was working his way along under the quest board. Elsie came in out of the square with her satchel and stood a moment, getting her bearings, and saw the charcoal-painted door off the main hall standing open for once, propped with a wedge. A lamp was burning inside, though the morning was bright, and she could see the edge of a table.

Cora was at the table inside, alone, sitting very straight with the black ledger open in front of her, writing in it. Her pen moved small and quick and steady along the line, and her black hair was in its low bun, smooth as lacquer, and a cup of something sat untouched by her elbow. Elsie stopped in the doorway. Cora's pen stopped too. She looked up, and her grey eyes found Elsie in the door and stayed there, and for a moment her face did not seem to know which expression to use.

"Good morning," Elsie said, and came in, and set her satchel on the corner of the table and opened it. She took out the top parcel, the one with the crossed-out line, and held it out across the ledger with both hands. Cora, it said across the brown paper, in Elsie's round careful hand, with the double bow of string under it. "I made buns," Elsie said. "Last night. Honey ones. The baker let me use the ovens. There's one each, and this one's yours." She held it there between them, smiling.

Cora did not take it at once. She looked at the parcel in Elsie's hands. Elsie saw her eyes go to the line drawn through black, strong, one sugar, and then to the word written under it, and stay on that. She read it for a long moment, as if it were a word in a language she knew only from books and was making sure of. Her pen was still in her hand. The lamp ticked. Then she put the pen down, very carefully, on the open ledger, and reached out and took the parcel by its string.

She did not open it. She held it a moment, and then put it into the deep pocket of her charcoal coat, unopened, and buttoned the flap of the pocket down over it. Then she picked up her pen again. "The goat," she said, in her clause-reading voice, "is in the afternoon. The party is not due at the hall until eleven." She looked at the ledger rather than at Elsie as she said it. Elsie watched her hand rest a moment on the buttoned pocket, flat, before it went back to the page.

"I know," Elsie said. "I know it is. I'm early." She smiled at the top of Cora's bent head. "I only wanted you to have it while it was fresh. They're best the first morning. By tomorrow they'd be bricks, and then they'd just be something I owed you." Cora's pen paused on the line again. She did not look up. "Thank you," she said, to the ledger, very low, in a voice Elsie had not heard her use before. Elsie closed her satchel and went out and left the door as she had found it.

She sat down on the long bench by the quest board to wait, with her satchel on her knees, and watched the hall fill. It filled slowly and then all at once. Adventurers came in by twos and threes, loud with the morning, in leather and mail and travelling cloaks, and the clerks came to their desks and uncapped their ink, and the party board grew its daily crust of chalk. By eight the great room was roaring. Elsie sat in the middle of it and swung her boots and watched everybody, and liked them all.

A party of three came along the board toward her, two young men and a woman with a bow, talking and laughing. The woman's eyes fell on the copper tag at Elsie's belt, and on the F, and on Elsie's face, and her laugh stopped. She said something low to the others. All three of them looked, and then they turned together, without a word, and crossed the whole width of the hall to walk along the far wall instead, past the desks, and did not look back. Elsie watched them go. She looked down at her tag, and then folded her hands on her satchel.

She hummed a little, under her breath, without quite meaning to. Then she got up and went and stood in front of the F-rank notices, close, and read them again, every one, from the top. She had read them all yesterday. She read them anyway, the way she read the notebook, because it gave her eyes somewhere to go that was not the far wall. Moonbells from the Glasswood's edge, for an apothecary. The lost goat in the Underbloom, three lanterns, with a hand-drawn goat in the corner. She read the goat twice.

Tamsin came in at ten by the hall clock, swinging through the doors with Margery over her shoulder, and her copper curls were dry for once, a great bright tangle of them, tied back with a bit of string and coming loose at the temples. She was limping, a little, on the bitten foot, and trying not to. She saw Elsie at the board and grinned her crooked grin across the hall, and came over with her boots ringing on the boards, and every head she passed turned to watch the axe go by.

"Sit," Elsie said, before Tamsin could say good morning, and took her by the wrist and sat her down on the bench, and sat down beside her. Then she bent and caught Tamsin's bitten leg behind the knee and lifted it, boot and all, and swung it up across her own lap, so that Tamsin's calf lay over Elsie's bare knees under the hem of the sage skirt. Tamsin made a small startled noise and grabbed the edge of the bench. "Elsie—" "Hold still," Elsie said. She unlaced the boot and worked it off.

She rolled the trouser leg up to the knee. Tamsin's calf was long and hard and freckled, and warm across Elsie's thighs. Elsie unwound yesterday's bandage, round and round, slowly, and peeled the last of it back from the bite. It was clean. The two little punctures had closed over and the skin round them was pink and healthy, with no heat in it and no swelling. Elsie pressed gently round it with her thumb to be sure. Then she opened the salve and smoothed a fresh layer over the bite with two fingers and wound a clean bandage on.

Tamsin held very still the whole time. She sat with both hands gripping the bench and her head tipped back, looking up at the rafters as if there were something written on them, and she hardly seemed to breathe. Her leg lay heavy and rigid across Elsie's lap. When Elsie's thumb pressed round the bite, the muscle in her calf jumped under it. And her ears went red. They went red from the lobes up, slowly, until they were scarlet to the tips in the bright morning light, and stayed that way. "Nearly done," Elsie told her.

She tied off the bandage, rolled the trouser down, and helped the boot back on and laced it. Then she reached into her satchel and took out the parcel that said Tamsin, and put it in Tamsin's lap on top of her knee. "Breakfast," she said. Tamsin looked down at it, red to the ears, and tore the paper open, and took out a honey bun and ate it in two bites, and then sat chewing with her eyes shut. "Elsie," she said thickly. "That's the best bun I've had in my life." Elsie laughed and gave her another.

Tilly came in later, at half past ten, and she came in quietly. Elsie almost did not see her. She was not running, and her vials were quiet, strapped down tight in their loops, and her braids were done so tight they pulled at her temples, and her goggles were not on her head but down round her neck, hanging there like a weight. Her apron was on the right way round. She came along the board with her eyes on the floor, and when she reached them she stopped and said, "Good morning," in a small voice.

"Tilly!" Elsie fished the last parcel out of her satchel and held it out. "I made you buns." Tilly looked at it. She took it in both hands, and held it against her apron, and looked up at Elsie with her big blue eyes. "Thank you," she said. Then she looked down again, and opened the paper, and began to pick at a bun. Elsie waited for the second thank you, and the third, and the apology for the first two. It did not come. She watched Tilly's bent head and the tight braids, and noticed the once.

At eleven they went up to the long desk together, the three of them, and Elsie asked for the goat. The clerk found the notice in his tray and laid it on the desk in front of them: the lost goat, the Underbloom, first floor, three lanterns, with the little drawing of a goat in the corner and a flower seller's name written under it in brown ink. He stamped it with the party's name and turned it round to Elsie. "Report to the clerk at the stair head," he said. Elsie folded the notice and put it in her belt pouch.

The clerk leaned back on his stool and called the party's name toward the side of the hall, loud and flat. "Fairweather party, Underbloom." Elsie turned to look. The charcoal-painted door opened, and Cora came out of it at once, as if she had been standing just inside with her hand on the latch, waiting to be called. She had the ledger under her arm and her coat buttoned to the throat. Elsie saw the flap of the coat pocket still buttoned down over the parcel. Cora crossed the hall and took her place a step behind them.

They ate at the Tipsy Heron across the canal before going down, because Elsie said a dungeon wanted a full stomach. The tavern was quiet at noon, with sun on the tables. Elsie went to the counter and bought stew for the three of them, and paid with a silver lantern and two copper pips, counted into the counter girl's hand. Then she carried the bowls to the table. Tamsin fell on hers. Tilly sat with her spoon in her hand and pushed the stew round and round her bowl, and round again, and ate none of it.

Elsie watched her for a while. Tilly had not said a word since the hall. She sat hunched over the bowl with her shoulders up near her ears, and her free hand kept going to the goggles round her neck and turning them over and over. Under the table her knee was jiggling. Elsie put down her own spoon and leaned across the corner of the table toward her, close, so that the words would be only between the two of them and not for the tavern. "Tilly," she said quietly. "What's wrong?"

Tilly's spoon stopped. She looked down into her stew for a long moment. Then she said, very fast and very low, all in one breath, "I'm frightened of being underground. Since I was small. Cellars and tunnels and anywhere with a ceiling made of earth. I can't breathe in them, I feel the weight of it all on top of me." She swallowed. "The rats were all right because I could see the hatch. I could see the light. But a dungeon goes down and down." Her knuckles were white on the spoon.

"I didn't say," she said. "Before. When Tamsin wanted the goat. I should have, I know I should have, I'm sorry." She looked up at Elsie, and her blue eyes were wet. "But the guild wants three. For a dungeon. And there wasn't a third, there was only me, and I wanted to be one. I wanted to be one of the three." Her voice cracked on it. "The College threw me out, and then you came along, and I didn't want to be the one who spoiled it."

Tamsin had stopped eating. She put her spoon down, and leaned her elbows on the table, and looked at Tilly with her green eyes very steady. "Then don't come down," she said. "Wait at the top. Sit by the fountain and eat a pie. It's only a goat. Elsie and me'll fetch it up and you can hold the lead." She said it gruffly and plainly, the way she said everything. "There's no shame in it, Tilly. None. I'd sit at the top of a lot of things if I could." She picked her spoon back up.

Tilly shook her head, hard, so that the tight braids swung. "I can't wait at the top," she said. "The licence needs three. Three names go down that stair, or the party stays up here, and I'm one of the three." She set her spoon down on the table with a click and straightened her back, and lifted her chin, and it was trembling. "I'm one of the three," she said again, more quietly, as if she were saying it to herself. "So I'm coming."

Elsie looked at her across the corner of the table: the lifted chin, the trembling in it, the white knuckles. Then she held out her hand across the table, palm up, between the bowls. "Then you hold my hand," she said. "The whole way down, and the whole way through, and the whole way up again. You hold on as tight as you like. And you let go only when you want to, and not a moment before." She smiled. "I don't mind how long. I've got two hands. I only need the one for the goat."

Tilly looked at the hand. She looked at it for a long time, lying there open on the scrubbed wood between the stew bowls, as if it were a thing she had read about and not quite believed in. Her lips parted. Then she put out her own hand, slowly, small and soot-creased and rough at the fingertips, and laid it in Elsie's palm. Elsie closed her fingers round it. Tilly's hand was cold, and it was shaking, and it held on hard. The pink came up into her cheeks. Elsie squeezed.

Cora had sat at the end of the table all this while with her own bowl, eating in small neat spoonfuls, quiet. Elsie saw her look at their joined hands on the table, a long look, grave and still, and then look down at her stew. She ate the rest of it. When they got up to go, Cora went to the counter by herself and took her own coins out of her coat and laid them on the wood for her bowl, before Elsie could reach for her purse, and nodded to the counter girl, and came back to stand a step behind.

The old flower market stood at the top of a cobbled hill, a great long hall of iron ribs and glass panes, green with age at the joints, with the sun pouring through the roof in long gold bars. Under it were stalls, dozens of them, crowded close in rows, and every stall was heaped with flowers cut that morning: buckets of roses and sweet peas and tall blue spikes Elsie did not know, armfuls of white lilies, trays of violets. The air was thick and sweet and wet. Elsie stopped just inside the doors and breathed it in.

They had not gone ten steps along the first row when a woman came out from behind a stall of sweet peas and stood in their way. She was young, in a green apron with shears in its pocket, and her eyes were red and swollen, as if she had been crying for days and had only just stopped. She looked at Tamsin's axe and Tilly's vials and Cora's coat, and then at the tag at Elsie's belt, and wiped her nose on her wrist. "Are you the guild?" she said. "Please. Are you the guild, for the goat?"

"Her name's Clover," the woman said, when Elsie said yes. The words came out of her in a rush. "She's white all over, a nanny, two years old this spring, and she's the sweetest goat in the city and the greediest, and she follows me here every morning and minds the stall. Three days ago a bunch of lilies fell off the cart and rolled down into the fountain, and she went down the stair after them before I could catch her collar. I heard her bleating. And then I didn't." Her face crumpled.

Elsie took her hand. "We'll bring Clover up," she said. "I promise we will. We'll find her and bring her up to you." She did not stop to think whether she should promise it; she only meant it, so she said it. The flower seller looked at her. Then she caught Elsie's hand in both of hers, tight, with her wet face bent over it, and held on, and Elsie felt the hard little calluses from the shears on the woman's palms and the shaking in her fingers. "Thank you," the woman said, into their hands. "Oh, thank you."

In the very middle of the market, where the rows of stalls opened out into a little round square under the highest part of the glass, stood a fountain. It was old and wide and dry, a stone basin with a cracked stone lily in the middle of it, and the stalls kept their distance from it, as if it were a well with no rope. Elsie came to the rim and looked over. Inside the basin, where the water should have been, a stone stair went down, round and round, into the dark under the market floor. Cool air came up it smelling of earth and flowers.

A guild clerk sat on a stool at the top of the stair, a thin young man with a ledger of his own on his knee and a pen behind his ear, and he stood up as they came. "Party?" he said. Elsie gave him the notice and their names. He wrote them in, the four of them, with the hour, and looked at the tag at Elsie's belt and then twice at Cora's coat. "Licence for the stair is a lantern a party," he said. "Paid going down." Elsie took a silver lantern out of her purse and laid it in his ink-stained palm.

Tamsin stood at the rim of the fountain looking down the stair with the eagerness of a dog at a door. "First floor's safe for F-rank," she said over her shoulder. "Counted safe, the guild says, and I've been down it. It's just old cellars. Nothing much worse than beetles." She pointed with Margery's haft down into the spiral. "There's a second floor under it, but there's a locked guild gate across the stair down. So we can't go wrong. We can't go deeper than the first."

Elsie stepped over the rim into the dry basin and turned at the top of the stair and held out her hand behind her without looking. Tilly took it. She took it at the very first step down, and gripped it so hard that Elsie felt the bones in her own fingers press together, and Elsie gripped back. Tilly's palm was cold and damp. Her other hand was on the stone wall of the stair, flat, as if she were holding the wall up. "I'm here," Elsie said. "Mind the step. It's a long one." Tilly nodded, and stepped.

The stair went down and round, and down and round, and the light from the glass roof went grey behind them and then went away. The walls turned from cut stone to old red brick, and the air turned cool and close. At the bottom the stair opened into a low cellar, and then another beyond it through a brick arch, and another beyond that. Elsie felt Tilly's hand jump in hers at the bottom step. She looked round. Tilly had shut her eyes tight and was walking blind, with her lips moving, holding on.

But it was not dark. Elsie stood in the first cellar and looked up and round, and her mouth fell open. The brick vaults were grown over with flowers: pale flowers, hundreds of them, thousands, spilling over every arch and down every wall and along the cracks of the floor, small and white and cupped like bells, and every one of them was glowing. They gave off a soft cool light, silver-green, like moonlight on water, so that the whole long cellar shone with it, and there was no need of any lamp at all. The air smelled of honey.

"Oh," Elsie said. "Oh, Tilly. Tilly, look. Look." She squeezed Tilly's hand. Tilly shook her head, eyes screwed shut. "Please," Elsie said. "Just for a moment. It isn't like a cellar. I promise it isn't." There was a long pause. Then Tilly opened one eye, a crack, and then the other, and looked up at the glowing vault, and her mouth fell open the way Elsie's had. The light shone silver in her blue eyes. She looked and looked, round and up and along, and she did not shut them again.

Something moved in the flowers along the wall. Elsie looked, and then looked again. It was a beetle, but a beetle as big as a cat, round and domed and slow, with a shell like a polished green-black stone that caught the glow and gave it back in rainbows. It was grazing. It moved along the wall with its head down in the flowers, munching, as placid as a cow in a field. Beyond it there was another, and another, a little herd of them all down the long vault, shining, grazing, grazing on as if the four of them were not there.

"Leave the beetles be," Tamsin said, ahead. "Bloom beetles. They're harmless unless you handle them. They only nip if you pick one up." At that exact moment Tilly, who was looking up at the ceiling with her mouth open, very nearly put her boot down on the back of one. It hissed. Tilly squeaked and leapt sideways into Elsie, and Elsie caught her round the waist with her free arm and held her up, laughing, while the beetle trundled off offended.

They went on through the vaults, one shining cellar after another, the arches getting older and lower as they went. At the end of the third, where the vault ran on long and straight into silver-green distance, Elsie stopped and cupped her free hand round her mouth. "Clover!" she called. Her voice went down the vault and came back to her off the brick, Clover, Clover, smaller each time. They all stood still and listened. And then, from very far off, faint and wavering and indignant, something answered. It bleated.

"There," Elsie said. "She's there. She's all right!" She started forward. Tamsin put out an arm across her. "Mind the side passages," she said. "All down this way." She nodded at the low arches that opened off the long vault to either side, every few yards, and Elsie saw that their mouths were choked with something green and ropy and glossy, hanging in loops. "Stranglevine. It grows in the side ways. Don't brush it. It catches hold of whatever touches it and it holds on, and it doesn't let go." Elsie kept to the middle.

A short stair went down between two vaults, six steps to a little landing and six more beyond, and they stopped on the landing for Tamsin to listen. Elsie, waiting with Tilly's hand in hers, glanced back. Cora had stopped a step above them. She had unbuttoned the flap of her coat pocket. As Elsie watched, she took out the brown paper parcel with her name on it, and unwrapped one end, and took out a honey bun, and ate it, in small neat bites, standing on the stair with her face turned away toward the wall of flowers as if the bun were nothing to do with her.

Elsie saw it. She turned back quickly, before Cora could see her seeing it, and looked down the next vault with her heart gone warm and bright as the flowers. Cora was eating the bun. Cora had kept it in her pocket all morning, buttoned in, and brought it down into the Underbloom, and was eating it on the stair. Elsie did not say anything. She walked on down the next vault with Tilly's hand in hers, and she hummed all the way along it, from one end to the other, a little tune of nothing, under the glowing arches.

Somewhere along that vault, or the next, Elsie felt Tilly's grip change. It had been a hard, cold, bone-pressing grip since the fountain, and now it loosened, a little: her fingers eased, and Elsie's knuckles stopped aching. Tilly was looking at the flowers. She had stopped watching the ceiling. A little way further she let go of the wall altogether and put her free hand out instead, and touched one of the glowing bells as she passed, very lightly, with one fingertip, and looked at the light on her finger. Her grip loosened a little more.

The bleating got louder as they went, closer and crosser, and led them in, vault by vault, toward the heart of the floor. The arches got older. The brick gave way to great dressed stones, and the flowers grew thicker, until the walls were solid with them and the light was bright enough to read by. Then the long vault ended in a round chamber, deep and old, with a stone rim round a sunken floor and a domed roof high overhead: an old cistern room, Tamsin said. The bleating came from inside.

But they could not get in. Across the arched doorway of the cistern room the stranglevine had grown in a thick green web, from the floor to the top of the arch and from one side to the other. Its strands were as thick as Elsie's wrist in places and as thin as string in others, glossy and dark, woven over and under and through each other in a great tangled mesh, with the light of the flowers coming through the gaps in green-gold slivers. It hung quite still. Elsie stood well back from it.

Through the gaps she could see into the room. The sunken floor of the old cistern was carpeted thick with the glowing flowers, more of them than anywhere yet, a pool of silver-green light, and in the middle of the pool stood a goat. She was white, and plump, and she was eating. She had her head down in the flowers and was tearing them up in mouthfuls and chewing with great contentment, her jaw going round and round. And she was glowing too. Faintly, all over, silver-green, from her nose to her stubby tail, like a lantern turned down low.

Elsie laughed out loud. She could not help it. It came up out of her and rang round the old stones, and Clover lifted her head and looked at them through the vine and bleated, with a mouthful of shining flowers hanging out of the side of her mouth. "She's lit up," Elsie said, helpless with it, holding on to Tilly. "Oh, look at her, she's eaten so many of them she's lit up. She's a lantern. She's a goat lantern." Tamsin snorted. Even Tilly giggled, a small surprised hiccup of a giggle, and clapped her free hand over her mouth.

"Clover," Elsie called softly. "Clover, come here, sweetheart." The goat looked at her and went on chewing. There was a gap in the web at about the height of Elsie's shoulder, a loose place where the strands bowed apart, and through it she could almost reach. She let go of Tilly's hand. She stepped close and put her arm through the gap, carefully, reaching for the goat's collar, stretching her fingers toward it. The vine moved. A strand slid round her wrist, smooth and cool and quick as a snake, and closed.

Elsie gasped and pulled. The strand tightened. And then something touched her ankle from below, low down by her boot, and she looked down and saw a second strand of vine curling round it above the boot top, winding up her bare shin, and it pulled. It pulled her forward, hard. Her body went against the web, her front pressed flat to the green mesh from her breasts to her knees, her cheek against a cool glossy strand, her arm through the gap to the shoulder. She could not move. She was held fast.

Tamsin swore and swung Margery up off her shoulder in both hands, high, the great blade gleaming in the glow, her feet planted. Then she stopped with the axe in the air. Elsie, with her cheek against the vine, saw her face change. "I can't," Tamsin said. "I can't swing. It's round her too close." The strand at Elsie's wrist ran right along her arm; the strand at her ankle ran up her leg against the skin. Anywhere the axe went, Elsie was. Tamsin lowered Margery slowly, and her knuckles were white on the haft.

Cora came past Tamsin in two long strides. She did not hesitate. She took hold of the strand round Elsie's wrist with both gloved hands, close in against Elsie's skin, and set her boots and pulled. Elsie felt the gloves hard against her wrist and the heat of Cora's body at her side, close, and smelled the clean starched smell of her coat. Cora's face was a hand's breadth from her own, set and white, her grey eyes fixed on the vine. She pulled until her breath came short. The vine held. It did not give at all.

"Leave it," Tamsin said, low and rough. "Cora. Leave it, you won't shift it." She was gripping Margery so hard her arms shook. "Stranglevine holds until it's cut or it's burnt. That's all. Cut or burnt. It doesn't tire and it doesn't let go." Cora let go of the strand, slowly, and stood with her gloved hands open and empty, and looked at Elsie pinned against the web, and her face did a thing Elsie had not seen it do: it went frightened. Elsie smiled at her from the vine to show it was all right.

Tilly did not wait to be asked. She had been standing frozen a step behind them with both hands pressed to her mouth, and now her hands came down and went to her belt. They were shaking so badly that the vials rattled in their loops. She pulled one vial free, and dropped it, and caught it before it hit the stone, and pulled another, one clear and one a deep cloudy blue. Her lips were moving. "Burnt," she was saying under her breath. "Burnt, burnt. I can do burnt. I can do burnt."

She bit the cork out of the clear vial and spat it, and thumbed the cork off the blue one, and tipped the clear into the blue, a thin bright trickle. The two ran together in the glass and turned purple, then black, then clear as water again. And then, with a small soft sound like someone blowing out a breath, a flame stood up in the neck of the vial. It was blue, a small, steady, perfect blue flame no bigger than a thumb, standing straight up out of the glass, without a wick.

Tilly's hands went still. Elsie, with her cheek against the vine, watched it happen: the shaking went out of them, all at once, the moment the flame stood up, the way it goes out of a musician's hands when they touch the strings. Tilly stepped in close, steady as a surgeon, and her face had gone quite calm and intent. She held the little blue flame to the strand of vine at Elsie's wrist, close, so close, a finger's width from Elsie's skin. Elsie felt the heat of it, sharp and small. She did not flinch. She watched Tilly's face.

The vine hissed. Where the flame touched it the glossy green went brown, and then black, and curled, and smoked, and the strand round Elsie's wrist jerked and loosened and fell away from her in a blackened coil. Her hand came free. Tilly was already crouching. She held the flame to the strand on Elsie's ankle, low, careful, close against Elsie's bare shin, and it blackened and shrank and let go, and dropped off her leg. Elsie stumbled back from the web, free, rubbing her wrist. The skin was pink where the vine had been and quite unburnt.

Tilly stayed crouched on the floor where she was. She was holding the vial up in front of her face, at arm's length, and staring at the little blue flame standing up out of it as if somebody else had made it, and handed it to her, and walked away. Her mouth was open. The flame burned steady and blue in her steady hand and lit her round face from below, and the soot on her cheek, and her wide blue eyes. "It worked," she whispered. "It went right."

Elsie dropped to her knees on the stone and threw her arms round her. She hugged her hard, both arms round Tilly's narrow shoulders and her cheek against Tilly's hair, careful of the flame, held well out to one side. Tilly was small and warm in her arms and her heart was going so fast Elsie could feel it through both their chests. "You're a genius," Elsie said into her hair. "You're an actual genius. Tilly, you are. You did that with two bottles. I love you. I love you, you clever thing. You got me out."

Tilly went pink. Elsie felt her go rigid in her arms and then saw it when she drew back: pink from the collar of her blouse up her throat and over her round cheeks and her forehead to the roots of her hair, deep and hot, every inch of her that showed. Her lips were parted. She was staring at Elsie from very close, as if she could not look anywhere else, and she was holding the little flame out at the full length of her arm, away from the both of them, very carefully, the way someone holds a full cup in a crowd.

Tamsin stepped up to the web with Margery. "Mind out," she said, and Elsie pulled Tilly back out of the way. Then Tamsin swung. She swung three times, short and clean and terribly fast: one stroke down the left side of the arch, and one down the right, and one straight across the top, and the whole great web of stranglevine came down off the doorway in a single tangled heap on the stones, writhing a little, and lay still. She set Margery's head down on the floor and leaned on the haft and grinned at them, breathing hard.

Clover lifted her head out of the flowers. She looked at the open door, and at the four of them standing in it, and then she came trotting out across the cistern floor and up the steps toward them, glowing, with her ears flapping and a few flowers still hanging out of her mouth. She came straight to Elsie. Elsie picked a glowing bell off the wall and held it out on her flat palm, and Clover ate it out of her hand with a soft lipping mouth and a great deal of noise, and then butted her in the stomach for another.

Clover wore a leather collar with an empty ring on it. Elsie got a roll of bandage out of her satchel and threaded the end of it through the collar ring and knotted it, twice, and wound the rest round her own hand for a lead. "There," she said. "Come on, Clover. Home." She walked off a few steps toward the vault, and the goat looked after her, and then trotted after her, as good as a dog, glowing at her heels, and pushing her nose into the back of Elsie's knee every few steps to see if there were more flowers.

Elsie glanced back at the cistern door. Cora had stopped beside it. She had the black ledger open on her palm, and her pen out, and she was writing. Elsie watched her write one line, in the quick small hand. Then she watched the pen go on, to a second line, and a third, without lifting, the pen moving steadily down the page while the goat butted at Elsie's knee and Tamsin hummed and Tilly looked at her flame. Cora wrote and wrote. It was more than one line this time. It was a good deal more.

Elsie opened her mouth to ask. But before she could, Cora shut the ledger, with a small exact snap, and tucked it under her arm, and capped her pen. She looked at Elsie a moment, a short grey look that Elsie could not read. Then she turned and walked off ahead of them all down the long vault toward the stair, straight-backed, her boots quiet on the stone, without a word, the charcoal coat going silver-green in the light of the flowers. Elsie closed her mouth. Clover butted her knee, and she followed.

They came up out of the fountain into the afternoon, round and round the stair and over the rim of the dry basin into the market, blinking, into the gold light pouring through the glass roof. Tamsin came first with Margery, and then Cora, and then Elsie with the goat on her bandage, and then Tilly last, with her flame put out and corked. Clover hopped up over the rim after Elsie, and stood in the sunlight in the middle of the flower market, white and plump and still very faintly glowing, and bleated at everyone.

The clerk on his stool at the stair head took his pen from behind his ear to write them out, and opened his ledger, and found their names, and then looked up to count them. He counted four, and then he saw the goat. He stopped with his pen in the air. He looked at Clover for a long moment, glowing faintly in the sunshine at Elsie's knee, and Clover looked back at him and chewed. "Four out," he said at last, slowly, and wrote it down, and then he went on staring at the goat while the ink dried.

Across the market, behind her stall of sweet peas, the flower seller looked up. Elsie saw her see them. She dropped her shears. They clattered on the cobbles, and she left them there, and ran: straight out from behind her stall and down the row, through the shoppers, her green apron flying. "Clover!" The goat bleated and pulled the bandage out of Elsie's hand and ran to meet her. The woman fell to her knees on the cobbles in the middle of the market and put both arms round the goat's neck and held on, with her face in the white fur.

When she could, she wiped her face on her apron and asked for the notice, and Elsie gave it to her, and she signed it on her knee there on the cobbles, crying, with Clover nibbling the corner of the paper. Then she got up and went to her stall and came back with four flowers, one for each of them: a sweet pea for Tamsin and one for Tilly, a white rose for Cora, who took it as if it were a summons, and for Elsie a pink carnation, the colour of her hair. Elsie held hers to her nose and wished her window open, to see.

They sat on the canal wall outside in the late afternoon, the three of them in a row, with their boots hanging out over the water. The stone was warm from the whole day's sun.
