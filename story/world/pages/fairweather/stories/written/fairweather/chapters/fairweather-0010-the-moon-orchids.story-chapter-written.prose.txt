Elsie woke to the bread smell coming up through the boards, warm and yeasty, and lay for a moment with her eyes shut, breathing it in. The round window was pale blue. Below it a gull was complaining on the gutter. She got up, washed her face in the cold basin and began to dress. Somewhere between the halter top and the second boot she found she was humming: the caravan song, the one the drivers sang on the long flat stretches south of the hills, about a lamp in a window. She did not know all the words. She hummed the parts she did not know and sang the parts she did, softly, lacing the front of her top. The gull stopped complaining to listen.

She took the chipped jug and knelt by the crate under the window. She watered the plants one by one, saying good morning to each. Mrs Pennywhistle had a new curl of frond. Captain needed pinching back. When she came to Doris the aloe she stopped and sat back on her heels.

"Doris, listen. You'll never guess. There were eels yesterday, under the baths, and one came straight at me, and Cora stood right in front of me. It bit her, here, on the arm, and she didn't make a sound. Not a peep. And then she let me bandage it." She touched one of Doris's fat grey-green leaves with her fingertip. "You'd have been useful. You'd have liked her. She's very tidy, like you."

Doris was silent. Elsie gave her an extra splash for listening and set the jug down.

Downstairs the bakery was full of heat and the clatter of trays. Agnes was at the counter in her floury apron. She looked up when Elsie came down the narrow stairs. She looked past her at the stairs, then back at Elsie, with one eyebrow up.

"Just me." Elsie laughed. "Four honey buns, please. They're for the others."

She counted four copper pips out of her purse onto the counter, one, two, three, four. Agnes swept them into her palm and took four buns off the tray, glossy and steaming and smelling of honey. She twisted each into its own square of paper. She put them in a bag and the bag in Elsie's hands. It was warm right through. Elsie held it against her chest all the way out of the door.

The guild hall was already filling when she got there, loud with boots and talk under the high timber roof, the morning light coming in long and dusty through the windows. Cora was at the board. She stood a little apart from the crowd in front of it, straight-backed in her charcoal coat, reading the F-rank notices with her ledger open in her hands. Her hair was back in its low smooth bun with every strand in place, as if yesterday at the baths had happened to someone else. She turned a page, and her cuff slid back an inch. Elsie saw the white edge of a bandage under it. A fresh one. Not the one Elsie had tied.

"Cora!" Elsie came through the crowd with the warm bag held against her chest. "Good morning. You've changed it. Let me see."

Cora turned. Her grey eyes went to Elsie's face, then to the bag, then back to her face. "Good morning, Fairweather."

Before Elsie had even held out her hand, she shifted the ledger to the crook of her other arm and unbuttoned her cuff. She rolled it back, neat and quick, to the elbow, and held out her bare forearm, palm up, between them. She did not ask why, or say it was fine. She stood there in the middle of the guild hall with her arm held out to Elsie and waited.

Elsie set the bag down on the ledge under the board. She took Cora's wrist in both hands and turned it to the light. She peeled back the edge of the new bandage, gently, with her thumbnail. The ring of punctures underneath had closed. The skin round them was pink, not red, and cool. There was no swelling, no heat and no smell. Elsie let out a breath. "Oh, good. It's healing clean, Cora. Look. That's lovely. That's just how it should be." She smoothed the bandage back down and pressed it flat with her fingertips. Cora looked down at her own arm in Elsie's hands. She took it back and rolled the cuff down again, slowly, one fold at a time, and did up the button.

"They do," came Tamsin's voice, loud, from the door. "They've got little holes."

"That's not ears. That's just holes. A hole isn't an ear. A drain's got a hole."

They came in together through the crowd, both of them talking at once. Tamsin was in her battered jerkin with Margery on her back, her curls tied up anyhow. Tilly was half a step behind with her goggles on her head and a fresh singe on one braid.

"It hears through the holes. So it's an ear."

"Then a teapot has ears."

"A teapot does have ears, Tilly, it's got a spout and a—"

"That's a handle!"

They arrived at the board still arguing. Tilly saw Elsie, went pink and stopped mid-word.

"Honey buns." Elsie picked up the bag and opened it. "One each. I got them hot." She took the top one out in its twist of paper. Before anyone else, she turned and held it out to Cora. Cora looked at it a moment. She took it, held it in her bandaged hand without unwrapping it, and inclined her head. Elsie gave Tamsin hers. Tamsin had the paper off and half the bun in her mouth in one movement.

"Bleff you."

Elsie gave Tilly hers. Tilly held it in both hands like something alive. "Thank you," she told the bun, and then, to Elsie, "Thank you." She went a shade pinker.

They stood in a little knot in front of the F-rank board, eating. Elsie licked honey off her thumb and looked up at the notices. The eels were gone, a clean rectangle of paler wood where the paper had been. Two of yesterday's notices were still up. One was the glasshouse: a night's watch over a glasshouse of moon orchids, out on the lake road, against thieves and moths, dusk to dawn, three lanterns. The other was the fireworks: four crates to be carried from a maker on Powder Lane to the festival barge out on the Mere, delivered whole, four lanterns. Under that, in red, in a big careful hand, someone had written: Do Not Drop.

Tilly had stopped chewing. She was staring at the fireworks notice with her mouth a little open and a crumb on her lip. She read it once under her breath, then again. Elsie watched her lips shape the red words: do not drop. The colour went right out of her face, as pale as Cora. Her eyes went very wide and very bright, and she clutched the honey bun against her apron with both hands.

"Fireworks," she whispered. "Real ones. Made ones. Elsie, those are star mixes. Those are somebody's star mixes in a crate, and they want us to carry them." She looked at Elsie, white to the lips and shining. "I think I might faint."

"Don't faint on the board." Tamsin swallowed the last of her bun. She leaned in past Tilly and read both notices with her finger, the glasshouse and the fireworks. She straightened up, wiped her hands on her trousers and grinned her crooked grin. "We can do both. Easy. The fireworks want carrying by day, yes? And the orchids want watching by night. Dusk to dawn. So we carry the fireworks this morning, and we sleep the afternoon, and we sit up with the orchids tonight." She spread her hands. "Fireworks by day. Orchids by night. Two notices, one day. Why not?"

Elsie looked at the two notices and at Tamsin. Her face lit. "That's two. Tamsin, that's two more. That's eight and nine." She held up her hands and counted, fast, on her fingers, the way she had yesterday. "Seven with the eels. Eight with the fireworks. Nine with the orchids. So that's only one left after tonight. One more, and I'm E-rank, and we can take your trolls."

Tilly made a small noise. She was up on her toes, then down, then up again, bouncing, the bun forgotten in her hands, the colour coming back into her face in two bright patches. "Two. Two in one day. And one of them's fireworks."

The clerk at the long counter was the same thin man with ink on his cuffs. He took both notices from Tamsin, read them, read the party's slate, and entered them in his great book in a slow, upright hand: the fireworks, the glasshouse, the party's name twice. He blotted the page. "Fireworks are to be on the barge by noon. The glasshouse is from dusk. Don't be late for either." Cora had come to stand at Elsie's shoulder. She opened her own black ledger on the end of the counter and wrote in it too, small and exact: the date, the two notices and the four names. Watching her pen move, Elsie saw her write Fairweather, pause, and then go on.

Powder Lane ran down toward the water behind the dyers' quarter, narrow and cobbled. It smelled of sulphur. Elsie wrinkled her nose at the corner. It was a sharp, yellow, eggy smell. It hung in the lane like weather and got into the back of her throat. Tamsin sneezed. Cora's mouth went flat. Tilly stopped dead in the middle of the cobbles and shut her eyes. She breathed in, slowly, all the way, the way Elsie's mother breathed in over a rose bush at the edge of a village. She let it out. "Oh. Oh, smell that. That's saltpetre. That's good saltpetre."

The firework maker's shop was a low stone place at the bottom of the lane with iron shutters and a sign shaped like a star. The maker himself came out to meet them, wiping his hands on a leather apron scorched in a dozen places: an old man, small, brown and wiry, with a fringe of white hair and no eyebrows to speak of. There were only two pink shiny patches where eyebrows had been, and a few stubborn singed hairs. He looked at the guild tags and nodded. "Four." He pointed with his chin at the shade of the wall. Four wooden crates stood there in a row, packed round with golden straw that stuck out between the slats. "Those four. Mind them."

Each crate had been stencilled on every side, in red paint, in letters as tall as Elsie's hand: DO NOT DROP. Tilly went down the row, crouching to read the low ones. "Do not drop. Do not drop. Do not drop." She went round the back of the last crate to read that side too. "Do not drop." She stood up and looked at the others with shining eyes. "That's sixteen. Sixteen do-not-drops. He means it."

The maker, leaning in his doorway, looked at Tilly properly then, for a long moment, with his head on one side. One of his scorched cheeks twitched.

"You've a nose for it."

Tilly went scarlet. "What do you use for your reds?"

"Strontium. What else would I use?"

"And do you bind your stars with rice paste or gum?"

"Gum. Rice paste cracks in the damp. Any fool knows that."

"I used rice paste," Tilly said. "It cracked."

He barked a laugh. After that they were away. They stood in the shop door talking about strontium, copper and charcoal ground fine as flour, about fuse rates and how wet was too wet, faster and faster, Tilly's hands flying, the old man's hands flying back. Tamsin sat down on a crate's edge and yawned. Cora stood. Elsie leaned on the wall in the sun and watched Tilly's face. She could not stop smiling.

At last the maker held up one scorched finger and went into the dark of the shop. He came back with a little twist of brown paper, folded small and tight, and put it into Tilly's hand. "Blue star. My mix. Try it on a small flame, a pinch, no more, and stand back, and tell me if you can find what's in it." Tilly looked down at the twist of paper in her palm. She did not close her hand on it. She held it there, flat and open and very still, as if it were a ring someone had just given her. Her eyes, when she looked up at him, were wet. "Thank you," she whispered.

The crates were next. Tamsin stood up, bent and took one in each arm, as easily as two loaves. She settled them against her hips, straw sticking out round her elbows. Elsie and Tilly took one between them, a rope handle each.

"On three," Tilly whispered. "One. Two. Three."

They lifted it together, carefully. It was heavier than it looked. Cora put her ledger inside her coat, bent and lifted the last one alone, straight-backed, her bandaged arm under its weight. Elsie opened her mouth.

"It's fine," Cora said.

They went back up Powder Lane and into the streets at a crawl. Tamsin led, crates on her hips. Elsie and Tilly came next, the crate swinging gently between them. Cora came last, watching all of them.

"Kerb," Tilly said, low and urgent, as if it were a wolf. "Kerb. Step. Kerb coming. Kerb. Mind the cobble, that one's loose, Elsie, mind it. Bridge. Bridge going up. Kerb."

People stepped out of their way. They looked at the red letters on the crates and stepped further. A woman pulled her child back into a doorway. Elsie bit her lip to keep from laughing. She minded the cobble.

"Thank you, Tilly. Thank you, Tilly. Thank you, Tilly."

On the Brightwater Bridge a dog came out of an alley at a run, a big scruffy yellow dog with its ears flat. It went straight for Tamsin's legs, barking fit to burst. Tamsin stopped. She did not kick it, or shout, or put down a crate. She stood absolutely still in the middle of the bridge with a crate on each hip, her face screwed up, her eyes on the sky, rigid as a statue in a square while the dog circled her boots and barked and barked. "Good dog," she said through her teeth, to the sky. "Lovely dog. Go away, dog." The dog barked twice more and sniffed her boot. It lost interest and trotted off. Tamsin let out her breath. Tilly applauded with her free hand.

The Lantern Steps went down into the water at the end of the lake road, broad and worn and green at the bottom where the Mere lapped them. A boatman sat on the lowest dry one with his pipe, by a long flat rowing boat. He looked at the crates and at the red letters, and took his pipe out of his mouth. "Do not drop. Right." He set the crates in the boat himself, one by one, with great care, two in the bow and two amidships. They climbed in round them. Elsie sat with a crate between her knees. The boatman pushed off from the step with an oar and settled. He began to row them out over the Mere toward the barge, slow and steady.

The Mere was flat as a plate and shining. The morning sun lay on it in a long white road that ran out from the boat to the far shore. The city went small behind them, all its bridges, roofs and copper domes. The air was cool and clean and smelled of water instead of sulphur. Elsie leaned over the side. She let her hand down into the lake and trailed her fingers through it as they went. The water was cold and silky and parted round her fingers in four little silver wakes. A fish flicked away under the surface. She laughed out loud. "Oh, it's lovely. Tilly, look, put your hand in, it's like silk."

Tilly did not put her hand in. Tilly was sitting very upright on the middle thwart with a crate in her lap, both arms locked round it, her eyes shut tight. Her knuckles were white on the slats. Her lips were moving. Elsie leaned closer and heard her counting under her breath, very fast, losing her place and starting again.

"Tilly?"

"I'm fine. I'm very fine. I'm holding the crate. I'm not looking at the water. The water's there, I know it's there, I'm not looking at it."

The boat rocked as the boatman pulled. Tilly made a small sound and held the crate tighter, and went on counting.

Elsie took her hand out of the lake and dried it on her skirt. She slid along the thwart until she was hip to hip with Tilly, put her hand under Tilly's elbow and held it. Just held it, firm and warm, through the sleeve. "There. I've got you. You can't go anywhere. If you go over, I go over, and I'm not going over, so." Tilly stopped counting. After a moment, slowly, she opened one eye. She did not look at the water. She turned her head, just a little, and looked at Elsie instead, from very close: one round blue eye, wide, and Elsie's face in it. Tilly's elbow was trembling in Elsie's hand. She kept looking at Elsie all the way to the barge.

The festival barge was enormous, a great flat-bottomed thing moored out on the open water, half-built. Its deck was a forest of new timber and scaffolding. Carpenters were everywhere, hammering, sawing, shouting to each other, hauling beams up on ropes. The whole barge rang with it across the water like a bell tower. Everywhere, strung from every rail, spar and pole, there were lanterns: hundreds of paper lanterns in every colour, red, gold, green and violet. All of them were unlit, swaying gently in the breeze off the Mere and knocking softly together. Elsie stared up at them with her mouth open as the boat bumped against the hull. "Oh. Oh, imagine them lit."

A rope ladder came down. The barge master came to the rail to meet them, a big bald man with a red beard and a voice that carried over the hammering. He had the crates hauled up in a net, one at a time, and set down on the deck. He went over them himself. He pulled the straw back from each one, looked inside and counted. He lifted out a long paper tube, turned it in his fingers and put it back. Every crate. Every tube. Tilly watched every movement of his hands with her heart in her face. At the last crate he straightened up and nodded. "All whole. Not a crack." He fished in his coat and paid them on the spot.

He paid it into Elsie's palm: three silver lanterns and a little stack of copper pips, ten of them, counted out one by one with a thick thumb. Elsie gave Tamsin a lantern and Tilly a lantern, and kept one for herself. After that, the pips. She gave Tamsin three and Tilly three, and put three in her own purse. One copper pip was left in her open hand. She held it out to Tilly. "Odd one's yours. Same as yesterday." Tilly looked at the pip. She took it and closed her hand round it with the other three and the lantern. She held her fist against her apron, next to the twist of blue-star paper in her pocket, looked at Elsie, and smiled.

"Now then." The barge master tucked his purse away and looked round at the four of them: at Tamsin with straw in her curls, Tilly holding her fist to her apron, Cora with her ledger and Elsie staring up at the lanterns. "You lot from the city? You'll be coming, then. Midsummer Lantern Night. The twentieth." He jerked his thumb at the scaffolding, the lanterns and the hammering. "This is all for that. You should come. Don't miss it. You'd be daft to miss it."

Elsie looked down from the lanterns. "What happens? What's Lantern Night?"

The barge master looked at her as if she had asked what water was.

"What happens? The whole Mere fills up, that's what happens. Every boat in Lanternmere comes out, every skiff and punt and barge and washtub, all of them hung with lanterns, and they all put out on the water at dusk, hundreds of them, till you can't see the lake for lights. And then when it's full dark, from here—" he stamped on the deck, "—we send up the fireworks. Your crates. Right over the top of all those boats. Gold and red and blue, and the whole lake lights up under them like it's on fire, and every boat on the Mere goes oooh." He grinned through his beard. "Grand night. Ask anybody."

Elsie turned round to the others. Her face was hot, her eyes were stinging, and she did not care. She looked at Tamsin, at Tilly and at Cora. Tamsin looked at her face and laughed out loud.

"We're coming. Course we're coming. All of us. We'll get a boat. We'll get a lantern as big as a barrel and hang it on the front, and Tilly can tell the boatman about every kerb in the lake."

"I would not—oh, a boat, Tamsin, with lanterns—"

"All of us," Tamsin said again, and looked at Cora.

Cora had her ledger open. She did not look up from it. "I'll be on duty."

Elsie stepped round in front of her, so that Cora had to look up from the page and see her. "Then you'll be on duty in a boat. With us. You can watch me just as well from a boat. Better, really. You'll be right there. I'll be right next to you the whole time, you won't have to look for me at all." She smiled at Cora, wide and certain.

Cora's pen had stopped. She looked at Elsie for a long moment, then down at the ledger. The pen did not move again.

The boatman rowed them back across the shining water with the empty boat riding high. Tilly sat with her eyes open this time, both of them, though she held on to the thwart with one hand and to Elsie's sleeve with the other. The moment the boat bumped against the Lantern Steps she was out of it. She went up two steps onto the dry stone and knelt down on it, there and then, in her apron. She patted it with both hands, flat, the way someone pats a horse. "Good. Good, solid, still step." The boatman, tying up, threw back his head and laughed till he had to take out his pipe. Tilly went crimson and went on patting.

The Tipsy Heron was cool and dim and half empty at that hour, smelling of last night's ale and this morning's sawdust. They took the table in the window. Elsie went up to the bar. "A pot of tea for four, please." She counted two copper pips from her purse onto the wet wood. The pot came big and brown and steaming with four thick cups. She carried it back herself. She poured, put sugar in Cora's, one spoon, and stirred it. She pushed it across without asking. Tamsin wrapped both hands round her cup and sighed into the steam. Tilly blew on hers in little careful puffs.

Cora set down her cup. "The glasshouse watch starts at dusk. Dusk till dawn. That's the whole night awake, and you've been up since dawn." She looked round the table at each of them in turn. "You should sleep this afternoon. All of you. Properly, in a bed, not in a chair. A watch is no use if the watcher is asleep on her feet by midnight." She picked up her cup again. "That's not a suggestion." Tamsin saluted her with her teacup. Elsie nodded, solemn. She yawned, enormously.

"I can't sleep in the day." Tilly turned her cup in her hands. "Sorry. I just can't. I lie there. It's too bright, and I start thinking about things, star mixes and fuses, and then it's evening and I've only thought." She looked at the twist of blue-star paper she had set beside her cup. "I'll just think about this all afternoon."

Tamsin leaned back in her chair until it creaked, stretched her arms over her head and grinned. "Watch me. Sleep's easy. Lie down, shut your eyes, done. I could sleep on a fence."

Cora looked at her over the rim of her cup. "I believe it."

Cora finished her tea and set the cup down square in its ring. She stood and buttoned her coat to the throat. "I have the morning to write up. I'll be in the Wardens' room." She tucked the ledger under her bandaged arm. "I'll meet you there at dusk, where the notice says." She paused, and looked at Elsie. "Sleep. Fairweather." She went out through the Heron's door into the bright square and across the bridge toward the guild hall, straight-backed. Elsie watched her through the window until the crowd took her.

Tamsin had swung her boots up onto the long back bench under the window. She lay down along it and put Margery on the floor beside her where she could reach her. She folded her arms across her chest and shut her eyes. "See? Easy." Her breathing went long and slow. Her mouth fell a little open. In the space of one breath she was asleep, with a piece of straw still in her curls and the sun on her freckles. A moment later a small kettle sound began in her throat. Tilly stared at her. Elsie laughed softly and tucked the straw out of Tamsin's hair. The two of them crept out and left her there.

Out on the canal path Tilly walked beside Elsie with her hands knotted together in front of her apron. She did not head for Pickle Row. At the corner where she should have turned off she stopped instead and looked at the cobbles.

"Elsie, could I— I can't sleep at mine, my room smells of sulphur, it's in the curtains from last week, from the star mix, and it's in the pillow, and I'll just lie there smelling it and thinking about fuses, and I wondered, if it's not— if I could nap at yours. In the attic. Just for the afternoon. I'll be very quiet. I won't take up any room." She went red to the ears.

"Of course."

The attic was warm and drowsy with the afternoon, the light coming slant and gold through the round window onto the floorboards. Elsie kicked off her boots and lay down on the narrow bed, on the side by the wall. She patted the quilt. Tilly took off her boots too and set them side by side, neatly. She took off her goggles and set them on the boots, then lay down on the very edge of the bed. On the very edge. She lay on her back with her arms straight down at her sides and her whole body stiff as a board, a good hand's width of quilt between her and Elsie, half of her hanging out over the floor. She stared up at the ceiling.

Elsie laughed and propped herself up on one elbow. "Tilly. You'll fall out. You're halfway on the floor already. Come here." She reached over and took Tilly by the waist, by the apron strings. She pulled her in across the quilt, the whole stiff length of her, until Tilly's hip bumped against Elsie's and Tilly's shoulder was against her chest. "There. Now you're in the bed." Tilly's singed braid had come loose across her cheek. Elsie tucked it back behind her ear with one finger, smoothing it down. Her fingertip brushed the rim of Tilly's ear, which was hot as a stove lid. "That's better." She lay back down with her head on the pillow beside Tilly's.

Tilly lay very still. She lay on her back exactly where Elsie had put her, with her hip touching Elsie's hip and her arms straight at her sides. She looked up at the sloped ceiling of the attic, at the boards, the beams and the slant of gold light across them. She did not move at all. Elsie could feel her breathing. It was very careful, Tilly's breathing: in, slowly, as if she were counting it, then a pause, out, slowly, and a pause, as if each breath had to be measured out and set down very gently so as not to spill. Elsie could feel Tilly's heart going under the apron, quick and light, like a bird's in a cupped hand.

"See," Elsie began, "it's not so bad. And you have to tell me about the blue star, and I'll tell you about the time I slept in a hay cart—" But the bed was warm and the light was gold. She had been up since before the gulls. She had carried a crate of fireworks across half of Lanternmere and rowed out over the Mere and back. Her eyes were heavy. She turned on her side toward Tilly. Her arm went out across Tilly's waist on its own, the way it would have gone across her mother's, loose and warm, and settled there, over the apron. Her cheek found the pillow. Within a few breaths she was asleep.

When Elsie woke, the light had moved. It lay across the far wall now instead of the ceiling, still warm and gold. The bread smell from below had changed to the afternoon smell, crust and cinnamon. She opened her eyes. Tilly was awake. Tilly was lying on her side, facing her, very close, her head on the same pillow. She was looking at Elsie: just looking, with her round blue eyes wide open and soft, from a hand's breadth away, as if she had been looking for a long time. Elsie's arm was still across her waist. Their eyes met. Tilly went scarlet, all at once, from the throat up, so fast Elsie could see it climb.

"Did you sleep?" Elsie smiled at her.

Tilly swallowed. "I counted the boards." It came out high and odd. She cleared her throat and tried again. "In the ceiling. I counted them. There are forty-one. Well, forty and a half, there's a half one by the window. And then I counted them again, to be sure, and it was the same. So that's twice. All of them, twice." She was still scarlet. She was not looking at the ceiling now, or at the boards; she was looking at Elsie's mouth, then at her eyes, then quickly at the pillow. "So I didn't really sleep. But I didn't mind."

They ate at the round window, sitting on the floor with their backs against the wall and the plant crate between them: bread from below, a wedge of hard yellow cheese Elsie cut with her little knife, and two of Tilly's pear fizzes, brought in her satchel and gone warm. The canal went gold under the window. Elsie knelt by her healer's satchel and packed it for the night, carefully, the way her mother had taught her: the pot of salve, clean linen rolled tight, bandages, the little scissors, a needle and thread, a twist of willow bark. She looked at the bandages and thought of Cora's arm. She put in an extra roll.

At sunset they walked out along the lake road. The city fell behind them, the houses spreading out into gardens and orchards. The Mere lay on their left all the way, going from gold to rose to violet as the sun went down behind the hills. The air smelled of cut grass and water. Tilly walked close to Elsie, so close their arms brushed. "And the blue, it's not copper, it can't be only copper, there's something under it—" Elsie listened and swung the satchel. At a bend in the road a high old brick wall began, running along beside them, with fruit trees showing over the top of it. Over the trees, catching the last of the light, rose the long white-framed ridge of a glasshouse, shining like a lantern.

The gate was green and arched, set into the wall, and two figures were waiting by it with an old woman between them. Tamsin was leaning on the wall with Margery over her shoulder and her curls tied up, looking rested and pleased with herself. She raised a hand when she saw them. Cora stood straight beside the gate in her charcoal coat with her ledger, exactly where she had said she would be, at exactly the time. The old woman was small, bent and brown as a walnut, in a straw hat and a gardener's apron, with a trowel stuck in her pocket and soil under her nails. She peered at Elsie and Tilly as they came up, and nodded.

"These the last of you?" The grower's voice was high and creaky, like a gate. "Good. Good. You'll do." She pushed the green gate open on its squealing hinges and stood aside for them. "Now listen, because I'll say it once. My moon orchids open tonight. Tonight, at moonrise, and that's it. One night in the year. They open when the moon comes up over that wall, and they glow all night, silver as a fish's belly, and by dawn they're shut again and that's that till next summer." She looked round at all four of them, fierce. "One night. A year of growing for one night. So you mind them."

She held up two crooked fingers. "There's two things'll come for them. Thieves, for one. A moon orchid bloom fetches a lantern in the city, and the whole town knows it's tonight, so they'll come over the wall. They do every year. Young ones, mostly, looking to get rich." She folded down one finger. "And lamp moths. Great soft things, big as your hand." She held up her palm to show them. "They come to drink from the flowers when they open. Can't help it, the light draws them. And they bruise the petals, sitting on them, and a bruised bloom goes brown by morning and won't fetch a pip." She folded the other finger. "So. Thieves and moths."

She led them up the garden path between beds of lettuce and beans to the glasshouse door. She opened it and showed them in with a sweep of her trowel. Just inside, at the near end, there was a little iron stove with a kettle on it and a scuttle of coal. There was a shelf with a tea caddy and some chipped cups, and against the wall a single narrow cot with a grey blanket folded at its foot. "Stove. Kettle. Tea's in the tin. Cot's for whoever's not watching." She pointed at each with the trowel. "I'm up at the house if the sky falls in. Don't wake me for less." She went off up the dark garden toward a lit window among the fruit trees. The door of the house shut behind her.

Inside, the glasshouse was long and warm and close, much longer than it had looked from the road, a great tunnel of white-painted iron ribs and panes going away into the dusk. The air was thick, green, sweet and damp, like breath. On either side, on long slatted tables, and hanging from the ribs above in moss baskets, there were orchids, row on row on row of them, hundreds of plants, their leaves dark, glossy and broad. On every plant, on long arching stems, there were buds: fat, pale, closed buds, as long as Elsie's thumb, folded tight as fists, all down the glasshouse as far as she could see. Elsie walked between them slowly with her hands clasped behind her back, so she would not touch.

They took their places. Tamsin planted herself at the door with Margery, the great axe head resting on the floor and her hands crossed on the haft. Her back was to the frame, where she could see the whole garden, the wall and the gate. "Anyone comes through here comes through me." Cora took the wall side, the long side of the glasshouse that faced the garden wall, and walked it once from end to end, slowly, looking out through every pane. She stood at the far end where she could see the whole length of it. She took out her ledger and her pencil, opened the ledger to a clean page, and waited.

Tilly went straight to the potting bench at the near end, by the stove. She cleared a space among the trowels, twine and clay pots, and unbuckled her satchel. She took out vials one after another and set them in a row: a green one, a cloudy one, a yellow one, a tiny brown one corked with wax, a little brass burner, a twist of paper, a glass rod. She lined them up by size, then by colour, then by size again, her tongue between her teeth. "I'm going to finish my smoke tonight. My moth smoke. The one that won't hurt anything. I've nearly got it." She pushed her goggles down over her eyes. "Tonight I'm going to finish it."

The dusk went blue, then deep blue, then dark. The stove ticked. Tilly's burner made a small blue flame on the bench. Over the top of the garden wall, beyond the black shapes of the fruit trees, the sky went pale, and paler. The edge of the moon came up: huge and low and the colour of cream, so close it looked as if it were resting on the wall. Its light came down through the glass roof in long cool bars. It touched the nearest orchid bud. The bud moved. It loosened as Elsie watched. A seam opened down it, and the petals began to unfold, slowly, one after another, the way a hand opens. The next bud along began, and the next, and the next.

They opened all down the glasshouse, in a slow wave, following the moonlight as it crept along the roof: hundreds of them, unfolding, one after another, with the faintest whispering sound like pages turning. As each one opened, it began to glow. A soft silver light came out of the heart of every flower, out of the petals themselves, pale, cool and shining, until the whole long glasshouse was full of it. Row on row of silver flowers hung in the dark like little moons. The light lay silver on the leaves, on the iron ribs and on all their faces. Elsie stood in the middle of it with her hands pressed to her mouth. Her eyes filled and spilled over, and she let them.

Cora had come down from the far end. Elsie did not hear her come. She was simply there, at Elsie's elbow, standing beside her in the silver light with her ledger closed in her hands. She looked at the flowers. She looked down the whole long glowing length of the glasshouse, at the moons hanging in the dark. Her face in that light was very pale, very still and very fine, like something carved. She turned her head and looked at Elsie instead. Elsie, wiping her wet cheeks with the back of her hand, saw her looking: Cora's grey eyes on her face, on her wet lashes and her open mouth, for a long moment, as if the flowers were somewhere else entirely.

"Aren't they—" Something soft bumped against the glass above Elsie's head. She looked up. A moth was there on the outside of the pane, pressed against it: a great soft moth as big as her open hand, pale and furred, its wings dusted with something that caught the orchid light and glowed faintly, like ash with embers in it. Another bumped beside it. Another came, and another, out of the dark garden, drawn to the light, until there were dozens on the glass all down the roof. They were bumping, fluttering and sliding, with soft muffled thuds like someone knocking with a mitten.

"Moths," came from the door. "Big ones."

They found the vents. Elsie saw one come in through a little propped-open pane up near the ridge, squeezing through and dropping, then flying, heavy and wavering, down through the silver air. It landed on an orchid. It settled right in the open heart of the flower, wings spread, put its long tongue down into it, and drank. Under its weight the silver petals bent and crumpled. When it lifted off again, lumbering, there was a bruise on the flower where it had been, a soft brown smudge spreading through the silver, like a thumbprint on wet paper. "Oh no." More were coming in. Three, five, a dozen, all down the roof.

Elsie went to the nearest one. It was sitting on a flower at the end of a table with its wings slowly opening and closing, drinking. She came at it softly, from behind, cupped her two hands and closed them round it, gently, the way she would have caught a moth in the caravan to put it out of the window. It fluttered against her palms, soft as a whisper, tickling. She laughed. She carried it down the glasshouse in her cupped hands, past Tamsin, who stepped aside, and out of the door into the dark garden. She opened her hands under an apple tree. The moth sat a moment on her palm, glowing, then lifted off heavily into the dark.

"Right." Tamsin leaned Margery against the door frame and rolled her shoulders. She went at the next moth the same way, from behind, hands cupped. She closed her hands on it. There was a small, soft, unmistakable crunch. Tamsin stopped. She opened her hands, slowly, and looked down into them. Her whole face fell. "Oh. Oh, no. Oh, I've squashed it. I didn't mean to squash it, I did it gentle, I did it the same as you." She looked up at Elsie with huge, woeful green eyes, holding out the flattened moth on her palms like an apology, and Elsie burst out laughing.

At the potting bench Tilly had not looked up once. She was hunched over the little blue flame with her goggles down and her tongue between her teeth. She tipped a pinch of yellow powder into a vial, then three drops of something cloudy. She swirled it and held it over the flame. It fizzed. It went pale. A thin curl of smoke came up out of the neck of the vial, pale as milk, and drifted up into the silver air and spread. It smelled of lemons, of lemon peel and something green under it, sharp and clean. It drifted toward the nearest orchids. The moths on them stirred. One by one they lifted off the flowers and drifted away from it, unhurried, toward the vents.

Tilly lifted the vial and walked slowly down the glasshouse with it held out in front of her, like a candle in a procession. The pale lemony smoke trailed behind her and spread between the tables. Wherever it reached, the moths left the orchids be. They lifted off and drifted up and away, toward the vents and out into the dark. The ones still coming in through the roof turned in the air when they met it, and went back the way they had come. Tilly stopped in the middle of the glasshouse. She pushed her goggles up onto her forehead and stared round at the silver flowers, clean and whole, and at the moths going away over her head. Then she squealed.

Elsie got to her in three steps. She threw her arms round her and lifted her right off her feet, smoke and vial and all. She swung her round in the silver light. "Tilly! Tilly, look at it! Look what you did!"

Tilly hung in her arms with her boots off the floor, clutching the vial up out of the way, laughing and gasping into Elsie's hair. "It's finished. Elsie, it's finished. My smoke's finished. It works. It doesn't hurt them, it just— they just go."

Elsie set her down. Tilly did not let go of her. She stood with one arm still round Elsie's neck and the vial smoking in her other hand, flushed, wet-eyed and shining. "It's finished."

It was a long while after that, with the moths gone, the orchids glowing undisturbed and the moon high over the roof, when Elsie heard it: a scrape, outside, somewhere along the garden wall. The scrape of a boot on old brick. A soft grunt followed, and a patter of falling mortar. At the door Tamsin had gone very still. She did not turn her head. Her hand closed on Margery's haft. "Quiet." Low, flat, easy and wide awake. "Company." Cora had already closed her ledger. Tilly snatched her vials to her chest. Elsie stood with her heart going, among the silver flowers, and looked out at the dark.

Two shapes came over the wall. Elsie saw them against the moon: two dark figures, one after the other, swinging a leg over the top of the brickwork and dropping down into the garden with a soft thump. They crouched and waited a moment, listening. They came on through the fruit trees toward the glasshouse, bent double, slipping from trunk to trunk. In the moonlight between the trees Elsie could see that each of them carried something limp and pale over one shoulder: sacks. Empty sacks, for flowers. They were coming straight for the glowing glass. One of them stumbled on a root.

"Hush!"

Tamsin stepped out of the doorway. She did not hurry. She walked out onto the garden path into the full moonlight and stopped there, tall and loose. She swung Margery up onto her shoulder, so that the great axe head stood up behind her copper curls with the moon running along its edge like water. She kept quiet. She only stood there with the axe, and smiled her crooked smile. The nearer of the two shapes saw her and stopped dead. It made a strangled noise, turned and ran back through the trees the way it had come. It flung itself at the wall, scrambled up it and dropped over the other side. Elsie heard boots running away down the lake road.

The other one turned to run too, the wrong way, away from the wall, and went straight into the lettuce bed. Its foot caught in the soft earth between the rows. It went down, full length, among the lettuces, with a cry and a crunch of leaves. Elsie was already moving. She ran down the glasshouse, out of the door past Tamsin and down the path, her satchel bumping on her hip. She got to the lettuce bed before Tamsin had even taken Margery off her shoulder. She knelt down in the earth beside the fallen figure and put out her hand. "Are you hurt? Did you hurt yourself? Here, let me see."

The figure in the lettuces rolled over and scrambled back on its elbows, away from her. It was a woman. A young woman, not much older than Elsie, thin as a rake, in a patched brown coat too big for her and a scarf over her hair, with a smear of earth across her cheek and a crushed lettuce leaf stuck to her sleeve. The empty sack had fallen beside her. She was crying with fright, in great gasping hiccups, her face crumpled. Her eyes went from Elsie to Tamsin coming down the path behind her with the axe, and back. "Don't. Please don't. Please. I didn't take any. I didn't touch them. Please don't hurt me."

"We won't hurt you." Elsie knelt in the dirt and put her hand on the young woman's arm. "Shh. It's all right. Tamsin, put Margery down, you're frightening her." Behind her the axe head thudded gently into the earth.

The young woman stared at her. Her mouth worked. "It's my brother. My little brother. He's got a fever, and it won't break, and he's so hot, he's burning up, and the apothecary wants paying first, and I haven't— and they said the orchids sell for a lantern, a lantern for one flower, and I thought, just one. Just one, for him. I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

Elsie sat back on her heels in the lettuces. "A fever. Listen. Listen to me. He doesn't need an orchid. You need moonbells. Blue ones, from the Glasswood's edge; they flower at night. My mother brews them for fever, and they bring it down, I've seen them bring it down." She took the young woman's earthy hand in both of hers. "There's an apothecary on Mortar Lane. Go there. Ask for a moonbell draught, for a child's fever. Say moonbells. Can you remember? Mortar Lane, moonbell draught."

The young woman nodded, staring, her wet mouth open. "Mortar Lane," she whispered. "Moonbells."

"Wait." Elsie opened her satchel on her knee and dug in it past the salve and the bandages. She found the end of the bread from supper, wrapped in its cloth, and put it into the young woman's hands. "That's for you. Eat some on the way, you're thin as a stick." She opened her purse, took out one silver lantern and held it out. The young woman only stared at it. Elsie took her other hand and opened the fingers. She put the lantern in her palm and closed the fingers over it. "For the draught. So you can pay him first, the way he wants. Go on. Go now, tonight."

Cora had come out of the glasshouse. Elsie heard her boots on the path and looked up. Cora was standing over them in the moonlight in her buttoned charcoal coat, tall and straight, with the glowing glass behind her. She had her ledger open. She looked down at the young woman in the lettuces with the bread clutched to her chest and Elsie's coin in her fist, and at Elsie kneeling beside her in the earth. Elsie could not read her face. Then she wrote. The pencil moved, small and quick. Cora closed the ledger.

"Go," she told the young woman, quietly. She stepped back off the path to let her pass, and did not move to stop her.

The young woman got up out of the lettuces. She stood a moment swaying, looking at Elsie. Her mouth opened, and shut again. She turned and ran. She ran through the fruit trees to the wall. She put one foot in a crack in the brickwork and scrambled up it, one-handed, clumsy, because her other arm was wrapped round the bread, holding it tight against her chest like something alive. At the top she looked back, once, over her shoulder, at the four of them and the glowing glasshouse. Then she dropped over the far side. Elsie heard her running away down the lake road toward the city, running and running, until the sound was gone.

Tilly made tea. She filled the kettle at the rain butt by the door and set it on the little iron stove. She fed the stove from the scuttle and found the tin and the chipped cups. When the kettle sang she made a big pot, strong. She poured four cups. She put one sugar in Cora's without asking, the way she had seen Elsie do it, and handed it to her with a shy little duck of the head. Cora looked at it, then at Tilly. "Thank you." They drank it standing among the orchids, in the silver light, the steam from the cups going up silver too, the flowers glowing round them on every side like a sky full of quiet moons.

Afterwards Tamsin yawned so wide her jaw cracked. "Right. I'll sleep now, and someone wake me at two, and I'll sit up and you lot sleep." She lay down on the narrow cot without taking off her boots and pulled the grey blanket up. She laid Margery along the top of her, on her chest, the great head by her chin and the haft down past her knees. She wrapped both arms round it as Elsie wrapped her arms round a pillow. She shut her eyes. Almost at once the kettle sound began, a soft whistling snore in the back of her throat. Tilly put her hand over her mouth. Elsie looked at Tamsin hugging her axe in her sleep. Her heart went soft as butter.

Cora took up the watch outside. She went out of the door and began to walk the wall, slowly, all the way round the garden in the moonlight: a tall dark figure moving along the bricks, stopping now and then to look up at the top of them, then going on. When she came round to the glasshouse door again she stood there a while, then went round again. Elsie and Tilly kept the inside. Elsie turned up a bucket by the potting bench and sat on it. Tilly sat on an empty crate beside her, close. They watched the orchids glow and the moths go by outside the glass, and listened to Tamsin snore. For a long time the two of them kept quiet.

The stove ticked. The moon moved over the roof. Tilly sat with her knees together and her hands folded tight in her lap. Elsie could feel her beside her, how still she was, the way she had been still on the bed. Tilly did not look at Elsie. She looked straight ahead at the silver flowers.

"Elsie. Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

Tilly's hands tightened in her lap. "Could it be the class? What I feel. When I'm near you. The way I— the way it is." She swallowed. "You're an Enthraller. That's what they do, isn't it? That's the word. Could it be that? Could it be your class, doing it to me?"

She stopped and went on looking at the orchids, and Elsie saw that her eyes were bright. "Because I can't tell any more. I used to be able to tell. I knew what was mine. I knew I liked fire and star mixes and pear fizz and being left alone, and that was me. And now I don't know. I sit next to you and I feel— all of this, all the time, and I don't know where it comes from. I don't know if it's mine." Her voice wobbled. She pressed her lips together hard. "I can't tell any more what's mine." It came out very small. "And what's yours."

Elsie turned on her bucket to face her. She did not laugh, or hurry, or tell her not to be silly. "Tilly. Listen. I've used my class once. On Tamsin, the night of the moonbells, because the red came and she'd asked me to. That's the Tether, and that's all." She shook her head. "I haven't used it on you. I haven't used it on anyone, to make them feel a thing. Not once, Tilly."

"The ledger agrees."

They both turned. Cora was standing in the doorway with the moonlight behind her, back from her round of the wall, and Elsie did not know how long she had been there. She came a step inside. She had her black ledger open in her hands. She held it up a little, to the orchid light, and read from it without looking down. "Skills used. Tether. Once. On Tamsin Reyes. By her leave." She closed the ledger and looked at Tilly. "That's every skill in my ledger since her Naming. Every day of it. That's all."

Tilly looked at Cora for a long moment. She looked down at her own hands in her lap. She opened them, slowly, and looked at the palms, as if something were written there too. They were trembling a little. "Then it's mine." It was hardly more than a breath. "It's mine," she said again, to her hands. "All of it. It's just mine." A tear ran down beside her nose, and she did not wipe it away.

"Of course it's yours. Whatever it is, Tilly, it's yours. It's been yours the whole time. Of course it is. You're the one who feels it, so it's yours." Elsie reached over, across the little space between the bucket and the crate, and took Tilly's hand, the nearer one. She folded it into her own, warm, the way she would have held a friend's hand in the dark. "And I'm glad. Whatever it is. I'm glad it's yours."

Tilly's other hand came over too. She took Elsie's hand in both of hers and held it, in her lap, tightly, the way she had held the twist of blue-star paper, as if it might break. She did not look at Elsie. She looked at the orchids, glowing silver all down the long glasshouse, row on row. Her face in their light was wet, flushed and very still, with her lips pressed together. Elsie waited. Tilly did not say. She sat holding Elsie's hand in both of hers and looking at the moon orchids, and whatever it was, she kept it.

They sat like that a long while. Then something soft drifted down out of the silver air, past Elsie's eyes, and settled on her head with a feather-light touch. Tilly looked up and laughed: a sudden, real, startled laugh, wet at the edges. "Elsie. Hold still. You've got a moth." It was a lamp moth, a small one, sitting on Elsie's pink-and-white hair clip with its wings spread, glowing faintly, like a brooch. Tilly let go of Elsie's hand with one of hers, only one, and reached up, very gently. She held out a finger beside the clip. "Come on. Come on, you. Off you get." The moth walked onto her finger, and Tilly laughed again.
