Elsie woke to grey light in the little square window and the smell of hay. A cockerel was going somewhere below, and the sheep had begun to shift and complain in the folds. She lay where she was, warm. For a moment she could not feel her left hand. The feeling came back slowly. It was still held. Cora's fingers were still wound through hers under the blanket, loose now with sleep, but there. Cora was asleep. She lay on her side, turned in toward Elsie in the night, and her bun had come loose. Half of it had slid down onto the hay, and a long black rope of hair lay over her shoulder and across the blanket, nearly to Elsie's wrist.

Elsie lay very still so as not to wake her, and looked. She had not seen Cora's face without its guard on before. Asleep, it had none. The straight dark brows had come apart. The mouth that stayed pressed flat all day had fallen a little open, soft and pink. Elsie could hear the small even breath going in and out of it. There was a wisp of hay caught in her lashes and a crease in her cheek from the blanket's hem. She looked younger. She looked so lovely that Elsie's chest went tight and warm, the way it went at a sunrise. Absurdly, she wanted to put her thumb on the crease and smooth it out. She didn't. She only lay and looked, and held the hand.

Cora's lashes moved. Her eyes opened, grey and unfocused, and looked straight into Elsie's from a hand's breadth away. For a moment she only looked, soft and drowsy, and something at the corner of her mouth began to turn up. Her eyes went down, to the blanket, to where their two hands lay twined together on the hay between them. Cora took hers back. She took it back so fast that the blanket jumped, the way a hand comes off a stove it hadn't known was lit, and held it against her own chest in a fist. "Good morning," Elsie whispered, smiling. Cora's face had gone white, then pink, high on the cheekbones. She didn't answer.

She was up before Elsie could say anything else. She got out from under the blanket in one long careful movement, without touching anyone, and went across the hay on her knees to the square hole of the ladder. Elsie heard her boots find the rungs. She rolled onto her elbow and looked down through the hatch. In the yard below, in the grey light by the sheepfolds, Cora stood with her back to the barn and her hands up behind her head and a mouthful of hairpins between her lips. She was twisting the long black rope of her hair up and round and pinning it, hard, pin after pin, until there wasn't a strand loose. Behind Elsie, Tamsin and Tilly slept on.

Tamsin woke half an hour later with a great creaking yawn that Elsie felt all down her own back. She sat up. There was hay in her copper curls, a great deal of it, stuck through them like straw through a nest. She blinked at Elsie out of the middle of it, green-eyed and slow and grinning. "Morning." It came out hoarse. At the far end of the row Tilly sat up too, all at once, like a jack out of a box. Tilly had hay everywhere. It was in her braids and her collar and her ears, down the front of her blouse, stuck to the soot on her cheek. One long stalk stood straight up out of her hair like a feather. "What?" She sneezed.

The well stood in the middle of the yard, with a stone lip and a bucket on a rope. Cora was already there, washing her face, her hair pinned and her coat buttoned to the throat. Elsie came down the ladder and across to her, picking hay off her skirt, with Tamsin and Tilly yawning behind. Tamsin began to wind the bucket up. Elsie beamed round at them all. "Did everyone sleep? I slept so well. It was so warm. Cora held my hand all night. Didn't you, Cora? All night long. And your hand was cold as anything, the whole time. Even under the blanket."

Tamsin let go of the handle. The bucket went down the well, rattling and banging off the stones all the way, and hit the water far below with a great hollow splash. Tamsin didn't go after it. She stood with her hands still up where the handle had been and stared at Cora. Tilly had gone very still, with a stalk of hay halfway out of her braid between her finger and thumb. And Cora turned scarlet. It came up out of her collar and went all the way up her neck and over her face to her hairline, a deep, painful, burning red. She stood in it with her wet hands at her sides and didn't look at anyone.

Elsie turned to her. "I'm going to knit you some gloves. Proper ones, wool, for your cold hands. My mother taught me. I can do fingers and everything." She smiled at Cora. Cora looked at the well. Elsie looked round at the others. Tamsin was looking at Cora. Tilly was looking at her own boots. The yard had gone so quiet that Elsie could hear a sheep chewing on the far side of the fold wall. She didn't understand it. It was only gloves. "I'd like to. If—if you'd wear them." Tamsin made a sound in her throat and went to fish for the bucket.

The headwoman came across the yard with a pot of porridge on her hip, four wooden bowls stacked in her other hand and her crook hooked over her arm. She set the pot on the well's lip and began to ladle. It was thick and grey and hot, with a puddle of honey in the middle of each bowl. She handed Elsie hers. "I was up. Sat at my door half the night with a lantern, watching the bridge." She ladled Tamsin's. "Fished. The pair of them. Fished all night in that pool, splashing about like children." She banged the ladle on the pot. "Counted my folds at first light. Not a sheep gone. Not one. Don't know what to do with myself."

They went down to the bridge with their bowls to say goodbye. The pool under the arch was deep and clear and green. The young troll was sitting in it up to his chest, with the morning sun on his mossy shoulders. He saw them on the bank. He saw Tilly. His great grey arm came up out of the water, streaming, and waved at her, slowly, side to side, the same big clumsy wave as last night. Tilly waved back with her spoon. Out in the deep middle of the pool his mother stood up to her waist, still as a stone. She didn't wave. She watched them a long time with her small dark eyes. At last she bent her head to the water again.

They set off south along the Wend with the sun coming up warm on their left and the meadows steaming. The river ran beside them quick and clear, talking over its stones, and Elsie kept looking at it, the way a person keeps looking at a mended thing to be sure it holds. The sheep watched them go. The headwoman stood at the top of the bank with her crook until they were round the first bend, and when Elsie turned to wave, she lifted the crook, once. Tamsin swung Margery up onto her shoulder. Tilly picked hay out of her collar as she walked, stalk by stalk, and dropped it behind her on the path.

After a while Tamsin dropped back to walk beside Elsie. She walked close, closer than the path needed, so that her bare arm brushed Elsie's shoulder with every step, warm from the sun. She was quiet for a bit, which wasn't like her. Ahead of them Tilly was telling Cora something about silt. Tamsin bent her head and spoke, low, just for Elsie, with her eyes on the path. "I'd hold your hand too. If it's cold." She cleared her throat. "Your hand. If it gets cold. I've got warm hands." She held one up and looked at it, as if to check. "Hot, mostly. Ask anyone."

Elsie gave her hand at once. She didn't have to think about it. She slipped it into Tamsin's, laced their fingers together and swung them, smiling up at her. "It isn't cold. But I'd like that." Tamsin's hand closed round hers. It was warm, as promised, and hard with calluses from Margery's haft, and it held on firmly, as if Elsie might be taken back. Tamsin's thumb moved once over her knuckles, slowly, before it lay still. She didn't look at Elsie. She looked straight ahead up the path. Her ears had gone red under her curls, and she didn't let go until they reached the next stile, where she had to.

Later in the morning, with the sun well up and the hills behind them, the meadows went wide and flat. Elsie took her little blue notebook out of her satchel and walked with it open, turning to the page of names. Under Tilly it said, in her careful round hand: her father. Clocks. Gear Street. Underground. Hold her hand. And lower down: blue flame, and cheek. She licked her pencil and fell into step beside Tilly. "Tilly. I need more of your favourite things. For your bit of the page. I've only got blue flame, and that isn't enough for a whole person. What else do you like best?"

Tilly lit up, and began counting on her fingers at once. "Oh! Oh, um. Pear fizz. Mine, not the shop one, the shop one's too sweet. And blue flames. The steady kind, with no wick, when they stand up straight and don't flicker and you can see right through the bottom of them. And the smell of sulphur. And the smell of—" She stopped. She stopped dead, with her mouth open and her fourth finger up. Her face went red, a slow red that started at her collar and filled her up like a glass, all the way to her hairline. She shut her mouth.

Elsie held her pencil ready. "The smell of what? I've got sulphur. What was the last one?"

"It's a secret." Tilly's voice had gone up very high. "It's a secret, it's not a thing, it's not anything, you can't write it down!" She put her hand flat over Elsie's notebook, as if Elsie might write it anyway, then snatched it off again as if the paper were hot. And she walked faster. She walked so fast that her braids bounced, all the way up to the front, past Tamsin, until she was ahead of everyone with her ears pink and her back very straight. Elsie looked at the page. She wrote pear fizz, and blue flames, and sulphur. After a moment she added a small question mark.

Cora was walking a little apart, on the river side, with her ledger under her arm. Elsie went down the page to Cora's name and fell into step with her. "Cora. Your turn. What are your favourite things?"

Cora glanced at the open notebook, at her own name on it and the lines under it, and looked ahead again. "Tea. Black. Strong. One sugar. As you know perfectly well, Fairweather. You've made it me often enough."

Elsie laughed. "I know. I've got it already. But you have to say it. It's the rule. What else?"

Cora didn't answer. Elsie waited. She walked beside her with her pencil over the page and waited, the way she waited for a shy child at a caravan stop, without hurrying her. The river went by, and a lark went up. They walked a long way like that.

"And lavender."

It came so quietly that Elsie nearly lost it under the river. Cora didn't look round. Elsie looked up at her, and breathed in, and there it was, faint, under the ink, in the charcoal wool of the coat beside her: lavender. "Oh! That's why your coat smells of it." Cora's step faltered, just once. Elsie wrote it down.

They came in through the north gate in the late morning. The same clerk sat in the window of the little gatehouse with the same mug and the big chained ledger open in front of him. He saw them coming up the road and put the mug down. He was grinning before they reached him. "Fairweather party." He wrote it without being asked, and spelled it right. "Four, in. Back from the toll bridge." He looked up at Elsie from under his eyebrows, still grinning. "Heard about you. Shepherd's lad came down at dawn with a cart of fleeces. Talking to trolls." He turned the ledger round for Cora to sign, and shook his head, and laughed into his tea.

The guild hall was full, as it was most mornings before noon: adventurers all along the quest board, a queue at the clerk's desk, the smell of wet leather and ink and somebody's breakfast. Elsie pushed open the big door and walked in with the others behind her. The talk stopped. It stopped all down the hall, table by table, head by head, like a wave going out, until the great timber room was silent and every face in it was turned toward the door. Elsie stopped too, startled, on the threshold. All at once the talk started up again, louder than before, a great rush of it, every voice going at once.

Elsie caught pieces of it as they went in. Two adventurers stood by the E-rank end of the board, a big bearded man in mail and a woman with a bow across her back, their heads together.

"—troll," the woman was saying, "under the bridge, and she just—"

"A bargain," said the man. "With a troll. A bargain."

"The Enthraller—" The woman looked up straight at Elsie, and stopped. The man looked too. He had a mug in his hand. He looked at Elsie a moment longer, at her pink hair and her muddy boots. He raised his mug to her, gravely, the way adventurers raise them to each other across a tavern, and drank.

A clerk was already coming through the crowd toward them, a thin young woman with ink on her fingers and a pen behind her ear. She stopped in front of Elsie and Cora and looked from one to the other, a little out of breath. "The guildmaster wants you. Both of you. Upstairs. Now, she said." She glanced at Tamsin and Tilly. "Just the Enthraller and the Warden." Elsie's stomach gave a small turn. She looked at Cora. Cora's face had gone smooth and still and correct, the face she wore for the guild. She tucked her ledger under her arm and nodded once. "Of course."

Tamsin put a hand on Elsie's shoulder and squeezed. "We'll be by the board. Right there. Go on." Tilly nodded hard, her eyes huge. Because she couldn't think what else to do, she hugged Elsie quickly round the middle and let go. Elsie followed the clerk and Cora up the wide wooden stairs at the back of the hall. Halfway up she looked back over the rail. Tamsin and Tilly were standing by the quest board side by side, both of them watching the stairs. Tamsin had her arms folded, with Margery leaning on the wall beside her. Tilly was twisting a braid round and round her finger.

The guildmaster's office was a long room under the roof, with a window over Brightwater Square. A sword hung on the wall above the hearth, old and plain and well kept. The guildmaster sat behind a desk heaped with paper. She was a broad grey-haired woman in her fifties, with a swordswoman's shoulders and big weathered hands. She looked up as they came in. "The old toll bridge. I've had the shepherds' runner, the gate clerk's tally and half my hall in here gossiping since dawn." She waved one big hand at two chairs in front of the desk. "Sit."

They sat. Cora sat the way she stood, straight, her ledger flat on her knees. Elsie sat on the edge of her chair with her hands clasped tight in her lap. The guildmaster leaned back and looked at her for a while, the way a buyer looks over a horse, not unkindly. "Now. I've heard it from everyone but you, and everyone tells it different. Tell me what happened at the toll bridge, Fairweather. Plainly. Not the way the Heron's telling it. The way it happened." She folded her hands on her stomach. "Take as long as you like."

So Elsie told her, in order, because she didn't know how to tell it any other way. "There's a headwoman, and she was losing sheep, one a night. And the pools were all brown, silted so shallow I could see the bottom, and the boys couldn't find a trout. The trolls were asleep under the arch. Tilly said the river was too slow. And the troll said fish, gone, and water, stopped, and pointed. So we went up, and there was the weir, and the jam, and a drowned cart in it, and Tilly made a bang. And the river came down clean." She swallowed. "And at dusk she caught a trout and gave it to her son. And then she nodded at me." Her eyes stung, telling it. She blinked.

The guildmaster was quiet a moment. She leaned forward and put her elbows on the desk. "You had a Berserker with you. Reyes. A good axe, I'm told, when she's herself. And two trolls asleep in the shallows, on a notice that says remove them." She looked at Elsie with pale, hard eyes. "Reyes could have had the big one's head off before it woke. That's how it's done. That's how it's been done on that notice for as long as there've been notices." She tapped the desk with one finger. "Why didn't you let her?"

Elsie thought about it. She wanted to give the right answer, and found she only had the true one. "Because I wanted to ask them first." Out loud it sounded so simple that she went pink. "What they needed. Everyone knew what the trolls were doing, but not why. Not the headwoman, not the books. And if they needed something, it seemed like a thing you ought to find out before. Because after, you can't." She spread her hands. "So I asked them what they needed. That's all."

The guildmaster looked at Cora. She didn't say anything. She only looked, with her grey brows raised. Cora opened her ledger. She turned to a page near the end. Elsie saw the small exact writing going on and on, over that page and the next. Cora read it aloud in her even guild voice, without hurrying, every line. "The trolls, sleeping. The Berserker's axe. The Enthraller's hand on its haft. The Enthraller between the troll and its child, with her arms out. Skills used: none. The question. The answer." She closed the ledger. "That is what I saw, Guildmaster. Exactly that." Elsie looked at her, and her heart swelled up warm and full.

The guildmaster sat back. She looked at Cora for a long moment, then at Elsie, and her mouth did something at one corner that wasn't quite anything yet. "Well." She picked up a pen and put it down again. "Then let me ask you something else, Fairweather, since you like questions. You're an Enthraller on my books. E-rank inside a week, and the whole hall in an uproar over you. Half of them think you'll be the end of us, and the other half want to buy you a drink." She folded her arms. "What is it you want from the guild? What are you after?"

Elsie didn't have to think about that either. "A house. There's one on Willow Canal with a blue door. It's to let. It's got a garden gone all wild, and a kitchen big enough for everyone, and a shed for Tilly's still, and I want to live in it with all my friends." She smiled. "That's what I'm saving for. That's what I want." The guildmaster stared at her, and laughed. It burst out of her, a great short bark of a laugh that seemed to take her by surprise, and she put her hand over her eyes and laughed into it, her broad shoulders shaking.

When she took her hand away she was still smiling. The smile changed her whole face, and made her look like somebody's aunt. "A house with a blue door. For your friends." She shook her head. "Do you know what adventurers ask the guild for, when I ask them that? Rank. Gold. Their names on a board. A dungeon all to themselves." She looked at Elsie. "A house is more than most of them ask for, Fairweather. And a good deal less than they should."

The smile went, slowly, and she leaned forward again. "Listen to me. The city's talking about you. You'll have heard it downstairs. Not all of it's kind." She picked a letter off the heap and put it down again without reading it, as if she knew what it said. "There are folk in this city who still remember the War of Strings. Who have names on the memorial in Lantern Square. An Enthraller who takes, they understand." She looked at Elsie steadily. "An Enthraller who talks to trolls frightens some of them more."

Elsie frowned. "But why? Why more? I didn't do anything to the trolls. I didn't use anything. Cora wrote it down." She looked at Cora, who was looking at her hands. "I only talked to them."

The guildmaster looked at her a long time. "Because it works. That's why. A thing that takes, folk can fight. They know where its edges are. A thing that asks, and gets a yes out of a river troll—" she spread her hands flat on the desk, "—they can't see where that stops."

She turned to Cora. "Warden Vane." Cora sat up straighter, if that was possible. "Keep writing. Every line, the way you read it me just now. Every skill, and every none." The guildmaster tapped the ledger on Cora's knees with one thick finger. "If the day comes that I have to stand up in front of the council with this girl, I want that book in my hand." She sat back and waved them at the door. "Go on. Down you go, the pair of you." Elsie got up. "And Fairweather—good luck with your house."

On the stairs, halfway down, Cora stopped. Elsie stopped a step below and looked up at her. Cora stood with one hand on the rail and her ledger held against her chest, looking at the wall with a strange, far-off look on her face. "She laughed. Three years I've reported to that woman, Fairweather. I'd not heard her laugh till today."

Elsie beamed. "She's got a lovely laugh. Hasn't she? Like a dog barking." And Cora's mouth twitched, at one corner, before she could stop it.

Tamsin and Tilly were on them before they reached the bottom stair. Tamsin grabbed Elsie's arm. "Well? What did she want? Are you in trouble? Is Cora in trouble? Do I need to hit anyone?"

Tilly was hanging off Elsie's other arm, talking over her. "Are we being thrown out? Is it the bang? It was the bang, wasn't it. It was a very big bang. I can pay for the cart. I can't pay for the cart. I can pay for some of the cart—"

Elsie laughed and took a hand of each. "Nobody's in trouble."

Elsie couldn't stop smiling. "She asked me what I wanted, and I said the house. The blue door. And she laughed. She laughed and laughed, with her hand over her eyes."

Tamsin stared at her, then grinned, slow and crooked. She let go of Elsie's arm to thump her lightly on the shoulder. "The guildmaster laughed. At you. Elsie, that's good. That's a good sign. I've stood in that office twice to be told off, and she didn't so much as smile." She looked at Cora, who was looking at the floor. "That's a very good sign."

They went to the board. The E-rank notices hung at the far end, fewer than the F and further apart, on better paper. Elsie's tag clinked at her belt as she went up on her toes to read them. There were three. Wolves out of the Glasswood on the north road, and a carter's pony taken. Lights and singing at Bellmoor barrow, out on the downs. Bandits robbing travellers at Hollin Ford. Elsie read them all twice, the way she read everything that mattered. Tamsin read over her head. Tilly read under her arm.

Tilly pointed at once. "Bandits pay best. Look. Fifteen lanterns. That's five each." She was already doing it on her fingers. "If we did bandits, and then the wolves, and then—"

"Bandits fight back." Tamsin's voice was flat, and Tilly stopped counting. "Wolves fight back too, but wolves run when it goes against them. Bandits have crossbows, and they don't run, because they'd hang." She looked down at Tilly. "Not bandits. Not yet."

"What's the barrow?" Elsie asked. "What's a barrow?"

"An old grave," Tamsin said. "A hill somebody built over somebody, a long time ago. Out on the downs." She leaned past Elsie and read the notice aloud, squinting at the clerk's small hand. "Bellmoor barrow. Lights seen on the barrow by night, and singing heard. Shepherds afraid to graze it. To be looked into. Ten lanterns." She straightened. "Lights and singing. At night. On a grave." She made a face. "Ten lanterns for that's cheap."

Elsie read the notice again. "Singing." She looked round at the others with her eyes shining. "Somebody's singing out there, at night, on a grave, and the shepherds are frightened, and they haven't been up to see who." She touched the paper with one finger. "I'd like to know who's singing. Wouldn't you? I'd like to know what they're singing about."

Tamsin looked at her, and at the notice. She laughed and shook her head. "Course you would. All right. The barrow."

Tilly nodded. Elsie beamed at them both. "Tomorrow. We'll go tomorrow."

They took the notice to the clerk's desk. The clerk, the same thin young woman with ink on her fingers, unpinned it and turned the big book round. She wrote in it: the Fairweather party, for Bellmoor barrow, the fourteenth. She blotted it. Beside Elsie, Cora had opened her ledger on her forearm and was writing the same, in her own small exact hand, at the top of a fresh page: Bellmoor barrow. Lights and singing. The fourteenth. Elsie watched her write it. There was a great deal of empty page under it, waiting.

Cora closed the ledger. "I have my report to write. On the toll bridge, for the guildmaster." She tucked the ledger under her arm. She looked at Elsie a moment longer, as if there were something else. She didn't say it. She nodded to Tamsin and Tilly and went across the hall to the charcoal-painted door in the far wall, where the Wardens kept their room. She opened it, went through and shut it behind her. Elsie, watching her go, saw the back of her neck above the collar of her coat. It was still faintly pink.

The Tipsy Heron was across the canal from the guild hall, loud and low-beamed, smelling of beer and onions. At noon it was full of adventurers. Tamsin pushed through them to the counter. Elsie saw her count it out there, copper after copper, out of the purse at her belt and into the tapster's palm: twelve pips for the stew and one more for the loaf. She came back with three bowls balanced along her arms and the loaf tucked under her chin. She set them down. "My treat. For the bridge. Don't argue." They sat at a scarred table by the window. The stew was thick and brown, full of barley, and very hot.

A woman came in and sat down at the next table, alone, with a mug. She was tall, taller than Tamsin, broad in the shoulder, with short dark hair and a plain strong face. Her left arm was strapped across her chest in a sling. There was a splint under the sling. Elsie could see the edge of it, and the yellowing bruise at the wrist where it ended. She lowered herself onto the bench carefully, the way people move when a bone is knitting. On the shoulder of her jacket was a stitched badge: a copper hound. Elsie knew whose arm that was before Tamsin had even looked up.

Tamsin had her spoon halfway to her mouth. She saw the woman. Elsie watched it happen. Tamsin's eyes went to the sling, to the badge, to the face, and stopped there. All the colour went out of her. It drained from under her freckles until they stood out like flecks of rust on white, and her green eyes went wide and fixed. Very slowly she put her spoon down in her bowl. She put it down so carefully that it didn't make a sound. She sat with her hands flat on the table either side of the bowl and didn't move.

Elsie felt it down the Tether. It came all at once, the way the troll's blow had come, but this didn't ring. It soaked. It was shame, cold and heavy, like being pushed under cold water in her clothes and held there. It went into her chest and her stomach and her hands until she could hardly breathe for it. She knew it wasn't hers. She knew whose it was. Under the table she reached across and put her hand on Tamsin's knee, flat and warm. She left it there. She didn't say anything. She only held on. Under her palm, through the worn cloth, she could feel Tamsin's leg shaking.

Tamsin's hand came down over Elsie's under the table and gripped it, hard, once. She let go and stood up. Her chair scraped. She walked the three steps to the next table, stiffly, like someone walking out onto ice, and stopped beside it. She stood there. The shield-bearer looked up from her mug. Tamsin stood in front of her with her arms hanging at her sides and her fists opening and closing. In all the noise of the Heron, the clatter and the shouting and somebody singing badly by the fire, the two of them were perfectly still.

"I'm sorry about your arm." Tamsin's voice came out rough. She cleared her throat. "I'm sorry about your arm. I should've said so a week ago. At the time. Before they took you off to be set." She swallowed. "I should've come and found you. I didn't. I kept away." Her fists had stopped opening and closing. They hung. "That's all. I'm sorry. I'm not asking you for anything. I just should've said it."

The shield-bearer looked at her. She looked at her a long time, up from the bench, her plain strong face not giving anything away, until Elsie, watching from the next table with her heart going hard, wanted to get up herself. At last the woman set her mug down. "I heard about the wolves. In the Glasswood. You in the red, with the wolves all round, and the little pink Enthraller behind you, and you didn't turn on her." She looked past Tamsin, at Elsie. "Heard about the Tether too. The whole hall did."

She looked back at Tamsin. "It was the red broke my arm. Not you. I didn't know that then. I thought it was you, and I said so, to the Hounds and to anyone who'd stand still for it." She shifted the sling against her chest. "I know the difference now." And she held out her good hand, her right, open, across the gap between them. "Reyes."

Tamsin looked at the hand. Then she took it. She shook it, once, hard, the way she'd shake on a bargain, held on a moment longer than the shake needed, and let go. The shield-bearer nodded and went back to her mug. Tamsin turned round and came back to their table. She sat down. She picked up her spoon and put it down again. Her eyes were very bright. She was blinking hard, fast, over and over, looking fixedly at her stew, her mouth pressed shut and working at the corners.

Elsie got up and went round the table. She put her arms round Tamsin's middle from the side, as far as they would go, pressed her face into Tamsin's shoulder and held on. Tamsin made a small sound. Her arm came round Elsie's back, heavy, and her hand closed on a fistful of Elsie's top. There was a scrape and a rush. Tilly was there too, coming round the other side, flinging her arms round the pair of them. She squeezed so hard that Elsie's cheek squashed against Tamsin's collarbone. Elsie could feel Tamsin's heart going under it, fast. "You were so brave," Tilly said, muffled, into somebody. "Tamsin. You were so brave."

After lunch they walked to Willow Canal. It was a quiet canal, green and narrow, with willows trailing in it and a little hump-backed bridge. The house was where it had been: narrow and tall, three storeys, its garden gone wild against the railings, its door blue. Elsie walked faster when she saw it. The card was still in the front window. TO LET, it said, in faded copperplate, and under it the agent's name on Coin Street. Elsie stood at the railings with her hands round the bars and looked at it the way she looked at Sunny when he put out a new leaf.

She worked it out on her fingers. She had done it before, in bed at night, but she did it again, because sometimes it came out different. Her own lanterns. Tamsin's. Tilly's. What the barrow would bring. She counted, moving her lips, ran out of fingers, and started again on the first hand. Four suns, the agent wanted, in gold, a month in hand. She came to the end of her counting and looked at her fingers, then at the blue door. She sighed, a long sigh that went all the way down to her boots.

Tamsin leaned on the railings beside her. "The barrow's ten lanterns. That's something. That's a start."

Tilly was counting on her own fingers, her lips moving too. She got to the end of them. She looked at the house, at her hands, and back at the house. "That's still a great many barrows." Her voice had gone small. "A great many. Is Lanternmere full of barrows? I don't think it is." She looked so stricken that Elsie laughed, and Tamsin laughed, and Tilly, after a moment, laughed too, a little wobbly.

Elsie opened the gate. It squeaked. She went up the short path to the step, through the long grass, the poppies and the bindweed. She stood in front of the blue door. Its paint was cracked, and the knocker was a brass hand holding a ball. She kissed two of her fingers and pressed them to the door, flat, in the middle of the blue, and held them there a moment. "Wait for us." Behind her at the railings Tamsin had gone quiet, and Tilly had both hands clasped under her chin.

At the corner where Willow Canal met the wider water, Tilly stopped. "I have to brew. For the barrow. If there's singing, there might be—I don't know what there might be. I need to brew for not knowing." She hugged Elsie, quick and tight, let go and went three steps. She turned round, came back and hugged her again, harder, with her face in Elsie's shoulder and a braid in Elsie's mouth. "Bye." It came out muffled in Elsie's top. She let go at last and set off up the towpath toward Pickle Row at a run, her vials clinking.

Tamsin walked her home. They went along the canals in the hot early afternoon, slowly, with Margery on Tamsin's shoulder and the unlit lanterns swaying a little over their heads in the breeze. Tamsin was quiet a long time. "I'm going to write to Nell tonight. About today. About the shield-bearer." She looked straight ahead. "She'll want to know. They'll be up past the lock by now, but the wharf office sends on." She was quiet again. "I'll tell her I said sorry. She'll like that."

At the Honeycomb's door Tamsin stopped. She stood looking at Elsie a moment, with the warm bread smell coming out round them, and her face did something complicated. She leaned Margery against the wall and hugged her. She hugged her hard, both arms round her, lifting her a little off the step, with her chin on top of Elsie's head and her cheek against Elsie's hair. She held her like that longer than a hug usually went. She set her down. "Tomorrow." She picked up Margery and went off along Thimble Canal toward the wharf office, and didn't look back.

Up in the attic it was warm and close and smelled of bread. Elsie opened the round window over the canal and sat on the end of her bed with the little blue notebook on her knees, open at the page of names. She went down it to Tilly. Under clocks and Gear Street, under hold her hand, under blue flame and cheek, she wrote the new ones out properly in her best round hand. Pear fizz (her own, not the shop's). Blue flames, the steady kind. The smell of sulphur. Then she left a gap, a good-sized one, three ruled lines of white paper. At the start of the gap she drew a very small question mark, and beside it a very small star.

She went on down the page to Cora. It was getting crowded there: black, strong, one sugar; ate the bun on the stair; quiet; and under those the lines from the Copper Baths, and the little wobbly heart. Below them all she wrote it again the way Cora had said it on the riverbank today, as though it were new: black, one sugar. On the line below, slowly, taking care over each letter, she wrote: lavender. She sat and looked at the word a while. She thought of Cora beside her on the bank, not looking round, saying it so quietly she'd nearly missed it. She thought of the coat. She found she was smiling at the page.

Downstairs the shop was quiet, the afternoon loaves cooling on the racks and Agnes behind the counter sorting pips into piles. Elsie leaned on the counter. "Agnes, where do I buy wool? Good wool, for gloves. Soft."

Agnes didn't look up from her pips. "Spindle Lane. Go to the one with the green shutters, not the one with the cat. The one with the cat cheats." She pushed a pile of pips along the counter. "Gloves. In summer."

"They're for Cora. Her hands are cold."

Agnes looked up at that, and looked at her for a long moment, and grunted.

Spindle Lane was narrow and cool and smelled of sheep and dye, every shopfront hung with skeins: reds, golds, blues, lavender, moss. Elsie went into the one with the green shutters and along the shelves inside, touching. There was a great deal of grey. None of it was right, until one was. It was a soft, cool, pale grey, like the Mere at dawn under its mist. She held the skein up to the window and knew it at once: it was the grey of Cora's eyes. She took it to the counter. She counted four copper pips out of her purse, one at a time, into the woman's hand.

"And needles, please." The woman brought out a tray of them, and Elsie chose a pair of smooth, pointed ones of polished birch, not too thick, good for fine work, for fingers. She tried the points against her thumb and smiled. She counted three more pips into the woman's palm, and the purse at her belt hung a little lighter. She came out into Spindle Lane with the grey wool held against her chest in both arms and the needles poking out of her satchel, humming.

She sat on the sill of the round window with her back against the frame and her boots up on the bed, the canal going by below in the late gold light. First she wound the skein into a ball on her knee, round and round. After that she cast on. She did it the way her mother had shown her by a caravan fire, looping the grey wool over the birch, stitch after stitch, counting under her breath. She thought about Cora's hand: long and narrow and cold in hers in the dark. She made the cuff a little longer than she would have for herself, for those wrists. Somewhere in the counting she had begun to hum.

Moonpenny sat beside her on the sill in its little clay pot. It was closed up for the day, its leaves folded, its one fat bud wrapped tight round itself like a fist, waiting for its night. Elsie talked to it while she knitted. "The guildmaster laughed today. And Tamsin said sorry to the shield-bearer, and you should have seen her face. And Cora likes lavender. I didn't know that. Well. I did, sort of, because of her coat. But I didn't know I knew." Moonpenny, being an orchid, kept its opinions to itself. Below the sill the rest of her plants sat in their crate, and Captain the mint smelled of mint in the warm.

The light went gold, then rose, then blue. The lamplighters' boats came along Thimble Canal at dusk: long narrow boats poled slowly down the middle of the water, a lamplighter standing in the bow of each with a long brass pole and a flame at its tip. They reached up to each paper lantern as they passed, and it bloomed red or gold, one after another, all down the canal, its light dropping onto the water in long shaking stripes. Elsie stopped to watch them. When she looked down at her needles again, she had a cuff done: a neat grey ribbed cuff, the length of her hand.

It was full dark, the lanterns all lit, when she saw her. A figure on the far bank of Thimble Canal, walking alone under the lanterns, coming this way. Elsie knew the walk at once, the way she had known it two nights ago: the straight back, the long even stride, the charcoal coat. Cora, walking along Thimble Canal again, alone, at night. Elsie put her knitting down, leaned out of the round window and waved, with her whole arm, over her head.

Cora looked up. She looked straight up the front of the Honeycomb to the round window, as if she had known exactly where to look. And this time she didn't stop and stand. She lifted her hand at once. She lifted it high, open, palm out, the way she had held it toward the troll under the bridge, except that there was no storm in the air round it now. It was only a hand, raised to Elsie in the lantern light. Elsie, leaning out over the sill with her elbows on the warm stone, felt her whole face go into a smile.

Elsie snatched up her knitting and held it out of the window for Cora to see: the grey cuff on its birch needles, the ball of wool dangling under it on its strand, turning in the air. She waggled it. She pointed at it, then down at Cora, and held it up higher. Down on the far bank, under a red lantern, Cora stood with her hand still half raised and looked up at it. She tilted her head slowly to one side, the way the young troll had tilted his at Tilly's squeak, and looked at it a long moment, puzzled. Then she lowered her hand and walked on along the canal.

Later she lit the candle stub on the little table, pulled up the stool and wrote to her mother. Dear Mum, she wrote. Today the guildmaster laughed. She is a swordswoman and Cora says she doesn't laugh, and I said I wanted the blue-door house, and she laughed with her hand over her eyes. Tamsin says that's a good sign. The house is still to let. We are a great many barrows away from it, Tilly says. I am knitting gloves for Cora, grey ones, because her hands are cold at night. I have done one cuff. I love you. Elsie.

She blew out the candle and lay down on her narrow bed in the dark, with the lantern light off the canal moving faintly on the sloped ceiling over her, and folded her hands on her stomach. She felt for Tamsin down the Tether, the way she did each night now before sleep, and had to lie very still to find what she was looking for, because it wasn't there. The shame was gone. All day it had sat at the far end of the thread, cold and tight and heavy. Now it had eased out of her the way a knot eases when someone patient works it loose, a turn at a time, until the cord lies straight.
