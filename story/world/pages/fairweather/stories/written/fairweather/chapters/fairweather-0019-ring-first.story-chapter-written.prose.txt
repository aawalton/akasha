Elsie woke before the dawn. The round window was a pale blue-grey square over the bed, the colour of a pigeon's breast, and the canal lanterns below it had burned low and orange. She lay on her back under the sloping boards and knew at once what day it was, before she knew anything else. It was the nineteenth. It was the day before Midsummer. She said it to herself under her breath, Midsummer Eve, to see what it sounded like, and it sounded like a bell.

She lay still and listened to the bakery waking up underneath her. The oven door clanked, down at the bottom of everything, and the peel scraped on the stone. A tray went down on the counter. Mrs Merriweather coughed once, the small dry cough she gave every morning at about this time. The bread smell came up through the boards, warm and brown, the way it always came. Elsie lay and breathed it in. Her stomach felt fluttery, as if a moth had got into it and couldn't find the way out.

She thought of tomorrow. She thought of the Mere at night, the black water going out and out under the sky, and all the little boats on it with their lanterns, like sparks floating. She thought of the festival barge out in the middle of the dark, and their own boat somewhere near it, small. She thought of Cora by the notice board in her reading-out voice: "Thieves on Midsummer night carry knives." The moth in her stomach turned over. She pulled the blanket up to her chin.

She sat up instead, because lying there wasn't helping, and shuffled along the bed on her knees to the round window. Moonpenny's pot sat on the sill with her one green bud shut tight. Beside the pot, where she had left them, lay the two grey gloves, the finished one and the one that wasn't. She took the second glove off the sill and laid it beside the first on her knee, the way she had last night. She looked at them together: one whole hand, and one with a thumb, a finger and a stub.

She found her place on the needles and knitted the second finger by the grey light from the window. Round and round, plain, plain, plain. The needles ticked. Every few rounds she stopped and held it up against the first glove's second finger, the long one, laying the two side by side on her knee to see how far she had to go. It was halfway. It was a little past halfway. Outside, a gull went over the canal and said something rude about the morning.

"Moonpenny," she whispered to the pot. "It's Midsummer Eve. Did you know?" She leaned her chin on the sill beside it. "Tomorrow's the night. The whole Mere's going to be out on the water." She looked at the shut green bud. "I've got a fluttery feeling. Right here." She put her hand flat on her stomach, over the halter. "And I can't think why. It isn't a bad feeling. It's only very busy."

She thought about it, with her hand still there. The flutter went on fluttering. She knew what it wanted. It wanted the same thing it had wanted on the night before the Underbloom, and the night before Bellmoor, and the morning she had walked into Lanternmere with her crate. "It wants baking." She nodded to Moonpenny. "That's what it is. It always does, more or less." She felt better at once for having decided, and put the gloves back on the sill.

Downstairs the shop was warm and dim and smelled of crust. Mrs Merriweather stood at the open mouth of the big oven with the long wooden peel, sliding out loaves, three at a time, onto the rack. Her face was red from the heat. Elsie came down the last step and waited until the peel was empty and the loaves were all out. "Mrs Merriweather, may I use the oven tonight? Please? I'd like to bake. For tomorrow. For the boat."

Mrs Merriweather set the end of the peel on the floor and leaned on it. She looked at Elsie over it, the way she looked at a loaf she hadn't decided about. "After the shop shuts. When the oven's cooling. Not before." She jerked her chin at the peel under her hand, long and worn and black at the end. "And mind the peel. It's older than you." She turned back to the oven.

"Thank you. I will. I'll mind it ever so." She opened the purse on her belt. "And may I buy what I need? Honey, please. A crock of it. And butter. And a bag of flour." She counted the copper pips out onto the counter one at a time, so Mrs Merriweather could see them all: three for the honey, one for the butter, one for the flour. Five pips, in a little row on the floured wood. Mrs Merriweather swept them into her apron pocket without counting them again.

"And could you leave the honey out? On the counter. For tonight. So I don't have to climb up on the bin for it after you've gone up." She pointed at the shelf over the flour bin, which was high, and which she had nearly fallen off once already. Mrs Merriweather grunted. It was a short grunt, low in her chest, and Elsie had lived over the shop long enough to know that this one was a yes, and the honey would be there.

On her way to the door Elsie looked at the window. The blue plate sat under the glass where it always sat, but it was empty now, bare, blue and shining. There were only a few crumbs on it and a smear of honey at the rim. Amara had taken every cake home. Elsie stopped and looked at the empty plate in the morning light. She smiled at it. She smiled at it all the way to the door, and went out with the bell jangling over her.

At seven Cora was on the guild hall steps, at the top, where she always stood, with her ledger under her arm and her coat buttoned to the throat. Brightwater Square was still cool and blue with shadow and the pigeons were only just waking up. Elsie saw her from the corner and broke into a run. She took the steps two at a time, her satchel bumping on her hip, and arrived at the top a little out of breath, beaming.

"Good morning, Cora! It's Midsummer Eve!"

Cora looked down at her. "Fairweather." She inclined her head, as she did every morning. Her eyes moved. They went up from Elsie's face to her hair, and stayed there, on one side of it. Her straight dark brows came together a very little. Elsie put her hand up to her head, wondering if there was a moth in it, or flour.

"Your clip. It's crooked." Before Elsie could find it herself, Cora's hand had come up. She took hold of the pink-and-white clip with two long fingers, eased it open and settled it back in Elsie's hair, a little higher, a little straighter. She pressed it shut. Her fingertips brushed Elsie's temple as they went. They were cool. She took her hand away and stepped back a pace, quite quickly. She looked at the doors.

Inside, the hall was quiet and smelled of fresh sawdust. Noor was at her corner table in her ring of empty tables, the same as every morning, in her long green coat with her coffee in front of her. The light from the high windows came down across the bare boards round her. She was sitting very straight, with her gloved hands round the cup, looking at nothing in particular. Elsie looked at the ring of empty tables, and the cup, and had an idea.

She went to the counter by the stair instead of across to Noor. "A cup of coffee, please. Black. Strong. Nothing in it at all." She counted out two copper pips and set them on the counter. The woman poured it from the big black pot, thick and dark and steaming, into a white cup on a white saucer. Behind her, Elsie could feel Cora come in through the doors and stop, the way she always stopped, where she could see the whole hall.

Elsie carried the cup across the hall in both hands, very carefully, over the bare boards, into the ring. The coffee rocked in the cup and she slowed down to keep it level. She set it on the table beside Noor's first cup, saucer touching saucer. She took hold of the good chair across the table and knocked on its seat twice, rap, rap, for luck. She sat down on it and smiled across the two cups at Noor.

Noor looked down at the two cups side by side. She looked at them for some time. "I haven't finished the first." She didn't look up. Her own cup was still more than half full.

"That one's for later. For when you have. So you don't have to get up and go all the way to the counter." She folded her arms on the table. "It's a long way to the counter. Through all those empty tables."

She leaned forward on her arms. "Noor. It's tomorrow. Midsummer. The barges." She looked at Noor's face, at the kohl-dark eyes, still on the cups. "So I'm asking again, the way you said. Will you come out on the Mere with us? In the boat?" She held her breath a little, without meaning to.

"Boats sink round me." Noor was looking at the coffee. "I told you so yesterday." She turned her cup on its saucer a little way, with two gloved fingers. "It was true. I didn't say it to be clever." She lifted her eyes to Elsie at last, dark and steady. "Ask me again in a year, and it'll be true then too."

"Well, Tilly's floating vials haven't changed overnight either. They still float. She said so. They bob." She counted on her fingers. "And Tamsin can still swim. She could swim yesterday and she can swim today. I asked her." She hadn't, quite, but she knew Tamsin could, because Tamsin had swum in the Baths' pool like an otter, and that was very nearly the same as asking.

Noor's mouth went thin. "Things go wrong near me in the dark. Worse than cups. Worse than chairs. Lines snap. A mooring line, a rope, a strap. Lanterns go out, all down a street, one after another, when I walk under them." She looked down at her gloves on the table. "On the water at night, with one lantern, that isn't a joke. That's how people drown."

"Cora has a lantern. A guild one. It drove a barrow-wight back down its own passage. I saw it." She nodded hard. "A barrow-wight, Noor. It couldn't bear it. It went back into the dark and it stayed there all night." She leaned in. "So I don't think a little bit of dark on the Mere is going to mind Cora's lantern very much. I think it'll keep its distance."

Noor looked past her, over Elsie's shoulder, across the hall. Elsie turned to see. Cora was at the clerk's desk with her ledger open on it. She had looked up from it. She was looking back across the hall at Noor, level and grey, over the top of the ledger, without a flicker. The two of them looked at each other across all those empty tables for a long moment. Both kept their chins exactly where they were. At last Cora looked down at her ledger again.

"The others won't want me in their boat." Noor had turned back to her cup. "Tamsin and the little one with the goggles. They'll be polite about it. And then they'll sit as far from me as the boat lets them, and watch the water."

Elsie shook her head. "But I've drawn you in already. Last night. With them. All of us together, in the boat." She was already reaching for her satchel.

She pulled her satchel round onto her lap and dug out the little blue notebook. She opened it on the table between the two cups, flat, and turned the pages until she came to the one with MIDSUMMER in capitals across the top. Underneath was the little rowing boat on its wavy lines of water, with the lantern on its pole at the front. In it sat five small pencil heads in a row. She turned the book round so it faced Noor, and pushed it across.

She reached over and put her fingertip by the last head, the one at the back, a little apart from the others. It had long straight hair that came down past where its shoulders would have been. "That one is you." She tapped the paper beside it, softly, so as not to smudge it. "There. At the back. In the boat."

Noor looked at the little drawn head. She looked at it a long time. She didn't say anything at all. Her face didn't move, and her eyes didn't move off it. After a while her gloved hand came up off the table. One finger lifted out of the others, black leather, and came down toward the page, slowly, until it was almost touching the little pencil head. It stopped there, a hair's breadth off the paper, and stayed.

"The hair's wrong." Her finger was still over it. "It's longer than that." She drew her hand back. "Much longer."

Elsie looked at the drawing and at Noor's hair, which came down past her shoulders, straight and black, all the way to the middle of her back. "Oh. So it is." She took the pencil out of the notebook's spine and leaned over the table. She made the hair longer, there and then, a long careful line down either side, past the shoulders, nearly to the bottom of the boat.

Noor watched her do it. When Elsie had finished she looked at the page a moment longer. She turned her cup a quarter turn on its saucer, with two gloved fingers, slowly. "I'll sit at the back." She didn't look up from the cup. "At the back. Not near the lantern." She turned the cup another quarter turn. "Don't put me near the lantern."

"Yes. Yes, the back. Wherever you like. The very back, or the side, or the bottom, if you'd rather lie down." She felt the squeal coming up. She felt it rise right up into her throat, high and huge, and she didn't let it out. She clapped both hands over her mouth instead and held them there, hard, and sat at the table with her eyes enormous over the top of her fingers, shaking.

The tips of Noor's ears had gone pink. Elsie could see them under the straight black hair, the way she had seen them yesterday, and the day before. Noor looked at her over the two cups, at Elsie's hands over her mouth and her eyes over her hands. "I can still hear you. Not squealing. It's very loud." Her voice caught on loud, and went soft.

Elsie took her hands down off her mouth. She reached across the table, between the two cups, and took Noor's gloved hand in both of hers. She held it hard. She squeezed it, both her hands wrapped right round it, the black leather warm from the coffee cup, and held on, and beamed at Noor over it until her cheeks ached.

Noor let her. She didn't pull it away. Her hand lay in Elsie's two hands on the table for a long moment, quite still. Slowly, her fingers moved. The long fingers in their black leather curled, a little, round the edge of Elsie's hand. They closed. They didn't close all the way. They held on a little, and they stayed holding. Noor looked at the notebook, not at Elsie.

The doors banged open. Tilly burst through them with Ines behind her, Ines's small cold hand caught firmly in Tilly's, being towed. Tilly was talking before she was through the doorway. She had a corked glass vial hanging on a string from each hand, swinging as she came. Her goggles were askew and her braids had come half undone. There was more soot on her cheek than usual, a great deal more. Every head in the hall turned to watch her cross it.

Tilly arrived at the table out of breath. "I was up half the night. At Ines's. Ines let me use her kitchen, she's got a range, a proper one, with a hob that gets properly hot. And I've made them. The floating vials. I've made six." She held up both strings and the two vials spun on them, catching the light from the high windows. "Look. Six. These are two of them. The others are in my apron."

"They bob. They really bob. We tested them. In Ines's washtub, in the yard, all six, one after the other, I held each one under and let go and up it came, bob." She bobbed her hand to show them. "And only one of them went off. A little. Only a very little bit of a bang, it hardly counts, and it was the first one, before I'd got the cork right."

"It was only a small bang," Ines whispered. She stood beside Tilly in her black lace with her hands folded in front of her. Elsie looked at her and saw a small round singed spot on the sleeve of the black lace, near the wrist, brown at the edges, about the size of a pip. Ines saw her looking at it. She turned her wrist over, gently, so the spot was underneath.

Ines pulled out the chair beside Noor and sat down on it, close, the way she had yesterday, so close that the black lace of her sleeve touched the green wool of Noor's coat. She folded her hands in her lap. Elsie watched Noor's cup. Noor's first cup, the half-full one, sat in its saucer at Noor's elbow, and the steam went up off it straight. It stayed exactly where it was.

Noor frowned at the cup. She frowned at it the way she had frowned at it yesterday, hard, as if it had broken its word to her. Elsie saw her do it. Elsie saw her stop. It wasn't all at once. Noor's brows came down a little from where they had been. The line between them went shallower. Her gloved hand lay flat on the table beside the saucer and stayed there. She went on looking at the cup. She just didn't frown at it quite so hard.

Elsie couldn't keep it in another moment. "Noor's coming tomorrow! In the boat. At the back. She said so."

Tilly shrieked. It was a real shriek, high and piercing, and every head in the hall turned again. Her hands flew up and one of the vials flew off its string. It sailed out over the table, dropped and struck the boards with a hard glassy clack. It bounced once, twice, rolled in a little half circle on the floor, and lay there. It didn't break.

Tilly scrambled under the table after it and came up with it in her fist, her goggles knocked even further askew. She looked at it, turned it in the light, and blew a speck of sawdust off the cork. She leaned across Ines and put it straight into Noor's gloved hand, the free one, and closed Noor's leather fingers over it with her own sooty ones. "That one's yours. It bounced. It's lucky. That's the lucky one now."

Noor opened her hand and held the vial up between finger and thumb to the light from the high window. It was a small fat thing of greenish glass, stoppered with cork and wax, with something pale and cloudy shifting inside. She turned it, slowly, one way and then the other, and watched the cloudy stuff turn after it. She didn't say thank you. She didn't say anything. She closed her hand round it, put it in the pocket of her long green coat, and buttoned the pocket over it.

Elsie remembered the notice. She dug in her satchel and found it, folded in quarters along its creases, and unfolded it on her way across the hall to the clerk's desk, with Noor's eyes on her back the whole way. She could feel them. She laid it flat on the desk under the clerk's nose, the festival seal in red wax at the bottom. "Please, may I add a name? To the barge guard? A fourth name."

Behind her, boots crossed the boards. Noor had followed her. She came up to the desk at Elsie's shoulder in her long green coat, with her gloved hands at her sides, and looked down at the clerk. "Noor Haddad. Hexer. E-rank." It might have been read off her own slate on the Leftovers. The clerk had dipped her pen. It came up out of the ink and hung there over the line, not writing.

The clerk looked up past them, to the end of the desk, where Cora stood. Cora didn't look up. She was writing in her ledger, her pen going steadily across the page in her small exact hand. "It's in order." She didn't lift her eyes from the ledger. "A party may take a fourth on a notice of this rank, and Miss Haddad is registered E-rank." She turned a page. The clerk looked at her a moment longer. She bent and wrote the name into the guild book.

Noor watched the clerk's pen. She stood at the desk with her face perfectly still and watched it go across the line, down and back. She watched her own name come out of the end of it, letter by letter, into the big book, under Fairweather, Reyes and Brandt. Elsie watched Noor watching. Noor's gloved hand had gone to the pocket where the vial was, and stayed flat over it.

"Four," Noor said. "Four is a lot of people to sink." It came out low and rough, nothing like a slate. Noor shut her mouth and looked hard at the guild book, at her own name in it.

The doors swung in again. Tamsin came through them from the docks side, in the sun, with Margery on her back. Her curls were tied up anyhow, and her face was brown, freckled and grinning. She swung in as if she owned the hall. She was looking round for them already, and when she saw them all at the clerk's desk, Noor among them, her eyebrows went up into her copper hair.

Tilly ran to meet her halfway across the boards. "Noor's coming." That was all it took. Tamsin whooped. She whooped so loud that the pigeons on the high sills went up with a clatter. She came the rest of the way across the hall in about three strides and clapped Noor on the shoulder, hard, with her big hand, the way she'd clap a bargeman. "About time. Welcome aboard."

Something went snap. Tamsin looked down. Her bootlace had broken. It had snapped clean in the middle, and the two ends lay on the boards on either side of her boot, and the boot sagged open at the top. Tamsin looked at it. She looked at Noor. Noor looked back at her, perfectly flat, with her hand still on the pocket where the vial was. Tamsin started to laugh. She laughed and laughed, until she had to lean on the clerk's desk with both elbows to hold herself up.

When she could talk, Tamsin wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist. "Four in a boat. Four's better than three." She straightened up and looked Noor up and down, all her long legs, all her long coat. "Five, with Cora. And the boat had better be a big one, with the length of you." She bent and knotted the two ends of her bootlace together without even looking at them.

In all the noise, Ines had come to stand at Elsie's elbow. She leaned up very close to Elsie's ear. "I'll watch the fireworks from the shore," she whispered. "Tomorrow night. From the Lantern Steps, or near. With Biscuit." She paused. "He likes the bangs. He sits on my shoulder and his bones rattle every time one goes up."

"I'll look for your boat. I'll look for the pink." She smiled the small careful smile that seemed to cost her something. "I must go home and sleep. I was up half the night watching vials bob in a washtub." She went off across the hall, small and quiet. In the doorway she was a little black shape against the bright square, for a moment, with her lace and her sleek bob. The sun took her.

When Ines had gone, Tamsin hooked her thumbs in her belt. "The festival lends the boats. From the Lantern Steps. They've got a whole row of them tied up there, I saw them going past this morning, all painted up." She jerked her head toward the doors. "We should go and see ours. See what she's like. Before we have to sit in her all night in the dark."

At nine the Lantern Steps were bright with sun and loud with gulls. The wide stone stairs ran down from the waterfront straight into the Mere, and at the bottom of them, all along the lowest steps, rowing boats were tied up nose to tail, bumping gently together. A brisk grey woman was going down the line of them with a slate in one hand and a stub of chalk in the other, touching each boat with the toe of her boot and making a mark.

She looked up as they came down the steps toward her, and spoke before any of them had said a word. "Odile Marsh. Festival master. You'll be the barge guard." She held out her hand, not to shake, but for the notice. Elsie gave it to her. Odile Marsh read it through, top to bottom, very fast, and looked at the stamp on it. She grunted. It wasn't a friendly grunt or an unfriendly one. It was a grunt that had been made a great many times before.

Odile Marsh's eyes went to Cora. They went to the charcoal coat, to the silver pin on it, and stayed there a moment. After that they came to Elsie. They went over her pink hair, her halter and her little satchel, all of it, quickly, as if she were a boat being counted. "So. You're the Enthraller's lot. Can you row?"

Tamsin got in before Elsie could answer, with her chin up. "I grew up on a barge. On the Long Cut. The Marigold."

Odile Marsh looked at her, at her brown arms and her freckles and the great axe on her back. "A barge isn't a boat." She marked something on her slate. "But it'll do."

She pointed down with her chalk. At the very bottom step, tied low on a short rope, sat a little rowing boat, smaller than Elsie had pictured it. It was painted green, a fresh grassy green, with a white line round its edge, and on its bow, in neat white figures, someone had painted a number: eleven. It rocked a little on the wash. "That's yours. Eleven. She's sound. Bring her back sound."

She went down the last steps and laid a hand on the bow. Fixed there, on a pole as tall as Elsie, was a lantern with a hood over it, black tin, so its light would go only one way, forward, onto the water. "Hooded. So you can see out and they can't see in." She bent and pointed under the middle seat. In the shadow there, tucked against the ribs of the boat, was a brass bell with a wooden handle, about the size of a teapot.

"The bell." She looked up at all of them, one after another, with her pale sharp eyes. "If you see a black punt, on the water, with muffled oars, you ring that. The moment you see it. Not when you've had a good look. Not when you're sure. The moment." She tapped the bell with her chalk, tink. "Ring first. Think after."

"They cut the lantern boats loose. The little ones, with families in them, and the empty ones tied out on the moorings. They slip in among them in the dark, quiet, cut the ropes, and tow them off. Out on the water, away from everyone, they strip them. Lanterns, purses, rings off fingers. Anything."

"Most of them run from a bell. A bell means a guard boat, and a guard boat means the rest of us coming." She paused. "Some don't." She looked at Margery. She looked at the big axe on Tamsin's back for a long moment, the curve of the blade showing over Tamsin's shoulder in its leather. She didn't say anything else about it.

"You can take eleven out this morning, if you've a mind to learn her. Shallows only. Inside the posts." She pointed with her chalk at a line of tall wooden posts standing up out of the water a little way off, a stone's throw, with the deep blue Mere beyond them. "Not past them." She marked her slate. "And bring her back dry. I've counted her."

Noor didn't come down. She had stopped on the top step, at the edge of the waterfront, where the stairs began, and she stood there with her hands in her coat pockets. "I'll get in a boat tomorrow," she called down, over all their heads. "Not before. Once is plenty." She sat down on the top step, folding her long legs under her coat, and stayed there.

Tamsin hopped down into eleven from the bottom step. She did it without looking, the way Elsie would have stepped off a kerb, and the little green boat barely moved under her. It dipped, a finger's breadth, and was still. She stood in the middle of it with her boots planted wide and Margery on her back. She turned round and held up one big hand for Elsie, grinning.

Elsie took the hand and stepped in. The boat tipped. It tipped all at once, sideways, far more than it had for Tamsin. The water came up green and bright at the edge. Elsie sat down. She sat down very hard and very fast on the middle seat, with a thump that she felt in her teeth, and her skirt went everywhere. She burst out laughing. She held on to the seat with both hands and laughed up at Tamsin.

Tamsin sat down behind her. She sat on the same seat, astride it, right behind Elsie, so close that her knees came either side of Elsie's hips. "Right. You're going to row. And I'm going to teach you." She unhooked the two oars from their pegs and fitted them into the rowlocks, one, two, with a wooden clunk. "Can't have you sitting about in the bow all night going ooh at the fireworks."

Tamsin reached round her. Her arms came round Elsie from behind, one on each side, brown and freckled and bare from the shoulder in the sleeveless jerkin. She took Elsie's hands, one in each of hers, and put them on the oar handles, and folded Elsie's fingers round the smooth worn wood. She put her own hands over the top. Her hands covered Elsie's entirely. They were warm and rough and very big.

Tamsin's chest was against Elsie's back. Elsie could feel it there, warm, right through the thin cream halter, the whole front of Tamsin pressed along her bare shoulder blades and the laces down her spine. She could feel Tamsin breathing. She could feel the hard leather of the jerkin's edge and the softness above it. Tamsin's chin had come down beside her ear, close, so close Elsie could feel the warmth of her cheek without it quite touching.

"Dip." Tamsin's voice in her ear had gone gruff and low. "Pull. Lift. Back." She moved Elsie's hands with her own as she said each word. The oars went into the water, came back and lifted out, dripping. "Dip. Pull. Lift. Back." Elsie's hair was blowing back in the breeze off the water, and a pink strand of it went across Tamsin's face. Tamsin wrinkled her nose. She didn't move her face away.

They pulled out from the steps together. The water slid away under the green hull. The steps went back, and the boats tied there went back. Suddenly there was the Mere all round them, blue, wide and glittering. Elsie felt Tamsin's arms working round her, either side, the long muscles going hard and easing, hard and easing, sure as a clock. Each pull drew Tamsin's chest in against her back and let it go.

"It's like dancing!" Elsie leaned into the next pull. "Isn't it? It's just like dancing. You lead and I follow." Behind her, Tamsin went quiet. The gruff voice stopped saying dip and pull. Her arms kept on moving, but the quiet went on. Elsie looked sideways. Tamsin's ear was right there by her cheek, and it had gone red. It had gone bright red, all the way round the edge, under the copper curls.

Elsie leaned back into her to see her face. She tipped her head right back against Tamsin's shoulder and looked up at her, upside down nearly, smiling. Tamsin's face was red too, under its freckles, and her green eyes were fixed somewhere far off over Elsie's head, very hard. "Look where we're going. Not at me. At where we're going."

Tamsin took her hands away. "Go on, then. On your own. Let's see." Her hands went off Elsie's and her arms went back. Suddenly there was just Elsie, with two oars that were much heavier than they had been a moment ago. She dipped. She pulled. The right oar went in deep. The left oar skimmed along the top of the water, slapping and spraying. All at once it caught.

The left oar jerked back. It jerked so hard the handle came back into Elsie's middle, and she let go of the other one, and the seat went out from under her. She went straight back off it, over backwards, and landed in Tamsin's lap. She landed flat on her back across Tamsin's thighs, with her head somewhere near Tamsin's middle. Her legs and boots were up in the air, kicking, and her skirt was round her waist.

She lay there. She looked up at Tamsin upside down, at the underside of her freckled chin and her copper curls against the sky. She started to laugh. She laughed until her stomach hurt. She laughed until she couldn't breathe, lying there across Tamsin's lap with her boots in the air and the little green boat rocking under them, the tears running sideways out of her eyes into her hair.

Tamsin stared down at her. She didn't laugh. Not at first. She stared down at Elsie lying across her lap, with her face gone very still and her lips apart. Her hands had come up. They hovered over Elsie, one over her middle and one over her shoulder, a finger's breadth off the halter, close enough that Elsie could feel the warmth of them, and stayed there. They didn't touch her. They just hovered.

Tamsin laughed too. It burst out of her all at once, the big rough barge laugh, and she shook with it. She put her hands round Elsie's waist, both of them, and lifted her. She lifted Elsie straight up off her lap and back onto the seat in front of her, the right way up, as if she weighed no more than a sack of feathers. She set her down and let go, still laughing.

Elsie pushed her hair out of her eyes and looked back at the steps. Cora was standing on them, halfway up, with her ledger open on her arm. She had stopped writing. Her pen was lifted off the page and she was looking out at the boat, very still. Elsie picked up the oar that had caught and waved it at her, high, dripping, with both hands. Cora looked down at her ledger again.

Elsie rowed them back in alone. She did it crooked, very crooked, one oar and then the other. Eleven went toward the steps in long slow zigzags like a hen crossing a yard. Twice she had to stop and turn them back round. Tamsin sat behind her with her arms folded and didn't offer to help at all. "You're a natural." They bumped into the bottom step. "A natural disaster."

By half past ten the sun was high and hot on the steps. Tilly had taken out one of her floating vials and gone down to the bottom step, where the water lapped, and crouched there with her braids hanging forward. She held the vial under the water with one finger, and let go. It came up. It bobbed on the little waves, fat, green and shining, turning gently round and round. Tilly clapped. Up on the top step, Noor watched it bob.

Tilly fished it out again and shook it. "There. They'll hold a person up. One each. If the person holds on to it properly, round the middle like this, and doesn't panic." She clutched the vial to her chest to show them. "That's important. The not panicking. If you thrash about you'll knock it out of your own hands and then it'll just bob off on its own without you."

"I'll panic," Noor said from the top step.

Tilly looked up at her, squinting against the sun, and thought about it. "Then you'll just have to hold on very hard. With both hands. Very, very hard. And panic at the same time. You can do both, I think, if you try."

Elsie climbed out of the boat onto the warm stone and went up the steps. She stopped at the step just below Noor's and sat down on it, with her back to Noor, and leaned back. She leaned right back against Noor's knees, without asking, the way she'd lean against a wall in the sun, and settled there, and tipped her face up to the sky with her eyes half shut.

Noor went stiff. Elsie felt it, through her back, through Noor's knees: the whole of her going still and hard as a post. Noor didn't move away. She didn't move at all. Elsie stayed where she was and waited, comfortable, warm. After a while, a long while, something touched the top of her head. It was one gloved hand. It came down very slowly and rested on Elsie's hair, lightly, on the top, and stayed.

Elsie shut her eyes in the sun. The light came pink through her eyelids. Noor's hand stayed where it was, on the top of her head, so light she could hardly feel the weight of it, only the warmth. The leather had been in the sun and it was warm, warm all through, and it smelled faintly of coffee. The gulls went round overhead, and the water slapped at the bottom step, and Noor's hand stayed.

After a while Elsie opened her eyes and took the second glove out of her satchel. She found her place on the needles and went on with the second finger, there on the step, with her back against Noor's knees. Plain, plain, plain. Noor's hand had lifted off her head when she moved. Elsie felt it go. A little later, she felt it come back. It rested on her hair again, as light as before, while the needles ticked.

Cora had come up and sat on the step beside them, not close, with her knees together and her ledger open on her knee. Elsie leaned over without stopping her needles to see what she had written, the way she always looked. Under the day's date, in Cora's small exact hand, there was one line. Lantern Steps. And under that the other line, the one Elsie always looked for first. Skills used, none.

At noon a stall at the top of the steps was selling hand pies out of a hot box. The smell came down the steps on the warm air, pepper, gravy and crust. Tamsin went up to it. Elsie watched her count the coppers out of her own purse onto the stall's board, one, two, three, four, five, a pip for each pie. She came back down with five pies stacked in her two hands, brown, shining and too hot to hold. She was blowing on her fingers. She held the first one out to Elsie. "Rowers eat first. Even the crooked ones."

Elsie took it in both hands, hot through her gloves. "Thank you. For the pie. And for teaching me. Even if I'm a disaster." Tamsin went pink under her freckles and turned to hand the rest round.

When she came to Noor, Noor's hand went into her coat, for her purse. Tamsin saw it and held the pie out further. "Don't. I feed the party. That's how it goes. Ask anybody." She jerked her chin back down the steps toward the guild hall, across the water. "And you're on the notice now. Clerk wrote you in. So you're the party, and I feed you."

Noor took the pie. Her hand came out of her coat empty and she took it from Tamsin and held it, in both her gloved hands, in front of her, a moment, the way she had held the little vial up to the light. She looked at it. She looked at it as if she weren't sure what it was for. She ate it. She ate the whole of it, neatly, in small bites, without a crumb wasted, and didn't say anything until it was gone.

Cora took hers from Tamsin with a small incline of her head, the one she gave the guildmaster, as if Tamsin had handed her a report. "Reyes. Thank you." She didn't sit to eat it. She stood at the edge of the steps with her ledger under her arm, very straight, eating her pie in small exact bites and looking out over the water toward the barge, a dark shape far out on the blue.

Tamsin sat on a step with her pie in one hand and counted on the fingers of the other, between bites. Elsie could see her lips moving. "Twelve." Her mouth was full. "Twelve lanterns for tomorrow." She held up her fingers. "Eight short for the house. So eight for the house." She folded eight down. "And four for pie." She wiggled what was left, delighted with it, and took another bite.

She stopped chewing. She looked up at Noor, sitting on the top step with her long legs folded under her coat. She looked at her own fingers. "Four on the notice now." She unfolded her fingers and started again, frowning. "Four of us. And twelve." She folded some fingers down, and put some back up, and looked at them. "Twelve doesn't go eight and four. Not with four of us. Does it?"

Elsie didn't stop to think. "Noor gets a whole share. Of course she does. A whole share, the same as everybody. She's coming in the boat." She looked at Tamsin, a little surprised that there was anything to count. "That's what a party is. You share it out."

"Course she does. Course. Whole share." Tamsin nodded firmly. She went on frowning at her fingers, though. She folded them down and up, and down again. She moved her lips. She took her other hand away from her pie to help, so the pie sat on her knee. She was trying to make it go. Elsie could see that it wasn't going, and that Tamsin was going to keep trying until it did.

"I don't want a share," Noor said from the top step. She brushed a crumb off her coat. "I'm not coming for the coin." She looked out at the water. "I'm coming to watch the fireworks from a boat." She paused. "A boat that sinks. I want that noted. When it does. That I said so."

"You get a share. You do. And we'll argue about it after." Elsie smiled up at Noor, very sure. "On the twenty-first. When we're all dry."

Noor looked down at her from the top step for a moment, with her face perfectly straight. "Ask me tomorrow."

Tamsin snorted. She snorted so hard she blew pie crumbs out of her nose and all over her own knee. She had to cough and thump her chest. Tilly fell about. She laughed so hard she couldn't sit up any more. She lay down flat on her back on the warm stone step, with her arms out and her braids spread. She laughed up at the sky until she had hiccups.

When Tilly could sit up again she wiped her eyes on her apron and stood up all at once. "I have to go. I have to brew more bobbers. Six isn't enough. Six is one each and one spare, and what if one bounces wrong, or a gull gets it." She was already backing up the steps. "Before the light goes. Ines said I could use the range again." She turned and ran off up the steps, her braids flying, her apron flapping round her knees.

Noor stood. She shook out her long coat and brushed the pie crumbs off the front of it with one gloved hand. "I'll be here at dusk. Tomorrow." She looked down the steps at all of them, at eleven bobbing at the bottom. "At the back." She turned and walked away along the waterfront, along the Mere, in her long green coat, with her hands in her pockets.

Elsie watched her go. Noor walked straight along the waterfront without looking round, the way she must have walked away from a great many places. People stepped out of her way as she came. She didn't look back. Elsie kept watching. At the corner, by the end of the long wall where the waterfront turned toward the town, Noor's head turned. She looked back, once, quickly, over her shoulder, at the steps. At last the corner had her.

Tamsin was looking at the corner where Noor had gone, but Elsie didn't think she was seeing it. She had her thumbs hooked in her belt and her jaw set. "I want to go to Coin Street. I want to ask him. The agent. If he'll wait. Only two days." She looked at Elsie. "Only till the barge pays. He might. If I ask him nicely." She thought about that. "If you ask him nicely."

At one, the letting agent's office on Coin Street was small and hot. It smelled of sealing wax and old paper. The agent sat behind a desk heaped with keys, hundreds of keys, on rings and on strings and loose, with brown paper tags tied to them. He was mopping his forehead with a big white handkerchief. When he saw who had come in, he put the handkerchief down and sighed, not unkindly.

Tamsin stood in front of his desk with her hands clenched at her sides. "The blue-door house. On Willow Canal. Will you hold it? Till the twenty-first. Only till then. We'll have the gold by then. We've got the barge guard tomorrow night, it pays twelve lanterns, we'll be paid the morning after, and that's enough. That's the rest of it."

The agent shook his head slowly. "I can't hold it, my dear. I'm sorry. I truly am." He spread his plump hands over the heaps of keys. "It isn't mine to hold. The owner says whoever brings me four suns first, and four suns first it is. I'd be out on my ear if I turned away gold on the doorstep for a promise." He picked up the handkerchief again. "Whoever brings four suns first."

Elsie stepped up beside Tamsin. "When will the others bring theirs? The other parties. The ones who came to look. Did they say?" The agent looked at her. He looked at her pink hair and her pink eyes, and at her face, for a moment longer than he needed to, as if he were deciding something.

"The C-rank party says after Midsummer. After their Midsummer job. Week after, at the outside, they said, but you heard them." He tapped a key on the desk. "And the other party that came to look, a day after, they say the twenty-second. The twenty-second, first thing." He tucked the handkerchief away. "That's what they've told me. I can't promise they'll keep to it."

Tamsin counted. Elsie watched her do it, on her fingers, against her leg, her lips moving. "Barge pays on the twenty-first. The morning after. Bring him the gold the twenty-first." She folded a finger down. "The others say after Midsummer, and the twenty-second." She looked up. Her eyes had gone wide. "That's a day. That's a day ahead. Of both of them."

Her face changed. Elsie saw it close up again, as fast as it had opened. "If we're paid on time. If the barge job goes right." She looked down at her clenched hands. "If the boat stays the right way up." There was a little laugh on the end of it, and it wasn't a laugh, not really. It was the sound you make on a step that turns out to be lower than you thought.

The agent looked at the two of them across his desk, at Tamsin's freckled face gone tight and Elsie beside her. He took out his watch on its chain and looked at it, and put it away. "I'll be in this office at eight o'clock on the morning of the twenty-first. At my desk. With the door open. That's all I can tell you."

"Thank you," Elsie said. "Thank you ever so much." She smiled at him across the keys. "On the twenty-first, I'll bring you a honey cake. A warm one. From the Honeycomb, on Thimble Canal, the morning we come. I promise." She was already thinking about it: which tin, and how to keep it warm on the walk, wrapped in a cloth inside her satchel. The agent blinked at her, and a little of the hot pink went out of his face.

Out on Coin Street the sun was blinding after the dark little office. Tamsin stopped on the cobbles outside the door. She pressed her knuckles against her mouth, both fists, hard, and stood there with her eyes shut. Elsie waited beside her. At last Tamsin took her fists down. "A day ahead." It came through her teeth, very quietly. "A day ahead's ahead."

Elsie hugged her. She put both arms right round Tamsin's middle, round the jerkin, the belt and Margery's haft down her back, axe and all. She squeezed as hard as she could. Tamsin made a sound into the top of Elsie's head. Tamsin hugged her back, hard, so hard that Elsie's feet came right up off the cobbles. She hung there in Tamsin's arms with her boots dangling, laughing into the leather.

Tamsin set her down at last. She wiped her nose on the back of her wrist and grinned, crooked and wet. "I'm off to the docks. To the wharf office. I'll leave a letter for Mum. About the house." She hitched Margery's strap. "About the blue door and the garden and the step. So the wharf office has it for her when the Marigold comes back through." She was already turning to go.

"I've got shopping," Elsie said. "On Spindle Lane." She looked round. Cora was standing a little way off along Coin Street, in the shade of a doorway, with her ledger, where she had stood all through the office. Elsie looked at her and then back at Tamsin, and put her finger to her lips. "It's a secret shopping!" It carried right along Coin Street. Tamsin's mouth twitched. She lifted a hand and went off down Coin Street toward the docks, with Margery bobbing over the crowd.

At half past two Spindle Lane was narrow and shady and full of colour. Every shop along it had wool in its window, or thread, or ribbon, hanging in skeins, in hanks and on wooden spools, red, yellow, blue and every green there was. Elsie went straight along it to the shop she knew, the one with the grey skeins hung in the window, all the greys from pale to slate, and pushed open its door. A little bell rang over her.

Cora came in after her. She had to. She came in through the door with the bell ringing a second time over her head and stopped just inside it, with her back to the door and her ledger held flat in front of her coat. The shop was small, close and soft, wool on every wall up to the ceiling. Cora stood in the middle of all that softness looking straight ahead, as stiff as a post that had wandered in by mistake.

Elsie looked over her shoulder at Cora. "The second glove. It's going to run out. The wool. I've been measuring." She held up her hands as if there were a ball of wool between them, a small one. "There's enough left for the third finger. Nearly. But not the last finger as well, not the little one. So I need one more skein. Only one." She turned back to the wall.

She went along the wall where the greys were and took three skeins down off their pegs, one after another. She held them together in her hands in the light from the window and looked at them. They were nearly the same grey. They were very nearly the same, all three, a soft cool grey like a winter morning. But they weren't quite. One was a little bluer. One was a little warmer. One was a little more silver, the way the Mere went before the sun was up.

She couldn't tell, with them all together. So she turned round. She held up the first skein beside Cora's face, beside her cheek, at the height of her eyes, and looked from one to the other. Cora went still. She had been still already, but she went stiller. She went perfectly still, like a pond going flat when the wind drops, with the skein of grey wool a hand's breadth from her face.

It was hard to see properly from down there. Elsie went up on her toes. She went right up on the tips of her boots to bring her face level, and put her free hand on Cora's sleeve to keep her balance. She held up the second skein in place of the first, close, very close, by Cora's cheek, almost touching the porcelain skin there, and looked.

She looked into Cora's eyes. She looked into them from a hand's breadth away, very seriously, with her brows drawn together, the way she'd look at a seedling to see whether it had blight. Cora's eyes were grey. They were cool clear grey, with darker grey at the edge, and little flecks that were almost silver near the middle, and they were looking straight back at her. Elsie looked at them for a long moment. After that she looked at the wool.

Cora didn't move. She didn't move a finger. Elsie thought, for a moment, that she wasn't even breathing. And colour came up in Cora's face. It came up slowly from under her collar, pink, along the fine high bones of her cheeks, the way the sky comes up pink over the Mere at dawn, until both her cheekbones were flushed with it and it reached her ears.

Elsie held up the third skein. She held it beside Cora's eye and looked, and her face broke into a beam. "That one. That one! That's exactly it. That's exactly the right grey." She came down off her toes, delighted, with the skein held to her chest. "The other two were nearly. But that one's right. The silvery one. It's exactly the same."

Cora let out a breath. Elsie heard it go. "I had better not ask what it's the right grey of." It was very nearly her report voice, very nearly level and exact, but it shook at the end, a little, the way a cup does on a saucer when it's set down too quickly.

"You'd better not. Because I can't lie. You know I can't. So if you ask me, I'll have to tell you." She held the skein tight against her chest with both arms, the way you'd hold a cat that wanted to get down. "And then the surprise would be spoiled. And I've been ever so careful with it."

She paid at the counter: four copper pips for the skein, counted out one by one into the shop woman's palm. She didn't need it wrapped. She took the skein and tucked it into her satchel, down beside the knitting needles and the second glove, on top of the notebook. She patted the flap down over it.

Out in Spindle Lane a flower seller had set up her bucket on the corner, where the lane met the canal, with the afternoon sun on it. There were roses in the bucket, and stocks, and a great purple armful of lavender. Elsie stopped. She bought one sprig of the lavender for a pip, held out on her palm, and lifted it to her face and breathed in. It smelled clean and sweet and a little sharp, like a cupboard where good linen was kept.

Cora was looking at the canal, not at the lavender. "Who's the lavender for?" Every word came out the same size as the one before.

"It's for someone I love." Elsie smelled it again, and smiled.

Cora didn't say anything at all. She turned her head from the canal and looked at the lavender instead, at the sprig in Elsie's hand, purple and grey-green, with the sun on it. She looked at it for a long moment. Her face didn't move. Her hand, holding the ledger, had gone tight on the edge of it, so that the leather creased under her fingers.

Elsie opened her satchel and tucked the sprig in carefully, beside the grey wool, so the flowers wouldn't crush. She buckled the satchel shut, both buckles, and patted it. She hummed as she did it, a little tune, up and down, and didn't notice she was humming until she noticed Cora listening to it.

They walked back toward the guild hall along the canals in the long afternoon. Elsie got the second glove out again and knitted as she walked, the way she had learned on the road to the mill, watching her feet and her needles both. Plain, plain, plain. The second finger grew round and grey between her needles, over one bridge, under the lanterns and over the next, until it was long enough. She decreased it, round by round, down to the tip.

She shaped it off, drew the wool through and pulled it snug. She held the glove up to the sun at arm's length. The light came through the grey wool, soft and silvery, through the thumb, the long first finger and the long second one. "Thumb. And two fingers." She wiggled it. "Two more to go." She smiled at it the way she smiled at Moonpenny.

Cora was watching the glove. Elsie saw her watching it, up there in the sun, with her grey eyes on the grey wool, steadily. Elsie lowered it and tucked it back into the satchel, beside the skein and the lavender. She buckled it. Cora watched it go in. She went on looking at the buckled satchel a moment after. At last she shifted her ledger from one arm to the other and looked straight ahead, at the road.

At six the porters came out and dragged the tall guild hall doors shut, one and then the other, with a long groan of hinges. The last of the day's crowd went down the steps into Brightwater Square in the gold evening light. Cora went up to the top step and stopped, the way she stopped every evening, at the place where she went off duty. She turned round to face Elsie on the step below.

"Fairweather." She inclined her head. But she didn't go. She stood on the top step a moment longer with her ledger held in front of her, looking at a point just past Elsie's ear. "The Mere is cold at night. Even now. Even in summer. Out on the water, after dark, the cold comes up off it."

"Wear something warm tomorrow." Her eyes came down to Elsie's halter and went away from it again very quickly. "Over the halter. Something with sleeves." It was her report voice, going faster than her report voice went. "Something with sleeves."

"I've got a shawl somewhere. In the bottom of the crate, I think, under Captain. It might smell a bit of mint." She looked up at Cora, and brightened. "Or you could lend me your coat again. Like before. At the barrow. That was warm." She smiled. "I was ever so warm in it."

Cora's mouth opened. Elsie watched it open. It stayed open, a little way, for a moment, as if words were coming. It shut again, and she hadn't said a word. The tips of her ears had gone pink, above the high charcoal collar, both of them, and the pink was spreading.

Elsie looked up at her and stopped smiling. "Cora. Yesterday. By the threads, in the square. I said I understood why you'd told me not to do it again, and you said it isn't why." She looked into the grey eyes. "So why was it? If it isn't that?"

Cora looked past her. She looked out over Elsie's head at Brightwater Square, at the long gold light across the cobbles and the pigeons and the canal beyond, for a long time. Her hands had gone tight on the ledger. "Not tonight." It was so low the pigeons nearly had it.

"Tomorrow?"

Cora was quiet. She was quiet so long that a whole barge went by on the canal at the bottom of the square, slowly, with a dog on its roof. Elsie waited and watched her face. At last Cora's lips moved. "Perhaps."

Elsie laughed. She couldn't help it. It came bubbling up out of her, bright, in the gold evening. "Everybody's saying tomorrow! All day! Everybody's saying tomorrow today." She counted it on her fingers. "Noor said ask me tomorrow. And now you've said perhaps tomorrow. And tomorrow's Midsummer!" She laughed again. "It's going to be ever so full. Tomorrow."

Cora's mouth went in at one corner. It went in and up, very slightly, and held there. It was nearly a smile. It was so nearly a smile that Elsie stopped laughing to look at it properly. At last Cora turned and went down the steps past her, straight-backed, her ledger under her arm. She went off across the square toward the canal and Wick Lane, with her long shadow going in front of her.

Elsie stood on the step and watched her go along the water. Past the first bridge, Cora walked straight on. At the second bridge she stopped. She turned and looked back, over her shoulder, at the guild steps, and at Elsie on them. Elsie was still there. She put both arms up over her head and waved them, both at once, wide, back and forth, like someone signalling a ship.

At seven the Honeycomb was shut, the blind down over the door and the shutters across the window. The big oven at the back was cooling, ticking softly to itself as the iron settled. The shop smelled of the day's last bread. Elsie tied on one of Mrs Merriweather's aprons, a long white one that came down nearly to her boots and had to be wrapped twice round her middle. She fetched down the bag of flour from where she had left it, and the butter from the cold shelf.

She was making honey cakes for the boat. She had decided that on the stairs. Enough for five, she thought, setting out the bowls, Elsie, Tamsin, Tilly, Noor and Cora. And then some over, in case. And then, she thought, measuring out the flour, some over again, in case of the case. People got hungry on the water at night. Tamsin got hungry anywhere. She doubled the flour, and looked at it, and added a little more.

Mrs Merriweather came through from the back with the crock of honey in her two hands, the big one, brown and heavy. She set it down on the counter by Elsie's elbow with a solid clunk, and wiped her hands on her apron. She looked at the bowls, the flour and the butter, and at Elsie in the long apron, all of it, once. She didn't say a word. She went up the stairs to bed, slowly, one at a time, and the door shut at the top.

Elsie beat the butter in the big bowl with the wooden spoon, round and round, until it went pale and soft. She hummed as she beat it. It was a little up-and-down tune, nothing in particular, the sort that comes from nowhere, and she didn't know she was humming it. She only knew that the butter was going nicely and the kitchen was warm and the flutter in her stomach had gone quiet for the first time all day.

Someone tapped on the shop window. It was a soft tap, two knocks with one knuckle, on the glass by the door. Elsie looked up. She went round the counter and lifted the edge of the blind. Outside on the canal walk, under the red paper lantern over the door, with the canal behind her going gold and red, stood Amara. She had her hands behind her back, and her braids over one shoulder, and she was looking at the window as if she weren't sure she ought to be.

Elsie pulled the bolt and opened the door, and the bell jangled. She was floury to the elbows. There was flour on the apron and flour on her halter and very likely flour on her face. "Amara! Come in, come in. Oh, you're just in time." She held up her floury hands. "I need someone to pour while I beat. I can't beat and pour at once, I've only got the two hands, and the honey's ever so heavy."

Amara came in. She came in slowly, over the step, and stopped just inside the door, and looked round. She looked at everything: the racks of trays with their cloths over, the long counter worn pale in the middle, the shelves of jars, the great black oven ticking at the back, the bins of flour, the hooks of copper pans. She looked at it the way you look at a place you've only ever seen through a window. Elsie realised she never had been in. Only on the step.

"I couldn't sleep." Amara was looking at the shelves. "I tried. I lay down and I couldn't, I kept getting up again." She turned a little pink in the lamplight. "And I thought the window might be lit. Your window. The bakery. I thought, if it's lit, then it's lit, and if it's not, I'll walk home." She looked at the lamp over the counter. "It's lit."

Elsie took down a second apron from the hook and gave it to her, and a wooden spoon, a long one with a burnt end. Amara put on the apron and tied it behind her, and then held the spoon. She held it out in front of her in her fist, at arm's length, as if it might turn round and bite her. Elsie laughed. "Have you ever stirred anything?" Amara looked at the spoon for a while. "Tea."

Elsie went round behind her at the bowl. She stood close behind her, right at her back, and reached round her, and put her own hand over Amara's on the spoon, her floury fingers over Amara's warm brown ones. "Like this. Not hard. Round and round, and then fold it over. Like you're tucking it into bed." She moved Amara's hand with her own, slowly, round the bowl, and the batter went round with it, gold and thick.

Amara went very still under her hand. She went so still that for a moment the spoon didn't move at all, and Elsie had to move it for both of them, round, and round. Elsie felt the stillness all along her front, where she was pressed against Amara's back, and under her hand. She kept stirring, gently, patiently, round and round with her hand over Amara's. After a while Amara breathed out. It was a long breath. It went out of her all at once, and her shoulders came down.

Elsie let go and stepped back. Amara kept stirring by herself, round and round, and then folding it over, the way she had been shown. She didn't look round. She kept her eyes on the bowl, very hard. Her braids had fallen forward over one shoulder, and the back of her neck showed between them, and Elsie saw that it had gone warm, a deep warm flush under the honey-brown skin, right down into the collar of her dress.

When the batter was right they spooned it into the little tins together, standing side by side at the counter, Elsie at one end of the row of tins and Amara at the other, working toward each other. They met in the middle. Their elbows bumped. Amara laughed, a little startled laugh, the way she had laughed on the step yesterday, as if Elsie had jumped out at her, and she put her hand up toward her mouth and stopped it halfway, because it had batter on it.

Amara spooned batter into the next tin. "My mother's had a gown made for me." She kept her eyes on the batter. "For the barge. For tomorrow night." She scraped the spoon on the rim of the bowl. "Gold. It's gold. All of it. To match the canopy on the barge, so we'll look like we're part of it, her and me."

"I tried it on today. This afternoon. The dressmaker came to the house with it." Her spoon had stopped. "I stood in it in front of the long glass in the hall. And it's beautiful. It is, it's beautiful." She looked down into the tin. "And I opened my mouth, in front of the glass, to try. Only me, only the glass, and the dressmaker behind me." She swallowed. "And I couldn't make a sound."

Elsie put down her spoon. "Have you told your mother yet? That you can't. With people listening."

Amara shook her head, a small quick shake, and kept her eyes down. Then she looked up, sideways, at Elsie. "Have you told yours? Whatever it is you can't. Your mother."

"Oh, I tell my mother everything. Every week. I write her a letter every week, wherever she is, and I tell her all of it. About the gloves, the barrow, Noor and the river trolls." She smiled. "I don't know how not to. I've tried, once or twice, to leave something out, for a surprise. It ends up in the letter anyway, at the bottom, in the bit after my name."

Amara was quiet a moment. She spooned batter into a tin, very carefully, and then another. She didn't look up from the tins. "What would you do? If you were me. If it were your mother and your barge and your gown." She smoothed the batter in the tin with the back of the spoon, round and round. "What would you do?"

Elsie thought about it properly, with her floury hands still on the counter. She couldn't lie, and she couldn't say a thing just because it was the thing Amara wanted to hear. "I'd tell her. I'd tell my mother. Because she'd want to know." She looked at Amara. "Because if it were me, my mother would want to know."

"You don't know my mother." It wasn't sharp. It was only very tired.

Elsie nodded. "No. I don't." She reached over and took the batter spoon out of Amara's fingers, gently, and laid it in the bowl. "But I know you."

Amara looked at her then. She turned her head and looked straight at Elsie, for the first time since she had come in, in the lamplight over the counter. Her brown eyes were very wide. There was flour on the end of her nose. It was a little white smudge, right on the tip, from the bag or the bowl or her own fingers, and she didn't know it was there. Elsie could tell she didn't.

Elsie reached up and wiped it off. She did it with her thumb, one quick soft stroke along the end of Amara's nose, the way she'd wipe a smudge off anyone. She held up her thumb between them so Amara could see the flour on it. "There. You had a little flour. Right there."

Amara didn't look at the thumb. She looked at Elsie's mouth. Her eyes went straight to it and stayed there, a moment, on Elsie's lips, and her own lips came apart a little. She looked away. She looked away fast, all at once, at the oven, at the big black oven door at the back of the shop, as if something in it had made a sound.

Elsie took up the long peel. She minded it, the way she had been told: both hands on it, slow, the end steady. She slid the tins onto it in a row and slid them into the cooling oven, to the back where it was still warm enough. She drew the peel out again with care. She hung it up on its hooks exactly where Mrs Merriweather kept it, and shut the oven door.

While they waited, Elsie washed the bowl. She stood at the stone sink by the back wall with her back to the shop and her arms in the warm water to the elbows, scrubbing the batter out of the bowl with a brush. She hummed. She hummed without thinking about it, the way she did. The tune that came out of her was the low wandering one, the tune she had heard on Bellmoor barrow in the dark, going up and down, wherever it liked, like someone walking on the downs.

Behind her, at the counter, someone hummed the next two notes. They came low and warm, so soft they were hardly louder than the water, the two notes that came after where Elsie had stopped. They were exactly right. They stopped dead, as if a hand had been clapped over them. The shop was very quiet. There was only the oven ticking.

Elsie didn't turn round. She kept her back to the counter and her arms in the water. She kept washing the bowl, slowly, round and round with the brush, as if the bowl needed every bit of her. After a moment she hummed the next two notes herself, softly, to the bowl. She stopped, and went on scrubbing.

There was a long moment. The oven ticked. The water lapped in the sink. Elsie scrubbed the inside of the bowl, which was quite clean now, very hard. Behind her, at the counter, very quietly, Amara hummed two more notes. They came out small and shaky and low. Elsie kept her eyes on the bowl. She didn't look round, not even a little. She scrubbed.

So they went on like that. Elsie hummed two notes, to the sink. Amara hummed two, behind her, at the counter. Elsie hummed two more. Back and forth, two notes each, the low wandering tune went between them across the dark shop, up and down. It was a little stronger each time it came from behind her. Soon the whole shop was full of the smell of honey, and the cakes in the oven smelled done.

Elsie dried her hands and took the cakes out. She took up the peel again, minding it, and drew the tins out of the oven one row at a time. She set them on the counter, gold, risen and crackling a little as they cooled. She turned round at last. Amara was standing at the far end of the counter. She had both hands pressed flat over her mouth, one over the other.

Her eyes over her hands were wide and wet. They were shining in the lamplight, and a tear had got out of one of them and was running down the side of her nose. "I did that." It came through her hands, muffled and shaking. "I did that with somebody listening. With you there. I did that with somebody in the room."

"I wasn't listening. I was washing a bowl." Elsie held up the bowl, which was dripping. "Very hard. Look. It's very clean now."

Amara stared at the bowl. And then she laughed. It burst out from behind her hands, a wet laugh, half a laugh and half a sob, and she bent over the counter with it.

When the cakes were cool enough to touch, Elsie took a sheet of the brown paper from the roll under the counter and laid six of them on it, warm, the best six, the ones with the brownest tops. She folded the paper over them and twisted the two ends shut, tight, the way Mrs Merriweather did, so they looked like a big brown sweet. She held the parcel out across the counter to Amara in both hands.

Amara took the parcel and hugged it to her chest. All at once, she came round the end of the counter and hugged Elsie. She hugged her hard, parcel and all, the warm brown paper squashed between them, her arms tight round Elsie's back. Her face went down into Elsie's hair, by her ear, and stayed there. Elsie could feel her breathing against her hair, quick and warm, and the warmth of the cakes through the paper against her stomach.

"I'll look for the boat." It came muffled, into Elsie's hair. "Tomorrow. From the barge. The one with the pink head in it." She held on a moment longer. Elsie felt her take a breath, as if she were going to say something else. She didn't say it. She let go and stepped back, with the parcel against her chest. Her eyes were still wet, and she was smiling.

Amara went out under the red lantern and along the canal walk toward the bridge, in the warm dark, with the parcel held against her. Elsie stood in the doorway and watched her. Halfway to the bridge Amara looked back. Further along she looked back again. At the foot of the bridge she stopped and turned right round. She looked back once more and lifted one hand, before the bridge took her.

Up in the attic at twenty past nine Elsie lit the candle and sat on the bed by the round window with her satchel open beside her. She took out the new skein of grey wool, the silvery one, and found its end. She wound it into a ball in her lap, round and round, over her fingers and off again. At last the skein was gone. There was a soft round ball of grey in her hand, the exact grey of Cora's eyes.

She took up the second glove and joined the new wool on, and began the third finger. She knitted by candlelight. Below the round window the canal lanterns were all lit, red and gold, swaying a little on their strings. Their light came up off the water and lay on her hands, on the needles and on the grey wool going round. Plain, plain, plain. The third finger grew, long, the same as the second.

She started the fourth finger, the little one. It was shorter than the others. She had measured it in her head again and again, against her own little finger and a knuckle more. Her eyes kept closing. They closed all by themselves, slowly, and she'd find she had stopped. She'd open them again with a start, find her place and go on. Plain, plain, plain. She kept going. She wasn't going to stop with only a little finger left.

She knitted the last round. She drew the wool through the top of the little finger and pulled it snug. She cast off and broke the wool with her teeth. She turned the whole glove right side out, carefully, finger by finger, poking each one through with the end of a needle. She laid it on her knee and smoothed it flat with both hands, the thumb, the four fingers, the cuff.

She laid it on the sill beside the first one. She turned them so they lay palm to palm, one on top of the other, the fingers matching, the thumbs together. Two grey gloves. A pair. They were long in the fingers, longer than her own hands by a whole knuckle, and the grey of them in the candlelight was the same grey all through, the old wool and the new, so that she couldn't see where one ended.

She took the sprig of lavender out of her satchel. It had kept well. She laid it between the two gloves, along the palms, where it would keep them smelling of it. She took a sheet of brown paper she had brought up from the shop and wrapped the gloves in it, neatly, folding the ends under. She tied the parcel with string, twice round and a bow.

She found her pencil. On the brown paper, in the middle, she drew a little lantern: a shuttered lantern, square, with a handle on top and lines coming out of its slide for the light. She looked at it. She didn't write a name, or anything else. Cora would know. She would know it was a lantern, and whose lantern, and that would be enough.

She opened her notebook to the Midsummer page. There was the boat, and the lantern on its pole, and the five heads in a row. She looked at the one at the back, with the long straight hair she had made longer that morning. Beside it, in the margin of the page, in her round hand, she wrote: yes! She underlined it twice.

She looked at the little boat a long while, with the pencil against her lip. The candle flame leaned and straightened. At last, very small, at the very back of the boat, behind the long straight hair, in the place where there was still a faint grey smudge, she drew a head with braids again. Long braids, a lot of them, all down its back.

She looked at it. She held the notebook up to the candle and looked at it a long time, the little head with the braids in the back of the boat. She put the pencil down. This time she didn't turn it round. This time she didn't rub it out.

She knelt up on the bed with her elbows on the sill by Moonpenny's pot. "We've got a boat, Moonpenny," she whispered. "A little green one, with eleven on her bow. And a hooded lantern, and a brass bell. Ring first. Think after." She leaned her chin on her arms. "And Noor stood at the clerk's desk and watched her name go into the book. She said yes, Moonpenny. Noor said yes." Last of all, very softly: "And Amara came to the shop. I was washing the bowl, and I hummed two notes, and she hummed two back. Back and forth, in the dark."

She put the brown paper parcel into her satchel, on top, where it wouldn't be squashed, and buckled the satchel shut, both buckles. She got down off the bed and set the satchel by the attic door, ready, so it would be the first thing she saw in the morning and the last thing she'd forget.

She blew out the candle. The attic went dark but for the canal light at the round window. She lay down on the narrow bed under the sloping roof and turned on her side. She hugged her pillow to her chest with both arms and held it there.

Under her breastbone the thread was there, the way it was every night now, running out through the round window and across the dark roofs toward the docks. At the far end of it was Tamsin, warm and steady, like a coal banked down for the night. Elsie held the pillow and felt it. Below on Thimble Canal a late punt went by under the lit lanterns. She heard its pole go in and come up, dripping. It knocked once on the stone of the near bridge as it passed under. Further off, softer, it knocked on the next.
