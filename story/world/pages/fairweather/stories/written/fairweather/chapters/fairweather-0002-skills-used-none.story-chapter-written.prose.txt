But the corner of her mouth moved, very slightly, and nearly went up, and then went still again, and she looked away over Elsie's head at the board. Elsie closed her hand on the coins and put them in her purse.

Tamsin had wandered over to the long quest board, and she came back tapping one of the F-rank notices with her finger. "The goat's still up," she said. "Underbloom, first floor. Lost goat. Three lanterns." She grinned. "Real dungeon. Proper one. We've got three names now, we can go." Tilly had got up off the floor with her pips. At the word dungeon she went quiet, all at once; her hands went to the vials at her belt and held on, and her lips pressed together, and the pink went out of her cheeks and left the soot.

Elsie looked at Tilly's hands on her vials. "Let's look at the goat tomorrow," she said, easily. "After lunch. We'll read the notice properly and ask about the floor, and think about it with full stomachs. I'm no good at thinking hungry." Tamsin shrugged and said tomorrow suited her. And Tilly let out her breath, a long one, and her hands came off the vials, and the colour came back into her face, and she said, "Yes, tomorrow, after lunch, that's good, that's very sensible," faster than she needed to. Elsie smiled at her and took her arm.

"The hall's business with you is done for today," Cora said. She said it to all three of them, but she was looking at Elsie when she said it. "I'll write up the morning." She inclined her head to them, the same small exact bow as at the Kettle door, and turned, and walked away across the crowded hall, straight-backed, with the ledger under her arm, the crowd parting a little in front of the charcoal coat. Elsie watched her all the way to the charcoal-painted door. Cora opened it and went in, and it shut behind her.

They sat on the canal wall below Brightwater Square in the full warmth of the afternoon, the three of them in a row with their legs hanging over the water, and ate pies out of paper. They were hot pork pies with thick crusts, and the gravy ran down Elsie's wrist and she licked it off. The canal below them was green and slow, with ducks on it. Tamsin sat in the middle with Margery across her lap and her bandaged ankle swinging. Tilly sat at the end with her goggles pushed up, picking the crust off in pieces and eating the middle first.

Tilly had gone quiet for a while, which Elsie had already learned was not like her. She sat looking at the water, turning a piece of crust in her fingers. "My father lives on Gear Street," she said suddenly, to the ducks. "Not far. Up past the clock tower. He thinks I'm still at the College." She threw the crust in, and a duck took it. "He doesn't know they threw me out. He doesn't know about the windows. He thinks I'm there right now. In a lecture." She laughed, a small unhappy laugh, and looked at her pie.

"He makes clocks," she said. "He's a clockmaker. He made the one in the tower, the works of it, when I was little; I used to sit in the case and hand him the screws." She turned her pie round in her hands. "I write him letters. Every week. About the lectures. I tell him what they were about and what the masters said and that I'm working hard and eating properly." She swallowed. "I make them up. The lectures. They're still going on without me, so I know when they are, and I write about the ones I'm missing."

Elsie put down her pie on the paper and reached across Tamsin's lap and took Tilly's hand. It was small and hot and rough at the fingertips from the burns, and there was soot in the creases of it. Tilly's fingers went stiff in hers, and then curled round. "You can tell him when you're ready," Elsie said. "Not before. Whenever that is. And when you do, I'll come with you, if you want. I'll stand right next to you. I'm very good with fathers." She thought about it. "I think I am. I'll be good with yours."

Tilly did not let go. She held Elsie's hand on the warm stone, across Tamsin's knees, with her fingers laced tight through Elsie's, and after a while her thumb began to move, slowly, back and forth over Elsie's knuckles, as if she were not aware of it. She was very pink. She started to talk about clocks. She talked about escapements and pendulums and the tower clock's great wheel and how her father oiled it, and she went on holding Elsie's hand the whole time, for a long time after there was any reason to, and Elsie let her.

Tamsin had been very quiet between them, eating her pie and looking at their joined hands in her lap. Now she wiped her mouth on the back of her wrist. "My mum runs a barge," she said. "On the Long Cut. Coal and timber, up and down. I grew up on it. Learned to swim at three, she says, because I kept going over the side." She grinned, crooked, sideways at Elsie. "She'd like you. She likes people who feed her." She looked down at her pie. "She'd like you a lot," she said, more quietly.

"Oh, I'd love to meet her," Elsie said at once, turning to her with her whole face lit. "I'd love that. Could I? Could we go on the barge? I've only been on a ferry. I'll bake her something. What does she like?" Tamsin had just taken a big bite of pie, and she choked on it. She coughed and spluttered and doubled over Margery, and her ears went scarlet, and Elsie had to let go of Tilly to thump her on the back. When Tamsin came up for air her eyes were streaming. "Honey cake," she wheezed. "She likes honey cake."

They walked Tilly home first. At the end of her street, a narrow row of tall leaning houses with washing strung between them, Tilly stopped and said she could go on alone from here, and squeezed Elsie's hand, and went off down it, turning round twice to wave. Then Elsie walked Tamsin down to the docks, where the barges lay along the stone and the air smelled of tar and water. Tamsin stood at the top of the dock steps with Margery on her shoulder. "Tomorrow," she said. "After lunch. The goat." She went down the steps, limping only a little.

The Honeycomb was shut for the hot middle of the afternoon, and the bakery was dim and smelled of cooling ovens. Elsie let herself in by the side door, climbed the stairs to the attic, pulled off her boots, and lay down on the narrow bed on top of the blanket in her clothes. The sloped ceiling was warm above her. Through the round window the canal glittered and the heat shimmered off the roofs. She meant only to rest her eyes for a minute, and she slept a whole hour, deep and dreamless, with one arm flung over her face.

When she woke the light had moved across the floor. She sat up and pulled the stool to the little table under the window, and got out her paper and her pen, and wrote to her mother, though she had written only yesterday. Dear Mum, she wrote. I know it's twice in one week and you'll think something's wrong. Nothing is wrong. I have a party. She looked at that and underlined it, twice. Its names are Tamsin and Tilly, and there's a Warden called Cora who comes too, and today we did rats. She wrote three pages.

When the letter was folded and addressed, she took out the little blue notebook and opened it to yesterday's page, under the three names, and found the pen again. Under Tamsin, she wrote: ankle, check it. She looked at the word Tamsin and thought of her eyes going flat in the cellar, and left it at that, but she pressed the pen hard enough that it showed through. Under Tilly she wrote: her father. Clocks. Gear Street. She thought, and wrote: honey cake for Tamsin's mum. Then she sat looking at the page with her chin on her fist.

At seven, when the Honeycomb had shut its doors for the night and the baker was scraping out the last trays, Elsie came down the stairs with the brown paper from the morning folded flat in her hand and gave it back. Then she asked, a little shyly, whether she might use the ovens. The baker looked at her for a moment, the way she had in the morning. Then she said firewood was two pips, same as ever. Elsie counted two copper pips out of her purse into the baker's floury palm, and the baker nodded at the ovens and went up to bed.

Elsie tied on one of the baker's aprons over her halter and got out the flour. She made honey buns, because that was what the baker had given her for luck, and because she was happy, and because she was a little worried. She kept seeing Tamsin's eyes in the cellar going flat like water under a cloud, and her hands knew what to do with that better than the rest of her did. She kneaded the dough hard on the floured board, leaning her weight into it, and set it to rise by the warm oven under a cloth, and wiped her hands.

While the buns rose she sat on a flour sack by the bakery window with her chin on her knees. Outside, the evening was going blue. The first lamplighter's boat came down Thimble Canal, slow and quiet, a woman standing in the stern with a long pole tipped with flame, and she reached up and touched the first paper lantern, and it bloomed gold. Then the next, and the next. Another boat came behind her. One by one the lanterns came alight along the whole length of the canal, and the gold ran out across the water.

When the buns came out of the oven they were brown and shining with honey, and the whole bakery smelled of them. Elsie let them cool on the rack and then wrapped them up in paper, three to a parcel, folding the ends under the way the baker did and tying each one with string. She made three parcels. Then she took the stub of pencil from the counter and wrote a name on each, carefully, in her round hand. On the first, Tamsin. On the second, Tilly, with a little curl on the y.

On the third parcel she wrote: black, strong, one sugar. Then she looked at it, with the pencil still in her hand. It was what she had written in her notebook, and it was right, but on a parcel it looked like a label on a tin. It did not look like a person. She drew a line through it, neatly, and under it she wrote Cora, and looked at that instead, and that was better. She thought of Cora drinking the whole cup to the bottom. She tied the string round it with a double bow.

She carried the parcels up the narrow stairs to the attic, stacked in her arms, warm through the paper, and set them down in a row by the door where she would see them first thing in the morning and not forget them: Tamsin, Tilly, Cora. She stood back and looked at the row of parcels on the floor by the door. They looked like a promise of something. She went and pushed the round window wide.

Then she knelt at the round window with her arms folded on the sill and her chin on her arms, and looked out at the lit canal. The lanterns swung on their strings. Boats went by below with people laughing in them, and then fewer boats, and then only one, and the water went still and black and gold. She was humming. She did not know when she had started, or what the tune was; it had come up out of her by itself while she watched. She went on humming at the round window as the voices thinned along the water, and the last boat passed, and the canal went quiet.
