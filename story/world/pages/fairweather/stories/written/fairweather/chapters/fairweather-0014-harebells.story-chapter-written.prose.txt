Elsie woke with something scratchy under her cheek. It was the glove. She had fallen asleep over it on top of the blanket, still in her skirt, and the grey wool had printed its ribbing into her face. She could feel the little ridges with her fingertips. The birch needles had slid out sideways onto the pillow. In the night the ball of wool had rolled off the bed and gone under the table, unwinding as it went, so that a long grey strand ran across the floorboards to it like a path. Downstairs the ovens were only just ticking as they warmed, and the first loaves hadn't gone in yet. The round window was the colour of the wool.

She followed the strand across the floor on her knees, winding as she went, and took the glove to the sill. The cuff was done, and above it the hand had begun to widen toward the thumb. She counted her stitches under her breath and went on, row after row, with her back against the frame. Below her the canal was grey and still, with a skin of mist lying on it. A heron stood in the shallows by the far steps, as grey as everything else. The sun came up over the roofs behind her. The mist went pink, then gold. Soon the whole canal was gold from bank to bank. The heron was gold too, and lifted off it slowly with its legs trailing.

She put the glove away in her satchel, wrapped in a clean handkerchief so it wouldn't snag, and filled the chipped jug at the basin. She went along the crate under the window. Mrs Pennywhistle had a new frond uncurling, and Elsie told her how clever she was. Sunny got a long drink, and Captain a longer one, because the mint drank like a horse in the warm. Doris got hardly a splash, because aloes drowned if you loved them too much. Moonpenny sat on the sill in her clay pot with her one bud wrapped tight round itself. Elsie gave her a careful little pour at the roots. "I'm going out to the downs. Somebody's singing out there at night, on a grave, and I'm going to find out who. I'll tell you all about it when I'm back."

The guild hall was only half awake at that hour, the lamps still lit along the walls though the windows were already bright, a clerk yawning at the desk, two old adventurers asleep upright on a bench by the door. Elsie came in out of the square with her satchel bumping and saw them at once, under the quest board at the E-rank end. Tamsin was leaning on the wall with Margery propped beside her and her arms folded. Tilly was sitting on the floor with her back to the board and her knees up. Cora stood a little apart from them both, straight as a post. All three of them had been waiting. All three of them looked round at the door at the same moment.

Tilly scrambled up, and her belt clinked. It clinked loudly, every corked vial along it knocking against its neighbour in a full glassy jostle, and kept on clinking for a moment after she'd stopped moving, the way a dresser of jars does when a door slams. Everyone looked at it. Tilly put both hands flat over the vials to hold them still. "They're fine. They're all fine. Lamp oil, mostly, and salve, and the blue ones, and a few—they're fine. In case of dark. Barrows are dark, Tamsin said." She took a breath. "They don't explode." She looked down at the row of corks under her fingers. "Probably."

Cora had her ledger under her arm, as she did everywhere, its black cover worn pale at the corners. But there was something new at her belt this morning. A lantern hung there on a short brass chain: a squat, heavy thing of brass and thick glass with a hinged hood over it and a slide across the front, shut, so that only a thin line of light leaked out at the bottom. Stamped into the hood was the guild's mark. Elsie looked at it with interest. It was the sort of lantern that meant to be carried somewhere it would be needed. Cora saw her looking and put her hand over it, briefly, as if to check it was there.

Elsie had been saving it up all the way across Brightwater Square. "Good morning, everyone!" She turned to Cora with her face shining. "Cora, your gloves have got a whole cuff now. A proper ribbed one. And I've started the hand. I did six more rows this morning, at the window, before the bread."

Cora looked at her. Her grey eyes went from Elsie's face to Elsie's satchel and back. A small line appeared between her straight dark brows. "What gloves?"

"I can't tell you. It's a surprise." Cora's line deepened. She opened her mouth. Behind Elsie, Tamsin made a sound. It was a snort, a big one, and it went into the turned-up collar of her jerkin, where she had ducked her face to catch it. Her shoulders were shaking. Tilly looked at the ceiling. Cora closed her mouth again, and turned away to the door.

The west gate was a squat old arch at the end of a long street of carters' yards. The morning traffic was already squeezing through it: milk carts coming in, a goat on a string, a woman with a basket of eggs on her head. Under the arch a stall was selling hand pies off a tray, hot, the pastry crimped round the edge and shining with egg. The smell reached Elsie halfway down the street. Tamsin was ahead of her before she'd finished breathing it in. "Four." Tamsin dug in her purse. Two pips each. Elsie watched eight coppers go one by one into the woman's floury palm. Tamsin stowed the warm paper parcels in her pack. "For later." Elsie had her own purse half out. "Put that away. I'm feeding the party. It's what the big one's for."

Outside the gate the road went between hedges for a while, past orchards and a mill. After that it began to climb. It climbed for a long time, steadily, until the hedges gave out. The canals and roofs of Lanternmere lay behind them and below, with the Mere beyond, flat and shining. At last they were on top. The downs opened out in front of them. There was so much sky. The land went away in long green swells, smooth as a sleeping animal's back, bare of trees, all the way to the edge of the world. The wind came over it with nothing to stop it and pushed at her skirt. Up in the blue, too high to see, the larks were singing, dozens of them, on and on without stopping for breath.

Tilly had been quiet up the hill because she needed the breath. Now she had it back. "Barrow-lights. That's what they'll be, the lights. I've read about them. Everyone says ghosts, but I think it's gas. Marsh gas. Old graves, you see, things rotting down for hundreds of years, and the gas comes up through the grass and catches somehow, and glows. That's the sensible answer." She skipped a step to keep up. Her eyes went big and bright. "Or it's something else entirely. Something that isn't in any book yet. Something better than gas. Oh, I do hope it's something better than gas."

Elsie laughed and looked across at Cora, who was walking on Tilly's other side with her hands behind her back and her coat buttoned to the throat in spite of the sun. "What do you think it is, Cora? The lights. Gas or ghosts?"

Cora considered the road ahead for a few steps. "I think that I shall write down what we find." She glanced at Elsie. "When we have found it. Not before."

Tilly sagged, a little. Elsie beamed at her. It seemed to her a very Cora answer, and she liked it. She liked that Cora would wait to see.

Tamsin had been walking on Elsie's left, a little behind, with Margery over her shoulder. Now she came up level. She walked close, the way she had along the Wend yesterday, close enough that her arm brushed Elsie's with every other step. On one of the steps their hands bumped together. They swung apart. On the next step they bumped again, knuckles to knuckles. This time Tamsin's hand turned over and caught Elsie's and held on. It held on firmly. Tamsin was looking out at the downs, at a hawk hanging over the grass, very interested in the hawk.

Elsie squeezed back. The wind was blowing and the larks were singing. It was a beautiful day to be walking somewhere, so she swung their joined hands. She swung them forward and back, high, like a child on the way to a fair. Tamsin groaned. "What are you doing? Stop that. I'm twenty-one. I've got an axe." Elsie swung them higher. Tamsin groaned again, louder, and rolled her eyes up at the sky. But her hand stayed exactly where it was. When Elsie let the swing die down, Tamsin's arm kept it going a moment longer, all on its own, before it remembered to go stiff.

They came to Bellmoor in the middle of the morning. The road dipped into a long shallow fold of the downs and there it was, lying along the bottom of the fold like something asleep: a long green mound, longer than the guild hall, smooth and round-backed. Round it, in a wide ring, stood the stones. They were tall grey slabs, taller than Tamsin, leaning a little this way and that, the way old teeth lean, with yellow lichen on their weather sides. The wind made a low sound going between them. Elsie stopped on the road and looked at it. She felt the hair on her arms lift. She couldn't have said why.

Harebells grew all over it. The mound was blue with them, a soft, nodding, shivering blue, thousands upon thousands of little bells on stems so fine the wind kept them all moving at once, so that the whole barrow seemed to breathe. There were more than Elsie could have counted. Away down the fold a flock of sheep was grazing, cropping the turf close and moving slowly along. They had spread out over the whole hillside, up and down and sideways, everywhere but here. A wide band of grass lay between the flock and the ring of stones, long and uncut. It was as if somebody had drawn a line round the barrow and told the sheep.

A figure was coming up the slope from among the sheep. It was an old woman, very small, bent and brown, in a long smock and a felt hat tied down under her chin. She came slowly on a tall shepherd's crook that she planted ahead of her at every step. She took a long time about it. When she reached them she stopped, leaned on the crook with both hands and looked them over one at a time, the axe, the goggles, the guild coat, the pink hair. "You'll be from the guild. About my notice." She nodded at the barrow. "I put it up. Me. The rest of them wouldn't."

"The sheep won't go near it. You'll have seen. Best grazing on the downs, that mound, and they'll go round it a mile sooner than set a hoof inside those stones. Never would, not in my lifetime, nor my mother's." She turned and spat neatly into the grass. "That I can live with. Sheep have their notions. But this spring there's been lights." She pointed with the crook. "At night. On top, all over. I've watched them from the fold. And with the lights—" She lowered her voice, though there was nobody to hear. "Singing."

"It's no song I know. And I know a few. No words to it, or none I could make out. Just the tune." She was quiet a moment, leaning on the crook. Her face had gone soft and strange. "It's the loveliest thing. I'll not pretend it's not. I've stood at my door of a night and listened to it till I'm cold through." Her mouth set hard again. "And it frightens me half to death. A voice like that, on a grave, in the dark. That's not natural. That's not anyone that ought to be singing."

"Have you been up to look?" Elsie asked. "To see who it is?"

The old woman stared at her as though she'd suggested a stroll to the moon. "Me? Up there? In the dark?" She gave a short, dry laugh with no fun in it. "Not for all the wool in Bellmoor, girl. Not for all the wool in the downs." She hitched her crook. "That's what I'm paying the guild for. Ten lanterns, for somebody young and daft to go up there and look." She nodded at them, one by one, as though they'd do. "And you all look young. And daft enough."

She went back down the slope to her flock the way she had come, slowly, planting the crook. The sheep lifted their heads as she came and went back to grazing. The four of them turned to the barrow. They walked the ring of stones first, all the way round, slowly, the long grass soaking their boots. Cora went from stone to stone and laid her bare hand on each, briefly, as if taking its temperature. Tamsin walked with Margery off her shoulder and in her hands. Tilly stopped at every third stone to peer at the lichen. Elsie walked between them all and looked at everything, the stones, the mound, the bells, the sky.

They climbed the mound at its eastern end, where it sloped up gently, wading through harebells to their knees. On the top the wind was stronger. Tilly, going ahead with her braids blowing, stopped all at once. "Oh." They came up beside her. In front of her, in the middle of the long round back of the barrow, was a hollow in the grass. It was a shallow, round dip, about the size of a person sitting down. The grass in it was pressed flat and pale, not broken, smoothed, the way grass goes when someone sits in the same place over and over. The harebells stood all round its edge, untouched. "Someone sits here," Tilly whispered. "Lots."

Cora opened her ledger on her forearm. The wind tried to take the pages. She held them down flat with the side of her hand, and wrote. Elsie, standing at her shoulder, watched the neat letters go down the page: a hollow on the barrow top, pressed. Sat in, often. One person. Cora looked at the hollow a while longer and added something. Elsie leaned to see. Harebells undisturbed, Cora had written. Comes and goes careful.

At the west end of the barrow the ground fell away more steeply. There, set into the end of the mound, was a doorway. It was low and narrow, two great upright stones with a third laid across their tops, the grass of the mound growing down over the lintel like a fringe. Behind it was dark. Elsie went toward it and stopped a few steps off, because the air changed. It came out of the doorway in a slow, steady breath, cold, colder than any cellar, colder than the cistern under the Copper Baths. It smelled of stone and earth that had not seen the sun in a very long time. It lifted the hair off her forehead. She stepped back.

Tamsin looked at the doorway. She swung Margery round off her shoulder, took a good grip and rolled her neck from side to side. "Well. If something's singing out here, it's in there. Stands to reason. Where else is it going to live?" She grinned at them over her shoulder, teeth and freckles. Before anyone could say anything she bent nearly double under the lintel and went in, axe first, into the dark. Elsie saw the copper of her curls for a moment. After that there was nothing at all.

Cora spoke to the empty doorway. "The notice asks what the lights and the singing are. It doesn't say one word about the inside." Her voice was quite level. She unhooked the lantern from her belt and held it in her left hand, still shuttered, with only that thin line of gold leaking out under the slide, and her other hand on the slide's catch. She ducked under the lintel after Tamsin, straight-backed as far as the roof would let her. The line of gold went with her into the dark and got smaller.

Elsie looked at Tilly. Tilly was standing very close to the doorway and not going any closer. Her face had gone pale under its soot. Her fingers were knotted together in front of her apron. Elsie remembered the page in her notebook, under Tilly's name: underground. She held out her hand. Tilly took it at once, gripping hard. They went in together. The passage was narrow, so narrow that Elsie's shoulders brushed stone on both sides, and low, and very cold. It smelled of old stone, of wet and dust at once. Under that it smelled of something old and dry, like a closed chest. Ahead of them the thin gold line of Cora's lantern swung. Their own breathing came back to them off the walls.

The passage turned and widened a little. On the left-hand wall there were niches cut into the stone, low dark shelves one after another, each the length of a person. Cora's thin line of light slid along them. Most were empty, or held only dust. In the third one something lay. Elsie saw it as a heap of grey, grey like ash, like old cobweb, and thought it was rags. Then it moved. It sat up. It sat up slowly, the way a very old person sits up in bed, a little at a time, with a dry sound like paper being folded. And the cold came off it. The passage had been cold before. Now it was cold the way deep water is cold, so that Elsie's breath stopped in her chest and her fingers ached in Tilly's.

It had a shape like a person and wasn't one. It was long and thin and grey all over. Where its face should have been there was a greyness that was darker in two places. It turned those two places toward Tamsin, who was nearest. Tamsin had Margery up. But the passage was too narrow to swing her, and Tamsin knew it. Elsie saw her shoulders hunt for room and find stone on both sides. A long grey arm came out of the niche. It came out quite slowly, almost gently, the way someone reaches for a cup. Its hand, if it was a hand, closed round Tamsin's bare forearm, just below the elbow.

Tamsin swore. It was a bargee's word, a short ugly one. It came out of her loud and high with shock and rang off the stone. She wrenched back. The grey hand let go, easily, as if it hadn't much minded either way. Tamsin stumbled back into Cora. In the thin line of light Elsie saw her arm. From the wrist to the elbow it had gone white, a dead, waxy, bloodless white, the freckles standing out on it like spilled pepper. Her fingers hung open. Margery's haft slid down through them. Tamsin caught the axe with her other hand and stared at her arm as though it belonged to someone else.

Elsie felt it down the Tether. It came along the thread from under her breastbone, quick, and went into her own left arm. Her arm went cold. It was cold from the inside out, the way a hand goes in snow. Soon it went past that, past cold, into nothing. She waited for the pain. She knew how hurt came down the thread. She had felt the wolf's teeth and the vine's squeeze in her own body. She braced for it. It didn't come. There was only the cold and then the dead blank after the cold, where Tamsin's arm should have been and wasn't. That frightened her more than any amount of pain could have, because pain at least meant the arm was there.

There was a click. Cora had thrown back the lantern's slide. The light came out of it all at once, gold and hard and much brighter than Elsie would have thought such a small lantern could hold. It filled the passage from wall to wall, every crack and every grain of the stone. The grey thing shrank. It drew back into its niche like a snail into a shell, folding itself up, its long arms crossing over the dark places where its eyes should be. It hissed. The sound was dry and thin, like sand poured on a hot stove. Cora took a step toward it, the lantern held out level in front of her at arm's length. It hissed again and pressed itself back against the stone.

"Out." Cora didn't raise her voice. "Behind me, all of you. Slowly. Don't run." They went. They went backward, step by step, down the narrow passage the way they had come. Tilly went first because she was last, then Elsie, then Tamsin with her dead arm held against her chest. Cora came last of all, walking backward with the lantern up high over her head so that its light poured down the passage ahead of her and into the niche. Her arm held steady the whole way. Elsie looked back past Tamsin and saw her face in the lantern light, white and set. At last there was sun on Elsie's back. There was grass under her heels, and the harebells, and the larks.

Elsie sat Tamsin down on the turf just outside the doorway. She knelt in front of her and took the arm in both her hands. It was cold as a stone in a stream. She began to rub it, briskly, between her palms, from the wrist up to the elbow and back, the way you rub the feet of a carter who has slept out in the frost. She rubbed hard. Tamsin watched her do it. For a long time the arm only lay in Elsie's hands like a thing she was holding. At last Tamsin hissed through her teeth. "Ow. Ow. There it is." The colour was coming back in blotches, red and white. Elsie could feel it too down the thread, a fierce prickling like a thousand pins, and she nearly laughed with relief.

"I didn't go red." Tamsin was staring past Elsie at the dark doorway. "It grabbed me. Something grabbed me in the dark, and I didn't go red." Elsie kept rubbing. Tamsin flexed her fingers, slowly, one at a time, and winced. She looked down at Elsie with her eyes gone wide all over again. "Elsie. I didn't go red."

"Of course you didn't." Tamsin's arm was warmer now, pink and blotched and alive, but the back of her hand was still cold, white across the knuckles where the knuckle wrap didn't reach. Elsie lifted the hand in both of hers, bent her head and kissed it there, on the back, on the cold place, the way her mother kissed a hurt to tell it someone had seen to it. Tamsin's skin was cool against her lips and smelled of leather. She looked up and smiled. "There."

Tamsin went red after all. It wasn't the other red. It came up over her face all at once, under every freckle, over her nose and cheekbones and across her forehead into her hair. Soon there was hardly a line to say where her face stopped and her curls began. She sat there in the grass with her hand still in Elsie's two hands and her mouth a little open. For once she was completely silent. Tilly, behind Elsie, made a small squeaking noise, the way a door does.

Cora was standing a little way off by the doorway with the lantern shuttered again and her ledger open on her forearm. She was writing. Elsie got up and went to read over her shoulder, as she did at every page. Cora let her, as Cora did. Barrow-wight, Cora had written. In the passage, third niche on the left. Slow. Numbs what it holds. Hates lamplight. Cora's pen went on. It wrote something else, one more line, short. As it wrote, Cora's shoulder came round, a little, and her elbow came up. The line went where Elsie couldn't see it.

Tilly had come round to stand by Tamsin and was looking down at her arm with great interest, the goggles pulled down off her head to hang round her neck. "Wights don't sing. I'm nearly sure. I've read about wights. A bit. They grumble." She thought about it. "They lie in the dark and grumble, and they grab, and they hate the light. That's all wights do. I've never read anything about one singing. They haven't got the—" She waved a hand at her own throat. "The bits."

Elsie looked up at the top of the barrow, at the pressed hollow and the harebells nodding round it in the wind. "Then the singer's someone else. Someone who sits up there, in the same place, and comes back. Somebody careful, Cora said. Not a thing in a hole." She sat down in the grass beside Tamsin, quite decided. "So we'll wait. Till it's dark. We'll wait and see who comes."

So they waited. They ate the hand pies at noon on the sunny side of the mound, sitting in a row with their backs against one of the standing stones, which was warm from the morning and rough through Elsie's top. The pies were still faintly warm too in their paper, mutton and onion with black pepper, the pastry gone soft at the bottom with gravy. Tamsin ate hers with her left hand, flexing her right on her knee between bites. Tilly ate hers in small careful bites round the edge, saving the middle. Cora ate hers neatly, holding the paper under her chin, looking at the doorway.

Afterward Tilly lay down. "I'm only going to rest my eyes." She lay back in the long grass. After a little shuffling, she wriggled over and put her head in Elsie's lap. She was asleep almost at once. Her mouth fell open a little. The soot on her cheek was smudged from the pie. Elsie looked down at her and smiled. One of Tilly's braids lay across Elsie's knee. Elsie picked it up and ran her fingers down it, slowly, to the end. It was singed there, the hair frizzled, brown and crisp. She stroked the singed ends with her thumb, over and over, smoothing them, the way she would have stroked a cat. She looked out over the downs.

After a while Tilly woke. Elsie felt her do it. The head in her lap went heavier and then lighter, all at once, and the breathing changed. Under Elsie's hand Tilly went stiff as a board, every bit of her, down to the toes of her boots. Her eyes opened. Elsie, looking down, saw them, very blue, staring up past her at the sky. They shut again, tight. Tilly's breathing came back, deep and slow and very even, a little too even, like someone counting it. Elsie went on stroking her braid. Tilly was tired, she supposed. It had been a long walk, and a fright in the dark, and she'd brewed half the night. She let her sleep as long as she liked. It was a good while.

Tamsin had gone up to the top of the mound to watch the road. Cora sat on the other side of Elsie with her back very straight against the stone and her ledger closed on her knees. Elsie, with Tilly's head still in her lap, reached out to the harebells growing beside her and picked one: a single stem with three little bells on it, palest blue. She leaned over and tucked the stem into the top buttonhole of Cora's coat, under her chin, so that the bells hung down over the charcoal wool. Cora went still. She didn't move to stop her. She sat quite still, with her chin up and her eyes on the horizon, while Elsie fixed the stem so it wouldn't fall, and she let her.

Up on the top of the mound, Tamsin was swinging Margery. There was nothing there to swing at. She was swinging at the air, at the wind, great slow two-handed sweeps that hummed. At every swing she stopped and shook out her right arm. She flexed the hand and swung again. Elsie watched her. Down the thread she could feel the prickling getting less and less, pins and then only warmth. At last Tamsin stopped with the axe head resting on the turf and looked down at her right hand, opening and shutting it. She looked down the slope at Elsie, and grinned, all teeth.

Elsie turned to Cora. "Whose grave is it? The barrow. Somebody built all this over somebody."

Cora looked at the long green mound beside them. "A queen's, they say. A long time ago. Before the city." She paused. "Which queen, the books don't say. Her name was lost a long while back. And it isn't in the notice."

Elsie looked at the barrow too, at the harebells and the grey stones and the dark little door at the end. "That's sad. All this. All these stones, to keep her. And they've forgotten her name."

She sat with her hand resting on Tilly's hair and looked at the grass growing over the queen. After a while she began to hum. It was the old caravan song, the one her mother had sung at fires, slow and rocking. She hummed it low, half under her breath, toward the mound, so that it wouldn't lie there so lonely in the sun, without a name. She didn't know she was doing it. Beside her, Cora had turned her head and was looking at her. In her lap, Tilly's too-even breathing hitched.

The afternoon went slowly by. Cloud shadows went over the downs, and the sheep went down the fold and back. At last the sun went down. It went down red, enormous and red, behind the far swells of the downs to the west. The long grass went red. The stones threw shadows a hundred yards long across the turf. The harebells on the barrow went from blue to purple to grey. The larks stopped. Elsie hadn't noticed them all day, they'd been so constant. Now they stopped, all at once. The silence came down over the downs with the dusk, so big she could hear the wind in the grass.

"If she comes," Elsie whispered, though there was nobody yet to hear, "the singer, whoever she is—" She looked round at the three of them in the red light. "We keep still. All of us. We let her sing. She sits up there by herself and sings where she thinks she can't be heard. If she sees us, she'll stop."

Tamsin looked at Margery, and back at Elsie, and lowered the axe into the grass. "Still. Right." Tilly nodded hard. Cora inclined her head.

They lay down in the long grass outside the ring of stones, on the eastern side, where they could see the top of the mound against the last of the light. The grass was taller than Elsie's head when she lay down in it. It smelled of earth and thyme, and the stalks rustled when she breathed. They lay in a row and kept still. Tamsin lay with Margery along her side like a sleeping dog. Tilly had her goggles pushed up and her hands folded on her vials so they wouldn't clink. Cora lay on her front with the lantern under her hand and her chin on her ledger.

The air cooled fast once the sun was gone. On the open downs there was nothing to hold the warmth in. The wind had a chill in it now that crept down through the grass. Tamsin shifted. She moved over until she lay close against Elsie's side, all down her length, shoulder to hip, her bare arm against Elsie's bare arm. She didn't look at Elsie. "Warmth. Sharing. Practical." She was hot as an oven, as she'd promised. Elsie sighed happily and tucked herself in against her, her cheek on Tamsin's shoulder and her cold knees against Tamsin's leg. Under her cheek, Tamsin's heart was going hard and fast. Elsie thought that Tamsin simply ran hot all through, even her heart.

Cora reached out in the dark and closed the lantern's slide all the way. The thin gold line under it went out. The last green had drained out of the west while they lay there. For a moment the dark was complete. Elsie's eyes opened up to it, and the sky came out. It came out all at once, it seemed, the whole sky over the downs, from one edge of the world to the other. There were more stars than Elsie had seen since the caravan roads, thick and white and close, as though someone had spilled them. The stones stood up black against them. The barrow lay black and long. The grass whispered. They lay in it, the four of them, and waited.

It was a quarter of an hour, or a little more, before anything happened. Elsie had begun to think about the stars, and about her mother under them somewhere on the southern roads, when Tamsin's shoulder tensed under her cheek. Elsie lifted her head. On the path from the east, the one that came along the bottom of the fold, someone was walking. There was no lantern. Just a figure, darker than the dark, coming along the path quietly and steadily, as if it knew every stone of the way. It turned off the path below the barrow. It went in between two of the standing stones and up the long slope of the mound, through the harebells, toward the top.

She was slim. That much Elsie could see against the stars as she reached the top of the barrow and stood there, a girl, a young woman, not tall, in a long dark skirt that moved in the wind. Her hair was long and heavy and hung down her back. It moved in a way that wasn't loose hair: braids, Elsie thought, many braids, swinging together. The girl stood a moment at the top. She turned her head. For a moment the starlight caught her face, lifted and young and anxious. She stepped down into the hollow in the grass, carefully. She sat down in it among the harebells and tucked her skirt round her knees.

She didn't sing straight away. She looked round first. She looked all round her, slowly, out over the dark downs to the east, the south, the west. Elsie, lying in the grass, held her breath and pressed her cheek flat to Tamsin's shoulder. The girl looked toward them. Her gaze went over the long grass where they lay and on, past, without stopping. She sat with her head a little tilted, listening, for so long that Elsie's held breath began to hurt. She listened to the wind and the grass and the far-off bleat of a sheep. At last her shoulders came down. She lifted her face to the stars. And she sang.

Elsie held very still. The voice came down off the barrow low and dark and warm, without words. It began to climb. It climbed slowly, the way the larks had climbed that morning, higher and higher, clear as water. It didn't strain or thin as it went. It opened, and filled the whole fold of the downs from side to side. Elsie had heard singing at every fair on the southern roads, singers in inn yards, her mother at the fire. This was something else. It went into her chest and through it, through her ribs and down her arms to her fingertips, the way cold water goes through you on a hot day. She lay in the grass with her cheek on Tamsin's shoulder and let it.

All over the barrow, lights began to rise out of the grass. They came up from among the harebells, small and pale, a soft greenish white, like the glow on rotten wood, like the inside of a shell. There was one, then three, then dozens, everywhere, all over the long back of the mound. They rose slowly out of the ground the way bubbles rise in honey. They drifted up to the height of a person's knee, to a person's shoulder, and hung there, swaying. At last, all together, they began to drift toward the girl in the hollow, toward the song, slowly, from every side.

They gathered round her. They circled her, slowly, round and round, the way moths go round a lamp on a summer night, never touching. The nearer ones went round faster and the farther ones drifted, so that she sat at the middle of a slow pale wheel of light. Elsie could see her better now by their glow. She had stopped looking at the sky. She was looking at the lights, turning her head to follow them as they went round, and singing to them, softer now, the way someone sings to a child they're putting to sleep. She held out her hand, palm up, and a light came and turned above it.

Tilly's fingers closed on Elsie's arm, hard enough to hurt. Elsie turned her head. Tilly's face was a hand's breadth away in the grass, lit faintly green. Her eyes were huge and shining, and her mouth was wide open. She didn't make a sound. She only shaped the words, very slowly and very clearly, with her lips, so that Elsie couldn't possibly miss them: barrow-lights. Her mouth pressed shut and wobbled at the corners as if she might burst. Her fingers on Elsie's arm squeezed and squeezed.

One of the lights came loose from the wheel. It drifted away from the others, down off the barrow, slowly, over the stones and out over the long grass, dipping and rising as if it were looking for something. It came to where they lay. It hung over Elsie, a little above her face, soft and pale, no bigger than a plum. She could see the grass stalks lit faint green round her and Tilly's open mouth beside her. It was so pretty. Before she had quite decided to, she lifted one finger and touched it. It was neither warm nor cold. It went out, soundlessly, the way a soap bubble goes at the touch of a finger. There was nothing there at all.

Elsie's eyes filled. She hadn't expected it to happen. It was the light going out like that, so easily, after it had come all that way to find her. It was the song still going on over her, warm and low. It was the queen under the hill with no name, and the girl up there singing to her lights all alone in the dark where she thought she was alone. It all came up into Elsie's eyes at once, and spilled over, and ran down sideways into her hair. She sniffed. She tried to do it quietly. It wasn't quiet. In the great hush of the downs it was a wet, small, unmistakable sniff.

The song stopped. It stopped dead, in the middle of a note, high up, as if a door had been shut on it. The silence afterward was enormous. Elsie could hear her own heart. All over the barrow the pale lights hung where they were, swaying a little, with nothing to follow. Up in the hollow the girl had gone perfectly rigid, her hand still held out with a light over it, her face turned toward the long grass where Elsie lay.

The girl got up. She got up fast, scrambling, her skirt catching round her knees, and stood in the hollow among the harebells with her hands pressed flat against her chest. "Who's there?" Her singing voice had been so big. This one was small and thin and high. "Who is that? Is someone there?"

Elsie couldn't lie. She couldn't have lain there in the grass and kept quiet, either, and let the girl stand up there alone wondering. So she sat up. She sat up out of the long grass, slowly, into the starlight, with her hair full of grass seed and her face wet, and put up one hand. "It's only me. I'm so sorry. I didn't mean to make a noise. I didn't mean to stop you." Her voice wobbled. She swallowed. "It was beautiful."

The girl made a sound. It was a small, sharp, hurt sound, as if Elsie had struck her. She put both her hands up over her face. She stood like that in the hollow, among the harebells and the swaying lights, with her face hidden and her shoulders up round her ears. She didn't move. Elsie had seen people stand like that before. A girl at a horse fair whose father had shouted at her in front of the whole crowd had stood just so, as if she could make herself small enough not to be there.

Elsie got up out of the grass and went to her. She went slowly, the way you go up to a frightened horse, between two of the standing stones and up the long slope of the barrow through the harebells, not hurrying, keeping her hands where the girl could see them if she looked. The lights drifted out of her way. Behind her she heard the grass rustle and part. She knew without turning that the others had got up too. There was Tamsin, tall and dark, with the axe left lying in the grass. There was Tilly, and there was Cora with the lantern shut at her side. They stayed where they were, at the edge of the stones.

When Elsie reached the top the girl took her hands down. Close to, by the faint glow of the lights still turning round them, Elsie could see her. She was a young woman of about her own age, with warm honey-brown skin and a soft, pretty, frightened face. She had long braids, dozens of them, falling over her shoulders and down her back. Her eyes were brown and very wide and wet at the edges. "My name's Amara." It came out all in a rush. "Please. Please don't tell anyone. Please, I'm asking you. Don't tell anyone you heard."

"I can't sing if anyone's listening. I can't. Not a note. If I know there's someone there, even one person, even someone kind—" She pressed her hand to her throat. "It shuts. Like a fist. I open my mouth and there's nothing in it. It's been like that since I was small." She laughed, but it wasn't a laugh. "So I come out here. Three hours' walk. Because there isn't anyone out here. There never was." She looked past Elsie, down at the three dark shapes at the edge of the stones. "There never was."

Elsie didn't need to think. "Then we'll keep it. All of us. We won't tell a soul. It's yours." She turned round where she stood on top of the barrow and looked down at the others: at Tamsin, at Tilly, at Cora, one after another, in the starlight. Tamsin nodded straight away, once, hard. Tilly nodded so fast her braids flew, both hands clasped under her chin.

Cora didn't nod. She came up the slope instead, slowly, through the harebells, with the lantern in one hand and the ledger in the other, and stopped a little below them. "Fairweather. The notice asks what the lights are, and what the singing is. The guild took ten lanterns of a shepherd's money to find out. I am a Warden of the guild." She lifted the ledger a little. "I have to write it down."

Elsie looked at her. She didn't say anything. She only looked at Cora, there on the slope below her in the starlight, with her wet face and her hand out a little toward Amara, and waited. Cora looked back. At last she looked away, at the lights, at the stones, at Amara's hands twisted together in her skirt. Her mouth tightened. "I shall write: lights on the barrow, harmless. Drawn by music. And a singer." She paused. "A singer, meaning no harm to anyone."

Cora turned to Amara. "I won't write your name. There's a line on the form for one. I'll leave it empty." Amara stared at her. All the breath went out of her in a long, shaky rush, so that her shoulders dropped and her whole body seemed to come down an inch. She put one hand on Elsie's arm to steady herself and seemed hardly to know she'd done it.

Amara was still holding Elsie's arm. "My mother would never forgive me. If she knew. Singing out here. To an empty hill. To sheep." She looked round at the downs. The moon had come up while she sang, low and yellow in the east. The harebells had gone silver under it. "She'd say it was a waste. She'd say—" Her voice changed, grand and cool and ringing, very good. "What is the point of a voice that's never heard?" Afterward she looked as though she wished she hadn't.

"Who's your mother?"

Amara looked at her strangely, as if surprised she had to ask. "Celestine Okafor."

Behind and below them, Tilly gasped out loud. Tamsin said a word under her breath. Cora's eyebrows went up and stayed there. Elsie didn't know why. She had been in Lanternmere a fortnight. "Celestine." Elsie tried it out. "Celestine Okafor. Oh, that's a lovely name. It sounds like stars." Amara stared at her. Something happened at the corner of her mouth, the first thing all night that wasn't fright.

"I have to go." Amara looked up at the moon, measuring it. "I have to be home before it sets, or they'll know I've been out. It's a long walk." She let go of Elsie's arm and stood back, smoothing her skirt. Elsie opened her mouth, but Amara was quicker. "I always go back alone. Please. That's—I like that part too. I'd rather." Elsie closed her mouth again, and nodded.

But she had her notebook out. She had got it out of her satchel without thinking, the way her hand went to it whenever she met someone she meant to keep. She opened it to a new page and found her pencil. "Before you go. What are your favourite things? For my book. I keep everyone's." Amara blinked at her. She blinked at the little blue notebook, and at Elsie, and at the notebook again, slowly, twice, the way someone blinks coming out of a dark room into the sun. "Mine?"

She thought about it. She stood on top of the barrow in the moonlight with her braids moving in the wind and really thought, with her head on one side, as if it were a hard question and a good one. At last she had it. "Honey cakes. The sticky kind, that you have to lick your fingers after." She looked out over the dark, silver downs. "This. The downs at night, when there's no one on them." She hesitated. "And being nobody in particular. Just walking down a street. Being nobody anyone's looking at." She looked at Elsie a little shyly. "Is that a thing? Can you write that down?"

"Of course you can." Elsie wrote it, bending over the page to catch the moonlight on it. Amara. Honey cakes (sticky). The downs at night. Being nobody in particular. Her pencil made a small scratching sound in the quiet. She looked up, delighted. "I live over a bakery. The Honeycomb, on Thimble Canal, at the top, in the attic. And they have honey cakes. The best ones. Agnes makes them. You could come. Any time, if you liked. If you were walking down a street being nobody in particular and it happened to be that one."

"Maybe." Amara turned and went down the barrow, through the harebells, between the stones, and out onto the path to the east. She walked quickly with her braids swinging, back the way she had come. Elsie stood on the top of the mound and watched her go. The path was pale under the moon. The dark figure went along it, smaller and smaller, until she reached the bottom of the fold where the path turned. There she stopped. She stood a moment. At last she turned round and looked back up at the barrow.

The figure went round the turn. "Oh!" Elsie came down the slope to the others with her notebook held against her chest. "Oh, wasn't she lovely? Wasn't that the loveliest voice? I think she might be my favourite singer in the whole world." Tamsin had picked up Margery and was resting the head on her boot. She looked at Elsie, not at the path. "I've heard one." Elsie supposed she meant someone on the barges, years ago. She was about to ask, but Tamsin had already turned away to get the packs.

They made their camp inside the ring of stones, on the south side, a long way from the barrow's west end and the low black doorway. Cora chose the spot. She paced it out from the door and paced it again. She set the packs down where she could see the doorway clearly over them. They unrolled the blankets on the turf. There were only two, Tamsin's and Cora's. Elsie hadn't thought to bring one, and Tilly had brought vials instead.

Tamsin lay down on one of them, and Tilly lay down next to her. Tamsin pulled the blanket over them both without being asked and without comment, the way she'd have pulled it over a sister. Tilly burrowed in. They lay back to back. Tamsin's breathing went long and slow almost at once, the way it did now at night. After a minute it began to catch at the back of her throat with the small rattle Elsie knew. Tilly lasted a little longer. Her goggles were still on her head. Soon she was asleep too, with one hand out from under the blanket, curled on the turf.

Cora didn't lie down. She sat with her back against one of the standing stones, the lantern on the grass beside her with its slide open a crack, so that a thin bar of gold lay across the turf toward the doorway, and her eyes on the doorway. "Someone should watch. It's slow. But it's awake now." She didn't look round.

Elsie got up from where she'd been sitting by the packs and came and sat down beside her, against the same stone. "Then I'll keep you company. So you're not watching by yourself."

The stone was cold at their backs. Elsie leaned sideways and settled. Her head came to rest against Cora's shoulder, on the charcoal wool. Cora went rigid. She stayed that way for a long breath. Then she moved, carefully. Elsie felt the coat being unbuttoned beside her, button by button from the throat down. One side of it came round her, over her shoulders and her bare arms, still warm from Cora inside it, so that the two of them sat inside one coat against the stone. Cora's arm stayed where it had settled, holding the coat closed round her.

Elsie, settling her cheek, found something tickling it. She lifted her head an inch to look. It was the harebell. It was still in Cora's buttonhole, the topmost one, under her chin, where Elsie had put it at noon. It had come through the barrow, the dusk, the singing and all of it. It was only a little limp, its three bells hanging pale in the lantern's crack of gold. "Cora! You've still got my harebell."

Cora looked down at it. "I forgot it was there." Her fingers came up and touched the stem, once, lightly, and went back to the coat.

Elsie put her head down on Cora's shoulder again, carefully, beside the harebell, so as not to crush it. The coat was warm. It smelled of ink and, under the ink, of lavender. Under the lavender, very faintly now, it smelled of harebells. Cora's breathing went in and out under her cheek, slow and steady, a little slower than her own. She found herself breathing in time with it. Across the ring, Tamsin's little rattle came and went. The thin bar of gold lay on the grass. Elsie's eyes closed. Somewhere between one of Cora's slow breaths and the next she slept, her cheek on the charcoal wool, the barrow dark and quiet at their backs.
