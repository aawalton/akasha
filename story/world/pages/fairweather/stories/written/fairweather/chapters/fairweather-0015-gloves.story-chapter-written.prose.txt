Elsie woke to a lark. It was going up somewhere over the downs, very high, and the sky was the colour of milk. Her cheek was on something warm that wasn't Cora's shoulder anymore. It was lower and softer and it moved, very slightly, with someone's breathing. It was Cora's lap. Some time in the night she had slid down off the shoulder and kept going, and now she lay curled on the turf with her head on Cora's knees. Cora's coat was over her. It was tucked in round her, all the way down to her boots, the way you tuck a blanket round someone small. It smelled of ink and lavender. She lay still and breathed it.

She turned her head. Cora was sitting exactly as she had been sitting when Elsie fell asleep, straight-backed against the standing stone, in her shirtsleeves now, with her hands folded on top of the coat where it covered Elsie's shoulder. The lantern was still lit on the grass beside her. Its slide was open a crack, and the bar of gold lay across the dew toward the black doorway, gone pale and thin now in the daylight. Cora's eyes were on the doorway. "It never came out," she said, without looking down. Her voice was rough from not being used. "The wight. I watched. It stayed in its hole."

Elsie looked up at her from her lap. From underneath, Cora's face was all jaw and cheekbone, and there were grey smudges under her eyes that hadn't been there yesterday. Her bun was as smooth as ever. A single hair had come out of it beside her ear, black against her white neck, and it moved in the wind. "Cora," Elsie said. "Did you sleep at all?" Cora's eyes came down to her and went back to the doorway. "A Warden doesn't need much," she said.

"That isn't an answer," Elsie said. She sat up, and the coat slid off her into her arms. It was warm right through from the night she'd spent inside it, warm as bread. She shook it out and put it round Cora's shoulders, the warm side in, and pulled the fronts together under Cora's chin, where the harebell still hung in the top buttonhole, limp and blue. Cora's shoulders were cold under her hands, cold right through the shirt. Cora sat very still and let her. After a moment her own hand came up and took hold of the coat where Elsie had held it, and Elsie let go.

Across the ring of stones there was a noise like a cat being sick. Tamsin had sat bolt upright under the blanket with her curls flattened on one side and something long and yellow coming out of her mouth. She spat. She spat again, harder, and pulled out the rest of it with her fingers. It was the end of one of Tilly's braids. "Ptah," Tamsin said. "Your hair tastes of sulphur." Tilly's head came up out of the blanket with her goggles knocked sideways. She looked at the wet braid. She looked at Tamsin. "You were eating it," she said, outraged. "You were eating my hair."

The sun was just up over the downs, and the dew was smoking off the turf, when the shepherd came up the slope from the fold. She came as slowly as she had come yesterday, on her crook, but this time she was carrying something in her other hand: a stone jug with a cloth tied over the mouth. She set it down in the grass in front of them and pulled off the cloth. It was milk, still warm, with a skin of cream on it. "You'll want that," she said, "after a night out here." Elsie had her purse half out of her belt before she'd thought. The old woman looked at it as if it were something the dog had brought in. "Put that away," she said. "I'll not take a pip for milk."

They passed the jug round. The milk was sweet and thick and warm, and it went down into Elsie like sunshine. When it came back to her she held it in both hands and looked up at the old woman leaning on her crook. "The lights are harmless," she told her. "They're like soap bubbles. You touch one and it just goes out." She thought about how to say the rest, so that it would be true and would keep. "And the singing is a girl," she said. "Only a girl. She doesn't mean any harm to anyone, not you, not the sheep. She just likes to sing."

The shepherd looked at her a long time with her faded little eyes. She didn't ask which girl. Elsie was glad. "But the sheep are right," Elsie went on, because that was true too. "About the door. There's a wight in the passage, in one of the shelves in the wall. It's slow, and it hates lamplight, and it numbs whatever it gets hold of. It got Tamsin's arm." Tamsin held up her arm and flexed the fingers to show it was mended. "So the sheep are quite right to keep off it," Elsie said. "They know."

The old woman grunted. She looked over at the west end of the barrow, at the low dark doorway with the grass hanging over it, and her mouth went thin. "A hurdle, then," she said. "I'll put a hurdle across it, and stake it, and that's an end of it." She nodded once, as though she'd settled the matter with the wight personally. She picked up her jug, shook out the last drops into the grass, and stumped off down the slope toward her flock. The sheep lifted their heads to watch her come.

They packed the blankets and set off east. The road went up out of the fold and over the first long swell of the downs, and as soon as they topped it the sun hit them full in the face. It was low and gold and enormous, and there was no getting away from it. Elsie walked with her hand up over her eyes. Tilly pulled her goggles down and walked with them on, smug. Tamsin squinted and swore at it amiably. Cora walked with her face lifted into it and her eyes narrowed to grey slits, and the light lay along her cheekbones like something poured.

After a mile Elsie got the glove out. She could knit and walk at once; she had done it behind the caravan for years, along every road in the south. She unwrapped it from its handkerchief and found her place. The needles began to click. The grey wool ran off the ball in her satchel, over her wrist, and into the glove, and the glove grew. Today it was growing a thumb. She had picked up the stitches for it at the side of the hand, and it stood out now, short and stubby, a little grey tube that got longer with every round. She held it up against the sun to look at it and was pleased.

Cora was walking beside her. Elsie could feel her watching the needles. She had been watching them for some time. At last Cora cleared her throat. "Fairweather," she said. She said it carefully, the way she lifted the slide on her lantern, a little at a time. "Those gloves." She paused. "Who are they for?"

"They're a surprise," Elsie said happily, and went on knitting. Cora didn't answer. Elsie glanced up. Cora was looking straight ahead along the road, at the sun, at the long gold downs. She went on looking at them. She looked at them for so long, and so steadily, without blinking, that Elsie began to worry about her eyes in all that light. Her mouth was pressed together. Her hand had come up to the top buttonhole of her coat and rested there, on the harebell, as if she'd forgotten it.

Behind them Tilly said, "I can't stop hearing it." She had pushed her goggles up again. Her voice had gone small and wondering. "The song. Hers. It's been going round and round in my head since I woke up. I keep nearly having it and then it goes up somewhere I can't follow." She hummed a bit of it, off key, and stopped, cross with herself. Tamsin walked a few paces without answering. "Me neither," she said at last, gruffly. "Can't shift it. Had it in my sleep." She shifted Margery on her shoulder. "Wasn't a bad sleep," she said.

It was the middle of the morning when they came down off the downs and in through the west gate. The hand-pie woman had gone. The goat on a string had gone, and the milk carts. Only the gate clerk sat in his box under the arch with his ledger open and his pen behind his ear. He looked up at the four of them, at the axe and the goggles and the guild coat and the pink hair, and licked his pen, and wrote them in, one after another, without asking their names. He seemed to know them already. "Back from the barrow," he said, writing. "Well, you've all got your arms and legs."

The guild hall was full and loud. Every lamp was out and the sun came in long yellow bars through the high windows. Cora went straight to the clerk's desk, and the three of them followed her. She had the report written already. She must have done it in the night, Elsie thought, by the bar of lamplight, while Elsie slept in her lap. It was two sheets in Cora's small exact hand, folded once. Cora laid it on the desk and turned it round so the clerk could read it. Elsie, at her elbow, saw the line on the form marked Name, and the space after it, empty and clean.

The clerk read it through. She read it twice, the second time with her eyebrows up. Then she took her stamp, inked it, and brought it down on the shepherd's notice with a thump: DONE. She unlocked the guild's iron box under the desk and counted out the reward onto the wood in two neat rows: nine silver lanterns, and beside them ten copper pips, because, she said, ten lanterns split three ways had started fights at this desk before. Elsie took her share: three silver lanterns and three pips. Tamsin took the same. Tilly got three lanterns and four pips, the odd pip pushed over to her by the clerk's finger.

Tamsin counted hers. She counted it in her palm with one finger, the three lanterns and then the pips, and then she counted it again from the start, slower, as if it might have changed. Then she grinned, all teeth, and closed her fist on it. "Ten lanterns," she said, "for a sit in the grass and a drink of milk." Tilly made a noise like a kettle. She flung both arms round Elsie's middle and hugged her, and Tilly was small but she hugged like a much bigger person; she hugged so hard that Elsie's boots came up off the boards. "We did a barrow," Tilly said into her top. "We did a whole barrow."

"I must write the night up for the guildmaster," Cora said. She had her ledger under her arm again and her coat buttoned to the throat, and the harebell still in the buttonhole, wilting now. "Properly. She will want it before noon." She looked at Elsie, and at the coins in Elsie's hand, and at Elsie again, as if there were something more she meant to say. Then she turned and went to the stairs at the back of the hall and up them, straight-backed, one hand on the rail, until her charcoal coat went round the turn at the top.

Elsie put her coins away in her belt pouch. She looked round the hall, as she did every time she came in, at the board and the benches and who was in. Her eyes went to the bottom corner of the party board, to the Leftovers, where the three of them had chalked their names on the last slate a fortnight ago. Their slate was gone; they didn't need it now. There was a new one in its place. Someone had chalked it in a narrow, upright hand. Noor Haddad, it said. Hexer. E-rank. And under that, underlined twice: Will work alone.

Below the board, in the corner, there was a table. A woman was sitting at it by herself. That wasn't strange in itself. What was strange was the tables round her. The guild hall was so full this morning that men were standing to drink, and parties were squashed six to a bench. But the tables nearest the corner stood empty, three of them, with their benches pushed in. They made a clear ring round the woman, like the long grass round the barrow, as if somebody had drawn a line.

She was slender, and long in the arm and leg, sitting very straight with one hand round a cup. She wore a long coat of dark green, the colour of a pine wood, buttoned close. Her hands were in black gloves, thin black leather that went up under her cuffs. Her hair was black and straight and very long. It fell down her back and over one shoulder as smooth as poured ink, without a single kink in it. Her skin was a warm ivory. Her eyes, from here, were dark and lined with kohl, and they were resting on the middle of the table.

A man came past with a mug in his hand, a big man in a mail shirt, heading for the door. His path went by the corner table. Elsie watched him see the woman. His whole body swerved. He went out round her table in a wide loop, the way you go round a dog you don't trust, and as he swerved his elbow knocked his own hip and his mug slopped, a great brown slosh of ale across the boards. He swore at it. He didn't look at the woman. He went on out the door, shaking his wet hand.

Elsie turned to Tamsin. "Tamsin," she said, low. "Who is that?" Tamsin looked over. She looked for a while, frowning, chewing the inside of her cheek. "Don't know," she said at last. "Haven't seen her before. Not to know." She shrugged. "Only that folk keep clear of that corner. Have done all week. Thought it was the draught off the door."

Tilly had gone up on her toes to look past Tamsin's shoulder. She came down again and leaned in close between them, and dropped her voice to a whisper. "That's how people swerve round bad luck," she whispered. "Did you see him? That's exactly it. Like when a black dog crosses the road, or someone's broken a mirror, and they don't want it catching." She looked at the woman and away again quickly, as if she weren't sure looking was safe either. "My aunt swerves like that round ladders."

Elsie looked at the woman in the green coat, alone in her ring of empty tables, with her gloved hand round her cup. She looked at the man's spilled ale soaking into the boards. "Oh," she said. "That sounds very lonely." She had a mug of tea in her hand, poured from the big urn the guild kept stewing by the clerk's desk for anyone come in off the road. She picked up her satchel. She went straight across the hall to the corner table, through the busy middle of it and out into the empty ring, without slowing down.

The woman looked up as Elsie came into the ring. Her eyes went over Elsie, the pink hair, the satchel, the mug, and settled on her face, dark and very steady under the kohl. Elsie stopped at the table and smiled. "Good morning," she said. "Is this seat free?" She put her hand on the back of the chair across from the woman. The woman looked at the hand. "It's free," she said. Her voice was low and cool and a little dry, like the inside of an old book. "You shouldn't."

"Thank you," said Elsie, and sat down. There was a crack. It was a short, flat, splintering crack, like a stick across a knee, and it came from somewhere under her. The chair's back left leg went out sideways. The chair went down, and Elsie went down with it, all at once, in a heap, and the mug went up. She landed on the floor on her bottom with her skirt round her thighs and her boots in the air. The tea came down after her. It came down mostly in her lap, lukewarm, with the leaves in it.

For a moment there was quiet round the corner. Elsie sat in the wreck of the chair with tea running off her knees. The woman at the table hadn't moved. She looked down at Elsie over the edge of the table, her face perfectly still. "There," she said. She said it without any satisfaction at all, as if she had been expecting it for some time and was only sorry to be right. "I'm a Hexer. Bad luck finds whoever sits near me. It bends that way. It's not personal." She looked at the broken chair as though it had done just what she expected of it.

Elsie looked down at herself. Her skirt was soaked, and there was a tea leaf stuck to her knee. She began to laugh. She couldn't help it; it came bubbling up out of her and wouldn't stop, and she sat on the floor and laughed with her wet knees in the air. "It's only tea," she said, when she could. "It was going cold anyway." She got up, shaking her skirt, and picked the tea leaf off her knee. She set the broken chair against the wall, out of the way. Then she pulled out the next chair along, the one beside it, and sat down on that one instead.

The chair held. Elsie beamed across the table. Behind her she heard boots, and turned. Tamsin was coming over through the empty ring with Margery on her shoulder and her chin up, and Tilly was coming behind her, clutching her vial belt in both hands. Tamsin looked at the chairs. She picked the one on the woman's other side, pulled it out, and sat down on it hard, with a thump, the whole of her weight at once, and looked at it, as if she were daring it.

Tilly sat down too, last, on the very edge of the chair beside Tamsin's, as carefully as if it were a hot stove. There was a snap. It was the strap of her vial belt, the old leather giving all at once at the buckle, and the whole belt slid off her hips with every vial on it. Tilly's hands moved faster than Elsie had seen them move. She caught the belt. She caught the two vials that jumped out of their loops, one in each hand, and a third against her knee, and sat there frozen with her arms full of glass and her eyes huge. Every one of them was still off the floor.

The woman in the green coat looked at Tilly with her arms full of vials. She looked at Tamsin sitting square on her chair with her axe. She looked at Elsie, in her wet skirt, beaming. She looked at them all, one after the other and then back again, with her kohl-dark eyes narrowed, the way a person looks at three strangers who have climbed into her boat in the middle of a lake. "You're mad," she said. It wasn't a question. She was quiet a moment. "Noor," she said. "My name. Since you're sitting at my table."

"Noor," Elsie said, trying it. It was the name on the slate, then, the one that would work alone. It was short and soft and round, like a pebble. "That's lovely. I'm Elsie." She leaned forward on her elbows. "And I'm an Enthraller," she said, as cheerfully as she said everything, because it was true, and it seemed only fair, when Noor had told hers. "So people keep their distance from me too. Not as far as from you, I don't think. But they do go round."

Noor's mouth moved at one corner, not quite a smile. "I've heard," she said. She tipped her head, very slightly, at the hall behind Elsie, the crowded benches and the noisy tables and the men at the bar. "The whole hall's heard. It's talked of you for a fortnight. The Enthraller with the pink hair, who ties up Berserkers and talks to trolls." She turned her cup a quarter turn on the table with one gloved finger. "I didn't think you'd be so short."

Elsie looked at the finger on the cup. It was long and slim inside the black leather, and the glove fitted it close, like a second skin, right up into the coat cuff. She had been wondering about the gloves since she sat down. It was summer, and warm in the hall. "Why do you wear gloves?" she asked. "It's so warm." Noor looked at her own hand on the cup. "I always have," she said. Then she looked away, past Elsie's shoulder, at the bare wall beside the board, and kept her eyes there.

Elsie reached across the table. She took Noor's gloved hand in both of hers, off the cup, and held it. She held it the way she'd held Tamsin's arm on the barrow, firmly, so there could be no doubt about it. The leather was thin and smooth and warm from the cup. She could feel the long bones of the fingers through it, and the knuckles, very still. She didn't explain. She only held on, and looked up into Noor's face, and smiled.

Noor went very still. She went still the way the harebells had gone still when the wind dropped, all at once and all over. Her hand lay in Elsie's two hands and didn't move. Her eyes had gone wide, so that Elsie could see the whole dark of them inside the kohl, and her lips had come a little apart. She didn't pull away. Elsie felt the fingers inside the glove go tense against her palm and then, very slowly, a little at a time, go loose again. Noor's throat moved. She stayed exactly as she was.

There was a little twang under the table. Elsie felt her left boot go slack round her calf. She looked down. Her bootlace had snapped, right in the middle, and the two ends were hanging down over the leather like a pair of dead worms. She laughed again. She laughed so suddenly that Tilly jumped and nearly dropped a vial after all. "That's my lace," she said, delighted, as though it had done something clever. She didn't let go of Noor's hand to see to it. She kept hold, and laughed, and let the boot flap.

Noor looked at the snapped lace, and then at Elsie, laughing, still holding her hand. Something went across her face too fast to read. "You don't understand," she said. Her voice had gone lower. "This is what it's like. A chair. A lace. A cup. Every day of it. The last two parties I was in broke up round me. Not in a fight. Just bit by bit. A sprained wrist, a lost purse, a bad fall on a stair. And then they look at me. And then they go." She said it without any feeling, as if she'd said it many times to herself. "Everyone leaves," Noor said.

Tamsin had been sitting with her arms folded, watching. Now she leaned forward, and the chair creaked under her and held. "Three parties dropped me," she said. She said it loud, the way she said everything, and a man at the next ring of tables looked round. "Three. For a lot worse than tea. I broke a shield-bearer's arm." Her green eyes were steady on Noor's face. She jerked her chin at Elsie. "She didn't drop me," Tamsin said. "She tied a string to me and kept me."

"The College threw me out," Tilly said. Her voice came out higher than she meant, and she cleared her throat and went on. She was still holding the vial belt in her lap like a cat. "The Alchemists' College. For exploding things. The east lab windows. All of them." She went pink. "And Elsie didn't mind that either. She let me blow up a weir. On purpose." She looked down at her vials and went pinker.

Noor looked from Tamsin to Tilly to Elsie. Her hand was still in Elsie's. "I'm not asking to join anything," she said. Her voice had sharpened again. "Your party. Anyone's. I don't want it. I can see where it ends." Elsie shook her head, smiling. "I know," she said. "I wasn't asking you to join. I'm asking you to lunch." She squeezed the gloved hand. "There's soup at the Heron. It's very good soup."

"No," Noor said. "Thank you." She said the thank you very correctly, like the clerk stamping a notice. Then she drew her hand back out of Elsie's. She did it slowly. She took a long time over it, longer than she needed, so that Elsie felt the leather slide across her palm, and then her fingers, and then away, a little at a time. Noor put the hand in her lap under the table, out of sight, and kept it there.

"All right," Elsie said. She wasn't hurt. She could see it wasn't a no that meant no, not all the way through. "Then I'll come and sit here tomorrow," she said, comfortably, "with my tea. And the day after. And the day after that." She nodded to herself, settling it. "Until you say yes. Some day you'll be hungry, and I'll be here."

Noor regarded her across the table for a long moment. Her face didn't change, but her eyes went, very briefly, to the broken chair leaning against the wall where Elsie had put it. "You'll run out of chairs," she said. She said it very dry, dry as the wight's hiss, and her mouth stayed perfectly straight, and something about the way it stayed straight made Elsie want to laugh again.

Elsie dug in her satchel and brought out the little blue notebook. She opened it to a clean page and found her pencil and wrote Noor at the top, in her round hand, and underlined it. "What are your favourite things?" she asked. "For my book. I keep everyone's." Noor stared at her. She stared at the notebook, and at the pencil, and at Elsie, with her lips a little parted, as though Elsie had asked her to stand on the table and dance.

The hall went on being loud round them. Noor sat with her hand in her lap and stared for so long that Tilly began to fidget with her vials. Then she looked away, toward the high windows, where the sun came in. "Rain," she said at last. She said it slowly, as if she were finding the words somewhere a long way down. "When the streets are empty. When everyone's gone in and there's only the rain." She was quiet a moment. "And black coffee," Noor said. "Strong. Nothing in it."

Elsie wrote it down. Rain, she wrote, when the streets are empty. Black coffee, strong, nothing in it. She thought about it with her pencil on her lip. Then, beside Noor's name at the top of the page, she drew a tiny raincloud, very carefully, a fat little cloud with five short lines of rain coming down out of it. She added a sixth line so it wouldn't look stingy. She was pleased with it. It looked just like a raincloud.

The notebook lay open on the table between them. Noor's eyes went down to it. She was reading it upside down, the way people do across a table: her own name, and the rain, and the coffee. Her gaze stopped on the little cloud. She looked at it for a while. The corner of her mouth twitched. It was a very small twitch, quickly put away, but Elsie saw it before it went, and was glad.

The big clock over the clerk's desk struck the quarter. Elsie shut her notebook and stood up, carefully, in case the chair had ideas. Tilly stood up with her belt hugged to her chest, and Tamsin stood up and swung Margery onto her shoulder. "Tomorrow, then," Elsie said to Noor, as though it were arranged. They went back across the empty ring and through the crowded middle of the hall to the door, Elsie's boot flapping. At the door she turned and waved, a big wave over her head. Noor was watching them go. After a moment she lifted one gloved hand off the table, not very high, and held it there.

Outside, the guild steps were warm with sun, and Brightwater Square was full of noise and pigeons. Tamsin stopped on the top step and looked at Elsie sideways. She was grinning. "You know what you do?" she said. "You collect people. Like stray cats. You see one sitting on a wall looking sorry for itself and you go straight over and pick it up." She counted on her fingers. "Me. Tilly. Cora, sort of. Now a Hexer." She shook her head. "Stray cats."

Elsie sat down on the step to knot her bootlace back together, two broken ends in a fat bow. "I don't collect them," she said, pulling it tight. "That makes it sound like stamps. I just find them. They're there, and I find them." She stood up and stamped her foot to test the knot. It held. She looked up at Tamsin, and over at Tilly, with the sun in her eyes. "And they're all wonderful," she said, "every one. So it's not my fault."

At noon they went across the canal to the Tipsy Heron, which was as loud as ever and smelled of onions. Elsie put her hand flat on the counter before Tamsin could. "Mine," she said firmly. "You did the pies yesterday. Soup and bread for three, please." The woman at the counter said four pips each. Elsie counted them out of her pouch into her hand, twelve copper pips, and put them on the wood in a little stack. Tamsin opened her mouth and shut it again. The soup was pea and ham, thick and green, in deep brown bowls, and the bread was still warm in the middle.

Tilly had a bottle of her own pear fizz out of her belt, the belt knotted round her again where the strap had gone. She pulled the cork with her teeth and drank it, and it fizzed up her nose, and she sneezed. Then, with her spoon in her fist and her eyes half shut, she sang the barrow song to her soup. She sang it very badly. She went wrong on the second note and didn't find her way back, and her voice cracked on the high part where Amara's had opened up like a door, and she sang it anyway, all the way through, to the peas.

Tamsin laughed into her bread. Then she stopped laughing and pushed her bowl a little away. "I wrote to Nell," she said. She said it to the table. "Before we went to the barrow. Told her about the shield-bearer. The Hounds' one. That I went and said sorry, and what she said back." She tore a piece off her bread and didn't eat it. "Left it at the wharf office to be sent on after the Marigold." She turned the bread over in her fingers. "And now I keep thinking what Nell'll say to it."

"She'll say she's proud of you," Elsie said at once. She was sure of it. She had seen Nell's face on the Marigold, and knew Nell wanted her sister home. Tamsin snorted. "Nell's never once said that," she said. "Not in nineteen years. She says I'm a great lump. She says I snore and I've got feet like a carthorse." She stared into her soup. "She doesn't say proud."

Elsie put her hand under the table and found Tamsin's hand on her knee, and squeezed it. Tamsin's hand was hot and rough, with the knuckle wrap scratchy over the back of it. For a moment it lay there. Then it turned over under Elsie's and squeezed back, hard. Elsie went to take hers away again, to pick up her spoon. Tamsin's fingers closed and kept it. Above the table Tamsin went on eating soup one-handed, looking hard at Tilly, as if Tilly's singing needed watching, and under the table her thumb moved slowly over the back of Elsie's knuckles.

At one o'clock Tilly pushed her chair back. "I'm going home," she announced, "to read up on wights. Properly. Everything there is. I've got a book on grave things under my bed somewhere." She came round the table and hugged Elsie goodbye, both arms round her neck from behind, her cheek against Elsie's hair for a moment, smelling of pear and sulphur. "Bye, Elsie," she said into it. Then she was gone through the crowd with her braids swinging and her belt knotted round her.

Tamsin walked her home. She didn't ask whether she should; she just came, with Margery on her shoulder, when Elsie got up. They went the long way, along Willow Canal, where the willows hung down into the water and the lanterns along the bank were all still unlit, swaying a little, pale paper in the sun. Halfway along was the house. It was narrow and tall and three storeys high, with a wild garden spilling over its railings and the blue door. The card was still in the window beside the door, a little yellower than last time. To let.

Elsie didn't stop. Stopping at the house felt like staring. But as she passed she reached out and pressed two fingers to the blue door, flat, for a moment, the way you'd touch the nose of a horse you meant to come back for. The paint was warm. She took her hand away and walked on. A step behind her, without a word, Tamsin reached out and pressed her own two fingers to the door too, in the same place, and then put her hand back in her pocket and caught up.

At the Honeycomb the shop door stood open and the smell came out into the lane, warm and brown. Tamsin stopped on the step. "I'll come by in the morning," she said. "Early. For the guild." She looked at Elsie for a moment longer, with something in her face that Elsie thought was probably tiredness, after a night on a hillside. Then she hitched Margery higher on her shoulder and went off along Thimble Canal toward the wharf, her copper curls bright in the sun, not looking back until the bridge, where she did.

Agnes was behind the counter with her sleeves rolled up, setting out the afternoon buns in rows with a wooden paddle. She looked up when Elsie came in and looked her over, the wet-stained skirt, the knotted lace, the grass seed still in her hair, and didn't say a word about any of it. "Mrs Merriweather," Elsie said, "may I use the big oven this evening? After you close? I want to bake." Agnes went on setting out buns. "Yes," she said. It was all she said. It was all she needed to.

Up in the attic the air was hot and close under the slope of the roof. Elsie pushed the round window open as far as it would go, and the canal air came in, cool, smelling of water and weed. She meant only to sit on the bed a moment and take off her boots. She got one boot off. Then she lay back across the bed with the other still on and her arm over her eyes, and the sounds of the canal came in at the window, a boatman calling, a duck, someone's washing flapping, and she was asleep before she could get to the second boot.

When she woke the light had moved right across the floor and gone gold. She sat up with the print of the blanket on her arm and pulled off the other boot at last. She filled the chipped jug at the basin and went along the crate. Mrs Pennywhistle, Sunny, Captain, a dribble for Doris. Moonpenny got the last of it, carefully, at the roots. Elsie knelt by the sill with her chin on her arms beside the clay pot. "There was a girl singing on the grave," she told her, "just as they said. Only she wasn't a ghost. And the lights came to her like moths. Oh, Moonpenny, you should have heard it."

She told Moonpenny all of it, every bit, and when it was told she got out the glove. She sat on the bed with her back to the wall and her knees up and knitted the fingers. They were fiddly. Each one had to be picked up from the hand in its own little ring of stitches and knitted round and round on the needles, and closed off neat at the tip. She did the forefinger, and the middle one, and the ring finger, and the little one, which was so small it made her smile. Then the thumb was finished and all four fingers too. She put it on her own hand. It was much too long in the fingers. That was right. Cora's were long.

She cast on the second glove at once, before she could stop to admire the first one too much. The new cuff began to grow on the needles, ribbing, two plain and two purl, round and round. The canal sounds came in at the window. Elsie knitted, and somewhere along the way she began to hum. It was a slow, climbing tune with no words to it, the one from the barrow, going up and up and opening out at the top. She didn't notice she was humming it. She hummed it all the way down the cuff.

At six she went down. The shop was shut and the blinds down, and Agnes was scrubbing the big table, and the ovens were still ticking with heat. "I need honey," Elsie said. "For honey cakes. Can I buy some off you?" Agnes went to the back without a word and came back with a comb in a dish, dark gold and dripping, wrapped in waxed cloth. "Three pips," she said. Elsie counted out three copper pips from her pouch, one at a time into Agnes's floury palm, and Agnes put them in the tin under the counter.

Elsie baked them in the big oven, a whole tray of little honey cakes, the sticky kind. She did them the way Agnes had taught her, the honey warmed and beaten in, the oven moderate and not hot. Agnes didn't leave. She stood at the end of the table with her arms folded and watched. When the first tray came out she took a cake up on the end of a knife and turned it over and tapped the bottom. "Brush the top with the last of the comb," she said, "when it's out, and back in the oven while you count twenty. That's your crust." Elsie did. The tops went dark and shiny and crackled when she touched them.

She chose the best ones, the roundest and stickiest, and set them out on a blue plate. Then she went through to the shop and put the plate in the window, among the empty bread trays, where it would show to anyone walking along Thimble Canal. She stood back to look at it. She moved it an inch to the left, into the last of the light. In case Amara came by, she thought. In case she happened to be walking down this street, being nobody in particular.

There were some left over on the tray. Elsie took a sheet of the brown paper Agnes wrapped the loaves in and laid four cakes in the middle of it. She folded the paper over them, one side and then the other, and turned the ends under neatly, so they would keep till morning. She found the stub of pencil in her pouch. On the top of the parcel, in her round hand, she wrote Noor. She looked at it. She added a tiny raincloud beside it, to match.

At dusk the lamplighters' boats came along Thimble Canal. Elsie heard them before she saw them, the knock of the poles and the men calling to each other. One by one the paper lanterns along the bank came alight, red and gold and green, and their colours went down into the water and shook there. People went by along the canal walk, going home, going out. Elsie sat on a stool in the shop with her chin in her hand and watched the window. The honey cakes sat on their blue plate, in a row, shining, under the first lanterns. The plate stayed full.

At a quarter past eight Elsie got up off the stool. She wasn't sad. She found she wasn't, quite, and was a little surprised. Amara had said maybe, and maybe meant maybe, which was a great deal better than no. Maybe meant some other evening. She took one of the cakes off the plate, since there were so many, and ate it standing in the dark shop. It was sticky and sweet, and the crust cracked just as Agnes had promised. She licked her fingers afterward. Amara had said you had to.

Up in the attic she lit the candle, pulled the stool up to the little table that rocked, and wrote to her mother. Dear Mum, she wrote. We went to a barrow on the downs, a real one, a queen's. She wrote about the harebells and the stones and the wight in the passage, and how Tamsin's arm had gone cold, and how Cora's lantern had driven it back. She wrote about the singer, carefully, as a girl whose name she had promised to keep. And she wrote about a woman in a green coat in the corner of the guild hall, who wore black gloves.

She sat and chewed the end of her pen. The candle flame leaned and straightened. Then she bent over the page again. I think I have two new friends, Mum, she wrote. One of them sings and the other one breaks chairs. She smiled at that, and dipped her pen. Only they don't know it yet. I think they will. She underlined will, and looked at the line a moment, very pleased with it, and blotted it.

She took the finished glove off the table to put it away and went to the round window to shut it. Thimble Canal was all lanterns now, red and gold, doubled in the water. A figure was walking along the canal walk below, a tall straight figure in a dark coat, with her hands behind her back. Elsie knew the walk before she knew the face. It was Cora. Elsie leaned out of the window. She held up the finished glove, grey, the whole hand of it, and waved it at her, back and forth in the lantern light, like a flag.

Cora stopped. She stopped on the canal walk just under a red lantern, so that the light fell on her face. She looked up at the window. She looked at the glove. She stood there and looked at it for a long time, with her face turned up and quite still, longer than it takes to see a glove. Elsie waved it again, smaller. Cora didn't wave back. After a while, slowly, she took one hand from behind her back and put it in the pocket of her coat, and kept it there.

Elsie shut the window. She didn't put the glove away after all. She set it on the sill instead, beside Moonpenny in her clay pot, with its grey fingers curled a little where her hand had been, so it would be the first thing she saw in the morning. The second glove lay on the table, only a cuff on the needles. The finished one sat by the orchid in the lantern light from the canal, waiting for its pair.
