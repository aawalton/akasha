She stopped and looked at them both, Elsie on the floor with Tamsin's hand in her lap, Tamsin red to the ears, and went a little pink herself. "Sorry," she said, a third time, very small, and stood there twisting her braid.

"What do you make?" Elsie asked, because she wanted to know, and because Tilly looked as if she would apologise a fourth time unless somebody gave her something else to do with her mouth. Tilly's face lit. "Oh! Lots of things. Well, three things, properly." She counted on sooty fingers. "A burn salve, that's the good one, it's really good, it's better than the College's. A fizz, for waking you up, it tastes of pears. And something that smokes." She glanced at the green haze still hanging under the rafters. "That one's not finished."

"A burn salve," Elsie said, and sat up straighter, delighted. "Oh, that's just what a healer wants. Do you know how hard a good one is to find on the road? My mother would have given her boots for one that worked." She beamed up at Tilly from the floor. "I'd love to see it. I'd love to carry some, if you'd let me." Tilly stared at her. Her mouth opened and closed. The stick of chalk she had been holding slipped out of her fingers, bounced once on the boards, and rolled under the slate, and she went pink to the ends of her braids.

Tamsin flexed her newly wrapped hands, looked at them, and then cleared her throat loudly, as if she had been away somewhere and had come back. "You know the rule," she said, to both of them, though she was looking at Elsie. "The guild won't license a party into a dungeon with fewer than three. Two's no good. Two's a pair. Two can go and pick flowers." She jerked her head at the grey slates. "That's why the Leftovers stay left over. You get one, maybe, if you're lucky. The third's the one you can't find."

Elsie looked at Tamsin, sitting beside her on the floor with the axe across her knees and the clean wraps on her knuckles. She looked up at Tilly, standing over them in the scorched apron with soot on her cheek and her chalk under the slate. She looked from one to the other and back again, and a small bright feeling came up in her chest like a bubble in a pot. "Then there are three of us," she said. Tamsin opened her mouth and shut it. Tilly said, "Oh," very quietly, and then, louder, "Oh!"

There was one empty slate left in the Leftovers, so grey with old chalk that the new would hardly show. Elsie got up, found a stub of chalk on the ledge, and wrote ELSIE across the top line in her careful round letters, as large as would fit. Then she held the chalk out. Tamsin got up off the floor with Margery over her shoulder, took it, and wrote TAMSIN underneath in one hard stroke that squeaked. Tilly fished her own chalk out from under the slate and wrote OTTILIE last, very neatly, and then, after a moment, added (TILLY) in smaller letters beside it.

Cora watched it all from a step away. Elsie, turning from the slate with chalk on her fingers, saw her looking: at the chalk in Elsie's hand, at the three names, at Tamsin's ears, which were still red, and at Tilly's pink cheeks under the soot. The grey eyes went from one thing to the next, slowly and exactly, as if Cora were reading the corner of the hall like a page. Her fingers rested on the cover of the black ledger. Elsie waited for her to open it. Cora's hand stayed where it was, and the ledger stayed shut.

"Can you make us feel things?" Tilly said. It came out of her all at once, very fast, the words tumbling over each other, as if she had been holding them since Elsie sat down and they had pushed their way out. "I mean. Because you're — because of the class. I read about it. Can you make people feel things they don't — I'm only asking. I'm not saying you would. I'm sorry. I'm only asking because I'd want to know, if I were us, and I am us." She stopped, breathless, and held onto her braid.

Elsie turned to her and answered it plainly, because it deserved a plain answer and because she could not have lied about it if she tried. "I've never used it on anyone," she said. "Not once. Not on you, not on Tamsin, not on the girl in the hall, not on anybody. I only found out what it was this morning." She held Tilly's eyes. "And I don't mean to. I'd rather have a friend who chose to be one than anything I could make." She looked at Tamsin too, so Tamsin would know it was for her as well, and Tamsin looked back and did not look away.

"It's true," Cora said. Everyone turned. She had not moved from her place a step behind them, and her voice was as flat and even as it had been in the little room. "If she had used a skill since her Naming, there would be a line in this ledger." She lifted it an inch, the way she had before. "There isn't one." She said it without warmth and without coldness, the way a person reads off a scale, and then she lowered the ledger to her side again. Tilly let go of her braid. Tamsin let out a long breath through her nose.

They went along the quest board together to the F-rank notices, at the low end near the desks, and read them off the wall shoulder to shoulder. Cellar rats, under the Crooked Kettle tea house; four lanterns. A lost goat, somewhere in the first floor of the Underbloom; three lanterns. Moonbells from the Glasswood's edge, for an apothecary; six lanterns. Tilly read each one aloud under her breath, moving her lips. Tamsin read them with her arms folded, already weighing them up. Elsie read them twice, slowly, because every one of them was the first quest she had ever stood in front of.

"The goat," Tamsin said at once, and tapped the notice with a wrapped knuckle. "That's a real dungeon. First floor of the Underbloom, that's proper. That's a start." She was grinning, a crooked eager grin, already half on her way. Beside her Tilly had gone white. Not pale; white, all at once, the soot on her cheek standing out like a thumbprint on paper. "The Underbloom," she said faintly. "As in under. As in down." Her hands had gone to the belt of corked vials at her waist, and her fingers were moving over the corks one by one, counting them.

Elsie saw Tilly's fingers on the vials, counting and counting again, and saw how white she had gone, and made up her mind on the spot. "Actually," she said brightly, "I'd like the rats first, if that's all right." Tamsin turned to look at her. "I haven't done a quest before," Elsie said, which was true, "and I'd rather start somewhere with a door I can find again." Tamsin opened her mouth to argue, looked past Elsie at Tilly's white face and busy fingers, and shut it again. "Rats," she said. "Fine. Rats." Tilly's fingers stopped on the corks.

"The rats are under the Crooked Kettle," Tilly read off the notice, her voice coming back a little. "It's a tea house. On the north side, by the bridge with the cats." Elsie clasped her hands together. "A tea house," she said, as if she had been handed a present. "Oh, that's a good start. That's the best start. There'll be tea after, I should think, if we do it properly, and they'll be glad of us, and we'll know where everything is." Tamsin snorted, but she was smiling. Tilly let out a small breath that was almost a laugh.

At the desk the thin clerk with inky cuffs had come back to his ledgers. He looked up when the three of them stopped in front of him, and then at the Warden standing behind them, and then down at his page, and dipped his pen. "Party name?" he asked. They looked at each other. "Put Elsie's," Tamsin said, and Tilly nodded hard. The clerk wrote it, and wrote the three names under it, and against them, in a neat column: Cellar rats, the Crooked Kettle, tomorrow, morning. He blotted it and turned the ledger round so they could see it was done.

"I'll be at the Crooked Kettle at eight," Cora said, to Elsie, as they turned from the desk. It was the first thing she had said since the ledger. "At the door. Not before you, and not after." She said it like a clause of the Accord, read out so there would be no mistaking it. Then she did not go back to the charcoal door, as Elsie half expected her to. She stayed with them, a step behind, across the hall and out onto the steps and through the rest of the long afternoon, until the hall bell rang for closing.

They sat on the guild hall steps in the late sun, the stone warm through Elsie's skirt, the square below them busy with carts and pigeons. Tilly sat a step down with her knees under her chin, and Tamsin sprawled across two steps with Margery beside her like a third friend. They argued about rats. Tilly said rats could be frightened off with the smoke if she could only get the smoke to work. Tamsin said rats could be frightened off by Margery. Tilly said the tea house would not thank them for the smoke. Tamsin said the tea house would not thank them for Margery either. Elsie sat between them and laughed.

"I won her off a barge," Tamsin said, patting Margery's haft. "On the Long Cut. Bargeman bet me I couldn't lift her over my head. I said I could lift her and him both. He said if I could, she was mine." She grinned. "I got her up, and him up, and then I dropped him in the canal." Elsie laughed so hard she had to hold onto the step. She laughed until her eyes ran and she was gasping, and Tamsin, delighted, told the end again with the splash in it, louder, and Elsie laughed till she cried.

The light went long and gold and then soft, and the square below them emptied, and at dusk the lamplighters' boats came up the canal past the foot of Brightwater Square. They were small low boats with two women in each, one rowing and one standing with a long pole, and as they went the one with the pole reached up to each paper lantern strung along the canal and touched it, and it came alight. One, and then the next, and then the next, all the way along the water to the bridge and round the bend, until the whole canal ran with soft coloured light.

Cora stood up from the step where she had sat all afternoon with her back very straight. She had opened the black ledger once, while the boats went by, and read something in it, and now she closed it and fastened the strap round it and held it against her coat. "I go off duty at the hall door," she said. "When the hall shuts, the guild's business is done for the day, and so is mine." She looked at the closed doors behind them, where the bell had rung for closing as the first boats came up the canal.

"What will you write?" Elsie asked. She asked it gently, looking up at Cora from the step, because she had been wanting to know since the little room and could not think of a way to ask that was not rude, and had decided in the end that asking plainly was the least rude way. "About today. In there." She nodded at the ledger. Cora looked down at her for a long moment in the lantern light, her face cool and still. "I haven't decided," she said. It sounded true, as true as black, strong, one sugar.

"Well," Elsie said, and smiled up at her, "I'll try to give you something good to write." She meant that too. She meant it so much it came out of her almost as a promise, and Cora seemed to hear it as one; something in the grey eyes moved and settled. Cora did not answer. She inclined her head the same exact degree she had in the Wardens' room, and turned, and walked away along the canal under the new-lit lanterns, tall and dark and straight-backed, the ledger held against her side, until the bend took her.

"Supper," Tamsin said, getting up and stretching till her joints cracked. "Heron." The Tipsy Heron was just across the canal from the guild hall, loud and lit and smelling of stew, and they crossed the little bridge to it and found a corner. Elsie went to the counter and bought two suppers. She put a silver lantern down on the wood for the two, and the girl behind the counter gave her back two copper pips, and Elsie carried the bowls over herself, one in each hand, and set them in front of Tamsin and Tilly before either of them could argue.

It was stew and bread, thick brown stew with carrots in it and a heel of bread on the side, and the other two fell on it. Tilly talked all the way through hers with her mouth full, waving her spoon, about rats and the Kettle and smoke and pears, and got gravy on her apron, which hardly showed among the scorch marks. Tamsin ate fast and steadily, and when Tilly left her crusts on the side of her bowl, Tamsin reached over without a word and ate those too. Elsie sat with her chin on her hands and watched them both, and could not stop smiling.

The tavern was loud around them, full of boots and laughter and somebody singing badly by the fire, and the lantern light came in gold through the window from the canal. Elsie looked at the two of them across the little table, Tilly with gravy on her apron and Tamsin with Tilly's crust in her mouth, and the feeling in her chest got so big she had to let some of it out. "I love you both already," she said. "I really do. I'm so glad it was you." It was simply true, and she said it the way she said every true thing, plainly.

Tamsin choked on a mouthful of stew. She coughed and spluttered and had to be hit on the back, and when she came up for air her face was red and her ears were redder, the way they had been on the floor of the Leftovers. Tilly did not choke. Tilly went very still with her spoon halfway to her mouth, and went pink, slowly and completely, from the collar of her blouse to her round cheeks to the very ends of her singed braids, and stayed that way, staring down into her stew as if there were an answer at the bottom of it.

Elsie looked at the two of them, Tamsin wheezing with her red ears and Tilly pink to the braids, both of them not quite looking at her. She thought that this must be what people looked like when they were glad. She had seen it on the road, now and then, on faces round a fire when the music was good, though not often turned toward her and not two at once. It warmed her right through, like soup. She was glad too. She reached over and squeezed Tilly's sooty hand, and then Tamsin's wrapped one, and sat back with both her hands warm.

They walked out together and parted at the little bridge, under the lanterns, with the canal black and gold below them. "Crooked Kettle," Tamsin said, walking backwards along the far bank with Margery on her shoulder. "Eight. Don't be late. The Warden'll write it down." Tilly, going the other way along the canal, turned round twice to wave, and the second time called back over the water, "I'll bring the fizz! The pear one! For waking up!" Elsie waved to them both until the dark and the lanterns took them, Tamsin one way and Tilly the other.

She walked home along Thimble Canal under the lanterns, the water lapping at the stones and the evening warm and still, the copper tag cool in the pocket of her satchel. A cat watched her from a windowsill. Somewhere across the water someone was playing a pipe. She found, halfway home, that she was humming, and could not have said for how long or what the tune was; it was nothing she knew, only something that had come out of her of its own accord, the way it did. She went on humming, a little louder, because there was no reason to stop.

The attic was dark and smelled of cold bread and fern. Elsie lit the candle and sat on the edge of the bed with her boots still on, and took out the little blue notebook, and turned to a clean page. She wrote, in her careful round hand: Tamsin. Under it: Tilly. Then, on its own line, with care: Cora. And beside it, so she would get it right every time: black, strong, one sugar. She read the page over twice, the way she read everything that mattered, and smoothed it flat.

Then she went and knelt by the crate under the window in the candlelight, still in her boots, to tell the plants. "I have a party now," she said to Mrs Pennywhistle, very quietly, so as not to wake the baker. "Three of us. It's on a slate." She told Sunny about Tamsin and Doris about Tilly, and she told Captain about Cora, and the tea, and the ledger. "And two friends," she said, to all of them, "already. And a Warden." She thought about that. "I think I have a Warden," she said.

She took her boots off at last and lay down on the narrow bed in her clothes, too tired and too full of the day to undo all the laces, and pushed the round window open for the air. The canal sounds came in, water and a far-off pipe and the lanterns creaking on their strings. She held the notebook open on her chest to read the page again, and got as far as Cora's name, and fell asleep with it there, rising and falling with her breath, and the window open over her onto the lit canal.
