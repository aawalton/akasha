Elsie woke to the smell of bread and the lake on her window.

The room was a narrow strip under the roof, the round window pale with mist. Then her bare feet found the cold boards.

She said good morning to her four plants by name. Then she washed, quick with the cold: her face and neck and under her arms, and the rest of her, the soft insides of her thighs and the small rose-pink patch of hair above her pussy. Her nipples stood up under the water and her skin came awake all over her.

The novice's outfit lay folded on the stool. The cream halter laced up the front and took both hands and a measured amount of breath; she smoothed the cloth over her small breasts until the straps sat straight. She set the little pink-and-white flower clip above her left ear.

She counted her coins on the bed, where they could not roll into a crack: thirteen silver lanterns and four copper pips. Two lanterns of that was the guild, and a lantern a week was the room, and the rest was hers. Then the satchel and its list: the salve, the bandages, the needle and thread.

Downstairs the baker put a warm roll in her hand before she could get a word out, and waved her out of the door for trying to pay.

She posted the letter to her mother at the corner shop, and on the way out stood aside on the bridge for two women walking arm in arm, and watched them all the way across.

The Hall of Naming stood at the end of the lakeshore road, white stone under a glass dome full of morning. The line at the foot of the steps was twenty long, and Elsie took her place at the back of it.

The girl in front of her turned round, small and neat and looking ready to be sick. "Are you nervous?" she asked.

"Yes," Elsie said, and then, because it was true twice: "Very."

The girl laughed, and Elsie held out both hands, and they stood holding each other's cold hands until the doors opened.

Inside, the light came down onto a silver circle set into the floor. A priestess in grey read the names off a roll. The first was a tall girl with her hair pinned, and when she stepped into the circle a pane of light opened above her, plain as a shop ledger.

Elsie stood in the line and hoped: a healer's class, like her mother's, the kind that goes where the hurt is and mends it. Simples and bandages. Then the priestess read out, "Fairweather, Elsie," and her legs carried her up the two steps and into the circle.

The light came down. The window opened above her.

She read it. Then she read it again, in case the pane of light had made a mistake, or she had. The letters did not change. Enthraller. The silence came off the hall in a wave, and every person in the room went still: the priestess with her roll half-lowered, the parents along the wall. Elsie looked up at her own class, and her ears rang with the quiet.

Then a chair scraped up in the gallery. A woman in a good dark coat walked out along it and through the little door at the end. The priestess looked at the roll a long moment, then turned the page and did not read out the next name.

Elsie stepped down out of the circle and said, into all that quiet, in her ordinary voice, "Oh! That sounds like a lot of responsibility." The priestess looked at her, and did not quite manage to smile back.

The hall did not clap for her. She walked its length with every eye going along with her, and out through the doors. The line outside looked at her all at once, then looked away, and made a lane wider than it needed to be.

She walked down from the Hall alone, with the city warm and ordinary around her. She got most of the way to the square before her eyes filled, and stood at the corner until it passed.

The guild hall stood across Brightwater Square under a green copper roof, and the noise of it came out through the open doors. Elsie went up the steps and joined the line.

The clerk was a thin man with ink on his cuff and three ledgers open. "Name," he said, without looking up. She gave it and he wrote it. Then he asked the question he had been asking all morning, flatly: "Class."

"Enthraller," Elsie said.

The pen stopped halfway through the E and stayed there with the nib on the paper. The clerk looked up at her over the top of his ledgers, and his face did the thing faces had been doing to her all morning. He set the pen down and went up the stairs two at a time.

In a while he came back down with the guildmaster behind him: a big grey woman with a swordswoman's hands.

"Right," she said at last. "Come upstairs. Bring the satchel."

Upstairs the office had a long table with a map pinned flat on it. The guildmaster put a single sheet in front of her. The Strings Accord. Every skill she used, written down and given to the guildmaster. And she was never to use a skill on a person who had not agreed to it first.

"You'll have a Warden with you," the guildmaster said. "Not to hover. To watch."

Elsie looked up.

"It isn't there because you've done anything. It's there so that if a thing went wrong, someone would see it early."

"Then I'm glad of it," Elsie said. "I'd rather have it arranged properly than half arranged." The guildmaster held the door for her, and the two of them went back down into the hall.

Down in the hall the clerk's ledgers were still open. Elsie counted the fee out onto the desk: two silver lanterns, side by side. The clerk stamped an F into a small copper tag and handed it to her, her own name on the back. She put the eleven lanterns and four pips left back in her purse, and the purse in the little pocket inside her satchel she had been saving for exactly this.

Then the guildmaster walked her back along the hall to a door painted charcoal behind the Wardens' counter. Beyond it was a small room with a window high up, and a young woman with a black leather ledger under her arm — tall and neat, coat buttoned to the throat. She looked at Elsie the way the hall had looked at her, except that this woman's face did not then look away.

"Corisande Vane," the guildmaster said. "Warden. We call her Cora."

The Warden came forward and put out her hand. Her grip was brief and exact. "I'll go with you," Cora said. "Every guild matter, and every dungeon. Every skill you use, I write it down, and it goes to the guildmaster." She said it flatly.

"Every skill," Elsie said. "All right." Then: "That's a great deal of walking and writing for you, when you've only just been given it." Cora's eyes moved, once, and settled again. "Thank you for coming," Elsie said. "It's a comfort to have somebody coming along." The guildmaster went back out into the hall, and left the two of them to it.

Cora did not answer that either; Elsie was beginning to understand it was not rudeness but a door that opened slowly. So on the way across the hall she said, cheerfully, "What do you like in your tea?"

The Warden's boots stopped on the boards.

"I'm asking because I'll be fetching it for you," Elsie said, "and I'd rather get it right than guess and be wrong twice a day for a year."

"Black," Cora said at last. "Strong. One sugar." She said it with no particular expression of any kind, and went on walking.

"Black, strong, one sugar," Elsie repeated. "I'll remember it."

They came out into the middle of the hall, and Cora walked a half step behind her. The boards were full. Elsie went along the F-rank notices first: cellar rats; a goat, gone down into the Underbloom and not come back; moonbells, at the Glasswood's edge. Then she found the other thing she had come for: the party board.

It was a rack of slates, each a party's face turned out to the room. Elsie put herself where she could be seen and asked, and twice the slate she asked about was full: four names up and no line left under them. The ones who looked away fastest were the ones who had watched her come out of the office upstairs.

At the end of the rack the last slate hung face to the wall, and the two beside it stood lower in the corner, their names crossed out. Elsie turned the last slate round and found a stick of chalk on the ledge beneath it.

Behind her, somebody was having an argument with a slate. "It says four," a woman said. "It's got three."

"It's got three because you scratched out Merrit," said somebody else.

"Merrit left at dawn and took the pot with her. What am I supposed to write under that?"

She was tall and lean, with a great axe strapped across her back, the head hanging by her hip.

She stopped talking when Elsie came near. "You want half a slate?" she said, and jerked her chin at the corner. "That one's got one name on it, which is mine. The rest is free." Elsie looked at the slate, then up at the axe.

"Is that — does it have a name?" she asked.

The woman's chin came up, and she said, "Margery," the way a person says a thing they expect to be laughed at.

"It's a good name," Elsie said. "It looks like a Margery."

The woman laughed, once, entirely unexpectedly. "Tamsin," she said. "Berserker. E-rank. This morning the Copper Hounds took me off their slate, over their shield-bearer's arm." She held her arm out and looked at it, then let it drop. "It wasn't a bad arm either. That's the thing. It was a decent arm."

Elsie did not think about it. She reached out and put her hand flat on Tamsin's wrist, on the underside where the pulse was, the way her mother put a hand on a wrist to count it, and left it there.

Tamsin stopped. All of her stopped: the laugh, the leaning, the second thing she had been about to say. Under Elsie's fingers her pulse went hard and quick, and her skin was warm the way a wall is warm late in the day. Her ears went red — not pink, not a blush coming on slowly, but red at once, from the lobe up, both sides, the way a match takes.

Elsie held on, because she held on to a pulse until it had told her what she needed to know. "You're all right," she said. Tamsin looked at Elsie's hand, then at Elsie's face, and let her hold on.

"Is the arm mending?" Elsie asked. Tamsin stared at her. "It's strapped," she said at last. "Splint, sling, and a lot of complaining, and they won't let me lift anything over my head." She thought about it. "Probably mending. Yes."

At the far end of the board something small arrived at speed and already talking. "— because if it's the same batch it'll be the seal, and if it's the seal it's the wax—"

It was a small woman with brass goggles pushed up into her hair, two braids with singed ends, and an apron far too big for her. She stopped dead when she saw the three of them, and the chalk in Elsie's hand.

"What do you brew?" Elsie asked, to be friendly.

She answered with three warnings attached: not to breathe the vapour, not to stand downstream of it, and not to be in the room when it went. Then she said, in a much smaller voice, "They threw me out on Wednesday. Over the windows. And my father thinks I'm still there, so."

Elsie smiled at her, her usual smile, used exactly as she used it on everyone. The small woman went red to the tips of both ears and dropped her chalk. She picked it up, and said, "Sorry," to the slate. Then she dropped it again.

"Ottilie," she said, over the second drop. "Brandt. Tilly, mostly. Alchemist. F-rank as of three days ago, if you count the day I registered, which the guild does, so I do."

"Right," Tamsin said, and took the notice off the board and set it flat on the ledge. Six lanterns, for an apothecary, for moonbells, which grew at the Glasswood's edge. "Split three ways, that's two lanterns each, and the apothecary pays on delivery, which is quicker than the guild pays. Unless anybody objects."

"The three of you could take it," Elsie said. "Split three ways, two each — you don't need me for it." She meant it.

"Have you been in a dungeon?" Tamsin asked.

"No," Elsie said.

"Mm." Tamsin turned the slate over in her hand. "I've been in a dozen. The edge of the Glasswood is not a dungeon, it's a walk. Three's what the guild wants and three's what we'll have, and you're coming, because I've a party of three and a slate that holds four."

Elsie chalked the names on the last slate in her ordinary, careful hand. Elsie. Then Tilly wrote Ottilie under it in letters far too neat for the rest of her, and added, in smaller chalk, (Tilly). Tamsin took the chalk next and wrote Tamsin in one stroke. Four names on a slate in the corner, the last slate on the rack.

"So what's your class?" Tilly asked, winding the chalk's string round one finger. "For the slate. We should have it right."

Elsie looked at her and did not look away, and said it plainly, because she had decided on the steps of the Hall of Naming to say it plainly, every time. "Enthraller."

Tilly's hands stopped winding the string. Tamsin stopped working at the buckle of her jerkin. "Enthraller," Tilly said. "Right. I've read about that. It was in a book my father has. It had a picture." She looked at the slate, and at the notice on the ledge, and at Elsie, in that order.

"So you can make me do anything," Tamsin said.

She said it flatly into the space between them, her face closed up, looking at the slate and not at Elsie. "No," Elsie said. "Because I haven't used it on anyone. Not once, not since the window opened over my head, and not on you, and not on the girl in the hall who held my hands." She stopped, and made herself say the rest of it plainly too. "I don't know yet what it does. I know its name and I know what the guild thinks of the name, and that's the whole of it." She waited. "You don't have to believe that today. You'll get to watch, though. That's what Cora's for, apparently."

They worked on the slate a while longer, mostly Tilly, who wanted the quest written on the back of it. Then she said, in a voice she was working to keep ordinary, "Does it — bonds. Do you have to touch someone. To have one."

Elsie opened her mouth to say she did not have the first idea, because she did not.

While Tilly asked it, the Warden looked at the slate, then at Tilly's hands and the chalk in them, then at Tamsin's ears, which had gone red again and not gone pale since, then at Elsie's right hand. Then she opened the black ledger and did not write a word in it, and closed it again.

Somebody's supper went past on a tray, and Tilly caught it and came back with bread and a jug and four cups, and the four of them ate standing at the board. Tilly interrupted Tamsin's story about the Copper Hounds eleven times to correct the parts about the mule. Cora stood outside the circle with her own cup, refusing Elsie's offer, and did not say a word, and stayed.

When the food was done Tamsin rubbed a mark off the slate and said, "Dusk tomorrow, then. East gate, the hour before," as a thing already decided which wanted only to be said out loud. "We go out at dusk and come back with a satchel full of flowers and the apothecary's six lanterns, and everyone's on time and everyone's sensible." She got a nod from Tilly, and one from Elsie.

"Dusk is the hour," Elsie said, and then heard herself say it. "My mother taught me moonbells. They open at dusk, all together, and they're shut again by midnight, so you have to be there in the dark. You steep them cold, for fever. That's the sort of thing I know."

Walking back down the hall, Elsie thought about the four of them at the board, and Cora standing just outside the circle. And it came to her plainly: the three of them were going to be friends. It was such a good thought that she started to hum, and Tilly joined in with a tune that was not the same tune at all.

By the time they came out onto the steps the light had gone soft and long. The lamplighters worked from the water, and the paper lanterns came on behind them, one after another, all the way down to the bridge and round the bend. Elsie watched it happen along the whole length of the line.

In the hall the crowd had thinned out to the late ones. Cora stood at the end of the rail with the ledger open on the flat of her hand, a quarter of a small page written close and dark. She read it through, then closed the ledger and tucked it away under her coat.

"What will you write?" Elsie asked her, because she had been wanting to know all afternoon.

Cora looked at her. "I haven't decided," she said. She said it the way she had said black, strong, one sugar: a true fact, offered flatly, with nothing underneath it. Then she looked past Elsie at the last slate, and then back at Elsie.

"Well," Elsie said, "I'll try to give you something good to write."
