Elsie woke to the smell of bread and the lake on her window.

The room was one narrow strip under the roof, the ceiling sloped down almost to the bed and the round window over the canal gone soft and pale with mist. Under the floor the Honeycomb's ovens were already working. The boards carried the knock of trays and the smell of a crust up to her, and she lay a while longer with the blanket under her chin and one warm palm flat on her belly, listening to the morning come in: a gull, an oar, a bell somewhere across the water. Then her bare feet found the cold boards and she stood up in the little room and stretched, which the sloped ceiling allowed in one direction more than the other.

She had four plants in a crate under the window and she said good morning to each of them by name. Mrs Pennywhistle the fern had put out a new frond in the night and had to be told about it, so Elsie told her, thoroughly, and Mrs Pennywhistle took it well. Sunny the lemon got praised for the two hard green beads that would be lemons by the end of summer. Doris the aloe only got a look, because Doris was thriving and knew it. Captain the mint got watered twice, because Captain drank more than the other three plants put together and would not say so. Then she filled the basin and washed: her face and her neck and under her arms, and then the rest of her, the soft insides of her thighs and the small rose-pink patch of hair above her pussy, quick, because the water was cold enough to take her breath. Her nipples stood up under it and her skin came awake all over her, and by the end of it she was properly up.

Her new clothes lay folded on the stool, and she put them on slowly, because she wanted to get them right. The halter was cream and cropped, laced up the front, and the lacing took both hands and a measured amount of breath; when it was snug she smoothed the cloth down over her small breasts and the pale skin of her middle and rolled her shoulders until the straps sat straight. The skirt was sage green and short, and the brown belt with its pouches went over it, and the gloves were the fingerless sort she had wanted since she was small, since before she had hands that would fit them. She laced her boots to the top and stood, and then set the little flower clip, pink and white, above her left ear, where she could feel it when she turned her head.

Then she put the coins away and packed the satchel, which had its own list: the pot of salve with the lid screwed tight, the roll of bandages, the folded cloth, the needle and thread, the willow bark in its twist of paper. Her mother's list, in her mother's order, and she checked off every line of it with a fingertip and said each thing quietly to herself as she went.

Downstairs the Honeycomb smelled so good it was nearly a sound. The baker was behind her counter with her sleeves rolled, a small round woman with flour to the elbows, and she took one look at Elsie in the new clothes and put a warm roll into her hand before Elsie could get a word out about it. She ate it walking, and the butter ran over her knuckles, and she had to stop twice on the bridge to lick them.

At the corner shop she posted the letter to her mother, and watched it go into the tray for the south road, the way she watched it every week, as if the tray itself were a kind of promise. The Hall of Naming stood at the end of the lakeshore road, white stone and a high glass dome, and the morning had filled the dome until the whole of it looked poured rather than built. "What will you write?" Elsie asked her, because she had been wanting to know all afternoon and had not found a way to ask it that was not rude.

Cora looked at her. "I haven't decided," she said. She said it exactly the way she had said black, strong, one sugar: as a true fact, offered flatly, with nothing underneath it. Then she looked past Elsie at the party board in the corner, and at the last slate with its face turned out and four names chalked on it, and then back at Elsie, and it seemed to Elsie that this woman had not decided a great many things and meant to decide them all properly.

"Well," Elsie said, "I'll try to give you something good to write."
