Elsie woke to the bread smell with the attic still black around her, and knew at once what day it was. The Marigold went through the lock this morning. She was out of bed before she had properly opened her eyes, feeling for her clothes on the stool in the dark: the halter, laced by touch, the sage-green skirt, the boots, the belt. She splashed her face from the chipped jug and pinned her clip in by feel above her ear. Below her the ovens knocked and the baker's tread went to and fro. Through the round window the canal was a dark gleam under the last of the lanterns.

The slice of honey cake sat on the little table where Agnes had left it last night, on its square of brown paper. Elsie had not eaten it. She had looked at it for a long while by candlelight, golden, with the dip in the middle, and then she had folded the paper up round it, corner over corner, the way Agnes folded a parcel, and set it by her satchel for the morning. Now she picked it up and held it to her nose a moment. It smelled of honey and the oven. She tucked it into the top of her satchel, upright, so it would not crush, and went quietly down the stairs.

The Long Cut wharf was grey and cold and smelled of coal smoke. Along the quay the barges lay nose to tail in the half-light, and the Marigold lay among them with her cabin chimney going, a thin blue thread of smoke standing straight up into the still air, and a lamp lit behind the geraniums. She sat lower in the water than she had two days ago, heavy with her coal. Up on the cabin roof Nell was coiling a rope, her copper curls tied up in their blue cloth, laying the rope down round and round in a neat flat spiral, and whistling, and she looked up and saw Elsie, and waved the rope's end.

Tamsin came off the plank as Elsie reached the boat. She had been aboard all night; she was in the same shirt as yesterday, rumpled, with her sleeves pushed up and her curls flattened on one side as if she had slept on them a little, or lain on them without sleeping. Her eyes were red. Not red from crying, Elsie thought, or not only: red and tired and swollen at the lids, the way eyes went after too long by a lamp. Under one arm she had a bundle of paper, thick, tied up crosswise with string, and she held it pressed against her side as if it might blow away.

"Hello," Tamsin said, and her voice was hoarse. She saw Elsie looking at the bundle, and held it up a little. "I wrote half the night," she said. "At the table. Mum kept the lamp going." She turned it over in her hands. "An answer to every one. Every letter Nell wrote me. All of them. I went through them one at a time and wrote back to each." She laughed, a croak. "The heron one took me an hour. I didn't know what to say about the heron." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "My hand's gone stiff as a board. Look." She flexed her fingers, inky to the knuckle.

Rosa came up out of the cabin with her shawl round her shoulders and her apron already on, and threw out her arms at the sight of Elsie. "There she is," she said. "Come to see us off. Didn't I say she would." Elsie went to her, and dug in her satchel, and brought out the folded paper, and held it out. "It's the honey cake," she said. "Agnes brought me a slice last night, and I saved it for you." Rosa looked at it. Then she unfolded the paper, right there on the quay, and took the slice in her coal-dark fingers, and ate it in four bites with her eyes shut.

Rosa licked her thumb, and folded the empty paper very small, and put it in her apron pocket, as if it were worth keeping. "Well," she said. "That's the next cake paid for, then. In advance. You owe me a whole one now." And she took Elsie's face between her two big hands, the way she had held Tamsin's face on the quay two mornings ago, and kissed her loudly on one cheek and then on the other, smack, smack, so that Elsie laughed and came away with a smudge of coal on each side. "Mind you bring it," Rosa said, letting her go. "I'll be counting."

Nell came down off the roof in one jump, landing light on the quay, and came straight to Elsie and hugged her, both arms round her neck, quick and hard. Her cheek was cold from the morning. She put her mouth close to Elsie's ear, under the curls, and whispered, so low that only Elsie could hear it: "Keep her safe. Will you? Keep her safe for me." Her arms tightened. Elsie felt her breath catch against her neck. "She won't look after herself," Nell whispered. "She doesn't know how. She'll go in first, and she won't think. So you think for her."

Elsie hugged her back as tightly. "I will," she whispered into the blue cloth over Nell's ear. "I promise I will." She held on a moment more. "But she keeps me safe too," she whispered. "You should know that. She stepped in front of a wolf for Tilly, in the Glasswood. She goes in first, you're right. But she goes in first in front of us." She felt Nell laugh, a small wet laugh, against her shoulder. "Good," Nell whispered. "Good. Then you're square." She let go, and stepped back, and wiped her nose on her wrist, and grinned her crooked grin, Tamsin's grin, with her eyes very bright.

Tamsin cleared her throat. She held out the bundle of letters to Nell, with both hands, awkwardly, as if it were a hot dish. "These are yours," she said. "Answers. One for each. You'll have to read them in order, I numbered them, and my writing's terrible, so you'll have to squint." Nell looked at the bundle. She took it slowly, and looked down at it in her hands, at the string and the thick inky edges and her own name on the top sheet in Tamsin's big sprawling letters, and held it against her chest with both arms crossed over it. Her mouth opened, and shut again without a sound, and she nodded, hard.

Rosa had her hand on the mooring rope. She looked at Tamsin across the plank, a long look, and her chin wobbled once and then held. "Your bunk stays made up," she said, quite steadily. "You hear? Fresh every Sunday, same as before. We're back through this lock in a fortnight, and there'll be stew." She pointed a finger at Tamsin. "And you'll be on that wharf when we come in, my girl, or I'll come up into that city and find you, axe or no axe." Tamsin laughed, cracked. "I'll be there, Mum," she said. Rosa nodded, and let go the rope.

At the end of the wharf the lock-keeper leaned on the long black beam, and the great gates swung slowly open, groaning, with the water pouring and roaring through. The Marigold slid out from the quay, Rosa at the tiller in her shawl, and nosed into the lock, and the gates closed behind her, and the water rose and lifted her, and the far gates opened onto the Long Cut beyond, going away north between its banks into the mist. Nell stood on the cabin roof among the geraniums with the letters held under one arm. With the other she waved her red kerchief over her head, round and round, and kept on waving it.

Tamsin walked to the very edge of the lock and stood there. She did not wave. She stood with her hands at her sides and her boots at the lip of the stone, and watched the long green barge go away up the cut, smaller and smaller, the red kerchief going round and round on the roof, the thread of chimney smoke trailing back over the water. Elsie stood a little behind her and let her watch. The barge reached the bend where the cut turned among the willows, and went round it, and the kerchief went round it last, still waving; and then there was only the empty water.

Elsie came up beside her and took her hand. Tamsin's fingers were cold, and stiff from writing, and they closed round Elsie's at once, hard. And down the Tether Elsie felt the ache in her: it was there, the same ache that had come down the thread at the head of the quay two days ago, deep and wide. But it had changed. It was not a cold held thing any more. It was warm all the way through, the way an ache is when someone has been missed and is going to be seen again, and Elsie stood holding her hand and felt it, and her own eyes stung.

"A fortnight," Tamsin said, to the water. Her voice was rough. "I'll be here." She looked down at the stone under her boots, the lip of the lock. "I'll be on this wharf when she comes in. And the time after that. And the one after that, and every tie-up she makes, I'll be standing right here." She squeezed Elsie's hand. "I'll bring Nell something. I don't know what. Something from a dungeon. A glowcap. She'd like a glowcap." She laughed, wet. "Don't tell her I cried at a lock." "I won't," Elsie said. "I'll tell her you stood very straight."

They walked back into the city along the canals as the mist came up off the Mere. It lifted slowly, in long soft rags, off the water and away through the bridges, and the sun came through it white and then gold, and the wet stones of the towpaths began to steam. The paper lanterns hung dark along the water, dripping. Boats were moving now, a baker's punt and a coal boat and a woman poling a raft of cabbages, and the city was waking around them, shutters going up, a dog barking, a window flung open and a song coming out of it. Tamsin did not let go of Elsie's hand, and Elsie did not ask her to.

They came along Willow Canal, which Elsie had not walked before: a narrow quiet canal with old willows trailing their long green hair into the water, and narrow old houses along it, tall and thin and leaning a little against one another like tired friends. Elsie was looking at the willows. Then she was looking at a house. It was one of the narrowest, three storeys of old brick gone soft and rosy with age, and in its front window, propped against the glass, was a white card with two words on it in neat black letters. To let.

Elsie stopped dead. Tamsin, still holding her hand, was pulled up short beside her. The house had a blue door. It was a bright, chipped, cheerful blue, the blue of a summer sky, with a brass knocker shaped like a fish. There were window boxes under every window, three storeys of them, all empty and full of old dry earth, waiting. And beside the house, behind a crooked brick wall, was a garden: a wild one, gone to seed, with roses climbing over the wall and spilling down the canal side of it in great tangled armfuls, pink and white, and an apple tree leaning over the top.

Elsie let go of Tamsin's hand and went up to the front window and pressed her nose to the glass, with her hands cupped round her eyes against the light. Inside was a kitchen. It was a big room, bigger than the whole of her attic twice over, with a stone floor and a black range against the far wall and a deep old sink under a window at the back that looked out into the wild garden. There was a long bare space in the middle of the floor where a table ought to be. A long table, Elsie thought, with six chairs round it. Room for all of them, and a pie in the middle.

Tamsin came up beside her and bent to read the card in the window, with her hands on her knees. "To let," she read aloud. "Apply to the letting agent, Coin Street." She straightened, and squinted along the canal, as if the agent might be standing there. "Coin Street's up past the counting houses," she said. "By the bank. Where all the men in good coats go to count other people's money." She looked back at the house, up at its three narrow storeys and the empty window boxes, and then at Elsie, who still had her nose to the glass. "Elsie," she said. "Elsie. What are you thinking?"

Elsie turned round. Her face was shining. "This is it," she said. "This is what I meant. At the Poppy, yesterday, when I said I wished we all lived under one roof, and everyone went quiet." She waved at the blue door, the window boxes, the garden. "All of us. Under this roof. You and me and Tilly. And Cora, if she'd come. A room each, and that kitchen, and breakfast together every morning at that long table. Like on the Marigold." She put her hand flat on the blue door, as if to feel whether it was warm. "Tamsin, look at it. It's waiting."

Tamsin looked at the house. She looked at it for a long time, at the blue door and the fish knocker and the roses spilling over the wall, and Elsie saw something go across her face that was not a grin at all, something soft and hungry and almost afraid; and then Tamsin looked at Elsie, standing there with her hand on the door, and the look stayed. Then she shook her head, slowly, and let her breath out. "Elsie," she said. "We can't pay for that. Look at it. Three floors, on a canal, with a garden. That's money. That's real money. We're F-rank. We get paid in goats."

"Not yet," Elsie said. She said it quite simply, as if it were obvious. "We can't pay for it yet." She took the little blue notebook out of her satchel, and the pencil, and opened it on her palm against the door, and turned past the page of names to a clean one. At the top of it she wrote, in her careful round hand: Willow Canal. The blue door. Underneath: Coin Street, the letting agent. She looked at it, and underlined the blue door twice. Then she shut the notebook and put it away, and took Tamsin's hand again, and they walked on. Elsie looked back at the house until they turned the corner.

At the guild hall the morning crowd was in, loud and jostling round the boards, and Tilly was waiting for them by the F-rank end of the quest board, bouncing on her toes. She had a smear of soot right down the bridge of her nose, as if she had leaned too close over something that had gone up, and her goggles were pushed up on her head with soot on the glass too. She saw them come in hand in hand. Her eyes went to their hands for a moment, quick, and then up to Elsie's face, and she waved, a big wave with both arms. "You're late," she said. "I've been here hours. Well. Half an hour."

"Tilly," Elsie said, and caught both her hands. "Tilly, listen. We found a house." And she told her, all in one breath, without stopping: the canal with the willows, and the narrow old house, three floors, and the card in the window, and the blue door, blue like the sky, with a fish for a knocker, and the window boxes, and the kitchen with room for a long table, and the garden with roses and an apple tree, and Coin Street, and all of them living there together. Tilly's mouth fell open. Her whole face lit up as Elsie went, brighter and brighter, until by the end she was bouncing again.

"Would there be room," Tilly said, "for a still?" She said it quickly, and then went pink and rushed on. "Only a small one. A copper one. I've got the copper, I've been saving it, it's under my bed, but my landlady says if I set it up in my room she'll put me out in the street, because of the smell, and the last time, with the window." She looked at Elsie, pleading. "It wouldn't have to be in the house. It could be anywhere." Elsie squeezed her hands. "There's a whole shed in the garden," she said. "I saw the roof of it over the wall. A whole shed, Tilly. You could blow it up as often as you like."

The charcoal-painted door opened across the hall, and Cora came out, ledger under her arm, buttoned to the throat, her black hair drawn back so smooth and tight it shone. She came straight across to them through the crowd and took her place a step behind Elsie. And in the buttonhole of her charcoal coat, at the throat, there was a sprig of lavender. It had dried in the night. It was paler than it had been, and the flower heads had gone a little grey and papery, and it was slightly bent; but it was there, tucked in, exactly where she had put it at the guild hall door the evening before.

Elsie saw it, and her whole face broke open. "Oh, Cora," she said. "You kept it. The lavender. You've still got it in." She beamed at the little grey sprig, and then up at Cora's face. "It's dried beautifully. They do, lavender, they keep their smell for months. You can put it in a drawer after, with your shirts." Cora did not look at her. She looked at the quest board. She looked at it very hard, with her grey eyes fixed on a notice about a sewer drain on the C-rank board, far over Elsie's head, and her jaw was set, and a faint pink came up along the line of her cheekbone and stayed there.

Tamsin had turned back to the F-rank board. "Moonbells are gone," she said. "Cat's gone, we did the cat. Flower cart's gone, we did that. Here." She put her finger on a notice low down in the corner, and Elsie bent to read it. Glowcaps, from the Underbloom, for a dyer on Vat Street, four lanterns. Glowcaps grow in damp corners of the first floor, the notice said, in a small careful hand. Pick them whole, caps and stalks, a full sack. Deliver to the dyer by noon for the day's vats. There was a little drawing in the corner of a mushroom with lines coming off it, to show that it glowed.

Tilly had come up to read it over Elsie's shoulder. At the word Underbloom her face went white. Elsie felt her go still against her arm, and heard her breath stop. The soot on her nose stood out dark against her skin. For a moment she did not say anything at all. Then she swallowed, and lifted her chin, and Elsie saw her make herself do it. "I went down for the goat," Tilly said. Her voice shook, but she said it. "Didn't I? I went all the way down, and all the way to the cistern, and I came back up. I did it once." She swallowed again. "So I can do it again."

Elsie turned and looked at her, close, and took her by both shoulders. "Are you sure?" she said gently. "Tilly. You don't have to. We can find another notice. There'll be one tomorrow, or the day after, up in the light." Tilly shook her head, so hard her braids swung. "I'm sure," she said. "I want the four lanterns. For the house. For the shed." She looked down at her boots, and her voice went small. "Only, if you'd hold my hand. On the stair. All the way down, like last time. And not let go till the flowers." "All the way down," Elsie said. "I won't let go once."

They took the notice to the long desk. The clerk entered the party in his big book: the glowcaps, the Underbloom, first floor, the Fairweather party, three names and the hour. He wrote Warden beside Cora's name, as the clerks did, and blotted it, and pushed the notice back across the desk with the stamp still wet. Beside them Cora wrote it in her ledger at the same time, in her small exact hand, the date and the place and the names, one under another; and Elsie, watching the pen, saw that the lavender in Cora's buttonhole trembled very slightly with each stroke.

The old flower market was full of morning, light pouring down through the iron and glass of its high roof onto rows of stalls heaped with flowers, and the dry fountain stood in the middle of it as before, with its cracked stone lily and the dark mouth of the stair going down. The clerk sat at the stair head on his stool, the same thin young man with the pen behind his ear, and he knew them. "Fairweather party," he said. "Again. No goat today?" He wrote them in, with the hour. "Licence is a lantern." Tamsin dug in her pocket and slapped one silver lantern down on his ledger.

Elsie stepped over the rim of the dry basin and turned at the top of the stair and held out her hand behind her, as she had before. Tilly took it. Her fingers were ice cold. They were so cold that Elsie gasped a little at the touch, and they gripped at once, hard, the bones pressing, as they had the first time; and Tilly's other hand had gone to her belt, to the vials, and was holding one of them very tight. Elsie squeezed back. "All the way down," she said. "I've got you." Tilly nodded. She was looking at the dark mouth of the stair, and her lips were pressed white.

They went down. Elsie hummed. She hummed the caravan song from the barge, low and steady, step after step, round and down and round, so that it went ahead of them into the dark and came back off the walls; and Tilly, beside her and a step behind, counted the steps out loud, under her breath, in a thin voice: "One. Two. Three. Four." The light from the glass roof went grey behind them and then went away. The walls turned from cut stone to old red brick. "Twenty-two. Twenty-three." Elsie hummed. Tilly counted. Their joined hands were slippery, and both of them held on.

And then the stair opened out, and the first floor was there, the same as before: the low brick cellars going away one into another through their arches, and every wall and every vault grown over with pale flowers, small and soft and many-petalled, white and faint blue and the palest pink, and every one of them glowing. The light was soft and even, like moonlight through mist, and the air smelled of damp stone and something sweet. Tilly stopped counting. She stood at the foot of the stair and looked up at the glowing ceiling, and let out a long, long breath, and her shoulders came down from round her ears.

"Damp corners," Tamsin said, reading off the notice. "Glowcaps grow in damp corners. So we want wet." She looked round the cellar, and pointed at the nearest wall, where a dark stain ran down the old brick from a crack in the vault above, and the flowers along it grew thicker and greener. "Drip-lines. See? Water comes down through the brick from the market. Follow the drips, find the damp." So they followed the drip-lines. They went from vault to vault with their eyes on the walls, tracing the dark wet streaks down the brick to the floor, Tilly still holding Elsie's hand, though her grip had eased.

In the third vault a bloom beetle was grazing along the foot of the wall: a great round domed thing as big as a cat, its shell green-black and polished, catching the glow of the flowers and throwing it back in rainbows. It moved along slowly, cropping the flowers with a soft steady crunching. Elsie stopped beside it. "Good morning," she said to it politely, and bent a little to see its face, which was small and blunt and busy, with feelers going. "Don't mind us. We're only after mushrooms. We won't touch your flowers." The beetle went on crunching. Tilly, behind her, made a sound that was half a laugh and half a whimper.

At the end of a long vault the drip-line ran down into a low alcove in the wall, an arched hollow the depth of a cupboard, where water dripped steadily from the brick above into a shallow pool on the floor, plink, plink, and the brick was black and slick with wet. And there they were. All round the edge of the pool and up the wet brick in clusters grew small round mushrooms on pale stalks, their caps a soft pale blue, and every one of them glowing: not white like the flowers but blue, a cool watery blue light like a lamp seen through deep water. "Oh," Elsie breathed. "Oh, look at them."

Tilly let go of Elsie's hand. She did it without seeming to notice, and crouched down by the pool on her heels, and reached out and touched one glowcap with her fingertip, very gently, and then she was picking them, quick and careful, pinching each one off low at the base of the stalk and laying it in the dyer's sack, and talking. "It'll be the stalks that matter, I bet you, the colour's in the gills but the fix is in the stalk, that's why she wants them whole. You'd want alum. Or iron, iron would darken it. I wonder if it glows after. On the cloth. I wonder if it glows in the dark."

Elsie crouched down beside her and picked too. The glowcaps were cool and slightly rubbery and came away with a small soft snap, and they lit her fingers blue as she held them. She laid them in the sack beside Tilly's. Tilly went on talking, about mordants and fixing and whether a glowing blue would fade in the sun; and all the time she talked and picked, her shoulder was pressed against Elsie's shoulder, warm, the whole length of her upper arm against Elsie's, and it stayed there. When Elsie reached for a cluster on the far side and leaned away, Tilly leaned with her, and their shoulders did not come apart.

Tamsin had been watching the walls. "There's more down there," she said, and nodded at a side passage that opened off the vault a few yards along, a low dark arch with a drip-line running into it. "Wetter, by the look. Smells of it." She hefted Margery off her shoulder. "I'll go and look. You two keep picking. I'll shout if there's a bed of them." "Mind the side passages," Elsie said, without looking up, because Tamsin had said it to her once. Tamsin laughed. "I know, I know. I'll mind." And she ducked under the arch with the axe and went in, following the drip-line, out of sight.

They heard it before they saw it: a rustle, quick and dry, and then a whip-crack, and then Tamsin's shout, sharp and furious and cut off. Elsie was on her feet. Through the low arch she could see, in the glow, the side passage and Tamsin in it, a few yards in, and something dark and glossy round her, round her legs and her arms, many strands of it, green-black, thick as rope, and as Elsie looked it pulled. It hauled. It whipped Tamsin up off her feet, axe and all, and swung her against the wall of the passage and held her there, a foot off the floor, wound tight. Stranglevine.

Tamsin roared. It was not a word; it was a great furious bellow that boomed off the vaults, and she strained against the vine with all of her, every muscle, her back arched and her arms wrenching, the cords standing out in her neck. Margery hung from her fist, pinned against her side, useless. Elsie saw the vine creak. She saw it give a little, a hair, and then, slowly, horribly, she saw it pull tighter in answer, the strands round Tamsin's ribs drawing in and sinking into her shirt, and Tamsin's roar broke off into a gasp, and her face went dark.

Elsie felt it. It came down the Tether all at once, a crushing round her own ribs, as if the vine were wound round her and not Tamsin: a terrible tight squeeze that drove the breath out of her and would not let her take it back. She doubled over with it. Her hand went to her own side. She could feel each strand, the bite of it, the pull, the way it drew tighter every time Tamsin heaved against it; and under the crushing, coming down the thread with it, she felt something else in Tamsin, something cold and wild, beating at the strands like a bird caught in a net.

Elsie ran. She did not decide to; she was simply running, toward the arch and the passage and Tamsin, with her hands out. And a hand caught her arm from behind and stopped her short, hard, so hard she was spun half round. It was Cora. Cora had her by the upper arm in a grip like a clamp, and her grey eyes were blazing. "No," Cora said. "Stop. Listen to me." She did not let go. "The vine takes whoever brushes it. Whoever. You go in there and touch it, it will have you too, and then I have two of you on a wall and no hands free." Her grip tightened. "Think."

Tilly was already at the arch. She had a vial out of her belt, a small one with a red wax stopper, and she was trying to pull the stopper, and she could not. Her hands were shaking. They were shaking so badly that the vial rattled against her belt buckle, and her fingers kept slipping off the wax, and she tried with her teeth and the glass clicked against them and she nearly dropped it. "I can't," she was saying, high and fast. "I can't, I can't get it, it won't, my hands won't, I can't—" Behind her in the passage Tamsin made a sound that was barely a sound at all.

Elsie pulled her arm out of Cora's grip, and Cora let her, and Elsie went to Tilly. She did not go to the vine. She took Tilly's two shaking hands in her own two hands, vial and all, and held them, firmly, wrapped round, and looked into Tilly's face. "Tilly," she said. "Look at me. Not at her. At me." Tilly's wide blue eyes came round to hers. "You can," Elsie said. "I know you can. I've seen you. Under the market with the goat, and in the Glasswood, and in the reeds. Your hands go steady when it matters. I know they do." She squeezed. "I know you can, Tilly."

And Tilly's hands went steady. Elsie felt it happen under her own: the shaking stopped, all at once, as if a string had been cut. Tilly drew her hands out of Elsie's. She pulled the red stopper with one smooth twist of her thumb, and stepped to the arch, and looked once along the floor of the passage to where the vine came up out of the brick in a thick black knot below Tamsin's boots; and she threw the vial, underarm, low and quick and dead on, and it burst against the root. Fire ran out of it. It ran along the vine's root in a bright licking line, and up into the strands.

The vine shrieked. It was a thin high whistling shriek, like green wood in a fire, and the strands writhed and smoked and blackened and sprang loose, all together, uncoiling from Tamsin's arms and legs and ribs like a fist opening. And Tamsin dropped. She fell straight down off the wall with Margery clanging on the brick, and Elsie was there, she had already moved, and caught her: Tamsin came down into Elsie's arms all at once, heavy and hot and gasping, and the weight of her took them both down to their knees on the floor of the passage, Elsie's arms locked round her, outside the scorched ring of the burning root.

For a moment Tamsin only breathed, in great dragging gulps, her face in Elsie's shoulder. Then she rolled off onto her back on the cold brick, and lay there with her arms flung out, and started to laugh. She laughed up at the glowing ceiling, wheezing and helpless, holding her ribs. "That's twice," she said. "That's twice now you've caught me. Once in the red, in the Glasswood, and now off a wall." She turned her head on the brick and looked at Elsie, kneeling beside her, and her green eyes were wet and shining. "I'm going to have to start falling on purpose," she said. "Just to see if you'll do it again."

"Hush," Elsie said. "Lie still." She knelt over her and began to check her, the way her mother had taught her, quick and careful: ribs first, her hands spread flat on Tamsin's sides over the thin shirt, pressing gently down one side and up the other, feeling for the give of a cracked one, watching her face for the flinch. Then her arms, wrist to shoulder, where the vine had bitten. Tamsin stopped laughing. She lay very still under Elsie's hands, so still that she seemed hardly to be breathing; and when Elsie looked up from her ribs to her face, Tamsin was watching her, and did not look away.

Cora was writing. She stood by the arch with the ledger open on her forearm, and wrote, steadily, a long line and then another, her pen moving fast. Elsie, sitting back on her heels with Tamsin's wrist still in her hand, looked up at her. "Is it skills used?" she asked. "Should it be? I didn't use anything. I don't think I did. I only talked to her." Cora finished the line. She looked up, and her grey eyes went past Elsie, to Tilly, standing by the burnt root. "No," she said. "It isn't you." She looked down at the page again. "It's Tilly."

Tilly was standing at the mouth of the arch with her hands held up in front of her face. She was looking at them. She turned them over, slowly, backs and then palms, the soot-smudged fingers, and held them out a little way from her as if they were a pair of gloves someone had handed her and she did not know whose. They were perfectly steady. Not a tremor in them. She stared at them with her mouth a little open and the soot still on her nose. "Look," she said, very quietly, to her own hands. "Look at them. They're not even shaking."

They filled the dyer's sack. Tamsin, on her feet again and rubbing her ribs, went back down the side passage with Margery and chopped the last of the blackened vine away from the wall, and beyond it, where the drip-line pooled in a hollow in the floor, the glowcaps grew thicker than in the alcove, a whole blue-glowing bed of them. They picked until the sack was full and bulging and lit blue from inside like a lantern. Tilly tied the neck. Then she picked it up in both arms, and hugged it to her chest, and would not let anyone else carry it, though it was nearly as big as she was.

They climbed the stair. Tilly went up it with the glowing sack in her arms and did not hold anyone's hand, and did not count; she went up talking about mordants, round and up and round, and Elsie walked behind her with her heart full. The red brick turned to cut stone. The grey light came, and then the gold, and they came up out of the dry fountain into the noise and the colour of the flower market, blinking. The clerk took his pen from behind his ear and wrote them out, and counted them, four, and looked at the glowing sack in Tilly's arms, and whistled.

Vat Street smelled like a giant's laundry: steam and wet wool and something sour and sharp, and the gutters ran with colour, red in one, yellow in the next, deep green past the corner. The dyer's yard was at the far end, under a lean-to roof hung with dripping lengths of cloth. The dyer herself was a tall stooping woman with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder and her hands and arms blue to the elbow, a deep blue, as if she had dipped them in the sky. She took the sack from Tilly and looked in it and grunted. Then she tipped a double handful of glowcaps straight into a great copper vat that was steaming over a fire.

The dye in the vat went blue as they watched, a pale glowing blue that brightened as it boiled, until it was the colour of the glowcaps themselves, shining up out of the copper into their faces. The dyer nodded, satisfied, and wiped her blue hands on her apron, and paid them on the spot: four silver lanterns, counted out into Elsie's palm. They shared them out on the edge of a trough. Elsie gave Tamsin a lantern and Tilly a lantern and kept one, and changed the last with the dyer for ten copper pips, and gave Tamsin three and kept three and gave Tilly three, and then put the odd one in Tilly's hand too.

"Come and see," the dyer said to Elsie, and took her by the elbow with a blue hand, and led her under the lean-to to a shelf at the back, out of the sun, where a bolt of cloth lay folded. She shook out a length of it. In the shade it glowed. It was a deep soft blue, like the sky an hour after sunset, and all through the weave there ran a faint cool light, as if the cloth had been dipped in moonlight and kept some. Elsie gasped. She put out her hand and touched it, and the light lay on her fingers. "Oh," she said. "Oh, it's like wearing the night."

At the Tipsy Heron, across the canal from the guild hall, they took a table by the window, and Elsie went to the counter before anyone else could and said four bowls of stew, please, with bread. "Four?" said Tamsin. "Four," Elsie said, and looked at Cora, who had sat down at the end of the bench with her own wrapped bread and cheese on the table in front of her. Elsie counted out one silver lantern and six copper pips onto the counter. When the bowls came she set one in front of Cora. Cora looked at it. Then she moved her own bread aside, and picked up the spoon.

Elsie could not wait. She had been holding it in all the way down the stair and back up and all through Vat Street, and now, with the stew steaming in front of them and all four of them at one table, it came out of her. "Listen," she said. "All of you. I want to ask you all something at once, so you all have to answer together." She put both hands flat on the table. "There's a house on Willow Canal. A narrow one, three floors, with a blue door. With a garden. And it's to let." She looked round at them, one face and then the next. "What if we took it? All of us? Together?"

Tamsin put her spoon down. "I've seen it," she said, to the others. "It's a good house." She pushed her bread round her bowl. "It'd cost more than all our purses put together, Elsie. You know that. More than everything in all our pockets, and the next month's, and the one after." Tilly had stopped eating. Her blue eyes had gone round and shining at the words three floors and garden, and now she said, quickly, before anyone else could: "I'd sleep in the shed. I wouldn't need a room. I'd sleep in the shed with the still, I'd like it, honestly, I sleep better near a still."

Elsie turned on the bench to Cora. Cora was eating her stew, slowly and neatly, with her ledger shut beside her bowl and the dried lavender in her buttonhole. "Cora," Elsie said. "Could a Warden live with her party? In the same house? Is that allowed?" Cora's spoon stopped. She set it down on the edge of the bowl, carefully, and looked at the table. "The Accord," she said, in her exact flat voice, "does not speak to it." She seemed to think that was the end of the matter. She picked up her spoon again. "It covers what an Enthraller may do. It doesn't say where her Warden lodges."

"So does that mean no?" Elsie asked. She asked it very seriously, leaning toward her, her stew going cold. Cora did not answer. She sat with the spoon in her hand and looked at her bowl, and Elsie waited, and Tamsin and Tilly waited, and the noise of the Heron went on round them, laughing and dice and somebody's dog. It went on a long while. Then Cora put the spoon down again. She looked up, not at Elsie but at the window, at the canal outside and the guild hall across it. "It means," she said, slowly, as if each word cost her something, "that it's allowed."

And she opened the ledger. She uncapped her pen and wrote, quickly, one line, bent over the page so that her shoulder half hid it. Tilly, beside her, leaned over to see. She craned her neck right round, quite openly, her braid swinging into her stew, trying to read the small exact hand upside down. Cora shut the ledger. She shut it with a snap, flat, on Tilly's nose almost, and laid her hand on top of it, and looked at Tilly with her grey eyes, level and cool, until Tilly sat back on the bench and went pink and wiped her braid off with her napkin, and said sorry, sorry, sorry.

Cora capped her pen. "I have my report to write," she said, and rose from the bench, and tucked the ledger under her arm. "For the guildmaster. On the Underbloom." She looked down at the four bowls, the four spoons, the empty one in front of her own place, and something in her face did a small thing that Elsie could not name. "Thank you for the stew, Fairweather." She inclined her head to the three of them, the small exact bow. Then she went out of the Heron and across the bridge to the guild hall, straight-backed in her charcoal coat, with the sprig of dried lavender at her throat.

Coin Street was up past the counting houses, as Tamsin had said: a wide clean street of tall stone fronts with brass plates by the doors, and men and women in good dark coats going in and out of them with papers under their arms, not looking at anybody. The letting agent's office was halfway along, a narrow door with a plate that said so, and up a flight of polished stairs. Elsie went in first, and Tilly came after her clutching her arm, and Tamsin came last, ducking under the lintel with Margery on her back and the vine's black scorch marks still on her shirt.

The agent was a plump man in a waistcoat with a gold watch chain across it, behind a big bare desk with only an inkstand on it. He listened to Elsie's question about the house on Willow Canal with the blue door, and opened a ledger, and ran his finger down a column. "Ah, yes," he said. "The Willow Canal house. Two suns a month, that one. And a month in hand before you set foot in it." He shut the ledger and looked at them over his spectacles: the halter, the soot, and, for rather a long time, Tamsin's axe. "That's four suns," he said, "to start. In gold."

Tilly let go of Elsie's arm. She began to count on her fingers, her lips moving, frowning, her soot-smeared nose wrinkled: suns into lanterns, lanterns into pips, a dyer's pay, three ways, and then again, folding down one finger and then the next and then starting over on the other hand. The agent watched her with his eyebrows up. At last she lowered her hands and looked up at Elsie, very solemn. "That's a great many glowcaps," she said. "That's a really great many glowcaps. That's every glowcap in the Underbloom, I think, and then we'd have to wait for them to grow back."

Elsie stepped up to the desk. "Please," she said. "Could you keep it for us? Only for a little while. Take the card out of the window, just till we've saved. We will save. We'll work hard. We've got a Berserker." The agent looked at her, and at Tamsin, and laughed: not unkindly, but as if she had said something very young. "My dear girl," he said. "I keep it for whoever comes up those stairs with the coin. Whoever comes first, with four suns in their hand, has it. That's the trade." He spread his plump hands on the bare desk. "Bring me the gold, and it's yours, blue door and all."

Outside on the clean grey pavement of Coin Street they stood in a row in the sun, with the men in good coats going past them. Tamsin had her arms folded, and her jaw was set, and she was looking up the street toward the counting houses with her green eyes narrowed. "Right," she said. "Bigger quests, then. We'll take bigger quests. E-rank, the three of us, as soon as they'll let us. No more goats." She looked round at Elsie, fierce. "Yes?" "Yes," Elsie said, at once. And Tilly, holding her own elbows tight, said, "Yes," and then, louder, so that a man in a good coat turned round: "Yes."

Tamsin held out her hand, palm down, in the middle of the three of them. Elsie put hers on top of it, at once, and Tilly put hers on top of Elsie's, small and sooty and steady. Three hands, stacked up together in the middle of Coin Street: one brown and freckled and scarred across the knuckles, one pale and slight in a fingerless glove, one small and black with soot. "Shake on it," Tamsin said. And they shook, all three hands at once, up and down, laughing, with the people in good coats going round them on either side like water round a rock.

"I'm going back to the hall," Tamsin said, when they had let go. "To look at the E-rank board. Properly. Every notice." She rubbed her ribs where the vine had been, and grinned, crooked. "I'll learn what's on it so I know what we're up against. Then I'll come and tell you." She squeezed Elsie's shoulder, and ruffled Tilly's braids so that Tilly squawked, and went off down Coin Street at a long loping stride, with Margery on her back and the people in good coats stepping out of her way, and turned toward the guild hall at the corner, and was gone.

Tilly walked Elsie home. They went along the canals in the afternoon sun, Tilly with her hands stuffed in her apron pocket and her braids swinging, and for once she was quiet; she walked along kicking a pebble, and Elsie let her. Then, halfway along Thimble Canal, Tilly said, not looking up from the pebble, in a small careful voice: "Did you mean it? About the shed?" She kicked the pebble. "You'd really give me the shed? For the still? You wouldn't mind the smell? Or the bangs? There might be bangs. There usually are bangs. I'm only asking. You don't have to have meant it."

"The shed," Elsie said, "and the room beside mine." She said it as if it were already settled, as if she could see it. "You'll have the room next to my room, so if there's a bang in the night I can come straight in and see you're all right. And we can knock on the wall to say goodnight." Tilly tripped. She caught her boot on the edge of the kerb and pitched forward, arms flailing, and Elsie caught her by the apron strings and hauled her back upright. Tilly stood on the pavement with her face scarlet from her collar to her goggles, and her mouth open, and could not seem to shut it.

At the Honeycomb's door, under the painted sign, Tilly turned round and threw both arms round Elsie's neck and hugged her, so hard and so sudden that Elsie staggered back against the doorframe. She hugged her for a long moment, her face pressed into Elsie's shoulder, her goggles digging into Elsie's ear. Then she let go all at once and went off down Thimble Canal at a run, braids flying, and Elsie heard her talking to herself as she went, fast and breathless, all the way to the bridge: "The room next to hers. The room next to hers. Knock on the wall. The room next to hers."

Up in the attic the afternoon sun came slanting in through the round window and lay across the floor in a warm gold oval. Elsie sat cross-legged on the bed with the notebook open on her knees, at the clean page where she had written Willow Canal and the blue door, and she began to draw. She drew the house: tall and narrow, three storeys, with the door and the fish knocker and the window boxes, all the windows. Then she drew it again beside, opened up like a doll's house, the front taken off, so she could see inside. A kitchen at the bottom with a long table. And rooms above it, one each: Tamsin's, Tilly's, her own.

She wrote the names in the rooms, in tiny letters. Tamsin, at the top, by the window looking out over the canal, because Tamsin liked to see out. Tilly, next to Elsie, with a door drawn in the wall between, and a little arrow out to the shed in the garden labelled still. And then, after a moment, she drew one more room, on the middle floor, small and square and quiet at the back over the garden, away from the street. She wrote in it, carefully, in her round hand: Cora. She looked at it for a long time, with the pencil against her lip, at the small square room with the name in it, and did not draw anything else.

She got up and went to the crate under the round window, and knelt by it, and touched Sunny's leaves. The little lemon tree had two green lemons on it now, small and hard and shining, and Elsie cupped one in her palm. "Sunny," she said, softly, so the others would not be jealous. "I think we might be moving. Not yet. But one day. Somewhere with more room." She stroked the leaf. "And there's a garden. A real one, outside, with an apple tree, and roses all over the wall. You'd have proper sun." She thought about it. "You might even go outside in the summer." Sunny's leaves trembled a little in the draught from the window.

The evening came on. Elsie sat by the window with her chin on her arms and felt for the thread, the way she did now, without thinking, the way a hand goes to a pocket to feel the coins there. Tamsin was at the end of it, across the city. She was busy, and loud: Elsie could feel the bustle of her, the restlessness, something like a crowd and a hurry and a great deal of talking. And then, all at once, in the middle of it, for a reason Elsie could not see, the feeling changed, and something came down the thread so warm and so soft and so fond that Elsie put her hand flat on her chest and smiled at the canal.

Below the round window the lamplighters' boats came by. The first one slid along Thimble Canal in the blue dusk with a woman standing in the stern, poling slow, and a second pole in her other hand with a little flame at the tip; and at each paper lantern on the towpath she stopped, and reached up with the flame, and the lantern caught and glowed, red or gold or white, and she poled on to the next. Elsie watched her come all the way along under the window, lantern by lantern, until the whole canal below was lit and shining, and the light was dancing on the water, and the boat had gone under the bridge.

She lit the candle and got out her paper and pen and wrote to her mother. Dear Mum, she wrote. I saw Tamsin's mother's barge off this morning, and gave her the last slice of honey cake, and she kissed me on both cheeks. Then we went down under the market for glowcaps, and Tilly saved Tamsin from a vine with her steady hands, and I caught Tamsin when she fell. And Mum, there is a house on Willow Canal with a blue door. She stopped, and held the pen still over the paper a long time. Then she wrote: I think I've found the place where I'll stay.

There were quick feet on the narrow stairs, taking them two at a time, and then a tap at the attic door, and before Elsie could get up it opened a crack and Tamsin's head came round it, curls everywhere, grinning. "Agnes let me in," she said. "She looked at me like I was a rat. Can I come in?" She came in without waiting. She had a whole fistful of papers, crumpled, written over in her big sprawling hand, and she held them up in the candlelight like a winning hand at cards. "E-rank notices," she said. "Copied out, the good ones. The clerk let me. I've got eleven. You've got to see them."

The only place to sit was the bed, so they lay on it, side by side on their stomachs, with the candle on the stool beside them and the notices spread out on the pillow in front of their two faces. The bed was very narrow. Tamsin's shoulder was pressed against Elsie's shoulder, and her hip against Elsie's hip, and her bare arm along Elsie's arm, warm, all the way down; and their heads were bent so close together over the papers that Tamsin's curls tickled Elsie's cheek. Tamsin read them out one by one, in a low eager voice, jabbing each with her finger. Wolves on the north road. A barrow on the downs. Bandits at a ford.

Halfway down the seventh notice Tamsin's voice began to slow. "River trolls," she read. "Under the old toll bridge at. At. Something. Taking. Taking sheep." The words got further apart. Her finger slid off the page. Elsie looked round. Tamsin had put her cheek down on Elsie's pillow, on top of the notice about the river trolls, and her eyes had closed, and her mouth had come a little open, and she was asleep: all at once, completely, the way a child goes, with the ink still on her fingers and the vine's scorch on her shirt and the long day written all over her face.

Elsie lay and looked at her for a while in the candlelight. Then, very carefully, so as not to wake her, she slid off the bed and drew the notices out from under Tamsin's cheek one by one, and stacked them on the stool, and pinched out the candle. She pulled the quilt up out from under Tamsin's legs and spread it over her, up to her shoulders. Then she lay down beside her, on what was left of the bed, on her side at the very edge, against Tamsin's warm back, the way Tamsin had lain at the edge of the bunk on the Marigold; and the bed creaked, and Tamsin did not stir.

The canal lanterns made gold shapes on the sloped ceiling. Elsie lay in the dark with her eyes open, listening to Tamsin breathe, slow and deep, and the bed creaking a little with each breath. After a while Tamsin stirred. She frowned in her sleep, and her lips moved, and she mumbled something thick and cross into the pillow: "Not the sheep. Leave the sheep. Troll. Get off the bridge, troll." Then, quite clearly and very sternly: "I've got an axe." Elsie clapped her hand over her mouth. It was no use. She shook with it, silently, and then not silently, and pressed her face into the pillow and laughed into it until her eyes ran.
