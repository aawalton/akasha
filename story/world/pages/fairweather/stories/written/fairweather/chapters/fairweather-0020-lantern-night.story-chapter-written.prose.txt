Elsie woke at first light. The round window over the bed had gone from grey to a thin clear gold, and the canal below was quiet but for one duck complaining somewhere under the bridge. She lay still under the sloping boards and worked it out. Yesterday had been the nineteenth. So today was the twentieth. And the twentieth was Midsummer.

"Midsummer." It came out small and croaky with sleep. The fluttery feeling was back at once, the moth in her stomach, only it seemed to have brought friends in the night. It wasn't turning over now. It was busy, all wings, as if it had a great deal to get done today and had better start.

She knelt up on the bed and shuffled along it to the round window. Moonpenny sat on the sill in her pot, with the canal light coming up gold behind her. The bud was fatter than yesterday. Elsie was sure of it. She bent close, until her nose nearly touched it. At the very tip, where the green folded shut, a thin line of white showed through, no wider than a thread.

"Happy Midsummer, Moonpenny." She touched the pot, not the bud. "You don't have to rush. Not on my account. I know it's the night and everything, but you open when you're ready." She looked at the white line a moment longer. "Only if you happened to be ready tonight, I wouldn't mind."

The satchel stood by the attic door where she had left it, both buckles done. She knew the parcel was in it. She had put it there herself. She got off the bed anyway, undid the buckles and looked. There it was, on top, brown paper and string, the little lantern drawn on it in pencil. She did the buckles up again and patted the flap.

Then the shawl. She knelt by the plant crate and dug. Mrs Pennywhistle's fronds tickled her wrist. Sunny's pot was in the way and had to be lifted out and set on the floor. Right at the bottom, under Captain, she found it: a soft blue roll, squashed flat, with a few of Captain's leaves stuck to it. She pulled it out and shook it, and the whole attic smelled of mint.

It was her mother's old blue shawl, faded nearly to grey along the folds. Elsie shook it out to its full width and held it up against the window. It was a good shawl. It was warm and soft and long enough to tie in front. It didn't have any sleeves at all. She looked at it for a while, and sighed. "Cora's going to say something, Moonpenny."

She held it to her face and breathed in. Mint first, green and sharp, from Captain. Underneath the mint, if she kept still and breathed slowly, there was something else, very faint. It was dust on a road in the sun, and wood smoke, and willow-bark tea, and her mother's hair. She knelt there by the window with her face in the shawl for a long moment, breathing.

Downstairs the shop was warm and the first loaves were out on the racks. On the long counter the honey cakes sat in their rows under a white cloth, rested overnight, the way they had to be. Over the shop door someone had hung a ring of birch, green leaves bound round a willow hoop with a red ribbon, and it turned a little in the draught from the street.

"Shop shuts at noon," Mrs Merriweather said from the oven. "Midsummer. Same as every year."

She came over wiping her hands on her apron, reached under the counter and brought out a tin, a big round one with a dented lid and a painted bee on it, mostly worn off. She set it down by the cakes. "For the boat. Bring it back."

Elsie counted the cakes into the tin one by one, in rings, the way they fitted best. Mrs Merriweather stood and watched her do it. When the tin was full she reached past Elsie's hand, took the cake off the very top and ate it. She ate it in three bites, standing there, looking at the wall. She swallowed. She grunted.

Elsie held the lid over the tin and looked at her. "Does that grunt mean good? It sounded like a good one. It was lower than the honey one."

Mrs Merriweather brushed the crumbs off her front. "It means mind the boat." She went back to the oven.

Elsie put the lid on the tin. She was smiling all the same.

At seven Brightwater Square was hung with birch rings. They hung from the lamp posts and the shop signs and over the doors of the tea houses, green and fresh, with ribbons of red and yellow and blue turning in the breeze off the canal. The pigeons sat in them. Cora stood at the top of the guild hall steps, under the biggest ring of all, with her ledger.

Elsie ran. She ran across the square and up the steps two at a time, with the satchel bumping and the tin clanking inside it. "Happy Midsummer, Cora!"

Cora inclined her head. "Fairweather." Then she stopped, a short pause, as though she were checking something against a list. "And to you."

Cora's eyes went down. They went to Elsie's shoulders, which were bare, the halter's ties and nothing else, and stayed there a moment longer than looking took. Her mouth went tight at the corners. "Have you," she asked the left shoulder in particular, "found something with sleeves?"

Elsie held up the blue bundle from under her arm. "I found a shawl. My mother's. It hasn't got any sleeves." She beamed. "I knew you'd ask. I said so to Moonpenny this morning. I said, Cora's going to ask about the sleeves. And you did. Very nearly the first thing."

"A shawl," Cora said, "is not sleeves." She opened her ledger on her arm, uncapped her pen and wrote a line, quick and small. Elsie leaned over to see, the way she did. Cora tilted the page away from her. She tilted it only a little, but enough, and went on writing with it tilted.

"The hall shuts at noon," Cora said, still writing. "For the festival. I lunch at my grandmother's." She capped her pen. "I shall be at the Steps at dusk." Elsie knew she would be there at dusk exactly, not a minute either side.

"Give your grandmother my love. Please. And happy Midsummer from me."

Cora didn't answer straight away. She looked at Elsie's face for a moment, quite still, with the ledger shut against her coat. "I will."

Inside, the hall was bright and half empty. Elsie looked across at the corner, already smiling, the way she did every morning. Noor's table was empty. The ring of empty tables was there round it, the bare boards and the chairs pushed in, and in the middle of it all there was nobody. Only the table, and the light from the high windows lying across it.

Her stomach dropped. It went right down into her boots. Elsie stood looking at the empty chair. Then she went to the counter by the stair anyway and asked for a black coffee, strong, nothing in it, and counted out two pips. She carried it across into the ring in both hands, set it at Noor's place, sat down across from it, and waited.

At half past seven the doors went. Noor came in, in her long green coat, fast, with her head down. Halfway across the hall she looked up and saw the table. She stopped. She stood quite still on the bare boards and looked at the cup sitting at her place, its steam still going up in a thin line.

She came and sat. She put her gloved hands round the cup. "I overslept."

Elsie looked at her face. There were shadows under the kohl, darker than the kohl.

"I didn't sleep at all," Noor told the coffee, "so I overslept. It can be done. I've done it."

"I hardly slept either. For the flutter. I've had it since yesterday, right here." Elsie put her hand on her middle. "Like a moth."

Noor looked at the hand. "Mine isn't a flutter." She turned the cup a little way on its saucer. "Mine's a stone. A big one. It just sits."

"If the boat sinks, you're not to come in after me."

Elsie was shaking her head before she had finished. "I will, though. I'll come straight in. I can't swim very well. But I'll come anyway."

"I mean it." Noor's dark eyes came up off the cup.

"So do I. And I can't lie. You know I can't. So mine counts for more than yours." Elsie nodded. "That's only fair."

Noor's mouth twitched. It went at one corner, quick, and was gone. She picked up the coffee and drank it. She drank all of it, steadily, without setting it down, until the cup was empty and only the dark grounds were left in the bottom. She put it back on the saucer with a click.

"It's tomorrow now," Elsie said. "You said to ask you tomorrow. About the share." She leaned on her folded arms. "So. Will you take one?"

Noor looked into the grounds at the bottom of the cup. "Ask me on the water."

At eight Tilly came in with a canvas bag over her shoulder that clinked at every step. Her eyes were pink at the rims and her goggles sat crooked in her hair. She put the bag down on the table with a clank that made Noor's cup jump. "Twelve. Twelve bobbers now. I brewed half the night. Ines fell asleep in her chair."

Tamsin swung in from the docks side behind her, with Margery on her back and her curls tied up anyhow. She dropped into a chair. "Left Mum's letter. At the wharf office. All about the house." She grinned all over her face. "And the clerk says the Marigold's due back on the twenty-second. The twenty-second!"

She leaned over and knocked twice on Noor's table with her knuckles, rap, rap. "For luck." The empty cup rocked on its saucer. It rocked one way and then the other, and settled. It stayed put. It sat there upright on the saucer, with its dark ring of grounds in the bottom.

Everyone looked at the cup. Tilly looked at it with her mouth open. Noor looked at it hardest of all, with her brows drawn down. Tamsin started to laugh. "Look at that. Even the cup's on the party now." She slapped the table again, more gently, and the cup stayed put again.

When she had done laughing she said, "Odile wants the guard at the Lantern Steps at dusk. I went past on the way. She's got the boats all in a row." She hooked her thumbs in her belt. "And I'll bring the supper. Pies. Don't anybody else bring pies."

"Then we must all sleep this afternoon," Elsie said. "For the night. Properly. Lying down." She looked round the table at them. "We'll be up till dawn, and you can't sleep in a boat." Tilly yawned. She yawned so wide that her goggles slid down off her hair onto her nose, and she had to push them back up with one finger.

Tilly opened the canvas bag. Inside lay the bobbers, a dozen fat green vials packed in straw. She held the bag open to Noor. "Pick one. Any one. Whichever you like the look of."

Noor didn't look in the bag. She patted the pocket of her long coat, once, flat, with her gloved hand. "I've got mine."

At nine they parted on the guild hall steps. Noor went off toward Wick Lane to sleep, and Tilly toward Pickle Row, yawning as she went. Elsie and Tamsin went the other way, down to the waterfront, where music had already started. The Midsummer fair had sprung up overnight all along the top of the Lantern Steps.

The waterfront was all stalls and ribbons. There were stalls of sugared nuts and stalls of paper lanterns and a stall frying fish, smoking. Ribbons were strung between the lamp posts. A woman on stilts, taller than the stall roofs, walked through the crowd selling tin whistles out of a bag at her hip, and a tail of children followed her, whistling.

One stall sold birch crowns. They hung on pegs all over it, rings of green birch leaves with ribbons tied on, red and blue and gold, trailing. Tamsin stopped dead in front of it. Elsie walked straight into her back. Tamsin didn't seem to notice. She was looking at the crowns.

"Mum made me one. Every Midsummer. On the barge." She was still looking at the pegs. "Off the birches by the lock. Till I was twelve." She shrugged one shoulder. "Then I was too proud. Told her they were for babies." She made a face at herself. "I was twelve."

Elsie bought two crowns, a pip each, and the stallwoman lifted them down off their pegs. Elsie took the one with red ribbons. She reached up quick, before Tamsin could duck, and put it on Tamsin's head, right on top of the copper curls, and pressed it down so it sat.

Tamsin went red. She went red from her collar to her ears all at once, under the freckles. "I look a fool." Her hand came up toward the crown and stopped in the air.

"You look like Midsummer." The green leaves sat in the copper curls as if they had grown there.

Tamsin took the other crown out of Elsie's hand. It had blue ribbons. She lifted it and set it on Elsie's pink hair, very carefully, slowly, and worked it down round the flower clip with her fingers, so the clip wasn't squashed and the leaves sat even all the way round. She frowned at it while she did it, the way she frowned at Margery's edge on the whetstone.

Then her hands stayed. They stayed where they were, on either side of Elsie's head, holding the crown, though the crown was on. Tamsin's green eyes came down from the leaves to Elsie's face, and down again, to her mouth. They stayed there. Elsie could feel the warmth of her palms beside her ears. Then Tamsin let go.

"We match now!" Elsie touched her own crown and then reached up and touched Tamsin's.

Tamsin cleared her throat. "That's what a crown's for." She turned round and walked on along the stalls, fast, so fast that Elsie had to skip to catch her up.

At the next stall Tamsin bought two cups of cold lemon water, a pip each, and handed one to Elsie without looking at her. They sat on the low wall over the Lantern Steps, where the stone was warm from the sun, with their boots hanging down. The lemon water was cold and sour and very good.

Below them, at the bottom step, eleven sat on the water among the other boats, green with her white line. Elsie waved at her. Tamsin turned her cup round in her hands. "Can't sleep in the day. Not in a room. I'll lie there all afternoon looking at the ceiling."

She was watching the water. "On the barge I could. Any time. Middle of the day, with the sun on the cabin roof. You'd lie down in your bunk and the water'd be moving under you, and you'd be gone." She drank. "Rooms don't move."

Elsie looked up at her. "Then come and nap in the attic. This afternoon. It's right over the canal. The window opens on it. The water's just under it all the time, going by." She thought about it. "The attic doesn't move. But the water does. You can hear it from the bed. You could pretend."

Tamsin choked on her lemon water. She coughed into her fist, hard, and thumped her own chest. When she could talk she didn't look at Elsie. She looked out at the Mere. "All right." Her ears had gone red again, both of them, under the birch leaves.

At noon Mrs Merriweather pulled down the blind and turned the sign, and the Honeycomb shut. Elsie and Tamsin climbed the narrow stairs to the attic. Tamsin had to duck at the top, and duck again under the slope of the roof. She unslung Margery and leaned her by the door, carefully, edge to the wall.

Elsie went straight to the round window and pushed it open on its hinge. The canal air came in, cool off the water, smelling of weed and wet stone and, faintly, of the birch rings hung along the walk below. Somebody's punt went by under the window, the pole going in and coming up.

Tamsin stood in the middle of the attic with her head bent under the boards and looked at the bed. It was narrow. It was a good deal narrower than her bunk on the Marigold. "I'll take the floor. Floor's fine. I've slept on worse."

Elsie looked at the floorboards. "The floor has splinters. I've had three."

Elsie pulled off her boots, set her crown on the sill by Moonpenny, and lay down on the bed right over against the wall, to leave the room. She patted the blanket beside her. Tamsin stood and looked at the patted place. She stood there a long moment. Then she sat down on the edge of the bed, unlaced her boots, and lay down.

She lay on the very edge. She lay on her back, straight as a plank, with her arms crossed on her chest and her eyes on the boards above. Half of her was over the side. Elsie could see daylight under her shoulder.

"You'll fall off like that." Elsie took hold of the jerkin at the side, by the buckle, and tugged. Tamsin came, stiffly, a little at a time, sliding along the blanket, until their shoulders touched. Elsie let go of the jerkin. Tamsin's shoulder against hers was warm and very hard.

Elsie turned on her side toward her. She tucked her head in under Tamsin's chin, the way she had in the bunk on the Marigold, fitted herself in along Tamsin's side, and laid her hand flat on the jerkin, over the middle of it. It was a good place. She had remembered that it was.

Tamsin's heart was going under her cheek. It went hard and fast, thump-thump, thump-thump, right up against Elsie's ear through the leather. Elsie lay and listened to it. "Your heart's going like a drum. Like the one at the fair."

"It's the stairs," Tamsin told the ceiling.

Elsie thought about it. "It was two flights. You carry Margery up Tanner's Row and don't even puff."

There was a pause. "Shut up and sleep."

Elsie laughed. She laughed into Tamsin's collarbone, where the jerkin ended and the skin began, warm and freckled, and shut her eyes. After a moment, slowly, Tamsin's arms came uncrossed. One of them came round Elsie's back, the whole long heavy weight of it, and settled there.

Tamsin's palm came to rest on Elsie's bare back, between the laces of the halter. It lay flat, warm and a little rough, and stayed. Elsie felt it there, and the heart under her ear going a little slower now, and the water going by under the open window. She fell asleep like that.

When Elsie woke, the light in the round window had gone long and gold, and a bell somewhere across the roofs was striking five. She was in the same place, tucked under Tamsin's chin. Tamsin was awake. She was lying on her back, holding her, with her green eyes open, looking up at the sloping boards.

"I slept," Tamsin said, without looking down. "A bit." Her hand was where it had been. It lay flat on Elsie's back between the laces, exactly where it had been when Elsie went to sleep. It hadn't moved.

"You snored." Elsie yawned. "Like a kettle. A little one."

Tamsin's chest moved under her cheek. "That was you."

Elsie lifted her head. "It wasn't. I'd have heard me."

Tamsin looked down at her then. "You've got a crease. On your cheek. From the jerkin." She lifted her other hand and ran her thumb along it, from the corner of Elsie's eye down toward her mouth, following the line the leather had pressed there. Her thumb was rough and warm. She did it very slowly. Elsie lay still and let her.

Tamsin sat up fast. She sat up so fast that the bed creaked and Elsie rolled into the warm place where she had been. "Pies." She swung her legs off and pulled on her boots, laced them wrong, and didn't stop to lace them again. She took up Margery. "See you at the Steps." She was gone down the stairs.

Elsie knelt up at the round window. Below, Tamsin came out of the shop door and went off along the walk beside Thimble Canal, fast, with Margery on her back. She still had her birch crown on. Its red ribbons went bobbing along over the heads on the walk all the way to the bridge.

Elsie looked down at Moonpenny. The bud had opened a little in the warm afternoon. The white at its tip had grown. It was a finger's width now, pale and soft as the inside of a shell, still folded, still waiting. Elsie held her breath at it.

She tied the blue shawl round her shoulders and knotted it in front, over the halter. She took her birch crown off the sill and put it back on, fitting it round the clip with her fingers the way Tamsin had done. She shouldered the satchel, with the parcel in it and the bee tin.

She sat on the edge of the bed a moment longer, took out her notebook and opened it at the Midsummer page. There was the little boat, the lantern on its pole, the heads in a row. Under the boat, in her round hand, she wrote: ring first, think after. She underlined it once.

At quarter past seven the Lantern Steps were crowded from top to bottom. Folk stood and sat on every step holding paper lanterns, unlit, on sticks and strings: red ones, gold ones, white ones painted with fish. Everyone was waiting. The sun hung low over the Mere and the water had gone the colour of honey.

Below, at the foot of the steps, the little boats were pushing off. They went by the hundred, families in them and couples and old men alone, each with a lantern lit at the bow. The lights went out over the water, spreading, as though someone had spilt a bag of sparks on the Mere.

Halfway down the steps Ines sat in a ring of bare stone. The folk round her had left a space, a step above and a step below and an arm's length either side, and she sat in the middle of it, small, in her black lace, with Biscuit on her shoulder, his white bones bright against the black.

A stall at the top of the steps was selling hot milk with honey out of a big copper pan. Elsie bought a cup for two pips and carried it down through the crowd with both hands round it, stepping over legs and lanterns and saying sorry, sorry, all the way down.

She gave it to Ines. Ines looked at the cup, and then up at Elsie. She took it in both her small hands and held it under her chin, where the steam came up into her face. "It's my favourite," she whispered. "Hot milk with honey."

Elsie sat down beside her in the ring of bare stone, close, shawl against lace. The folk on the step below gathered up their lanterns and shifted further off. Elsie turned and waved at them, a big friendly wave. They stared. One of them stared at her crown.

Tilly came down the steps with her clinking bag, rested and pink, her braids done up fresh. She stepped into the ring as if it weren't there and sat down on Ines's other side, so that Ines was in the middle. "I slept five hours. Five! I feel like a new pot."

Tamsin came down after her with a covered basket on her arm. The smell came first, pepper and gravy and hot crust, and heads turned after it. Tamsin set the basket on the step. "Pies. Five pips' worth. Worth every one." She lifted the corner of the cloth to show them, and let it fall.

At dusk, as the last of the sun went down into the Mere, Cora came down the steps. She came then exactly, as she had said she would, straight-backed through the crowd, with her brass lantern hanging at her belt and folk stepping aside for her coat.

She stopped in front of Elsie and looked at the shawl. She looked at it from the knot down to the ends and back up. "It has no sleeves."

"I said so. This morning. I told you."

Cora shut her eyes. She stood there with them shut for a moment, like someone counting to ten.

Then she opened them, and they went up to Elsie's hair, to the birch crown with its blue ribbons sitting round the clip. Cora looked at it a long moment. Her face didn't change at all. She didn't say anything about it. She turned and looked out at the water.

The Mere went violet. The little boats were out over all of it now, hundreds of lights, and the festival barge sat far out in the middle under its lanterns. Elsie stood up and watched the waterfront, the long wall along the top of the steps, for a long green coat. It wasn't there. Not yet.

At the bottom step Odile Marsh stood with her slate and called the guard boats, one number after another, in a voice that carried over the whole crowd. One. Two. Three. Boats pushed off. Elsie stood on her toes on the step and looked along the wall, looking, looking.

Noor came along the waterfront as the last light went. She came with her hands in her coat pockets, walking straight, not fast. The crowd along the wall stepped out of her way as she came, and closed up again behind her.

She stopped on the top step. She looked down over all the heads to the bottom step, to eleven sitting on the black water. She looked at it for a while. Then she looked at Elsie. Then she came down.

"Eleven," Odile Marsh called. They gathered at the bottom step. Odile looked the five of them over and marked her slate. "Eleven rides at buoy eleven. Off the north reed beds. That's the darkest side." She tapped the bell under the middle seat with her chalk. "Ring first."

Ines had come down behind them to the bottom step, with Biscuit and her cup. "I'll look for the pink," she whispered.

Elsie touched her crown, and the leaves rustled. "I'll wave. When the fireworks go. I'll wave the whole time, so you'll see."

They got in. Tamsin went first, one step down into the boat without looking, steady as a post. Tilly went next, and wobbled, and sat down with a squeak and a clink. Elsie went third. The boat tipped under her and she sat down very hard on the middle seat, and laughed.

Cora stepped in without a wobble. She went past them all to the bow and sat down by the lantern pole with her knees together. She opened the little door of the hooded lantern and lit it from her own brass one. Its light went out forward over the water in a long white path.

Noor stood on the bottom step and looked at the boat. She looked at the strip of water between the step and the hull, black now. Elsie watched her breathe in. It was a long breath. Noor let it out, and stepped down into the stern.

The boat dipped under her. It dipped and rocked, and righted itself. It didn't tip. Noor sat down at the very back, on the little seat in the stern, as far from the lantern as a person could sit in that boat, and folded her long gloved hands in her lap.

The mooring rope held. The lantern burned. Elsie kept watching, and the rope and the lantern and the boat all stayed just as they were. Noor looked along the boat at the lantern in the bow for a long moment, at the white light burning steady. Then she looked away, out at the water.

Tamsin took the oars. Elsie sat down beside her on the middle seat and took one, the right one, and Tamsin let her have it. "Dip," Tamsin said. "Pull." They pulled together, an oar each, side by side, and eleven went out from the steps onto the Mere.

By half past eight they rode at buoy eleven, a painted float bobbing on its chain off the edge of the reeds. The reeds stood up black behind them, taller than a woman, rustling. In front of them the whole Mere was lit with boats, lights on every stretch of water as far as Elsie could see.

Far off in the middle of all the lights, the festival barge glowed. Its rails were strung with lanterns and its deck was covered by a great canopy of gold cloth, lit from beneath, so that the whole barge shone like a lamp floating on the water.

At ten, across the water, a woman began to sing. Her voice came over the Mere huge and clear, as if she were right beside them, and it filled all the dark. The whole Mere went quiet. Talk stopped in every boat. Oars stopped. There was only the voice.

"That's Celestine," Elsie said. "Amara's mother." She turned round on the seat, away from the barge, toward the reeds. "I'm not listening." She folded her hands in her lap. Beside her, Tamsin looked at her sideways and didn't say anything.

Tamsin pulled the cloth off the basket and handed round the pies, still warm, in their paper. Elsie opened the bee tin of honey cakes on her knees and passed it down the boat, to Tilly, to Cora in the bow, and back to Noor in the stern.

Noor took a cake out of the tin and looked at it. "I've had four of these already. This week." She ate it. She ate it in two bites, brushed the crumbs off her gloves, and passed the tin back along the boat.

Elsie turned on her seat to look at Noor. "We're on the water now. You said ask you on the water. So. Will you take a share?"

Noor looked back at her down the length of the boat. "Put it toward the house."

Tamsin choked on her pie. She coughed and coughed, and Tilly thumped her on the back. "That's not how a share goes," Tamsin said, when she could. "You get it. It's yours. That's the whole point of a—"

"It is now," Noor said. "That's how it goes now."

Noor looked out at the lights on the water. "I'd like to know where my coin went. For once. Somewhere with a door." She was quiet a moment. "Somewhere that stays where it is." She pulled her coat closer round her knees.

Elsie got up. She climbed back over the seat, past Tilly, with the boat rocking under her and the tin sliding off her knee, and threw her arms round Noor's neck and hugged her. Eleven rocked hard. Water slapped the hull. "The boat," Noor said, into the shawl.

Noor didn't hug back. Her arms stayed where they were, stiff, in her lap. But her gloved hand came up, the near one, and took hold of Elsie's wrist where it lay against the green coat. It held on tight. It held hard enough that Elsie could feel each long leather finger.

After a while Noor's hand let go, and Elsie climbed back to her place beside Tamsin, and the boat steadied. In the bow Cora sat by the lantern with her back to them. She was rubbing her hands together over the warm tin of the lantern hood, slowly, palm against palm.

It had turned cold. A wind had come round off the open water while they ate, and it went straight through the shawl. Elsie hadn't noticed till now. She looked at Cora's hands in the lantern light. The fingertips were white, all of them, white to the first knuckle. Cora stopped rubbing and tucked them under her arms, into the charcoal coat, and sat very straight.

Elsie opened her satchel. She took out the brown paper parcel with its string and its bow and the little lantern drawn on it, leaned forward over the middle seat, and held it out to Cora across Tilly's knees. Tilly leaned back out of the way, holding her pie up out of reach.

Cora turned her head and looked at the parcel. She looked at the lantern drawn on the paper for a long moment. Her hands stayed under her arms. She didn't take it. Elsie held it out further. "It's for your hands. That's what it's for. Your hands are cold."

Cora took it. Her hands came out from under her arms and took it, and she set it on her knee. She pulled the end of the bow. Her fingers were stiff and slow on the string, and it took her two tries to get the knot undone. She folded back the paper. The sprig of lavender fell out, into her lap.

She lifted the gloves out. She held them up in the lantern light, palm to palm, as Elsie had laid them, the long grey fingers matching. She didn't put them on. She didn't say anything. She held them and looked at them, and the lantern light came through the grey wool where it was thinnest, at the tips of the fingers.

"They're the grey of your eyes. Exactly. That's why I held the wool up to your face in the shop on Spindle Lane. I had to see which skein." She smiled. "You said you'd better not ask. So I didn't tell you. But that's what it was the right grey of."

Cora put them on. She put on the left one first, slowly, finger by finger, and then the right. They went on all the way. They fitted to the knuckle, the long grey fingers reaching right to the ends of Cora's long fingers, just as Elsie had measured them in her head over and over. Cora spread her hands in the light and flexed them, once, twice.

"Thank you, Fairweather," Cora said. Her voice broke on the name. It broke right in the middle of it, and she stopped. She turned round on the bow seat, away from them all, to face the reeds, with her gloved hands in her lap. Elsie could see her back, very straight, and the knot of her bun, and the lantern light on the nape of her neck.

After a while Cora's hands moved. She picked the lavender up out of her lap. She tucked it into the top buttonhole of her charcoal coat, the one up at her throat, where the harebell had been, and pressed it in with one grey fingertip so that it stayed.

Tilly was looking from the gloves to Elsie. She looked at the grey gloves on Cora's hands, and at Elsie, and back, and her mouth had gone into a small round o. She didn't say anything. She looked down at the pie in her hands and turned it round and round, and didn't eat it.

On the middle seat Tamsin shifted. She slid along toward Elsie until her hip was against Elsie's hip, warm through the skirt, and stayed there. She didn't say anything either. She was looking at Cora's back. Elsie leaned against her a little, glad of the warm.

Across the water Celestine's song came to its end. The last note went out over the Mere and held, and stopped. For a heartbeat the dark was quiet. Then the cheers came, from every boat at once, far and near, and little bells rang all over the water like rain on a roof.

At eleven the singing stopped. The cheering had died away and the bells had stopped, and all over the Mere the lantern boats hushed and waited for the next song. Oars lay still. Faces turned toward the barge. Elsie waited too, with her hands in her lap, and listened to the water slapping the hull and the reeds creaking behind them.

Elsie looked at the gold canopy, far off in the middle of the lights. She thought of Amara somewhere under it, in the gold gown made to match it, the one she had stood in before the long glass and not made a sound. Elsie pressed her hands together in her lap. She hoped Amara had somebody to stand beside.

Behind her, Noor spoke, low and sharp, nothing like her flat voice. "The reeds. Something moved. There."

Elsie turned. Noor was sitting forward in the stern, staring past them all at the black wall of reeds. "There's no lantern on it."

A long black punt slid out of the reeds. It came without a sound, low on the water, and Elsie could see four shapes in it, hunched, dark against the lights beyond. Their oars were wrapped in something. The oars went in and came up, and they didn't make a sound.

It glided away from them, out along the line of empty lantern boats. Those boats were tied nose to tail along a mooring line beyond buoy eleven, their lanterns lit, with nobody in them. The punt slid toward the end of the line, quick and smooth, like an eel going through weed.

Elsie didn't think. She bent and snatched the brass bell out from under the seat by its wooden handle, stood up in the rocking boat, and rang it. She rang it with all she had, her whole arm, over and over, as loud as it would go.

The bell clanged out over the water. It went out across the whole dark Mere, clang, clang, clang, and all across the water faces turned. Lanterns swung round. Elsie could see faces in boats far off, all turning north toward eleven. Then another bell answered, off to the right, and another, further off, and another.

The punt didn't run. Elsie kept ringing and watched it. Two of the empty lantern boats were already cut loose from the line and drifting, their lanterns swinging, and a tow line ran from them to the punt's stern. The four shapes bent to their oars, and the two boats came away after them.

Cora stood up in the bow. She reached for the lantern and threw back its hood, all the way. White light flooded out across the water and struck the punt full. There were four men in it. They flung up their arms against the light, all four, and turned their faces away.

Tamsin dug in her oars and rowed straight at them, hard. Eleven leapt forward. Elsie sat down with a thump, the bell in her lap. Tilly grabbed the side of the boat with both hands and hung on and shrieked, a long high shriek, as the spray came over the bow.

The thieves poled for the reeds with their prizes dragging behind them. One of them stood in the stern with a long pole, shoving off the bottom, hard, and the punt swung. Then the pole cracked. It cracked clean in two with a sound like a shot, and the man went staggering with half of it in his hands.

The tow line snapped. It went with a twang Elsie heard over her own bell, and the end of it whipped back across the water. The two lantern boats drifted free, turning slowly, their lanterns still lit. The punt slewed round sideways, without its pole, and ran into the reeds.

It stuck fast. It ran in among the stems with a hiss and stopped dead, its nose in the mud. The men in it swore. Eleven came on. One of the thieves stood up in the stuck punt, cornered, with the reeds at his back, and lifted a long iron boathook in both hands.

Tamsin shipped her oars. She came up off the seat in one movement with Margery off her back and up, in both hands. She stepped over the middle seat. She put herself in front of Elsie, between the end of the boathook and Elsie, with her boots planted wide and the boat steady under her.

Under Elsie's breastbone the thread to Tamsin went hot. It went hot all at once, like a coal blown on. Elsie felt it and knew what it was. In front of her Tamsin's breath had started to come short and hard, through her teeth, and her shoulders had gone up, and her hands on Margery's haft were white at the knuckles.

Elsie put her hand on Tamsin's back. She laid it flat between the shoulder blades, on the leather, and kept it there. "Tamsin. I'm here. I'm right here behind you. Stay here with me."

Tamsin's breathing slowed. Elsie felt it slow under her hand, one breath and then the next, each a little longer, as Tamsin came back along the thread to where Elsie was. The thread under her breastbone stayed warm but stopped burning. Tamsin didn't go red. She stood with Margery up and looked at the man with the boathook. "Drop it or swim."

Tilly let go of the side of the boat. She pulled a vial off her belt, a small corked one, and threw it overarm, hard, past Tamsin, into the water right beside the punt. Bang. Green fire leapt up out of the water in a flash, and the noise slapped back off the reeds.

The punt rolled over. The water heaved up under it and it went over sideways, slowly and then all at once, and all four thieves went into the black water among the reeds, with the boathook and the broken pole, in one great splash.

Three of them came up and found their feet. They waded off into the reeds up to their chests, cursing, shoving the stems aside, and the reeds closed behind them. The fourth didn't find his feet. He came up thrashing, out where it was deeper, and went under, and came up again with his mouth wide. He couldn't swim. Elsie could see that he couldn't.

Noor stood up in the stern. The boat swayed under her and Tilly grabbed the side again. Noor stayed standing. She put her gloved hand to the pocket of her long coat, the buttoned one, unbuttoned it, and took out Tilly's lucky vial.

She threw it. She threw it underarm, not hard, and it went in a little arc through the lantern light and landed in the water by the man's hand, and bobbed. Elsie was up on her knees on the seat. "Hold it round the middle! Round the middle! Don't panic!"

He grabbed it. He got one arm round it and then both, and it held him, and he came up with his face out of the water, gasping, clinging to the little green vial with both arms against his chest. His eyes were huge and white in the lantern light.

Odile Marsh's boat came rowing up out of the dark with three more guard boats behind it, their bells going. They came in round the edge of the reeds. Two of Odile's oarsmen leaned out over the side, got the man by the collar and the belt, and hauled him in, dripping, still hugging the vial.

Odile Marsh stood in her boat with her slate under her arm and looked about her. She looked at the two cut lantern boats drifting free. She looked at the punt, upside down in the reeds. She looked at the two halves of the pole floating, and at the end of the snapped tow line. Then she looked at Noor.

"That pole was new this spring," Odile said flatly.

Noor stood in the stern with her hands at her sides. "Things break near me," she said, just as flat.

They looked at each other across the water.

Odile Marsh grunted. "Then I want you near every thief on the Mere." She marked her slate. Her oarsmen roped the two drifting lantern boats to her stern. The wet man sat in her bow wrapped in a blanket. She turned her boat round and began to tow them all home toward the Steps.

Halfway out she turned in her seat and called back over the water. "Pay's at dawn. At the Steps." She lifted her chalk at them. "Ring first. That was good ringing." She turned back round, and her boat went away into the lights.

Noor sat down at the back of the boat. She sat down slowly. She looked along the boat at the lantern on its pole in the bow. It was burning. It burned white and steady, its hood still thrown back, just as it had burned all night.

"It didn't sink," Noor said, very quietly.

Elsie turned round on her seat. "No. It didn't."

Noor kept looking at the lantern. "It didn't sink."

Elsie reached back over the seat and took Noor's gloved hand. Noor's fingers closed round hers. They closed all the way this time, round the whole of Elsie's hand, and held on tight, and stayed.

In the bow Cora had sat down again with her ledger open on her knee. She was writing in it with her new grey gloves on, the pen in her grey fingers. Elsie leaned forward to see, the way she did. This time Cora didn't tilt the page. She held it still and let her look.

Under the date, in Cora's small exact hand, it said: Midsummer barge guard. Thieves, four. And under that, on a line of its own, the line Elsie looked for first: Skills used, none.

Tamsin sat down hard beside Elsie. She sat down so hard the seat jumped. She was trembling. Elsie could feel it through the seat and through her hip, all down her side. "I didn't go red," Tamsin told her own hands. "I didn't. Elsie, I didn't."

"I know. I felt it. Right here." Elsie touched her breastbone. "It went hot, and then it didn't." She leaned her head on Tamsin's shoulder, crown and all, and the birch leaves crackled against the jerkin. Tamsin's arm came round her. It came round and held on, and the trembling went on a while under Elsie's cheek, and slowly eased.

From the barge, small and shaky, a second voice began to sing. It came over the water thin as a thread, much smaller than Celestine's. It wobbled on the first note. It wobbled on the second. On the third it steadied, and held, and went on.

It was the barrow tune. Elsie knew it at once: the low wandering one that went up and down wherever it liked, like someone walking on the downs at night. The new voice sang two notes of it. Then Celestine's big voice came in under it with the next two, softly. Then the new voice again, two more. Back and forth.

Elsie's eyes filled. They filled right up and spilled over, and she couldn't stop them. She turned her face away from the barge, toward the reeds. "I'm not listening." Her voice shook. She was smiling so hard her face hurt.

Tilly leaned round Tamsin to look at her. "What aren't you listening to?"

Elsie wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, one and then the other. "Nothing. A bowl." She sniffed, and laughed, and wiped her eyes again. "I was washing a bowl."

At midnight the first firework went up. It went up from the barge's bow with a long rising whistle, a thin gold line climbing up and up into the black, and burst. Gold spilled out over the whole Mere, wide as the sky, and came drifting down in sparks, and every face on the water was lit gold.

Blue stars came after. They burst one after another, high up, a clear bright blue, each with a soft crack that came a moment after its light. Tilly shrieked. "That's Powder Lane blue-star! That's his! I'd know that blue anywhere! That's the very one he gave me a twist of!"

Tilly grabbed Elsie's arm. She grabbed it with both hands above the elbow and held on, and turned her face up to the sky. She held on through every blue one, squeezing at each burst, her mouth open, her goggles catching little blue stars of their own.

Elsie looked round the boat. Gold light, and then blue, and then gold again. Four faces turned up, lit and lit again. Tamsin, with her birch crown crooked. Tilly, with her mouth open. Cora in the bow, straight-backed. Noor in the stern. Her boat. All of them, in one small green boat on the Mere.

Noor was looking up. Her head was tipped back against the stern post and the gold light lay on her face. The sharp lines had gone out of it. One corner of her mouth had gone soft, the corner that twitched. Elsie looked at it and kept looking, in case it went.

Cora wasn't watching the sky. Elsie turned to show her a gold one and found Cora already looking, not at the fireworks but at her, at Elsie, with the gold light in her grey eyes. Their eyes met. Cora looked up at once, quickly, at the stars, and kept her chin up there.

"I love you," Elsie said, out loud, over the bangs. "All of you. I do." Tamsin coughed. She coughed into her fist and looked hard at the sky. Tilly squeezed her arm, tight, with both hands.

"I heard," Noor said from the stern.

At one they tied up at the Lantern Steps. Most of the crowd had gone home, and the steps were scattered with burnt-out lanterns and twists of paper from the sugared nuts. Ines was still there. She was asleep sitting up on a step halfway down, with her back against the step behind, and Biscuit curled in her lap.

Ines woke as they climbed out. She blinked up at them. "I saw the pink. When the gold ones went, I saw it." She looked at Elsie. "And I saw the bell. I heard it. Everyone did. Everyone on the steps turned round."

Noor sat down on the step beside Ines, close, coat against lace. Biscuit lifted his small white skull out of Ines's lap and looked at her. Then he got up and climbed out of Ines's lap, across the gap, into Noor's. Noor went still. She went still all over, the way she had on the Steps when Elsie leaned back against her knees.

Biscuit turned round three times on the green coat, treading it with his little bone feet, and lay down in Noor's lap in a curl. He began to purr. He purred with no throat to purr with, a soft rattle from somewhere in his ribs, and Elsie could hear it from where she stood.

Noor looked down at him. Slowly she lifted one gloved hand and laid it on his little white skull, very lightly. Biscuit pushed his head up into her palm. She kept her hand there. She left it on him, and he purred on.

Tamsin hoisted Tilly's bag up onto her own shoulder. Tilly was swaying where she stood, half asleep, her braids coming down. "I'll walk her to Pickle Row," Tamsin said. "Before she walks into a canal." She looked at Elsie. "See you at dawn. Here."

Tamsin reached out and straightened Elsie's birch crown. She did it with one finger, a little push at the side, so that it sat level again over the clip. She looked at it. Then she turned and went up the steps with Tilly, an arm round her, Tilly's head on her shoulder, the red ribbons of her own crown going up into the dark.

"I will walk Fairweather to Thimble Canal," Cora told the top of the steps. "It is on my way." Elsie looked at her. Wick Lane was the other way altogether. It wasn't on Cora's way at all, and Cora must know it wasn't. Elsie didn't say so.

Elsie bent and hugged Noor goodnight round the neck, Biscuit and all, the warm green coat and the little rattling bones between them. Noor stayed where she was and let her. "Dawn," Noor said, into the shawl.

Elsie and Cora walked along the dark canals. The paper lanterns along the walks were burning low now, red and orange, and some of them had gone out. The water lay black and still. The windows were all shut, the city asleep, and their boots were the only sound on the stones.

Cora walked with her gloved hands at her sides. Every few steps Elsie saw her look down at them. She looked at the right one, and then the left, and once she turned the right one over and looked at its palm. Then she looked ahead again and walked on.

"It's tomorrow now," Elsie said. "Or it's today, really. It's after midnight." She walked a few steps. "You said perhaps tomorrow. About why. Why you said don't do that again." She looked up at Cora's face in the low light. "Will you tell me? Like you said, perhaps?"

Cora stopped on the bridge over Thimble Canal. She stopped at the top of its hump, put her gloved hands on the stone parapet, and looked down at the water. She looked down a long time. Elsie stood beside her and waited. A lantern under the bridge guttered and went out.

"When you were out on the dome," Cora told the water, very low, "I didn't think of the Accord. I didn't think of my ledger. Or my report. Or the guildmaster." Her gloved hands were tight on the stone. "I didn't think of any of it."

"I had your ankle. In my hand. And I knew." She stopped, and began again. "I knew that if you went off the dome, I would go off it with you before I let go." Her knuckles had gone white inside the grey wool.

"That is why I said don't do that again." Her voice had gone down almost to nothing. "Because I couldn't bear it. If you went." She looked at the water. "Not the threads. Not the war. Not my grandmother's candles." She stopped. "That."

Elsie's eyes filled. They filled and ran over before she could do anything about it. "I understand. I'd hold on too. If it were you out on the dome, I'd hold on, and I wouldn't let go either." She wiped her eyes. "That's what friends do. Isn't it?"

Cora turned her head and looked at her. She looked at Elsie a long moment, there on the bridge in the light of the last lanterns. Her grey eyes went over Elsie's face as if she were reading it. "Yes," she said at last, very quietly. "That is what friends do."

Elsie hugged her. She stepped in and put her arms round Cora's middle, round the charcoal coat, and laid her cheek against Cora's chest, by the lavender in the buttonhole. Cora went still. Then her gloved hands came up. They came up slowly and held on to Elsie's back over the shawl, both of them, flat and then tight. They stood there on the bridge a long time.

Cora let go. She stepped back a pace and stood looking at Elsie, and touched the lavender at her throat with one grey fingertip. "Goodnight, Fairweather. Dawn." She turned and went back over the bridge the way they had come, straight-backed, toward Wick Lane.

Up in the attic the round window was full of the last low lantern light from the canal, red and dim. Elsie came in and stopped in the doorway. On the sill, in the red light, Moonpenny had opened.

It was one white flower, pale as the moon, wide open on its stem. Its petals had unfolded all the way, soft and broad, with a little curl at their edges. It smelled faintly sweet in the dark, something like honey and something like rain, and the smell filled the whole small room.

Elsie knelt up on the bed in front of it with her hands on the sill. "You waited," she whispered. "You waited for Midsummer. You did it." She looked at the white flower a long time. "We all did. Every one of us."

She lay down on the narrow bed with the birch crown still on and the shawl still round her, and didn't take either off. Under her breastbone the thread to Tamsin was there, running out through the window and away over the roofs. It was warm. It was warm and quiet, like a hand laid flat.

Below the round window the paper lanterns on Thimble Canal were guttering out. They went one by one along the water, red and then dark, red and then dark, all the way to the bridge.
