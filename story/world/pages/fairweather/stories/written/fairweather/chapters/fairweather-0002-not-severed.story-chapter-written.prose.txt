The ovens woke her again at four, and this time Elsie was out of bed before the fire downstairs had finished catching.

It was quest day. She knew it before she knew anything else, before she knew which way was up under the sloped ceiling, and she sat bolt upright with the quilt round her waist and said "Moonbells!" aloud to the dark, and banged her head on the beam. She rubbed it, laughing, and found her candle by feel. Then everything happened at once. Her nightshirt went over her head and landed on Sunny. The skirt went on backwards and had to come off and go on again. She laced the cream halter up the front in a hurry, crossways over her breasts, eyelet by eyelet, and pulled the cord too tight, so that the laces pressed little ridges into her soft skin, and she had to loosen it two eyelets and tie the bow again with her chin tucked to see it. Her gloves. Her boots, hopping. Her belt hung on the bedpost where she had left it, pouches and copper tag and all, and she took it down and buckled it at the second hole, and the tag clinked against her hip, and she patted it twice, the way she had yesterday, to make sure it was still hers.

She was halfway to the door when she remembered the plants, and turned round.

They sat in their row on the sill of the round window, dark shapes against the faint grey of the canal. Elsie crouched in front of them with the candle. "I'm going adventuring," she told them, low, because it was a thing that deserved to be said low. "Properly. With a party. There's a quest, and it's moonbells, Mrs Pennywhistle, like Mama's, so you're not to worry." She watered them quickly, a splash each, an apology to Doris. Then she touched one of Captain's leaves and held it a moment between finger and thumb. "You're in charge," she told him. "Keep an eye on the others. Sunny will try to lean toward the window and fall off, and you're to stop him." Captain smelled of mint, and kept quiet, which she took for yes.

She packed her satchel on the bed. Bandages, two rolls. The brown bottle of tincture. Honey salve, scissors, the little tin of willow bark, a clean cloth, needle and thread, her notebook, her pencil. She buckled it shut, and stood a moment with her hand on the flap, and then unbuckled it and tipped the whole lot out on the quilt again and packed it again, slower, the way her mother packed, so that what you needed first was on top. Then she filled her water flask from the jug, corked it, hung it across her body by its strap, and went down the stairs two at a time.

The bakery was gold and roaring. The baker was at the long table with her arms in dough to the elbow, and she looked up as Elsie came flying through, and lifted one floury hand off the dough and waved it at her, a white wave, flour falling off it in a little cloud. "Mind yourself, chick," she called.

"I will!" Elsie called back, already at the door, and blew her a kiss over her shoulder, and was gone out into the dark along the canal.

The east gate stood at the end of a long straight street, two squat stone towers with an arch between them and the great doors folded back for the day. Beyond it the mist lay over everything, white and low and thick, so that the road out of the city ran into it and disappeared, and the sky above was just beginning to go from grey to the colour of a pearl. It was cool. Elsie's arms and her bare waist prickled with it. Under the arch, with her back against the stone, Tamsin was already there.

She stood with her arms folded and one boot crossed over the other, and Margery was strapped across her back, the great axe head wrapped in sacking and standing up past her shoulder like a second, uglier head. Her copper curls were tied back with a bit of string and had already escaped it on both sides. When she saw Elsie coming up the street she straightened off the wall, quickly, as if she had been caught at something, and then stood very still and let Elsie come to her.

"You're early," Elsie said, delighted.

"So are you." Tamsin's eyes went down Elsie and up again, boots to clip, fast, and then away to the mist. "You'll freeze in that."

"I'll warm up when we walk." Elsie hugged her anyway, quick and hard, and Tamsin made a small noise in her throat and stood stiff for it.

When Elsie stepped back, she saw Cora.

The Warden was there too, two steps off from the gate in the mist, so still and so grey in her charcoal coat that Elsie had taken her for part of the wall. The black ledger was under her arm. Her coat was buttoned to the throat. Her hair was in its low bun, not a strand loose, exactly as it had been at midnight under the lantern by the Honeycomb's door, and her face had the same smooth, level look, as if she had simply stood there in the dark all night and the dawn had come up round her. "Good morning, Cora!" Elsie said, so glad to see her that it came out louder than she meant. Cora inclined her head one exact inch.

Tamsin cleared her throat. When Elsie turned back, Tamsin was holding up both hands in front of her, palms out, the way a child shows that it has washed. The white bandages were still on her knuckles in their neat figures of eight, and they were clean. Not a mark on them.

"Kept them dry," Tamsin said, gruffly, to a point somewhere over Elsie's shoulder. "All night. Slept with them out of the blanket." She turned them over, to show the backs. "Didn't even wash my face properly."

"Oh, Tamsin." Elsie took both her hands and looked them over, front and back, the way her mother did, and pressed her thumbs lightly along the knuckles to feel for heat under the cloth. There was none. Under her thumbs Tamsin's hands had gone completely still. "They're perfect. You did so well." She looked up, beaming. Tamsin's ears had gone red, right to the tips, and the red was spreading down into her freckles.

Running feet came slapping up the street behind them, and a voice before them. "I'm here! I'm here, I'm not late, I'm early, I was early, I was early by an hour—" Tilly came out of the dimness at a gallop, her goggles bouncing on her head, her vial belt clinking, her apron flapping round her knees, and pulled up in front of them gasping. One of her blonde braids was shorter than the other. The end of it was black and frizzled and still faintly smoking.

"Sorry," she panted. "Sorry. There was a very small fire. On Pickle Row. In my room. Very small. It's out. The landlady put it out, with a bucket, and she said some things, and I said sorry, and I am, I am sorry, it's only the end of the braid, and a bit of the curtain, and I didn't want to be late, so I came." She held up the singed braid between two fingers and looked at it sadly. "I was making a fire-starter. For tonight. In case. It works."

"It does look like it works," Elsie agreed, and pinched the smoking end out between her gloved fingers, and Tilly went pink.

Tamsin had the notice out of her jerkin already. She unfolded it, in half and in half again, and smoothed it against her thigh, and held it up to the brightening sky to read, in the voice of someone laying out a battle plan. "Right. Apothecary on Mortar Lane. Two dozen open moonbells, from the Glasswood's edge. Delivered by morning. Six lanterns." She folded it again with a snap. "Glasswood's an hour east. We go in now, while it's cool, we pick, we're back by noon, we eat like kings."

Elsie had been nodding along. Now she stopped. She looked at the notice, and at the dawn coming up pearl-coloured over the mist, and back. "Tamsin," she said, puzzled, not wanting to be a nuisance, "moonbells only open between dusk and midnight. Don't they? They're shut tight all day. Mama used to wake me up in the dark to go and pick them."

It was very quiet under the arch.

Tilly went white. It went out of her face all at once, soot and all, and her mouth fell open, and she put both hands up to her cheeks. "You said that," she wailed. "Yesterday! At the board! You said they only open at night, you said it right in front of me, and I was there, I was standing right there, I was talking about steeping them cold, I know they open at night, I've known since I was twelve, I've got a chapter on it, why didn't I say? Why didn't I say anything?"

Tamsin looked at the gate. She looked at the dawn, which went on coming up. She looked at the notice in her hand for a long moment, as if it might change its mind. Then she said a word, clear and carrying and very short, the sort of word that is learned on a canal barge, and up in the little window of the gate tower the gate clerk, who had been eating a roll, choked on it and coughed, and kept coughing.

Elsie laughed.

She did not mean to. It came up out of her all at once, from somewhere under her ribs, at the word and the cough and Tamsin's face, and Tilly with her hands on her cheeks, and the whole grand dawn that none of them had any use for. She laughed until she had to grab the gatepost to stay up, until there were tears in her eyes, until she was bent over the post with her forehead on the cold stone, wheezing. Tilly stared at her. Then Tilly's mouth wobbled, and a giggle came out of it, a small one, and then a big one, and then she was laughing too, helplessly, holding her stomach, her singed braid swinging. Tamsin held out as long as anyone could have. Then her crooked grin broke over her face, and she threw her head back and laughed, loud as a bell, so loud that a flock of pigeons went up off the gate tower all together.

Elsie, wiping her eyes, glanced over at Cora. Two steps back, the Warden had opened her ledger and was writing in it. Her pen moved, small and exact. Her face did not change, not really. But the corner of her mouth moved, once, and was still again.

Elsie straightened up off the gatepost with her heart full. "Well," she said, and took a big breath of the cool air. "That means we have a whole free day. Before the work. A whole day, all of us." She held out her arms as if to show them the size of it. "That's a gift, isn't it? We should spend it together."

Tamsin looked at Tilly, and Tilly looked at Tamsin. "Breakfast," Tamsin said.

They found a tea house on the canal a few streets in, just putting its chairs out under its awning, its windows steamed up and its doors open. Elsie went in first, before anyone could stop her, and stood on her toes at the counter and asked for two pots of tea, please, the black one, and two loaves, and counted the coins out onto the wood the way her mother had taught her: two pips and two pips for the pots, and a pip and a pip for the loaves, so that both of them could see. The girl at the counter swept them up and smiled at her. Elsie carried the first pot out herself.

They took a table by the water. Tamsin sat with her back to the wall, where she could see the street. Tilly sat next to Elsie, close, their elbows bumping. And Cora stood. She had stopped two steps off from the table under the edge of the awning, the ledger under her arm, as if the table had been measured and found to be someone else's.

Elsie took the second cup off the tray. She poured it full from the black pot, dark and strong, so strong it looked almost red against the white china where the light came through it, and she dropped in one lump of sugar from the bowl and stirred it till it was gone. Then she got up and pulled out the fourth chair, and set the cup down on the table in front of it, and the saucer under it, and turned the handle toward the chair, and sat down again in her own place and poured for Tilly. She did not say anything about it. She did not look to see.

For a long moment Cora did not move. Out of the corner of her eye, buttering bread for Tilly, Elsie saw her looking at the cup on the table: just looking at it, the steam coming off it into the cool air, as if it were a letter addressed to her in a hand she knew. Then the Warden drew out the chair and sat down in it, very upright, and laid the ledger beside the saucer, and lifted the cup in both her long hands.

She drank it. She drank it all, while it was hot, without stopping, her grey eyes on the canal. When the cup was empty she set it down in its saucer with a small, exact click, and folded her hands on the ledger, and did not say a word. There was colour high on her cheekbones again.

When the bread was down to crusts Elsie got her notebook out of her satchel and opened it on the table to the page with everyone's names on it, and licked her pencil. "Birthdays," she announced. "I need everyone's. Tamsin first."

"Middle of winter," Tamsin said, through crust. "The night of the first big snow. Mam says I came in a snowstorm and screamed the whole barge awake, and I've been doing it since."

Elsie wrote it down, and drew a snowflake beside it: Tamsin, first snow. "Tilly?"

Tilly opened her mouth and shut it again. "It's—hold on." She started counting on her fingers, frowning, her lips moving. "It was spring, and it was the week after the College's spring term starts, and Papa always says I was born on a Thursday, so if the term started on a Monday—" She ran out of fingers, started again from the thumb, and looked up stricken. "The ninth. Of the second month of spring. Or the tenth. The ninth. I think it's the ninth."

"The ninth," Elsie said, writing, "or the tenth. I'll ask your papa." She drew a little flower. Then she turned in her chair to Cora, pencil ready. "Cora?"

Cora set her second cup down. "My birthday," she said, "is not relevant."

"All right," Elsie said cheerfully. She bent over the notebook and wrote, in her round, careful hand, so that anyone leaning could read it upside down: Cora: ask again. Then she underlined ask, twice. When she looked up Cora was looking at the notebook, and then quickly at the canal.

Tilly had gone quiet beside her. She was turning her empty cup round and round in its saucer by the handle, and the china made a small grating noise. "I walk past it every day," she said, to the cup. "Gear Street. Papa's shop. It's on my way to everywhere, I can't help it, it's in the middle of the city, and every day I walk past it, and every day I tell myself I'm going in, I'm going to go in and tell him, and every day I walk past. On the other side of the street. Fast." She turned the cup. "I've been doing it for a week."

Elsie put the pencil down. "We could go now," she said. "Today. We've got the whole day. All three of us could go with you."

"Yes," said Tilly at once. Then her face changed. "No. No, I can't, not today, I've got soot on, I've got—" She looked down at her singed braid, and at the scorched apron. Then she looked at Elsie, and her chin came up. "Yes. Yes. If you come. Yes."

Gear Street ran down off the hill toward the lake in a long, narrow curve, and every second shop on it was a maker of something small: spectacles, spring scales, lockets, music boxes. The clockmaker's was halfway down. They heard it before they saw it. It was a soft, many-voiced ticking, a hundred clocks all going at once, each one a little out of time with the next, so that the sound came through the open door like rain on a roof. In the window there were clocks of every size, from a great brass-faced longcase taller than Tamsin to little gilt carriage clocks no bigger than a fist, all of them saying half past seven to within a minute or two.

Tilly stopped dead on the cobbles in front of it. Elsie, who had been holding her elbow, stopped with her.

Through the glass, past the clocks, there was a long workbench under a lamp, and a man bent over it. He was grey and thin and stooped, with Tilly's round face gone long and lined, and a jeweller's loupe screwed into one eye. In front of him on a square of green felt lay a pocket watch with its back off, and he was doing something to its insides with a pair of tweezers so fine they flashed. His lips were pursed. He was humming, or seemed to be. He did not look up.

Tilly's hand found Elsie's and gripped it. Her fingers were cold and they were shaking, and they held on hard enough that Elsie felt the bones of her own hand move. "I can't," Tilly whispered. Her eyes had filled right up, the blue swimming. "I can't. Look at him. He's so happy. He thinks I'm at the College. He tells the customers, my daughter at the College. I can't go in there and make him not happy. I can't, Elsie. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, I said yes."

Elsie turned so that she was between Tilly and the window, and took Tilly's other hand too. "Then not today," she said. "That's all right. That's completely all right. He'll still be here, and so will we. We'll come back whenever you're ready. Next week, or next year. As many times as you like."

Tilly's mouth crumpled. She nodded, hard.

Tamsin had been standing a step off, her arms folded, scowling at the clocks as though they had insulted someone. Now she unfolded her arms and reached out and put one big bandaged hand flat on top of Tilly's head, right on her goggles, the way you would put a hand on a dog that had been frightened by thunder. "Come on, Brandt," she said gruffly. And she turned Tilly round by the head, quite gently, and steered her off down the street.

Tilly did not let go of Elsie's hand. She held it all the way down Gear Street, past the spectacle maker and the music boxes, sniffing, her shoulder against Elsie's arm. At the corner, where the street turned onto the lake road and there was no more reason to, she held it still. Elsie felt her fingers tighten when they came to the corner, as if Tilly had thought about letting go and decided against it, and Elsie squeezed back, and they went round the corner hand in hand.

Down on the lake road Tamsin stopped and squinted at the sun. "We should sleep," she said. "Before tonight. Night's work, and we've all been up since the ovens." Tilly nodded, and Elsie nodded. But Tamsin did not turn toward the docks, and Tilly did not turn toward Pickle Row, and they all stood there on the cobbles for a little while not going anywhere, until Elsie, who had been looking from one of them to the other, said, "Mine's closest."

The baker was at her counter when the three of them came in through the shop, with the Warden behind them. Elsie said good morning, and introduced everyone, and said they were all going up to sleep, because of the moonbells, which only opened at night. The baker looked at Tamsin, and the axe, and at Tilly, and at the charcoal coat and the ledger, and then at Elsie. Her eyebrows went up and up. She did not say a word. She went back to arranging buns.

The attic was full of people. Tamsin had to duck under the beam and still hit her head on it, and swore, quieter this time. Tilly walked straight to the window. "Oh," she said. "Oh, you've got plants."

"This is Mrs Pennywhistle," said Elsie, delighted, and went down the row. "And Sunny. And this is Doris. She doesn't like a fuss. And this is Captain." Tamsin, rubbing her head, nodded to each of them as if they were people, and said "Morning" to Captain gravely. Tilly crouched in front of Doris. She looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached out and took one of Doris's thick, spiky leaves very carefully between her finger and thumb, and shook it, up and down, once. "How do you do," she said. "I'm Tilly." Elsie had to sit down on the bed.

The bed was the next thing. It was one narrow bed under the slope of the ceiling, made for one girl and not a large one. Tamsin looked at it. Tilly looked at it. Cora had gone without a word to the chair by the round window and sat in it, upright, the ledger on her knees.

"We'll all fit," said Elsie, and took off her boots and her belt and climbed in, and lay down right in the middle of it on her back, and held up the quilt on either side.

Tamsin took her boots off very slowly. She unbuckled Margery and leaned the axe against the wall. Then she lay down on the very edge of the bed on Elsie's left, on her back, stiff as a plank, her arms by her sides and her eyes on the ceiling, with a hand's breadth of bare mattress between her and Elsie and the other half of her hanging off into air. Tilly, on the right, curled up on her side like a cat, her knees drawn up and her hands tucked under her chin, facing Elsie. Her nose was an inch from Elsie's bare shoulder. Elsie could feel her breath on it, quick and warm.

The attic was warm. The ovens' heat came up through the floor, and the sun lay on the round window, and the whole room smelled of bread. Elsie's eyes grew heavy at once. She turned her head on the pillow and, without thinking about it at all, laid her cheek on Tamsin's shoulder, the bare freckled shoulder beside the strap of her jerkin, because it was there and it was warm, and she reached down under the quilt and found Tilly's hand and held it.

Under her cheek Tamsin's heart was pounding. Elsie could feel it right through the shoulder, a heavy, hurried thud, thud, thud, much too fast for a girl lying down. Tamsin's whole body had gone rigid. Her arm lay along Elsie's side, and Elsie's breast was pressed against it, the soft small weight of it through the cream halter resting on Tamsin's bare forearm, and Tamsin held that arm absolutely still, as if it were lying against a hot stove and she had been told not to move it. She was barely breathing.

Elsie opened her eyes a crack, sleepily, to see why.

Through her lashes she saw Tilly instead. Tilly was not asleep. She was lying very close with her blue eyes wide open, and she was staring at Elsie's mouth. Only that. She stared at it the way she had stared yesterday at the board, with her lips a little parted, and Elsie felt her fingers tremble in her own hand under the quilt. Then Tilly saw that Elsie's eyes were open, and she shut her own eyes hard, screwed them up tight like a child pretending to be asleep, and her whole face went scarlet.

Elsie thought: how lovely. They were so glad to be here, both of them, so happy to be together, that they could not even sleep. She remembered lying awake like that the first night with a new caravan, too full of it to close her eyes. She smiled, and wriggled down a little deeper between them, and snuggled closer to both, her cheek on Tamsin's shoulder and Tilly's hand held tight against her stomach.

Across the room, in the chair by the round window, Cora was watching them.

She sat with the light behind her, so that her face was in shadow and the edges of her were bright, and she was looking at the three of them in the bed: at Elsie in the middle, at Elsie's head on Tamsin's shoulder and Tilly's hand in hers. She did not look away when Elsie looked at her. She went on looking, steadily, her hands flat on the closed ledger.

Elsie smiled at her, sleepily, over Tamsin's shoulder.

Cora opened the ledger. She uncapped her pen. She bent her head and began to write, and the sound of the nib on the paper, small and scratching and steady, was the last thing Elsie heard.

They woke in the afternoon in a tangle. At some point in the hours between, the bed had lost all its edges: Tamsin had rolled in off the brink of it and lay half under Elsie with an arm flung across her, and Tilly had curled tighter and tighter until she was tucked under Elsie's chin, and the quilt was on the floor. Elsie woke because something was in her mouth. She opened her eyes. Tilly was looking at her from very close, and Tilly was holding a blonde braid, and the end of the braid was in Elsie's mouth.

Tilly squeaked. It was a high, small, horrified squeak, like a kettle. She snatched the braid away and sat bolt upright and hit her head on the beam. "Sorry!" she said. "Sorry, sorry, I don't know how it got there, I was asleep, it was asleep, it moves, it's always in things—" Tamsin woke up with a snort and an arm across Elsie's stomach, and saw where her arm was, and took it back as if the stomach were on fire.

Elsie lay there and laughed.

Later, while Tilly was washing her face at the basin and splashing water everywhere, Tamsin went to the round window and stood beside it with her back to the room. She stood there a while. Then she said, without turning round, low, so that it was just for Elsie, "Tonight. If I go red." She stopped. "What can your class do? Your skills. Is there anything you could do. If I went red."

Elsie came and stood beside her at the window and looked out at the canal, where the light was going golden. "I've got two," she said. "Captivate, and Tether." She said the names as if they were medicines she didn't much like the taste of. "I don't know what they do, really, not properly. I've never used either of them. Not once." She looked at Tamsin's profile, the freckles and the set jaw. "And I wouldn't. Not on you. Not unless you said yes."

Tamsin was quiet. Her hands were on the windowsill, by Sunny's pot, and the bandaged knuckles were white. "Tether," she said. "That's a tie, isn't it. A line." She turned her head and looked at Elsie straight, green eyes, no grin in them at all. "Then yes. To Tether. Now. In advance." Her voice was rough. "If I go red, and I come at you, or at Brandt, then you've got my yes. You don't stop to ask me. You can't ask me, in there. So I'm saying it now."

Elsie looked at her a long time. "Are you sure?" she said.

"Yes."

"You're really sure. Truly. You can say no, and I won't mind at all."

"Elsie." Tamsin's mouth did something that was not quite the crooked grin. "I've been sure since the Leftovers."

Behind them, the chair by the window creaked. Cora was still in it, and she was looking at them now, and she had certainly heard every word. "The consent," she said, flat and level, "is noted." And she opened the ledger on her knee, and wrote it down.

The guild had a booth by the east gate, a little wooden box painted green with a hatch in the front and a lamp hung over it already lit, though the sky was still light. A clerk sat in it with a ledger of his own and a box of stamps. He was a round, bored young man, and he took one look at Elsie and sat up much less bored. He took a longer look at Cora behind her, and the silver pin on her collar, and sat up straighter still.

"Glasswood licence," Tamsin said, and slapped the moonbell notice down on the ledge. "Three of us. Tonight."

"One lantern," the clerk said. He wrote out the paper in a hurry, the party's names in a column, Elsie Fairweather, Tamsin Reyes, Ottilie Brandt, and stamped it twice with a thump, and slid it across the ledge.

Tamsin was already digging in her jerkin. Elsie got there first. She laid a silver lantern on the ledge with a click, flat, where the clerk and everyone could see it, and picked up the licence before Tamsin could. "Mine," she said. "You paid for supper at the Heron."

"That was different," Tamsin said. "You'd fixed my hands."

"And you're going to keep us all safe tonight, so it's the same." Elsie folded the stamped paper and put it in the pouch on her belt and buttoned the pouch. Tamsin grumbled something under her breath about how that was not how it worked, and folded her arms, and let her.

The clerk wrote in his own ledger. "Party of three out at half seven," he said, "with Warden." He looked at the copper tags on their belts, and then up at them, and his voice went flat and official, as if he had said it a thousand times and was going to say it right this time in front of a Warden. "F-rank goes a mile into the Glasswood, to the white posts. You'll see them. White posts, painted. No farther. Not one step past. Anyone past the posts on an F-rank licence answers to the guildmaster." He looked at Elsie. "Understood?"

"Understood," Elsie said. "Thank you. White posts."

The cart track ran east from the gate through the meadows, two ruts and a strip of grass between them, and they walked it for an hour as the sun went down behind them. The grass was high on both sides and full of little white flowers and buzzing, and their four shadows ran out ahead of them long and thin on the track, and grew longer, and longer, until they reached all the way to the next rise. The air smelled of hay and warm earth. Larks went up out of the grass and hung singing.

Tilly talked about moonbells the whole way. She walked on Elsie's right and talked with both hands: about how you must pick them open and not a minute after they close, because once they close the virtue goes back down into the root and doesn't come out again till the next night; about steeping cold, never boiling; about the three ways to tell a moonbell from a bluecap, which is poison, which she then admitted she had been confusing all week; about a paper she had wanted to write at the College and now never would; about whether moonbells could be distilled, and what happened if you did, and whether it would explode. "Probably," she said. "Probably it would. Most things do. For me."

Tamsin walked on Elsie's other side and said very little. She walked close, closer than the track needed, so that every few steps her bare arm brushed Elsie's. Elsie felt it each time, warm and quick. Margery's handle stuck up over Tamsin's shoulder against the darkening sky. Every so often Tamsin turned her head and looked out over the grass, east, toward the low line of something glittering on the horizon, and her jaw was tight. Behind them, two steps back, Cora walked in silence, and the track was quiet enough that Elsie could hear her boots.

They came to the Glasswood at dusk.

It rose out of the meadow all at once, a wall of trees at the end of the track, and the trees were made of glass. Their trunks were clear as water. Their branches were clear. Their leaves were thin clear panes that caught the last of the sunset and held it, so that the whole edge of the wood was full of red and gold and violet light, moving. And when the evening wind came over the meadow and through the branches, the leaves touched each other and the trees chimed: a soft, high, wandering chiming, like a thousand small bells hung up and forgotten, rising and falling with the wind.

Elsie stood at the end of the track and could not speak.

Then the moths came. They drifted up out of the long grass at the edge of the wood, a few at first and then dozens, and their wings were glass too: thin and clear and veined with silver, and as they rose they caught the light and shone with it, and when they passed in front of the trees you could see the trees through them. They drifted round Elsie's head. One landed on the back of her glove and sat there, opening and closing its wings, and the light came and went in them.

Elsie gasped out loud.

"Glimmer moths," Tamsin said beside her, gruffly, watching her face and not the moths. "They're harmless. Thousands of them, once it's dark. You'll get sick of them." Elsie knew she would not.

Tamsin stepped forward then onto the first of the roots, and turned round to face them, and her voice went serious. "Listen. Both of you. The trees drop glass. Branches, leaves, bits. It lies about in the roots and the moss, and you won't see it, and it cuts like a knife. Worse than a knife. So mind your hands. Gloves on. Feel before you put your weight down." She looked at Tilly's bare fingers until Tilly went red and promised to feel before she put a hand down.

They went in among the roots.

Under the glass trees the light was strange: the last of the sunset coming down through clear branches in long coloured shafts, and the moths turning in it. The roots were glass too, where they rose above the moss, great clear knuckles curling down into the ground, and between them, in the shady places, there were hollows full of dark moss and fern. In the first hollow they came to, Elsie saw something blue.

As she watched, it opened. A little closed bud on a thin stem, no longer than her finger, unfolded as the light went: one petal and then the next curling back, until it hung there open, a small bell, deep blue, with a glow at the heart of it like a coal. Then another beside it opened. Then three more. All down the hollow, as the dusk deepened, the moonbells were opening, blue after blue in the dark moss, like someone lighting lamps one at a time down a long street.

"Oh," said Tilly, with great reverence, and went down on her knees.

They gathered on their knees side by side in the moss, the three of them shoulder to shoulder, and counted aloud. Elsie showed Tilly how her mother had done it, pinching each stem low down with a thumbnail so the bell came away whole and the root stayed for next year. Tilly did it beautifully the first time. "One," said Elsie. "Two," said Tilly. "Three," said Tamsin, who had been watching, and held one up in her big bandaged fingers as delicately as if it were an egg. Tilly took each bell as they picked it and laid it in her apron pocket, one by one, very gently, on a bed of moss she had put in the bottom for them. "Seven. Eight. Nine."

Kneeling there, reaching for the next bell, Elsie felt Tamsin watching her. It was a warmth, a weight on the side of her face. She was bent forward in the moss, her short skirt riding up the backs of her thighs, the laced halter gone loose from the walk, and the weight of the look moved over her, slow, down, and up again. She turned her head. Tamsin was looking at the moths. She was looking at them very hard, a little flushed, her jaw set, as though the moths had asked her a difficult question.

"Fourteen," said Tilly, and then shrieked.

There had been a hare in the hollow, or something like a hare: rabbit-sized and grey and covered in spines like a hedgehog's, crouched back against a glass root where the three of them had cornered it without knowing. When Tilly's hand came reaching through the ferns it shot out of its corner and bit her on the ankle, hard, and was gone into the dark in a rattle of spines.

"Thornhare," Tamsin said, on her feet, too late, Margery half off her back.

Tilly sat in the moss holding her ankle, rocking, white, apologising to the hare. Elsie had her satchel open before Tamsin had finished saying the word. She eased Tilly's boot down, and there were two neat deep punctures above the bone, and a ring of smaller pricks round them where the spines had gone in. She picked the broken spine-tips out with her tweezers, one, two, three, while Tilly whimpered and Tamsin stood over them with the axe. She cleaned it with the tincture, which made Tilly yelp and grab Elsie's shoulder. She dabbed on honey salve and bound it up snug in a clean white figure of eight. "There," she said, and kept her hand round Tilly's bandaged ankle a moment, warm, and Tilly stared at her hand there and forgot to whimper.

By the time it was full dark the moths were everywhere, thousands of them, as Tamsin had said, so that the whole wood glowed softly with their wings and they could see by it to pick. Tilly counted the last one into her pocket and sat back on her heels. "Twenty-four," she said, astonished, as if it had been a long shot. "Two dozen. Open. All of them." Elsie looked back the way they had come. The white posts, which they had passed nowhere near, stood somewhere far off in the trees, too far to see.

Then, in the trees, Elsie saw eyes.

Two at first, low down between the glass trunks, round and yellow and shining like lamps. Then two more beside them. Then more. Seven pairs, eight, in a loose ring among the trees, all at the height of a big dog's head, and all of them still. As her eyes found the shapes round them she saw long, lean bodies, and fur that stood up along their backs in bristles like a hedge, catching the moth-light in points.

"Bramblewolves," Tamsin said, very low. She had come round in front of Elsie and Tilly without seeming to move, and Margery was in her hands, the sacking off the head. Her voice was flat and calm in a way Elsie had not heard from her. "Don't anyone run. Listen to me. Stand up slow. Stay together. Don't run. They go for whoever runs."

Tilly stood up. She stood up on her bitten ankle, and her face in the moth-light was white as paper, and her eyes went from the wolves to the dark behind them, and Elsie saw that Tilly didn't decide anything. Her body did. Tilly turned, and ran.

The wolves went for her. All of them, all at once, a rush of grey bristling shapes out of the trees past Elsie and Tamsin, low and silent and very fast, after the small running figure with the flapping apron.

Tamsin roared.

Elsie felt it in her chest. It was not a shout. It came up out of Tamsin from somewhere very deep and very old, and as it came her whole body changed: her shoulders came up, her face went dark with blood, red flooding up her throat and over her freckles until her face was red all over, and her green eyes went red too, the whites of them, shot through. And she went after the wolves with Margery swinging. The great axe went round in a flat, whistling circle and caught the first wolf and threw it ten feet into a glass trunk, which rang like a struck bell. The next swing caught another. The wolves broke. They scattered yelping in every direction among the glass, and Tilly, who had fallen, lay in the roots with her arms over her head.

Tamsin did not stop.

She wheeled round with the axe up, panting, and her red eyes swept the wood for the next thing that moved, and the next thing that moved was Tilly, lifting her head out of the roots. Tamsin turned toward her. There was nothing in her face. Elsie saw that clearly, in the moth-light, and Elsie's stomach turned over at it: Tamsin's face with no Tamsin in it, just red and teeth and breath, turning toward the small fallen shape in the roots and lifting the axe.

Elsie was between them before she knew she had moved.

She stood in front of Tilly with her arms out wide, as wide as they would go, and looked up into Tamsin's face. "Tamsin," she said. "Tamsin, it's me." The face looked down at her. There was nobody in it at all. There was no knowing in the red eyes, no Tamsin behind them, nothing but the axe and the breath, and Margery hung there above Elsie, and began to come down.

From the corner of her eye Elsie saw Cora. The Warden had come up out of the dark on Elsie's left and stopped there, and her hand was raised: her right hand, held up and out toward Elsie and Tamsin, fingers spread, the silver pin on her collar flashing, and her face white and still. Elsie remembered the counter at the guild hall, and Cora's level voice: any bond you make, with any person, I can sever, at any time. The hand stayed there in the air. It was held. It did not move.

And Elsie remembered Tamsin at the window. You've got my yes. You don't stop to ask me. I'm saying it now.

She did not know how. She reached for it anyway, the way she would have reached for Tamsin's hand in a crowd: not with her arms, which were still out wide, but with something behind her ribs, something she had felt there since the silver circle and had never once touched. She reached out with it toward Tamsin, and said the name of it, out loud, to make it hers: "Tether."

And then the Tether woke.

It was a line. That was the nearest Elsie could come to it. A warm line, running from somewhere under her breastbone straight out across the space between them into Tamsin, and running both ways at once, so that she could feel Tamsin at the other end of it the way you feel someone's weight at the other end of a rope. And she knew in it, the way you know you are awake, that the end of it was in Tamsin's hand. That Tamsin could let go of it any time she liked. That she could break it as easily as breaking a thread.

So Elsie poured everything she had down it.

She did not think. She poured. Tamsin's name, over and over. Margery, and the sacking on her head, and the chalk snapping on the slate. Bandages, white ones, clean all night. The Leftovers and the sad face on the board. The tea house, and the snowflake in the notebook. The attic, and Tamsin's heart under her cheek. Come back. Come back. It's me. Come back.

Down the line, she felt the red falter.

It was like feeling a fire waver at the end of a long hall. The red stuttered. It thinned. And Margery, coming down, slowed in the air above her, and slowed, and stopped, a foot from Elsie's face, so close that Elsie could see the moths reflected in the blade, and a nick in the edge, and a bit of grey wolf-fur caught in the nick.

Tamsin's eyes cleared.

Elsie watched it happen. The red went out of the whites of them first, and then the green came back, and then Tamsin was in her face again, all at once, the way a lamp comes on in a dark window. She saw Elsie. Elsie saw her see her. Her eyes went past Elsie to Tilly, crouched in the roots behind her with her arms over her head, and then up to the edge of the axe, a foot from Elsie's face. And her hands opened. Margery fell out of them and thumped down into the moss.

They came low and slow round the glass trunks on the left, heads down, bristles up, their lamp eyes on the three girls standing close together and the axe lying in the moss. The wolves yelped and turned tail and fled, the whole pack, crashing away through the glass trees into the dark, until the sound of them was gone, and there was only the ringing of the trees and the green fire guttering out in the moss.

It sat just inside the first trees, a low stone hut with a turf roof and a door that stood open, and it was empty. Tilly carried her apron pocket in both hands the whole way, cupped against her stomach, like a girl carrying a bird. The moonbells inside were safe, every one. She checked twice.

The hut had a fireplace, a table, a bench, and one narrow bunk against the wall, with a straw mattress and a folded blanket. "Tilly gets the bunk," said Elsie at once. "Because of her ankle."

Tilly sat down on the edge of it. She looked down at her bandaged ankle, and at the blanket, and then up at Elsie, very pink in the moth-light from the door. "Will you," she said. "In it. With me. Please. I don't want to be on my own in it. Not after the wolves."

"Of course," said Elsie.

Tamsin said, from the door, that she would sleep on the floor, then. Right next to the bunk. She did not say why.
