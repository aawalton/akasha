Tamsin said, "Mm," to the window, and scratched the back of her neck, and looked at Elsie out of the corner of her eye with an expression Elsie could not read at all, a sort of crooked rueful twist of the mouth, and then grinned at her, and the look was gone.

Elsie unwrapped herself from the coat. She was mostly dry now, under it, and warm right through, and the coat was warm too from her; she held it out to Cora in both hands, folded over her arms like a gift. "Here," she said. "Thank you. It's still warm. I warmed it back up for you." Cora took the coat. She put it on, one arm and then the other, and settled it on her shoulders, and did up the buttons, one at a time, all the way from the bottom to the throat, slowly, with her bandaged hand, and smoothed the front. When she had done the last button she stood a moment with her hand flat on it, over her collarbone, and then took it away.

"Right," said Tamsin, and rubbed her hands together, and the rueful look was entirely gone. "Come on. Come and see." She led them down the length of the hall, past the F-rank board and on, to the next section of the long quest board, where the notices were pinned higher and the paper was better and the sums were bigger, and a carved letter over it said E. She stood in front of it with her hands on her hips. Then she reached up and tapped one notice, twice, with one finger, firmly, the way someone taps a door they have been waiting a long time to knock on. "That one," Tamsin said. "That's mine. I've been looking at it for days. Trolls."

Elsie read it. The notice was in a big square farmer's hand: river trolls, two, living under the old toll bridge on the River Wend, north of the city, and coming up at night onto the meadows to take sheep. The shepherds of Wend Meadows wanted them stopped, by any party fit for it. At the bottom someone had drawn a little picture of a troll, round and lumpy, with a sheep under its arm. Elsie looked at the picture for a long time. "They take sheep," she said slowly. "Two of them. Under a bridge." She looked at the sheep under the drawn troll's arm, and it looked surprised.

"How big is a troll?" Tilly asked. She asked it in a very small voice, from behind Elsie's shoulder, where she had gone when she saw the picture, and her eyes were very round. "Roughly. In feet. Is it bigger than a person? Is it bigger than a cart?" Tamsin looked down at her. Then a slow, wide, delighted grin spread across Tamsin's face, crooked and wicked and bright. "No idea," she said cheerfully. "Not a clue. Big, I should think. Bigger than a goat." She tapped the drawing. "We'll find out. That's the fun of it." Tilly made a faint sound and held on to the back of Elsie's sleeve. Elsie, looking at Tamsin's grin, could not tell whether she meant it.

Elsie was still looking at the drawn sheep. "Are they hungry?" she asked. They all looked at her. "The trolls," Elsie said. "Are they taking the sheep because they're hungry? Or are they just thieves?" She frowned at the notice. "Because if they're hungry, that's different. If they're hungry, it's not their fault, not really, and there might be something else they could eat. And if they're just thieves, well." She looked round at the others. Tamsin opened her mouth, and shut it, and shrugged. Tilly shook her head. Cora looked at the notice for a long moment with her brows drawn together and said she did not know. The clerk, who had come up behind them with his book, spread his inky hands.

The clerk took the troll notice down himself, carefully, and carried it back to his counter, and entered it in his great book: E-rank, the old toll bridge, the River Wend, and the party's name, all four names now on an E-rank line. He blotted it. "Mind you leave early," he said. "Farmers like their trolls dealt with before supper." Cora opened her ledger on the counter and wrote it in too, in her small exact hand: the date, the notice, the names. "We leave at dawn," Tamsin said, and slapped the counter. "Dawn tomorrow. Honeycomb door." Elsie looked at the word dawn in Cora's ledger, upside down, and her stomach turned over, half with fear and half with joy.

Cora shut her ledger. "I'll write to the guildmaster," she said, "about the rank. Both of you." She looked at Elsie and at Tilly. "She'll want it on record. An Enthraller raised to E in—" she paused, and Elsie saw her count, "—in a week." She tucked the ledger under her arm. Her coat was buttoned to the throat again, and her hair was perfect, and there was only a little dampness left on the front of her shirt cuffs to show anything had happened on the Lantern Steps at all. "Dawn," she said to Tamsin. "I'll be at the Honeycomb door." And she turned and walked away across the hall to the charcoal-painted door, and went through it, and it shut behind her.

Out on the guild hall steps the sun was high and hot, and the square smelled of warm stone and the canal. Tamsin stretched until her back cracked. "Sleep," she said. "That's what we want now. Proper sleep, all afternoon, in a bed. You can't fight trolls half asleep." Tilly was looking down at herself: at her damp apron, and her lake-wet braids, and her muddy boots, and the soot on her hands from the blue flame. She sniffed at her own sleeve and wrinkled her nose. "I need a bath," she said. "Before anything. Before sleep. I smell of lake. I smell like the inside of Tamsin's boot."

At the corner of Pickle Row Tilly stopped. She looked down the narrow lane toward her boarding house, and then back at Elsie, and then she threw her arms round Elsie's neck and hugged her, quickly, tightly, her damp braids against Elsie's cheek. She let go. She took two steps toward the lane. Then she turned round and came back and hugged her again, harder, her face pressed into Elsie's shoulder for a moment, and Elsie felt her take a breath there. "E-rank," Tilly whispered. "See you at dawn." Then she let go and went off down Pickle Row at a half-run, her satchel clinking, and did not look back until the very end of the lane, where she turned, and waved, and was gone.

Tamsin walked Elsie home along the canal. She walked close, so that their arms brushed, and carried Elsie's boots for her by their laces over one shoulder, beside Margery, and talked about trolls. Where she would stand, and where Tilly should stand, well back, and what Margery would make of a troll, and whether a troll could swim, and whether the bridge would be very old and mossy, and she hoped it would. Elsie walked barefoot on the warm cobbles and listened and laughed. "I'll be at the Honeycomb door at dawn," Tamsin said, at the bridge. "On the step. Right on it. Don't keep me waiting, I'll knock the baker up."

At the Honeycomb door Tamsin stopped and handed Elsie her boots. Then she looked down, at the little copper tag hanging at Elsie's belt, and reached out, and flicked it with one finger, so that it spun on its ring and flashed in the sun and clinked against Elsie's buckle. "E," Tamsin said, and grinned, her crooked delighted grin, the red line from the axe haft long gone from her cheek, her curls dried wild in the sun. "E-rank Elsie." She looked at Elsie a moment longer, as if she would say something else. Then she turned and went off along Thimble Canal with Margery on her shoulder, whistling, and Elsie stood in the bakery doorway with her boots in her arms and watched her go.

In the attic she peeled off her damp skirt. It was still knotted at the hip, stiff with lake water and smelling of the Mere, and she untied the knot with her teeth and shook it out and hung it from the catch of the round window, where the sun came in, to dry. It dripped onto the sill. She took her other skirt out of her bag, the brown one, the one she had worn on the road, and put it on, and it was dry and soft and smelled of the caravan still, of woodsmoke and canvas, faintly. She stood a moment in the middle of the attic in the warm light with her hand on it. Then she looked at her tag, and smiled.

Moonpenny sat in the middle of the plant crate where Elsie had set her, between Mrs Pennywhistle and Sunny, her one bud tight and pale. Elsie knelt down in front of the crate. "Right," she said. "Proper introductions. I promised." She put her hand on each pot in turn. "Moonpenny, this is Mrs Pennywhistle. She's a fern, and she's very old and very grand, and she likes to be misted. This is Sunny. Sunny has two lemons and is very proud of them. This is Captain; he's mint, and he'll try to take over, so don't let him. And this is Doris." She touched Doris's fat leaf. "Doris is an aloe. She's a good listener. You'll like her. Everyone, this is Moonpenny."

She lay down on the narrow bed in her dry skirt with the sun coming slant across the quilt, and the bread smell coming up warm through the boards, and the canal noises far below. She held her tag. She held it in her closed hand, against her chest, the little copper square warm from her skin, and every now and then she opened her fingers and looked at the E, and closed them again. She meant to get up in a moment. She meant to write to her mother, and count her pips, and think about trolls. When she woke, the sun had moved right across the floor to the far wall, and it was mid-afternoon, and the tag was still in her hand, printed into her palm.

She went down the narrow stairs with her hair sticking up on one side. The bakery was quiet in the afternoon lull, the trays half empty, the ovens ticking as they cooled, and Agnes was at the counter with a cup of tea and a ledger of her own, adding up flour. She looked up. Her eyes went to Elsie's face, and then down, to Elsie's belt, to the little copper tag hanging there; and stopped. She looked at it for a long moment. She put her cup down. "Well, now," Agnes said. She said it slowly, in her gruff floury voice, with her eyebrows up, and nodded, once, as if something had been settled to her satisfaction. "Well, now."

Agnes reached into the case without looking and took out a honey bun, the last on the tray, glossy and fat, and twisted it into a square of paper, and held it out across the counter. Elsie reached for her purse. Agnes waved her hand at it, flapping, the way she would wave a fly off the bread. "Put that away," she said. "Don't insult me." She pushed the bun into Elsie's hand and closed Elsie's fingers on it, floury, and let go. "E-rank," she said, and grunted, and picked up her tea again. Elsie stood holding the warm bun in both hands and could not think of anything to say at all, so she came round the counter and kissed Agnes on her floury cheek, and Agnes said, "Get off."

She sat at the little table by the bakery window with the bun and a sheet of paper and the stub of pencil from her notebook, and wrote to her mother. Dear Mama, she wrote. I am E-rank. They stamped it on my tag this morning with a press, clack, and Tilly cried, and so did I. She ate a bite of bun and went on. There was a ring in the Mere, a lady's mother's ring, and I found it at the bottom with Tilly's blue light, and I went right under with my eyes open, and the lady is going to give it to her sweetheart tonight. And I have been given a moon orchid. Her name is Moonpenny. She will open next summer and I will write and tell you.

After closing, when the last customer had gone and the shutters were down, Elsie asked Agnes if she might borrow the ovens. Agnes looked at her over her spectacles. "What for?" "A cake," Elsie said. "A honey cake. For tomorrow. For E-rank, and for trolls." Agnes considered this. Then she jerked her head at the big brick oven at the back, still warm, the embers banked. "It'll want feeding," she said. "Wood's dear." Elsie counted two copper pips out of her purse and put them on the counter beside the till. Agnes looked at them, and at Elsie, and took them, and put them in the till, and the drawer shut with a ring. "Mind you clean up," she said.

She baked it alone. She had her notebook open on the floury table, propped against the flour crock, at the page where she had written out the honey cake in her careful round hand, the way her mother made it. On the road it went in a pan over the fire; this was a real oven, a baker's oven, and she was a little afraid of it. She measured the flour into Agnes's big bowl, and the butter, and the eggs, two, cracked on the rim, and the honey, a great golden slow spoonful that would not leave the spoon until she scraped it, and a pinch of cinnamon. She beat it with a wooden spoon until her arm ached, humming. The bakery was dim and warm, and the oven glowed red behind its door.

Agnes came through from the back with a broom while Elsie was beating. She stopped behind Elsie's shoulder. She did not say anything; she leaned over, floury, and read the notebook page, her lips moving, one line after another, down to the bottom. Then she reached past Elsie with one thick finger and tapped a word. "Hot," Agnes said. "Says hot oven. Wants a moderate oven. Hot, it'll catch on top and stay raw in the middle, and you'll cry." She took Elsie's pencil out of her hand, crossed out the word hot, and wrote moderate above it in a big square hand, and gave the pencil back. "There," she said, and went back to her sweeping. Elsie looked at the word moderate in Agnes's writing, and loved it.

The cake came out risen and golden and cracked across the top, smelling of honey and warm butter so strongly that Elsie stood in front of the oven with the cloth in her hands and just breathed. She turned it out onto the board. It held. She set it on the side to cool, and laid a clean cloth over it, gently, tucking the corners, the way her mother covered a cake to rest overnight. Then she picked up the wooden spoon from the bowl. There was batter all down it, thick and golden, and she licked it, slowly, all the way down one side and then the other, her eyes shut. It tasted of honey and the road and her mother's hands.

Upstairs, she went to the round window to close it against the night air. The canal below was blue with evening, and the lamplighters had been along, and the paper lanterns hung lit along Thimble Canal, red and gold, their light lying in long wavering stripes on the water. A figure was walking along the far bank. Elsie knew the walk before she knew anything else: the straight back, the long even stride, the dark coat buttoned to the throat, the bun. Cora. She was walking alone along the canal under the lanterns, slowly, with her hands behind her, looking at the water, not as if she were going anywhere, but as if she were just walking.

Elsie unlatched the window and leaned out and waved. She waved with both arms over her head, wildly, the way she waved to the caravan drivers coming in at the end of a long day, so hard her bob flew about. "Cora!" she called down, not too loud, because of the neighbours. "Cora! Up here!" Cora stopped. She stood still on the bank under a red lantern and looked up, all the way up the front of the Honeycomb, to the round window under the eaves, and saw her. For a moment Cora simply stood there, looking up, her face pale in the lantern light. Then, after that moment, slowly, she lifted one hand.

Cora walked on a few steps, to the little humped bridge across Thimble Canal, and up onto it, and stopped there at the top, in the middle, under the lanterns, with her hands on the stone parapet. She did not walk on. She stood on the bridge and looked up at the round window, and at Elsie in it, leaning out with her elbows on the sill and her chin in her hands, smiling down. Elsie smiled at her. Cora stood there a long while. The lanterns swayed a little in the evening air and their light moved over her face and away and back. Then she took her hands off the parapet, and went down the far side of the bridge, and walked on along the canal, and did not look back.

At nine the attic was dark, and Elsie lay on the bed in her nightdress with the window open, listening to the canal. And down the Tether, faint and warm at the back of her mind where Tamsin lived now, there was a fizzing. Tamsin was wide awake. Elsie could feel it as plainly as if Tamsin were in the room, walking up and down: a bright, jittery, restless crackle, like a fire with green wood on it, like a dog that has heard the word walk, all up and down the thread. Trolls, Elsie thought, and smiled in the dark. She was thinking of trolls. Wherever she was lying, she was lying there thinking about trolls, and could not stop.

Elsie turned on her side toward the round window. She shut her eyes, and began to hum. She hummed the caravan song, the slow walking one about the lamp in the window, softly, under her breath, and she did not send it down the thread on purpose, exactly; she only hummed it with the thread there, warm, and let it go where it would. She hummed it through once, and again. And slowly, down the Tether, the fizzing changed. The crackle eased. The green-wood spitting settled into something steadier, a warm low glow, like a fire that has caught properly; and the walking up and down slowed, and slowed, and Elsie felt Tamsin, somewhere in the city, lie down.

She got up again, quietly, in the dark, and lit the candle stub. She set her boots by the attic door, side by side, the toes together, ready. Beside them she set her healer's satchel, and opened it, and checked it by the candle: the pot of salve, the clean linen rolled tight, three rolls of bandages, the little scissors, the needle and thread, the willow bark. She put in a fourth roll of bandages from the shelf, for trolls, and buckled it shut. Then she stood the satchel against the boots, and looked at them there together by the door, small and neat and ready for the morning, and her stomach turned over again, the way it had in the guild hall.

There was a tap at the attic door. Elsie jumped. "It's me," said Agnes's voice, gruff, through the boards. "Don't get up." A pause. "I've been to look at your cake under the cloth," Agnes said. "Smells right." Another pause, longer. "Moderate oven," she said, and Elsie heard something in it that might have been a laugh, very small. Then the stairs creaked as she went down. Elsie sat on the edge of the bed in the candlelight with her hands pressed together under her chin and grinned at the door, and said, to the boots and the satchel and the dark, "Smells right," under her breath, as if it were a blessing.
