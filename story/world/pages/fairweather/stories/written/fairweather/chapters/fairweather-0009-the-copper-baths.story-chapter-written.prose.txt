Elsie woke to the bread smell, to warmth, and to something tickling her mouth. She opened her eyes. The attic was still dim, the round window only just turning from black to blue. The warmth was Tamsin, all of her, lying along Elsie's back in the narrow bed with one long freckled arm flung over Elsie's waist and the hand tucked loosely against her stomach. The tickling was Tamsin's curls, a great soft copper mass of them spread over the pillow and over Elsie's face, and one of them was in her mouth. Elsie blew it out. It came back. She lay there and laughed silently into the pillow. She did not move the arm.

Behind her Tamsin's breathing changed. It hitched and went quiet. Elsie felt her come awake all at once against her back: the arm over Elsie's waist went stiff, and the whole long body behind her went still as a plank. There was a pause. The arm lifted, very slowly, off Elsie's waist, and Tamsin sat up in the bed. Elsie rolled over to look at her. Tamsin was sitting bolt upright among the quilt with her curls in a wild bush round her head and the notices still stacked on the stool beside the bed, staring round the attic. As Elsie watched, she went red, from her collar to her hairline.

"I," Tamsin said. "I only meant to read the notices. I didn't mean to—I wasn't going to stay. I just shut my eyes for a moment, at the trolls." She pushed her hair out of her face, and it sprang straight back. "I'm sorry. I've taken your whole bed. You had about a hand's width."

Elsie lay on her back and grinned up at her. "You snore. Did you know that? Like a kettle. A little kettle, just coming to the boil, all night long." She laughed, a real laugh, at the look on Tamsin's face. "And you talk to trolls in your sleep. You told one to get off a bridge."

Tamsin stared at her. She laughed too, helplessly, her face still scarlet, and fell back onto the pillow beside Elsie with her arm over her eyes. "I do not snore," came from under the arm.

"Like a kettle. Wheee."

Tamsin laughed harder. Then she stopped laughing. She lay there and did not get up. She stayed lying on her back beside Elsie in the narrow bed, her arm across her eyes and her shoulder against Elsie's shoulder, quite still, a good while longer than she needed to, while the bread smell came up through the floorboards and the gulls started up over the canal.

At last Elsie got up and went to the crate under the round window. She watered the plants one at a time with the chipped jug, telling each one good morning. Tamsin sat on the edge of the bed with her elbows on her knees, watching. When Elsie came to Sunny, Tamsin got up, came over and crouched down beside the crate. She looked at the little lemon tree with its two green lemons, and cleared her throat, and gave it a small stiff bow from where she crouched. "Good morning, Sunny. Pleased to meet you. Elsie talks about you." Elsie was so delighted she spilled the jug.

Downstairs the bakery was hot and loud with the first trays out. Agnes was at the counter in her floury apron, sliding loaves onto the racks. She looked up as they came down the narrow stairs. She looked at Tamsin, in yesterday's shirt with her curls every which way and the quilt marks still on her cheek. She looked at Elsie. Then she looked back at Tamsin, slowly, for a long time. She kept quiet, very pointedly. She went back to her loaves and slid another one onto the rack. Elsie saw the corner of her mouth move, very slightly, under the flour.

"Two honey buns, please." Elsie counted two copper pips out of her purse onto the counter. Agnes took the pips. She took two honey buns off the tray, glossy and steaming, and twisted each into its paper. Without being asked, she took a third and twisted that into paper too. She set it on the counter in front of Tamsin and pushed it an inch toward her with one floury finger. She did not take a pip for it. Tamsin looked at the bun, and at Agnes. "Thank you?" Agnes grunted and went back to the ovens.

The guild hall was filling as they came in, and Tamsin did not stop at the F-rank end at all. She went straight down the long hall past the F-rank notices and the Leftovers to the E-rank board, with Elsie half running to keep up. She stood in front of it with her copied notices in her fist, holding each one up beside the board to find its twin. "There. Wolves on the north road. Still up. And the barrow. And the trolls, there, look, the trolls are still up." She turned to Elsie with her eyes alight. "We could take the trolls. We could take them today."

A clerk was pinning a new notice to the board beside them, a thin young man with ink on his fingers and a pin in his mouth. He took the pin out and looked at Tamsin's copies, at Tamsin, and at Elsie with her F-rank tag on her belt. "Not as a party, you couldn't. Party takes notices at the rank of its lowest member, miss. That's the rule. One F on the slate and it's F-rank notices for the lot of you." He pushed the pin into the board. "Your friend there's F. So it's F for you, Reyes, while you're with her."

Tamsin's face fell so hard that Elsie wanted to put her arms round her. The clerk saw it too. "Cheer up. It's not for ever. The guild raises an F to E once she's done ten notices well. Ten, signed off, no complaints from the client." He nodded at Elsie. "She does ten, she's E, and you can take your trolls." He tucked the rest of his pins into his cuff and went off down the board. Tamsin turned to look at Elsie, and Elsie looked back at her. At the same moment they both turned and looked at the F-rank end of the hall.

"Ten." Elsie held up her hand and began to count on her fingers, frowning. "The rats under the Crooked Kettle. That's one. The goat. Two. The moonbells. Three." She went on to the other hand. "Pudding the cat, four. The flower cart, five. The glowcaps yesterday, six." She looked at her hands: one whole, one with a thumb tucked down. She did the sum again, to be sure, moving her lips. "Six. That's six. So that's four more." She looked up at Tamsin, and her face lit. "Only four more, Tamsin. Four. That's a week, if we're quick."

"Four what?" Tilly had come up the hall behind them at a trot with a stoppered bottle in each hand, green glass, frosted with cold and fizzing gently, her goggles askew. "What are you counting? You're counting on your fingers, I saw you from the door. Here." She held out one of the bottles to Elsie. "Pear fizz. The new kind, with the pear in later. I made it last night, I made two, one's for you." She pushed it into Elsie's hand. "I brought it to the Honeycomb on the sixth, remember, and you weren't there. So this is that one again. Only better."

Elsie took the cold bottle in both hands and beamed at her. "Thank you, Tilly. Oh, it's cold. Four more notices. Four more, and I'm E-rank. The clerk said. Ten done well and they raise you, and we've done six. Four more and we can take Tamsin's trolls."

Tilly squeaked. She actually squeaked, high and sudden, and jumped. The bottle in her other hand jerked. The stopper came out with a pop, and a great green fountain of fizz shot up out of it and came down all over her apron, her boots and a good part of the floor. Tilly looked down at it. "Oh. Oh no."

The charcoal-painted door opened across the hall, and Cora came out with her ledger under her arm and her black hair drawn back smooth and tight. She came straight through the crowd to them and stepped neatly round the puddle of pear fizz without looking at it. She took her place a step behind Elsie and inclined her head. "Fairweather."

"Good morning, Cora." Elsie turned round to her with the cold bottle against her chest, smiling. "Did you sleep well? You look as if you did. You've got colour. Did you have breakfast? I've got a honey bun if you didn't."

"Well enough. Thank you." Cora did not say whether she had had breakfast. Elsie's eyes went to her coat, to the buttonhole at the throat, where the sprig of lavender had been yesterday, dried and grey and bent but there. It was not there now. The buttonhole was bare, a plain neat slit in the charcoal cloth. The coat was buttoned up past it as straight as ever. Elsie looked at the empty buttonhole a moment longer than she meant to. Cora saw her looking. Cora's grey eyes went to the board. Her jaw set a little, and she did not explain.

"Look, new ones." Tamsin was at the F-rank end now, reading. Three new notices had gone up since yesterday, their pins bright. Elsie read them over her shoulder. Eels in the cistern under the Copper Baths, fouling the pipes, four lanterns. A night watch on a glasshouse of moon orchids, out on the lake road, three lanterns. Fireworks, to be carried out to the festival barge on the Mere, with care, four lanterns. Under the fireworks someone had written in a different hand: do not drop. Tilly, still dripping, read that one twice and went a little pale.

"Eels." Tamsin put her finger on it at once. "That one. Eels."

Elsie looked at her. "Not the orchids?"

"Orchids just sit there. You watch them. All night. In a glasshouse. Watching flowers." Tamsin made a face. "And I'm not carrying fireworks with Tilly in the party, no offence, Tilly."

"None taken," said Tilly.

"Eels." Tamsin's grin came out, crooked and fierce. "Eels move. Eels have heads. Eels can be hit." She pulled the pin out of the notice. "I've wanted to hit something since that vine."

They took the notice to the long desk. The clerk entered the party in his big book, eels, the Copper Baths, the Fairweather party, three names and the hour. He wrote Warden beside Cora's name and stamped it. Beside them Cora wrote it into her ledger at the same moment, in her small exact hand, the date and the place and the names, one under another. Elsie watched her pen go down the page. It was the left hand doing the steadying today, flat on the ledger, and the right doing the writing. The lavender was not in her buttonhole, and Elsie found she kept looking at the place.

The Copper Baths stood on the lake shore at the end of a long stone quay, under a great dome of copper gone green with weather, round and smooth as an upturned bowl, with a little lantern tower on top. Steam curled out of vents all round the base of the dome and drifted off across the water in white wisps. The air outside smelled of hot stone, wet wood and something faintly green, like moss. There were steps up to a pair of tall doors with copper handles shaped like fish. Elsie stood at the foot of the steps and looked up at the steaming dome. She felt her face go warm just from the look of it.

A painted sign hung on the doors on a chain. Elsie read it aloud. "Women's day. Gentlemen are respectfully reminded of the days kept for them." Underneath, someone had chalked: so mind it. Tamsin laughed. Tilly read the sign and read it again. Elsie saw her lips move over the word women's. A slow pink came up Tilly's neck into her face and spread out to the tips of her ears, and she looked very hard at the copper fish on the door handles. "That's nice. That's very. That's sensible. Women's day. That's nice for them."

The bath-keeper met them inside the doors, in a hall full of steam and echoes and the smell of soap: a stout red-faced woman with forearms like hams, in a long rubber apron that squeaked when she moved and rubber boots to the knee. She looked at Tamsin's axe, Tilly's vials, Cora's coat and Elsie's satchel, and nodded, satisfied, as if they would do. "Guild, is it. About time. Come on, then. Down here." She led them through a door marked private and down a twisting iron stair, into the roaring heat of the boiler room.

The boiler room was a black iron cave full of noise: two great round boilers like sleeping beasts, glowing red at their fire doors, and pipes as thick as Elsie's waist running away from them up into the dark ceiling, hissing and knocking. A boy in a vest was shovelling coal. The keeper raised her voice over the din and jerked a thumb at the floor. "Eels. Mere eels. Long as a woman, some of them. They come in through the cistern outflow from the lake, and they get up into the pipes, and they foul them. Last week a pipe burst in the steam room. I had a lady screaming."

She held up one thick hand with a ring of old white scars round the base of the thumb. "They bite. Hard. And when they bite they hold, and they don't let go for asking. But they're slow out of water, slow as slugs on land, and hot water kills them. A bucket from the boiler, they're done." She wiped her hands on her apron, squeak, squeak. "So you go down, and you kill them, and you bring them up, and I'll pay you for every one. Or for the lot, whichever. I'm not fussy. I just want my pipes back."

Elsie looked at the keeper's scarred hand. She thought about the eels down there in the dark, long and slow, that had only come in from the lake. "Do they have to be killed? Could we drive them out instead? Back to the lake? If they came in through the outflow, couldn't they go back out the same way?"

The keeper looked at her as if she were simple. She shrugged her big shoulders, and the apron squeaked. "If you can. Don't care how they go. Out, dead, dancing a jig. Long as they're gone. If you can drive an eel, love, you're welcome to try."

At the back of the boiler room, past the second boiler, a square iron hatch stood open in the floor. An iron ladder went down through it, rung after rung, into the dark. Cold air came up out of the hatch against the heat, smelling of wet stone and lake water and something fishy and rank. From far down there came the sound of water moving, slow, lapping against brick. The keeper set a lantern on the rim of the hatch and stepped back from it. "Cistern. There you go. Mind the bottom rung, it's loose."

Tilly came to the edge of the hatch and looked down the ladder into the dark. Elsie, beside her, felt her go stiff. Tilly's face had gone the colour of tallow. Her hand was at her belt, gripping a vial. She did not say anything. She only looked down the ladder at the dark, and swallowed, and swallowed again. Elsie found her other hand and took it. She squeezed, hard, and held it a moment. "I'll go first. Then you'll know it's all right, because I'll be at the bottom. And I'll hold the ladder for you." She let go and picked up the lantern. Swinging herself over the rim onto the ladder, she went down.

The cistern was a long brick vault, arched and low, black with age and wet. The lantern light shook on water that filled it from wall to wall. Elsie stepped off the bottom rung into it and gasped. It was cold, lake-cold, and it came up over her boots to her knees. At the far end of the vault, where the light only just reached, an iron grate as tall as a door was set into the wall, rusted brown, with daylight coming dimly through its bars from somewhere beyond and the slap of open water. The outflow, Elsie thought. The way to the Mere. She held the ladder, and Tilly came down.

Something touched Elsie's leg. It slid past her shin under the water, long, smooth, cold and muscular. It kept on sliding, and sliding, much longer than anything ought to, before it was gone. She looked down. In the lantern light she saw them: long dark shapes moving slowly through the black water all round their legs, sliding and turning, a dozen of them, more, as long as Elsie was tall, their backs breaking the surface now and then in a slick dark curve. Tilly saw them too. She made a sound like a kettle. She grabbed Elsie's arm in both hands and held on.

Elsie stood still, with Tilly clamped to her arm, watching the eels slide round them. She thought. They were slow. They did not want her. They were only moving, round and round, in the cold dark water, looking for something. Looking for the way out, perhaps, as she would be. Behind her the others came down the ladder. "Tamsin. Tilly. Listen. What if we open that grate, at the end, the outflow? And then make the water behind them warm, here, at this end? So it's nasty here, and nice there, by the lake." She looked at the dim daylight through the bars. "They'll swim for the lake. Won't they? Wouldn't you?"

Tilly let go of Elsie's arm with one hand. The other hand stayed clamped on, but her eyes had changed; they had gone sharp, and thinking. "Warm. I can brew warm. I can brew warm in the water." She was already pulling at her belt with her free hand, feeling for vials by their shapes. "Not killing warm. Not boiling. The keeper said hot kills them, and you don't want them killed, do you, so not that. Just nasty warm. Bath-too-hot warm. The kind of warm that makes you want to get out." She nodded fast. "I can do that. I can do that, I think. Give me a moment."

Tamsin waded off down the vault toward the outflow, Margery on her back, pushing through the black water with long strides. The eels slid out of her way. She reached the grate and took hold of two of its bars, one in each fist. She set her boots and pulled. It did not move. She pulled harder, and Elsie saw her shoulders bunch and the cords stand out in her arms. With a long shrieking scream of rusted iron that rang round the whole vault and set Elsie's teeth on edge, the grate swung open on its hinges. Fresh daylight and the sound of the lake came pouring in.

Tilly had two vials in her hand, and a third between her teeth. She mixed them in the neck of the first, swirling it, her lips moving. Elsie saw her hands were shaking. The mixture caught and fizzed, white and furious, and Tilly's hands went steady. She waded to the far end of the vault, the end behind them, by the ladder, and dropped the fizzing vial into the water there. It sank, glowing faintly. For a moment the water lay black and still. Then the water round it began to steam: a soft white breath rising off the black surface, spreading. Elsie felt the warmth come creeping through the water against her legs.

The eels felt it. All down the vault the long dark shapes stirred and turned, and the water began to churn. They thrashed, slapping the surface, tails flailing up out of the black and coming down again with a crack like wet rope. The whole cistern was suddenly full of noise and spray and writhing bodies. As Elsie watched, they began to turn the same way. One after another, the long backs bent toward the far end of the vault, toward the open grate, the daylight and the cold clean water of the lake. They went, sliding and surging past Tamsin's legs and out through the outflow.

All but one. Elsie saw it come out of the churn: a big one, bigger than the rest, its back as broad as her arm. It had turned the wrong way. Away from the grate. Away from the lake. It came down the vault toward the warm end, toward the ladder, straight at her, fast now, faster than she would have believed, its blunt head low in the water and a ripple running before it like the bow of a boat. Elsie saw its eye in the lantern light, small and pale and stupid. She saw its mouth open. She stood there, knee-deep in the water, and could not seem to move her feet.

And Cora stepped in front of her. Elsie did not see her come. One moment there was the eel, the ripple and the open mouth. The next there was the charcoal coat, between, its back to Elsie, so close it brushed her. Cora had put up her arm. The eel struck it. It came up out of the water in a great black glistening loop and bit down on Cora's forearm, through the coat sleeve, and held on. The long body thrashed, twisted and slapped the water, its whole weight hanging from her arm. Cora staggered, caught herself, and stood.

Cora was silent. That was the thing Elsie would remember after: that she did not cry out, or gasp, or say a word. She went white. Elsie, behind her, saw it go across the side of her face like a cloud going over: white to the lips, so white the dark brows stood out like ink. Her jaw set hard. She stood with her bitten arm held out and the great eel hanging from it, writhing, the water churning round her boots. She did not try to pull it off, and she did not let go of the ledger in her other hand. She only stood between Elsie and it, perfectly silent, her jaw set.

Tamsin came down the vault through the water like a bull. She did not swing Margery; the vault was too low, and the eel was round Cora's arm. She grabbed the eel instead, with both bare hands, behind its head, where the body was thick as a man's wrist and slick as soap, and held it. She worked her thumbs in at the hinge of its jaw, either side, and pushed. Elsie saw the muscles stand up in her arms. The eel thrashed. Tamsin grunted, set her teeth and pushed, and pushed. The jaws came open, slowly, slowly, and let go of Cora's arm.

Elsie was there. She did not remember moving. She was simply there, beside Tamsin, and she had the eel's tail end in both arms, the cold slick heavy length of it, thrashing against her chest. It was strong. It slapped her face and her shoulder and nearly had her off her feet. Elsie hung on. "Together!" Tamsin nodded. The two of them carried it between them, writhing and flailing, Tamsin at the head and Elsie at the tail, down the length of the vault through the water toward the open grate. They staggered, the eel twisting in their arms like a live rope.

At the grate they swung it, one, two, and let go. The eel shot out through the bars into the open water like an arrow from a bow, a long dark streak, and was gone into the lake. Behind them, as if that had settled it, the last few eels that had been circling in the cistern came after it. They slid past Elsie's legs one after another, unhurried now, and out through the outflow into the Mere, the last tail flicking at the bars as it went. Elsie leaned on the wall, gasping, soaked to the skin and slimed down her whole front, and watched the water go still.

Tamsin took hold of the grate and swung it shut. It went with the same long rusted scream and clanged home against its frame with a boom that rolled round the vault. She dropped the latch over it and leaned on it, breathing hard, and laughed. The cistern was empty. The water lay still and black from wall to wall. Slowly, from the ladder end, Tilly's warmth came spreading down the vault through it: Elsie felt it reach her legs, a soft swirl of warm water round her knees, like stepping into a bath that had been run for her. A thin steam lay on the surface. Tilly, by the ladder, let out a breath.

Elsie turned round. Cora was standing where she had been, by the ladder, with her bitten arm held against the front of her coat and her other hand still holding the ledger, quite still. Her face was white. The sleeve of her charcoal coat was torn at the forearm in a ragged ring, dark, and wet with more than water. A thin thread of red was running down over her knuckles and dripping off her fingertips into the warm water, where it spread and faded. She was not looking at her arm. She was looking at the far wall, with her jaw set.

Elsie waded to her. "Cora. You stepped in front of me." Her own voice shook. "You stepped right in front of me. It was coming for me, and you just—you put your arm up, and it bit you instead."

Cora did not look at her. She looked at the wall, then at the water, then at the ladder, anywhere but at Elsie's face. "It was my duty. A Warden keeps her charge from harm. That's all." She shifted the ledger in her good hand. "It's what I'm for." She would not meet Elsie's eyes.

Up in the boiler room, in the roaring heat and the red light from the fire doors, Elsie sat Cora down on an upturned coal bucket and knelt in front of her on the iron floor. "Let me see." Cora let her. She held out her bitten arm without a word. Elsie took it gently by the wrist. The coat sleeve was buttoned at the cuff, three small black buttons. Elsie undid them, one, two, three, carefully, and folded back the cuff. She rolled the sleeve up slowly, fold over fold, past the torn place, past the bite, up to the elbow, and laid the arm across her own knee.

The bite was a ring of small neat punctures, round as a cup's mouth, dozens of them, close-set, in the pale skin of Cora's forearm, each one welling slow and red. Not deep, Elsie thought. Not torn. It had held, but it had not shaken. She opened her satchel and took out the clean cloth and the brown jar of salve, the good one. She wet the cloth from her water flask. She cleaned the bite with it, gently, every puncture, wiping the blood away and wiping it again as it welled. She opened the salve and smoothed it on with her fingertip, round and round the ring.

Cora's bare forearm lay across Elsie's knee in the red light. It was pale, paler than the rest of her that Elsie had seen, as if it had been buttoned away from the sun all its life. It was fine-boned and slender, with a delicate wrist, the blue of veins showing at the inside of it, and soft dark down along the skin. Elsie held it by the wrist to keep it still. Under her thumb, on the inside of Cora's wrist, she felt Cora's pulse. It was going fast. As Elsie's thumb settled on it, it jumped, hard, and went faster. Poor Cora, Elsie thought. It must hurt more than she's letting on.

Elsie bandaged it. She took a roll of clean bandage from her satchel. She wound it round Cora's forearm slowly and carefully, over and round, each turn laid half over the last, firm but not tight, the way her mother had taught her, smoothing each turn flat with her palm before she made the next. She did not hurry. She tucked the end in neatly at the inside of the elbow. All the time, Elsie could feel it, Cora was watching her hands. Not the bandage: her hands. Every turn, every smoothing, Cora's grey eyes followed Elsie's fingers, close, intent and unmoving, as if she were learning them by heart.

When it was done, Elsie held the bandaged arm a moment in both hands, looking at her work. It was neat. Bending her head, she kissed the bandage, lightly, once, just over the bite, the way her mother used to kiss Elsie's grazed knees and cut fingers when she was small, in the wagon, after the salve and the binding, to finish it off. She straightened up, satisfied, and smiled. Cora had gone very still. Not the stillness of before, of bracing: a different stillness, as if she had stopped breathing altogether. Her arm lay in Elsie's hands without moving. Her grey eyes were on the place where Elsie's mouth had been.

"What," Cora said at last, "was that for?"

Elsie looked up at her, surprised. "The kiss? It helps a hurt mend. My mother swore by it. Salve, bandage, and a kiss on top. The salve does the healing and the bandage does the keeping, and the kiss tells the hurt it's been seen to, so it can stop fussing." She patted the bandage gently. "She was a healer. She'd know."

Cora looked at her. She opened her mouth and closed it. She looked down at her own arm, at the white bandage, for a long moment.

There was a clang from the back of the boiler room. The keeper had gone down the ladder a few rungs, with her lantern, and was peering into the cistern below. Now she came up again fast, squeaking, her red face split in a huge grin. "Clean! Clear as you like! Not an eel in it. Water's warm, too, warm as the pool, I'll be blowed, how'd you do that?" She clapped her big hands together and let out a whoop that rang off the boilers, and the boy with the shovel looked round, startled. "You drove them. You actually drove the beggars out. I didn't think it could be done."

She paid them there and then, out of a leather purse at her belt: four silver lanterns, slapped down one after another on top of the nearest boiler pipe, where it was cool enough to touch. "And worth every pip." Elsie gathered them up. She gave one to Tamsin and one to Tilly, and kept one. She held out the fourth to the keeper. "Could you change this one? Into pips?"

"Can do." The keeper counted ten copper pips into her palm.

Elsie gave Tamsin three, kept three for herself, and gave Tilly three. One copper pip was left in her hand.

There in the boiler room, with the pip still in her palm, a window opened in the air in front of Elsie's face. She had not wished for it. It simply came, small and clear and steady, its light falling on her hands.

Tamsin was already halfway up the stair with her boot in her hand.

Cora had closed her ledger. She stood up off the coal bucket, slowly, and tucked the ledger under her good arm. She straightened her torn sleeve down over the bandage as far as it would go. She looked at the keeper, at Elsie, at the boiler. "I'm on duty. I'll wait in the hall. I'll wait in the entrance hall until you're done, Fairweather, and then I'll walk you to the guild." She inclined her head and turned toward the stair, very straight, with her bandaged arm against her coat.

"But you can watch me just as well from the pool. Better, really. You'd be right there. You can't watch me from the entrance hall, Cora, there's a wall in the way." Elsie reached out and took Cora's good hand, the one that was not bandaged, the one with the ledger under that arm, by the fingers. She held it and gave it a little tug toward the stair. "And the warm water will be good for your arm. It will. My mother said warm water for a bite, after. Come on."

Cora looked down at the ledger under her arm. She looked at it for a moment, as if it might tell her what to do, then at Elsie, holding her hand and smiling up at her, soaked to the skin and slimed with eel, with her clip crooked and her face full of hope. Elsie saw something go across Cora's face, quick, and settle. Cora did not say yes. She did not say anything at all. But when Elsie tugged her hand again, toward the stair, she came. She let herself be pulled along, step after step, up the iron stair after Tamsin, her fingers held loosely in Elsie's, and did not take them back.

The great pool lay under the copper dome like a jewel in a bowl. It was long and wide, tiled all over in green, every shade of green, from pale leaf to deep bottle, so that the water looked green as a summer pond, and warm, steaming softly. Above it the inside of the dome rose high, round and dim, coppery-gold, with light falling down from the lantern at its top in long slanting shafts through the steam. The air was hot and wet. It smelled of minerals and soap. Everywhere there were women, in shifts, in the water, on the steps and round the edges, talking and laughing, their voices echoing up into the dome.

Elsie changed in the steamy changing room, hanging her halter, skirt, belt and boots on a hook. She came out in her plain white shift and went down the green-tiled steps into the water. It was warm. It was so warm and so soft after the cistern that she sighed out loud, a long sigh, and sank in up to her chin. She let herself go back, and float. She lay on her back on the green water with her arms out, her eyes shut and her hair fanning out round her head. The wet shift clung to her, the steam drifted over her face, the voices echoed far above, and she smiled.

There was a great splash beside her, and a wave washed over her face. Elsie spluttered and opened her eyes. Tamsin had dived in from the side and come up right beside her, shaking her wet curls out of her eyes, laughing. "Did you see the eel's face when we threw it? It looked so offended, it looked like a—" She stopped. She stopped laughing in the middle of a word and looked at Elsie, floating there on her back in the wet white shift. She stared: a long open stare, her lips parted, the water dripping off her chin. Very suddenly and very hard, she looked up at the ceiling of the dome, as if there were something up there she needed urgently to see.

Tilly had not got into the water. She was sitting on the pool steps in her shift, on the second step down, with the water up to her chin. Her knees were drawn up under the shift and her arms wrapped round them, so that only her head showed, round and pink above the surface. She still had her brass goggles on her head, pushed up on her damp hair. Her face was crimson. She was looking very carefully at the water just in front of her nose, not at Elsie, not at Tamsin, not at any of the women in the pool. She was holding very still.

Elsie rolled over and paddled across to the steps. "Tilly. You've still got your goggles on. In the bath." Tilly looked up at her, startled, and put a hand up to her head. Elsie reached up out of the water and lifted the goggles off Tilly's damp hair, gently, untangling a strand that had caught in the strap. She climbed half out to set them on the warm tiles at the edge, safe, with the lenses up. "There. They'll be right there. I'll keep an eye on them." She slid back into the water beside Tilly on the step. "Now. Your hair's still all in braids. Turn round."

Tilly turned round on the step, slowly, with her back to Elsie. Elsie took the end of one braid and untied it. She began to undo it, working the plait loose with her fingers all the way up the long fair rope of it to the nape of Tilly's neck, then the other, until Tilly's hair lay loose down her back in the water, crimped from the braids and singed at the ends. Elsie scooped up warm water in her cupped hands and poured it over Tilly's head, and again. She began to wash her hair, working her fingers slowly through it from the scalp down, gently, the way her mother had washed hers in a river. She hummed while she did it.

Tilly sat perfectly still. She sat on the step with the warm water lapping at her chin and her loose hair floating out round her shoulders. She did not move at all while Elsie's fingers went through it. Her eyes were shut. Elsie, leaning round to pour more water, saw that they were squeezed shut, tight, and that Tilly was breathing very carefully: slow, measured breaths, in and out, as if she were counting them, or as if she had to concentrate to remember how. Her shoulders under the wet shift were stiff as a board. But she did not pull away. When Elsie stopped for a moment, Tilly's head tipped back, just a little, after her hands.

Tamsin was swimming lengths. She swam up the long green pool and back with a strong lazy overarm, her copper head going under and coming up, under and up, the water streaming off her bare freckled shoulders. Up to the far end, turn, and back. Every time she came back, Elsie noticed, she ended the length a little nearer to the steps. At last she was finishing each one right beside them, stopping there and hanging on to the edge with her chin on her arms, getting her breath, a yard from Elsie, before she pushed off again. She did not say anything. She only kept coming back.

Cora came out of the changing room last. Elsie, still washing Tilly's hair, looked up at the sound of the door, and her hands stopped. Cora was in a long plain shift, white, to her ankles, with her bandaged arm held against her and her bare feet pale on the green tiles. Her hair was down. Elsie had only seen it pinned. It fell from the crown of her head straight, black and glossy all the way past her shoulders, past her shoulder blades, past the small of her back, to her waist, a long black shining fall of it, swinging a little as she walked, like a curtain of dark silk.

"Cora!" Elsie called it right across the pool, over all the voices, her hands still in Tilly's hair. "Cora, your hair! Oh, look at it! It goes all the way down!" Her voice rang up into the dome. "It's like a waterfall at night. I'd no idea you had all that, all this time, put away in a bun. Oh, you should let it down every day. It's beautiful. You look so different with it down, Cora, honestly, like ink poured out." She beamed across the water, delighted, with soap on her hands.

Half the women in the pool turned to look. They turned in the water and on the steps, heads coming round all over the great green pool, to see who the girl was shouting about. They saw Cora, standing at the edge in her long white shift with her black hair down to her waist. Someone murmured. Someone else laughed, kindly. Cora stood frozen for one moment under all of it, white, and then red, a deep slow red that came up her throat and over her face to the roots of her hair. She walked straight down the green steps into the pool, steadily, without stopping, until the water was up to her shoulders and her hair floated out black around her.

Elsie gave Tilly's hair a last pour of water. She swam over to her across the pool in a slow breaststroke and came up in front of her where she stood in the water to her shoulders. "Does it sting? The water? On your arm? I didn't think, it might sting, with the salt from the minerals."

Cora was holding her bandaged arm up out of the water, crooked against her chest, dry. Her face was still pink. She looked at Elsie, and away, at the steam, and back. "No. It's only warm." A pause. "It's very warm." She let her eyes close for a moment.

The steam room was small and round and tiled in white, with a slatted wooden bench running all the way round the wall. The steam was so thick in the air that Elsie could hardly see the far side, only shapes. It smelled of hot cedar and eucalyptus. The four of them sat on the bench wrapped in big rough white towels the keeper had brought, up under their arms, their wet hair dripping. The heat was enormous. It pressed on Elsie's face and shoulders like a hand. Her skin went pink at once and beaded, and sweat ran down between her shoulder blades under the towel. She leaned back against the hot tiles and sighed.

Elsie sat between Tamsin and Cora on the bench. There was not much room. Tamsin was on her left and Cora on her right, close. Elsie's bare damp shoulders, above her towel, touched both of theirs at once: Tamsin's warm, freckled and slick with steam on one side, Cora's cool, pale and very smooth on the other. It was lovely, Elsie thought. Like being tucked in. She wriggled a little, to settle, and her shoulders slid against both of theirs. She felt both of them go still at the same moment, as if they had been startled. She smiled with her eyes closed and leaned back.

Tamsin's thigh was pressed against Elsie's under the towels. All along its length, from hip to knee, warm, solid and damp, Tamsin's long leg lay against Elsie's leg. The towels had ridden up so that above the knee there was only skin on skin. Tamsin did not move it away. She sat with her back against the tiles, her head tipped back and her eyes shut. Elsie, turning her head on the tiles, saw that her chest was rising and falling fast under the towel, quick and shallow, as if she had just run up all the stairs in the guild hall. The steam, Elsie thought. It is very hot. She patted Tamsin's knee.

On her other side Cora sat bolt upright, as straight as on any bench in the guild hall, with her towel tucked tight under her arms and her black hair hanging down her back in a wet rope. Her eyes were fixed on the steam in front of her. She did not lean back against the tiles. She held herself so still that she might have been carved. Her bandaged arm lay along her own thigh, and her hand rested by her knee. Her fingers, Elsie saw, were a finger's width from Elsie's bare knee. Not touching. A finger's width away. They stayed exactly there. They did not move closer, and did not move away.

Tilly sat opposite, on the far side of the round room, alone on the bench in her towel, a small pink shape in the steam with her loose hair crimped down her back. She was fanning herself with both hands, fast, flapping them in front of her face. "It's very hot." The others were too hot to answer. Tilly looked across the steam at the three of them on the far bench, Elsie in the middle with her shoulders against the other two, and fanned harder. "It's very hot in here. Isn't it? The steam. It's very, very hot." She fanned herself so hard her hair blew.

Elsie opened her eyes and looked up at the white dome of the little room, beaded with steam, and felt warm all the way through, down to her toes. "I want every morning to be like this one. Eels and all. I want to keep it. I want to fold it up and put it in my notebook like the lavender." She let her head fall sideways, without thinking, the way she would have with her mother, and leaned it on Cora's shoulder: her damp cheek on Cora's cool bare shoulder, above the towel, and her wet pink hair against Cora's neck. She sighed and settled, and shut her eyes again.

Cora stopped breathing. Elsie felt it through her cheek. The shoulder under her face went rigid. The slow rise and fall of Cora's breath simply stopped and stayed stopped, one moment, two, three, so long that Elsie nearly lifted her head to see if something was wrong. Then, very slowly, Cora let her breath out. It went out of her in a long unsteady sigh that Elsie felt all down her side. Slower still, as if each part of the movement had to be decided on, Cora tipped her head over, a little, and a little more, until it came to rest against Elsie's, her cheek on Elsie's wet hair.

The cold plunge was a deep square stone tank at the end of the steam room passage, full to the brim with water so clear and so cold it looked blue. Tamsin went in first, feet first, with a roar. Elsie went in after her and came up gasping and laughing with every inch of her skin on fire. Tilly stood on the edge in her towel and dithered. Tamsin reached up and pulled her in by the ankle. Tilly went under, came up and shrieked. She shrieked so loud and so long, a pure high piercing scream that rang off the stone, that the keeper came running down the passage in her squeaking boots with a bucket in her hand. "What is it? Eels?"

Afterwards, dressed again, they sat on the warm stone steps outside the doors of the Copper Baths in the noon sun, with their hair drying and their skin tingling and pink. Elsie's halter was back on and laced, her skirt, belt and boots, her clip in her damp hair. Cora sat a step above her with a comb and a handful of pins, and was putting her hair back up. She did it without a mirror, quickly and exactly, twisting the long black rope of it up and round into the low tight bun. She pinned it, pin after pin, until there was not a single strand loose, not one, and the waterfall was gone.

Elsie watched the last pin go in. "Oh. Oh, that's a shame." She looked at the neat severe bun where all that black hair had been. "It was so beautiful down. It's like putting a lovely thing back in a box." She sighed. "Will you let it down again sometime? Just for me to see?" Cora did not answer. She slid the last pin home, lowered her hands and sat looking out at the lake, very straight, with her face composed. But Elsie, a step below, could see the backs of her ears, and the backs of her ears had gone pink.

Cora stood up and tucked the ledger under her good arm. "I have my report to write. For the guildmaster. On the Copper Baths." She looked down at the three of them on the steps, Elsie, Tamsin and Tilly, damp and pink and sun-warmed. Something in her face eased and then set again. "Fairweather." She inclined her head, the small exact bow. She went off along the stone quay toward the city, very straight-backed in her charcoal coat with the torn sleeve. As she went, Elsie saw, she had one hand resting lightly over the bandage on her arm, as if keeping something there.

At the Tipsy Heron, across the canal from the guild hall, Tamsin went up to the counter before either of the others could move and slapped down one silver lantern and two copper pips. "Three stews. Big ones. With bread." She turned round to Elsie and Tilly at the table, grinning, with her damp curls springing up as they dried. "On me. To celebrate eels. To celebrate driving eels. To celebrate eels being politely asked to leave." When the bowls came she set one in front of each of them. She sat down and ate like a wolf.

Tilly had been counting again, on her fingers, between spoonfuls. "That's seven. Seven notices, now, with the eels. So that's three from E." She held up three fingers. "Three." She put down her spoon and frowned. She counted again on the other hand, slower. "And the blue house. The blue-doored house, on Willow Canal. The agent said four suns. Four suns in gold, to start." She looked at the three fingers on one hand and the four on the other. "Four suns. That's a lot of suns."

Tamsin mopped her bowl with her bread and considered it. "Four suns is eighty lanterns. And eels pay four lanterns a notice." She chewed. "Between three. So that's—" She stopped and looked at the ceiling. She moved her lips, and gave up. "That's a great many eels. That's every eel in the Mere. That's eels till we're old. We'd have to drive eels out of every cistern in Lanternmere and then go round again and start on the fish." She put the bread in her mouth and grinned round it. "That's a great many eels, Elsie."

Elsie held her spoon up in the air. "Then we'll be very good at eels. We'll be famous for eels. People will come from all over the city. They'll say, send for the Fairweather party, they're ever so good with eels. And they'll pay us extra." She nodded, satisfied. Tilly laughed. It burst out of her all at once, through a mouthful of stew. She laughed so hard into her bowl that she snorted and had to be thumped on the back by Tamsin, and laughed again.

In the afternoon they walked back by Willow Canal, without anybody saying that was where they were going. The willows trailed their long green hair in the water. The narrow old houses leaned together along the bank, and halfway along, there it was: the blue door with its fish knocker, the empty window boxes, the roses spilling over the garden wall, the apple tree. Elsie's heart turned over. The white card was still in the front window. To let. It had not been taken down. Elsie let out her breath, and Tilly, beside her, did the same.

Elsie went up to the blue door. She did not knock. She stood in front of it a moment. She reached out and touched it, very gently, with two fingers, on the sun-warmed blue paint just below the fish, and held them there, the way a person might touch somebody's hand in passing. The paint was chipped under her fingertips and warm from the afternoon. "There you are. Wait for us. We're coming. It might take a while. But we're coming." Behind her Tamsin cleared her throat and looked at the canal.

At the end of Willow Canal they stopped. "I'll walk Tilly back," Tamsin said. "To Pickle Row. It's on my way, near enough." It was not on her way, Elsie knew, not near enough or any way at all, but she did not say so. Tilly looked at Tamsin, surprised, then pleased, then shy about being pleased. They went off together along the canal toward the back streets, Tamsin long and loping and Tilly trotting to keep up, talking. Elsie turned the other way and walked home along the water on her own, in the afternoon sun, humming, with her hair dry at last and smelling faintly of the baths.

Up in the attic Elsie hung her damp shift over the sill to dry. She sat down on the bed with the little blue notebook on her knee, and the pencil, and turned to the page of names. She went down it to Cora: black, strong, one sugar; ate the bun on the stair; quiet. Under quiet, she wrote, in her careful round hand, slowly: Stepped in front of me. She looked at it for a long time. She thought of the charcoal coat between her and the open mouth, and Cora's face going white, and not a sound out of her. She pressed the pencil hard and went over the words again, so they would not fade.

Under that, after a moment, she wrote: Cora's hair down. She looked at those words too. She thought of the long black shining fall of it past Cora's waist, Cora walking into the water with her face red, and her cheek coming down at last onto Elsie's wet hair in the steam. Elsie smiled. Beside the words, without quite deciding to, she drew a little heart. It came out wobbly, lopsided, one side bigger than the other, because the pencil was blunt and her hand was not quite steady. She looked at it. She thought about rubbing it out, and did not. She shut the notebook on it.

At dusk the lamplighters' boats came along Thimble Canal below the round window. Elsie knelt on the bed with her elbows on the sill and watched them come: the first boat sliding out of the blue evening with a woman in the stern, poling slow, and the long pole with the flame at its tip in her other hand. At each paper lantern on the towpath she stopped and reached up, and the lantern caught and glowed, red or gold or white, one at a time. She poled on to the next. Lantern by lantern the canal lit up below, until the whole length of it shone, and the light lay shaking on the water.

She lit the candle and got out her paper and pen. Dear Mum, she wrote. Today we went to the Copper Baths, which is a bathhouse by the lake under a great green dome, because there were eels in the cistern. We didn't kill them, Mum. We let them go. Tilly made the water warm and Tamsin opened the grate and they all swam back out to the lake, every one. One of them bit Cora, who is my Warden. She stood in front of me so it bit her instead. I cleaned it and bandaged it and kissed it better, like you used to. Then we had the pools for free, and Cora let her hair down.

Later, in the dark, with the candle out, Elsie lay on her back and felt for the thread. Tamsin was at the far end of it, somewhere across the city. But she was not asleep, and she was not easy. Something was coming down the thread from her that Elsie had not felt before: a quiet, restless thing, turning and turning, like someone lying awake in a bed that will not get comfortable, or pacing a small room. It was not pain. It was not the red. It was only that Tamsin could not seem to settle. The not-settling came down the thread and into Elsie's chest, and would not let her settle either.

Elsie got up. She knelt on the bed by the round window and leaned her forehead against the cool glass, with the lit canal below. She began to hum. She hummed low and slow, the caravan song, the one Rosa had sung on the barge. She hummed it down the thread as if Tamsin could hear: verse after verse, round again to the start. For a long while the restlessness at the far end went on turning. Then, a little at a time, it slowed. It turned less, and then less, like a dog going round and round before it lies down. Elsie kept humming, her forehead on the glass, and felt it ease.
