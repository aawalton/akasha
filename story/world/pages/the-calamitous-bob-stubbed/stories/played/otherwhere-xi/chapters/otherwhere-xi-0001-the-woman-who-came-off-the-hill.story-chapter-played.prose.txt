The last thing you remember is your back window. A book open on your knee, the evening going blue outside the glass, your eyes getting heavy.

Then cold, wet grass against your cheek, and a smell of earth and something like old smoke.

You open your eyes.

Grey stone. A tall slab of it, a few feet from your face, rough and spotted with pale green lichen. Beyond it, another. And another. They stand in a ring around you, each one taller than a man, leaning a little, with nothing on top of them but sky. The sky is pale, washed-out blue, the sun not up yet.

You push yourself up, and your arms are wrong.

They are thin. The wrists are narrow, the hands small and pale, a few freckles across the backs. You turn them over. They do what you tell them, a half second late, as if they are still learning how.

Hair falls across your face when you look down. Long, heavy, dark red. You pull it back, and it keeps going, past your shoulders, past your shoulder blades.

Your shirt is your shirt. The old dark grey one, but it hangs off you now, the collar sliding off one shoulder, the hem down near the middle of your thighs. Your black tights are still on, soaked through at the knees. Your feet are bare, and small.

Your chest is not your chest. You look, once, and then you look at the stones instead.

You get up. The ground is lower than it should be.

In the middle of the ring sits a flat grey stone, waist high, with a picture cut into its top: a door, and beside it a key. Someone has left things on it. A green copper coin. A dried flower, brown at the edges. A crust of bread with something's tooth marks in it.

Outside the ring, the hill falls away. A road, two wheel ruts and a strip of grass, curls down the slope and into a valley full of white mist. Where the mist thins, you can see roofs. A few of them, dark and sharp-edged, and a thread of smoke going straight up.

Birds are starting somewhere below. Your breath shows in the air. Your feet are going numb in the wet grass.

The coin on the stone catches the first of the light.

You set off toward the roofs to find someone.

The ring opens on the road side between two of the stones. Just outside the gap a ninth stone lies fallen outward, half sunk in the grass, lichen on the side that used to face the sky.

The road is two wet ruts with a strip of grass down the middle. The ruts are full of small sharp stones, so you walk the grass.

Downhill is harder than it should be. Your stride comes out short, every time, and the ground arrives before you expect it. Twice the slope takes you by surprise and you pitch forward, arms out, and catch yourself a step later than you meant to.

Juniper and gorse crowd the slope on both sides, and thyme grows low in the grass strip. It crushes under your bare feet, and the smell comes up sharp.

The mist thins as you go down. More roofs come out of it, flat ones, a few at first and then a spread of them, and more threads of hearth smoke standing up into the still air.

Your feet ache with the cold now, deep in the bones. The wet tights sit cold against your knees at every step.

From below, past a bend where the road turns around the hill, comes a knocking of bells. Sheep bells. Then a dog starts barking, hard and fast.

The dog comes round the bend first: shaggy, grey, rough-coated. It stops in the middle of the road with its legs planted and its hackles up, barking at you.

Sheep follow it round the bend, goats among them, bells clanking. Behind them walks a boy. Fifteen, maybe, lanky, in a wool cloak, a shepherd's crook in one hand.

He sees you and stops dead. His free hand goes to his belt, where a sling hangs looped.

The sun clears the ridge to the east. Light runs down the road and lies between the two of you.

"Smoke! Down," he snaps.

The dog drops flat in the road. It keeps growling, low in its chest.

The boy's eyes go to your bare feet, then up your legs in the black tights. His face goes red. He looks at your face instead, and keeps his eyes there.

He glances past you, up the road to the ring of stones on the hilltop, then back at you.

"Sardanal keep you," he says. He stays where he is, well back.

The words aren't English. They aren't in any language you've ever heard. You understand them as plainly as if they were.

Behind him the flock spills onto the verge and starts cropping the wet grass.

"Excuse me, sir," you say. "What are those stones behind me?"

The words come out in his language. They are sounds you never learned, and your mouth shapes them as easily as English.

The boy blinks at the "sir." Then he grins, and one of his front teeth is chipped.

"Sir!" His voice cracks on it. "I'm Tobin. Tobin Ashlar. I don't get sirred." His hand comes away from the sling.

He comes a few steps nearer. He's sunburnt, with a mop of black curls, and the cloak is a sheepskin, worn wool side in.

"That's the Old Waystone. Maradoc's ring." He stares at you. "Don't you have waystones where you're from?"

He goes on before you can answer. "Travellers stop there for a safe road. You touch the key on the altar."

"Who raised it is anyone's guess. Mam says Maradoc did, and that it's older than the Old Empire."

"The one lying down fell in a storm when my grandfather was little."

"It hums sometimes, on still nights. Smoke won't go inside it. The dogs all stop at the stones."

His eyes run over you again, from your bare feet up to your red hair. The blush climbs back up his neck. He keeps talking anyway.

"Did you sleep up there? Travellers do, for Maradoc's keeping. Where are your shoes?"

"Were you robbed? Was it the soldiers from the old tower? Are you from Param?"

Smoke creeps forward on its belly and sniffs at your bare toes.

"The Old Empire..." you say. "Is that the one that's overrun by the dead? Where am I, precisely? I think these waystones may have taken me much farther than most."

Tobin's eyes go wide. "Harrak? You know about Harrak?"

"That's over the sea, in Param. The dead took it before anyone's grandmother was born."

"Joss the carter says a witch-queen rules the dead lands now. He hears things in Imra."

He points his crook at you, pleased. "So you are from Param. I knew it."

"Here's the Tavel valley. Asmirel. Prince Tavaris's land, may Sardanal bless him."

"Tavelford's down there, where the smoke is. Imra's two days on, down the river."

Then the rest of what you said seems to catch up with him. The grin goes.

He looks up the road at the ring, then back at you. "Waystones don't take anybody anywhere."

"They're shrines. You touch the key, and then you walk. On your own feet." He sounds less sure than he did.

Smoke gives up on your toes and sits down against your shin, warm and heavy, leaning.

"Mam knows all the old tales. She'd know if..." He stops and chews his lip.

"Our farm's the first house down the road. I've got the flock till noon, but Mam's home."

He points with the crook. Far below, a stone house and a sheepfold are coming out of the mist.

"Would you take me to talk to your Mam?"

Tobin looks at the flock spread along the verge, then down the hill at the farm, and scratches at his curls.

"I'm not meant to leave them." Then, fast: "Smoke can hold them. An hour, anyway."

He crouches by the dog. "Smoke. Keep them."

The weight leaves your shin. Smoke trots off up the road to the flock.

You go down the road together, Tobin walking an arm's length off to one side.

He talks the whole way. He tells you whose terrace is whose, and which ridge the scalehounds come down off in spring. He asks you things and answers them himself. "You've got sheep in Param? You must. Everyone does."

Lower down, the grass strip thins out. The road stones bite into your soles, and your feet begin to throb.

The sun climbs. The wet knees of your tights start to steam.

Barefoot, you're slow. It is the better part of an hour before you reach the house: low, stone, with a blue door. Along the yard wall a pen of hurdles is loud with ewes and new lambs.

Two small girls are feeding hens in the yard. They stop dead and stare at you, mouths open.

The blue door opens. A tall, rawboned woman steps out with a stick in her hand. She wears a faded red robe and a brown headcloth, and grey threads her dark hair.

"Mam, she came down from the Old Waystone," Tobin blurts. "She's from Param. She knows Harrak."

The woman looks at your bare feet, at the tights, at your loose red hair. Her mouth goes flat.

She glances up the hill toward the ring. Her hand tightens on the stick.

She goes back inside without a word. When she comes out she has a folded brown cloth, the same as the one on her head.

She holds it out to you, still without a word.

Then, flat, to her son: "Tobin. The flock."

He goes red and stays where he is.

The woman turns her eyes on you. "What do you want at my door?"

"I'm sorry to have pulled your boy from his duties, Ma'am," you say. "He mentioned that you knew the old stories, so I have come seeking your wisdom. For much truth is preserved only in old stories. What do you know of the Waystones? Are there tales of travellers who arrive at them not by any road?"

The woman's eyes go to her son. "He pulled himself." Then: "Flock. Now."

Tobin goes. He jogs back up the road, and looks over his shoulder twice.

The headcloth is still held out. The woman steps in and lays it over your hair herself. She tugs it straight at your brow with two hard fingers, and steps back.

The two girls have crept up behind her, the hens forgotten. They stare at the red hair showing under the cloth.

"There are tales," the woman says, flat. "My grandmother's."

"A stray steps out of a waystone at dawn. No road dust on her feet."

Her eyes drop to your feet, grey with the road's dust now, and come back up.

"They speak the tongue of wherever they land. Never learned it."

"One came out at a waystone near Imra, in my grandmother's grandmother's day. A man. He stayed a year. Mended the mill. Walked off one dawn and wasn't seen again."

"Maradoc doesn't waste a stray, Gran used to say. He sets each one where there's work wants doing."

For a moment her eyes go to the lambing pen along the wall, loud with ewes.

"The wayfarer priest comes through each spring. Soon. He'd know more than me."

She leans on the stick. "Or it's a tale, and you're a runaway with a clever story."

"Which are you?"

"A runaway would be dressed more practically than this," you say. "I think you know that."

You hold your hands up, empty. No bundle, no cloak, no shoes. Nothing to show for a road.

"I was at home, quiet, in the evening, with a book. Then I was in the middle of the waystones. It seems I've been pulled into the schemes of the gods. Now I have to find out why."

The woman looks at you for a long moment. "A book," she says, flat.

"Books are lords' things. Priests' things. I can't read a word of one."

She takes the hem of the grey shirt between finger and thumb. Good cloth. Unpatched.

"The gods are busy, and far off." She lets the hem drop. "They don't pluck women off hearths."

She looks up the hill at the ring, then down at your feet, grey with the road's dust.

Behind her, a ewe bleats long and loud in the pen. The two girls go still.

"I have forty ewes lambing and not hands enough. Two girls and a boy of fifteen."

"I don't pay coin. I've none to pay. I pay in bread, and a bed by my hearth, and a roof."

"I can put a robe on you. It'll hang on you, but the valley won't stare."

She glances at the girls, then back at you. "You'll leave the gods at my door. The wayfarer comes. He can judge your tale when he does. Till then you're a woman who came off the hill."

"Work first. Then I'll know what you are."

In the pen, another ewe answers the first.

"I'll gladly work," you say. "But I'll warn you: I have never touched a sheep. You'll have to teach me what to do."

The woman looks at you a moment longer. Then she nods once, short, like a gate shutting.

She goes in and comes out carrying a robe. Faded red, ankle-length, thin at the elbows.

"Put it over what you have on. It'll swim on you, and the valley won't stare."

You pull it on. The sleeves swallow your hands and the hem drags on the yard stones.

The two girls watch from the woodpile. The older one whispers, and the little one giggles.

The woman looks at you. "Your name?"

"Nala," you say.

She repeats it once, flat, as if weighing the word on her palm. Then, to the girls: "Pell. Lissa. This is Nala. She works here now."

She puts a wooden bucket in your hands and points you at the rock cistern.

Water first, then fodder, then the pen. The day has a plain order and it goes on and on.

The yard stones press up through your soles at every step. The bucket handle bites.

She teaches as you go. Move slow. Come at a ewe from the side. Never grab the wool.

The girls trail you everywhere with small buckets of their own, telling you which of the hens bite.

They show you the pen: ewes, lambs, and one ewe lying apart with her head turned away.

The woman goes still when she sees her, and takes the bucket from your hands without looking.

The ewe is down and does not get up. The woman is over the hurdles and kneeling in the straw.

"One lamb," she says, hands in the fleece, feeling. "It's turned." She does not look up.

Straw is scattered where the ewe has dragged herself round and round the pen.
