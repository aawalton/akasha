"I'll gladly work," you say. "But I'll warn you: I have never touched a sheep. You'll have to teach me what to do."

The woman looks at you a moment longer. Then she nods once, short, like a gate shutting.

She goes in and comes out carrying a robe. Faded red, ankle-length, thin at the elbows.

"Put it over what you have on. It'll swim on you, and the valley won't stare."

You pull it on. The sleeves swallow your hands and the hem drags on the yard stones.

The two girls watch from the woodpile. The older one whispers, and the little one giggles.

The woman looks at you. "Your name?"

"Nala," you say.

She repeats it once, flat, as if weighing the word on her palm. Then, to the girls: "Pell. Lissa. This is Nala. She works here now."

She puts a wooden bucket in your hands and points you at the rock cistern.

Water first, then fodder, then the pen. The day has a plain order and it goes on and on.

The yard stones press up through your soles at every step. The bucket handle bites.

She teaches as you go. Move slow. Come at a ewe from the side. Never grab the wool.

The girls trail you everywhere with small buckets of their own, telling you which of the hens bite.

They show you the pen: ewes, lambs, and one ewe lying apart with her head turned away.

The woman goes still when she sees her, and takes the bucket from your hands without looking.

The ewe is down and does not get up. The woman is over the hurdles and kneeling in the straw.

"One lamb," she says, hands in the fleece, feeling. "It's turned." She does not look up.

Straw is scattered where the ewe has dragged herself round and round the pen.
