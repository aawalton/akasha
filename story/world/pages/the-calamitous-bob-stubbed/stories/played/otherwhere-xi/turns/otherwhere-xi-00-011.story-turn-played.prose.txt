"I might as well, for now," you say. "It's good to be needed somewhere."

She nods, once. "Bed and board. The robe. Sandals. And a lamb of your own, at the end."

"That's the wage. There's no coin in it." She goes inside.

She comes back with a pair of hide sandals, worn soft, and drops them at your feet.

Your soles are bruised from the road and the yard stones. The sandals are too long by a thumb. She kneels, knots the thongs tighter herself without a word, and stands.

Then the morning. Fodder forked down from the stack. The fold mucked out. Bucket after bucket hauled from the cistern.

She shows each task once, in few words, and watches. When you get it wrong she takes it out of your hands and starts it over.

The fork keeps slipping in your small hands. Your shoulders burn, and blisters start across your palms.

Pell and Lissa follow you everywhere, telling you the ewes' names and which hens peck.

The turned lamb sucks, its tail going. By noon its ewe is up on her feet.

At noon Tobin comes down the road with the flock, Smoke running ahead.

He sees the lamb first, then you, and his mouth falls open. "You did that?"

You eat on the floor mat by the hearth: bread and hard cheese. The woman is served first. Then she sets bread, cheese and a cup of goat's milk on the mat in front of you.

Tobin talks through his bread. The lamb, the ring, Param, how he knew you were something the moment he saw you.

His mother cuts across him. "Anyone asks, she's a hired hand from down the valley. That's all."

"Tobin. You hear? Pell. Lissa."

The girls nod, solemn. Tobin goes red and nods.

Then she looks at you. "No waystones. No gods. No books. You'll say the same."

It isn't quite a question. Across the mat, Tobin has stopped chewing.
