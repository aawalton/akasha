You take the crock and grease your hand and arm to the elbow, the way she shows you.

In your heart you pray. For a healer's path. For a sign of why you were brought here.

"Between her pushes," she says. She has the ewe's head now. "Slow. Now."

You go in. It is hot and tight and wet, and the ewe clamps down on your wrist like a fist.

"Wait it out. Don't pull against her."

The squeeze passes. You go deeper.

You find a head, then a tangle you can't make sense of by touch alone.

"Follow the neck down to the shoulder. The leg goes back from there."

Your fingers find it: a thin leg folded back along the lamb's side, the hoof small and sharp.

"Cup the hoof in your palm so it can't tear her. Push the lamb back first, then bring it."

Your arm shakes. The body you're in answers a half second slow, but your hand fits where hers wouldn't.

You push back against the next heave, cup the hoof, and draw the leg forward.

"That's it. That's it. Now both feet and the nose together, and let her do the work."

The ewe heaves once more. The lamb slides out in a rush into the straw, slick and yellow.

It doesn't move. She is already there, clearing its mouth with a finger, rubbing it hard with a handful of straw.

The lamb coughs. Sneezes. Shakes its head. At the hurdles Pell whoops, and Lissa squeals.

The ewe turns her head and starts to lick. Within minutes the lamb is up on folding legs.

The prayer goes unanswered. There is no voice and no light. There is straw, and blood, and the ewe muttering to her lamb.

The woman sits back on her heels. She looks at your arm, red to the elbow, and then at your face.

"Clean," she says. "Near enough."

It is the nearest thing to warmth you've heard in her voice.

She dips a cup in the bucket and sets it down in the straw by your knee.

"You've a place at my table for that. That's how it's done here." She stands, knees cracking.

"Half the flock still to drop before the month's out. Will you stay the lambing?"
