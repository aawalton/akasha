You watch her hands, and do what you can.

"Her head," she says without looking up. "Kneel. Hold it in your lap. Talk to her."

You kneel in the straw and take the ewe's head. It is heavy and hot, and it trembles.

Her eye rolls up at you, white-rimmed. You talk low, nonsense, anything. The trembling eases.

"Tallow. The crock on the shelf inside the door. And water from the cistern. Go."

You run. The robe's hem catches at your feet. The crock is on the shelf where she said.

You haul a bucket up from the cistern, and water slops cold down the front of the red robe. Your mouth is dry. The water smells of stone and snowmelt.

Back in the pen, she greases her hand and forearm to the elbow and goes in.

You hold the head again. Her jaw sets. Her whole arm strains, and she grunts.

Long minutes. The ewe heaves and cries. The woman swears under her breath and pulls her arm out.

She sits back on her heels, breathing hard, and looks at her hand. It is big and cracked, and bleeding a little.

"Leg's back. I can feel it. I can't get round it. My hand's too big for her."

Pell has crept up to the hurdles. "That's how the other two went, Mam."

"I know what I lost." Her eyes stay on the ewe.

Then she looks at your hands, holding the ewe's head. Small. Pale. Narrow at the wrist.

"Your hands," she says. "They're small enough."

"Grease to the elbow. In slow, between her pushes. Find the leg, cup the hoof, bring it forward."

"I'll talk you through it. I'll hold her." She holds out the tallow crock.

The ewe heaves again, and cries.
