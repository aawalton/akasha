The last thing you remember is the back window. A book open on your knee, the light going soft over the yard in Provo, your eyes getting heavy on a sentence you meant to finish.

Then nothing. No dream. Just a gap.

Now there is water. Close by, a low, easy sound, water moving over stones and not in any hurry. Birds, a lot of them, busy and near. A frog somewhere, then another.

You are lying on your side in grass. It is cool and wet with dew, and it has soaked through your shirt at the shoulder. The air smells green, and clean in a way you did not know air could smell, and under that there is mud and something sweet you cannot name.

You open your eyes.

Long pale leaves hang in front of your face, strung on thin branches that sway and drip. A willow. Its trunk leans out over a river a few steps away, and its roots go down into the water. The river is clear enough to see the stones on the bottom, brown and gold and green with weed, and small fish hanging still in the current.

You sit up, and the world tips. Your balance is wrong. Everything is closer to the ground than it should be.

You look down.

The shirt is yours. Your dark grey one, the one you fell asleep in. It hangs off you like a tent, the collar slid off one shoulder, the hem down to the middle of your thighs. Below it are your black compression tights, still snug. Your feet are bare, small and pale and wet with dew.

The hand you put out to steady yourself is small too. Narrow, with thin fingers and short nails, the skin so fair it is almost pink at the knuckles, and freckled on the back. You turn it over. You close it and open it, and it does what you tell it.

Something heavy slides forward over your shoulder and swings against your arm. Hair. Long, dark red hair, a whole thick rope of it, far longer than yours has ever been.

When you breathe out, the sound that comes with it is a woman's.

Mist lies along the river and in the low places between the hills. And there are hills everywhere, round and green and gentle, rolling away in every direction until they go blue with distance. Downstream the slopes are cut into steps, each one a flat sheet of water that holds the sky, with green shoots standing up out of it in rows. Farther off, where the terraces end, a thread of smoke goes straight up into the still air, then another beside it.

Near the water, a cart track of packed earth runs along the bank, two ruts worn deep by wheels. Where it meets the river there is a squat grey stone, waist high, with marks carved down its face.

You get up. Your legs hold. The grass is cold under your feet.

The marks on the stone are not letters. They are dense, angular shapes, each one a little square of strokes, and you have never learned a single one of them.

You can read them anyway.

Three Stones Village. Five li downstream.

Behind you, upstream, the hills rise higher and darker, furred with pine and the pale green feathers of bamboo. Something in there gives a long, low call, and every bird along the bank goes quiet at once.

Then, one by one, they start again.

You turn your back on the willow and start down the cart track, the way the marks on the stone said. You go slowly, and you keep listening.

The packed earth is cool under your feet. The ruts are full of pebbles, small and sharp, so after a few steps you move up onto the strip of grass that runs between them, where the going is soft.

Walking is strange. Every step lands short of where you meant it to, and twice you almost stub a toe reaching for ground that is still half a step off. Your legs are shorter than you expect. You keep forgetting, and then finding out again.

The hair swings as you walk, heavy against your back, left and right with every stride.

Every little while you stop. You stand still on the grass and listen, and look back up the river at the hills behind you, dark with pine, the bamboo pale along their edges. The track behind you stays empty. The birds keep on singing in the willows and the reeds.

The sun comes up over the hills, and the mist lifts off the water and thins away.

The terraces begin. They step down the slope beside the track one after another, each a flat pane of water held in by a low earth wall, and young rice stands in them in straight rows, green and thin as grass.

Your mouth is dry. Your stomach is empty, and it has started to gnaw. The river runs beside you the whole way, clear and cold, talking over its stones.

Far off across the paddies there are people. Small figures in wide straw hats, bent over, knee-deep in the water, working along the rows. A grey water buffalo stands on a field bank with its head down, pulling at the grass. White egrets step through the shallows on long legs, one slow foot and then the other.

Near the top of the terraces, a wall beside the track is broken open.

The gap is wide, wider than your arms can reach. The mud of the wall has slumped out in a fan, and the water has drained through it into the terrace below. In that corner the rice is flattened, the shoots torn and pressed down into the muck.

You look for what did it. Prints, a trail, anything. The ground is churned to soup, and you can't make anything of it. It could have been anything. It could have been a wall that gave way on its own.

You step out onto the slumped bank to see better.

The mud gives under your bare foot. Your foot goes out from under you and you slide down, fast, into the terrace below, and land in cold water up to your knees.

The mud grips your feet. Each time you lift one, it pulls back with a wet suck, and you have to wrench it free. You wade for the bank, and under the water your feet come down on the young shoots, a whole row of them, pressing them flat.

You climb back up onto the track. You are soaked to the thighs, and the hem of the shirt drips. Your tights are mud to the knee, and your feet are caked in it.

On the next terrace down, a man in a straw hat straightens up and turns toward you. He comes splashing across the paddy.

"Hey! Hey! Get out of there! That's my rice!"

He is lean and burnt dark by the sun, somewhere in the middle of his life, his trousers rolled up to the knee and a weeding hoe in his hand. He reaches the bank below you, gets his first proper look at you, and stops short.

He stares. At your hair. At your face and its freckles. At your bare, muddy feet, and the grey shirt hanging off you.

The hoe comes down to his side.

"Heavens and earth," he says. "What are you?"

You look down at him from the track, and you answer.

"I am a spirit of knowledge who recently achieved physical form. If you can deliver me safely to the nearest orthodox sect, you will be rewarded. If that is beyond you, I will have to find another."

The farmer stares up at you from the flooded terrace. His mouth moves, and no sound comes out of it.

His eyes go to your hair again. To your pale, freckled face, and the strange grey shirt. The colour drains out from under his sunburn until his face is grey.

The hoe drops into the water with a splash. He goes down on his knees in the paddy, straw hat and all, the water up to his hips. He bows until his forehead nearly touches the surface, and he stays there.

"Forgive this one, honored spirit. Forgive the shouting. This one didn't know."

Water drips from the brim of his hat. He speaks into the paddy, still on his knees.

"This one is Zhao Jun. This one farms these top terraces." He keeps his eyes off the trampled row. "The rice is nothing. Walk where you please."

Your mouth is still dry. Your stomach clenches, empty.

He lifts his head a little. "A sect. Honored spirit, this one has never been past the county. The only sect anyone here can name is the Hidden Spring Sect, somewhere north of Lanqiao. Lanqiao is the market town, thirty li down the river. Past there, nobody here knows the road."

"Please don't go to another," he says quickly. "Come down to Three Stones. The headman is learned. My wife will feed you. There's rice, and tea. Let this one take you down."

He glances up the bank at the broken wall, then back at you, and wets his lips.

"Honored spirit, since you know things," he says. "What is it that breaks my walls at night?"

"I do not have all knowledge," you tell him, "but I might still be able to help. Let us talk while we walk. What possibilities have you considered? What have you eliminated, and how?"

Zhao Jun scrambles up out of the paddy, water pouring off his trousers. He gropes in the mud for his hoe, fishes it up, and climbs onto the track. Then he bows and sweeps a hand down the track, ushering you ahead of him.

You go. He follows a pace behind you and off to one side, his feet careful, as if he is afraid to tread on your shadow.

For a while there is only the slap of his wet feet. Then he starts, and once he starts, all of it comes out.

"It started this month. Three walls now, all at night, between midnight and first light. All of them up here, on my terraces nearest the bend. Nobody else's."

He hitches the hoe up on his shoulder.

"First I thought it was the Fang boys, drunk, playing a trick. Loud young fools. But a wall went the night they were both down in Lanqiao. So it wasn't them. Then I thought rain, or crabs digging in the banks. But a rotten wall just sags and slides. These are torn open, the mud shoved out in a fan, like something went straight through."

His voice drops.

"And by each gap it's not only trampled flat. Whole clumps are gone, pulled up and eaten. Roots and all." He jerks his chin back up the track. "That corner up there, where it's all torn, that was the same."

He walks a few steps without speaking.

"Once I found a print in the mud. Deep. Split in two, like a cloven hoof." He holds up his hand beside you, fingers spread as wide as they go. "This broad."

The hand comes down.

"I said a buffalo got loose. But nobody's buffalo is loose at night. I asked every house. Tie Bo, the hunter, talks about a great boar up in the woods. But boars don't come down this low. Not in spring, not this near the village. Everyone knows that."

He swallows.

"One night I sat up with a torch and a gong to scare it off. I heard it out there in the dark. Grunting. Big. Bigger than any pig I ever heard." The tips of his ears go red. "I dropped the gong and ran home."

He lowers his voice further, and leans closer.

"Only my terraces, honored spirit. Only the ones nearest the old willow. I thought maybe I'd offended the willow's spirit. I left rice at its roots. Twice." He shakes his head. "The walls still broke."

His eyes slide sideways to your red hair, and then quickly away.

The track runs on down between the terraces. The sun is warm now, and it lies on your wet legs and starts to dry the mud stiff on your tights. Your mouth is dry. Your stomach growls, loud in the quiet. Zhao Jun keeps his eyes on the track ahead as though he heard nothing.

Ahead, on a terrace beside the track, a woman in a straw hat is bent over, weeding.

Zhao Jun lifts his hand. "Hong! Hong, come here!"

She straightens. She shades her eyes with one muddy hand and looks up at the track, at her husband, and at you walking in front of him. She goes still.

Zhao Jun turns back to you. His face is eager and frightened at once.

"So? Honored spirit, what is it?"

"It is a mystery for sure," you say. "It could be a mundane beast such as a bull or a boar, but it could also be a spirit beast. In either case, this misfortune may turn out to be great good fortune instead. If you have the means, recruit a few stalwart hunters or warriors to watch with you at night until it returns, and then slay the beast. If needed, offer them a share in the spoils. Spears would be best, with a strong bar across the haft, so you can plant the base in the ground in case of a charge. If you succeed in slaying the beast, at the least you will have a bounty of meat for your village. At most you may have a valuable corpse that could be sold to the sect for greater rewards. If you do not succeed in slaying the beast, you may still succeed in discouraging it from feasting on your rice."

Zhao Jun stopped walking somewhere in the middle of it. At *spirit beast* the colour went out of his face, and it has stayed out. At *sold to the sect* his eyes went wide. Now he lets out a long, slow breath.

"A bar across the haft," he says. "Tie Bo has a spear like that. An iron bar on it. For boar. He's hunted boar twenty years. His brother..."

He stops, and shakes his head.

Xu Hong has come up the terrace wall onto the track while you were talking. She is a square woman, sun-browned, with mud to her elbows, and she has been close enough to hear. She looks you up and down: your hair, your bare feet, the wet, muddy tights.

Her eyes narrow, just for a heartbeat. Then she bows, deep and careful, her hands pressed together.

"Honored spirit," she says.

She turns to her husband, and her voice drops, sharp and low. "Spirit beast? Hunters? Paid with what?"

Zhao Jun winces. "With a share. Of the meat. The honored spirit said so."

"And a night hunt with village men needs the headman's leave," Xu Hong says. "You know that. You'll go and tell Headman Gu all of it, then? About the walls? Everything?"

Zhao Jun looks down at his feet and keeps his mouth shut.

Your stomach growls again, loud in the quiet. Xu Hong's eyes flick to your middle.

She straightens. "The honored spirit will eat first. Our house is just below. There's rice."

Then she bows to you again. "And after, would the honored spirit speak to the headman? He won't refuse a spirit. He might refuse my husband."

"Gladly," you say.

Xu Hong bows again and leads the way. You follow her, and Zhao Jun follows a pace behind you with his hoe on his shoulder.

The Zhao house sits just below the track. Two rooms of mud brick under a thatched roof, and a yard of packed earth swept clean. By the door a girl of about fourteen is feeding sticks into a fire, and a little one of six squats beside her.

Both of them freeze when they see you. The little one ducks behind her sister's legs and peers out around them.

"Mei, the good bowl. Lan, stop staring," Xu Hong says.

Both girls keep staring.

Inside it is dim and smells of smoke. Against the back wall stands a small shrine, a few wooden tablets on a shelf, a stub of incense burning in front of them. They put you in the seat that faces the door. The rest of them stay on their feet until you sit.

Mei brings rice in a glazed bowl, the only bowl you see without a chip in its rim. With it come pickled mustard greens, half a salted duck egg, and a cup of weak, hot tea.

The family eats from plain bowls, squatting by the wall. Every one of them watches you from the corners of their eyes.

The rice is plain. The greens are sour and salty. It is the best thing you have ever eaten.

You drink the tea, and then another cup, and the dryness in your mouth is gone at last.

Mei pulls a stool close to the fire for you. You sit, and your wet tights and the hem of the shirt begin to steam. Little Lan edges nearer, and nearer, one small shuffle at a time, until she is staring at your hair from an arm's length away.

When the bowls are empty, Zhao Jun puts on a clean jacket to walk you down to the village. Xu Hong stays behind. Before you go, she presses both hands together and bows to you once more.

The track leaves the terraces and runs in among walls of rammed earth, under roofs of grey tile and thatch. Where it enters the village, a huge camphor tree throws its shade over a stall, and a woman there is setting out stools.

She sees you. She drops a stool, and leaves it lying where it fell.

Dogs start barking. Children come running out of every yard and trail along behind you, keeping a safe distance. Doors open. Faces come up over walls. The whispering follows you all the way in.

At the heart of the village, three tall grey stones stand in a ring around a small shrine. Facing the square is a house with a gate roofed in tiles, the only one like it you have seen here. Zhao Jun knocks, and calls out.

A servant boy opens the gate. He stares at you, and then turns and runs back inside.

In the courtyard beyond, an old man sits at a low table spread with ledgers and a pot of tea. He is past sixty, thin and grey, with one leg stretched out stiff in front of him. Behind him, in the doorway of the house, a grey-haired woman stands very still with a cloth in her hands.

Zhao Jun bows almost to the ground. "Headman Gu. This one has brought an honored spirit."

The old man looks at you for a long moment. At your hair, your face, your bare feet, the strange shirt.

He takes up a cane and rises, slowly. He bows to you, politely, but not deeply.

"Honored guest. Forgive an old man. Folk at the Lanqiao fairs have claimed to be spirits before. They took a good many coins from people who believed them." He rests both hands on the cane. "Would you permit this old man a question or two, before we speak of anything else?"

"Of course," you say. "As a spirit of knowledge, I am always happy for honest questions, and answering them is the least I can do for the hospitality you have already given."

Headman Gu inclines his head. He lowers himself back onto his seat, easing the stiff leg out in front of him.

From the table he takes up a thick book, its cover worn soft at the corners, and opens it and turns it around to face you.

"The almanac from Lanqiao," he says. "Few in this village can read. Would the honored guest read me a line?"

He sets a thin finger on a column of characters, written small and close.

You have never seen a book like it. The page runs top to bottom, right to left, dense with little square marks. You look at the column under his finger, and it reads itself to you, the way the stone by the river did. You read it aloud without a stumble.

"Ninth day of the Month of the Snake. Favorable: weeding, mending walls, offerings at the shrine. Unfavorable: long journeys, moving house."

Behind Zhao Jun, over by the gate, somebody draws a sharp breath.

Gu's eyebrows lift, a little. He takes his finger off the page and closes the book.

"A reckoning, then," he says. "Forty households. Each owes three dou of rice. Ten sheng to the dou. How many sheng in all?"

"Twelve hundred," you say, as soon as he has finished.

Gu pulls a wooden abacus across the table. He begins to flick the beads, slow and careful, his lips moving.

It takes him a long time. The courtyard is silent, except for the click of the beads. Zhao Jun is holding his breath.

At last the beads stop. Gu looks down at them for a while. Then he looks at you.

"Twelve hundred," he says quietly. He pushes the abacus away.

In the doorway of the house, the grey-haired woman has lowered her cloth. She is watching your face.

Gu folds his hands on the ledgers and looks up at you, and the polite smile is gone.

"One more, honored guest. The last." He lifts his eyes briefly to the sky above the courtyard. "The moon. Why does she wax and wane? Why is she full at mid-month, and a thin bow at its ends?"

"This is a deep secret," you say, "and in some places men have died for speaking it. I would not want to risk your repaying your hospitality with unkindness, so let me test your readiness for this secret first. When the sun rises in the morning and sets in the evening, what is moving?"

Over at the gate, somebody whispers it. *Men have died.* The whisper passes back from mouth to mouth, down the lane.

There are more faces at the gate now than there were before. A dozen at least, pressed together, craning to see in.

Gu's eyes go to the gate. His mouth tightens.

"Out. All of you. Go and weed something."

The servant boy runs at them, flapping his arms, and shoos them back and swings the gate shut and drops the bar across it. Through the planks the voices go on, farther off now and louder. *A spirit. A secret. Men died for it.*

Zhao Jun stays where he is, pressed flat against the courtyard wall, as if he hopes to pass for part of it.

Gu turns back to you. He studies you for a long moment before he answers.

"The sun moves, of course. Anyone with eyes can see it. It rises out of the eastern sea and is carried across Heaven's dome through the day. At night it passes beneath the Earth, and by dawn it is back in the east." He lifts one shoulder. "That is what the almanac teaches, and what my eyes tell me."

He sets both hands flat on his ledgers.

"There. This old man has answered the honored guest's question. He hopes he has passed." His eyes stay steady on yours. "Now, if you please: why does the moon wax and wane?"

"That answer merits the lesser knowledge," you say, "which should not put you at risk. The light of the moon is a reflection of the light of the sun. But the sun and the moon do not circle at quite the same rate, and the sun is farther away. When they are aligned, the moon is dark, as the reflected light does not reach us. When they are opposed, the moon is bright with reflected sunlight. Waxing and waning moons are the transitions between."

Gu is silent. His eyes go up past you, to the pale daytime sky above the courtyard wall, and they stay there.

"The bright side," he says at last, slowly. "The bright side of the bow always faces the sun. At dusk, the thin moon hangs just over where the sun went down, lit on that side. Always." He is still looking up. "I have watched that my whole life. I asked my father why. He did not know."

He looks down at the almanac by his hand. After a moment he lays his palm flat on its cover, and leaves it there.

Then he takes up his cane, gets to his feet, and bows to you again. This time the bow is deep.

"This old man has been rude to a guest. The honored spirit will forgive him. You will stay in my house. The east room is the best in Three Stones. It is yours."

In the doorway, the grey-haired woman lets out a long breath. She comes forward across the courtyard, drying her hands on the cloth.

"Clothes first," she says to her husband. "Look at her. And a bath. Then rooms."

Gu lifts a hand to her, gently, without taking his eyes off you.

"But there is a thing, honored spirit. You heard them at the gate. By noon every house will say a spirit came, with a secret men died for. By the next market, Lanqiao will say it too. Frightened people say foolish things. Some will say fox. Some will say demon. The Fang boys will be at my gate by afternoon."

He leans on the cane.

"Tonight, at dusk, I will call the village to the Three Stones, to the shrine of the earth god. Stand there with me, and make an offering to Old Grandfather of the Three Stones. Let them see you honor him, and hear you speak kindly to them. Then they will be easy." His voice stays level. "If the honored spirit will not, this old man cannot promise what they will say."

"I will," you say.

Gu lets out a long breath and bows again. "Then this old man's house is honored."

His wife takes over at once. She is Gu Meilan, she tells you, and everyone calls her Auntie. She takes you by the elbow and steers you across the courtyard to a kitchen shed at the back.

A wooden tub stands there. Auntie Gu heats water in a big iron wok over the stove and carries it to the tub, bucket by bucket, until steam rises off it. She sets out a cloth and a lump of grey soap. Then she goes out and pulls the door shut behind her, and you are alone.

The water is hot. The mud of the paddy comes off your legs and feet in brown clouds, and the water goes brown with it.

When you come out, there are clothes folded on a stool. "My married daughter's old things," Auntie Gu says. A blue hemp jacket that crosses over at the front and ties at the side. Loose trousers. A cloth sash. A pair of shoes.

The shoes are cloth, flat-soled, and a little big. After a morning barefoot they feel like a gift.

The jacket's sleeves fall past your fingertips. Auntie Gu turns them back, twice. She looks you over, tucks a strand of the red hair behind your ear, and nods, satisfied.

The east room has a wooden bed with a quilt on it, a chest, and a lattice window that looks onto the courtyard. The door has a bar on the inside. It is yours.

Before the midday meal, Auntie Gu makes the house's offering at a small shrine in the main room. She lights three sticks of incense from the little lamp there, and raises them to her brow. She bows three times. She plants the sticks in the sand of the burner, and sets down a cup of wine and a little rice.

Then there is rice for everyone, and a dish of greens, tofu with a little pork, and tea. You eat well.

In the afternoon, loud young voices come to the gate. Two of them, so alike they could be brothers, both demanding to see the spirit.

Gu's voice answers from the courtyard, dry and short. "At dusk. Like everyone else."

The voices go away, grumbling.

The shadows stretch across the courtyard. At last, out in the square, the servant boy beats a gong. Its sound rolls out over the rooftops, and all through the village doors open.

Gu leads you out on his cane. Auntie Gu follows a step behind, carrying a tray.

The whole village is at the Three Stones. Men, women, old folk, children, packed in around the square. The three tall grey stones stand around a little stone house, waist high, and inside it is a painted clay figure.

Two big young men with the same broad face shove their way to the very front, grinning. Near the back, a lean, weathered man with a bow on his shoulder stands apart from the rest, silent. The woman from the tea stall under the camphor holds a basket covered with a cloth in one hand, and a small boy by the other.

At the front stands an old woman with a sharp face. Her eyes go straight to your feet. Then they drop to your shadow on the ground, and come back to your feet, and stay there.

Gu stops at the shrine. On its ledge sit a small lamp, a cup of rice wine and a bowl of rice. He hands you three sticks of incense and steps back one pace.

The whispering dies away to nothing.

Somewhere in the crowd a small child asks, too loud, "Is she going to do magic?" A hand hushes it.

On the ledge of the shrine, the flame of the little lamp stands straight up in the still dusk air.
