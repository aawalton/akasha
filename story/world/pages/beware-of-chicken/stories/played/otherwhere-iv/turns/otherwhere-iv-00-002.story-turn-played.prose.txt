You turn your back on the willow and start down the cart track, the way the marks on the stone said. You go slowly, and you keep listening.

The packed earth is cool under your feet. The ruts are full of pebbles, small and sharp, so after a few steps you move up onto the strip of grass that runs between them, where the going is soft.

Walking is strange. Every step lands short of where you meant it to, and twice you almost stub a toe reaching for ground that is still half a step off. Your legs are shorter than you expect. You keep forgetting, and then finding out again.

The hair swings as you walk, heavy against your back, left and right with every stride.

Every little while you stop. You stand still on the grass and listen, and look back up the river at the hills behind you, dark with pine, the bamboo pale along their edges. The track behind you stays empty. The birds keep on singing in the willows and the reeds.

The sun comes up over the hills, and the mist lifts off the water and thins away.

The terraces begin. They step down the slope beside the track one after another, each a flat pane of water held in by a low earth wall, and young rice stands in them in straight rows, green and thin as grass.

Your mouth is dry. Your stomach is empty, and it has started to gnaw. The river runs beside you the whole way, clear and cold, talking over its stones.

Far off across the paddies there are people. Small figures in wide straw hats, bent over, knee-deep in the water, working along the rows. A grey water buffalo stands on a field bank with its head down, pulling at the grass. White egrets step through the shallows on long legs, one slow foot and then the other.

Near the top of the terraces, a wall beside the track is broken open.

The gap is wide, wider than your arms can reach. The mud of the wall has slumped out in a fan, and the water has drained through it into the terrace below. In that corner the rice is flattened, the shoots torn and pressed down into the muck.

You look for what did it. Prints, a trail, anything. The ground is churned to soup, and you can't make anything of it. It could have been anything. It could have been a wall that gave way on its own.

You step out onto the slumped bank to see better.

The mud gives under your bare foot. Your foot goes out from under you and you slide down, fast, into the terrace below, and land in cold water up to your knees.

The mud grips your feet. Each time you lift one, it pulls back with a wet suck, and you have to wrench it free. You wade for the bank, and under the water your feet come down on the young shoots, a whole row of them, pressing them flat.

You climb back up onto the track. You are soaked to the thighs, and the hem of the shirt drips. Your tights are mud to the knee, and your feet are caked in it.

On the next terrace down, a man in a straw hat straightens up and turns toward you. He comes splashing across the paddy.

"Hey! Hey! Get out of there! That's my rice!"

He is lean and burnt dark by the sun, somewhere in the middle of his life, his trousers rolled up to the knee and a weeding hoe in his hand. He reaches the bank below you, gets his first proper look at you, and stops short.

He stares. At your hair. At your face and its freckles. At your bare, muddy feet, and the grey shirt hanging off you.

The hoe comes down to his side.

"Heavens and earth," he says. "What are you?"
