"I do not have all knowledge," you tell him, "but I might still be able to help. Let us talk while we walk. What possibilities have you considered? What have you eliminated, and how?"

Zhao Jun scrambles up out of the paddy, water pouring off his trousers. He gropes in the mud for his hoe, fishes it up, and climbs onto the track. Then he bows and sweeps a hand down the track, ushering you ahead of him.

You go. He follows a pace behind you and off to one side, his feet careful, as if he is afraid to tread on your shadow.

For a while there is only the slap of his wet feet. Then he starts, and once he starts, all of it comes out.

"It started this month. Three walls now, all at night, between midnight and first light. All of them up here, on my terraces nearest the bend. Nobody else's."

He hitches the hoe up on his shoulder.

"First I thought it was the Fang boys, drunk, playing a trick. Loud young fools. But a wall went the night they were both down in Lanqiao. So it wasn't them. Then I thought rain, or crabs digging in the banks. But a rotten wall just sags and slides. These are torn open, the mud shoved out in a fan, like something went straight through."

His voice drops.

"And by each gap it's not only trampled flat. Whole clumps are gone, pulled up and eaten. Roots and all." He jerks his chin back up the track. "That corner up there, where it's all torn, that was the same."

He walks a few steps without speaking.

"Once I found a print in the mud. Deep. Split in two, like a cloven hoof." He holds up his hand beside you, fingers spread as wide as they go. "This broad."

The hand comes down.

"I said a buffalo got loose. But nobody's buffalo is loose at night. I asked every house. Tie Bo, the hunter, talks about a great boar up in the woods. But boars don't come down this low. Not in spring, not this near the village. Everyone knows that."

He swallows.

"One night I sat up with a torch and a gong to scare it off. I heard it out there in the dark. Grunting. Big. Bigger than any pig I ever heard." The tips of his ears go red. "I dropped the gong and ran home."

He lowers his voice further, and leans closer.

"Only my terraces, honored spirit. Only the ones nearest the old willow. I thought maybe I'd offended the willow's spirit. I left rice at its roots. Twice." He shakes his head. "The walls still broke."

His eyes slide sideways to your red hair, and then quickly away.

The track runs on down between the terraces. The sun is warm now, and it lies on your wet legs and starts to dry the mud stiff on your tights. Your mouth is dry. Your stomach growls, loud in the quiet. Zhao Jun keeps his eyes on the track ahead as though he heard nothing.

Ahead, on a terrace beside the track, a woman in a straw hat is bent over, weeding.

Zhao Jun lifts his hand. "Hong! Hong, come here!"

She straightens. She shades her eyes with one muddy hand and looks up at the track, at her husband, and at you walking in front of him. She goes still.

Zhao Jun turns back to you. His face is eager and frightened at once.

"So? Honored spirit, what is it?"
