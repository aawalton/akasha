"Yes," you say, "it sounds like poison is the best option to improve our chances. We could poison the heads of the spears as well as the stakes. I will come, and I will pray for your success."

Tie Bo nods, slowly. "Then I'll ask her at first light."

He is quiet a moment. Then he says the rest of it, because you should hear it from him and not from anyone else.

"Poison on a spirit beast, though. The old tales say it's no use. They say a poisoned bait kills the village dogs, and the beast goes on eating." He lifts the bow and settles it on his shoulder. "But it's worth the trying, and I'll take it if she gives it. I'll smear the heads too, if there's enough. Poison or no poison, we go up after it. The stakes and the men I'd be sure of, whether there's poison or not."

He looks at you a moment longer than he needs to.

"And I'll not pretend to you. It's my brother's boar. I'd go up that hill with a spear and no plan at all, if that were all I had."

You walk back up the dark track to the headman's gate. The lamp is still burning inside.

Zhao Jun comes out. His eyes are red. He stops in front of you and bows.

"Thank you, honored spirit. I told him everything, even the parts I didn't want to. He said you were right to send me. He said the hunt may go, and he'll speak of shares."

Then he goes home up the dark track, and Tie Bo goes another way, and the gate shuts behind you.

Auntie Gu is waiting with a lamp. She sets out a late bowl of noodles and stands over you until you eat them.

The east room is warm. The quilt is heavy. The lattice window is a pale square of night sky.

You sleep the whole night through without waking once.

You wake to cocks crowing and a house already at work: water slopping in the yard, a fire going in the shed. Auntie Gu has tea and rice and greens on the table, and you eat while the sky outside goes grey. By the time you are dressed the mist is off the terraces and the sun is over the ridge.

A knock at the gate: Tie Bo, with his bow and a bundle, and mud on his boots from the fields.

He leads you down the lane and out along the channel where the ducks are, to the edge of the village.

Granny Hua's place is the last in the west lane. A hut, a garden, racks of herbs set out to dry. Bundles of stems and roots hang from the eaves, and the air around the hut smells bitter and green.

An old woman is already out with a hoe. Small, square-shouldered, grey hair knotted at the back of her head.

She looks up, and sees Tie Bo, and then sees who walks beside him. She stops hoeing.

She plants the hoe in the ground and comes to the gate. She stands there, looking you over from your feet to your hair.

"You're the one, then," she says. Her voice is dry as husks. "The spirit. Every soul in the village has an opinion about you already. But I'll not take the word of a crowd, and I'll not take Tie Bo's word either."

She looks you up and down again, taking her time about it.

"But you came in the daylight, and that's something. Not many spirits would."

She folds her arms.

"Well, honored spirit. Out with it. What do you want with an old herb-woman?"
