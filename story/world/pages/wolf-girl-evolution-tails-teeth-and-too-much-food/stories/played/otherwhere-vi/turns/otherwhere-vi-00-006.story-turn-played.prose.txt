You back away from the edge of the mud, one slow step at a time, then turn up the west slope into the pines to go round the boars wide.

Under the trees it is near black. The slope is steep and laced with roots, and you climb it slowly, the staff planted uphill, the stone in your other hand. Below you the grunting goes on.

You keep well up the slope. Down in the mud the big one huffs once, loud, and then goes quiet.

Halfway round, a pine lies across the slope where the wind threw it down. The trunk is chest-high on you, and its roots stand up out of the ground in a wall of earth.

You brace the staff, find a broken stub of branch, and set one bare foot on it to climb over.

The stub snaps.

Your foot skids on the bark, and you pitch backward off the trunk.

You hit the slope and go down it, sliding and rolling, through roots and loose stones and needles, the dark turning over and over. The stone flies out of your hand. Somehow your fingers stay locked on the staff.

You fetch up hard against a root near the foot of the slope. The mud and the alders are close below you now.

Pain arrives everywhere at once: your hip, one elbow, a long burning scrape down your shin.

Below you the four small ones squeal all together and scramble in behind the big one.

The big one huffs. It clacks its teeth with a hard, wooden sound, and comes at you through the mud.

It is low, bristled and heavy, and it is fast.

It stops short a few lengths off, head down, blowing hard through its snout, its teeth clacking.

Somewhere in the alders behind it, the small ones squeal again, shrill and all together.
