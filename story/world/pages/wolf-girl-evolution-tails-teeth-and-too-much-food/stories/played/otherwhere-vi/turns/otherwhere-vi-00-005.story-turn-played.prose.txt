The bank below the fall is cobble and gravel, loose stones of every size heaped along the water's edge.

You bend and pick one up. It is about the size of your fist, rounded smooth by the water, cold and heavy in your small hand.

You keep moving downstream along the bank, the stone in your fist, and as you go you watch the edge of the pines for a fallen branch.

A little behind you, on the far side of the gully's mouth, a lean shape slips down onto the bank.

It is the wolf that stayed on the lip, the one that lowered its head. It keeps well back, farther than you could throw.

When you stop, it stops. When you walk, it walks. It never comes any closer.

Of the second wolf there is no sign at all.

After ten minutes or so, you come to a stand of alders where spring floods have piled driftwood against the trunks. Dead limbs of pine and alder lie jammed together, grey and bare in the moonlight.

You pull at a pine limb first. It snaps short in your hands, brittle and dry.

Deeper in the pile there is a pale alder limb, as thick as your wrist and taller than you are, wedged fast. You set the stone down on the gravel, within reach, and take hold of the limb with both hands. You work it back and forth, tugging.

It takes a few minutes. Then it comes free all at once, and you stagger a step backward with it.

It is light, but sound. You snap off the side twigs one by one, and when you stand it up beside you, it reaches a head above you.

You take the stone up again in your other hand and walk on, the staff thumping on the gravel.

The wolf has not moved on while you worked. When you go, it trails you again, the same distance back.

Your soles are bruised now. Every stone underfoot is a dull hurt through the heel and the ball of the foot.

The cold has not let go of you either. You are shaking in the wet tights, and you cannot stop.

The smell of musk and churned mud grows stronger the farther down you go.

Ahead, the stream spreads out into a wide stretch of trampled mud and shallows under the alders. From it come grunts, wet slapping, and the suck of mud.

In the moonlight, dark bristled shapes are rooting in the mud. One big one. Four smaller.

The big one lifts its head, snout up and working, and stands very still.

Behind you, the wolf stops. It looks past you toward the mud, then turns and trots back upstream.
