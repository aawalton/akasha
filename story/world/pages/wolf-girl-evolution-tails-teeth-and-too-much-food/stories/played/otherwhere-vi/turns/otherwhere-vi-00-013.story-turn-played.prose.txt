You turn the mug in your hands. "I'm Nala. I'm from very far away, and I'm not entirely sure how I got here, or where even 'here' is. Could you help me get oriented?"

The younger man lets you finish before he speaks. The old one turns his head to bring his ear toward you.

Jory answers you politely, and he doesn't ask you twice about far away.

He tells you where you are. This is the Carrow, which they call the Big Water, at the south edge of the Weald.

He reckons country by burns and ridges, not by miles. A day up the stream is the fells. West down the water lies Wenmarch, the town. South across the river is the Crown's country. North above the pines are the Bare Backs, and he says plainly that nothing lives up there.

Wat puts in that the wolves hold the middle of the Weald, and that folk don't cross it after dark.

He says the bear came down off the fells this autumn. The east slopes are no place to be, alone.

He says the nearest who can do for a hurt foot is the herb-wife at Brackenford, half a day down.

Jory says the raft won't go down till the burn is out, and that is five days off.

Wat takes your cut foot onto his knee and works pine-tip salve into it with a hard thumb. It stings like fire, and then the foot is easier than it has been all day.

He wraps it in cloth. "You won't wear that out in a morning's walking."

Jory pulls his shovel up onto his shoulder and goes back out to tend the clamp. At the doorway he says the gate at Brackenford will want a name, a village and a road. A stranger with no village is held a runaway there. He says it as one plain thing among others.

Wat spits into the fire and looks at you across it for a while. Then he asks you straight out, the way he would ask about the weather, whether anybody is hunting you.
