You shout back across the gravel. "Hello! I've been lost in the woods for a night and a day. Would you grant me a traveler's hospitality?"

Your call goes out over the water and the cold gravel, loud in the still morning.

The man at the mound answers at once, in a thick Weald burr. "Come up. Come to the fire."

The dog trots up to you, sniffs at your tights, and leans its whole weight against your legs.

You cross the gravel to them, the staff in one hand, favouring the cut foot.

The younger man comes to meet you. He is near thirty, steady-faced, and slow of speech.

He looks once at your bare feet and the torn shirt, and doesn't mention either.

"In by the fire with you," shouts the old one from the hut, grey and hunched.

The hut is poles and turf over a stone hearth, with a good fire burning on it.

Fern and hides make up two beds. A kettle sits on the coals, and the air smells of bacon.

You sit down on a hide by the hearth and find you cannot stop shaking.

The young man puts a mug of hot tea in your hands and a bowl of bread and fish beside you.

You drink, and the heat goes down into your chest and out along your arms.

The bowl is empty very quickly. You had been hungrier than you knew.

The old man tells the young one to bring the salve pot and the cloth for your feet. He says it as if reading off a fact about the fire. "You'll not go cold in this hut."

Your shaking slows, and stops. You sit with the heat on your face and your hands.

The dog lies down with its chin across your good foot and sighs through its nose.

The young man sits down opposite you with a mug of his own and looks at your hands.

He asks your name, and where you hail from, and what road brought you here.
