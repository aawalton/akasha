You get up slowly, the staff held out crosswise in front of you, between you and the big beast.

Your left hip gives a hot wrench as your weight comes onto it. You stand anyway.

You start backing away. One step. Another. Your eyes stay on the beast, and the staff stays between you. If it comes again, you will jam the butt into the ground and lean your weight on it.

Behind you the ground is open gravel bank, running downstream along the water's edge.

The beast follows a few steps as you go, huffing, clacking its teeth.

It does not rush again.

You keep backing, the staff low and ready, your hip catching at every step.

Among the alders the small ones' squealing thins to grunts, then stops.

The big one halts. It swings its head toward the alders, then turns and trots back into the mud to them.

You keep backing until the grunting is well behind you. Then you turn and walk, limping.

Below the wallow the valley narrows. Birch trees stand pale among the alders, and the stream runs loud over shallow riffles.

Your left hip hurts at every step. Your elbow throbs. The scrape down your right shin stings, and blood from it has run down inside your tights, sticky and cold.

You are shaking hard now, all through you. Your fingers are clumsy on the staff.

On the drier bank under the pines, low shrubs carry small red berries, dark in the moonlight.

Your breath smokes. The wet cloth at your hips has gone stiff, and cold as metal.
