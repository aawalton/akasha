"Yes," you tell Wat. "I made it through a night in the forest alone. I know enough to fear the dark, but I can be afraid without panic."

Wat looks at you a moment longer. Then he nods once, as if a price has been agreed.

"Then you sleep now," he says. "The afternoon through. I'll wake you at dusk to eat."

He pushes a heap of fern and hides nearer the fire for you with his foot.

You lie down on it. The heat of the hearth is on your back, and the dog settles at your feet.

You are asleep almost before you have finished lying down.

You sleep deep and still. If you dream, you don't remember it.

Wat's hand on your shoulder wakes you. The doorway is gold, and the light outside is going.

You haven't moved all afternoon. You are stiff all over, but your head is clear.

When you stand on your foot, it is sore rather than burning. The cloth over the salve is still clean.

Supper is bacon, and bread, and a pot of barley and fish that the three of you eat straight from the pot with wooden spoons.

The great moon comes up over the river, huge and round and full, and lights the gravel white.

Jory walks you round the clamp. Once an hour, he says, all the way round, looking and smelling.

He shows you what a flare looks like: a red glow, or a lick of flame through the turf, and a sharper smoke.

He puts a shovel in your hands and shows you how to smother a glow with damp earth off the heap.

"If it won't go down, shout for me," he says. "Don't be proud about it."

He shows you the stump by the hut door where the watcher sits between rounds.

Far off in the north, the wolves begin to howl. Many voices, long, under the full moon.

Jory listens with you a moment. "They don't come near the fire," he says, "or the dog."

He goes in. The door-hide falls closed behind him, and you are alone with the clamp and the moon.
