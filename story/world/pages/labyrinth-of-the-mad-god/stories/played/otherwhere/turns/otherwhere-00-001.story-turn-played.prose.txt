The last thing you remember is the back window. Evening light going orange over the yard in Provo, a book open on your knee, the page half turned.

Then there is nothing. Not sleep. Not dark. A gap, like a skipped track.

Now there is noise. A long roar that builds, breaks and hisses away, then builds again. Surf. Something shrieks overhead, and something else answers it. Gulls.

You are lying on your back. The ground under you is warm and gives when you shift. Sand. The sun is already high enough to press on your eyelids, red through the skin.

You open your eyes and shut them again at once. The sky is a hard, clean blue with no cloud in it. You try again, squinting, and sit up.

The beach is black. Not grey, not dirty. Fine black sand, running away on both sides in a long curve until the haze swallows it. The sea is blue, but when a wave lifts and the light goes through it, there are swirls of rose and green in the water that should not be there.

Your head feels packed with wet cotton. You reach for the last hour and find only the back window, the book, the orange light. After that, fog. You push at it. It does not move.

You put a hand up to rub your face, and the hand is wrong.

It is small. Narrow, pale, with long fingers and nails grown out past the tips, ragged and uneven. You turn it over. The wrist is thin. You make a fist and the fist is small too, and the arm it belongs to is slim and freckled at the elbow.

Something heavy slides off your shoulder and falls across your chest. Hair. A thick rope of it, dark red, long enough that when you pull it forward it reaches well past where your shoulder blades must be. You did not have hair like this. You did not have hair this long at all.

You look down.

Your own grey shirt, the loose one you wear around the house, hangs off you like a tent. The collar has slid off one shoulder. Under it the shape of you is not the shape you went to sleep with. Your black shorts have slipped low on hips that are narrower and curved differently, and the drawstring does nothing. Only the black compression tights are right, snug from waist to ankle as if they were made for these legs.

Your feet are bare. The shoes are gone.

"Hello?" you say, and stop.

The voice is a woman's. Clear, a little hoarse, pitched higher than anything you have ever said out loud. It comes out of your own throat.

Nala, you think. The name is just there, waiting, the way your own face used to be in a mirror. If you are going to be someone else, that is who.

You look around properly now. Behind you the beach slopes up to a line of trees that are almost palms, though the fronds are too broad and grow the wrong way. Past them the forest closes in thick and green. Above the canopy, a long way inland, a mountain rises out of the island's middle, and from its top a thin band of dark smoke leans away on the wind.

An island, then. A decent-sized one.

There are no boats. No road, no wire, no contrail across that clean sky. Not so much as a bottle cap in the sand. Small crabs skitter at the water's edge. The gulls wheel and cry. Nothing else moves.

The sun is climbing, and the black sand is already warm under your palms. Your mouth is dry.
