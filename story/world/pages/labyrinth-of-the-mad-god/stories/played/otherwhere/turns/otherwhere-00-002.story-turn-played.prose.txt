You get up. The shirt slides off your shoulder and you hitch it back. The mountain is inland, somewhere past the trees, and you start toward it.

You go slowly. You keep your steps short and set each foot down flat, and every few paces you stop and look: up the beach, down it, along the treeline, back at the water.

The sand is warm under your bare soles, warmer the further you get from the wet edge, but it doesn't hurt yet. The beach runs up at an easy slope, and the climb to the trees takes a few minutes.

Up close the trees are stranger. The fronds are broad as tabletops and hang straight down from the crown, overlapping, so each trunk wears a ragged green skirt. A few big green husks lie in the sand beneath them, each about the size of your head, old and split open.

At the treeline you stop and look the forest over. It is a wall. Trunks stand close together, grey and green with moss, and the gaps between them are dim. You look along the edge for a way in. Every gap looks like every other. You pick one that faces the mountain, as near as you can judge, and step through.

The light goes green. The heat of the beach falls away behind you, and the air in here is still and wet and cooler, heavy to breathe. The surf goes muffled.

The floor is deep leaf litter, soft on top. Under it are roots, and between the roots are stones with edges. You learn that fast. You start feeling for each step with the ball of your foot before you trust your weight to it.

Within twenty paces the mountain is gone. The canopy closes overhead and all you can see is trunks. The ground rises, though, gently and all one way, away from the sea. You keep it rising under you and call that your bearing.

A creeper hangs between the trunks. It strings the gaps in loops and curtains, thin green stems set with small hooked thorns. You try to slip past one loop and it catches your sleeve, and when you pull free it rakes your forearm, leaving three shallow red lines that bead up. A step later it gets your hair. The heavy rope of it snags behind you and jerks your head back, and you have to stop, reach around, and pick the strands off the hooks one at a time.

You learn the body as you go. Your reach is shorter than it should be; you grab for a branch and close your hand on air. You weigh less, and you feel it every time you step up onto a root, how little there is to lift. And the hair catches on everything.

Something bites the ball of your left foot. You stop. Under the leaves is a stone edge, sharp as a broken plate. When you turn your foot up there is a small cut, bleeding a little, stinging. It isn't deep. You go on, slower.

Your mouth is dry. Not badly, yet.

All this time the canopy has been loud. Hooting, chattering, a crash of branches now and then, high up and never quite in one place. You stopped hearing it as separate sounds a while back.

Ahead the creeper hangs in a thick curtain. You part it with your forearm and push through, and a stem snaps with a crack louder than anything you have done since you woke.

The racket above you stops dead.

Then a bark, sharp, from somewhere overhead. Another answers it, and another, the same short harsh call taken up by one voice after another until it runs all through the trees around you.

Branches shake. Leaves come down. Heavy bodies are moving through the canopy, toward you.

They come down into a gap just ahead where the sun gets through, around a tree hung with red fruit the size of your fist. Apes. Long-armed, brown, each about as big as a large dog, and every tail ringed in bands of bright copper. You count a dozen and stop counting; more are moving in the leaves behind them. All of them are barking. All of them are looking at you.

A half-eaten red fruit flies out of the branches and bursts on the trunk beside your head. A stick follows. It bounces off your shoulder.

Higher up, on a thick limb, an old ape with a grey muzzle sits still. It isn't barking. It watches you.

Behind you and to the left, low in the undergrowth, the way you came, something rustles.

The biggest of the near ones drops to a low branch almost straight above you, eight or nine feet up. It pulls its lips back off long yellow teeth and slaps the branch with both hands, hard, so the whole limb jumps.
