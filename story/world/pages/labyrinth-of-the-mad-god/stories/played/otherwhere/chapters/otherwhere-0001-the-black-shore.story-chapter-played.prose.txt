The last thing you remember is the back window. Evening light going orange over the yard in Provo, a book open on your knee, the page half turned.

Then there is nothing. Not sleep. Not dark. A gap, like a skipped track.

Now there is noise. A long roar that builds, breaks and hisses away, then builds again. Surf. Something shrieks overhead, and something else answers it. Gulls.

You are lying on your back. The ground under you is warm and gives when you shift. Sand. The sun is already high enough to press on your eyelids, red through the skin.

You open your eyes and shut them again at once. The sky is a hard, clean blue with no cloud in it. You try again, squinting, and sit up.

The beach is black. Not grey, not dirty. Fine black sand, running away on both sides in a long curve until the haze swallows it. The sea is blue, but when a wave lifts and the light goes through it, there are swirls of rose and green in the water that should not be there.

Your head feels packed with wet cotton. You reach for the last hour and find only the back window, the book, the orange light. After that, fog. You push at it. It does not move.

You put a hand up to rub your face, and the hand is wrong.

It is small. Narrow, pale, with long fingers and nails grown out past the tips, ragged and uneven. You turn it over. The wrist is thin. You make a fist and the fist is small too, and the arm it belongs to is slim and freckled at the elbow.

Something heavy slides off your shoulder and falls across your chest. Hair. A thick rope of it, dark red, long enough that when you pull it forward it reaches well past where your shoulder blades must be. You did not have hair like this. You did not have hair this long at all.

You look down.

Your own grey shirt, the loose one you wear around the house, hangs off you like a tent. The collar has slid off one shoulder. Under it the shape of you is not the shape you went to sleep with. Your black shorts have slipped low on hips that are narrower and curved differently, and the drawstring does nothing. Only the black compression tights are right, snug from waist to ankle as if they were made for these legs.

Your feet are bare. The shoes are gone.

"Hello?" you say, and stop.

The voice is a woman's. Clear, a little hoarse, pitched higher than anything you have ever said out loud. It comes out of your own throat.

Nala, you think. The name is just there, waiting, the way your own face used to be in a mirror. If you are going to be someone else, that is who.

You look around properly now. Behind you the beach slopes up to a line of trees that are almost palms, though the fronds are too broad and grow the wrong way. Past them the forest closes in thick and green. Above the canopy, a long way inland, a mountain rises out of the island's middle, and from its top a thin band of dark smoke leans away on the wind.

An island, then. A decent-sized one.

There are no boats. No road, no wire, no contrail across that clean sky. Not so much as a bottle cap in the sand. Small crabs skitter at the water's edge. The gulls wheel and cry. Nothing else moves.

The sun is climbing, and the black sand is already warm under your palms. Your mouth is dry.

You get up. The shirt slides off your shoulder and you hitch it back. The mountain is inland, somewhere past the trees, and you start toward it.

You go slowly. You keep your steps short and set each foot down flat, and every few paces you stop and look: up the beach, down it, along the treeline, back at the water.

The sand is warm under your bare soles, warmer the further you get from the wet edge, but it doesn't hurt yet. The beach runs up at an easy slope, and the climb to the trees takes a few minutes.

Up close the trees are stranger. The fronds are broad as tabletops and hang straight down from the crown, overlapping, so each trunk wears a ragged green skirt. A few big green husks lie in the sand beneath them, each about the size of your head, old and split open.

At the treeline you stop and look the forest over. It is a wall. Trunks stand close together, grey and green with moss, and the gaps between them are dim. You look along the edge for a way in. Every gap looks like every other. You pick one that faces the mountain, as near as you can judge, and step through.

The light goes green. The heat of the beach falls away behind you, and the air in here is still and wet and cooler, heavy to breathe. The surf goes muffled.

The floor is deep leaf litter, soft on top. Under it are roots, and between the roots are stones with edges. You learn that fast. You start feeling for each step with the ball of your foot before you trust your weight to it.

Within twenty paces the mountain is gone. The canopy closes overhead and all you can see is trunks. The ground rises, though, gently and all one way, away from the sea. You keep it rising under you and call that your bearing.

A creeper hangs between the trunks. It strings the gaps in loops and curtains, thin green stems set with small hooked thorns. You try to slip past one loop and it catches your sleeve, and when you pull free it rakes your forearm, leaving three shallow red lines that bead up. A step later it gets your hair. The heavy rope of it snags behind you and jerks your head back, and you have to stop, reach around, and pick the strands off the hooks one at a time.

You learn the body as you go. Your reach is shorter than it should be; you grab for a branch and close your hand on air. You weigh less, and you feel it every time you step up onto a root, how little there is to lift. And the hair catches on everything.

Something bites the ball of your left foot. You stop. Under the leaves is a stone edge, sharp as a broken plate. When you turn your foot up there is a small cut, bleeding a little, stinging. It isn't deep. You go on, slower.

Your mouth is dry. Not badly, yet.

All this time the canopy has been loud. Hooting, chattering, a crash of branches now and then, high up and never quite in one place. You stopped hearing it as separate sounds a while back.

Ahead the creeper hangs in a thick curtain. You part it with your forearm and push through, and a stem snaps with a crack louder than anything you have done since you woke.

The racket above you stops dead.

Then a bark, sharp, from somewhere overhead. Another answers it, and another, the same short harsh call taken up by one voice after another until it runs all through the trees around you.

Branches shake. Leaves come down. Heavy bodies are moving through the canopy, toward you.

They come down into a gap just ahead where the sun gets through, around a tree hung with red fruit the size of your fist. Apes. Long-armed, brown, each about as big as a large dog, and every tail ringed in bands of bright copper. You count a dozen and stop counting; more are moving in the leaves behind them. All of them are barking. All of them are looking at you.

A half-eaten red fruit flies out of the branches and bursts on the trunk beside your head. A stick follows. It bounces off your shoulder.

Higher up, on a thick limb, an old ape with a grey muzzle sits still. It isn't barking. It watches you.

Behind you and to the left, low in the undergrowth, the way you came, something rustles.

The biggest of the near ones drops to a low branch almost straight above you, eight or nine feet up. It pulls its lips back off long yellow teeth and slaps the branch with both hands, hard, so the whole limb jumps.

You turn and run.

Back the way you came, as fast as you can go. The barking swells behind you and above you, and the canopy comes with you. Branches crash overhead, first behind, then level, then ahead. They are keeping pace without trying. Fruit smacks into the litter at your feet. A stick whips past your ear, another cracks off a trunk, red pulp bursts on the leaves beside you.

Low to your left, something in the undergrowth breaks away from you, fast, a rush of leaves going the other way. You don't see what it is.

The creeper curtain is where you left it. You hit it at a run. It takes your shirt and your hair at once and holds, and for a second you are stopped dead with your head dragged back and the thorns in your collar. You wrench forward. Something tears. Hair rips out at the roots, a whole hank of red left hanging on the hooks behind you, and you are through and running.

Your left foot comes down on a root. The cut sole flares white-hot, the foot rolls under you, and you go down hard.

You land on your hands and one hip in the leaf litter. Your hair is across your face. Above you, directly above, the barking goes up into a shriek.

Something hits the side of your head, above your right ear.

White light. A ringing, high and thin. For a moment the ground is gone and so is the sky, and you cannot tell which way either of them is.

Then the litter is under your palms again. You find an egg-sized stone lying beside your hand. The side of your head is hot and wet. You touch it and your fingers come away bright red, and more is running, warm, down your neck and into your collar. Your scalp is split. Your head throbs with every heartbeat. The heels of your hands are scraped raw and your hip aches where you hit.

You get up. You run again, one hand clamped to the side of your head, blood slick between your fingers.

You stop picking gaps. You just go downhill, toward the surf. You can hear it now, under the barking, and you go at it straight, through whatever is in the way.

Light opens ahead. You burst out between the skirted palms onto the sand.

The sand is hot now. Much hotter than when you left, hot enough that it stings, and the cut sole burns with every step. You stumble a few more paces down the beach, out into the open, before you look back.

The barking stops at the treeline as if it has hit a wall. The branches along the edge shake, then settle. Every ape stays in the trees. Their noise pulls back into the forest, grumbling, and fades.

You are breathing hard. Your heart is slamming. The side of your head is wet and your hand is red.

One ape has stayed. It is a half-grown one, smaller than the rest, with a white blaze on its brow. It sits at the very edge of the fronds, in their shade, with a red fruit in one hand.

It isn't barking. It takes a bite of the fruit and watches you, head tilted, chewing.

You turn your back on it and go down the beach.

The dry sand is hot now, properly hot, hot enough to hurt. Every step stings the cut sole, and you go down toward the water on the sides of your feet, half hopping.

Near the waterline the sand goes dark and packed, and when you step onto it, it is cool. Cool and damp and firm, even in this sun. You stand there a moment with the sea running up around your ankles and back, and the sting in your foot fades to an ache.

You keep to the wet sand after that. You work the shore on either side of where you came out, a few hundred paces one way, then back and a few hundred the other.

Along the top of the wet sand runs a line of wrack: dried weed in black tangles, broken shells, driftwood bleached grey-white and smooth as bone. You go through it with your toes and then your hands.

Among the shells are shards of black glass. Glossy, dark as oil, some as long as your finger, their edges thin and keen. You pick one up by its thick end and draw it across a strip of dried weed. It slices clean through. You barely pressed.

Crabs the size of your hand scuttle in the wash, sideways, claws raised. When a wave rolls a piece of driftwood through them, they pinch at it.

You look for water the whole time. There is no stream on this stretch of beach, no spring, no pool. The only water is the sea.

From the wet sand you look both ways along the shore.

North, the beach runs straight and open, with no shade anywhere along it, to a black headland far off in the haze.

South, perhaps two miles away, taller palms in double rows break the line of the trees. Two rows, as if they line something that runs down to the shore there.

You go up under the palms, keeping to the strip of shade their skirts of fronds throw on the sand, and turn over the fallen husks. The first is split and empty. The next is dry, light as paper. The one after that is soft and black inside and smells of rot. You check one after another.

The twelfth, or near it, is whole. Green, heavy, the size of your head. When you shake it, liquid sloshes inside.

The husk is hard as wood. You dig at it with your fingers, then with your nails, and they don't mark it at all.

The ape has kept pace with you the whole time. It moves along the treeline in the shade, from one skirt of fronds to the next, and it never sets foot on the sand. When you stop, it stops. It watches whatever you are doing.

The sun is past its height and hammering down. Your mouth is dry. The split in your scalp throbs.

You stand in the palm shade with the heavy nut in the crook of your arm and a shard of black glass in your hand.

A few trunks away, in the shade at the edge of the trees, the ape has stopped too. Its eyes are on the nut.

You sit down in the shade with your back to a trunk and wedge the nut between your knees.

First you try the easy way. There is a driftwood log a step away, grey and half sunk in the sand, and you lift the nut in both hands and bring it down on the log. It thuds. You do it again, harder. Thud. When you turn it over there is a dull bruise on the green where it hit, and the husk hasn't cracked.

So you take the shard of glass, set its edge to the husk, and start to saw a line around the middle.

It bites, but slowly. The husk is a thumb thick, woody and stringy, and the glass cuts it one pale groove at a time. You saw, turn the nut a little, saw again. Fibres fray up under the edge and you pick them away and keep going.

It is long, hot work. The heels of your hands are still raw from the fall, and they sting every time the shard shifts in your grip. Sweat runs down out of your hair and finds the split in your scalp, and that stings too.

While you work, the ape comes closer. Trunk by trunk, along the shade, never onto the open sand. Then it sits and watches.

At last the groove runs all the way round. It is deep, but it isn't through.

You work the point of the shard into the groove to finish it and lever. The glass snaps. The piece in the husk stays in the husk, and at the same moment the nut gives, splitting along a crooked crack that wanders off your groove. The halves come apart uneven in your hands, and most of the water runs out between your knees into the sand.

You snatch up the halves. Each still holds a little water in the bottom.

You drink from yours. It is sweet, faintly cloudy, a few mouthfuls, and then it is gone, too fast.

Inside, the husk is lined with a thin layer of white flesh. You scrape some loose with a fingernail and eat it. It is bland and soft.

You look at the shard. It snapped short; what is left is a stub too small to hold.

You hold the other half out toward the ape.

It comes along the shade to the edge of the fronds nearest you, and stops there, just out of reach. It sits. It shifts its weight forward and reaches, halfway, then pulls its hand back and holds it against its chest.

You hold the half-nut out again.

"You might as well take it," you say. It comes out low and tired, the new voice gone rough at the edges. "I'm clearly going to die without help anyways."

The ape's head tilts at the sound of you. Its eyes go from your face to the nut, and back to your face, and back to the nut.

It leans in. One long arm stretches out toward you, the fingers opening, closer than before. You can see the creases in its palm, dark and leathery, and the dust on its knuckles.

Its fingertips stop a hand's width from the husk.

Deep in the forest behind it, a single sharp bark cracks out. Then another. The same call, twice.

The ape's head snaps round toward the sound. Its hand jerks back to its chest.

The bark comes again, harder, from somewhere far back under the canopy.

The ape looks at you one more time. Then at the nut.

It turns and goes. It bounds up the nearest trunk in two long reaches and away into the green, fast, without a sound.

You get up with the half-nut in your hand and go looking for water. South, toward the taller palms.

You keep to the wet sand at the edge of the sea, where it is cool underfoot. The sun is at its worst now. The glare comes up off the black sand as hard as it comes down, and down here at the waterline there is no shade at all. You squint against it until your face aches.

You check every dip along the way, every place the sand darkens as if something might be seeping up from underneath. Every hollow holds water, and every time you taste it, it is the sea.

Your mouth gets drier as you walk. Your lips start to stick together, and you have to work them apart. The glare gets in behind your eyes and your head throbs with it, the split in your scalp keeping time.

The taller palms come closer. Two rows of them, side by side, running inland from the top of the beach.

Between the rows a stream comes out of the forest. It spreads wide and shallow over the black sand, a sheet of moving water no deeper than your ankle, and runs out into the sea.

You kneel at the edge where it meets the waves and cup a handful to your mouth.

Salt. Not as bad as the sea, but too salt to drink. You spit it out.

Upstream the water narrows between banks of reeds and deep grey mud, and runs back into the trees. Farther up, where the forest closes over it, it looks clearer, sliding over black stones.

You look the banks over before you go any closer.

In the mud by the reeds are wide drag marks, smooth troughs, as if something heavy slid down into the water. Beside them are prints. Four toes, splayed, and at the end of each toe the deep gouge of a claw.

You follow the marks with your eyes into the reeds.

Something lies there, half in the water and half on the mud, twenty paces upstream.

A lizard. Longer than you are tall, seven feet of it, mud-brown, with a purple sheen along its back where the sun hits. Its head rests flat on the mud. Its eyes are half-closed. A tail as long as its body trails away into the water.

As far as you can tell, it hasn't seen you.

Farther up, past it, another tail lies among the reeds, as still as the first.

The nearer lizard's tongue slides out, forked and dark. It tastes the air, and slides back in.

You stay where you are, kneeling at the stream mouth with the salt still on your tongue, and speak to the empty air.

"Okay, isekai protocol. System? Status? Character sheet?"

The air in front of you stays empty. The light stays the same hard white it has been all day. Your words hang there a moment and are gone.

You wait. Behind you the surf builds and breaks and hisses back. The gulls cry. The stream slides past your knees over the black sand.

"If you left me here with truly nothing, I might as well fucking die now," you say. "And then I won't be any entertainment for anyone."

Still the air stays empty. The surf, the gulls, the stream. Only those answer.

Your voice carries. It goes up the stream between the reeds, the new voice cracking a little on the last word.

Twenty paces upstream, the nearer lizard's head lifts off the mud and turns toward you.

Its half-closed eyes open all the way. They fix on you.

Its tongue slides out, forked and dark, and tastes the air in your direction.

You reach for the glass. Your fingers find only the rough husk of the half-nut. The shard snapped off in it, two miles back up the beach.

The wrack line lies a few strides up the beach. You drop the half-nut, get up, and run for it, and snatch at the tangle of weed and shell until your fingers find a black edge. You pull it free. It is as long as your finger, keen along one side, and you grip it by the thick end.

Then you turn and go. One last rush, everything you have left.

You don't go straight at it. You swing wide through the shallows, splashing, circling to come at its side.

The lizard's head swings to follow you. Its mouth gapes open, pink and wet inside, and it hisses, a long flat sound like air out of a tire.

You close the last strides and throw yourself at its neck, arm out to lock around it, the shard up and aimed at the small eye under the ridge of bone.

Your feet go out from under you.

The grey mud is deep and slick, and it takes your heels and slides them away. You land short, on your side, in the mud by its shoulder, the neck still out of reach.

The lizard twists. It moves faster than anything that size should, the whole length of it bending like a whip, and its jaws come round at you.

You get your left arm up.

The teeth close on your forearm. They are hooked, and they hold, and when you wrench your arm back they tear through the skin coming out.

The pain is enormous. It fills your whole arm and your chest and the top of your head, and for a second there is nothing else. Blood runs down your forearm, over your wrist, and drips off your fingers into the mud.

The lizard backs a step. Its jaws hang open. It hisses again, and behind it its tail lashes the water, slap, slap.

You are on your knees in the mud. The shard is still in your right hand. Your left arm is bleeding.

Off to your side, the reeds are moving. The second lizard is coming through them, at you.

You come up off your knees and charge it again.

The shard is up in your right hand, point toward the small eye under its ridge of bone. You reach with your left arm to hook it around the thick neck, to pull yourself in close and hold on while you drive the glass home.

The arm won't close. The torn muscle in your forearm gives, and the fingers won't grip, and your hand slides off the wet scales. You stumble into its shoulder instead, all your weight against it, and it is like falling against a log.

Its head whips round. The jaws open wide.

The hooked teeth take you in the side, under the ribs, and hold.

It wrenches its head. Your feet leave the mud. It flings you down, and the grey mud slaps up around you, cold and heavy, in your hair, in your mouth.

There is no pain. Not at first. There is only a great weight across your middle, and a cold that starts in your side and spreads.

Your right hand is empty. The shard is gone. You try to feel for it and you can't find your fingers.

The reeds thrash. The second lizard reaches you, and the two of them close over you, brown and heavy, their breath hot and rank.

Past them, above the tops of the reeds, the sky is hard and blue and very far away.

Somewhere behind you the surf builds, breaks, and hisses back.

Then there is nothing.
