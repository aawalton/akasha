You get up from the cot, sit down by the hearth and wait. You rest your sword arm on your knee, and the stiffness from ten days of drill starts to ease out of your shoulder.

The stretch of stone floor where you drilled lies empty in the morning light. From where you sit, the notice board on the wall beside the hearth is in plain view.

The morning wears on toward half past nine, and you are still sitting by the hearth.

You get up from the hearth, draw your sword and walk back out onto the long stone floor.

You drill cuts and guards there from morning until the light goes. That night you pay for supper at the hall's table and the cot by the hearth.

On the second morning, a cut turns wrong in your grip, and the edge of the blade opens your forearm. Blood runs down to your wrist and drips onto the stone floor.

You set your feet on the stone floor again and lift the sword, the cut on your forearm still raw.

You drill the rest of that day, and the cuts run clean until the light goes. That night you pay for supper and the cot by the hearth, and in the morning you are back on the floor. You drill through that day too, pay for a second night at the hall, and wake to another morning.

You drill a third day and pay for a third night. The next morning, your first cut goes where you mean it, and the guard after it comes up without thought.
