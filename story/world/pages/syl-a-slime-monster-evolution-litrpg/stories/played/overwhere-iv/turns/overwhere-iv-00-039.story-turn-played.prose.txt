"Sure," you say. "It can wait for tomorrow, right?"

Ilsa nods. "It's waited a few hundred years. Tomorrow's fine." She pins the notice back up with your name on it.

At dusk you eat barley stew and bread in the gatehouse mess, then sleep behind your curtain.

By morning your shoulder has stopped aching. The warmth behind your ribs is full again.

At dawn, from the drill yard, you watch Garrett's cart roll out of the gate, east for Aubrin, with the report aboard.

Drill after, spear work in the yard until your arms burn. Then porridge.

Half a mile north, up the brook, you find the reeve's back pasture behind tall hedges. They hide the field from the wall and the road.

An ox team stands hitched to a timber sledge. An old herdsman sits on the sledge, squinting at you as you come. He cups a hand to his ear.

The oak fills the middle of the field. Eighty feet tall, four feet thick, centuries old.

It leans, plainly, toward a stone byre at the edge of the pasture.

Cut straight through, it will come down where it leans. Right across the byre roof.
