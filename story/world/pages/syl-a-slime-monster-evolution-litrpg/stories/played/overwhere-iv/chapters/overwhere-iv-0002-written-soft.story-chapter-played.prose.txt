"Well, isn't that interesting," you tell the slime at your boot.

You sit down in the grass of the willow bend, lay the spear across your knees, and get to work.

Push. The air creases, and the slime is a hand's width off. Pull. It is back. Each fold takes its sip.

You try the second slime, then the third. Then one bobbing two strides away, out in the open grass. Distance hardly matters at first. The warmth reaches as far as you can see clearly, up to a point. Past twenty strides or so it just won't take hold, however hard you stare.

By the seventh fold, the warmth has run thin, like a hearth burned down to coals. You reach for an eighth, and the warmth stays where it is. It is still there, but too low to lift.

So you sit back against a willow trunk and wait. The slimes settle round your boots like sleeping cats. You watch the light move on the water.

After an hour, the warmth has crept back up. It isn't full, but it's enough to work with.

You fold again, slower now, paying attention to the exact moment the warmth catches. You find you can feel it: a small click behind your ribs, like a latch dropping, just before the crease.

On the second fold of the new round, a window opens.

Proficiency gained. [Dimension Magic LV 1] improved to [Dimension Magic LV 2].

You turn to the far slime, the one past twenty strides, and push. This time the warmth takes hold. The air creases, way out there in the grass, and the slime jumps.

The warmth runs thin again soon after. You sit and rest, and watch the sky go gold over the brook.

The bells in town ring the evening. You shoulder the spear and walk back through the gate.

The mess smells of barley and onions. Long tables and benches fill the room, with a cauldron hanging on the hearth and a basket of bread under a cloth. Sergeant Holt sits at the nearest table with Wat and two watchmen, one lanky and loud, one grey and quiet.

Holt shoves a bowl of stew and a heel of bread across at you. "Eat. You look like you've been working."

Your stomach answers for you, loudly. You have not eaten since you woke.

Wat leans over the table, grinning. "Dunny Carrow says you speared seven slimes like a born hand." He lets that sit a moment. "And he says," he goes on, louder, "they went to you like lambs. Walked right up and waited to be stuck."

The lanky one laughs. The grey one looks up from his stew, and keeps looking. So does Holt.

"Yeah, it was the strangest thing," you say. "Any idea why they would do that? I know I'm pretty, but I've never had men throw their lives at my feet like that." You laugh.

Wat chokes on his stew. The lanky watchman howls and slaps the table so hard the bowls jump.

"Jelly," the lanky one says, loud, once he can breathe. "Slimes go to whoever smells of it. Known fact."

"Dell, you smell of jelly every time you come off the common," Holt says. "They don't follow you."

Dell opens his mouth, finds it empty, and shuts it again.

Holt tears her bread in half and looks at you across the table. She isn't smiling now.

"Country folk say slimes creep toward strong mana. Like moths to a lamp. I've never seen it." She dips the bread. "Till Dunny saw it today, maybe." She chews. "You've some magic in you, I'd guess. A strong lot."

She points the crust at you. "The hall keeps a cracked crystal behind the counter. Any newcomer can put a hand on it, and it won't cost you a copper. Tells you colour, not much else. For a proper testing it's Aubrin, four days east."

Down the table, the grey watchman has stopped eating. He sets his spoon down in the bowl, very carefully. He looks at you for a long moment, as if he is weighing something. Then he looks down at his stew again.

"Should we go try the crystal?" you say. "I don't mind being tonight's entertainment. Don't expect it'll be too interesting, but at least it's new. I know how small towns can be."

Dell is up off the bench before you finish, bread still in his fist. Wat scrambles after him. Holt sighs, wipes her bowl round with the last crust, and stands. The grey watchman comes last, without a word.

The square is blue with dusk. Lamplight spills from the hall door, and voices with it.

By the hearth sit four young adventurers, muddy to the knees, with two grey wolf pelts spread on the floor between them. Ilsa looks up from the counter, sees who has come in, and sets her pen down with great interest.

"She wants a go on the crystal," Dell announces to the whole hall.

A young man by the fire in a scorched red coat sits up at once and comes over. "Merrit. Fire." He smiles, though not warmly. "Lit it red. Brightest in Millbrook. Go on, then."

Ilsa lifts the cracked crystal down from its shelf and sets it on the counter. Beside it, on a nail, hangs a card of colours in neat ink.

"Hand flat on top. Hold it a breath," Ilsa says. "Most get a glow. One colour, maybe two."

You lay your palm on the crystal. It is cool and smooth under your hand.

The warmth behind your ribs wakes. It goes down your arm like water running down a slope.

The crystal flares.

It isn't a colour. It is clear, and bright as a lamp, and the room behind it bends. Through it you see the hearth, the benches and the faces fold at an angle, as if you were looking at them through folded glass. For a heartbeat, the whole hall is full of that clear, bending light.

Then comes a sound like river ice. The crack across the crystal runs on, a finger's length farther.

The light goes. The crystal is only cloudy glass again, with a longer crack in it.

The hall has gone silent. Merrit's smile is gone.

Ilsa looks at the card of colours on its nail, then at the crystal, then at you.

"That's not on the card," she says quietly, and reaches for her pen.

"What... what was that?"

Your hand is still flat on the crystal. You lift it away, slowly.

"Crystal's cracked," Merrit says, too fast. "You saw it. Old glass does odd things. That's all."

By the hearth, a thin young woman in a hooded jerkin shakes her head. "Room bent, Merrit. I saw it."

"So did I," says Wat. He is staring at you as if you have grown a second head.

A round-faced girl gets up from the hearth, comes to the counter, and takes your hand. She turns it over, palm up.

"Did it hurt you? It made a noise like ice." She presses her thumbs gently into your palm. "You're not burnt. Good."

The broad young man by the fire has kept his eyes on you the whole time. He looks like he's counting something.

Ilsa has pulled a thick, worn book out from under the counter. She runs her finger down a page.

"I've never seen a clear one," she says, without looking up. "I can't name it. Ask anyone in this hall and they'd be as stuck as me." She turns a page. "The guild handbook says rare legacy affinities can show lights no card holds. It doesn't name any by their light." Her finger stops. "It says report them at once."

In the doorway, the grey watchman is buttoning his coat. He catches your eye, just once. He tips his head toward the dark square outside, and goes out.

Ilsa dips her pen and holds it over a fresh page. "I'm bound to send word to Aubrin, Nala."

"That's fine," you tell Ilsa. "What are they going to do? Rob me again?"

You turn to Holt. "For now, we might as well get to sleep. Training in the morning, right?"

"Dawn," says Holt. "Wall laps first. You'll hate them."

Ilsa's pen is already scratching as you all go out. She does not look up.

The square is dark now. Across it, in the porch of the little shrine, a figure stands back out of the lamplight. It stays perfectly still as you pass, with Holt and Wat and Dell talking round you. When you look again, the porch is empty.

Back at the gatehouse, Holt sends you off to bed and goes out to see the night guard on the gate.

Behind the curtain, you unlace the big boots, pull the rags out of the toes, and lie down on the cot. The blanket is rough and smells of cedar. The warmth behind your ribs has crept back up while you ate and talked. It isn't full, but there is more of it.

The bunk room goes quiet. A cough. The creak of a cot. Far off, the slow turn of the mill wheel.

Then a voice, low, just the other side of the curtain. The grey watchman.

"Oswin Pike," he says, barely above a breath. "Don't answer loud. Just listen."

"That light. Keep it to yourself from here on. Don't show it off, not for anyone. And stop Ilsa's letter if you can. Before it goes east."

A pause.

"I've seen slimes follow someone before. Once. Years back."

"Why?" you whisper back. "What is the threat? I can't guard against an unknown."

A long quiet. Outside the curtain, a bare cot creaks as he sits down on its edge.

"Years back, a mage came through on the Aubrin road. Walking. Slimes trailing after him." His voice is dry and even. "Like ducklings. Off the verges, out of the ditches. Same as yours today."

"Lads threw stones at him for sport. He'd catch one with his eyes, and it'd vanish in the air. Then drop in the dust behind the one who threw it. Clean as that."

"A month on, word came he'd died at an inn in Aubrin. Poison. They never took anyone for it." A breath. "I reckon he was killed for that magic. I've never said so to anyone. Till now."

"Aubrin's great houses buy rare gifts. Buy the person, I mean. Keep them. And a gift that won't be bought gets buried."

"Guild letters in Aubrin go through a lot of hands. Some of the clerks are paid by those houses."

"I don't know which house. I don't know what his magic was called." The cot creaks again.

"I know he's dead."

You lie awake a long while after Oswin's cot creaks and his steps go off across the boards. The dark behind the curtain is very dark. Then sleep takes you.

A hand on the curtain. Grey light.

"Up," says Holt. "Boots. Yard."

The warmth behind your ribs is full again, round and heavy. Your mouth is dry and your stomach hollow. Breakfast comes after drill, Holt says. Not before.

Laps are the wall walk: the whole ring of the town, up on top of the wall, with steep stone steps at every tower. From the top beside the gatehouse you look down on the common side.

A dozen slimes are heaped against the foot of the wall, right below the bunk room. Quiet. Waiting.

Dell sees them too, and whistles. "There's your lambs, then. Come courting in the night."

You run. You give it everything you have. The first lap is fine. On the second, your lungs start to burn.

On the third, at the far tower, the rags slip in your left boot and your foot slides off the edge of a step. You go down hard on the stone. Your shin and knee take it, and the pain is white and sudden.

You get up. The knee holds. You keep going, but slower, and the others pull away along the wall.

Dell laps you on the fourth, grinning back over his shoulder as he passes. Wat slows beside you, until Holt shouts him on.

You finish the last lap walking, limping down the tower steps, a long way behind everyone.

Holt doesn't say a word about it. She hands you a practice spear and points at the line.

Spear forms. Thrust, guard, sweep, recover. Holt shouts them, and you do them, fifty times over, until your arms shake. Then shield work, with wicker shields, blocking Holt's slow, heavy blows until your shoulder aches.

Holt steps back and looks the line over. "Sparring. Blunt spears, shields. Till one yields." She points her spear. "Wat, you're with Oswin." She points it at Dell, then at you. "Farrow. You're with her."

Dell swings his spear round and grins. "Go gentle on me, lamb-girl."

You square up to Dell in the packed-earth yard, blunt spear low, wicker shield up. Your knee throbs with every shift of weight.

Dell circles, loud and loose, talking the whole time. He's going easy on you. You can tell, and it stings.

You do exactly as drilled. Guard. Thrust. Recover. Dell slaps your spear aside without even looking at it.

Holt steps in and stops you. She pushes your back foot a hand's width farther out with her boot and lifts your shield elbow with two fingers. "Again."

You listen, and the next exchange is better. You hold your guard. Dell has to actually block.

Then he lunges, big and showy. You catch it on the shield with a crack of wicker, and as he comes through, his whole left side hangs open.

You see it too late. By the time your spear moves, he has already recovered, laughing.

On his next lunge you go for the opening. Your bad knee buckles as you step into it.

Dell's spear butt thumps into your ribs under the shield. You land hard on the earth, knee first.

The breath goes out of you. Everything hurts at once. You lie there, and the yard goes quiet.

"Yield," you manage.

Across the yard, Wat winces. Oswin keeps quiet and watches you.

Dell hauls you up by the hand. "Lamb-girl bleats," he announces to the whole yard, grinning.

Holt looks at you a long moment. "You saw it. His left, after the lunge." She jerks her chin at the line. "Saw it and were late. Again, Farrow. Same as before."

You don't go back to the line. You limp across the yard to the well and sit down on its low stone rim. There's an upturned bucket by the rope, and you set your hurt leg up on it. The knee pulses hot under your tights.

Holt watches you go. Then she hands the line to Oswin with a look and crosses the yard.

"Show me," she says. It isn't unkind. She crouches in front of you, waiting.

You roll the tights up over the knee. It is swollen and going purple, and the shin below it is scraped raw.

Holt presses the swelling with two fingers of her short hand. You hiss. Holt nods.

"Not broken. The steps did this, not Dell." A grunt. "Good. I can't use one who hides a hurt."

She goes back. Dell spars Oswin now, loud as ever. Oswin barely moves his feet.

Dell lunges big. Oswin steps aside and taps the butt of his spear on Dell's open left ribs.

"Again!" Dell yelps, and lunges again, and Oswin taps the same spot. Wat laughs out loud.

From the well, with only watching left to do, you see it plainly: the lunge, the lean, the gap.

Sitting still, you notice how dry your mouth is, dry as chalk. Your stomach growls, loud enough that Wat looks round.

Holt calls drill done. The others rack their spears and head for the mess, and the smell of porridge drifting out of it.

Holt stops by the well on her way in. "Porridge first. Then take that knee to the hall. Orla heals the watch for a copper or two. Go today, or you'll run laps on it tomorrow."

Breakfast in the mess is porridge with a spoon of honey stirred into it, and a cup of small beer. You eat every scrap. The hollow in you goes. The dryness goes. For the first time since dawn, you feel whole.

Then you limp across town to the hall.

At a table by the hearth sits the round-faced girl from last night, a bag of bandages open beside her. Orla, then.

She knows you at once. "The crystal girl." Her eyes go straight to your hand. "It never hurt you?"

You show her the knee instead, and the ribs where the spear butt landed. She lays her palms on them, one and then the other, and a soft rose light rises under her hands.

Warmth sinks in. The swelling drains out of the knee. The scrape on your shin closes over pink. The ache in your ribs lifts away like a hand taken off them.

In a few minutes, you flex the knee. It doesn't twinge at all. It feels better than it did before the tower steps, even.

You have no coin. Orla only smiles. "Two copper on the hall's slate. The watch always pays."

Ilsa is at the counter, pencil stuck through her hair. You ask her for a word, low, and she follows you down to the end of the counter, away from the tables.

You tell her plainly. A mage whose gift drew slimes, the way yours does, poisoned at an inn in Aubrin. Great houses there that buy rare gifts, and bury the ones that won't be bought. Clerks in the guild who are paid to pass names on.

Ilsa listens without writing. Her face goes careful. Her eyes flick once to the drawer under the counter.

"The handbook says report it at once. I'm bound." A pause. "But it doesn't say how I word it."

Her eyes stay on you, bright and calculating. She wants something. It shows.

Across the hall, at the hearth, the thin young woman in the hooded jerkin closes her book. She has been sitting very still. She doesn't look over.

Ilsa leans in on her elbows. "I can write it soft. An old cracked crystal, a faint light. No name." Her voice drops further. "But you swear to me you'll work my board. Millbrook's, not Aubrin's. You make this hall matter."

"Deal," you say. "I have a lot of growing to do. Rather do it with someone I can trust anyways."

Ilsa holds your eyes a long moment. Then she puts out her ink-stained hand, and you shake on it. Her grip is firm.

"Then it's sworn. I keep my word, Nala. I expect the same." She lets go. "I don't forgive the other thing."

She opens the drawer under the counter and takes out a folded page, unsealed. As it comes out, you glimpse your own name written on it.

She takes a fresh sheet, dips her pen, and writes right there on the counter where you can see every word. The new report is short: an old cracked crystal, a faint odd light, and nothing more. It doesn't name a colour, or say the room bent, or give your name. Ilsa blots it, folds it, and seals it with a drop of wax.

Then she walks the first page down the hall to the hearth and drops it on the coals. It curls, and catches.

The hooded woman sits a pace from the fire, and watches it burn.

Ilsa comes back dusting her fingers. "Goes east on the next cart. Now." She taps a notice pinned by the counter. "My board. Hobb farm, on the Tangle's edge. Overrun with pest slimes. Three copper a core, and a hot dinner at Hobb's table. He wants every last one gone."

She reaches under the counter and sets down a pair of old leather gloves, worn soft and a little big.

"From the hall's box of things left behind. Wear them, the jelly stings. And mind the wolves."
