The last thing you remember is your back window. A book open on your knee, the evening going blue outside the glass, your eyes getting heavy.

Then sun on your face, long grass tickling your ear, and something cool and wet bumping gently against your ankle.

You open your eyes. Blue sky. A few white clouds. You turn your head, and a round blue blob the size of a melon is sitting against your foot, see-through, with a small red stone floating in its middle. It wobbles. It bumps your ankle again, curious, and rolls away through the grass.

You sit up, and your body is wrong.

Your arms are thin, the wrists narrow, the hands small and pale with freckles across the backs. Long dark red hair falls over your shoulder. Your old grey shirt hangs off you, the collar slipping to one side. Your black tights are still on. Your feet are bare, and small.

You look down at the rest of yourself once, and then you look at the blob instead.

You are on a wide green common beside a brook. Sheep graze a little way off. A wooden footbridge crosses the water, and beyond it a road runs up to a small town with a grey stone wall, a mill wheel turning slowly beside its gate.

The moment you wonder what is happening to you, a window opens in front of your eyes. Flat, clear, steady, like a pane of glass with writing on it.

Name: Nala

Race: Human LV 1

Class: -

Status: Healthy

You did not ask it for your name. It knew.

Then the window flickers, once, the way a screen does when something too big is trying to load, and for a moment there is a line under the others that is too bright to read. It settles. The line is gone. The feeling it left is not: a warmth behind your ribs, patient and huge, that feels like yours.

On the road, a cart has stopped. The driver is staring at you.

From where you sit in the grass, you call across to him. "Hello! I seem to have gotten lost. Could you tell me where I am?"

The voice that comes out is higher and lighter than you expect. It lifts at the ends of the words with a faint lilt you did not put there.

The driver is a big man, forty or so, with the reins gone slack in his hands. Two brown horses stand at the shafts. The cart behind him is stacked with grain sacks, and while he stares, the horses drop their heads and crop at the verge.

His eyes go to your bare feet, then to the grey shirt slipping off your shoulder, then to the black tights. He goes red to the tips of his ears. He looks hard at the sky instead.

"Lost?" he says to the sky. "Lady, you've been robbed, is what. East road, was it? Brigands on that road all month, stripping folk to their smallclothes." He jerks his chin toward you without lowering his eyes. "Look at your feet."

"You're in Millbrook, love." He says it slowly, setting each word down on its own, the way you would give directions to someone a long way from home. "Southern, by your talk. Wendmere Vale, this is, barony of Tarrow. That's the town, up past the bridge."

Something cool bumps your ankle. The melon-sized slime is back, bobbing against your foot, the red stone turning slowly in its middle.

"Garrett Pell. I carter for the vale." He is already twisting round on the seat to dig underneath it. He hauls out a patched brown wool coat, and you can smell the horse on it from where you sit. He holds it out at arm's length, still not quite looking at you.

"Marta at the Brook & Barrel's kind to strays. She'll see you fed, and keep the gawkers off you." He gives the coat a small shake. "Take it, go on. I'm hauling this grain to my sister's mill by the gate. Ride in with me?"

"Gladly, thank you."

You cross the footbridge to the road. The planks are warm from the sun under your bare soles. The slime with the red stone rolls after you through the grass as far as the water's edge, then stops there and bobs.

Garrett reaches down a big hand. You take it and climb, and you go up onto the seat far too easily, as if half of you stayed on the road. You have never been this light.

He drops the coat across your lap and busies himself with the reins, eyes front.

"I find I need to take my mind off of recent events," you say. "Would you talk to me about yourself and the area?"

He brightens at once. "Well, that I can do. Get up, Barley. Moss, you too."

The horses lean into their collars, and the cart rolls up the road toward the grey wall and the turning wheel.

"Twenty years hauling these roads. Grain to the mill, wool and jelly barrels east to Aubrin." He settles back. "Two girls at home, Pip and Wenna. My sister minds them while I'm out. Their mam passed. Fever, three winters back." He says it plainly, and clicks his tongue at the horses.

"The vale's quiet, mostly. Sheep, apples, market every seventh day. You'll like it." He tips his head. "Mostly. Goblins out of the Tangle been taking sheep off the edge farms this month. And the Red Hand on the east road. That'll be who had your boots, I'd wager."

His voice drops. "The baroness would've seen to them, once. Old Maud. Firm, but fair. She's been poorly since summer. Her nephew Cedric runs things now. Smooth as butter, cold as the brook. I keep clear."

Then he is off again, cheerful. "Need a salve, go to Old Selby. Witch, they say. Best back-rub in a jar I ever had. Brookside Four at the hall are good lads, but green. And stay clear of Crowstone Quarry."

The mill comes up beside the gate, a tall stone building with its great wheel creaking round in the race. You listen for the grinding and hear only the wheel and the water. Under a lean-to by the mill door, grain sacks are stacked high, and the ones at the bottom sag, dark with damp.

Garrett stops talking. He looks at the sacks a moment too long, then away.

"Hesper's shaft's cracked," he says. "Weeks now. Can't grind, can't pay for the mending till she grinds."

The cart rolls to a halt at the open gate, in the shadow of the wall. A guard in a leather jerkin steps out, spear grounded, and looks straight past Garrett at you.

"Day, Garrett. And who's this?" He turns to you. "Name and business in Millbrook, miss?"

"Nala," you say. "Suppose my business is not getting robbed again, for now." You spread your empty hands on the coat. "Not that I have anything left to be robbed of."

The guard is young and lanky, all wrists and neck under the leather. His mouth twitches. He fights it back into a straight line, and holds it there for about a breath.

He loses. The grin breaks through, and he ducks his head and reaches up to a shelf inside the gate arch for a fat ledger. He opens it on his knee and writes slowly, the tip of his tongue at the corner of his mouth, saying it aloud as the pen goes. "Nala. Robbed."

"Wat Barrow, miss. Gate watch." His eyes drop to your feet, and he winces. "Won't be asking you for toll, then."

Garrett leans over. "Found her on the common, Wat. Red Hand, sure as rain."

Wat's grin goes. "Then the captain'll want you. Anyone the Red Hand's had goes to him first. Orders. He's after where they hit you, how many, and which way they ran off." He taps the ledger with the pen. "After, you see Sister Anwen at the shrine. She gives out bread at dusk."

A door opens in the gatehouse wall. A broad older man in a worn leather coat steps out into the arch.

Wat straightens so fast his spear knocks against the stone. "Captain Hale, sir. Robbed traveller."

Hale looks at your bare feet. He looks at the coat in your lap. He looks at your face. He takes his time over each.

"Robbed," he says. "Where on the road, miss? How many of them? Which way did they go?"

"I'm afraid all I really remember is the glint of light off a blade," you say. "My mind focused in on that and seems to have forgotten to pay attention to anything more. I'm not local, so Mr. Pell can give the location better than I. I'm sorry I can't be of more help."

Hale's eyes go to Garrett.

Garrett shrugs. "Found her on the common, sat in the grass."

"The Red Hand work Hollin Wood," Hale says. "Past the second bridge. A day's walk east."

He looks down at your bare feet, and stays on them a moment longer than on anything else.

"I've seen men lose all but the knife, after," he says at last. "It happens."

He does not say he believes you. He nods once to Wat, and Wat bends over the ledger and writes something more.

You pause. "If you'll pardon the question, though, I find I'm feeling a great desire to get stronger now. Say, strong enough to fight off a group of bandits. Any suggestions for where I should start?"

Hale snorts, though not unkindly. "Everyone does, after. Most want it for a week." He folds his arms. "Levels come from fighting. And fighting kills fools. So don't start with bandits."

He tips his head up the lane, toward the square. "The adventurers' hall, on the square. Start on slimes and pests, and learn how you move. Win your first real fight and the System'll likely offer you a class. Then you've a start."

"Or be in the gate yard at dawn. I drill recruits. I keep anyone who stays the course. Six copper a day, a bunk and board. I'm short of hands." He says it flatly, as a fact about his watch rather than a favour to you.

He steps back into the gatehouse and pulls the door to behind him.

Wat lets out a breath and steps back from the cart. "He likes you. You can tell." He considers the shut door. "Sort of."

Garrett turns the horses aside from the arch, into the mill yard beside the gate. He reins in by the lean-to of sacks, swings down, and heaves the first sack onto his shoulder.

Inside the mill the wheel turns and turns, and the stones stay silent.

You climb down off the seat with the coat over your arm. "Thank you for the ride, Garrett. I'm sure I'll see you around for a while."

Garrett grins at you from under his sack. "Keep the coat. Millbrook's small; you'll not lose me."

You walk back to the gate barefoot. The paving stones are warm from the sun. Wat looks up from his ledger as you pass under the arch, and his eyebrows go up.

You knock at the gatehouse door.

"In," says Hale's voice.

Inside is a guardroom with a cold hearth and a bench along one wall, and past it a cramped office. Hale sits behind a desk with a thick roll book open in front of him.

"I'd like to sign up for drill," you tell him. "And I'd like to bunk here, starting tonight, if I can."

Hale looks at you for a long moment. "That was quick." He leans back. "Pay's at week's end. Quit before, you don't see a copper of it. Still want it?"

"I do."

He turns the roll book round to face you and dips the pen. "You swear to keep the town's peace, and do as you're told on the wall. Say it."

You say it back to him, word for word.

Hale writes your name in the roll himself, in square, careful letters: NALA. He sets the pen down beside it for your mark, and you put your hand to the page.

"Holt!" he calls, not loudly.

A woman fills the doorway. She is square-built, with a pale scar running along her chin.

"New recruit. Kit her out. She's bunking in." Hale is already back in his papers.

Sergeant Holt looks you up and down, starting at your feet. "Right. With me."

She leads you through a mess room of long tables and benches to a stores cupboard, and starts pulling things down off the shelves and piling them in your arms. A wool tunic. A padded jerkin. A practice spear with a blunted head. Last of all, a pair of old boots, huge in her hands. She stuffs rags into the toes before you can say anything. Her left hand is short two fingers, and she works the rags in with it quick as anything.

The bunk room holds twelve cots. Four of them are stripped bare. At the far end hangs a curtain on a rope.

Holt draws it back on two cots and a chest. "Mine, and now yours. The lads stay their side of this."

She takes the kit from you and drops it on the empty cot. "Brenna. Sergeant to you on the yard. Supper's barley stew at dusk. Drill's at dawn: wall laps, spear, shield, then sparring. Late once, you run it twice. Till supper, your time's your own."

She lets the curtain fall behind her.

Behind the curtain you change. The wool tunic goes on first, and falls to your knees. The padded jerkin goes over it, stiff, smelling of someone else's sweat. Then the boots. They clump on the boards, and the rags hold your feet in, mostly. You walk like a child in her father's shoes.

You leave the practice spear on the cot, with the carter's coat folded beside it.

At the gate, Wat sees you go by in the kit and gives you a solemn nod. The grin behind it gets away from him anyway.

The cobbled lane climbs from the gate and opens into a market square. An inn stands on one side, and a smithy rings from another, hammer on iron. A small shrine sits in the corner, and across from it a narrow stone building with a sign over the door. A board beside the door is thick with pinned notices, some fresh, some curling in the sun.

Inside, it is dim and quiet. A long counter runs across the back. There are a few benches, a hearth, and walls papered with more notices. An old man in furs dozes by the fire with his cap pulled down over his eyes.

Behind the counter, a sharp-featured woman looks up from a ledger. Her dark hair is pinned up with a pencil, and her fingers are inky. Her eyes go to the watch jerkin, then down to the boots, and they light up.

"You're the one from the gate. Barefoot on the common, robbed on the road, signed on with Hale." She sets the ledger aside. "Ilsa Crane. I keep the hall. News gets here before people do."

On the shelf behind her sits a fist-sized crystal, cloudy, with a crack running right across it.

Ilsa leans on the counter. "Want work? Slimes. Safe, steady, three copper for each core. Farms want pest slimes cleared, and the common's got more every day. Ask anyone why and they shrug." She reaches over and taps a notice on the wall. "Wolves and goblins pay better. They also bite."

She looks you over again. "Bronze tag is a silver to register. You've not got a silver, have you?" She answers herself before you can. "Watch pays at week's end. I'll let you owe it against your first cores."

She opens the ledger, turns it round to face you, and holds out the pen. "Shall I put you down?"

"Sure! I'd appreciate that," you say. "A few questions first, though. I assume the guard doesn't mind adventurer work on the side?"

Ilsa laughs. "Off duty? Hale's glad of it. He likes his people levelled."

"What's the best way to dispatch a slime? Anything I need to be wary of?" You spread your hands. "Assume I'm completely new to this, because I am."

Ilsa looks pleased to be asked. She straightens behind the counter and holds up a finger.

"One sharp thrust, through the jelly, into the core. The red bit in the middle. Crack it, it stops. It slumps down to a puddle, and you fish the core out. Cracked or whole, I pay the same. Alchemists grind them anyway."

A second finger. "Jelly stings bare skin like nettles. Worse in the eyes. Wear gloves if you can get them."

"Third. It'll cling to a hand or a boot. Pull free, steady. Don't flail; you only splash yourself."

"Fourth, work them on dry ground. One rolls into the brook, it floats off and you've lost it."

She folds the fingers down. "And one rule: never clear the common bare. Town keeps some for jelly. Children milk them. For proper pest work, the Tangle-edge farms want theirs gone." She tilts her head. "But there's wolves out there."

She dips the pen and writes in the ledger without waiting for more: NALA, WATCH, OWES 1 S. She turns the book round for your mark. Then she opens a drawer and roots through a tin that clinks.

She sets a small bronze tag on the counter, strung on a leather cord through a hole punched in one end.

"Bronze. You're the hall's now, and the hall's yours. Bring me cores and I'll take the silver off."

"Thanks!"

You tuck the bronze tag under your jerkin and clump back down the lane to the gatehouse. In the bunk room the practice spear lies on the cot where you left it. The shaft is smooth with other people's hands, and the blunted iron head is heavier than it looks.

Wat watches you go out through the gate with it. "Slimes? Mind your eyes, miss."

Out on the common, the slimes are everywhere. Thirty or so small ones bob in the grass, and here and there a bigger one sits, big as a melon. At the far end, down by the water, three children with jars are working. An old shepherd sits on a stile by the sheep.

You pick a fist-sized slime on dry ground, well away from the brook, and set your feet.

Something odd happens as you come near. It stops wobbling. Then it drifts toward you, slow and calm, like a cat coming to be stroked.

Your first thrust is clumsy. The spear goes in at a bad angle, but the blunt head punches through the jelly anyway and meets the core with a small, hard crack. The slime slumps into a puddle on the grass. The red core in the middle of it dulls to grey.

A window opens in front of your eyes.

Blue Slime defeated. Experience gained.

You fish the core out with two fingers. The jelly nettles your skin at once, a hot prickle, and you wipe your hand hard on the grass.

Then you go slowly. You work on your stance, then your grip, then how far to step in before you thrust. Every slime you come near does the same thing as the first: goes still, then drifts toward you and waits.

The second kill is cleaner. By the fourth you can judge how deep the core sits. Each one opens its little window as it falls. Your body learns the thrust faster than you expect it to, the step and the drive and the pull back all running together into one motion.

You take seven in all, every one of them small. You keep well clear of the children's end.

After the seventh, the window stays open, and a new line comes up under it.

Racial Experience threshold reached. Human is now LV 2.

Warmth runs down through your arms and legs. The spear feels lighter in your hands. Your breathing, a little ragged a moment ago, settles and goes deep and even.

Seven cores clink together in the jerkin's inside pocket. On the stile, the old shepherd is still watching you.

As you wipe your hands, a melon-sized slime rolls out of the long grass toward you. A red stone floats in its middle. It bumps against your boot.

"Oi!" A boy of about ten comes running up from the brook, his jar swinging. "Not that one! That's mine!"

He plants himself between you and the slime, fists on his hips, jelly to the elbows. "That's the fattest on the whole common. I saw it first. You spear it and I'll tell."

You smile at him. "You're welcome to it, lad. I'd never interfere with a great hunt like that."

He squints up at you, suspicious of being agreed with. Then he grins, drops to his knees by the slime and unstoppers his jar.

You shoulder the spear and walk upstream along the brook, away from the children and the stile. Past the footbridge, a screen of willows hangs down over a grassy bend. Once you are under them, the town and the road are gone behind a green curtain of leaves.

Three small slimes bob in the bend. As you step in under the willows, all three turn toward you.

You crouch by the nearest one. "Hello," you say. "Can you hear me?"

It sits there, blue and blank.

"Go left," you tell it. Then, "Right." Then, "Stay."

It only drifts an inch closer.

You stop talking and try it silently. You fix your eyes on the slime and will it, hard, to move.

Behind your ribs, the warmth you woke with stirs, like something huge turning over in its sleep.

All three slimes shiver at once and press in against your boots.

You hold on to the warmth. Then you push it at the nearest slime, the way you would push a door.

The air around the slime creases, like a sheet folded along a line. Then it lies flat again.

The slime is a hand's width farther from you. It never rolled. It was there, and then it was here.

Something leaves you with the push: a small, cool gap behind your ribs, like a sip taken from a full cup.

A window opens.

New skill acquired: [Dimension Magic LV 1]

You reach again, and this time you pull. The air creases. The slime is back at your boot.

A second sip goes. The warmth sits a little lower behind your ribs, still huge, still patient.

You try to fold it sideways, toward the brook. The warmth won't answer. The slime stays where it is.
