It's Sixthday, and everything hurts.

You find this out when you try to sit up. Your thighs hurt from the stairs, all those long flights of Ladder Lane and the ramp below the Mouth. Your back hurts from kneeling in the rubble. Your shoulders hurt, and your hands, and muscles in your arms you didn't know this body had, all of them stiff and sore and complaining at once. You lie back down and laugh at the ceiling, and that hurts too.

You get up anyway. Slowly. Like an old woman.

---

At porridge the widow sets your bowl in front of you, and then doesn't go back to the stove. She stands at your elbow with the ladle.

"Seven," she says. "Your week's up tomorrow. Rent for the week ahead is two marks, and I take it tomorrow morning, Restday, same as everyone."

"Yes," you say. "Of course."

She goes back to the pot.

Under the table, where she can't see, you open your purse in your lap and look into it. You know what's in there. You count it anyway. Four copper pennies, lying in the bottom of the leather, small and brown and worn. Four.

Two marks is twenty-four pennies.

You count them again, as if there might be more of them the second time. There aren't. You close the purse and sit very still with your hands on it and look at your porridge.

"What's wrong?" Wren says. She's across the table, spoon halfway, watching your face. "You've gone white as Elowen."

You tell her. Two marks tomorrow morning. Four pennies in your purse. You keep your voice down so the widow won't hear, and it comes out flat and small.

Wren puts her spoon down. "Right," she says. "Right. Eat your porridge. We'll think on the stairs."

---

On the stairs Wren grabs the banister to swing herself round the turn at the first landing, the way she always does, and stops. She sucks a breath in through her teeth. Then she lets go of the banister and tucks her bitten arm in against her side, and goes on down, a bit slower.

"Your arm," you say.

"It only aches when I grip." She doesn't look back at you. "Stop looking at it."

"I'm not looking at it."

"You're looking at it so hard I can feel it." But she glances back up at you over her shoulder, and there's a little crooked smile on her mouth. Then she's going on down, still talking. "Listen. Here's the other thing. Elowen's last paid night at the Kettle is tonight. After tonight she hasn't got anywhere to go."

You stop on the stair. You'd known that. You'd known it last night, when she said *one more*, and somehow you hadn't put it together.

"Every room in Corbel House is let," Wren says. "Full up. But a second lodger sharing a room pays half a mark a week. That's the house rule. Half a mark, on top of the room."

She stops at the bottom of the flight and turns and looks up at you, and waits.

You look at her. Then you think of room 7. The narrow bed under the slanted ceiling, barely wide enough to turn over in. The washstand, the chest, the pegs. Elowen's pack on one of the pegs, beside yours.

Wren grins. "It's your room," she says. "So it's you that asks her."

---

Elowen is outside the Brass Kettle when you come down into Coppergate, sitting on the step. Her pack sits beside her already packed, buckled shut, with her bundle of candles tied on top and her chalk box tucked in under the flap.

She stands up when she sees you, and tries to smile.

"I was going to come and find you," she says. "Both of you. I've got — " she looks at the pack — "I've got eleven pennies. I was going to go up to your house and ask the widow if she had a cupboard. Or a corner of the kitchen. Or something." She laughs, not very well. "I can sleep in a cupboard. I'm small."

You open your mouth to ask her, and it all comes out in the wrong order.

"Share my room," you say. "Room 7. With me. It's half a mark, the widow takes half a mark for a second person, and it's — I mean, there's only — the bed is — " You stop. Your face is hot. "The bed's narrow," you finish. "Very narrow. I should say that. It's really very — "

"Yes," Elowen says.

You haven't finished. She's said it right across the end of your sentence, fast, as though she was afraid you'd take it back. Then she hears herself, and she hears what you just said about the bed, and she goes pink. It starts at her collar and goes all the way up into her ears, and she looks down at her pack.

"Yes," she says again, more quietly. "Please. Thank you."

Wren, behind you, makes a noise that might be a cough.

---

The three of you take the corner table again, empty at this hour, and count it out.

"Two marks for the room," Wren says, tapping the table. "Half a mark for Elowen. Two marks and a half." She looks at you.

"Thirty pennies," you say.

"Thirty pennies." Wren holds her hand out flat. "Purses."

You put yours on the table and tip it out. Four coppers. Elowen unties hers and tips it out beside them: eleven. You push them together into one small heap, and Elowen counts it with one finger, moving each coin across as she goes, and you both watch.

"Fifteen," she says.

"Fifteen more," you say. "Before the market shuts tonight."

"And it's Restday tomorrow," Wren says. "Market's closed. Dealers are all shut. So whatever we find, we sell today, or it's no use to us. Not for the widow, it isn't."

Fifteen pennies. Yesterday the three of you came up with sixteen between you, and lost one of those to the guild. That was a whole day's sifting. That was a good day, Wren said.

You sit and look at the coins. Then you pull your pack onto your lap and take out the map, and unfold it on the table, pushing the coins aside to make room.

There's your chalk mark. The little white arrow you drew yesterday on the edge of the map, beside the dotted line of the side passage marked *uncertain*, pointing across from the right-hand wall to the left. Where the passage really is. You put your finger on it.

"This passage moved," you say. "Here. It's not where the map draws it, it's on the other side now. Wren, you said it'll have shifted this spring. So it's new." You look up. "If it's new, it's never been sifted. There'll be rubble in it, if it's anything like the rest. And whatever's in that rubble is still there. We'd be the first."

Wren looks at the map for a long time. Then she looks at you.

"That," she says slowly, "is a scout's thought."

You feel it all the way down, warm, like the ember-stone in your hand.

Wren slaps the table. "Right. We go down now. Now, this minute. And we stay down till the dealer's packing her baskets." She's already standing. "Come on. Elowen, bring your pack, we'll leave it with the warden."

---

The warden signs you into the Mouth book, *party of three*, and the names, and looks at the clock on the rock wall and writes down the hour.

You light your lamp, and get it first time.

Down the Long Hall, Elowen chalks fresh arrows as you go, beside yesterday's. On every fourth pillar there are two arrows now, side by side, yesterday's a little fainter and today's sharp and white. She draws each one carefully, though there's no need, and though you're all walking fast.

At the end of the Long Hall you turn toward the Well Room, and there it is, opening on the left, dark, the passage the map gets wrong.

"Wait here," Wren says. "When I call, come. Not before."

She goes in with her lamp shuttered low. You watch the light of it go away from you down the passage, a dim gold shape on the walls, getting smaller. Then it turns a corner and is gone.

You wait. Elowen stands close beside you. You stand close together and keep quiet, and listen. You can hear the drip of water somewhere far off, and your own heart.

"Come on!" Wren's voice comes back, small and echoing. "It's all right. Come and see."

---

The passage runs narrow and low, so low you have to duck your head in places, and so narrow your shoulders brush stone on both sides. It bends once, and again. And then it ends.

It ends in a heap of broken stone. A great slope of it, where the ceiling has come down, piled almost to the roof. Wren is standing at the foot of it with her lamp held up, and when you come round the last bend she turns and looks at you, and you can see her grinning even in the half dark.

"Look at it," she says. "Look."

You look. The stone is broken sharp. Every edge is clean and fresh, pale where it's split, and there's no dust on any of it. Back in the side room yesterday the rubble was grey and soft-edged and furred with dust, old as the hills. This is new. This came down this spring, maybe this month. You put your hand on it and it's sharp under your palm.

Untouched. You're the first to see this.

You get out your map. Elowen gives you a stub of chalk without being asked, and you kneel and draw the passage onto the map where it really is: out of the left-hand wall, narrow, a bend and another bend, and a little heap of scratches at the end for the rubble. Your hand isn't steady, and the lines are a bit crooked. You don't care.

Then the three of you kneel at the foot of the heap and start to sift.

You shutter your lamps right down, so low the passage is nearly dark, and look for the glow.

It takes minutes. Only minutes. Elowen is turning over a slab no bigger than a book when she gasps and stops, and puts her hand in under it, and brings it out closed. She opens it in front of you. An ember-stone, small and round, breathing orange in her palm.

"Oh," she says. "Oh, look. Look, I found one."

The second and third are yours. They come out of the same crack, one behind the other, as if they'd rolled in together. You draw out the first, and feel something else in there, warm against your fingertips, and draw that out too, and kneel there with one in each hand, laughing.

"Three!" Wren says. "*Three*, and we've barely started!"

She reaches up for a big block near the top of the heap, a slab of stone as wide as her chest, and tries to shift it to get at what's under it. She can't use her bad arm, so she does it one-handed. She gets it half lifted. Then she gasps, sharp, and drops it, and it slams back down onto the heap, and she sits back on her heels with her arm tucked tight against her side and her face screwed up.

"That's it," you say. "Sit down."

"I'm fine — "

"Sit *down*." You don't know where the voice comes from. It sounds like Elowen's in the side room yesterday. "Elowen and I will lift. You pick the cracks. You've got the pin and you've got a good hand, and you're better at cracks than either of us anyway."

Wren glares at you. Then she sits down on a flat stone at the foot of the heap with a great deal of grumbling, all of it under her breath, and gets out her bent pin, and starts probing the cracks you and Elowen open up for her, one-handed, her bad arm in her lap.

You and Elowen lift.

It's hard work. The blocks are heavy and sharp, and you can only shift the big ones together, the two of you shoulder to shoulder at the heap, getting your fingers under an edge and heaving, and rolling them off down the slope to thud on the floor. You're both soon sweating, even in the cold of the Deep. Elowen's hair sticks to her forehead. Your arms, already sore from yesterday, burn.

You heave one block over, and as it goes your hair comes loose and falls down across your face in a curtain, dark red, all of it at once, so you're blinded. You stand there holding the next block with your hair in your mouth.

Elowen laughs. It's a lovely laugh, startled out of her. She reaches over and pushes your hair back off your face with both hands, tucking it back behind your ears, carefully, one side and then the other. Her fingers are cold and dusty against your temples. She's very close, and she's still laughing a little, and then she stops laughing, and takes her hands away.

"There," she says.

"Thank you," you say. You both bend to the block.

---

The stones keep coming. That's the thing. That's the miracle of it. They keep coming, one and then another, out of cracks and from under slabs and down in the gaps between the blocks: small and warm and orange, each one glowing at you in the dark like an eye opening.

You keep the count aloud. You can't help it.

"Four," you say, when Wren pulls one out of a crack with her pin. "Five." That's Elowen's, from under a wedge of stone. You find the sixth. "Six." Wren, then Wren again. "Seven. *Eight.*" Each time you say it you're a little more out of breath, and it isn't only from the lifting.

By late afternoon there are twelve.

Twelve small ember-stones laid out on the flat stone where Wren is sitting, in two rows of six, glowing up at you. And the rubble is picked down to the floor. You've moved every block that can be moved, and Wren has gone through every crack. There's bare stone floor under the last of it, and that's all.

You sit back on your heels and look at the twelve stones.

"Twelve at two pennies," you say. You're working it out aloud, slowly, because you're tired and your head is full of dust. "That's twenty-four. And the guild takes its tenth. Out of twenty-four, a tenth is two, near enough. Two and a bit, and they round it down." You look up. "Twenty-two. If she buys them all."

"If she's still there," Wren says, and stands up. "What time is it? Come *on*."

---

You hurry back. You follow Elowen's arrows down the Long Hall at almost a run, the pillars going past one by one, the double arrows looming up white out of the dark and away behind you. Your lamp swings and the light swings with it. Your legs ache. You don't slow down.

The warden looks up when you come through the gate in a rush. She signs you out, and looks at the clock, and writes down the hour, unhurried, while Wren hops from foot to foot. Elowen grabs her pack from where it's been leaning by the warden's table. Then you're running up the ramp, all three of you, toward the light.

You come out of the Mouth into Coppergate in the low gold light of evening, and the market's ending.

Stalls are coming down all round the square. Awnings are rolling up. And over at the far side, the ember-stone dealer has her baskets covered already, all but one, and she's tying a cloth over that last basket, knotting the string.

Wren gets there first. She puts the twelve stones down on the counter in a rush, scattering them, glowing.

"Please," she says. "Please, one more. One more sale. We need it, we've got rent in the morning, all three of us — please — "

The dealer looks at Wren. She looks at the twelve stones, and at you and Elowen coming up behind, dusty to the elbows. She sighs, a long sigh.

Then she unties the string, and folds the cloth back off the basket, and gets out her brass scale. She weighs the stones. She picks them up one at a time and holds each up to the evening light and turns it, checking, while you stand there with your heart going.

"Two pennies apiece," she says at last. "Twenty-four." She counts out pennies onto the counter, and takes two of them back, and drops them in the little box with the guild's sign on it. "Less the guild's two. Twenty-two."

Wren splits it on the counter, sliding the coins with one finger. Seven for you. Seven for Elowen. Seven for her. And one left over in the middle, on its own.

She looks at it. Then she pushes it onto your pile.

"Nala's passage," she says. "Nala's penny."

You pick up your pile. Eight copper pennies. You put them in your purse with the four that were already there, and feel the weight of them. Twelve pennies. A silver mark's worth, in copper. Elowen puts her seven with her eleven, and holds the purse in both hands.

Twelve and eighteen. You do it in your head. Thirty.

"Thirty," you say. "That's two marks for the room. And half a mark for Elowen."

Elowen hugs you.

She does it right there in the middle of Coppergate, with the stalls coming down all round you, throwing both arms round your neck and holding on hard. She's warm and dusty and she smells of sweat and stone and herbs, and her cheek is against yours, and she's laughing into your hair. You hold her back. You don't want to let go, but she does. She lets go of you and turns round and hugs Wren just as hard.

Wren yelps. "*Arm!* Arm, arm, arm — "

"Sorry! Sorry, I'm sorry — " But Elowen doesn't let go of her right away, and Wren doesn't make her.

Afterwards Wren takes four of her seven pennies and twists them up in a scrap of cloth, tight, and knots it, and tucks it into her shirt. For Saltby. You watch her do it.

---

"Supper," Wren says, on the Kettle's step.

You look at each other. Four pennies each, for the Kettle's stew. You've got twelve, and every one of them belongs to the widow in the morning. Elowen has eighteen, and every one of those does too.

"We can't," you say.

"We can't," Elowen agrees.

"No," Wren says. "We can't." Then she goes off across the square to a baker's stall that's just closing up, and comes back with a round brown loaf under her arm. She's paid for it with one of her own three pennies.

"Party supper," she says, and holds it up. "Come on."

---

You climb the back stairs of the Kettle, three narrow flights and then a ladder-steep stair at the very top, to Elowen's room under the eaves.

It's tiny. The ceiling slopes down so low on both sides that there's only a strip down the middle where anyone can stand, and even there you have to stoop. So does Elowen. Wren is the only one of you who can stand up straight in it, and she does, in the middle of the floor, beaming, with her head just clearing the beam.

"Look at that," she says. "Made for me."

There's a pallet on the floor, and a hook, and a small window like a slot. You sit on the floor, the three of you, knees to knees, and Wren tears the loaf in three with her good hand.

There's no lamp. Elowen holds up her hand and calls her light, and it comes, the small soft one this time, and sits on her fingertips, and she sets her hand on her knee with the light on it like a candle. It lights up all three of your faces. It lights up the bread.

You eat. The bread is plain and brown and still a little warm, and it's very good.

"This is the last night I'll sleep up here alone," Elowen says.

She says it to the light on her fingers, thoughtfully, the way you'd say a thing to yourself. And then she hears it. You watch her hear it. Her face changes, and the pink comes up from her collar into her cheeks, all the way to her ears, and she stares at her bread.

"I didn't mean — " she says. "I meant — the last night *up here*, I meant I'll be — "

Wren laughs. She laughs so hard she has to put her bread down, and then she has to hold her arm, rocking, saying *ow, ow, ow* in between, tears in her eyes. Elowen covers her face with her free hand. The light on her other hand shakes.

You're laughing too. Your face is hot.

---

You leave Elowen to her last night at the Kettle, and you and Wren climb Ladder Lane in the dark.

In the kitchen at Corbel House the widow is wiping down the long table. You stop in the doorway.

"I've got a second lodger coming," you tell her. "Into room 7. Tomorrow."

The widow straightens up and looks at you. "Half a mark on top of the two," she says. "Both paid in the morning."

"Yes," you say. "I know. I've got it." You haven't, not on you, not all of it, but Elowen has the rest, and you'll have it in the morning, and that's enough. You can feel the twelve pennies in your purse against your hip.

The widow nods once and goes back to her wiping.

---

On the top landing you say, "Let me see it," and Wren holds out her arm without arguing, for once.

You unwind the binding in the light from under your door. Under it the bite is clean. The two punctures have closed over, dark and dry, and the skin round them is pink but not angry, not swollen, and there's no smell to it at all. Elowen's herbs, or Wren's own toughness, or both.

"It's closing," you say. "It's clean." And you're glad. You're more glad than you'd have thought, standing there holding her arm. You wind the binding back on.

"Told you," Wren says. Then her mouth goes crooked. "Room 7, then. With Elowen. You do know that bed's barely wide enough for one? I've seen it. You'll be sleeping on top of each other. One of you'll be on the floor by midnight. Or you'll both stay put, and then where will you — "

She stops.

She stops teasing, just like that, and looks at you. It's a long look. In the half dark on the landing you can't read it. Her hazel eyes are on your face and she isn't grinning.

"Night, Nala," she says.

She goes into room 8, and shuts the door.

---

You lie in your narrow bed in the dark.

After a while you stretch your arms out, both of them, one to each side. Your right hand finds the wall. Your left hand finds the edge of the mattress, and the cold air past it. You lie there measuring. It's barely wide enough for one. Barely wide enough for you, and you're small.

Tomorrow night Elowen will be in it with you.

You think of her knee against yours under the Kettle's table last night, warm through the cloth, and how you both kept still. You think of her hands pushing your hair back off your face in the rubble, cold and dusty, careful, one side and then the other, and how she stopped laughing.

You think of Wren's look on the landing, and wonder what it meant.

You fall asleep with twelve pennies in your purse, and your new passage drawn on your map.
