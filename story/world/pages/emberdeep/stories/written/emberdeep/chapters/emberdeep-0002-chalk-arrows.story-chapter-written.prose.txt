You wake, and before you open your eyes you lift your hands in front of your face.

Then you open them. Small and pale, freckled across the backs, the nails short and square. The same hands. The orange light round the shutter, the ceiling slanting down close above you, the scratch of the wool blanket, the weight of long hair tangled under your cheek. Room 7. You're still Nala.

You lie there and wait to find out how you feel about it. You fell asleep last night thinking about the Deep, about the cold breath of the Mouth and the careful labels on the map, and not about home at all. And now something in your chest lets go, slow and warm, and you realise you're glad. Glad to still be here, glad to still be her. More glad than sorry. You're not sure what that says about you, and for now you let it be.

Someone hammers on your door.

"Seven!" Wren's voice, through the wood. "Porridge! And then the party. Properly this time. Up, up, up."

"I'm up," you call, and your voice comes out high and sleepy and a girl's, and you laugh at it, alone in your bed.

---

The widow has the porridge on the table at seven exactly. Wren sits down across from you, tips her chair back on two legs, and puts her boots up on the corner of the table.

"Boots," the widow says, without turning round from the stove.

The boots come down. "She's got eyes in her back," Wren tells you, not quite quietly enough, and the widow's ladle stops for a moment and then goes on.

You eat fast, because Wren does. Then you go down Ladder Lane together in the cold bright morning, Wren two steps at a time and you one, hurrying, down through the long flights to Coppergate and across the square to the Brass Kettle.

Elowen is waiting outside it. She's standing very straight by the door with a new box of chalk under one arm and a bundle of candles tied with string under the other, and when she sees you both coming she lifts the chalk a little, like a flag.

"I got the good ones," she says. "The chalk. The man said it doesn't smudge."

At this hour the Kettle is empty except for a boy sweeping and the smell of last night's beer. The three of you take the corner table, the same one, and sit close round it with your elbows on the wood.

"Right," Wren says. "Party. This is how mine worked, before." She counts it off on her fingers. "Finds split even. Whoever finds it, whoever sells it, doesn't matter, it all goes in one pile and it comes out in equal shares. The scout goes first, and that's me. And we keep our heads. Everybody comes home. Being a hero is how you end up in the Lantern House with your leg in a splint." She puts both hands flat on the table. "So. What can you do?"

Elowen goes first, because she's been sitting forward on the bench waiting to. "I can make light," she says. "You've seen. Only a small one. And I can dress a cut, or a bite, or a burn. With these." She touches the pouch at her belt. "My mother taught me. I know what stops bleeding and what keeps a wound clean."

They both look at you.

You think about it honestly. "I can't do anything yet," you say. "Not anything that matters down there. But I can carry rope. And I can read a map."

You wait to see if that's enough.

"That's more than most coppers," Wren says. "Most coppers can't read a map. Most coppers walk in holding it upside down and come out the wrong side of a wall." She grins. "The rest I'll teach you. Both of you."

There's a moment, sitting there with the two of them, when you nearly say it. *I was a man yesterday morning. My name was Alan. I fell asleep over a book and I woke up here.* You can feel the shape of the words. You don't know what you'd say after them, or what their faces would do. You don't even know how you'd begin. So you keep it, and drink your small beer.

"So," Wren says. "We go down today."

Elowen's face goes white.

"Just the first level," Wren says. "Just to walk it. See the Long Hall, see the Well Room, poke round a bit, come back up. That's all."

Elowen looks at her, and at you, and swallows. Then she nods.

---

At the guild hall Wren goes straight to the party board. She finds her own name halfway down the list, *WREN TOLLEY, iron, scout, locks*, and rubs it out with her sleeve in three hard swipes, leaving a pale smear on the slate and a white streak on her cuff. Then she takes a stub of chalk from the ledge and, on the other side of the board, among the parties, she writes in big round letters:

*TOLLEY, MARSH, FAIRWEATHER.*

She steps back to look at it. Elowen reads it with her lips moving. You read it three times. It's only chalk. It's just your borrowed name in a row with theirs. You can't stop looking at it.

"There," Wren says, and dusts her hands off. "That's us."

---

You cross Coppergate, between the stalls going up for the day, and walk in under the arch of the Mouth.

The light changes the moment you pass beneath it. The sun is behind you, and the sky, and the noise of the market, and ahead of you a broad ramp runs down into the mountain, its stone worn smooth and dipped in the middle by more feet than you can imagine. You walk down it. The daylight thins behind you as you go. It goes grey, and then blue, and then it's only a pale shape back up the slope, and the air gets colder with every step.

At the bottom of the ramp is a gate: heavy iron bars set into the rock, standing open, with a lamp burning on a hook beside it. Next to the gate a woman in a guild coat sits at a table with a big book open in front of her.

"Party?" she says.

"Tolley, Marsh, Fairweather," Wren says. "First level."

The warden writes it in the book, *party of three*, and the names, and looks at a clock on the rock wall above her, and writes down the hour beside them. She turns the book round for Wren to make her mark, and Wren does.

"Sign out when you come up," the warden says.

"What if we don't?" you ask.

"Then you're searched for," Wren says, cheerfully. "Anyone not signed out of the Mouth book by next morning, the guild sends a party down after them. So don't forget to sign out, or you'll have a lot of very cross silvers looking for you while you're in the Kettle." She turns to you. "Light your lamp."

You get the tin lamp out of your pack. You practised this yesterday, all afternoon, and now your fingers are cold and clumsy and the little catch on the door slips out from under your thumb. You try again. The second time it catches, and the lamp wakes, and warm light spills out round your hands and across the floor.

Beyond the gate the dark is total.

You stand in the gateway with the lamp held up and look into it. The light goes out from you a little way, and stops, and past where it stops there's only blackness, thick and close and complete. The air is cold, and very still. You can hear your own breathing. You can hear Elowen's.

Elowen's hand finds yours.

She doesn't say anything. She doesn't look at you. Her fingers just slide into yours and close, cold and tight. You close yours back, and let her keep it.

---

The Long Hall opens up round you in the lamplight a little at a time.

There are pillars. That's the first thing. Great square pillars of dressed stone, each one wider than the three of you standing together, rising out of the light into the dark overhead so you can't see where they end. They march away ahead of you in two long rows, one pillar and then the next and the next, each a little fainter than the last, until the lamplight gives up and the rows go on without it. The floor between them is flagged and smooth and dusty. The walls are too far off to see.

"Who built it?" you ask. Your voice goes up into the dark and comes back changed.

"Couldn't tell you," Wren says. "Whoever it was, they were gone long before anyone came here. This was old before the town was built. Before the first house on Coppergate." She walks on a few steps, then stops and turns. "Lamp. Shutter it down."

You fumble at the shutters until the light narrows to a band.

"Lower. Like that. A wide light shows you everything and shows everything you. Keep it low, and when the scout stops, you stop. Not after. Not a step after. When I stop, you stop, and you wait, and you listen."

"When the scout stops, we stop," Elowen says.

"Good."

Elowen lets go of your hand to take a piece of the new chalk out of its box. She goes to the nearest pillar and draws an arrow on it, big and clear and white, pointing back the way you came, toward the gate.

Wren whoops. It goes echoing off up the Long Hall in pieces. "*Yes!* Look at that! Elowen, I could kiss you." She thumps the pillar beside the arrow. "Chalk is the best thing a copper ever brought down the Deep. My first party, we had a copper who'd brought a *harp*." She shakes her head at the arrow, delighted. "Chalk."

Elowen ducks her head over the chalk box, and when she lifts it again her face is shining.

She chalks an arrow on every fourth pillar after that. You hold the lamp for her while she does it.

At the end of the Long Hall you stop and get out the map, and kneel on the flags with the lamp beside it, and follow the line of the hall with your finger. Here's where it ends; here's the wall you can just see now, coming up out of the dark ahead. And here's the turn. To the left, a short passage, and then a square room with a circle drawn in the middle of it. *Well Room*.

"Left," you say. "Then it's just there."

Wren looks over your shoulder. "Go on, then," she says. "You lead."

You lead. Halfway down the short passage the map shows a side passage opening on the right, drawn in a dotted line with a little mark beside it: *uncertain*. You hold the lamp up to the right-hand wall as you go, waiting for it.

It isn't there. The wall runs on, solid. You walk on a few more steps, and the light finds a dark opening on the *left* instead, where the map shows only wall.

You stop. "That's wrong," you say. "The map's got a passage on the right, here. It's on the left. It's moved."

Wren stops beside you and looks at the opening, and at the map, and at you. Then she gives you one short nod, the kind you'd give to someone who's done a thing well and doesn't need telling twice.

"Shifted," she says. "They do that. Spring, it'll have moved since the map was drawn. Good eye. Mark it."

You don't have a pencil. Elowen gives you a stub of chalk, and you make a small white mark on the edge of the map by the dotted line, an arrow pointing across to the other side, and feel absurdly proud of it.

---

The Well Room is square and high, and in the middle of it is a well.

It's a round wall of old stone, waist-high, worn smooth on top by hands, with an iron bar across it and a bucket on a chain. Wren lowers the bucket. It goes down a long way. You hear it splash, far below, and then the chain clanking as she winds it back up, hand over hand.

The water is clean and very cold. You drink out of your cupped hands, and it's so cold it makes your teeth ache and your throat hurt, and it tastes of stone. Elowen drinks, and gasps, and laughs. Wren drinks with her whole face, and comes up dripping.

"Right," she says, wiping her mouth on her sleeve. "Finds."

She shows you where to look. Rubble: anywhere stone's come down and lies in heaps. Cracks: in the walls, in the floor, anywhere a crack goes back deep. The foot of a fallen wall, where things wash down and catch. "Ember-stones get into the cracks," she says. "Don't ask me how. You find them in the rubble mostly. Not many. A handful on a good day. You look for the glow, if your lamp's low enough."

There's a side room off the Well Room, small and half-choked with rubble where part of its ceiling has come down. You kneel in it, the three of you, with the lamps shuttered low, and sift through the broken stone with your bare hands. It's slow. Your fingers get cold and grey with dust. You move a stone and look under it, and move another.

And there, in a crack between two blocks of the fallen wall, something glows.

It's faint. A dull orange gleam, deep in the crack, so faint you think at first it's your lamp reflecting. You shutter your lamp right down to be sure. It's still there. You work two fingers into the crack, and then three, and close them on something small and round and warm, and draw it out.

An ember-stone. Smaller than the one you held in the market, the size of a hazelnut, with the slow orange light breathing in it. It's warm. It's warm in your cold dusty hand like something alive.

You hold it up, and you laugh. You don't mean to. It just comes out of you, loud and bright, and it goes echoing out of the side room and away down the passage, on and on, and comes back to you thinner from somewhere a long way off.

"First one!" Wren says. "Look at her! Look at her face!"

Elowen is smiling at you in the lamplight with dust on her nose.

Wren goes back to sifting, grinning. A minute later she's on her stomach at the foot of the fallen wall, peering into a deep crack low down, where something glows. "There's one in here," she says. "Big one, I think — " and she reaches in up to her elbow.

Something comes out of the crack.

It comes out fast, a grey heaving shape as big as a cat, all wet teeth and claws, and it goes straight up Wren's arm. She yells. Then it's off her, a long bald tail whipping past your lamp, and it's gone, out of the side room and into the dark of the Well Room and away, before you can even get to your feet.

Wren sits up, holding her forearm, and swears. She swears for a long time. She swears at the rat and the rat's mother and the rat's mother's mother, and at the crack, and at the Deep, and at herself, in words you've never heard put together like that, some of which you're fairly sure she's making up as she goes.

"I'm fine," she says at the end of it. "It's nothing. It's a rat. It's — "

"Sit down," Elowen says.

You've never heard her voice like that. It's quiet, and it isn't asking. Wren looks at her and sits down on a block of fallen stone.

"Sleeve," Elowen says.

Wren rolls her sleeve up. You hold the lamp close, opening the shutter wide. The bite is on the inside of her forearm, two deep punctures side by side, and blood is running out of them freely, down her wrist and between her fingers and dripping off onto the dust.

Elowen goes to the well and comes back with the bucket full. She kneels at Wren's feet and washes the bite with the cold water, pouring it slowly over Wren's arm with her cupped hand, again and again, while Wren hisses through her teeth. Then she opens the pouch at her belt and takes out a pinch of something dry and grey-green, crumbles it between her fingers, and packs it into the punctures with her thumb.

"I need something to bind it," she says, without looking up.

You haven't got any cloth. You look down at yourself. Then you take the hem of your shirt in both hands, find where the stitching is thin, and tear. It takes two tries. The strip comes away long and pale and uneven, and you hand it to her.

Elowen binds the bite with it, round and round, firm and neat, and tucks the end in under itself so it holds.

Her hands are steady the whole time. You watch them. They don't shake once, washing or packing or binding, not even when Wren swears again. And then it's done, and she sits back on her heels, and puts her hands in her lap, and they start to shake. They shake hard. She looks down at them, and laughs, a startled little laugh, and holds them up for you to see.

"Look at that," she says. "*Now* they go."

Wren turns her arm over and looks at the binding. It's tidy, the strip lying flat and even, the end tucked in.

"Elowen Fairweather," Wren says. "You're a marvel."

Elowen goes red, all the way up into her hair.

---

You keep sifting. You're more careful now. You've got your knife out, and before you put your fingers into any crack you poke the knife in first and wait. Wren does the same, and the three of you leave it unspoken. Elowen chalks a little cross by the crack the rat came from.

By afternoon you've got eight small ember-stones between you, laid out on a flat stone in a row, glowing.

Then Wren leads you on, past the Well Room, along another passage and another, to look at the far end of the level. The passages are narrower here, and lower. You follow your map as you go and it's right, mostly. At the very end the passage opens out into a small round chamber, and on the far side of it, a stair goes down.

It's broad and steep and cut into the rock, the steps worn hollow, and it goes down past where the lamplight reaches. A cold breath of air comes up it, colder than the Long Hall.

"Dry Stair," Wren says. "Second level's down there." She looks at it for a moment with her hands on her hips. "Not today."

You're glad. You're very glad. You don't say so, but you feel it all through you, and you're the first to turn round.

---

On the way back, in a narrow passage between the Dry Stair and the Well Room, the wall moves.

That's what it looks like. A patch of the wall low down at your left shifts and pours, and it isn't the wall, it's crawlers. Pale things, flat and crab-like, the size of your palm, coming out of the cracks in the stone in a spill, dozens of them, their legs clicking on the floor like rain on a roof. They're round your boots before you can step back. They nip. You feel them nipping at the leather, at your ankles, and one gets your calf through your breeches, a sharp pinch that makes you yelp.

"Light!" Wren shouts. "Open it up, open it — "

Your lamp is shuttered right down, the way she told you. You fumble at the catch. Your fingers are cold and there are things clicking round your feet and the catch slides and won't go.

Elowen steps past you and holds up her hand.

The light that comes to her fingertips isn't the small light from the market. It comes all at once and it's big, white, blazing, a light like noon, so bright you throw your arm over your eyes. Every crack in the walls jumps out sharp. Every crawler is a pale flat shape with a black shadow behind it.

The crawlers scatter. They go all at once, as if the light had shoved them, pouring away back into the cracks, into the dark under the walls, clicking, gone. In a few seconds the floor is empty.

Elowen stands there in the middle of the passage with her hand up, blazing white, breathing hard. The light pours off her fingers and over her face.

"Gods," Wren says. "I've never seen crawlers clear off that fast. Never."

Elowen lets the light go. It goes out, and the passage is dark again except for your lamp, and you blink against the purple shape of it still printed on your eyes. She lowers her hand and looks at it.

"I didn't know I could make it that bright," she says. Her voice is small. "I didn't know."

"You were brave," you tell her.

She shakes her head. She keeps shaking it. And then she stops shaking it, and stands there looking at you, and doesn't say anything at all.

---

You follow Elowen's chalk arrows back down the Long Hall to the gate, one pillar after another, white arrows looming up out of the dark one by one and pointing you home.

The warden signs you out of the Mouth book, and looks at the clock, and writes down the hour.

You climb the ramp. The light grows ahead of you, grey and then blue and then gold, and you come up out of the Mouth into the last of the daylight with the sun low over the rooftops, the three of you dusty to the knees and stiff all over and grinning like fools.

At the finds market the ember-stone dealer is packing up her baskets. Wren puts your eight stones out on her counter in a row. The dealer weighs them on her brass scale and looks at each one, turning it in the light, and nods.

"Two pennies apiece," she says. "That's sixteen, less the guild's tenth." She counts out pennies onto the counter, and sets one aside in a little box marked with the guild's sign. "Fifteen."

Wren scoops them up and splits them right there on the counter, sliding the coppers into three piles with one finger. Five, five, five. She pushes one pile to you and one to Elowen.

You pick yours up. Five copper pennies, warm from the dealer's hand. Your first wages in this world. You put them in your purse with the three already there, and the purse is heavier by five.

Wren takes three of her own five pennies and twists them up tight in a scrap of cloth, and ties the scrap off with a knot, and tucks it into her shirt.

"What's that for?" you ask.

"Home," Wren says. "Saltby. It's on the coast. Fishing village. My mam and da are fishers, and there's eight of us, and I'm the second." She shrugs. "I send half home. Bit more, this time. They'll be glad of it."

---

At the Brass Kettle you eat supper at the corner table: stew and bread and small beer, four pennies each. You count out four coppers from your purse onto the table, and four stay behind in your purse, of the eight you held.

You're hungrier than you've ever been. The stew is the best thing you've ever eaten.

"That's my second night," Elowen says, partway through. "Here. Of the three." She stirs her stew. "One more."

Wren hears it. She looks at Elowen for a while over her mug, thoughtful, with her binding showing white at her wrist, and keeps whatever she's thinking to herself.

Then she raises the mug. "To the party," she says.

"The party," you say, and Elowen says it too, and you knock your mugs together and drink.

Under the table, Elowen's knee comes to rest against yours.

It's a small thing. Her knee, warm through the cloth between you, pressed lightly against the side of yours. You feel it all the way up. You don't move your leg, and Elowen doesn't move hers, and the two of you go on eating your stew and listening to Wren as if your knees weren't touching at all, and they stay touching till the end of supper.

---

When the Kettle starts emptying out, Elowen goes up to her room under the eaves. You and Wren climb Ladder Lane together in the dark, slower than this morning, your legs aching on every step.

On the top landing, by the light from under your door, you see that the binding on Wren's arm has gone dark. Blood has soaked right through it.

"Wren. Your arm."

She looks down at it. "Oh. It's fine."

"It isn't. Come here." You open door 7 and take her by the other wrist, and she lets you, and you sit her down on the edge of your bed.

You light your lamp. You pour water from the jug into the basin, and kneel in front of her, and unwind the binding, and the cloth sticks and she hisses. You wash the bite in the basin, cupping the water over it the way Elowen did, slowly, until it runs clean and pink. The punctures are still oozing a little. Then you take the hem of your nightshirt from where it hangs on the peg, and tear a fresh strip from it, and bind her arm with it, round and round, as firm and neat as you can, and tuck the end under.

Wren is quiet. For once she doesn't say anything at all. You can feel her watching. She watches your hands working on her arm, and then, when you glance up, she's watching your face.

You're very aware of her arm in your hands. Her bare forearm, warm and sun-browned, the skin soft on the inside where the bite is, and harder and freckled on the outside, with fine sandy hairs that catch the lamplight. You can feel her pulse under your fingers when you tie the binding off. You hold her arm a moment longer than you need to.

"There," you say.

Wren looks at the binding. "Thanks," she says, gruffly. She clears her throat. "Haven't had anyone fuss over me like that since I left Saltby."

She gets up. On her way to the door she puts her good hand on top of your head and ruffles your hair, hard, the way you'd ruffle a little sister's. "Night, Nala," she says.

Then she's gone across the landing, and her door closes.

---

You lie in bed with the lamp out and turn the day over.

The dark beyond the gate, total and cold. The pillars going away into it. The stone in the crack, warm in your cold hand, and your own laugh coming back to you from the far end of the passage. Elowen's hands, steady and then shaking. The crawlers pouring out of the wall, and the white blaze of her light, and her face in it.

You think of Elowen's knee against yours under the table, the small warm weight of it that both of you left where it was. You think of Wren's arm in your hands, her pulse under your fingers, and her eyes on your face.

You're tired. You're tired all through, in your legs and your back and your hands and the backs of your eyes, the deep good tiredness of a body that's worked all day and been fed and lain down, and you can't remember ever being tired like this as Alan. You fall asleep in it.
