Your arm is dead.

That's the first thing you know, Firstday morning, before you've even opened your eyes: your left arm has gone completely numb from the shoulder down, heavy as a log, prickling faintly at the fingertips. And there's a weight on it. A warm weight, and soft hair under your chin, and slow breath against your collarbone.

Elowen is asleep on your arm.

She's curled into you in the narrow bed with her head on your shoulder and her hand fisted loosely in the front of your nightshirt, and her mouth is a little open, and she's breathing slow and deep. The orange light round the shutter lies across her cheek. Her ash-brown hair is everywhere, over your chest and in your mouth.

You could move. You'd only have to ease your arm out from under her, slowly, and she might not even wake.

You stay still. You lie there with your arm going from numb to pins and needles to numb again, and you watch her sleep, and you don't move it at all.

After a while her eyelashes flicker. She stirs, and stretches against you, all down the length of you, and opens her eyes, green and blurry, an inch from yours.

You see her remember. It goes across her face like light across water: the bed, you, last night, the kiss. Her eyes go wide. She goes pink, from her collar up, all the way.

Then she leans up, quickly, and kisses you. Just once. Just a quick soft press of her mouth on yours, warm and sleepy, and then she's pulled back and hidden her face against your shoulder again, and you can feel her smiling into your nightshirt.

"Morning," she says, muffled.

"Morning," you say. "I can't feel my arm."

She sits bolt upright. "Oh — oh, I'm sorry, I'm *so* sorry — "

You're laughing. You can't stop laughing, lying there shaking your dead arm while the blood comes back into it in a hot buzzing rush, and after a moment she's laughing too.

---

You dress in the narrow room. There's barely space for one person to dress in it, and there are two of you, and you bump elbows reaching for your shirts, and bump hips at the washstand, and both reach for the same peg at the same moment and knock your heads together. You laugh under your breath every time, so Wren won't hear through the door. Elowen laces her bodice with her back to you, and then turns round and does yours for you, because you've tied it crooked again. Her fingers are quick at your ribs. You both leave the kiss unspoken. You keep catching each other's eyes and looking away, smiling.

When you open the door Wren is on the landing.

She's leaning against the wall by door 8 with her arms folded, already dressed and booted. She looks at you. She looks at Elowen behind you. She looks from one of your faces to the other — your face, you can feel, is hot, and Elowen's is pink to the ears — and something happens behind her eyes, quick, and is gone.

"Porridge won't wait," is all she says.

She goes down first, two steps at a time, and doesn't look back.

---

At porridge you open your purse under the table out of habit, and it's still empty. Of course it is. Elowen's is too. Wren tips hers out beside her bowl and two pennies roll out onto the table.

"That's the lot," she says. "The party's whole fortune. Two pennies." She scoops them back up.

She picks up her spoon. She grips it hard, and looks at her hand doing it. She reaches out and grips the edge of the table, hard, so her knuckles go white, and lets go. Then on the way out of the kitchen she grabs the banister at the foot of the stairs and swings herself round on it, all her weight on her bitten arm, and lands on the bottom step.

"Right again," she says, and holds up the arm and flexes the fingers at you. "Look at that. Right as rain. Good as new. The Pools, I'm telling you."

---

On the steps outside Corbel House she stops and turns round.

"Here's the thing," she says. "The first level near the gate's picked over. Everyone goes there, every copper in Emberdeep, and they've had every stone out of every crack from the gate to the Well Room. Your passage was luck. We won't get luck like that twice." She looks at you both. "We should try the second."

Elowen's face goes white.

"It's allowed," Wren says, quickly. "Copper party of three can go as far as the third level. You heard the clerk read it. So we're allowed. And I've been down there plenty. It's wetter, and there's worse things in it than rats, but the stones are bigger. A lot bigger."

Your stomach turns right over. You think of the Dry Stair, broad and steep, going down out of the lamplight, and the cold breath coming up it. You remember how glad you were when Wren said *not today*.

You nod.

Elowen looks at you nodding. She swallows. Then she nods too.

---

At the guild hall the clerk tells you the second level's map is three pennies.

Wren has two.

You stand at the desk looking at the two pennies in Wren's palm. Then you take your own map out of your pack, the first level's, and turn it over. The back of it is blank. Thin, pale, empty paper.

"I'll draw it," you say. "The second level. As we go. On the back of this."

Wren stares at you. Then she laughs, delighted, and claps you on the shoulder so hard you stagger. "*Listen* to her. That makes you the party's cartographer, then, Nala Marsh. Every party worth anything has one." She pockets her two pennies again. "Elowen, give the cartographer some chalk."

---

The warden signs you into the Mouth book: *party of three*, the names, *second level*, and the hour.

You walk the Long Hall past Elowen's arrows, two to a pillar now and white in the lamplight, and on past the Well Room, and down the far passages to the round chamber at the end of the level. And there's the Dry Stair, going down.

You go down it.

The steps are broad and steep and worn hollow in the middle, so you have to set your feet carefully at the edges. It goes down a long way. It's colder with every step. The lamplight goes ahead of you down the stair and finds more stair, and more, and the cold breath you felt last time comes up past you steadily, raising the hair on your arms. Elowen is behind you with one hand on your shoulder. You can feel through your shirt how cold her fingers are.

At the bottom the air changes.

It turns damp. Wet. It smells of stone and water and something green. You hold up your lamp and the walls shine: water is seeping down them, all over, in thin bright sheets, and pooling on the floor in puddles that catch the light. Somewhere ahead of you there's the sound of dripping, a lot of dripping, echoing.

You stop at the foot of the stair, and turn your map over, and draw. The bottom of the stair, a square. The passage going off ahead. A little mark for the water. Then you walk on, and at the next turning you stop again, and draw that too. At every turning. Wren waits for you each time without a word.

---

The walls start to glow.

Faintly. At first you think it's your eyes. Then you shutter your lamp down and you can see them: little pale mushrooms growing in clusters out of the wet stone, each cap no bigger than a fingertip, and every one of them giving off a soft cold glow, greenish-white, like moonlight on frost.

"Gloomcaps," Elowen says at once. She's gone straight to the wall. She touches one with a fingertip, very gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Oh. Oh, look at them. Mam's got a jar of these, dried. I've never seen them growing." She turns to you, and her face is bright. "The apothecaries on Coppergate buy them fresh. A penny for six. For sleeping draughts."

She takes out her herb pouch, and starts picking them. She does it carefully, pinching each one off at the base so the cluster's left to grow again, and laying them in the pouch on top of her herbs.

Wren watches the water.

"Here's what's down here," she says, low, while Elowen picks. "Lizards. White ones. Blind, no eyes at all, about as long as your arm. They hunt in the water. They feel you moving in it." She looks at you both. "And their bite festers. It goes bad, and it stays bad, and you end up in the Lantern House for a week having it cut clean. So keep out of the deep water. And watch the surface. If it moves wrong, you shout."

---

The passage opens out, and you come to the edge of the Drowned Hall.

It's wide. Wider than the Long Hall, wide enough that the far wall is only a darkness. And its whole floor is under water: still, clear, cold water, knee-deep, lying flat as glass across the hall from wall to wall. Your lamps shine down through it and light it all the way to the bottom. You can see every flagstone down there, pale and clean, and the pillars standing up out of the water, and their reflections standing down.

There's no way round. You step in.

The cold is like being bitten. It goes through your boots and your breeches in an instant and closes round your shins and your knees, and all three of you gasp out loud at once. Elowen says something in a very small voice that might be a word her mother wouldn't like. You wade on, gasping, your feet going numb, the water pushing at your legs.

At the hall's edge, where part of the wall has come down in a heap of fallen stone, Wren bends to look. She reaches into the heap. When she brings her hand out, there's an ember-stone in it, and it's big. The size of a walnut. It glows in her palm like a coal.

"*There*," she says. "See? Second level. Stones are bigger. Market pays four pennies each for these."

Four pennies. A whole supper, in one stone.

You search the hall's edges after that, all three of you, wading along the walls through the cold water and climbing up onto every heap of fallen stone. Your feet go from aching to numb to nothing. You can't feel your toes. You find one, wedged among the rubble at the foot of a pillar, the size of a walnut and warm as a hand. And then another, a little further on. Elowen finds one stuck in a crack in the wall above the waterline, glowing at her, and has to stand on tiptoe to work it out.

She picks gloomcaps as she goes, wherever they grow along the walls, and counts them into her pouch under her breath. "Fifteen. Sixteen. Seventeen." She reaches up for one last one. "Eighteen."

---

You're turning to go on along the wall when you see it.

A ripple. Out in the open water, between you and Elowen. A long low V-shaped ripple sliding across the flat surface — and it's moving the wrong way. The water in the hall moves, very slowly, one way, toward the far end; you've been feeling it pushing at your legs. This ripple is going against it. Toward Elowen's legs. Fast.

"*Elowen!*"

You shout it so loud it cracks. She turns. And something long and white comes up out of the water at her.

It's a lizard. It's as long as your arm and white as bone, no eyes at all where its eyes should be, just smooth blank skin, and its mouth open, coming up out of the water at her knees in a rush of spray.

Elowen throws up her hand, and the light comes. It comes blazing white, as bright as it was with the crawlers, brighter, so bright the whole Drowned Hall jumps out of the dark around you, every pillar and every ripple, and you're blinded with it.

The lizard keeps coming. It's blind. The light doesn't slow it at all.

You don't think. You're already moving. You lunge through the water and grab Elowen by the arm and haul her back toward you, hard, so she stumbles and falls against you. And with your other hand you've got your knife out, and you stab down at the white thing in the water.

The knife skids. It hits the lizard's back and slides off, as if it had hit wet stone, and then catches as it slides and cuts. A long cut down its side. Something dark spreads in the water.

The lizard twists. It flips round on itself in a wrench of white body and whips away, and it's gone, down into the water and out across the hall and away, vanished. The ripple of it fades. The surface goes flat.

Elowen stands there in the knee-deep water, gripping your arm, shaking. Her other hand is still held up, and the light is still burning on her fingers, white and fierce, lighting up her face. She's breathing in great gulps.

Wren comes splashing over through the water, fast, sending spray everywhere. Her face is white. She grabs Elowen by the shoulders and looks at her, and then she's bending down, plunging her hands into the freezing water, feeling Elowen's legs through her clothes, her knees and shins and calves, both legs, all the way down. Checking. Looking for the bite.

"Whole," Wren says. She stands up. "She's whole. It didn't get her." She lets out a long breath, and turns to you. "Nala. You did well. That was well done." Then her voice goes hard and quick. "And now we're getting out of the water. *Now.*"

---

There's a ledge at the edge of the Drowned Hall, a shelf of dry rock a little above the water, and you scramble up onto it, all three of you, and sit there dripping.

That's when your hands start shaking.

You look down at them. They're shaking so hard you can't hold your knife, and you put it down on the rock. You can't stop them. You press them together and they shake together. The knife skidding on the white back. The open mouth coming up out of the water at Elowen's knees.

Elowen takes your hands.

She takes them both in both of hers, and holds them in her lap, and doesn't say anything. Her hands are cold and wet and still trembling a little themselves. She just holds onto yours, tight, steady, and doesn't let go. She holds them until the shaking slows. Until it stops.

---

You keep to the dry edges after that, all of you. You climb along the ledges and the fallen stone at the margins of the hall and stay out of the water wherever you can, all three of you, keeping your feet dry as if your lives hung on it.

In a fallen wall at the far side of the hall Wren finds a fifth stone. Walnut-sized. She holds it up, grinning, and the grin is a little thin, but it's there.

Before you leave you sit on the ledge and get out your map, and draw the Drowned Hall onto it. The wide shape of it. The pillars, little dots. The ledges round the edge. And out in the middle of the water, where the lizard came up, you draw a small careful cross, and beside it, in your best letters: *LIZARD*.

Wren looks over your shoulder. Then she holds out her hand for the map, and you give it to her, and she studies it by the lamplight for a long time. She turns it to the light. She traces the passages with her finger, the Dry Stair, the turnings, the Drowned Hall, the cross.

"That's clearer than the guild's," she says at last. She hands it back. "Honestly. That's better than theirs."

---

You climb the Dry Stair, and walk the Long Hall by Elowen's arrows, and sign out of the Mouth book. The warden writes down the hour. Your feet squelch in your boots all the way up the ramp.

At the finds market the ember-stone dealer weighs your five stones and holds each one up to the light, turning it.

"Four pennies each," she says. "Second level, are they? Good ones. That's twenty." She counts out the coppers, and takes two back for the little box with the guild's sign on it. "Less the guild's two. Eighteen."

Wren splits it on the counter. Six for you. Six for Elowen. Six for her.

Then Elowen takes you both across Coppergate to an apothecary's shop, a narrow dim place that smells of a hundred dried things at once, and tips her eighteen gloomcaps out onto the counter for the old woman there. The old woman counts them, sniffs them, and pays three pennies.

Elowen gives one to you, and one to Wren, and keeps the third.

"They were your gloomcaps," you say.

"They were the party's," Elowen says firmly. "It was the party's hall."

You put the penny in your purse with your six. Seven copper pennies. It's the first coin that's been in your purse since you tipped the last of it into the widow's palm on Restday, and you can feel it there, the small dear weight of it against your hip.

Wren takes half her share and twists it up in a scrap of cloth, and knots it, and tucks it into her shirt. For Saltby.

---

At the Brass Kettle you each count four pennies onto the table for supper. Three pennies stay in your purse.

You eat at the corner table, damp to the knees, your boots still squelching. The stew is hot and the Kettle is warm and loud, and for a long while the three of you hardly talk. You're too tired. You just eat, and feel the warmth coming back into your feet.

Then Wren looks up from her bowl.

"Got lizards in Fennick?" she says. Lightly. Teasing.

You put your spoon down.

You look at your stew for a moment. You think about the landing on Restday night, and how the quiet fell, and the lie of it, sitting in your chest ever since like a stone. And you think about Elowen's hands holding yours on the ledge until they stopped shaking. And Wren's face, white, as she came splashing through the water.

You decide to tell them some of the truth.

"I don't know," you say. "I don't know anything about Fennick. Not a thing. It's a blank."

They're both looking at you.

"I mean it," you say. "My memory of this — of being here — it starts in room 7. On Fourthday morning. I woke up in that bed, and there was a pack on the peg with my name stitched in the flap, and a letter in it with *Fennick* on it, and a purse, and a key. That's how I know my name's Nala Marsh. It says so in the letter." You swallow. "Before that, it's blank. I don't know Fennick. I don't remember the reeve, or the road, or anything."

Under the table Elowen's hand finds yours, and takes it, and holds it hard.

Wren is quiet for a moment. She looks at you very steadily. And then she nods, slowly, as if something's been settled.

"Well," she says. "That explains a lot. Not knowing what an ember-stone was. Not knowing what the *Deep* was. Gods." She almost laughs. "I thought you were just from very far up a very small valley."

"I'm sorry," you say. "That I didn't say. On Restday."

Wren shakes her head. "I couldn't tell you where Fennick even is," she says. "Hardly anyone up here could. It'll hardly ever come up. You won't have to answer for it much." She reaches across the table and taps her spoon against your bowl. "And the party knows now. That's enough."

---

Wren walks you both up Ladder Lane, between you, with an arm through each of yours, one through yours and one through Elowen's. She talks the whole way up about lizards, and how big that one was, and how it'll be bigger every time she tells it. Elowen laughs. You can feel Wren's arm in yours, warm and wiry, all the way.

On the top landing Elowen goes into room 7 first.

You go to follow her. Wren catches your sleeve. She holds you back, gently, there on the landing, and Elowen's door swings almost shut behind her.

"Nala," Wren says, quietly. "Is it you and Elowen now?"

You look at her. Her face is half in shadow.

"Yes," you say.

It's true. But it isn't all of it. You can feel the rest of it there, sitting right behind the yes — Wren in the Pools, her one hazel eye opening, that small smile; Wren on the landing on Sixthday night, the look you couldn't read; Wren now, with her hand on your sleeve. There's more to say. A lot more. You don't have the words for it yet. You don't know how to begin. So you keep it, for when you do.

Wren nods. She looks down.

"She's good," she says. "Elowen. She's good, all the way through." Her voice catches. Just once, on *through*, and she clears her throat. "So you be good to her."

You reach for her hand.

You find it in the dark, and hold it. Wren's fingers close round yours. She squeezes back, hard, so hard it almost hurts. Then she lets go, and turns, and goes into room 8, and shuts the door.

---

In room 7 Elowen is already in the narrow bed, against the wall, and she's holding the blanket open for you.

You get into your nightshirt and get in beside her, and she lets the blanket fall over you both. You lie face to face. Your knees touch. In the dark you can just see the shine of her eyes.

"Thank you," she whispers. "For pulling me out of the water."

"You'd have done it for me."

"I'd have tried." She's quiet. "I'd have *tried*."

You kiss her. Or she kisses you; you're not sure which of you moves first. It's longer than last night. Slower. Her lips are soft and sure this time, and they don't tremble. Her hand comes up and rests on your waist, on the curve of it, through your nightshirt, warm and light, and stays there. You kiss her for a long time, and when you stop, her hand stays on your waist.

She falls asleep like that, her forehead against your chin.

You lie awake a little longer, with Elowen warm against you. You can hear the house settling. Across the landing, room 8 is silent.

You think of Wren's voice catching on *through*.

You fall asleep thinking of it.
