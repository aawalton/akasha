The last thing you remember is your back window. A book open on your knee, the evening going blue outside the glass, your eyes getting heavy.

Then warm stone at your back, and something chattering at you from very close by.

You open your eyes. A black-and-white bird with a long tail sits on a stone ledge an arm's length from your face, head cocked, one bright eye on you. It says chak-chak-chak, scolding, and hops back.

You sit up, and your body is wrong.

Your arms are thin, the wrists narrow, the hands small and pale with freckles across the backs. Long dark red hair falls over your shoulder. Your old grey shirt hangs off you, the collar slipping to one side. Your black tights are still on. Your feet are bare, and small, and dusty.

You look down at the rest of yourself once, and then you look at the bird instead.

You are sitting against a little stone shrine, roofed with slate, with a worn carved figure inside it you cannot make out. Two dirt roads cross in front of you. Behind you, big grey beeches crowd close, their leaves going gold. A wooden signpost leans at the corner. One arm points north, toward fields and, far off, the top of a town wall with smoke above it.

You can read the sign. You have never seen these letters before, and you can read them like a street sign at home.

Then you notice the air.

It is thick with something. Not smell, not sound. Faint lines of it run between the trees and along the roads like threads in water, and when you look at them they get clearer, and when you lift your hand they lean toward your fingers as if they have been waiting for you. It does not frighten you. It feels like yours.

The bird shrieks and bolts into the beeches. Down the east road, a cart is coming, and someone on it is singing badly.
