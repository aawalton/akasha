"Yes," you tell the father. The man's shoulders drop with relief.

You turn to the washerwoman. "I'll need to take care of the fox first, and I can't risk running out of mana. But I could help you in the evening, if you'd like to come back then."

"Years I've waited," the washerwoman says. "An evening's nothing. I'll come at the dusk bell."

"Fairley," the father says, and you walk out the east gate beside him, along the Thornmere road.

You keep to the way the thread from his son's bite pointed yesterday: east.

After an hour the road bends past a stone farmhouse and a coop with feathers on the ground.

Past the coop, across the barley stubble, a dark smear shows to your sight, low under a hedgebank.

You cross toward it. It sits in a hazel copse on the bank, under a hole in the roots.

An old badger sett. The ground around its mouth has gone gray and bare.

Down under the earth the smear is curled and still. The fox.

Fairley grips his stick. "That's where it lies up by day, then."
