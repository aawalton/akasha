You sit on the bench, glad of it. The weight comes off your raw feet, and you let out a long breath.

Outside, the noon bell rings slow and deep over the square.

Marda looks at your feet and grunts. She levers herself up on her cane, goes to the box of old gear, and drops a pair of worn leather boots on the floor by the bench.

"Loan. Like the knife."

She sits again, heavily, and pours herself one cup of cider from a jug on the desk.

"Three corrupted beasts reached the fields this winter. Used to be one a year, if that. A boar, Level 14. A stag, 17. A wolf, 15."

She lets that sit.

"Whatever your level is, it isn't that. Any one of them would kill you. Don't think otherwise."

"First you see black smoke at the mouth. Then the whites of the eyes go black. Then the eyes go. Arrows and blades barely slow them. They dodge magic. But holy magic kills them fast."

"A bite from one festers. Oozes black. Lays a curse on you that won't lift. And it takes the hurt first. The injured, the grieving. It finds them."

"When one dies, what's left gathers into a blightstone. Enough Purify cracks a blightstone, and it bursts into a clean glimmerstone."

She taps the desk. "Purify is a spell. You want it. With a card like yours, you should be able to learn it. Once you can cast it, I'll put you on blight work. Not before. Until then you grow on clean beasts at the edge, and you keep clear of anything corrupted."

"I'm writing Thornmere again today. A fourth letter. This time I'm naming a white-gold card."

She sets down her cup and looks at the pink seam on your forearm.

"Now. That bite. You closed it yourself, didn't you?"

"Yes," you say. "Is there something blighted around I could test with? I'd like to see if I can unlock Purify without buying it from the skill store."

Marda looks at you a long moment. "Never heard of anyone doing that." She pauses. "Doesn't mean it can't be."

She reaches under the desk and unlocks a box lined with lead. With a pair of iron tongs she lifts something out and sets it on the desk beside her cider jug.

It is a stone the size of a walnut, black as pitch.

To your sight it has no glow at all. There is only a dark smear, turned in on itself.

"From the stag," Marda says. "Don't touch it bare-handed. Go on, then."

You look for white-gold in the currents. In here there's only thin blue and yellow, running along the walls.

Then you remember the card. Your own mana lit it white-gold.

You reach inward and push out a trickle of your own mana. It comes out as a white-gold thread.

You draw it down into the black stone.

The blight bites back. Cold shoots up the thread into your hand, and the thread tries to slip.

You hold on. You pull the thread through the dark, slowly, like drawing a needle through tar. Where it passes, the black comes apart in strands and burns away in a thin white-gold light.

When you let go, your own mana is running low. The stone on the desk is a clear shade paler than it was.

Marda has half risen from her chair, both hands on her cane, staring at it.

A blue box opens.

[New skill acquired – Cleansing Weave.]
[Cleansing Weave – At [Basic] level, draw holy current through blight to unpick it.]

"Not quite Purify yet," you say, "but part way there. If you don't mind a few steps, I think I can finish it off after a rest or two."

Marda sinks back into her chair slowly. Her eyes stay on the paler stone.

She takes up the tongs and lays the stone back in the lead box, in a corner apart from the others.

"That one's yours to come back to, then. I'll not send it to Thornmere."

She locks the box and sits back. "Crack it clean, and the glimmerstone that comes out is yours. Same for any bounty stone you clean after that."

"Post opens at the dawn bell, shuts at the dusk bell. I'm at this desk the whole of it."

You head back out for another twenty frostcaps. There's still the afternoon.

You walk the two miles south between the winter fields in Marda's loaned boots. The boots help, but your soles are still raw from this morning's walk, and every step reminds you.

At the crossroads the magpies chatter at you from the gold beeches.

You go in behind the shrine and west along the edge path, past the huge old beech. The blue current runs on west, past the lightning-split beech, deeper in toward the sound of water. You follow it.

Where it brushes an old beech root, frostcaps glow cold on the north side.

By afternoon the frost holds only in the shade, but the frostcaps there are firm and good. You cut each one at the root with the loaned knife, and leave the gray, ash-smelling ones be.

Root by root, the count climbs. An hour and a half in, you have fifteen.

The shade under the trees is cold. Your fingers go stiff, and your stomach aches, empty.

The current bends down toward a brook, where frost still lies thick on a row of big roots. Frostcaps glow there, plenty for the last five.

Two antlered rabbits graze among those roots, each with a small glow about it.

You circle wide through the beeches, so that you come at the rabbits from the open side. To reach you now, they would have to leave the roots and cross the bare frosted ground.

A twig cracks under your boot.

Both heads come up. Their glows gather at the antlers.

They come off the roots together, bounding across the open ground at you.

You hold until they're well clear of the roots. Then you reach into the blue current and pull.

It comes hard. You sweep the lash low across both throats as they close.

You're a hair late. The second one is already mid-leap, and the lash only just catches it.

Both rabbits drop in the frost and lie still.

The end of the lash snaps on past them into the roots behind. Three frostcaps there bruise gray. Enough good ones still stand for your last five.

You reach inward and find only a thin trickle left. You're nearly empty.

Two dead rabbits lie on the frosted ground, a faint glow at the base of each one's antlers.

You pick the rest of the good frostcaps off the brook roots, cutting each one at the root. There are eight. Your last five, and three over. Twenty-three in all.

You take each dead rabbit by the hind legs, one in each hand, and start back.

Back past the split beech, the old beech and the shrine, then up the north road. The rabbits get heavier every mile, and your raw soles burn inside the loaned boots.

The light is going amber over the winter fields by the time you reach the south gate.

At the post, Marda looks up from her letter at the two rabbits and raises her eyebrows.

You collect the first rabbit from the floor inside the door, by the gear box.

With three rabbits hanging from your hands, you cross town to the Wool Square. Round the side of the Crook and Candle, through the yard by the stable, to the back kitchen.

Supper smells come out the door. Mutton, onions, bread. Your stomach clenches.

The cook is thin and stooped and bald, his forearms scarred with old burns. He looks at the three rabbits. He turns the two fresh ones over and presses a thumb to the base of each one's antlers.

"Five copper apiece," he says. "Same with the stone in or out. Stones are still in these two. Dig 'em out first. A stone left in is coin thrown in the pot."

You set the two fresh rabbits on Dunstan's block and work the loaned knife in at the base of the antlers. It takes a minute or two each. Out comes a stone the size of a pea, fainter than your first.

You wipe both clean and pocket them beside the one you already have.

Dunstan grunts, takes all three rabbits, and counts fifteen copper into your hand.

You walk back across town to the south gate as the light goes gray.

The post is still open. Marda is at the desk, her lamp lit.

You set out your twenty-three frostcaps in front of her. "Will the post buy all of these?"

Marda shakes her head. "Post only buys what a posted job asks. The frostcap job's filled this week. Brannagh Tull posts it. She reposts it every market day."

She taps one of the frostcaps with a finger. "But she'll take up to forty more this week at her shop, a copper each. Lane off the Wool Square. Open till full dark."

You find the lane off the Wool Square, and a crooked green sign over a low door.

Inside it's dim and low-ceilinged, hung with bunches of drying herbs, and it smells of mint and woodsmoke. An old woman, bent and sharp-eyed, looks up from the counter. A one-eared gray cat watches you from beside her elbow.

"Marda sent me," you say, and set out your twenty-three frostcaps.

The old woman turns each one over and checks every root cut, muttering to the cat as she goes.

"Clean. All of them." She counts twenty-three copper out onto the counter.

As you reach for the coins, your sleeve rides up your forearm, over the pink seam of the bite.

The old woman's hand shoots out. She catches your wrist and turns it to the lamp.

She studies the seam, close. Then she looks up at you, sharp as a thorn.

"Who closed this?"

"I did," you say, "but I'm still learning. Did I do something wrong?"

Brannagh snorts. "Wrong? It's knit true. Cleaner than my own needle leaves."

She lets go of your wrist and looks at you a long moment. The cat stares too.

"I've two in my back room, bitten by the blighted beasts. Garrick Dole and Ivy Marsh. Garrick, the wolf got his calf three weeks back. Ivy, the boar had her hand a fortnight since."

Her mouth goes thin. "Both bites weep black and won't close. There's purple creeping out from them. They ache and scratch and get weaker every day. Heads still clear, for now. I've tried every salve I know on them."

She jerks her chin at the curtain behind the counter.

"Look at them for me. Now, tonight. A potion each for your trouble, whatever comes of it."

"I'd like to," you say, "but my mana's running on empty already. I don't have a big supply. Unless you have something for that, I might have to wait until morning."

Brannagh grunts. She bends stiffly and rummages under the counter, and sets a small stoppered flask on the boards. The liquid inside is faintly blue.

"Mana draught. Out of Thornmere. I don't brew them myself, and that's the only one I've got. Worth a silver. It's yours against the work, if you'll do it tonight."

"If not, come see them now anyhow, and be back here at first light."

"Okay," you say. "I'll try."

You unstopper the flask and sip. Small sips, waiting between each one.

Warmth spreads out from your middle. The empty place inside you fills, slowly, until it's full.

Brannagh draws back the curtain. The back room is narrow, lit by one lamp, with two cots in it. The currents in here are thin and green, drawn to the hanging herbs. There's no white-gold anywhere.

On the near cot sits a thin, wiry woman of about forty, her black hair gone gray at the temples. Her left hand is bound in a rag gone black. Purple creeps up her forearm, halfway to the elbow.

She looks you over. "Come to take the hand off, then?"

You unwind the rag. Across the back of the hand, the bite weeps black. To your sight the blight sits in the wound like a dark smear, sunk deep into the flesh.

You push out your own white-gold mana and reach in with the mending weave.

This time you don't think of stitching. You think of suction. Drawing the black out.

The first pull slips. Cold shoots up the thread into your arm and aches there. The black stays where it is.

Ivy hisses through her teeth, then laughs. "That it?"

You set your jaw and pull again, slower, holding the thread tight against the cold.

This time the black comes. It draws up out of the wound in a dark thread and clots at the lip. It hardens into a black stone the size of a seed.

Brannagh sucks in a breath.

The cold ache is back in your arm, deeper. Inside you, most of what the draught gave you is spent.

To your sight, about half the black is gone from the wound. Half is still sunk in the flesh.

"Okay," you say. "This works. It's just a matter of time."

You tip up the flask and sip. There's barely a swallow left, and then it's empty. A little warmth comes, no more. The empty place inside you fills only a thin way.

You cross to the far cot. Brannagh lifts the lamp and shakes the big man there gently awake.

He's about sixty, big-framed and white-bearded, gone gaunt in bed. His eyes wander a moment before they find you.

The bite is on his right calf. Purple runs up past the knee. To your sight the black here sits deeper than in Ivy's hand, knotted down into the muscle.

He looks at you a long moment. "Go on, then, lass."

You push out your white-gold thread, reach down into the calf, and pull.

The blight bites back hard. The cold rams up the thread, past your elbow and into your shoulder.

You hold on through it, teeth set, and drag.

The black comes up slowly and clots at the lip into a seed-sized stone.

Your arm throbs, cold to the shoulder. You're shaking a little.

To your sight, about a third of the black is out. The rest is still knotted in the calf.

Inside, you're nearly empty again. The flask on the cot beside you is dry.

"I'm dry," you say, "but I'll check in morning and night until you're both clear. Ivy in the morning, Garrick at night. For now, I'm going to sleep."

From the near cot, Ivy snorts. "Mind you do come back. I've cattle to drive."

Garrick turns his head on the pillow and thanks you, slowly and gravely.

Brannagh sets two small flasks of healing potion in your hands. "One each, like I said. Morning, then."

She holds the curtain for you.

You walk out into the dark lane and across the Wool Square to the lit window of the inn.

The common room is warm and loud. Tobin sits by the hearth with a cup of cider.

You take off his heavy coat, still smelling of apples, and give it back to him. Then you hold out twenty-two copper, double what he spent on you.

Tobin takes the coat. He counts out eleven copper and pushes the rest back across the table.

"Eleven was what it cost. I'll not take a copper more."

At the counter you pay Bet eight copper for a bed, from your own purse.

Bet's eyebrows go up, pleased. "Paying your own way already? What work did you find, then?"

She looks at the man's shirt hanging off you. "And you'll want something warmer than that. I've a box."

"Did some harvesting for the Post," you say, "so I can pay my own way now. I'd happily buy some more clothes, if you're willing to sell cheap."

Bet laughs, loud enough to turn heads. "Frostcaps, was it? Brannagh'll be glad of a steady picker."

She hauls a box out from under the counter and roots through it. She lays out a wool tunic, a gray hooded cloak, and two pairs of wool stockings.

"Haven't got skirts or trousers your size. The tights you've got under'll serve with the tunic. Tunic's three copper. Cloak five. Stockings a copper a pair."

She looks you over again, closer this time, at the gray in your face.

"And you've not eaten, have you. Mutton and onion pie tonight, new bread. Three copper."

"Deal," you say. "I'll take it all."

You count thirteen copper onto the counter. Tunic, cloak, both pairs of stockings, and supper.

Bet sweeps the coins into her apron and piles the clothes into your arms.

You pull the tunic on over the man's shirt and swing the gray cloak around your shoulders.

Warm again, the way you were in Tobin's coat. This time the warmth is your own.

Bet sets a wedge of mutton and onion pie in front of you at a trestle, and a heel of new bread.

You eat every crumb. The shaking in your hands stops somewhere in the middle of the pie.

"Know where I could get a pack?" you ask, when Bet comes by for the plate.

"Hob Sadler's, by the north gate. New leather, thirty copper. He opens at dawn."

Bet taps the box with her foot. "Or there's an old canvas knapsack in the bottom of that. Patched, mind. But it holds. Four copper, and it's yours."

"I'll take the patched one," you say. "That's perfect." You count out four copper.

You thank Bet, climb to the same small room under the eaves, and sleep.

The night passes quiet but for the wind.

You wake before the dawn bell, rested all the way through. Your feet don't hurt. The empty place inside you is full.

Frost lies thick and white on every roof in the gray light.

You go straight to Brannagh's lane. Brannagh lets you in without a word and draws back the curtain.

Ivy is sitting up waiting, the black rag already off her hand. "Well? Finish it, then."

You push out your white-gold thread, reach into the bite, and pull.

Cold bites up into your arm. You hold it. The last of the black draws up and clots at the lip into another seed-sized stone.

To your sight the wound is clean now. There's no dark left in it at all.

You change the thread from a pull to a stitch, and draw the mending weave across the bite.

It goes easily. The torn flesh knits under the white-gold light and closes to a clean pink seam.

Ivy flexes her hand. Makes a fist. Opens it. Then she swears, long and colorful.

Then she laughs, and keeps laughing until she has to wipe her eyes with her good sleeve.

"My cart and the cattle road are yours whenever you need carrying. Free. Mind that."

Brannagh has sat down hard on the stool by the door. For a long moment she doesn't say a word.

Then she tips three seed stones into a clay cup with a spoon and puts the cup in your hands. "Yours. I'll not touch them."

Before you leave, the purple on Ivy's arm has already paled at its edges to a yellow bruise.

You walk back down to the south gate, the cup in your new knapsack, and into the Guild post.

Marda looks up from the desk. The board by the door has a fresh notice pinned at the top.

SNOWROOT, A BASKET FROM THE WREN BROOK BANKS, FOR BRANNAGH. 15 COPPER.

Below it the rest still stand. The rats, 30 copper. The salt wagon, 40. The blighted boar. The blight bounty. And at the bottom, in its rough hand, Cal Fenn.
