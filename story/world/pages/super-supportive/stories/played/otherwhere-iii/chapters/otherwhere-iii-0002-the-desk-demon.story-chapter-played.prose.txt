"You've done so much already," you say. "I think that's all I need."

You look down at the card in your hand, the address, MY DESK.

"I need to go to the Artonan Consulate first, though. That's just down the Red Line, right?"

Priya nods. "Red Line south. Get off at Monroe, downtown. It's about five minutes' walk west." She reaches over and taps the card with one finger. "Half an hour on the train, and Saturdays they open at nine. Back here by five."

The ER bathroom is small and white and smells of bleach. You lock the door and stand in the middle of it with the bag.

The sweatpants go on over the tights, and the tie at the waist pulls in tight enough to hold. The coat is the kind that crackles, and it swallows you to the knees. The boots go on over the hospital socks, one and then the other, and you lace them. They are a size too big, and your feet slide in them a little, but they are boots.

You stand there and let it land. You are warm. All of you, all at once, for the first time since the platform.

Into the empty bag go Denise's clogs, and the grey fleece, and the knotted plastic bag with the wet wool socks inside it. You carry it out through the waiting room and out the glass doors into the snow.

It is lighter now, and thinner, and the cold only finds your face. One block west to Lawrence. You climb the stair, slush and salt again, and your feet stay dry. At the turnstile you tap the transit card on the reader. It beeps, and the gate clicks open.

A southbound train comes in a few minutes. You get on and sit by a window with the bag on your knees, and the city goes by, stop after stop, the recorded voice naming each one. Half an hour of it. Rooftops and water towers, then the train drops underground and it is tunnel and platform, tunnel and platform.

"Monroe."

You climb up out of the ground into downtown. The buildings go straight up on both sides, glass and stone, their tops lost in white. The snow is only a few flakes now, drifting. The streets are Saturday-quiet: a bus, a cab, a man walking a dog in a coat.

You walk west. Five minutes.

The building is a blocky grey cube of concrete and glass, set back behind a security fence. The gate in the fence runs on a track, and it stands open. Out front, three tall poles fly long streamers, many colors, lifting and falling in the wind off the street.

Across the street, a clock on a bank sign reads 9:23.

You go in.

The lobby is big and hushed. The floor is pale terrazzo, polished, and your boots squeak on it. The ceiling is dark wood, and set into it are hundreds of tiny lights in patterns that are almost constellations, and aren't any you know. Big plants stand in pots along the walls, and somewhere in them crickets are chirping.

Two tourists stand in the middle of the floor in matching knit hats, holding up their phones.

They are photographing the front desk, and what sits behind it.

Behind the desk sits someone about five feet tall. His skin is smooth and grey, like a stingray's. His eyes are black all the way across, shark's eyes. His nose is wide and flat, with four slits in it where you would expect two, and he has no ears that you can see. Horns curve round his skull, two dozen of them or more, and sweep down and flare into a spiky collar at his throat, like a choker.

Ropes of light chain him to the desk. They glow gold and trail from him like the tendrils of a jellyfish, drifting, and where they wrap his wrists the skin is raw.

He keeps his black eyes on a bank of security monitors set into the desk, away from the phones pointed at him.

You step aside, over by the big plants along the wall, and wait. The crickets go on somewhere in the leaves near your head. The tourists take their pictures: one of the desk, one of each other with the desk behind them, grinning, one more of the desk.

Behind the desk, one grey hand moves. It passes over something below the counter, out of your sight, and comes back to rest.

A minute later the tourists both frown at their phones. One shakes his. The other holds hers up high, turning it this way and that.

"WiFi's dead," he says.

"Figures," she says, and they grumble their way across the floor and out through the glass doors, and the doors sigh shut behind them.

The lobby is empty. There are the crickets, and under them a faint, even hum that comes from the golden ropes.

You cross the terrazzo to the desk. Your boots squeak on it, every step, loud in the quiet. Up close the ropes are brighter, and they move on their own, slow, like weed in a current. The skin where they wrap his wrists is raw, and it doesn't look new. It looks old, and sore, and long past healing.

You stop at the counter and keep your voice low.

"You don't know me, but I have a feeling I can trust you."

"And I have a tale to tell that I don't think you've ever heard before. Could we speak privately?"

His black eyes stay on the monitors. The four slits of his nose flare, all together, and you hear the soft pull of air as he breathes in. Breathes you in.

The pause goes on. It goes on longer than it needs to.

His head tilts a fraction toward you.

"Privately." His voice is high, higher than you expected, and under it there is something like glass breaking, a long way off.

He lifts one wrist an inch off the desk. The glowing ropes sway with it, drifting, like jellyfish.

"I am bound to this desk. This lobby is as private as I am allowed." The wrist settles again. "And now it is empty. I made it so."

His eyes stay on the screens.

"I am bored, little stranger, and you are not boring. Tell me your tale."

You take a breath.

"My name is Nala," you say. "And I would recognize your name, though we have never met and I cannot recall it."

His eyes stay on the screens. You go on.

"I am human, but I was not born on this Earth, nor on any world you or the Artonans know of. I know things that should be impossible for any human to know. Such as the inner workings of the family compound of the 'Arths. The true nature of wizardry, and of affixation."

The ropes sway, slow, in the quiet.

"I know that you come from a proud line—"

He goes still.

It is not the stillness of someone listening. It is total. The slow drift of the golden ropes stops with him, every strand hanging where it was, and the hum under the crickets seems to hold on one note.

You keep going. Your voice sounds very loud to you.

"—the last keeper of a tradition that is different from both. I come from a world where this world is a story, written in a book. And somehow, just a few hours ago, I crossed over to this one. But what magic brought me, I do not know."

You stop.

A dry click sounds in his throat. Then another. Small and sharp, like words bitten off before they could leave.

The four slits of his nose flare wide. He draws a long breath, in, and you understand that he is breathing you.

"Fear," he says at last, in that high voice with the glass breaking under it. "A great deal of fear. And no lie in it."

He clicks again. Then his face goes flat. Blank, like a shutter coming down. He doesn't answer any of it, yes or no.

"Some things are not said aloud inside these walls, little stranger. Not to me. Not to anyone here."

"The humans call me Gorgon," he says. "It will do."

His black eyes stay on the monitors.

"Now. What is it you want from me, Nala-not-of-this-Earth?"

"Gorgon," you say. "That's right. It was driving me mad, not remembering."

You put both hands on the counter.

"And the year is 2037 by Earth's reckoning? Then I've come both over ten years into the future, and likely across a parallel timeline as well."

You look at the black eyes, which are on the monitors and not on you.

"Okay. I can't tell you how much it means to me just to be believed." You let out a breath. "I'd forgotten you could sense emotions as well, or I'd have had even more reason to come to you."

He keeps watching the screens.

"My biggest problem is that I don't know what I should ask for. But I know that you are wise beyond human understanding, and kinder to us than we have any right to expect." You stop, and start again. "I'm not anxious to go back where I came from. This world is fascinating to me. But my aspirations go beyond normal human desires. I know the true battle being fought, and the price paid by those who wage it. I know you are no friend to chaos. I want to become a knight. Not just an Avowed, and certainly not a mortal. And I don't know how to even start."

You keep your voice down. "I tried to bargain with the Earth Contract for my silence. It never heard me once. I know it's young yet. Still growing."

He goes flat. It happened at the word knight, and it holds.

A dry click sounds in his throat. Then a second one. After that he says nothing at all.

"Not that," he says at last. "Not here. Not to me."

The golden ropes flicker along the length of the desk, quick, like water over a stone.

"Ask me for what I am permitted to give."

You press him. You ask the question another way, and another. The click comes, dry and small, and the click is all that gets past his throat.

He glances once at the bank of monitors, at the empty lobby they show.

"Nothing said here is kept," he says.

Then, level, the way a readout is level:

"The Contract will not choose you. That door shut while you grew. Selection comes once, between fifteen and seventeen. Everyone in this city knows it."

He keeps his black eyes on the monitors. He does not look at you.

With two grey fingers he draws a leaflet out of a stack at the corner of the desk and sets it on the counter. CONSULATE 4 — SPRING CLASSES, it says. Artonan Life. Logograms. Culture. Beginners Wordchaining. Under that, in small print: free to residents, weekday evenings downtown, and some classes carry credit.

From the same stack he takes an enrollment form and fills in the top line in neat block letters.

"Nala Arthur," he says. "That is all they ask of anyone here."

He rolls a pen across the counter to you with one finger.

"The office upstairs takes those Monday. The desk here takes them today."

The crickets chirr in the plants along the wall. The ropes hum. Across the street, the clock on the bank sign reads 9:40.

"Okay," you say. "That's a place to start, at least."

You put your hand flat on the leaflet.

"I'm sorry. I can tell I said something wrong, or hit something sensitive. I know your bonds limit what you can tell me, and I certainly haven't earned the trust it would take for you to strain them."

He doesn't answer that.

"But if you're willing, maybe one thing. If I wanted to get the attention of the Earth Contract, how could I do that?"

The ropes drift.

"I know I'm not the normal age. But if it wanted to, it could communicate with me. And I know things it doesn't want widely known. So it needs either to kill me, which would violate the treaty, or to contract me to silence, which would mean an affixation." You take your hand off the leaflet. "I'm not taking the slow path. I'm getting its attention one way or another. I'm hoping to do that without causing more issues than I have to."

He goes still.

The pause runs. It runs long enough that you hear a cricket you hadn't heard before, off in the plants to your left, and the hum under it that comes off the ropes.

Then one dry click sounds in his throat.

"You ask what every stranger asks," he says, "and it is the one thing everyone here already knows. The Contract has no ears. It doesn't hear anything said into the air. Not by you. Not by me."

"A civilian reaches it one way only. By telephone, through the Avowed Communications Network."

His grey hand comes back across the counter. He takes the pen, turns the form over to its blank back, and writes on it. Neat block letters, one line, long. When he is done he pushes it toward you.

"Public number. Any phone will do, even a bad one. There's one on the lobby wall." He puts the pen down. "The greeting is a machine's. Do not expect an Artonan to pick up. Answer what it asks plainly. It punishes prank calls."

His laugh is a long hiss, and then a second one.

He turns back to the monitors.

You fold the form into your coat pocket and walk to the wall phone, past the elevators, past the plants with their crickets.

The receiver hums when you lift it. You read the long number off the form and dial it.

Two clicks. A flat tone. Then a voice that isn't anyone's at all.

"Earthling."

On the small screen above the keypad, a silver sigil blooms, bright, and it stays there, and it doesn't wipe away.

"You are not registered with this network. State your name, your reason and your urgency."

You hold the receiver close and keep your voice low and even.

"My name is Nala. I was not born on this Earth, or on any planet you know of." "I believe my soul must have passed through chaos to get here, and it fundamentally altered my being."

The line hums in your ear.

"I am requesting affixation, despite not being in the standard age group. In addition, I am in possession of confidential information that you do not want widely shared. So I would like to make an additional contract to ensure I don't share it. In exchange, I would like to negotiate appropriate compensation."

On the screen, the sigil sits there, silver.

"For example. I know that the standard patterns of affixation you encourage are motivated by a reduction in the risk of destabilization of the Avowed. But they also come at the cost of a significant decrease in the end power of the Avowed."

You stop.

A pause on the line. It goes on longer than a machine's pause should. On the little screen the silver sigil pulses, once, brighter, and settles.

The flat voice comes back.

"Caller Nala. No record of your birth or arrival exists on this world. Your presence is flagged as an anomaly."

The words come evenly spaced, each one the same weight as the last.

"Affixation is not granted on request. It is offered through selection alone. This network will not make a contract of silence with an unregistered caller, or bargain."

"You report passage through chaos. A chaos screening is ordered before any other matter."

"Remain where you are. The Artonan staff of the nearest consulate have been notified."

"This call is logged under the name Nala."

The line clicks, and goes dead.

The sigil stays on the little screen, silver and fixed.

Across the lobby, behind the desk, Gorgon has turned his head from the monitors, toward you.

You set the receiver back in its cradle. The sigil stays behind on the little screen.

You walk back across the terrazzo to the desk, boots squeaking, every step loud in the empty lobby. By the time you get there he is facing the monitors again.

"Thank you," you tell him. "If I someday have the power, I will help you if I can."

The four slits of his nose flare. He breathes you in. His eyes stay on the screens.

Then he laughs. It is a hiss, long, and then again, and again, over and over, like air let out of something in short pulls. It stops all at once.

"A promise to the chained," he says. "The ones who make those mostly forget them."

The ropes sway at his wrists.

"You smell as if you mean it. I will remember that you did."

One dry click sounds in his throat. He doesn't speak of it again.

You sit on a bench by the big plants along the wall to wait. The crickets chirr in the leaves behind your head. The ropes hum. Through the glass, across the street, the clock on the bank sign creeps past ten, and then past quarter past.

Twice, tourists push in through the doors, phones already up. Twice they stand a minute frowning at the screens, and go out again.

At 10:31 the elevator chimes, and its doors slide open.

A woman steps out. She is tall and lean, with grey hair, and she wears plain grey robes that fall straight to the floor. Her feet are bare on the stone. She is nearly human to look at. Nearly.

She crosses first to the desk, and inclines her head to him.

"Gorgon," she says. Her voice is courteous, the English careful.

He dips his horned head in return.

Then she turns, and looks at you. She studies you a long moment from across the lobby, without hurry.

"I am Onn-desveth," she says. "I oversee this consulate." Her English is formal, and a little slow, each word set down whole. "The Contract tells me there is a person here with no record, who says she passed through chaos."

She walks nearer. She stops an arm's length from you, and holds out one long hand, palm up.

"I would screen you for chaos now. It takes a few minutes. It is done by touch. May I?"

"Yes," you say.

You lay your hand in hers. It looks very small there, and very freckled, against the long grey palm.

Her fingers close over it. They are cool and dry. She shuts her eyes.

There is no light. There is no sound. You wait for something, a tingle, a warmth, a pull, and there is only her hand holding yours. The crickets chirr in the plants. At the desk the golden ropes hum.

The minutes go by. Her brow creases once, a single line between her eyes, and then smooths out again.

Past her shoulder, behind the desk, Gorgon has his black eyes on the monitors. The slits of his nose are wide.

Onn-desveth opens her eyes. She lets go of your hand slowly, as if setting something down.

"There is no chaos in you," she says, each word set down whole. "None. You are clean."

She doesn't step back. She looks at your face the way a person reads a line twice.

"But you are not what I expected. What I sense in you is faint. And it is new." She tilts her grey head. "I have not met it in a human before."

"The Contract says it holds no record of you anywhere. Not of your birth. Not of your arrival."

"So I will ask you plainly, and I would like a plain answer."

"Where do you come from?"

"I come from Earth," you say. "But not this Earth."

"I assume you're familiar with the multiverse theory of quantum mechanics? I know you have skills and spells that can pierce the boundaries between worlds." You keep your voice level. "As best I can tell, I'm from about eleven years in the past, and from a version of Earth that isn't too far distant, but isn't adjacent either."

"As far as I was aware, yesterday was in October 2026. And no Artonan had ever visited Earth."

Onn-desveth is quiet for a long while. Her grey eyes stay on your face. The crickets go on in the plants.

"A person carried between worlds and back across years, both at once," she says at last. "I do not know of any magic that does that. None that I have studied."

She lifts one hand slightly, and lets it fall.

"I cannot prove your tale. I cannot disprove it either. What I sense in you is new-made. That is the only thing I hold that fits what you say."

"A world no Artonan ever visited," she repeats, slowly, as if she is tasting it.

Her head tilts.

"Then you would not know anything of us. Yet you told the Contract you passed through chaos, as one who knows what chaos is."

At the desk, a single dry click sounds from Gorgon. His eyes stay on the monitors.

Onn-desveth keeps hers on you.

"So. What else do you know of us?"

"That is part of why I believe my Earth can't be too far distant," you say.

"In my world, your world exists as a story. In a book. All the way down to the headlines I saw on the news last night." "I know a great deal, but filtered through the lens of a story. So far, every detail from the story has been accurate."

"That doesn't mean I can speak Artonan, though. Even though the main character of the story could."

"And I suspect specific events could diverge now. My actions will have ripple effects. Like a hypothetical time-travel scenario."

Onn-desveth goes very still.

For a long moment she doesn't speak. The crickets fill the quiet. The ropes hum at the desk.

"A book," she says, quietly. "About us."

She glances once across the lobby toward the desk, and back. Gorgon's eyes stay on his monitors.

"You must not say this to anyone else. Not in this building. Not outside it." Her voice stays low and even, each word set down whole. "If my superiors learn a human holds such a book in her head, it will go badly for you."

She folds her long hands in front of her.

"And for me, who heard it first."

"So I offer you a contract. Private. Sealed properly, by a tattooist, with my name on it. You speak of the book, and what it tells, to me alone."

"In return, I report you as I found you. Clean. Unregistered. Nothing more. And I will not tell anyone of the book."

A pause.

"I will also give you a place in our spring classes."

"If you refuse, you are free to walk out that door. But then I must report all that you told me."

She lifts one hand a little, palm out, as if to slow you.

"I would not have you sign today. Take time. Weigh the terms. You may bring someone you trust to hear them read. That is your right."

She takes a card from her sleeve, and presses it into your hand.
