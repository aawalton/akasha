The last thing you remember is your own bed. The ceiling fan ticking round in the dark in Provo, the sheet pulled up, your phone face down on the nightstand.

Then there is nothing. Not sleep. A gap, like a skipped track.

Now there is cold. It is in your feet first, a hard ache, and then everywhere the heat is not. The heat is above you. It presses on your face and the top of your head, dry and orange, the way a space heater does.

You are sitting up. Your back is against glass, and the glass is freezing through your shirt.

You open your eyes.

A bench. A narrow shelter of glass panels and a metal roof, and in the roof three long lamps glowing orange, humming. Beyond the glass, snow is coming down slant through white light, fine as sifted flour. Past the snow there is a pair of train rails, and past them a second shelter, empty, and past that the backs of brick buildings with fire escapes, all their windows dark.

A train platform. Up in the air, on steel.

Your head feels packed with wet cotton. You reach for the last hour and find the bed, the fan, the dark. After that, fog. You push at it. It does not move.

The lamps click off.

The cold comes down on you at once, like a lid. You flinch and grab your arms, and your hands close on arms that are wrong.

They are thin. Your fingers meet around them almost. You hold one hand out under the white platform light. It is small, narrow, pale, freckled on the back, with long fingers. It is shaking. It is yours; it goes where you tell it.

Something heavy slides off your shoulder into your lap. Hair. A thick sheet of it, dark red, straight, long enough that it pools on your thigh. You did not have hair like this.

You look down.

Your own grey shirt, the loose one you sleep in, hangs off you like a sack. The collar has slipped off one shoulder. Under it the shape of you is not the shape you went to bed with. Your black shorts sit low and loose on hips that curve differently. Only the black compression tights are right, snug from waist to ankle, as if they were made for these legs.

Your feet are bare. They are white at the toes and going numb.

Beside the door of the shelter there is a steel post with a round button on it and a small sign: PUSH FOR HEAT.

You push it. The lamps hum and glow again. The heat on your scalp is almost painful.

"Okay," you say. "Okay."

The voice is a woman's. Low for a woman, a little rough from sleep, and nothing like anything you have ever said out loud. It comes out of your own throat.

Nala, you think. The name is just there, waiting, the way your own face used to be in a mirror. If you are going to be someone else, that is who.

You look around properly.

Along the platform, blue signs hang from the roof beams. BELMONT, they say, in white letters. An arrow and the words Red Line. A lit board overhead scrolls orange text: Howard, 6 min. 95th/Dan Ryan, 11 min.

On the far wall of the shelter a backlit poster shows a boy and a woman in a hospital room, both smiling a little. The words across the bottom read: HURT IN A SUPERHUMAN INCIDENT? THE ANESIDORA COMPASSION FUND IS HERE FOR YOU. There is a phone number and a seal you do not know.

At the far end of the platform a man in a puffy coat and a knit cap stands under the lights with his hands in his pockets, looking at his phone. He has not looked your way.

There is a stair going down at your end, the steps white with snow. Wind comes up it.

The lamps click off again. You count. Eleven seconds before your teeth start.

They knock together hard enough that you feel it in your jaw.

You clamp them shut and look at the poster again. The boy in the hospital bed. The woman beside him with her hand on the blanket. The words across the bottom, lit from behind.

"Anesidora?" you say, softly, to yourself. The woman's voice again, low and rough. "I know that name."

You do. It sits in you like a word on the tip of your tongue, from somewhere that is not here.

You lift your head and speak to the air, to the snow, to whatever might be listening.

"Earth Contract, are you listening?"

You wait. You make yourself go on.

"I think you'll want to pay attention to me. I wasn't in your world a moment ago."

The air in front of you stays empty. You don't hear a voice in your ears. You don't see a box or a light at the edge of your sight, however still you hold your eyes. There is the wind coming up the stair, and the fine hiss of snow on the metal roof over your head, and that is all.

The lamps stay dark.

Your teeth start again and you can't stop them this time. Your feet have gone past aching. You press one against the other and feel almost nothing, as if they belong to someone standing a little way off. The toes are wooden.

At the far end of the platform, the man in the puffy coat glances up from his phone.

He looks at you: a woman with a lot of red hair, barefoot in a sleep shirt on a train platform in the snow, talking to the air.

He looks back down at his phone.

Overhead, the orange board scrolls: Howard, 4 min. 95th/Dan Ryan, 9 min.

The man puts his phone in his pocket. He starts walking down the platform toward you, his boots leaving dark prints in the thin snow, toward your shelter and the steel post with the heat button on it.

You get to it first. You press PUSH FOR HEAT with the heel of your hand, and the lamps hum and come up orange over you, and the heat lands on your scalp and the backs of your hands.

You stand.

The shorts go at once. They slide down off hips that give them nothing to hold, and you catch them at your side with one hand and bunch the waistband in your fist. Your feet are somewhere below you. You can tell you are standing on them the way you can tell you are standing on a floor through thick boots: pressure, and nothing else. Two blocks of wood.

The man in the puffy coat reaches the shelter and stops at the edge of it, just inside the heat, a good long step away from you. Up close he is maybe fifty, with grey stubble and wet snow on his cap. He looks at your bare feet. Then he looks at your face. His hands stay in his pockets. He keeps his mouth shut.

The orange board flips: Howard, 1 min.

Under your feet, faint through the wood of them, the rails start to hum.

Headlights come out of the snow to the south, two white eyes and then a long shape behind them. The train slides in along the platform, car after car of ribbed steel, every window bright. It slows, and stops, and the doors open with a two-note chime.

The man steps into the car ahead of yours.

You step aboard, holding your shorts up. Warm air blows low along the floor, over your feet, which barely feel it. Behind you the doors chime again and slide shut.

"This is a Howard-bound Red Line train," a recorded voice says, a man's, calm and flat. "The next stop is Addison."

The train pulls, and you sway with it.

There are three other people in the car. A man in a work jacket asleep against a window, his mouth open. A student with a backpack between her knees and earbuds in, scrolling. A woman in blue scrubs near the middle doors, a lunch bag on her lap.

The student glances at your feet. She looks away again, back down at her phone.

By the doors a screen runs a loop of news under a bank ad. Footage from a helicopter over grey water, and a caption: SKIFF PURSUES EARTH-SHAPER BENEATH LAKE MICHIGAN. The shot cuts to a crowd on a pier, pointing.

Then the heat reaches your feet.

It starts in the toes as a prickle and climbs. Within a minute it is burning, a thousand pins driven in and twisted, and your eyes water with it. You press your lips together and hold on to your shorts.

The train slows. "Addison," the voice says. The doors open on a platform like the last one, snow blowing across it, nobody waiting. They chime and close. The car rolls on north.

The woman in scrubs is looking at you. She is in her forties, tired in the face, her hair pulled back hard. Her eyes go to your feet first. Then they come up, slowly, to the shirt hanging off your shoulder, the fist at your hip, your face.

"Honey," she says, "where are your shoes? Do you need some help?"

"Yes, please," you say quietly.

Your voice comes out lower than you meant, and a little cracked. You swallow and go on.

"My stupid ex-boyfriend left me here with nothing, and I'm a thousand miles from home."

"I mean, I don't usually mind the cold, but this is a little much for bare feet."

Her tired face has changed. It went flat and hard at "left me here," all at once, like a door shutting, and it stays that way.

She pats the plastic seat beside her. "Sit down, honey. Get those feet up off the floor."

You sit. The shorts bunch at your hip and you keep your fist in them.

She unzips her lunch bag. There is a thermos in it, and something wrapped in foil, and she digs past them and pulls out a rolled pair of grey wool socks.

"Spares. I always carry spares." She holds them out.

You take them. They are thick and soft and warm from sitting in the bag. You unroll one and work it on over your foot and the end of the tights, and it catches on your toes, and the wool drags across them. It is rough, and it is wonderful. You breathe out through your teeth.

She leans over before you get the second one on and looks at the bare foot, at the toes. They are red and swollen, puffy at the tips.

"Frostnip," she says. "You caught it early." She sits back. "Keep 'em warm and dry, and they'll be fine by tomorrow. I'm Denise. ER nurse, Uptown Memorial."

You pull the second sock on. The burning goes on under the wool, but now it is a burning you can stand.

"Sheridan," says the recorded voice. The doors open on snow, on an empty platform, and chime, and close again.

"My stop's Lawrence," Denise says. "Two more. Come in with me. We'll get you seen and warm." She says it plainly, the way she might tell you the time. "We see anybody who walks in. No ID, no money. Our social worker can find you a bed and some shoes."

She looks at your face a little longer. Her eyes are steady and very tired.

"A thousand miles, huh? Where's home?"

"Out West," you say. "Small town in the mountains."

"Lots of nature, but less in the way of opportunities."

Denise nods, slowly. She looks at you a moment more, and then she lets it lie. She doesn't ask anything else about it.

"Mountains," she says. "So you know cold. Just not in bare feet." One corner of her mouth lifts.

"Wilson," says the recorded voice. The doors open, and wind throws snow in across the floor, and they chime and slide shut on it.

Inside the grey socks your toes still throb with every beat of your heart. But the burn has changed. The pins are going, and what is left is a deep, hot ache, like a bruise that has been sat on too long.

Denise zips her lunch bag closed and tucks it under her arm.

"Lawrence is next," says the voice.

The train slows. Lights slide past the windows, then a platform, then the lit blue sign: LAWRENCE. Denise stands and steps to the doors, one hand on the pole.

The doors open on the cold. It comes in at once and finds your ankles. Denise puts her arm out and holds one door back with it, and she looks at you over her shoulder.

"This is us, honey. It's one block to the ER. You coming?"

"Definitely," you say. "Thank you."

You get up, one fist in your waistband, and step off after her. The doors chime and slide shut behind you, and the train pulls away north, its lights sliding off down the track.

This platform is newer than Belmont. The walls are glass, and the snow ticks against them and runs down. Denise goes straight for the stair without looking round to see if you are behind her.

The stair is slush. It lies grey and thick over a crust of salt, and new snow is blowing in over it. On the first step the wool takes it up. By the third the socks are soaked through, cold and heavy, clinging, and the cold goes into your toes like it was waiting there. They flare up hot and sharp all over again. You suck air through your teeth.

Denise's hand is at your elbow. It stays there all the way down.

At the bottom, Lawrence Avenue is dark. A diner with its chairs up on the tables. A laundromat, the machines in the window standing open. Across the street a long marquee, unlit, the name on it in big dark letters: ARAGON.

"This way," Denise says, and turns east.

She walks fast. The snow comes at you sideways, and you follow her through it, the socks slapping on the wet sidewalk. One block. Ahead, a red sign glows over a driveway: EMERGENCY.

Glass doors slide open, and the warm air rolls out over you.

A guard sits just inside, beside the grey frame of a metal detector, a paper cup in his hand. "Morning, Denise."

The waiting room is bright after the street. Rows of joined chairs, blue plastic. A TV up in a corner, muted. A few people waiting. A man asleep across three seats with his coat over his face. From somewhere at the back, a long wet cough.

Denise steers you by the elbow to a window in the far wall. Behind it sits a big man in dark blue scrubs, maybe in his thirties, with a shaved head and a calm, broad face.

"Marcus, this is my friend from the train," Denise says. "Frostnip on the toes. No coat, no shoes, nothing."

Then she turns to you and squeezes your arm. "I clock in at five-thirty. I'll come find you on my break, okay?"

Marcus pulls his keyboard closer and smiles at you. It is an easy smile.

"We'll get you out of those wet socks in a sec," he says. "First, what's your name, hon? Birth date, and an address if you've got one."

"Nala Arthur," you say. It comes out smooth, the way your own name always has. "January 22, 1986. 1350 Apple Ave, Provo, Utah."

A beat.

"No local address."

The triage window gives back only dim shapes, washed out by the bright room behind it, but you can make out Denise's shape behind your own. She lifts a hand in a little wave and pushes through a door marked STAFF ONLY, and it swings shut after her.

Marcus types while you talk. Two fingers, quick and steady, clacking. N-A-L-A. The street. The city. The keys keep up with you.

At the birth date his fingers stop.

They stay there on the keys. He looks up, past the monitor, at your face. He takes his time about it. It is a long look, and a mild one.

"Eighty-six?" he says. It is easy, with no edge in it at all. "Hon, that'd make you fifty-one."

He leaves his hands where they are.

"Say the birth date for me one more time?"

You laugh. It comes out of you light and a little embarrassed, in that low, rough voice.

"Sorry, I meant 2016. '86 was my mom's birth year."

"I guess I filled that out on one too many forms last year, when she passed. My brain isn't braining too well after all that cold."

His face softens at once, all of it together, the way a big man's face does.

"Aw, I'm sorry, hon."

He backspaces, three quick taps, and types the new year. Then he glances up at your face once more, and nods, a small nod, to himself.

He rolls his chair to the side of the window and reaches through a gap for your arm. He wraps a cuff round it. It looks too big going on, your arm is so thin. He clips a plastic sensor on your fingertip, then runs a thermometer across your forehead, brow to temple, cool and smooth.

The cuff squeezes, hard, and lets go with a sigh.

He reads the thermometer and hums.

"Ninety-six point one. You're chilled through, girl." He points down at your feet. "Wet socks off. Let's not keep that ice on you."

The socks come off grey and sodden, heavy with slush, and your toes underneath are red and puffy and stinging. Marcus holds out a plastic bag, and Denise's socks go into it, and he knots it shut and hands you a new pair: thin, blue, with rubber treads in rows on the soles. They are dry. It is the best thing you have felt all night.

He gets up. Behind him is a steel cabinet with a glass door, stacked with folded white blankets. He opens it and heat breathes out, and he pulls one down and comes round and drapes it over your shoulders. It is hot, almost too hot, like laundry straight from the dryer.

He takes your wrist and snaps a paper bracelet round it. The print on it reads ARTHUR, NALA. 01/22/2016. A barcode.

"Frostnip's gonna wait behind the worse stuff, so it could be a while," he says. "Grab any chair."

The chairs sit in rows under the muted TV. A clock on the wall above the doors reads 5:14.

On the TV, a shot from high over the lake: grey ice to the horizon, broken in a long jagged line. The captions scroll underneath. Skiff's chase is still the lead.

The man is still asleep across his three chairs with his coat over his face. Somewhere down the row, someone coughs.

You go to the back, the hot blanket round your shoulders and your fist still in your waistband, the rubber treads squeaking on the tile. The corner seats are the farthest from the triage window and from the guard by the doors. When you sit, the window is only a bright square across the room, and Marcus a blue shape behind it.

The only one near you is the man asleep across his three chairs. His coat rises and falls over his face. He sleeps on.

You pull the blanket close and speak low, into the edge of it.

"Earth Contract, if you can't hear me, you're really not doing your job."

You wait a breath, and go on.

"I'm afraid I'm in possession of some unbound sensitive information, and I'd like to make a deal for my silence."

Your voice is steady. It is still a stranger's voice, but it is steady.

"For example. I know the layout of the island off Anesidora, where you work with the Avowed to kill demons, as training and to relieve pressure. I know you forcibly affix humans who are at risk of absorbing chaos and becoming demons. I know some of the skills have much more potential than others. Such as 'Let Me Handle Your Luggage,' as you so helpfully translate it."

You keep your eyes on the empty air a foot in front of you.

"It really is in your best interest to make a deal with me now. Before I come into contact with any of the people I could disclose this to, in ways that would be very inconvenient for you."

You stop.

The air in front of you stays empty. You don't hear a voice in your ears. You don't see a box, or a light, or a single line of text. The air doesn't shift. The blanket is warm on your shoulders, and that is all.

Up on the wall the TV runs its captions in silence. Beside you the sleeping man snores, once, and settles. The clock over the doors ticks on.

It is the same quiet as on the Belmont platform.

The clock reads 5:25 when the STAFF ONLY door swings open and Denise comes out. She has a hospital badge clipped on now, and her arms are full. She comes straight down the rows to you and sets it all on the chair beside yours: a pair of worn black clogs, scuffed pale at the toes, and a grey fleece jacket, folded.

"From my locker," she says. "Clogs'll be big, but they beat socks on slush. Fleece is yours for now."

She glances back toward the desk, then at you.

"I've got five minutes before I clock in." Her voice drops a little. "Is there anybody back home I can let you call? The desk phone's free."

"Thank you," you say, "but I'm on my own for now."

"Yesterday I would have had you call my boyfriend, but that ship has sunk."

You look up at her.

"Thank you for your kindness. You've really been a lifesaver today."

Denise nods, once. She lets the phone go. She doesn't ask again.

She takes a pen from the pocket of her scrubs, flattens the towel against the wall and writes on it, pressing hard so the pen won't tear through. Then she comes back and folds it into your hand and closes your fingers over it.

You can see the writing through the thin paper. DENISE, in capitals. Under it a phone number.

"You need anything, you call that," she says. "Doesn't matter the hour."

On the chair beside you sit the clogs and the folded fleece. The paper towel is in your fist, soft already at the creases.
