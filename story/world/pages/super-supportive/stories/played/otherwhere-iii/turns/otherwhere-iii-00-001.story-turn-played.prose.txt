The last thing you remember is your own bed. The ceiling fan ticking round in the dark in Provo, the sheet pulled up, your phone face down on the nightstand.

Then there is nothing. Not sleep. A gap, like a skipped track.

Now there is cold. It is in your feet first, a hard ache, and then everywhere the heat is not. The heat is above you. It presses on your face and the top of your head, dry and orange, the way a space heater does.

You are sitting up. Your back is against glass, and the glass is freezing through your shirt.

You open your eyes.

A bench. A narrow shelter of glass panels and a metal roof, and in the roof three long lamps glowing orange, humming. Beyond the glass, snow is coming down slant through white light, fine as sifted flour. Past the snow there is a pair of train rails, and past them a second shelter, empty, and past that the backs of brick buildings with fire escapes, all their windows dark.

A train platform. Up in the air, on steel.

Your head feels packed with wet cotton. You reach for the last hour and find the bed, the fan, the dark. After that, fog. You push at it. It does not move.

The lamps click off.

The cold comes down on you at once, like a lid. You flinch and grab your arms, and your hands close on arms that are wrong.

They are thin. Your fingers meet around them almost. You hold one hand out under the white platform light. It is small, narrow, pale, freckled on the back, with long fingers. It is shaking. It is yours; it goes where you tell it.

Something heavy slides off your shoulder into your lap. Hair. A thick sheet of it, dark red, straight, long enough that it pools on your thigh. You did not have hair like this.

You look down.

Your own grey shirt, the loose one you sleep in, hangs off you like a sack. The collar has slipped off one shoulder. Under it the shape of you is not the shape you went to bed with. Your black shorts sit low and loose on hips that curve differently. Only the black compression tights are right, snug from waist to ankle, as if they were made for these legs.

Your feet are bare. They are white at the toes and going numb.

Beside the door of the shelter there is a steel post with a round button on it and a small sign: PUSH FOR HEAT.

You push it. The lamps hum and glow again. The heat on your scalp is almost painful.

"Okay," you say. "Okay."

The voice is a woman's. Low for a woman, a little rough from sleep, and nothing like anything you have ever said out loud. It comes out of your own throat.

Nala, you think. The name is just there, waiting, the way your own face used to be in a mirror. If you are going to be someone else, that is who.

You look around properly.

Along the platform, blue signs hang from the roof beams. BELMONT, they say, in white letters. An arrow and the words Red Line. A lit board overhead scrolls orange text: Howard, 6 min. 95th/Dan Ryan, 11 min.

On the far wall of the shelter a backlit poster shows a boy and a woman in a hospital room, both smiling a little. The words across the bottom read: HURT IN A SUPERHUMAN INCIDENT? THE ANESIDORA COMPASSION FUND IS HERE FOR YOU. There is a phone number and a seal you do not know.

At the far end of the platform a man in a puffy coat and a knit cap stands under the lights with his hands in his pockets, looking at his phone. He has not looked your way.

There is a stair going down at your end, the steps white with snow. Wind comes up it.

The lamps click off again. You count. Eleven seconds before your teeth start.
