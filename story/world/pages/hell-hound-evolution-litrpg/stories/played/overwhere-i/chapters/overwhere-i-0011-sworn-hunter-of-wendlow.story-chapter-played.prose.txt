You climb over the tail-race wall and cross onto the apron, the stone warm and wet underfoot. You circle the pinned body wide, well clear of the head, keeping a good long stride between you and those fangs in case anything in it has a last twitch left.

It reeks: river mud and scorched flesh and something rank beneath, like a fishmonger's drain in high summer. The flies have already found it, settling in a buzzing crust along the torn crown.

You spin up a water disc above your palm, thin and fast, a blur of silver hissing in the sun, and set it to the thick neck just behind the skull. It bites. Scale and gristle and bone spray away in a fine pink mist, and you hold it there, steady, for the better part of a minute, until it shears clean through.

The head rolls free on the stone, heavy as a sack of meal, and comes to rest on its side. The jaw sags open. The fangs are as long as your hand.

Up the bank, Jory and both barge crews come picking their way down to the apron in a straggling line, and at the mill a bar scrapes back and the door creaks open.

You kneel beside the spikes and cut into the pale belly, working toward the liver. It's slow, slick, stinking work, twenty minutes of it, the flies crawling over your knuckles, until you lift the liver aside and there it is beneath: a green bag the size of your fist, whole and glistening.

You draw out Mother Sallow's waxed twine and tie off the duct, two tight turns and a knot.

Then your blade slips on the slick flesh. The point nicks the sac. It bursts in your hands.

Bile sprays your wrists and forearms, green and hot, and the skin there lights up like you've plunged your arms into nettles. You hiss through your teeth and shake them.

The jar stays empty.

In the gut beside where the sac lay, something catches the light. You pull it out: a brass-buckled belt, slimed and stained, with a bowman's knife still in its sheath.

Jory has come up beside you. He goes quiet, staring at the belt in your hand.

"That's Dickon's."

For a moment he only looks at it. Then he digs in his coat and presses a gold coin into your stinging palm and folds your fingers over it.

"From both crews, like I said. You earned it."

A stout, grey man in a flour-dusted apron has come down from the mill. He names himself Tam Hobb and eyes the head on the stone, then you.

"Eighty pound if it's an ounce. A long haul to town." He jerks a thumb back at the mill. "Take my mill barrow. Send it back on any barge coming down; I mean to grind by afternoon."

"Eh, thanks," you tell Tam, "but they said the fangs would be enough. Just cut off the head to make sure it was dead."

You crouch by the head and spin up a water blade again, a thin, fast, hissing disc, and work it carefully around the jaw where the great teeth root. Bone parts like wet clay. Both fangs come free clean and whole, one and then the other.

Each is as long as your hand, hooked like a sickle and yellow-white, heavy for its size, the point still sharp enough to prick. You wrap them in a scrap of cloth and stow them in your pack.

Then you look down at yourself. Bile, blood, river muck and worse, from your fingertips to your elbows and spattered over your shirt and tights, and the bile still stinging on your forearms.

You draw water and fire together, gently, into a soft warm steam, and play it over your skin and clothes like a bath you're standing in. The muck lets go. Bile, blood and river slime roll off you in a hot grey cloud and patter onto the stone, and the nettle sting in your forearms eases into a dull warmth.

Then air and fire: a hot, dry wind that wraps around you and tugs at your hair and shirt for three minutes, and leaves you dry and smelling of nothing worse than warm stone.

Tam Hobb watches the whole thing, then snorts, half a laugh. "Handy trick. I'll be grinding by two, then."

The crews whistle, low and long, and start back up the bank toward their barges, talking over each other.

Jory lingers. His eyes are on Dickon's belt in your hand, the brass buckle dulled with gut-slime, the bowman's knife still in its sheath. Slowly, he holds out his own hand.

You hand Dickon's belt over.

Jory takes it in both hands, the maimed one and the whole, and bows his head over it a moment.

"I'll carry it to his mother at Brennock Ford." He clears his throat and looks up. "The Wend Maid owes you passage anywhere on the Wend, any time. Free."

You leave them on the weir and take the towpath back north under the willows. The afternoon is warm and still, the river sliding by on your left, and the walk passes quietly, two hours of birdsong and the slap of water on the bank.

A little before half past two you come up to Wendlow's gate, dusty to the knee, mouth dry from the road. The watch knows you now. You pay your copper, and the watchman waves you through with a grin and a nod.

At Antler Hall you unwrap the cloth on the counter, and the two fangs lie there, hooked and yellow-white, each as long as your hand.

Grete picks one up and turns it over, testing the point with her thumb.

"Eighty pounds of head is too much to haul. These will do." She sets it down beside its twin. "Box is empty since your last lot."

She pulls a slip toward her and writes a chit for fifteen gold in that square hand, signs it, and slides it across.

"Paid with the other when the tax rider comes tomorrow."

She carries the fangs to the wall and hangs them on a nail beside the head of Harl Voss, then goes to the board, takes down the river serpent's slip with your name chalked under it, and wipes the chalk away with the heel of her hand.

Coming back, she leans in over the counter.

"That letter went to the magistrate yesterday," she says, low. "That's all I'll say of it."

She straightens and nods at the board behind her, bare now, its old nail holes empty.

"Board's bare till the rider brings fresh slips tomorrow," Grete says, and dips her pen in the ink.

You find a quiet spot out of the street's way, a sunny step with a wall at your back, and lay your wool tunic out across your knees. The bolt-torn rent at the left ribs gapes a hand-span long, the edges frayed; the sleeve is slit from wrist halfway to elbow. Your cloak's hem hangs ragged too, chewed by brambles and rock.

If water and earth can knit flesh, they ought to manage a bit of wool.

You start with the pairs you know, and a few single threads first. Fire, a fingertip's worth, run along the edge of the rent: the wool browns and curls and starts to smoke, and you snuff it fast, pinching out a scorched brown border that wasn't there before.

Air frays the threads looser, combing them out into fuzz. Water alone soaks the cloth through and does exactly what water does to cloth.

So you work through the pairs, one after another, through the long warm afternoon. Fire and air singes. Air and water leaves it wet and fluffed. Fire and earth stiffens a patch into something like burnt toast. The tunic takes each one badly and lets you know it.

Earth with water, last. You bind them together, that heavy, wet pull behind your breastbone, and work it into the rent, picturing the fibres reaching for each other the way the graze on your arm did.

Under your fingers the wool creeps. The loose fibres crawl together, tangle, and lock, matting into each other like a cat's coat left out in the rain. Over five slow minutes the rent at the ribs felts shut into a stiff, ridged seam, a hand-span long, darker than the cloth around it.

You turn the tunic over and do the slit sleeve the same way. Another ridge, stiff as a scar, but whole.

Your throat has gone dry as dust over the long afternoon. You pull the tunic back on and roll your shoulders, and the stiff seams rasp against your ribs and hold.

Supper at the Bell and Barrel is eel stew, rich and peppery, with black bread to mop it and a mug of small beer. Bram takes eight copper for it and the bed together.

Nell Cooley sets down your bowl and eyes the ragged hem of your cloak where it trails off the bench.

"I stitch tears, two copper apiece," she says. "Say the word."

The taproom is loud tonight. The bargemen have the long table and their mugs up, toasting the river serpent's death over and over, each toast louder than the last. The river is open again.

Word has caught up with you. You can see it travel down the room: a nudge, a nod in your direction, a murmur. *That's her. The one who did for the quarry bandits, and the wyrm too.* Heads turn. A few stay turned, staring outright, and one old bargeman lifts his mug to you across the room.

You sleep behind a bolted door and wake rested at first light on day nine.

Porridge with honey and small beer, two copper, and then up the high street to Antler Hall.

Grete looks up from her ledger as you come in and waves you to a bench by the hearth.

"Rider's due mid-morning. Sit."

The fire ticks. Near ten, hooves clatter up the high street, a lot of them, and through the open door you catch the tax rider going by, four guards around him and a strongbox strapped behind his saddle.

By half past ten the Board's share sits in a canvas bag on Grete's counter. She counts out twenty-six gold in neat stacks and pushes them across to you.

"Both chits, paid." She tears the two slips across and drops the halves into a box under the counter.

Then she pins two fresh slips to the board beside the reeve's old one, smoothing each flat with her palm.

"Eighteen gold: a mantis-beast, Level 20, taking sheep and a shepherd on Coldbrook Downs. A day's walk north. Pays on its head or its forelimbs." She taps the second. "Twelve gold: Cal Ferrin, the Ferryman, Level 16, robbing folk at Lowcross ferry. Alive or dead. Half a day east. And the reeve's eight for the Greyfen crystals still stands."

From the canvas bag she draws a folded note, sealed with a blot of red wax. She breaks it, reads it, and her mouth tightens a fraction. She holds it out to you.

"Magistrate Varne asks Nala Arthur to the Moot Hall at noon." Her eyes meet yours. "Mind your tongue up there."

You fold the note into your pack and take your leave of Antler Hall.

The high street runs down to the square, timber and plaster leaning out over the road. The Bell and Barrel stands across the square, and its taproom is near empty at this hour. The fire ticks in the hearth.

You take the bench by the hearth and spread the cloak over your knees, hem up to the light. Brambles have chewed it through in three places, the worst at the back where rock tore it. The rest of the wool is sound, and the tunic's felted seams still hold from yesterday.

You take the hem between your fingers and set the earth-and-water weave into the first tear. The wet pull settles behind your breastbone. The chewed strands creep together and lock, and the tear shuts in a stiff, dark ridge, like the ones along your tunic.

You work on to the second place and the third. All the while the well behind your breastbone drains, a steady trickle for every minute the weave holds. The last ravel felts shut with that well near half spent. You shake the cloak out and hold it up: the hem hangs whole, ridged and darker than the cloth.

The leggings are sound. The grey shirt and black tights you came in with don't take the weave at all. Your boots are leather and don't want mending. You pull the cloak on and roll your shoulders.

You cross the square to the Moot Hall, grey stone with its door up a broad flight of steps. Two watchmen at the door look at your sword, then at your antler badge, and let you by.

Inside, a thin clerk at a desk reads the seal on your note and writes you in his book. You take a bench at the side among the morning's other business: a drover, and a hen-woman.

The square bell rings twelve. The clerk takes up his book and the room goes quiet.

The Moot Hall is one long room: a raised bench, the clerk's desk below it, and presses of tax rolls along the wall. Behind the bench sits a thin, silver-haired woman in grey velvet, ink on her fingers.

The drover's boundary quarrel is heard and settled fast. The hen-woman is sent to the watch.

The clerk calls, "Nala Arthur," and you come forward to stand before the bench.

The clerk names your judge: "Odile Varne, Magistrate of Wendlow, who sits for the margrave."

On the bench before her lies a folded sheet, its red wax seal broken open.

"Grete Holm sent me this the day it came off the quarry road," Varne says. "She didn't write a word to go with it. The seal on it is the merchants' counting-house's, and Harl Voss didn't keep that kind of company."

She opens the sheet flat with two inked fingers and leaves it lying between you.

"So," says Odile Varne, "who else has read what is written here?"

"Only you and Grete as far as I'm aware," you say. "I figured with the seal it must be something someone cared about, but it's not something I can hunt, so I never bothered to look inside."

Varne's eyes rest on your face for a long moment. She's quiet and very still, as if she's listening for something under the words.

Then her shoulders ease a fraction, and she nods once, as if a sum has come out even.

She folds the sheet along its creases, turns in her chair, and lays it in a press behind the bench. The key grates in the lock. She turns back to you.

"Then it stays between the three of us. Grete knows to hold her tongue, and so will you."

She lays her inked hands flat on the bench.

"A hunter who can keep quiet is worth something to me. And a stranger with no lord and no papers gets asked about in my town, sooner or later." She lets that sit a moment. "For your silence, I'll write you papers as a sworn hunter of Wendlow, under my seal. Papers like that answer any gate, any reeve, any watch that asks who you are."

She draws a clean sheet toward her, wets her pen, and holds it over the page.

"Your home, and your lord," Varne says. "To write on them."

"I was born in the wilds, no home, no lord, no papers," you say. "Would Wendlow be fine to claim? I'm tired of the questions."

At *born in the wilds*, Varne's pen stops a breath above the page. Her eyes stay on your face.

Then the pen moves on, and she doesn't ask about it again.

"Wendlow will do." She writes in a small, quick hand, saying it as she goes. "Home, Wendlow. Lord, the margrave, served through this bench."

She looks up.

"That makes you mine to call. A sworn hunter answers this bench against outlaws and beasts, and is paid at the Board's rates for it, the same as any slip on Grete's wall." The pen scratches on. "There's no fee for the papers. And a sworn hunter of Wendlow passes its gate without toll."

She sands the sheet, tips the grit off, and sets it aside, unsealed. A stick of red wax and a brass seal lie beside it.

Below the bench, the clerk rises. He lifts a heavy book with iron-shod corners from his desk, carries it up, and lays it open on the bench between you.

"The oath's spoken here," Varne says, "one hand on the town book, the clerk to witness. Then I seal them."

"I don't mind killing things when paid," you say, "but what else is in this oath? I value my freedom."

Varne sets down her pen and folds her inked hands on the bench.

"Three things. Keep the margrave's peace. Answer my call. Speak true." She ticks them off on her fingers. "My call is against outlaws and beasts, paid at the Board's rates, as I said. Speaking true means that when I ask what you've seen on my roads, you tell me straight."

She spreads the hand again.

"Otherwise you go where you like and hunt for whom you like, in Wendlow or far past it. You may turn a call down if you give this bench your reason. Ignore one, and the oath is broken. A broken oath voids the papers and strikes your name, and you're simply a stranger again." She tips her head toward the sheet. "And you may hand your papers back before this bench any day you like, and be quit of it."

"One more, since you ask. In war the margrave may summon the march's sworn hunters. So far in this war he's left us be." Her mouth thins. "That may change."

She glances at the locked press behind her, then back to you.

"The letter bargain stands apart from the oath, whatever you choose."

She draws a breath.

"I've written the margrave three times for riders to clear the east road. Three times, refused. So you know where you stand: swear, and the east road is my first call on you."

"Sure, that sounds fine," you say. "What's on the East road that needs killing again? So I'm not going in blind."

"Swear first. Then you'll have all I know of it." Varne nods to the open book.

You lay your hand flat on the page. The vellum is cool and dry under your palm, older names faded brown down its margin.

The clerk comes round to stand at your shoulder and speaks the oath a line at a time, in a dry, careful voice. You say each line after him.

"To keep the margrave's peace."

"To keep the margrave's peace."

"To answer the bench's call against outlaws and beasts."

"To answer the bench's call against outlaws and beasts."

"To speak true."

"To speak true."

He takes the book back to his desk, dips his pen, and writes your name at the foot of a long column of others. Then he signs beside it as witness and sands the line.

Varne holds the stick of red wax to a candle until it drips, lets a coin of it pool at the foot of your sheet, and presses her brass seal down into it. She lifts the seal away clean.

She folds the sheet once and holds it out across the bench.

"Nala Arthur, sworn hunter of Wendlow."

You take it. One folded sheet, light in your hand, the red wax still warm under your thumb.

"Now, the east road." Varne laces her inked fingers. "My first call: rid it of what's left of Harl Voss's crew. The Board proved Voss and eight of his men, your work. The crew was reckoned at a dozen-odd. Who is left of them, and where, is the first thing I want you to find out."

She taps the bench once.

"Two gold a man at the Board's rate, the same as before: his right ear with its tag, or him alive. Take what time you need, but I want word before the next tax rider, ten days from now."
