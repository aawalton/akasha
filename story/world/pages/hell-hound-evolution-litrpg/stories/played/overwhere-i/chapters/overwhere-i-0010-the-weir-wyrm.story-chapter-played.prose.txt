"Two hour walk is all?" You tap the second slip. "The Wyrm sounds like a nice warm up. I'll take that tomorrow."

Grete picks the Wyrm slip up off the counter, turns, and pins it to the board behind her. She takes a stub of chalk from the ledge beneath and writes Nala Arthur under it in square white letters.

"Stands till it dies. Pays on the head, or the fangs if the head's too big to haul."

"For today," you say, "I'm looking for a nice place to stay, as well as somewhere to sell miscellaneous loot from my adventures. Oh! And someone who can turn Ghost-Eye here into a proper casting focus."

You dig into your pack and bring out the eye: a hard pearl of clouded crystal, milky as the day you plucked it, and set it on the counter between you, where it rolls a half-turn and stops.

"Recommendations?"

Grete knows it at once. She doesn't touch it, only leans to look.

"Drake-pearl. An alchemist pays about four gold for one of those."

She straightens and counts off on her scarred fingers.

"Bell and Barrel by the square. Clean, a silver for bed and supper, and a bathhouse. Blades and bows go to Wil Harrow, smith on Anvil Lane. He buys fair. Pelts and monster parts: the guild counting-house pays least. Mother Sallow pays more. She's an alchemist by the river stairs, buys parts for her draughts."

The last finger.

"For a focus, Ilse Varrow. Blue door on Glass Street. All of it's a quarter hour's walk from here."

"Great!" You scoop the pearl back into your pack and fish out the little wrapped bundle beside it: Ghost-Eye's ears, both of them, stiff and grey-green. You set them on the counter. "These go with the head."

Grete sweeps them up and sets them beside the skull behind her. "They ride with the head. Its bounty's paid already."

You reach for the sack again, for the ears and the tags, and Grete tips her chin at the iron-bound chest at the end of the counter.

"Those went in with the payout. They're the Board's now."

Next, from the bottom of your pack, Ewan Dell's little tin token, from Sallow Hythe. You set it on the counter.

Grete turns it over with a fingertip, and her face softens a shade. "Marta Dell's. I know her. It goes west to her on the next cart."

Last, you lay the sealed letter beside it, out of the dead man's sack, its wax still whole.

Grete picks it up, looks at the seal, and breaks it with her thumb. She unfolds the paper and reads. Partway down the page she goes still, the same stillness as before, but colder.

"That's the guild counting-house seal," she says, low. "Only the factor uses it."

She folds the letter once along its crease and slides it into the pocket of her vest.

"Don't speak of this to anyone. It goes to the magistrate." Her eyes meet yours. "I'll leave it unpaid. It may be worth more to you that way."

You leave Antler Hall with the badge in your pocket and the pearl in your pack and walk a quarter hour through Wendlow, down out of the high street and along lanes that narrow and twist, until you find Glass Street and, halfway along it, a door painted bright blue.

Inside is a narrow room lined with glass cases. Coils of fine wire hang from pegs, trays of rings glint under the cases' lids, and somewhere among the shelves a few stones hum, a sound you feel in your back teeth more than hear.

Behind the counter is Ilse Varrow: small and sharp, about fifty, ink on her fingers, a pair of spectacles hanging on a cord at her chest.

You set the drake-pearl on the counter. Ilse lifts her spectacles to her nose and bends over it, close enough that her breath clouds the crystal.

"A ring," she says, straightening. "Three gold, three days, reach to fifty yards. A rod: four gold, four days, sixty yards. A rod fills one hand while you hold it. The pearl treats every element alike, lengthens all."

She taps the counter beside it.

"Half before I start. Once it's set, it belongs to the focus for good, past drawing on or selling."

Ilse holds out her palm for the pearl.

"Ring or rod?"

"Let's do the ring," you say. "I like to keep my hands free."

You lean on the counter, eyes on the trays of rings under the glass.

"Anything we can do to make it stronger? I've gold to spare, say up to fifty gold. What options do you have for me?"

Ilse settles her spectacles on her nose and gives you a long look over the top of them, the look of a woman who has heard customers say *fifty gold* before and has learned to wait for the coins.

"Plain ring, I'll have it set by the afternoon of day ten." She raises one inky finger. "The honest part first. A focus buys reach, or holds mana. The force is all yours to bring. And reach counts for held workings. Loosed beams, slugs and bullets fly as far as they ever did."

She turns to a case behind her, unlocks it with a key from her apron, and lifts out a small velvet tray. On it sits a second pearl, clouded and milky, a little smaller than yours.

"Mine. Beside yours, two pearls reach seventy yards. Seven gold, setting included, and a day more on the ring."

She sets the pearl tray down and slides another along the counter beside it. In its hollow lies a dull grey stone, smooth as a river pebble.

"A well-stone. Twenty gold, three days more. Holds forty mana. Fill it from your own, ten a minute, easy. Draw on it, and the forty is back in half a minute."

She taps the counter between the two trays.

"Half up front covers the extras too; the rest when I hand the ring over. Band metal's all one to a focus. And I set at my own pace, whatever you pay."

Ilse folds her inky hands on the counter.

"So. Plain, second pearl, well-stone, or both?"

"Eh, just the second pearl for now," you say. "Range should cover what I need most."

Ilse nods once, briskly, as if you've passed a small test. "Ten gold all told. Five now, with your pearl, and five when I hand it over."

You count five gold coins onto the counter, one after another, and set the drake-pearl beside them.

Ilse sweeps the coins and the pearl together into a little felt-lined box and snaps the lid shut.

She reaches across, takes your hand in her dry, inky one, and loops a twist of fine wire around your finger, pinches it snug with her inky fingertips, slides it off, and ties a paper tag to it with a scrawl you can't read upside down.

From a drawer she takes a small tin claim tag stamped with a number and presses it into your palm, closing your fingers over it with her own.

"Afternoon of day eleven. Bring the tag and the other five, and the ring's yours."

"Great! I'll be back," you tell Ilse, and tuck the claim tag away.

Anvil Lane is five minutes off, and you hear it before you find it: the ring of a hammer on iron, steady as a heartbeat. Wil Harrow's forge fronts the lane with its doors thrown wide, heat rolling out into the street. The smith himself is broad and bald, his scalp shining with sweat, one forearm mottled pink and white with old burn scars from wrist to elbow.

You lay the arming sword and the fine crossbow on his bench. As you pull your hand back, the bronze antler badge slips out of your pocket with it and clinks down on the wood beside them.

He sets down his hammer and goes for the crossbow first. He works the crank, listening to the ratchet click, and runs a thick thumb slowly along the lath.

"City work, this." He says it the way some men say a woman's name. "I want it."

Then the sword. He draws it a hand's width from the scabbard, turns it to the light, and finds the levy stamp near the hilt. His eyes go from the stamp to the antler badge, and stay there a moment. Then he slides the blade home and lets the question lie.

"Three gold six for the bow. Two gold two for the blade."

He counts it out on the bench, coin by coin, gold and then silver.

"Plain soldier's short swords I take too, sixty copper apiece, if you've any about you."

From Anvil Lane it's ten minutes downhill to the river, where a flight of worn stone stairs drops to the water. At their foot a low door stands open on a cellar so full of steam it breathes out at you, sharp with herbs and something sour beneath.

Mother Sallow is stooped and old, wrapped in shawls, a clay pipe clamped in her teeth among a dozen bubbling pots. When you bring out the grubboar tusks she snatches them up before you can set them down, turning them in knobbly fingers.

"Old ones grind finest."

She pays you two silver and four copper out of a pouch at her waist, then squints at you through the steam, at your blade and your road-worn boots.

"You've a hunter's look. After the Weir Wyrm?" "Bring me its bile sac within a day of the kill, and I'll pay two gold for it."

"Deal!" you say. "How do I find the bile sac?"

Mother Sallow puffs her pipe until the bowl glows, and talks around the stem.

"Green bag, size of your fist, under the liver, behind the heart. Tie off the duct with twine, cut the sac free whole, and stopper it in a jar." She jabs the pipe stem at you. "Burst it and it's worthless, and the bile stings skin something fierce."

She rummages along a shelf, comes back with a stoppered clay jar and a hank of waxed twine, and presses both into your arms.

"Bring the jar back full."

You climb the river stairs and walk ten minutes up through the town to the square, where the Bell and Barrel stands on the corner under a painted sign of both.

The keeper is a big, red-faced man who wipes his hands on his apron and names himself Bram Cooley. "Nine copper for bed and supper. Three more for a bath."

His wife Nell runs the bathhouse out back, a tiled room of steam and copper tubs. She fills one for you hot enough to sting, and you sink into it and stay there, eyes closed, until the last of the road has soaked out of you and the water has gone grey.

Supper is mutton pie with a thick brown crust, a heap of buttered greens and a mug of small beer, at a corner table in the taproom with your back to the wall.

The room is loud and full, and it's talking about you without knowing it. A lone woman hunter, they say, who walked into the bandits' stone pit on the east road and walked out with their heads. Somebody's cousin heard it at the gate. Somebody else says she's seven feet tall. The talk flows around the room and past your table, and every eye in it slides over you without stopping.

At the next table two bargemen sit hunched over their mugs.

"Wyrm took a bowman clean off a barge," one grumbles. "Below Hobb's weir, two days back. Bow in his hands and all."

The other only shakes his head and drinks.

You climb to a clean little room under the eaves, shoot the bolt, and are asleep almost before your head finds the pillow.

You wake to grey light at the window, first light on day eight, rested all the way through, mouth dry as a cracker.

Knuckles rap on the door. "Porridge and small beer downstairs, miss," Nell calls through it, "while the pot's hot."

Breakfast is a bowl of porridge with a spoon of honey melting into it and a mug of small beer, in the taproom by the window. You pay Nell two copper on your way out.

Then the river. The towpath runs flat down the Wend's east bank, packed hard by hooves and boots, under a long line of willows trailing their fingers in the current. You walk it for two hours. The morning comes up clear and warm, a light west wind blowing in your face off the water, smelling of mud and green things.

At a bend the willows thin, and the whole of it opens out below you, two hundred yards off: the weir, the pool, the far bank.

Hobb's weir is a slanting stone apron across the river, sixty yards from bank to bank, the water sheeting white down its face. The mill squats at its east end, stone below and timber above, its great wheel standing still. Above the weir, at Hobb's landing, two barges lie moored and idle, their crews sitting on the decks and keeping well away from the rails.

You draw two lenses of water out of the air and set them in line on a thread of wind, one before the other, and put your eye to the near one. The far bank leaps toward you.

Below the weir lies the plunge pool, deep and slow and dark, some fifty yards across, its surface turning in lazy eddies. Against its far west bank a barge lies half-sunk, canted over, its bow stove in like a kicked crate.

A broad slick runs up the weir apron from the pool, a smear of crushed weed and mud, wide as a cart, as if something heavy hauls itself out there and slides back. On the apron's dry top lie fish bones, bleached white, a snapped oar, and a single torn boot.

You swing the lenses west. In the alders on the bank, hung over the branches like washing, is a long shed skin, grey-green and ragged, lifting a little in the wind. You follow it from end to end, and keep going, and it keeps going.

You sweep the pool for the Wyrm itself. Around the sunken barge, along its stern, up the west bank and down the east, back over the open water, again and again. The dark water gives you back eddies and sun-glare and your own patience, and nothing more. Whatever is down there is staying down.

You let the lenses go. They fall apart into a little patter of drops on the towpath.

Down at Hobb's landing, one of the bargemen is on his feet, a hand shading his eyes. He's spotted you. He waves his arm over his head, broad and urgent, beckoning you down.

You go down to hear what he has to say. It's three minutes along the towpath to Hobb's landing, the roar of the weir growing as you come.

The bargeman meets you at the foot of his plank: a lean man, sun-browned to leather, his left hand short two fingers. He looks you up and down, lingering on the blade at your hip, and something in his shoulders lets go.

"Jory Pell, master of the Wend Maid." He jerks a thumb at the barge behind him. "You'll be the Board's hunter, at last, with that sword."

On the other barge, the Pike, a crew of three sit in a row on the deck and watch you in silence.

"It rose under my bow at dusk two days back," Jory says, "and took my bowman Dickon clean off the deck. Just gone. His bolts skipped off its back like hail off slate."

He jerks his chin across the water at the stove-in hulk against the far bank.

"The Heron. Stove in at dawn a week ago. Her crew swam clear. Lucky." He spits over the side. "Near noon on sunny days it hauls out on the weir stone to lie in the sun. I've watched it. Keep off the pool's edge, mind. It took the miller's dog off the bank a week back."

"Where it lives, I'd guess the pool. Past that, it's anyone's guess." He rubs the stumps of his fingers with his thumb. "Both crews will add a gold between us if it dies. We need to get downriver."

Jory squints up at the sun climbing over the weir, already warm on the stone.

"Today's sunny enough, I'd say."

You pick your spot from the landing: the mill's tail-race wall, a chest-high run of mossy stone at the weir's east end, twenty yards from the eastern third of the apron where the slick of crushed weed is broadest. Close enough to hit anything. Low enough to hide behind.

You leave the towpath and creep toward it along the bank, slow, heel and toe, keeping the mill between you and the pool. Ten yards out, a loose stone turns under your boot and drops against another with a dull thud.

You freeze. Out on the pool a single ring spreads across the dark water, wide and slow, and smooths away.

You cover the last stretch and lie down flat behind the wall, cheek to the cool moss, and wait.

The sun climbs. Eleven comes and goes. Heat beats down on the weir stone and shimmers off the bare apron, and the water sheets white over its face, and the pool turns its slow eddies.

Near half past eleven the water by the weir humps up and parts, slow and careful.

A head breaks the surface first, long and flat and grey-green, and then the rest of it comes, hauling up out of the pool onto the apron in long muscular heaves. It's huge: twenty-five feet if it's an inch, thick as a man's waist, its scales dull and wet and plated like old armour. It drags itself onto the sun-warmed stone and lies belly-down along the slick, its head resting flat on the rock, the scaled crown turned up to the sky.

One eye stays open, fixed on the bank.

You let it settle. You let it lie there a long minute, and another, until the great body slackens on the warm stone.

Then you reach behind your breastbone, past the familiar warmth of fire and the cool of wind and the weight of earth, for the element you've barely touched. Lightning answers like a held breath, sharp and bright and crackling in your fingertips, every hair on your arms standing up. You call it twice, two points in the clear air above that flat skull, and bring them down together.

Both bolts crack down onto the Wyrm's head at once. The thunder slams off the mill wall like a hammer on a bell, so loud you feel it in your teeth, and the whole length of the beast jerks and goes rigid, twitching on the stone, its tail drumming once against the apron.

Over the lip of the wall, you fix your eyes on its crown and let the fire come up through them.

Twin beams lance from your eyes across the twenty yards and burn into the scaled crown, and the scales give. They blacken, crack, and split, and the beams bite through into what's beneath. Smoke boils up off the apron, thick and greasy, and the stink of scorched flesh rolls over you. The great head sags against the stone, burned and torn open.

The beams die. White afterglow swims across your sight in two blinding smears, and the weir dissolves into glare.

Through it, a long blur heaves on the stone and drags itself toward the water.

Half-blind, you fix on the blur as best you can, a long grey smear in the glare, and the wet drag of scales over stone.

You reach down into the apron with earth, two workings at once, one at the blur's middle and one further back, and pull the stone up hard. You shape it as it comes: sharp, and angled back toward the mill, away from the water.

The apron cracks like a dropped plate. Two stone spikes thrust up out of the weir under the blur, a yard and more of grey rock each, and punch through the Wyrm's pale belly and out through its back. The weight of it slams down onto them, and the spikes hold. It's pinned to the ledge.

The long body bucks once on the spikes, a great convulsing heave that throws water off the apron in sheets. It thrashes, tail whipping, and goes slack, its tail stretched out a yard short of the pool.

Your sight clears in patches, then all at once. You raise your palm, fix on the scorched ruin of its head, and loose a beam into it. Fire pours into the torn crown, and you hold it there, burning on, until the smoke rolls thick and black off the stone.

You let the beam go. The Wyrm hangs on the stone spikes, still, its great body draped over them like wet rope, the pool lapping a yard below the tip of its tail.

Up at Hobb's landing, Jory Pell lets out a whoop that carries clear over the roar of the weir.
