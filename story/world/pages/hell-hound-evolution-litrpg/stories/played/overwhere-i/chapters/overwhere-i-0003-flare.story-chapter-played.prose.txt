"I'll stay until I get bored," you say brightly. "I hear there are some other beasties nearby you might prefer to be rid of. And I'm a big fan of spending gold."

"Is there a nice inn here? I could really use a good meal and a bath. Oh! And some new clothes, and a pack, and some shoes. And maybe someone I could hire to make the hide into a rug or blanket? That could be fun."

Somebody along the benches laughs. The red-bearded man grins wider.

Agathe keeps from smiling, but her face eases.

"You're standing in the inn. The Tarred Stag. Stay and hunt for us, and the village purse keeps you here a week, bed and board."

The red-bearded man thumps his chest. "Garrick Pell. Mine's the Stag. Bath's two copper, hot."

"There's no shop in Fenwatch," Agathe says. "Folk sell from their doors. Bet Ashdown sells tunics, leggings and cloaks. Tobin's mother. Ask him the way. Aldo Brack by the brook is the tanner. Boots, belts, packs. He'll cure the pelt for you too."

At the table Garrick breaks your gold coin into a heap of silver and copper from his takings. You pay him two copper for the bath.

He sets a bowl of goat stew in front of you, with oat bread and a mug of dark ale. You eat every scrap. The ale is thick and a little bitter. You drain it, and a cup of water besides.

In a back room Garrick fills a wooden tub with steaming water, bucket by bucket. You sink into it to the chin. The grit of the ford and the dust of the fallen stones soak away.

Clean, warm and fed, you dress again in the same old grey shirt and black tights.

Out in the hall, Garrick calls through the door: "Brute's stewing now. Feast on the green at dusk!"

There are a couple of hours of daylight left. You decide to get your errands done before the feast.

Tobin is loitering outside the Stag, and he leaps at the chance to show you the way. He talks the whole way through the lanes: about the Brute, about the burn, about how he can't get anyone to believe him.

His mother's house is timber and wattle under reed thatch, with cloth hung by the door. Bet Ashdown is thin and quick, about forty, with reed-cut fingers and her son's sunburn. She looks you over, then looks at Tobin, and her mouth goes tight. From her face, he's said too much.

She brings out what she has made up: homespun tunics, leggings and cloaks in browns and greens. You pick out a tunic, leggings and a cloak.

"Four silver and five copper," she says.

You pay it. She pins the tunic and leggings on you and says she'll have them taken in by dusk.

Next Tobin leads you down to Otter Brook. The tanner's pits stink long before you reach them.

Aldo Brack is stooped and sour, about fifty, with his hands stained brown to the wrist. He has packs and belts hanging ready. Boots he makes to the foot, ready the morning after next. He kneels, measures your bare feet with a knotted cord, and grunts. The boots are ordered, to be paid for on fitting.

Until then he sells you hide foot-wraps tied with thongs, for two copper. You lace them on at once.

You take a leather pack down off the hooks. His price for a pack is two silver, but he lets you have it for a silver and eight copper.

Tobin runs to the Stag for the Brute's rolled pelt and brings it back, puffing. Brack unrolls it, eyes the burned hole, and sniffs.

"Rug or blanket. Hole shows either way. Three weeks. A silver when it's done." He drops it straight into a pit.

Back at Bet's door at dusk, the clothes are ready. You change in the back room. The tunic and leggings fit you now, and the green cloak falls to your knees. You stow the old grey shirt and black tights in the new pack.

Outside, the smell of stewing meat drifts across the whole village from the green. Firelight glows at the end of the lane, and you can hear voices gathering, and someone laughing.

You follow the firelight to the green. The whole village seems to have turned out.

A big fire burns in the middle, and Garrick stirs a great iron pot of Brute stew beside it. Benches have been dragged out of the Stag. Children run between them. Somebody plays a reed pipe, badly.

You take a bowl of the stew. It's dark, gamy and tough, and very good after hours of stewing.

People come up to you in ones and twos, to thank you, to stare at you, to ask you questions. You smile, and deflect, and turn every question back into one of theirs.

A reed-cutter tells you fen-fever has half his crew abed this summer, and the healer's worn thin.

Another says the western channels have run warm since spring and stink faintly of rotten eggs. "Dead fish in them," he adds. "And the reed's gone yellow and brittle out there."

An old woman lowers her voice. Reed-cutters have seen pale lights in the deep fen at night. "Marsh-ghosts."

Somebody mentions that Osric Fenn the pedlar is due from Wendlow in two days, as if that were big news.

Garrick, passing with the ladle, grumbles that the levy collector comes after harvest. "Coin, or two young men to march north." The talk around him goes quiet for a moment.

Across the fire, Tobin is telling the burn story again. The faces round him plainly don't believe a word of it.

A tall, stooped woman with cropped white hair and scarred hands stares at you for a long while. When you meet her eyes she looks away, silent. She stays on the green.

Late in the evening a broad, soot-dark man comes up to you with a lean grey-green beast at his heel. It has a lizard's muzzle, slanted yellow eyes and a spiked steel collar. It watches you calmly.

"R-Rowan. Coalby." His words catch. "Your Brute took my goats. They b-blamed Sedge. Now they can't. Anything I have that's any use to you, lady, you ask."

The fire burns low. The green empties slowly toward the lanes and the Stag.

Then, far off to the west, over the ridge and the fen, a long howl rises on the still night.

At the man's knee, Sedge lifts its lizard head and answers it, low and long.

You sleep in one of the Stag's small guest rooms, on a straw mattress under a wool blanket.

You wake rested at first light to hens, a cockerel, and the smell of smoke from the hearth in the hall.

Downstairs Garrick sets out oat bread, a boiled egg and a mug of small ale. The village purse covers it.

"Where's the bounty board?" you ask.

Garrick laughs until his beard shakes. "Board? That's in Wendlow, three days east. We've the reeve's slate. By the door, lady."

Beside the Stag's door hangs a slate in a wooden frame, chalked in a careful hand. You read it easily, though you have never seen these letters before in your life.

REEDLURKERS robbing the eel traps at the fen edge: 3 silver a head.

GRUBBOARS rooting the oat strips south of the palisade: 5 silver for the old boar.

Below a scratched line, copied from Wendlow's Board:

GHOST-EYE, Drakewolf, the Greyfen: 25 gold, head.

HARL VOSS, deserter, Cutter's Quarry: 30 gold. His men: 2 gold each.

Your boots won't be ready till tomorrow, so you go looking for the Grubboars.

South of the palisade, the oat strips among the stumps are torn up in long furrows, freshly rooted. Heavy cloven tracks and churned mud lead off south into wet alder woods.

You follow them for a quarter hour through the alders, your foot-wraps squelching, to a muddy wallow.

Five huge-snouted boars lie bedded in the mud, grunting in their sleep. One is bigger than the rest, grey-bristled and scarred. That's the old boar. The four sows lie round him.

You reach for the pressure and focus on water, and you will a sphere of it round the boar's head.

Water rises out of the wallow and wraps his whole head in a trembling ball. It holds.

You keep hold of that pull and reach again. A second sphere closes cleanly round the head of the nearest sow.

Two pulls sit behind your breastbone, cool and wet and steady.

The boar heaves up out of the mud, shaking his head, bubbles bursting from his snout. He bucks and kicks back like a mule, hooves smashing into the alder roots, but the water stays on. The sow thrashes and rolls in the mud, squealing silently inside her sphere.

The other three sows lurch up, snorting, and find you at the edge of the wallow.

They lower their heads and charge you together, mud flying from their hooves.

You let go of the sow's sphere. The water collapses and splashes into the mud.

The freed sow staggers up, coughing, and joins the charge.

Two at once you can do reliably, so you spend the freed pull on wind. You will it to open ahead of you and push behind. The plan is simple: keep the sows running until the boar is done, then do it again, until they give up or all five are down.

All four sows come at you fast. You're already gone, flung sideways out of their path.

You run a wide circle round the wallow, the wind throwing you forward in long strides. You stay close to the boar, and the water on his head holds.

The sows chase, turn, and chase again, crashing through the alders with their tusks low. Each time they close, one long wind-driven stride carries you clear. Every tusk misses.

Each minute you renew both pulls, and feel the draw of them behind your breastbone.

Within a minute the sows are blowing, flanks heaving, their charges slowing to a trot.

In the wallow the boar's kicking weakens. He sinks to his knees, then falls on his side.

Near the third minute he stops moving. You let the water fall.
