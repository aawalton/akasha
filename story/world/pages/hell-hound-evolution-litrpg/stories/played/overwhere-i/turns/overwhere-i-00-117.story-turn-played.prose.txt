You fold the note into your pack and take your leave of Antler Hall.

The high street runs down to the square, timber and plaster leaning out over the road. The Bell and Barrel stands across the square, and its taproom is near empty at this hour. The fire ticks in the hearth.

You take the bench by the hearth and spread the cloak over your knees, hem up to the light. Brambles have chewed it through in three places, the worst at the back where rock tore it. The rest of the wool is sound, and the tunic's felted seams still hold from yesterday.

You take the hem between your fingers and set the earth-and-water weave into the first tear. The wet pull settles behind your breastbone. The chewed strands creep together and lock, and the tear shuts in a stiff, dark ridge, like the ones along your tunic.

You work on to the second place and the third. All the while the well behind your breastbone drains, a steady trickle for every minute the weave holds. The last ravel felts shut with that well near half spent. You shake the cloak out and hold it up: the hem hangs whole, ridged and darker than the cloth.

The leggings are sound. The grey shirt and black tights you came in with don't take the weave at all. Your boots are leather and don't want mending. You pull the cloak on and roll your shoulders.

You cross the square to the Moot Hall, grey stone with its door up a broad flight of steps. Two watchmen at the door look at your sword, then at your antler badge, and let you by.

Inside, a thin clerk at a desk reads the seal on your note and writes you in his book. You take a bench at the side among the morning's other business: a drover, and a hen-woman.

The square bell rings twelve. The clerk takes up his book and the room goes quiet.

The Moot Hall is one long room: a raised bench, the clerk's desk below it, and presses of tax rolls along the wall. Behind the bench sits a thin, silver-haired woman in grey velvet, ink on her fingers.

The drover's boundary quarrel is heard and settled fast. The hen-woman is sent to the watch.

The clerk calls, "Nala Arthur," and you come forward to stand before the bench.

"Before Odile Varne, Magistrate of Wendlow, who sits for the margrave," the clerk says.

Odile Varne, Magistrate of Wendlow, looks you over from boots to hood, unhurried. On the bench before her lies a folded sheet, its red wax seal broken open.

"Grete Holm sent me this the day it came off the quarry road," Varne says. "She didn't write a word to go with it. The seal on it is the merchants' counting-house's, and Harl Voss didn't keep that kind of company."

She opens the sheet flat with two inked fingers and leaves it lying between you.

"So," says Odile Varne, "who else has read what is written here?"
