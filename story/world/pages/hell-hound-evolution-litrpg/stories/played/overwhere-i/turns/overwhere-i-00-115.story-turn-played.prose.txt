You find a quiet spot out of the street's way, a sunny step with a wall at your back, and lay your wool tunic out across your knees. The bolt-torn rent at the left ribs gapes a hand-span long, the edges frayed and stiff with old blood; the sleeve is slit from wrist halfway to elbow. Your cloak's hem hangs ragged too, chewed by brambles and rock.

If water and earth can knit flesh, they ought to manage a bit of wool.

You start with the pairs you know, and a few single threads first. Fire, a fingertip's worth, run along the edge of the rent: the wool browns and curls and starts to smoke, and you snuff it fast, pinching out a scorched brown border that wasn't there before.

Air frays the threads looser, combing them out into fuzz. Water alone soaks the cloth through and does exactly what water does to cloth.

So you work through the pairs, one after another, through the long warm afternoon. Fire and air singes. Air and water leaves it wet and fluffed. Fire and earth stiffens a patch into something like burnt toast. The tunic takes each one badly and lets you know it.

Earth with water, last. You bind them together, that heavy, wet pull behind your breastbone, and work it into the rent, picturing the fibres reaching for each other the way the graze on your arm did.

Under your fingers the wool creeps. The loose fibres crawl together, tangle, and lock, matting into each other like a cat's coat left out in the rain. Over five slow minutes the rent at the ribs felts shut into a stiff, ridged seam, a hand-span long, darker than the cloth around it.

You turn the tunic over and do the slit sleeve the same way. Another ridge, stiff as a scar, but whole.

You pull the tunic back on. It sits stiffly where the seams run, rasping a little against your ribs when you breathe, but it holds.

The sun has slid low and gold across Wendlow's roofs while you worked. Your throat is dry as dust, and from the square, supper smoke drifts up from the Bell and Barrel's chimney, smelling of mutton and bread.
