You bed down against the cart's near wheel with the sack wadded under your head, salt-stiff canvas and all. The embers tick. Sleep takes you before you can decide whether you're comfortable.

Osric's hand on your shoulder, shaking. Grey light between the pines, dew on everything, your hip stiff as a board. The mule has its nose in a feed bag and then it's back in the shafts, ears up, looking almost forgiving. Tobin climbs onto the tailboard yawning. At six by the sky, the wheels turn.

"Remember what I said," you tell Osric once the well is behind you. "The magic. Gentle."

He twists round on the box to watch you, one hand tight on the reins.

You let a breath of air gather at the cart's back, not a gust, a lean, the way a hand rests on a friend's shoulder blade to keep them walking. You feel the weight of the bed take it, and the axle under the weight, and you hold it soft enough that the wood never groans.

The cart rolls faster. The mule feels the traces slacken and quickens its step, head bobbing easy, as if the road had tilted downhill without asking. Osric looks from the mule to you and back, and lets out a long whistle. On the tailboard Tobin sits with his legs swinging, grinning at the pines sliding past.

You keep a watch on your well as it drains, steady as a cup tipped slow, and the moment it dips below four-fifths you let the push go and simply ride, back against a crate, eyes half-shut in the sun. The mule slows to its own pace. When the warmth behind your breastbone has topped itself up again, you take the push back up, and the mule picks up its step as if it had been waiting. All morning it goes that way, a stretch of push and a stretch of rest, your reserve never sinking far.

By noon the pines thin and fall away behind, and the road runs out between hedges into open farmland, fields of cut stubble gold under the sky. Osric pulls up by a gate.

"An hour," he says. "Push or no push. I rest the beast at noon."

The afternoon road runs quiet between the hedges, the stubble fields running off on either side, the road empty ahead and behind, without a rider or a cart or a man with a crossbow anywhere you look.

About three, the road tips downhill toward water. Brennock Ford is five farms and their barns gathered round a ford paved with flat stones, the river talking over them. On the near bank stands an inn two storeys tall, smoke rising straight from its chimney, a painted board over the door.

A drover has his cattle in the shallows, letting them drink before the westbound road. He looks up as the cart comes down.

"Going to Wendlow?" He spits. "Mind the guild. Sold me a pouch of crystals as full. Got them home and every one dark and empty as a dead man's eye."

Osric clucks to the mule and pulls up in the inn yard.

"We night here. I always do. The mule's done, Tobin's done."

He hooks a thumb east, down the road past the ford.

"Push on and we reach Wendlow after the gate shuts. From here, it's noon tomorrow."
