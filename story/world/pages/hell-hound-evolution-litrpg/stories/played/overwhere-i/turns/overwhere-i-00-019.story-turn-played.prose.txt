Your boots won't be ready till tomorrow, so you go looking for the Grubboars.

South of the palisade, the oat strips among the stumps are torn up in long furrows, freshly rooted. Heavy cloven tracks and churned mud lead off south into wet alder woods.

You follow them for a quarter hour through the alders, your foot-wraps squelching, to a muddy wallow.

Five huge-snouted boars lie bedded in the mud, grunting in their sleep. One is bigger than the rest, grey-bristled and scarred. That's the old boar. The four sows lie round him.

You reach for the pressure and focus on water, and you will a sphere of it round the boar's head.

Water rises out of the wallow and wraps his whole head in a trembling ball. It holds.

You keep hold of that pull and reach again. A second sphere closes cleanly round the head of the nearest sow.

Two pulls sit behind your breastbone, cool and wet and steady.

The boar heaves up out of the mud, shaking his head, bubbles bursting from his snout. He bucks and kicks back like a mule, hooves smashing into the alder roots, but the water stays on. The sow thrashes and rolls in the mud, squealing silently inside her sphere.

The other three sows lurch up, snorting, and find you at the edge of the wallow.

They lower their heads and charge you together, mud flying from their hooves.
