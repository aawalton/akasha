The Ford Inn's back room is quiet and smells of old soap: a copper tub against the wall, a bench, a three-legged stool, a window full of slanting afternoon light. You drag the stool into the light, sit, and roll your sleeves to the elbow. The salve has done what it can. The bolt graze along your forearm is still an angry red furrow, crusted at the edges, and the rest of you aches under your shirt.

Magic can throw a cart down a road. It ought to be able to close a cut.

Air first. You lay a breath of it along the graze and picture the skin drawing together under it like a zip. The wind is cool and pleasant, and the cut stays a cut. You let it go.

Water alone, a thin sheen held over the wound, imagining it washing the hurt away. Then earth, a slow heavy press, imagining the flesh setting firm as clay. The graze sits there under each, raw and unchanged, as if you'd been politely asking it a question.

Fire, then. Only a thread of it, drawn along the edge of the furrow. The skin hisses and puckers shut where it touches, and the sting comes a heartbeat later, hot and bright, and you flinch and let it go. That's a sear, not a heal. You'd rather keep the arm.

You start on pairs. Fire bound with air, a warm drying breath meant to coax the edges together, and the graze flares like you've laid it on a stove. You hiss through your teeth and shake your arm out, blowing on it, and move on to the next pair.

The door creaks. A girl of ten or so, the innkeeper's by the look of her apron, peeks round the edge of it, finds you sitting there with your sleeve up and a flicker of something in the air over your arm, and goes wide-eyed. She is gone with a scurry of feet before you can say a word.

The light through the window slides gold and then amber across the floor. Near six you try water and earth together. Not water to wash, not earth to press: the two bound in one, and you hold a single picture in your head, flesh knitting to flesh, the edges of the wound reaching for each other and joining like the two sides of a seam.

A heavy, wet pull settles behind your breastbone, the one you know from the fen, and holds. Under the weave, slow as a minute hand, the graze begins to close.

You don't dare breathe on it. You keep the picture steady and work the weave along the furrow, end to end and back, and then over the shallowest of your other hurts, watching the warmth behind your breastbone drain. The forearm graze is a pink seam now, tender to the touch and shut. Before you let the weave go, you slide it down to where the bolt went into your thigh, deep under the bandage, and hold the same picture there. The pull behind your breastbone goes on draining, and the wound under it stays exactly as it was, as if the weave were sliding off glass. It takes no hold at all. Your well touches four-fifths, and you let it go.

From the common room comes the smell of mutton stew, thick and peppery, and over the clatter of bowls, the drover's big laugh at the hearth.

The door opens again, and this time it's the innkeeper herself, a broad woman with flour on her forearms and her daughter's eyes. She puts her head in.

"Stew's two copper, bed's eight, the tub three. What'll it be?"
