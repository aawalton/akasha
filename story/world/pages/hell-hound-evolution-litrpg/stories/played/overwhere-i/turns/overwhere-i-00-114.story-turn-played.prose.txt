You hand Dickon's belt over.

Jory takes it in both hands, the maimed one and the whole, and bows his head over it a moment.

"I'll carry it to his mother at Brennock Ford." He clears his throat and looks up. "The Wend Maid owes you passage anywhere on the Wend, any time. Free."

You leave them on the weir and take the towpath back north under the willows. The afternoon is warm and still, the river sliding by on your left, and the walk passes quietly, two hours of birdsong and the slap of water on the bank.

A little before half past two you come up to Wendlow's gate, dusty to the knee, mouth dry from the road. The watch knows you now. You pay your copper, and the watchman waves you through with a grin and a nod.

At Antler Hall you unwrap the cloth on the counter, and the two fangs lie there, hooked and yellow-white, each as long as your hand.

Grete picks one up and turns it over, testing the point with her thumb.

"Eighty pounds of head is too much to haul. These will do." She sets it down beside its twin. "Box is empty since your last lot."

She pulls a slip toward her and writes a chit for fifteen gold in that square hand, signs it, and slides it across.

"Paid with the other when the tax rider comes tomorrow."

She carries the fangs to the wall and hangs them on a nail beside the head of Harl Voss, then goes to the board, takes down the river serpent's slip with your name chalked under it, and wipes the chalk away with the heel of her hand.

Coming back, she leans in over the counter.

"That letter went to the magistrate yesterday," she says, low. "That's all I'll say of it."

She straightens and nods at the board behind her, bare now, its old nail holes empty.

"Board's bare till the rider brings fresh slips tomorrow," Grete says, and dips her pen in the ink.
