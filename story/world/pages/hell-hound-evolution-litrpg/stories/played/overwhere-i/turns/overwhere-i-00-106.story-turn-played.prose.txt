"Great! I'll be back," you tell Ilse, and tuck the claim tag away.

Anvil Lane is five minutes off, and you hear it before you find it: the ring of a hammer on iron, steady as a heartbeat. Wil Harrow's forge fronts the lane with its doors thrown wide, heat rolling out into the street. The smith himself is broad and bald, his scalp shining with sweat, one forearm mottled pink and white with old burn scars from wrist to elbow.

You lay the arming sword and the fine crossbow across his bench.

He sets down his hammer and goes for the crossbow first. He works the crank, listening to the ratchet click, and runs a thick thumb slowly along the lath.

"City work, this." He says it the way some men say a woman's name. "I want it."

Then the sword. He draws it a hand's width from the scabbard, turns it to the light, and finds the levy stamp near the hilt. His eyes go from the stamp to your bronze antler badge, and stay there a moment. Then he slides the blade home and lets the question lie.

"Three gold six for the bow. Two gold two for the blade."

He counts it out on the bench, coin by coin, gold and then silver.

"Plain soldier's short swords I take too, sixty copper apiece, if you've any about you."

From Anvil Lane it's ten minutes downhill to the river, where a flight of worn stone stairs drops to the water. At their foot a low door stands open on a cellar so full of steam it breathes out at you, sharp with herbs and something sour beneath.

Mother Sallow is stooped and old, wrapped in shawls, a clay pipe clamped in her teeth among a dozen bubbling pots. When you bring out the grubboar tusks she snatches them up before you can set them down, turning them in knobbly fingers.

"Old ones grind finest."

She pays you two silver and four copper out of a pouch at her waist, then points the stem of her pipe at the antler badge.

"You after the Weir Wyrm, hunter?" "Bring me its bile sac within a day of the kill, and I'll pay two gold for it."
