You run.

Straight for the log, straight through the ferns, blind. They slap at your waist and drag at your legs. A root jars up through your heel. A bark scale bites into the ball of your foot and you keep going on it. Your breath is loud now, and so is everything else.

Behind you the quiet is not creeping anymore. Something tears through the ferns, fast, a heavy rushing rip of stems and leaves, and it is gaining so quickly that the sound of it seems to jump toward you.

It is far faster than you are.

Ten yards short of the log's mouth, a heavy weight hits you from behind, high and low at once.

Teeth close on the back of your neck and shoulder. You go down under it, face first into the ferns and the moss.

The pain is huge. It is hot and it is everywhere at the top of your back, and then it is a single point, the teeth, pressing in. Blood runs warm down under your collar, under the grey shirt, along your ribs.

It does not let go. You can feel its breath on your skin, hot and wet. You can feel its weight across your back, solid and pinning. A growl comes through its teeth and into your bones.

You claw forward. Elbows, knees, elbows, dragging yourself through the ferns toward where the mouth has to be, and dragging it with you. Your arms are too short. Your hands reach for a root you are sure is there and close on moss. You reach again, and your fingers find the torn edge of the roots, and you haul.

Your head and shoulders go into the gap between the roots.

The jaws open.

They close again, harder, deeper, on the back of your neck and shoulder.

The second bite is worse than the first. Your arms start to shake. The edges of your sight go grey, though there is nothing to see.

It holds you there, half in the mouth of the log, your legs and hips still out in the ferns. In front of your face the hollow is dry and close. It smells of rot and earth.

Its jaws shift on your neck. Its weight settles on your back for another bite.
