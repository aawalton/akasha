The last thing you remember is your reading chair. A book, a lamp, the house quiet around you.

Then a voice. Not in your ears. Inside your head, deep and booming, in words that are not any language you know. It comes once, and you look up from the page. It comes again, harder, pressing on the inside of your skull, and the chair is gone.

There is nothing under you. Nothing around you. Black, with no stars in it. The air rips out of your open mouth and does not come back. Your ears spike with pain. Your eyes sting and dry. You tumble, or you think you do, with nothing to tumble against.

Then light, and a hard, soft landing on your back, and air.

You lie there and breathe. That is all you do for a while. In, out, big ragged gulps of it, cool and wet and green-smelling. Your chest hurts. Your ears ring. Slowly the ringing drops away and other sounds come in under it. Water, trickling. Leaves moving high up. Something ticking and clicking in the undergrowth, like a clock with too many hands.

You open your eyes.

Trees. Enormous ones, straight as pillars, going up and up to a roof of leaves so far above you that the light comes down through it gold and slanting and thick with drifting specks. The bark is grey and overlapped like scales. The leaves are a green that leans toward blue.

You are lying in moss, deep and springy, in a round hollow full of ferns taller than you are sitting.

You sit up, and everything is wrong in a way you feel before you understand it. Your weight sits differently. Your arms are too short to reach where they are reaching.

You look down at yourself.

Your dark grey shirt, the one you were wearing in the chair, hangs loose to the middle of your thighs, the collar slipping off one shoulder. Your black compression tights, snug as ever. No shoes. Your feet in the moss are small and pale, and so are the hands braced beside them, narrow and freckled and fine-boned.

A heavy fall of dark red hair slides forward over your shoulder.

You say something. You are not sure what, only that the voice that says it is a woman's.

Blue light flashes in front of your eyes, and you flinch back from a box that is not there. It hangs in the air, square and clean-edged, and turns with your head when you turn it. Words fill it, and you understand them before you have finished reading.

Welcome to Davrar

Davrar will help you survive

Adapting you to the local biosystem

Calibrating to your understanding and determining your capabilities

Please, be patient, this will not take long

Then it is gone.

The clicking in the ferns has stopped.

At the low side of the hollow a spring wells up between two roots and runs away downhill as a small, clear brook. The light is going longer and more golden. Somewhere far off through the trunks, something whistles, three rising notes, and something else answers it.

Under the canopy, in the deepening shade, a few faint green lights have begun to drift.

"Okay… isekai protocol."

The words come out in the new voice, clear and a little hoarse, and too loud in the quiet.

"System?"

The air in front of you stays empty.

"Status?"

Empty.

"Davrar?"

You wait. Where the box hung before, there is only air, and slanting gold, and a drifting speck.

"Davrar…" Slower this time, turning it over. It snags on something, a name from somewhere else, half there and half gone. "I remember that name."

Then, because it is the only thread you have: "Nathan, have you merged with the system yet? If so, could you send me some help? I know you can, and wouldn't want me to go through what you did."

Your voice goes out across the hollow and stops somewhere in the ferns. There is no answer. There is no voice inside your skull, no blue light, and the hollow looks exactly as it did before you spoke. Your own voice was the only human sound you could hear, and now it has stopped too.

The clicking in the ferns stays quiet. It stays quiet a long while. Then one clicker starts up off to your left, tentative, and another answers it, and then the whole undergrowth is at it again, the too-many-handed clock wound back up.

Minutes go by. The golden light slants lower between the grey-scaled trunks. The moss under you still holds the day's warmth, but the air has started to cool, and you feel it first on your bare feet.

Far off, the three rising notes come again. The answer comes back.

More of the faint green lights come drifting down out of the canopy, slow as dust. One brushes a fern frond near your knee, and for a moment the frond glows along its edge, the same soft green, then fades.

Blue light flashes in front of your eyes. A new box opens, square and clean-edged, and turns with your head when you turn it.

Davrar has recognized you, Nala.

You have become fully integrated into Davrar, and are ready to begin!

As you have no Talents, classes or skills despite being of mature age, Davrar has deemed you to be at a Disadvantage.

Therefore, Davrar will provide more explanations than usual, and your class, Talent and skill gain and progression will be accelerated until you are no longer at a Disadvantage.

Davrar is here to help you survive and prosper. It will offer you Talents and utility skills according to your innate talents and abilities, and allow you to choose a class to suit your deeds.

Talents and utility skills can develop every 10 ranks, but require Insight to do so.

Each class will grant different and unique class skills according to the theme of the class.

To unlock classes, reach level 9 and choose from the presented list. Classes develop at levels 27, 81, 243 and so on. There are ways to acquire more than one class; good luck discovering them!

To level up, overcome challenges. The more dire the challenge, the more you will be rewarded.

Davrar hopes you will survive and prosper.

The box hangs in the air in front of you.

Beyond the rim of the hollow, the shade between the trunks has gone blue-grey.

You are barefoot, with nothing in your hands, and the brook runs away downhill from the spring.

*Okay. No help yet. But if I survive, I get something.*

You look at the spring, welling up clear between its two roots. *Spring gives fresh water. As good a place to start as any. Spiral search pattern outward, learn what's nearby, eyes peeled for danger. Pay close attention to everything. Maybe I can get an inspect skill.*

You get up and start at the water, and circle outward from it slowly, ring by ring.

The brook bed below the spring is rounded grey stones, smooth under your bare soles, the water cold enough to ache. Dead branches lie everywhere under the trees. The ones hung up off the ground, propped on roots or on each other, snap dry in your hands.

The moss is littered with shed bark, the grey scales off the trunks, each about the size of your hand and hard as horn. You run a fingertip along an edge and it opens a thin red line in the skin. You suck the finger and go on, more careful with them.

Forty yards east of the spring lies a fallen giant, one of the grey-scaled trees, longer than a house. Its root end is a torn-up wall of roots and earth, and behind it the trunk is hollow. You get down and look in. Dry inside, and big enough for you to crawl into. You reach in and pull at the core. The wood there has rotted to a soft dry punk, full of fibre, and it crumbles to shreds between your fingers.

Sixty yards down the brook, tall canes crowd the bank, hung with ripe berries, dark red. Back in the shade of the hollow grow low bushes with berries of a different kind, blue-black, and a sweet smell coming off them. You don't eat either. You mark where each one grows.

Eighty yards down the brook, one bank has been cut back by the water, and a seam of stone shows in it, dark and glassy, like flint.

On the south rim of the hollow you find a small trail pushed through the ferns, a body's width wide and low. Along it lie droppings like little pellets, and the fern tips beside it have been nibbled short. Something small grazes here.

Overhead the canopy closes everywhere. You can't see past the hollow from anywhere in it, not a hill or a sky line, just trunks going on into the dimness.

The last of the light goes grey as you work along the upslope rim.

There, one trunk stands scored with claw marks, deep ones, the bark scales shredded and hanging. A musky smell comes off it. Near the roots lies scat, full of grey fur and small pieces of bone. You crouch in front of it. The highest of the marks are level with your own head.

You stay crouched there, studying them, while the last light goes.

Full dark finds you there, at the farthest point of your circuit from the spring and the log. Under the canopy it is black now, but for the faint green specks drifting down and the brief soft glow of a fern where one touches. The ticking around you comes in patches now, busy in one stretch of ferns, quiet in another close by. To your ear it is only the evening going on, the way it has all along.

A heavy grey shape bursts out of the ferns behind you, close, fast and low.

It swerves off a stride short of you with a hiss, and is gone into the black.

You turn from the clawed trunk and start back toward the fallen log, quietly. *Get inside. Narrow passage, all that rot. Whatever that was, maybe it won't get at me in there, maybe it won't even smell me.*

The log's mouth is eighty yards off, down the slope and across the hollow, and you can't see it. You can't see anything. You go by feel. Ferns to your waist, cool and brushing. Moss under them, then a root, then moss again. The hard edge of a bark scale under your heel, and you shift your weight off it before it cuts.

You set each bare foot down slowly, and keep your breath slow and shallow through your mouth.

The green specks drift around you in the black. They don't light anything. They are only points to steer between, a few here, one there, a fern edge glowing and fading as one brushes past.

Your mouth is dry. You haven't drunk anything since you landed.

Around you the ferns tick and click as you pass, falling quiet right at your feet and starting up again behind you.

About halfway to the log, the ticking behind you stops.

It stops in a patch. You hear the edges of it as you go. The quiet is not staying where it started. It is moving. It slides along behind you, low in the ferns, and the ticking dies ahead of it and picks back up where it has been.

It is closing in on your back. It is coming on faster than you walk.

The pieces fit together all at once: something large is moving under the ferns, toward you, from behind.

Ahead, somewhere in the dark, the log's mouth is still forty yards off.

You run.

Straight for the log, straight through the ferns, blind. They slap at your waist and drag at your legs. A root jars up through your heel. A bark scale bites into the ball of your foot and you keep going on it. Your breath is loud now, and so is everything else.

Behind you the quiet is not creeping anymore. Something tears through the ferns, fast, a heavy rushing rip of stems and leaves, and it is gaining so quickly that the sound of it seems to jump toward you.

It is far faster than you are.

Ten yards short of the log's mouth, a heavy weight hits you from behind, high and low at once.

Teeth close on the back of your neck and shoulder. You go down under it, face first into the ferns and the moss.

The pain is huge. It is hot and it is everywhere at the top of your back, and then it is a single point, the teeth, pressing in. Blood runs warm down under your collar, under the grey shirt, along your ribs.

It does not let go. You can feel its breath on your skin, hot and wet. You can feel its weight across your back, solid and pinning. A growl comes through its teeth and into your bones.

You claw forward. Elbows, knees, elbows, dragging yourself through the ferns toward where the mouth has to be, and dragging it with you. Your arms are too short. Your hands reach for a root you are sure is there and close on moss. You reach again, and your fingers find the torn edge of the roots, and you haul.

Your head and shoulders go into the gap between the roots.

The jaws open.

They close again, harder, deeper, on the back of your neck and shoulder.

The second bite is worse than the first. Your arms start to shake. The edges of your sight go grey, though there is nothing to see.

It holds you there, half in the mouth of the log, your legs and hips still out in the ferns. In front of your face the hollow is dry and close. It smells of rot and earth.

Its jaws shift on your neck. Its weight settles on your back for another bite.

You twist. You try to come round in the mouth of the log, to get your hands on the jaws and catch them, and then your legs up and around its neck, and squeeze until it can't breathe.

The roots hem your shoulders in on both sides. The weight on your back pins your hips flat to the ground. You wrench anyway, and get half round, one shoulder scraping on the torn wood.

One hand finds it. Fur, coarse and thick. A cheek, hard as bone under the fur. The hot, wet edge of a lip, and teeth behind it.

You grip, and your fingers have no strength left in them. They slide off.

Your legs are still out in the ferns, under its weight. You can't get them free. You can't get them up.

The jaws open.

They close a third time, deep in the side of your neck.

The pain goes very far away. It is still there, but it is somewhere a long way off, happening to someone else.

In front of your face the dry dark of the log, with its smell of rot and earth, goes darker. Then there is nothing.

You don't feel it drag you out of the log's mouth by the neck, or up the slope into the thick ferns.

You don't wake.

You die there, in the ferns above the hollow, on your first night in Davrar.
