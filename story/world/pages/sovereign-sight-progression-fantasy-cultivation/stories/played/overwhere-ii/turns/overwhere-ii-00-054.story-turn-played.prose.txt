At the Lantern the woman behind the counter wraps you bread, cheese and cold mutton for the road, for two coppers. At the tinker's stall on the green you buy a leather waterskin for three more.

You fill it at the well and drink deep. Then you set off south, with the market still waking behind you.

Day four is grey and still. Low cloud hangs just above the Whitecombs' snow line.

You pass Callow Beck without stopping and climb the goat track you know now.

In the high cwm the black pool lies quiet. The salt crust around it lies bare and still.

You eat your bread and mutton on a rock and drink from the skin. Then you climb on above the pool.

Above the cwm the goat tracks are buried in old snow, and you pick your own way up the scree.

A slab of ice-glazed scree shifts under your foot, and you go down hard.

You slide thirty feet before you can stop yourself, the stones raking your side and hip.

Your arms and legs come out unmarked, the new skin smooth under the grit. But the skin over your ribs is scraped raw, and bruised deep beneath.

It takes you the best part of an hour to climb back to where you fell, by a longer, safer way.

Then you see the prints: big webbed paws, frozen into old ice on the scree, all heading downhill.

Greymaw tracks, long-strided and far apart. The pack was running when it made them.

You follow them up. A little past midday you stand under the crag.

It is a grey buttress, split from top to bottom, the crack wide enough to walk into. Fallen blocks the size of carts lie heaped at its foot, burying the mouth of an old den.

The den reeks faintly of rotten salt, the same sourness as the greymaws' bite.

Cold air breathes out of the split in the crag. It smells of the sea.

Your well stirs, and you feel something new: a slow, vast pull from far below your feet. It is like a tide under the mountain, drawing on your well the way the moon draws the sea.

The rock underfoot shivers once, faintly, and is still.

The pieces fall together. A midwinter quake split this crag, buried the den and sent the pack running downhill.

Five hours back down to the Ford, by your reckoning, and the day already half gone.

The split yawns in front of you, dark and breathing cold, and the pull from below goes on.
