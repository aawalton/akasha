You step down into the cwm toward the pool, spear low, to see what the goats will do.

Both heads snap up at once. Both goats come at you, heads down, coral horns first.

You reach with Undertow and pull, hard, on the lead goat as it charges.

Its Water tears out of it in a rush. Its legs fold, and it slides dead across the scree to your feet.

The second is three strides behind. You turn the same pull on it without a pause.

It drops even faster. Its own speed rolls it on past you, to a stop against a boulder.

Both horns stayed a stride short of you. It was over in a handful of heartbeats.

Where you drew beside the pool, the black water ripples toward you, the way the tarn's did.

Each goat is as heavy as a big ewe. You could drag one by hand, but two, down that track, is too many.

So you hold Undertow on both and pull them after you: a steady draw, like towing on a rope.

As you draw, you feel a small hard knot of Water behind each goat's breastbone.

The descent takes longer than the climb. Your throat burns dry the whole way down. Your head aches, and your strength ebbs a little with every hour without water.

Late in the afternoon you haul the goats into the yard at Callow Beck.

Ebba comes out and stops short. She crouches by the nearer goat and lifts its scaled left ear. Her own notch is cut in it, plain as day.

"Bramble," she says. "And that's Sorrel. I raised them both from kids."

She stands, jaw tight, and shouts her two grandsons out of the byre.

"Fetch peat. We burn them on the midden, now. And don't you lay a hand on them."

She turns back to you.

"I've no coin to spare, but I've hot oatcakes and goat's butter, and you've earned them."

The grandsons come back with armloads of peat and start dragging the first goat toward the midden.
