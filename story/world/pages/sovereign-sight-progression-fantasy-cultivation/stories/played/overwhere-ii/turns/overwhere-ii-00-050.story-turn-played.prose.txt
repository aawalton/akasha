"Either is fine," you say. "I'll learn what anyone can teach me. And now I think I need an early night's sleep. I have a hunt in the morning."

Anselm laughs softly. "Good hunting, then," he says. "And Threll's keeping."

You take your leave and walk back across the green to the empty watch cottage.

You sleep deep and dreamless in the bunk, and wake before dawn feeling whole again.

Day three dawns clear and cold. Frost whitens the green, and the Whitecombs stand sharp against the sky.

You gear up: knapsack on your back, purse at your belt, the watch spear in your hand. Your mouth is dry and your stomach hollow. You've had nothing since Anselm's bread and cheese last evening.

You take the lane south out of Wendle Ford, toward the white mountains.

Two hours on, the lane climbs to a longhouse where a beck comes down out of the snow. Goats crop the slope below it. A grey-haired woman stands by the wall and watches you come, hard as a gatepost.

She looks you up and down in silence. Then her eyes go to the watch spear.

"That's one of Dray's," she says. "You'll be the one who killed the greymaws. Ebba Callow."

She jerks her chin up the mountain.

"At midwinter the snow in the high cwm above us melted black, all in one night. Since then my goats won't graze above the beck. Two strayed up. They never came back. And the beck's run faint salt ever since. Taste it, if you doubt me."

Her mouth goes tight.

"I've not been above the beck since. I've climbed these hills since I could walk."

She points out a goat track winding up toward a notch under the snow line.

"That's your way. Three hours' climb. Be down off the mountain by dusk."

She presses a round of goat's cheese, wrapped in a cloth, into your hands.

You climb. The track steepens and the air thins, and the farm shrinks to a toy below you.

Once the rock trembles underfoot: a long, low shudder, like thunder felt rather than heard.

By noon you top the notch into the high cwm, a bowl of rock just under the snow line.

A black pool lies in the bowl, thirty paces across, free of ice, ringed with a crust of grey salt. The wind off it smells of the sea, far from any sea.

Your well stirs and leans toward the pool, the way it leaned toward the tarn, but weaker.

You spot them before they spot you: two goats, grazing on the salt at the water's edge.

They are grey-scaled like fish, and their horns are rough and branching like coral.

Both keep their heads down. The wind blows from them to you, and they haven't caught your scent.
