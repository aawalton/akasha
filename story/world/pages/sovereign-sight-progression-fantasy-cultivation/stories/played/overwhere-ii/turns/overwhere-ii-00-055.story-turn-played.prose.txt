You turn your back on it and start down. With every step the pull fades behind you, until your well lies quiet again.

This time you take the longer, safer way over the scree, testing each stone before you trust it, and you keep your feet all the way to the cwm.

You pass the quiet pool, then Callow Beck. You unwrap Ebba's round of goat's cheese and eat it as you walk, and drink from your skin.

Dusk has gone to dark by the time you cross the green. The last of the market stalls are packed away, and the trodden grass is empty under the stars.

Tam Oakes lets you in. Dray looks up from the fire.

You tell him all of it: the webbed prints frozen in the scree and all running downhill, the grey crag split from top to bottom, the den buried under fallen blocks the size of carts.

"A quake," Dray says slowly. "That's what the thunder was, all winter. It shook them out of their beds."

He nods to himself. "That's sure word, and it's what I paid for."

He counts out the other half of the silver bar in coin and pushes it across the table to you.

Then you tell him the rest: the cold air breathing out of the split and smelling of the sea, and the slow, vast pull from far under the mountain.

Dray sits very still. The firelight shows every line in his face.

"Under the mountain," he says. "And the tarn, and the pool, all leaning the same way."

He sets his hands flat on the table. "I'll write to Lady Varrow tonight, and to the garrison at Carrowmouth. Wat Brisk sleeps at the Lantern and leaves at first light; the letters go with him."

He looks at you. "I'll ask you one thing. Keep the pull under the mountain to yourself till I've word back. Folk here have had a hard enough winter without that to lie awake on."

He rubs his eyes. Then his hand stops, and he looks up again.

"And Crake. His men robbed a drover on the Carrowmouth road ten days ago. A day's walk below the Ford. That's the nearest he's been."
