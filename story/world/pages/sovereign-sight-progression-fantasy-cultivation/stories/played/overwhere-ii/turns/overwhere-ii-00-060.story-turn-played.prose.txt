You go back to the Lantern for a second breakfast and drink a full jug down to the bottom. Then you walk back to the cottage.

You sit on the bunk and call up what you know of anatomy from Earth, muscle by muscle: where each one starts, where it ends, what it pulls.

You start small: the muscles of your left hand, one at a time, origin to insertion. The short muscle at the base of your thumb comes first.

Undertow sinks into it, and it cramps hard. For a minute it knots like a fist inside your palm. Then it loosens.

When it lets go, it feels denser than before, and stronger, as if it has been packed tight.

The next goes the same way, and the next. Held to one muscle at a time, the tide runs cleaner and faster than it ever did over skin, filling each shape you name and stopping at its edges.

Hand, forearm, upper arm. Then the right arm. By evening both arms are done.

You eat twice what you used to, and you are thirsty all day long.

On day six a knock comes at the cottage door. It is Hob's boy, come to say your spear is ready at the forge.

You work on through days six and seven: legs, hips, back, belly, chest, neck and jaw.

Each muscle cramps, lets go, and settles denser and stronger, and the ache moves on to the next.

You eat at the Lantern morning and night, sleep long, and keep yourself rested.

Your heart you can't touch. The tide slides off it, as if it is not yet its turn.

By the evening of day eight, every muscle and tendon you can name is refined whole.

You move around the cottage. Your body feels like a drawn bow.

You eat, drink, and go to bed early, and sleep takes you at once.

In the night you dream of Hollow Tarn, black and still under a starless sky.

A stair of black water goes down into the tarn, step after step, out of sight.

The vast, cold dark under your well presses up toward you, patient, and waiting.

You wake in the dark of the cottage with your heart pounding, and the pull still in your chest.
