You follow Dray into his house. He lays out ink and paper on the kitchen table.

He asks you each step of the fight in turn, and writes it down plain, for near an hour.

The lane, the loft, the keys, the bolts. The charge, the dart, the pull, the spear. The venom.

He reads it all back aloud. It is true, every line. You set your mark to it.

Below your feet, the prisoners sit in his cellar, a watchman at the hatch.

Then you go back to the watch cottage, and to your routine.

That afternoon your skull refines whole. The dull headache lingers until you sleep.

After, your thoughts feel steadier, and the tide comes easier to hold.

On day eighteen you turn the tide on your hips. It goes hard.

The marrow ache is deep and grinding, and it takes into the next morning to finish.

For a day you limp between the cottage and the Lantern, slow as an old woman.

But by the morning of day nineteen, both hips are dense and cool and done.

On day nineteen you take the long bones of your arms, then your shins. They go sweetly.

Loop by loop, bone by bone. By nightfall all four are refined, with barely an ache.

Only your thighs are left, the two biggest bones in your body.

Between times you eat at the Lantern: salt, milk, bone broth. You sleep hot each night.

Each night, in the last hour of sleep, the black stair comes. The call from below grows louder.

On the morning of day twenty, hooves clatter on the ford road.

A tall, grey-bearded man in a good riding cloak dismounts outside the Reeve's house.

He lays a bare hand flat on the doorpost stone and stands a moment, eyes half shut.

Dray comes out to meet him, bows, and then looks up the lane toward the watch cottage.

"Nala!" he calls. "Sir Edric Hale is here, from Lady Varrow. He's asking for you."
