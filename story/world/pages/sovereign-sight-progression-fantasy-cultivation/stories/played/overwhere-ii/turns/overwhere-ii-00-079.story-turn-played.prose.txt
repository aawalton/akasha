You take the bread and cheese and eat as you walk, and drink deep from the skin of small beer.

The hollow in your belly fills. The dry rasp leaves your throat.

"Anything else you need from me, Dray? Otherwise, I'm back to refining."

"Nothing on the road," he says. "The watch holds the prisoners. Go on, refine."

"At the Ford I'll write the whole fight down for the Carrowmouth watch. I'll want your mark on it."

"And Lady Varrow's man, Sir Edric Hale, comes within days to see the crag. I'd like you at the Ford when he does, to tell him of the crag and the pull yourself."

You drop a few paces back into the quiet, and turn Undertow inward, to the bones of your skull.

Your mind holds the tide; your feet only walk. It goes easier than you feared.

A slow, deep pressure builds behind your brow and jaw, like a storm that won't break.

The headache stays dull and never splits. You keep the tide steady, mile after mile.

By noon the work is well along, the bone of your skull dense and cool under the ache.

A little after noon the column comes down the last hill, and Wendle Ford lies below.

Folk come out of doorways to stare at the hurdle with its sheeted shape, and at the roped prisoners.

Somebody shouts for the Reeve. Children run alongside. Hob's hammer stops.

Dray halts the column in front of his house and turns to you.

"Inside, then. Ink and paper, and you tell it while I write."
