The last thing you remember is your back window. A book open on your knee, the evening going blue outside the glass, your eyes getting heavy.

Then hay, prickling your neck, and the smell of dust and old wood and cold.

You open your eyes. Grey light comes in through gaps between wide roof boards above you. You are lying in a loft, half buried in loose hay, and your breath smokes.

You sit up, and your body is wrong.

Your arms are thin, the wrists narrow, the hands small and pale with freckles across the backs. Long dark red hair falls over your shoulder, full of hay. Your old grey shirt hangs off you, the collar slipping to one side. Your black tights are still on. Your feet are bare, and small, and very cold.

You look down at the rest of yourself once, and then you look at the roof boards instead.

A ladder goes down through a square hole in the loft floor. Below is a stone barn, empty except for a cart with one wheel off and a row of iron hooks along the wall. The big door is open a hand's width. Outside, snow lies grey in the shadow of the wall, and water runs in the ruts of a muddy lane going downhill.

At the bottom of the lane is a valley: small fields, low stone walls, a river, a few roofs with smoke going up. Across the valley, white mountains climb into cloud.

Then you notice the other thing.

It is in your chest, just under your breastbone. Not a pain. A depth. As if you had always had a well there and only now looked down into it, and could not see the bottom. When you breathe in, something in the well stirs, cold and clear and very large, and runs a little way out along your arms toward your fingers before it settles.

It feels like yours.

Somewhere down the lane, a dog starts barking, and a man's voice tells it to hush.

The dog grumbles on. You shift in the hay, cross your legs, and shut your eyes, and you turn your attention inward, to the thing under your breastbone.

The well is there at once. Cold, clear, deep. You look for the bottom and can't find it.

Then you see it.

Behind your closed eyes there has only ever been dark. Now there is a light in your chest, blue-green, like sun coming down through deep water. Five bright threads run out of it: one to each palm, one to each sole, one up to your throat.

Something is already moving along them. It runs out from the well and back in, loop after loop, smooth and sure. You didn't start it. It moves like a thing long practised, as if it has been running all night while you slept.

You take hold of it and push it faster.

It answers at once, eager.

Warmth pours down your arms and into your legs. The ache goes out of your bare feet. Your breath stops smoking so thickly, and the cold of the loft falls back from your skin.

You open your eyes. The grey light is full of dust motes, every one of them sharp. You can see the grain in each roof board. Outside, meltwater ticks in the ruts of the lane. On the roof above you, a crow shifts its feet.

You feel light, and strong. As if you could spring from the loft floor and catch the rafters.

Down the lane, a quarter mile off at least, the dog is still grumbling, and you hear it as if it were in the barn. A man's voice carries up, slow and deep.

"Hush, Burr. Hush. It's nowt but a fox."

The dog gives one more sulky bark. Then quiet. Somewhere below, a door shuts.

You close your eyes and turn inward again. The light, the threads, the loops. And at the edge of it, one oddness. Down in the barn, the wall of iron hooks feels flat. Numb. A dead patch in a sense you didn't know you had. Everything else around you, the boards, the hay, the stone, the crow on the roof, feels faintly alive.

You push the flow down the threads into your palms and hold it there.

It gathers. It presses to get out, like a breath held too long, and your palms tingle, cold.

You can hold it back. It strains against you like a dog on a short lead.

The longer you hold it, the harder it presses, and the colder your palms prickle.

You speak, for the first time since you woke.

"Okay, seems like this might be a cultivation world, but the dead iron is different."

The voice is wrong. It comes out higher and lighter than yours ever was. A woman's voice, a little husky. Your palms are still full and pressing, and you keep your hold and go on.

"Millennial Mage maybe? I guess I should start with isekai protocol anyways. System? Status?"

The air in front of you stays empty. No screen. No chime. No voice in your head, no words hanging in the grey light.

You wait. Meltwater drips in the lane. The crow shuffles on the roof.

It gives one hoarse caw, as if in reply, and flaps off over the hill.

In your palms the flow keeps straining against your hold, cold and patient.

Then claws scrabble on stone below.

Through the loft hole, you see a rough grey collie standing on the barn floor. It has pushed in through the gap in the big door. Its nose is working and its hackles are half up, and it is looking at the ladder.

It barks once, sharp, straight up at you.

From down the lane the man's voice calls, nearer now and puffing. "Burr! Burr, come by!"

Footsteps crunch up the lane toward the barn, uneven, one foot landing heavier than the other.

You let go.

The pressure in your palms slides back up the threads and pours into the well. Then you take hold of the loops and drive them. Faster. Faster again.

Warmth floods every limb, and lightness comes with it, down your arms and legs to the soles of your feet. Your heart stays slow and easy. You could run up the hillside behind the barn and not breathe hard at the top.

Below, the collie keeps its eyes on the loft hole. It growls, low, and stays at the foot of the ladder.

The footsteps reach the barn. The big door scrapes wider on its stones, and a man steps in: big and bearded, forty or so, in a sheepskin coat, leaning on a shepherd's crook.

"Burr, what's got into you—"

He follows the dog's stare up toward the loft.

You spring from the edge.

Ten feet of air, and then the flagstones. You land in a deep crouch, one fist to the stone, your hair swinging forward around your face. The landing doesn't jar you at all. It is as easy as stepping off a stair, and your knees, your ankles and your bare feet don't even sting.

Dust and loose hay drift down around you in the grey light from the door.

The man jerks back a step and brings the crook up across his body in both hands.

Burr barks once, hard. Then he sidles up, sniffs your fist, and leans his whole weight against your leg.

The man stares. At your bare feet. At the hay in your hair. At the shirt hanging off your shoulder.

"Burr, come by," he says, low.

The dog stays where he is, leaning on you.

"You're Talented, then," the man says slowly. It isn't quite a question, and his hands stay tight on the crook. "That's Aske's barn you've been sleeping in, lass. Who are you, and where've you come from?"

You rise out of the crouch. Burr shifts with you to keep his weight on your leg.

"I'm Nala. I mean you no harm. I'm on a mission but seem to have gotten lost. Could you tell me where I am precisely?"

He studies you for a long moment, the crook still across his body. Then he lowers it a little.

"Garth Marsh," he says. His voice is slow, with a soft burr on it. "Marsh Croft, a quarter mile down the lane. This is Tern Hollow, the Askes' upland barn. I'm minding their sheep while they're at market."

He looks at you a while longer.

"Wendlemere, lass. North side of Carrow." He frowns. "You've not heard of it."

"Carrow. One of Teyr's isles." He says it slowly, the way you'd talk to a child who has knocked her head.

His eyes drop to your bare feet on the cold flags, then to your thin shirt, and the frown deepens.

"A mission, is it. An Aspirant who's lost her road, or a runaway? Either way, no boots." He shakes his head. "Talented or no, you'll not walk far barefoot in the thaw. Snow's still lying on the fells."

He glances out the door, down the lane, his jaw working.

"I've porridge on, and a fire. You can come down and eat, if you've a mind to." A pause. "My lass is abed poorly, mind. So you'll keep your Talent to yourself under my roof."

He turns and limps out into the lane without waiting for an answer, and whistles once for Burr.

Burr looks up at you, then out at his master, and stays leaning on your leg.

"I'll gladly accept the porridge and then be on my way," you call, and follow him out.

Burr trots at your heel down the muddy lane. The stones sting your bare soles, but they don't cut. The flow keeps you warm. Your breath barely smokes, while Garth's puffs out white beside you with every step.

Marsh Croft is a low cottage with a turf roof, a stone fold full of ewes, and a byre. Iron nails are driven all along the fold's top rail, and a row of them over the cottage door.

You feel them as you pass under. A flat, numb band overhead, the same dead patch as the hooks in the barn.

Inside is one smoky room: a peat fire, a table, a ladder up to a loft, and a box bed by the hearth. It smells of peat smoke and wet wool, and under that something bitter, like a herb poultice.

A girl lies in the box bed. She is thin and freckled, nine or so, her face flushed with fever, and she watches you.

Garth ladles porridge into a wooden bowl, adds a lick of honey, and hands it to you with a cup of water.

It is hot and plain, and it is the best thing you have ever eaten. The cold goes out of your bones.

"Are there any threats in the area I should be aware of?" you ask between spoonfuls.

Garth snorts. "Aye. Greymaws. Wolves big as ponies, scale in their fur, stinking of rotten salt. Came down off the Whitecombs at midwinter. Forty sheep this winter, a watchman and a carter."

"Wolves aren't all that's come down off those mountains, neither."

He doesn't say what else.

"They hunt at dusk and dawn. Shun fire and cold iron; that's why the nails. Found their prints below Aske's barn two nights back. Salt-rank. You slept lucky, lass."

"And keep off deep still water after dark. Folk say the Deep Children rise and call you in."

He looks at the girl in the bed, and his voice drops.

"A greymaw had my Wren at the fold. A week back. Bit her calf. Goody's poultices slow it, but the bite's gone black with rot."

The girl pulls the blanket up to her chin and says, bravely, "It doesn't hurt much, Da."

Burr pads over and flops across your bare feet with a sigh, as if he means to stay.

Garth watches the dog a long moment. Then he looks at you, his rough hands knotted together on the table.

"You're Talented," he says quietly. "I don't hold with it, as a rule. But I'll ask."

"Is there aught you can do for her?"

"I'm not certain," you say. "I would gladly do so if I can. But I'd need to visit one of the stones first. Do you know how far it is to the nearest?"

Garth's brow creases. "A Charterstone? There's none in Wendlemere. Nearest's at Carrowmouth. Down on the coast, in the harbour square. Three mile to Wendle Ford, then the valley road. Two days' walk from the Ford. One, if you can get a seat on a hound cart."

He shakes his head. "But it's dark now, lass. It gave its marks on Threllsnacht eve, a fortnight gone. Won't give another till next Threllsnacht. Two Carrowmouth lads took theirs this year."

He turns his cup in his big hands.

"I don't see what a stone's to do with rot, any road. That's where Aspirants go for their marks. Not for mending." A pause. "I sent to Varrow Keep for help a week back. No answer yet."

His jaw works. He looks at the blanket over Wren's bad leg, and doesn't say any more.

In the box bed, Wren pushes herself up on one elbow. Her eyes are bright with fever, and they are fixed on you.

"Are you an Aspirant? Truly? Did you come from the Spires? Is that why you've got no boots?"

"Wren," Garth says. "Lie down."

Wren does not lie down. "Can you do magic? Can you show me? Just a little one?"

You set your bowl down on the table.

Wren goes quiet at once, watching you. You shut your eyes. Across the table, Garth's stool creaks as he leans back.

You go down to the well, to the bright loops running out and back, and past them, reaching for whatever lies underneath.

There is something there. Under the flow, something new: a tide. It pushes outward and pulls back, over and over, like surf on a beach.

You touch it, and a name comes up in you. You didn't reach for it. It is simply there, sure, as if you had always known it.

Undertow.

You let your sense of it spread. It runs out from you in every direction, about ten paces, and then it stops.

Inside that circle, you can feel. The table, the hearth, the walls, the people in the room. You feel them the way a shore feels the sea coming in.

The iron nails over the door are a dead band. The tide slides around them and won't touch them.

The push wants to shove outward. The pull wants to draw things in toward you. And the pull wants more than that. It wants to draw something out of whatever your hand might touch.

Then, in the box bed, you feel Wren's leg.

Under the blanket it is a knot of salt, sour and clotted, wrong against everything else in the room.

The pull leans toward it at once, hungry, the way a tide leans toward a low place in the sand.

You open your eyes. Wren is staring at you, her lips parted. Garth hasn't moved.

"What are you doing?" Wren whispers. "Was that magic? It didn't look like anything."

"Do you have an iron pot or bowl of some sort?" you ask Garth. "I think I can help after all. If not, any container will do."

Garth stares at you for a long moment. Then he gets up, takes the pot off the fire, and scrapes the last of the porridge out of it.

He sets it on the floor by the box bed. Plain black iron, still warm.

You reach for it with your sense. The tide slides over it easily. It is nothing like the numb nails over the door.

"Wren, I'm going to put my hand on your leg. It shouldn't hurt."

Wren nods hard, her eyes huge. Garth turns back the blanket.

Her calf is swollen around a black bite. Dark veins creep out of it, up toward her knee.

You lay your palm over the bite. The skin is hot. The sour knot of salt is right there under your hand.

You shut your eyes, find the tide, and let the pull reach in. Slowly.

It wants everything it touches. You narrow it, hard, down to the sour and the wrong.

Something warm and clean is tangled all through the rot. Her own life, flowing. You ease the pull around it, strand by strand, and take only the salt.

Deeper down, under the rot, you brush against a small, still pool. Asleep. Like your own well, in little.

The pull leans toward it. You hold it back, and the pool stays where it is, untouched.

Garth sucks in a breath.

"It's cold," Wren whispers, and shivers. "It's all prickly."

Under your palm, the black veins ebb. Thread by thread, they draw back toward the bite.

It takes a few long minutes. You keep the pull narrow the whole way, and you never let it slip.

Then the last of the knot comes free, and your palm is suddenly gritty and heavy.

You open your eyes. Across the bed, the hair on Garth's forearms stands up in gooseflesh.

You lift your hand. A mound of damp grey salt sits in your palm. It reeks of rotten fish and brine.

The bite underneath is an ordinary red wound now, clean and puckered. The dark veins are gone.

You tip the salt into the iron pot. It lies there, grey and faintly glistening.

Down in your own well you feel a small, cool ebb where the work drew from you.

Wren wriggles her toes and gasps. "Da. Da, it doesn't hurt. It really doesn't."

Garth kneels by the bed and presses his big hand to her forehead. She is still warm. He looks at the clean wound, then at the grey salt in his pot, and then at you.

His voice comes out rough. "What in the Ancestors' name are you?"
