You kneel by the she-wolf's carcass and lay your torn left forearm against her scaled flank.

You reach into the bite with Undertow. The thin sourness is there, threaded through the torn flesh.

You push.

The sourness slides out of your arm whole and easy, easier than any rot you have drawn. It soaks into the carcass, and the dead flesh under your arm greys like old ash.

It stays there. To your inward sense, your arm feels clean.

In the same breath you pull on the wolf, reaching for any clean life left in her.

There is none. Only cold, dead brine, pooled in the broken chamber behind her breastbone.

A thread of it trickles into your wound and stings like seawater in a fresh cut.

You let the pull go at once. The sting fades to a dull ache.

The crescent bite is clean now, but still open, ragged and bleeding a little.

Behind the cottage door, Burr is barking himself hoarse.

Garth crosses the yard with the lantern held high and stops at the edge of its light. He looks from the she-wolf to the three dead greymaws in the lane, then back to you.

"That's the white-eye herself," he says. "The one that leads them."

His eyes go to your arm.

"You're bleeding. Come in to the fire and let me see that arm."

You follow Garth's lantern into the cottage, and he bars the door behind you.

Burr sniffs your bloody sleeve and whines. On the settle by the fire, Wren sits up, wide-eyed.

You sink onto the hearth stool and half shut your eyes. You drive the loops fast through all five threads.

Then you gather the flow in your left palm and turn it inward, and push it into the torn flesh.

It comes unsteadily. The restless brine in your well rides in with your own clear Water.

The wound stings as if it's packed with sea salt. You hiss through your teeth.

Under the sting, your own Water goes to work. The torn edges draw together, knitting from the ends inward.

Then the sting of the brine swamps it, and the closing stops halfway.

The ends of the crescent are sealed in shiny pink skin. The middle is still open, raw and weeping.

The deep throb is gone. What's left aches like a scraped knee.

Garth sets the lantern down and stares at the half-healed bite.

"It closed!" Wren says. "Half of it did. Can you do the rest?"

Garth fills a basin from the kettle and sets a clean rag beside it.

"Let me wash that before you try anything more," he says. "There's wolf spit and salt in it yet."

Garth kneels by the hearth stool and sponges the bite with warm water, gentle around the edges.

You shut your eyes and turn Undertow inward, on your own well.

The foreign brine sloshes there, restless and brackish, stirred all through your own clear Water.

You pull on its salt, the way you pulled the rot out of Wren. The salt comes loose.

It slides down the threads to your right palm and gathers there, grain by grain.

Minute by minute the brackish taste thins. Your well settles. Cold, clear, and still.

When you open your eyes, a crust of grey salt fills your right palm, smelling faintly of the sea.

Wren leans over the arm of the settle to stare at it. Garth only glances at it, and goes on washing.

He dabs the bite dry and sits back on his heels.

You gather the clear flow in your left palm and push it into the open middle of the bite.

The flow slips. Your mind is worn thin from the day's fine work, and it won't hold the push. The pressure slides back up the threads into the well, and the wound stays as it was.

You try to gather it again. It slips again, like a word on the tip of your tongue.

The middle of the crescent is still open, raw and weeping, its washed edges pink.

"You're done in," Garth says. "You've been pulling rot out of folk since morning."

"Let me bind it, and then you eat. Wren, fetch the clean linen from the chest."

"Thank you," you say. "Guess I'm more tired than I thought."

Wren brings the linen, and Garth binds the bite snug from wrist to elbow.

"We should also check the wolves for reservoirs while we can," you say. "Those could be worth quite a bit if they have them."

Garth ties off the binding and sits back, thinking.

"The Reeve pays two silver a head for any greymaw brought in dead," he says. "The chest bones I've only heard of. Carrowmouth folk buy such things, they say. Not here."

He takes his stout skinning knife down from its peg and lifts the lantern.

"Wren, bar the door behind us, and keep Burr in."

Out in the lane the three drained greymaws lie where they dropped, already stiffening.

Garth kneels on the nearest and saws down through its scaled chest, grunting at the work. Behind the breastbone the knife grates on bone.

In the lantern light you see it: a fist-sized knot of polished grey, layered like a shell, and whole.

You reach for it with your inward sense. It is dry and silent, empty of any Water.

Garth wipes his brow. The knot is still fast in the chest, with half the cut yet to go.

"Tough as ram's horn," he says. "That's half an hour a beast with this knife, and there's four."

Up the fell, the she-wolf's carcass lies dark against the heather.

"The rest ran," Garth says, "but they hunt dusk and dawn both. Now, by lantern, or in the morning?"

"Leave it for morning," you say. "I have some things to try then."

Garth nods. You go back in together, and he drops the bar across the door behind you and dishes up supper.

You eat, and then you sleep hard by the fire. The night stays quiet, without a single howl.

You wake at first light, rested, your mouth dry with thirst. You lift the edge of the linen on your arm. The bite has closed in the night to a crescent of pink new skin.

Out in the lane the three greymaws lie stiff where they fell, untouched. The crows have kept away from them.

You crouch by the half-cut one and reach for its chamber with Undertow.

The pull finds almost nothing to hold. The knot of bone is dry and silent, and it slips away from your sense like a wet stone.

You try again, and this time you grip it by the Water in the flesh around it.

That works. The flesh's cold brine pours up your arm into your well as you pull.

You pull the chamber toward you and push the flesh away from it. The two fight each other for long minutes, the carcass twitching in the grass.

With a wet crack the grey knot tears loose. A spray of rank brine spatters your face.

Your well sloshes brackish again, restless under your breastbone.

The next two go quicker, a few minutes each, and each ends in a wet crack. Three whole chambers lie in the grass beside you, grey and layered like shells.

You climb the fell to the she-wolf. She lies stiff in the heather, twice the size of the rest.

To your sense her chamber is twice as big as theirs, with a crack running through it where the spear went in.

You ease it rather than tear it, holding the push and the pull steady and gentle together.

For a quarter hour you work it loose a hair at a time. The crack never opens.

It slides free into your hands, whole, cracked but holding.

As it comes free, the floor of your well gives, like a step down into colder water.

Below it opens a vast, cold, patient dark. It frightens you a little.

Down at the croft, Garth leans on the gate and shades his eyes up at you.

"Four!" he shouts. "The Reeve'll not believe it. Come down, the porridge is on."

You eat porridge with Garth and Wren at the table, hot and thick. Your mouth is still dry. You drink two cups of water straight off, one after the other.

Wren keeps looking at the grey chambers piled at the end of the table. She looks away, and then her eyes go straight back to them.

"I'll take these to the Reeve," you say, "and tell him what happened."

"Do," Garth says. "The bounty's for beasts brought in dead, mind. He'll want to send a cart for them."

He stays to see to the ewes. Wren waves from the door, Burr at her knee, his tail going.

You set off down the river lane with the four chambers bundled in your arms. The she-wolf's is heavy and cold on top, the crack running across it like a seam.

The morning is bright and cold. Beside the lane the river runs loud over its stones.

A quarter hour down, a young man in orange robes comes hurrying up the lane toward you. It's Keeper Anselm. He is muddy to the knees, and grey under the eyes like a man who hasn't slept.

He stops short. He stares at the chambers in your arms, then at the linen on your forearm.

"I felt it last night, at dusk, from the shrine," he says. "A great drawing, up at Marsh Croft."

He swallows.

"I came running, and lost my nerve at the foot of the fell in the dark. I'm sorry."

His eyes go back to the linen.

"Was that the greymaws? Are you hurt?"

"I'm fine," you say. "Took out the alpha and thinned the pack, though some of them escaped. I was just going to report to the Reeve."

Anselm's eyes go to the biggest chamber on top of your bundle. It's twice the size of the rest, with the crack running through it.

"The white-eye?" he says. "You killed the white-eye."

He lets out a long breath.

"Know if there is anyone here who would be interested in buying the reservoirs?" you ask. "Or a way I could use them in my training?"

"Not here," he says. "No one in the valley has the coin, or the use for them. The thaumists in Carrowmouth buy Aberrant chambers. My carrier could take them down. He comes for my letters on market day, the day after tomorrow."

He looks at the chambers again, more slowly.

"As for training, I know of only one use. A whole chamber can be filled with Water. A Talent can pour their own Water into it and keep it there."

He reaches out and touches the crack in the white-eye's chamber with one finger, then shakes his head.

"Not this one, I'd think. Whatever went in would seep out."

He draws his hand back and tucks it into his sleeve.

"I'll walk with you to the Reeve, if you'll have me. Then the shrine, as we said?"

"Sounds good," you say.

Anselm falls in beside you, and you walk on down the river lane toward the Ford together. Every few steps his eyes drop to the chambers in your arms. He keeps his questions to himself.

In Wendle Ford, folk at their doors stop what they're doing and stare at the grey knots of bone as you pass.

You come to the Reeve's house beside the green, under the only slate roof in the village, where Col lay.

Anselm knocks. Stout, fussy Tam Oakes opens the door, the village reeve you met at Col's bed. He sees the chambers and his mouth falls open.

"Reeve!" he calls back into the house. "Reeve Dray, you'll want to see this!"

A grey, square man in a leather jerkin comes to the door behind him, chewing his breakfast.

"Reeve Dray," Anselm says, "this is Nala, who drew the rot from the Horne girl and from Col."

The Reeve looks you up and down: a small red-haired stranger in a borrowed cloak, with no pack. Then his eyes drop to the chambers in your arms, and he stops chewing.

"Is that the white-eye's?" he says. "Nothing else on the fells grows one that size."

He lifts the cracked chamber off the top of your bundle and turns it over in his thick hands.

"My watch lost a man to that beast. And you, a slip of a lass, put her down?"

He looks up from the chamber to you.

"Where are the carcasses, and who saw it done?"

"Up by Garth's place," you say. "You'll need to send a cart for the carcasses. I got the alpha and thinned the pack, but a few of them got away."

Behind the Reeve, Col Ashby limps into the passage on a stick, his leg bound from knee to ankle.

"If she says she did it, Reeve, she did it," he says. "She pulled the rot out of me clean."

Dray grunts. He turns his head and bellows over his shoulder for the Cray brothers and the watch cart.

"Four carcasses at Marsh Croft, and Garth Marsh's word," he says, turning back. "That'll do me for proof. Two silver a head, once the cart's back. They'll be back by noon."

He weighs the white-eye's chamber in his hands a moment longer.

"And the watch swore a silver bar from its own chest for the white-eye. You'll have it."

He hands the cracked chamber back to you, gently, as if it might break further. You settle it on top of the others.

"A few left, you say. Without her they'll scatter up the fells and pick off lambs. Lambing's three weeks off. I want them dead before it."

He looks at you squarely.

"My watch can't catch them. Would you lead the hunt, for pay?"

"Sure," you say, "but I'll hunt alone. For pay, I'd like the best spear you can get me to hunt them with. I'll put in up to two silver bars for it, if the hunt itself isn't enough."

You shift the bundle in your arms.

"I'd also like a pack if you have a spare. Getting tired of carrying these in my hands."

Dray steps back into the passage. He comes back with a canvas knapsack on leather straps.

"Take this," he says. "It was the watchman's the greymaws took this winter. He'll not want it."

You stow the four chambers in it, the white-eye's on top, and shrug it onto your back. It sits snug and light.

"The hunt's worth a silver bar beyond the heads," Dray says, "and I'll put that to the spear. The watch has only plain iron spears. There's nothing better this side of Carrowmouth. Except Hob Tarrant's forge. He can make a boar spear with a cold-iron head. Three days and a silver bar."

He rubs his jaw.

"Alone, though. That I don't like. I've buried one man to that pack this winter."

He rolls his sleeves to the elbow. His forearms go grey and hard as you watch, and when he flexes his fingers they grate like millstones.

"You hunt alone for me once you have bested me. A watch spear against my hands."

He jerks his head toward the green.

"Out on the green, now, if you're willing."

"You sure?" you say. "I already bested the alpha with her pack. The stragglers are no threat to me on their own. I would hate to injure you without need."

Dray gives a short bark of a laugh. A grim smile creases his tired face.

"I'm at First Depth, lass. I'd like to see you try."

The smile goes.

"Killing the white-eye proves you can kill. It doesn't show me how you fight a man. Alone on the fells, one mistake is a grave. I'll see you fight before I send you."

He takes a plain iron-headed spear from the rack by the door and strides past you, out onto the green.

Word runs ahead of him. Doors open around the green, and folk drift out to line its edge, wiping their hands on aprons, pulling children back by the collar.

Anselm stands by the Reeve's gate, white-faced, his hands knotted in his orange sleeves. In the doorway behind you, Col leans on his stick, grinning.

Out on the grass, Dray plants the spear butt-down a few paces from him and stands back. His stone forearms hang grey at his sides. He rolls his shoulders.

"Spear's there," he says. "Take it up when you're ready, or hunt with the watch."

"Oh good," you say. "Then you'll heal fast if I do hurt you."

A ripple of laughter runs round the green. Dray's grim smile widens.

You pull the spear from the turf. You drive your loops fast, and the flow runs hot down your arms and legs until your whole body hums with it.

You walk toward him slowly, spear low. He waits, stone arms loose, his weight on his toes.

At ten paces you commit. You lunge and thrust at his chest.

In the same heartbeat you surge Undertow: a pull on Dray, dragging him toward you, and a push on the spear, driving it ahead of your arm.

The tide takes him. He lurches a half step forward, and his eyes go wide.

But he rides the pull instead of fighting it, and turns his shoulder into the thrust. His grey forearm sweeps across. The pushed point skids off stone with a shriek of iron.

Your own pull has carried him inside your reach, close enough to touch.

His stone fist slams into your ribs like a falling wall.

The world tips. You land flat on your back in the wet grass, and the spear spins away out of your hand.

Your breath is gone. Your ribs throb where he struck, bruised but whole.

The green is dead silent.

Dray stands over you, breathing hard. He stares down at you as if you were a flood coming down the valley.

"That pull," he says. "I felt the whole river take hold of me."

He holds out one grey hand. As you watch, the stone fades from it to flesh.

"You're no small Talent, lass. But you fight like one. Again?"

"Sure," you say.

You take his hand, and he hauls you up. Your ribs twinge as you stand.

You fetch the spear from the grass, and the two of you square off again.

This time you keep your distance. You circle, spear low, watching his feet.

Dray stalks you, stone arms raised. You can see him bracing, his weight set back against the pull he expects.

You hold the pull back. You only feint, a jab at his knee, a jab at his face, and let him come to you.

He swings, leaning his whole weight into it. You slip back, and the stone fist hisses past your face.

You feel him set his feet against a pull that isn't coming, and you push instead.

The push catches him braced the wrong way. He staggers, overbalanced, arms flung wide.

For a heartbeat his flank is open below the stone.

You step in and touch the spear point to his side, just above his belt.

Dray freezes. He looks down at the iron resting on his jerkin, and lets out a long breath.

The stone drains from his arms. He steps back and raises one open hand.

"Struck true," he says. "That'll do."

A roar goes up round the green. Col bangs his stick on the doorframe and whoops. By the gate, Anselm lets out a shaky laugh and sags against the gatepost.

"You learn fast," Dray says. "Hunt alone, then, and bring me heads. The cart's back by noon, and I'll pay you then. See Hob about that spear meanwhile."
