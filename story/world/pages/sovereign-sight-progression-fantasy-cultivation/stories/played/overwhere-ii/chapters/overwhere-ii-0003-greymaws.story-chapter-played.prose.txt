"I'll put no price on a girl's life," you say. "Anyone I can heal will be healed. Whatever you wish to contribute, I will accept, as it will allow me to help more."

Horne stares at you. Then he turns and goes heavily down the stairs without a word.

He comes back with a small leather pouch, presses it into your hands, and closes your fingers around it.

Inside is a bar of silver as long as your finger, heavy and cold, stamped with a mark.

"I pay my debts," Horne says roughly. "To the copper. Don't you shame me by giving it back."

You turn to Goody. "I understand Tansy was the worst case, but are there others? I think I need a meal and a nap first, but then I should be able to help the rest. And animals too. Garth has some ewes that need healing, once the people are seen to."

Goody counts on her bent fingers. "Col Ashby of the watch. Greymaw laid his leg open, and it's gone grey. He's at the Reeve's house, by the green. Then Pim Sallow, the carter's widow. Eel spine in her hand at Threllsnacht. Slow grey rot." She folds down the last finger. "And Garth's ewes. That's the lot I know."

Tansy's mother rises from the bedside at last. "You'll eat here. And sleep in the spare bed. Please."

Down in the warm kitchen she sets out bread, butter, cold mutton, cheese, and a jug of small beer.

You eat like a wolf. All of it tastes better than anything ever has.

Garth, leaning by the range, watches you put away half a loaf, and grins for the first time since you met him.

Someone knocks at the front door. Quick, polite, insistent.

Horne opens it on a young man in orange robes, thin and earnest, out of breath from running.

"Master Horne. Forgive me. I'm looking for—"

His eyes find you at the table and stop there.

"You," he says softly. "It was you. I felt it from the shrine. Like the whole river turning over."

"I'm not sure what you're talking about," you say, "but we can talk as we go."

The young man opens his mouth, closes it, and inclines his head politely. He doesn't argue.

"Goody, I'm ready for the next patient. Could you lead the way?" You look to the range. "Garth, you can go home to Wren. I'll stop by soon to see to the ewes."

Garth takes your hand in his big rough one and holds it a moment. "I'll have the fold ready."

He limps off up the river road toward home, his crook tapping on the stones.

Goody sets off for the green with her shawl pulled tight. The young man falls in beside you.

"Anselm," he says. "Keeper of the shrine to Threll, here at the Ford. Since last autumn. My Talent is small. Tidesense. I feel it when Water is drawn nearby, that's all."

"This morning I felt a slow tide from the mill, an hour long. Steady. Like breathing."

He glances at you sidelong. "And at dawn, up in the hills, something so vast it frightened me. I thought the Sea itself had come up out of the fells. I didn't sleep again."

He says it simply, without accusing, and then falls quiet beside you.

The green is a patch of muddy grass ringed by cottages. One house beside it has a slate roof. A stout, fussy man in a good waistcoat opens its door before Goody can knock.

"Goody. Keeper." He peers at you. "And who's this? Is this the one they're saying—"

"Reeve Oakes," Goody cuts in. "She's here for Col. Where is he?"

The Reeve leads you to a back room. A broad, stubborn-jawed man of about thirty lies abed there, his leg bandaged from knee to ankle. The room smells sour and briny, like the salt from Tansy's arm.

"Don't need a stranger," Col growls. "I need my leg."

But he doesn't send you away.

Anselm touches your sleeve. "May I watch? I'll hold him steady if you need it."

"Sure," you say.

Anselm's face lights up. He kneels at the foot of the bed.

Goody unwinds Col's bandage. Underneath is a long gash down his calf, puckered and grey at the edges.

You sit beside him, rest your palm on his knee above it, and shut your eyes.

You find the tide and let it breathe. Out, a thin wash of your own flow. In, the narrow pull.

Col's rot is a coarse grey knot, not a tangle. After Tansy, it is almost easy to find.

Col grunts at the cold of your hand on his knee. Then he goes quiet.

Once, between waves, you open your eyes. Col is staring at the ceiling, and he doesn't blink. Anselm's hand on his ankle has gone rigid. The young Keeper is breathing slow and deep, like a man standing at the edge of the sea.

You close your eyes again.

Out. In. The grey knot loosens and comes up, thread by thick thread, and you keep every thread clear of his life.

It takes half an hour, by the shadow crossing the floor. When you look, the grey has faded from the gash, leaving a clean red line. A palmful of grey salt lies heavy on your hand, stinking of brine.

You tip it into the Reeve's hearth. Goody heaps kindling on until the fire roars up and spits the salt to ash.

Col flexes his foot, then his knee, and lets out a long breath.

"That's my leg," he says hoarsely. "That's my leg back." He looks at you, gruff. "The watch owes you, Nala. I owe you."

Reeve Oakes is already talking. To Goody, to Col, to the doorway. Wonders, he says. The whole valley.

You sit back. Your well is full, but your mind is heavy with the fine work. You want sleep.

Anselm stays kneeling. He stares at his own hands, then at you.

"That was no small Talent," he says quietly, for your ears only. "A Keeper must write to Carrowmouth of any great new Talent in his charge. I'm bound to. I write by the carrier every week." He holds your eyes. "I'd rather hear it from you first, whatever it is."

"It's called Undertow," you tell Anselm, "but I'm still learning what it does. You could help me with that, once I'm done with the urgent needs."

His lips move on the name. "Undertow." He frowns. "I know no canticle that names that Talent."

Then he smiles, quick and shy. "But I know the Keepers' teaching. Talents, Depths, Descent. Gladly."

You turn to Goody. "One more person, right? Then a nap, then the ewes."

Goody snorts. "Pim Sallow. South end, by the carters' yard. Mind her tongue, it's sharp."

Pim is sixty or so and lives alone in one room. She greets you at the door with, "I've no coin, so don't ask."

Her right palm has a hard grey swelling where an eel spine went in at Threllsnacht.

You reach with the tide. The rot is small, old and hard, a pebble of it, settled in for a long stay.

You work it the same way. Out, in, out, in, keeping the pull narrow, though your mind drags now.

It takes a quarter hour. A thumb-sized crust of grey salt comes away on your palm, and Goody burns it in the grate.

The swelling is gone. Pim flexes her hand and stares at it, and for once she is quiet.

Then she presses a basket on you: six brown eggs and a crock of honey. "Don't you dare say no."

Back at the mill, Tansy's mother shows you to the spare bed and draws the curtain.

You are asleep before your head settles. You sleep hard, without dreams, for hours.

You wake in afternoon light with a clear head. The tiredness is gone, and the well is brimming.

Anselm walks you to the edge of the village. "Tomorrow, at the shrine? I'll teach what I know."

The walk up the river and the lane to Marsh Croft takes you an hour in Anni's boots.

Wren is sitting up at the door with Burr. She shrieks and waves the moment she sees you.

Garth has the two lame ewes penned apart in the fold, their scratched legs sour with rot.

You lean on the rail and reach for both at once. You don't touch them. You draw across the gap.

The rot comes up out of their legs like smoke off a chimney, and two crusts of grey salt gather on your palm.

Garth burns them in the hearth. The ewes, bewildered, put their weight down on all four legs.

The sun is low now over the western fells. The light is going gold, and the shadows are long.

Burr stands up suddenly, stiff-legged, hackles up, staring up the fell.

Far above, from the screes, comes a long howl. Then another answers it, closer.

Garth's face goes grey. He reaches for the old boar spear over the door.

"Greymaws," he says.

"Give me the spear," you say. "You take Burr and Wren inside. I'll take care of the greymaws."

Garth stares at you. Small, red-haired, in his dead wife's cloak. Then he puts the ash spear in your hands.

He scoops Wren up, drags Burr in by the scruff, and bars the door behind them.

The spear is long, and heavy for your size. You drive the flow hot, and it feels like a broom handle.

You stand in the lane before the fold. Behind you the ewes crowd together, bleating.

The sun slides down behind the western fells. The light goes grey-gold, then grey.

They come down the fell fast and silent. Four shapes against the heather, big as ponies.

Three run ahead. The fourth hangs back on the slope above. It is twice their size, and it waits.

The smell reaches you first. Rotten brine, like a beach full of dead fish.

Then you see them clearly. Grey scale in shaggy fur. Webbed claws. A second row of teeth.

Your tide reaches out on its own, and you feel them. Sour with rot. And deep inside each one, something else: behind the breastbone, a hard chamber of bone, brimming with Water. Dense, cold, and strong.

The pull leans toward those chambers the way it leaned toward Wren's rot. Hungry.

The first greymaw leaps for your throat.

You shove with the tide. It flies back, tumbling.

It rolls, shakes itself, and gets up unhurt, snarling.

The second comes in low. You drive the spear into its shoulder, and it shrugs the point off.

So you stop holding the pull back.

You reach into the nearest greymaw's chest, grip the bone chamber, and pull hard.

Water floods out of it and up your arm into your well, brackish and cold and strange.

The greymaw staggers mid-stride. Its legs fold. Its eyes glaze. In ten heartbeats it is down, and still.

You turn the pull on the second. It yelps once, stumbles, and drops into the mud.

The third wheels to run. Your reach catches it at the fold wall, and it slides down dead.

Thick grey salt crusts your palm, reeking of rot. You close your fist on it.

Your well is brimming, sloshing with foreign Water, restless under your breastbone.

Three pony-sized greymaws lie in the mud. In the fold, the ewes have gone silent.

Up on the slope, the big one rises to its feet.

A she-wolf, twice the size of the others, grey-scaled. One eye is milk-white. The other is fixed on you.

She lifts her muzzle and scents the air, as if she smells the sea. Then she starts down toward you.

You run at her.

The spear is in both hands and the flow roars hot through your legs. The heather flies past. Above you the she-wolf lengthens into a charge, huge and silent, jaws open.

At ten paces you reach with the tide for the bone chamber in her chest, and pull.

It is deep. Far deeper than the others' were. It fights you like a rope in a current.

In the same moment you shove with the push against her body, straight into her charge.

Push and pull at once. The tide strains two ways inside you, and you feel it stretch toward breaking.

You hold it.

Her charge slows, as if she is running through deep water. Her legs go heavy.

You drop to one knee and plant the butt of the spear in the turf, point up, square in her path.

She comes on anyway, too heavy to stop, and meets the braced point full in the chest.

The iron head punches through scale and sinks deep. The crossbar slams up against her breast. Something bursts inside her, and a cold gush of brine sprays from the wound, reeking of the sea.

Even so, her jaws snap sideways as she falls, and catch your left forearm.

The teeth grind through the tough skin of your arm. Hot pain. Blood runs down to your fingers.

Then the jaws go slack. She slumps over the spear, and her milk-white eye goes dull.

You wrench your arm free, gasping.

Inside you, something in the tide gives way once more. Push and pull have stopped fighting each other. You know, the way you knew the name, that you can do both at once, whenever you will it.

High on the fell, the rest of the pack begin to howl. Thin, broken, fleeing howls. They fade away up the screes, farther and farther, until the fell is quiet.

Your well sloshes, brimming with cold foreign brine. It churns under your breastbone, but it doesn't hurt you.

Your forearm throbs, torn in a ragged crescent. Under your sense, a thin sourness is seeping into the wound.

Behind you, the cottage door scrapes open. Garth stands in it with a lantern, staring.
