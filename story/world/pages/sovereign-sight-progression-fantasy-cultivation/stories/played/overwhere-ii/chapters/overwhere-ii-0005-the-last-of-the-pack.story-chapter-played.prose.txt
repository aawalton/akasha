"Great," you say. "All right if I use this guard spear for the hunt? I'd rather not wait three days for them to scatter. I'll go start a commission with Hob and then start tracking."

"Take it," Dray says, "and bring it back after. Hob's is the stone shed on the ford road."

Hob Tarrant's smithy is open at the front, its forge glowing red in the back. Cold-iron nails and charms hang in rows along the wall behind it, and the whole row sits in your sense like a dead patch, flat and numb.

Hob is a huge man in a leather apron. As you step up on his left, he cups his right ear toward you and frowns.

You move round to his right. He hears you out, nodding slowly.

"Boar spear, cold-iron head, ash shaft," he says. "Three days. A silver bar. Half now, half when I hand it over."

You pay him with your silver bar. He counts twenty-five silver pieces back into your palm in change.

From the Ford you jog back up the river lane, the watch spear in your hand and the knapsack snug on your back. You pass Marsh Croft and keep going.

Above the croft the pack's trail is easy to read: webbed prints in the mud, smears of grey slime on the stones.

Higher on the fell you pass a half-eaten ewe, torn open in last night's flight. The smell of it follows you a long way up.

Tufts of grey, scaled fur are snagged on the heather, leading on up toward the corrie.

The climb is long and steep. The flow in you keeps your breath easy all the way.

Toward noon you reach the screes, grey rockfalls piled below the corrie's lip. The stink of rotten salt thickens. At the foot of a deep cleft, sheep bones lie scattered among the rocks.

Above you, on the skyline, stands a cairn hung with scraps of cold iron.

You creep within ten paces of the cleft, and Undertow feels them through the rock.

Five sour knots, close together, deep in the cleft. One of them shifts unevenly, favouring a leg.

They are sluggish and slow, drowsing out the day. All five lie still at your coming.

You drive your loops fast. The flow runs hot in your arms and legs, and you edge up the scree, setting each foot down on solid stone.

A pebble shifts under your boot. You freeze. In the cleft, the five sour knots lie still.

You reach the mouth of the cleft unheard, spear low. The reek of rotten salt hangs as thick as fog.

In the gloom inside, five grey shapes lie heaped among the sheep bones.

You step into the mouth and lunge at the nearest.

Pull and push go out together in one surge. The greymaw slides toward you and the spear drives ahead of your arm, and the point punches through scale.

It strikes the bone chamber. A cold gush of brine bursts out over your hands, and the greymaw sags dead.

The pack wakes snarling. For the first few heartbeats they are slow and clumsy, legs tangling, heads swinging.

The cleft is narrow. Two can come at you at once, and no more.

The first lunges. You push, and it slams back into the rock wall.

The second you pull straight onto your thrusting spear. It dies with a gurgle of brine.

The one you threw comes again. You push it off its feet, then pull it back onto the point.

A third gush of brine. Three lie dead in the mouth of the cleft.

The fourth scrambles over its dead packmates. You step aside and spear it through the chest.

It thrashes, jaws snapping a hand's breadth from your face. Then it goes still.

At the back of the cleft the limping greymaw hangs back and does not come.

As the fourth dies, it scrabbles up the back of the cleft and out over the rim.

It lurches away up the scree on three legs, toward the corrie lip and the tarn beyond.

You scramble out of the cleft and up the scree after the limping greymaw.

On three legs it is slow. You gain on it with every stride, the flow hot in your legs, the loose stones skittering away under your boots.

It lurches over the corrie lip. You crest it a few heartbeats behind.

Below you lies the tarn, a black lake cupped in grey crags, still as poured ink.

The moment you see it, your well stirs under your breastbone and leans toward the water. It pulls at you like a tide drawn by the moon.

The greymaw limps along the east shore, where worn stone steps run down into the dark water.

You run it down beside the steps. It turns at bay, snarling, its bad leg trembling under it.

You pull it onto your thrusting spear. The point sinks through scale into its chamber.

A gush of cold brine spills over the steps, and the last greymaw dies.

As you pull, the black water of the tarn ripples toward you, against the wind. Your well leans harder toward it as the ripples come. They lap at the lowest step, and still.

You kneel and saw the dead greymaw's right ear off with the spear's edge.

The crags stand silent, empty of birdcall. The water lies flat as glass. The air smells faintly of the sea.

The worn steps go down into the tarn and on, out of sight, into the black.

You turn your back on the tarn and climb back over the corrie lip.

With each step down the scree, the pull on your well fades. Once the tarn drops out of sight behind the lip, it's gone, and your well settles quiet under your breastbone.

In the cleft the four dead greymaws lie where they fell, stiffening in the cold.

You saw off each right ear with the spear's edge. Five ears weigh down your knapsack on top of the chambers.

Then the long walk down: past the half-eaten ewe, past Marsh Croft, down the river lane beside the loud water.

By mid-afternoon your mouth is dry and your throat rough with thirst.

In the yard of the Reeve's house by the green, four greymaw carcasses lie in a row, flies already at them.

Dray is sitting on his step with a mug in his hand. He stands when he sees you.

You swing the knapsack down and tip the five ears out onto the step beside him.

Dray stares at them, then at you. He counts them twice with one thick finger.

"Five," he says. "The whole pack, in two days, and not a scratch on my watch."

He goes inside. He comes back with a heavy leather purse and a small cloth bag.

"Two silver a head for nine heads. Eighteen pieces, counted." He sets the purse in your hand. "The watch's silver bar for the white-eye, as sworn. And a bar for the hunt, which you'll put to Hob's spear."

He presses the cloth bag on top of the purse. The silver is heavy in your hands.

"I'll send two men and a pony up for the den carcasses, and burn them."

He sits back down on the step and looks at you for a long moment.

"The valley needs someone like you, Nala. The watch cottage by the ford stands empty. It's yours, if you'll stay."
