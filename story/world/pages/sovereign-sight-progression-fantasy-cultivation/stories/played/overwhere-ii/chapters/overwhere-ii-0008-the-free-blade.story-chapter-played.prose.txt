"Can we catch him?" you ask. "I'm sure I can make it at a run, but I'd need a guide who could keep up with me."

"You don't need a guide," Dray says. "The road runs right past the tollhouse door. Twelve miles, downhill. But you'll have company. At First Depth I can run all night like a horse at a trot."

He turns to his men. "Col, you and the Pells hold the prisoners. Bind Rob's arm properly. We'll be back by dawn."

"He's got half an hour on you," Wil says from the straw. "He jogs and walks. He won't think anyone's coming."

You and Dray leave the barn and the lamplit yard, and run.

Down the black lane. Out onto the valley road. Away downhill.

Your Locks are shut. Behind them your penned Water presses, cold and restless, with nowhere to go. You run on refined muscle alone.

You run faster than any man could, long and easy, and Dray's stone-heavy stride keeps pace beside you.

An hour after dark the half moon rises, and the road shows pale between the hedges.

You run on. Your breath comes steady, and the miles fall away behind you.

An hour out, your refined ears catch it ahead: quick, light feet on the road. Then a stumble.

Then a scramble of thorn, and a sharp intake of breath, off to the right.

The footsteps have stopped. Someone small has gone to ground in the hedge bottom.

You slow, and Dray slows with you, both of you breathing hard in the moonlight.

In the black of the hedge: the pale glint of a too-big coat, and a boy holding his breath.

You step back and let Dray take the lead.

"Out you come, lad," he says, gruff but not unkind. "The Reeve's law doesn't hang a boy of thirteen."

The thorns rustle. A thin, freckled boy crawls out in a too-big coat. The whistle-seller from market day.

He holds a little clasp knife out in front of him, shaking so hard the blade jitters.

Dray doesn't reach for it. "Put that away. Crake's dead. She killed him at Ashlin."

"Liar. He can't die. He's—" The boy's voice cracks.

Dray lifts one fist into the moonlight. Before the boy's eyes it greys and hardens into stone.

"I was there, lad. It's over."

Pip stares at the stone fist, then at you. The knife drops into the road.

He cries, hard, the way a child does. Then, all at once, he goes quiet.

"There's a stash," he says, hoarse. "Under the tollhouse hearth. Bottles and coin and his book. I'll show you. Just don't hang me."

"You won't hang," Dray says. "You'll work for Tam Oakes at the Ford, under watch, not in a cell." He turns to you. "We go on to Grey Shaw tonight, and take that stash before anyone else does."

You walk the last miles at the boy's pace, Dray's hand on his collar.

Your mouth is dry after the long run. You haven't drunk since the afternoon.

Near ten, birches loom white in the moonlight, and a tollhouse squats beside an old toll bar.

One end of its roof is gone. Inside are cold ashes, and a broad, flat hearthstone.

Dray levers the stone up with his stone hands. Beneath it is a dug pit, lined with sacking.

Rows of small stoppered bottles, cold to the touch, each one sloshing faintly. Stolen Water.

A heavy iron coin box, locked. And a fat leather ledger, its pages close-written.

"The keys are on his belt," Pip says, very small. "Were."

Dray holds a bottle up to the moon, his face grim.

"By law this goes to the watch. But this came out of Talents like you and me. You've earned a say in it. What would you have done with it?"

"Aren't you and me the watch in this case?" you say. "So we could decide what to do with it anyways. If the chained Talents need it, I don't mind passing it to them. What's it good for?"

Dray barks a short laugh, the first since Ashlin.

"Near enough. A Reeve holds recovered goods for Lady Varrow's court. But by custom, whoever takes them has a third. That's you."

He counts the bottles by moonlight, lifting each one. Twenty-two, each a single draught, cold and dense.

"A Talent drained dry drinks one of these and fills back up in an hour, not days. Mends faster, too. It only fills a well, mind. Depth you still earn yourself. Thaumists in Carrowmouth brew it into elixirs that ease refining. A silver bar a bottle." He sets the last one down. "That's why Crake drained people. Twenty-two bars, sitting in a hole. Some of this is the captives' own Water. Drunk back, it'll speed their healing. So, aye. The bottles go to the freed captives first. I'll see to it."

He hefts the iron coin box. It's heavy, and something inside slides and clinks.

"Whatever's in here goes back to the folk his ledger robbed, as far as it reaches. Your third is a third of this coin, on top of the five bars for Crake. Once we've the key."

He flips open the ledger and tilts it to the moon, reading. His jaw tightens.

"Names. Every Talent he drained, every bottle sold, and who bought them. Oriel Vance. A thaumist in Carrowmouth. Lady Varrow will want this book badly."

Pip, sitting against the cold hearth, flinches at the name.

Dray shuts the book. He looks at you, then at the boy, then at the road back.

"We can sleep here till first light, or carry it all back to Ashlin tonight."

Outside, the half moon hangs over the birches, and the road back to Ashlin runs pale and empty.

"Let's go back and meet up with the rest tonight," you say, "just to prevent any issues with the prisoners."

Dray nods. "Aye. I'd rather have my eyes on Harl myself."

You pack the bottles in sacking, and Dray tucks the ledger inside his coat. You take the coin box.

Pip walks between you, quiet, his hands empty, his too-big coat flapping.

The road climbs back up the valley under the half moon, twelve long miles of it.

The boy's pace is slow. The hours stretch. The moon swings west over the hedges.

Your mouth goes dry, your belly hollow, your legs heavy with the miles.

Somewhere past midnight, the night chill on your skin turns clammy.

A cold sweat breaks out on your arms and neck, and dries to a fine grey salt. Your penned Water, pushing at your choked Locks, is finding its way out where it can.

It is past two by the time Ashlin's lane opens before you. The farm is dark but for one lamp.

Col rises from the barn door with his spear. "Reeve. All quiet. Harl's sulking."

In the barn, the Loddons sleep back to back in the straw, Bet curled beside them.

Rob Reed dozes against a post, his arm bound tight and clean.

Crake lies under a sheet by the wall, his ring of keys still at his belt.

Dray unhooks the keys and tries them one by one in the coin box. The fourth turns.

He tips it out on the straw by lamplight: silver bars, eleven of them, and a heap of silver pieces. He counts the pieces into stacks. Fifty.

"Eleven bars and fifty pieces," he says. "Crake's year of selling folk's Water."

He slides four bars out of the heap and holds them out to you.

"Your third. Four bars, near enough to the coin."

You take the four bars. They're cold and heavy in your palm.

"Thank you," you say.

"Don't thank me. You earned every one of them, and Crake's five besides."

He sweeps the rest back into the box, locks it, and hangs the keys at his own belt.

"The watch pays the five bars once they've seen him. That'll be some days yet. We march at first light. The Pells carry Crake; the rest of us walk the prisoners home."

He digs in his pack and sets a waterskin and a heel of bread on the straw beside you.

"There's the well in the yard too, if you'd rather. Then sleep. You've run two dozen miles."

He settles against the barn door, spear across his knees, to take the next watch himself.

The lamp gutters low. Around you the Loddons breathe slow and even in the straw.

Under your skin, your penned Water presses at the shut Locks, a dull ache at your throat and palms.

You lie down in the straw, wrapped in Anni's cloak, the bars in your purse, and sleep.

You sleep black and deep. Your sleep stays empty of dreams; even the stair stays away.

Near dawn, something stirs you: a loosening in the soles of your feet.

Penned Water rushes through them like a held breath let go, and the ache there eases.

Then your palms open, one and then the other. The same rush. The same relief.

Last of all, your throat. The dull ache you have carried since dusk drains away.

You sink back under. In that last hour of sleep the dream returns.

The black stair, going down. The call from below, louder than it has ever been.

You wake to grey light in the barn doorway. The Loddons are stirring, and the Pells are lifting the hurdle.

Your Water is yours again. You can feel it, deep and full. But it moves stiff and slow.

Your sense of the damp in the straw comes faint and slow, like a limb gone to sleep.

The needle dart still hangs in the side of your neck, a small cold sting when you turn your head.

Your mouth is parched, your tongue thick, your stomach a hollow knot.

Outside, Dray is already on his feet, with Harl roped between Col and Rob.

"Morning," he says. "We march in a quarter hour. Half a day to the Ford, at the prisoners' pace."

You reach up and draw the needle dart out of your neck.

It slides free: a finger-long iron needle, its grooved tip dark with dried venom.

A bead of blood, a small sting, and the prick begins to close.

The column moves out. The Pells carry the hurdle. Harl walks roped between Col and Rob, and Pip keeps at Dray's side.

The Loddons walk behind in a roped line, Bet beside Kit, her hand on his arm.

You fall in with them, and as you walk, you begin to cycle your Water.

The first loops grate through your Locks like a rusted hinge, stiff and slow.

You keep on, loop after loop, in time with your stride, as easy as breathing.

Each loop runs a little smoother than the last. The Ford road climbs between greening hedges.

By mid-morning the grating is gone. Your Locks run free.

Undertow answers you full again, and the world's water comes back clear around you: the dew in the hedges, the ditch beside the road, the sweat on the Pells' backs.

Your body knows its own lack now too. Your mouth is parched, your belly a hollow knot.

Dray drops back beside you with the watch's pack open: bread and cheese, and a skin of small beer.

"You've not eaten since yesterday. Here. There's plenty."

You take the bread and cheese and eat as you walk, and drink deep from the skin of small beer.

The hollow in your belly fills. The dry rasp leaves your throat.

"Anything else you need from me, Dray? Otherwise, I'm back to refining."

"Nothing on the road," he says. "The watch holds the prisoners. Go on, refine."

"At the Ford I'll write the whole fight down for the Carrowmouth watch. I'll want your mark on it."

"And Lady Varrow's man, Sir Edric Hale, comes within days to see the crag. I'd like you at the Ford when he does, to tell him of the crag and the pull yourself."

You drop a few paces back into the quiet, and turn Undertow inward, to the bones of your skull.

Your mind holds the tide; your feet only walk. It goes easier than you feared.

A slow, deep pressure builds behind your brow and jaw, like a storm that won't break.

The headache stays dull and never splits. You keep the tide steady, mile after mile.

By noon the work is well along, the bone of your skull dense and cool under the ache.

A little after noon the column comes down the last hill, and Wendle Ford lies below.

Folk come out of doorways to stare at the hurdle with its sheeted shape, and at the roped prisoners.

Somebody shouts for the Reeve. Children run alongside. Hob's hammer stops.

Dray halts the column in front of his house and turns to you.

"Inside, then. Ink and paper, and you tell it while I write."

You follow Dray into his house. He lays out ink and paper on the kitchen table.

He asks you each step of the fight in turn, and writes it down plain, for near an hour.

The lane, the loft, the keys, the bolts. The charge, the dart, the pull, the spear. The venom.

He reads it all back aloud. It is true, every line. You set your mark to it.

Below your feet, the prisoners sit in his cellar, a watchman at the hatch.

Then you go back to the watch cottage, and to your routine.

That afternoon your skull refines whole. The dull headache lingers until you sleep.

After, your thoughts feel steadier, and the tide comes easier to hold.

On day eighteen you turn the tide on your hips. It goes hard.

The marrow ache is deep and grinding, and it takes into the next morning to finish.

For a day you limp between the cottage and the Lantern, slow as an old woman.

But by the morning of day nineteen, both hips are dense and cool and done.

On day nineteen you take the long bones of your arms, then your shins. They go sweetly.

Loop by loop, bone by bone. By nightfall all four are refined, with barely an ache.

Only your thighs are left, the two biggest bones in your body.

Between times you eat at the Lantern: salt, milk, bone broth. You sleep hot each night.

Each night, in the last hour of sleep, the black stair comes. The call from below grows louder.

On the morning of day twenty, hooves clatter on the ford road.

A tall, grey-bearded man in a good riding cloak dismounts outside the Reeve's house.

He lays a bare hand flat on the doorpost stone and stands a moment, eyes half shut.

Dray comes out to meet him, bows, and then looks up the lane toward the watch cottage.

"Nala!" he calls. "Sir Edric Hale is here, from Lady Varrow. He's asking for you."

You come down the lane to the Reeve's door and give the knight a crisp guard's salute.

"Sir."

Sir Edric's grey brows lift a fraction, as if he has never seen a salute quite like it.

He answers with a short bow from the neck, the way a knight greets a sworn equal.

His eyes rest on your face a long moment, then drop, briefly, to the slow rise of your breath.

"Nala," he says, low and unhurried, the vowels clipped. "Sir Edric Hale, of House Varrow."

"I heard on the road in that Oswy Crake is dead, and by your hand. In Lady Varrow's name, I thank you. He's been a blight on that road a long year."

He glances south, across the valley, toward the white heads of the Whitecombs.

"The stone here hums. Faintly. Even the Reeve's doorpost leans toward those peaks."

"Dray writes that you found a crag split open up there, and something pulling under it. I'd have you lead me up to it tomorrow, and tell me of the pull, there, on the spot."

"Gladly, Sir."

Sir Edric inclines his head. "First light, then, at the ford. I'll lodge at the Lantern. We'll ride to Callow Beck and leave my horse where the tracks begin."

You go back to your routine for the rest of the day: Lantern broth, the cottage, the tide.

You turn it on your thighs, the last and largest bones in your body.

They go smoothly, steadily, loop after loop, a deep warm ache and no worse.

By midnight both are dense and cool. Every bone in your body is refined.

You lie in the dark and feel it: a frame like oak, like iron, from skull to heel.

In the last hour of sleep, the black stair comes. The call from below is louder still.

At first light Sir Edric waits at the ford on a tall grey horse, his cloak beaded with mist.

You walk at his stirrup two hours south across the valley to Callow Beck. He tethers the grey.

Together you climb the goat tracks into the Whitecombs. He climbs steadily for his years, but slower than you.

He won't be hurried on rock; he tests each hold. You wait for him at each bend.

Near the top, the cold salt air comes down to meet you, out of nowhere, from the stone.

The split crag stands as you left it: rock torn open, a dark crack breathing sea-cold air.

And under your feet, under the whole mountain, the vast slow pull, deep as the sea itself.

Sir Edric pulls off his glove and lays his bare hand flat on the split stone, eyes half shut.

He stands a long moment, very still. His jaw tightens.

"Tell me of the pull," he says, without opening his eyes. "What you feel, here, now."

You stand still beside him and let yourself feel it, and tell him plainly.

"A slow, vast pull, far below. Like a tide under the mountain, drawing on my well. It's stronger than when I first stood here, on day four. And it comes in slow swells, rising and easing."

You feel the rest as you say it. "And now, with my bones refined, I feel it in my frame too. A deep tug, down through my heels."

Sir Edric listens without a word, his hand still flat on the stone, his eyes half shut.

When you're done he takes his hand away, slowly, and flexes the fingers as if they're cold.

"Then we feel the same thing, from two sides," he says. "The mountain's root is bowed upward under us. Like a dam with a flood behind it. This crack runs down past where I can follow. Deeper than any fault I've touched. And there are new cracks, hair-fine, running east and west along the range from here."

He pulls his glove back on. His face is calm, but he takes a long moment before speaking again.

"I ride for Varrow Keep at dawn, to tell Lady Varrow this myself. In her name, I'd ask you to come to the Keep, and tell her what you told me."

"I can do that."

Something in Sir Edric's face eases. "Good. We leave the Ford at dawn."

You climb down off the Whitecombs together, and the pull fades behind your heels with every step.

That night at the Lantern you eat broth and bread, and buy a road bundle for the morning.

In the last hour of sleep, the black stair comes. The call is louder again.

At dawn Sir Edric waits at the ford on his grey. You take the road east, up the valley.

He rides at a steady walk. You keep pace beside his stirrup easily, past scattered farms.

Somewhere past noon he speaks. "A word about Lady Varrow, before you meet her. She's cold of manner. Don't take it to heart. She never forgets a debt."

The valley narrows. Toward evening an old grey tower-house rises on a crag at its east end.

Varrow Keep. Its roofs are patched, its walls stained dark with old rain.

You pass under the gate as the light goes, and behind you the bar thuds down for the night.

A steward takes you in hand: a hot supper in the hall's corner, a small cold room, a straw bed.

"The Lady receives guests in the morning," he says. "Not after dark."

In the last hour of sleep, the black stair comes again. Louder.

In the morning, after bread and small beer, the steward comes for you.

He leads you up a winding stair to a long, draughty hall, a fire smoking at one end.

Sir Edric stands beside a high-backed chair. In it sits a woman in grey, very upright, watching you.

You bow, and wait to be spoken to.

The woman in the chair watches you a long moment without a word.

She is lean, and tall even seated, in a fine grey gown worn thin at the cuffs.

Her dark hair is pinned tight, with one streak of white in it. Her pale eyes go to your hands, then your face.

The air around the chair is cold. A thin skin of frost lies on its carved arms.

"Nala. I am Imre Varrow."

Her name, and straight on. Her voice is low and even.

"Oswy Crake robbed my road for a year, and took three Talented off it. That road was mine to keep. You kept it for me. I don't forget that."

She lifts a hand. A servant steps forward with a silver bar on a square of grey cloth.

"From the House. Take it."

"Thank you, Lady Varrow." You take the silver bar from the grey cloth.

It is cold from the room, and heavy. The servant steps back.

Lady Varrow gives one short nod, as if a matter is closed.

"Now. The crag. Sir Edric has told me what the stone told him. Tell me what you felt."

You tell it again, as you told Sir Edric on the mountain.

The slow, vast pull far below, like a tide under the mountain, drawing on your well. Stronger now than on day four, and coming in slow swells. A tug down through your heels.

Lady Varrow listens without a word, her pale eyes on your face.

Now and then her glance goes to Sir Edric. Each time, he gives the smallest nod.

When you finish, the hall is quiet but for the fire. The frost on the chair arms has thickened.

"Reeve Dray writes that you came to the Ford with no papers. Where do you come from, Nala?"

"I'm not quite sure. I can't remember anything past a few weeks ago."

Lady Varrow's pale eyes stay on your face, then drop to your hands, then rise again.

She goes very still, and takes the answer seriously.

"I have heard of that. Folk the Sea has touched, who lose what came before."

Sir Edric shifts his weight beside the chair, but stays silent.

"Then tell me what you do remember. Where did you first wake? And who saw you first?"

"Garth Marsh was the first I met. I think I've got the name right. Names seem to slip from me. I don't really remember anything before that. His daughter had the rot, and I used my Talent to pull it out of her."

At Garth's name, something shifts in Lady Varrow's face.

"Garth Marsh. Yes. I heard of that, weeks ago. A stranger who drew greymaw rot from his girl. Garth sent to me first, begging a healer. I had no Talent to spare him. So the girl's life is one I owe you as well."

Under her hands, the frost on the chair arms creeps thicker, white and crisp.

She looks at you a long moment, and lets whatever she is thinking lie unsaid.

"A Talent that draws rot is worth more to this valley than any sword. We have no healer like it."

She turns her head. "Edric. The glass."

Sir Edric crosses to a chest by the wall and brings back a disc of dark glass in a silver ring.

Lady Varrow holds it up, between her eye and you.

"This came from the Spires. It shows a Talent for what it is. Will you let me look at you?"

"Yes. I'd appreciate it if you'd tell me what you see."

Lady Varrow raises the dark disc to her eye and looks at you through it, a long, unblinking moment.

Faint light moves in the glass, pale as moonlight on water. From your side you can't make it out.

She reads aloud, slowly. "Undertow. Major. Surface."

She lowers the glass a finger's breadth, then lifts it again, as if to be sure.

"It shows the well too, as a glow. Yours burns sea-green, bright as a lamp. It shines like a Second Depth's well."

Sir Edric draws a slow breath beside her chair.

Lady Varrow sets the glass down on her knee. The frost on the chair arms gleams.

"Four Talented are sworn to this house. Not one of them is Major. Not one shines half so bright in that glass."

She is silent a moment, her pale eyes level on yours.

"Whatever is under the Whitecombs, my valley has to be held against it. Swear to House Varrow. A silver bar a month, and a room here at the Keep. And a knight's rank. Stand with us when it comes."

"I will not swear. But I will help, if I can. I've refined my skin, muscles and bones since meeting Garth, and I still have room to grow. But I'm lacking in guidance. I have a feeling I'll need all the strength I can get to face whatever lies under that mountain. If you can help me grow my strength, I will stay to face it."

Lady Varrow's face stays exactly as it was. Only the frost on the chair arms glints.

Then her pale eyes narrow, just slightly.

"Skin, muscle and bone. Since Garth Marsh. That is three weeks. Most Talented take years over that."

"Then no oath. A lesser bond. You stay a free blade. The Keep calls on you at need. A silver bar for every summons you answer. And in return, the guidance you ask for. Mine, and this house's."

"I have Descended twice. Refine your organs before you go down. Few ever have the time, or the Water. And spar with my Talented here. You have fought one Talent, and nearly died of it. Learn to fight them well."

She holds your eyes, level and cold.

"Will you take that bond?"

"Acceptable. Is there a place here I could stay while I focus on refining?"

Lady Varrow's face stays still. She gives one short nod.

"Sir Edric. Witness it."

She holds out a lean hand. Frost glitters along her knuckles.

You clasp it. The cold bites your fingers, then fades.

"Witnessed," Sir Edric says. "Nala, free blade of Varrow Keep."

"It goes in the rolls tonight," Lady Varrow says. "A room, yes. The top of the old north stair. Bare, but quiet. You'll have that stair to yourself. You eat in the hall with my Talented, at the second table. The well is in the lower court. Deep and cold, from the crag's own spring."

"Counsel, since you asked for it. Every Tribulation differs. Mine came as cold that tried to stop my heart. Make yours strong before you go down."

"My Talented train in the lower court at first light. Will you start with them tomorrow, or refine first?"
