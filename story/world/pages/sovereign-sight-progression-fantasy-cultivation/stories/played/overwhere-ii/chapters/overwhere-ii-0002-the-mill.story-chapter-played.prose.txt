"Just a Talented," you say. "I hadn't tried to use my Talent quite like that before. I'm glad it worked."

Garth stares at you. One eyebrow climbs, and his mouth twists, as if to say: just, is it.

But he only nods, slowly, one hand still on Wren's hair.

"Anyone else get bitten you know of?" you ask. "I wouldn't mind some more practice with my new technique."

Garth gives a short, rough laugh. "Practice." He shakes his head. "Aye. There's others. Two of my own ewes, out in the fold. Greymaw raked them through the rails. Gone lame and sour."

"And at the Ford, the miller's girl, Tansy Horne. Bitten ten days back. She's bad, they say. Worse than Wren was. Horne's rich as a lord, and he's spent silver on her for naught."

"Goody Brannoc at the Ford tends all the rot-sick. She'd know who else."

Wren tugs at your sleeve. "Will you come back? Will you tell me what Talent you've got?"

Garth gets up, stiff on his bad leg, and crosses to a wooden chest under the loft ladder. He kneels and lifts the lid slowly, as if it is heavier than it is.

He takes out a pair of worn leather boots and a folded brown wool cloak, and holds them for a moment.

"These were my Anni's. Wren's mam. She's three winters gone." His voice is steady and quiet. "She'd not have you walk the thaw barefoot, after this. Nor would I."

He holds them out to you, the boots on top of the cloak.

"Thank you. I'll gladly accept." You take the boots and the cloak from him carefully, with both hands.

Garth nods once and looks away, at the fire.

You sit on the stool and pull the boots on. They are a little large, but with the laces pulled tight they fit. The cloak is heavy, soft brown wool, and it smells faintly of cedar from the chest.

"How far to the Ford?" you ask. "Ten days is a long time. I'd hate to be too late for her."

Garth frowns. "Ten days since she was bit, lass. Not ten days she's got. There's no knowing how long she's got."

"The Ford's three mile down the lane and along the river. Hour and a half, at my pace."

"Could you take me there now?" you ask. "I suspect I'll need a guide before they let me see her. I hate to ask you to leave your girl. Or you could just introduce me to Goody Brannoc."

Garth looks at Wren, and his jaw sets. She is still flushed, but her eyes are bright and clear.

"Go, Da," Wren says at once. "I'm better. Burr can mind me. Go on."

Garth breathes out slowly. "Goody brought Wren into the world. She'll hear me out, at least. And Horne will let in anyone Goody says." He nods. "Aye. I'll take you."

He reaches for his crook and his sheepskin coat by the door.

You stand. As you do, your tide brushes the iron pot by the bed, and you stop.

The grey salt in it is not dead. It feels sour and slow, faintly crawling, like a thing that is seeking.

Its faint reach leans toward the nearest warm life. Toward Wren, lying an arm's length away.

"Wait," you say. "We need to dispose of the rot first, but I'm not certain how. Maybe put an iron lid on it and bring it with us? Goody might know what to do with it. Otherwise, we'll have to test things carefully."

Garth stops in the doorway with his coat half on. He looks at the pot, then at Wren, and his face hardens.

"Goody burns every poultice that's touched rot. Hot fire, straight off. Never uses one twice."

He takes the pot's iron lid down from its hook by the hearth and hands it to you.

You set the lid on the pot and press it down, your sense still reaching.

The salt feels just the same. The lid is plain iron, like the pot, and your tide feels the salt right through it. The salt's slow, sour reach passes through the lid as if it weren't there.

It is faint. But it is still leaning toward Wren, patient as damp creeping through a wall.

Garth glances at the peat fire in the hearth, burning low and red. Then up at the nails over the door.

"Well?" he says. "Your call, lass. You can feel it; I can't."

"Hmm. I don't think the iron is stopping it. Let's try burning. If that doesn't do it, I'll pull it back into the pot, and we'll bring it with us."

Garth grunts. He drags a basket of dry gorse and split ash out from beside the hearth, heaps it on the peat, and works the bellows until the fire roars up yellow and hot.

You lift the lid and tip the grey salt into the heart of the flames.

It spits and crackles like wet fat. A stink of rotten brine rolls through the room. Wren pulls the blanket up over her nose, and Burr sneezes and backs away under the table.

You keep your tide on it. The sour, crawling reach shrinks. It flickers. Then it goes out.

When the flames die back, all that's left on the peat is a smear of clean white ash.

You feel the pot, the hearth, the whole room. There's nothing sour anywhere now but the smell.

Garth props the door open to let the stink out, kisses the top of Wren's head, and takes up his crook.

You set out down the lane together. Behind you, Burr whines at the door by Wren's bed.

The valley opens up as you walk. Bare brown fields, sheep on the slopes, smoke rising from farms. Garth limps along steadily and says little. You slow your stride to match his, warm inside the cloak.

Across the river, the white mountains stand up into the cloud.

"Whitecombs," Garth says, with a nod at them.

After an hour and more, the lane meets the river and runs along beside it to a village. Stone houses. A ford, with a stone footbridge beside it. At the east end, a mill wheel turning.

Garth leads you past the mill to a small, crooked cottage by the mill race, with bunches of herbs hung drying under the eaves.

He knocks. "Goody. It's Garth Marsh. I've brought someone."

The door opens on a tiny, bent old woman with sharp black eyes, wiping her hands on a rag.

Her nose wrinkles, as if at a smell. Then her eyes go to your face, and she goes very still.

The rag falls from her hands. The colour drains out of her lined face.

"Liss?" she whispers. "Liss Aske?"

"Who? I'm Nala, just a Talented passing through. Garth here asked if I could help his Wren—"

The old woman's eyes never leave your face. Her hand dives into her apron and comes out with an iron nail.

She is quick. Before you can step back, she presses the nail flat against the inside of your wrist.

It is cold and dead and numbing on your skin, like the hooks and the nails over Garth's door. That's all.

Goody stares at your wrist for a long moment. The skin is unmarked. She lets out a shaking breath.

"Cold iron," she mutters. "Doesn't burn you. Then you're no drowned thing come back."

"Goody," Garth says, low. "What's got into you?"

"Liss Aske." Goody is still staring at your face. "My dearest friend, when I was a girl. Red-haired, just like this. Drowned in Hollow Tarn, fifty-one winters gone. Twenty-five, she was."

"And here's her face at my door, not a day older." Her voice cracks on it.

Garth looks at you sidelong. "My mam told me of her. The Aske girl the tarn took."

Goody's nose wrinkles again. She leans in close and sniffs at you like a hound.

"And you smell of the Sea, girl. Clean and deep. I've never smelled that on a living soul."

Garth clears his throat. "Goody. She drew the rot out of Wren. This morning. Out of her leg. Pulled it out like a splinter, into salt on her hand. We burned it after. Wren's leg is clean."

Goody's sharp black eyes snap from Garth to your hands, and she goes stiller than before.

"Clean," she repeats. "Rot. Drawn out." Her mouth works. "I've buried four this winter."

Without taking her eyes off you, she grabs a shawl from the peg behind the door.

"Horne's girl, Tansy. It's past her elbow. She's three days left in her, if that. Whoever you are, Liss's face or no, will you come to the mill now?"

"Yes, if you'll take me."

Goody is already out the door, her shawl round her shoulders, moving fast for someone so bent. You and Garth follow.

The mill is a stone's throw along the race. It is a big stone building with a house joined on, hung with slate, the finest thing you've seen in this valley. The great wheel turns in the race and groans as it goes.

Goody hammers on the house door. It opens on a heavy, grey, hard-faced man who fills the doorway.

"Sedge," Goody says. "This is Nala. She's to see Tansy."

His eyes go over you. The loose shirt under a dead woman's cloak. The boots too big for your feet.

"Talented," he says flatly. "A stranger. I've had my fill of promises."

"She drew the rot out of Garth's Wren this morning," Goody snaps. "I'll vouch for her. Move."

Horne looks past you at Garth. Garth nods once. Horne's jaw works, and then he steps aside.

Up a stair is a warm room. A girl of fourteen or so lies propped on pillows, grey-faced and sweating. A worn woman sits by the bed holding her good hand, and she doesn't look up when you come in.

Tansy's right arm lies on the blanket, bandaged. Black veins crawl out above the wrappings, past her elbow, up toward her shoulder. The room reeks of a bitter poultice.

You reach out with your tide, and your breath catches.

This is nothing like Wren's small knot. It is a sour tangle, soaked all through the arm, threaded deep into the girl's own life, strand around strand, far deeper than Wren's ever went.

And there is so much of it. It would heap up far past what one palm could hold.

Behind you, Horne's voice is low and hard. "If you hurt her, Talented, I'll see you hang for it."

The woman by the bed looks up at last. Her eyes are red and raw.

"Please," she whispers. "Please."

"This will be much harder than Wren," you say. "The rot is intertwined with her life force. I'm amazed she has lasted this long. You have a strong daughter."

Tansy's mother presses her lips together hard and nods. Tears run down her face.

"I need a container to hold the rot, and a fire ready to burn it. I'm starting now."

Goody is already moving. She comes back with a wide earthenware basin and sets it by the bed.

"Kitchen range is lit below," Horne says roughly. "Hot as a forge. I'll stoke it."

You sit on the edge of the bed, lay your palm on Tansy's forearm above the bandage, and shut your eyes.

You find the tide and let the pull reach in, narrowed as fine as you can make it.

The tangle is everywhere. Sour threads, wound through and through the girl's own warm life.

You take the nearest thread and ease it free. Slowly. It is like the knots of computer cords you untied so many times back home, one loop at a time.

It comes. Then the next one comes. A few grains of grey salt prickle up under your palm.

Behind you, Goody draws a sharp breath. The air in the room turns cold, cold enough to raise gooseflesh, and it smells of the sea.

You work on, thread by thread, the pull straining in your grip like a dog on a lead.

Further in, the threads get finer. They cross and double back on themselves, and you can't tell where one ends and the next begins.

You tug at one that feels sour. It isn't only sour. Warm life comes with it, all in a rush.

The pull seizes on that warmth, hungry, and drinks before you can clamp it back.

Something warm and foreign pours up your arm and into your own well. The girl's own life.

You wrench the pull shut. Your palm comes away with only a pinch of grey salt on it.

Tansy gives a thin gasp. You open your eyes. Her face has gone from grey to ashen, and her lips are a pale blue. Where your fingers still brush her wrist, her skin is cold. She is shivering hard, and her eyes roll up and flutter shut.

Her mother cries out and clutches at her. Your tide reaches across to the arm and finds the black tangle nearly whole.

A big hand closes on your collar. It hauls you up off the bed and back against the wall.

"What did you do to her?" Horne roars, his face an inch from yours. "What did you do?"

"Quiet!"

The word cracks through the room like a slammed door.

Horne blinks, startled into silence, his fist still knotted in your collar.

You don't look at him. Pinned to the wall, you shut your eyes and reach for the bed.

The girl's stolen warmth is still there in your well. It sits apart from your own cold flow, like a pocket.

You turn the tide around. Not the pull this time. The push, as gentle as you can make it.

The warm pocket slides out of you, across the room, and back into Tansy, easy as water running downhill.

You keep the push going a breath longer and let a thin stream of your own cold flow follow it. You feel it settle into her like a cool hand on a fevered brow.

In the bed, Tansy draws a long, shuddering breath.

You open your eyes. The blue is fading from her lips. Colour creeps back into her face. She is still grey and sick, but no worse than when you came in.

Under the tide, you feel your own flow wrap around the black tangle in her arm. The rot goes still and sluggish, held, the way cold honey stops running.

Horne's grip loosens. He stares from his daughter to you, breathing hard.

Tansy's eyelids flutter open. "Mam?" she whispers. "I'm cold. I was so cold."

Her mother sobs and gathers her in, rocking her.

"Sedge Horne," Goody says, very quiet and very hard. "Let go of her."

Horne lets go. He steps back and looks at his own hand as if it belonged to someone else.

You straighten your collar. The rot in Tansy's arm is held, but nearly all of it is still there.

Horne clears his throat. His voice comes out hoarse. "Can you get it out of her? The rest of it?"

"I'm making progress, but this will take time," you say, and sit back down on the edge of the bed.

Horne doesn't answer. He pulls a stool over to the door and sits, his hands clenched between his knees.

You lay your palm on Tansy's arm again, shut your eyes, and find the tide.

This time you don't fight it. You let it breathe the way it wants to, in and out, like surf.

Out: a thin wash of your own cold flow into the girl, steadying her, filling her up.

In: the pull, narrow, lifting one sour thread while her life is full and easy to tell apart.

Out again, gentle. In again, one thread. Out. In.

The rhythm fits the tide like a hand in a glove. With every wave, the fine work gets easier.

The crossed threads loosen as you work. Where you can't tell one from the next, you wait a wave, and then you can. The pull takes only the sour now. Whenever a little warmth starts to follow it, the next push carries it straight back.

Grey salt builds under your palm, grain by grain. Each time your hand is full, you open your eyes, tip it into Goody's basin, and go on.

The room stays cold enough for gooseflesh. Goody stands by the window, breathing in the smell of the sea, silent. The sun moves across the floorboards. Tansy sleeps, her breath slow and even.

The black veins ebb, a finger's width at a time. Back from high on her arm. Past her elbow.

Your head is clear and cool. But deep in the work, something in the tide is straining. Stretching.

The last threads lie deep in the bite under the bandage, finer than hair. You reach for them.

Something gives, like a door swinging open inside you.

The tide widens.

All at once you can feel that you could draw from anything in your reach. Not only what you touch.

You lift your palm an inch off the girl's skin. The pull goes on, reaching across the gap.

The last sour threads slide up out of the bite and gather in a grey crust on your raised palm.

Then there is nothing sour left in Tansy's arm. Only her own warm life, whole and clean.

You open your eyes. The basin holds a double handful of grey, stinking salt. Tansy's arm lies pale on the blanket. The black veins are gone, and the bite is an ordinary wound.

Goody unwinds the bandage and bends to sniff the bite. Her face crumples.

"Clean," she whispers. "Clean."

Tansy's mother puts her face in her hands and weeps without a sound.

Horne takes the basin in both hands and carries it downstairs. Below, the kitchen range roars and spits.

Your stomach growls, loud in the quiet room. You are suddenly ravenous, as if you'd done a long day's work.

Horne comes back up the stairs and stops in the doorway, his big hands empty, his eyes wet.

"Name your price," he says hoarsely. "A silver bar. More. Anything I have. It's yours."
