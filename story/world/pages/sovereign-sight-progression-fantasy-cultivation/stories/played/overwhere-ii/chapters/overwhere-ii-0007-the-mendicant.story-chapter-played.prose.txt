"What can you tell me about Crake?" you ask. "What are his crimes? How does he pick his victims? What's known about his abilities? Does he work alone?"

As you ask, you reach for Undertow. The tide rises from your refined legs, over your hips, into the thin skin of your belly.

"I know him from the watch notices in Carrowmouth," Dray says. "Oswy Crake is a Mendicant. He steals Talents' Water. He drinks it at a touch, and sells it bottled in Carrowmouth."

The tide reaches the raw scrape over your ribs. It can't refine the skin there until it has healed it, and the healing stings, and then the refining stings again on top of it. The work slows to a crawl. You keep your face still and listen.

"Three Talented have gone missing on the Carrowmouth road this past year," Dray goes on. "He picks the ones who travel alone and wouldn't be missed. And he doesn't work alone. He rides with four men. They carry saltsteel manacles, and darts that choke a Talent's Locks."

The tide climbs over your chest to your collarbones. You open your mouth to ask where he camps.

"Where does he—"

At your throat, where a thread ends, a cold jolt shoots through you.

The tide lurches out of your grip. Water spills, and your well drops near dry.

Across your trunk the refining slips away undone, like a wave running back down the sand.

Your skin prickles hot and raw from hip to jaw. Over your ribs the scrape has closed, healed smooth, but the deep bruise beneath it throbs.

You sit very still, breathing, while the Sea begins to fill your well again. Your mind feels scraped thin, the way it did after two limbs. You can't refine any more tonight.

"You've gone white," Dray says. "Are you all right?"

He eyes you a long moment. Then he answers the question you didn't finish.

"Where he camps, I don't know. Only that the drover was robbed near Grey Shaw. A Talent alone on that road is what he hunts. Just so you know what you'd be walking into."

"I'm fine," you say. "Just working on refining while we can. Is he Talented himself? If so, what depth?"

Dray grunts. His look says he doesn't quite believe the "fine," but he lets it go.

"He is. The Carrowmouth notices name his Talent Siphon. First Depth, same as me. And they carry one warning in big letters: never let Crake lay a hand on bare skin. His touch is what drinks. Get a hand on you, and he pulls your Water out through it."

He leans back, and the chair creaks under him.

"A First Depth thief with four men, manacles and venom. That's a match for any lone Talent. Including you, I'd say. Maybe especially you, with a well as deep as yours."

"Yeah, I'll need to prepare a bit before I'm ready for that," you say. "Sounds like you'll get to keep me a while longer after all."

Dray's tired face eases into something close to a smile.

"Glad to hear it. The cottage is yours for as long as you like. Better you take him ready than take him soon."

You bid him good night and cross the dark green to the Drowned Lantern.

The woman behind the counter sets down mutton stew, bread and ale for a copper. You eat every scrap.

Then you walk back to the watch cottage and sleep the night through.

You wake at dawn on day five, rested and clear-headed, your mouth a little dry.

The raw prickle has gone from your skin. The bruise over your ribs has faded to a dull yellow ache.

Your mind feels whole again. Sleep has washed away the wear of last night's lost refining.

Down the ford road, the ring of Hob's hammer carries through the cold dawn air.

At the Drowned Lantern the woman behind the counter sets down hot porridge, bread and a jug of small beer for a copper. You eat and drink it all.

Fed and watered, you walk back to the watch cottage and bar the door behind you.

You sit on the bunk and breathe slow. This time you keep your mouth shut.

Undertow rises from your refined legs into your hips and belly, giving and drawing.

The tide climbs over the yellow bruise on your ribs, and the ache under it eases.

It spreads across your back and chest, slow and even. You keep your mind on it and on nothing else.

At your throat the cold jolt comes. You ride it like a wave, the way you rode it at your sole, and the tide runs on.

Up your jaw it goes, and over your face.

The skin round your eyes and lips burns worst of all. Your eyes stream. You hold.

About an hour after you began, the tide reaches the crown of your head, and stops.

The last of your skin settles over you whole: one tight, cool shell, scalp to sole.

You lie back on the bunk and sleep, deep and dreamless, for an hour and a half.

You wake to a deep, dull ache in your arms and legs, like the day after hard labour.

You haven't done anything to cause it. But you can feel it happening: your Water seeping down into your muscles, on its own.

You go back to the Lantern for a second breakfast and drink a full jug down to the bottom. Then you walk back to the cottage.

You sit on the bunk and call up what you know of anatomy from Earth, muscle by muscle: where each one starts, where it ends, what it pulls.

You start small: the muscles of your left hand, one at a time, origin to insertion. The short muscle at the base of your thumb comes first.

Undertow sinks into it, and it cramps hard. For a minute it knots like a fist inside your palm. Then it loosens.

When it lets go, it feels denser than before, and stronger, as if it has been packed tight.

The next goes the same way, and the next. Held to one muscle at a time, the tide runs cleaner and faster than it ever did over skin, filling each shape you name and stopping at its edges.

Hand, forearm, upper arm. Then the right arm. By evening both arms are done.

You eat twice what you used to, and you are thirsty all day long.

On day six a knock comes at the cottage door. It is Hob's boy, come to say your spear is ready at the forge.

You work on through days six and seven: legs, hips, back, belly, chest, neck and jaw.

Each muscle cramps, lets go, and settles denser and stronger, and the ache moves on to the next.

You eat at the Lantern morning and night, sleep long, and keep yourself rested.

Your heart you can't touch. The tide slides off it, as if it is not yet its turn.

By the evening of day eight, every muscle and tendon you can name is refined whole.

You move around the cottage. Your body feels like a drawn bow.

You eat, drink, and go to bed early, and sleep takes you at once.

In the night you dream of Hollow Tarn, black and still under a starless sky.

A stair of black water goes down into the tarn, step after step, out of sight.

The vast, cold dark under your well presses up toward you, patient, and waiting.

You wake in the dark of the cottage with your heart pounding, and the pull still in your chest.

You sleep again till dawn. At the Lantern you eat breakfast for a copper.

At the smithy you step to Hob's right side, so he can hear you over the forge.

He takes the spear down from its pegs: a seven-foot ash shaft, and a dark, leaf-shaped head. A crossbar sits just below the head. When he lays it across your hands, the weight sits forward, heavier at the business end than the watch spear ever was.

"Balanced for bracing against a charge," Hob says. "Cold iron bites the Sea-twisted where plain iron cuts. Keep it oiled. That's the other half bar, and not a copper more."

You pay him.

In your inward sense the spear's head sits at the end of the shaft as a dead, numb patch. Undertow won't touch the iron. But the tide is still there in your arms, ready to drive them behind it.

You cross the green to the shrine. Inside, cool and dim, Anselm is sweeping by the altar vessel.

You tell him your skin is refined whole, and every muscle too, in four days.

Anselm sets the broom down very slowly.

"Four days," he says. "My skin alone took me three years. Refining goes outside in: skin, then muscle, then bone, then organs, and last blood. Most Talented Descend once skin and muscle are whole, and refine bone after. But each part refined before a Descent makes its Tribulation easier to bear. I don't know any Keeper practice for bone. I've only heard it aches deep, and goes slow. The canticles say Descent first calls in a dream of water going down, once you are ready."

He looks at you closely, his tired eyes sharp.

"Has it called you yet?"

"I've heard the call a few times," you say. "I've ignored it for now. I'll answer when I'm ready and not before."

Anselm lets out a long breath, and his shoulders drop.

"Good. The canticles praise the one who answers only when ready. The call doesn't compel. It comes again, stronger, until it's answered. One answers where the dream shows, or in still, quiet water. Don't Descend in haste, I beg you. A botched Descent can maim, or kill. And tell me before you go, so someone knows where you went. The senior Keeper in Carrowmouth could guide you through it, if you wait."

You go back to the watch cottage and begin on your bones, smallest to largest.

The three tiny bones of each ear go first, in minutes. Afterward, every sound rings a shade clearer.

Then the bones of your fingers and toes, many in a day, each one drinking the tide slowly.

Each refined bone aches deep in the marrow, like a fever, and then goes heavy and still. It feels like stone under water. You weigh a little more each day.

You crave salt, milk and bone broth. The woman behind the Lantern's counter starts keeping a pot of broth on for you.

You run hot at night. Each night the dream of the tarn's black stair comes again, stronger.

On day eleven, market day, the carrier's cart comes up the valley road. Anselm finds you on the green.

"The Keepers have answered," he says. "Senior Keeper Maud Ashby will come up once the road dries. They expect that by the end of the month. They bid me keep you near, and keep you from Descending till she comes."

Among the stalls your sharpened ears pick out a boy's voice, hawking whittled whistles.

He is thin and freckled, about thirteen, in a coat too big for him.

He isn't watching the buyers. He is watching you.

You turn and look straight at him, and he sees you see him.

In a blink he is gone into the market crowd, whistles and all.

A moment later you glimpse the too-big coat at the far end of the green, heading down the valley road.

You keep to your routine. Broth and bread at the Lantern, then hours on the bunk in the watch cottage, refining bone.

Your hands and feet finish first. Then your ribs, one at a time, each one aching like a fever before it goes heavy and still.

Your spine is the worst. Each vertebra sends a cold shock down your arms and legs, and you sit through every one.

Every night the dream of the black stair comes back, stronger. By the second week you wake from it each night cold and salt-damp, the pull deep in your chest.

By the morning of day sixteen your hands, feet, ribs and spine are refined whole. Your skull, hips and long bones are still to do.

You are finishing your broth at the Lantern when the door bangs open.

A woman stumbles in, red-eyed and wild-haired, mud to the knees.

"Is there a healer here?" she asks the room. "They say a woman here drew rot out of a child."

The woman behind the counter glances at you before she can stop herself. The stranger sees it and comes straight over.

"Please. My boy, at Ashlin Farm, half a day down the road. He's burning with fever. He can't keep water down. Please, I've no one else."

You watch her as she talks. The tears are real enough. But her eyes keep going to the door.

"How old's the boy?" the woman behind the counter asks.

The stranger hesitates, a breath too long.

"Eight. Nine come summer."

The woman behind the counter leans close to you. "Never seen her before," she murmurs. "I know most faces in this valley."

The stranger grips your sleeve.

"Please. Will you come?"

"Come with me," you say.

You take the woman's arm and walk her out of the Lantern and across the green to the Reeve's house.

She goes white. Halfway across she tries to twist free, but your grip holds like an iron band.

Tam Oakes lets you in. Dray looks up from his table.

"I'm more certain than not that this woman is trying to lead me to the bandits," you tell him. "Whether by intent or coercion, I don't know. Could we turn this to our advantage?"

You watch her as you say it. Under your fingers her pulse leaps, and her breath stops.

Dray looks her up and down, slow. Whatever he sees settles it. He believes you.

"Crake sent you," he says.

At the name she breaks. She sags against your grip and weeps.

"Don't kill my brothers. Please. They're fools, but they're all I have."

Dray presses her, quiet and steady, and it all comes out. Her name is Bet Loddon. She has no son. Crake paid her two silver, and she is afraid of him.

"They're at Ashlin Farm," she says. "It's been empty since autumn. All of them: Crake and four men. My brothers are in the barn's hayloft, with crossbows on the yard. Big Harl's inside the farmhouse with Crake, by the hearth. And Pip watches the road. I'm to bring you up the front lane alone, before dusk. A lamp in the window means they're ready. If I'm not back by nightfall, he'll know it's gone wrong, and move on."

Dray sits back. For the first time since you met him, he looks almost eager.

"Ten bars on his head, and three Talented gone missing on that road. I want him. I know Ashlin. There's a beck gully behind the barn; you can reach its back wall unseen. So we turn his trap. You walk up the lane as bait. My watch and I come up the beck behind. Stone has no skin, so his touch can't drink through my hands. But we have to move by noon, to be there before dusk. Will you walk in as the bait?"

"Gladly," you say. "I'll bring my new spear; can't be too safe with the wolves around."

You grin, eager. The corner of Dray's mouth twitches in answer.

"Good. A spear in a lone traveller's hand won't alarm anyone on a road with wolves. My watch and I leave an hour ahead, by the fields east of the road, out of the boy's sight. When you reach the lane's end, stop and talk. Hold the crossbows' eyes on you while I close. When I'm at the barn's back wall, you'll hear a single crow's caw from the gully."

An hour later Dray and his four watchmen slip out of the Ford eastward, across the fields, and are gone behind the hedgerows.

At noon you set off down the valley road with Bet beside you, the cold-iron spear on your shoulder.

The road runs down the valley between budding hedges, past fields and empty folds.

Bet walks close, eyes down, too frightened to look left or right.

You watch the hedges and the road ahead. Both stay empty.

By mid-afternoon the Ford is long behind you, and Bet hasn't said a word in an hour.

Then, low, without looking up, she speaks.

"Will my brothers hang?"

"I don't know," you say. "But this way you'll at least have a chance to plead their case, and a bit of goodwill to spend on them. If you can get them to surrender quietly, all the better. But don't give away the trap. That would backfire and end with all of you dead."

Bet is quiet a long moment. Then she nods, and her shoulders come down a little.

"I won't give it away. I swear it." Her hands still shake. "Wil will listen to me. If it goes badly for Crake, I'll call to him."

The light goes amber, then grey. The valley road turns off into a lane between high hedges.

At the far end, a lamp burns in the window of a long, low stone farmhouse.

Ashlin Farm: the house on the left, a stone barn on the right, an open yard of trodden mud between them.

The barn's hayloft door stands half open, black, looking straight down the lane.

Behind the barn the gully stays silent. The crow's caw hasn't come yet.

You walk the lane with Bet a step behind, the cold-iron spear easy on your shoulder.

You step out past the hedges' end into the open yard, to stop and talk as Dray asked.

Your refined ears catch it at once: the creak of a crossbow stock settling in the loft.

And a second sound: a soft jangle of keys, from the dark doorway under the loft, inside the barn.

Bet said Crake would wait by the farmhouse hearth. But someone is in the barn, right below the loft.

Two strings thrum.

You are already moving, throwing yourself sideways.

Two bolts thud into the mud where your legs just stood, buried to the fletching. They were aimed low, at your legs, not your heart.

Behind you, Bet presses flat against the hedge at the lane's mouth, untouched.

In the loft, two shapes hunch over their crossbows, winding the strings back.

Then, from the beck gully behind the barn, a sound comes: a man's roar, where the crow's caw should be.

Iron rings on iron down in the trees. Someone was waiting on Dray's path.

From the dark of the barn door comes a soft, pleasant voice, almost smiling.

"Nimble. They said you were. Put the spear down, healer, and nobody bleeds tonight."

You come up off the mud and charge the barn door, spear levelled, your Water gathered and ready to Push.

In the loft the Loddons are still bent over their strings, winding. The air above you stays empty.

Twenty paces of yard. On your refined legs you cross them in a heartbeat.

In the dim of the barn stands a lean man, smiling, a ring of keys at his belt. Crake.

You drive the cold-iron spear straight at his heart.

He slips aside, quick, but not quick enough. The point takes him full in the chest.

It doesn't sink. It grinds on rings under his jerkin, and a few burst loose and skitter across the straw.

The blow lifts him off his feet and slams him back against a post. His breath goes out of him in a grunt.

His heart is armoured. You felt it through the shaft: a coat of rings over his chest and belly.

Crake doesn't fall. He comes off the post along the shaft, bare hands reaching for yours.

You wrench the spear back and around, and keep his fingers off your own.

But as he gives ground his left hand flicks from his cuff. Something stings your neck.

A dart, needle-fine. It broke your refined skin, where the bolts would not have.

Cold spreads from the prick, slow and heavy, like river silt seeping toward your Locks.

This is the dart Dray warned of: the venom that chokes a Talent's Locks.

It creeps through you the way the rot crept through Wren.

Crake has backed out of the spear's reach. He rubs his chest, still smiling.

"There. Now we wait a minute, you and I."

Overhead, two crossbow strings click home.

Your Talent bursts out of you. You throw Undertow at Crake's bare head like a rope.

It catches.

You haul down and forward, hard. His tucked chin jerks up off his ring coat, and his head snaps toward you. The smile is gone. His throat is bared above the rings.

In the same instant you push up the shaft, haul the spear back, and drive it forward.

Undertow drives your arms. The cold-iron point moves faster than a spear should move.

It takes him in the bare neck. His skin turns it for half a breath. Then it gives.

The point goes through.

Crake's hands come up to the shaft. Under them is only wood, and cold iron, and he can't drink from either.

He sags off the point onto the straw. Cold brine spills out under him with the blood, more than a body should hold: the Water he stole, running back into the dirt.

In moments he is still.

From the lane comes Bet's voice, high and cracking. "Wil! Wil, he's down! Put it down!"

Above you, in the loft, a long silence. Then a crossbow clatters onto the boards.

"Wil?" A younger voice, wild. A second crossbow clatters down after the first.

Across the yard the farmhouse door bangs, and small feet run for the hedges.

Behind the barn, the clash in the gully has gone to shouting, and a man's howl of pain.

Then the cold in your neck reminds you it is there. It is heavy now, nearly at your Locks.

You can still feel your Water answering you, but only just. Seconds, not a minute.

The venom creeps on, the way the rot crept through Wren.

You reach for your Water and Push, hard, out through the prick in your neck.

It comes, but slow and thick, like pushing through cold mud. The venom has a head start.

A little dark, bitter-smelling fluid beads at the prick. The dart shifts, but stays in.

The cold slides past the push, quick as a closing door, and reaches your Locks.

One after another they clench shut: throat, palms, soles. The Push dies in you.

Your Water is still there, deep and full, but penned in your well. You can't reach it.

No Undertow. Not a ripple. Not even the soft sense of the water around you.

Your body is still your own: refined skin and muscle, and your legs steady under you.

The needle dart still hangs in the side of your neck.

Crake lies still in the straw at your feet, the brine soaking out around him.

Overhead, boards creak. A man's voice comes from the loft, steady but hoarse.

"We're unarmed. Both bows are down. We're coming down the ladder, if you'll let us."

Out in the lane Bet is sobbing. Behind the barn, the gully has gone quiet.

"Come on down," you call up. "If there are any others and you help us subdue them, that will count in your favour."

The ladder creaks. A broad, bearded man comes down first, empty hands held open. Wil.

A thin, wild-eyed younger one follows, shaking so hard the rungs rattle.

Unbidden, both of them kneel in the straw, hands open on their knees.

"There's no one else," Wil says. "Just Big Harl down the gully, and the boy, Pip. He ran."

His eyes go to the dart in your neck, then away.

"That venom shuts a Talent's Locks till about dawn. No longer. It wears off."

Bet stumbles in from the yard and drops beside her brothers. She pulls the younger one's head against her.

Boots and voices come round the barn's corner. Dray, mud to the knees, his fists still grey stone.

Two watchmen march a big man between them, bound at the wrists, limping, one arm hanging. Behind them a third watchman clutches his forearm, blood running between his fingers.

Dray takes in the barn: Crake in the brine, the kneeling Loddons, the dart in your neck.

"Gods. You did it." Then, sharper: "Are you hurt? Is that one of his darts?"

Before you can answer, Wil lifts his head. His voice is low and steady.

"Crake has three Talented chained in a cellar in Carrowmouth. In the Salt Lanes. I know which cellar. I'll tell it, Reeve, if it buys my brother's life and mine."

You tell Dray how it went. The creak in the loft, the keys in the barn, the bolts at your legs. The charge, the ring coat, the dart. Then the pull on Crake's head, and the spear through his neck. And the venom, and your Locks shut tight, and Wil's word that it wears off by dawn.

Dray listens. As he does, the grey fades out of his fists, back to flesh. When you finish he looks at Crake a long moment.

"I've seen First Depth fighters do less," he says. "Far less."

Then you speak for the three Loddons: clemency, under guard, and a chance to work it off.

Dray rubs his jaw. He looks at the kneeling brothers, then at Bet.

"Robbers hang under the Reeve's law. But a man who yields and names his master may go to the mines instead." He turns to the elder brother. "If your cellar proves true, Wil, I'll write Lady Varrow for you both. The choice is hers. Three years in Varrow Keep's quarry, under guard. Not the rope." His eyes move to Bet. "Bet's no robber. She speaks before Lady Varrow, then she can go home to Carrowmouth."

Bet bends over her brothers' shoulders, weeping again, but differently now.

Wil bows his head to the straw. Then he leans close to Dray and murmurs the cellar's place.

Dray nods, grim. "A rider goes to the Carrowmouth watch at first light."

By the door, the bound man spits.

"Not you, Harl," Dray says. "You hang, unless you've something worth your neck."

He turns back to you. "And Crake's head was worth five bars dead. Every coin of that is yours. We bed down here tonight. The Pell twins carry him home on a hurdle at dawn."

The wounded watchman sits on a bucket, grey-faced, while another binds his slashed forearm with a strip of shirt.

"The boy Pip," Wil says, low. "He'll run for Grey Shaw, to the old tollhouse. Crake's camp. There's a stash there. Bottled Water, coin, and Crake's ledger. Pip'll grab what he can and vanish. He'll be there near midnight."
