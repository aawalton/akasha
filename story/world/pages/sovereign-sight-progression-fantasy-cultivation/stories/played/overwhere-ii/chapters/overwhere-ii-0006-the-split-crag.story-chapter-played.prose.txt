"Thank you for the offer," you say, "but I expect I'll need larger challenges than I can find here to grow. If you have any larger issues I can take care of before I go, I'm willing. After that I'll be on my way."

You heft the purse.

"I'd also like to take the chambers from the last five wolves, once they're carted back."

Dray nods slowly, as if he'd expected just that answer.

"Aye. A Talent your size was never going to sit in a watch cottage. When you go, I'll write you a Reeve's letter naming you. It'll serve as papers."

He waves a hand at the carcasses in the yard.

"Keep whatever you cut from them. I only want the carcasses burned. My two men and the pony go up for the den four at first light. They'll be back by mid-afternoon tomorrow. They'll not go past the cairn, mind. The one by the tarn, fetch yourself or leave."

He turns his mug in his hands and looks south, across the valley, toward the white peaks of the Whitecombs.

"Larger issues," he says. "There's two."

He takes a slow swallow from the mug.

"Greymaws don't come off the Whitecombs for nothing. Something drove them down. I fear it'll follow. I've no one fit to climb up there and look. I'd pay well."

He sets the mug down on the step.

"The other's a man. Oswy Crake, a Water thief on the Carrowmouth road. Ten silver bars on his head."

"Great," you say. "I'll scout the Whitecombs tomorrow. Then we can talk about Oswy Crake after, if I'm successful solving the first. Could I borrow a bunk until I leave, though? I'd like to stay close while I'm working on these."

"Sleep in the watch cottage while you work for me," Dray says. "It stands empty; it's yours free."

"For the Whitecombs, go first to Ebba Callow at Callow Beck. She knows the tracks. Bring me sure word of what drove the greymaws down, and it's a silver bar."

He walks you across the green to the ford, to a squat stone house beside it.

Inside is one room with a sleeping loft, a rope bed, a table and a cold hearth. The dead watchman's few pots hang by the chimney, grey with dust since midwinter.

Dray nods to you from the doorway and leaves you to it.

Your throat is dry as dust. Out past the door the river runs loud, a stone's throw away.

A knock sounds on the open door. Anselm stands there, the hem of his orange robes still muddy.

"They told me at the green you'd be here," he says. "You said the shrine, once the Reeve was done?"

"Sorry, I've been a bit busy," you say. "We can talk now. I'm free for a few hours at least."

Anselm's tired face lights up. He leads you across the green to the shrine of Threll.

Inside it is cool and dim. Before the altar stands a stone vessel of Water that glows faintly, blue-green in the gloom.

He sits you on a bench beside it and sits down facing you, his hands folded in his sleeves.

"The Keepers teach that every Talent draws from the Sea, and grows by Depths," he says. "Surface first, then First Depth, and on down. Each Depth is reached by a Descent. And each Descent brings a Tribulation, a trial in the Sea you must face alone."

He leans forward.

"But you don't Descend until your body can bear it. That's what the daily practice is for. Skin first, whole, before any muscle. An hour at dawn and an hour at dusk. You slow your Current, and press your Water out into the skin, one limb at a time."

He gives a small, rueful smile.

"It prickles like nettles, then burns like a scald, then goes numb. Then you move on. I've spent three years on my skin, and I've never gone deeper."

He rolls back one orange sleeve and holds out a thin forearm, pale and smooth.

"Will you try it with me now? Your left arm first."

You unwind Garth's linen from your left forearm. Beneath it the bite is a closed pink crescent.

You shut your eyes and slow your loops, as Anselm showed you.

You press your Water out along the thread to your left palm, and on into the skin of your arm.

Then you turn Undertow on it. You push your Water out to the skin, and pull it in to soak.

Push and pull, in turn, like surf on a shore.

The nettles, the scald and the numbness come all at once, in one hard, searing wave.

You hold the tide steady through it. You don't let it surge.

Beside you, Anselm's breath comes short and ragged, like a man's at the edge of the sea.

The wave rolls slowly up from your fingertips: your wrist, your forearm, past your elbow. It climbs to your shoulder and stops there, with nowhere left to go.

The searing ebbs. You open your eyes. The light through the door has moved; a quarter hour has passed.

Your whole left arm, fingertip to shoulder, has a faint pearly sheen. It feels tight and cool.

The pink crescent of the greymaw bite is still there, sealed under the new skin.

Beside it, the skin of your right arm feels thin and raw, as if asking to be matched.

Anselm stares at your arm, then at your face. His own bare forearm hangs forgotten at his side.

"I felt it," he says. "A tide turning over and over, right beside me. That was the whole arm, in a quarter hour. My skin took me three years."

He shakes his head slowly.

"Every Keeper teaching I know keeps the Talent out of the refining. How did you think of that?"

"It just fit," you say, and shrug.

You turn inward to your well. It's near brim-full, barely lowered by the first arm. Your mind still feels clear enough for another.

You slow your loops and send your Water down the thread to your right palm.

You push it into the skin and pull it in to soak. Push and pull, like surf.

The searing wave rises from your fingertips, and you ride it up your wrist.

Halfway up your forearm your attention slips. The push surges too hard.

Tiny beads of blood well at your pores all along the forearm, stinging.

You rein the tide back to steady, and the beading stops.

The wave climbs on past your elbow to your shoulder, and ebbs.

Your right arm matches your left now, pearly and tight and cool, speckled with dried blood.

You're thirstier than ever, and a hollow hunger has opened under your ribs. Your head feels worn thin, the way it did after Tansy.

Anselm has watched it all in silence. Now he holds out a wet cloth, and a clay cup of water from the shrine's jug.

"You bled," he says. "Were you pushing too hard?"

"A bit," you say. "Nothing some food, drink and sleep won't fix."

You drain the cup of water in one go. Then you take the cloth and wipe the dried blood from your forearm.

Anselm refills the cup. He goes into the back and comes out with bread, a wedge of hard cheese and a jug of small beer.

"Then eat," he says. "I'll not have the valley's hero faint in my shrine."

You eat. The bread is coarse and the cheese is sharp, and bite by bite the hollow under your ribs fills.

Anselm sits across from you, watching you eat, turning his cup in his thin hands.

"There's a thing I must tell you," he says, "before I do it."

He sets the cup down.

"I write to the Keepers in Carrowmouth each week. The carrier comes on market day. A Keeper must report any great new Talent in his charge. Yours is the greatest I've seen."

He meets your eyes.

"I mean to write of you. Your name, Undertow, what I've felt, what I saw today. I'd rather you heard it from me first."

His hands tighten on the cup.

"Is there anything you'd have me leave out?"

"What do you expect the consequences to be," you ask, "either way?"

Anselm sets down his cup. He thinks it through, frowning.

"If I write it all, the Keepers in Carrowmouth will send a senior Keeper up the valley," he says. "As soon as the road dries. They'll want to see you for themselves. They'll urge you to take the Chartermark and go south to the Spires."

He turns the cup a quarter turn on the table.

"And a full letter reaches the Spires within a month. A Talent like yours will draw them. Letters pass through many hands in Carrowmouth, too. A great Talent's name draws ill notice as well as good."

He looks up.

"If I write it bare, only that a great Talent is in my charge, it slows them. But they'd still come to see. That much I must write, whatever you ask. If I hid you and they found out, they'd take the shrine from me."

He spreads his thin hands, apologetic.

"So: all of it, or the bare bones. Which would you have?"

"Share it all," you say. "I expect to take the Chartermark sooner or later anyways. No reason to slow that down."

Relief spreads across Anselm's tired face. He smiles warmly.

"Thank you," he says. "I'll write it tonight, all of it, and it goes with the carrier on market day."

Then a small frown creases his brow.

"You should know, though: the Charterstone in Carrowmouth gives marks only at Threllsnacht. That's nearly a year off."

He turns his cup slowly between his palms.

"If you keep near, I'd expect the Keepers to teach you through the year until then. If you go wandering far, they'll only have to find you first."

"Either is fine," you say. "I'll learn what anyone can teach me. And now I think I need an early night's sleep. I have a hunt in the morning."

Anselm laughs softly. "Good hunting, then," he says. "And Threll's keeping."

You take your leave and walk back across the green to the empty watch cottage.

You sleep deep and dreamless in the bunk, and wake before dawn feeling whole again.

Day three dawns clear and cold. Frost whitens the green, and the Whitecombs stand sharp against the sky.

You gear up: knapsack on your back, purse at your belt, the watch spear in your hand. Your mouth is dry and your stomach hollow. You've had nothing since Anselm's bread and cheese last evening.

You take the lane south out of Wendle Ford, toward the white mountains.

Two hours on, the lane climbs to a longhouse where a beck comes down out of the snow. Goats crop the slope below it. A grey-haired woman stands by the wall and watches you come, hard as a gatepost.

She looks you up and down in silence. Then her eyes go to the watch spear.

"That's one of Dray's," she says. "You'll be the one who killed the greymaws. Ebba Callow."

She jerks her chin up the mountain.

"At midwinter the snow in the high cwm above us melted black, all in one night. Since then my goats won't graze above the beck. Two strayed up. They never came back. And the beck's run faint salt ever since. Taste it, if you doubt me."

Her mouth goes tight.

"I've not been above the beck since. I've climbed these hills since I could walk."

She points out a goat track winding up toward a notch under the snow line.

"That's your way. Three hours' climb. Be down off the mountain by dusk."

She presses a round of goat's cheese, wrapped in a cloth, into your hands.

You climb. The track steepens and the air thins, and the farm shrinks to a toy below you.

Once the rock trembles underfoot: a long, low shudder, like thunder felt rather than heard.

By noon you top the notch into the high cwm, a bowl of rock just under the snow line.

A black pool lies in the bowl, thirty paces across, free of ice, ringed with a crust of grey salt. The wind off it smells of the sea, two days from any sea.

Your well stirs and leans toward the pool, the way it leaned toward the tarn, but weaker.

You spot them before they spot you: two goats, grazing on the salt at the water's edge.

They are grey-scaled like fish, and their horns are rough and branching like coral.

Both keep their heads down. The wind blows from them to you, and they haven't caught your scent.

You step down into the cwm toward the pool, spear low, to see what the goats will do.

Both heads snap up at once. Both goats come at you, heads down, coral horns first.

You reach with Undertow and pull, hard, on the lead goat as it charges.

Its Water tears out of it in a rush. Its legs fold, and it slides dead across the scree to your feet.

The second is three strides behind. You turn the same pull on it without a pause.

It drops even faster. Its own speed rolls it on past you, to a stop against a boulder.

Both horns stayed a stride short of you. It was over in a handful of heartbeats.

Where you drew beside the pool, the black water ripples toward you, the way the tarn's did.

Each goat is as heavy as a big ewe. You could drag one by hand, but two, down that track, is too many.

So you hold Undertow on both and pull them after you: a steady draw, like towing on a rope.

As you draw, you feel a small hard knot of Water behind each goat's breastbone.

The descent takes longer than the climb. Your throat burns dry the whole way down. Your head aches, and your strength ebbs a little with every hour without water.

Late in the afternoon you haul the goats into the yard at Callow Beck.

Ebba comes out and stops short. She crouches by the nearer goat and lifts its scaled left ear. Her own notch is cut in it, plain as day.

"Bramble," she says. "And that's Sorrel. I raised them both from kids."

She stands, jaw tight, and shouts her two grandsons out of the byre.

"Fetch peat. We burn them on the midden, now. And don't you lay a hand on them."

She turns back to you.

"I've no coin to spare, but I've hot oatcakes and goat's butter, and you've earned them."

The grandsons come back with armloads of peat and start dragging the first goat toward the midden.

The peat catches. Bramble and Sorrel burn on the midden in a stink of salt and scorched scale.

You stand with Ebba by the smoke and tell her what you saw up there: the black pool, the salt crust, the goats' charge.

"Black water up there," she says, "and my goats turned to fish. At my age."

She watches the fire a while.

"Greymaws denned under the crag above that cwm since my grandmother's day. At midwinter I heard that crag split in the night. Like the sky breaking."

The last of the goats falls to ash. You take your leave and walk north toward the Ford.

Your throat is raw and your head pounds. Every hour without water leaves you a little weaker.

Night has fallen by the time you cross the green to the Reeve's house. Four greymaw carcasses lie in its yard under sacking. The watch is back from the den.

Tam Oakes lets you in. Dray sits by the fire with his boots off, and looks up.

You tell him all of it: the pool, the goats, the ripple in the black water, and how your well leaned toward it, the way it leaned toward the tarn.

"A black pool smelling of the sea," Dray says slowly. "I've seen one before, on the coast, in the militia. A tidepool. They draw Aberrants in, and turn beasts that linger by them. Like your goats."

He rubs his jaw.

"But the greymaws ran downhill. A pool draws things in. Something drove them out."

You tell him what Ebba heard: the crag where the greymaws denned split at midwinter.

"Then that's where the answer is," he says. "An hour above the pool, by the sound of it."

Then his face goes still.

"Your well leaned at the tarn too, you said. Hollow Tarn's no puddle. If that's a tidepool..."

He doesn't finish. He counts out half a silver bar in coin and sets it on the table.

"For the pool. The rest of the bar when you bring me what drove them off that crag."

He takes a long look at your cracked lips, then pushes a jug of water across the table to you.

You drink the whole jug, slowly. The ache in your head begins to loosen.

You thank Dray and cross the green to the Drowned Lantern for a hot meal and more to drink. The woman behind the counter takes your copper and sets down a bowl of mutton stew, a heel of bread and a mug of ale.

Warm and full, you walk back to the watch cottage and bar the door.

You sit on the bunk, bare your left leg, and begin as Anselm taught you, Undertow giving and drawing.

The tide climbs from ankle to hip, and the skin drinks it in, as your arms did.

Last you come to the sole, where your threads end. A cold jolt shoots up through your loops into your well.

The tide lurches. Water spills from your grip, your well drops low, and your breath catches.

You hold on and finish the leg. But your well is near dry, and you must wait an hour for it.

The Sea fills it back to the brim, slow and steady, while you sit in the dark.

You begin the right leg, ready this time for the jolt at the sole.

When it comes, you ride it like a wave. The refining runs smooth and swift, root to toe.

Your right leg shines faintly pearly in the candlelight. Both legs feel tight and cool, like your arms. Now only the skin of your trunk, neck and head feels thin and raw, asking to be matched.

You lie down, and sleep takes you at once.

You wake at dawn on day four, rested and whole, your mouth a little dry.

Out on the green, stalls are going up for market day. A carrier's cart stands by the Lantern.

At the Lantern the woman behind the counter wraps you bread, cheese and cold mutton for the road, for two coppers. At the tinker's stall on the green you buy a leather waterskin for three more.

You fill it at the well and drink deep. Then you set off south, with the market still waking behind you.

Day four is grey and still. Low cloud hangs just above the Whitecombs' snow line.

You pass Callow Beck without stopping and climb the goat track you know now.

In the high cwm the black pool lies quiet. The salt crust around it lies bare and still.

You eat your bread and mutton on a rock and drink from the skin. Then you climb on above the pool.

Above the cwm the goat tracks are buried in old snow, and you pick your own way up the scree.

A slab of ice-glazed scree shifts under your foot, and you go down hard.

You slide thirty feet before you can stop yourself, the stones raking your side and hip.

Your arms and legs come out unmarked, the new skin smooth under the grit. But the skin over your ribs is scraped raw, and bruised deep beneath.

It takes you the best part of an hour to climb back to where you fell, by a longer, safer way.

Then you see the prints: big webbed paws, frozen into old ice on the scree, all heading downhill.

Greymaw tracks, long-strided and far apart. The pack was running when it made them.

You follow them up. By half past one you stand under the crag.

It is a grey buttress, split from top to bottom, the crack wide enough to walk into. Fallen blocks the size of carts lie heaped at its foot, burying the mouth of an old den.

The den reeks faintly of rotten salt, the same sourness as the greymaws' bite.

Cold air breathes out of the split in the crag. It smells of the sea.

Your well stirs, and you feel something new: a slow, vast pull from far below your feet. It is like a tide under the mountain, drawing on your well the way the moon draws the sea.

The rock underfoot shivers once, faintly, and is still.

The pieces fall together. A midwinter quake split this crag, buried the den and sent the pack running downhill.

Five hours back down to the Ford, by your reckoning, and the day already half gone.

The split yawns in front of you, dark and breathing cold, and the pull from below goes on.

You turn your back on it and start down. With every step the pull fades behind you, until your well lies quiet again.

This time you take the longer, safer way over the scree, testing each stone before you trust it, and you keep your feet all the way to the cwm.

You pass the quiet pool, then Callow Beck. You unwrap Ebba's round of goat's cheese and eat it as you walk, and drink from your skin.

Dusk has gone to dark by the time you cross the green. The last of the market stalls are packed away, and the trodden grass is empty under the stars.

Tam Oakes lets you in. Dray looks up from the fire.

You tell him all of it: the webbed prints frozen in the scree and all running downhill, the grey crag split from top to bottom, the den buried under fallen blocks the size of carts.

"A quake," Dray says slowly. "That's what the thunder was, all winter. It shook them out of their beds."

He nods to himself. "That's sure word, and it's what I paid for."

He counts out the other half of the silver bar in coin and pushes it across the table to you.

Then you tell him the rest: the cold air breathing out of the split and smelling of the sea, and the slow, vast pull from far under the mountain.

Dray sits very still. The firelight shows every line in his face.

"Under the mountain," he says. "And the tarn, and the pool, all leaning the same way."

He sets his hands flat on the table. "I'll write to Lady Varrow tonight, and to the garrison at Carrowmouth. Wat Brisk sleeps at the Lantern and leaves at first light; the letters go with him."

He looks at you. "I'll ask you one thing. Keep the pull under the mountain to yourself till I've word back. Folk here have had a hard enough winter without that to lie awake on."

He rubs his eyes. Then his hand stops, and he looks up again.

"And Crake. His men robbed a drover on the Carrowmouth road ten days ago. A day's walk below the Ford. That's the nearest he's been."
