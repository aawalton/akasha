The cold wakes you.

It is under you first, a hard wet bed of stones that shift when you breathe, and then it is everywhere at once: in your back, your thighs, the soles of your bare feet, the thin cloth plastered to your skin. You open your eyes on a sky going from grey to violet. Shingle. Wet shingle, rattling softly as a wave draws back down it somewhere close by your feet. The air tastes of salt and weed and something metallic, like a coin on the tongue.

You're wearing a shift. Linen, thin as a handkerchief, soaked through and clinging. Nothing under it. Nothing on your feet.

The last thing you remember is a window. Your back window, at home, the evening light going amber on the garden and then grey. A book open on your knee, the words starting to swim, your chin dropping. You were Alan, dozing over a book, and you let your eyes close for a moment.

Then this.

Above the cove, on a shoulder of dark rock, stands a tower. White stone gone blue in the dusk, and capped in red iron like a bead of blood on a bone. A lighthouse. Its glass crown is dark.

You sit up. Hair comes with you: a heavy, sodden rope of it slaps between your shoulder blades, and more of it sticks across your mouth and cheek in cold strands. You drag it away. It's long. It's dark red, darker where the water holds it, and it goes on past your shoulders, past your shoulder blades. You've never had hair that reached your collar.

You look at your hands.

They're small. Pale, narrow-boned, the fingers slim, the backs of them flecked with freckles like a spill of cinnamon. You turn them over. The nails are short and the palms are smooth, and they are shaking.

"What the fuck," you say, and the voice that says it is a woman's. Light, a little hoarse with cold, pitched well above anything that ever came out of your throat. You clap a hand over your mouth. Your breath is warm against your palm, and it's the only warm thing anywhere.

You were Alan. You remember that as plainly as the window and the book.

And you are Nala.

The name is simply there, the way your own name is there when someone calls it across a room. Nala. You try it silently and it fits. It doesn't feel borrowed, and that frightens you more than the hair.

Then the cold closes its hand. It takes hold properly now; your jaw starts to rattle, and the shaking is in your whole body, deep and helpless, your shoulders jerking with it. You try to curl your toes and can't tell whether they move. Your feet are white lumps at the ends of your legs. You can see them. You can't feel them at all.

Above you, light.

It catches small at first, a yellow spark in the glass at the top of the tower, and grows, steady and warm. Then the light begins to move. Something up there turns, slowly, heavily, and a long arm of brightness swings out from the tower over the sea.

It passes across a line of rocks offshore. Black rocks, jagged, a whole reef of them heaving up out of white water like the backs of something waiting. The beam lights the spray on them and swings on, round across the cove, over the shingle, over you. For an instant you are lit entirely, every stone around you shining wet, your own pale legs blazing. Then it's gone, and the dusk is darker for it.

Up on the rock, a smaller light: a lantern, swinging. It starts down. There must be steps cut into the face of the rock, because the lantern drops in short regular jolts, turning back and forth, coming toward the cove.

You try to get up, and your legs won't answer, so you sit there shaking in the shingle and watch it come.

Boots crunch on the stones. The lantern stops above you, and the woman holding it is tall, all legs, in a heavy navy jumper and big sea boots. A thick dark braid lies over one shoulder of the jumper. She thrusts the lantern toward your face, and you squint into the glare.

"Did you come off a boat?" Her voice is low and sharp. "Well? Did you?"

"I don't—" Your teeth knock together. "I don't know. I was somewhere else, I was—"

You stop. Where? At a window in a house that isn't in this world, in a body that isn't this one.

"Nala," you say. "I'm Nala."

The lantern stays where it is. Behind it you can make out her face now: fair skin roughened by wind, grey eyes, straight dark brows pulled together, a wide mouth set hard.

"Morwenna," she says. "I keep the light." She glances out at the reef, then back at you. "And there was no boat on the Teeth today."

She sets the lantern down on the shingle. Then she takes hold of the hem of her jumper, hauls it up over her head and off, and before you can say a word she's dragging it down over yours. Your head comes out through the neck of it with your wet hair caught inside; she pulls your arms through the sleeves one after the other, as if you were a child.

It's warm. Warm from her, the heat of her body still in the wool, and it closes around you, and you make a small sound you didn't mean to make. It smells of wool and lamp oil. It falls past your hips, past your thighs, to your knees, and the sleeves swallow your hands.

She picks up the lantern again.

"Up," she says.

You get your hands under you and push, and you're nearly standing, and then your feet fold under you like wet paper and you go down onto the stones on one knee.

She makes a short sound through her nose. She crouches, takes your arm and hooks it over her shoulders, gets her other arm around your waist, and straightens. You come up with her. Your feet touch the ground, and you can't feel them touch.

"Lean on me," she says. "Don't try to help."

The steps are steep and slick, cut into the black rock and worn into dips at their middles. She takes most of your weight. She half carries you, her hip hard against your side, her breath steady above you, and the lantern swings from her free hand, throwing your two shadows up the rock and down again.

Your head comes to her chin. You notice it on the fourth step, or the fifth: her jaw is just there, over your temple. You have never been this small beside anyone in your life. Alan was the one who ducked through doorways. Now you're tucked under someone's arm like a bundle of sailcloth, and she climbs as if you weigh nothing much.

Her hand on your waist is cold. You can feel the cold of it through the wool, each separate finger. It stays exactly where it is, and it keeps its grip, all the way up.

At the top, the wind finds you. The tower goes up into the dark beside you, its beam wheeling overhead, and at its foot crouches a cottage: granite walls, a slate roof, a single chimney. From a low shed beside it, two goats start bleating at the sound of you.

"Hush, Bramble," she says. "Sorrow, hush now. It's only me." Her voice is different with them, softer, almost coaxing, gentler than anything she's said to you.

The goats hush.

Inside there's one room. A hearth with the fire banked low, a table, a single chair by the hearth, a bed against the far wall under a patched quilt, a ladder going up to a loft. Pots, coils of rope, a row of brass on the mantel. She lowers you onto the hearthstones and goes straight to the fire, raking it open, piling on wood and driftwood until it roars. She hangs a kettle over it on a hook, and beside the kettle a black pot that starts to smell of fish and onion as it warms.

Then she goes to a chest at the foot of the bed and comes back with a folded shift and a grey blanket, and drops them in your lap.

"Get out of that wet thing," she says, and turns her back.

She stands facing the door, arms folded.

You get the jumper off first, carefully, and lay it on the hearth. Then you peel the shift up. It fights you, wet linen sucking at your skin, and you have to drag it over your head and your hair, and then it's off, and you're naked by the fire.

You look down at yourself.

It's the first time you've seen it whole. Pale skin, so pale it's almost blue in places with the cold, and pebbled all over with gooseflesh. Freckles on your shoulders and across your chest, fainter on the soft slopes of your small tits. Your nipples are pink and drawn up tight, hard as little stones. Your waist goes in. "Are you decent yet?" Her voice from the door is very dry.

"Yes. No. One second." You snatch up the dry shift and haul it on, inside out by the feel of the seams, and wrap the blanket round yourself on top of it. "Yes."

She turns round. She looks you over without any expression at all, then takes you by the elbow and sits you in the one chair, close to the fire. A mug comes into your hands: tea, scalding, black. You wrap your fingers round it and the heat stings your palms.

She kneels on the hearthstones in front of you, takes your bare feet into her hands, and starts to rub them.

Her hands are cold too, and hard, with calluses along the palms and the insides of the fingers. She works them over your soles, your toes, the backs of your heels, firm and unhurried, the way you'd knead dough.

At first you feel only pressure. Then a prickle. Then the feeling comes back all at once, and it is pain: hot needles, a thousand of them, driven up through the soles of your feet into your shins. You hiss through your teeth and jerk your feet back.

She keeps hold of them. Her grip tightens on your ankles, and she keeps rubbing.

"That's the blood coming back," she says. "It'll pass."

You grip the mug and breathe and look anywhere but at your feet. You look at her. Her head is bent over her work. The fire lights one side of her face, and there is a scar there, thin and pale, cutting through the end of her left eyebrow so the dark hair stops and starts again either side of it. You follow the line of it with your eyes. You wonder what did it. You wonder whether it hurt. You keep looking.

She glances up.

Her grey eyes catch yours straight on, and you look away first, at the fire, at the mug, and your face goes hot all the way to your ears. You can feel it, the heat flooding your cheeks, every freckle lit up like a map.

She goes on rubbing your feet.

Later, your feet throbbing and warm and alive again, she ladles stew into two bowls and pulls a stool up opposite your chair. The stew is fish and potato and onion, salty, thick with pepper, and you eat it too fast and burn your mouth.

"Where are you from?" she asks.

"Far away." You turn the spoon in your fingers. "Very far. I don't know how I came here."

She looks at you for a long moment over her bowl.

"The tide leaves things in that cove," she says flatly. "Whatever the sea gives up."

She eats a mouthful. Then she adds, "The old women in town will tell you the Morrow Head lamp calls home what the sea has lost."

"Do you believe that?"

"I believe in oil," she says, "and clockwork."

"Where is this? Where am I?"

"Morrow Head. Penmorrow's a mile south, along the cliff path." She tips her spoon toward the wall, as if you could see through granite.

"Thank you," you say. "Really. For coming down for me. For the jumper, and all of it. Thank you."

"Mm."

"I mean it. You didn't have to—"

"Don't make a fuss of it." She scrapes her bowl. "I'll walk you to Penmorrow in the morning. Someone there may know something."

Warmth is soaking into you now from the outside and the inside both: the fire on your shins, the stew in your belly, the tea. Your hands have stopped shaking. Something loosens in your chest, and you laugh, just at being warm, at being alive, and the laugh comes out high and light and girlish, and that's so absurd that you laugh again, at the laugh.

The corner of her mouth twitches.

It's there and gone. She stands, takes your bowl, and sets both bowls in a basin by the door.

"I have to go up and wind the clockwork," she says. "It wants winding every four hours, or the lens stops turning."

"I could come. Help."

She looks pointedly at your feet. "Not up ninety-six steps on those."

She takes the lantern and goes out, and the door knocks shut behind her. You hear her boots on stone, then a heavier door, then the stairs, faint and climbing, round and round inside the wall of the tower.

You sit with the fire. It pops and settles. The sea is a long low roar beyond the walls, coming and going.

Then, faint, down the stairwell through some gap in the stone: her voice. Talking. Quiet, unhurried, the way you'd talk to a dog, or to yourself.

"...I know, I know. Patience."

A clank of metal. A pause.

"There's a girl in my chair," she says up there, very low, to the lamp. "And I don't know why."

Then the long ratcheting sound of the clockwork, steady, on and on.

When she comes down, her eyes stay off you. She climbs the ladder to the loft with an armful of blankets, and you hear her up there shaking out a straw mattress, thumping it into shape, laying the blankets over it.

"Up you go," she says, coming down. "Mind the third rung."

You climb in your blanket, slowly, your feet still tender. The loft is narrow and low under the slates, folded canvas at one end and the mattress made up neatly at the other. You kneel on it. Below, she stands at the foot of the ladder a moment, then goes to her own bed, picks up her pillow, and reaches it up to you.

You take it. Your fingers close over hers on the linen. Hers are cold, still cold after all that fire, and for a moment both of you keep hold. Her hand stays where it is under yours. You can feel the calluses.

Then she draws her hand back.

"Goodnight, Nala," she says.

It's the first time she's said your name. She says it plainly, like a fact she has decided to keep.

You lie down. Through the slates you can hear the sea breaking out on the Teeth, and above you a faint glow slides across the underside of the roof, slips away, comes round again, slow as breathing, as the great lens turns up in the tower.

You lay your hand on your chest. Under the shift your heart is beating small and quick, a little bird of a heart, nothing like the heavy one you had. You think of her hands. Cold, and hard, and steady on your waist on the steps. Cold on your feet, holding on through the pain.

You fall asleep listening to the sea, not knowing whether you'll wake as Nala or as Alan.
