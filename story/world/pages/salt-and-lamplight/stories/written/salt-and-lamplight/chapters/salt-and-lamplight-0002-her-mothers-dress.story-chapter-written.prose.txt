Grey light, thin as milk, leaks between the slates over your face.

You lie very still under the blankets and take stock before you open your eyes properly. The straw mattress rustles under you. The air is cold on the tip of your nose. Your hair is everywhere, a warm tangle under your cheek and across your throat. You move your legs, and they are short and light, and the shift slides on smooth skin.

Still small. Still her. Still Nala.

You slip a hand under the shift and lay it on your chest. Your tits are soft and warm from sleep, small enough that your palm covers one, and the nipple stirs and tightens against your hand at the touch. You keep your hand there. You can feel your heart under it, and the slow rise of your breathing, and the sheer ordinary weight of a body that has slept a whole night and woken up the same. Dreams go thin at the edges when you wake. This is solid all the way through.

Below, the cottage is quiet. When you creep to the top of the ladder and look down, the fire is already made up and crackling, a kettle hung beside it, and the bed against the far wall is straightened, the patched quilt pulled square. The chair stands empty. Her boots are gone from beside the door.

You need to piss. Badly, suddenly, the way you'd forgotten a body could need anything.

You climb down, wincing as your tender soles take the rungs, wrap the blanket round you and go out into the raw morning. The wind slaps your face awake. Behind the goat shed you find a little stone privy with a plank door, and Bramble and Sorrow watch you through the slats of their shed with yellow eyes as you go past.

Inside, you stand in front of the wooden seat for a moment, blanket bunched in your fists, before you understand that standing is no use to you now. You hitch up the shift and sit, the wood cold as ice on the backs of your thighs, and wait, and then it comes, a hot rush out of you lower down and further back than you expect, splashing, and you have to shift your hips and lean forward to stop it running back along your skin. It goes on and on. When it's done you sit there with your elbows on your knees, face hot, and laugh once, under your breath, at yourself, at all of it. Then you find the rag hanging on the nail and work out what that's for too.

"If you want to see the lamp put out," Morwenna's voice calls across the yard from the tower door, "it's now."

You scramble out. She's standing in the open doorway at the tower's foot in her navy jumper, the braid over her shoulder, and when you reach her she turns and starts up without a word.

Ninety-six steps. You count them, because counting gives you something other than your feet to think about. The stair winds up inside the white wall, stone treads worn to shallow bowls, an iron rail cold under your hand. By twenty your soles are burning. By forty you're gripping the rail with both hands and hauling. She stops on a landing above you and waits, silent, her hand on the rail, and when you come level she climbs again, never more than a few steps ahead.

At the top, a trapdoor, and then light.

The lantern room is all glass. The sea lies wide on every side of you, grey and moving and enormous, running out to a line where the sky starts, and under your feet the floor is iron, and in the middle of the room, rising higher than your head, stands the lens. It's a great beehive of glass, ring on ring of prisms cut like ice and set in gleaming brass, and inside it the lamp still burns, small and yellow in the morning. All that glass gathers the little flame and hurls it out to sea.

"Oh," you say.

Morwenna is already at work. She turns down a brass wheel, and the flame shrinks and gutters out, and a thread of smoke goes up into the cowl. She draws the burner out on its arm and holds out her hand toward you without looking.

"Here," she says. "The wick wants trimming every morning. Too long and she smokes. Too short and she starves." She shows you: the little scissors, the charred crust snipped away level, the wick turned up a hair to check it, turned down again. Her long fingers are quick and exact. "Straight across. Never at a slant."

She puts the burner back. Then she takes two soft cloths from a drawer, hands one to you, and goes round to the far side of the lens.

You polish. The glass is cold and faintly greasy with smoke, and the cloth squeaks on it. You work in circles, and through the prisms you can see her on the other side doing the same, her shape split into bands of grey and navy, and then she leans close to rub at a ring level with your face, and her face swims up through the glass enormous, bent, one grey eye huge and stretched sideways, her mouth swollen into a long curve.

You laugh before you can stop it, high and bright, and it rings off the glass.

On the other side, her mouth goes wide and wobbly through the prisms, and you see her press her lips together hard. A breath comes out of her nose that is almost a laugh. She bends back to the glass and rubs at it with a great deal of attention.

Downstairs she milks the goats. You stand at the shed door and watch: her forehead against Sorrow's flank, the milk hissing into the pail in two thin streams, her hands squeezing and pulling in a rhythm so even it looks effortless. Bramble butts at your hip until you scratch her between the horns. Breakfast is a heel of bread, a cup of milk still warm from the goat, and an egg each, boiled in the kettle. You eat yours too fast again.

Afterwards, Morwenna kneels at the chest at the foot of her bed and lifts the lid.

She stays there a moment. Then she lifts things out, slowly, one after another, and lays them on the quilt: a dress of brown wool, plain and heavy, carefully folded. Grey stockings, darned at the heels. A knitted shawl the colour of oatmeal. A pair of boots, black leather gone soft and brown at the creases, small. Everything is folded and smells faintly of the chest's wood and of lavender.

They're a smaller woman's clothes. Too small for Morwenna by a hand's breadth.

"Whose are they?" you ask.

"My mother's." She sets the boots down side by side on the floor. "She's no use for them."

She stands and turns away to the fire.

You look at the dress on the quilt, and then down at the shift you slept in, plain linen, a little short in the body, soft with old washing. It came out of the same chest. You understand it all at once, and you hold still.

The dress is long in the sleeve; the cuffs come down over your knuckles and you have to fold them back. But the body fits you, near enough. The laces are at the back. You reach behind you and fumble, and the laces slip through your fingers, and you twist half round trying to see over your own shoulder, and get one end caught in your hair.

"Stand still."

Her hands are at your back. She draws your hair aside over one shoulder, and you feel her fingers through the wool, quick and sure, tugging the laces through, pulling the dress snug to your waist. One cold knuckle grazes the bare nape of your neck. Then the bow is tied and she steps back fast, a full stride, as if she'd stood too near the fire.

"There," she says, already turning for her coat.

The cliff path runs south along the top of the land, a narrow trodden line through brown bracken and wind-flattened grass. The wind comes hard off the sea, shoving at you, snatching your hair out of the shawl and whipping it across your face, and far below on your right the sea throws itself against the foot of the cliffs and bursts white, and the boom of it comes up through your boots. Her mother's boots. They're a little big, and you've stuffed the toes with rag.

Where the path drops into a dip, the ground is black with running water, the stones in it slick as soap. She stops ahead of you, turns, and holds out her hand. Her mouth stays shut.

You take it. Her fingers close around yours, cold and hard, and she leads you down through the wet, and her grip holds you up when your boot slides, and up the other side.

On the dry turf beyond, she lets go.

You walk on with your hand at your side. The skin of your palm stays cold where hers was, a print of cold, and you curl your fingers in over it and keep them curled.

Then the path rounds a shoulder of the headland, and there below you is Penmorrow.

Grey stone and grey slate, houses stacked one above another up a steep bowl in the hillside, their chimneys smoking, and at the bottom a harbour held in a curved arm of stone wall. Boats are coming in through the gap, brown sails dropping one by one. Gulls wheel and scream over the water.

The path becomes a lane, and the lane a stepped street between walls, and then you're on the harbour front among nets and barrels and the stink of fish and tar. People stop. A woman with a basket on her hip stops. Two men coiling rope stop. A girl gutting fish at a trestle stops with her knife in the air. Every face turns to the keeper, and then to the stranger beside her in a brown dress and borrowed boots.

A fisherman sits on an upturned crate by the wall, a pipe in his teeth. His eyes come to you and stay. They go down you slowly, over your face, your throat, the front of the dress where it lies over your tits, your waist, down your hips, and up again, unhurried, as if he has all morning.

You know that look. You've watched men give it. You've never once been on the other end of it. Your skin goes tight all over under the wool, and your face burns, and for a moment you forget to walk.

Morwenna is somehow between you and him. She hasn't looked at him; she only shifted her stride, so her shoulder is there, and the navy wool blocks his view of you, and she walks on. You walk on with her, close at her side.

The Anchor is a long low house at the end of the harbour with a painted sign of a black anchor creaking over the door. Before you reach it, the door opens and a small wiry woman comes out wiping her hands on a floury apron. Iron-grey hair scraped into a tight bun. A long nose. A thin mouth that looks like it knows the answer to most things. Her sharp grey eyes are on you before she's finished stepping down.

Then they drop to the brown dress, and she stops wiping her hands.

She looks at Morwenna. She looks at her for a long moment, and Morwenna looks back.

"She'd nothing to wear," Morwenna says.

The woman's mouth thins further. Then she nods, once, and drops the matter.

"Dilys, this is Nala," Morwenna says. "I found her in the cove last night, off the tide. She calls herself Nala. She's from a long way off, and she can't say how she came."

"Can't she." Dilys looks you up and down, but it's a different look from the fisherman's: quick and practical, like a woman pricing cloth. "Well. I've had word from every boat on this coast, and every one's come home this season with the crew it went out with. There's not a family from here to the Point short a woman, either. You're no one's that I know of, my love."

She goes inside and comes out with a bun in a cloth, still hot, glazed and studded with currants, and presses it into your hands.

"Oh — thank you, how much do I—" You stop. You put your hand to the dress where a pocket would be, and there's no pocket. You haven't a penny in this world.

"Don't be daft," Dilys says. "Eat it."

You eat it. It's sweet and buttery and so good your eyes sting.

"Is there a room here I could take?" you ask, with your mouth full. "I could work for it. Anything."

"Full to the rafters." Dilys jerks her chin at the inn behind her. "Salting crews, up from the south for the herring, and they'll be in every bed I've got till the season closes." She looks past you at Morwenna. "You'd best stay up at the light, then." Her eyes stay on Morwenna, bright, as if she's daring her to say otherwise.

"Till something's known," Morwenna says. Her voice is flat as slate.

"It'll do you good," Dilys says. "Someone to talk to besides those goats."

Morwenna's jaw sets. You can see the muscle tighten under her windburned skin.

"I'll fetch the oil from the chandlery," she says, and goes, her boots loud on the cobbles.

Dilys watches her go. Then she steps closer to you, close enough that you smell the flour and woodsmoke on her, and speaks low.

"She's kinder than she lets on, that one," she says. "Mind that."

When Morwenna comes back from the chandlery with two cans of oil, a door stands open across the way, painted a bright chipped blue, and on the bench beside it sits the smallest, oldest woman you have ever seen. She is bent nearly double, wrapped in a rusty black dress and a red shawl pinned with a brass brooch, her thin white hair plaited in a crown round her head. Shells clack at her wrist on a string. She lifts a blackthorn stick and crooks it at you, beckoning, and her pale blue eyes fix on your face and stay there.

"Granny Bray," Morwenna says under her breath, like a weather report.

You go over. Morwenna follows a step behind.

"Let me see you," Granny Bray says. Her voice is high and cracked and delighted. She looks you over, slowly, every inch of your face. "Out of the cove. Ha. The cove gave a stranger once before, you know. In my grandmother's day. A stranger, washed up there one autumn night."

"Granny—"

"She stayed up at the light," Granny Bray says over her, to you. "With the keeper. Stayed there the rest of her life, she did."

"Granny," Morwenna says, sharp as a slammed door.

Granny Bray only smiles at you, a smile with a few teeth in it, and reaches out and pats your hand with her own, which is dry and light as a leaf.

You walk home with a can of lamp oil each. Yours is heavy, and the handle bites your fingers. Morwenna walks fast, long-legged, her eyes on the path ahead, and the silence comes off her like cold off a stone. You have to half run to keep up, the borrowed boots slipping on your heels.

"Do you mind?" you ask, breathless, at her back. "Me staying?"

"It's what a keeper does," she says. "That's all it is."

It stings. It stings more than it should; you feel it right in your chest.

"I won't be a burden," you say. "I'll work. For my keep. I'll do whatever needs doing."

"I'll hold you to that," she says, and keeps her eyes on the path.

At the cottage she goes straight into the tower. You stand in the yard with the oil can, your face hot, and then you set it down by the door and go into the goat shed. You've never mucked out anything in your life. You find a fork and a barrow and you go at it anyway, forking the wet, stinking straw out into the barrow, too much at once so it slides off, the barrow tipping, Bramble butting you in the back of the knee, Sorrow eating your shawl. Your arms ache. Your hair comes down. You keep going till the floor is bare stone and there's clean straw from the stack spread over it, uneven, but clean.

Morwenna finds you there. She stands in the shed door and looks at you: at the straw stuck in your hair, at the muck smeared dark along the hem of her mother's dress.

"Oh God, the dress," you say. "I'm sorry, I didn't think, I'll wash it, I—"

"She did worse to it every day of her life." Morwenna steps into the shed. "She'd do the goats in it. And the garden. And the lamp."

She reaches out and picks a straw out of your hair, just above your ear. Her fingers stop there a moment, at your temple, cold against the warm skin, the straw held between them.

Then her hand drops, and she tosses the straw onto the barrow.

At dusk she takes you up the ninety-six steps again. It's easier this time, a little. In the lantern room the sea has gone to dark pewter and the sky to violet, and out on the Teeth the swell is breaking white.

She hands you the taper. "Go on."

You hold the little flame to the wick, and your hand shakes, and for a moment the wick only blackens. Then it catches. A small blue flame runs round the rim and turns gold and steadies, and the whole great lens around it fills with light, every prism lit at once, and you're standing inside it.

Morwenna winds the clockwork, the long ratcheting crank of it, and lets the weight drop. Something clicks, and the lens begins to turn, slow and heavy and smooth, and the beam swings out of the glass over the sea.

You stand side by side at the window and watch it go. It sweeps the Teeth, lighting the black rocks and the spray on them, swings away over the dark water, comes round again. Your shoulder is close to her arm. There's a finger's width of air between them, and the two of you stand there and keep it, exactly that wide.

"Granny's stranger," Morwenna says, looking out to sea. "It's likely an old woman's tale."

You watch the beam pass over the rocks.

"What if it isn't?" you say.

She stays silent. The beam comes round, lights her face for an instant, sea-grey eyes and set mouth, and passes on. Then she turns from the window and goes to the trapdoor and down, first, her boots ringing on the stone stair.

That night in the loft you lie awake for a while under the blankets, under her pillow. Below, the fire has burned low. You can hear her down there, awake: the creak of the chair, the small hiss of the spare lamp, and every little while the soft dry whisper of a page turning.

You lie and listen to it, your second night as Nala.

Then, below, the pages stop turning. The quiet stretches, and stretches, and over your head the lamp's glow comes round on the slates.
