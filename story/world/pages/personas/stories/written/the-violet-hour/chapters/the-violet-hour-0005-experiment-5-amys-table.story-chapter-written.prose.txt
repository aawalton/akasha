Amy's table had a card at every place, each one with a name on it in her small even handwriting, and Natalie found hers and sat where it told her to. She would never have thought to assign seats at her own table. Sitting down, she could see exactly why Amy had. Nobody hovered. Nobody did the little dance by the chairs, working out who went where and deferring and switching at the last second. Everyone came in, found a name, and sat.

"You made place cards," Zadi said, holding hers up.

"I made place cards," Amy said. "You're across from me so I can tell if you stop eating."

"I don't stop eating."

"I know. It's one of the things I like about you."

The kitchen was warm and very clean, the counters clear, everything that was not dinner already put away.

The soup came first, a squash soup that was better than Amy would admit it was. Zadi said so, at length. Amy told her the recipe was on a card in a box and she could have it if she wanted. Then came a plate of greens, then the main, each in its turn, because Amy had decided the order in advance.

A card stood propped on the sideboard with the whole meal on it top to bottom, like an itinerary, and every time a plate went out Natalie caught her checking it and going still a moment when the evening matched the card.

"Did you have a diagram," Zadi said. "For the seating. Tell me the truth."

"I had a diagram," Amy said, like it was the plainest thing in the world.

"I love that about you."

"You laugh every month," Amy said, "and then you eat in the right order and go home happy."

Zadi laughed. Natalie didn't, quite. She had spent a lot of years feeding people.

Natalie started to get up with her empty soup bowl, and Amy said, without turning from the counter, "Leave it."

"I'm right here. I can carry a bowl."

"Everything's handled. Sit down and be a guest." So Natalie sat and was a guest, and watched Amy run the dinner she had built. It was a strange thing, to sit with her hands empty while somebody else did the feeding. At her own table she was never still; here there was nothing to carry, because Amy had carried all of it already, in her head.

There were coasters, cork ones, squared up at every place. Natalie set her glass on hers and caught Amy clocking that she had, and the two of them let it pass without a word. By the door, on a low table, containers were already stacked with names taped to them, hers and Zadi's, the leftovers portioned out before the meal was half eaten.

The main was a chicken done with lemon, and it was good, and Natalie said so twice. Amy nodded and looked pleased and then asked if anyone wanted more before there wasn't any. The bread was good store bread, and when Zadi emptied the basket Amy brought out a second one she already had bagged on the counter. She had bought the spare when she did the shopping, in case the first ran out, and it had.

"This is the best I've eaten in weeks," Zadi said. "I've been living on toast and self-pity."

"I know about the toast," Amy said. "There's a container by the door with your name on it."

"How's the reading," Natalie said to Zadi, because she knew Zadi had run dry.

"It isn't," Zadi said. "I finished the blue one and nothing's caught me since. I've been rereading a cookbook for the pictures, which is a low place to be."

Amy said nothing to that. But when the plates were cleared — she cleared them, stacked and in order — she came back with a book and set it down by Zadi's coffee. "You said you had nothing," she said. "I saw this at the used place last week. I don't know if it's any good. It looked like you."

Zadi picked it up and turned it over and was quiet for a second. "Thank you," she said, in a smaller voice than the one she had been using all night.

The coffee came at the end, decaf, in cups Amy had set out before anyone arrived. On the sideboard the itinerary had come down to its last line, and then Natalie watched it come down to nothing — the main gone, the coffee poured, every item on the list met. Amy read the finished card, looked around for the next thing to do, and did not find one.

And then she sat down. She poured herself the last of the coffee and sat back with it. The talk kept going, looser now, off the plan and onto nothing much — the cold coming on, a neighbor's dog, whether the squash had been better than last year — and Amy put in a word here and there and mostly listened.

The card behind her was done, every line of it, and there was nothing left on it to do. Her coffee cooled at her elbow, forgotten. Outside, the sleet had come on, half rain and half snow, ticking at the dark glass, and none of them moved to go out into it yet.
