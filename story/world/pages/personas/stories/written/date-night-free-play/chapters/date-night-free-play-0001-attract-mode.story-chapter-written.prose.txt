The racer's start-line countdown goes off for nobody — three, two, one — and both front seats sit empty, the wheel jerking left on its own for a ghost that already took the first corner clean. Aura flipped it to attract mode an hour ago and hasn't looked back at it since. Awen has looked at it four times. There's a dead spot in the steering column, off-center, maybe four degrees left, and she keeps not mentioning it.

"You're doing the thing." Aura hasn't turned around. She's at the change counter with the takeout spread open, one carton already gutted, chopsticks going. "The face. At my machine."

"It has a dead spot."

"It's had a dead spot since before you."

"I know." Awen crosses to her. The whole room is dark except the cabinets, and the cabinets throw the only weather in the place — red off the racer, a cold blue wash from the rhythm cab in the corner, its marquee light still ticking at the ballast the way it has for months. "That's not a defense. That's a timeline."

The chopsticks come up over Aura's shoulder without her looking. Awen takes them blind; they've done this enough that the handoff just lands. She steals from the gutted carton on principle, which is the rule, the carton Aura opens first is the one they fight over.

"Off the cab, by the way," Aura says. "House rules. Dumplings stay off the Street Fighter cab."

"We're nowhere near the—"

"Pre-emptive."

Awen's bag is on the counter where she set it down, the twice-taped corner of the thing inside just showing past the zip. She turns it so the corner doesn't show. Not the point of putting it away; the point is she looked. The joint isn't true yet — the evening still has ornament on it, and she can't hand it over with ornament still on.

Aura came around the end of the counter a minute ago at half her usual speed. Half. Awen clocked it and said nothing, because you don't reach for a thing somebody's carrying carefully before they've set it down; you let them find the counter. Whatever it is, Aura will race straight past it about six more times before she lets it land. That's fine. Awen has all night and a dead carton to defend.

"Racer," Aura says. "Right now. You're down two in the room and I can feel you not caring, which is worse than losing."

"I'm eating."

"Bring it." Already moving — of course she's already moving, she's at the seats before the sentence lands, dropping into the left one, the good one, the one without the dead spot. "Loser gets the last dumpling taken from them at a time of my choosing."

Awen looks at the wheel in the right seat. Four degrees. She could fix it in ten minutes with the machine cold and would enjoy every one of them.

She sets the carton down and gets in anyway.

The light-tree drops green and Aura is gone. Not fast — gone, a whole car-length up the track before Awen's even cleared the start gate, her ghost-line the perfect one, the one the machine wants you to drive.

Awen doesn't chase the ghost. She drives the wheel she's got. Four degrees of slack before the front end answers, so she turns four degrees into nothing, early, feeds the correction in before each corner asks for it. The car goes where she meant instead of where the column's backlash would send it. It's slower on paper. It's honest. And that's worth about a length a lap, corner after corner.

"You're doing something." Aura doesn't look over. She never looks over; she watches the road like it owes her money. "I can hear you doing something over there. Stop it."

"Driving."

"Sure. That's your fixing-my-machine-with-your-face sound — I'd know it in my sleep."

"Can't fix it from here." Awen takes the hairpin. The wheel fights her the way it always does and she lets it, hands already four degrees ahead of the fight. "I'm just being polite to it."

Aura's shoulder drops a half-inch on the next straight. Awen has seen that shoulder a hundred times; it used to mean the race was over. Tonight she just files it, keeps her line, and comes out of the chicane closer than she went in.

"Artemis drove like you," Aura says, apropos of nothing — or of the gap Awen's been eating into all lap. "Never met a straight shot she wouldn't complicate. Drove me up a wall."

"You didn't have cars."

"Chariots. Same principle, worse suspension." The gap holds; the gap shrinks. "She'd have loved you. It would've been unbearable. Two of you 'respecting the mechanism' at me while I—" the last corner comes up and Aura takes it flat-out fast, the ghost-line, the greedy one, "—won."

She doesn't win. Not clean, anyway. Her fast line runs her wide at exit, one hair wide, the width of the slack Awen's been turning early against all lap, and there's a gap on the inside exactly one car wide — and Awen is already in it, four degrees ahead of the fight, the front end for once pointed exactly where she put it, drawing level at the line with the machine that's been lying to her all night doing, at last, precisely what she told it to

"That didn't happen."

Aura's out of the seat before the replay finishes drawing it, standing over the screen like standing closer will renegotiate the result. The photo-finish freezes both cars at the line and then, unkindly, gives it to the inside one by a bumper.

"It's on the board." Awen stays put a second longer, hands still on the wheel, the four degrees of slack gone quiet now she's stopped fighting it. "You can glare at it as long as you want. It'll keep saying the same thing."

"You drove the broken one."

"I drove the one nobody else wanted. That's just manners."

Aura makes a sound that isn't a word. Then, with enormous dignity, she reaches over and taps the cabinet twice — some private thing, machine to owner — and steps back. "Fine. Invoking the sulk. House rules: one song's length, and you don't get to talk to me during it."

"You wrote that rule so you'd have a legal way to lose."

"I wrote that rule because I'm a genius." She's already drifting toward the rhythm cab in the corner, the one whose marquee keeps ticking its broken little pulse, weight up on the balls of her feet again — the sulk apparently involves picking a song loud enough to be a statement. "Dumpling's still yours, by the way. Whenever. I pay my debts. I just do it resentfully."

"I know. It's one of your best qualities."

"It's my worst and my best at once. I'm complicated." She reaches the rhythm cab. Doesn't start it. Her hand goes flat on the panel and stays there, and for a second — one whole second, which on Aura is a geological event — she just stands with her back to Awen, like there's something to read in the flicker.

Awen watches the back of her not start the song.

Then Aura says, "Hey, so—" at completely normal speed. And stops. And starts a different sentence, faster, the real thing — whatever it was — folded back up and put away: "—you are going to want to sit down for how badly I'm about to lose to you at this next, too, out of spite."

"You said no talking during the sulk."

"The sulk hasn't started. I haven't picked the song." She finally jabs the panel, and the cabinet wakes up loud, some four-on-the-floor thing with too many arrows, and she rolls her shoulders like a woman cracking her knuckles before a heist. "Now it's started. Get over here and lose politely."
