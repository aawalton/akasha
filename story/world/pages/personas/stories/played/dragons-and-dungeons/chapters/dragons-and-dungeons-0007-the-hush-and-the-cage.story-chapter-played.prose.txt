I moved.

And the world went deaf.

The hush dropped the instant my foot left the tree-line — the river's voice, the birds, the wind in the reeds, all of it sucked flat into a ringing nothing. I was running across thirty open paces of bank in a silence so total I could hear my own pulse and nothing else, and behind me Wren came low and fast at my heel, the iron kin-fire vessel clutched to her chest, her bootfalls making no sound that lived.

The slavers saw us at fifteen paces. The boss's mouth opened to shout — and nothing came out. His face did a terrible thing as he understood that the sound would not leave his throat. He was still reaching for that understanding when Tygryth arrived.

I caught it only sidelong, because my eyes were on the cage, but I will remember it the rest of my life. The dusk-skinned man did not change so much as stop pretending. One stride he was a tall man; the next there was too much of him, a darkness that moved like poured oil and folded the boss into it without slowing. No fire — he had promised no fire near the cage, and he kept it — just a vast quiet weight and a single economical motion, and the boss was simply down and done, the ledger fluttering its pages into the dead air. The wader in the shallows turned to run, and the river that had no sound took his thrashing and gave nothing back; a shadow crossed the water, and he was under it, and then he was under it. Two terrors, two breaths.

That left the two haulers, and slow men who cannot call to one another make exactly the mistake Tygryth had named. One bolted sideways for the tree-line. The other, big as a door, panicked the only direction left to a cornered animal: straight at the cage. At me. A skinning-knife came up in his fist, and his silent roar was all the more horrible for making no sound — and he was mine now, fifteen feet and closing, between me and the thing I had sworn to guard.

Behind me, Wren hit her knees at the cage and her hands were already on the lock — a heavy slatted hasp, no keyhole, just an iron pin driven through a bored hole and bent. "Brute lock — no finesse, I just need the pin out, give me a count of ten," she hissed into the hush. "Keep him off me."

I had no need for a quick kill, and the man deserved to be Tygryth's for dessert. So I went to meet him calmly, to keep my body between him and the ones I protected, and set myself defensively — to disarm first, then to disable. He was bigger and faster than a man that size should be, but he was afraid and I was not, and fear makes a body loud and stupid. I did not meet his charge; I angled it, stepping off the line so my shoulder turned past the knife instead of into it, and the open bank that had been his advantage became thirty paces of nothing he could use. He slashed. I let the arm come, caught the wrist on the outside with both hands, and instead of fighting his strength I added to it — pulled the knife the way it already wanted to go, past my ribs and into empty air, and rotated. The wrist has only so many degrees in it. The skinning-knife dropped soundless into the mud. He grabbed one-handed and got a fist of my tunic, and that cost me — a knee drove into my already-cut ribs and the world whited at the edges, the old wound waking up furious — but I rode it, folded his caught arm into a lever, and put him down, face to the bank, the joint locked just short of breaking. Alive. Disabled. Breathing into the silent mud. Tygryth's, if Tygryth wanted him.

Behind me Wren's count hit its end; the bent iron pin shrieked, soundless, out of the hasp under both her hands. "Got it — got it—"

Across the bank, the second hauler nearly reached the trees. Nearly. A darkness poured over the ground faster than a running man, and there was, briefly, less world where he had been. Four for four. And as the last of them fell, Tygryth let the hush go.

The river came back all at once — a roar of returned sound, water and reeds and birds crashing into my ears — and into that returning noise, the cage door swung open. The cloth slid off.

She was young — my age, maybe less — and she was unmistakably his. Where Wren wore a faint scatter of scales, this one was half-armored in them: obsidian-black, glassy, drinking the light down her throat and the backs of her hands and climbing one side of her jaw. Black hair was matted to a face gone sharp with hunger. Around her neck the dragon-make collar — the same iron, Tygryth's iron — sat open now, fallen loose where the slavers had locked it, a raw weal beneath. Her eyes came up out of the cage, deep smoked amber, slit-pupiled, and they did not go to me, or to Wren, or to the open door. They went to the dead slavers. To the meat. And something behind them kindled.

Tygryth had gone utterly still. When he spoke, the man-shape could not hold all of what was in his voice. "Another. Another of mine. Caged and shipped like cargo on my own road." Lower, to me, wrecked and wondering: "She has her great-grandmother's scales gone dark. I do not know her name. But I would know that blood blind and across a battlefield."

And there, with the cloth not yet settled in the mud and a starving dragon rising in the open cage, Aria caught me by the sleeve of attention and drew me up out of the field.

---

Hold a moment, she said, before the cloth comes off. Come back up to the table with me. And the bank thinned and went, and there were the candles again, the moon on the balcony, my cup and hers, the campaign humming pleasantly between us.

"There's something I've been saving like a sweet," she said, chin on her hand, the smile going all the way up to her eyes — and then the air changed. I felt it before I heard it: a warmth moving through the cold hall, the candle-flames all leaning the same way at once, the smell of woodsmoke and something richer beneath it. The far door of the hall did not so much open as concede — her family, she'd warned me, had never in three centuries learned to knock — and someone came sweeping in out of the dark on a draft of cold night air, black wings folding down behind her with a sound like silk pulled off a blade. She did not pause on the threshold the way guests do. She crossed the room in one slow sweep of those amber eyes, and found me.

Aria was already grinning before she turned, and rose, hostess to her bones, one hand out toward the newcomer and the other toward me, bridging us across the candlelight. "Alan — this is my cousin. Mari. Black dragon, and the single most appetite ever poured into one shape — for food, for fights, for everything with a pulse and a good story. She is exactly as much as she looks like, and then a little more." She let the delight spill over. "And here is the conceit I have been dying to spring. You walked in last session and chose to play yourself. I loved it so much that I decided my own blood would do the same. My cousins aren't characters I puppet for you — they sit at this table and play themselves, woven into the tale as Tygryth's scattered children. So the fierce caged thing you are about to meet across the water, the one whose cloth is about to fall — that is her. That is Mari."

Mari circled the table once, unhurried, taking my measure the way I was taking hers, the violet glitter under her eyes catching the candlelight. She planted both palms on the table across from me and leaned in until the obsidian crown of her horns threw its shadow over my hands, and grinned, warm and delighted, every tooth a small white promise. "So you're the one," she purred. "Cousin, he's lovely — you didn't say he was lovely." Back to me, head tilted, frank as only a dragon can be: "Mari. Aria's blood, which makes me the dangerous kind of family." Her eyes sparked. "She tells me I'm to play myself in this little tale of yours, woven in as one of the old slave-king's scattered get. Which means somewhere in the story to come, monk, you and I are going to meet for the first time all over again — and I'll be at my very worst. Starving. Feral. Half-mad in a cage." She tipped her chin, the grin going wicked-soft. "Do try to be charming when you find me. I bite when I'm hungry." And she dropped into the chair like she owned it, because at that table, that night, she rather did.

Aria settled half a step back, opening the space between us like a curtain drawn. "So — no script from me for this part. I only do the introductions; the rest is hers to say and yours to answer." She lifted her cup, her eyes bright. "There. Properly introduced, the both of you. Now go on — the cloth's about to fall, and she's waiting. Go and meet her."

---

The candles thinned, and the bank came back, and the cloth was in the mud, and she was rising in the open cage on unsteady legs — feral, starving, free for the first time in who knew how long, her amber eyes moving from the carcasses of her captors to the strangers who had made them carcasses. The sound that came out of her was not a word; it was low and cracked, more growl than voice, scraping up a throat that had forgotten how. Her legs would not hold straight, but she was up, swaying, one black-scaled hand white-knuckled on the bars she had been caged behind a breath before. She did not look at our faces. She looked at the meat — and something in her kindled, hot and shameless. She took one staggering step toward the dead men over the lip of the cage as if the rest of us were not there at all. Then her eyes snapped up, amber and slitted, and she showed me her teeth — not a smile, a warning. "Don't," she rasped, the first whole word she'd shaped in longer than she could count. Her breath sawed. "Don't — touch — what's mine." She did not seem to know which of us she warned. All of us. She had not yet decided whether free meant safe; it never had before.

I should perhaps have been wary. Instead I was, on the instant, smitten — her fierceness and her beauty pulling at me in a way I had not known was possible. I held the last thug down under my foot, and I looked her in the eyes, and I stood up tall, and I bowed. A full butler's bow, one arm folded at the waist, the whole picture of courtly grace ruined gloriously by the man whimpering beneath my boot, and I offered her the feast she had been baring her teeth to keep. "My lady," I said, in my best butler's voice, "dinner is served."

The silence that followed was a different kind than the one Tygryth had cast.

She froze. I watched her read me for the trick — the offered hand is the one that closes, the open door is the deepest part of the cage — and find none. She found only a man bowing, as if she were something worth the bend of his spine, and that landed stranger and harder on her than any blow. A sound caught in her throat, half a snarl that did not finish, because the snarl had nothing to push against. So she did the only honest thing in her: she dropped down off the cage-lip into the cold mud beside the meat, low and coiled, and she ate — fast, graceless, both scaled hands, the way a thing eats that has learned food gets taken back. But she did not look down. She kept her eyes on me the whole time, amber and unblinking over the kill, watching for the moment the smile would turn. It did not. Between one tearing mouthful and the next, low, wary, almost an accusation: "Why." She swallowed. "Why give it. You don't know me."

Tygryth made a sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite a growl — the noise an ancient thing makes when a mortal does something so audacious it loops back around to charming. His molten-gold eyes went from me, to the dead men, to his newfound blood, and back. "Three hundred years," he said slowly, "and I have never once seen a man court a dragon by laying her enemies at her feet like a cat with a kill." Then the protectiveness surfaced, old and immense and freshly woken. "She is mine, monk. Newly mine. I have buried more of my children than you will ever meet, and I find I am particular, suddenly, about this one." But the wry edge would not quite leave him. "Though I will allow — the bow was very good. Do not let it go to your head. Either of yours."

My eyes went serious as I answered her, and direct. "What these men and those like them did to you can have no excuse, no justification, no redemption. I am glad to help them perform one last service for you, as the smallest partial restitution for their crimes." Then, more softly: "And you looked hungry. I'm afraid I have never been able to deny a woman what her heart desired. It is a weakness of mine."

The first half she understood. I could see it — restitution was a thing she had teeth for, and something in her chest unclenched a half-turn it had been locked at so long she'd stopped feeling the grip. She bared her teeth at the dead man under her hands, but it was different now, less guard than agreement, and tore another mouthful like she was collecting a debt. The second half slid off her, because she had nowhere to put it. She bristled, eyes narrowing, a low rough scrape of sound in her throat. "Don't," she said. "Don't make it pretty. The justice—" she gestured with a bloodied hand at the corpse "—that, I take. That's clean. But the other thing—" she could not even name it "—that's how the soft cages start. A kind word. A warm hand. Then the door." Her voice cracked, and she plainly hated that it did. Quieter, almost to herself, still watching me as if I might yet turn: "I don't know how to be wanted and not owned. I never learned the difference. So go slow. Or don't — but know I'm watching for the catch." And she kept eating. But she did not look away from me once.

Tygryth exhaled through his nose, slow, the sound of a very old creature deciding how much rope to give. "A weakness," he repeated, dry as ash. "Yes. I can see precisely how much of a weakness it is." His gold eyes rested on me, heavy, amused, and underneath the amusement something with teeth. "I freed you of nothing, monk — you did that yourself. So understand I say this as a friend and not a debtor: that one has been in a cage. Be certain what you offer her is a door, and not another collar with a kinder voice." A pause, and the wry curl returned despite him. "That said. You feed her, you bow, you bleed for strangers and call it a weakness. I begin to see why the gods that wrote me down made me come down to live among your kind. Insufferable. Carry on."

Wren, crouched at the cage edge with the kin-fire vessel still hugged to her chest, watched the black-scaled girl tear into her grim feast and muttered to me sidelong, half scandal and half wonder. "I found a cousin, freed a dragon, and you're already making eyes at her over a pile of corpses. Is every day with you people going to be like this?" A beat, softer, her eyes still on her newfound blood. "Don't answer. Let her eat."

So I let her eat. The river ran bright, the far shore waited under its haze, and for one long breath the whole bank kept its own counsel while a starving daughter of dragons filled herself, free.
