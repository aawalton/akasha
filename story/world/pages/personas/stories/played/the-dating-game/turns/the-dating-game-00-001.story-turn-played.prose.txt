Saturday comes in through the blinds on Apple Avenue in thin gold bars across the bed. No alarm went off; you didn't set one. The house has the particular quiet of a place one person lives in: one mug in the drying rack, one coat on the hook, one pair of shoes kicked off by the door last night. Nothing is on the calendar. Nobody is expecting you anywhere. Monday won't be any different: you're retired, the money is settled, and nothing is left that needs you by a particular hour.

You cook breakfast in bare feet, the tile cold, butter hissing in the pan. Out the kitchen window the street is already up. A sprinkler chatters across the lawn next door. A kid on a bike coasts past with a fishing pole across the handlebars, headed for the river. Above the rooftops Y Mountain holds the morning, the white letter bright on the brown slope, and for a moment the light along the ridge runs the wrong way, climbing instead of falling, as if the mountain has breathed out. Then it's only sunlight again.

That's Provo, and you stopped remarking on it years ago. Some mornings the canyon wind comes down smelling of somewhere else entirely. Now and then a shape goes over high up, too big for any hawk, and nobody on the sidewalk bothers to look. People here are sometimes more than they seem, and most of them are simply getting on with their Saturday.

The eggs set at the edges and the toast pops up. Outside, the whole valley is warming under a clean blue sky.
