The sun goes down behind the hills and you walk out to the gate in the last of the light. The lamp sits in its iron bracket where it has probably sat for three hundred years, and you light it — one small flame, and the gate posts go warm and gold, and the message is sent down the hill to anyone with eyes: the door is open.

You walk back up the path to where Amy waits in the doorway, and take your place beside her — a Master and his tender of the web, side by side, table laid, nothing to prove. The house glows behind you. The evening settles blue.

And before long there's a figure on the path — unhurried, straight-backed, coming up through the gorse with a basket on one arm. She passes the lit lamp, and you'd swear she dips her head to it, just slightly, the way you'd greet a person.

Aelwyn, dressed for an evening. The Greenreach field kit is gone, the garden washed away. She wears a long gown of deep forest green cut on plain, clean lines — closer to a good riding-coat than a court dress, wool with a low sheen, no train to catch on gorse, a warden's freedom of movement kept in every seam. A soft brown underlayer shows at the throat and cuffs; a narrow worked-leather belt carries a single small knife she didn't quite leave behind, softened almost to jewelry. Her boots are the good pair, cleaned to oxblood. The working half-braid has been let out and redone: auburn-chestnut hair washed and loose down her back, with one fine braid crossing the crown to hold it from her face — the nearest thing to a coronet she will ever wear, and pointedly not one. Emerald eyes bright in the lamplight, strong dark brows, no paint she doesn't already carry. Her one adornment is a small carved-wood pin at her shoulder, leaf-work, clearly made by hand and not bought. In the basket: the garden's last good greens, washed and crisp and arranged the way you arrange a thing you're proud of — and tucked beside them, three rooted cuttings wrapped in damp cloth.

At the doorstone she stops — one breath, before she steps through — the way you pause at any threshold that has weight to it. Then she comes in the proper way, and lets you both see that she knows what it costs her to.

"I've stood at this house a hundred times and never once at its door." Warm, plainly honest, the emerald eyes going once around the lit hall and back to the two of you. "Every other visit I climbed the wall. This is — new."

She lifts the basket a little, the sunrise breaking through.

"The last good greens out of the garden — yours or mine, we'll settle whose over supper." A glint of the tease. "And three cuttings that want rooting before the cold. A gardener doesn't come to a garden empty-handed — even when the garden's suddenly hers to walk into." A nod, warden-courteous, to Amy first and then to you. "Amy. Master of Hearthholt. Thank you for the lamp — I saw it lit from the bottom of the path."
