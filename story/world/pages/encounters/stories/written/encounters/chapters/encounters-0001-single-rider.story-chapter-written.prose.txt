The single-rider line for the VelociCoaster runs down a long gray switchback of chain and steel, half in shade, and at half past one on a Thursday you have most of it to yourself. Heat comes up off the concrete. Somewhere above the trees the train screams through its top hat, and a second later the riders on it scream too, and the noise rolls back down over the queue and fades. The regular line, on the other side of a fence, is a wall of families and teenagers and damp backpacks, and the sign at its mouth said ninety-five minutes. You walked past it with your hands in your pockets and turned in under the small sign that says SINGLE RIDER, and now you stand alone at a bend in the rail, sweat at the small of your back, in no hurry at all.

Footsteps slap on the concrete behind you, quick and light, and stop.

A girl ducks under the last chain and lands in the line a step behind you, breathing hard, as if she ran the whole length of the Jurassic Park path to get here. She has a water bottle in one hand, a big scuffed one with stickers on it, half full and sweating, and she puts the back of her wrist to her forehead and blows out a breath.

You look, because she is right there and it would be stranger not to. She is slim and athletic, built light, with light olive skin gone gold in the sun. She wears a matching set in pale sky blue: a square-necked sports bra that leaves her whole stomach bare, flat and toned, and high-waisted compression shorts that stop at mid-thigh and hug every line of her. Her chest is small under the blue. Her hair is jet black, long and sleek, pulled up into a high braided ponytail that swings when she moves and lies down between her shoulder blades when she stops. Dark brows, dark almond eyes, almost no makeup. She looks about twenty. She looks like she could run the line again if she had to.

She pulls a phone from the waistband of her shorts, thumbs it awake, and checks something. Her mouth moves.

"Ninety-five," she says, under her breath, to the phone, and a small fierce satisfaction comes into her face. "Ninety-five. Good."

"Are you racing someone?" you ask.

She looks up as if the rail had spoken. Color comes up into her cheeks, fast, all the way from her collarbones.

"Kind of," she says. "My friends took the long line. I told them single rider would be faster and they said it wouldn't, and I said, okay, bet, and I ran." She holds up the bottle a little, as if that explains the rest. "So I have to ride first. That's the whole bet."

Then she meets your eyes, straight on, and smiles. It is a small smile, shy and sweet, a little crooked at one corner, and it lasts exactly as long as she holds your look. A beat later her eyes drop to the rail between you and stay there, and she takes a drink of water to have something to do with her mouth.

The line shuffles forward a few yards. You move with it, and she moves with you.

For a while she is all short answers. You ask if she is local, and she says, "Gainesville." You ask who she came with, and she says, "Three friends." You ask how long they are here, and she says, "Fall break," and then, after a second, as if she worries that was rude, "Like four nights." Each answer comes out quick and polite and then stops, and she looks at the queue ahead, or at the coaster track overhead, or at her water bottle, anywhere but at you for long. She stands a step back from you with her weight on one hip and her free hand tucked into the crook of her elbow.

"I'm in Orlando for a few days," you tell her. "Staying at the Royal Pacific."

"Oh, nice," she says. "That's the fancy one. With the, like, bamboo."

"That's the one."

She nods and looks at the track.

At the next bend there is a drinking fountain bolted to the wall, set back in a little alcove of fake rock. She stops in front of it, unscrews her bottle, and holds it under the arc of water, patient, while the line ahead of you opens up by ten feet. You stop too, and wait at the turn of the rail with your hands on the chain. When the bottle is full she screws the lid on and turns, and finds you still there. She looks at the empty concrete in front of you, and then at you.

"You didn't have to wait," she says.

"I'm not in a hurry," you say. "You're the one with a bet."

That gets the edge of a laugh out of her, quick, through her nose, and she ducks her head and walks past you to close the gap.

Something loosens after that. Maybe it is the ten minutes. You have been in the line ten minutes, and she has been standing close enough that her braid brushes your arm when she turns her head. She looks at you sideways, then fully.

"Okay," she says. "How many coasters have you ridden? Like, total. Lifetime."

You think about it and give her a number.

Her eyebrows go up. "That's it?"

"That's a lot."

"That is not a lot. That's like a starter number. That's a number for someone who goes on vacation once and rides the, the little dragon one, with the kids." She is grinning now, the shyness slipping. "Do you count the ones you closed your eyes on? Because if you count those, I don't know, man."

"I don't close my eyes."

"Everybody says that." She has the phone out again, and she thumbs into it, fast, and tips the screen toward you. "Look. Look at this."

It is a note in her phone. A long list, scrolling and scrolling: ride names, park names, and after each one a number out of ten. Some of them have little comments in parentheses. *(rough on the neck). (the BEST, ride it twice). (overrated, sorry). (cried, good way).* She scrolls with her thumb so you can watch it go by, and it goes by for a while.

"Every ride I've ever ridden," she says. "Rated. Since I was like twelve."

"That's a lot of rides."

"That's a real number." She takes the phone back, pleased with herself, and points at the track overhead with it. "This one's going on there in like forty minutes. If it's a ten, I'll tell you."

From there she talks. It comes out fast now, words tumbling over each other, and she laughs before she gets to the end of her own jokes, so that the punchline comes out half swallowed and she has to say it again. She tells you about a coaster in Ohio that she swears tried to kill her. She tells you a wooden coaster in Georgia rattled her so hard she bit her tongue and bled, and she still rated it an eight, because it was fun. She asks you which ride you would ride if you could only ride one more ride ever, and argues with your answer before you finish giving it. Her hands move when she talks. The water bottle swings.

"Okay," she says, as the queue tips you up a ramp and into the loading building, under the steel ribs of the roof, where the noise of the launch is huge and close. "Okay. You know it launches, right? Like, twice. From nothing. Super fast."

"I've heard."

"Bet I don't scream."

You look at her.

"I'm serious," she says. "First launch. Total silence. I'm a rock." She sets her jaw to show you the rock. "If I scream, I buy you a butterbeer. If I don't, you buy me one."

"Deal," you say, and she holds out her hand, and you shake on it. Her palm is cool and damp from the bottle, her grip firm, and she pumps your hand once, hard, like a girl who has made a lot of bets.

At the front of the single-rider queue an attendant in khaki with a headset looks down at a row that has two empty seats in it, side by side, and then at the two of you.

"Two?" she says.

"We're not together," the girl says quickly, and then, "but yes, two," and goes red again.

The attendant waves you both through. You climb into the row, her on the left and you on the right, and the lap bar comes down over your thighs with a hiss and a clunk and locks. Hers comes down too. She pulls on it twice to make sure. The train is low and open, with no shoulder harness at all, and her bare knee settles against yours as she shifts in the seat. She leaves it there. Her skin is warm. The train lurches, and rolls out of the station, slow, into the dark of a short tunnel, and ahead the track straightens toward the launch.

"Rock," she says, to herself. "Rock, rock, rock."

The train stops on the launch track. A voice says something about raptors. There is one second of quiet.

The launch hits like a door slamming you in the back. The train goes from nothing to seventy in a blur of green and steel, and the girl beside you screams, high and loud and helpless, and then the scream breaks into laughter in the middle and keeps going. Her hand shoots out and clamps on your forearm, fingers digging in, and she hangs on through the first hill, through the rollover, through the long low turn over the lagoon, shrieking and laughing at once, her braid whipping your shoulder. You are laughing too. The second launch shoots you up the top hat and over, and for a moment there is no weight at all, just sky and her hand on your arm, and then you are diving, and the whole ride is roaring wind and screaming and her.

The train hisses into the station. The lap bars pop.

She doesn't let go of your arm until you are both on your feet.

On the exit ramp, still breathing hard, with her bottle tucked under one elbow and her braid half undone at the end, she says, "Okay. That doesn't count."

"It counts."

"It does not count. That was a launch scream. That's involuntary. That's like, that's a reflex. You can't bet on a reflex." She is walking backward down the ramp in front of you so she can argue to your face. "It's like betting someone they won't blink when you clap at them."

"You said first launch. You said total silence. You said rock."

"I said rock," she admits.

"You screamed."

"I *laughed*."

"You screamed and then you laughed."

She stops walking backward. She looks up at you, and her mouth twists, and she lets out a long breath through her nose.

"Fine," she says. "Fine. I owe you a butterbeer." Her hand is still on your arm, you realize; she has had it there since the station, loosely, the way a person holds a railing. She doesn't take it off. "I never leave a bet unpaid. Ask anybody."

"Who would I ask?"

The answer arrives before she can give it. At the bottom of the exit, by the gift shop doors, three girls come out of the regular exit at once and stop, and one of them points.

"There she is!"

The one pointing is tall, five ten at least, long-limbed and slim, with a pale freckled face and a wide grin and a mess of thick copper-red hair piled up in a high bun, loose strands of it stuck to her neck. She wears an oversized faded sage green tee that falls to mid-thigh and has slid off one bare freckled shoulder, and white sneakers. If she has shorts on under the tee, the tee hides them. Behind her, small and neat, comes a girl barely five feet tall with warm brown skin and round tortoiseshell glasses, a small gold hoop in her nose, sharp winged liner, her black hair pulled into a high tight ponytail. She wears a black string bikini top and tiny black shorts, her midriff bare. The third is tall too, slim and calm, with very long straight black hair hanging loose to her waist, parted in the center, gold hoops in her ears, a white ribbed halter top tied behind her neck, a short black pleated skirt. A film camera hangs on a strap around her neck.

The tall redhead comes down the last of the ramp and stops in front of you and looks you up and down, slowly, all the way down and all the way back up, with her green eyes narrowed.

"Okay," she says to you. "I see you. Just so you know. I'm looking."

"I noticed," you say.

"Good." Then she turns to the girl in blue, and raises her voice so that a family coming out of the gift shop turns its heads. "Sofia. Babe. Does single rider come with a man every time? Is that the deal? Because I'll do single rider."

Sofia goes crimson. It goes all the way down her throat to the square neck of the sports bra.

"Kenzie," she says. "Shut *up*."

"I'm just asking about the policy."

The small one in the glasses has been watching you this whole time, steady, over the top of her frames. She steps up beside Kenzie.

"What's your name?" she asks.

"Alan."

"Alan," she repeats, once, quietly, the way someone files a thing they mean to keep. She says nothing else. Her eyes stay on you.

The girl with the long hair lifts the film camera from her chest, without a word, and raises it, and frames Sofia standing beside you with her hand still on your arm. The shutter clicks, a small mechanical sound. She winds the film on with her thumb and lets the camera drop back down on its strap, and her face stays calm and unreadable through all of it.

"Hana!" Sofia says. "Warn a person."

Hana only looks at her.

Sofia turns to her friends, and her chin goes up a notch. "I lost a bet," she announces. "I owe him a butterbeer."

"Then go pay it," Kenzie says at once. She winks at Sofia, one big slow wink, and Sofia's face goes redder still. Then Kenzie turns back to you and points at your chest, a finger an inch from your sternum. "And you. If she comes back unhappy, I will find you."

She swings the finger toward the path. "Okay. Seuss. We have a schedule. Priya, where's the map. Hana, stop taking pictures of strangers. Let's go, let's go."

She herds them off, a hand on each of the other two girls' backs, the green tee flapping, and the three of them go off down the path toward the bright candy colors of Seuss Landing, Kenzie talking the whole way. Priya looks back at you once over her glasses. Then the crowd closes behind them.

Sofia lets out a breath and looks up at you, and laughs, embarrassed and a little defiant.

"So that was them," she says.

"That was them."

"Kenzie's harmless," she says. "Mostly. Come on. Butterbeer's this way."

You walk into Hogsmeade together a little after two. The path bends, and the jungle green of Jurassic Park gives way to snowy roofs, crooked chimneys, a steam train standing at the station, frost painted on the shop windows in ninety-degree heat. Over the top of it all the castle rises up on its crag, gray stone and turrets against a hard blue sky, and the crowd thickens around you as you pass under it. Sofia tips her head back to look at the towers and almost walks into a stroller. You put a hand on her bare back, just above the waistband of her shorts, to steer her around it, and she glances at you and doesn't say anything, and you take the hand away.

At the butterbeer cart the line is ten deep. She buys two frozen ones with her own card before you can get your wallet out, and turns around holding a plastic cup in each hand, the slush in them pale gold and topped with a thick cream, and holds one out to you at arm's length, ceremonial.

"Bet paid," she says. "In full. Witnessed." She waves at the cart, the line, the man who sold them to her. "Everybody saw."

"Paid in full," you agree, and take it, and her fingers brush yours on the cold cup.

You find a bit of shade by a stone wall and drink them. The butterbeer is cold enough to hurt and sweet as candy, and it gives her a cream mustache that she doesn't know about until you tell her, and then she wipes it off with the back of her wrist and laughs so hard she has to lean on you. Her hand lands on your arm. She leaves it there for the end of the laugh, and then takes it back, and a minute later, when you say something dry about the frost on the windows, she laughs again and her hand is back on your arm. After that she touches you every time she laughs, and she laughs a lot. She touches your arm to make a point. She touches your wrist to get you to look at something. She bumps your shoulder with hers when you tease her.

She asks questions. She asks them one after another, quick, like she is filling in a form about you. What you do for work. Where you grew up. Whether you have ever been on a cruise. What the worst food you have ever eaten is. Whether you are a morning person. Every answer you give her, she turns over and finds something in it to tease.

"You're a morning person," she says, disgusted. "Of course you are. You probably make your bed."

"I do make my bed."

"With the corners. I bet you do the corners."

"What's wrong with corners?"

"Nothing's *wrong* with corners. It's just very—" She does a thing with her hands, squaring off a box in the air. "Very corners."

You ask her a question back, finally, and she tells you about diving.

"Springboard," she says. "I did it competitively. From when I was nine all the way through high school. Every morning before school, in a pool that was so cold. Like, so cold. Then meets every weekend." She takes a long pull of the butterbeer through the straw. "I quit when I was eighteen."

"Why?"

She shrugs one shoulder. "I was done. I wanted to have a Saturday." She looks at the slush in her cup. "I miss it sometimes. The feeling when you hit one right. You know before you're in the water. You just know."

She wants to be a physical therapist, she tells you. She works the front desk at a rec center on campus, scanning IDs and handing out towels and telling guys to wipe down the bench press, and in the slow hours she studies at the desk. She says it the way people say things they have decided for good: plainly, without dressing it up.

She tells a story about a man at the rec center who tried to argue his way past the desk without an ID, and how she won, and she starts laughing at the end of it before she gets there, and has to stop and start again, and she laughs so hard she snorts, and claps her hand over her mouth, appalled.

"You have the sweetest laugh," you tell her.

She goes red. It comes up all at once, the way it did in the line, all over her face and throat. She looks at you, and opens her mouth, and you can see her look for something to say, and not find a thank you anywhere.

"Staring contest," she says instead. "Right now. Right here."

"What?"

"Staring contest. Winner picks the next ride." She sets her cup on the stone wall and squares up to you, chin raised, eyes wide open. "Go."

You look at her. She looks at you. Her eyes are dark brown, nearly black in the shade, and very steady. A family goes past. A man in a wizard's robe goes past, sweating. Her mouth starts to twitch at one corner, and she presses her lips together hard to stop it, and her eyes start to water, and she holds them open, and holds them, and her nose wrinkles, and then she blinks, and bursts out laughing, and grabs your arm.

"No," she says. "No. Okay. Rematch. I want a rematch. Later. That one doesn't count."

"That one counts."

"It counts," she agrees, "but I want a rematch. You pick."

You pick Hagrid's.

The line for Hagrid's is the longest in the resort, and the board at the entrance says seventy-five minutes, and it is longer than that. It winds up through a dim stone courtyard and into the greenhouses and the creature pens, past cages that rattle and growl, and the two of you talk the whole way. She tells you about her family. She is the oldest of three, she says, and her house is the loudest house on the street. She asks you about places you have traveled and wants to know which one you would go back to. She points at the things in the cages and makes up names for them. She refills her water bottle at a fountain in the queue and offers it to you, and you drink from it, and she watches you do it. When the line stalls in a hot stone room for ten minutes, she leans back against the wall and against your arm, and stays there.

The ride is a motorbike with a sidecar, and she takes the bike, and you fold yourself into the sidecar, and then it launches out into the trees. Halfway through, the track runs into a dead end in the dark, and the bike stops, and something snarls, and the floor gives way, and the whole train drops backward into the black, and Sofia shrieks and her hand comes back over the side of the bike and finds yours and grabs it, hard. She keeps it. Through the backward run, through the second launch, through the swooping turns and the forest and the hill, until the bikes slide back into the station and stop. Then, slowly, she lets go.

On the way out she takes out her phone and opens the note and types into it with her thumb, quick, and turns the screen to you before you are out of the gift shop.

*Hagrid's Magical Creatures Motorbike Adventure (IOA). 10. (sidecar guy screamed).*

"I didn't scream."

"You definitely screamed."

"You grabbed my hand."

"For balance," she says, and pockets the phone, and doesn't look at you, and is smiling.

Out on the midway by the lagoon, past the cartoon colors of Toon Lagoon, there is a row of carnival games, bright and loud, with stuffed animals hanging from the rafters. A basketball toss, the hoops set high and a little too small. She stops in front of it.

"You're on. What's the stake?"

"Within reason."

"Nothing illegal." She takes a ball in both hands and squares her feet. "Mostly nothing."

"That's it?"

"That's a lot of fountains."
