You wake on your back.

Velvet under your palms, soft as moss and warm from your own body. A velvet chaise longue, you find when you lift your head, and you are lying on it fully dressed, shoes and all, as if you sat down for a moment and the moment went on for hours. You try to remember sitting down. You try to remember the street outside, the door, the drive here, the word *hotel* said aloud by anyone at all. There is a clean white blank where all of it should be, and the chaise under you, and the ceiling above.

The ceiling is far up, coffered in dark wood, and a crystal chandelier hangs from the centre of it, a slow cascade of cut glass throwing the light back on itself in a thousand small pieces. Lower down, green-shaded lamps glow on the side tables, each one pooling its light on polished wood and leaving the corners amber and deep.

It is a lobby. A grand old hotel's lobby, the kind built when money wanted to be seen: dark panelled wood, brass polished until it shines like honey, oxblood velvet on every seat. Beside you stands a leather sofa, cracked soft with age, and a brass luggage cart a few steps off from it. Before a wide stone hearth lies a thick rug, and the hearth itself is cold, swept clean, not so much as a scrap of ash in the grate.

Across the lobby the revolving front doors stand still. Through their glass, where the street should be, there is brick. Bare red brick, laid flush against the panes, mortar lines neat as ruled paper. Your eyes go to the windows along the walls, tall and handsome, one after another. Every one is the same. Glass, and brick behind the glass. There is no daylight anywhere, no night sky either, only the lamps and the chandelier and the brick.

At the back of the lobby a grand staircase rises, broad carpeted steps climbing up into darkness. A brass gate closes it off at the foot, its bars shut, a heavy lock hanging at the latch. Beside the staircase is a lift with a brass door, handsome as everything else here, and beside the lift door, where the call buttons belong, there is only smooth wood.

The front desk runs along one side, a long counter of black marble with a brass service bell resting on it. Behind it rises a wall of pigeonholes for room keys, dozens of little wooden compartments, and every one of them is empty.

At the desk stands a woman.

She is tall, and pale, and strikingly beautiful, somewhere in her late twenties, in a black concierge's suit cut close to her body, the jacket fitted at the waist, the lines of it severe. Her black hair is pinned up in a sleek chignon without a strand out of place. Her eyes are a cool grey, and her mouth is painted red, the only warm colour on her.

Much closer, leaning one hip on the luggage cart a few steps from your chaise, is another woman, and she is everything the first one is not. Petite and slim, mid-twenties, pretty, freckled across every bit of skin you can see. A copper-red bob sits under a little red pillbox cap. Her eyes are green, and her short red bellhop's jacket sits snug over small, perky tits, the brass buttons running in a neat line down her front. A short red skirt hugs slim hips and a small, pert ass.

She is the one who sees your eyes open.

"Oh, he's *up*," she says, and looks down at you with a grin that shows a gap between her front teeth. "Guest's up. I'd started to think he was part of the furniture."

The concierge does not smile. "Mind your manners," she says from the desk, dry as paper.

The bellhop laughs, a big bright laugh that fills the room. "My manners are the only thing in this building that isn't stiff."

The concierge lets that pass. She turns her grey eyes on you instead, and they settle there, cool and level.

"Good evening, sir," she says. "Welcome to the hotel."

Her voice is low and even, every word set down in its place.

"When you are on your feet," she says, formal and even, "I will check you in."
