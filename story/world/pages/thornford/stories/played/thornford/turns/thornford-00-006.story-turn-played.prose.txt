"All right, I'll see you tomorrow Shaestrel."

She smiles at that, quick and pleased, and steps out into the rain. The door shuts behind her.

You shoot the bolt home again and turn the lamp up.

The measurements are all in the book, in pencil. You pin brown paper to the cutting table and draft the gown from them: bodice, sleeves, the long panels of the skirt, chalking and rubbing out until the lines sit right. Then you pin the paper pieces to unbleached calico and cut a toile for her to stand in tomorrow.

The treadle machine rattles under the lamp, and the rain keeps on against the window. You tack the calico together seam by seam with long loose stitches, easy to pull out once she's tried it on. By the time it hangs from the hook on the back-room door, the lane outside has gone dark and quiet, and your shoulders ache.

Before you bank the lamp, you thread a fine steel needle and take up the cut end of the silk to try a stitch where it won't show.

The point won't go in. Under it the weave draws tight, the way skin flinches, and the needle slides off.

You press harder. The needle bends in your fingers. The silk lies smooth under the lamp, without a mark on it.
