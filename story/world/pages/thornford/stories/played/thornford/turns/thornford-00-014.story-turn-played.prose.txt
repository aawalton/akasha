You draw the bolt and open the door.

Shaestrel stands on the step in the first sun, the hood of her cloak down, with a loaf in the crook of her arm. It's still warm. You can smell it from the doorway.

"Knocked," she says. "Like a mortal."

She comes in past you, breaks the loaf open on the end of the cutting table and hands you the heel.

You eat standing up and get to work. The sun comes in across the floor as you tack the bodice pieces together, and the silk goes green wherever it lies in the light. The needle goes through it like a thought.

She watches you for a while from the stool by the treadle machine, tearing bread into small pieces. Then she stands and shakes the crumbs from her cloak.

"I'll come back at lamp-lighting, for the silk," she says, and goes out by the door, into the full sun, like anyone else on the Row.

You sew all day. Bodice first, then the sleeves set in at the shoulder where you moved the seam, then the long panels of the skirt, joined one to the next and gathered in at the waist. The treadle rattles. The Row goes past outside in the dry, and children shout down by the river. You stop once to eat the rest of the bread.

By evening the gown hangs whole from the hook on the back-room door. Only the hem is left, pinned and waiting to be set on her.

When the light through the window turns low and gold, you light the lamps.

Three knocks, light and quick. Shaestrel comes in, pushing her hood back, and stops just inside the door with her eyes on the gown.
