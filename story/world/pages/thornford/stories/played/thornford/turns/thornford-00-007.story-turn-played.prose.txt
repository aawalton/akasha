You sleep in the back room with the rain on the roof.

Grey light comes in at the window, and the rain is still at it. You put the kettle on and go back to the table.

You go over the paper pattern line by line against the figures in the book, trueing each seam with the long rule until it meets its partner exactly. You let the toile out at the hip by a hair, where you can see it would pull. Then you press the silk. It takes the iron's heat without a mark and lies flat on the cutting table, grey and green, ready for the shears once she's stood in the calico.

By noon you have your finest needles sorted into a paper, and a reel of silk thread so close to the grey-green that you have to hold them side by side to tell them apart.

The rain keeps on all afternoon. The Row goes by outside in hoods and pattens. When the rain-dark gets too deep to sew by, you light the lamps.

The bell above the door rings.

The bolt is shot home. The door opens anyway, and Shaestrel comes in out of the wet, shaking the rain off her hood, with fresh birch leaves in her hair.

She looks past you at the calico hanging on the back-room door.

"Is that what I'm to stand in?" she says. "It's the colour of porridge."

She unpins her cloak.
