Six strikes somewhere down toward the market as your last customer leaves with her hemmed skirt. You shoot the bolt behind her, turn the card in the window and sweep the loose threads off the cutting table into your palm. Out on Weavers' Row the rain has thickened, and two doors along someone is dragging their shutters to.

The rent book sits on the shelf under the counter, open at the page you'd rather not read again. It's dim enough under the rain that you light the lamp over the treadle machine.

The bell above the door rings.

The bolt is still shot home in its bracket. The door stands open anyway, letting in the smell of the river and wet cobbles, and a woman steps over the sill and pushes it shut behind her with her heel.

She's small and quick, in a grey cloak beaded with rain, the hood pushed back. Her hair is dark green and falls long past her shoulders, with fresh birch leaves pinned through it. Where her ear comes to a point, the lamp shows pink through it. Her eyes are greener than the leaves.

She carries a bolt of cloth in both arms, the way you'd carry a sleeping cat.

"You'll be the seamstress," she says, in the lilt of the hill farms above the valley. "Nala."

Her eyes go to the rent book under the counter, and back to you.

"Narrow shop, red hair, behind on the rent, they told me."

She crosses to the cutting table, sets the bolt down and unrolls a turn of it with the flat of her hand. It's silk, grey-green like the underside of a willow leaf, and so fine the lamplight sinks into it. It hisses softly over the scarred wood and your chalk marks.

"I need a gown for the Beltane dance," she says. "Made by mortal hands, every stitch of it, so it's still a gown when the sun comes up. You've three days. I'll not be paying you in coin."

She smooths the silk once more and looks up at you, her mouth shut on a smile.

"I've something better than coin, if you'll hear it."
