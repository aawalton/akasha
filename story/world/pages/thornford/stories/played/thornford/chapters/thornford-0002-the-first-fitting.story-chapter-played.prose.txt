You sleep in the back room with the rain on the roof.

Grey light comes in at the window, and the rain is still at it. You put the kettle on and go back to the table.

You go over the paper pattern line by line against the figures in the book, trueing each seam with the long rule until it meets its partner exactly. You let the toile out at the hip by a hair, where you can see it would pull. Then you press the silk. It takes the iron's heat without a mark and lies flat on the cutting table, grey and green, ready for the shears once she's stood in the calico.

By noon you have your finest needles sorted into a paper, and a reel of silk thread so close to the grey-green that you have to hold them side by side to tell them apart.

The rain keeps on all afternoon. The Row goes by outside in hoods and pattens. When the rain-dark gets too deep to sew by, you light the lamps.

The bell above the door rings.

The bolt is shot home. The door opens anyway, and Shaestrel comes in out of the wet, shaking the rain off her hood, with fresh birch leaves in her hair.

She looks past you at the calico hanging on the back-room door.

"Is that what I'm to stand in?" she says. "It's the colour of porridge."

She unpins her cloak.

[A fix to the last turn: Shaestrel comes in at half past eight.]

"Porridge is great. My goal is to make you look delicious anyways."

You wink.

Shaestrel laughs, a quick bright sound, and her eyes go over you.

"Delicious," she says slowly, as if she's tasting it.

She hooks one finger through your apron string and draws you a step closer, near enough that you smell cut grass and rain. Then she lets go, reaches up and tugs the cord at each shoulder. Her dress slides down her and lands about her feet as a drift of fresh birch leaves.

She steps out of them and lifts the calico off its hook on the back-room door herself.

"Dress me, then, seamstress," she says, and holds it out to you.

You ease the calico over her head and settle it on her shoulders. It's rough against her skin, and she wrinkles her nose at it, then holds still.

You start at the shoulders. The seam sits a little forward, so you unpick a few tacking stitches and pin it back, and once the pin is in your fingers trail on down to her collarbone. At the sides you take it in a touch under the arm, then smooth the calico down over her ribs with the flat of your hand, slower than the work needs.

She lets you. When you pin the waist, your hands stay there a breath after the last pin is in.

You kneel to pin the hem at the floor and run your palm down the back of her calf to straighten the fall of the cloth. She hums the same low quick tune as last night, and it catches when your hand reaches her ankle.

You come back up to check the waist.

She lays her hand over yours against the calico. Her fingers are cool.

"You've pinned this waist twice, seamstress."

She keeps your hand where it is.

"Can't be too sure its fitting right. I could check it all again, more thoroughly..."

"More thoroughly," Shaestrel says. "Now there's a fine word."

She lets go of your hand and slides the tape from around your neck, then loops it round your waist and draws it snug, her knuckles resting against your ribs. She reads the figure off with her head tipped.

"There. I have your measure now as well."

"Thoroughly can wait for the silk," she says, and tucks the tape back round your neck.

"I guess its time for me to start on the silk then."

You lift the calico off over her head, careful of the pins. She shivers once, takes her grey cloak from the stool and wraps it round herself. Then, instead of going to the door, she hitches herself up onto the end of the cutting table and tucks her bare feet under the hem.

"Go on, then," she says. "I'd like to see the first cut."

You unpick the toile seam by seam and carry every pinned change onto the paper: the shoulder set back, the sides taken in under the arm, the hem marked to the floor.

Then you unroll the silk. It runs out across the table grey and green, and you smooth it flat with both hands and pin the paper to it piece by piece. The shears are cold and heavy in your hand. The first cut goes in along the bodice with a long soft sound, and the silk falls away from the blades clean.

She watches the whole while with her chin on her knees, and her eyes follow the shears.

The lamp burns lower. When the last piece of the skirt comes free, a length of silk is left on the table, about as long as your arm.

Shaestrel slides off the table, gathers it up and drapes it over your shoulders. It's cool, and lighter than it looks.

"That suits your hair," she says.

"Why thank you. I'm afraid my hair stays with me though, so if you are interested in it as an accessory, you'll have to bring me to the ball."

Shaestrel laughs. She reaches up and winds a lock of your hair round one finger, red against the grey-green on your shoulders.

"Then I'll bring you," she says. "Across the ford, on Beltane night, to the dance."

She tugs the lock, very lightly.

"And you'll make yourself something from that length, so we match. I'll not have you on my arm in porridge."

She lets the lock slip free, takes your hand from the silk and brings it up, and presses her mouth to your knuckles.
