Six strikes somewhere down toward the market as your last customer leaves with her hemmed skirt. You shoot the bolt behind her, turn the card in the window and sweep the loose threads off the cutting table into your palm. Out on Weavers' Row the rain has thickened, and two doors along someone is dragging their shutters to.

The rent book sits on the shelf under the counter, open at the page you'd rather not read again. It's dim enough under the rain that you light the lamp over the treadle machine.

The bell above the door rings.

The bolt is still shot home in its bracket. The door stands open anyway, letting in the smell of the river and wet cobbles, and a woman steps over the sill and pushes it shut behind her with her heel.

She's small and quick, in a grey cloak beaded with rain, the hood pushed back. Her hair is dark green and falls long past her shoulders, with fresh birch leaves pinned through it. Where her ear comes to a point, the lamp shows pink through it. Her eyes are greener than the leaves.

She carries a bolt of cloth in both arms, the way you'd carry a sleeping cat.

"You'll be the seamstress," she says, in the lilt of the hill farms above the valley. "Nala."

Her eyes go to the rent book under the counter, and back to you.

"Narrow shop, red hair, behind on the rent, they told me."

She crosses to the cutting table, sets the bolt down and unrolls a turn of it with the flat of her hand. It's silk, grey-green like the underside of a willow leaf, and so fine the lamplight sinks into it. It hisses softly over the scarred wood and your chalk marks.

"I need a gown for the Beltane dance," she says. "Made by mortal hands, every stitch of it, so it's still a gown when the sun comes up. You've three days. I'll not be paying you in coin."

She smooths the silk once more and looks up at you, her mouth shut on a smile.

"I've something better than coin, if you'll hear it."

"Better than coin, eh? I'm listening."

Her smile opens a little, enough to show the edge of her teeth.

She hitches herself up onto the edge of the cutting table beside the silk, and her boots swing clear of the floor. The silk slides with her. Where it folds it turns green, and goes grey again as it settles.

"I'm of the Spring Court," she says. "You'll have guessed that from the door."

She tips her head at the bolt, still shot home in its bracket.

"So here's my bargain. You make me a gown from this silk, with your own two hands, and have it done by moonrise on Beltane night. Do that, and you may ask me for one thing."

She holds up a finger.

"And if it's not done by moonrise, you owe the Spring Court a year of sewing on our side of the ford."

She wets the pad of her thumb on her tongue and holds it out to you, the way a bargain's struck at the cattle market.

"Deal."

She laughs, a short bright sound, and presses her wet thumb to the back of your hand where it rests on the table's edge. Her skin is cool, and smells of cut grass.

For a breath the lamp flame stands up tall and still. Then it flickers on as before.

She slips down off the table, unpins the grey cloak at her throat and drops it over the stool by the treadle machine. Under it she wears a pale green dress held up by two green cords, and her arms are bare.

"You'll want my measure, I'd think."

She lifts her arms out from her sides.

[A correction to the last turn: she hasn't told you her name yet, so the telling now calls her "she" until she does.]

"Of course, but this is silk, I would never make a dress from this that was less than my best work. I'll need you to strip so I can take accurate measurements. This gown must fit you perfectly."

Her brows go up. Then she grins, and shows all her teeth at once.

"You've not even asked my name."

She tugs the bow of the green cord at her left shoulder, then the one at her right. The dress slides down her and is gone before it reaches the floor. What lands around her feet is a drift of birch leaves, fresh and green, like the ones pinned in her hair.

She steps out of them and lifts her arms from her sides again, bare in the lamplight.

You take the tape from around your neck and start where you always start, at the nape. Her skin is cool under your fingers. Nape to waist, across the shoulders, round the bust, the waist, the hips, shoulder to wrist with the arm bent. You read each figure off the tape and write it into the book on the cutting table in pencil. She hums while you work, something low and quick that you don't know.

When you kneel to take the length from waist to floor, the leaves crackle under your knee.

She looks down at you over her shoulder.

"Have you thought yet what you'll ask me for?"

A birch leaf slips out of her hair and spins down past your face.

"I'm definitely thinking, but it won't be your name. If you want me to know it, you'll have to tell me. I have a feeling it will be something you will quite enjoy."

She laughs down at you, bright and sudden.

"Shaestrel," she says. "You may have that one for nothing."

She crouches among the leaves until her face is level with yours, close enough that you smell cut grass again. Her eyes go over your face slowly, the way you'd run a seam for a dropped stitch.

"Enjoy it, will I," she says. "Hm. You'll ask me for something with a door in it."

She stands, takes the grey cloak from the stool and swings it round her shoulders, and the leaves stay where they fell.

"I'll come back tomorrow when the lamps are lit, for the first fitting. Have something for me to stand in."

At the door she slides the bolt back with one finger.

"All right, I'll see you tomorrow Shaestrel."

She smiles at that, quick and pleased, and steps out into the rain. The door shuts behind her.

You shoot the bolt home again and turn the lamp up.

The measurements are all in the book, in pencil. You pin brown paper to the cutting table and draft the gown from them: bodice, sleeves, the long panels of the skirt, chalking and rubbing out until the lines sit right. Then you pin the paper pieces to unbleached calico and cut a toile for her to stand in tomorrow.

The treadle machine rattles under the lamp, and the rain keeps on against the window. You tack the calico together seam by seam with long loose stitches, easy to pull out once she's tried it on.

By the time it hangs from the hook on the back-room door, the lane outside has gone dark and quiet, and your shoulders ache.

The silk lies folded on the cutting table under the lamp, grey where the light is flat on it and green along the folds.
