On Sunday you wake before dawn in Bea's bed.

It's dark in 15. Darker than your room; her curtains are thicker. The radiator is ticking, and the room smells of her, warm, of her sheets and her skin and the tea gone cold in the mug on the floor. Her bare arm is over you, heavy with sleep. Her face is pressed into your hair, and her breath is slow and warm on the back of your neck.

You lie still a while, and feel her breathing.

Then you ease out from under her arm. Slowly. An inch at a time.

She stirs anyway.

"Rock?" she mumbles. Into the pillow, eyes shut.

"Rock," you whisper.

"Sunday," says Bea. Her hand pats the warm place where you were, vaguely. "Go on. Shiv's waiting."

You lean down and kiss her shoulder. Bare and warm, freckled faintly at the top. She makes a small pleased sound and burrows deeper.

You find your clothes in the dark, by feel, scattered across her floor. Your skirt under the desk chair. Your jumper by the door. One sock you can't find at all. You give up on it. And before you go, you tear a corner off one of the papers on her desk, and write on it by the grey line of the window, and leave it on the pillow by her face.

*Back by ten. N.*

---

The rock is grey in the first light.

The mere is flat and still and dark, steaming faintly. The fells are black shapes against a sky just beginning to go pale. And on the flat slab of the rock, where it runs out into the deep water, Shiv is already there.

She's in her parka, hood down, sitting very still. Her knees drawn up. Looking out at the water.

She doesn't hear you at first. Then your feet crunch on the shingle, and she turns.

She grins. But it comes slow this morning. It takes a moment to get there, and when it does it's not quite all the way across her face.

"Sunday," she says. "No register. No whistle." She looks round at the dark water and the black fells. "Just us and the fells."

You strip on the rock, shivering, your skin going to gooseflesh in the cold air. Shiv stands and drops the parka and her hoodie, and she's in her swimsuit underneath.

She holds out her hand.

You take it.

You jump off the rock together, hand in hand, the way you did the very first time. Out over the black water, and down—

---

The cold takes your breath.

All of it, all at once. That great white shout through every nerve, the water closing over your head like a fist, the dark, the roar.

You come up breathing out. Slow. The way she taught you. Out, and out.

Beside you, Shiv surfaces, and shakes the water off her face. And then she lies back. Floats on her back on the black water, her arms spread, her face to the sky, her eyes open, looking up.

You float beside her for a moment.

Then you swim.

Out. Away from the rock, out into the mere, farther than you've ever gone. Farther than the buoy would ever let you. Past where the swimmers stop, out into the deep cold middle of the water, just the two of you, side by side, your arms coming over, the shore empty, the safety boat shut up in its shed, only the fells and the paling sky and the sound of your own breathing.

And back. Slowly. To the rock.

---

On the rock after, you sit wrapped in the parka together.

Both of you inside it, Shiv's arm round you, the big navy coat pulled round you both like a tent. Your wet hair dripping. Your skin roaring. Her body warm against yours through the cold.

Shiv is quiet.

For a long time. She looks out at the water, with the parka round you both, and doesn't say anything. And you let her be. You sit with her and watch the light come up over the fells.

"My mam," Shiv says, at last, "swam the sea at Salthill every morning of her life."

You turn your head.

"Every morning," Shiv says. "Winter too. January, February. Hail, the lot. Off the steps at the diving tower, in her old black swimming cap with the flowers on it. Every single day." She's looking at the water. "Fifty years, near enough."

"She taught me there," she says. "I was four. She took me down the steps and threw me in off the bottom one. Just — threw me. In the sea. And she stood on the step and shouted: *kick, Siobhan*."

She laughs.

And the laugh goes thin. Thin and high, and stops.

"She died last winter," Shiv says. "January." She swallows. "I came here on what she left."

You look at her.

You didn't know. Any of it. This is new to you. The storm she told you about, her mam weeping on the quay; the mam who hit her da with a handbag and then hugged him. You thought she was at home in Galway. You thought she was alive.

You don't say anything. There isn't anything to say.

You put your hand over hers, on the cold stone, inside the parka.

"Every dawn I go in," Shiv says. Very quiet. "Every morning, off this rock. It's her." She's staring at the water. "That's all it is. That's all it's ever been. It's swimming with her."

The mere lies flat and grey. A bird calls, far off, over the water.

"And the register," Shiv says. "Being counted. Signing in, signing out, some girl with a stopwatch." She shakes her head. "She never signed a thing in her life. Not once. She just went in."

And then she cries.

Suddenly. Hard. All at once, as if something inside her has given way. Her face goes into her hands, and her shoulders shake, and great dragging sobs come up out of her, loud and raw and ugly, bent over on the rock.

"I've not," she gets out. "I've not done this. In front of anyone. Not once. Not at the funeral, not after." Another sob. "Christ. *Christ*."

You hold her.

Inside the parka, with both arms, as tight as you can. Her wet hair against your cheek, cold, smelling of the mere. Her whole body shaking against yours. You hold her, and rock her a little, the way Bea rocked you, and you don't let go.

And the light comes up pink over the fells.

---

When it passes, she sits up.

She wipes her face on the sleeve of the parka. Roughly, both eyes, and her nose. She sniffs, hard.

"Christ," she says. "Sorry." She gives a short wet laugh. "You came for a swim."

"I came for you," you say.

Shiv looks at you.

A long moment. Her sea-green eyes red-rimmed, wet, very steady on yours.

And then her crooked grin comes back. Slowly. All the way.

She studies your face. Her head on one side.

"You've a look on you," Shiv says.

You don't say anything.

"Lindqvist?" says Shiv.

You go pink. You can feel it. All the way up. And you nod.

Shiv laughs. Properly now, a real laugh, her head going back.

"Good," she says. "About *time*. She's been mad for you since the first morning."

"And you?" you ask.

Shiv shrugs. Inside the parka, against you.

"I want you too, Ashby," she says. Easily. As if it's the plainest thing. "You know I do."

"When it's right," she says. "I'm not in a hurry." She looks out at the water, and back at you. "I've a whole year of Sundays."

And she kisses you.

Slow. Her mouth cold and then warm. She tastes of salt; of tears, and the mere.

---

You walk back up to Thornfield at nine.

Bea is awake in 15. She's sitting up in bed with two mugs of tea on the bedside floor, one for her and one for you, still hot, and your note on the pillow beside her. She looks up as you come in.

And she reads your face.

"Shiv's all right?" she says.

You sit on the end of the bed and tell her a little. Not all of it. Not the crying. Just: her mam. Last winter. The sea at Salthill.

Bea goes quiet.

She holds her mug in both hands and looks into it for a while.

"I'll be kind to her today," she says.

---

The Sunday roast is at one.

The whole crowd at one table. All of you, for once. You and Bea and Shiv, and Kit, and Lin, and Priya; and Amara too, sliding onto the end of the bench beside Priya with her tray and a dazzling smile, as if she always sits with you.

Bea sits beside Shiv.

And she fills Shiv's plate. Without asking. She just reaches over and takes it, and fills it, beef and gravy and carrots and a Yorkshire pudding, and potatoes, extra potatoes, a great heap of them, until it's a mountain, and puts it back down in front of her.

Shiv looks at the plate. Then at Bea.

She doesn't say anything. She lets her.

Under the table, Bea's other hand comes to rest on your knee.

Just resting there. Warm. Her fingers curling a little round the inside of your knee.

And across the table, Kit sees it.

You see her see it. Her grey-green eyes going down, to where Bea's arm disappears under the table, and resting there a moment. And coming back up, to your face.

---

After the meal, Kit falls into step with you in the cloister.

The cold stone arches, the grey light coming through them, the long flagged walk round the quad. The others have gone ahead. Kit walks beside you, her hands in her coat pockets, her eyes front.

"Lindqvist," she says.

It isn't a question.

"Yes," you say. "Last night."

Kit walks a few steps in silence. Her steps tapping on the flags. Her face perfectly composed. You can't read it at all.

Then:

"Good," she says. "She's decent." A pause. "She said so."

You look at her.

"Are you all right?" you ask.

Kit considers it. Seriously. You watch her do it, walking, her eyes on the far end of the cloister, as if she's checking something inside herself very carefully and taking her time over it.

"Yes," she says.

She sounds surprised.

"I find I am," says Kit.

She walks on. Then she says, still looking ahead:

"Library. Tonight. Seven." A pause. "You still owe me an hour of silence."

Her ears are pink.

---

In the afternoon, the boathouse lends out rowing boats, the way it does on Sundays.

Shiv wants to go out. She finds you after lunch and asks you. She doesn't make a joke of it.

So you go.

Shiv rows. You sit in the stern and watch her. She rows the way she swims: easily, without seeming to try, long strokes, the oars going in clean, the little boat sliding out over the flat water. She takes you out to the very middle of the mere. And there, she ships the oars, laying them in along the sides of the boat, and lets you drift.

You drift.

Shiv lies back in the bottom of the boat with her head on a folded lifejacket, and closes her eyes.

And she sings. Under her breath. Low. A song in Irish, slow and rising and falling, a tune you don't know, the words strange and soft.

"One of hers," she says, between verses, without opening her eyes.

You lie back too, in the stern, your head on the gunwale. And you listen.

You don't understand a word of it. It doesn't matter. You can hear what it is anyway: the way it goes up and comes down again, patient, like a tide; the way her voice catches once, on a high note, and goes on. You think of a woman in a black swimming cap with flowers on it, standing at a sink in Galway with her hands in the water, singing this. You think of a small Shiv at the kitchen table behind her, listening, the way you're listening now. You let the boat hold you, and the song hold you, and you don't say anything at all.

The boat turns slowly in the middle of the mere. Round and round, with the faint current. And the fells go round you. Gold in the low afternoon sun, every one of them in turn, the bracken burning on their flanks, the snow on the tops gone rose. The sky huge and pale over you. The water lapping at the hull. Shiv's voice, low, going on and on.

"She'd have liked you," Shiv says.

Her eyes are still shut.

"She'd have said you're too thin," she says. "She'd have fed you. She'd have stood you in the kitchen and fed you till you couldn't move."

---

You row back.

Badly. In circles. You can't help it; you've never been able to row. The oars won't go in together, and the boat goes round and round, and one oar comes out of the water altogether and nearly hits you in the face. Shiv sits up in the bottom of the boat and laughs at you. Laughs so hard she has to hold on to the sides. Laughs so hard she nearly goes over the side.

---

At seven it's the library reading room.

The green lamps glowing down the long oak tables, the windows black. Kit is already there, at the same table. She sees you come in, and moves her books.

You sit beside her.

You open your Sigils notes. Kit opens her book. And you work. For an hour, in silence, the lamps humming, the pages turning, the far end of the room going quiet.

Under the table, Kit's foot rests against yours. Just resting. Her ankle against your ankle. She doesn't move it, and you don't either.

At eight she writes on the corner of your page. Small and neat.

*Monday's practical. I'll take a middle bench. Watched. Ish.*

You look at it.

You write back underneath: *I know.*

Kit reads it. And she tears the corner off your page, carefully, along the fold, and folds it very small, and puts it in her pocket. And keeps it. And goes back to her book, and her foot stays where it is.

---

At nine you walk with Shiv to the Drowned Bell.

It's quiet on a Sunday. Nearly empty. A few old men at the bar, a couple in the corner. The fire is low in the grate. The landlady is drying glasses and nods at you both.

Shiv goes to the old jukebox.

She puts a coin in. And presses the buttons she always presses. And the one Pogues record comes on. That song. The one she plays every night.

She comes and sits down by the fire.

"Her song," Shiv says. "Mam's. She'd sing it doing the dishes. Every night of my life." She's looking at the fire. "Every night."

You sit with her by the fire with half a cider each. And you listen to it. All the way through.

And when it ends, Shiv gets up, and puts another coin in, and it plays once more.

---

Walking home along the black shore path, Shiv takes your hand.

She swings it. Back and forth, between you, like children walking home from school. The mere lapping in the dark. The stars out thick and close.

"Next Sunday," she says.

At Thornfield's door she stops, and kisses you goodnight. Slow, and salt.

And she goes off into the dark, whistling the song. You can hear it a long way, in the dark.

---

Upstairs, Bea's door is ajar.

Her lamp is on, low. You push the door open.

Bea is in bed. She looks up at you, and doesn't say anything. She just smiles, and lifts the covers.

You get in.

And you fall asleep in 15, with Bea warm at your back, her arm over you, and the Pogues still going round and round in your head.
