On Sunday the storm wakes you before dawn.

It's the window. Rain is being hurled at it, flung in great handfuls, rattling on the glass like gravel, and the old frame is shaking in its socket, knocking and knocking as if something outside wants in. The wind is a long roar over the roof. Somewhere a gutter is pouring.

You lie in the dark and listen to it for a while. Then you get up, and pull the blanket round your shoulders, and go to the window.

The mere is white.

You've never seen it like this. It's always been flat, grey, still, a sheet of pewter, a mirror. Now it's alive. Waves are running down the length of it, row after row, white-capped, and breaking on the shingle below the boathouse in long bursts of spray. The far shore is a smudge. The fells have gone. Just gone, swallowed in a low grey cloud that's pouring down off the tops like smoke.

---

At first light there's a knock.

It's Shiv. Soaked. Soaked through, from her hood to her trainers, in the big navy parka with water streaming off the hem and running off her nose and chin, her hair plastered flat under the hood, puddling on your doorstep.

"Twenty yards," she says. "Twenty yards, that's all it was. Look at me."

She's grinning.

"Rock's off," says Shiv. "Even I'm not that daft."

And she comes in, and drips on your floor, and stays.

---

You sit on your bed together with your backs against the wall, wrapped in one blanket. Shiv has taken off the parka and the wet hoodie and is in a T-shirt and your second-best jumper, which is too short in the arms for her, and her bare feet are tucked in under the blanket against yours, and they're freezing. You can see the mere from here, through the streaming glass, white and heaving.

"At home," Shiv says, watching it, "the storms come straight in off the Atlantic. Just sea between you and America. They hit Galway Bay like a train."

She tells you about it. About the sea going over the prom at Salthill, over the wall and across the road, and the whole town out in their coats to watch. About the windows at home taped up in crosses. About her da.

"He took me out in one, once," she says. "In his boat. I was eleven. Just round the point and back, to see it. Waves like houses." She laughs, softly. "My mam stood on the quay the whole time. When we came in she was crying. Proper crying. And then she hit him. With her handbag. And then she hugged him." She's quiet a moment. "I was never so happy in my life as out there."

You don't say anything. You lean your head against hers.

After a while her head gets heavy on your shoulder.

She's asleep. Just like that, between one breath and the next, slumped warm against you under the blanket with her wet hair in your neck. It smells of rain, and of the mere, and faintly of her. You don't move. You sit for an hour with her asleep on you and the storm throwing itself at the glass, and watch the white water, and listen to her breathe.

---

When you go down, there's a notice pinned to the Thornfield board in Yusra's neat hand.

*GALE. Boathouse SHUT. Swimmers stood down. Quad paths flooded: take care, wear boots. Warden.*

Someone has drawn a small sad fish underneath.

---

At one o'clock it's the Sunday roast.

The whole house goes together. Thirty girls, more, crammed in the Thornfield porch in coats and wellies and bin bags, peering out at the quad. The quad is a lake. The paths are gone under brown water, and the lawns are islands, and the rain is coming across it sideways in grey sheets.

"On three," somebody says.

You go on two.

The whole house runs the flooded quad together, shrieking. Water to your ankles, then your shins, cold enough to make you gasp, splashing up your legs. Bea has your hand and is laughing so hard she can barely run. Somebody falls over and is hauled up. The wind takes three umbrellas inside out at once. Shiv, ahead of you all, has her boots off and is carrying them, one in each hand, barefoot, splashing straight through the deepest part with her head back and the rain on her face.

You burst in through the Great Hall doors in a mob, soaked to the knees, dripping, shrieking, Bea still laughing, Shiv barefoot on the flagstones with her boots in her hands.

Kit is at the end of a table.

Nearly dry. Composed. Her black bob barely damp at the ends, her long skirt dry to the hem, and at her feet, furled, a huge black umbrella the size of a small tent, dripping a neat puddle on the flagstones. She watches the mob of you come in streaming and squelching, and she looks, very slightly, smug.

She makes room beside her for you on the bench. Bea sits down on your other side. And the two of them look at each other past you, and nod, politely.

Roast beef. Roast potatoes, crisp at the edges. Gravy in big jugs, and carrots, and Yorkshire puddings risen up like crowns. You eat with your wet socks steaming. The candles floating over the tables gutter and swing in the draughts each time the doors bang open, the little flames bending flat and leaping up again, and the long hall flickers like firelight.

---

After lunch the storm gets worse.

You can hear it. The wind rises from a roar to a howl, and the rain on the high windows of the hall becomes a drumming, and the doors bang so hard that two of the porters go and shut them, and drop the bar across, and stand with their backs to them shaking their heads.

"Paths are under," one of them says to the nearest table. "Proper under. Stay put, ladies, or go back the short way, and go together."

Thornfield is the short way. Ashcombe, across the far side of the quad, isn't.

Kit is standing by the barred doors looking out through the little window in them at the brown water, her coat over her arm. Her face is perfectly blank.

"You're cut off," says Bea.

Kit doesn't turn round. "I'm aware."

"Come back with us," says Bea. "There's cocoa. There's a kitchen. Come on."

Kit turns round and looks at Bea. Then at you. Then she puts her coat on.

---

The Thornfield kitchen is the warmest room in the world.

The windows are steamed solid. The big saucepan of cocoa is going on the stove. The rain hammers on the glass and you can't see anything through it but grey, and you don't care, any of you. Lin is in the corner on a stool with her sketchbook on her knee, drawing everyone, quick, quietly, one after another. Priya came back with you too, cut off from her own house, and has produced a pack of cards from her tote bag.

"Right," she says. "I'm going to teach you a game. It's from Leicester. My nani taught me. You won't know it."

You've never heard of it, any of you. It has a lot of rules, and Priya explains them all very fast, and then explains them again when Bea puts her hand up.

Kit wins the first hand.

Then the second.

Then the third. Each time she lays her cards down with a perfectly blank face, not a flicker, not a twitch, as if she's laying out a patience, and Priya stares at them and says "*How*," and Kit says, "Beginner's luck," in a voice like a closed door.

Bea gets up and makes tea.

She makes it in the big brown pot, properly, and pours it, and puts a mug down in front of Kit, milk already in. Then she sits down. Opposite Kit. Not beside you; opposite Kit, across the table, with her own mug in both hands.

"Thank you," says Kit. Stiffly.

Bea looks at her across the table.

"I know about Friday," Bea says.

Plainly. Not loud. Just across the table, the way she'd say it's raining.

"Nala told me," she says. "Some."

Kit goes white. Then pink, from the collar up. Her hand, on the table, flattens on her cards, pressing them down.

The kitchen goes very quiet. Priya stops shuffling. Lin's charcoal stops. Somewhere the rain is drumming on the glass, and the cocoa is ticking in the pan.

"I'm not cross," Bea says. "I was a bit. On Friday. I'm not now." She turns her mug round in her hands. "I'm in it too, that's all. With her." She nods at you, sideways. "I wanted you to know. So it isn't a secret. So you don't have to wonder."

Kit looks at her.

A long moment. Her grey-green eyes on Bea's brown ones across the table, and the kitchen holding its breath.

"That's," Kit says. And stops. She swallows. "That's very decent of you."

"I'm *very* decent," says Bea.

And grins. With both dimples, enormous, right across her face.

And Kit laughs. Out loud. Surprised, a real laugh, startled right out of her, and she puts her hand over her mouth, and laughs again behind it, and her ears go scarlet.

Priya starts shuffling again. The kitchen breathes out.

In the corner, Lin catches your eye, and turns her sketchbook round to show you.

It's Bea and Kit. Across the table from each other, with the mugs and the cards between them. Both laughing.

---

At six the lights flicker.

They go dim, all of them at once, a long brown fading, and the kitchen goes *ohhh*. Then they come back, and hold.

"Right," says Priya. She gets up and goes to the teapot, and draws a warming sigil on its side with one finger, neat and quick, spiralling in. "In case," she says. "If the power goes, the tea doesn't."

The teapot steams gently all evening.

---

At nine there's a crash upstairs.

A huge one. Glass. And then a roar, as if the storm has suddenly come indoors, and shrieking.

You're on the stairs before you've thought.

At the end of the top corridor, the big window has blown in. The whole of one pane is gone, and half of another, and the wind is pouring through the hole in a howling blast, and the rain with it, sheeting in sideways, soaking the floorboards, the curtain streaming out flat into the corridor like a flag. There's glass everywhere. Glittering on the boards in a great spray, crunching. Girls are coming out of their doors in pyjamas and nightdresses, shrieking, barefoot.

"*Back.*"

Yusra.

She's there already, from nowhere, in a jumper and jeans, her hair down loose, long and black and wild in the wind. She's standing between the girls and the glass with one arm out.

"Back," she says again. Low, level, carrying. "Everyone back. Shoes on, all of you, now, before anyone takes a step. You. Towels. Every towel in the airing cupboard."

She's pointing at you.

You run for the towels. You come back with your arms full, and she takes them and throws them down over the worst of the glass, and the water, and turns to the girls crowding the corridor.

"Bed," she says. "All of you. Rooms. Doors shut. Lindqvist, take them." Bea is there in her enormous jumper; she nods and starts herding. "Ashby," says Yusra. "Stay. I need hands."

---

There's a plank. A long rough board from the cupboard under the stairs, and a hammer, and a tin of nails.

"Hold it there," Yusra says. "Across the frame. Both hands. Lean on it."

You hold it. You hold the board across the broken window with both hands while the wind tries to tear it off you, leaning your weight into it, and the rain comes round the edges and straight into your face, cold, blinding. Yusra drives in nails. One, two, three, the hammer quick and sure, her face set and wet, her hair whipping.

Your hands are on the board beside hers. Your shoulder is against her arm. You can feel her moving as she swings the hammer, her arm against you, warm through the wet wool. You're both soaked. Her jumper is black with rain. Your hair is in your eyes.

"Good," she says, between nails. "Hold it. Good."

The board goes in. The howling drops to a whistle round the edges.

Yusra reaches up to clear the last of the glass from the top of the frame, a big jagged shard still stuck in the putty, and pulls it free.

It slips.

You see it go across her palm. You see her face not change. And then the blood. Bright red, very bright in the corridor light, welling up across her palm and running down, in a quick thin line, to her wrist.

You take her hand.

You don't think. You just drop the board, which holds, and grab a towel from the stack, a clean one, and take her hand in yours, and press the towel to her palm. Hard. Both hands. The way she'd tell you to.

Yusra goes still.

She stands there and lets you. Her hand is in both of yours. You can feel her pulse under your fingers, going steady and fast. The wind is whistling through the gaps round the board, and the rain is ticking on it, and the two of you are standing in the wet corridor at the end of the top floor, dripping, with her hand held between yours.

"That's right," she says quietly.

You look up. She's looking at you.

"Pressure," Yusra says. "Good."

And she smiles. Slightly. Just at one corner of her mouth. Looking at you.

---

She binds it herself, after.

She has a kit in her room, a canvas roll, and she fetches it and kneels in the corridor light under the lamp and does it herself, quick and practised: a pad, a strip of white bandage torn with her teeth, round and round and tied off, one-handed, in about forty seconds. You watch her do it. You've never seen hands so sure.

She stands up and looks at the board, and the towels, and the glass, and you.

"Thank you, Nala," Yusra says.

Your name. Again.

"Go to bed," she says. "Dry off first."

And she reaches out with her good hand and touches your wet sleeve. Once. Just at the elbow. Then she turns away and starts gathering towels.

---

In your room you strip off the wet things and dry your hair and get into bed, shivering, and lie in the dark.

Across the corridor, through the two doors, you can hear Bea's room. Two voices. Bea's, and Kit's. Both of them laughing at something, Bea's big warm laugh and Kit's short startled one, and then a hiss of *shh*, and more laughing.

Kit is sleeping on Bea's floor tonight. You saw her on your way along the corridor, through Bea's door ajar: a nest of blankets on the boards, and Kit sitting cross-legged in the middle of it in a pair of Bea's pyjamas, far too long, rolled up at the ankles, looking extremely dignified about it.

You lie and listen to them talking. Low, now. On and on. You can't make out the words, just the sound of it, the two voices going back and forth under the noise of the storm, and the storm going on and on above them.

And then, a little before midnight, the wind falls.

You hear it go. The howl drops to a moan, and the moan to a sigh. The rain on the window softens from gravel to a patter, to a whisper. Across the corridor, the voices have gone quiet.

Out beyond the glass, under the low cloud, the mere goes dark and quiet again.
