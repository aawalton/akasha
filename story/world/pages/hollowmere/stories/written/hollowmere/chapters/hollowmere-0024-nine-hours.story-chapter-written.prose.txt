On Wednesday you wake at six, and you're rested.

It takes you a moment to understand it. The room is dark, the curtains still drawn, the radiator ticking as it warms. Your body feels heavy and whole and easy, the way it hasn't felt in days. You slept. Straight through, from the moment you turned out the lamp to now, without once surfacing into the dark to wonder what time it was. All night, the way you said you would. The way you promised, at the foot of the stairs, with a flask between your hands and hers.

You lie still and let yourself know it.

Bea is a warm weight at your back, still asleep.

She's curled round you, her knees tucked in behind yours, her breath slow and damp against the back of your neck. One arm is flung over you. Her hand has come to rest on your hip, open, palm up, and you can feel the blisters on it: two hard shiny rounds at the base of her fingers, hot against the cotton of your nightshirt. She doesn't stir. She makes a small sound, somewhere between a sigh and a word, and settles closer.

You lie there a minute longer, because it's warm, and because it's Bea.

Then you lift her hand off your hip, very gently, and lay it down on the sheet.

You slide out from under the covers. The floor is cold. You find a jumper on the chair, one of Bea's, enormous and smelling of her, and pull it on over your nightshirt, and a pair of thick socks. Bea rolls into the warm place you've left and buries her face in your pillow.

You go out, and pull the door to behind you, and go down the stairs to the Thornfield kitchen in the half-dark.

The house is asleep. The stairs creak under your socks in the places they always creak. The hall smells of last night's cumin and the cold coming under the front door.

In the kitchen you put the light on over the stove, only that one, so it isn't too bright.

You fill the kettle, and put it on. You cut two thick slices off the loaf and put them under the grill, and stand there in Bea's jumper with your arms wrapped round yourself, watching them.

The window over the sink is black when you start. Then, slowly, while the kettle grumbles towards the boil, it goes grey. Charcoal first. Then slate. Then the pale grey of a wet morning, with the shapes of the trees coming up out of it, and the outline of the porch roof, and the mist lying low across the lawn towards the mere. The kettle clicks off. The toast smells of toast.

The clock over the door says ten past six.

At quarter past, the back door opens.

Cold air comes in, and a breath of mist, and Yusra.

She's in her coat, buttoned to the throat, the brass badge at her collar. The shoulders of the coat are dark with damp, beaded with tiny drops of mist that catch the light over the stove. Her bag is over her arm. Her hair is coming down from its knot.

She has your flask in her hand. The old tartan thermos with the dented lid.

She stops in the doorway when she sees you.

And she stands there. One hand still on the door handle, the cold coming in round her, the flask in her other hand. She doesn't come in, and she doesn't close the door. She just looks at you, across the kitchen, at the stove, in a jumper too big for you, with toast under the grill.

You look back.

"You're up," she says.

Her voice is rough with the night. Low, and level, and very tired.

"I slept," you say. "All night." You hold her eyes. "I said I would."

Yusra almost smiles.

It's there and gone, at the corner of her mouth, the way it was in the library. She lets go of the door handle at last, and closes the door behind her, and the cold stops coming in.

She comes to the table and puts the flask down on it. Carefully. Standing it upright, in the middle, as if it matters where it goes.

"It was still warm at two," she says.

She looks at the flask, not at you.

"Thank you, Nala."

In the light over the stove you can see how tired she is.

She looks grey with it. Not pale; grey, the way the window was grey a few minutes ago, the colour gone out from under her skin. Her eyes are rimmed red, the lids heavy, and the shadows under them are darker than they were yesterday in the library, darker than you've ever seen them. Her hair is coming loose from its knot in long black strands, falling round her face and down her neck, and she hasn't pushed it back. She's always pushed it back. She stands by the table with her bag still on her arm, and sways, very slightly, like a tree in a wind you can't feel.

You don't ask if she wants anything.

You make her tea. A clean mug, a fresh bag, water off the boil. Milk. Sugar, two spoons, sweet, the way the flask was. You take the toast out from under the grill and butter it thick, right to the edges, while it's hot enough to melt, and cut it in halves, and put it on a plate.

You take her bag off her arm and hang it on the back of a chair. You pull the chair out.

You put the tea and the toast in front of the chair.

Yusra looks at them. Then at you.

And she sits. She sits down slowly, still in her coat, and puts her hands round the mug, and lets you.

She eats one slice.

Slowly. One half, and then the other, in small bites, chewing each one for a long time, as if she's forgotten how hungry she is and is only now finding out. Her hands are wrapped round the mug between bites. She drinks the tea in long, careful mouthfuls.

The kitchen is quiet.

There's the clock over the door, ticking. There's the kettle on the side, cooling, making the small pinging sounds a kettle makes as it cools. Upstairs, far off, a pipe knocks once and goes still. The window over the sink is fully grey now, the mist pressed up against it.

You sit across from her.

You don't say anything. You wrap your hands round your own mug and you sit, and you wait, and you watch her eat. The second slice is still on the plate. She looks at it, and doesn't take it, and doesn't push it away either.

You wait a while longer.

Then you ask it.

"What does the work keep in?"

Yusra puts her cup down.

Very carefully. As if it might break. She sets it down on the table without a sound, and takes her hands away from it, and lays them flat on the wood on either side.

And she looks at it.

For a long time. At the cup, at the last of the tea in the bottom of it, gone pale and cooling. The clock ticks. The kettle pings. She doesn't look up, and she doesn't get up, and she doesn't tell you to go and write your essay. Her hands stay flat on the table.

"I had a girlfriend," she says.

Low. To the cup.

"Two years. Since my first year here." A pause. "She's a doctor in Leeds."

You don't move.

"Junior doctor," says Yusra. Her voice is very even. "We did the long distance thing. Trains on weekends, when she wasn't on call. Letters." Something moves in her face. "She wrote proper letters. Phone on Sundays."

She turns the cup a quarter turn on the table. Then back.

"In August she sent me a text."

Her voice stays level. That's the worst of it. It doesn't shake and it doesn't drop, and her face doesn't change. She says it the way she'd read out a temperature.

"She'd met someone," Yusra says. "On her rotation."

You hear yourself say it.

"A text."

Yusra nods.

"Four lines," she says. "Two years, four lines." She almost laughs. "I've still got it. I read it." A pause. "I read it most nights."

The clock ticks.

"I haven't told anyone," she says.

She looks up at you then. For the first time since she started. Her eyes are very dark, and very steady, and red at the rims.

"Not my mum. Not my dad. Not anyone here." She holds your eyes. "You're the first."

You reach across the table.

You take her hand. The right one, flat on the table by the cup. You turn it over, gently, and there's the thin pink line across the palm, from the base of her thumb to the other side, healed clean.

You hold it.

She lets you.

Her fingers are cold. Cold right through, from the walk up from the infirmary in the mist, cold the way stone is cold first thing in the morning. You take her hand in both of yours. You fold your fingers round hers, and hold them, and you don't let go. You hold them until the cold goes out of them, slowly, and they warm, and you can feel her pulse under your thumb, slow and tired and steady.

"I can't sleep," she says.

She's looking at your hands round hers.

"I lie there," she says, "and I'm doing sums." Her mouth goes tight. "Two years. What I missed. What I gave up, to get on a train every Friday. What I was." Her voice drops. "What I thought I was. To her."

"So I work," she says.

"The infirmary. The library. Every shift going. If I'm working I'm not lying there doing sums." She looks at the window. "And a warden doesn't get asked how she is. Not ever. That's the point of a warden. You ask her."

She stops.

She looks back at you.

"You did," she says.

You hold her hand.

"You're allowed to be sad," you tell her.

Your voice comes out quieter than you meant it to.

"You're allowed," you say again. "Two years is a lot. It's a lot to have taken off you. And by text." You hear your own voice go hard on the word. "Four lines. You're allowed to be sad about that, Yusra. You don't have to keep it in a ward."

Yusra looks at you.

And one tear runs down her face.

Just one. Out of the corner of her left eye, and down her cheek, slowly, catching the light from over the stove. She doesn't blink it away. She doesn't turn her head. She lets it run, all the way down to her jaw, and somehow that's worse than if she'd sobbed, worse than anything. She sits there with her hand in yours and her face perfectly still and lets it fall.

Then she lifts her free hand and wipes it away with her wrist. One stroke. Neat.

You squeeze her hand.

"Pressure," you say softly.

Yusra laughs.

It comes out of her wet and surprised, a short sound, half a breath, as if it got out before she could stop it. Her eyes go bright. Her mouth curves, really curves, for the first time this morning.

"Good," she says.

She takes the second slice of toast.

She finishes it, slowly, with her other hand still in yours across the table. She drinks the last of the tea, cold now, and puts the cup down.

"I need to sleep," she says.

And she stays sitting.

She doesn't get up. She sits there in her damp coat with her hand in yours and the empty plate in front of her, and she looks at you. Just looks. Her eyes are so tired they've gone soft at the edges.

You get up.

You keep hold of her hand, and come round the table, and she stands up with you. You take her bag off the back of the chair and put it over your own shoulder. And you walk her out of the kitchen, and up the stairs.

Up the first flight, and the second. Past the landing window, grey with mist. The house is quiet round you, every door shut, the whole of Thornfield still asleep; somewhere below, a pipe knocks and goes still. Yusra climbs beside you with her hand in yours, slowly, a step at a time, her shoulder brushing yours on the narrow stair. Once she stops, halfway up the last flight, and just breathes, and you wait with her till she goes on.

At the head of the stairs is room 1.

Yusra finds her key in her coat pocket. It takes her two tries to get it in the lock.

Her room is neat as a ward.

You've never been in it. It's smaller than you thought it would be, and very plain, and very clean. A narrow bed, made tight, the blankets tucked square at the corners like a hospital bed. A desk under the window with her books stacked on it, spines out, in order of size. A jug of water and a glass, upside down on a folded cloth. A lamp. Her coat hook by the door, and a pair of shoes underneath it, side by side, toes to the wall. The curtains are open, and the grey light lies flat on everything.

Yusra sits down on the edge of the bed.

She's still in her clothes. Her coat, her trousers, her jumper, the badge. She bends, and starts on her shoes. Slowly. She unlaces the left one, a loop at a time, and works it off her heel, and sets it on the floor. Then the right. Like it's hard work. Like her hands are very heavy and the laces are a long way down.

You put her bag on the desk.

She looks up at you.

"Will you sit," she says.

It isn't quite a question.

"Just till I'm off." Her voice is low. "That's all."

There's a chair by the bed, a plain wooden one. You pull it closer, and sit on it.

Yusra shrugs out of her coat, and lets it fall behind her on the blankets. She doesn't get under them. She lies down on top of them, on her side, facing you, in her jumper and trousers, her knees drawn up a little, her head on the pillow.

There's a throw folded at the foot of the bed. Grey wool, folded in a perfect square.

You take it, and shake it out, and lay it over her. Over her shoulders, down to her stockinged feet. You tuck it round her back.

She closes her eyes.

Then her hand comes out from under the throw.

It moves across the blanket, slowly, to the edge of the bed, where your hand is resting. It finds yours. Her fingers slide between your fingers and close.

And hold on.

"Lines," she says.

Her eyes are closed. Her voice is very quiet, slurred at the edges with sleep.

"I know," you say. "I'm just sitting here."

Her thumb moves once. Over the back of your hand. Once.

And then she's still.

Her breathing slows.

It doesn't happen all at once. For a while she's only lying with her eyes shut, and you can tell she's awake: her fingers tighten on yours now and then, and a crease comes and goes between her brows. Then the crease stays gone. Her breaths get longer, and deeper, and further apart. Her mouth comes a little open. Outside, a bird starts up in the wet trees, and stops, and starts again.

It takes ten minutes. You count them on the clock on her desk.

Then her hand goes loose in yours.

Her fingers uncurl, all at once, the way a child's do. Her breath goes in and out, slow, all the way down.

She's asleep.

You sit a while longer.

You don't move your hand. You sit on the wooden chair beside the narrow bed and watch her sleep.

Her face is different asleep. All the guard is gone off it. The tight set of her jaw, the line between her brows, the way she holds her mouth when she's being the warden: gone. Her face is smooth, and young, younger than you've ever seen her, her lashes dark on her cheeks, the shadows under her eyes still there but softer now, like bruises on someone who's resting. A strand of black hair has fallen across her cheek. It moves when she breathes.

You leave it where it is.

Then you lift her hand, gently, and lay it down on the blanket beside her face.

You stand up, and go to the window, and draw the curtain. The grey light goes, and the room goes dim and quiet and brown.

You go out, and close her door behind you. Very quietly. Holding the handle down so the latch doesn't click, and letting it up slowly once the door is shut.

---

Bea is up when you get back to 15.

She's sitting on the edge of the bed pulling on her tights, one leg in, hopping the other foot into it, her honey-blonde hair half out of its bun, a wild tangle from the pillow. Her rowing kit is laid out on the chair. She looks up when you come in.

"Where've you been?" she asks. "Your side's cold." She points at you. "And that's my jumper."

You sit down on the bed beside her.

You tell her Yusra came in off nights, and you were up, and you made her toast.

Bea stops with her tights halfway up her thigh.

She looks at your face. For a moment. A long, steady look, the way she looks at the water before she puts an oar in it.

"Is she all right?" Bea asks.

"She will be," you say.

You think about room 1, and the throw, and the hand going loose in yours.

"It's hers to tell," you say.

Bea looks at you a moment longer. Then she nods. Once. As if that settles it.

She stands up and wriggles the tights the rest of the way up, and pulls on her rowing top, and her fleece over it, and grabs her bag. At the door she stops, and comes back, and bends down, and kisses you. Quick and warm, on the mouth, tasting of toothpaste.

"You're a good egg, Nala," she says.

And she goes off to row, banging the door behind her.

---

At ten it's Sigils, in the Drawing Room, and the morning passes in a haze.

The long tables, the tall windows, the smell of ink. The tutor chalks the day's sigil on the board, and you dip Lin's soft nib, and you hear the instructions, and they go past you. You're thinking about a cold hand warming between your hands. About four lines on a phone. About a girl lying awake in a narrow bed, doing sums.

You copy the wrong sigil. Your hand draws the warming spiral, loose and easy, all the way in, the way it's learned to; and the board shows something else entirely.

Lin, beside you, leans over without a word. She puts one inky fingertip on the board's sigil, and then on yours, and draws the right one in the margin for you in her clean, small line.

Twenty minutes later you do it again. A fresh page, the same spiral, your hand going round on its own.

Lin corrects it again. This time she looks at you sideways, a small soft look, and doesn't ask.

---

At lunch Kit sits down beside you.

She thumps a library book down on the table next to your plate. Fat, old, dark green cloth, with *WARDS AND THEIR MAKING* stamped on the spine in flaking gold.

"Look at this," she says. "No, look." She opens it to a page she's marked with a bus ticket. "There's an essay in the back. Some woman from eighteen-ninety-something. She says a ward is a promise you make to the thing inside it." Kit taps the page. "I like that. I'm going to steal it. Not the words. The idea."

She eats a forkful of pie, and reads a line, and eats another.

Then she looks up.

"Grandmother says Penhallow wants what happened to you," Kit says. "Not what a book says." She narrows her eyes at you. "So what happened to you?"

You think about room 1.

The grey light. The narrow bed. A cold hand in both of yours, warming, slowly, until you could feel the pulse in it.

"Something," you say. "I'm still writing it."

Kit looks at you for a long moment. Then she shrugs, and goes back to her book.

---

In the afternoon you go to the library and write the essay properly.

The big reading room, the green lamps, the long oak tables. You take the table by the tall window again, the one with the fells in it. You spread your notes out, and Lin's soft nib, and a clean sheet.

And you write.

Three pages, in one long go. You don't stop. You don't cross out. It comes the way water comes when you take the plug out: all at once, and in one direction, and faster than you can quite keep up with.

You write about a bandage. About how it keeps the dirt out and the blood in. About how the hurt stays in too, for a bit, while it mends, and how that isn't a failure of the bandage. That's what it's for.

You write about the shell round a flame. About how a ward drawn too tight starves the flame, and a ward drawn right gives it room: holds in the air it needs, and the heat, and keeps it still while it burns.

You write about a wetsuit, and how you're the heat.

You write about the hurt kept in while it mends, and how a ward isn't a wall. It's a held breath. It's a hand round a hand.

You keep Yusra's name out of it.

You don't write *room 1*. You don't write *four lines*, or *Leeds*, or *two years*. You don't write about a girl with a thin pink line across her palm, sitting very still while one tear runs down her face.

But she's in every line. You can feel her in it. In every sentence about mending.

When you're done, you put the pen down. Your hand aches.

Lin is behind you.

You didn't hear her come. She's standing at your shoulder with her sketchbook under her arm, reading. She reads all three pages, slowly, while you sit there, and you don't stop her.

When she gets to the end she says, "Oh."

Very softly. Just that.

Then she goes back to her own table, and opens her sketchbook, and starts drawing again. And doesn't look up.

---

At six you go back to Thornfield.

On the way up the stairs you pass room 1.

The door is shut. Under it, there isn't any light; the curtain is still drawn, the way you left it. You stand a moment on the landing and listen, and through the door you can just hear it: breathing. Slow and deep and even.

Sleeping.

You go on along the corridor very quietly.

---

Dinner is in the kitchen.

Bea is back from the boathouse, damp and pink and starving, her blistered hands wrapped round a mug of tea, telling Lin about a bird that landed on the stern. Priya and Amara are cooking side by side again. Priya at the stove, stirring a vast pot of dal, and Amara beside her at the counter, cutting coriander, still badly, but with her sleeves rolled up and her tongue between her teeth in concentration. Their elbows keep touching. Both elbows stay where they are.

The radio's on.

At half past seven Yusra comes down the stairs.

You hear her before you see her. Her step on the stair, slow, not heavy. Then she's in the doorway.

Her hair is washed. It's down, still damp at the ends, long and black and wavy over her shoulders, pushed back off her face. She's in a soft grey jumper, a loose one, with the sleeves down over her hands, and the badge isn't on it. Her face has colour in it. The shadows under her eyes are still there, but they're shadows now, and not bruises.

She looks rested.

She stands in the doorway, and looks across the kitchen, past Bea and Lin and the steam off the dal and Amara with her coriander, and finds you.

"Nine hours," she says.

Only to you. Under the radio. Low.

"Nine," you say.

Yusra nods.

She comes into the kitchen, and takes a plate of dal from Priya, who hands it over without stopping her singing. And she comes round the table, and sits down beside you.

Her shoulder rests against yours.

All through dinner. She eats, and listens to Bea talk about the bird, and says *mm* to something Priya says about cumin, and the whole time her shoulder is against yours. Warm through her soft grey jumper. Leaning, just a little.

When Bea passes the bread down the table to you, Yusra takes it, and passes it on.

---

After dinner Yusra goes up first.

She rinses her plate, and dries her hands, and goes out into the hall. You hear her step on the stairs.

At the turn of the stairs she stops.

You can see her from where you're sitting, through the kitchen doorway: the turn of the stair, the landing window dark behind her now, her hand on the banister.

She looks back. Down the stairs, through the door, across the kitchen. At you.

And holds your eyes.

A long moment. The radio plays on behind you, and the dal steams, and the house rolls on round you both.

Then Yusra turns, and goes on up.

---

In 15, Bea's already in bed.

She's sitting up against the pillows with a towel spread over her lap, rubbing cream into her blisters. A little pot of it open on the blanket, smelling of lanolin and something like lavender. She works it in with her thumbs, wincing, round and round the shiny red patches at the base of her fingers.

You get undressed, and into your nightshirt, and get in beside her.

She puts the lid back on the pot. Wipes her hands on the towel. And leans over, and puts her chin on your shoulder, and looks at you from very close.

"You look happy," she says. "And sad."

You think about it.

"Both," you say.

Bea kisses your cheek. Soft, and warm, and smelling of lavender.

"That's allowed," she says.

She reaches over and turns the lamp down, till it's only a glow.

You lie awake a little.

Bea's breathing goes slow beside you, and her hand finds your hip in the dark, open, the blisters slick with cream. The house settles round you. A pipe knocks. Far off, somewhere down the corridor, a door closes.

You think of a narrow bed in room 1. A grey throw, folded square at the foot of it. The curtain drawn. A girl with her hair washed and her face smooth, lying down in the dark at the head of the stairs.

You hope she sleeps all night too.
