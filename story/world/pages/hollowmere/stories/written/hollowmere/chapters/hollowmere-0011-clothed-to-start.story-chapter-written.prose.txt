On Thursday you're at the boathouse before the light.

The register is out on its folding table again, the pen on its string, the safety boat drawn up at the water's edge with its two third-years hunched in waterproofs. The red-cheeked second-year has her whistle and her stopwatch and her woolly hat. The steam is coming off the mere in the same soft wisps.

There are fewer girls on the shingle this morning. Fifteen, perhaps. The ones who came yesterday for the shrieking haven't come back for more, and the ones who have are quieter about it, stamping their feet and blowing on their hands and eyeing the water like an old enemy.

You sign: *N. Ashby*.

Shiv comes along the shore with her hood up, the same as yesterday, the same scowl. She reads the register upside down, picks up the pen, and writes under your name in her big sprawl: *Doyle (reluctant, again)*.

"Again," you say.

"Every day," says Shiv. "I'll be reluctant every day. Put it in the minutes."

The whistle goes. The girls wade in. There's shrieking still, but less of it, and shorter, and a good deal of determined gasping. You and Shiv go in past them, under all at once and up breathing out, slow, and swim. Out through the shallows, past the bobbing heads, into the open grey water, side by side, all the way to the orange buoy.

You hang there, the two of you, one hand each on the cold slick curve of it, breathing. Just breathing. In and out, slow, the cold pressing on your chest and your breath steaming between you. The fells are dark against a sky going from grey to pale gold. The shore is a long way off.

"It's fine, this," Shiv says after a while. "The swimming. The swimming's fine."

"But."

"But the register." She wrinkles her nose. Water runs off her chin. "Signing in. Signing out. Some girl with a stopwatch writing down how long you were in. Feels like being counted."

You think about her rock, and the dark, and the two of you jumping off it hand in hand with only the fells watching.

"We can still go to the rock," you say. "Sundays."

Shiv looks at you. Then the crooked grin comes, slow, all the way across her face.

"Sundays," she says.

---

At ten it's History in the Long Room.

Kit is there before you, at the end of a bench halfway back, in black, her book open and her pen uncapped. She looks up as you come in, and moves her bag off the seat beside her, and goes back to her book. You sit. She doesn't say anything about last night. She doesn't look at you any differently. She's entirely composed, cool and neat and pale, her bob perfectly straight, as if she never read a poem in her life.

Only her ears are a little pink.

The lecturer comes in, an old woman with a stoop and a voice like a creaking gate, and puts her notes on the lectern, and begins.

Today it's the trials of the cunning-women.

The room goes quiet for it. Quieter than it ever goes for the guilds or the charters. The lecturer reads out names: women in villages who sold charms for warts and lost cattle and sick children, and were taken to the assizes for it. Women who were ducked in ponds. Women who were hanged. She reads them in her creaking voice, plainly, one after another, and every pen in the room stops for a while. You sit very still. Out of the corner of your eye you see Kit's hand, flat on her page, the silver ring catching the light.

"And the last of the trials," says the lecturer, "came in 1763, with the repeal of the old statute—"

Beside you, Kit's pen moves.

She reaches across, without looking at you, and writes in the margin of your notes, small and neat: *1736*. Then she underlines it. Twice. Hard enough that the nib squeaks.

You look at the numbers. Then at her. She's facing front, her face perfectly blank, and her pen is already back on her own page.

You think of her grandmother, in this same room, with this same lecturer, forty years ago, hearing the same wrong date. You press your lips together very hard.

Halfway through, a folded slip of paper slides across the desk onto your notes.

You open it under the edge of your book.

*Bus at 6. Film at 7. I'll choose. You'll hate it.*

You read it twice. You can feel her not looking at you, very carefully, a foot away.

You uncap your pen and write underneath, in your own looser hand: *I'll love it.*

You fold it and slide it back.

Kit unfolds it. Reads it. For a second her face does something, just at the corner of her mouth, very quickly. Then she folds the note again, and again, and again, into a very small hard square, and puts it in the pocket of her skirt, and keeps her hand there over it for the rest of the lecture.

---

At lunch Priya finds you in the Great Hall with your soup half-eaten and takes you by the sleeve.

"Debating," she says. "Amara's on. It's the first one. She's been practising at me over lunch all week, I *have* to see it, come on, it's only next door."

"I've got soup—"

"Bring the bread," says Priya, and drags you.

The annexe is a long panelled room off the Great Hall, with rows of chairs and a table at the front and a motion chalked on a board: *This house believes a spell belongs to its focus.* Thirty girls, perhaps, and a second-year in the chair with a little bell. You and Priya squeeze into the back row. Priya gets out a notebook and three pens.

Amara is at the front table, sitting very upright, in a fitted cream jumper and tailored trousers, her braids up in a high ponytail and the gold hoops in her ears. She looks entirely at ease. She looks as if the room were her own.

When the bell goes she stands up, and she has the room in four minutes flat.

You can't afterwards remember all of it. Her grandmother's ring, held up, gold, catching the window. A spell, she says, isn't a thing you have; it's a thing that happens between you and what you cast through; and the focus is the half that stays. You go home. You forget. You die. The ring remembers. Her voice is warm and quick and certain and funny, and she turns her head to look at one girl and then another as if she's talking to each of them alone, and the girl from the other side who stands up after her looks, poor thing, already beaten.

When the vote's taken it isn't close.

Priya is scribbling furiously in green. "She's *so good*," she whispers. "She's *horrible*. I love her."

---

After, at the door, Amara finds you.

She's bright from winning, glowing with it, her eyes very dark and shining. She stops in front of you in the doorway with the crowd going past on either side.

"Freckles," she says. "You came to watch me."

"Priya brought me," you say.

"Mm," says Amara. She looks at you, and smiles. "But you stayed."

And she reaches out and tucks a strand of your hair behind your ear. Lightly. Her fingertips brush the edge of your ear on the way, and your skin prickles all down that side of your neck.

"You and Ashworth," she says, still lightly. She's watching your face. "Notes in History. I saw."

Of course she did. She sits two rows in front, and turns round whenever she likes.

"Kit's my friend," you say.

Amara's smile goes slow. It spreads, unhurried, all the way up to her eyes.

"Lucky Ashworth," she says.

Then she turns, braids swinging, and walks off down the corridor to her next class, and doesn't look back.

Priya, beside you, has stopped writing.

"Well," she says, after a moment. "*That* was interesting."

---

At two it's the Practice Hall, and the warming charm.

The demonstrator hands round the stones from her basket, smooth grey pebbles from the shore, each about the size of an egg. You take yours to your bench and hold it in your palm and look at it.

Last time it went faintly warm, like a stone in the sun, and that was all. You remember Lin's voice in the Drawing Room yesterday, soft. *You draw the spiral too tight. Like you're holding your breath.*

So you breathe.

You draw the spiral in the air over the stone with one finger, and this time you breathe out as you draw it. Slowly. All the way round, coiling in, loose, the turns spaced even, the way your second copy went yesterday when the tutor said *better*. You let your shoulders drop. And then, still on the out-breath, you call the heat. Not hard. Not pushing. Just calling it, gently, on the breath, the way you'd call a cat.

The stone warms.

It warms steadily, from the middle out, into your palm. Not in a rush. It just keeps coming, steady, like a cup of tea held in both hands, until the stone's as warm as bathwater, and then it stays there. It holds. You hold it, and it holds.

You wait for the pounding behind your eyes. It doesn't come. You touch your lip with the back of your other hand, carefully. It's dry. Your head is clear.

The demonstrator is walking the benches. She stops at yours, and reaches out and touches the stone in your palm with two fingers, and leaves them there a second.

"That's the way," she says, and nods, and moves on.

At the next bench, Bea is glaring at her stone with the full force of her being, her tongue between her teeth, strands of honey-coloured hair falling out of her bun. She draws the spiral. She calls. She calls again.

Then she makes a noise. A high, small, astonished noise, like a startled mouse.

"It's *warm*," she says. "Nala. Nala, feel. It's *warm*."

You reach over and touch it. It's faintly warm. Barely. Like a stone in the sun.

"It's warm," you agree.

And Bea starts to laugh, and so do you, both of you, the stupid helpless kind of laughing, leaning on your benches, until the demonstrator looks over.

Across the hall, Lin is at a bench under the window with her stone held in both hands, and from the still way she's holding it you can tell it's warm already, and has been for a while. She looks up. She catches your eye across the hall. And she breathes out, slowly, deliberately, just for you to see, and smiles.

---

At quarter to seven there's a knock on 14. A soft one.

It's Lin. She's in a long pleated skirt and a soft grey cardigan, her ponytail over one shoulder, and she's hugging a big sketchbook to her chest with both arms like a shield. Her face is very pale.

"Life drawing," she says. "It's my first one. It's at seven." She swallows. "Would you walk me over? Just to the door."

"Of course," you say, and get your coat.

You walk her down the stairs and out across the quad. It's cold and clear and nearly dark, the sky a deep blue over the roofs, and the grass is already going stiff with frost. Lin walks close beside you, holding the sketchbook. She doesn't say much. You don't ask her to.

The Drawing Room is lit up. You can see it from halfway across the quad: its long windows glowing gold with lamplight, the skylights dark above them, shadows moving behind the glass as girls set up their easels.

At the door Lin stops.

She stands on the step with her sketchbook against her chest, and looks at the door, and doesn't open it.

"I'll be in the library," you tell her. "Finishing my essay. The reading room. If you want to come and find me, after."

Lin looks at you. She nods.

Then she closes her eyes, and breathes out. Slowly. All the way. The way she taught you.

She opens her eyes, and opens the door, and goes in, and the door closes behind her on the gold light.

---

The library reading room is high and hushed, the green lamps glowing in a long row down the oak tables, and the dark pressing at the tall windows.

Kit is already there. She's at the same table as Sunday, with her books spread out round her in a careful wall. She looks up as you come in. And without a word she moves two of them, and makes room.

You sit down across from her.

You get out your essay. The pages you've written so far, and the books Priya found you, and your cheap black pen from Kendal market with Lin's soft old nib in it, polished. You fill it. You find your place.

And you write.

You don't speak, either of you. The lamps hum. Pages turn. Somewhere at the far end of the room a girl coughs and is shushed. Kit's pen moves across her page in small sharp strokes, and stops, and moves again, and once, when you look up, you find her looking at you, and she looks straight back down. The nib is soft and easy in your fingers, and the ink comes out smooth and even, and the lines go down the page one after another, looser than they ever went before.

At nine the door opens, and Lin comes in.

She's pink. She's pink to the tips of her ears, pink right down into the collar of her cardigan. She comes down the long room between the tables with the sketchbook shut and pressed to her chest, and she sits down in the chair beside you without a word, and puts the sketchbook on her knee, and keeps both hands flat on it.

You wait.

"The model sat nude," Lin whispers, very low. "A second-year. Two hours." She swallows. "I drew her eleven times."

"How was it?"

Lin looks down at the sketchbook. She takes a breath. Then she opens it on her knee, and turns it, so you can see.

It's a woman in charcoal. Again and again, page after page. Sitting, with her arms round her knees. Standing, with one hand on the back of a chair. Lying on her side, propped on an elbow, her hair spilling. They're quick, all of them, a few long lines each, fluid and sure, the charcoal sweeping and then stopping exactly where it should. They're not careful, the way Lin's sigils are careful. They're alive. You can feel the weight of the woman in the chair. You can feel the warmth of the room she sat in.

"Lin," you say. "These are beautiful."

Across the table, Kit has put down her pen.

She leans over. She looks at the drawings for a long time, without a word, while Lin turns the pages slowly, one after another. Her face is very serious.

"These are very good," Kit says at last. Quite seriously. As if she's giving evidence.

Lin goes pinker. You wouldn't have thought it possible.

She closes the sketchbook. She looks at it. And then she turns her head to you, and says, very low, so low you barely hear it under the hum of the lamps:

"I'd like to draw you. Sometime." She swallows. "If you'd sit."

"Yes," you say.

Lin looks down at her hands, flat on the sketchbook. Ink and charcoal on her fingertips.

"Clothed," she says. "To start."

Across the table, Kit picks up her book, and stares at it very hard indeed.

---

Kit goes at ten.

She gathers her books into a neat stack, and puts on her coat, and stands a moment by the table. Then she nods to you, once, and to Lin, once, and goes, her steps quiet on the boards.

Lin stays till half past, drawing in the back of her sketchbook, small things, your pen, your hand, the green lamp. Then she gets up too. On her way past she touches your shoulder, lightly, with her fingertips, just for a second. And then she's gone.

You write on.

The reading room empties round you. The lamps go off one by one down the far end, until there's just yours and a handful of others, and then just yours. Your hand aches. Your pages pile up. You write about the thread you feel from your chest to the light. You write about breathing out. You write about the stone going warm in your palm, steady as tea, once you stopped holding your breath.

It's nearly midnight when you write the last line.

*Intent comes from wherever the wanting lives.*

You look at it for a long time. Then you put the cap on your pen.

---

Outside it's bitterly cold, the frost white on the grass, the stars sharp and close above the roofs.

You stop halfway across the quad and look round.

Every window is dark. The Great Hall, the lecture rooms, the houses, Ashcombe across the way where Kit will be asleep, Thornfield ahead of you, all of them dark, the whole ring of grey stone buildings black against the stars.

All but one. Down in the west range, one window is lit. A low, steady lamp, behind a white blind.

The infirmary.

It's Thursday. Ten till six.

You stand there in the cold, your breath going up in clouds, your essay held against your chest, and look at it. You think of Yusra at the desk under that lamp, in her reading glasses, with her book open, and the six white beds in a row behind her, and the long night ahead. Her dark head bent over the page. Turning it.

You stand there a while.

Then you go on, up to Thornfield, and up the stairs.

Bea's door is shut, and the line under it is dark. Her lamp is out. You go past it quietly, on your toes, the way she'd want you to go past it at midnight, even though she told you to knock.

In 14 you put the finished essay on your desk, squared up neat, and lay Kit's black handkerchief on top of it.

You're asleep almost before you've lain down.
