On Tuesday it's raining again.

Soft and steady, the kind that settles in for the day: a hush on the window of 15, a fine grey veil over the mere, the gutters ticking. The room is dim. It's warm under the blanket.

Bea's alarm goes.

Bea groans. A long, wounded groan, at the alarm, at the rain, at the whole idea of Tuesday. She slaps the phone silent and burrows back under the blanket.

And puts her cold feet on you.

Both of them, flat against your calves, icy, like two fish from the mere. You yelp. Bea laughs into the pillow, wickedly, and keeps them there.

---

She skips the run.

She stands at the window in her vest, looking at the rain coming down on the shore path, and makes a face, and says something rude about it. Then she drops to the rug and does press-ups instead.

You count them for her.

Sitting up in bed with the blanket round your shoulders, counting out loud while she goes down and up, down and up, in the grey light. *Twenty-six. Twenty-seven.* Her shoulders working. Her hair coming loose. *Forty.* She's red in the face. *Forty-one.* She grunts. *Fifty*, you say, and she collapses on the rug, and lies there with her arms out, breathing, staring at the ceiling.

"Fifty," Bea says. "That's a good number. That's a race number."

---

At breakfast she eats in silence.

For once. Bea, who talks through every meal of every day, who talks with her mouth full and her spoon waving: silent, on the bench beside you, staring down at her porridge. Stirring it. Round and round, the golden syrup going in long slow spirals.

Priya looks at you across the table. You shake your head, a little.

Lin, beside her, quietly slides the toast rack an inch closer to Bea's elbow. Bea doesn't notice. She stirs.

The kitchen goes on round her: the radio, the kettle, somebody laughing by the sink, the rain on the window over it. Bea sits in the middle of all of it like a stone in a stream, and stirs, and stares.

Then Bea says it. To the porridge.

"Four days," she says.

You put your hand on her back, between her shoulder blades. She leans into it, just a little, and keeps stirring.

---

At nine group F climbs the library tower.

The slit windows on the stair are streaming. Every one of them a blur of grey water, and the stone of the stairwell cold and damp, and your footsteps loud. In Penhallow's study the round window is running with rain, so the mere below is only grey through it, wavering, like something seen from under water.

The ward essays are on her desk.

In a pile. Every one marked; you can see the green ink from the doorway, bright on the white pages, at the top of each and down the margins and at the end.

You sit. Penhallow folds her hands.

Then she hands them back.

One at a time. She picks each one up off the pile, and says the name, and a word or two, and holds it out, and moves on to the next. No speeches. Just the name and the word.

"Ashworth. Better."

Kit takes hers.

β+, in green, at the top. You can see it from your chair. And under her last line, in the same green hand, two words: *Whose door?*

Kit reads it.

And goes quiet. Not the closed, sharp quiet she goes when she's hurt. Another one. Thoughtful. She sits with the page in her lap and looks at those two words for a long time, with her finger resting under them.

"Doyle."

Shiv takes hers. And folds it at once, without looking at it, in half and in half again, and pushes it into the pocket of her hoodie.

Unread.

Penhallow watches her do it. And lets her. She doesn't say anything at all; she just picks up the next one.

"Zhao. Yes."

Lin's has an α− on it.

You see it as she takes it: the alpha, in green, and the little minus beside it. An early alpha. Rare. And under her last line, the question: *And when you are ready?*

Lin looks at it.

Then she holds it to her chest. Both arms crossed over it, holding it flat against her cardigan, her eyes down. Her face doesn't do anything at all. But she holds it there, tight, as if it's warm.

"Osei. Interesting."

Amara's is a β+. She frowns at it, hard, her mouth going tight at the grade. Then she reads the question under it.

And laughs out loud.

Surprised, sudden, a bark of a laugh. She covers her mouth with her hand, and looks at Penhallow over it, and shakes her head.

"Raman. Hm."

Priya's is a β, with, underneath: *Is heat the only thing?*

"Rude," says Priya.

And grins at Penhallow. Penhallow's mouth twitches.

---

Yours comes last.

It's the last one on the pile. Penhallow picks it up. And holds it a moment, before she gives it to you. Just a moment, looking down at it in her hands, at the first page.

"Nala," she says.

She holds it out.

You take it.

α−. In green, at the top, the alpha and the little minus.

You turn to the last page. To your last lines, about a bandage, and a hand round a hand, and the hurt kept in while it mends.

And under them, in green:

*What are you mending, Nala? Or is it whom?*

You look up.

Penhallow is already looking at you. Over her glasses, on their chain. Kind blue eyes, in her weathered face. And very sharp.

"Bring me that one," she says, "when you know."

She reaches out, and pushes the shortbread tin your way. Across the desk, an inch, two inches.

"Not before," says Penhallow.

---

Then she sits back.

"Friday," she says. "The focus question. What it remembers, and should it." She looks round at you all. "What have you got?"

Kit sits up.

"It should remember," Kit says. "A focus that forgets is just jewellery."

Penhallow laughs.

Delighted, out loud, her head going back. "*Just jewellery*," she says. "Oh, I'm having that. Put it in, Ashworth."

Kit goes pink.

Then Lin speaks.

"A drawing is a focus," Lin says. Quietly, the way she says everything. "It remembers the hand." A pause. "And the one who sat."

You feel your face go hot.

Hot all at once, from your collar to your hair. Nine drawings, in Lin's private folder. One over her bed, in the centre, of a girl on a window seat, bare to the lamplight.

You look at your essay very hard.

Beside you, Kit grins.

You can feel her grinning, without looking. She bumps your knee with hers, under the table.

Penhallow taps the desk.

"And the cost?" she says.

She looks round at all of you.

"Foundations says the body keeps the account," Penhallow says. "Every spell is paid for, out of the caster. So." She lets it sit. "Who pays for memory?"

The room goes quiet.

You look at the question. You think of a ring cool in your palm, that felt like something. You think of a button warm from the air. Of a tin medal, dented, worn every day for years.

Who pays?

"Think about that," Penhallow says. "Friday."

And she claps her hands. Once, briskly.

Done.

---

On the tower stairs, Kit falls in beside you.

She has her essay in her hand still. She's looking at it as she goes down, at the two green words, her other hand trailing on the cold damp wall.

"Whose door," Kit says.

She shakes her head slowly.

"She knows it was Gran's," she says. "I said so. On the first page. Gran's house. Gran's door." She turns the page over and back. "So why ask?"

You think about it. About an oak door, iron-studded, with a ward worn into the wood. A small girl running her hand over it. The only door she ever wanted to be on the inside of.

"It's your door too," you say.

Kit stops.

On the stairs, in the light of a streaming slit window. She stops, and looks at you.

And nods. Slowly. Once, and then again.

---

At the foot of the stairs she puts her essay away, and straightens her bag, and turns to you with a different face.

"Tell Bea," says Kit, "that Ashcombe's won the Fell Cup six years running."

You stare at her.

"Thornfield last won it in 1987," Kit says. "Before any of us were born."

"Kit—"

"Then," says Kit, "say: *so?*"

She looks at you, very straight.

"Just that," she says. "*So?*" And she goes off into the rain.

---

At lunch you tell Bea.

You wait till she's eaten most of her shepherd's pie, because you're not a monster. Then you tell her. Ashcombe. Six years. Thornfield, 1987.

Bea puts her fork down.

Carefully, on the side of her plate.

"Six years," she says.

"Six years."

"Nineteen," says Bea, "eighty-seven."

You nod.

Bea looks at her plate for a moment.

Then she starts laughing.

Helplessly. Her shoulders going first, and then all of her, bent over the table, her hands over her face, shaking.

"Oh, good," she says, through her fingers. "Oh, *good*. No pressure, then. Not a scrap. Wonderful. *Wonderful*."

You wait till she comes up for air.

"So?" you say.

The way Kit told you. Just that, plainly.

Bea stares at you.

She stares for a long moment, with her hands still half over her face and her eyes wet from laughing.

Then she grins.

And the grin stays. It doesn't go. It settles on her face and stays there, wide, her dimples deep, her eyes going bright and hard and certain.

"So," says Bea.

She picks up her fork.

"So we row," she says. "So who cares. So I'll have the best person on the jetty."

---

At two it's the Practice Hall, and the calling charm again.

The same row on every bench: thimble, pen, button, spoon. The rain loud on the high windows.

Bea faces her spoon.

You watch her. She doesn't jab this time. She holds her hand out low, and soft, and draws the backwards hook as if she's stroking a cat, and asks.

The spoon comes.

Gently. It lifts off the bench and drifts to her, level and slow, and lands in her palm with hardly a sound.

Bea cheers.

Out loud, both arms up, the spoon held high like a trophy. Half the hall turns round.

The demonstrator, three benches down, looks over.

"Better, Lindqvist," she says.

And Bea bows. To the bench. To the spoon, solemnly, holding it up. And to the room, with a flourish, right down to the floor.

---

You call the thimble across the whole bench.

Not six inches, this time. You stand at one end, and put the thimble at the far end, three feet away, and hold out your hand. You draw the hook. You ask, gently.

And the thimble comes.

All three feet, through the air, steady, towards you. You catch it.

Your head stays clear.

You wait for it: the pressure behind the eyes, the warning. It doesn't come. You do it again, and it comes again, and your head is still clear. You breathe out.

At the next bench down, Kit calls a pen.

Not from her own bench. From *yours*: from the row in front of you, the pen at your elbow, the length of two benches away. Against the rules. You feel it go past your arm, a little breath of air, and land in her hand.

The demonstrator sees it. You're sure she sees it.

She looks the other way.

---

In the afternoon you and Lin take the focus essay to the Snug.

Up the short stair in the old library, into the little panelled room. Someone's lit the grate, and it's burning low and orange. The rain runs down the one small window. The green sofas sag.

You write. Lin draws.

She's curled at the other end of the sofa with her sketchbook, drawing the grate, the fire, your hands on your notebook. Charcoal on her fingers. The scratch of it. For a long time, there's only that, and the rain, and the fire.

Then she stops.

She sits with the charcoal still in her hand, and looks at the fire.

"Penhallow asked when I'm ready," she says.

You put your pen down.

"I'm ready to say I'm staying," Lin says. "I said it. In the letter." She turns the charcoal in her fingers. "Now I wait to hear it back."

You reach across the sofa.

You take her hand. Her charcoal hand, black-fingered, smudged, warm.

Lin looks at it in yours.

For a long time. Your fingers round hers, the charcoal coming off on your skin.

"And I'm ready for the other thing," she says.

Very quietly.

"Nearly."

You remember the window seat. Her drawing pinned over her bed. Her crying, and kissing you, and saying it.

"Soon," you say.

The way she said it.

Lin smiles. And goes pink, slowly, from her collar up. And takes her hand back, gently, and goes back to her drawing.

You can see her smiling at it the whole time.

---

In the evening Bea is in the kitchen with Morwenna.

They're at the end of the big table, heads together, with the salt cellar between them. And they've drawn the race on the table in salt. A long pale line of it for the shore. Little heaps for the boathouse, and the reeds, and the village jetty at the far end.

Morwenna is drawing with one finger.

She's tall, in her navy fleece, her ponytail under the headband, and she's very serious. She draws the start, at the boathouse. Then the line of the course, down the mere. Then a curve, where the shore bends.

"The bend by the reeds," she says.

Then the jetty.

Bea nods. And nods. Very serious, her chin on her hands, staring at the salt.

"Stroke rate up at the bend," Morwenna says.

She taps the curve.

"Ashcombe always fades there," she says. "Always. Six years, and they fade there every time. They just win anyway." She looks at Bea. "Not this year."

Bea takes a biro out of her pocket.

And writes on her hand.

She shows you later, upstairs, holding it out on her palm. In biro, in big wobbly capitals: *BEND*.

And under it, smaller: *Nala*.

---

At half past nine you fill the flask.

The old tartan thermos with the dented lid. Sweet tea. Three sugars. Yusra's on tonight, ten till six; you know it now, the way you know the stairs.

You wait at the foot of them.

At quarter to ten she comes down.

Coat, bag, badge. Her face set for the night. And she finds you there, waiting, with the flask in your hands against your chest.

"Again," Yusra says.

"Every time," you say.

Her mouth twitches.

She takes the flask. And her fingers stay on yours. Round the dented tartan, over the backs of your hands, warm. She doesn't let go.

She looks up the stairs.

Empty. The landing dark, every door shut.

She looks down the hall.

Empty. The kitchen door closed, the radio faint behind it.

You hear the house round you: a pipe knocking somewhere above, the rain on the fanlight over the front door, the hall clock. Her fingers are warm on yours. Her eyes come back to your face, and stay there.

Then she leans in, and kisses your cheek.

Very quickly. Warden or not. Her lips warm and gone, a breath against your skin.

And she stands back.

"Saturday," she says. Low. "After the race. I'll be waiting at the front door."

"I'll be cheering hoarse by then," you say.

Yusra almost laughs. You see it start, and catch, at the corner of her mouth.

"Good," she says. "I'll make you tea."

She goes out into the rain.

With her lantern from the porch, and your flask in her bag. The front door swings shut behind her on the wet dark.

---

In 15, Bea is asleep already.

Face down, her face pressed into your pillow, not hers. One arm flung out across the blanket, palm up. *BEND* on it in biro, and *Nala* underneath, smudged a little now.

You sit on the edge of the bed by her lamp.

You take out your essay, and turn to the last page.

*What are you mending, Nala? Or is it whom?*

You read it. Again.

What are you mending.

Whom.

You think of Yusra's palm. The thin pink line across it, healed clean.

You think of Bea's sums. The columns of them, in her big round hand, and the small boy with her dimples.

You think of Lin's letter. The envelope in the slot. The small soft sound it made, dropping.

You think of Shiv's water. The buoy, and the mist, and *kick, Siobhan* said quietly to the mere.

You think of Kit's door.

You fold the essay away.

You put it on the floor, under the bed, and turn out the lamp, and get in beside Bea.

And Bea, without waking, reaches back for you.
