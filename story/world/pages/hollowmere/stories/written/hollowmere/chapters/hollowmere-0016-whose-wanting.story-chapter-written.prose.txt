On Tuesday there's frost on the shingle.

It crunches under your bare feet on the way to the water, white and sharp, and the mere is steaming in the dawn, great soft clouds of it rising off the flat black surface into the cold, so thick you can barely see the buoy. You and Shiv go in through it, under and up, breathing out, and swim out into the white, and touch the buoy, and turn, and swim back, through the steam, with the sun coming up behind it gold.

On the shingle the red-cheeked second-year looks at her stopwatch, and then looks at you.

"Ashby," she says. "Five minutes."

Shiv whistles. Long and low, through her teeth.

"Five," she says. "From your feet in the shallows to five minutes, in two weeks." She shakes her head. "I've created a monster."

She takes your towel from you and wraps it round your shoulders, and rubs your arms through it, briskly, up and down, to get the blood back. Your teeth are going. Her hands are warm even through the towel.

"Penhallow today," she says. "Essays back." She looks at you sideways, rubbing. "Scared?"

"Yes," you say.

Shiv shrugs. "Mine's three pages and one joke," she says. "And I'd do it the same again." She gives your arms a last hard rub. "So there's that."

---

At breakfast Bea puts toast in front of you.

Two slices, buttered, with marmalade. She pushes the plate at you across the table, and folds her arms, and watches.

"I'm not hungry," you say.

"Eat it," says Bea. "You can't get an essay back on an empty stomach. It's a known fact."

"Is it."

"It's science," says Bea. "Ask Priya."

You eat the toast.

---

At nine it's Penhallow's study, up the winding stair.

The round window is full of the bright morning mere, still steaming a little at the edges. The shortbread tin is on the desk. And beside it, squared up in a neat stack, are the essays, the six of them, with a flash of green showing at the edges of the pages.

"Before I hand these back," says Penhallow, "a word for Ashby, who won't know how we mark."

She looks at you over her glasses.

"Alpha, beta, gamma," she says. "Best to worst. With plus and minus to split the difference. Most first-year essays are a beta of one kind or another. A gamma means start again." She taps the stack. "An early alpha is rare."

She hands them round.

Priya's comes back first. She looks at the top, and her face lights up, and she hugs it to her chest. "Beta *plus*," she mouths at you across the table, delighted.

Shiv's. She glances at it. Beta. She shrugs, and folds it in half, and puts it in her pocket.

Lin's. Beta plus. She goes pink, and puts it face down on her knee, and then turns it over again to look.

Kit's, and Amara's. Penhallow hands them out together, one to each, and they both look at the top of their own page; and then both of them, at the same moment, glance sideways at the other's. Beta plus. Beta plus. They look at each other's pages, and then both look away, quickly, out of the round window, in opposite directions.

Yours.

Penhallow puts it down in front of you on the desk.

At the top, in green ink, in her sharp slanting hand: *α−*.

You look at it. You look at it for a long time.

And at the bottom, under your last line, under *intent comes from wherever the wanting lives*, there's one more line in green. Just one. A question.

*Whose wanting?*

You stare at it.

When you look up, Penhallow is watching you over her glasses. Her kind blue eyes are steady on yours.

"Think about it," she says. "Friday."

Kit leans over. She looks at your page. She sees the alpha, and says under her breath, very dry: "Of course you did."

But when you look at her, she's smiling.

Priya squeaks. She actually squeaks, and leans right across Shiv to hug you sideways, hard, round the shoulders, her glasses knocking your ear. Under the table, Lin's fingers touch your sleeve. Just for a moment. Just there.

---

Penhallow spends the hour on mending.

"Is a mended cup," she asks, "the same cup?"

And the room splits in two.

Priya says of course it is, it's the same china, the same atoms, the same cup. Amara says it isn't, it can't be, it's been broken; the break is part of it now, the mend is part of it, it's a new thing that happens to be made of the old one. Lin says quietly that it's the same cup with a different story. Kit says it depends whether you can see the seam. Amara says that's cheating. Kit says it's *precision*.

Penhallow listens to all of it, with her hands folded.

"Doyle," she says. "You've been very quiet."

Shiv leans back in her chair.

"It's a new cup," she says, "with an old cup's habits."

Penhallow laughs. Out loud, a real bark of a laugh, startled out of her. She opens the shortbread tin and pushes it across the desk.

"Have two," she says.

On the way down the tower stairs afterwards, Amara is laughing about her beta plus. Lightly, airily, all the way down, to Priya, to anyone. "Beta *plus*," she says. "The *humiliation*. I'll have to change my name. I'll have to leave the country." Her laugh is bright and easy, and goes round and round the stairwell after her, all the way to the bottom.

---

At eleven it's the Foundations lecture, in the Long Room.

Amara usually sits in front of you, two rows down. Today her seat is empty.

It stays empty all through the first half. You notice it the way you'd notice a tooth gone, your eyes going back to it.

At the break you slip out to the bathroom along the corridor from the Long Room. It's an old tiled room, cold, with high frosted windows and a row of cubicles with dark wooden doors.

It's quiet. And then, not quite quiet.

Someone is crying in the end cubicle. Very quietly. Trying not to. A small, stifled, catching sound, over and over, muffled, as if into a sleeve.

You stand at the basins.

There's a hand resting on the inside of the end cubicle's door, on the handle. You can see the wrist over the top edge, where the door doesn't reach the ceiling: a slim dark wrist, and on it, a gold watch.

You know that watch.

"Amara?" you say.

The crying stops. All at once. A sharp silence.

"Go away, Freckles," says Amara. Her voice is thick.

You don't go away. You sit down on the cold tiled floor outside the end cubicle, with your back against the wall beside its door, and your knees drawn up.

A long silence.

"I do this," Amara says at last, through the door. Her voice is low and blurred. "After every test. Every single one. Even when I win." A breath that catches. "*Especially* when I win. Isn't that stupid."

You think about it.

"You argued a room flat in four minutes last week," you say. "I saw it. I was there. You had them in four minutes, and the girl after you didn't stand a chance."

There's a pause. And then a laugh. A wet one, choked and damp, but a laugh.

"That's the trick," says Amara. "You make it look easy." A sniff. "And then you come in here."

You sit on the floor and listen to her breathe.

"What do you do," she says, after a while, through the door, "when you're frightened?"

You think about that too.

"I swim," you say. "In a freezing mere. At dawn. To a buoy."

Silence.

"Mad," says Amara. "That's utterly mad, Freckles."

But she's laughing now. Properly. You can hear it change: the wet catch going out of it, the real laugh coming through, low and warm and helpless. And then the bolt slides back.

---

She comes out at last.

Her eyes are red. Her face is wet. She doesn't look at you. She goes to the mirror over the basins, and runs the cold tap, and fixes her face. Fast. Expert. In silence. Cold water; a tissue, pressed under each eye; a small gold compact out of her pocket, a pad of powder, a flick, another; a lipstick, once round. Her braids tucked back. Her chin lifted.

In under a minute she looks exactly as she always does. Poised. Polished. Untouchable.

She looks at your reflection in the glass, behind her own.

"Tell no one," Amara says.

"I'm good at that," you say.

She looks at you in the mirror for a while. A long while. As if she's really looking at you, for the first time, past the freckles.

Then she turns round, and bends, and kisses your cheek. Quick and fierce. Her mouth warm on your skin for a second, and the smell of cocoa butter.

"Alpha minus," says Amara. "Damn you."

And she's gone.

---

At lunch she's at the long table with Priya, and she is dazzling.

She's loud. She's brilliant. She's telling a story about her grandmother and a goat, with her hands going and her gold hoops swinging, and the whole end of the table is laughing, and Priya is laughing hardest of all, gazing at her. There isn't a trace of the end cubicle on her anywhere.

Just once, across the table, she catches your eye.

And winks.

---

At two it's practical, and the cracked cups again.

You take your cup to your bench. You look at the crack. You think about yesterday, the mending going halfway and stopping, the seam where it gave out.

This time you breathe out longer.

You draw the sigil, the split line and the join and the little closed loop, and as you call, you keep breathing out. Slowly. Longer than you think you can. All the way out, and then a little further. And you feel the crack close under it. Feel it, almost, like a zip in your fingertips. The two sides of the china sliding together, sealing, past the halfway point where it stopped yesterday, on, on, down, all the way down to the base.

You open your eyes.

It's whole. There's a line left, faint, a pale hairline where the crack was. But it's closed. All the way.

The demonstrator is beside you. She picks up the cup and holds it up to the high window, turning it in the light, looking along the line.

"Yes," she says.

Beside you, Bea's cup has gone warm again. Steaming gently. Completely uncracked; completely unmended. She glares at it. She glares at it with the whole force of her being.

And then she starts to laugh. She laughs and laughs, holding on to the edge of the bench, until she has to sit down on the floor.

Across the hall, Lin mends hers on the second try, and when she holds it up you can see the line left on it is so fine it looks drawn in pencil.

At the end bench, back to the room, Kit mends hers. You see her shoulders lift and fall. You don't see the cup. You don't need to.

Two benches down from her, Amara isn't looking at her own cup.

She's looking at Kit's back.

At Kit's turned back, and her lowered head, and the way she's standing between her cup and the whole room. Amara looks for a long moment, frowning slightly, her mended cup forgotten in her hand. As if she's doing a sum, and it won't come out.

---

At dusk the Practice Hall is empty again.

This time you don't sit with your back to her. You sit on the front bench, facing the floor, and you look at Kit, openly, from the very start.

She stands in the middle of the floor. She has taken her coat off and laid it on a bench, and pushed up the sleeves of her black jumper to the elbow. Her forearms are very pale in the dim light.

She closes her eyes, and breathes out, before she starts. A long one. You watch her shoulders drop.

Then she opens her eyes, and looks at you, and lifts her hand.

The sigil. The stroke, the loop, the hook turning in. The word.

Her hand stops in the air.

You see it go. The freeze coming down over her like frost. Her hand rigid at the end of the hook, her body stopped, her face whitening, whitening. Her eyes locked on yours.

You hold her eyes. You don't look away.

"Out," you say. Softly.

She breathes out.

And the glim comes.

Small. Wavering. A tiny pale flicker above her stiff hand, trembling, uncertain, so faint at first that you think it'll go out like yesterday's. But it doesn't. It steadies. It gathers itself, and brightens, just a little, into a pale bead of light, hanging in the air above her hand.

It holds.

"One," you say. Quietly. "Two."

Kit's eyes are on yours. She doesn't blink.

"Three. Four."

The light is trembling. Holding.

"Five."

On six it goes out.

Kit lowers her hand. Slowly, very slowly, as if it's heavy. She stands there in the middle of the dim floor, looking at you.

"Five," she says. "Watched."

Her voice is shaking. Her eyes are shining, very bright, in the dusk.

She does it again. And again. The second time it holds to four. The third time, she breathes out before you have to tell her, and the glim comes at once, pale and small and steady, and you count, and it holds, five, six, seven, eight, before it goes.

---

After, you sit together on the front bench in the near-dark, and she puts her head on your shoulder.

You sit like that a while. The high windows have gone from grey to blue to nearly black.

"Tomorrow," Kit says, into your shoulder. "Two pairs of eyes."

"Whose?" you ask.

Kit is quiet a long time.

"Bea's," she says at last. "She's decent." A pause. "She said so."

You laugh, and she feels it, and her mouth goes in at the corner against your jumper.

Then you tell her about the alpha. And the question. You take the essay out of your bag, and she sits up and holds it to the last of the light from the window, and reads the green line at the bottom. Then reads it again.

"Whose wanting," Kit says.

She hands it back.

"Penhallow always asks the one you can't answer yet," she says. "Granny said. Every time. The one you can't answer *yet*." She looks at you in the dark. "She means you will."

---

You walk her back to Ashcombe across the quad.

The frost is coming down again already. The lawns are going white and stiff, crunching underfoot, and the stars are out, sharp and close. At Ashcombe's door she stops on the step, and turns, and kisses you.

Slower tonight. Much slower. Her cold hands on your face, her mouth careful and warm, taking her time.

Then she goes in.

---

At Thornfield you go up the stairs and knock on 15.

"Come in," calls Bea.

She's in bed with a book, in a huge jumper, her hair down. You sit on the end of her bed and tell her. About the hall. About the five, and the eight. About tomorrow.

"Two pairs of eyes," you say. "Kit wants yours."

Bea sits up very straight in bed. The book falls off her knees.

"Me?" she says. "Really? *Me?*"

"You," you say. "She says you're decent."

Bea's face does something extraordinary. It goes pink, and then pinker, and then it breaks open into the biggest grin you've ever seen on it, both dimples, all her teeth.

"I'll sit very still," Bea promises. Solemnly. Her hand on her heart. "I'll be the stillest person in the *world*. I won't breathe. I won't *blink*."

Then she asks to see the essay.

You give it to her. She turns to the top. She sees the *α−* in green.

And she whoops. A great loud whoop, both fists in the air, that rings off the walls.

Through the wall, the girl in 16 bangs, hard, three times.

"*Sorry!*" Bea shouts at the wall, not sorry at all.

---

On the way back across the corridor you pass room 1, at the head of the stairs.

It's dark. The line under the door is black.

It's Tuesday. Yusra is at the infirmary all night.

---

In 14 you sit on your bed under the lamp, and take the essay out of your bag, and turn to the last page, and read the green question again.

*Whose wanting?*

You think about it.

You think of Alan. In the chair by the back window, that last evening, with the book going heavy on his chest, and the light going. Alan who wanted girls, always, quietly, all his life, from a distance.

And you think of this body. Small and light and new, with its freckles and its red hair and its cold feet. This body that has been kissed now on a rock, and a jetty wall, and in the dark of a cinema, and in a window seat with charcoal on its cheek. This body that wants, too, in its own way, fiercely, with every sense in it new.

Whose wanting?

You can't say which.

You lie back on the pillow, with the essay open on your chest and the lamp still on, and you're asleep before you've decided, with the question still open.
