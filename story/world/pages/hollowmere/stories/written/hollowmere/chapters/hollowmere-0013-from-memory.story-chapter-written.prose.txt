On Saturday you let the swimmers go without you.

Shiv will be reluctant on her own this once. Instead, at seven, you go down to the boathouse wrapped in the old fisherman's jumper Bea bought you, the sleeves pulled down over your hands, your breath going up white in front of you.

It's Bea's first morning.

The mere is grey and flat and very still, and the mist is lying on it in a thick white layer, so the far shore is gone and the fells float above it like islands. The boathouse doors are open. Girls in leggings and fleeces are carrying long narrow boats out over their heads, four to a boat, staggering a little, and laying them on the water at the landing stage with enormous care.

Out on the water, a small motor launch is idling, and standing up in the back of it, in a sleeveless navy fleece, with a megaphone, is the captain.

"*Novices!*" she bawls. Her voice rolls out across the mist. "*Fours! Coxes in first! Bow pairs, don't fall in, I am not fishing you out!*"

You find Bea at once. She's climbing into the front of the second boat, the seat nearest the prow, her honey hair scraped back into a tight knot for once, not one strand loose. Her face is set. Fierce. She looks the way she did at breakfast on Wednesday when she told you to knock.

She doesn't see you. She's looking at her oar.

You go and stand at the end of the jetty with your hands in your sleeves.

The boats push off. They slide out into the mist one by one, with the coxes in the sterns calling out in high clear voices, and the oars come up together, more or less, and go in, more or less, and the boats lurch forward.

Bea's boat gets five strokes.

On the sixth, something goes wrong. One of the oars in the middle goes in at a slant and won't come out, and jams, and twists, and the girl holding it nearly goes over backwards off her seat, and the whole boat slews sideways and stops dead in the water with a great slap and a shriek.

"*CRAB!*" bawls the captain through the megaphone. "*That, ladies, is a crab! Don't panic!*"

You cheer.

You don't decide to. It just comes out of you, too loud, across the water: "*Come on, Bea!*"

It rings off the mist. Everyone in Bea's boat turns round to look at you on the jetty, a small red-haired girl in an enormous jumper, waving. Then the whole crew starts laughing. The cox laughs. The girl who caught the crab laughs, hanging on to her oar.

Bea goes scarlet. Scarlet from her collar to her hairline; you can see it from the jetty.

And then she grins. Right across her face, both dimples, at you.

"*Sit UP!*" bawls the captain. "*From backstops! Again!*"

On the second length, the boat finds it.

You watch it happen. The oars come up together, and go in together, and come out together, four blades flashing in and out of the grey water at once, and the boat stops lurching and starts to run, long and smooth, the mist curling off its wake. And at the front of it, Bea. Her back straight, her arms long. Her blade going in clean, every stroke, every single one, without a splash.

---

On the landing stage after, the crews come in dripping and steaming and stiff, hauling their boats up out of the water.

The captain comes up the stage in her navy fleece, the megaphone under her arm. She's tall and long-limbed and wind-flushed, her dark ponytail tucked under a navy wool headband, grinning wide. She stops in front of Bea, and claps her on the shoulder, hard enough to rock her.

"Bow," says Morwenna. "You're keeping it."

And goes on.

Bea stands there. She looks after her. Then she looks round, and finds you at the end of the jetty, and her whole face lights up like a lamp.

---

Brunch in the Great Hall runs until eleven.

Bea eats three eggs. Three fried eggs, and toast, and beans, and a sausage, and half your mushrooms, and talks the whole time with her hands. About the crab, which wasn't her fault, it was Clare at three. About the captain, about *bow*, did you hear, *you're keeping it*. About the way the boat felt when it started running, like it was alive, like it lifted. Her hands go up and show you the oars going in. Her fork goes up and shows you the boat. Egg yolk drips on the table, and she doesn't notice.

You sit and eat your toast and watch her and feel happier than you have any right to.

Priya comes and sits down across from you with a tray.

She's frowning. She's in a red corduroy pinafore with her hair knotted up with a pencil through it, and her tray has a coffee and a croissant on it, and she isn't touching either of them.

"Dev rang," she says. "Last night."

"Oh," says Bea, putting her fork down. "Is he all right?"

"He's fine. He's fine. He's always fine. He told me about his week for forty minutes." Priya turns her coffee cup round on its saucer. Round, and round. "And I couldn't think what to say. To him. I just — sat there. With the phone. I kept saying *mm*."

You wait. Bea waits.

"I kept thinking about the debate," says Priya. She's looking at the coffee. "Amara. The ring. The way she held it up to the window." She stops.

And goes pink. Bright pink, right up under her tortoiseshell glasses.

Bea looks at you. You look at Bea. It's a whole conversation, that look, and it lasts about a second.

"Priya," says Bea gently. "Thinking about things is allowed, you know."

Priya laughs. Too high, too quick.

"I *know* that," she says. "Obviously. I'm a scientist. I think about everything." She picks up the croissant, and puts it down. "Did I tell you about the mouse? At charmcraft? The clockwork one? Because I found out how it runs without a key, and you are not going to *believe*—"

Bea lets her. You let her. You sit and listen to the whole thing about the mouse.

---

At two o'clock you go down a floor and knock on room 8.

The door opens at once. Lin is standing there in a canvas apron spotted all over with paint and ink, her ponytail over one shoulder, a stick of charcoal in her hand. She looks very nervous and very pleased.

"Come in," she says. "Sorry. It's a mess."

It isn't a mess. It's the most beautiful room you've seen at Hollowmere.

The walls are drawings. Every inch of them, pinned up edge to edge from the skirting board nearly to the ceiling: the fells in pencil, in ink, in charcoal, in every weather. Hands, pages and pages of hands, holding cups, holding pens, folded, open. The mere at dawn, flat and pale. Girls on the quad, small, quick, walking with their books and their scarves. A heron, standing in the shallows on one leg, so still and grey you'd swear it was about to move.

You stand in the middle of the room and turn slowly round.

"Lin," you say.

"Sit," says Lin, quickly, before you can say anything else. Her ears are pink. "Here. Please."

There's a window seat with a flat cushion on it, and behind it the long window and the quad below, the grey lawns and the stone buildings and the afternoon. She sits you there, half turned, with your back to the corner of the window frame, so the light comes in over your shoulder and falls across your face.

She stands back and looks at you. She comes and tilts your shoulders a little. She stands back again.

"Just be still," she says. "You can talk. I'll tell you when you can't."

She sits down on a low stool across the room with a board on her knees and a big sheet of paper clipped to it.

And she draws.

At first it's quick. Her hand flies across the paper, big sweeping strokes, the charcoal whispering, her eyes going from you to the page to you to the page, quick, quick, as if she's catching something before it gets away. Then it slows. Her strokes get smaller and softer. She leans in. The only sound in the room is the scratch of the charcoal, and her breathing, slow and even, in and out, the way she taught you to breathe for the sigils.

Once she puts the board down and gets up, and comes over to you.

She doesn't say anything. She just reaches out and puts two fingers under your chin, very gently, and turns your face a little, an inch, towards the light. Her fingers are cool and dusty with charcoal. She looks at you. Then she goes back to her stool and picks up the board again.

You can feel where her fingers were for a long time after.

"Tell me about home," she says, after a while, drawing.

So you tell her about Hebden Bridge.

You tell her what's on your letter. A rented room above a shop. A town in Yorkshire, in a valley. You tell her what you imagine: a steep street, a river, stone houses climbing up the hillside on top of each other, a shop with a bell over the door. You tell her about the room you imagine over it, small, with a window over the street.

You've never seen it. You don't say that. But you hear it in your own voice as you talk: the guessing, the *I suppose*, the *I think*.

Lin draws for a long minute.

"You talk," she says at last, quietly, not looking up from the paper, "like someone who's new here. To all of it."

The charcoal scratches on.

You look out of the window at the quad. At the girls going by with their books. At the grey stone and the green lawn and the sky.

"I am," you say. "In ways I can't explain. Not yet."

Lin's hand pauses on the paper.

Then she nods, once, and draws on. She doesn't ask. She just lets it be, and the charcoal scratches, and the light moves slowly across the floor.

---

After an hour she sets the charcoal down, and turns the board round.

It's you.

It's you, in the window, half turned, the light falling over your shoulder. Your hair long and dark down your back. The freckles, a scatter of small soft marks across your nose. Your mouth. Your eyes, looking out at something past the edge of the paper. It's done in charcoal, in quick lines and soft smudges, and it isn't careful, and it's more *you* than any mirror has ever shown you. More you than the face in the glass on the back of your door. It's the person you've been learning to be, sitting in a window, looking out, and Lin has seen her.

You can't say anything for a moment.

"There are two others," Lin says.

She gets up quickly, before you can speak. She goes to the wall by the bed and unpins two sheets from among the hands and the herons, and brings them over, and holds them out.

They're you. Both of them.

At the Welcome Dinner. You know it at once: the long table, the candles floating, your gown. In the first you're looking down the table at something, your face lit and still. In the second you're laughing. Head back, eyes screwed up, laughing at something, and beside you, roughed in with three quick lines, is a blonde bun with strands falling out of it.

"From memory," Lin says. She's scarlet. "I noticed you. The first night. At the dinner." She swallows. "I'm sorry. It's odd. I know it's odd."

"It's lovely," you say.

You mean it. You look at the girl laughing at Bea's joke at the Welcome Dinner, the first night, the night you'd only just woken up in this body, and Lin was watching from down the table, and went home and drew you.

"It's lovely, Lin," you say again.

---

Lin sits down beside you on the window seat.

Close. Her shoulder against your shoulder. Charcoal on her fingers, black on the tips, a smudge of it on the back of her hand. She looks down at the drawings on your knee.

"May I," she says, very quietly.

And stops. And can't finish it. You watch her try. Her mouth opens, and closes.

"Yes," you say. Before she has to.

Lin looks at you.

And then she leans in, and kisses you.

Lightly. Very briefly. Her mouth is small and soft and warm, and tastes of tea, and it's on yours for a second, two, and then it's gone.

She pulls back. She looks at you, astonished, as if she's surprised herself more than she's surprised you. Her eyes are wide and dark under her fringe. Her hand has come up and touched your cheek without her noticing, and there's charcoal on your cheek now, you can feel it, a soft dusty smudge.

"I've wanted to," Lin says, "since the dinner." Her voice is very small. "That's the first girl I've ever kissed."

So you kiss her again.

Slowly this time. For her. So she'll have it to keep: a slow one, a gentle one, without any hurry in it at all. And Lin goes still, and then soft, and her charcoal hand comes up and rests on your jaw, very lightly, and stays there.

---

In the evening it's cocoa in the Thornfield kitchen.

The whole top floor is crammed in. Girls on the benches, girls on the floor with their backs against the cupboards, girls in dressing gowns and pyjamas and jumpers, the big saucepan going on the stove and the steam on the windows and somebody's radio playing too quietly to hear. Bea is sitting up on the counter by the sink, swinging her heels against the cupboard doors, holding court about the crab.

You squeeze onto the end of the bench below her, between her knees and the wall.

Bea looks down at you. And stops talking mid-word.

Her eyebrows go up.

She leans down, and takes your chin in her hand, and turns your face to the light, the way Lin did. There's a smudge on your cheek. You'd forgotten it. Bea licks her thumb and wipes it off, firmly, the way you'd clean a child's face, and holds up her thumb to look at it. It's black.

"Lin?" she murmurs, under the noise.

You nod.

Bea looks at the ceiling. A long look, up at the cracks in the plaster.

"There's a queue now, is there," she says.

But she's smiling as she says it. Mostly. The corners of her mouth are, and her dimples are, even if her eyes are doing something more complicated. And she reaches down under the edge of the counter, where the girls on the floor can't see, and finds your hand, and takes it. And keeps it.

---

At ten Yusra comes in.

She has her clipboard. "Boiler list," she says, in the doorway, "anyone I've missed—" And then she stops, and looks round at the kitchen: the crowd of girls, the steaming pan, the radio, Bea on the counter.

Her eyes find you.

On the end of the bench, between Bea's knees and the wall. Your hand in Bea's under the counter. Her dark eyes rest on you. Just a moment. Steady.

Then she looks round at all of them.

"Lights out on this floor by midnight, ladies," she says.

She takes three names for the boiler list, and writes them down, and turns to go. And as she passes the end of the bench, passing you, close, she says, quieter, only to you:

"Nala."

Just your name.

And she's gone, down the corridor, her footsteps going away.

Above you, Bea is very quiet. She doesn't say anything. She just squeezes your hand under the counter. And keeps holding it.

---

In 14, in the dark, you lie awake.

You lie on your back with the handkerchief under your pillow and look up at the ceiling, and you count. Like coals in a grate. Slowly.

Shiv, on the rock, with the mere running off her.

Bea, on the jetty wall, tasting of sugar.

Kit, in the dark of the cinema, cool and then warm.

Lin, in her window, tasting of tea.

And a name. Said once, low, in a kitchen doorway, by someone passing.
