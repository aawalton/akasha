At half past six on Tuesday morning, somebody bangs on your door.

Not knocks. Bangs, with the flat of a hand, three times, cheerful and relentless, and then a voice through the wood: "Nala! Nala Ashby! Half six! I said half six!"

You surface from a sleep so deep it felt like falling. For a second you don't know where you are, and then you do, all at once: the narrow bed, the knocking radiator, the weight of long hair across your face, the small soft body curled under the blanket. Still here. Still her. Grey light at the window.

Bang, bang. "I can hear you breathing."

"Absolutely not," you say into the pillow, loud enough to carry.

"Absolutely yes."

"Absolutely, completely, utterly not."

There's a pause on the other side of the door. Then Bea's voice, sly and reasonable: "If you come now, we're back by half seven. The hot water's still on at half seven."

You lie there and think about yesterday's shower, the shriek, the cold like a sheet of knives.

"I hate you," you say, and get up.

---

You haven't got running kit, so you go in leggings from the trunk and the grey jumper, and a pair of trainers you find at the bottom under the books that fit as if they were bought for these feet, because they were. Bea is waiting in the corridor in a faded rowing club top and tights, bouncing on her toes, her bun already coming down. She looks you up and down, says, "Good enough," and sets off down the stairs at a trot.

Outside the cold is enormous. The sky over the fells is pearl-grey going to pink, and the mere lies flat and dark and steaming faintly at the edges where the air is colder than the water. The shingle crunches under your feet. Bea starts running along the shore, easy and loping, and you start running after her.

And it's wonderful.

That's the shock of it. You'd braced for misery and instead your new body takes off like it was waiting for you to ask. It's so light. Your feet barely seem to touch the stones; your stride is short and quick and springy, your arms pumping, your hair whipping out behind you in its band, and the cold air pours into your chest and you feel fast, actually fast, flying along the edge of the water with the fells going by. You catch Bea up. You pass her. You hear her shout something delighted behind you and you laugh out loud, the high bright laugh, and keep going.

For about four minutes.

Then your body gives out all at once, as if someone has pulled a plug. Your lungs catch fire. Your legs turn to wet string. You stagger to a stop and bend over with your hands on your knees, dragging air in, your chest burning, your heart hammering so fast you can feel it in your teeth.

Bea jogs up and circles you, not even out of breath, laughing so hard she has to stop too.

"Oh my god," she says. "Oh my god, you went off like a whippet. I thought you were going to leave me for dead."

"I thought so too," you wheeze.

"You haven't got any base. You're all sprint." She pats you on the back, still laughing. "We'll build you a base. Walk it off. Come on, walk."

You walk. Your lungs slowly stop burning. The light comes up over the fells, and the pink spreads along the clouds, and the mere goes from black to pewter.

That's when the girl walks out of it.

She comes up out of the water fifty yards ahead of you, where a slab of rock runs out into the mere, wading in through the shallows as calmly as if she were stepping out of a bath. She's naked except for a white swim cap. Steam is rising off her skin into the cold air in a soft pale haze all around her, so that for a second she looks like something out of a story, something that lives in lakes.

You stop walking. You can't help it.

She's slim and wiry, a swimmer's body, all long lean muscle, her shoulders strong, her stomach flat and hard. Her skin is very fair and flushed pink all over with cold. Small tits, high and firm, with pink nipples drawn tight and hard as pebbles. Water runs down her ribs and her belly and through a sandy tuft of hair over her cunt and down her thighs. She walks up the shingle to a heap of clothes on the rock and peels off the swim cap, and her hair comes out, strawberry-blonde, chin-length, flattened and wet.

She has freckles. Across her nose and her cheeks and her shoulders, like yours.

She picks up a big navy parka and shrugs into it, unhurried, and zips it to her chin, and stands there in it with her bare legs steaming under the hem. Then she turns and sees the two of you, and her mouth goes up on one side in a crooked grin.

"Morning," she says. Irish, the voice warm and amused. "Don't tell the warden."

You're staring. You know you're staring. You can't seem to stop. The pink of her skin, the water on her thighs, the freckles on her wet shoulders, the grin.

She clocks it. You watch her clock it: her sea-green eyes go over your face, and over your red cheeks, and the grin goes wider.

"Grand," she says, and picks up her shoes, and walks off barefoot up the shingle towards the college with the parka swinging round her bare legs.

Bea, beside you, lets out a long breath.

"Well," she says faintly. "Swimming's forbidden."

"Yes," you say.

"It's in the handbook. In bold."

"Yes."

Bea looks at you sideways. "Your face is the colour of your hair."

"Shut up," you say, and she laughs all the way back to Thornfield.

The shower runs hot.

You turn it on not expecting anything, and the water comes out of the big chrome head steaming, and you stand there with your hand under it as if you've been handed a present. Then you strip and step in.

It's bliss. There isn't another word. Yesterday the cold hit every inch of this skin at once and today the heat does, a slow gold pouring over your scalp and down the back of your neck and over your shoulders, running down between your small tits and over your belly and between your legs, and this body takes it in everywhere, all the new soft skin of it loosening and flushing pink. Your aching legs go loose. Your tight lungs go loose. You tip your face up into the spray and close your eyes and make a sound you didn't know you could make, a long low hum of pure pleasure.

You stay a long time. You wash your hair properly, working the shampoo through all the heavy red length of it. You stand with your forehead against the tiles and let the hot water drum on the back of your neck. You only get out when someone thumps on the door and says, "Some of us want hot water too, Ashby," and you realise it's quarter to eight.

The Great Hall at breakfast is a different place. The floating candles are gone, or put out, and daylight comes pouring through the tall windows in long white shafts full of dust. The noise is enormous: six hundred girls talking, cutlery clattering, a hatch at one end where women in white aprons are dishing out porridge and eggs and toast. There isn't a gown in sight. Half the hall looks like it's just rolled out of bed.

You sit with Bea at the end of a long table, with toast and a bowl of porridge each, and Bea puts golden syrup on her porridge in a spiral and then honey on top of the syrup, and catches you looking.

"I've just run five miles," she says with dignity.

"You've run about two."

"I've just run *two and a half* miles," Bea says, "and I'm a growing girl," and licks the spoon.

You eat your toast. You're starving. This body, it turns out, gets hungry fast and full fast; you finish one slice and half of the next and suddenly can't manage another bite, and Bea eats the rest of your crust without asking.

At nine o'clock you climb the library tower.

The stairs spiral up inside the stone, narrow and steep and worn into dips in the middle by two hundred years of feet, with little slit windows every turn letting in slices of cold air. You go up behind two girls you half recognise from your table last night, and you're breathing hard again by the top, your legs reminding you about the shore.

The door at the top is open. Inside is the most crowded room you've ever seen.

Books. Books everywhere, floor to ceiling on every wall, stacked on the floor in leaning towers, stacked on the windowsill, stacked on the chairs so that the girls ahead of you have to move piles before they can sit. Papers in drifts. A brass instrument of rings and arms on a side table that you can't begin to guess the purpose of. A threadbare rug. The smell of old paper and pipe smoke and tea. And at the far end, a round window like a ship's porthole, looking straight down the tower's flank to the mere far below, grey and silver in the morning light.

On the desk, in front of the window, among the papers, sits a big square tin with a tartan lid. Shortbread, it says on the side.

"Come in, come in, mind the Aristotle," says a voice, and the woman behind the desk stands up.

She's lean and upright, sixty or nearly, with short silver hair and a weathered fair face that looks like it's spent a lot of time outdoors in the wind. Round glasses sit on her nose on a chain. She's in green tweed, a jacket and a long wool skirt, and her eyes behind the glasses are blue and kind and very, very sharp. When she speaks it's with a soft Cornish burr.

"Agnes Penhallow," she says. "Doctor, if you must, Penhallow if you mustn't. Sit, sit. Move the books, they won't mind."

You move a stack of books off a stool and sit.

There are six of you. You look round at the others, really looking for the first time; last night they were faces down a candlelit table, and you were too busy with your forks.

Kit, first, because your eyes go to her first. She's taken the chair furthest from everyone, in the corner by the bookshelves, in black again, a high-necked black jumper and a long black skirt, her bob glossy, her hands folded in her lap with the silver ring on one finger. She isn't looking at any of you. She's looking out of the round window with careful attention, as if the mere were doing something fascinating.

And then the door bangs again and the last girl comes in, and it's her. The swimmer. Her strawberry-blonde hair is still damp, tousled and drying in curls at the ends, and she's in a grey hoodie and running tights with the big navy parka over one arm, and there's a faint flush of cold still in her freckled cheeks. She drops onto the last free chair, sees you, and winks.

You feel your face go hot again, immediately, all the way up.

"Shiv," she says to the room, by way of introduction, with a lazy wave. "Siobhan Doyle. From Galway. Sorry I'm late, I was somewhere I wasn't supposed to be."

"I'm sure you were," says Dr Penhallow, unperturbed, and turns to the girl next to her.

She's tall, the tallest of you by a head, and slim the way models are slim, and sitting very straight, one long leg crossed over the other. Her skin is deep brown, her cheekbones high, her brows shaped, her mouth full. Her black hair is in long box braids gathered up into a high ponytail that falls down her back. Small gold hoops in her ears, a gold watch on a slim wrist, a fitted cream knit and sharply tailored trousers. She looks like she's come from a photo shoot. She looks entirely at ease.

"Amara Osei," she says, in a south London voice, smooth and assured. "Lovely to meet you all properly."

Next to her a girl in round tortoiseshell glasses is already writing something in a notebook. Her skin is warm brown, her eyes big and dark behind the lenses, and there's a little gold stud in her nose. Her thick curly black hair is knotted up on her head with a pencil stuck through it. She's wearing a bright mustard corduroy pinafore over a striped top, and at her feet sits a canvas tote bag absolutely stuffed with notebooks.

"Priya Raman," she says, looking up with a quick bright smile. "Hello! Hi. Sorry, I'm writing down everyone's names."

And beside Priya, last, a girl who has made herself as small as possible on her chair. She's slim and slight, about your size, in a soft oatmeal jumper and a long pleated skirt, with a notebook open on her knee. Her skin is smooth and light, her eyes soft and dark brown under a soft black fringe, and her long straight black hair is pulled into a low ponytail that hangs over one shoulder. Her fingertips, resting on the edge of the notebook, are smudged blue-black with ink.

"Lin," she says, so softly you barely catch it. "Lin Zhao."

"Nala Ashby," you say, when it's your turn.

Kit says, "Kit Ashworth," to the window.

"Lovely," says Dr Penhallow, settling back into her chair and steepling her fingers. "Six of you. A good number. Now. Before we do anything else, I like to know who I'm dealing with." She looks round at you all over the top of her glasses. "Hands up, please, anyone here who has never cast a single spell. Not one. Not a kettle, not a candle, not a lock. Never."

You put your hand up.

It's only when it's up that you realise it's the only one.

You feel the whole room turn to look at you. Shiv with her head tilted, interested. Priya, pen paused. Lin glancing up and then quickly away. Amara's shaped brows lifting, very slightly, towards her braids. And Kit, turning from the window at last, her grey-green eyes on your raised hand, and her mouth going flat and thin.

You keep your hand up. You don't know what else to do with it.

Dr Penhallow beams.

It's a real beam, sudden and wide, creasing her whole weathered face. She leans forward, prises the lid off the tartan tin, takes out a thick finger of shortbread, and holds it across the desk to you.

"Honesty," she says. "Rarest thing in this tower. Well done."

You take the shortbread. It's crumbly and buttery and still a little warm, as if the tin keeps it that way, which, you realise, it probably does.

"Right," says Dr Penhallow, and sits back. "What's a spell?"

"Intent, shaped through a focus." Kit's voice, cool and clipped, before anyone else has drawn breath. She's sitting very straight in her corner now. "The caster forms the intent; the focus gives it a shape it can take in the world."

"Good," says Dr Penhallow.

"Though a held focus is a special case," says Amara smoothly, before Penhallow's nod has quite finished. She doesn't look at Kit; she looks at the tutor, pleasant and attentive. "Because a held object can carry a shape already, from whoever made it, so the intent doesn't have to supply the whole form. It can lean on the object."

There's a short silence.

Kit's eyes, across the room, have gone very cold. Amara smiles pleasantly at the bookshelves.

Dr Penhallow looks from one of them to the other over her glasses. "Well," she says, dry as dust. "I've two people competing to come top of the year, and it's only quarter past nine." She takes a piece of shortbread for herself and bites it. "Both correct. Neither of you gets one of these yet; I'll see how you do on Friday."

Shiv laughs out loud. Priya hides a smile behind her notebook. Kit's jaw is tight; Amara looks, if anything, more pleased.

Then Dr Penhallow turns her chair a little, towards you, and explains it again, plainly, for you. A focus is the thing you push your intent through, she says. It can be a word you say, a gesture you make, a shape you draw in the air, or a thing you hold. Your intent is like water; the focus is the pipe. "Without the pipe," she says, "you've a puddle on the floor and a headache. With it, you've a fountain. Most of what you'll learn here, Miss Ashby, is pipes."

"Pipes," you say. You can follow that. You write it down.

Priya has her hand up. "Is the intent itself quantifiable?" she says. "Like, could you measure it? Is there a unit?"

Dr Penhallow's eyes light up. "Ah," she says. "People have tried. Go on."

"And does the shape of the focus matter geometrically, or only symbolically? Like, if I drew a sigil slightly wrong but the caster thought it was right, would it work?"

"Ooh," says Dr Penhallow.

"And is it the same for spoken foci? Does accent matter? Does *volume*?"

"And?" says Dr Penhallow, delighted, because Priya clearly has another.

"And can two people put intent through one focus at once?"

Dr Penhallow answers every one of them, at length, with relish, leaning back with her hands behind her head, and you understand about a third of the answers. Priya writes them all down in three different colours of pen.

You glance sideways, at the notebook open on Lin's knee.

She hasn't been writing much. But her margins are full. All down both sides of the page, in fine black ink, are sigils, dozens of them, small and exact, some of them shapes you recognise from the plates in your *Sigil Primer* and some you don't. And they're beautiful. They're cleaner than the printed ones, every line sure and even, the curves perfect, each one drawn as though she'd done it a thousand times and loved it every time.

Lin feels you looking. Her head turns, just a little, and her eyes meet yours, and then drop to the page, and she slides her sleeve across the margin to cover it. Her cheeks go pink. She looks very hard at Dr Penhallow.

You look away too, quickly, and eat the rest of your shortbread.

At eleven, the Foundations lecture fills the Long Room.

It's well named. It's a long high hall on the ground floor of the east range, with rows of benches stepped up steeply from a floor far below, and every row is packed with first-years, a hundred and more, notebooks open, gowns over chairbacks, a murmur rising off them like warmth. Down on the floor, so far away she's mostly a voice and a pair of hands, a lecturer stands at a lectern in front of a huge blackboard covered in chalk diagrams.

You find a place halfway up and squeeze in. You've got a notebook and, you discover, patting your pockets, no pen.

Someone drops into the seat beside you in a rush of corduroy and tote bag.

"Hi! Hi. Me again," says Priya. "Do you need a pen? You need a pen." She's already digging in the tote, which seems to be at least half pens. She comes out with a blue one and presses it into your hand, and then gets out three of her own: black, green and red. "I do the main points in black," she explains in a whisper, "and examples in green, and red's for things I don't understand yet, so I can ask. There's always a lot of red at the start."

"There's going to be a lot of red for me," you whisper back.

"That's fine! That's what it's for."

The lecture begins. It's about something called the conservation of intent. Priya's pens move very fast. In between, under her breath, she talks to you.

"I did physics," she whispers, during a pause while the lecturer wipes the board. "At Leicester. Three years. Got a first and everything, and then I was sat in the last lecture of my degree and I thought: but this is all just *stuff*. Particles. Fields. It's all just stuff being stuff. And magic's stuff being stuff because somebody *wants* it to, and even the experts don't really know how, and I thought, well, that's much more interesting, isn't it?" She grins at you, a quick bright flash behind the glasses. "My parents were thrilled. Not."

You smile. "Are they still not?"

"Oh, they're fine now. Mum's told everyone at the temple I'm studying 'advanced energy.'" Priya draws a neat green box round something. "My boyfriend's fine about it too. Dev. He's back home in Leicester, he's doing a PhD in fluid dynamics, he thinks this is hilarious." She glances at your page. "Are you following this? You can say if you're not. I don't mind explaining. I love explaining. Honestly, I'll explain anything you lose track of, it helps me remember it."

You look at your page, which has *conservation of intent* written at the top, underlined twice, and then four lines of increasingly desperate scrawl.

"I lost track about five minutes in," you admit.

"Right," says Priya happily, and pulls your notebook halfway onto her own.

She explains half the lecture to you in the margins. Little diagrams in green. Arrows. A note in black that says *intent ≈ energy: doesn't vanish, goes SOMEWHERE (heat/light/headache!!)* with the headache underlined. When the lecturer says something about thresholds Priya draws a little cartoon of a stick figure pushing a boulder up a hill and writes *threshold = top of hill* next to it, and suddenly it's obvious. When she says something about dissipation Priya writes *where the headache comes from* and you think of the kettle yesterday, the faint warmth under your palm and the ache behind your eyes, and you understand it in your body before you understand it on the page.

By the end of the hour you've filled three pages, mostly in Priya's handwriting, and you understand much more than you feared. Not all of it. But the shape of it. Pipes. Hills. Water that goes somewhere.

"Thank you," you say, when it's over and the benches are emptying.

At two o'clock you meet in the Practice Hall.

It's a bare stone hall at the back of the west range, high and cold and echoing, with tall plain windows and a floor of broad old boards. The boards are scorched. All over the floor, in patches, there are black marks, some small as a hand and some as big as a table, and one long streak by the far wall that looks as if something burned a line straight across it. The air smells faintly of smoke and lamp oil.

There are perhaps forty first-years, several tutorial groups together, standing about in a loose crowd. Bea's somewhere at the back with her own group; she waves at you and mouths *oh my god*. At the front stands the casting demonstrator, a brisk compact woman in a canvas apron with her sleeves rolled up and a whistle on a cord round her neck.

"Right," she says, and the hall goes quiet. "The glim. Your first spell, most of you, and the one you'll use more than any other for the rest of your lives. One word, one sigil. It makes a light. That's all it does. Watch."

She holds out her left hand, palm up. She says a short word, low and clear, a sound with a lilt in it like *leer*, and with the first finger of her right hand she draws a small sigil in the air just above her open palm: a little upright stroke, a loop round it, and a hook at the bottom that curls back on itself.

A bead of light rises out of her palm.

It's soft and white and about the size of a marble, and it lifts gently from her hand and hangs in the air at the height of her eyes, glowing steadily, not flickering at all, lighting her face from below. It just hangs there. It's the most beautiful thing you've ever seen.

She closes her hand, and it goes out.

"Word. Sigil. Intent. You're asking for light, so want light. Not hard, not soft. Just mean it." She steps back. "First volunteer."

Amara steps forward before anyone else has moved.

She holds out her palm. She says the word, easily, her voice smooth. She draws the sigil, quick and assured, the gold watch glinting on her wrist.

And a glim rises off her hand. It's bright and steady, brighter than the demonstrator's, and it lifts and hangs at her eye level and stays there, glowing, perfect.

The whole hall turns to look. There's a murmur, and someone at the back actually claps. Amara looks at the light on her hand, and then round at the room looking at her, and she glows too: her face lit from below, her eyes shining, her smile wide and warm and pleased.

"First try," says the demonstrator. "Very nice. Next."

Kit steps up.

Her right hand lifts to draw the sigil. The upright stroke. The beginning of the loop.

And stops.

It just stops. Her finger hangs in the air above her palm, mid-loop, frozen, as if it's caught on something you can't see. One second. Two. Her whole body has gone rigid. Her face is pale already but it goes paler, white as paper, and her palm stays dark.

The hall is very quiet.

Kit lowers her hand. "Draughty in here," she says, in a voice like a knife, to the room at large. "Somebody should do something about the windows. Or is that a third-year spell?" And she steps back into the crowd with her chin up.

A few girls laugh, uncertain. The demonstrator says, "It happens. Next," and the crowd shifts, and every eye goes back to Amara, who is still standing there with her glim hanging over her hand, being congratulated.

You're not looking at Amara. You're looking at Kit.

She's stopped at the edge of the crowd, not far from you, very straight, staring straight ahead. Her right hand hangs at her side. And it's shaking. A fine fast tremor, in all the fingers, the silver ring catching the light as it trembles, and she's pressing it against the black wool of her skirt to make it stop, and it won't stop.

Everyone else is still watching Amara. You're the only one who sees it.

You look away, quickly, before she can catch you looking.

Priya goes up next and does everything exactly right, the word and the sigil, and a wobbling, uncertain light about the size of a pea rises a few inches off her hand, quivers, and pops like a soap bubble. "Oh!" she says, delighted. "Oh, it *popped*. Why did it pop? That's so interesting." Shiv goes after her and gets a single fat spark that jumps off her palm and lands on her hoodie, and she slaps at it and swears, loud and fluent and in Irish, and half the hall laughs, and Shiv laughs hardest of all.

Then it's you.

You step out onto the scorched boards. Your heart's going. You hold out your left hand, palm up, and look at it, small and pale and freckled, and it doesn't look like a hand that can make a light.

You say the word. It comes out too quiet. You draw the sigil the way you saw her draw it: the stroke, the loop, the hook.

Your palm stays dark.

You try again. Louder, the word. Slower, the sigil. You think *light*, the way you thought *hot* at the kettle, pushing with everything you've got.

Your palm stays dark. And there it is, the headache, arriving behind your eyes, the same small tight ache as yesterday, only a little worse.

"Keep at it," says the demonstrator, not unkindly, and moves on down the line.

You stand there with your dark palm and your aching head, and someone comes to stand beside you.

It's Lin. She's been at the edge of the group the whole time, quiet; you didn't see her move. She doesn't say anything. She takes a scrap of paper out of the pocket of her skirt, and a pen, and, holding the paper flat on the back of her notebook, she draws the glim sigil on it. Slowly. Much slower than the demonstrator, so slowly you can watch every part of it form: the stroke, the loop, the hook.

Then she points with the end of the pen at the hook.

You look. And then you look at your own hand, and draw it in the air again the way you did it, and you see.

Your hook is wrong. You've been curling it outward, away from the loop, the way you'd write the tail of a *j*. Hers curls inward, back towards the centre, closing in on itself like a fern before it opens.

Lin reaches over and, with one ink-smudged fingertip, traces the inward turn in the air above your palm. Just the hook. Inward. Her fingertip passes a breath from your skin.

"Like that," she says, barely audible.

You hold your hand out again. You say the word. You draw the stroke, the loop, and the hook, curling it inward, the way her finger went.

Over your palm, a spark of light flickers.

It's tiny. A pinprick of white, there and gone, a single heartbeat long, like a firefly winking out. It doesn't rise. It doesn't hang. It just flickers on, and dies.

But it was there. You saw it. Your palm, for one heartbeat, made a light.

It's not a glim. It's something.

You look up, and Lin is looking at you, and she smiles: small and sudden, a quick soft curve of her mouth that lights up her whole face, there and gone almost as fast as the spark was. Then she looks away, down at her notebook, her cheeks going pink again.

"Thank you," you say.

She nods, without looking up.

At the end of the hour the demonstrator blows her whistle and says, "Glims. Most of you'll have one within the week. Some of you will take a month. Either's fine. Work at it." She looks round the hall. "And don't practise in your rooms with the curtains drawn, I'm not having another fire."

By the time you cross the quad back to Thornfield the light is going. The sky over the fells is deep blue and gold, and the lamps are coming on along the paths, and the cold has come down sharp. Your head still aches faintly. You've got Priya's notes in your bag and the taste of Dr Penhallow's shortbread somewhere at the back of your memory and the spark, the tiny spark, still bright behind your eyes.

You pass the Practice Hall on the way. It should be dark by now. But there's a light in the window.

You slow down. You don't mean to look. You do anyway.

Kit is alone inside. She's standing in the middle of the empty hall on the scorched boards, in her black, her small pale face lit from below, and she's casting.

Glim after glim. You watch her do it: the word, the sigil, quick and exact, and a bead of light lifts off her palm, bright and steady and perfect, and rises and hangs in the air beside her. Then again. And again. She doesn't stop or hesitate or falter; her hand moves through the sigil as smooth as water, every time. There are a dozen lights already, more, hanging in a ring around her at the height of her shoulders, a slow-turning crown of small white stars in the dark hall, lighting her from every side. Her face is fierce and fixed and utterly concentrated.

They're better than Amara's. All of them.

You stand at the window, staring.

And Kit turns her head, and sees you.

Her face changes. All the fierce concentration goes out of it at once, and what's left is stricken: white and naked and horrified, her mouth open, her eyes wide and fixed on yours through the glass. For a second you see her, all of her, with nothing in front of it.

Every light in the ring goes out at once.

The hall is dark. You can't see her anymore. You can only see the reflection of the quad lamps in the window, and your own pale face in it.

You lift a hand. A small one, half a wave, half something else, you don't know what.

Then you turn away from the window and walk on, across the quad, eyes ahead, not looking back. You leave her the hall to herself.

---

The Thornfield kitchen in the evening is warm and steamy and smells of toast again. Bea is at the counter with the kettle, and when you come in she spins round with a mug in each hand, sloshing.

"I did *half a glim*," she announces.

"You did not."

"I did! I did half of one. It came out of my hand and went about an inch and then it sort of fizzled, but the demonstrator *saw* it and she said 'good' and she doesn't say 'good,' she didn't say it to anyone else in my group, ask anyone." She pushes a mug at you, the fat blue one with the chipped handle, already milky and sweet. "How did you do? Tell me everything. Tell me *everything*, I've only had you across a room since breakfast, waving."

You sit on the counter and tell her. The tower and the books. The shortbread, and Dr Penhallow beaming, and *honesty, rarest thing in this tower*. Kit and Amara and *it's only quarter past nine*. Priya's three colours and her boulder going up the hill. Amara and the freckles.

"*Criminal*?" Bea says, scandalised and thrilled. "She said *criminal*? And what did you say?"

"I went red."

"Of course you went red. You're red now."

"I'm not."

"You are a bit." Bea leans on the counter beside you, eyes shining. "And the swimmer? The naked girl from the lake? Was she — "

"She's in my group."

Bea shrieks, and claps her hand over her mouth. "No."

"She winked at me."

"*No.*" Bea collapses against the cupboards. "Oh my god. Oh my god, Nala, you've been here *one day*."

You don't tell her about the Practice Hall. About the dark palm and the frozen hand, the shaking fingers pressed against black wool, the ring of lights in the dark and the face at the centre of it. You tell her about everything else.

There's a soft knock on the open kitchen door.

It's Lin. She's standing in the doorway with her notebook held against her chest, in a soft jumper with the sleeves down over her hands, looking as if she might bolt at any moment. "Sorry," she says, very quietly. "I'm on the floor below. I just. I wanted to give you this."

She holds out a page, carefully torn from the notebook along a fold.

You take it. It's the glim sigil. Drawn large, in fine black ink, perfectly: the upright stroke, the loop, and the hook, curling inward. Beside each part there are small, precise arrows in a second, finer pen, showing which way to draw it and in what order; and at the hook there's a curving arrow turning in on itself, and beside it, very small, in neat handwriting: *in, not out.*

You look at it for a long moment. You can feel your throat going tight, which is silly. It's a piece of paper.

"Lin," you say. "Thank you. This is. Thank you."

She goes pink and looks at the floor.

"Tea," says Bea decisively, and turns to the row of mugs. She picks a yellow one with a daisy on it and makes tea in it without asking how Lin takes it, the way she did with you, and holds it out. "Sit down. Stay. You have to stay, you're the first person I've met from downstairs. What's downstairs like? Is it warmer? I bet it's warmer."

Lin takes the yellow mug in both sleeve-covered hands and sits on the edge of a chair. She doesn't say much. She answers Bea's questions in a few soft words each: Manchester; yes, a bit warmer; no, she doesn't run. She sips her tea. She watches the two of you, her dark eyes going from one face to the other. And when Bea, telling her about the half-glim, acts it out with her whole arm and nearly knocks the kettle off the counter, Lin laughs. Once. A small surprised sound, quickly hidden behind the mug. But Bea hears it and looks enormously pleased, and so, you realise, do you.

A shadow crosses the doorway and stops.

"Ashby."

Yusra. She's in a dark jumper with the brass badge at her collar and a stack of folders under one arm, on her way somewhere. She looks at the three of you, and the mugs, and then at you.

"First day of classes," she says. "How was it?"

You don't know quite what makes you do it. Maybe Lin's page, still in your hand. You set your mug down on the counter, hold out your left palm, and say the word, and draw the sigil over it, stroke, loop, the hook curling in.

A spark flickers over your palm. Tiny and white, there for a heartbeat. Then gone.

And the headache comes back with it, a hard throb behind your eyes, sharper than before, so that you wince and press your fingers to your brow.

Yusra watches it all, the spark and the wince, with no change in her face. Then she puts her folders down on the end of the counter.

"Some take a month," she says. "A spark on the first day's not nothing." She takes a glass off the drainer and fills it at the tap and lets it run cold first, watching the water, and fills it to the top. "That headache's you overreaching. Drink water. A lot of it. More than you think." She holds out the glass. "And stop for tonight."

You take the glass. Her fingers brush yours as you do, warm and dry, against the cold wet glass, just for a moment.

"Room 1," she says, and picks up her folders, and goes.

Bea waits until her footsteps have gone. Then she looks at you over her mug with her eyebrows halfway to her hairline. Lin, very carefully, looks at her tea.

You drink the water. All of it.

---

In bed, with the light off, you've propped Lin's page up against the wall at the back of the desk where you can see it. There's just enough light from the window, from the grey mere outside, to make out the pale square of it and the dark shape of the sigil on it.

You lie on your side under the blanket and hold your hand up in the dark and trace the sigil in the air over your palm. You don't say the word; you're not casting, you've stopped for tonight, the way Yusra said. You're just drawing. The stroke. The loop. The hook, curling in.

In, not out.

The stroke. The loop. The hook.

Your head has stopped aching. Your legs ache instead, pleasantly, from the shore this morning. Your hair is spread over the pillow. The radiator knocks.

The stroke. The loop. In, not out.

Your hand drifts down onto the blanket, and your eyes close.

You think of Kit.

Not the Kit at dinner with her forks, or the Kit at lunch with her coffee and her book and her furniture. The Kit at the window. The ring of lights around her, perfect, better than anyone's, and her face when she saw you: white, open, horrified. And then the dark.

She can do it. She can do it better than anyone. She just couldn't do it today, in front of all of them.

You don't know why. You think you might understand it anyway, a bit. And you know, lying there, that you're not going to tell anyone. Not Bea. Not anyone. What you saw through that window belongs to Kit, and you're going to keep it for her whether she wants you to or not. It stays between you and her.
