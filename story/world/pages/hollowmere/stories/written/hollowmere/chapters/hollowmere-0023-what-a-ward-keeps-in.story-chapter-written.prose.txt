On Tuesday the light comes grey through Bea's thick curtains, and Bea is gone already.

Her side of the bed is still warm. You put your hand flat in the hollow where she was, and it's warm, and smells of her.

There's a note on your pillow. A torn-off scrap of lined paper, in Bea's big round hand, the letters leaning every which way.

*Outing. Back for lunch. EAT. B x*

The *EAT* is underlined three times.

---

You eat toast in the Thornfield kitchen.

It's early, and quiet. Two girls you half know are at the far end, bent over coffee. You make toast under the grill and butter it and sit at the table with it, and you're halfway through the second slice when Lin comes down.

Her hair is still wet from the shower, combed straight back, dripping a little onto the shoulders of her cardigan. She makes tea, and brings it over, and sits beside you.

She looks lighter.

You can see it the moment she sits down. She doesn't hold the mug the way she held the spoon yesterday, as if it might break. She holds it the way anyone holds a mug of tea on a cold grey morning: both hands, close, letting it warm her fingers. Her hair drips onto the table, and she wipes it up with her sleeve without thinking.

You can see it. Her face isn't the face from yesterday, the far-away face at the candle. The shadows are still under her eyes, but her shoulders are down, and her hands round the mug are easy.

"I slept," Lin says. She sounds faintly surprised. "All night. With the letter under my pillow." She sips her tea. "The pencil one."

---

At nine group F climbs the library tower to Penhallow's study.

The round window is full of the mere, grey and flat and calm this morning, with a faint mist on it. The books are stacked everywhere. The shortbread tin sits on the desk, lid on.

You take your chair by the round window, between Kit and Lin. Shiv is sprawled in the chair by the door with her legs stretched out, her hair still damp from the mere. Priya has three pens lined up on her notebook, red, green and black. Amara is sitting very upright, in a camel jumper, with her gold watch catching the grey light.

Penhallow taps it, once, with one finger.

"Wards," she says. "Who drew one yesterday?"

Every hand goes up.

"Good." She folds her hands. "Who knows what it's for?"

Fewer hands. Priya's goes down. Shiv's goes down. Lin's wavers and goes down. Amara's stays up. And Kit's stays up, very straight.

"Ashworth."

"A ward keeps things out," says Kit. "Draught. Damp. Harm. Whatever you draw it against."

Penhallow nods. And waits. Looking at her over her glasses, as if there should be more.

"It keeps the flame still," says Amara.

"Half right," says Penhallow. "Both of you." Her mouth twitches. "Again, I see."

Kit and Amara don't look at each other. Very carefully.

Penhallow looks round the room. At all six of you.

"What does a ward keep out?" she says. "And what does it keep *in*?"

Silence.

"That's your essay," says Penhallow. "Due Friday. Same as before." She taps the desk. "And bring me what happened to you. Not what a book said."

Priya groans. Out loud, and then puts her hand over her mouth.

Shiv grins.

Lin writes the question down in her notebook, very neatly, and underlines *keep in*. Twice.

Then Penhallow turns to you.

"Nala," she says.

Not *Ashby*. You notice it. So does Kit, beside you.

"A shell round a flame," Penhallow says. "What's inside it? Besides the flame."

You think.

You think of Lin's room, and the walls, and the drawing pinned over her bed, in the middle of everything. You think of Bea's bed, and the lamp low and gold, and the covers lifted for you without a word. You think of the rock on Sundays, and Shiv, and the parka round you both.

"The air it needs," you say.

Penhallow's eyebrows go up. Over the rims of her glasses.

"Go on," she says.

"A flame needs air," you say slowly. "To burn. If the ward's drawn too tight—" You stop, and think about it. "Too tight a ward starves it."

Penhallow looks at you for a long moment.

Then she reaches out, and pushes the shortbread tin an inch across the desk. Towards you.

"Put that in the essay," she says.

---

On the tower stairs afterwards, Kit falls into step beside you.

She's quiet. She walks down the winding stair beside you with her bag over her shoulder, and her hand wrapped round the strap, twisting it. Twisting it tight, round and round her fist, until the knuckles go white.

"Amara said *brave*," Kit says.

Low. To the stairwell.

"Amara Osei," she says. "To *me*." She shakes her head, slowly, as if she still can't make it fit. "I keep hearing it. In my head. All night. *That's brave, Ashworth*."

"It was brave," you say.

Kit is quiet for a few steps. The stair winds down and round, the narrow windows going past, slits of grey sky and grey water. Your footsteps echo up the stone.

"It didn't feel brave," she says. "It felt like being sick." A pause. "And then it was done."

At the foot of the stairs, she stops.

She bumps your shoulder with hers. Quickly. Hard enough to rock you.

And she goes off down the corridor, fast, her bag swinging, without looking back.

---

At lunch Bea comes in from the boathouse.

Red-cheeked, windblown, her hair escaping its knot in every direction, smelling of the mere and the cold. She drops onto the bench beside you and eats three plates. Three: shepherd's pie, then more shepherd's pie, then sponge and custard.

Between the second and the third, she shows you her hands.

Palms up, on the table, like trophies. Two new blisters, one on each palm, at the base of the fingers. Fat and shiny and angry.

"Look," she says, delighted. "*Look*."

"Bea—"

"Morwenna," says Bea, through custard, "says the four's nearly a boat." She holds up a finger. "*Nearly*."

She eats a spoonful.

"Saturday week," she says. "The Fell Cup." She points the spoon at you. "You're coming. On the bank. Shouting my name. Very loud."

"I will," you say.

"Louder than last time."

"I will."

Across the table, Lin has her notebook out. She turns it round and slides it to Bea, and shows her the essay question, *what does a ward keep out, and what does it keep in*, with *keep in* underlined twice.

Bea reads it. She frowns at it, chewing. Then she taps the page with her spoon.

"Out," she says. "The rain." She taps again. "In: me." She pushes the notebook back. "Done. Pass."

Lin laughs. Out loud, surprised, her hand going to her mouth. You haven't heard her laugh like that in days.

---

Later in the afternoon you take the essay to the library.

The big reading room, the green lamps, the long oak tables. You find a table by one of the tall windows, where you can look out at the fells, brown and grey and white-topped under a low sky. You spread out your notes, and Lin's soft nib, and a clean sheet of paper.

Two tables over, Yusra is sitting.

She has her reading glasses on. Four books open around her, propped against each other, and a pile of notes in her neat hand, and a cup of tea at her elbow.

Her bandage is gone.

You can see her palm, resting flat on the page. There's a thin pink line across it, from the base of her thumb to the other side. Healing clean. Fine, and straight, and already fading at the ends.

She looks tired.

You can see it from here. The shadows under her eyes, dark, bruise-coloured. The tea at her elbow has a skin on it; it's gone cold, and she hasn't noticed. And she's looking at the same page. She's been looking at the same page since you sat down, and she hasn't turned it.

You know she hasn't slept a whole night since the summer. You know it the way you know the mere is cold. She hides it under work, under lists and books and boiler men and night shifts. And it shows.

You work for an hour.

It's hard going. You know what a ward keeps out; you drew one yesterday, and felt the draught bend round the shell of it. But *keeps in* sits on the page and looks at you. You write about the flame, and the air it needs. You write about Penhallow's tin, pushed an inch towards you. You write half a page, and cross it out, and write it again. You look out at the fells. You look at Yusra, and back at your page.

Then you pick up your notebook, and get up, and go across, and sit down opposite her.

Yusra looks up. Over the top of her glasses.

"Ward essay," you say. "For Penhallow." You put your notebook down. "What does a ward keep in?"

Yusra looks at you a moment. Then she takes her glasses off. Folds them. Puts them on the table.

"Ask a healer," she says.

She turns her hand over on the table. Palm up. The thin pink line.

"A bandage is a ward," she says. "It keeps the dirt out." She touches the line with one finger. "It keeps the blood in."

You look at her palm.

"And the hurt?" you ask.

Yusra looks at it too. At her own hand. For a long moment.

"That stays in too," she says. "For a bit." Her voice is low. "While it mends."

You write that down. In your notebook, carefully, in Lin's soft nib. *A bandage keeps the dirt out and the blood in. The hurt stays in too, for a bit, while it mends.*

Yusra watches you write it.

And something softens. At the corner of her mouth. Just there, just a little.

You put your pen down.

"You're not sleeping," you say. Quietly.

Yusra's eyes come up to yours.

Very dark. Very steady. Looking straight at you across the table.

"I'm on tonight," she says. "Ten till six."

"That's not sleeping either," you say.

A long pause.

The lamp hums between you. Somewhere far down the room, a page turns.

"I keep busy," Yusra says at last. "It's what I do." She looks at her books. "Work's a ward."

She almost smiles.

"What's it keeping in?" you ask.

Yusra doesn't answer.

She picks up her glasses, and unfolds them, and puts them back on. She looks down at her book. She turns a page. A page she hasn't read; you saw her not read it.

"Go and write your essay, Nala," she says. Low.

But under the table, her foot comes to rest against yours.

---

You stay.

You don't go back to your own table. You open your notebook on her table, opposite her, and you write. And she reads, or pretends to. And her foot stays where it is, against yours, under the table. Her ankle against your ankle. Warm through your tights.

Twenty minutes.

You write more in those twenty minutes than in the whole hour before. About bandages. About the thin pink line. About the hurt staying in, for a bit, while it mends. You don't look up much. You don't need to. You can feel her there, across the table, the weight of her ankle against yours, steady, and the small sounds of her pages turning, and once, very quietly, her breath going out in something that might have been a sigh, or might have been a laugh.

Then she closes her books, one by one, and gathers her notes, and stands up.

At the door of the reading room she turns back. Once.

She looks at you, across the long room, the green lamps between you.

And goes.

---

In the evening it's dinner in the Thornfield kitchen.

Priya is cooking dal. Loudly. She has three pans going and the spice tin open and the extractor fan roaring, and she's singing along to the radio, badly, and the whole kitchen smells of cumin and garlic and frying onions.

And Amara has turned up to help.

She's standing at the counter in a beautiful cream silk shirt, with the sleeves rolled up, chopping onions. Very badly. In great uneven lumps, slowly, her knife going everywhere. And she's crying. Tears pouring down her face.

"It's the *onions*," she says. Loudly. To the room. Before anyone can ask.

Priya, at the stove, takes one look at her, and hands her a tea towel. Grinning.

Amara takes the tea towel. And laughs into it. Her face buried in it, red, shoulders shaking.

She stays to eat. Of course she does. She sits beside Priya at the table with a bowl of dal, and under the table, you can see from where you're sitting, her knee is resting against Priya's knee. And Priya's knee isn't moving away.

---

Shiv comes in late.

Her hair is wet from the shore, dripping down her neck, and she's in her parka with the hood down. She looks at the table, and the dal, and the pile of warm chapatis on a plate. She steals one off the top. And comes and hops up on the counter beside you, and sits there eating it, her heels drumming on the cupboard.

She leans over and reads your notebook, upside down, on the counter.

"*What does a ward keep in*," she reads. She chews. "Keeps in. Like a wetsuit."

You look up at her.

"Heat," says Shiv. "A wetsuit keeps the heat in. Your own heat. That's all it does. Doesn't keep the cold out at all, really. Lets a bit of water in, even. Then holds it there, next to you, and you warm it up yourself." She takes another bite. "*You're* the heat."

You write it down.

In the corner, on her stool, Lin is drawing. The whole kitchen, in quick lines in her sketchbook: Priya at the pot, stirring, mouth open, singing. Amara at the onions, with the tea towel. Shiv on the counter. And you, at the table, laughing at something Shiv said.

---

At half past nine you fill a flask.

You find one in the back of the kitchen cupboard, an old tartan thermos with a dented lid. You wash it out. You make tea, strong, and you put sugar in it, three spoons, sweet. The way you'd want it if you were sitting up all night.

You screw the lid on tight, and wait.

You wait at the foot of the stairs, in the hall, with the flask held in both hands against your chest. Behind you the kitchen is still going: Priya laughing, Amara's voice, the radio, the clatter of Shiv washing up very badly. The front door has a draught under it, and the hall is cold. The flask is warm through the tartan, warm against your palms, like the stone in practical.

At quarter to ten, Yusra comes down the stairs.

She's in her coat, buttoned. Her brass badge at her collar. Her bag over her arm. Her face is tired, and set, and composed for the night.

She sees you at the foot of the stairs.

You hold out the flask.

Yusra stops. She looks at it. Then at you.

"What's this?" she says.

"A ward," you say. "Keeps it hot."

Yusra looks at you for a long moment.

Then she takes the flask.

Her fingers close over yours on it. Over the backs of your fingers, round the dented tartan. And they stay there. Her hand over your hand, warm, a moment longer than they need to. A moment longer. And another.

"Nala," she says.

Just your name.

Then: "Go to bed." Quietly. "Properly. All night." A pause. "One of us should."

"I will," you say. "If you sleep tomorrow."

Yusra lets go of your hand. She tucks the flask into her bag.

"We'll see," she says.

And she goes out of the front door, into the dark.

You go to the window by the door and watch her. She's taken a lantern from the hook in the porch, and it swings beside her as she walks, a small warm light going away down the path towards the west range, along by the trees. Getting smaller. Bobbing.

Until the trees take it.

---

In 15, Bea is already in bed.

Sitting up against her pillows, with a book about rowing propped on her knees, all diagrams of boats. She's holding it very carefully, by the edges, with her fingertips, because of her blistered palms.

You get in beside her.

She leans over and kisses your hair.

"Where've you been?" she asks.

"Making tea," you say.

Bea looks at you. Sideways. A long, fond, knowing look.

"For the warden," she says.

You don't answer.

"You're soft, Nala," says Bea.

She's smiling.

She goes back to her rowing book. And within ten minutes she's asleep, sitting up against the pillows, with the book open face-down on her chest and her blistered hands curled loose on top of it. You take it off her, gently. You close it, and put it on the floor. You ease her down onto the pillow. And you reach over her and turn out her lamp.

---

By your own lamp, the little one on your side, you open your notebook.

You write the essay's first line.

*A ward keeps out the cold, and keeps in the heat.*

You look at it.

You cross it out.

And underneath, you write:

*A ward keeps in what is mending.*

You look at that one for a long time.

It's true. You can feel that it's true, the way you could feel the stone go warm. A bandage over a cut palm. A parka round two people on a rock at dawn. A pencil letter under a pillow. A flask of sweet tea going down the dark path to the west range in a canvas bag. Every one of them a shell, a thin one, drawn round something that hurts, to keep it warm while it heals.

You leave it. And turn out the lamp, and lie down beside Bea, and sleep.
