On Saturday Bea's alarm goes at six.

It barely gets going. One bar of its bright little tune, and Bea is out of bed before it stops: flinging back the blanket, letting the cold in, slapping the phone silent on the bedside table, on her feet. She's bouncing on her toes on the rug in her vest and knickers, in the grey half-dark, shaking out her arms like a boxer.

"Up," she says. "Up, up, up."

You groan into the pillow.

Bea leans over the bed, and kisses you. Hard and quick, on the mouth, her hair falling round your face.

"Good luck charm," she says.

Then she picks your jumper up off the chair, and throws it at you. It lands on your head.

"Up," says Bea. "Bank. Shouting."

---

The quad is white with frost.

Every blade of grass is stiff with it, silver-white and crunching under your boots. The paths are pale. The windows of Ashcombe across the way are still dark, and the sky over them is the colour of a pearl, pink at one edge. Your breath smokes in front of you in great slow clouds. You've got Bea's jumper on under your coat, and gloves, and you're still cold.

Behind the academy the fells rise up, brown and grey and huge, and the tops of them are still snowy. A clean white line along the high ridges, catching the first of the light.

Bea walks fast beside you, almost jogging, her kit bag bumping on her shoulder.

She doesn't talk. For Bea, that's how you know.

---

At seven the novice fours carry their boats down from the boathouse.

You stand on the bank, on the frosty grass by the jetty, and watch them come. Each boat comes out of the big boathouse doors upside down, long and narrow and gleaming, carried on the shoulders of four rowers, walking in step, with a cox going in front calling *left, left, mind the post*. They look like enormous beetles. Three of them, one after another, down the slipway to the water, and rolled over, and set on it, rocking.

Bea's is the second.

She's at the oar nearest the bow. You find her at once. She's in a Thornfield vest and leggings, her arms bare in the frost, goosebumped. Her honey-blonde hair is scraped back hard off her face in a tight knot, not a strand loose. She's settling into her seat, sliding it back and forth, checking her footplate, and then she looks up.

And she finds you on the bank, and grins.

A huge, bright, nervous grin.

On the jetty, Morwenna stands with a megaphone in one hand and a stopwatch in the other, in her sleeveless navy club fleece, her dark ponytail under a navy wool headband.

She's booming at them. A big calm voice, carrying out over the water without any trouble at all, unhurried, like the voice of the weather. "Thornfield, square your blades. Ashcombe, you're drifting. Bow, sit up. Lindqvist, sit *up*. Good."

Bea sits up.

---

At ten past seven, Shiv arrives.

She comes down the path at a slouching amble, with her hands in her parka pockets and a woolly hat pulled down to her eyebrows, bright orange, with a bobble. She has a flask under one arm. She comes and stands beside you on the frosty bank, close, her shoulder bumping yours.

"Have I missed it?"

"They haven't gone yet."

"Grand." She unscrews the flask, and pours, and hands you the cup. Tea, hot and very strong. "Breakfast," says Shiv.

You drink it. It burns your tongue. It's wonderful.

---

The four pushes off.

The cox calls something, and the rowers lean, and the blades go in.

Ragged, at first. You can see it even from the bank. One oar in a fraction late, one a fraction deep, the boat rolling a little, side to side, and a splash where somebody's blade comes out wrong. The cox shouting. Morwenna's megaphone booming.

Then they catch.

All four blades at once. In, together, a single clean bite of the water; and out, together, feathered flat; and in.

And the boat leaps.

It actually leaps. It goes forward under them like something let off a leash, sliding, fast, a long clean ripple fanning out behind, and the four backs swinging together, and Bea at the bow, her bare arms straight, her whole body driving.

Beside you, Shiv cups her hands round her mouth.

And bellows.

Something in Irish. Long, and loud, and rolling, across the whole width of the mere, so that a heron lifts off the far shore in alarm.

Out on the water, Bea laughs.

You see her do it. Her head goes back and she laughs, out loud, and her blade wobbles at the catch, and for a second the boat lurches, and she nearly catches a crab. She gets it back. Just. And keeps rowing, shaking her head.

"What did you say?" you ask.

Shiv grins her crooked grin.

"Something about her arse," she says. "In a kind way."

---

You shout Bea's name.

The way you promised. You cup your hands, the way Shiv did, and you take a great breath of the frosty air, and you shout it out across the mere: *Bea!* And again, louder. *BEA!* Your voice is small and high and cracks on it, and you don't care.

She can't wave. Her hands are on the oar.

But she pulls harder. You can see it. Her shoulders go into it, her back straightens, her blade goes in deeper and comes through faster, and the boat lifts under the extra push.

You shout again.

---

They row the length of the mere twice.

The first time, out to the far end and back, they're uneven: good for ten strokes, then a wobble, then good again. Morwenna's megaphone follows them up and down the water. You can't hear the words from the bank, only the boom.

The second time, the boat runs clean.

All the way. From the jetty to the far end and back, the four blades going in together every time, the boat sliding, smooth, fast, a long steady shape on the silver water. Not a splash. Not a wobble. Stroke after stroke after stroke, like a held note.

On the jetty, Morwenna lowers her megaphone.

And whoops.

A great big whoop, without the megaphone, and it carries just as far.

---

Bea comes off the water steaming.

Literally steaming: the heat coming off her bare arms and her shoulders in the cold air, rising off her in a haze. She's soaked to the knees from launching, her leggings dark and clinging. The tape on her palms has gone pink and then red where the blisters under it have opened, and are bleeding through. She doesn't seem to notice at all.

She's glowing.

She comes up the bank towards you with her hair coming out of its knot at last, and her face red and shining, and her eyes enormous.

"Did you see it run?" she says.

She's breathless. She grabs your arms.

"At the end. Did you see? It *ran*."

"I saw," you say.

Bea kisses you.

Wet, and cold, and hot, all at once: her mouth hot, her cheeks icy, her vest soaked against your coat. In front of everyone. The other rowers, the coxes, Morwenna on the jetty, Shiv with her flask. She kisses you hard on the mouth, and somebody wolf-whistles, and Bea doesn't stop for a moment longer.

Then she lets go.

"Go on," Bea says.

She's smiling. Out of breath, steaming, bleeding through her tape, smiling.

"Nine," she says. "Your walk." She puts her hands on your shoulders, and turns you round, and gives you a little push up the bank. "Tell me everything after."

She pushes you off.

---

At nine, Yusra is waiting at the Thornfield front door.

She's standing on the step in boots and a dark wool coat, belted, with a rucksack on her back. Proper walking boots, old and scuffed and laced high. Gloves in her hand.

Her hair is plaited.

A single long black plait down her back, neat and heavy, the end of it tied with a band. You've never seen it like that. And the badge is gone from her collar. Without it, in the plait and the old boots, standing on the step in the cold, she looks younger.

And nervous.

You can see it. In the way she's holding her gloves, both hands, turning them. In the way she looks up when you come round the corner of the house, quickly, and then away at the fells, and then back.

"Morning, Nala," she says.

"Morning."

She looks at your boots. Then at you.

"Harrow Fell," she says. "Two hours to the top." A pause. "Can you manage?"

"I can," you say.

---

You go out through the back gate.

It's in the wall behind Thornfield's garden, an old wooden gate, stiff on its hinges, and Yusra knows the trick of it: a lift and a shove. Beyond it the path climbs at once. No warming up. Straight up the side of the fell, through bracken that has gone rusty-brown with the autumn, every frond of it stiff and silvered with frost, crackling when you brush past.

Yusra walks fast.

Her long legs eat the path. She goes up it the way she goes along the top-floor corridor, straight and quick and certain, and you keep up. You keep up, but it costs you: within ten minutes your breath is coming hard, and your calves are burning, and your heart is going in your ears. This body runs light, but it doesn't run long.

Yusra glances back.

And she slows. She doesn't say anything. She just shortens her stride, and her pace drops to yours, and stays there.

---

At first you talk about easy things.

Bea's boat, and the way it ran at the end; Yusra asks for every stroke of it. The essay, and Penhallow, and *Tuesday, green ink*. The infirmary's quiet nights: how on a good night every bed is empty, and Yusra reads, and the night porter brings her a biscuit at three, and they don't talk.

You climb.

The bracken thins. The path goes up between grey rocks, splashed with yellow lichen, and the air gets colder and thinner and cleaner with every turn.

Halfway up, there's a stile in a stone wall.

You stop at it, and lean on it, and get your breath. And you look back.

The academy is small below you.

Small, and far, and perfect, like a model of itself. The towers, the quad, the long ranges of the houses, the library tower with its round window catching the sun. Thornfield, with its garden. The boathouse, tiny, at the edge of the water. And the mere below it all, long and still and silver, bright as a coin. A coin somebody has dropped in the bottom of the valley.

Yusra takes her rucksack off, and gets out a bottle of water, and hands it to you.

You drink. It's so cold it hurts your teeth.

"I don't do this," Yusra says.

She's looking at the view, not at you.

"Ask people to walk." A pause. "Ask people anything."

You hand her the bottle back.

"I'm glad you asked me," you say.

Yusra looks at the view. At the mere, and the academy, small and far below.

And she nods. Once.

Then she puts the bottle away, and swings her rucksack back on, and climbs on over the stile.

---

Higher up, there's snow.

Thin, at first: a dusting in the grass, white in the hollows, caught in the lee of the rocks. Then more. A crust of it, an inch deep, lying over the tussocks, glittering. Your boots squeak in it. Every step: a high, dry, ridiculous squeak, like a mouse, like a wet finger on glass.

Yusra laughs at the sound.

Out loud. She stops on the path and laughs, and takes a step, squeak, and laughs again.

"I'd forgotten that," she says. "That noise."

She tells you about walking on the moors above Leeds.

With her dad, on Sundays, when she was small. Up onto the tops on a bus, and then walking, miles, in all weathers, her hand in his. Him naming every bird. Her mum at home with a pot of soup, and the two of them coming back muddy to the knees. Snow, sometimes, like this. She'd squeak in it on purpose, she says, all the way along the path, to make him laugh.

"Who walked with you?" she asks. "When you were small?"

You don't know.

You don't know. The girl whose life this was had a life, and it had Sundays in it, and someone in them, maybe, or maybe not; and you can't see any of it. It's a blank. A door you've never been through.

"I can't remember," you say. "Who walked with me before." You look at the white slope. "Only that the fells feel right underfoot. Like I know them."

Yusra stops on the path.

She turns, and looks at you. In the snow, in the bright cold, with the wind pulling a strand of black hair out of her plait.

"That's a lonely thing to carry," she says.

Quietly.

"Whoever you were."

You look back at her.

"Less lonely now," you say.

Yusra holds your eyes.

A long moment. The wind going past. Your breath and hers, smoking between you.

Then she turns, and walks on.

But closer to you. Her arm brushing yours, now, as you go. Her shoulder, on the steep bits. She doesn't move away.

---

The top is a cairn of grey stones.

A heap of them, taller than you, built up by every walker who ever came up here, with snow lying in the cracks between them and frozen to their tops. The wind up here is hard and clean, fierce, pouring over the ridge from the west with the whole open sky behind it. It pushes at you. It roars in your ears.

And below you is the whole valley.

All of it. The mere, long and silver. The academy at the head of it, small as a toy. The village, a scatter of grey roofs and one church spire, and the thread of the road going away. And beyond, the far fells: ridge after ridge of them, one behind the other, going grey and then blue and then paler blue, all the way to the edge of the world, where they meet the sky and you can't tell which is which.

You stand and look.

You can't speak. You don't try.

Yusra bends, and picks a stone up from the ground at the foot of the cairn. A small grey one, flat. She puts it on the cairn, on a ledge, carefully, so it sits.

Then she picks up another, and hands it to you.

You put yours on top of hers.

---

The wind is fierce up here.

It gets into your eyes. They stream. Tears run down your face, sideways, pulled by the wind, and you can't see; you're blinking and wiping, half-blind, laughing at yourself.

Yusra's hands come onto your shoulders.

And she turns you. Round, out of the wind, so your back is to it, and she's in front of you, between you and the cairn, close. The roar drops a little. You blink, and wipe your eyes, and she's there. A foot away. Her hands still on your shoulders.

"Down there," she says, "I'm your warden."

Close. Low. You can hear her under the wind.

"I keep lines," Yusra says.

You look at her.

"Up here?" you ask.

Yusra looks at your mouth.

Then your eyes.

"Up here it's just me," she says.

And she kisses you.

Once. Slowly. Her cold hands coming up off your shoulders to hold your face, her gloved fingers cold on your cheeks, along your jaw. Her mouth is warm. Warm, in all that cold, soft and warm and careful, as if she's asking.

You kiss her back.

You lean up into her, and kiss her back, and your hands go to the front of her coat and hold on.

And Yusra makes a small sound.

Low in her throat, against your mouth. A sound like something giving way. Something long held, held all summer, held hard, coming loose all at once. And her hands tighten on your face. And she holds on.

When she draws back, her eyes are wet.

From the wind. Or not. You can't tell, and you don't think she can either.

"That was over the line," she says.

Her voice isn't steady. Her hands are still on your face.

"I don't care," Yusra says. "Not up here."

And she laughs. A little shakily. A short, surprised, breathless laugh, like the one in the kitchen, at *pressure*.

She takes her hands from your face.

And takes your hand.

---

She leads you down off the top.

Not the way you came: the other way, over the shoulder of the fell, down a little path you'd never have seen, through the snow, out of the wind. The roar drops behind you. Then it's gone.

And there's a tarn.

Hollin Tarn. Small, and black, and perfectly still, lying in a hollow below the summit like water in a cupped hand. Ringed all round with rushes, tall and brown and rattling faintly, rimed with frost. Out of the wind entirely. The snow lies round it on the slopes, white on black. There isn't a sound.

It's still, and quiet.

So quiet you can hear the snow ticking as it settles. So still the water holds the sky, and the fell above it, upside down, perfectly.

You sit on a flat rock by the water.

Yusra sits beside you, and swings her rucksack off, and unpacks it.

Cheese sandwiches, in greaseproof paper, cut in triangles. Two apples, red and green. And a flask.

An old flask, a steel one, battered.

She unscrews it, and pours. The steam comes up white between you. It smells of tea, and sugar.

"Sweet tea," Yusra says.

She holds the cup out to you.

"A ward," she says. "Keeps it hot."

You laugh.

You laugh so hard you spill it. It comes over your gloves, hot, and down onto the rock, steaming. You can't stop. You laugh, and bend over it, and laugh, with the tea dripping off your fingers, and your eyes streaming again, and your whole chest aching with it.

Yusra watches you laugh.

And her whole face is soft. The whole of it. The line between her brows gone, her mouth open a little, her eyes on you. She doesn't look like a warden at all.

---

You eat looking at the tarn.

The sandwiches are thick and the cheese is sharp. The apple is so cold it makes your teeth hurt, and so sweet it doesn't matter. The tea is hot and sweet and perfect.

Yusra sits close.

Her shoulder against yours. Her hip against yours on the rock. And after the sandwiches, her hand in yours: she takes off her glove, and then yours, and holds your bare hand in her bare hand, on the rock between you. Cold, and then warming.

You sit and look at the black water.

"I don't know what this is," she says.

She's looking at the tarn.

"I'm two years above you," Yusra says. "I'm your warden. I've been broken all summer."

You don't say anything. You hold her hand.

"And you've got Bea," she says.

She says it carefully.

"I've seen you two."

You tell her the truth.

That Bea knows. That you told her on Thursday night, in bed, the same night you said yes; and that Bea looked at you a long moment, and smiled, and said: go.

Yusra is quiet a while.

The tarn lies still. A rush rattles.

"Bea Lindqvist," she says.

And then: "Huh."

You wait.

"That's generous of her," Yusra says. Softer.

She turns your hand over in hers. Looks at it.

"I can't promise anything," she says. "I'm still mending."

You think of a bandage, and a thin pink line across a palm, and a sentence written three times over in a notebook.

"Mending takes a while," you say.

Yusra breathes in.

Then she leans her head on your shoulder. There, by the black water, on the cold rock. Her cheek against your coat. Her plait falling down your arm.

She closes her eyes.

And she breathes out. A long breath. All the way out.

---

You sit like that till the cold gets into your bones.

Till your feet are numb in your boots, and your fingers have gone white where they're not in her hand, and you're shivering, and so is she. You both stay put till then. The sky goes on being upside down in the tarn.

Then Yusra lifts her head.

She kisses you once more. Quick. Cold lips, and warm breath.

And she stands, and holds out her hand, and pulls you up.

---

On the way down she holds your hand.

All the way. Down off the snow, and through the rocks, and down the steep rusty slope of bracken, slipping, steadying each other, laughing when you skid. Her hand in yours, through her glove and yours. The academy getting bigger below, and bigger, the roofs and the towers coming up to meet you.

Till the back gate is in sight.

The old wooden gate in the garden wall, and the roofs of Thornfield behind it.

Yusra stops.

She looks at the gate. Then she lets go of your hand.

Gently. Her fingers sliding out of yours, slowly, a last squeeze, and gone.

At the gate she straightens her coat.

She tugs it straight at the hem, and runs a hand over her plait, and lifts her chin. And you watch it happen: you watch the warden come back. Into her shoulders, and her mouth, and her eyes. Like a coat being buttoned.

"Thank you, Nala," she says.

Level. Low.

"Go and get warm."

---

You go through the garden.

But at the Thornfield back door, she turns back.

She's on the step, her hand on the door. And her mouth twitches. At the corner, just there.

"Same time next Saturday?" she says.

Next Saturday is Bea's race. The Fell Cup.

"After the race," you say.

Yusra nods.

"After the race," she says.

---

In the afternoon you sleep.

You don't mean to. You go up to 15 to get your boots off, and you sit on the bed, and you lie back for a moment, and then you're asleep, worn out, your legs heavy as stones, your face burning from the wind.

You sleep an hour.

You wake to the smell of cocoa.

Bea is sitting on the edge of the bed, with two mugs. Showered, in a jumper and leggings, her hair wet, plasters on both palms. She's watching you. When you open your eyes she holds a mug out.

"Well?" says Bea.

You sit up. You take the mug.

You tell her.

The fell, and the snow that squeaked. The stile, and the academy small below. The cairn: the stones, one on top of the other. The tarn, black and still. And the wind, and Yusra's hands on your shoulders, and *up here it's just me*.

And the kiss.

Bea's eyebrows go up.

And up. And up, as you go on, until they're lost in her wet fringe.

"Yusra Haddad," says Bea, "kissed you on a mountain."

You nod.

"Good," Bea says. "She needed it."

She grins. Her dimples deep.

"So did you."

You hold your cocoa. You look at her.

"Do you mind?" you ask.

Bea thinks about it.

Honestly. You can see her do it: she looks into her mug, and her face goes serious, and she turns the question over and actually thinks, the way she'd think about a stroke, or a blister.

"A bit, maybe," she says. "Not much."

She looks up at you.

"You came back to 15," Bea says.

---

In the evening it's soup and toast in the Thornfield kitchen.

Tomato soup, from a big pan on the stove, and toast in stacks, and the radio on, and everyone tired from the day. Bea is falling asleep over her bowl. Somebody is telling a long story about a sheep.

Yusra is at the far end of the table.

She has her books spread out, and her reading glasses on, and a cup of tea. Her plait is still in. She's reading, turning pages, making notes in her neat small hand. The warden at the end of the table, the way she is most evenings.

She looks up once.

Across the room. Past the soup and the toast and the girl with the sheep story. Only at you.

Then back at her page.

But her mouth is soft.

---

You fall asleep early in 15.

Bea's arm over you, heavy and warm. Her breath in your hair. The radiator ticking.

Your legs still aching from the fell.

A day at Hollowmere ends.
