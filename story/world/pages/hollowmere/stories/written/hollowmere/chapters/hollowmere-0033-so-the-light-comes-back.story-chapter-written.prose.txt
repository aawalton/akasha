On Friday Bea is up before light.

You wake to the sound of her. Not her alarm; her feet. Socked feet on the floorboards of 15, in the dark, going up and down. To the window. Back to the door. To the window. Back. Soft, steady, endless, like a caged animal in a very small cage.

You lift your head off the pillow.

She's a shape against the grey of the curtains, in her pyjamas and her thick socks, her arms wrapped round herself. Pacing.

"Tomorrow," Bea says.

To the window. Not to you.

"Tomorrow," she says again, at the door, and turns, and goes back.

---

You get up.

You catch her on her way past. By the wrists, both of them, gently, the way you'd catch a bird. She stops. She looks at you in the dark, wild-eyed.

You walk her backwards to the bed, and sit her down on it.

And you sit beside her, still holding her wrists, and look at her.

"Breathe out," you say.

"I am breathing."

"Out. All the way. Long."

Bea glares at you.

Then she does it. A long, shaky breath, going out of her, out and out, her shoulders coming down with it. Then another. You hold her wrists and breathe out with her, slow, till her hands stop trembling in yours.

She laughs.

Shaky, and wet, and cross.

"You do that to Kit," she says. "Breathe out." She shakes her head at you. "It works on me too."

A pause.

"Annoyingly," says Bea.

And she leans in, and kisses you.

---

At nine group F climbs the library tower.

The focus essays go in.

One by one, onto Penhallow's desk, in a pile. Priya's in three colours. Amara's typed. Shiv's, folded once. Lin's, with a small drawing in the corner: a ring, this time, very fine.

You put yours in her hand.

The way you did before. You go round the desk, and hold it out, and she takes it.

And she reads the last line.

There and then, the way she did last time. Lifting her glasses on their chain, and settling them on her nose, and turning to the last page.

*It should remember, if what it remembers is someone being loved.*

She looks up at you.

"Someone being loved," Penhallow says.

A pause.

"Hm."

Her mouth barely moves. But her eyes smile. Over the rims of her glasses, the kind blue eyes, crinkling at the corners.

"Tuesday," she says.

---

Kit puts hers on the pile.

Without looking at it. She lays it down, face down, quickly, and goes back to her chair by the round window, and sits with her hands knotted in her lap. Tight. Her knuckles white.

You glance at her. She doesn't glance back.

Penhallow sits, and folds her hands.

"Ashworth," she says. "What does your focus remember?"

Kit looks at the window.

At the grey sky in it, and the mere. Her hands knotted.

"Whoever it's for," Kit says.

Quietly. Steadily.

"That's the point of it."

Penhallow looks at her.

A long moment. Over her glasses. Not saying anything. The room very quiet; the clock on the shelf ticking.

Then she reaches out, and takes the lid off the tin, and pushes it across the desk.

To Kit.

Kit looks at it. And takes one. A single round of shortbread, in her pale fingers.

---

Penhallow goes to her little board.

She chalks next week's question on it, in her quick slanting hand, and taps it once with the chalk, and says, "Friday."

You copy it down.

Lin writes it down too, very neatly, in her small clean hand, and underlines the key word.

Shiv groans. Out loud, her head going back against the chair.

And Kit watches the clock.

You see her do it. Not the board: the clock on the shelf. Watching the hands. And going, slowly, slightly pink.

---

At eleven it's Sigils, in the Drawing Room.

Kit's desk is empty.

You notice at once. Her place beside yours, the stool pushed in, no ink, no pen, no Kit. The tutor glances at it, and frowns, and marks something.

Amara leans across from the next desk.

"She's at the forge," Amara says, low. "Since the tutorial. Went straight there. I saw her go." She raises one shaped brow. "*Mysterious*."

---

In the afternoon you go to the library with your study.

The big reading room, the green lamps. You practise the calling charm at a quiet table in the corner: a pen, and a button, and a thimble from your sewing kit, at one end of the long oak table. Calling them across. Six feet. And sending them back, gently, with a push of the sigil the other way.

Across, and back. Across, and back.

The thimble is easiest. It comes like a pigeon to a hand, every time, and goes back like one too. The button rolls a little before it lifts, as if it's making up its mind. The pen is the worst; it wants to come point first, like a dart, and you have to ask it twice as gently to turn and come sideways.

A second-year at the next table looks up, watches the thimble go back and forth for a while, and smiles to herself, and goes back to her book.

When your head starts to ache, you let it rest.

You put your hands in your lap, and look at the fells through the tall window, and breathe, until it eases. Then you go on.

---

At half past four Kit knocks at the Thornfield kitchen door.

You open it.

She's in a dark coat, a good one, long and black, buttoned. Her black bob is wet, combed straight, the fringe dark and damp on her forehead. She's scrubbed. Her face is pink with it, her hands are pink with it. She smells of soap.

And she has soot under one fingernail.

One. The nail of her right forefinger, a thin black crescent she's missed.

She sees you notice.

And she hides the hand. Quick, behind her back. And then laughs, at herself, at being caught.

"I scrubbed," Kit says. "For twenty minutes. I scrubbed."

---

Bea is at the kitchen table.

With a pint of milk. Not a glass: the pint, the bottle, drinking from it, her race-nerves having apparently decided that what she needs is calcium. She looks up at Kit over it.

"Have her back by the last bus, Ashworth," Bea says. "Big day."

Kit draws herself up.

"Ten o'clock, Lindqvist," she says. Very gravely. Hand on heart. "On my honour."

Bea snorts milk.

Down her chin and onto the table, coughing, spluttering, laughing, while Kit stands in the doorway with her hand still on her heart and her face perfectly straight.

---

The five o'clock bus from the village to Kendal.

Forty minutes, along the valley road. The bus is old and warm and smells of diesel and wet coats, and nearly empty. The light is going outside: the fells going from brown to grey to dark, the sky over them deep blue and then indigo, the first lights coming on in farmhouses, small and yellow and far apart.

Kit sits by the window. You sit by the aisle.

For a long time she looks out at the fells going dark.

Then, halfway there, without looking round, she takes your hand.

On the seat between you. Her fingers finding yours, cool, and lacing through them, and holding on. She keeps looking out of the window.

You keep holding on.

The bus grinds up a long hill and down the other side. A sheep looks at you over a wall in the headlights. The windows steam up, and Kit draws a small ring in the steam with her free finger, and then a second one linked through it, and then rubs them out with her sleeve before you can say anything.

---

Kendal.

Grey stone, everywhere, in the lamplight. Wet streets shining from an earlier shower, every puddle full of lamps. The market hall shut and dark, its big doors closed. Shop windows lit. People going home with their collars up.

And on the corner, the cinema, lit up.

Its old sign glowing. Its doors open. The posters in their glass frames.

---

Friday night's film is old, and foreign, and subtitled.

There are six people in the whole cinema.

You count them. An old man in a flat cap, near the front. Two women together, halfway back. A student with a notebook. And you, and Kit.

You sit at the back.

The lights go down. The film starts.

And after a while, Kit puts her arm round you.

Stiff, at first. You can feel it: her arm along the back of your seat, and then, carefully, down onto your shoulders, rigid, as if she's following instructions from a book. She doesn't look at you.

Then easier.

Slowly, as the film goes on, her arm softening. Her hand coming to rest on your far shoulder, warm. Her side against your side.

The film is French, and slow, and beautiful.

Two women in a house by the sea. One of them painting the other. Long silences. The sound of the sea. Firelight on a face. A brush moving on canvas. The two of them looking at each other, and looking, and not speaking, for minutes at a time.

Kit watches it with her mouth open.

You watch her watching. Her face in the light from the screen, rapt, her lips parted, her eyes enormous.

At the end, when the lights come up, she wipes her eyes.

Quickly. One swipe of her wrist, each eye. And pretends she didn't.

---

Outside, in the cold, she stops on the pavement.

Under a lamp. Her breath smoking.

"That was," Kit says.

She stops.

"That was," she says again.

She gives up.

And kisses you. There, under the lamp, on the wet pavement, outside the cinema, with her cold hands on your face.

---

"Dinner," she says, when she lets you go.

She looks slightly embarrassed.

"I've booked that too," says Kit. "Don't laugh."

You don't laugh. She takes your hand, and leads you down the high street, and then down an alley, narrow and dark between two old buildings, the cobbles wet.

At the end of it is a lit window.

Gianni's.

Six tables. Candles in bottles, the wax running down them in thick coloured drips. Checked cloths. A small loud Italian man in an apron, who throws his arms up when you come in. The smell of it: garlic and bread and tomatoes, hot and rich, wrapping round you at the door.

"Signorina!" he cries, to Kit.

He sits you in the corner by the window. And brings bread without asking: a basket of it, warm, with oil in a saucer.

---

You eat pasta.

And drink one glass of red each, which goes straight to your head. Kit's cheeks go pink with it.

Kit talks.

About the film first. The light in it. The painter's hands. How the one being painted kept looking back. How it was about looking, the whole of it, about being looked at and looking back. She talks with her fork, waving it, more than you've ever heard her talk about anything.

Then about the forge.

The fire. The way metal goes soft when you get it hot enough. The way you have to listen to it. How she goes there when she can't think, and then she can.

Then she stops.

She puts her fork down.

"I've been making something," Kit says.

She's looking at the candle.

"Since week four. Tuesdays." A pause. "I haven't shown anyone."

---

She takes a small parcel out of her coat pocket.

Wrapped in a bit of soft cloth, black, tied with thread. She puts it on the table, by the candle, between your plates.

"It's a focus," Kit says.

Very fast.

"For you. You can say no." She swallows. "It's not, it doesn't mean anything." A pause. "It does."

You look at her.

Then you unwrap it.

The thread. The black cloth, folding back.

A ring.

A silver ring, plain and narrow. Bright, new, polished. And worked round it, all the way round the band, fine as a hair, a line.

A hook.

You turn it to the candle to see. The hook turns in. The way the glim's does. The way Lin showed you, on the first day, with her page drawn to show it: not out, but in. Towards the one who wears it.

"So the light comes back to you," Kit says.

Very fast.

"That's Lin's line. I stole it." She's looking at the candle. "I asked her."

---

You can't speak.

For a moment, you can't. You hold the ring in your fingers, by the candle, and look at it, and then you look up at her across the table.

Kit is scarlet.

Scarlet to the ears. Right up, under the damp black fringe, the whole of her face, in the candlelight.

"It's to remember," Kit says.

She makes herself look at you.

"That's the point," she says. "A focus should remember." She swallows. "I want it to remember me."

---

You put it on.

On the third finger of your right hand.

It fits exactly. Not loose, not tight. It slides on over your knuckle and sits at the base of your finger as if it was poured there.

You look at her.

"How," you say.

Kit laughs. Embarrassed. Her hand coming up over her mouth.

"Lin's drawing of you asleep," she says. "Your hand's in it. Open on the pillow." She looks at the tablecloth. "I measured."

---

The ring is warm.

At once. As soon as it's on. Warmer than silver should be, warmer than your skin, a small steady warmth round the base of your finger. As if it already knows you.

As if it's been waiting.

You reach across the table.

You take her hand. Her right hand, with the black crescent under the nail that she missed. Her sooty hand.

And you lift it, and kiss her knuckles.

There, in Gianni's, by the candle, in front of the small loud Italian man.

---

Gianni comes over.

With two little glasses, on a tray. Something sweet and golden in them, catching the candlelight. He sets one in front of each of you.

"For the ring," he says.

And winks.

Kit dies.

She actually dies: puts her face in her hands, on the table, and groans, and stays there. Her ears flaming. Gianni laughs, delighted, and pats her on the shoulder, and goes away humming.

---

At nine Gianni's closes.

Gianni turns the sign on the door and blows out the candles one by one, and the last other table pays and goes out into the cold. Then he shoos you out. With a paper bag of leftover bread, pressed into Kit's hands, and a blessing in Italian, loud and long, with his hand on his heart, that follows you all the way up the alley.

---

You walk to the bus stop.

Slowly. Holding hands. Through the wet lamplit streets.

You keep turning the ring.

With your thumb. Round and round on your finger, feeling the fine raised line of the hook go under your thumb, and round. Warm.

---

The ten o'clock bus. The last one.

Nearly empty. The driver, and an old woman with shopping, near the front, asleep.

You sit at the back.

And Kit kisses you.

The whole way home.

Slow, and deep. Her hand in your hair. Her mouth on yours. Forty minutes, along the dark valley road, the bus rocking and grinding, the fells black outside the windows, the odd farmhouse light going by. She kisses you like she's got all the time in the world and means to use every minute of it.

---

At the village, you walk up the dark lane to the academy.

Her arm round your waist. Your head on her shoulder, though she's barely taller than you. The stars out. Your footsteps on the frosty lane.

At Ashcombe's step she stops.

The door is shut. The lamp over it lit. She stands on the bottom step, so she's a little above you, and looks down at you.

"I'm a coward," Kit says.

Low.

"I'd ask you up." A pause. "Not tonight. Bea's race."

You look up at her.

"Soon," you say. "After."

Kit nods.

And kisses you once more. Hard. Her hand finding your right hand, your ringed hand, and holding it. Tight.

---

"I'll be on the bank tomorrow," Kit says.

Still holding your hand.

"Shouting for Thornfield." She lifts her chin. "In front of all of Ashcombe."

"They'll string you up," you say.

Kit grins.

"Let them," she says.

She looks at your hand in hers. At the ring.

"I've got my own house now," says Kit. "Sort of."

---

She goes in.

Up the steps, and opens the door. At the door she turns.

She looks at you. Standing in the light from the hall behind her, small and dark in her long coat.

She lifts her hand.

And goes.

---

In 15, Bea is asleep.

Flat on her back, in the middle of the bed. Her arms crossed on her chest, her hands on her shoulders, like a knight on a tomb.

You undress in the dark.

Quietly, by feel. And slide into bed beside her.

She stirs.

"Back by ten?" she mumbles. Her eyes shut.

"Twenty to eleven," you say. "The bus."

Bea smiles, with her eyes shut.

"Ashworth," she says. "Liar."

---

She finds your hand in the dark.

Under the blanket, her fingers sliding into yours. Your right hand.

And she feels the ring.

She goes still.

Her fingers on it. Feeling it. The smooth band, and the fine raised line of the hook going round.

"Oh," Bea says.

Soft. Awake now.

"Kit?"

"Yes," you say.

Bea is quiet.

Then she takes your hand, ring and all, and holds it against her chest. Flat, over her heart, under her crossed arms.

She holds it there a while.

"It's lovely," she says.

Her voice is soft and sleepy and warm.

"Sleep now," says Bea.

---

You lie awake a little.

Turning the ring with your thumb. Round, and round. Warm as a hand.

Until Bea's breathing, slow and steady beside you, takes you under.

A day at Hollowmere ends.
