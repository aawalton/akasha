Bea is up at five.

You hear her before you're properly awake: the creak of the boards in 15, the click of the kettle, the small tight sound of someone trying very hard to be quiet. When you open your eyes she's sitting on the edge of the bed in her rowing kit, the green Thornfield top, her hair scraped back, her face white as paper in the lamplight. Saturday of week five. The Fell Cup.

She has one slice of dry toast on a plate on her knee. She eats it in tiny bites, like a mouse, staring at the wall, chewing each bite far too long.

"I'm fine," she says, before you've said a word. "I'm completely fine. I'm eating toast. Look."

You sit up behind her and put your hands on her shoulders. They're up round her ears.

"Breathe out," you say.

She does. It shudders.

"Again. Longer."

She breathes out again, slow, all the way to the bottom, and her shoulders come down an inch under your hands. She reaches back without looking and finds your right hand and holds it, her thumb on the ring, turning it.

"Jetty wall," she says. "You'll be on the jetty wall."

"Jetty wall."

"Shouting."

"Shouting," you say. "You'll hear me from the boathouse."

She laughs, a small cracked laugh, and squeezes your ringed hand hard, and finishes the toast.

---

At eight she goes down to the boathouse.

You walk her to Thornfield's door. Morwenna is waiting on the gravel in her navy fleece and navy headband, her dark ponytail swinging, with the other three of the four and their little cox, all of them in green, all of them stamping and blowing on their hands in the cold.

"Lindqvist," Morwenna says. "Good. Let's go and frighten some people."

Bea goes with them down the path towards the water. Halfway along, at the corner where the path turns through the rhododendrons, she stops, and turns, and finds you on the step.

She waves. A big wave, her whole arm, her blistered hand open.

You wave back with both of yours, until the rhododendrons take her.

---

The morning is cold and still.

You stand on the step a while longer, your breath smoking. The mere lies below the academy like a sheet of glass, without a ripple anywhere on it, grey and silver, the fells upside down in it. The reeds along the shore are white with frost, every blade furred. A thin mist lies on the water and lifts as you watch, slowly, in rags, peeling off the surface and thinning into the pale sky.

Somewhere a coot calls. Somewhere further off, from the boathouse, a voice shouts an order, small and clear across the water, and oars knock.

It's a perfect day for it. Bea said last night, lying in the dark, that if it was flat she'd have no excuses. It's flat.

You go in and put on every jumper you own.

---

By half past nine half the academy is on the shore path.

You come down with Lin and Kit's text in your pocket, *On my way. Don't start without me. K.*, and the path is crowded already, girls three deep along the water's edge in scarves and hats and gloves, steam rising off flasks, house colours everywhere. Ashcombe blue, a great deal of it, in a smug block by the boathouse. Thornfield green, scattered and noisy. Somebody's brought a drum.

Banners. A bedsheet on two broom handles that says GO ASHCOMBE in very neat letters. A cardboard sign that says ROW, YOU BEAUTIFUL IDIOTS in green paint.

Lin stands close to you in her long coat, her sketchbook already open, her pencil moving, catching the crowd in quick lines. Her hair is loose again under her hat.

---

Shiv arrives at a run, her parka flapping, a long bundle under her arm.

"Make room," she says, "make room, mind your heads," and shakes it out across the path with a crack. It's a bedsheet: a whole double bedsheet, painted in thick green letters, words you can't read, Irish, a great sprawling sentence of it, and underneath, in green, a drawing of an oar that is plainly, unmistakably, very rude.

The Thornfield girls round you cheer. Somebody whistles. Two Ashcombe girls further along look round and go stiff.

Shiv hands one end to a second-year and holds the other high, beaming.

"What does it say?" you ask.

Shiv grins her crooked grin. "Thornfield," she says. "Row." She considers. "And something about Ashcombe's mothers."

"Shiv."

"In a kind way," she says. "Mostly."

---

Kit comes along the path from the direction of Ashcombe.

She's wearing an Ashcombe scarf. Long, blue, striped, wound twice round her neck, as she's worn it every cold morning this term. She walks straight past the block of blue by the boathouse, and girls there call her name, *Ashworth, here*, and she gives them a little nod.

Then she stops, in front of them all, and unwinds the scarf.

She takes it off. She folds it, carefully, in half and in half again, and puts it in her coat pocket. And she walks the last ten yards along the path to you and Lin and Shiv's bedsheet, bare-necked in the cold, and stands next to you, and looks straight ahead at the water.

---

"*Traitor*," someone shouts, from the blue.

Kit goes scarlet. You watch it climb her neck and fill her face to the ears. Her hands are fists in her pockets.

Then she turns her head, and opens her mouth, and shouts back, high and cracking:

"*Thornfield!*"

The green round you erupts. Shiv howls. Somebody bangs the drum. Kit turns back to face the water, so red she's nearly purple, and says, very quietly, to you alone, "I think I'm going to die."

"You're not going to die."

"I've never shouted in public in my life," Kit says. "My mother would have me shot." She breathes out. Then the corner of her mouth goes up. "Thornfield," she says again, very softly, to herself, trying it.

You take her hand in your gloved one. She holds on.

---

Lin has her sketchbook on her arm, drawing Kit's red ear. Priya and Amara are a little further along, sharing one flask, passing it back and forth, Priya in a bright yellow hat with a bobble and Amara in a long camel coat, gold at her ears. Amara salutes you with the flask lid. Priya waves both arms.

And at the back, against the wall of the path, away from the crowd, Yusra.

She's in her boots and a dark coat, no lanyard, no clipboard, her hands in her pockets. Off duty. She isn't cheering. She's watching, the way she watches everything, quietly, taking in every face. Her eyes find yours over the heads, and stay a second, and her chin dips. A nod.

You nod back.

---

"Jetty wall," you say to Kit and Lin. "I promised."

So the three of you leave the path by the boathouse and walk on, along the shore, away from the crowd. The path narrows and runs through the trees and out again along the water, past the swimmers' buoy and Shiv's rock, and the noise of the academy thins behind you, and the frost crunches under your boots.

Kit walks with her hand in yours. Lin walks on your other side with her sketchbook shut under her arm. Half a mile. The whole length of the course. You watch the water as you go, flat and grey and empty, and try to imagine five boats on it, and Bea in one, and your stomach goes over.

"She'll be all right," Lin says, quietly. She's watching your face.

"I know," you say. You don't, and Lin knows it, and takes your arm.

---

The village is out on the jetty wall.

All of it, it looks like. Old women in headscarves and wellingtons with their arms folded on the stone. Children sitting along the top of the wall with their legs dangling, in bobble hats, eating crisps at a quarter to ten in the morning. A small fat terrier in a tartan scarf, being held up by a girl of six so it can see. The woman from the village shop, in her apron under a big coat, with a brass handbell in her fist that she keeps lifting and testing, one small *ting*, as if to be sure it still works.

They make room for you at the wall without being asked. An old woman shoves along and pats the stone. "Academy?" she says. "Which house?"

"Thornfield," you say.

She laughs, and pats your hand. "Oh, pet," she says. "Never mind."

---

On a little folding table at the end of the jetty, on a tea towel folded square, sits the Fell Cup.

You go and look at it. It's smaller than you thought: a pewter cup on a short stem, two handles, dented in three places, one handle a little bent. It's been polished till it shines, but the dents shine too. Round the base, scratched in a hundred different hands, are names of houses and years. Ashcombe, Ashcombe, Ashcombe, Ashcombe, Ashcombe, Ashcombe, the last six. You turn your head to read further down, and further, and find it, small and old and nearly worn away: *Thornfield 1987*.

Kit looks at it over your shoulder, at the six blue names in a row, and then at the old worn one under them. She touches the dent in the rim with one fingertip, very lightly, the way she touches a ring at the forge to see if it's cooled.

---

At ten, far down the mere, a flag drops.

You don't see it fall so much as see the place where it was, a speck of white at the boathouse end, half a mile away, gone. Then, a beat late, the sound comes over the water: a faint shout, thin as a bird.

And five boats leap forward.

They're tiny from here. Small as insects, as pond skaters, five dark slivers on the grey, each with its little legs of oars going all at once. They spread out across the mere and come on, and for a long time they seem not to move at all, only to grow, very slowly, the way a thing far off does.

The wall goes quiet. The children stop eating their crisps.

---

For a long minute they're only specks.

Then the colours come clear. Blades, flashing as they turn: blue, and red, and yellow, and white, and there, near the middle of the mere, green.

"Green," Kit says. "Green. That's them. There."

You see it. Your heart is going so hard you can feel it in your teeth. You can't make out a single person in the boat, only the four blades lifting and dipping, lifting and dipping, and the little lump of the cox at the stern, and somewhere in the front of it, at bow, Bea.

The shopkeeper rings her bell once, by accident, and the whole wall laughs, and goes quiet again.

---

Ashcombe is ahead from the start.

Of course it is. You can see that much even from here: the blue boat out in front of the others, a length clear and pulling away, beautiful, smooth, the blades going in and out of the water like one blade. Six years. They row like a boat that knows it wins.

But the green boat is moving too. It was third. You watch it creep up on the red one, and draw level, and inch past. Second.

"Second," Lin says. She's stopped drawing. Her pencil is still in her hand, forgotten.

"Come on," Kit says. She has your arm in both her hands, gripping it. "Come on. Come on, come on, come on."

---

The boats reach the bend.

The mere turns there, by the reeds, a long slow curve where the water runs shallow and the reeds stand out from the shore, white with frost. Ashcombe goes into it first, a length clear, still smooth.

You think of Bea's hand. Her palm, the night before last, in the lamplight, with BEND written across it in biro in big capitals, and under it, smaller, your name. *Morwenna says Ashcombe always goes wide at the bend*, she said. *Always. Six years and they still do it. They're so sure, they forget. So at the bend we go. Everything we've got. That's the whole plan. That's it.*

You hold your breath.

---

Thornfield's stroke rate goes up.

You can see it from the wall, plain as anything: the green blades suddenly quicker, in and out, in and out, faster than before, and all together. Absolutely together. Four oars like one oar. Like a heartbeat speeding up.

"Oh," Lin says.

The green boat leans into the bend and comes round it tight, close to the reeds, so close you think they'll touch, and the gap between it and the blue boat is closing, you can see it closing, the dark water between them getting smaller.

---

Ashcombe fades at the bend.

Exactly the way Morwenna said they would. The blue boat swings wide, a little, a lazy curve out into open water, and loses its line, and for three strokes its blades go ragged, out of time, one oar catching late.

And the green boat is on them.

Half a length. A quarter. The green bow comes up level with the blue stern, and keeps coming.

---

You're shouting before you know it.

You don't decide to. It just comes up out of you, out of your chest, huge, much bigger than your small body ought to hold: *Bea! BEA! BEA!* Over and over. Her name, hoarse, cracking, your hands cupped round your mouth, your whole self leaning out over the wall.

The old woman beside you looks up at you, startled, and then grins and starts shouting too, *Go on, green, go on, lass!*

---

Shiv's voice comes down the shore.

You'd know it anywhere: from somewhere back along the path, half a mile off, faint and furious and perfectly clear, howling in Irish. Long, rolling, magnificent sentences of it, you can't understand a word of it, and every word of it is plainly obscene.

Beside you, Kit is shouting "*Thornfield!*" Again and again, no longer cracking, her voice gone high and strong, her face scarlet, tears standing in her eyes from the cold or something else. "*Thornfield! Thornfield!*"

Lin isn't shouting. Lin has both hands pressed flat over her mouth.

---

The last two hundred yards.

Thornfield and Ashcombe, level. Dead level. Bow to bow, blade to blade, two boats side by side on the grey water with a yard between them, coming straight at the jetty. Behind them the other three are falling back, red and yellow and white, one length, two, out of it.

It's just the two of them now. Green and blue. And the sound of them reaches the wall at last: the knock of the oars in the gates, the rush of the hulls, the coxes screaming, two small voices, *Push, push, PUSH.*

---

You can see Bea now.

At bow, at the front of the green boat, nearest you. Her face screwed up tight, her mouth open, her teeth bared. Her hair has come loose from its scraped-back knot and is whipping across her face, honey-blonde and dark with spray, and she isn't brushing it away, she can't, both her hands are on the oar. She's pulling with everything. With her whole body, her back and her legs and her blistered hands, every stroke all the way out to the end and back, as if the whole world is this oar and this water and the next stroke.

You've never seen anything so beautiful in either of your lives.

---

The jetty wall roars.

The whole village at once, old women and children and the girls from the academy, a wall of noise. The shopkeeper has given up on testing her bell and is ringing it like a madwoman, over her head, both hands, *clang clang clang clang*. The terrier in the tartan scarf is barking its head off in the little girl's arms. Somebody's hat goes in the water.

The two boats come at you side by side, so close now you can hear the blades bite, hear the girls in them grunting at the catch.

---

Thornfield crosses the line a third of a length ahead.

You see it happen. The line is a buoy and a post with a flag on it off the end of the jetty, and the green bow goes past the post and the blue bow goes past a heartbeat later, a gap you could fit a person in, the length of a girl lying down. Not much. Enough.

The umpire, a stout woman in a waxed jacket standing in a launch by the post, raises her flag.

Green.

---

For a second there's silence on the wall.

A real silence. The whole wall holds its breath at once, all of you, as if it can't quite have seen what it saw. The bell stops mid-swing. Even the dog stops.

Then everyone from Thornfield screams at once.

You scream. Kit screams. Lin takes her hands off her mouth and screams, a high astonished shriek you've never heard from her. Back along the shore path the green girls are screaming too, a thin roar down the water, and the drum is going, and somewhere in the middle of it Shiv's voice, wordless now, just howling.

---

Thornfield's first Fell Cup since 1987.

Kit is hugging you. Both arms round your neck, her face in your shoulder, nearly knocking you off the wall. Then she's hugging Lin, and Lin is hugging her back, laughing. Then she's hugging the old woman beside you, who pats her on the back and says *There, pet, there*. And Kit is crying, openly, tears running down her face in the cold, laughing through it, scarlet to the ears.

"They did it," she keeps saying. "They did it. They did it."

---

Out on the water, past the line, the green boat drifts.

Bea has collapsed. She's folded right over her oar, her forehead on her hands, her back heaving. The whole four has, all of them slumped, the cox flat on her back in the stern with her arms flung out.

You watch Bea's back rise and fall, rise and fall.

Then she sits up. Slowly. She pushes the wet hair off her face with one shaking hand, and turns her head, and looks for the jetty wall.

---

She finds you.

You see her find you: her eyes going along the crowded wall, the old women, the children, the dog, Kit, Lin, and stopping. On you.

She lifts one arm. It's shaking so hard you can see it from here. And she points, straight at you. Just at you. Out of the whole crowd on the wall, her arm out across the water like a line drawn between you.

You wave both your arms back over your head, wildly, like someone signalling a ship, and you're laughing, and you can't see properly for crying.

---

The four rows in to the jetty, slow, ragged, gone to pieces now it's over.

Hands reach down to hold the boat. Bea gets an arm on the jetty and climbs out, and she's soaked, absolutely soaked, her green top black with spray, her legs gone to string. She staggers, and nearly goes in. A hand catches her elbow.

She doesn't look at them. She walks straight down the jetty, through the people, through the noise, and straight into your arms.

She's cold and wet through and shaking, and she smells of the mere, and she holds on to you like you're the last solid thing in the world.

---

"Did you see?" Bea says into your neck. Her breath is hot. "Did you see? Did you see it?"

"I saw."

"BEND," she says. "BEND. We went. We went at the bend, like she said, and they went wide, and we, we just." She's laughing and gasping both. "I heard you," she says. "I heard you. All the way down. From the bend. *Bea, Bea.* I heard you all the way."

You hold her tighter. Her heart is going like a bird's against you.

---

She kisses you.

Wet and laughing, her cold mouth on yours, her soaked arms round your neck, there at the end of the jetty in front of the whole village and half the academy. She tastes of the mere and of salt. She kisses you hard, and then softly, and then she's laughing too much to kiss you at all and just presses her forehead to yours.

The wall cheers. The old women whoop. Somebody wolf-whistles. The shopkeeper rings her bell, *clang*, once, with great ceremony.

Bea goes pink under the wet, and kisses you again, harder, on purpose.

---

Morwenna comes down the jetty in her navy fleece with her headband askew, and goes to the little table, and lifts the Fell Cup off its tea towel.

She holds it up over her head. The dented pewter flashes in the low sun.

"*THORNFIELD!*" she booms, in a voice that carries clear across the mere, and the shore path half a mile away roars back.

Then she turns, and finds Bea in your arms, and holds out the cup to her.

"Bow," Morwenna says. "Go on. You heard them first."

---

Bea takes the cup in both shaking hands.

She looks at it. At the dents, at the names round the base. Then she lifts it up over her head, high as her arms will go, and the whole wall cheers again, and Bea is crying and laughing at once, the tears running down into her grin, her hair stuck to her face.

Back along the shore path, small and far but plain as day, Shiv's bedsheet goes up on its broom handles and waves, green letters and rude oar and all, back and forth, back and forth, like a flag.

---

Lin has her sketchbook open again.

She draws fast, standing at the wall, while Bea holds up the cup: three lines. You watch them go down. A curve for Bea's raised arms, a slash for the cup, a scribble for the wild hair. That's all. And it's Bea, absolutely Bea, more Bea than a photograph, every bit of the moment in it.

Lin tears the page out, neat along the stitching, and steps forward and gives it to her.

Bea looks at it, and her face crumples all over again. "Lin," she says. "Lin." She hugs Lin with the cup still in one hand, and Lin, pink, lets her.

---

Yusra comes to the edge of the crowd.

She's come along the shore path; she must have walked the whole half mile behind you. She stands at the back of the people on the jetty until they part for her a little, and steps through, in her dark coat and boots, and holds out her hand to Bea.

Bea, startled, shifts the cup and takes it. Yusra shakes her hand, once, firmly, the way you'd shake hands on a deal.

"Lindqvist," Yusra says. "Good."

---

Bea stares at her, astonished.

Then she throws both arms round Yusra and hugs her, wet top and pewter cup and all.

Yusra goes rigid. Her arms stay out at her sides. She stands there in the hug like a post, her face doing something complicated.

Then she laughs. A short, surprised laugh, out of her, as if it got past her before she could stop it. And she lifts one hand and pats Bea's soaking back, twice, awkward and gentle.

"You're very wet," she says.

"I've won a cup," Bea says into her shoulder.

"So I see."

---

Over Bea's shoulder, Yusra looks at you.

Her dark eyes are steady and warm. Across the noise of the jetty, under it, she says, just to you:

"Front door," she says. "When you're ready. No rush."

You nod. She nods back, and lets go of Bea, and steps back into the crowd.

---

The four carry the boat home.

Up out of the water and onto their shoulders, upside down, the four girls and the little cox under it like a long green beetle, and back along the shore path to the boathouse, half a mile, in a crowd of singing girls from Thornfield. The singing is terrible. It's a song you don't know, and half of them don't know it either and sing anyway.

Bea is at the front, under the bow, and she isn't carrying it so much as riding along on the cheer, her feet barely touching the ground, her face turned up, singing loudest of all.

You walk alongside with Kit and Lin, carrying the cup between you.

---

By noon Bea is in the bath.

The big claw-footed one at the end of the top-floor corridor, full to the brim and steaming, with the door shut and the Fell Cup on the windowsill beside it where she can see it. You can hear her from the corridor, singing. The same terrible song, over and over, with the words wrong, splashing.

You knock. "Bea?"

"*Go!*" she shouts through the door, happily. "Go on! I know what time it is!"

---

"Go," Bea shouts again through the door, and you hear a great slosh of water. "Walk with your warden. Go on. I'm fine. I'm *better* than fine. I've won a cup." A pause. "I'm a legend now," she says, grandly. "Thornfield legend. They'll put me on the wall."

You laugh against the door.

"Love you," you say.

"Love you," Bea sings, to the tune of the terrible song, and splashes. "Go."

The door opens a crack, and a wet pink arm comes out round it, dripping on the boards, and waves you off down the corridor.

---

Yusra is at the Thornfield front door at half past twelve.

Exactly half past. You come down the stairs and she's there on the step outside, in her walking boots and her dark coat, the old rucksack on her back with its straps pulled tight. She's looking out at the quad with her hands in her pockets. When the door opens she turns.

"You came," she says, as if it was in doubt.

"I said I would."

"You did." She looks at you a moment. Something in her face eases. "You did."

---

"Not the fell today," Yusra says. "You've been shouting all morning. You'll be tired." She tips her head towards the water. "The shore. The far side of the mere. It's quiet there. Hardly anyone goes."

"All right."

"It's flat," she says. "Mostly. I checked."

You walk.

---

Round the mere, away from the noise.

Past the boathouse, where the green boat lies on its trestles and the last of the singing is going on inside. Past the end of the academy grounds, through a gate, and onto a path you've never taken, along the water's edge. It goes in among alders, low and twisted, their roots in the water, their last leaves yellow. The mere shows bright between the trunks, silver and blue now the mist has gone, every ripple catching the sun.

Behind you the academy's noise fades, and fades, until it's only birds, and the lap of the water, and your boots on the path.

---

You go round a bend in the path, and the academy is gone behind the trees.

Yusra takes your hand.

At once. Without looking at you, without breaking step, as if she's been waiting for the exact moment the last window went out of sight. Her hand is warm from her pocket. She laces her fingers through yours and holds on, and walks on, looking straight ahead.

"That was a good race," she says.

---

"You shouted," you say.

Yusra looks offended. Deeply. She turns her head and gives you a look of great dignity.

"I did not."

You wait.

"Once," she says. Then, after a few more steps, with her eyes on the path: "Maybe twice."

You laugh, and she bumps your shoulder with hers, and doesn't let go of your hand.

---

On the far shore, where the path comes out of the alders, there's an old oak lying by the water.

It came down a long time ago, by the look of it: the trunk is grey and smooth and bare of bark, and its roots stand up in a great dry fan at one end, and the other end runs out into the mere. The top of it is wide and flat enough to sit on, warm in the sun.

You sit on it. Yusra sits beside you and swings the rucksack off and unpacks it: a flask, two tin mugs, a paper packet of sandwiches. Cheese and pickle, cut in triangles. She pours tea and hands you a mug, and the steam goes up straight in the still air.

Across the water, small and far, the academy sits on its slope, with its towers and its windows flashing.

---

She sees the ring.

You've taken your glove off for the mug, and she notices it at once. She goes still. Then she reaches over and lifts your hand, gently, by the fingers, and turns it to the light, so the narrow silver catches the sun, and the fine raised line worked round it.

"Kit's work?" she says.

---

"Yes," you say. "A focus. She made it at the forge. She gave it me last night."

Yusra looks at the ring a long moment. She tilts your hand a little this way, a little that. She looks at the hook, the tiny hair-fine hook worked into the silver, turning inward, round and in, towards your finger.

"The hook turns in," she says.

"So the light comes back to you."

Yusra is quiet. Then she says, "It knows you." Not a question. She lays your hand back on your knee, carefully, as if setting down something that might spill.

---

"Everyone's giving you things," Yusra says.

She says it lightly, looking out at the water. "Rings. Drawings. Bedsheets, probably, before long, knowing Doyle." She lifts her mug. "I haven't got a thing," she says. "Tea."

You look at her.

"Tea is a ward," you say. "You said so. On the fell."

---

Yusra laughs.

Really laughs: her head goes back and it comes right up out of her, a proper, helpless laugh, the first you've ever heard from her that wasn't cut short. It rings off the water. A moorhen bolts out of the reeds.

When she stops, she's still smiling. She looks at you, and her eyes are bright.

Then she sets her mug down on the oak, and leans in, and kisses you.

There on the fallen tree, slowly. Her hand comes up to your jaw and holds it, her thumb at your cheek. Her mouth is warm and tastes of tea.

---

It's longer than on the fell.

Warmer. Less afraid. On the fell, by the cairn, her hands were cold on your face, and the last kiss was quick, and then she was on her feet, as if she couldn't let herself stay. Now she stays. She kisses you slowly and deeply, taking her time, her thumb moving on your cheek, and you kiss her back, your hand finding the front of her coat and holding it.

When at last she pulls back, she doesn't go far. Her forehead stays close to yours.

She's smiling. Properly, all the way, the smile reaching her eyes and staying there.

---

"I slept seven hours last night," Yusra says.

She says it quietly, as if confessing something. "Seven. Three nights now. In a row." She looks at the water. "I haven't done that since the spring."

You wait.

"That's you, I think," Yusra says. Then, being honest, because she is: "Partly."

---

She leans on you.

Her head comes down onto your shoulder, and her weight settles against your side, and she stays there. You put your arm round her. The two of you sit on the old oak with your tea going cold on the bark, looking out at the bright water, and the moorhen comes back, and the light moves slowly across the mere.

You sit until the sun starts down the sky behind the fells, and the shadows of the alders reach out over the water, and the cold comes up off the mere into your boots.

---

You walk back the way you came.

All the way along the far shore, through the alders, her hand in yours. And at the last of the alders, just before the bend where the academy will come into sight, Yusra lets go of your hand.

She does it gently. She squeezes your fingers once first. Then she puts her hand in her pocket, and her shoulders square, and she's your warden again, Yusra Haddad of the top floor, upright and correct, walking a correct distance from you up the path.

But her eyes stay soft. All the way back. Every time you look at her.
