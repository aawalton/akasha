On Thursday you wake to rain.

It's coming down steady on the window of 15, running in long crooked lines down the glass, drumming on the sill outside. The light through Bea's thick curtains is the colour of dishwater. The radiator ticks. Somewhere a gutter is overflowing, a loud uneven splatter onto stone.

And Bea is asleep with her face in your neck.

She's curled into you, all of her, her knees against your thighs, one arm across your waist, her nose pressed under your jaw. Her breath is warm and slow on your throat. Her honey-blonde hair has come out of its bun in the night and is everywhere, across your shoulder, across your mouth, tickling your cheek.

You lie still.

You lie still and listen. To the rain, and to the gutter, and to Bea breathing. To the house waking up round you: a door down the corridor, a tap running, somebody's feet on the stairs. It's warm under the blanket, and it smells of Bea, and you don't want to move at all.

Then Bea wakes.

You feel it before you hear it. Her breathing changes, and her arm tightens round your waist, and her eyelashes move against your neck. She lifts her head an inch. She listens.

And she groans.

A long, deep, heartfelt groan, at the rain, at the window, at the whole wet grey world outside it. She reaches down, grabs the edge of the blanket, and hauls it up over both your heads, so that you're in the dark together in a warm tent that smells of sleep and lavender.

"Five more minutes," she says.

Her voice is thick and muffled. She finds your collarbone in the dark, and kisses it. Once. A soft, sleepy, lingering kiss, just there, on the bone.

And she's asleep again.

Straight back, between one breath and the next. Her head drops onto your shoulder, heavy. Her mouth goes slack against your skin. You lie there under the blanket in the dark, with the rain drumming above you, and you laugh without making a sound, and you don't move for a good deal longer than five minutes.

---

At breakfast the Thornfield kitchen is steamy and loud.

Everyone is in from the rain or about to go out into it: wet coats on every chair back, wellies in a heap by the back door, the windows fogged white. Somebody has made a vast pot of porridge. Bea is eating it with golden syrup, a great deal of golden syrup, and telling Lin about a dream she had in which her oar turned into an eel.

You're halfway through your tea when Yusra comes through.

She has her reading glasses on, pushed up a little, and a fat book under her arm, a finger keeping her place in it. Her hair is pushed back off her face, long and black and wavy, the way it always is. Her badge is at her collar. She's going from the back stairs to the front door, through the middle of everyone, the way she does every morning.

But she looks rested.

You can see it as she comes. Her face has its colour. The shadows under her eyes are only shadows. She walks the way she walked in the first week, before you knew anything: straight-backed and quick and sure.

She passes behind your chair.

"Slept again," she says.

Low. Only to you. Without stopping, without even looking down; just the words, dropped as she goes by, like a coin into your hand.

And she's gone, out through the hall, and the front door opens and shuts on the sound of the rain.

You grin into your tea.

You can't help it. You duck your head over the mug and grin into it, and the steam comes up warm on your face, and when you look up Bea is watching you over her porridge with one eyebrow raised, and you grin at her too.

---

At ten it's History of Magic, in the Long Room.

The rain is loud on the long windows. The room smells of wet wool and old radiators, and the lamps are on, though it's morning. You sit near the back, with Kit on one side of you and Lin on the other. Amara is two rows ahead, very upright, her braids up in a high ponytail, her pen already moving.

The lecturer comes in, and sets down her notes, and goes to the board.

She chalks a date on it. Big, in the middle.

*1858.*

"The Healers Act," she says.

Her voice, like a dry leaf, carries anyway, the way it always does; a dry, papery voice that somehow reaches the back of the room without ever getting louder.

"The year," she says, "in which a healer first had to pass an examination in order to be licensed."

She taps the date with the chalk.

"Before it," she says, "anyone with a warm hand and a story could set up as a healer. Anyone at all. A wise woman in a village. A man with a cart and a bottle and a good line in talk. A widow who'd once eased her husband's cough." She looks out at the room. "Some of them were very good. Most of them were not. And there was no way at all to tell them apart, until it was too late."

She lets that sit.

"Many people," she says, "died of it."

The rain drums on the windows.

Beside you, Kit is writing.

She's writing down every word. Not her usual sharp shorthand, three words to a line and a dry remark in the margin: every word, in full, fast and small and neat, her silver ring flashing as her hand goes. She doesn't look up. She doesn't murmur a single joke.

When the lecturer turns to the board again, Kit leans an inch towards you.

"My grandmother used to tell me about them," she whispers. "The old unlicensed ones. Stories her own grandmother told her, by the fire."

Then she's writing again.

Two rows ahead, Amara's hand goes up.

"Who set the first examination?" she asks. Crisp, and clear, the way she asks everything.

The lecturer turns from the board, and almost smiles.

"The colleges," she says. "Together." A dry pause. "And they argued."

A ripple of laughter goes along the rows. Amara writes it down.

You write the date down.

*1858. The Healers Act.* You look at it on the page, and you think of Yusra. Yusra in the library at midnight, with four books open round her, propped against each other. Her reading glasses. Her neat notes. The tea gone cold at her elbow. You think of her on her nights in the infirmary, and her days among the books, and the thing she's working for, all of it, hour after hour, every hour she has.

You find you've written the date down twice.

*1858. 1858.* One under the other.

You leave them both.

---

After, in the corridor, Kit falls into step beside you.

The corridor is crowded and noisy, full of girls shaking out umbrellas and complaining about the rain. Kit walks close to you, her bag over her shoulder, her notebook still in her hand, and she's frowning at it.

"It was the first time," she says.

"What was?"

"The Act." She taps her notebook. "It was the first time the colleges had to prove anything. To anyone. Before that, they just were. They were the colleges, and that was enough."

She shuts the notebook.

"Before that," Kit says, "it was just who your grandmother was."

She says it lightly. Dry, the way she says things. But she isn't smiling.

"After," she says, "it was what you could do."

You look at her. At her pale sharp face, and the black bob, and the fringe, and her hand tight on the strap of her bag. You think of the Practice Hall at dusk, and a glim held to eight. Then ten. Then fifteen. You think of a middle bench, and a ward round a flame, dead still, with every eye in the room on it.

"Which do you like better?" you ask.

Kit is quiet for a few steps.

"After," she says.

---

At lunch the rain is still going, and the dining hall smells of soup.

Amara eats fast. Faster than you've ever seen her eat: a bowl of soup and a roll, gone in four minutes, and then she's on her feet, her gold watch flashing as she checks it, smoothing down her fitted jumper.

"Debating," she says, to the table. "Motion's ridiculous. I'm going to win anyway."

And Priya stands up too.

She gathers her tote of notebooks, and puts her glasses straight, and pushes her chair in.

"I'm going to watch," Priya says. "To watch," she says again, to the table at large, quite firmly, and follows Amara out.

The table watches them go.

Then Bea raises her eyebrows at you.

Across the table, very slowly, both of them, right up to her fringe, with her spoon halfway to her mouth.

You raise yours back.

Beside you, Lin ducks her head over her soup, and hides a smile behind her hand. You can see it anyway. You can see it in her ears, going pink.

Then Shiv comes in.

She drops into Priya's empty seat with a plate of soup and a hunk of bread, and her hair is wet. Soaking. Her strawberry-blonde gone dark and dripping, flat to her head, from the dawn swim, a wet patch spreading on the shoulders of her grey hoodie. She smells of the mere: cold, and waterweed, and stone.

"You didn't dry it," Bea says, scandalised.

"It's raining," says Shiv. "What's the point." She tears off a bit of bread and dunks it.

She eats for a while. Then she looks at you, across the soup.

"Sunday," Shiv says. "The rock." She chews. "You're still coming?"

"Yes," you say. "Rain or not."

Shiv looks at you a moment longer.

Then she nods. Once. And goes back to her soup, and you can see she's pleased: it's in the set of her shoulders, and the way she dunks the next bit of bread.

---

In the afternoon you take your essay to the Snug.

It's up a short stair in the old library, a little panelled room tucked away above the stacks, where hardly anyone comes. Oak panels, dark with age. An iron grate with a fire laid in it, and someone has lit it, so it's burning low and orange. Two green sofas, sagging and soft, their cushions worn shiny. A rug. The rain on the one small window, and the fire, and the quiet.

You curl up on the end of one of the green sofas, nearest the grate, with your knees up and your essay on them.

You read it over once more.

All three pages. Slowly, from the beginning. The bandage, and the shell round a flame, and the wetsuit, and the air a flame needs. The hurt kept in while it mends. It's all there. It reads better than you thought it would. You change one word in the second paragraph, and then change it back.

Footsteps on the short stair.

Kit comes up into the Snug with two mugs of tea, one in each hand, steaming.

She doesn't say anything. She hands you one, and sits down on the other end of the sofa, and tucks her feet up under her. Black tights, black skirt, a black jumper with the sleeves pulled down over her hands, round her mug.

"Your essay," she says. Holding out her free hand.

You give it to her.

She reads it.

She reads it the way she reads everything: fast, and very still, her grey-green eyes going along the lines. Her feet tucked under her. Her tea going cold on the arm of the sofa. The fire ticks and settles. The rain goes on at the window. She turns a page. Then the last page.

When she's finished she doesn't say anything.

She sits with the three pages on her lap, and looks at the fire, and she's quiet. For a while. Long enough that you start to wonder.

Then:

"*The hurt kept in while it mends*," Kit says.

She says it slowly, as if she's tasting it.

"That's good, Nala." She looks up at you. "That's really good."

There's no edge on it at all. She means it, plainly, all the way through.

You feel your face go warm.

"Can I read yours?" you ask.

Kit goes still.

Then she pulls a few folded sheets out of her bag, and holds them a moment, and gives them to you without looking at you.

It's about a door.

The warded door of her grandmother's house. A big old door, oak, iron-studded, with a ward on it older than anyone living, worn so deep into the wood you can feel it with your fingers. Kit writes about being small and running her hand over it. About how it kept out the cold and the damp and the whole of the world. About how it kept in her grandmother's kitchen, and the smell of bread, and the dog asleep by the range. About how it was the only door in the family that she ever wanted to be on the inside of.

Kit, at the other end of the sofa, is blushing.

You can see it. She's staring very hard at the fire, and the pink is creeping up her throat, and into her face.

You read it twice.

Then you put it down on your knee.

"It's the best thing you've written," you tell her.

Kit's ears go scarlet.

Scarlet again, right to the tips, the way they did in the Practice Hall. She looks at the fire. She looks at her tea. She doesn't look at you.

"It's all right," she says.

"It's the best thing you've written, Kit."

She doesn't answer that.

But after a moment she shifts on the sofa. She unfolds her legs, and moves along, closer, and leans her shoulder into yours. There on the green sofa, in the empty Snug. Her shoulder against your shoulder, her arm against your arm, warm through her black jumper.

And she leaves it there.

The fire burns low. The rain runs down the window. You sit, the two of you, shoulder to shoulder, with your essays on your knees.

Then you turn your head, and kiss her.

Softly. Once. By the fire.

Her mouth is warm from the tea. For a heartbeat she's very still; and then she kisses you back, gently, her cold fingers coming up to touch your jaw.

And she laughs.

Against your mouth, low and surprised, a small breath of a laugh.

"Rain," she says.

---

At five you go back to Thornfield, and find Yusra in the kitchen.

She's at the counter by the window, alone, making a sandwich. Brown bread laid out on a board, a block of cheese, a jar of chutney, a knife. Her bag on the chair beside her, packed for the night. It's Thursday; the infirmary.

She looks up as you come in.

You sit on the edge of the table, near her, and you tell her about the lecture.

About the date on the board. 1858. The Healers Act. The first examination, the first time a healer had to prove she could do it before anyone would let her. About the warm hands and the stories, and the people who died of them. About Amara's question, and the colleges, together, arguing.

Yusra stops buttering.

The knife stops, halfway across the bread. She stands very still, with it in her hand.

"That's mine," she says.

She looks at the bread, not at you.

"The exam I'm working for." Her voice is low. "That's the one. The colleges still set it. Then I'm licensed."

She puts the knife down.

You remember the infirmary, and your nosebleed. A girl with a light in her hand checking your eyes, and telling you about her father the doctor and her mother the midwife. About what she wanted.

"Hands that make things better," you say.

Yusra looks at you.

Straight at you, sideways, from the counter. Her very dark eyes. For a long moment she just looks.

"You remember everything," she says.

"So do you," you say.

Something in Yusra's face goes soft.

You see it happen. Something round her mouth and her eyes, letting go, just for a moment. The warden's face going, and another one under it.

Then she looks back down at the bread, and picks up the knife, and goes on buttering.

---

At seven it's life drawing, in the Drawing Room.

The rain is louder here than anywhere. The skylights overhead are black, streaming with water, the rain hammering on the glass above you like fingers on a drum. Lamps stand round the room in place of them, tall ones, throwing warm pools of light onto the floor and the easels and the dais in the middle.

The model tonight is a second-year from Ashcombe.

She's tall and quiet, with her hair in a long plait, and she's clothed: a long grey shift, plain linen, down to her ankles. She sits on a stool on the dais, one foot on the rung, her hands loose in her lap, and she's very calm. She looks at a point somewhere above the door, and doesn't move.

You sit beside Lin, with a drawing board on your knee and a stick of charcoal in your fingers.

It's the first time you've drawn.

The last time you were in this room, you were on the dais. Now you're on a chair with a board, and the charcoal is black and light and strange in your hand, and you don't know where to start.

You start.

Your first drawing is terrible.

You know it's terrible while you're doing it. The head is too big. The shoulders are a coat hanger. The arms come out of the wrong places, and the hands are two bunches of sausages, and the plait looks like a rope tied round her neck. You stare at it.

Lin leans over and looks at it.

And laughs.

Kindly, softly, under her breath. Not at you; with you, a little warm bubble of a laugh, her shoulder against yours.

Then she reaches over, and puts her ink-stained fingers round your wrist, and moves it. Loosens it. Lifts your hand off the paper a little, so only the charcoal touches.

"Look more," Lin says, low. "Draw less."

She keeps her fingers on your wrist.

"Look at her," she says. "Then let your hand go."

You try again.

You look. You look at the model for a long time, the way Lin looks at things, at the line of her neck going into her shoulder, at the way the grey linen falls from her knee. Then you let your hand go.

The second drawing is less terrible.

The head is the right size. The shoulders slope. The hands are still sausages, but there are fewer of them.

The third one has a shoulder in it.

Her left shoulder, the one nearest the lamp, where the linen pulls across it. It's nearly right. It isn't right, but it's nearly right; you can see the bone under the cloth, and the weight of the arm hanging from it. You look at it, and something in your chest lifts.

Lin's drawings, beside you, are quick and sure.

She draws the way she always does: her hand never still, the charcoal flicking across the paper, a whole figure in a dozen lines, then another, then another. But she's lighter tonight. You can see it. Her mouth is soft. Her shoulders are down. Once, between drawings, she hums something under her breath, and catches herself, and goes pink, and keeps drawing.

---

After, in the corridor, Lin stops by the window.

The corridor's emptying. Girls going past with boards under their arms and charcoal on their faces, out into the rain. Lin waits till they've gone. Then she opens her bag.

She takes out an envelope.

A sealed envelope, white, square. Addressed in ink, in her small clean hand, to her mother, at an address in Manchester. There's a stamp on it already. Straight, in the corner, exactly square.

"I copied it out," she says.

She's looking at the envelope, not at you.

"Last night. Every word." She swallows. "In English and in Cantonese. Both. So she can read whichever she wants."

She holds it in both hands, in front of her.

"Will you come with me," Lin says, "to post it?"

She looks up.

"Now?" she says. "Before I can think."

---

You walk with her through the rain.

Across the quad, under one umbrella: yours, the cheap black one, too small for two. You hold it over both of you, and Lin walks close against your side, with the envelope inside her coat, held flat against her chest with one hand. The rain hammers on the umbrella. The quad paths are running with water, and the lamps along them are blurred haloes in the wet, and your shoes are soaked within twenty steps.

The post box is by the porters' lodge.

An old red pillar box, with a little roof on it, shining wet under the lodge lamp. The slot is a dark line in it.

Lin stops in front of it.

She takes the envelope out of her coat. And she stands there, holding it, in front of the slot.

For a long moment.

The rain drums on the umbrella over you both. Water runs off its edge in a ring all round you. Lin holds the envelope in front of the slot, an inch away, and doesn't move. Her hand is shaking. You can see the envelope trembling in it, the corner of it quivering in the light from the lodge.

You take her other hand.

Her free one, hanging cold at her side. You take it in yours and hold it.

Lin breathes out.

A long breath, all the way out. The way you breathe out on the rock before you jump. The way Kit breathed out at the test.

And she puts the letter in the slot.

And lets go.

It drops.

You hear it. A small soft sound, inside the box, paper on paper.

"Oh," Lin says.

She stares at the slot.

"Oh," she says again. "I've done it."

And she starts to laugh. And to cry, at the same time, both at once, her face crumpling and her mouth laughing, tears and rain on her cheeks together. She laughs, and sobs, and laughs, standing there in front of the post box with her hand still up where the letter was.

You put your arm round her.

You hold her, there under the umbrella, by the porters' lodge, in the rain. She turns into you and puts her face in your coat and laughs into it, and cries into it, shaking, her hands fisted in the front of it, until she's done. It takes a while. You hold the umbrella over both of you, and the rain comes down all round, and you hold her till it's over.

When she lifts her face, it's wet, and blotched, and shining.

"Thank you," she says.

Her voice is rough.

"For holding my hand." She swallows. "I couldn't have. Not on my own."

She goes up on her toes, and kisses your cheek.

Wet. Rain and tears and her mouth, warm in the middle of it.

---

Back at Thornfield, at half past nine, you fill the flask again.

The old tartan thermos with the dented lid. You wash it out, and make the tea strong, and put three sugars in it. Sweet. You screw the lid on tight.

You wait at the foot of the stairs, with the flask held in both hands against your chest.

At quarter to ten Yusra comes down.

Coat buttoned. Badge at her collar. Bag over her arm. Her face composed for the night.

She sees the flask in your hands.

And she stops. On the stair, three steps up, one hand on the banister.

She looks at the flask, and then at you.

"You'll spoil me," she says.

"Good," you say.

Yusra comes down the last three steps.

She takes the flask. And her fingers rest on yours again, over the backs of your hands, on the dented tartan. Like Tuesday. Warm. A moment longer than they need to.

She doesn't let go.

"Saturday," she says.

Low.

"I'm off. All day." She's looking at the flask, at your hands under hers. "If you'd walk with me." A pause. "Up the fell. In the morning."

You think of Saturday.

Bea trains at seven on Saturdays. Seven, in the rain or not, out of the boathouse, in her four.

"Yes," you say. "After I've waved Bea off on the water."

Yusra nods. Once.

"Nine, then," she says. "At the front door."

She almost smiles.

Then she takes her hand from yours, and tucks the flask into her bag, and goes out of the front door into the rain.

---

In 15, Bea is sitting up in bed with her rowing book.

Propped on her knees, all diagrams of boats, held carefully by the edges because of her blisters. She looks at you over the top of it as you come in.

She looks at you for a long moment.

"You've got a face on," she says. "A good one."

You get undressed, and into your nightshirt, and you tell her.

You tell her about Lin. The envelope, addressed in ink. The post box in the rain. The letter in the slot. The small soft sound it made.

Bea whoops.

Out loud, a proper whoop, flinging her arms up so the rowing book goes flying off the end of the bed.

"She *did* it," she says. "Lin did it!"

You get in beside her.

And you tell her the other thing. That Yusra asked you to walk on Saturday. Up the fell, in the morning, on her day off.

Bea goes quiet.

She looks at you. A long moment, by the light of the lamp. Her warm brown eyes on your face, steady, the way she looks at the water before she puts the oar in.

Then she smiles.

"She's lonely," Bea says. "I've seen it."

She reaches over, and tucks your hair behind your ear.

"Go," she says. "Just come and watch me row first."

"I promise," you say.

Bea leans out of bed, and fishes her rowing book off the floor, and puts it on the bedside table.

Then she pulls you in.

She pulls you down against her, under the blanket, your head on her chest, her arms round you. She's warm, and she smells of lavender and blister cream. The rain is still going on the window.

You lie with your ear over her heart. You can hear it going, steady and strong, the way she rows: in, and out, and in.

You fall asleep to it.
