On Saturday you sleep through half past six.

You sleep straight through it, deep and dreamless, and so, it turns out, does Bea, because there is no knock. The radiator knocks instead, now and then, the only sound. The light comes up grey and then gold over the mere, and climbs the wall, and finds the bed, and still you sleep.

You wake at ten.

Your mouth is dry as paper. Your head is soft: not aching exactly, just woolly and tender, as if someone has packed it with cotton and then left it out in the sun. You lie and blink at the ceiling and remember the cider, and the fiddles, and the mile of dark shore at midnight, and Bea spinning you round in front of the fire. Your feet hurt. You're smiling.

The sun is full on the mere. When you sit up and look out of the window it's blazing, the whole long water bright as foil under a high blue sky, the fells sharp and gold, not a cloud anywhere. You put your hand over your eyes.

Something hammers on your door.

"Nala!" Bea. Hoarse, urgent, and quite clearly also only just awake. "*Nala*! Brunch stops at *eleven*! It's ten past ten! And then it's *Kendal*, everyone goes to Kendal on Saturdays, there's a bus, there's a *market*, get *up*!"

"I'm up," you croak.

"You're *not*, I can hear you lying down."

You get up.

---

Brunch in the Great Hall is enormous and slow and exactly what you need. The tall windows are full of sun. The hatch at the end is dishing out eggs and bacon and sausages and fried bread and grilled tomatoes, and down the middle of every table run racks of toast, mountains of it, refilled as fast as it goes, with pats of butter and pots of marmalade. Half the hall looks like you feel. Girls in hoodies with their hair scraped back, girls in sunglasses indoors, girls with their heads down on their folded arms between mouthfuls.

You and Bea get plates piled high and sit down near the window, and you've barely started when Lin slides onto the bench opposite. She's in a soft grey jumper with her hair in its low ponytail, and there are shadows under her eyes, and she gives you a small, shy, bleary smile.

"Morning," she says, very quietly, as if loud noises might break something.

Then Priya arrives, in a pinafore the colour of a tangerine, with her curly hair knotted up anyhow and a pencil through it and her glasses slightly crooked, and puts down a plate with four slices of toast on it and only toast.

"I had *one* cider," she says, aggrieved, to the table at large. "One. Amara kept buying me cider. I don't think Amara drinks cider. I think Amara watches other people drink cider."

Bea laughs so hard she has to put down her fork.

You eat everything on your plate. It's the best food you've ever had.

---

Back in room 14, getting ready, you go through the pockets of your coat for your key and find your purse.

You'd half forgotten it existed. It's a small cheap purse, brown, a bit scuffed, and you sit on the bed and open it properly for the first time. A bank card, blue, with your name raised on it: MISS N ASHBY. A library card for Hollowmere Academy with a little photograph on it of a startled freckled girl with red hair, a photograph taken by someone, somewhere, before you were here. And notes, folded in the back.

You take them out and count them. A twenty, two tens, a five, and some coins, and another twenty folded small behind the others.

Sixty-two pounds.

You count it twice, carefully, sitting on the edge of the narrow bed with the coins in your palm. Sixty-two pounds. And it's yours. Not borrowed, not lent, not a fiver slapped down on a charity shop counter for you. Yours: in your purse, with your name on the card.

It's such a small thing. You know it's a small thing. But you sit there holding it, and it feels like the first thing in this whole place that's truly, simply yours, and your throat goes tight, and you have to put the money back in the purse quickly and stand up and go and find your shoes.

---

The bus to Kendal goes from the village, and it's packed with Hollowmere girls.

Every seat. And the aisle too, girls standing and swaying and holding on to the rails, in coats and scarves and sunglasses, gowns left behind, everyone talking at once. You and Bea squeeze into a seat halfway down, you by the window, Bea's hip jammed against yours. Lin and Priya get the seat behind, by some miracle, and the bus lurches off along the shore road with a roar.

Forty minutes. Out of the valley, up over the shoulder of the fells and down again. Stone walls running up the hillsides in long grey lines like seams. Sheep, hundreds of them, white dots on the green, and some of them right by the road, staring at the bus with their mad yellow eyes. Little farms. A waterfall coming down a black cleft in the rock. The sun on all of it. You lean your head against the window glass and watch it go by, and Bea talks, and you listen, half of you watching the fells.

Somewhere in the middle of a long green valley Priya leans over the back of your seat. Her arms fold on the top of it, and her chin goes on her arms, between your head and Bea's.

"Nala," she says. "Can I ask you something?"

"Course."

"How did you know?" Her big dark eyes behind the tortoiseshell glasses are curious and warm. There's nothing nosy in it; she sounds exactly the way she sounded in Penhallow's study asking if intent could be measured. "That you liked girls. I mean, how did you find out? Was there a moment?"

You think about it.

It's a strange question, for you. You think back, all the way back, to being Alan at twelve, at fifteen, at twenty; to every girl you ever looked at and wanted. To the morning you woke in this body and looked at Bea laughing in the kitchen and felt it, exactly the same, as if it had come with you through whatever door you came through.

"I didn't really find out," you say. "It was never something I had to find out. It was just there. Always. Like my hair." You touch the end of your red plait. "I never had to wonder about it. It was just a thing that was true."

Priya considers this. She tilts her head.

"I've only ever gone out with boys," she says. "Three. Well, two and Dev. And Dev's lovely. I've never once thought hard about girls. Not once. Not even a little bit." She frowns, slightly, at the back of your seat. "Isn't that odd?"

"Is it?" says Bea.

"I think hard about *everything*," says Priya. "I thought hard about whether to have toast or a croissant this morning for nearly five minutes. I've thought hard about particle physics and the Krebs cycle and why cats knead blankets. And I've never once thought hard about that." She sits back in her seat, slowly, her eyes going distant and thoughtful behind the glasses, looking out of the window at the sheep. "Huh," she says, to herself.

Beside her, Lin looks at you over the seat back. Just for a second. Then she looks out of the window too.

---

Kendal is grey stone. Grey stone houses, grey stone churches, grey stone walls and steps and alleys, all of it glowing faintly gold in the sun, crowded together along a river under the hills. And in the middle of it, a square, and the square is full of the Saturday market.

Stalls, under striped awnings, packed in rows so close you have to go sideways between them. Fruit, piled in glossy heaps: apples, red and green and russet, plums, pears, blackberries in little punnets. Cheese, great wheels and wedges of it, a smell you can follow by your nose from the far side of the square. Wool, in heaps and skeins and balls of every colour, soft and oily and smelling of sheep. Boxes of secondhand books on trestle tables, spines out, three for a pound. Jars of honey. Pies. Flowers. And at the far end, under a dark green awning, a stall with a hand-painted sign that says CHARMS — HOUSEHOLD & GARDEN — LICENSED.

Priya sees it and is gone.

You lose her completely. One moment she's beside you and the next she's at the charms stall, her tote bag bumping on her hip, leaning over the trays of little paper packets and brass tokens and bundles of knotted thread, picking things up and turning them over. By the time you catch up she's quizzing the stallholder, a round grey-haired woman in a waxed jacket, about every single one. "But how does this one *know* it's frost and not just cold? Is there a threshold? Is it the *rate* of change?" And the stallholder, who looked wary at first, is now leaning on her trestle and explaining with both hands, delighted.

Lin finds a box.

It's under a table of old books, a battered shoebox, and it's full of nibs. Old steel nibs, hundreds of them, loose and rattling, and pen holders of wood and bone and black plastic, some of them very old. Lin crouches beside it in the crowd, oblivious, and starts going through it, one at a time. She holds each nib up to the light and turns it in her inky fingers and looks at the tip, and puts most of them back, and keeps a few in her palm. Her face is the face from the charity shop: open and absorbed and completely happy.

Bea buys a bag of apples, big red ones, and eats one as you walk, crunching loudly, and offers it round. "Bite," she says to you, holding it out. You take a bite. It's cold and sharp and sweet. "Bite," she says to Lin, crouched by the shoebox. Lin takes a tiny bite, without looking up. "Bite," Bea says to Priya, and Priya takes one absently, mid-question, and goes on asking.

At a stationery stall on the edge of the market you see a pen.

It's a fountain pen. Not an expensive one; a cheap black one with a silver clip, in a cardboard box with a cellophane window, with a little pack of ink cartridges taped to the side. Four pounds fifty. You pick it up and look at it, and think of the cream paper and the essay card in your coat pocket. *Where does intent come from?*

You buy it. With your own money, out of your own purse: four pounds fifty out of the sixty-two. The stallholder puts it in a paper bag and hands it to you, and you say thank you, and walk away holding the paper bag in both hands.

It's the first thing you've bought. The first thing, in this whole life. You feel absurdly, ridiculously proud. You keep putting your hand in your coat pocket to touch it.

---

Off the square, down a narrow side street, there's a shop with a bow window and gold lettering, and Priya stops dead outside it.

*F. Harrowby & Daughters*, says the lettering. *Licensed Charm-Makers. Est. 1861.*

Inside it's dim and hushed and full of small sounds: ticking, and a soft chiming, and something whirring. Brass bells hang from the ceiling on cords, dozens of them, and they ring very softly as the door opens and closes, each one a different note. There are glass cases everywhere, lit from inside, full of things you can't name: rings and lockets and little brass boxes, and pens, and spectacles with odd lenses, and something like a compass with no north. Behind the counter, over the till, there's a framed certificate on the wall, cream and crimson and gold, with a seal on it and a lot of copperplate writing. A licence.

Priya goes straight to one case at the back and presses her nose against the glass.

You go and look. The case is full of clockwork. Tiny charmed machines: a brass bird that preens its wing, over and over, without any key you can see; a little mill with a wheel turning in no water; a beetle of green enamel and gold that walks slowly round in a circle on a mirror and stops and turns and walks the other way. A silver orrery, planets the size of peas, all of them turning, very slowly, round a sun that glows.

Priya's breath fogs the glass. Her whole face has lit up from inside. Not curious, now: hungry. Her eyes are going from one machine to the next and back, and her lips are moving slightly, and her hands are pressed flat on the case on either side of her face.

"That's what I want to do," she says. Barely a whisper. "That. Exactly that."

You've never seen anyone want something so much.

---

The bookshop has a café upstairs, and Bea leads the way.

The shop is old and crooked and creaking, with books on every wall and up a narrow wooden staircase, and at the top there's a low room under the eaves with mismatched tables and armchairs, and windows looking out over the grey roofs of the town to the fells, and a counter with cakes under glass domes. It smells of coffee and old paper. It's crowded and warm.

And Kit is there.

She's at a corner table by the window, alone, with a pot of tea and a cup and a slim book open in front of her. She's in black, with her coat over the back of her chair. She's reading. You see the book as you squeeze past her table on the way to a free one, because it's open face-up and you can't help seeing it: a slim hardback, a pale blue cover, and on the cover, in plain italic letters, a name and the word *Poems*.

Kit sees you see it. She snaps it shut. Fast, with a sharp little clap.

"It's for a friend," she says.

You look at her. At the shut book under her pale hand, and the silver ring, and her chin up.

"Course it is," you say.

Kit's ears go pink.

They go pink right to the tips, under the edges of her black bob, and she looks very hard out of the window at the roofs. And behind you, Bea, who has just reached the top of the stairs, sees her, and lights up, and waves. A big enthusiastic wave, her whole arm, the apple bag swinging.

"*Kit!* Hi!"

Kit looks at Bea. Then she nods. A small, stiff, precise nod, as if she isn't sure what to do with a wave like that, and a nod is the nearest thing she has.

---

You find a table, the four of you, squeezed into a corner under the sloping ceiling. And you go up to the counter, and buy Bea a hot chocolate with cream on top and a scone with jam and clotted cream, and carry them back to her, and set them down in front of her.

"There," you say. "A down payment. On the scones."

Bea looks at the scone. She looks up at you.

"The *scones*," she says.

"I owe you scones. For the jumper. That's the first one."

Bea's whole face goes bright. She scrambles up out of her chair, right there in the crowded café, and throws her arms round you across the corner of the table, a huge tight hug, and as she does her elbow catches the sugar bowl.

It goes flying. It sails off the edge of the table in a glittering arc and lands on the floor and bursts, sugar everywhere, across the floorboards and under the next table and over Priya's shoes.

"Oh *no*," says Bea, still hugging you.

Priya looks at her sugary shoes and starts to laugh. Lin covers her mouth with both hands. You're laughing so hard you have to hold on to Bea to stay standing, and Bea's laughing into your shoulder, and the woman behind the counter sighs and comes over with a dustpan, and your table is the loudest in the room, and you don't care at all.

When you sit down again, still laughing, you look up, and across the café you see Kit.

She's watching. Over the top of her book, open again, her eyes are on your table. On the four of you, squashed together under the eaves, with sugar on the floor and Bea's cream all over her nose and Priya still giggling. Her face isn't cold. It's very still, and very quiet, and there's something in it you can't quite name. Then she sees you looking, and her eyes drop back to the page.

---

On the way out, you stop at her table.

You don't plan it. Your feet just stop there, with the others clattering on ahead down the stairs. Kit looks up at you, guarded.

"We're all going to the cinema," you say. "Tonight. At seven. The four of us. There's a film on."

Kit looks at you a moment.

"I've got reading," she says.

"Come and bite us in the dark," says Bea, from behind you. She's come back up the stairs to see where you went, and she's leaning in the doorway with her apple bag and sugar still on her jumper. "Go on. You can be rude about the costumes. You'd be *brilliant* at being rude about the costumes."

Kit stares at Bea.

She just stares at her, for a long moment, as if Bea is a language she's never heard spoken. Bea grins back at her, dimples and all, completely unbothered.

And then Kit says, "Fine."

She says it flatly. She looks, for a second, as surprised as anyone that she's said it. Bea's mouth falls open. Then Bea whoops, actually whoops, in the hushed bookshop café, and Kit closes her eyes briefly in pain.

---

The afternoon goes slow and golden. You wander, the four of you, and then, when Kit has paid for her tea and come down the stairs with her book under her arm, the five of you. Through the narrow stone ginnels between the houses, so narrow you can touch both walls, with steps going up and down and little doors and windows and cats. Down to the river, wide and brown and fast, with ducks fighting in the shallows and a stone bridge arching over it. Along the bank in the long low sun.

Kit walks beside you. She barely speaks. Bea talks for all five of you, and Priya asks questions, and Lin walks quietly at your other side. But Kit stays at your elbow the whole way, matching your pace, her hands in her coat pockets.

Only once does she speak. On the bridge, she stops, and touches your sleeve, and points upward.

High on the side of the church by the river there's a gargoyle. A stone face, jutting out under the gutter, weathered and stained green. It's long and lean, with a sharp chin and deep-set eyes, and its mouth is open in what looks like an expression of intense and slightly exasperated interest, as if it's just heard someone give a very wrong answer.

"Penhallow," says Kit.

You look at it. You look at Kit. Kit's face is perfectly straight.

You laugh so hard you have to sit down on the parapet of the bridge.

---

The cinema is small and old and smells of popcorn and dust. Inside, the screen is framed in faded gilt, and the seats are red velvet, worn shiny on the arms, and creak when you sit in them. The film is a costume drama. It is about a woman in a great many large dresses who loves a man she cannot have, and it is very long.

You sit in a row near the back. Bea on one side of you. Lin on the other. Kit beyond Lin, very straight in her seat, her coat folded on her knees. And Priya at the end, with a vast bucket of popcorn that she passes up and down the row.

The lights go down.

You're watching the woman in the dresses having an argument in a garden when, partway through, in the dark, something touches your hand.

It's on the armrest between your seat and Lin's. Your hand is lying there. And Lin's hand comes, slowly, carefully, and finds it. Her fingers slide over the back of yours, and stop, and then turn, and slip between yours, and hold.

You don't look at her. You keep your eyes on the screen. But your heart is going so hard you're sure she can feel it in your fingers.

Lin's hand is small and cool. It stays.

On the screen the woman in the dresses gets a letter, and reads it, and the music swells, and she sinks down onto a staircase and weeps. And on your other side, Bea sniffs. Loudly. Then again. And her head comes over and lands on your shoulder, and she leans on you, and sniffs into your coat.

"It's so *sad*," she whispers thickly.

"I know," you whisper back.

You sit there in the dark with Lin's hand in yours and Bea's head on your shoulder, and the film flickering, and you can't follow the plot at all.

Down the row, in the light off the screen, you can see Kit. Her face is lit silver and blue, profile clean against the dark. And she's smiling. On the screen, a minor character, an old aunt in an enormous cap, has just said something sour and dry about the hero's waistcoat, and the rest of the cinema sits silent through it. But Kit is smiling at it. A real smile, small and private and wicked, the corner of her mouth curled up, her eyes bright on the screen.

You watch her smile, instead of the film, for a long moment.

---

The film runs late.

It's quarter to ten when the lights come up, and the last bus back is at ten, and the bus stop is the other side of the town. You all run. Through the dark streets, under the lamps, past the closed market stalls, Bea in front shouting directions, Priya with her popcorn bucket still in her arms, spilling it, Lin with her scarf flying, Kit running in silence with her coat flapping and her book clutched to her chest.

You make it. The bus is still there, engine running, the driver leaning on the wheel, and you all pile on, gasping, and fall into seats.

The bus is dark inside, just a dim light at the front, and it's half empty: everyone else caught the early ones. Bea and Priya collapse into a seat together near the front and start arguing, breathlessly, about the ending. Lin sits across the aisle.

And Kit sits down beside you.

The bus pulls out. The town falls away behind, and the road climbs up into the black fells, and outside the windows it's dark, completely dark, just the shapes of the hills against the stars. The engine rumbles under you. Kit sits very straight, her book on her knees.

For a long time she's silent.

Then, quietly, so quietly you almost miss it under the engine, looking straight ahead at the back of the seat in front:

"It's years since I saw a film with anyone."

You look at her. In the dark of the bus you can just see her profile, pale, very still.

"It won't be years till the next time," you say.

Kit doesn't answer at once. She looks down at her hands, folded on the pale blue book in her lap, the silver ring.

"Perhaps," she says.

---

Off the bus at the village, the five of you walk the dark mile of shore.

The stars are out again, blazing, as thick as last night. The mere is black and silent beside you, with the stars lying on it. The bracken whispers. Bea walks in front with Priya, still arguing about the ending. Lin walks close on one side of you, and Kit, silent, on the other.

At the college gates, open tonight, lit by the lamp, Kit stops.

"Ashcombe's that way," she says, and nods across the dark quad, to a tall house on the far side with a few lit windows. She looks at the four of you. She hesitates. Then she lifts one hand, and gives a small stiff wave, as if she's not completely sure how it's done.

Bea, ahead, turns round and waves back with both arms over her head, wildly, the whole of her.

Kit turns and walks away across the dark quad, very fast.

---

Back in room 14, you sit at the desk with the lamp on.

You take the new pen out of its cardboard box and fit a cartridge into it, the way the instructions say, and wait for the ink to come down. You put the card from Penhallow in front of you, propped against the wall. *Where does intent come from?* You put a sheet of cream paper beside it.

And you write.

Slowly, with the new pen, holding it loose the way Lin taught you. It's about the thread: the thin bright thread from your chest out to the light, and how you have to hold on to it. It's about the mere: the black water closing over you, and the cold knocking every thought out of your head, and coming up, and wanting air more than you've ever wanted anything. And it's about wanting light the way you wanted air. About how maybe intent doesn't come from your head at all, but from somewhere lower and deeper and older than thinking. From wherever the wanting lives.

You write one paragraph. You read it back.

And this time you keep every word.
