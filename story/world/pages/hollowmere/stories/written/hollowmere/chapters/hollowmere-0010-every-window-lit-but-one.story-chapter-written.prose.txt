On Wednesday you wake clear-headed.

The hammer is gone. You lie still in the grey dark for a moment, testing it, waiting for the pounding to start up behind your eyes again, and it doesn't. Your head is light and clean and quiet, like a room someone has aired. You breathe in, and out, and it's just breathing.

Your right hand is closed round something soft. You open it. Kit's black handkerchief, crumpled, the small grey *K* in the corner, with a faint rusty stain on it. You fell asleep holding it.

You get up and wash it in the little sink in the corner of your room, in cold water with a sliver of soap, rubbing at the stain until it's gone. You wring it out and smooth it flat and hang it over the radiator to dry, and the radiator knocks, as if it approves.

Then you go down to the boathouse.

---

It's the cold-water swimmers' first morning, and it's nothing like Shiv's rock.

There's a register. A real one, a cloth-bound book open on a folding table outside the boathouse doors, with a pen on a string. There's a safety boat, one of the old blue rowing boats, pulled up at the edge of the water with two third-years in waterproofs sitting in it holding the oars and a coil of rope and an orange ring. And there's the red-cheeked second-year from the societies fair, in a fleece and a woolly hat, with a whistle on a cord round her neck and a stopwatch in her hand.

Twenty girls, more, are standing about on the shingle in the grey light, in swimsuits and dressing gowns and towelling robes, stamping and hugging themselves and laughing nervously. Steam is rising off the mere in soft wisps.

You sign the register: *N. Ashby*.

Shiv arrives last.

She comes along the shore with her hood up and her hands jammed in the pockets of the parka, scowling. She stops at the folding table and looks at the register, and the pen on its string, and the girls in their dressing gowns, and the safety boat, with an expression of deep suffering. Then she picks up the pen and signs, in her big sprawl, under your name: *Doyle (still reluctant)*.

She looks up and catches your eye, and the scowl cracks, just slightly, at one corner.

The second-year blows the whistle.

It's a long shrill blast, and on it twenty girls drop their robes and wade into the mere off the shingle, all at once, in a crowd, and the shrieking starts. It's tremendous. It goes up off the water and rings off the fells: twenty voices all at once, *oh my god, oh my GOD, it's FREEZING, why did I, oh my god*, splashing and gasping and clutching each other, some of them going straight back out again and some of them standing waist-deep and screaming at the sky.

You and Shiv walk in past them all.

You don't shriek. You wade in up to your thighs and your waist and then you go under, all at once, the way Shiv taught you, and come up breathing out, slow. Beside you Shiv does the same. And then the two of you swim. Out, past the shrieking crowd in the shallows, past the girls bobbing and gasping where it gets deep, out into the open grey water, side by side, your arms coming over, the cold roaring on your skin, all the way out to the orange buoy the second-year said was the limit.

You stop there. You hold on to the buoy, the two of you, and tread water, and look back at the shore: the shingle and the boathouse and the safety boat and twenty small figures shrieking in the shallows, and Thornfield behind them, and the fells going pink.

Shiv is grinning at you. You're grinning back. Neither of you says anything. You don't have to.

On the shingle afterwards, wrapped in your towels, teeth chattering, the second-year clicks her stopwatch and stares at it.

"Four minutes," Shiv says. She sounds, despite herself, a little impressed. She looks at you sideways, water dripping off her chin. "Four minutes. You." She shakes her head. "With a *nanny*."

---

In the Thornfield corridor, back upstairs, Yusra is going door to door with a clipboard.

She's in her neat knit and wool trousers, badge at her collar, her hair pushed back. "Hot water," she's saying to the girl in room 9. "Any trouble this week? Anything cold, anything not coming through? The boiler man's in on Friday, I want a list." She writes something down. She moves on to the next door.

You come up the stairs with your wet hair dripping down your back and your towel round your neck.

She sees you. She's passing you in the corridor on her way to room 11, and she doesn't stop walking, but she looks at you.

"Ashby," she says. "Headache?"

"Gone," you say.

She nods. And her dark eyes stay on you as she passes, one beat longer than they need to. One beat. Steady. Looking at your face, as if she's checking for herself.

Then she knocks on room 11. "Hot water," she says. "Any trouble?"

---

At breakfast Bea is cross with you.

You know it the moment you sit down. She's sitting very straight, with her porridge, and she doesn't bump your shoulder. She looks at you sideways, and her cheeks are pink, and her mouth is set.

"Someone said," says Bea, "that you had a *nosebleed*. In practical. Yesterday. All over the floor."

"It wasn't all over the—"

"And that you went to the *infirmary*. At *eleven o'clock at night*." She puts down her spoon. "To Yusra."

"I knocked on her door first, and she wasn't—"

"You went past *my* door to knock on hers." Bea turns on the bench to face you. Her brown eyes are fierce. "I'm *right there*. I'm across the corridor. I'm *literally six feet* from your door. And you were ill, and you went past me in the middle of the night, and I didn't even know until Freya from room 9 told me at the sink."

"I didn't want to wake you," you say.

"*Knock* next time," Bea says. Fierce and pink, the colour going right up to her hairline. "Even at eleven. *Especially* at eleven. I mean it, Nala."

You look at her. At her fierce pink face, and her hands clenched on the table.

"I promise," you say.

Bea looks at you a moment longer. Then she nods, sharply. And picks her spoon back up. And bumps your shoulder, once, hard.

---

At ten it's Sigils in the Drawing Room, the cold white light coming down through the skylights onto the slanted desks and the ink pots and the stacks of cream paper.

Lin is at the desk beside you. She looks up when you sit, and gives you her small quiet smile, and then her eyes go to your face and stay there a moment, checking. She's heard about the nosebleed too. She doesn't say so. She just looks, until she's satisfied, and then goes back to her ink.

"The warming sigil," says the tutor, drawing it on her easel: the spiral, coiling in, with the short stroke through the centre. "You've all cast it, or tried. Now draw it properly. Ten."

You draw ten.

Lin's are perfect. You can see them out of the corner of your eye, a neat row of them down her page, each spiral coiling in smooth and even, the turns evenly spaced like the grooves on a record. Yours drift. You can see that too. They start out round and then, as they coil in, they tighten, the turns crowding closer and closer together until they're nearly touching at the centre, cramped and squeezed.

Lin leans over. She looks at your page.

"You draw the spiral too tight," she says softly. She points with the end of her pen at the centre of one of yours, where the lines bunch up. "Here. Like you're holding your breath."

You stop.

You are. You realise it as she says it: you've been holding your breath. Every time you draw the spiral in, your chest goes tight, and you stop breathing, and your hand closes in with it. You've been doing it all morning. You've probably been doing it yesterday, with the stone. Pulling the thread tighter and tighter.

You let your breath out. You dip the nib.

Your second copy is looser. You breathe while you draw it, slowly, in and out, and the spiral coils in easy, the turns staying open, all the way to the centre.

The tutor comes past. She stops at your desk, and looks, and taps your second sigil with the end of her pen.

"Better," she says.

---

After lunch it's the free afternoon, and at two o'clock Bea knocks on your door.

She's in her biggest jumper, the cream one that comes down past her knees, with her hair in a fresh bun and her cheeks pink from the cold of the corridor. She leans in the doorway of room 14.

"Village," she says. "Scones." She folds her arms. "You owe me."

---

You walk the shore path, the mile to Hollowmere village.

It's a still afternoon. The mere is flat and silver, a long sheet of it, perfectly calm, the fells reflected in it upside down so that the whole valley seems to go down as far as it goes up. The fells are brown now, the bracken gone rust and bronze, and the trees along the shore are turning, gold and copper. The air is cold and smells of leaves.

Bea talks the whole way. About rowing. About Saturday, and training, and the boat she'll be in, a four, and the girl who'll be stroke, who's apparently terrifying. About the captain, the broad-shouldered third-year from the fair. "She *smiled* at me," Bea says. "When I signed before she'd finished. She stopped talking and *smiled*. Isn't that good? I think that's good. I think she thought I was keen." She goes on. The captain's name, which is Morwenna. Morwenna's arms. Morwenna's opinions on catching a crab.

Halfway along she stops talking.

You notice the quiet. You look at her. She's walking beside you with her eyes on the path ahead, and her face has gone pink, and she doesn't say anything for a few steps.

Then she takes your hand.

She just reaches over and takes it. Her fingers slide into yours, warm, and hold on. She doesn't look at you. She keeps walking.

You keep walking too. You walk the rest of the way to the village hand in hand, along the silver water, neither of you saying a word, Bea's hand warm and tight in yours.

---

The village shop smells of bread. There's a tray just out of the oven at the back, the girl behind the counter says, and you buy two cheese scones and two fruit ones, warm, in a paper bag that goes see-through with butter at the corners. Three pounds twenty. You count it out of your purse into her hand, coin by coin, and Bea watches you do it with her dimples showing.

You eat them on the low wall by the jetty.

The wall is cold stone, and the mere is right below it, lapping softly at the jetty posts. You sit close, the two of you, Bea's shoulder against yours, with the paper bag between your knees. The scones are still warm. The cheese ones are sharp and crumbly. The fruit ones are sweet, with sugar crusted on top. There are crumbs everywhere, down your jumper, in your lap, on the wall. A duck comes and looks at you hopefully.

"So," says Bea. Her mouth is full. She swallows. "What did Yusra say? Last night. At the infirmary."

You look at the water.

You tell her. You tell her about the lamp and the empty ward and the mint draught. About Yusra's mother and father, and their hands. About *I'm your warden. There are lines I keep. You should know that.* And about the door, at the end, and *Goodnight, Nala.*

Bea is quiet.

She sits with half a fruit scone in her hand and looks at the jetty for a long time.

"She likes you," Bea says at last. "Yusra. Everyone can see it. The way she looks at you." She turns the scone over in her fingers. "Even with the lines."

"Yes," you say.

You don't pretend. You don't say *I don't know* or *I don't think so*. She asked, and it's true.

Bea nods slowly.

"Does that bother you?" you say.

Bea thinks about it. You watch her think, honestly, the way she does, her brow furrowed, looking out at the mere.

"A bit," she says. "Less than Shiv did." She looks at you sideways. "I'm getting used to you."

She says it like a joke. It isn't, quite.

And then she reaches out, and brushes a crumb off your lip with her thumb.

It's a small careful movement. Her thumb, warm, at the corner of your mouth, sweeping the crumb away. And then she doesn't take her thumb away. She leaves it there. Resting at the corner of your mouth, light, on your lower lip. Her eyes are on your mouth.

You keep very still.

Then Bea leans in and kisses you on the mouth.

Quick, and soft. Her lips on yours for a second, warm, and she tastes of sugar: of the crust on the top of the fruit scone, sweet and gritty and warm. It's over almost as soon as it's started. She pulls back, an inch, two inches, and looks at you. Her eyes are wide. She looks frightened and brave and astonished at herself, all at once, waiting to see what you'll do.

You kiss her back.

Slower. You lean into her and kiss her properly, your mouth on hers, slow and soft, taking your time. Her lips part. You feel her breath catch. Her hand comes up, cold from the wall and the afternoon, and lands on your cheek, and stays there, holding your face, her cold fingers against your jaw, while you kiss her on the jetty wall with the mere lapping below you and the duck watching.

"Oh," Bea says against your mouth.

Just that. *Oh.* Soft, astonished, her lips moving against yours as she says it.

And then she laughs. Right there, against your mouth, a small bright surprised laugh, and you feel it on your lips. And she kisses you again.

---

You walk home along the shore at dusk, hand in hand.

The mere has gone from silver to pewter to deep blue, the fells black against a sky going green and gold at the edges. A few lights are coming on in Thornfield's windows ahead. The air is cold and very still. Bea's hand is in yours, warm, her fingers laced through yours, and she swings your arms a little as you walk.

She hums the whole mile.

You don't know the tune. Something she makes up as she goes, maybe, wandering up and down. She hums it under her breath, the whole way, and every so often she looks at you sideways and goes pink and laughs and goes on humming.

---

At eight o'clock it's the poetry society, in the Snug.

The Snug is a small room at the end of a corridor in the old part of the library, up a short stair, with dark oak panelling on every wall and a low ceiling and a fire burning in a little iron grate. There are old sofas, sagging, upholstered in faded green velvet, and a few armchairs, and a bookcase full of battered anthologies, and a threadbare rug. It smells of woodsmoke and old books. There are seven girls in it, scattered on the sofas, curled up with their feet under them, with mugs of tea and books on their knees.

And in the corner chair, the high-backed one closest to the fire, in black, with a slim book on her knee, sits Kit.

She looks up as you come in. Her grey-green eyes find you in the doorway. She doesn't smile. But she doesn't look away either, and something in her face settles, very slightly, as if she'd been waiting to see whether you'd actually come.

You sit on the end of a sofa, near her.

"The rule," says the girl who runs it, a tall third-year with silver rings on every finger, "is one poem each. Aloud. Your own or someone else's. Anything you like." She smiles round at the newcomers. "And afterwards, the room stays quiet. We don't clap. You just let it sit."

You haven't brought a poem. You didn't think. You get up and go to the bookcase and take out a battered anthology at random, its spine held together with tape, and flick through it, and find a short one about the sea. You go back to the sofa.

When it's your turn, you read it.

Badly. You know it's badly. Your voice comes out too high, and too fast, and you stumble over a word in the second line, and your face gets hot, and you rush the end. It's about waves, and a woman waiting on a shore. You get to the last line and stop, abruptly, and look up.

Silence. The way the rule asks. Seven girls on the sofas, quiet, letting it sit. The fire crackles. A log shifts in the grate.

In the corner chair, Kit's mouth twitches at the corner.

Just once. The small pull, the one you know now. She flattens it at once. But it was there. You look at your knees, and feel your face burn, and feel, under the burning, absurdly happy.

The poems go round. A girl reads one of her own, about her grandmother's kitchen, that makes the girl beside her cry. Another reads something long and fierce in Welsh that the rest of you can't follow and all of you feel. The fire burns lower. The room gets warmer.

Kit's turn comes last.

She opens the slim book on her knee. Her pale hand lies flat on the page, holding it open. And as you watch, her knuckles go white.

Her whole hand, pressed on the page. The skin over the knuckles draining white, tight, the silver ring standing out. Her face is very still. She's looking down at the page.

Seven girls are watching her. Waiting.

She starts. "There is a house—" she says, low, and stops.

She stares at the page. The room waits. The fire crackles. You can see her throat move as she swallows. You can see her hand on the page, white at the knuckles, starting very faintly to shake. You've seen that hand shake before. In the Practice Hall, against the black wool of her skirt.

You look away from her.

Deliberately. You turn your head and look into the fire. Right into the heart of it, at the little iron grate and the glowing bed of coals in the bottom of it. And you start to count them. Slowly. One. Two. The big one at the back, gone grey at the edges. Three. The small bright one that keeps flaring. Four. You don't look at her. You keep your eyes on the fire, and count, and you give her the one thing you can give her: one pair of eyes in the room that isn't watching.

Five. Six.

Kit reads.

Her voice is low and steady. Not loud, but very clear, every word placed exactly, the way she places her pen on the desk in the Drawing Room. The poem is about a house. A big old house at night, with every window lit, room after room, gold and warm, people moving behind the glass. Every window lit but one. And the poem is about that one window. The dark one. The room behind it, and who might be in it, and what she might be doing in the dark while the whole rest of the house is full of light.

She reads it all the way through without stopping.

Then she stops, and the room is silent, the way the rule asks. The fire crackles. Every girl stays still.

You look back.

And Kit is looking straight at you. Across the little panelled room, over the top of the slim book, past the other girls on the sofas, her grey-green eyes are fixed on you. And they're bright. Very bright, in the firelight, wet at the edges.

She knew. She knew you looked away. She knew why.

---

Afterwards, at the door of the Snug, while the others are gathering their mugs and books, you take the handkerchief out of your pocket.

It's dry now, washed and folded into a neat square, the *K* in the corner. You hold it out to her.

Kit looks at it. Then at you.

"Keep it," she says.

You don't argue. You put it back in your pocket.

She walks you back across the quad.

It's frozen again tonight. The grass crunches silver under your feet, and the sky is clear and enormous and blazing with stars, and your breath and hers go up in white clouds side by side. Kit walks with her hands deep in her coat pockets and her chin down in her collar. She doesn't say anything. Neither do you. It's like the library on Sunday night: easy. The easiest silence there is.

At Thornfield's door she stops on the step.

She doesn't look at you. She tips her head back and looks up at the stars.

"Saturday," Kit says. "The cinema." A pause. "If you meant it."

"I meant it," you say.

Kit nods. Once, sharply, at the stars.

Then she turns and goes back across the quad towards Ashcombe, quickly, her coat flapping, her breath trailing behind her in the cold. You watch her go. And just before she passes out of the light from Thornfield's door, she glances back, and you see her face for a second in the lamplight. She's almost smiling.

---

Upstairs, Bea's door is ajar.

Her lamp is on. A slice of warm gold light lies across the dark corridor floor from the gap. As you come along to your door, her voice comes out through it, soft and sleepy:

"Night, Nala."

"Night, Bea," you call back.

---

You fall asleep in your narrow bed with Kit's handkerchief folded under your pillow, the small *K* against your cheek through the cotton.

And the taste of sugar still on your lips.
