On Thursday you wake in room 8.

In Lin's narrow bed, under her blanket, with the wall close on one side and the smell of charcoal and jasmine tea all round you. The curtains are open a crack. Grey light is coming in off the quad and lying across the walls, across all the drawings: the hands and the faces and the kitchen and the quad, grey on grey, and over the bed the girl on the window seat, and beside her the thin blue letter.

The candle on the desk is a cold stub in its saucer.

Lin is awake already.

She's sitting up at the end of the bed, against the wall, with her knees drawn up and her sketchbook on them. In her long grey jumper, her hair down round her shoulders. A stick of charcoal in her fingers.

She's drawing you.

You can tell from the way she looks: at you, and down, and at you again. Quick, sure glances. Her hand moving on the page.

"Don't move," she says.

Quietly. Not looking up from the page.

"Nearly."

You don't move.

You lie still, with your cheek on her pillow, and watch her. Her eyes going from you to the page and back. Her small mouth, intent. The charcoal whispering. A line of grey on the side of her hand where it's rubbed against the paper. The light getting slowly brighter on the wall behind her.

Then she stops.

She looks at the page a moment longer. Then she turns the sketchbook round, and shows you.

It's you.

Asleep. Your hair across your face, a dark tangle of it over one cheek and your nose. Your mouth a little open. One hand open on her pillow, palm up, the fingers loose. The blanket at your shoulder. Done in a few quick lines and a few soft smudges, and it's you, the way you must have looked an hour ago, before you knew anyone was looking.

Under it, in her small neat hand: *Thursday*.

---

She puts the sketchbook down.

And comes up the bed on her knees, and kisses you good morning.

Shy again. You can feel it in her: the shyness come back with the daylight. Her mouth soft and careful on yours, as if she isn't sure she's allowed, after last night, in the grey light, with the window open a crack.

Then she pulls back, and looks at you, and laughs.

At herself. A small, rueful laugh, her face going pink.

"I don't know why I'm shy," she says. "After."

"I know," you say.

And she laughs again, and kisses you again, less shy.

---

You go up to 15 in yesterday's clothes.

Up the stairs from the middle floor, past the landing window, quietly, with your shoes in your hand.

Bea is sitting on her bed, lacing her running shoes.

She looks up when you come in.

And searches your face. You see her do it. Her eyes going over you, quick and careful, the way she looks at the water before the oar goes in. Reading you.

"Well?" says Bea. "Is she all right?"

"She's all right," you say. You sit on the end of the bed. "She's lovely."

Bea's whole face goes soft.

All of it. Her mouth, her eyes, her shoulders.

"Good," she says. "Good."

Then she grins.

"You've got charcoal on your neck," Bea says.

She licks her thumb, and leans over, and rubs it off. Firmly, the way she'd do it to her little brother. Just under your ear. You squirm, and she holds your chin still with her other hand, and keeps rubbing, and inspects the result.

"There," she says. "Decent."

She stands up.

And bounces. On her toes, on the rug, shaking her hands out.

"Two days," says Bea. "Two days." She presses both hands flat on her stomach. "I'm going to be sick."

---

You run the shore with her.

She runs too fast. Far too fast, from the very first step: straight out of the gate and away along the frosty path like a thing let loose, her ponytail flying, her breath smoking. Running it all out of her. Two days.

You let her.

You don't try to keep up. You jog along behind at your own pace, and watch her get smaller up the shore, and don't mind.

After a mile she slows down.

You see it, far ahead: her stride shortening, her arms dropping, her head coming up. Then she stops, and stands at the edge of the water with her hands on her hips, breathing, looking at the mere.

When you catch her up she falls in beside you without a word, at your pace, and you run back together.

---

At breakfast Lin comes down late.

She comes into the kitchen pink. With her hair down. Long and straight and black, all of it, loose down her back, the way it was last night, the way you've never seen her wear it in daylight, in front of anyone.

She sits down beside you.

And eats everything.

Toast, three slices. A boiled egg. Porridge, a whole bowl, with honey. Another slice of toast. Steadily, without saying a word, as if she hasn't eaten in a week, her shoulder warm against yours.

Across the table, Priya looks at Lin.

Then at you.

Then back at Lin. Lin's hair. Lin's pink face. Lin's third slice of toast.

And Priya opens her mouth.

Under the table, Bea kicks her.

Priya shuts her mouth again. She looks at Bea, injured. Bea looks back at her, wide-eyed, innocent, and passes her the marmalade.

---

At ten it's History of Magic, in the Long Room.

The lecturer comes in, and puts down her notes, and goes to the board.

She chalks a date on it.

*1816.*

"The Glass Winter," she says.

Her voice, like a dry leaf, going out over the rows.

"The year with no summer. Snow in June. Frost in August. Crops failing from here to the Baltic." She taps the date. "And across the north, from the Pennines to Scotland, every spell anyone cast that year came out thin."

She lets that sit.

"Glims guttered," she says. "Like candles in a draught, however well they were drawn. Kettle charms took an hour to boil a pint. Warding charms let the damp through. And the menders—" she pauses, "—the menders cracked the cups they were mending. Drew the sigil, and watched the crack run the other way."

A little murmur goes along the rows.

"The scholars of the day couldn't say why," the lecturer says. "It was, and is, a mystery."

Beside you, Kit is writing fast.

Every word again, the way she did for the Healers Act. Her pen racing.

On your other side, Lin has her pencil out. And in the margin of *your* notes, very small, she draws a cup.

A little teacup, on its saucer. With a crack running down it.

Two rows ahead, Amara's hand goes up.

"Did they ever find out why?" she asks.

The lecturer smiles.

Her dry, thin smile.

"Next week," she says. "Patience, Miss Osei."

---

After, Kit walks with you to the Practice Hall.

Across the quad in the cold. Kit has her hands in her coat pockets and her eyes on the frosty grass, and she's frowning, thinking.

"Thin spells," she says.

She shakes her head slowly.

"Imagine it," says Kit. "Doing it all right. Every line right. And it just comes out thin. And every book you open just shrugs at you."

She walks a few steps.

Then she looks at you sideways.

"Tomorrow," Kit says. "Six. The cinema." A pause. "I've booked it."

She stops on the path, and looks at you properly.

"Still on?"

"Still on," you say.

Kit nods, once. And walks on. But you can see the corner of her mouth.

---

At lunch Amara goes to debating.

She eats fast, the way she does on Thursdays, and stands, smoothing her jumper, checking her gold watch. And Priya stands up with her.

Carrying Amara's notes.

A thick sheaf of them, in a folder, held against her chest in both arms. Beaming. She follows Amara out of the Great Hall, a step behind, like a squire with a shield.

Bea watches them go, and looks at you, and doesn't say a word, with tremendous eloquence.

Shiv sits down with you.

In Priya's seat, with her soup, her hair damp from the dawn swim.

"Saturday," Shiv says.

She tears her bread.

"I've written something rude in Irish," she says, "on a bedsheet."

Across the table, Bea chokes on her soup.

She actually chokes: coughing, spluttering, her eyes watering, Lin thumping her on the back. When she can talk, she points her spoon at Shiv.

"Shiv," she says. "You *can't*."

Shiv grins her crooked grin.

"Watch me, bow," she says.

---

At two it's the Practice Hall, and the calling charm again.

Further now.

The demonstrator has you put the thimble and the pen and the button and the spoon at the far end of the bench. The whole length of it. Six feet of scarred wood between your hand and the thimble.

You hold out your hand.

You draw the backwards hook. You ask.

The thimble comes.

The full six feet. Slow, and steady, lifting off the far end of the bench and travelling all the way along it through the air, level, as if it's on a thread. You watch it come. It takes its time. And you catch it, clean, in your palm.

You do it again. And it comes again.

The third time, your head aches.

Behind the eyes, the dull pressure, coming on. You notice it. You hold the thimble, and you think of the Long Room on Monday, and the Foundations lecturer, and the line you underlined.

*The body keeps the account.*

You stop.

You put the thimble down, and fold your hands in your lap, and breathe out.

At the next bench, Bea calls her spoon.

Six feet. You watch her do it: her hand low, her sigil soft, her face very calm. And the spoon lifts off the far end of the bench, and comes, the whole way, gently. And arrives in her palm, light as a leaf.

Bea stares at it.

For a long moment, in total silence, the spoon in her hand.

Then she cheers. Both arms up. Half the hall turns round again.

Lin calls a pen.

Down the whole length of the bench, the six feet, and it comes to her the way it always comes to her: floating, turning a little, unhurried, like a leaf on a stream going gently down to the sea.

The demonstrator is watching.

She stands at the end of Lin's bench, with her arms folded, and watches the pen go all the way to Lin's hand.

And nods.

---

In the afternoon you finish your focus essay in the Snug.

Up the short stair, on the green sofa by the iron grate. The fire lit. And Kit, who has come up the stair after you with two mugs of tea and handed you one without a word, and sat down on the other end of the sofa with her own essay.

You write.

You write about Kit's ring. The first one she made, plain and uneven, cool in your palm, and how it felt like something. How it came to your hand across the kitchen table, and Kit said it knows you a bit now.

You write about Lin's drawing. The one of you asleep, this morning, with *Thursday* under it. How a drawing remembers the hand, and the one who sat.

You write about a tin medal.

You don't say whose. A small boy wears it every day under his school jumper. It's dented, and the ribbon has gone grey, and he won't take it off.

And at the end, you write the last line.

*It should remember, if what it remembers is someone being loved.*

You look at it.

You leave it.

Across the sofa, Kit has been reading it. Upside down, from her end, her head tilted, her own essay forgotten on her knee. You didn't notice when she started.

She doesn't say anything.

She reaches over, and puts her hand on your foot, where it's tucked up on the sofa between you. And squeezes it.

Once. Firmly.

In place of speaking.

---

At seven it's life drawing, in the Drawing Room.

The lamps in place of the skylights. Rain starting on the glass above, softly. The easels round the dais.

The model tonight is Morwenna.

The rowing captain. She comes in in a dressing gown, nods round at everybody, and climbs up onto the dais, and takes the dressing gown off, and folds it, and puts it on the floor.

And sits down nude on the stool, as calm as on the jetty.

She's tall and long-limbed and lean, her arms strong from years of rowing, her skin fair and wind-flushed at the face and pale everywhere else. Her dark brown ponytail, without its headband. She folds her arms across her chest, and puts one foot up on the rung of the stool, and looks round the room at all of you.

Amused.

Faintly, openly amused, one eyebrow up, as if the whole thing is a very good joke and she's in on it.

Lin draws her fast.

Six times in the hour. Six drawings, one after another, her charcoal flying, each one quick and sure and alive. Morwenna's long arms. Her folded hands. Her shoulders. The set of her jaw.

You draw her twice.

The first is stiff. The second time, it's good. Actually good. You look at it, and you can see her in it: the length of her, the strong arms.

"Look more," Lin murmurs, beside you. "Draw less."

You look more.

Your third drawing has Morwenna's grin in it.

Nearly. Not quite. But it's there, almost: the amusement, at the corner of her mouth, the eyebrow. You look at it and you can hear her megaphone.

---

At the end, Morwenna gets down and puts on her dressing gown, and ties it, and comes round to look at the drawings.

She goes from easel to easel. Nodding. Saying *ha* at some of them. Stopping a long time at Lin's, and looking at all six, and saying "Christ, you're good," to Lin, who goes scarlet.

Then she stops at yours.

At the third one. She looks at it.

And she laughs.

A big, loud, delighted laugh, her head back.

"That's me," Morwenna says, "telling Lindqvist to sit up."

She points at the grin.

"Spot on," she says.

She looks at you. She knows who you are; she's seen you on the bank, shouting.

"Tell Bea to sleep tonight," says Morwenna.

---

At half past nine you fill the flask.

Yusra's on tonight. Ten till six, the way she is on Thursdays.

You wait at the foot of the stairs.

At quarter to ten she comes down.

She sees you there, waiting, with the flask held against your chest. And her face eases. You see it happen: the tightness going out of it, the warden's set softening at the edges. Just at the sight of you.

"Every time," Yusra says.

Before you can. She says it first, low, with her mouth twitching.

You hand her the flask.

She takes it. And her fingers stay on yours, round the dented tartan. Holding your fingers on it.

"You weren't on my floor last night," Yusra says.

Low.

"I'm the warden," she says. "I notice who's missing. Fourteen and fifteen, quiet as the grave all night." She looks at you. "And Lin Zhao at breakfast with her hair down, eating like a horse."

You go still.

Completely still, at the foot of the stairs, with your fingers under hers on the flask.

Yusra's mouth twitches.

"Good," she says.

She looks down at your hands, and hers.

"She's been lonely, that one," Yusra says. "Like me."

She takes the flask, and tucks it in her bag.

"Saturday," she says. "After the race. Front door."

And she touches your cheek. Once. Her fingertips, light, along your cheekbone.

And goes.

---

In 15, Bea is lying on the rug.

On her back, in the middle of the floor, in her pyjamas. Staring at the ceiling. Her hands folded on her stomach.

"I'm visualising," she says.

She doesn't look at you.

"Morwenna says visualise." A pause. "I'm visualising being sick in the boat."

You lie down on the rug beside her.

On your back, on the floor, shoulder to shoulder, looking at the ceiling with her. There's a crack in it, running from the light fitting to the corner. You both look at the crack.

"I've got a message from Morwenna," you say.

"What?"

"Sleep tonight."

Bea groans.

A long, deep, heartfelt groan, at the ceiling.

Then she laughs.

And then she takes your hand. There on the rug, between you, her blistered fingers sliding through yours.

"Will you be at the jetty?" she says.

"At the wall," you say. "Shouting."

Bea turns her head, on the rug, and looks at you.

"Where I kissed you," she says.

---

You get into bed.

Bea gets in beside you. And lies awake. A long while, in the dark, on her back, beside you.

And she rows.

Small movements, of her hands, on top of the blanket. Her fingers curling round an oar that isn't there. Her wrists turning, feathering, squaring. Over and over, slowly, in the dark. The catch, and the drive, and the finish, and the recovery. Down the mere in her head, to the bend by the reeds, and the jetty.

You hold her.

You put your arm over her, and your face in her shoulder, and hold her, and feel her hands move.

Till the movements stop.

Till her hands go still on the blanket, and her breathing slows, and lengthens, and she's asleep.
