On Friday you wake before the alarm.

The room is grey. The radiator ticks. On your desk, squared up neat where you left it at midnight, is the essay, and on top of it Kit's black handkerchief, folded, the small grey *K* in the corner turned up.

You get up and stand at the desk in your bare feet. You lift the handkerchief off and turn to the last page.

*Intent comes from wherever the wanting lives.*

You read it once more in the grey light. It still looks true. It looks more true, if anything, now you've slept on it.

You find a card folder in the bottom of your trunk, a stiff buff one, and slide the pages into it, and tuck the handkerchief back under your pillow, where it lives now.

---

At nine it's Penhallow's study, up the winding stair of the library tower.

The round window is full of the mere, grey and still this morning, with mist lying on it in long low bands. The books are stacked everywhere, on the shelves and the floor and the windowsill, and the shortbread tin sits on the corner of the desk with its lid on. Penhallow is in her green tweed with her glasses on their chain, standing by the desk as you file in.

"Essays," she says. "On the desk. Pile, please."

They go down one after another. Amara's, typed, in a clear plastic sleeve. Priya's, thick, with coloured tabs sticking out of it. Shiv's, three pages, folded once down the middle as if it's been in a pocket. Lin's, in a beautiful hand. Kit's, very neat, very short.

You step up with your folder.

"Ashby," says Penhallow, and you stop. She's looking at you over her glasses. "I said bring it yourself."

You remember. *Bring it to me yourself.* Not onto the pile.

So you hold it out to her, and she takes it out of your hands.

She opens the folder where she stands. She doesn't read it from the start. She turns straight to the last page, the way you'd look at the end of a book to see if it's worth it, and her eyes go down to the bottom.

Her eyebrows go up. All the way up, over the rims of her glasses.

"Hm," says Penhallow.

She closes the folder and puts it on top of the pile. Square on top.

"Tuesday, then," she says. "Green ink." And she sits down.

You go to your chair. Across the table, Kit has her face turned to the window, but the corner of her mouth has gone in at the side, the way it does when she's hiding a smile.

Penhallow spends the hour on the focus question.

She asks it straight out, the question Kit and Amara have been writing about all week: is a spell the caster's, or the focus's? And then she sits back with her hands folded and lets them go at it.

Amara has the ring out within a minute. Her grandmother's gold ring, held up to the round window, the way she held it up yesterday in the debate. It remembers, she says. Every spell it's helped make. Kit says a ring is a ring; it doesn't remember, it wears, like a step wears, like a doorhandle, and what looks like memory is only the groove. Amara says a groove *is* a memory. Kit says then a doorstep remembers every boot that's ever crossed it, and does Amara want to argue with a doorstep. Amara says she'd win.

Penhallow doesn't say a word. She just watches them, and every so often she pushes the shortbread tin an inch towards whichever one's just landed a hit, and then an inch back.

By ten both of them are flushed. Amara's eyes are bright and her braids are swinging. Kit's ears are scarlet and she's leaning forward with her elbows on the table, which she never does. You and Lin and Priya and Shiv sit and watch it like a tennis match.

"Good," says Penhallow, at the end. "You're both half right. Keep at it." She opens the tin. "Both of you. One each."

---

At eleven it's Sigils, in the Drawing Room.

The skylights are bright today. The tutor sets the warming sigil again, a page of them, and you sit at your slanted desk with your pen, with Lin's soft nib in it, and draw.

Your spirals are loose now.

Breathe out, and draw. The pen goes round and round, coiling in, the turns spaced even, all the way to the centre without crowding, without cramping. You draw ten. Then ten more. When you sit back and look at your page beside Lin's, the two columns are almost alike. Hers are still more beautiful. Hers have a kind of grace to them, a lightness at the turns. But yours go all the way in now, and they look like they're breathing.

Lin looks across at your page. She doesn't say anything. She just smiles her small quiet smile, down at her own ink.

---

On the stairs after, Kit falls into step beside you.

She doesn't say hello. She just appears at your elbow among the crowd of girls going down, her books held flat against her chest with both arms, eyes front.

"Change of plan," she says.

Your stomach drops. Just a little.

"The film I want," says Kit, to the stairwell, "only shows on Fridays. So. Tonight." A pause. "Bus at six."

"Tonight," you say.

Kit nods. Once, sharply. And then she's walking away, fast, down the last of the stairs and out across the hall, her black bob swinging, without looking back.

---

At lunch you find Bea at the long table with a bowl of soup and a heel of bread, and you sit down beside her, and before anyone else can sit down near you, you tell her.

"I'm going to Kendal tonight," you say. "With Kit. To the cinema."

Bea goes still. Her spoon stops halfway to her mouth.

"Like a date?" she says.

You think about it. About the note in History, folded very small. About *if you meant it.* About Kit walking away down the stairs as fast as she could.

"I think so," you say. "Yes. I think it is." You look at her. "I wanted to tell you. Before anyone. I want to be honest with you about it. About all of it."

Bea puts her spoon down in the bowl. She looks at it for a long time. At the soup. At the spoon in it. Her cheeks have gone pink, and you can see her thinking, all of it going across her face the way everything does with Bea.

"Thank you for telling me first," she says at last.

"Bea—"

"It stings a bit," she says. Honestly, straight out, looking at you. "It does. I'm not going to pretend." She takes a breath. "Less than I thought, though. Less than I thought it would."

And under the table she bumps your knee with hers. Once. Firmly.

"Wear the green jumper," Bea says.

"The—"

"The green one. The soft one. You look lovely in it." She picks her spoon back up, and points it at you. "And tell me after. Not everything." The pink goes deeper. "Some."

---

The afternoon is for study.

You sleep instead. You lie down on your bed in 14 meaning to read for ten minutes, with Priya's books on intent, and wake an hour later with one of them open on your chest and the light gone gold and slanting.

Then you try on three jumpers.

The grey one is too big; it hangs off you like a sack. The fisherman's jumper Bea bought you is too thick, too much, you'd boil in a cinema. You stand in front of the little mirror on the back of the door in your vest and your skirt and look at yourself for a while: the long dark red hair, the freckles, the small pale face that's yours now. Then you take the green one off its hanger. Soft wool, the colour of moss. It slides on like water.

You look in the mirror.

Bea was right.

---

At six the bus comes into the village, out of the dusk, its windows lit and nearly empty.

You walked the mile along the shore path to get here, in the cold, with the mere going dark beside you. And Kit is at the stop already. Early. In a long black coat buttoned to the throat, her hands in her pockets, standing very straight under the one streetlamp with her breath going up white.

She sees you come into the light, and looks at the jumper.

She looks away. Down the road, at the bus coming.

She looks back.

"Green," she says. As if accusing you of it.

You get on. The driver nods. There are two old men at the front, and that's all, and you go to the back, and Kit sits down beside you by the window, and the bus pulls out.

It's forty minutes to Kendal. Forty minutes of dark fells going past, black against a sky that's not quite black yet, and farm windows lit yellow here and there in the folds of the hills, and the bus roaring in low gear up the long climbs. And Kit talks.

You didn't know she could. Not like this. She tells you about the Ashcombe kitchens, where the cook is called Mrs Hale and has been there since before the war, and keeps a hen in the scullery that is absolutely not allowed, and feeds the Ashcombe girls toast at midnight if they're polite and cold if they're not. She does Mrs Hale's voice. She does the hen.

Then she tells you about her grandmother.

Her grandmother at Hollowmere, forty years ago and more, crossing the same quad. Her grandmother who sat in the Long Room under the same lecturer, the lecturer younger then, with dark hair, giving the same date wrong.

"Forty years," Kit says, to the black window. "Forty years, the same date, wrong, every single autumn. Granny says she used to mouth it along with her, to annoy the girl in front."

You laugh out loud. One of the old men at the front turns round.

Kit looks at you sideways in the dark of the bus, and her mouth goes in at the corner.

---

Kendal is grey stone and wet streets and lamps.

It's been raining while you came over the fells, and the pavements shine, and the lamps make long gold puddles in the road. You walk from the bus station along the river with your hands in your pockets and your shoulders nearly touching, past shut shops and lit pubs, to the cinema.

It's small. You remember it: the red plush seats, worn bald on the arms; the smell of dust and old carpet and something sweet. The woman in the box office is knitting.

You reach for your purse.

Kit has already pushed a note under the glass.

"I chose," she says, taking the two tickets, before you can say anything. "I pay. That's the rule."

"Whose rule?"

"Mine," says Kit. "I just made it."

---

The Friday film is old and foreign. Black and white, with subtitles. The house holds six people.

You and Kit sit near the back, in the middle of an empty row. The four others are far down at the front, close to the screen, four grey heads in a row, watching. They don't turn round. They don't look at anything but the screen the whole time.

The lights go down.

The film is about two women in a house by the sea, one summer.

It's slow. It's very slow. It's mostly the two of them: walking on a grey beach, and sitting at a table in a white kitchen, and not quite saying things to each other. The sea comes in and out. A shutter bangs. One of them plays the piano badly, and the other one listens from the stairs. You can't follow all the subtitles. It doesn't matter. You sit in the dark with the silver light on your face and the slow grey sea on the screen, and you love it. You love it more than you can say.

Halfway through, Kit's hand finds yours.

On the armrest between you, in the dark. Her fingers slide over the back of your hand, and turn it, and lace through it, and then they hold. Hard. Hard enough that her silver ring presses into your knuckle.

You look at her.

Her face is silver in the light from the screen, all sharp lines and shadows. And she isn't watching the film. She's watching you.

She leans in.

And stops. An inch away. You can feel her breath on your mouth, quick and shallow. You can see her eyes, very wide, grey-green gone almost black in the dark. Down at the front the four grey heads are still turned to the screen, still and patient, the sea coming in and out over them.

You close the inch.

You kiss her. Softly. Just your mouth on hers, very gently. And Kit makes a small sound, low in her throat, surprised, and kisses you back.

Her mouth is cool. And then it's warm. Her lips are thin and soft and she kisses carefully, so carefully, as if she's been thinking about how for a long time and wants to get it exactly right. Her other hand comes up to your face. Her silver ring is cold against your jaw.

She pulls back. An inch. She looks at you.

Then she kisses you again, harder. Her hand drops from your face and catches in the front of your green jumper, and holds on, her fingers tight in the soft wool, pulling you closer.

When it ends, you're both breathing as if you've run.

You watch the last half of the film with your fingers laced in hers. After a while she leans over, slowly, and puts her head on your shoulder, her hair cool against your neck, and leaves it there. On the screen the two women stand on the grey beach in the wind, and one of them takes the other's hand.

---

The lights come up.

Kit sits up very straight at once. Composed. Coat buttoned. Hands in her lap. Face perfectly blank.

Her ears are scarlet.

Out on the wet street the rain has stopped. The pavements shine. You walk back along the river towards the bus station, and Kit walks beside you with her eyes on the paving stones.

"I've never done that," she says. Quietly. To the pavement. "With anyone watching."

"There wasn't anyone watching," you say. "They were all at the front. They were watching the sea."

"*You* were," says Kit.

And she almost laughs. It catches in her chest and comes out as a sort of breath, and she shakes her head at the pavement, and her mouth goes in at the corner and stays there.

---

The last bus is at ten.

It's emptier than the one coming. Just you and Kit, and a sleeping man in a fleece, and the driver. You sit at the back again. Kit sits close this time. Right up against you, her shoulder to yours, her hip to yours. And when the bus pulls out she takes your hand, and draws it in under the flap of her long black coat on the seat between you, and holds it there in the warm, out of sight.

The fells go by in the dark. The farm windows are mostly out now.

Somewhere past Staveley, she says it.

Quietly. Looking straight ahead at the back of the seat in front.

"I've known since I was fifteen," Kit says. "That I want girls. Only girls."

You don't say anything. You hold her hand under the coat.

"Not one person at home knows," she says. "Not one in my family. Not my mother. Not Granny. Not one." She turns her face away, to the black window, where your two faces hang reflected over the dark fields. "I've never said it. Out loud. Until now."

You squeeze her hand. Hard, the way she held yours in the cinema.

"I'll keep it," you say. "The way I've kept the window."

Kit's reflection in the black glass looks at your reflection for a long moment.

Then she nods. Just once. And her hand turns over under the coat, and holds yours back.

---

From the village it's the mile along the shore path in the pitch dark, the mere invisible beside you, only the lap of it on the shingle to tell you it's there. The stars are out, thick and close. You walk hand in hand, and you don't talk much.

You come through the gates at half past eleven. The porter in his lodge looks up from his paper and sighs, and looks down again.

At Thornfield's door, Kit stops on the step.

She looks at you. Then she leans in quickly, and kisses you once more, quick and hard, on the mouth.

And she's gone. Back across the frosted quad towards Ashcombe, her black coat flying, fast, before you can say a word.

---

Upstairs, Bea's door is ajar.

Her lamp is on. The light falls out across the corridor in a long gold wedge. And Bea is sitting up in bed in an enormous jumper, with her knees drawn up and her arms round them and her hair coming down out of its bun, waiting.

"Well?" she says.

You go in, and sit down on the end of her bed.

And you tell her some of it. The bus, and Mrs Hale's hen. The tiny cinema and the four grey heads. The film, the two women and the slow grey sea. The dark. Kit's hand on the armrest. The kiss.

Not everything. Some.

Bea listens with her knees up and her chin on them, her brown eyes on your face the whole time. She doesn't interrupt. When you get to the kiss her eyebrows go up, and stay up.

Then she laughs. Softly. Into her knees.

"Kit Ashworth," she says. "Who'd have *thought*."

And she reaches out and pulls you down beside her by the sleeve of the green jumper, and puts her arms round you, and holds you. Just holds you, for a minute, with her chin resting on the top of your head, and her jumper smelling of laundry and her, and her heart going steady under your ear.

"I'm glad," she says into your hair. A pause. "Mostly."

"Mostly's honest," you say.

Bea laughs again, a little wet this time. She kisses your forehead, firmly, like a stamp.

"Bed," she says. "Go on. Go."

---

In 14 you don't bother with the light.

You lie down on top of the covers in the dark, and you're asleep almost at once, with two different mouths remembered, one tasting of sugar and one cool then warm, and the green jumper still on.
