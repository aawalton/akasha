On Sunday you wake at six on your own.

No knock. No alarm. Your eyes just open, in the grey dark, and you're awake, all at once, completely. The radiator ticks. The window over your head is pale. And you know where you're going before you've even sat up, the way you know your own name: the knowledge is already there in your body, in your legs and your chest and the backs of your arms, a bright cold pull towards the water.

You get up. Knickers, bra. The fisherman's jumper over the top, heavy and scratchy, down past your hips. Trainers, no socks. The key. You go down through the sleeping house as quietly as you can and let yourself out.

The shore is grey and silent. Mist lies on the mere, thick this morning, in a low white blanket over the water so that you can't see the far side at all, only the tops of the fells floating above it like islands. The shingle is wet and cold. The air is very still.

Shiv is on the rock.

She's sitting at the landward end in the parka, the same as Thursday, peeling off her socks, her bare feet white on the dark stone. She hears your feet on the shingle and looks up.

And this time she doesn't grin. She just smiles. A small warm smile, with no edge to it at all, as if she's been waiting and isn't surprised, only glad.

You climb up onto the rock beside her without a word and pull the jumper over your head.

---

You go in together off the edge, hand in hand.

The cold is still a fist. It hits all of you at once, the same as it did on Thursday, so total that it knocks every thought out of your head; the black water closes over you and the burn races over every inch of your skin. You come up gasping, and the shriek is right there in your throat, rising.

And you breathe out.

Slow. Through your lips. Like blowing on soup. You don't think about it; your body remembers, from Thursday, from Shiv's mouth beside yours. The breath goes out of you shaking, and the shriek goes with it, and you breathe out again, and again, and the clench in your chest lets go faster this time, much faster, and then you're treading water in the mere with your heart pounding, breathing, alive.

Shiv's hand is still in yours. She's laughing softly.

"Look at you," she says. "Look at you breathing."

Two minutes this time. You tread water face to face in the black mere, holding hands under the surface, close enough that your knees bump as you kick. The mist is lifting around you as the sun comes up behind the fells. It rises off the water in long slow curls and drifts, white and gold, thinning and lifting, so that the world appears round you a piece at a time: the shore, the dark trees, Thornfield's grey roofs, the fells going pink. It's like being inside a cloud that's slowly opening. Your whole body is burning with cold and you've never felt anything so beautiful in your life.

"Right," says Shiv at last, softly. "Out. Before you go blue."

---

On the rock she wraps you in her parka again. She zips it to your chin and rubs your arms through it, hard, the way she did on Thursday, and you stamp your feet on the cold stone with your teeth chattering so loudly you can hear them. She's naked and dripping. She doesn't seem to feel it.

Then she stops rubbing.

She lifts her hands, and puts them on your face.

They're cold. Wet and freezing from the mere, her palms on your cheeks, her fingers along your jaw and under your ears, in your soaked hair. You stop stamping. You stop chattering, almost. You look at her. Her face is very close. Water is beaded on her freckles, dozens of tiny drops on her nose and her cheekbones, catching the pink light. Her sea-green eyes are on yours and they're not laughing at all.

"Still might," she says. Quietly.

You look at her mouth.

"Then do," you say.

And she kisses you.

Her mouth is cold. That's the first thing: cold as the water, her lips chilled and wet, pressed to yours. And then, almost at once, warm. The warmth comes up through the cold from underneath, from inside her, her mouth softening and opening against yours, and it tastes of the mere: of clean cold water and stone and something green, and under it, faintly, of her.

Your whole burning body goes quiet.

All of it. The roaring of the cold in your skin, the chattering, the stamping, the pounding of your heart: it all goes still and quiet under her mouth, as if someone has laid a hand flat on the surface of you and smoothed it. There's only this. Her cold hands on your face. Her mouth on yours, slow and unhurried, moving softly. Her breath warm against your cheek.

It's your first kiss in this body.

You didn't know. You didn't know it would be like this. Your lips feel everything. Twice as much as they ever did, ten times as much: every tiny movement of her mouth, the softness of her lower lip, the cool slick of water, the faint rough edge where her lip is chapped from the cold, the warmth of her breath, the shape of her smile against you when she smiles into the kiss. It's like the shower on your first morning, every inch lighting up at once, only all of it gathered into your mouth. You make a small sound. You hear it come out of you, soft and high, and you don't care.

She pulls back.

Just an inch. She's grinning now, the crooked grin, slow and wide across her freckled face, and the drops of water are still beaded on her freckles, shining.

"Grand," says Shiv. "That's settled, then."

You stand there in her parka, your lips tingling, your face in her cold hands, and you laugh. A shaky, breathless laugh. And then, because it's true, and because you said it at the Bell with cider in you and you want her to hear it again without:

"Shiv. There are others. I told you. There are others I want, too."

"I remember," says Shiv.

And she kisses you again.

---

The shower afterwards is hot, and it's wonderful, and you barely notice it.

When you get out, the little mirror over the bathroom sink is steamed solid. You wipe a patch clear with your hand and look at yourself: a small freckled girl with soaked red hair and pink cheeks and a mouth that looks, somehow, different. Softer. A little swollen. You touch it with your fingertips. Your lips are still tingling.

You laugh out loud. At yourself. At the girl in the mirror, touching her own mouth like someone in a film. The laugh rings round the tiled bathroom, and somebody in the next cubicle says "*What?*" and you laugh harder, and can't explain.

---

In the late morning you go down to the laundry.

Thornfield's laundry room is in the cellar, down a stone stair at the back of the house, and it's low and warm and damp and smells of soap powder and hot cotton. There are three washing machines and two tumble dryers, all of them going, rumbling and churning, and an airer on the wall, and a long table for folding, and a queue. On Sundays, it turns out, the whole house does its washing. There are six girls ahead of you with baskets.

One of them is Bea.

She's sitting on an upturned basket by the end machine, with her full basket on her knees and her chin on her hand, and when she sees you come down the stairs with your armful of clothes she brightens. "Oh thank god," she says. "Company. Sit. You can't put that in like that."

"Like what?"

"Like *that*. All in a heap. Have you never done laundry?" She looks at you with real alarm. "Nala. Have you never done laundry?"

You think about Alan's washing machine. About throwing everything in on one setting and hoping.

"Not properly," you say.

So Bea teaches you. While you wait in the queue she goes through your armful, item by item, sorting it into heaps on the folding table. Darks. Lights. Wool. "That's wool, that's never going in the dryer, it'll come out doll-sized. That, hand wash. That's fine." She holds things up to the light and squints at the labels.

Then she holds up one of your bras. A plain pale pink one, small.

"And *these*," she says, very seriously, like a teacher, "go on the airer. Never the dryer. *Never*. The dryer eats the wires and spits them out at you, and then you're walking about with a wire poking you in the—"

She stops. She's still holding your bra up between you, by one strap, at eye level, in the middle of the crowded laundry room. You're both looking at it.

You crack up. Both of you, at the same moment, helplessly. Bea drops the bra on the heap and folds over on her basket and laughs until she's crying, and you're laughing so hard you have to sit down on the floor, and the other girls in the queue are staring, and one of them is laughing too without knowing why.

When Bea finally sits up, wiping her eyes, she looks at you.

Properly. The laugh fades out of her face slowly, and she looks at you with her head on one side, her brown eyes going over your face. Your mouth. Your cheeks. Whatever it is that's been showing on you since the rock, the glow you can feel on your own skin.

"Something's happened," Bea says.

You look at her. You could say no. You don't want to.

"Shiv," you say. "This morning. On the rock. We went in again, and then she—" You touch your mouth without meaning to. "She kissed me."

Bea goes still.

It's only for a second. Her face doesn't change, exactly; it just stops, all of it, the way water stops when you put your hand flat on it. Her eyes stay on yours.

Then she smiles. Wide, with both dimples.

"Oh, I'm *glad*," Bea says. "Nala, that's brilliant. That's *lovely*. I'm so glad." It comes out very bright. A little too bright, like a light turned up one notch past where it sits comfortably. "Shiv! Of course Shiv. I *knew* it. On the rock! That's so *romantic*."

The washer beside her clicks and stops. Bea jumps up.

"That's mine," she says. "That's me." And she turns her back to you and opens the door and starts pulling her wet washing out into the basket, piece by piece.

It takes her a long time. Much longer than it should. She takes each thing out and shakes it, and looks at it, and folds it over the side of the basket, slowly, very carefully, with her back to you, her head bent. And you sit on the floor and watch her, and see her lift her wrist, once, quickly, to the corner of her eye. You see her blink. Hard, twice, the way you do when something's got in your eye and you're trying to get it out.

You don't say anything. You don't know what to say. You sit on the cellar floor among your heaps of washing while the machines rumble, and your chest aches.

---

At one o'clock there's Sunday roast in the Great Hall.

It's the last hot food until Monday morning, Bea told you on the way over, quieter than usual: after this it's cold supper in your own kitchen, toast and cheese and whatever you've got. So everyone comes. The hall is packed and warm and roaring, and smells of roasting beef and gravy and Yorkshire puddings, and the hatch is serving plates piled so high they slump.

Group F ends up at one table. You're not sure how; it just happens, the way it happened at lunch on Tuesday. Priya waves from a half-empty table and you go and sit by her, and Lin comes and sits opposite you, and then Amara arrives with her tray and takes the seat across from Priya, and then, a moment later, Kit, looking for somewhere quiet, finds the only free seat in that whole stretch of the hall, which is directly opposite Amara.

They see each other at the same moment. Kit, standing with her tray. Amara, seated, her knife and fork poised.

There's a pause.

Then Kit sits down. Precisely. And Amara says, "Ashworth," and Kit says, "Osei," and they both pick up the gravy boat at the same time.

It goes on from there.

"I've been thinking about our little question," says Amara pleasantly, letting go of the gravy boat with a gracious gesture. "*Is a spell the caster's, or the focus's.* I assume you're arguing the caster."

"I'm arguing what's correct," says Kit, pouring gravy.

"Which is the caster, obviously, for you. All that will. All that *intent*." Amara slices a potato. "Very Ashworth."

"And you're arguing the focus, because of your held-object point," says Kit. "Which you made on Tuesday. Before Penhallow could nod. Very Osei."

"At least I *made* a point."

"At least mine was the question."

It's polite. That's the terrible thing. Every word of it is perfectly polite, and pitched just low enough for the table, and they both go on eating their roast beef with perfect manners, and every single word is a little knife. Priya is watching them like a tennis match, her fork suspended halfway to her mouth. Lin is looking at her plate.

You put your fork down.

"What do you actually think?" you say.

They both look at you.

"Not what you're arguing," you say. "Not for the essay. Just. What do you actually think? Whose is it?"

There's a silence. Amara looks at you, her brows up. Kit looks at you with her knife halfway through a slice of beef.

And then, for a moment, they both answer honestly.

"I think," says Amara slowly, and she's not performing now, you can hear it, "that the caster's the one who wants. The focus can't want anything. But the focus *remembers*. A good held focus remembers every spell it's ever helped make. My grandmother's ring does. So a spell's the caster's, but it's also a bit the focus's, because the focus has been there before, and knows the way."

Kit is quiet a moment.

"I think the spell's the caster's," she says. "Entirely. The focus is a tool. A pipe, Penhallow says. A pipe doesn't own the water." She stops. "But I think a focus can carry a shape, from use. Like a path worn in grass. It doesn't own the path. But it makes the path easier to walk." She frowns down at her plate. "So you don't have to supply the whole shape every time. You can lean on it."

Amara looks at her.

"That's what I said," says Amara.

"It isn't."

"It's *exactly* what I said, Ashworth, apart from the word *remembers*."

"Remembering implies a mind," says Kit sharply. "A worn path isn't a mind."

"There," says Amara, sitting back. "*There*. That's the only point. That's the whole of it. A focus remembers, or a focus is worn. That's the *only* thing we disagree on."

They look at each other across the table. And you can see it land on both of them at once: they agree on everything. Everything except one word. Kit's mouth goes thin. Amara's eyes narrow. They both look, for a moment, deeply and identically annoyed.

Priya puts her fork down and starts to laugh.

Someone sits down beside you with a full plate.

Shiv. Her hair is damp and curling and she's in her grey hoodie, and her plate is heaped with beef and three Yorkshire puddings, and she sits down right beside you on the bench, close, without asking. She doesn't look at you. She picks up her knife and fork.

Under the table, her knee bumps yours.

Once. Deliberately. Her knee against yours, warm through your jeans, and then it stays there, resting against you.

She doesn't say a word. She cuts her beef. She eats. She keeps her eyes on her plate, and the corner of her mouth goes up, very slightly.

Across the table, Lin's eyes flick down, and up.

She's seen it. You can tell. Her soft dark eyes went to the space under the table where Shiv's knee is resting against yours, and came back up, and now she's looking at her plate. You watch her face, ready for it to fall. It doesn't. She looks thoughtful. Calm. As if she's been handed something to consider and she's turning it over in her mind, slowly, the way she turns old nibs over in her fingers to look at the tips. Then she goes back to her roast potatoes.

---

In the afternoon the boathouse lends rowing boats.

It's down on the academy shore, past Shiv's rock, a long low wooden building with a slipway going down into the water and a row of old wooden boats pulled up on the shingle, blue and green and red, their paint flaking. On Sundays, Bea says, you can sign one out. She signs one out. She writes her name in a ledger on a stand by the door without even reading the rules, as if she's done it a hundred times, and drags a blue boat down the slipway into the water herself, and holds it steady for you to climb in.

Then she takes the oars.

She rows like she was born doing it. You knew she'd rowed at school; you didn't know it would look like this. Long clean strokes, unhurried, the blades going into the water without a splash and coming out dripping, the whole of her moving together, legs and back and arms, in one smooth rhythm. The boat slides out from the shore as if it's being pulled on a string. Bea's face is calm and absorbed. Her bun is coming down. The strands blow across her face and she doesn't push them away.

She rows you out into the middle of the mere.

Then she stops. She lifts the oars and lets them rest, dripping, and the boat glides on a little way and slows and stops, and sits there, rocking very gently, on the dark still water.

It's silent out here. The fells rise all around you, close and huge, gold and brown and green in the afternoon sun, their tops clear and sharp against the sky. The academy is small and far away on the shore, the grey stone and the library tower and Thornfield's roofs, like a model of itself. The water slaps softly against the boat's sides. A bird calls somewhere. Bea sits with the oars across her knees and looks at the water.

"Can I be honest?" she says.

"Yes."

She doesn't look at you. She looks at a drop of water running down the blade of one oar.

"When you said about Shiv," says Bea. "In the laundry. It went in me like a splinter." She lifts one shoulder. "I was glad. I *am* glad. I meant that. But it went in like a splinter, too, and I've been walking round with it all day, and I don't know what it is." She looks up at you, finally, and her brown eyes are wet. "Isn't that stupid?"

You look at her. At her face, flushed from rowing, and her loose hair, and her wet eyes. At the splinter in her.

And you tell her the truth.

"It's not stupid," you say. "Bea. Wanting Shiv doesn't take anything away from wanting to be near you. It's not like that. It's not a — there isn't a set amount. Wanting her doesn't use any of you up." You lean forward in the boat. "It never would. Not ever. You're—" You stop, because you don't know the end of the sentence. "You're you. You fell asleep on my shoulder. You bought me a jumper. You're the first person I met here. Whatever Shiv does, all of that stays. It doesn't work like that for me. It never has."

Bea is quiet a long time. The boat rocks.

"I don't know yet," she says at last, slowly, "what I want. With you. I really don't. I've been trying to think about it, and I can't, it's all — " She waves a hand. "But I know I want to be in your boat. Whatever that means. I know that."

"You're in my boat," you say.

Bea looks at you. And then she laughs. It comes out of her wet and surprised, a hiccupping laugh with tears in it, and she wipes her eyes with the back of her wrist.

And then she puts the blade of one oar in the water and flicks it, hard, and splashes you.

A great cold sheet of mere water, all down your front. You shriek. She laughs harder.

---

She lets you try rowing.

You swap places, carefully, the boat tilting alarmingly, both of you clutching the sides. You sit on the middle seat and take the oars, and they're much heavier than they looked, and much longer, and they don't go where you want them to. You pull. One blade bites, and the other skims across the top of the water and sprays Bea, and the boat swings round.

You pull again. It swings round again.

You go in a slow, stately circle in the middle of the mere. Then another one. You try to correct it, and go round the other way.

Bea is laughing so hard the boat rocks. She's lying back in the stern with her arms over her face, shaking, and every time you try a new stroke and the boat turns another lazy circle she makes a high helpless noise, and the boat rocks harder, and the water slops over the side.

"*Both*," she gasps. "Both *together*, Nala, you have to pull both *together*—"

You pull both together. You go in a circle.

---

In the evening the library is open until midnight, so you take your essay and the new pen.

The reading room is quieter on a Sunday night than it was on Friday afternoon, only a few girls scattered along the long tables under the green lamps, heads bent, the gold stars on the dark blue ceiling far above. The windows are black. It's warm, and very still.

At the far end of one table, alone, under a lamp, sits Kit.

She has books spread out round her and an essay in progress, many pages of it, in small precise handwriting. She looks up as you come in. You hesitate in the doorway with your notebook against your chest.

Kit looks at you for a moment. Then, without a word, she reaches out and moves a stack of her books. Sideways, along the table, off the place beside her. Making room.

You go and sit down in it.

You work in silence for an hour.

Neither of you speaks. You write, and cross out, and write again, and read a page of one of Priya's books, and write a sentence. Kit writes steadily beside you, page after page, turning them over with a small crisp sound. Now and then she reaches for a book and looks something up. Now and then you hear her breathe out, sharply, through her nose, when she disagrees with something. The green lamp makes a pool of warm light round the two of you and the dark goes up all round, to the stars.

It's the easiest silence you've had since you arrived. You don't have to be anything in it. You don't have to explain anything, or hide anything, or work out who Nala Ashby is supposed to be. You just sit beside Kit in the lamplight and work, and she lets you.

At the end of the hour Kit puts down her pen.

"Can I see it?" she says. Low. "Your paragraph."

You hesitate. Then you slide the page across to her: the paragraph you wrote last night with the new pen, about the thread and the mere and wanting light.

Kit reads it. You watch her eyes go along the lines. She gets to the end, and goes back to the beginning, and reads it again. Slower this time.

She puts it down.

"This is good," she says. She sounds faintly put out about it. "Annoyingly."

You don't know what to say. Your face is warm.

"Penhallow will like it," Kit says. She taps the page with one finger. "Not because of anything you read. Because you wrote it. It's yours. You can tell it's yours." She pushes it back across the table to you. "Most people read three books and write down what the books said. You've written down what happened to you. That's what she wants. That's what she always wants, my grandmother said. She hardly ever gets it."

You glow. You can feel yourself doing it, sitting there in the lamplight, a slow warm glow spreading up from your chest into your face.

---

You walk out together at ten, into the cold dark quad, your breath smoking under the lamps.

At the bottom of the library steps Kit stops. She's looking across the quad, not at you, towards Ashcombe, her books against her chest.

"Would you," she says. Very offhand. Very casual. Looking at Ashcombe. "Go to the cinema again. Sometime."

"Yes," you say.

Kit nods. Once, sharply, as if a box has been ticked.

And then she's walking away, quickly, across the frosty grass towards Ashcombe, her bob swinging, her coat flapping, so fast that she's gone into the dark before you can see her face at all.

---

Back at Thornfield the kitchen is warm and bright, and it's cold supper: toast and cheese, and a jar of pickle someone's left on the side.

Bea is there, at the counter, with the kettle. And Lin is there too, up from downstairs, sitting at the table in her cardigan with her hair loose.

Lin looks up when you come in. She waits until you sit down. Then she puts something on the table in front of you: a small twist of paper, folded neatly, about the size of a sweet.

You unfold it. Inside, on the paper, there's a nib. One of the old ones from the shoebox in Kendal. It's been cleaned: all the old rust and ink and grime gone from it, the steel polished till it shines, the tip clean and fine and gleaming. It's lying on the paper like a small silver leaf.

"It's soft," Lin says quietly. "Like the one I lent you." She looks at the nib, not at you. "It's yours now."

You pick it up off the paper very carefully, between finger and thumb, and hold it in your palm like treasure. You look at her.

"Lin," you say. You don't know what else to say. "Thank you."

She goes pink, and looks at the table, and smiles.

Bea turns from the counter with three mugs in her hands. She hasn't asked anyone how they want it. She just puts them down: the yellow daisy mug in front of Lin, the fat blue chipped one in front of you, and her own. And as she sets yours down she bumps your shoulder with hers, gently, the way she did on the shore on your first day.

You look up at her.

And her smile is real again. Not too bright. Not one notch up. Just Bea: warm, with both dimples, her eyes clear.

---

In bed, the radiator knocks.

You lie in the dark on your back, your hands folded on your stomach. Your mouth is still remembering the mere. You can still feel it, if you think about it: cold and then warm, the taste of clean water and stone, the drops on her freckles.

One kiss, you think.

Five girls.

And Monday.
