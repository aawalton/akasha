On Wednesday the world has gone grey again, and very still.

The fells are hidden. The far shore is hidden. The mere lies flat and pale under a lid of mist so thick that from the shingle you can see perhaps twenty yards of water, and then only white. The safety boat is a dim shape. The whistle sounds muffled, as if it's coming from another room.

You and Shiv swim out into it.

It closes round you at once. No shore, no boathouse, no other swimmers, just the grey water under your chin and the white all round, and the sound of your own breathing, and Shiv's beside you. You find the buoy by its orange glow, looming up out of the mist suddenly a few yards ahead, and grab it, and hang there.

It's very quiet. The kind of quiet that makes you want to whisper.

"That question," Shiv says, treading water. "Penhallow's. On your essay. What did it mean?"

"I'm still thinking," you say.

"*Whose wanting*," Shiv repeats. She looks at you through the mist, water running off her nose, her hair plastered down. "Yours, eejit." She says it with enormous patience, as if to a small child. "Whose else would it be?"

You laugh.

You laugh with your mouth open, and a wave of mere slaps in, and you swallow it, and choke. Cold, peaty, foul. You cough and splutter and go under and come up spluttering, and Shiv is laughing too now, and thumping you on the back, and she keeps thumping you on the back, grinning, all the way to shore.

---

At breakfast there's a slip of paper in your pigeonhole.

A small printed form, with your name filled in by hand. *N. ASHBY. Please call at the Bursar's Office, 2 p.m. today, to sign papers.*

You stand in the hall by the pigeonholes and read it twice.

Bea reads it over your shoulder, her chin practically on it.

"Oh, that's just the bursary forms," she says. "Everyone gets one. I had mine last week. You sign for your money and they ask your blood group. It's *so* dull." She steals your toast.

---

At ten it's Sigils, in the Drawing Room.

"Mending," says the tutor, at her easel, and draws it: the line, the split into two, the two halves running side by side, the join, the small closed loop where they meet. "A page, please. Slowly. It wants a steady hand at the join."

You breathe out, and draw.

Lin's, beside you, are perfect again. They come off her nib one after another, the halves running exactly parallel, the joins so clean you can't see where one stroke ends and the next begins. Yours aren't perfect. But they come out loose and even, the splits wide and easy, the loops closed. The tutor stops at your desk on her way past, and looks, and nods. Just a nod. And goes on.

A scrap of paper slides onto your desk from the left.

It's a tiny drawing in ink. A teacup, with a crack down it, and the crack mended, with a seam. And on the cup, two dots and a curve: a small smiling face.

You look across. Lin is looking very hard at her own page, and her ears are pink.

You fold the scrap and put it in your pocket.

---

At lunch Kit comes and sits with you and Bea.

She puts her tray down opposite you both and sits very upright, and arranges her cutlery, and looks at Bea across the table.

"Lindqvist," she says. Very formally. "Are you still free this evening? At six."

"Free," says Bea. "*Free*? I've cleared my whole diary."

"You don't have a diary," says Kit.

"That's beside the point," says Bea, with great dignity.

Kit's mouth goes in at the corner, and she picks up her fork.

---

At two you go to the porters' lodge.

Behind the lodge, through a door with a frosted glass panel, there's a small panelled room: dark wood, a green-shaded lamp, a long desk with a blotter on it, and filing cabinets all along one wall. It smells of old paper and furniture polish. Behind the desk is the bursar's clerk.

She's elderly and brisk, very thin, with her white hair in a bun and her glasses on the end of her nose. She doesn't get up.

"Ashby," she says. "Sit."

You sit.

She has a file in front of her. A buff cardboard file, quite thin, with *ASHBY, N.* written on the tab. She opens it and turns it round and puts a form in front of you, and a pen.

"Allowance," she says. "For the term. Eighty pounds a week, paid Mondays. Sign there. And there." She taps.

You sign. You sign in your new hand, the careful one you've been learning, *N. Ashby*.

"Good," says the clerk. She turns a page in the file. "Now. Next of kin."

She runs her finger down the page.

"Your file doesn't list any," she says. "Not one." She reads. "You were in the care of Calderdale council."

You sit very still.

The room is very quiet. The lamp hums. Somewhere outside, through the frosted glass, the porter is whistling.

The clerk looks up at you over her glasses.

"Until eighteen, it says," she says. Not unkindly. Just plainly, the way she said *eighty pounds*. "You knew that."

You look at her. At the file.

"Yes," you say.

Because the Nala who sat in this chair before you would have. Because she knew it. She'd have known it all her life.

Your hands are cold in your lap. You fold them together to keep them still.

"An emergency contact, then," the clerk says. She slides another form across. "Someone we can ring if you're ill. Anyone at all. It needn't be family." She looks at you. "A friend will do. Or leave it blank, if you'd rather. Plenty do."

You look at the empty box on the form.

You think of the cheap phone in your trunk, with not one name in it. Not one number. Not one message, not one photograph. You think of the room above the shop in Hebden Bridge on your letter, the room you've never seen. You think of a girl, alone in a room above a shop, with her trunk stencilled N. ASHBY and her acceptance letter, and her full bursary, and not one person in the world coming to see her off.

You pick up the pen.

*Beatrix Lindqvist,* you write. *Thornfield House, room 15.*

The clerk reads it upside down. She nods once, and blots it.

Then she hands you a receipt for the allowance, a small pink slip, and closes the file.

"Good day, Miss Ashby," she says.

---

Outside it's cold. The mist hasn't lifted. The quad is grey and dripping and empty.

There's a low stone wall by the lodge, under a dripping tree. You sit down on it, in the cold, with the pink receipt in your hand.

And you find you're crying.

A little. Not much. Quietly, the tears just coming, running down your cold cheeks, and you let them.

Not for yourself. You're all right. You have Bea across the corridor, and Shiv in the mere, and Kit in the hall, and Lin with her charcoal. You have a phone that's about to have names in it.

For her.

For the girl whose life this was. Who grew up in care, in Calderdale, with an empty box in her file where a family should be. Who grew up alone, and worked, and studied, and passed whatever it was she had to pass, and won a full bursary to a college of magic, and packed a trunk, and came here. Anyway. Alone. With an empty phone.

And then didn't wake up in her own bed.

You don't know where she is. You don't know if she's anywhere. But you're sitting in her body, on a wall, in the cold, holding her receipt.

"I'll look after it," you say. Under your breath. To her. "Your life. As well as I can."

The tree drips on you.

You wipe your face on your sleeve, and get up.

---

The afternoon is free. Wednesday afternoons always are.

You walk the shore path alone. Out of the gates and along the water, the mile to the village, in the mist. The mere is a pale flat blank beside you, the shingle grey, the path wet underfoot. You don't meet anyone. You don't want to.

At the village you go down to the jetty, and sit on the low wall.

The wall where Bea kissed you. You put your hand flat on the cold stone where she sat.

And you sit a while, and watch the mist lift off the water.

It goes slowly. It thins, and lifts, and tears into long white rags that drift up off the surface and away, and behind them the mere comes back, grey and then silver, and then the far shore, dark, and then, last of all, the fells, one after another, appearing out of the white, their tops still dusted with snow.

---

Back at Thornfield, you knock on 15.

Bea is at her desk with her books. She turns round on her chair, and sees your face, and stops.

You sit on the end of her bed and tell her about the form.

Just the form. Not the file, not the council, not the rest. Just the box that said *emergency contact*, and the clerk, and what you wrote in it.

Bea goes quiet.

"Me?" she says. "As your — your emergency person?"

You nod.

Bea gets up off her chair, and crosses the room, and hugs you. So hard. Her arms right round you, squeezing, so hard your ribs creak.

"There isn't anyone else?" she asks, into your hair. Careful. Very careful.

"There isn't anyone," you say.

Bea doesn't say anything. She just holds on. Longer. Much longer. Rocking you a little, without seeming to notice she's doing it.

---

At six it's the empty Practice Hall.

You sit on the front bench with Bea beside you. Bea is sitting astonishingly straight. Her back like a ruler, her hands folded in her lap, her feet together on the floorboards, her eyes very wide and fixed on Kit.

"I'm being still," Bea whispers. "I'm being *so* still."

Kit stands in the middle of the floor with her sleeves pushed up. She breathes out. She looks at you.

Then she looks at Bea.

And freezes.

Two pairs of eyes. You see it hit her. Her hand comes up and draws the stroke and the loop, and stops in the air at the hook, and stays there. Rigid. Her face goes white, so white. Her eyes go from you to Bea and back, and she isn't breathing.

"Out," you say.

She can't. You can see her trying. Her chest is locked, her shoulders up round her ears, her hand stiff in the air. She can't let it go.

Beside you, Bea is desperate to help.

You can feel it coming off her. She's leaning forward. She's gripping her own knees. And, as if somehow it'll make her stiller, or help, or something, she takes a great breath and holds it. She holds it so hard her face goes pink, then red, then a deep alarming crimson, her cheeks puffed out, her eyes bulging slightly—

And she squeaks.

A tiny, high, strangled squeak, escaping out of the side of her mouth.

Kit stares at her.

And laughs.

Helplessly. Startled right out of her. A sudden sharp crack of a laugh, from her chest, and her shoulders drop with it, and her breath goes out in it all at once—

And the glim comes on the laugh.

It's just there. Small, at first, a flicker above her hand. Then steady. A pale clear bead of light, hanging in the air between the three of you, while Kit is still laughing.

"One," you say. "Two. Three. Four."

Kit's eyes are on it. Bea's eyes are on it. Bea's face is still crimson.

"Five. Six. Seven."

On eight Bea gasps for air, a huge whooping gasp, and the glim goes out, and all three of you are laughing. Bea doubled over on the bench. Kit with her hand over her face. You holding on to the edge of the bench.

"I'm sorry," Bea gasps. "I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, I was being *still*—"

Kit casts it again.

With both of you watching. Bea with her breath going in and out quite normally this time, being told very firmly by you to *breathe*. Kit breathes out before she starts. The sigil. The word. The glim, small and pale, then steady. You count. It holds. To ten.

Bea claps. Then claps both hands over her mouth.

Kit lowers her hand. She's flushed, high on her cheekbones, pink right up into her hair. She looks at the two of you on the bench.

"Thursday," she says. "Three."

She looks at Bea.

"Bring Lin," she says. "If she'd come."

---

At eight it's the poetry society, in the Snug.

Up the short stair in the old library, into the panelled room with its iron grate and its fire, and its sagging green sofas, and seven girls on them, and the third-year with silver rings on every finger in the armchair by the hearth.

Tonight Kit reads first.

She's in the corner chair, in black, with her slim book on her knee. When the third-year nods to her, she opens it. And her knuckles whiten on the page, the way they did a week ago. And her eyes come up from the page and look across the room.

At you.

Last time you looked away. Into the fire. Counting coals.

This time you hold her eyes.

You don't look away. You look at her, steadily, gently, the way you did in the hall. You let her see you looking.

Kit breathes out.

And reads. Low and steady.

It's a short poem. About a girl learning to swim in cold water. A little at a time. Her feet first, off a rock, shrieking. Then to her knees. Then a minute, two, the cold going in and in. Then out, a long way out, to where the water's deep, breathing out through it. It's only a few lines. Kit's voice doesn't shake once.

Then the silence after, as the rule asks. No clapping. Just the fire.

In the silence Kit keeps looking at you.

And you keep looking back.

---

Your turn comes later.

You take the battered anthology from the Snug's shelf again, and find a short one, about a lamp in a window. Someone walking home in the dark across a field, and seeing it, and knowing it's for them. You read it slowly. You don't rush the ends of the lines. You let it be quiet where it wants to be quiet.

It's better. Not good, maybe. But better.

In the corner, Kit's mouth goes in at the corner.

And the third-year with the rings, by the fire, murmurs: "Better."

The fire cracks.

---

Kit walks you back across the quad.

The mist has gone. The night is cold and clear and full of stars. At Thornfield's door she stops on the step, and looks at you in the light from the porch lamp.

"That poem," Kit says. "Was for you."

"I know," you say.

She kisses you. On the step, quick and sure, her hands cold on your face.

Then she goes. Back across the quad towards Ashcombe, in the dark. And under the lamp, as she turns, you can see her ears are scarlet.

---

In 14 you open your trunk.

At the bottom, under the books, wrapped in a jumper, is the cheap phone. You take it out and switch it on, and the small screen lights up blue in the dim room.

Contacts. You scroll. There aren't any. Still not one. A clean empty list.

You take it across the corridor and knock on 15.

"Can I have your number?" you ask.

Bea, in bed, sits up, and grabs her own phone off the bedside table. She reads her number out. Twice, slowly, watching you type it. Then she scrolls through hers and finds Lin's, and reads that out too.

"Kit's?" she says.

"I'll ask her," you say. "Tomorrow. In History."

Back in 14, you sit on your bed and type them in. Slowly. Carefully. Checking every digit.

*Bea.*

*Lin.*

Then you open a note, and type the rest. The ones without numbers.

*Shiv — the rock — Sundays.*

*Kit — Hall — six.*

You look at the screen.

Two names, and a note. Glowing small and blue in the dark.

It isn't much. But it's more than the girl before you had in this phone.

You switch it off. You slide it under your pillow, beside Kit's black handkerchief, and turn out the lamp.
