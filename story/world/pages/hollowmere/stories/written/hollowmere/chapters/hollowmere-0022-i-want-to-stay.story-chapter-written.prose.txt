On Monday you wake in 15 again.

The light is grey at the edges of Bea's thick curtains. The bed beside you is empty and still warm. And Bea is up, standing by the radiator in her vest, hopping on one foot, pulling on a pair of running tights.

She sees you're awake.

"Run," she says. "Monday."

You groan into her pillow.

"You've gone soft," says Bea, getting the other leg in, "on swimming. Swimming isn't running. Swimming is floating about in cold water looking pleased with yourself." She throws your trainers at the bed. "Up."

---

You run the shore with her.

Out along the path in the cold grey morning, the mere flat and pale on your left, the shingle crunching, the fells standing up sharp and still with their white tops. Bea's slow pace, the one she keeps for you: easy, loping, her ponytail swinging. She talks for the first five minutes, and then she stops talking, and you just run.

Ten minutes. The point where it used to give out, that first week. It doesn't.

Fifteen. Your chest burning, your legs heavy.

Twenty.

You finish it. Twenty minutes, all the way, at her pace, and you stagger to a stop on the shingle below the boathouse with your hands on your knees, gasping, red in the face, and alive.

Bea whoops. A huge whoop, her arms in the air, that sends a heron flapping up off the shallows.

"*Twenty!*" she shouts. "Nala! Twenty minutes!" And then: "Right. Do the last bit again."

"*What*—"

"The last bit. To the boathouse and back. Go on. You've got it in you." She's jogging on the spot, grinning. "*Go*."

You do the last bit again.

---

At breakfast the post is out.

The pigeonholes in the hall outside the Great Hall are stuffed: envelopes and parcels and postcards, girls crowding round them. Lin is standing at hers, by herself, with a thin blue envelope in her hand. Airmail-thin, pale blue, the stamp in the corner.

You watch her from the doorway.

She opens it there, standing up. She reads it. She stands very still while she reads it, quite still, in the middle of the crowd of girls jostling for their post, as if she were alone in the hall.

Then she folds it. Small. Into quarters, and then again. And puts it in the pocket of her long pleated skirt.

At the table, she sits down beside you with a bowl of porridge.

She doesn't eat it. She holds the spoon, and looks at the porridge, and the porridge goes cold and grows a skin.

"Lin?" you say. Quietly. "Are you all right?"

Lin doesn't look up.

"My mother," she says. Very quietly.

And stops.

---

At ten it's the Foundations lecture, in the Long Room.

Lin sits beside you. Most days her pen is out the whole hour, drawing in the margins, the lecturer's hands, the girl in front's plait, the window, whatever's there. Her pen is always moving.

Today it lies still on the desk beside her notebook.

Her hands are in her lap.

You look at them, folded together, still, the charcoal on the fingertips. And under the desk, you reach across, and rest your hand on her knee.

Lin doesn't move. She doesn't look at you.

But she lets it stay.

---

At two it's the Practice Hall.

On every bench there's a candle. A plain white candle in a brass holder, lit, the flames small and yellow in the grey afternoon light. And on the board at the front, chalked large, there's a new sigil: a circle, not quite closed, with three short strokes standing out from it like the rays of a sun drawn by a child.

"The ward," says the demonstrator. "Week four." She stands by the board. "A ward is a shell. A faint one. You draw it round a thing to keep the world off it. Today, a flame." She nods at the high windows. "There's a draught in this hall. There always is. A ward drawn round your candle will still the flame, so the draught can't touch it." She claps her hands. "Benches."

You go to your bench.

And Kit walks past the end bench.

You see it. You see her come in through the doors, and head for the end bench, the one at the far end with its back to the room, the one she always takes; and you see her get there, and look at it, and walk past it.

She takes a middle bench.

Right in the middle of the hall. Between yours and Amara's. In full view of everyone.

She puts her bag down. She looks along the benches, once. At you.

And breathes out.

Then she turns to her candle.

---

You draw your ward.

The circle, not quite closed, the three short strokes. On the out-breath, slowly, round the candle flame. And you feel it go round. A faint thin shell, like a soap bubble, curving round the little flame.

A draught comes down from the high windows. The flame bends in it. Leans over, hard, flattening, nearly going out—

And then holds. Straightens. And stands, wavering a little, inside your bubble.

At the next bench, Bea's candle goes out entirely.

A little puff of smoke, and the flame's gone. Bea glares at it. She strikes a match from the box on her bench, and relights it, with a black look, and draws her ward again, and it goes out again.

She relights it again. With an even blacker look.

You don't laugh. You very carefully don't laugh.

---

On the middle bench, Kit draws her ward.

And Amara, at the next bench, is watching her. Openly. Not pretending not to. Leaning on her own bench with her arms folded, watching Kit's hands.

Kit knows it. You can see she knows it. Her shoulders go up, a little. Her hand, drawing the circle in the air round the flame, slows.

Slows.

And goes on.

The three short strokes. The ward closes round her candle.

And the flame stands still.

Perfectly still. Not a flicker. Not a waver. A small gold drop of fire, upright and motionless, standing in the draught from the high windows as if the air round it were made of glass. Every other candle in the hall is bending and jumping. Kit's is a gold drop, hanging there, still as a painted flame.

The demonstrator, walking the benches, stops.

She looks at it a long moment.

"Ashworth," she says. "Good."

Kit keeps her eyes on her flame. She doesn't look up. She doesn't move.

Her ears go scarlet.

---

Amara is staring at Kit's flame.

Then at Kit. At Kit's scarlet ears, and her rigid back, and her eyes fixed on her candle, not looking at anyone. And something in Amara's face changes. You see it happen. Something moves behind it, and settles, like a sum that's finally come out.

"Oh," says Amara.

Kit's jaw tightens.

"*That's* why the end bench," Amara says. Low. Just to Kit, across the gap between their benches. Not loud. Not for anyone else.

Kit's jaw sets. Hard. She stares at her flame.

And Amara says: "That's brave, Ashworth."

Kit turns her head.

She looks at Amara. Astonished. Completely, openly astonished, her mouth a little open, her grey-green eyes wide.

Amara doesn't look back. She has already turned to her own candle. She draws her own ward, quickly, and it goes round her flame, and wobbles. It actually wobbles. Her flame leans in the draught and jumps.

Amara doesn't seem to notice.

---

Lin's ward, on her bench under the window, is perfect.

A fine clear shell round her flame, so thin you can barely see it, and the flame inside standing straight and calm. It's the best ward in the hall after Kit's, maybe better.

But Lin's face is somewhere else entirely.

She's looking at the flame, and not seeing it. Her eyes are far away. Miles away. In Manchester, maybe. You can't tell.

---

After practical, Lin touches your sleeve.

Just her fingertips on your sleeve, in the doorway, as everyone's going out.

"Will you come up?" she says. Quietly. "To 8?" A pause. "I need to show you something."

---

Room 8 is the same as before. Drawings on every wall, edge to edge. The fells, the hands, the mere at dawn, the girls on the quad, the heron in the shallows.

Lin sits down on her bed.

You sit beside her.

She takes the blue envelope out of her skirt pocket, and unfolds the letter. It's written in pen, in characters you can't read: close and neat and running down the thin blue page in columns.

"My mother writes every Monday," Lin says. "Every Monday since I came. In Cantonese." She smooths the page on her knee. "I'll read it to you. In English."

She reads it to you slowly. Translating as she goes, her finger moving down the columns.

The restaurant is busy. Saturday they had a wedding party, forty people, and the new cook burned the rice. Her father's back is bad again; he can't lift the big pans; he has to sit down between orders, and he won't go to the doctor. Her sister is in London, and rings on Sundays, and is well, and has a new job, and doesn't come home.

And then the last line.

Lin stops. She looks at it.

"She asks," Lin says, "if I've told the academy yet." Her voice is very level. "That I'm only staying the one year. Then home."

You look at her.

Lin looks at the letter.

"They expect it," she says. "It was always — that was always how it was going to be. One year. Then home, to help. I never said no." She folds the letter in half. "I never said anything."

She's quiet a moment.

"I thought I'd go back," Lin says. "Before I came. I really did. I thought one year would be enough." She looks down at her hands. "It isn't."

She looks round at her walls.

At the fells, and the heron, and the hands. The mere at dawn. The girls on the quad. All of it. Everything she's drawn in a month. Every wall of the room covered in it.

"What do you want?" you ask her.

*Whose wanting*, you think.

Lin's eyes fill.

"I want to stay," she says. Her voice cracks on it. "To draw. To finish. To be here." She takes a breath. It shakes.

"With you," says Lin.

---

You hold her while she cries.

Quietly. The way she does everything. Her face against your shoulder and her body very still and the tears just coming, soaking warm through your jumper, and her charcoal fingers holding on to your sleeve.

"I don't know how to tell them," she says, after a long while, into your shoulder. "Dad's back. The restaurant. My sister went. I'm the one who stays home. I'm the one who's supposed to stay."

You hold her.

"Today," you say, "you only have to tell yourself."

Lin goes still.

"That's all," you say. "Today. Just you. You've done that. You just did." You stroke her hair. "Them later."

She's quiet for a long time.

Then she nods. Against your shoulder. Once, and then again.

---

After a while she sits up, and wipes her face.

She gets up off the bed, and kneels, and reaches under it, and pulls out a big flat folder. Grey card, tied with tape.

"The drawings," she says. "From Thursday."

She unties the tape.

"I can look at them now," Lin says.

She lays them out on the floor. One by one. All nine. Across the boards between the bed and the window, in rows, until the floor is covered in them.

They're you.

On the dais, in the lamplight. Sitting on the stool with your knee drawn up and your arm resting on it. Your face turned to the windows. Your hair down your back. Again, and again, from where she sat, nine times, each one a little different, a little closer, a little surer. Quick, and sure, and alive, the charcoal going in long confident lines and soft smudges of shadow. Your whole body, all of it, the way it was on the dais under the lamps.

Seen whole. And seen kindly.

There isn't anything cruel in them. There isn't anything hungry in them, either. They're just you, exactly you, and someone looking at you very hard and very gently for a very long time.

And in one of them, the last, you aren't looking at the windows.

You're looking straight out of the paper. At the artist.

At her.

Lin reaches out and touches the edge of that one, with one charcoal fingertip.

"You looked at me," she says. "Then."

You kneel down on the floor among them.

And you find you're blushing. Deep, hot, all the way up, all the way down. And smiling. And you can't stop either.

---

"Would you sit for me?" Lin asks.

She's kneeling on the other side of the drawings, very pink.

"Here," she says. "Now. Just for me." A pause. She looks at you. "Not clothed. This time."

"Yes," you say.

You undress in her room.

In the low afternoon light, the gold coming in through her window over the quad. You take off your clothes, one thing after another, and fold them on the end of her bed, and you sit on her window seat, the way you sat last time, with the quad behind you and the light on you.

Lin picks up her board, and her charcoal.

And she draws you. For an hour. Slowly. Not quick, the way she did on Thursday, but slow, long lines, careful, coming back to things, looking a long time between strokes.

And this time you watch her the whole time.

You don't look out of the window. You look at her. At her face, bent over the paper, and then lifting, her eyes on you, and then down again. At her charcoal hand. At the small crease between her brows when she's thinking. At the way she bites her lip.

She knows you're watching. She lets you.

---

When the light goes, she puts the charcoal down.

She sets the board against the wall, and gets up, and comes across the room, and kneels on the floor in front of the window seat.

She looks up at you.

She reaches out and touches your bare knee. With one charcoal fingertip. Very lightly. And when she takes it away, there's a small grey mark there, a smudge, a fingerprint.

She looks up at you.

And then she kisses you.

Slowly. Rising up on her knees, her hands coming to rest on your bare waist. Her mouth soft, and careful, and warm. You kiss her back. And she trembles. All of her, under your hands, a fine fast trembling, like a bird held.

"Soon," Lin says, against your mouth.

You feel her breath.

"I want to," she says. "Soon. When I'm not crying."

And you laugh, both of you, together, her forehead against yours.

---

You dress.

Lin keeps the drawing. The hour's one, the slow one. She looks at it a long time, and then she climbs up on her bed and pins it on the wall over her pillow. In the middle of everything. Among the fells and the heron and the hands. Right in the centre.

---

In the evening the Thornfield kitchen door bangs open, and Priya bursts in.

She's back from charmcraft. There's oil on her cheek and her glasses are crooked and her eyes are huge, enormous, behind them.

"Amara held my hand," Priya says.

To the whole room. To anyone at all.

"In the basement," she says. "Under the bench. With the clockwork."

At the stove, Bea drops a spoon. It clatters on the floor.

Priya sits down. Very suddenly, on the nearest bench, as if her legs have gone.

"I have to ring Dev," she says. She's staring at the table. "I have to." A breath. "I have to, don't I."

She gets up. She goes out.

She's gone an hour.

When she comes back she's pale. Pale and calm. Very calm, quite still, as if something heavy has been lifted off her.

"I've done it," she says.

Bea makes her cocoa. A big mug, too much sugar. And she sits down with her, on one side, and you sit down on her other side. Priya holds the mug in both hands.

"I feel light," Priya says. Wonderingly. "I feel so *light*."

---

At eleven there's a knock on 15.

You and Bea are in bed. Bea calls *come in*, and it's Lin, in her pyjamas and a cardigan, with a sheet of paper in her hand. Lined paper. Written on in pencil, half a page. In English.

"To my mother," Lin says.

She holds it out a little, as if to show you it's real.

"I haven't sent it," she says. "I wrote it. That's the first thing."

Bea moves over. She shifts across in the narrow bed and pats the covers, and makes room.

Lin sits on the end of Bea's bed, between your feet and Bea's. And she reads it to you. Aloud. In a whisper, in the lamplight. About the drawings, and the walls of room 8, and the heron, and Dr Penhallow's green ink. About how much there is still to learn.

And then the last line.

*I want to stay.*

---

Lin sleeps in her own room.

She goes down to 8 with her pencil letter folded in her cardigan pocket, and you hear her door close softly below.

And you sleep in 15. With Bea warm at your back, her arm over you. And the charcoal mark still on your knee, small and grey, where Lin's fingertip touched it.
