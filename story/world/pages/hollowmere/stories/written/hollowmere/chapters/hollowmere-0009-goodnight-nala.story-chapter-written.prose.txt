On Tuesday, dawn finds you on Shiv's rock.

Three minutes in the mere now. You go in off the edge with her hand in yours, and the cold hits like a fist the way it always does, and you come up and gasp once, one sharp breath, and then breathe out, slow, and that's all. No shriek. No panic. You tread water face to face with her in the grey light with your heart slamming and your skin roaring, and you watch the light come up pink on the fells, and you count with her: one minute, two, three.

On the rock afterwards she wraps you in the parka and rubs your arms, and you stamp your feet and chatter, and when you can talk again you tell her.

About Bea. About the Practice Hall, and the corner, and Bea's glims going up halfway and fizzling. About finding her here, on this rock, yesterday afternoon, with her knees up. About turning her hook inward with your hand on hers, and the little gold light, and Bea shrieking. And about the kiss on your cheek, slow, and staying there. *I think I'm finding out what I want.*

You watch Shiv's face as you say it. You don't know what you're expecting.

Shiv grins.

"Good," she says. "She's lovely, that one." She says it the way she'd say the water's cold, or the sun's up: as if it's plainly, obviously true and she's pleased about it. "Lindqvist. Always laughing. Good."

And she leans in and kisses you. Her mouth is salt and cold from the mere, and then warm, the way it is every time. It feels like a reply. *Yes. Good. That too.*

---

At breakfast Bea sits close.

Closer than usual. On the bench beside you, her hip against yours, her shoulder against yours, her honey-blonde hair in a fresh bun that's already starting to come down. She's shy this morning, and bright with it: she keeps glancing at you sideways and then away, pink-cheeked, and laughing at things that aren't funny.

She bumps your shoulder once as she sits down. And again, two minutes later, before the porridge has even come, for no reason at all.

You bump her back.

She goes pinker, and grins into her tea.

---

At nine o'clock Penhallow's study smells of shortbread and old paper, and the essays are due on Friday.

"Three days," says Dr Penhallow, surveying the six of you over her glasses with every sign of enjoyment. "I don't want to read them yet. I want to hear one line. Each of you. One sentence of your argument, the one you'd stake the essay on. Raman."

Priya, eagerly: "That intent can be measured indirectly, by what it costs the caster, the way you can measure a current by the heat in a wire."

"Hm," says Penhallow, pleased. "Osei. Ashworth."

Amara: "That a focus remembers."

Kit, at the same moment: "That a focus is worn."

They look at each other. Penhallow's mouth twitches.

"Zhao."

Lin, very softly: "That the hook turns in because the light has to come back to you. Or it would just keep going."

Penhallow looks at her for a long moment. "Oh, that's nice," she says quietly. "Doyle."

Shiv, slouched: "That a rule everyone breaks isn't a rule, it's a hope."

Penhallow snorts. "Ashby."

You've been dreading it. You say it anyway, because it's the truest line you've got.

"That it comes from wherever the wanting lives."

Dr Penhallow says, "Hm."

That's all. She doesn't smile. She doesn't frown. She just looks at you over the top of her glasses, and then she picks up her green pen and pulls a notebook towards her, and writes something down in it. Carefully. For a long time.

Which is worse. Much worse. You sit on your stool with your face hot, trying to see what she's writing upside down, and you can't.

---

At lunchtime the societies fair fills the Great Hall.

The long tables have been pushed back against the walls and the whole floor is crammed with trestles, dozens of them, each one draped with a cloth and hung with a banner, hand-painted, bright: oars crossed, a quill, a cogwheel, a wave. Girls stand behind them shouting offers. Girls swarm between them with sign-up sheets and leaflets and free biscuits. The noise is like the Bell on Friday night, only with more clipboards.

"*Rowing*," says Bea, the moment she's through the doors, and is gone.

You find her two minutes later at a table under a banner of crossed blue oars, already signing a sheet while a broad-shouldered third-year captain is still in the middle of a sentence. "—and we train four mornings a week, so it's a serious commit—" says the captain, and Bea says, "Yes," and hands back the pen, and the captain stops, and looks at her, and starts to smile.

Amara signs for debating without seeming to stop walking. A girl at the table under a banner of two crossed speech bubbles holds out a pen as she passes, and Amara takes it, signs, hands it back, and moves on, all in one smooth motion, as if she'd been planning it since she was eleven.

Priya hovers at charmcraft.

The charmcraft table is covered in small machines. Brass and wood and wire, whirring and ticking, and in the middle of them a clockwork mouse, about the size of your thumb, running round and round on the cloth in tight little circles. Priya isn't signing anything. She's crouched at eye level with the mouse, asking the society's secretary a stream of questions. "But where's the key? There isn't a key. So how is it wound? Is it wound? Is it the *charm* that's doing the winding, or is the charm *replacing* the spring?" The secretary is beginning to look hunted.

Lin lingers at life drawing.

You watch her from a little way off. The table is quiet compared to the others, with a few charcoal sketches pinned up on a board behind it: figures, nudes, quick and fluid. Lin stands in front of it for a long time. She reads the sign-up sheet. Then she reads it again, all the way down, slowly. Then she picks up the pen, and signs. Very small. At the very bottom of the sheet, in tiny neat letters, as if she hopes it will go unnoticed.

Then she sees you watching, and goes pink, and puts the pen down very fast.

At a table near the windows there's a banner of a blue wave, and under it a hand-lettered sign: COLD WATER SWIMMING SOCIETY. THE MERE AT DAWN. LEGALLY.

You stop.

"We swim the mere," says the girl behind the table, a cheerful second-year with very red cheeks. "Every morning at dawn, all year. With permission. There's a safety boat, and a register, and you sign in and out." She beams. "It's completely legal."

Shiv, beside you, reads the sign over your shoulder. She snorts.

"Swimming with a nanny," she says.

The second-year's beam falters.

You pick up the pen. You sign: *N. Ashby*.

Shiv looks at your name on the sheet. She looks at you. Her mouth twists up on one side.

Then she takes the pen out of your hand, and bends, and signs directly under you, in a big sprawling scrawl: *Doyle (reluctant)*.

And hands the pen back to the second-year with a wink.

---

At the poetry table, there's Kit.

It's a small table at the very end of the hall, almost in a corner, with a modest banner of a quill and a single sheet of paper on the cloth. The girl who runs it has gone off somewhere. Kit is standing in front of it alone, in black, reading the list of meetings pinned to the front. There's a pen in her hand already. She's holding it the way you'd hold something you're about to put down and walk away from.

She sees you coming.

"For a friend," she says.

You don't say anything. You walk up to the table. You take the pen out of her hand, gently, and lean over the sheet, and sign your name on it: *N. Ashby*. Then you hand the pen back to her.

Kit looks at your name on the sheet.

She looks at it for a long moment. Then she bends over the table and signs, directly under you, neat and fast: *K. Ashworth*. She puts the pen down.

And she walks off, without a word, very straight, across the hall and out of the doors. Her ears are pink.

---

At two o'clock it's the Practice Hall, and the warming charm again.

The smooth grey stones are laid out on the benches. You pick yours up. It's cold and heavy in your palm. Down the bench, Amara closes her hand round hers and draws the spiral over it, and in a minute there's steam curling off it. At the end bench, back to the room, Kit sets hers down, and the air above it shimmers.

Yours is barely warm.

Again. The same as yesterday. You draw the spiral and call the heat in, and it comes in a trickle, the stone going from cold to cool to faintly, faintly not-cold, and stopping there. Like a stone that's sat in a patch of weak sun for five minutes. That's all.

You try again. It goes a bit warmer, and stops.

You push.

You don't decide to. You just do. You grip the stone tighter. You find the thread in your chest, the thin bright thread you held on to for your first glim, and you grab it, and you pull. Harder. You think of Amara's stone steaming and Kit's air shimmering and Bea in the corner with her glims fizzling halfway and Penhallow in her study writing down your one line and saying *hm*. You pull on the thread with everything you've got. Harder. And harder. Until your jaw is clenched and your eyes are watering and the thread in your chest is pulled so tight it's singing.

The stone goes hot all at once.

Blazing hot, in your palm, so suddenly that you nearly drop it. A rush of heat, like opening an oven.

And something warm runs from your nose onto your lip.

You put your hand up. Your fingers come away red.

Blood. Bright red, on your fingertips. And then it's dripping, off your lip, off your chin, onto the stone in your hand and onto the scorched floorboards at your feet, bright startling drops on the old black wood. Pat. Pat. Pat.

The hall tilts.

The floor swings up and sideways, slow and wrong, and the high windows slide across your vision, and your knees go soft.

And the demonstrator is there. Out of nowhere, a strong hand under your elbow and another flat on your back, steadying you, holding you upright.

"Overreaching," she says. Not unkind. Brisk and calm. "There it is. Everyone does it once. Sit." She lowers you onto the bench. "Head forward. Not back. *Forward*. Pinch here." She takes your fingers and puts them on the soft part of your nose, just below the bone, and presses. "Hold that. Breathe through your mouth. And that's all from you today."

You sit with your head bent forward and your fingers pinching your nose and blood dripping off your chin onto your knees. Your head is pounding. The hall is still tilting gently round you.

Someone sits down on the bench beside you.

Kit.

She doesn't say anything. She presses something into your free hand. You look down at it through blurred eyes: a handkerchief. Black cotton, folded into a square, soft and clean. In one corner, stitched very small in grey silk thread, a letter: *K*.

You hold it to your nose. It smells faintly of lavender.

Kit sits beside you on the bench. She doesn't talk. She doesn't ask if you're all right, or tell you it happens to everyone, or say anything at all. She just sits there, in silence, close, while the rest of the hall goes on casting round you. Her shoulder is against yours. It stays there, warm and steady, a small solid pressure through her black jumper and yours.

She stays until it stops.

---

By evening the bleeding has stopped. The headache hasn't.

It's the worst yet. Worse than the kettle, worse than the first spark, worse than anything. A hammer behind your eyes, a slow heavy pounding that comes with every beat of your heart, so that you can feel your pulse in your skull. The light hurts. The radiator knocking hurts. You lie on your bed in the dark with your arm over your eyes and Kit's black handkerchief folded in your hand, and it doesn't get better.

At eleven you give up.

You can't sleep. You can't do anything. You get up, slowly, wincing, and go down the corridor in your nightshirt and socks, and knock on the door of room 1.

Silence.

You knock again. Nothing. There's no light under the door. It's dark and silent, the room behind it empty.

"She's not there."

You turn. A girl is standing in her doorway further down the corridor, in pyjamas, with a toothbrush in her mouth. She takes it out.

"Yusra," she says. "She does night shifts. At the infirmary. Tuesdays and Thursdays." She looks at your face in the dim corridor light, and her expression changes. "Are you all right? You look awful."

"I'm fine," you say. "Thanks."

---

You go in your coat over your nightshirt and the fisherman's jumper over that, and your trainers, across the cold dark quad.

It's frozen hard tonight. The grass crunches under your feet, silver under the few lamps. The stars are out, sharp as broken glass. The cold air on your face helps, a little; the hammer behind your eyes eases a notch as you walk. Every window in the college is dark except a few. You go across to the west range, where the girl said, and along it, and find a door with a small brass plate: INFIRMARY. And a light inside.

You push it open.

It's a long white room. Six beds, three down each side, with white iron frames and white sheets pulled tight, and every one of them empty. At the far end there's a door standing open onto a small room lined with shelves of bottles and jars, glinting: a dispensary. The air smells clean and faintly of something sharp, like eucalyptus. Most of the room is in shadow.

One lamp is lit, on a desk near the door.

And Yusra is at the desk.

She's in a white tunic, short-sleeved, buttoned at the shoulder, like a nurse's. Her long black hair is tied back tight off her face. She has her reading glasses on, and a fat textbook open in front of her under the lamp, and a mug of something beside it. She's writing in the margin with a pencil.

She looks up.

"Ashby."

And she's up. Out of her chair and round the desk at once, fast, before you've even shut the door behind you. Her hand goes under your chin. Her fingers, cool and firm, tilt your face up into the lamplight, and she looks at you. Close. Her dark eyes going over your face, your eyes, your nose, the faint smear of dried blood you missed at the edge of your nostril.

"What happened?" she says.

You tell her. The warming charm. The stone that wouldn't warm. Pushing. Pulling on the thread. The stone going hot all at once and then the blood, on the floorboards, and the hall tilting. And the headache, since.

She listens without letting go of your chin. Then she takes a small silver light out of the pocket of her tunic and clicks it on and shines it in your eyes. One, then the other. She's very close. You can feel her breath on your face. You can see each separate eyelash behind the lenses of her reading glasses, and the faint lines at the corners of her eyes, and her mouth, full and serious, an inch from yours. The light is bright and hurts and you don't look away from her.

She clicks it off.

"Pupils are fine," she says. "Sit."

She sits you on the nearest bed. She brings you a glass of water and stands over you while you drink all of it. Then she goes into the dispensary, and you hear bottles clinking, and she comes back with a small glass with a little cloudy green liquid in the bottom.

"Drink that."

You drink it. It tastes of mint, strongly, and of something bitter under the mint, and it goes down cold and spreads out warm through your chest.

Yusra pulls a chair over from the desk and sits in it, facing you, close. Her knees are almost touching yours. She takes her glasses off and looks at you.

"You push," she says, "like someone with something to prove."

You look at her.

"Who to?" she says.

You don't know. You open your mouth to say something easy, and it doesn't come. You think about the stone, and Amara's steam, and Bea in the corner. You think about Penhallow writing down your line. And under that, deeper, you think about a man in a chair by a back window who never cast a spell in his life because spells weren't real; who woke up in this body with an empty head, and has been running to catch up ever since.

"I don't know," you say slowly. "Maybe to myself." You look at your hands. "Maybe to whoever I was before."

Yusra looks at you for a long moment.

She doesn't ask. You can see the question in her face: *who were you before?* You can see her decide not to ask it. She looks at you steadily, with her dark eyes, and lets it lie.

---

She lets you stay an hour.

You lie back on the white bed on top of the tight sheets, with your coat over you, on the empty ward. She turns the lamp down low, so that it's only a small gold pool on the desk and the rest of the long room is soft shadow. The windows are black. The headache eases. Slowly, a little at a time, the hammer behind your eyes slows and softens and becomes a dull ache and then a faint pressure.

Yusra sits at the desk with her book. But she doesn't read it much. After a while she closes it, and turns her chair, and talks to you, quietly, across the dim ward.

About why healing.

"My dad's a GP," she says. "In Leeds. Has been for thirty years. And my mum's a midwife." She turns her pencil over in her fingers. "So I grew up with it. People coming to the door at all hours. Mum going out at three in the morning with her bag. Dad on the phone in the kitchen, talking someone through it." She's looking at the lamp, not at you. "And what I remember most is their hands. Both of them. Their hands, doing things. Taking a pulse. Holding a baby. Pressing on someone's stomach to find where it hurt." She's quiet a moment. "Hands that make things better. That's all I ever wanted. To have hands like that."

You look at her hands, turning the pencil. Long, and strong, and steady.

"You do," you say.

Yusra looks at you. She doesn't answer.

The lamp hums faintly. The ward is very quiet.

"I'm your warden," she says at last.

Quietly. Looking at you across the dim room, her face half in the lamplight.

"There are lines I keep. I have to keep them. You should know that." She holds your gaze. "You should know I keep them."

You understand her. You understand all of it, everything she's saying and everything she isn't.

"I know," you say.

She holds your eyes. And you hold hers. Neither of you looks away. Across the dim white ward, the lamp between you, for a long, long while, you just look at each other, and the ward stays silent, and you both know exactly what the silence is full of.

---

At last she walks you to the infirmary door.

She opens it for you. The cold of the night comes in, sharp and clean. You step out onto the frozen step, and turn, and she's standing in the doorway in her white tunic, with the lamplight behind her.

"Goodnight, Nala," she says.

Nala.

Not Ashby. Not Ashby, the way she's said it every time since your first morning, with the clipboard. *Nala.* Your name. The first time she's ever used it.

The door closes.

You cross the dark quad back to Thornfield alone, over the frozen grass, under the hard bright stars, with your own name ringing in your ears.
