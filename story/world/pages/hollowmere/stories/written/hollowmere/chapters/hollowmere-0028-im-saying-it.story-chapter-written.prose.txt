On Sunday you wake stiff from the fell.

You know it before you open your eyes. Your calves are two hard knots, aching all the way up the backs of your legs, and your thighs are sore, and your feet. When you try to move, everything below the waist complains at once. Yesterday's climb is still in you: the stile, the snow, the long steep bracken coming down.

And you can't move anyway, because Bea is asleep across your legs.

Somewhere in the night she's turned right round. She's lying crossways on the bed, on her front, with her head on the far edge of the mattress and her whole warm weight pinning your shins to the sheet. One arm hangs off the side. Her hair is a honey-blonde fan across the blanket. She's snoring, very softly, a small whistling snore, like a kettle a long way off.

You lie and look at the ceiling, and laugh without a sound.

Then you ease out from under her.

Slowly, an inch at a time, sliding your legs free, while she grumbles and shifts and doesn't wake. You sit on the edge of the bed. You stand, and your calves scream, and you sit down again.

Bea mumbles into the blanket.

"Rock," she says. Her eyes don't open. "Go." A pause. "Say hi to Shiv."

And she's asleep again.

---

The shore path is cold and bright.

A clear Sunday morning, the sky pale and high, the sun not over the fells yet but lighting the tops of them gold. Your breath smokes. Frost on the path, on the grass, on the dead bracken along the shore. You walk stiffly, like an old woman, your legs complaining at every step, and every step it gets a little easier.

The mere is still and grey.

Flat calm. Not a ripple anywhere, a long sheet of pewter all the way to the far shore. And mist lifting off it, slowly: thin white wisps rising off the water, curling, drifting, coming apart in the cold air, as if the whole mere is breathing out.

You come round the last bend, and there's the rock.

The flat slab of grey stone, running out into the water where it's deep straight off. And Shiv is already on it.

She's at the edge, stripping bare in the cold: the parka dropped on the stone, then the grey hoodie over her head, then the rest, in a heap behind her, with her towel folded on top and her flask beside it. Her back to you. Slim and wiry and pale as the frost, her strawberry-blonde hair sticking up every which way, her arms crossed over her chest, bouncing a little on her toes against the cold.

She hears you on the path, and turns.

"You came," she says.

She sounds pleased. She sounds, for a moment, a little surprised.

"Rain or not," you say. You look up at the clear sky. "It's not even raining."

Shiv grins. Her crooked grin, wide and delighted.

She pulls her old swim cap on, tucking her hair up under it, and turns back to the water, and lifts her arms, and dives.

---

You sit on the rock.

You fold yourself down onto the cold stone, slowly, wincing, your calves protesting, and sit with your knees up and your arms round them. You pull her towel off the heap and hold it ready on your lap, folded, so it'll be warm from you when she comes back.

You watch her swim.

She surfaces a long way out, with a gasp you can hear from the rock, and shakes her head, and starts.

Her arms go over and over. Steady, unhurried, one and then the other, each one lifting out of the grey water and reaching and going in clean, hardly a splash. Her head turns to breathe every third stroke. She leaves a long thin V spreading out behind her on the still water, and the mist parts round her as she goes through it, and closes again behind.

Out she goes, towards the buoy.

You watch her get smaller. A pale head, and pale arms turning over, in the middle of the grey.

She reaches the buoy, and turns.

And as she turns she rolls onto her back, and lifts her face to the sky, and shouts something. You can't hear what. One long shout, up into the pale morning, ringing off the water. It goes out across the mere and comes back off the fells, faint.

Then she rolls over, and swims back.

---

When she climbs out she's blue-lipped and laughing.

She hauls herself up onto the rock, streaming, gasping, her skin gone pink and then white and mottled with the cold, her lips blue, her teeth chattering. She's shaking all over. And laughing, breathless, between the chattering, the way she always is when she comes out.

You're on your feet already.

You wrap the towel round her shoulders. Round and round, pulling it close, rubbing her arms through it, her back. Her skin is like ice through the towel. She leans into you, dripping all down your coat, and lets you.

"G-god," says Shiv. "Th-that's the stuff."

She pulls her hoodie on over the towel, and then the parka over that, and sits down on the rock, and you sit beside her.

She unscrews the flask.

Pours. The steam comes up off the tea in a white cloud between you.

And she gives you the first cup.

You take it. She pours herself another into the flask's lid, and holds it in both hands, and her hands are shaking so hard with the cold that the tea slops, and she has to hold it close against her chest to keep it still.

She looks out at the water.

At the buoy, and the mist, and the long grey stillness of it.

"Kick, Siobhan," she says.

Quietly. To the water. Not to you.

Then she turns her head, and looks at you, sitting there on the cold rock with her tea in your hands.

"Every Sunday," Shiv says. "She'd like you."

---

You drink your tea, and she drinks hers, and the shaking slowly goes out of her hands.

"Go on, then," she says. "The fell. Bea's been telling everyone you went up it."

So you tell her.

About the stile, and the academy small as a toy below. About the snow that squeaked. About the cairn, and the two stones, one on top of the other. About the tarn, black and still, ringed with rushes, out of the wind.

And about Yusra.

Her hands on your shoulders, turning you out of the wind. *Up here it's just me.*

Shiv whistles.

A long, low, impressed whistle, through her teeth.

"The warden," Shiv says.

She shakes her head slowly.

"God. Good for her." She drinks her tea. "She's been a ghost since summer, that one. Walking round like she's not really there. I noticed." She looks at the water. "Didn't know what it was. Good for her, though. Good for her."

She's quiet a moment.

Then, lightly, not looking at you:

"There's a queue, Nala."

You laugh. "That's what Bea said."

Shiv laughs too.

"Bea's right," she says.

She finishes her tea. Screws the lid back on the flask. Then she leans over, and puts her wet head on your shoulder.

Just leans it there. Her damp hair soaking into your coat, cold, and her cheek against the wool. You can feel her breathing.

"I'm still in it," she says. "The queue." A pause. "When it's right."

You think of a boat adrift on the mere, and an Irish song sung low over the water. You think of her crying on this rock, in your arms, the first time in front of anyone. And what she said after, smiling, with her face still wet.

"A whole year of Sundays," you say.

Shiv smiles. You feel it, against your jumper, through your coat.

"You remembered," she says.

---

You walk back along the shore together.

Her arm through yours. Her towel round her neck, dripping down the front of her parka. Her hair drying in tufts in the cold air. The sun has come over the fells now, and the mist on the mere is burning off, going gold, and then gone, and the water is blue.

You walk slowly, because of your calves.

Shiv doesn't say anything about it. She just walks slowly too.

---

At one it's Sunday roast in the Great Hall.

The long tables, and the high windows full of the bright day, and the smell of it all as you come in the doors: roast beef and roast potatoes and Yorkshire puddings, and the steam rising off great dishes all down the tables, and jugs of gravy at every few places.

Priya drowns hers in gravy.

She takes the nearest jug and pours, and keeps pouring, until her beef is an island and her potatoes are reefs and her Yorkshire pudding is a small brown boat afloat in the middle of it all. Amara, across the table, watches this with open horror.

"It's a sauce, Raman," she says. "Not a moat."

"It's a *roast*," says Priya, and pours a little more.

Bea fills Shiv's plate first.

Again. Before her own. Without asking, without a word: the best of the beef, and four roast potatoes, and the biggest Yorkshire pudding on the dish, piled up on Shiv's plate and set down in front of her, and then Bea starts on her own.

Shiv looks at the plate.

Then, under the table, she kicks Bea's ankle. Not hard. Fond.

Bea grins at her, and kicks back.

Then Bea has thirds.

Training yesterday, she says, with her mouth full; she's still hungry from it. She goes back up the table with her plate, and comes back with it full again, and eats the lot.

Lin is beside you.

She's eating very slowly. Pushing her peas round and round her plate with her fork, in circles, round a roast potato, round again.

"Anything yet?" you ask her, low.

Lin shakes her head.

"Not till the end of the week," she says. "If she writes straight back. It'll be a week at least." She chases a pea round the potato. "The end of the week."

She doesn't eat the pea.

---

In the afternoon everyone scatters to essays.

Kit to the library. Priya and Amara to wherever Priya and Amara go now. Lin to her window seat with her sketchbook and the focus question. Shiv to sleep, she says, frankly, and wanders off yawning.

You go up to 15, to start the focus essay with Bea.

*What does a focus remember, and should it?* You've got a page of notes from the forge, from Kit, from the ring cool in your palm. You've been looking forward to this: the two of you on the bed, side by side, with your books.

You open the door of 15.

Bea is at her desk.

She's sitting very still, in the window light, with a pile of coins on the desk in front of her and a notebook open beside them. Counting. Moving the coins one at a time, from one pile to another, with one finger. Her lips are moving. She's very quiet.

Bea is never quiet.

She hears you.

She looks up, quickly, and closes the notebook. Fast. And sweeps the coins off the desk into her hand, and drops them into a mug on the windowsill with a clatter.

Too quickly.

"Hi," she says, bright. "Hi. You're back. How was Shiv?"

You stand in the doorway.

And you see it. The quiet. The way she went quiet, at the desk, before she knew you were there. The way she's being loud now, to cover it. The way she went quiet at sixteen for a whole week, and the house rolled on round her.

You come in, and shut the door.

You don't answer about Shiv. You sit down on the end of her bed, facing her, and you fold your hands in your lap, and you wait.

Bea looks at you.

"I'll always notice," you say.

---

Bea looks at you for a long moment.

Sitting at her desk, with her hand still on the closed notebook. Her bright face, not bright any more.

Then her face crumples.

It goes all at once, her mouth and her chin and her eyes. And then, in the middle of it, she laughs. A wet, cracked, surprised laugh. And she wipes her eyes with the heel of her hand.

"You're a menace," she says.

Then she opens the notebook again.

She turns it round on the desk, and holds it out to you, and you get up and take it.

Columns of sums. Pages of them. Neat, careful columns in Bea's big round hand, the letters leaning every which way, but the numbers very straight. Dates down one side. Amounts. *Train, Mum, return. Prescription. Taxi, night.* And at the bottom of each page, totals, underlined, and under each total, a smaller number, circled.

You look at her.

"My little brother," Bea says.

She's looking at her hands.

"The youngest. Nine." She swallows. "He's got a weak heart. Since he was born."

You sit down on the bed again, with the notebook.

"There's appointments," Bea says. "In Bristol. All the time. And the train fare for Mum, every time, and his medicines, and the things the NHS doesn't cover. Special food. The monitor. The taxi when it's three in the morning and the bus isn't running."

She turns her hands over. Looks at the blisters.

"So I send most of my bursary home," she says. "Every month."

She says it plainly. Not proud. Not sad. As if she's telling you she rows at bow.

"Mum doesn't ask," Bea says. "I just do it. I just send it."

---

You look round 15.

You've slept in this room most nights now. You know it. And you look round it now, and you see it.

The mugs. All the mugs, on the windowsill, on the desk, on the shelf: more mugs than plates, because mugs are fifty pence at the charity shop. The jumpers in the open wardrobe, every one of them oversized, every one of them second-hand, bobbled at the cuffs. The rowing book, from the library. And the one good pair of running shoes, by the door, new, the only new thing in the room. Because she needs them. Because she runs the shore every morning.

"That's why the café year," you say.

Bea nods.

"Saving," she says. "A whole year. So I could come here at all." She shrugs, one shoulder. "And I still send most of it home."

Then, quickly:

"He's all right," she says. "Mostly. He's all right. He's brave." She looks up at you, fierce. "He's braver than me by miles. He's had more needles than I've had hot dinners, and he just holds his arm out."

She gets up.

She goes to the mirror, and takes down a photo from the ones pinned round the frame, and brings it to you.

A small boy, in a school jumper, grinning straight at the camera. Fair hair sticking up. A gap where his front tooth should be.

And Bea's dimples. Both of them. Deep.

"That's him," says Bea.

She sits down beside you on the bed, and looks at it with you.

"He wants to know about the race," she says. "I told him I'm at bow."

Her mouth twitches.

"He thinks bow means ribbon," Bea says. "He thinks I've got a ribbon on. In my hair. For rowing."

You laugh.

You can't help it. And Bea laughs too, wet, her eyes spilling over, and leans sideways, and puts her forehead on your shoulder.

"I'm all right," she says. Into your jumper. "I am. I'm all right."

You put your arm round her.

"You don't have to be all right," you tell her. "Not with me."

Bea goes still.

Completely still, against your shoulder. Not breathing, for a moment.

And then she holds on.

Very hard. Both arms round you, tight, her face in your neck, holding on so hard it hurts your ribs. Shaking a little. You hold her back.

"You're the only one here who knows," she says into your neck.

Muffled. Wet.

"Not even Yusra. Just you."

You hold her.

You hold her for a long time, on the end of her bed, in the window light, with the photo of the small grinning boy in your other hand.

---

At six she rings home.

From the porters' lodge phone, the way she does every Sunday. You knew she rang home. You didn't know it was a thing she did alone, every week, at six on the dot, like going to church.

You walk down with her.

Across the quad in the blue dusk, the lamps coming on along the paths. Bea has a pocketful of coins, the ones from the mug, and she jingles them as she walks.

At the lodge she goes in, to the old phone on the wall inside the window.

You sit on the bench outside.

It's cold. The bench is iron and the iron is freezing, right through your coat, and your breath smokes in the lamp over the lodge door. You pull your hands up inside your sleeves, and sit, and watch her through the window.

She's laughing.

The moment the call connects: laughing, and talking fast, very fast, her free hand waving, the phone tucked between her ear and her shoulder. Her whole face lit.

Her brother first, it looks like.

She does a rowing action. With one arm, the free one, out in front of her and back, out and back, a big exaggerated stroke, and her face is all concentration, and she's explaining. The phone slips off her shoulder. She nearly drops it. She catches it, laughing, and you can see her mouth say *sorry, sorry*, and then she's laughing again.

Then her sisters.

One after the other. You can tell. She talks differently to each of them: brisk and teasing with the first, rolling her eyes, and softer with the second, nodding, listening a long time.

Then she goes quieter.

She turns to the wall. Her back to the window, to you. Her head bent.

Her mother.

She talks to her mother last, and longest. You watch her back. Her shoulders, which were up round her ears when she started, come down. Slowly. And soften. Her free hand goes up to the wall, flat, and rests there. Her head bends further. She nods, and nods, at whatever her mother is saying.

You sit on the cold bench and wait.

---

When she comes out her eyes are wet.

But she's smiling.

"He's all right this week," Bea says. "He says go fast."

You stand up.

And she walks straight into your arms. There by the lodge, under the lamp, in front of the porter's window. Straight into you, her face in your shoulder, her arms round your waist under your coat.

And stays.

She doesn't cry. She just stays there, holding on, breathing.

"Thank you for sitting," Bea says.

Into your coat.

"I've always rung alone," she says.

You put your hand on the back of her head.

"You don't have to now," you say.

---

Walking back across the quad in the dark, she holds your hand.

And swings it.

Swings it, high, back and forth, like a girl. Like a small girl walking home from school. The lamps going past, and the frost glittering on the grass, and her hand swinging yours, and every so often she skips a step.

---

In the Thornfield kitchen, Yusra is making tea.

She's at the counter, in her soft grey jumper, with the kettle just boiled and one mug out. Her glasses pushed up on her head.

She looks round as you come in.

She looks at Bea's face. The red eyes, and the smile. Then she looks at you.

And she takes down two more mugs.

She doesn't say anything. She makes three cups of tea, and puts sugar in two of them, a good deal of sugar, and stirs them. Then she brings them across the kitchen, and puts the two sweet ones down on the table in front of you both.

Then she takes her own, and goes back up the stairs with it.

Bea stares after her.

She stares at the empty doorway where Yusra went out. Then at the mug in front of her.

"She made me tea," Bea says.

She sounds stunned.

"Yusra Haddad," says Bea, "made me tea."

You smile into yours.

---

In the evening, in 15, you write.

Both of you, side by side on the bed, with your backs against the wall and your notebooks on your knees, and your knees touching. The lamp on. *What does a focus remember, and should it?*

You write a page about the forge. About a plain silver ring, cool in your palm, that felt like something.

Bea writes about her brother.

About his tin medal. She tells you about it as she writes: the hospital gave it to him when he was small, a little tin medal on a red ribbon, *Brave Heart*, the kind they give to every child. He wears it every day. Every single day, under his school jumper. He won't take it off for baths. It's gone dull and dented and the ribbon is grey.

"Does it remember him?" Bea says.

She's looking at her page.

"It should," she says. "He's earned it."

She crosses it out.

She looks at the crossed-out line for a long moment.

And then she writes it again, underneath, the same words, harder, so the pen digs into the paper.

---

At ten she puts the essays on the floor.

Both notebooks, side by side, on the rug. And she reaches over and turns the lamp down low, till it's only a glow, gold, on the wall and on her face.

She lies down facing you.

On her side, on the pillow, a few inches away. You lie down facing her. Her hair spread out on the pillow behind her. Her eyes very dark.

She's quiet a long time.

"I love you, I think," Bea says.

Very quietly.

She swallows.

"That's what this is," she says. "I've never been in love, and I said it might be, and it is. That's what this is." She takes a breath. "I'm saying it."

Her voice wobbles, and steadies.

"I love you," says Bea.

---

You look at her.

In the lamplight. Her face, so close. Her dimples, there even when she isn't smiling, two small shadows. Her lashes, wet. Her blistered hand, which has come up, and is resting on your cheek, the rough patches warm against your skin.

You tell her you love her too.

It comes out easy.

Easy as breath. Easy as breathing out on the rock before you jump. You don't think about it. You open your mouth, and it's there, and it's true; you can feel that it's true, the way you could feel the stone go warm.

Bea laughs.

And cries. At once, both, the way Lin did at the post box; her face crumpling and her mouth laughing.

And she kisses you.

And kisses you. Your mouth, and your cheek, and your eyes, and your mouth again, laughing between them. And she pulls you in, and holds you, so tight, both arms round you, crushing you to her, so tight you squeak.

You actually squeak.

A small, high, ridiculous squeak, like boots in snow.

And Bea laughs at the squeak. Helplessly, into your hair, shaking with it, holding you just as tight.

---

You fall asleep face to face.

Her hand in yours, between you, on the pillow. Your fingers laced. Her forehead nearly touching yours.

Her breathing slow against your mouth.

A day at Hollowmere ends.
