At half past six on Wednesday morning, Bea bangs on your door.

You knew she would. You lie in the grey light listening to it, three flat cheerful thumps and then her voice, "Nala! Up! Half six!", and you groan into the pillow, a long low wretched sound. Every muscle in your legs has set overnight like plaster. When you shift under the blanket your calves complain, and your thighs complain louder, and somewhere at the backs of your knees there's an ache you didn't know a person could have.

"I can hear you groaning," Bea calls. "Groaning's good. Groaning means you're awake."

"Groaning means I'm dying."

"Hot water at half seven."

You lie there for exactly as long as it takes to remember yesterday's shower. Then you get up, very slowly, like an old woman, and go.

---

"Slow," Bea says, on the shingle. "Slow this time. I mean it. You are not allowed to go off like a whippet. You're going to run at my pace, and my pace today is *granny*."

"I can do granny," you say.

You can't, at first. Your new body wants to go; the stiffness shakes out of your legs in the first hundred yards and then they're light again, springy, eager, and you have to hold them back like a dog on a lead. Bea runs beside you at a slow easy jog, chattering about porridge and her sisters, her breath puffing white, and every time you start to pull ahead she says "Granny," and you drop back.

It's harder than sprinting. It's also better. You find a rhythm, short steps, breathing in for three and out for three the way Bea shows you, and the shore goes by: the shingle, the bracken coming down to the water, the grey mere with the light coming up pink over the fells beyond it. Your hair bounces in its band against the back of your neck.

You last ten minutes.

Then your lungs give out, the same as yesterday, all at once, a hot ragged burn in your chest, and you slow to a stagger and stop with your hands on your hips, gasping. But Bea is looking at her watch, and grinning.

"Ten minutes," she says. "That's double. You've doubled it in a day. At this rate you'll be running a marathon by Friday."

"I'll be dead by Friday."

"Dead and *fit*," Bea says. "Walk. Come on."

You walk. Your breath comes back. Out on the mere, a long way off, something is moving through the grey water: a small white dot, a capped head, and behind it a narrow wake, and the steady flash of an arm coming over, and over.

As you watch, the swimmer stops. Treads water. Turns towards the shore. And one arm comes up out of the mere, pale and dripping, and waves.

At you.

You wave back. You feel your face warm. But only a little this time, a faint heat in your cheeks, not the full scarlet flood of yesterday, and you keep your hand up for a moment longer than you need to before you put it down.

Out on the water, Shiv turns over onto her back and goes on swimming.

Beside you Bea keeps very quiet. You glance at her. She's looking straight ahead, along the shore towards Thornfield, with her mouth pressed shut and both dimples dug deep in her cheeks, trying very hard not to grin and failing completely.

"Don't," you say.

"I didn't say anything."

"You're thinking it."

"I'm thinking about breakfast," Bea says, and grins the whole way home.

---

The hot water runs hot. You stand under it with your eyes shut and your forehead against the tiles while it pours over your stiff shoulders and down your back, over the curve of your ass and down the backs of your aching thighs, and the ache melts out of you a little at a time. Steam fills the cubicle. You turn round and let it drum on your chest, and you just stand there in it, loose and warm, your mind gone soft and blank, for a long, long time.

Until a fist hammers on the bathroom door, and someone outside says, very clearly, "*Some* of us have *also* run along a lake," and you yelp and turn it off.

---

At ten you go to Sigils, in the Drawing Room.

It's at the top of the north range, up a stair that smells of beeswax, and it's long and high and full of light. The ceiling is set with skylights all the way along, big panes of glass that let the cold bright morning straight down onto rows and rows of slanted wooden desks, the kind with a hinged lid and a groove for pens and a little round hole in the corner for an ink pot. The ink pots are already there, one to a desk, black glass with brass lids. Beside each one lie a wooden pen holder and a little tray of steel nibs, and a stack of thick cream paper, heavy and slightly rough, that you can't stop touching.

The sigils tutor is a small brisk woman with grey hair scraped back and a pair of half-moon spectacles and ink on both her hands, and she stands at the front of the room with a pen in hers and doesn't use the blackboard at all. She draws on a big sheet of paper pinned to an easel, in ink, and every line she makes is sure and black and perfect.

"Sigils are drawn," she says. "Not doodled. Not scribbled. Not *gestured*, though you'll gesture them soon enough, once you've earned it. Drawn. On paper. In ink. Ink doesn't forgive. That's the point. By the time you trace a sigil in the air, your hand will have drawn it a thousand times on paper, and it'll know where to go without asking you. Today, it doesn't know anything. Sit."

There are three tutorial groups in the room, eighteen girls or so. You look for somewhere to sit, and see Lin at a desk near the middle, alone, already unscrewing her ink, and you go and take the desk beside her.

She looks up when you sit. She doesn't say anything. But something in her face goes quietly pleased, a softening around her eyes and the corner of her mouth, and she ducks her head and goes back to her ink.

A moment later someone takes the desk directly in front of yours. A small straight back in black, a glossy black bob, a silver ring as she sets out her nibs. Kit. She doesn't turn round. She sits very straight on her stool, her shoulders level, and keeps her eyes on her paper as if it's the only thing in the room.

You look at the back of her neck, pale under the edge of her hair, for a second longer than you mean to. Then you look at your own paper.

"Glim sigil," says the tutor. "You've all seen it. Some of you have even cast it." She draws it on the easel: stroke, loop, hook. "Ten of them. Neatly. Begin."

You fit a nib into the holder. You dip it in the ink. You put it to the paper and draw the stroke.

A blot. A big wet black one, spreading out from the end of the stroke into the fibres of the paper like a spider. You stare at it. You try again further down the page, more carefully, pressing harder so it won't slip, and the nib digs in and scratches and goes right through the paper with a horrible little rip, and catches, and flicks a spray of fine black dots across the desk and onto the back of your hand.

"Oh, *hell*," you whisper.

Lin's hand comes across the gap between the desks. She sets something down by your ink pot, very quietly, and takes her hand back. It's a nib. A different one from the ones in your tray: rounder at the tip, a little worn, the steel gone soft and dark with use.

"That one's softer," she says, so low you barely hear her. "Yours are new. New ones are sharp."

You change nibs. Lin watches you do it, out of the corner of her eye. Then she leans across, just a little, and says, "You're holding it too tight. Like you're holding on to it." She shows you, lifting her own pen: held loose, low down, the fingers relaxed, the holder resting lightly against the web of her hand. "Let it sit. And less ink. Just dip the end."

You try. Your fingers keep tightening on the pen by themselves, clamping down.

Lin hesitates. Then she reaches over and, with her ink-stained fingertips, very lightly, moves your fingers on the pen: loosens the first one, slides the thumb back, tilts the holder a few degrees. Her touch is cool and quick and very careful, and it lasts about two seconds. Then her hand is gone again, back on her own desk.

"Like that," she says, looking at her own paper.

You dip the nib, just the end. You put it to the paper.

And you draw it slowly. The room has forgotten you; Lin's bent over her own page and Kit's back is a black wall in front of you and the tutor is at the far end of the room. There's just the paper and the pen and the quiet. The stroke, straight up. The loop, round. And the hook at the bottom, curling in, not out, closing back on itself the way Lin drew it on the scrap of paper in the Practice Hall.

You lift the pen.

It's clean. Not beautiful. Not like the tutor's, and nowhere near like Lin's. But clean: every line where it should be, the ink even, the hook turned in. A glim sigil, on cream paper, in your hand.

You do another, under it. It's a bit wobbly. The third one's clean again. You do all ten, and by the tenth you've stopped holding your breath.

The tutor comes down the rows. She stops at desks, looks, says a word or two. "Heavier on the loop." "Again." "Better." She reaches your desk and looks at your page for a moment and says, "Good. Again, faster," and moves on, and you feel it warm in your chest all out of proportion.

Then she stops at Lin's desk, and stays there.

She doesn't say anything at first. She picks Lin's page up off the slanted desk and holds it up in both hands, close to her spectacles, and looks at it. Then she turns, and holds it up high, facing out, for the whole long room to see.

Ten glim sigils, in two neat rows of five. Each one identical to the others. Each one perfect, the strokes even and black and fine, the curves smooth as poured water, the hooks turning in with a little flourish at the very end that the printed ones don't have, as if the line knew it was finished and was pleased about it.

"This," says the tutor, "is a sigil."

Every head in the room turns.

Lin goes scarlet. Not pink: scarlet, all at once, from her collar to her ears and right to the tips of them, burning red under her soft black fringe. She stares down at her inkwell as if she might climb into it. Her hands are in her lap, gripping each other.

The tutor hands the page back, and says, "Keep doing that," and moves on, and the heads slowly turn away again.

Lin sits very still. She doesn't move for a long time.

When the lesson ends and the room fills with the scrape of stools and girls packing their things, you lean across the gap between the desks, close, and say under your breath, "She's right, you know."

Lin glances at you.

"It is a sigil," you say. "Yours are the best ones I've ever seen. Including the book."

She doesn't answer. She lifts her hand, sleeve and all, and puts it over the lower half of her face. But above it her eyes have gone crinkled and bright, and you can see, at the edge of the sleeve, the corner of a smile she's hiding in the wool.

---

You come out of the Drawing Room into the stone corridor in a crowd of girls, and the crowd thins out down the stair, and someone falls into step beside you.

Kit.

She doesn't look at you. She walks with her eyes fixed straight ahead down the corridor, her books held against her chest, her pace exactly matched to yours. Her face is pale and set.

"Whatever you think you saw last night," she says. Low. Clipped. The words come out flat and fast, as if she's said them to herself several times already.

She doesn't finish it. She doesn't need to.

You walk a few steps. You think about it. About the ring of lights in the dark hall and her face in the middle of them.

"I saw the best glims in the year," you say. "Better than Amara's. Better than the demonstrator's. That's what I saw." You keep your eyes ahead too, the way she's doing. "They're yours. It's yours. And I haven't told anyone. I'm not going to."

Kit stops walking.

You stop too, a step ahead, and turn. She's standing in the middle of the corridor with her books against her chest, and she's looking at you. Properly. Not through dark glass this time, and not stricken: in the plain grey light of the corridor she's actually looking at you, her grey-green eyes on your face, searching it, wary and sharp and very bright.

Whatever she's looking for, she seems to find it.

"Thank you," she says.

It comes out stiff. Awkward. As if it's a word in a language she's only read about, and saying it aloud costs her something.

And you can't help it.

"Stop thanking me," you say.

Kit's mouth twitches. The same pull at one corner that you saw at dinner, over the fork. She tries to flatten it, and it won't flatten, and then it breaks, and she laughs. Once. A short surprised sound, bright and real, that seems to startle her as much as it does you. Her hand comes up to her mouth.

For a second she looks about fifteen and completely undone.

Then she's walking again, past you, fast, almost a stride, her bob swinging and her books clutched tight and her back very straight, and you can see, from behind, that the tips of her ears have gone pink.

You stand alone in the stone corridor, grinning at the floor.

---

Lunch is quick and loud, and halfway through it Bea slams her hand flat on the table between your plates.

"Wednesday afternoon," she says. "Free. By *old custom*. Since about seventeen-something. There aren't any classes on Wednesday afternoons, and everyone walks to the village. *Everyone*. It's tradition. We're going."

"Are we?"

"We are. Finish your sandwich."

She collects you from the table, and you go up to Thornfield for coats, and on the way down you stop on the floor below yours and knock on a door Lin pointed out yesterday. Lin opens it in her soft jumper with a pen in her hand.

"We're walking to the village," Bea says over your shoulder. "It's tradition. Do you want to — "

"Yes," says Lin.

Bea blinks. Then she beams, both dimples. "Brilliant," she says. "Coat."

---

The shore path to the village is a mile of shingle and bracken.

It runs right along the edge of the mere, sometimes on the stones and sometimes up on the bank where the bracken has gone rusty-gold with autumn and stands as high as your waist, so that you walk single file through it with the dry fronds catching at your coat. The fells are bright today. The sun has come out properly, and the slopes across the water are green and brown and gold, every wall and sheep picked out sharp in the clear air, the tops dusted with cloud shadow moving fast. The wind comes cold off the mere, straight into your face, smelling of water and peat, and it whips your hair out of its band and into your mouth.

Bea walks in front, talking. Lin walks behind you, quietly, with her hands in her coat pockets, looking at everything.

Halfway along you hear feet crunching fast on the shingle behind you, and somebody falls into step at your elbow as if she's been walking with you all along.

"Afternoon," says Shiv.

She's in the big navy parka, hanging open over a grey hoodie, and her strawberry-blonde hair is damp again, tousled and drying in the wind, curling at the ends. Her cheeks are pink with cold. She doesn't ask if she can come. She just walks, hands in her pockets, matching your pace, as if somebody invited her and it was obviously you.

"You waved," she says to you, sideways, with the crooked grin.

"You waved first."

"I did. You went a bit red, though."

"I did *not*."

"A *bit*," says Bea, from in front, without turning round.

Shiv laughs.

---

Hollowmere village is one street.

It runs along the shore, a single row of stone cottages and shops with slate roofs, grey-blue and wet-looking even in the sun, with chimneys smoking and flowers in boxes going leggy at the end of the season. On one side is the street and on the other is the mere, with a low wall between, and at the far end a wooden jetty runs out into the water with a few small boats knocking against it.

The first building you come to is the pub. It's low and whitewashed and very old, with tiny windows, and a sign swinging over the door painted with a bell sinking into green water, and under it in faded gold letters, THE DROWNED BELL.

"Best pub in Cumbria," says Shiv. "Low beams. You'll crack your head. Big fire. And the jukebox." She puts her hand on her heart. "The jukebox is older than any of us. It's got one Pogues record on it and I've played it every night since I got here. Came a week early. Couldn't wait."

"A whole week?" Bea says.

"A whole *week*," Shiv agrees, pleased.

The shop is next, small and crammed, with a bell on the door that jangles. It sells everything: bread and milk and stamps and newspapers, a rack of postcards of the mere, and on a shelf behind the counter, in little paper packets with hand-drawn labels, charms. *Milk-Cold*, says one. *Kettle (Standard)*. *Door Lock, 3-Month*. *Lost Things*. They're cheap, fifty pence or a pound. You stand and read every label while Bea buys a bag of mint humbugs.

There's ink, too, and paper. Lin goes straight to it. She stands in front of the shelf for a long time, turning bottles over in her hands and reading the labels carefully, and in the end she buys one small bottle of black ink, and holds it in both hands as she carries it to the counter, the way you'd carry a bird.

Further along there's a charity shop, and Bea's whole face changes.

"Oh," she says. "Oh, yes."

Inside it smells of old wool and dust and lavender. There's a rail of black gowns, second-hand, some of them very old. There are shelves of books. There are jumpers, heaps of them, and Bea goes through them like a terrier going through a hedge, and starts throwing things at you.

"Try that. No. Try that. *No*. Oh. Oh, wait. Try *this*."

It's a fisherman's jumper. An old one, cream gone slightly yellow, heavy and thick, in a dense cabled knit, with a high round neck. It's too big. When you pull it over your head it comes down past your hips and the sleeves come down over your hands, and it smells of the shop and faintly of the sea, and it is the warmest thing you've ever worn.

"Oh my god," says Bea. "Oh my god, yes. Stay there."

She puts a hat on you. A wool hat, dark green, with a fat pompom. She pulls it down over your ears and tucks your red hair out from under it in front, arranging it over your shoulders, and steps back and looks at you with her hands clasped under her chin.

"You look like a little fisherman," she says, delighted. "A little fisherman who fishes for *hearts*."

"That's terrible."

"It's *perfect*. Look in the mirror."

You look in the mirror. A small pale freckled girl in an enormous cream jumper and a green pompom hat looks back at you, with her red hair in a tangle round her shoulders and her cheeks pink from the wind. She looks happy. She looks about twelve and also completely herself, and you can't stop smiling at her.

Across the shop, Lin has found the books. She's crouched in front of the lowest shelf, and when you go over she's holding one up in both hands. It's a small thick hardback with a cracked cloth cover, faded green, the gilt on the spine almost worn away. *Sigils for the Home & Garden*, it says on the title page, in an old-fashioned typeface, *with Two Hundred Plates. 1953.*

She opens it to show you. The plates are hand-drawn, page after page of them, sigils for doors and drains and seedlings and cold larders, small and fine and strange.

"Look," Lin whispers. "Look at the line on that."

She's holding it like it's treasure. Her face, bent over it, is completely open, absorbed and soft and lit up from inside.

At the counter, when you go to pay for the jumper, Bea gets there first. She slaps a fiver down before you can get your purse out of your coat.

"Bea, no — "

"Yes."

"I can — "

"You can buy the scones," Bea says. "Next week. Big ones. With cream." She waves it off, the whole thing, with one hand, as if it didn't matter at all. "I owe you, anyway. You came running."

You don't know what to say. You put your purse away, and pull the jumper's sleeves down over your hands, and say, "Thank you," and Bea says, "*Scones*," and that's the end of it.

---

Out on the street again, the jumper on and the pompom hat still on your head, you nearly walk into Kit.

She's coming along the pavement on her own, in a long black coat buttoned to the chin, a paper bag from the shop in one hand. She's walking fast, eyes down, the way she walked away from you in the corridor this morning. Then she sees you, and stops walking.

She turns to you. Not away: to you. And she looks at you straight on, in the plain cold daylight, with the mere bright behind her and the wind lifting the edge of her black fringe. She takes in the fisherman's jumper, and the ridiculous hat, and your face under it. She doesn't flinch from it, or flatten her mouth, or say anything cutting. She just looks, for a long moment, her grey-green eyes steady and clear and very hard to read, as if she's checking something against what you said in the corridor and finding that it holds.

Your heart is going quick under all that wool.

Then Bea's hand is on your elbow, steering you along the street towards the jetty and the smell of baking.

---

In the tearoom by the jetty you squeeze round a corner table, the four of you, knees bumping. The scones come warm and craggy and golden, each one the size of a fist, with a pot of cream and a pot of jam, and when you break one open, steam comes out of the middle.

You split one with Bea, half each, cream and jam piled on so thick it falls off the sides. Shiv orders two for herself and eats them both, steadily, without comment, and licks the cream off her thumb. Lin has one of her own and eats half of it, very slowly, in small bites, with her new book open on the table beside her plate and her new ink in her coat pocket, one hand resting on it.

You're halfway through your half when somebody touches your shoulder.

Amara. She's sweeping past your table with three older girls, second-years by the look of them, all of them tall and sharp and laughing, and she doesn't stop walking. Her hand rests on your shoulder for a moment as she passes, light, the gold watch at her wrist, and she leans down just enough to say, warm and amused, close to your ear: "Freckles."

And she's gone, out of the door in a gust of cold air.

You go red. Of course you do. All of it, from the high neck of your new jumper to the green pompom hat.

Shiv takes one look at you and laughs. She laughs so hard she can't stop, and puts her forehead down on the table among the crumbs and stays there, her shoulders shaking, making small helpless wheezing sounds into the tablecloth. Bea is pressing her napkin to her mouth. Even Lin is smiling, behind her teacup.

"I hate all of you," you say.

Shiv lifts her head, wipes her eyes, looks at you, and puts it straight back down again.

---

Walking back, the light is going. The sun has dropped behind the fells and the sky is a long clear wash of gold and green, and the mere has gone the colour of pewter, very still now that the wind has dropped. Lamps are coming on in Thornfield's windows, far ahead along the shore.

Before you reach the college, Shiv stops.

"Here," she says, and takes your sleeve. "Come and see."

She steers you down off the path and onto the shingle, towards the slab of rock that runs out into the water: the same one she walked out from, yesterday morning, naked and steaming. Up close it's broad and flat and dark grey, smoothed by water, crusted with yellow lichen at the landward end. You walk out along it after her, to the very edge, where it drops straight into the mere. The water there is dark and clear, and you can see down a long way, and then you can't.

"This is where I go in," Shiv says. "Every morning. It's deep straight off, so you don't have to wade. You just go." She looks at you sideways, with the grin. "Put your feet in."

"What?"

"Your feet. Go on. I dare you."

Bea and Lin have come out onto the rock behind you. Bea says "*Nala*," in a warning voice that's half a laugh.

You look at the water. You look at Shiv's grin. And you sit down on the edge of the rock and pull off your trainers and your socks and roll your jeans up to your knees and, before you can think about it, put both feet straight into the mere.

The cold is total.

It isn't cold like the shower. It's cold like being bitten by something enormous. It grabs your feet and your ankles and your shins all at once, so completely that for a second you can't feel anything but the cold itself, a white ringing nothing, and then the pain arrives, and you shriek. A high, piercing, girlish shriek, louder than any noise you have ever made in either of your lives, and it goes ringing out across the still water, and comes back off the fells.

Shiv cackles. She throws back her head and cackles like a witch, delighted, bent double.

You snatch your feet out. They're bright pink. They feel like they belong to someone else. Bea is crying with laughter. Lin has both hands over her mouth.

"Oh my *god*," you gasp. "You *swim* in that? You swim in that *naked*?"

"Every morning." Shiv crouches beside you, still grinning, and hands you your socks. Then she says, easily, as if it's the most natural thing in the world, "Come in with me some morning."

You look at her. At the sea-green eyes and the freckles and the damp hair curling at her ears.

"Maybe," you say.

And you mean it. You mean it more than you expected to, sitting there with your feet throbbing pink and numb on the cold rock, the dark water under you. You'd thought you'd say no. You find you don't want to.

Shiv's grin goes a bit softer at the edges. She doesn't say anything else. She just nods, as if that's settled, and stands up and offers you a hand.

On the last stretch of the path, with Thornfield's lit windows close now and the dusk gone blue, Bea falls back beside you and links her arm through yours. She tucks it in tight, her side against your side, and you walk like that, matched, the last of the way home. The fisherman's jumper is scratchy against your neck and warm all the way through, and Bea's arm is warm through it, and your feet are coming back to life in your trainers, tingling.

---

In the evening the Thornfield kitchen is steamy and bright, and Lin comes up from the floor below with her new ink and the old green book.

She doesn't say much. She sets them on the kitchen table, and sits, and opens the book to show you the plates, turning the pages slowly. You make tea; Bea has gone to shower. Then Lin turns to a fresh page of her own notebook and draws the glim sigil, large, with the new ink, and pushes it across the table to you, and looks at you.

You understand. You hold out your left palm over the table.

Lin sits beside you. Close, on the next chair, watching your hand. You say the word. You draw the sigil in the air over your palm, slowly, carefully, the way you drew it on paper this morning: the stroke, the loop, and the hook curling in, in, not out, the way she drew it.

The spark comes.

It's still tiny. A single white point, a pinprick. But this time it doesn't just flicker and die. It lifts. It rises off your palm, a whole inch into the air, and hangs there, wavering, a tiny star over your hand. One heartbeat. Two.

Then it goes out.

Lin makes a sound. A small, soft, pleased sound, an *oh* with a laugh in it, completely unguarded.

And without thinking, you grab her hand.

Her inky hand, lying on the table beside the book. You just take hold of it, in the rush of it, and squeeze, and it's small and cool and stained at the fingertips and it fits in yours.

She lets you.

She doesn't pull away. She sits there with her hand in yours, quite still, and you can feel her pulse going quick under your thumb, at the base of her wrist. You look at her. Her eyes are on your joined hands. Her face is very pink.

After a moment, gently, she takes her hand back. She does it slowly, as if she doesn't want you to think it's because of anything, her fingers sliding out of yours one at a time.

"Goodnight," she says. Softly. Still pink. She gathers up the green book and the ink and her notebook and holds them against her chest, and stops in the kitchen doorway, and looks back at you for a second. Then she goes down the stairs.

---

Alone in room 14, you take out the phone.

The empty one. You sit on the bed and hold it, and look at its blank screen, and then you open the keypad, and type a number.

Alan's number. Your old number. You've known it for years. Your thumb knows it without asking you, the way Lin said a hand would learn a sigil; it goes from key to key in the old pattern and the eleven digits come up on the screen, one after another, in a row.

You look at them.

You don't know what would happen. You don't know if it would ring. You don't know who would answer, if anyone did: if it would be you, the old you, in the chair at the back window with the book; or a stranger; or a recorded voice saying the number has not been recognised. You don't know which of those would be worst.

You sit there a long time, your thumb over the green button.

Then you delete it. One digit at a time, back to the beginning, until the screen is empty again. You put the phone face down on the blanket.

Your head aches. It's been aching since the kitchen, since the spark, a dull pressure behind your eyes, not as bad as yesterday's. But it's there. And you don't want to lie in the dark with it and the phone and the number.

You get up, and go down the corridor, and knock on room 1.

---

Yusra opens the door in a soft grey jumper, with a pair of reading glasses on her nose. You've never seen her in glasses. They make her look younger, and a little startled, and then she sees who it is and the startled look goes.

"Ashby," she says. "Come in."

Her room is the same size as yours, and nothing like it. It's neat the way a hospital ward is neat. The bed is made tight, the corners square. The books on her shelves are lined up by height, their spines flush with the edge of the shelf, and the ones on her desk are squared in a pile, except for one big heavy textbook lying open under the lamp, its pages dense with diagrams of hands and the veins in them. There's a kettle on a tray with two mugs, upside down. And on the windowsill, against the dark glass, a small blue bowl with three lemons in it, bright yellow, the only colour in the room.

She sits you on her desk chair. Then she takes your wrist.

She does it without asking, as if she's done it a thousand times: her fingers on the inside of your wrist, two of them, warm and dry, pressing lightly on the place where your pulse is. She looks at her watch. She's quiet, counting. You sit very still and look at her face, the dark brows, the reading glasses, her mouth moving very slightly as she counts, and you can feel your pulse going under her fingers and you hope it isn't telling her anything.

"Headache?" she says.

"Yes."

"Where?"

You touch the front of your head, behind your eyes. She nods, and lets go of your wrist, and looks at you over the glasses.

"Lighter than yesterday's," she says. "I can see it on you. Yesterday you looked like you'd been hit with a door." She almost smiles. "You're overreaching less already. Your body's learning where the edge is."

She fills a glass at the little sink in the corner and gives it to you, and shakes two paracetamol out of a bottle into your palm. You take them. You drink the whole glass. She takes it back and fills it again, and gives you that too, and sits down on the end of her bed facing you, and takes her glasses off.

"So," she says. "How was today?"

And she listens. She actually listens: her elbows on her knees, her dark eyes on your face, not looking at the textbook or her watch or the door. You tell her about the scones the size of fists, and Shiv eating two, and Amara and the freckles. About the charity shop, and Bea throwing jumpers at you, and the fisherman's jumper, and Bea paying for it. About the slab of rock, and putting your feet in the mere, and the shriek. She laughs at that, properly, a low surprised laugh that changes her whole face.

You don't tell her about Kit. About the corridor, or the window, or the laugh. You leave all of that out.

When you've finished she shakes her head, slowly.

"Doyle," she says. "I know all about Doyle and that rock. She'll have you swimming in the mere by the end of term." She gets up. "And I'll pretend not to know a thing about it."

She walks you to the door. You step out into the dim corridor, and turn to say goodnight, and her hand comes down on your shoulder.

It rests there a moment. Warm and steady, through the wool of the fisherman's jumper. Her thumb moves once, just slightly, against the cable of the knit.

"Sleep, Ashby," she says.

You go back down the corridor to room 14 and get into bed, and pull the blanket up, and you're asleep almost at once.
