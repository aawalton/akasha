On Monday of your second week, at half past six, Bea knocks.

You're already up. You've been up ten minutes, sitting on the edge of the bed in leggings and the grey jumper with your trainers on and your hair in its band, listening for her. When the three flat cheerful thumps come you're at the door before the third one lands, and you open it, and Bea's fist is still raised.

She blinks at you.

"Oh," she says. "You're *dressed*."

"I'm dressed."

"You're dressed and you're *up* and you're not groaning." Bea looks you up and down in the dim corridor with her mouth slightly open. "Who are you, and what have you done with Nala?"

"Come on," you say, and go past her down the stairs.

---

The shore is grey and still, the mere flat as poured lead under a low sky. You run with Bea. You chose it, lying awake before dawn: the shore with Bea, this morning, instead of the rock. And when you're ten minutes along the shingle a white dot moves out on the water, and stops, and an arm comes up out of the mere and waves at you, and you wave back, and keep running.

Bea glances sideways at you. She doesn't say anything. But she runs a little closer, after that.

Granny pace. Short steps. In for three, out for three. Your new body has learned it now; your legs find the rhythm on their own, and your lungs settle into it, and the shore goes by, the bracken and the shingle and the grey water, and you keep going, and keep going. Past the place where you stopped on Wednesday. Past the boathouse. On and on.

When your lungs finally give, you stagger to a halt with your hands on your knees, and Bea looks at her watch, and whoops.

"*Fifteen!*" She punches the air. "Fifteen minutes! Nala! A week ago you went off like a whippet and died after four! You're a *machine*!"

"I'm dying," you gasp.

"You're dying *fit*," says Bea, and grins, and slaps you on the back.

---

Walking back, she goes quiet.

You notice it after a while. Usually on the way back she talks the whole time, about breakfast and her sisters and whatever she dreamed about. This morning she walks beside you with her eyes on the shingle, and doesn't say anything, and her hands are pulled up inside the sleeves of her rowing top.

"Practical's today," she says at last.

"Two o'clock."

"Mm." Bea kicks a stone. "And I still haven't got a glim." She says it lightly. Too lightly. "Week two. Everyone in my group's got one now except me and one other girl, and she had flu."

"You've got half of one," you say.

"Two-thirds of one," Bea says, and makes a face, trying to make it a joke. "By my estimate. Two-thirds. Which is like having two-thirds of a bicycle." She laughs. It doesn't sound right.

You look at her hands. They're jammed down hard into the ends of her sleeves, clenched, the wool stretched tight over her knuckles.

---

At ten o'clock the Foundations lecture fills the Long Room again. You find a place halfway up, and a moment later Priya drops in beside you in a rush of corduroy, today's pinafore bottle-green, and starts unpacking her pens: black, green, red. She gives you a bright good-morning smile and gets straight to work.

The lecture is about thresholds again. Priya draws the boulder on the hill in the margin of her notes. You follow most of it this time.

Halfway through, Priya's red pen moves across from her notebook to yours.

She writes in your margin, small and neat, in red, the colour for things she doesn't understand yet:

*Have you ever kissed a girl?*

You stare at it.

You feel it start in your chest and go all the way up, hot and fast: you go scarlet. Your neck, your cheeks, your ears, your scalp. You look sideways at Priya. She's looking at the lecturer, the picture of attention, her pen poised, but her eyes keep flicking sideways to your page.

You pick up the blue pen. You write underneath, very small:

*Yesterday.*

Priya's eyes drop to the page. And go enormous. Huge, behind the tortoiseshell glasses, dark and round and fascinated. Her mouth falls open. She looks at you. You look determinedly at the lecturer, your face burning.

Her red pen moves again.

*What's it LIKE?*

You think about the rock. About Shiv's cold hands on your face, and her mouth.

You write: *Cold, then warm.*

Priya reads it. She reads it again. Then she takes her red pen and underlines it, slowly, carefully, three times. *Cold, then warm.* And she sits back on the bench and looks at it, frowning a little, tapping the pen against her lip, with exactly the face she wore on the Kendal bus. The face of someone thinking hard about something she has never once thought hard about before.

---

At two o'clock the Practice Hall smells of smoke and cold stone, and the demonstrator is in her canvas apron with her whistle round her neck, and she splits you in two.

"Glims," she says. "Who's got one? Hands up."

Hands go up. About two-thirds of the room. Yours. Amara's, straight and assured. Kit's, quickly, at the side. Lin's, a little shyly, half-raised.

"Right. Benches." She points to the long wooden benches against one wall, where a row of small grey stones are laid out at intervals. "You're on the warming charm. The rest of you, the corner." She points across the hall, to the far corner, where there's nothing but bare scorched boards. "Keep at your glims. No shame in it. Week two. Some of you'll have it by Friday."

The room splits. You go to the benches, with Amara and Kit and Lin and twenty others. And Bea goes to the corner, with a dozen girls, the ones without glims, shuffling across the hall with their heads down.

You look back.

Bea is standing in the corner among the others. She sees you looking. And she grins, a huge grin, both dimples, and gives you two thumbs up, enthusiastic, both hands. *Go on. You're brilliant.*

You turn back to the bench.

And at the last moment, out of the corner of your eye, before your head's quite round, you see the grin go out. All at once. Like a light switched off. Her face left behind it is white and tight and miserable.

---

The warming charm, the demonstrator says, is a stone.

Every one of the stones on the bench is the same: smooth and grey and flat, about the size of a duck's egg, worn soft by water. You pick yours up. It's cold and heavy and fits your palm exactly.

"Hold it," says the demonstrator. "Close your hand round it. Not tight. Draw the sigil over it with your other hand." She draws it, on the air: a spiral, coiling in, with a short stroke through the centre. "And call the heat in. Not *make* heat. *Call* it. It's already there, in the air, in you, in the stone. You're asking it to come and sit in one place."

Amara's stone is hot in a minute.

You hear her laugh, softly, delighted, down the bench, and look, and she's holding her open palm up with the stone on it and a thin wisp of steam curling up off it in the cold air of the hall. Girls nearby turn to look. Amara smiles at them.

Kit has taken the bench at the very end. Alone, with her back to the room. She's facing the wall, hunched slightly over her hands, so that her hands are hidden from the whole room. And after a minute or so she sets her stone down on the bench beside her and takes her hand away and shakes her fingers out, as if they're stinging, and you can see, even from here, the faint shimmer in the air above the stone.

Yours goes faintly warm.

You hold it, and draw the spiral, and call. You think of the sun on the rock. Of Bea's arm through yours on the shore. Of a mug of tea. And very slowly, the stone in your palm goes from cold to cool to not-cold, and then, just faintly, to warm. Warm like a stone that's been left out in the sun for an afternoon. Not hot. Barely more than your own hand. But warm.

Beside you, Lin's is warmer. You can tell from the way she's holding it, her fingers curled loose and easy round it, her face calm. She glances at your hand. Then, without a word, she holds hers out to show you: her grip, the stone sitting in the hollow of her palm with her fingers lying loose along it, just touching, the way she showed you to hold a pen. Not clutching. Letting it sit.

You loosen your fingers. The stone goes a little warmer.

All hour you keep glancing at the corner.

You can't help it. Every few minutes your eyes go across the hall to the girls by the scorched wall, and to Bea among them. She's working hard. You can see her doing it, over and over: the word, the sigil, her face screwed up in concentration. And every time, a light starts to come. A little gold-white bead rises off her palm, an inch, two inches, halfway to her eyes. And every time, halfway up, it wobbles, and fizzles, and goes out like a spark landing in water.

She tries again. It rises halfway and fizzles.

Again. Halfway. Fizzle.

You watch her shoulders go up a little higher each time.

---

At the end of the hour, the demonstrator blows her whistle, and Bea is first out of the door.

You see her go. Fast, almost running, head down, through the crowd and out of the doors of the Practice Hall and gone, before you've even put your stone down. You go after her. By the time you get out into the quad she's nowhere. Not on the paths. Not in the Thornfield kitchen. Her door is shut, and when you knock, there's no answer.

You find her an hour later on Shiv's rock.

You only think to look there because you've looked everywhere else. And she's there, alone, sitting at the landward end of the slab where Shiv peels off her socks, with her knees pulled up to her chest and her arms wrapped round them, staring out at the water. The mere is grey and choppy this afternoon under a lowering sky, little waves slapping against the edge of the rock. The wind is pulling at her hair, which has come entirely out of its bun.

She doesn't turn round when she hears your feet on the shingle. She knows it's you.

You climb up and sit down beside her on the cold rock. Close, but not touching. You look at the water too.

For a long time neither of you says anything.

"I was let in by mistake," Bea says.

She says it to the mere. Flat and quiet.

"I think they got my application mixed up with someone's. Or they needed to make up the numbers. Or something." She hugs her knees tighter. "And they're going to find out. Hollowmere. They're going to find out, and send me home. Before the end of term. I know they are. They'll call me into some office and say *I'm terribly sorry, Miss Lindqvist, there's been an administrative error,* and I'll have to go back to Bristol and tell my mum and dad and the café and everyone, and—"

Her voice cracks. She stops.

"Everyone's got one but me," she says. "Everyone. Even you." She turns her head and looks at you at last, and her eyes are red and wet and furious, mostly at herself. "And you'd never even *seen* a kettle charm. A week ago. You stood in the kitchen and stared at the kettle like I'd pulled a rabbit out of it."

"I know," you say.

"So what's *wrong* with me?"

You look at her. At her wet furious face and her wild hair and her knees pulled up tight.

"Bea," you say. "I'm the one who doesn't know anything. About anything here. I'm the one who didn't know what a focus was. Or which fork to use. Or that you don't put bras in the dryer. I didn't know *any* of it. I'm the one who should get sent home. Not you."

And you almost tell her.

It comes right up to your mouth. The whole of it. *I'm not Nala. I mean I am, but I wasn't, a week ago. A week ago I was a man called Alan in a chair by a window with a book, and then I woke up here, in this body, in that bed across from yours, with no idea of anything. That's why I didn't know what a kettle charm was. That's why my phone's empty. That's why.* It's right there, the whole truth, pressing against the back of your teeth. She's sitting right beside you, red-eyed and frightened, and she's the first person you met here, and you could just say it.

You hold it close a while longer.

Not yet. You don't know why not yet. You just know that it's too big, today, on this rock, with her already crying. That it'll keep. That you'll know when.

Instead you say:

"Can I tell you something? About how I got mine?"

Bea sniffs. She nods.

So you tell her about the thread. The thin bright thread from the middle of your chest out to the light, and how you have to hold on to it, and how when you let go, it goes out. And you tell her about Lin, in the Practice Hall on your first day, drawing the sigil on a scrap of paper, slow, and pointing at the hook. *It turns in. Not out. You've been doing it like the tail of a j.* And how the first time you did it Lin's way, you got a spark.

Bea is listening now. Her eyes have stopped being furious. She's looking at you, frowning slightly, her chin on her knees.

"Show me," you say.

She blinks.

"Right here," you say. "With only me watching. The corner of the Practice Hall's got a dozen people in it and the demonstrator walking round. Here's just me." You hold her gaze. "Show me."

Bea looks at you a long moment.

Then she lets go of her knees. She wipes her face with both sleeves, hard. She sits up on the cold rock and holds out her left hand, palm up, over her lap.

She says the word.

She draws the sigil over her palm. The stroke, straight up. The loop, round.

And the hook flicks out.

You see it. Exactly the way yours did, in the Practice Hall a week ago. Outward, away from the loop, the little tail of a *j*.

You reach out and take her hand. Her right hand, her drawing hand, the one with the finger raised. You hold it lightly by the wrist and the back of the fingers, the way Lin held yours on the pen in the Drawing Room. And you guide it. Back to the bottom of the loop. And round, slowly, the other way. Inward. Curling back on itself, closing in, like a fern before it opens.

"Like that," you say. "In. Not out."

Bea's breath catches. She does it again, by herself, with your hand still resting on hers: the stroke, the loop, and the hook curling in.

A light rises off her palm.

It's small. Small as a pea. And it's gold-tinged, warmer than yours, the colour of a candle flame rather than a star. It wobbles as it comes, unsteady, the way hers have been wobbling all afternoon. It rises an inch. Two inches. Halfway to her eyes, where all the others fizzled.

It wobbles there. You hold your breath. Bea holds hers.

And it steadies.

It stops wobbling. It just steadies, all at once, and goes on rising, smooth and slow, up and up, and stops at the height of her eyes, and hangs there. A little gold light in the grey afternoon, over the grey choppy mere, with the wind blowing round it and not touching it at all.

It holds.

Bea stares at it. Her mouth is open. The light from it is shining on her face, gold, on her wet cheeks and her red nose and her wide brown eyes. She doesn't move. She doesn't breathe. Three seconds. Five. Ten.

Then she laughs.

It bursts out of her: a huge cracked laugh, wet and wild and disbelieving, from somewhere down in her belly. And the glim bobs with it, jumping in the air in front of her face as she laughs, bobbing up and down like a cork on water. But it stays. It doesn't go out. It bobs and dances and stays, and Bea sees it staying, and shrieks. A high, ecstatic, ear-splitting shriek of pure joy that goes ringing out over the mere and comes back off the fells.

And she throws her arms round you.

So hard. So fast. She flings herself sideways at you on the rock and wraps both arms round your neck and your shoulders and hugs you with all her strength, and you're knocked backwards, and for one terrifying second you both tip towards the edge of the slab and the grey water, and you grab a handful of rock with one hand and her jumper with the other, and you don't go in. You're both laughing. You're both shrieking. The glim has gone out somewhere in the middle of it. Bea doesn't care. She's hanging on round your neck, laughing into your hair.

And still holding you, she kisses your cheek.

Not quickly. Not a peck, a friend's kiss, a thank-you. Slowly. She turns her face, with her arms still round you, and presses her mouth to your cheek, just below your cheekbone, and it's soft and warm and wet from her tears, and she doesn't take it away. She stays there. Her mouth against your skin, her breath warm on your face, her whole body pressed against your side on the cold rock.

You keep very still.

Your heart is going so hard you can feel it in your throat. You can feel her mouth on your cheek, every bit of it, the softness and the warmth, the way you felt Shiv's yesterday, ten times more than you ever would have before. You don't move. You hardly breathe.

"I think," Bea says. Into your cheek. Her lips moving against your skin as she says it. Very quietly. "I think I'm finding out what I want."

You keep very still.

"Take your time," you say.

And Bea laughs again. Shaky, this time. A small, trembling, astonished laugh. She doesn't let go. She stays there, holding on to you, her forehead against your temple now, her arms tight round you, laughing a little and shaking a little, on Shiv's rock in the wind.

---

In the evening it's cold toast in the Thornfield kitchen, and Bea casts her glim for anyone who comes in.

Anyone. Everyone. A girl comes in to fill a hot-water bottle and Bea says, "*Look*," and casts it. Another comes in for milk, and Bea says, "Look, look, *watch*," and casts it again. It comes every time now. Small and gold, wobbling a little at the start and then steadying, rising to her eye level and hanging there. She's giddy with it. She can't stop.

Lin comes up from downstairs with her notebook. Bea casts it for her the moment she's through the door. And Lin's face lights up, and she claps, fast, her inky hands together, and says, "Oh, *Bea*," softly, and Bea goes pink with pleasure.

Then a shadow stops in the kitchen doorway.

Yusra. Passing, with a folder under her arm, her badge at her collar. She stops, and looks in, and sees the gold light hanging in the air over Bea's palm.

She looks at it for a second. Then at Bea.

"Lindqvist," says Yusra. "Good."

And goes on down the corridor.

Bea stares after her. Then she turns to you with her eyes enormous.

"She said *good*," Bea whispers. "She said *good*, Nala. *Yusra* said *good*. To *me*."

She glows all evening.

You've never seen anyone glow like it. She makes too much toast and gives it away. She tells the story of the rock to Lin, twice, and leaves out the cheek, both times; you see her leave it out. And every time she passes your chair she bumps your shoulder with hers, gently, the way she always does, and it means something different now. You can feel that it does.

And once, sitting at the kitchen table, with Lin across from you looking at Bea's sigil in her notebook, Bea's hand finds yours under the table.

Just finds it. On your knee. Her fingers slide between yours, warm, and hold on, and stay. She doesn't look at you. She goes on talking to Lin about the hook. But she holds your hand under the table for a long time, and you hold hers back.

---

In bed, you lie awake.

The radiator knocks. The window over your head is dark, with stars showing through a gap in the clouds. Across the corridor, behind the door of room 15, Bea is asleep, or not.

Your cheek is still warm.

The place where her mouth was. Just below your cheekbone. You can still feel it there, if you lie still enough: the softness, and the warmth, and her breath, and her lips moving against your skin as she said it.

*I think I'm finding out what I want.*

You lie in the dark with your hand over the warm place on your cheek, and don't sleep for a long time.
