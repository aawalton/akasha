On Monday the world is washed clean.

You wake to quiet. Not the hush of rain, not the soft knock of the radiator, but real quiet, deep and still, and light coming round the edge of the curtain so bright and white it hurts. You get up and pull the curtain back.

Blue. A cold hard blue, without a cloud in it, from one edge of the sky to the other.

The mere is flat again. Flat as glass, pale and shining, and littered all over with branches and twigs and leaves, floating, turning slowly, a whole wood's worth of wreckage scattered across the water as far as the far shore. And the fells are back. Every one of them, sharp-edged against the blue, so clear you can see the walls running up them and the scree on their flanks, and on the high tops, for the first time, a dusting of white.

Snow. Already.

---

At the boathouse the swimmers are back on.

The register is out. The whistle is round the second-year's neck. The shingle is strewn with weed and driftwood from the storm, and the girls stepping over it in their robes are quieter than usual, looking out at the flat bright water with something like respect.

You sign. Shiv signs under you, just a scrawl this time, *Doyle*, and a little drawing of a cloud with lightning coming out of it.

You go in together and swim out through the floating wreckage. Twigs bump against your arms. A whole branch, still with yellow leaves on, drifts past your face. The water is colder than it's ever been, a hard, clean, bitter cold that makes your skin sing, and the sun is coming up over the fells and laying a long gold road across the mere, straight to the buoy.

You hang there, the two of you, breathing.

"Better than a storm," Shiv says. She looks round at the blue sky, the white tops, the gold water. "Worse than a rock."

And she splashes you, a flat slap of cold water right in the face, and grins with every one of her teeth.

---

You come up the Thornfield stairs with your hair dripping and your towel round your neck.

As you reach the top corridor, Bea's door opens.

Kit comes out first.

She's in Bea's jumper. A big soft cream one, cable-knit, much too large, the sleeves turned back twice at the wrists and the hem hanging nearly to her knees, over her own long black skirt. Her bob is brushed perfectly straight. Her face is perfectly composed.

She sees you, and stops.

She lifts her chin.

"I'll return it," Kit says. Of the jumper.

Behind her in the doorway, Bea snorts.

---

The three of you walk to breakfast together across the drying quad.

The flood has gone down, but the lawns are still sodden, squelching, and the paths are covered in a skin of drying mud and leaves and the odd lost umbrella. The air is so cold and clear it's like drinking water. Bea walks in the middle, an arm through yours and, after a moment's hesitation that you see her decide on, an arm through Kit's, and talks the whole way: about the storm, about the window, about whether the boathouse roof is all right, about the snow, *snow, already, Nala, is that normal here*. Kit lets her arm be taken. She walks very upright. She doesn't pull it back.

Amara is at the doors of the Great Hall.

She's waiting for someone, or pretending to, leaning on the doorframe in a camel coat with her braids up and her gold hoops catching the sun. She sees the three of you coming. Her eyes go to Kit. To Kit coming across the quad from Thornfield, arm in arm with Bea, at eight in the morning, in a large cream jumper.

One of her beautifully shaped brows goes up. Slowly. All the way.

"Ashworth," says Amara. She sounds delighted. Absolutely delighted. "*Wool*. *Cream*." She lets the pause hang. "Whatever next."

Kit walks past her like a queen.

She doesn't look at her. She doesn't speed up. She takes her arm out of Bea's and walks in through the doors with her chin up and her back straight and the cream jumper hanging off her like a royal robe, and Amara, behind her, laughs out loud.

---

After breakfast you go back up to change for the lecture.

In the top corridor a glazier is fitting new glass into the end window. He's a cheerful man in white overalls with a pencil behind his ear, kneeling on a dust sheet, with the board you held on Sunday night leaning against the wall beside him, all its nails pulled out. He's whistling. The new pane goes in shining, catching the blue sky.

Yusra is standing by him with a list.

She's in her neat knit and wool trousers again, the warden's badge at her collar, her hair pushed back off her face. Her left palm is bandaged, a clean white pad bound round with white, very neat.

She sees you coming along the corridor.

She lifts the bandaged hand. Slightly. Just a few inches, palm out, so you can see it.

"Healing," she says. "Clean cut." Her dark eyes rest on you a moment. "You did right."

Then she turns back to the glazier, who has come all the way out from Kendal first thing, she tells him, and so had better do it properly.

"Double-check that frame," Yusra says.

---

At ten it's the Foundations lecture in the Long Room.

Today the lecture is about mending.

"Why," she says, "does a broken thing want to be whole?"

She lets it sit. The Long Room is warm and full and quiet, the sun pouring in through its tall windows in long gold bars.

"A cracked cup," she says, "remembers being a cup. Every part of it was made together, in one firing, in one shape. When you mend it, you aren't forcing anything. You're reminding it. You're helping it back to something it already wants." She smiles. "That is why mending is easier than making. And why it is very much harder than it looks."

Kit sits beside you, taking neat sharp notes.

Lin sits on your other side. She isn't taking notes. She's drawing, very lightly, in pencil, in the margin of your page. The lecturer's hands: one held up, explaining, the fingers spread; one resting on the lectern. Small, and quick, and exactly right.

---

At two it's the Practice Hall.

On every bench there's a teacup. White china, plain, the cheap kind, and every one of them has a crack in it: a single dark line running from the rim down towards the base. On the board at the front, chalked large, is a new sigil: a line that splits in two and then comes back together, joined, with a small closed loop where it meets.

"Mending," says the demonstrator. "Before we start." She claps her hands, twice, sharp, and the hall goes quiet. "Notice for you all. On Friday, at two, each first-year casts a glim and warms a stone."

A murmur.

"Before the whole hall," says the demonstrator. "One at a time. It's a test." She looks round at them all, not unkindly. "Everyone passes who's practised. So practise."

Beside you, Kit has gone perfectly still.

Not a flicker on her face. It stays exactly as it was, cool and pale and composed. But her hand is on the bench beside her cracked cup, and as you look, the knuckles go white. White as the china.

You don't say anything. You can't, here.

Kit picks up her cup without a word and carries it to the end bench, alone, and sits with her back to the room.

You pick up your cup.

You draw the mending sigil over it, on the out-breath, slowly, the split line and the join and the little closed loop, and call. Gently. The way you'd call the heat into the stone.

The crack begins to close. You can see it: the dark line drawing together from the rim, the two sides of the china sliding to meet, sealing, a quarter of the way, halfway down—

And stops.

Halfway. The bottom half of the crack is still there, dark and open. The top half is closed, but not smoothly: there's a seam where it closed, a pale raised line, like a scar. You can see exactly where the mending started and where it gave out.

Beside you there's a small noise.

Bea is staring at her cup. It hasn't mended at all. The crack is exactly where it was. But the cup is steaming very faintly in the cold air of the hall, and when she touches it, she snatches her finger back.

"It's *warm*," she whispers, appalled. "That's the wrong *spell*."

You bite your lip hard.

At the end bench, Kit lifts her own cup.

You see her shoulders lift and drop. One breath. Her hand moves over the china, quick and small, in the shadow of her own body, out of everyone's sight.

And the cup is whole.

Just like that. In one smooth breath. You see it from three benches away when she sets it down: white and plain and perfect, the crack gone, not a seam, not a line, as if it had never been broken in its life.

---

After practical Kit walks out fast.

She doesn't wait. She's out of the doors before the demonstrator has finished talking, and across the quad, walking very quickly, her black coat flapping, not towards Ashcombe but past it, down the slope, towards the shore.

You go after her.

You catch her up at the water's edge, below the boathouse, on the shingle still strewn with storm wrack. She has stopped right at the edge, where the flat bright water laps at the stones, and is standing very straight, looking out at the mere and the white-topped fells.

She doesn't turn round.

"Before the whole hall," Kit says.

Her voice is quite level.

"One at a time. I'll freeze. You know I will." She's looking at the water. "You've seen me freeze."

You have. The first glim in the Practice Hall, with forty girls watching; her hand in the air, stopped; and after, at her side, shaking.

"My mother will hear," says Kit. "She hears everything. She'll hear by Friday night. An Ashworth." The word comes out sharp. "An Ashworth who can't cast a glim in front of people."

You come and stand beside her. Not too close. Just beside her, at the edge of the water, looking where she's looking.

You think about it for a while. About the ring of perfect glims you saw through the window, alone, at dusk. About the cup on the end bench, whole in one breath, with her back to the room.

"Then we practise," you say. "Being watched."

Kit turns her head.

"A bit more each day," you say. "Starting small. Starting tonight."

"Small."

"One pair of eyes," you say. "Mine." You look at her. "Then more."

Kit looks at you. A long, long time. Her grey-green eyes on yours, very steady, the wind lifting the ends of her black hair.

"Yours," she says.

---

At dusk the Practice Hall is empty.

The benches are cleared, the cups put away, the chalk wiped off the board. The long hall is dim and grey and cold, the light failing in the high windows, the scorched old floorboards going dark. It's the same hall. The same windows. You stood outside one of them once, at this hour, and looked in, and saw her.

You walk to the far end, and sit down on the floor with your back against the wall. Facing away from her.

"Go on," you say, to the wall.

Silence behind you. A long one.

Then a very small sound. A breath, let out.

And the light comes.

You don't see it. You see its light: a soft pale glow blooming on the stone wall in front of you, throwing your own shadow long across the floorboards. Steady. Perfectly steady. It doesn't flicker once. It hangs there behind you, and you sit and watch your shadow and don't move, and after a while the light goes out.

"Good," you say. "Now I'm going to turn sideways."

You shift round, so you're sitting side-on to her, looking at the wall to your left. She's at the edge of your sight, now. A dark shape at the far end of the hall. You don't look. You look at the wall.

Another breath.

The glow comes again, at the edge of your eye. A little smaller, this time. A little paler. But it holds. It holds, and holds, and then goes out.

"Good," you say. "Kit. That's good."

You turn round, and look at her.

She's standing in the middle of the floor at the far end of the hall in her black coat, her hand already lifted. She looks back at you.

She draws the sigil in the air. The stroke, the loop, the hook turning in. She says the word.

And freezes.

You see it happen. Her hand stops, in the air, at the end of the hook, and stays there. Rigid. Her whole body goes still. Her face goes white, so white it seems to glow in the dimness, and her eyes are fixed on you, wide, and she isn't breathing.

You don't look away. You keep looking at her. Gently. As gently as you know how.

"Kit," you say.

Her eyes are on yours.

"Breathe out," you say.

A second passes. Two.

Then she breathes out.

You see her chest go down, her shoulders drop. And in the air above her stiff hand there's a flicker. Light. A small, wavering, uncertain thing, half a glim, less, trembling, so faint you can see the wall through it—

And it's gone. In a second. Out, like a match.

But it was there.

Kit stares at the empty air above her hand where it was. She stares for a long time.

Then she laughs. A shaky laugh, half a breath, high and unsteady, and she lowers her hand, and it's trembling.

"That was terrible," Kit says.

You get up off the floor. You walk across the dim hall to her.

"That was the first one," you say.

Kit looks at you. And then she takes a fistful of the front of your coat and pulls you in, and kisses you, hard, once, in the dark hall, her mouth fierce and cold and shaking.

She lets go.

"Tomorrow," she says.

---

At ten that night, the Thornfield kitchen door bangs open, and Priya bursts in.

She's back from charmcraft. She's glowing. Her glasses are pushed up into her hair, her cheeks are flushed, there's a smear of oil on her nose, and she's carrying a small tin box in both hands as if it were a baby.

"*Clockwork*," she says, to the whole kitchen. "You would not *believe*. There's a whole *drawer* of escapements, Nala, a *drawer*—"

You and Bea are at the table with cocoa. Bea pours her a mug.

Priya sits down opposite. She talks about the drawer. She talks about the escapements, and the secretary, and the mouse, and a thing called a fusee that you don't follow. And then she stops.

"Amara came," she says.

You wait.

"To charmcraft," Priya says. "She came. Amara. She *hates* clockwork. She *said* so. Twice. In the first five minutes. 'I hate clockwork.' And then again, 'I really do hate clockwork, Priya.'" She looks at you. Her eyes are very big behind the glasses. "And she stayed. The whole two hours. She sat on the bench next to mine and handed me screwdrivers."

She sits down very suddenly. Even though she's already sitting. Something in her sits down, all at once, and she goes quiet, and looks into her cocoa, and stares at it, and doesn't drink it.

Bea reaches over, and pats her hand. Quietly. And leaves her hand there.

---

In 14, before bed, you sit at your desk with your timetable.

You turn it over, to the blank back, and uncap your pen with Lin's soft nib in it, and write a list.

*Tuesday. Wednesday. Thursday.*

And beside them: *Kit. Hall.*

Under it, smaller:

*Friday.*

You look at that for a while.

Then, underneath, smallest of all, just for yourself, in letters so tiny you can barely read them:

*breathe out.*
