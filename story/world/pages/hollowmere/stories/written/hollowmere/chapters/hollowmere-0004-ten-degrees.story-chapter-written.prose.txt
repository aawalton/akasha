On Thursday you wake at six, before Bea's knock.

You don't know what wakes you. The room is dark grey, the window over your head a pale tall shape, the radiator quiet for once. You lie on your back with your eyes open and your hair across the pillow and the first thing in your head, clear as if someone has just said it aloud beside the bed, is Shiv's voice on the rock last night. *Come in with me some morning.*

Some morning.

You lie there and listen to your heart, which has started going a little fast.

Then you get up. You don't let yourself think about it. You pull on knickers and a bra in the dark, by feel, and then the fisherman's jumper straight over the top, the heavy cream wool coming down past your hips, scratchy against your bare thighs. You push your feet into your trainers without socks. You take the iron key and go out into the corridor in your bare legs and your big jumper, and creep past Bea's door, past room 1 at the head of the stairs, and down, and out.

---

The shore is grey and silent. The mere lies flat as a sheet of slate, steaming faintly where the cold air meets it, and the fells across the water are black against a sky going slowly pearl and then pink along the top. The shingle is wet and very cold under the thin soles of your trainers. Your legs are bare to the knee-length hem of the jumper, and the air bites them, and you walk faster.

Shiv is already on the slab of rock.

She's sitting at the landward end in the big navy parka with her bare legs stuck out in front of her, peeling off her socks one at a time and stuffing them into her shoes. Her strawberry-blonde hair is dry for once, tousled from sleep. She hears the shingle crunch, and looks up, and sees you.

She doesn't say anything at first. She just grins: slowly, a crooked grin that starts at one corner of her mouth and spreads, and goes on spreading, until her whole freckled face is lit with it.

"Some morning," she says. "Grand."

She stands up and unzips the parka and drops it on the rock. Under it there's only a hoodie, and she pulls the hoodie off over her head in one movement and drops that too, and stands there naked on the rock in the grey light, quite unbothered, pale and wiry and pink-skinned with cold, freckles across her shoulders like spilled sugar. She pulls the white swim cap out of the parka's pocket and snaps it on, tucking her hair up under it. Then she holds out her hand to you.

You look at the hand. You look at the black water.

You pull the fisherman's jumper off over your head.

The cold hits your bare skin at once, everywhere, and you start shaking before you've even folded the jumper, a deep hard shiver that runs up from your knees. You kick off your trainers. You stand on the cold rock in your knickers and bra, arms wrapped round your middle, teeth already chattering, and the mere at your feet is black and still and smells of stone and deep water and you know it's going to be colder than anything you've ever felt.

"How cold is it?" you say.

"Ten degrees, about," Shiv says cheerfully. "Bit less, maybe."

"*Ten?*"

"The trick," Shiv says, "is not to think. You think, you don't go. Don't think." She takes your hand. Hers is cold and dry and strong, and her fingers fold round yours firmly, as if she means not to let go. "On three. Together. One."

You don't think.

"Two."

You hold on.

"*Three*."

You jump together, hand in hand, off the edge of the rock into the mere.

The cold hits like a fist.

It hits all of you at once, the whole of your body, so hard and so total that there's no room for anything else: it knocks every thought out of your head and every bit of breath out of your chest. Black water closes over you. Your skin is on fire, it's ice, it's burning, it's a thousand needles at once, and your heart slams, and you're going down, down in the black cold, and then you kick, and you come up.

You surface shrieking. You can't help it. The shriek comes ripping out of you as your head breaks the surface, high and raw and wordless, ringing off the still water and the fells, and you gasp and choke and shriek again.

And Shiv's hand is still in yours.

She never let go. Her fingers are locked round yours, under the water, and she's right there beside you, her capped head and her wet freckled face inches from yours, and she's laughing, laughing out loud, her other arm round your back holding you up.

"Breathe out," she says. "Out. Slow. Come on. Out, like you're blowing on soup."

You can't. Your chest is locked. Your breath is coming in short sharp shrieking gasps, in and in and in, and your whole body is clenched like a fist.

"Out," Shiv says again, steady and close, her arm firm across your back. "*Slow*. With me." And she breathes out, a long slow hiss through pursed lips, right by your face, and you watch her mouth and copy it, and the breath comes out of you shaking. "Again." Again. Out, slow. The gasping eases. The clench in your chest loosens one notch, then another. The panic lets go of you, a bit at a time, and what's left is the cold.

And then it isn't only cold.

You're treading water, the two of you, face to face, in the middle of the black mere. The fells all round are going pink along their tops. The water is so clear and still that you can see the light spreading across it, gold and rose, and the steam lifting off it in soft drifting wisps between you and the shore. Your whole body is burning. Every inch of your skin is roaring, alive, singing with cold, from your scalp to your toes; you can feel your own heart in your fingertips and your lips and the soles of your feet. You have never been so awake in either of your lives. You can feel every part of this small body at once, its edges, its heat burning out into the water from the inside.

Shiv grins at you across six inches of mere, water running off her cap and down her freckled face.

"Well?" she says.

You don't have words. You laugh instead, breathless and chattering, and she laughs back, and for one long minute you tread water together in the cold pink dawn, holding hands under the surface, with the steam going up round you both.

"Right," Shiv says, "out, before you go blue," and tows you back to the rock.

She hauls herself up first, in one easy pull, and then reaches down and grips both your wrists and hauls you out after her, and you come up out of the water onto the rock in a long streaming slither, gasping, and your legs won't hold you. Shiv grabs the parka and swings it round you before you can fall over. It's huge on you and still warm from her, lined with something soft, and it smells of her: salt and soap and wool. She zips it up to your chin with you inside it. Then she starts rubbing your arms through it, hard, up and down, fast, her hands brisk and rough.

"Blood," she says. "Get the blood going. Stamp your feet."

You stamp your feet. Your teeth are going like castanets. Your skin under the parka is pink and burning and prickling all over like it's been slapped.

Shiv is still naked. She's standing right up close to you on the rock, rubbing your arms, and she hasn't bothered to dress herself. Her wet shoulders are inches from your face. You can see the water beading on her skin and running in little rivulets down from the edge of the swim cap, over her collarbones, over the freckles scattered across her shoulders, gold-brown on the cold pink skin, dozens of them, hundreds. A drop runs from the hollow of her throat along her collarbone, through the freckles, and over her shoulder. You watch it go. You can't stop watching it.

She sees you look.

You know she does. Her hands slow on your arms. You lift your eyes, too late, and find hers already on your face, sea-green and close and amused, and you feel your cheeks go hot even through the cold.

She doesn't step back. She doesn't make a joke of it. She stays exactly where she is, close, her hands resting on your upper arms through the parka now, quite still. Her eyes move over your face. She's not grinning. Her mouth is soft, and a bit open, and her breath is going a little quick.

Neither of you moves.

"*NALA!*"

A voice, from along the shore. You both turn.

It's Bea. She's come running along the shingle in her rowing top and her tights, on her half-six run, her bun bouncing, and she's stopped dead twenty yards away and is standing there staring at the pair of you. At Shiv, stark naked and dripping on the rock. At you, wrapped in a parka to your chin with your wet hair plastered to your head and your bare legs bright pink beneath the hem. Her mouth is wide open.

"You went *IN*?" Bea shrieks.

You nod. You can't speak. Your teeth are chattering too hard.

"You went in the *MERE*? In the forbidden *MERE*? In *SEPTEMBER*?" Bea puts both hands on her head. "Oh my god. Oh my *god*. I'm telling Yusra." She's grinning. She's grinning so hard her dimples are about to fall into her face. "I'm telling Yusra *right now*."

"Don't you dare," says Shiv comfortably, and bends to pick up her hoodie at last.

---

The shower is the best of your life.

You stand under it with the hot water pouring over your head, and your skin, which is still pink and stinging from the mere, goes from burning cold to burning hot in one long glorious rush. It's like being born. You make sounds. You don't care who hears. You stay until the ache goes out of your fingers and toes and the last of the shivering stops, and even then you stay a little longer, just because you can.

All through breakfast your skin goes on humming. You sit beside Bea in the Great Hall with your hair still damp, eating porridge, and the whole surface of you feels new and bright and tingling, as if you've been scrubbed down to some fresh layer underneath. Bea can't stop looking at you.

"You went in," she keeps saying, under her breath, and shaking her head. "You went *in*."

---

At ten o'clock, History of Magic fills the Long Room.

It's the same room as the Foundations lecture, the benches stepped up steep and packed with first-years, the lecturer far below at her lectern. This one is old and slow and very thorough, with a long grey plait and a voice like a dry leaf, and she begins, as she announces, at the beginning: with the old guilds.

You find a place halfway up, near the end of a bench, and get out your notebook and the blue pen Priya hasn't asked for back.

Someone comes up the steps of the benches and stops at the end of your row.

You look up. It's Kit. She's in black, a long skirt and a high-necked black jumper, her books held against her chest, and she's standing there at the end of the bench looking at the space beside you. She doesn't say anything. There's a pause. A long one, two seconds, three, while she looks at the empty place on the bench and you look at her.

Then she sits down beside you. Silently. She sets out her books and her pen, squares them precisely in front of her, and fixes her eyes on the lecturer, and doesn't look at you once.

You look at your notebook. Your heart is doing something.

The lecture goes on. The old guilds of the fourteenth century, with their oaths and their masters and their secret marks. The guild wars. A king called Henry; then another one; then a long and complicated quarrel about who was allowed to cure the sick by charm and who was allowed to bless the harvest and who was allowed to curse whom. You half know some of it. That's the strange thing. The names are names you know from Alan's history, from school, from documentaries you half watched on the sofa: Henry, Elizabeth, the Civil War, Cromwell. But they've gone strange here, like a song you know played in the wrong key. Elizabeth had a court sigilist. Cromwell's men burned the guild halls. Kings you remember dying of illness here died of something the lecturer calls a *miscast*, and every face in the room stays perfectly calm.

Then the lecturer says a date, and you frown.

It's a date you know. Or you know a different one. It's the date of a battle, and in Alan's history the battle happened a whole lifetime later, and was about something else entirely, and here it was about the guilds, and here it was won by the other side. You sit there frowning at the date on the blackboard with your pen stopped over the page, trying to fit it together with what you remember, and it won't fit.

"What's the matter?"

It's Kit. Low, beside you, her voice barely above a breath. She hasn't turned her head. Her eyes are still on the lecturer. But she's seen your face.

"Nothing," you say. "Sorry. Nothing."

Kit is quiet for a moment. Then, still looking straight ahead, still barely above a breath, she says:

"She's got that date wrong, by the way. It was the following spring. She's been getting it wrong for forty years. My grandmother had her for this and she got it wrong then."

You look at her.

Kit's face is perfectly still. Her eyes are on the blackboard.

"And she'll tell you next that the Guildmaster of York had the longest beard in England," Kit murmurs, "which isn't true either. The Guildmaster of Lincoln did. He could tuck it into his belt. There's a portrait. It's the most appalling thing you've ever seen. It looks like he's being eaten by a sheep."

You make a sound. A small one, through your nose. You press your lips together.

Down at the lectern, the lecturer says, "And the Guildmaster of York, whose beard was famously the longest in England — "

Your shoulders start to shake.

Kit doesn't look at you. Her face doesn't change at all. She writes something neat in her notebook. And then, after a perfectly judged pause, under her breath: "Lincoln. Sheep."

You put your hand over your mouth. You're shaking with it, silently, your whole body going, tears coming up in your eyes, and you can't stop, and the more you try the worse it gets. Kit carries on, a deadpan murmur at the very edge of hearing, a running commentary on the guildmasters' beards and the lecturer's dates and the Licensing Act of 1858, which, she informs you, was drafted by a committee of eleven men, nine of whom had never cast so much as a kettle charm, and one of whom was later found to have been charming his own snuff. She says it all without the faintest flicker of expression. Her mouth stays perfectly straight. Only, once, when you have to bend over your notebook and press your face into your sleeve to keep from laughing out loud, you see from the corner of your streaming eye that the very corner of her mouth has given, just slightly, and that she's holding it there on purpose.

She's funny. She's quietly, wickedly, devastatingly funny, and you had no idea.

Two rows down, a head turns.

It's Amara. She's twisted round in her seat, braids swinging, to see what the muffled noise behind her is. Her eyes find you, red-faced and shaking with your sleeve over your mouth. And then they move, and find Kit beside you, writing neatly in her notebook with a perfectly straight face.

Amara's eyes narrow.

Only a little. Only for a moment. Her gaze goes from you to Kit and back, and her shaped brows draw together, very slightly, as if she's doing a sum and doesn't like the answer. Then she turns back round to face the front.

Beside you, Kit has gone very still. She writes another line. She doesn't say anything else for the rest of the lecture.

---

At two o'clock it's practical casting in the Practice Hall again: the bare stone, the scorched boards, the smell of smoke, forty first-years in a loose crowd. Glims, says the demonstrator, one at a time, at the front, where she can see you. A line forms. You end up in it near the front, just behind Kit.

Amara goes first again.

She steps out onto the scorched boards before anyone else has moved, as she did on Tuesday, and holds out her palm, and says the word, and draws the sigil, smooth and quick, the gold watch glinting. And her glim rises off her hand bright and steady and perfect, again, and hangs at her eye level, glowing.

The hall murmurs. Someone near the front says, "Again," admiringly, under her breath. Amara smiles, and lets the light hang, and lets them look.

Then the demonstrator says, "Next," and you see who's next in line.

Kit.

She's standing at the front of the line in her black, very straight, her chin up. She's looking at Amara's light. Her face is calm and pale and closed. And her right hand, down at her side against the black wool of her skirt, has already started to tremble.

You see it. A fine fast shake in her fingers, the silver ring catching the light. Exactly like Tuesday. She's pressing it flat against her skirt. It isn't stopping. Every eye in the hall is about to swing from Amara's glim to her.

You don't think. It's like the rock this morning: you don't let yourself think.

You step out of the line, in front of Kit, before she can move.

"I'll go," you say. Loudly. Much more loudly than you need to, loud enough that it rings off the bare stone walls. "Can I go? I'll go next."

Every eye in the hall swings to you.

You feel it land. Forty faces turning, all at once, away from Amara and the front of the line and onto you: the girl who'd never cast a spell. The one who put her hand up alone in Penhallow's tower. The one with the dark palm on Tuesday, who got one wobbling spark. You can feel them looking. Curious, and surprised, and some of them already a little sorry for you.

The demonstrator raises her eyebrows. "Go on, then, Ashby."

You walk out onto the scorched boards. You don't look back at Kit.

You hold out your left hand, palm up. It's small and pale and freckled and it doesn't look any more like a hand that can make light than it did on Tuesday. Your heart is going like it did on the rock.

The word. You say it clearly, the lilt in it like *leer*.

The stroke, straight up. The loop, round. And the hook, curling in, in, not out, the way Lin drew it, the way you've drawn it a hundred times on cream paper with a soft nib and in the dark over your bed before you sleep.

And you mean it. You mean light with everything you have. With the whole of this new small burning body, the body that jumped into the black mere this morning and came up alive. You want light the way you wanted air in that water.

A light rises off your palm.

It's small. Smaller than Amara's, smaller than the demonstrator's, a white bead the size of a pea. It wavers as it comes, unsteady, flickering at the edges like a candle in a draught. But it lifts. It lifts off your palm, an inch, two inches, and keeps going, slow and wavering, up and up, until it's level with your eyes.

And it hangs there.

You stare at it. It's right in front of your face, a tiny trembling star in the air. It holds. Three seconds. Five. It's wavering, but it's holding; you can feel it, somehow, a thin bright thread running from the middle of your chest to that point of light, and you hold on to the thread with everything you've got. Ten seconds.

Your first glim.

Somewhere behind you, Priya squeals. A real squeal, high and delighted. And Shiv whoops, loud, a whoop like she's at a football match, and someone laughs, and someone else starts to clap.

Every eye in the hall is on you, and on your little wavering light.

Not on Kit.

Behind you, where you can't see, where every eye in the hall has turned away, something happens. You only catch it from the corner of your eye: a second light, rising. Clean and white and steady. It lifts off a small pale palm at the front of the line and climbs, smooth as a bubble in water, and hangs there at the height of a girl's eyes, perfectly still, perfectly bright, not wavering at all. And it stays.

The demonstrator's head turns from your glim to Kit's. She looks at one, and then the other.

"Ashby," she says. "Ashworth. Good." Then, to the room, briskly: "Next."

Your glim gutters out.

It flickers, and shrinks, and goes, like a match burning down. The thread in your chest goes slack. And the headache arrives, right on time, behind your eyes: but lighter than Tuesday's. Much lighter. A dull warm pressure instead of a hard throb. You can stand up straight with it.

You turn round. At the back of the hall, at the very back, behind everyone, Lin is standing with her notebook clutched to her chest, and she's beaming at you. Her whole face. Not hiding it behind her sleeve or ducking her head. Just beaming, wide open and shining, like you've given her a present.

---

At the end of the hour, in the crush at the door, Kit passes you.

She doesn't stop. She doesn't turn her head. She just goes by, close, her shoulder almost brushing yours, and says, low, so that only you can hear it:

"You did that on purpose."

You shrug.

That stops her. She turns, there in the doorway, with girls pushing past on either side of you, and looks at you. A long moment. Her grey-green eyes on your face, steady, the way they were on the village street yesterday. Something in her face is very open, and very careful, and she doesn't seem to know what to do with it.

"Thank you," she says.

And then you both hear it, at the same moment: the word. *Thank you*. And what you said last time, and what she said the time before, and the forks, and the water glass. You see it hit her. You feel it hit you. Your mouth starts to go, and so does hers, the corner pulling, and you're both standing in the doorway of the Practice Hall in the crush of girls, nearly laughing at the same time, and trying very hard not to.

Kit gets hers under control first. Just barely. She presses her lips together hard and turns and goes, quickly, out into the quad, her bob swinging.

You lean against the doorframe and let yourself grin.

---

On Thursday evening there's a note taped to the kitchen door in neat black capitals: COCOA, 9 P.M. ALL WELCOME. Y.H.

"Cocoa," Bea says, pulling you down the corridor by the hand. "From the warden. Come *on*."

The whole floor has come. The kitchen is packed. There must be fifteen girls in it, more, crammed in on every chair and along the counters and sitting on the floor with their backs to the cupboards, in pyjamas and dressing gowns and big jumpers and thick socks, their hair down or piled up for the night, talking and laughing all at once. The window is steamed up solid. It's warm, and loud, and smells of chocolate.

Bea has been down and fetched Lin up from the floor below. Lin is sitting on the end of the counter, in soft flannel pyjamas with a cardigan over them, her hair loose for once instead of in its ponytail, black and straight and falling down her back. She looks smaller like that, and younger, and she's holding the yellow daisy mug in both hands. When she sees you come in she goes a bit pink and smiles.

And Yusra is at the stove. "Thursday cocoa," she says to the room as you come in, over the noise. "Thornfield custom, top floor, every week. Mugs on the side."

She's off duty. You can see it at once. She's in a loose dark jumper with the sleeves pushed up to her elbows, and soft trousers, and her long wavy black hair is down, falling over her shoulders, and there's no badge at her collar. She has a big battered saucepan on the hob, full of milk, and as you watch she draws a sigil over it with one finger, quick and casual, a shape you don't know, without even looking, the way you'd flick a light switch. The milk begins to steam at once. She stirs in cocoa from a tin, and sugar, a lot of it, and a pinch of something from a jar that makes the whole kitchen smell of cinnamon, and starts ladling it out into a row of mugs.

When she's done, and everyone has a mug, she hops up and sits on the counter by the stove with her own, her long legs dangling, and leans back against the cupboard behind her, and drinks it. She looks entirely different like this. Easy. Unbuttoned. Young.

You're staring again. You make yourself stop.

Bea, beside you, stands up on a chair.

"Excuse me," she announces to the kitchen at large, at the top of her voice. "*Excuse* me. Can I have everyone's attention. My friend Nala, here, who has *never cast a spell in her life*, until this week, who didn't even know what a kettle charm *was* on Monday, made a *glim* today. In practical. In front of *everyone*."

The kitchen turns to look at you. You feel your face go hot.

"Bea — "

"Show them," says Bea. "Go on. Show them."

"I can't just — "

"Yes you can. Show them."

They're all looking at you. Fifteen girls in pyjamas, with mugs. Lin on the counter. Yusra, by the stove, with her cocoa, her dark eyes on you over the rim of the mug, waiting.

You put your mug down.

You hold out your left palm, there in the crowded steamy kitchen. You say the word. You draw the sigil: the stroke, the loop, the hook curling in. You mean light.

It rises.

Small, and white. But steadier than it was this afternoon: it doesn't waver as much. It lifts off your palm and climbs, slowly, up through the warm chocolatey air, to the height of your eyes, and stops there, and holds. A tiny clear star, in the middle of the kitchen, with fifteen girls looking at it and their faces lit faintly from below.

The kitchen cheers.

Actually cheers. Somebody whistles. Bea jumps down off the chair and hugs you from the side, hard. Lin, on the counter, puts her mug down and claps, fast, with her inky hands, and her face is shining. And over by the stove, Yusra lifts her mug to you, across the whole crowded room. She doesn't smile. But she holds the mug up, and holds your eyes, and dips her head, just slightly.

Your glim goes out. You don't even mind.

---

Later, the kitchen empties. Girls drift off down the corridor in ones and twos, yawning, taking their mugs, calling goodnight. Bea's gone to fetch more milk from her room for tomorrow, she says, and Lin's been taken off downstairs by a girl from her floor. You stay to wash up, because someone should, and because Yusra is still there.

She's rinsing the big saucepan at the sink. You bring her your mug.

"A glim in four days," she says, without looking round. Quietly, under the noise of the tap. "From nothing. From never having cast a spell in your life." She turns off the tap. "That's good, Ashby. That's actually good."

You don't know what to say. You stand there holding your mug.

Yusra looks round at you. Her hair is still down. Her dark eyes are steady and very close.

"I said some take a month," she says. "You're not some."

She takes the mug out of your hands. Her fingers close round it over yours for a second, warm from the washing-up water. Then she turns back to the sink, and rinses your mug for you, carefully, inside and out, and sets it upside down on the drainer.

---

Bea follows you into room 14.

She has her cocoa, a fresh mug of it, which she must have made herself after the kitchen emptied, and she doesn't ask, she just comes in after you and sits down on your narrow bed and folds her legs up under her, cross-legged, with the mug in her lap. She's in big flannel pyjamas with clouds on them and a pair of thick bed socks, and her bun has come down entirely, so that her honey-blonde hair is falling loose round her shoulders.

"I'm not tired," she announces. "I want to talk."

So you sit on the bed beside her, with your back against the wall under the window and your knees up, and you talk.

About the mere, again, and her face when she saw you. About Kit and the glims, a bit: only the part Bea saw, which was you stepping out of the line. About Bea's half-glim, which has become two-thirds of a glim, she says, by her own estimate. About Bristol. About the café, the Bluebell, and the man who came in every day for a year and ordered a cheese scone and never once said thank you. About her little sisters and their hair.

And then, out of nowhere, staring into her cocoa, Bea says:

"I've kissed girls and boys both, you know. A few. Not loads." She turns the mug round in her hands. "I like girls best. I always have, really. Boys are fine. Boys are nice. But girls." She blows out a breath. "And I've never been in love. Not once. Twenty-one, and never." She glances at you sideways. "Is that weird? That's weird."

"It's not weird."

"Have you?" Bea says. "Been in love?"

You think about it. About Alan's life, which had loves in it, a long time ago. About the fact that you can't tell her any of that, and that everything you might say would be true and also completely impossible. About who you are now, sitting on this narrow bed in this small body, and the three girls in your head at night, and now more than three. About Shiv on the rock this morning, close and wet and still.

"It's complicated," you say.

Bea looks at you. For a long moment, in the lamplight, she watches your face with her warm brown eyes, and you can see her deciding whether to push.

She doesn't.

"Okay," she says. Softly. And nods, as if that's an answer, and she's put it somewhere safe for later.

She lies back.

She stretches out across your narrow bed beside you, lying on her back with her head on your pillow and her cocoa balanced on her stomach, and goes on talking, up at the ceiling. About the rowing club at school, and the cold mornings on the river. About her mum's Swedish Christmas. About what she wants to be when she's licensed, which changes twice in the telling. She talks slower and slower. Her sentences get longer, with gaps in them. You take the mug off her stomach and put it on the desk before it spills. You lie down too, in the end, because there's nowhere else to be, on your side next to her on the narrow mattress, the two of you squeezed in together under the window.

"And the thing about Gothenburg," Bea says, very slowly, "is that my nan always said, she always said that the herring — "

She stops.

You wait. She doesn't go on. Her breathing has changed, gone deep and slow and even.

She's asleep. Mid-sentence. Her head has rolled sideways onto your shoulder, and it's resting there, warm and heavy, and her loose hair has fallen across your neck and your collarbone, soft and smelling of shampoo and faintly of cocoa.

You lie very still.

The radiator knocks. Clank. A pause. Clank-clank. Bea's warmth is all down your side, from your shoulder to your hip to your knee, the whole long line of her pressed against you through the flannel. Her breath is slow and warm on the side of your neck. One of her hands has come to rest against your arm, the fingers loosely curled.

You could wake her. You could tell her to go across the corridor to her own bed.

You lie in the dark with Bea asleep on your shoulder, her hair across your throat, and let her stay.
