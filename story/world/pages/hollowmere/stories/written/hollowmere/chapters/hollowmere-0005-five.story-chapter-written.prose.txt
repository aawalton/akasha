You wake on Friday in grey light, and Bea is still asleep on your shoulder.

She hasn't moved all night, as far as you can tell. Her head is a warm weight in the hollow under your collarbone, and her honey-blonde hair is everywhere, across your throat and your chin and the pillow, and one of her arms has found its way over your middle in the night and is lying there, heavy and slack. She's breathing slow and deep through her mouth. And at the corner of it, on the shoulder of your nightshirt, there's a small damp patch.

She's drooling on you. A little.

You lie still and look at the ceiling and try not to laugh, and your shoulders shake anyway, and that's what wakes her.

She stirs. She makes a small grumbling noise into your nightshirt. Then her eyes open, an inch from your neck, and you watch her take it in: the wall, the window, the narrow bed, your collarbone, the damp patch. Her head comes up off your shoulder all at once. Her face goes scarlet from her chin to her hairline.

"Oh my god," Bea says. "Oh my *god*." She stares at you. "Did I fall asleep mid-herring?"

You nod.

"Mid-*herring*?"

"Your nan," you say gravely. "Gothenburg. The herring. You never finished."

Bea makes a sound of pure anguish and drops her face straight back down into your pillow, and laughs into it, a muffled helpless laugh that shakes the whole narrow bed. You lie beside her and laugh too. The radiator knocks. Outside the window the light is coming up.

"I've got to run," Bea says at last, into the pillow. "I've *got* to run, or I'll go mad, and I'm late." She scrambles up and off the bed, all elbows and cloud pyjamas, hair wild, and grabs her empty cocoa mug off the desk, and nearly trips over her own bed socks on the way to the door.

At the door she stops. She looks back at you.

You're still lying on the bed where she left you, on your side, your red hair tangled over the pillow. And Bea's face, in the doorway, goes soft. Not embarrassed anymore. Just soft, and warm, and a little unsure, her brown eyes on you for one long second, as if she's seeing something she wants to keep.

Then she goes.

You lie there a while longer. Then you get up and kneel on the bed and look out of the window.

The mere is grey and still under the early sky, with mist lying on it in long low drifts. And out in the middle of it, small and far off, a white dot is moving steadily across the water, trailing a thin dark wake. A white cap. An arm coming over, and over.

You lean your forehead on the cold glass and watch it go, and think about the rock. About the black water closing over you, and her hand never letting go of yours. About her wet shoulders, inches from your face. About her mouth.

---

At nine o'clock, Dr Penhallow's study is exactly as you left it on Tuesday, only more so. The tower of books by the door has grown. The brass instrument on the side table has been moved, and an arm of it now points at the ceiling. The round window looks down on the mere, silver this morning with the mist lifting off it, and the tartan tin of shortbread sits on the desk among the papers.

The six of you squeeze in. Kit in her corner. Amara upright beside the desk, legs crossed. Priya with a notebook already open. Shiv sprawled, hair damp, steaming faintly. Lin on her stool, small and quiet, with ink on her fingers. And you, on the stool you cleared of books on Tuesday, which someone has put the books back on.

Dr Penhallow settles behind her desk and looks at you all over her glasses with great satisfaction.

"Well," she says. "I've had a note from the Practice Hall."

She lifts a slip of paper from the clutter and reads from it. "Glims, group F, first week. Three." She looks up. "Osei. Ashworth. And Ashby."

You feel the room turn, very slightly, at your name. But you're watching something else.

When Penhallow says *Ashworth*, Amara's head turns. Not far. Just enough to look across the cluttered study at Kit, in her corner. A long, considering look, with her shaped brows drawn very slightly together, the same look she gave the two of you in the Long Room yesterday.

Kit doesn't look back. She looks at the round window, and the mere beyond it, with careful and total attention.

"Ashby," says Dr Penhallow.

You jump. "Yes?"

"Four days ago you'd never cast a spell in your life. You told me so, and I gave you a biscuit." She leans back in her chair and steeples her fingers. "So I want to know. What did it feel like?"

You think about it. About the scorched boards, and every eye in the hall on you, and the light coming up off your palm.

"Like a thread," you say slowly. "Running from here" — you touch the middle of your chest, just under your breastbone — "out to the light. A really thin one. And I had to hold on to it. And when I stopped holding on, it went out."

The study is quiet.

Dr Penhallow looks at you over her glasses. She doesn't say anything. She just looks, for a long moment, with her kind sharp blue eyes very steady on your face, as if you've said something she's been waiting a long time to hear and isn't quite sure what to do with. Priya's pen has stopped. Even Shiv has stopped slouching.

Then Penhallow reaches out, prises the lid off the tartan tin, and takes out a piece of shortbread, and holds it across the desk to you.

Your second piece.

You take it. You're the only one in the room who's had one yet. You can feel Amara noticing that, without looking.

"Right," says Dr Penhallow briskly, as if the moment were perfectly ordinary. "Essays."

She sets each of you one. Short, she says, two thousand words, due next Friday, here, at nine. And each a question picked for its writer. She has them written out on slips of card, and she hands them round one at a time, and reads each one out as she does.

"Raman." A card to Priya. "*Can intent be measured?* You asked me on Tuesday. Now you can tell me."

Priya looks at the card as if it's a birthday present. "Oh," she says. "Oh, *yes*."

"Osei. Ashworth." Two cards, at the same time, one to each side of the study. "The same question for both of you. *Is a spell the caster's, or the focus's?*"

Amara's back stiffens. Across the room, Kit's does too, at exactly the same moment, her shoulders going straight as a rule. Neither of them looks at the other. Neither of them looks at their card, either. They both look at Penhallow, with identical expressions of polite and perfect calm.

"I look forward," says Penhallow mildly, "to reading them side by side."

"Zhao." A card to Lin. "*Why does the glim's hook turn inward?*"

Lin goes pink. Not scarlet, like in the Drawing Room. A slow soft pink, starting at her cheekbones. She takes the card in her inky fingers and holds it and looks down at it. And you can't help it: across the piles of books, you grin at her, wide. She glances up and sees you, and goes pinker, and looks down again, but the corner of her small mouth is turning up.

"Doyle." A card to Shiv. "*Is a rule still a rule, if everyone breaks it?*"

Shiv laughs out loud. A real laugh, head back, delighted. "Ah, Doctor," she says. "You've been talking to people."

"I've been looking out of my window at six in the morning," says Dr Penhallow, with perfect composure, and Shiv laughs again, harder, and doesn't deny a thing.

"And Ashby."

A card to you.

You take it. The handwriting on it is small and slanting and very neat, in green ink.

*Where does intent come from?*

You look at it. You write it down at the top of a fresh page of your notebook, in Priya's blue pen, under the date. And as you write it you feel the size of it. It's not a small question. It's not the kind of question you can look up. It sits on the page and goes down and down, like the mere off the edge of Shiv's rock, dark and deep and further than you can see.

"Off you go," says Dr Penhallow. "Friday next. Nine sharp."

---

You're last out. The others clatter off down the spiral stair, and you're stepping through the doorway after them when Penhallow says, behind you:

"Ashby. A moment."

You stop, in the doorway, and turn.

She hasn't got up. She's still sitting behind the desk, leaning back, with her glasses pushed up on her head now and her hands folded on her tweed. Without the glasses her eyes look younger. Bluer.

"You ask questions," she says, "that a girl born to all this would never think to ask. A thread from your chest. You know how many students I've asked that, in thirty years? Most of them say *it felt like casting*. Because they've been doing it since they could walk, and they've never once had to wonder." She looks at you steadily. "That's worth more than a glim. A glim, anyone can learn. That, you can't teach."

You don't know what to say. You stand in the doorway with the card in your hand.

"So write me something honest," says Dr Penhallow. "Not clever. Honest. And bring it in yourself. Don't leave it in my pigeonhole with the others. Bring it up here, and give it to me."

"I will," you say. "I promise."

She nods, once, and puts her glasses back on, and picks up a book, and you're dismissed.

You go down the spiral stair slowly, one hand on the cold stone wall, holding the card.

---

At eleven, in the Drawing Room, the sigils tutor sets the kettle charm.

"You'll have seen it done," she says, drawing it on her easel in sure black ink, "by every mother and aunt and older sister you possess. Doing it is another matter. Ten. Neatly."

It's the one Bea drew over the kettle on your first morning. Half a figure eight with a hook on the end. You've seen it, and tried it, and got a kettle faintly warm under your palm. Now you draw it on cream paper with Lin's soft nib, holding the pen loose the way she showed you, loading just the end with ink.

Your loops are better. You can see it. They come round smooth and even now, the line not wobbling at the top of the curve the way it did on Wednesday. But the hooks are still slow. Every time you get to the hook your hand hesitates, just slightly, and the ink pools a fraction, and the end comes out heavy.

Kit has taken the desk in front of you again. Black jumper, black bob, straight back. She's working steadily, and for most of the hour she doesn't move except for her pen hand.

Then, once, she turns round.

She does it quickly, twisting on her stool, and looks down at your page. Her grey-green eyes go over your ten sigils, one after another, assessing. She looks for perhaps three seconds.

"Heavier on the loop," she says.

The tutor's exact words. In the tutor's exact voice. The brisk clipped bark of it, the faint northern edge, even the little impatient lift at the end. Perfect.

You snort.

You can't stop it. It comes out of your nose, loud, in the hushed room, a sudden undignified snort of laughter, and you clap your hand over your face, too late.

At the front of the room, the tutor's head comes up.

Her eyes sweep the rows. They come to rest on your desk, and on you, red-faced, with your hand over your mouth. And then they move to the desk in front of yours, where Kit Ashworth is sitting facing forward with her back perfectly straight, her pen moving steadily across her paper, her bob immaculate, her whole posture radiating such serene and diligent innocence that it's like looking at a painting of a saint.

She's turned back round before the look even lands. You didn't even see her move.

The tutor looks at you a moment longer, frowning slightly. Then she goes back to her easel.

You bend over your page with your face burning and your shoulders shaking, and draw a hook so badly you have to start the whole line again.

---

In the afternoon you go to the library to study.

The reading room is at the heart of the library tower, two floors below Penhallow's study, and it's the most beautiful room you've seen at Hollowmere. It's high and long and hushed, with a gallery running round it at the level of the second floor, and books on every wall, up to a ceiling painted dark blue and scattered with small gold stars. Long oak tables run down the middle, and on each table there's a row of reading lamps with green glass shades, lit even in the daytime, casting soft pools of light on the wood. The only sounds are pages turning, and the scratch of pens, and someone coughing, very quietly, a long way off.

Priya finds you in the doorway, hugging her tote, and steers you to a table.

"Intent," she whispers, eyes shining. "Where it comes from. Oh, that's a *good* one. That's better than mine. Come on. We'll find you some books."

She knows where everything is already. You don't know how. She leads you up a spiral iron stair to the gallery and along it, scanning the spines with one finger, and pulls out a book, and another, and another, reading the first page of each as she goes, reading faster than you'd have believed anyone could read, flicking through a whole chapter in the time it takes you to read the title. "No," she whispers. "No. *Yes*. This one. And this. Oh, and this one's old but it's lovely, he has a whole bit about breath." She piles three books into your arms, and they're heavy and smell of dust and leather, and she takes you back down and sits you at a table under a green lamp and sits beside you with her own pile, and opens a notebook, and gets out her three pens.

You open the first book. You read the first page twice.

A shadow falls across the table. Someone sets down a pile of books opposite you with a soft thump, and pulls out a chair, and sits.

Amara. She settles in, crosses her long legs, doesn't open a single one of her books, and props her chin on her hand. She looks at you across the green lamp. Her gold hoops catch the light.

"You and Ashworth," she says, low and amused, pitched just under the library hush. "Thick as thieves, suddenly."

You look up. Priya's pen has slowed beside you.

"She's funny," you say.

Amara's brows go up.

"Kit Ashworth," she says, "has never been funny in her life. Not once. I've known of that family since I was eleven. They're about as funny as a tax return."

"She's very funny," you say. "You should sit nearer."

Amara's eyes narrow.

They do it slowly, the bright dark eyes going thoughtful and sharp, fixed on your face across the table as if she's trying to see behind it. For a moment she looks almost cross. You hold her gaze and don't look away.

Then she laughs.

She throws her head back and laughs, delighted, a real laugh, too loud for the reading room, so that heads turn up and down the long tables and someone says *shh*. Amara doesn't care. She leans forward over her books, grinning at you, her braids sliding over her shoulder.

"Oh, I *like* you," she says. "I like you more every day. It's very inconvenient." She taps one long finger on the table between you. "The Bell. Tonight. There's a folk session, every Friday. Fiddles, the lot. You're coming."

"Everyone goes," Priya whispers, from beside you. "Honestly. Everyone. It's lovely."

You look at Amara's grin, and Priya's hopeful face over her glasses.

"Yes," you say. "All right. Yes."

"Good," says Amara, and finally opens a book.

You turn back to yours. You get out your notebook and write *Where does intent come from?* at the top of a fresh page, and under it you write half a page. It's about the thread. About your chest. About the mere, and wanting light the way you wanted air in the black water. You read it back, and most of it sounds like nonsense, and you cross most of it out.

But you leave one line. You look at it a long time, under the green lamp.

---

In the evening, Shiv collects you.

She turns up at Thornfield at eight, in the big navy parka, hair damp and curling, and bangs on your door and then on Bea's. "The Bell," she says. "Come on. Session starts at half eight and the good benches go by nine." She's grinning. Bea comes out of room 15 in a jumper and jeans and lipstick, actual lipstick, pink, and looks at you, and says, "Oh, you look nice," and you look down at yourself, in the fisherman's jumper and your jeans, with your red hair loose for once, and feel your face warm.

On the way down you knock on Lin's door. She opens it already in her coat, with a soft scarf wound twice round her neck, as if she's been waiting.

The shore path in the dark is a different place. The bracken is a black whispering mass on one side. The mere on the other is invisible except where it catches the lights of the village ahead, long wavering streaks of gold on the black water. The wind has dropped. The sky is huge and clear and freezing, more stars in it than you have ever seen in your life, thick as spilled salt from one side of the fells to the other. Your breath smokes. Bea links her arm through yours. Shiv walks ahead with a torch, swinging it, singing something under her breath in Irish. Lin walks on your other side, close, her shoulder occasionally bumping your arm in the dark.

---

You hear the Drowned Bell before you see it. Music spilling out of it into the night, fiddles and stamping feet and voices, and gold light pouring from its tiny windows onto the street.

Inside, it's packed solid.

The ceiling is so low you have to duck at the door, and blackened oak beams run across it with horse brasses nailed to them. A fire is roaring in a huge stone fireplace at one end, so big you could stand up in it, and the heat hits you in the face as you come in. The room is solid with bodies: girls, mostly, Hollowmere girls, half the college by the look of it, crammed shoulder to shoulder on benches and stools and standing along the walls, with pints and ciders and glasses of wine, and some locals too, old men with caps and old women with sherry, everyone talking and laughing over the music. And in the corner by the fire, the musicians: two fiddles, a squeezebox, a bodhrán, and a woman with a tin whistle, playing something fast and bright that makes your feet want to move before you've even got your coat off.

"There," says Bea, pointing. "There! Priya!"

It's Priya, waving wildly from a bench right by the fire, and beside her, long legs crossed, a glass of red wine in her hand, Amara. They've saved a bench. Amara lifts her glass at you, and you squeeze through the crowd towards them.

On the way, you see Yusra.

She's on a stool right by the musicians, close to the fire, off duty: her hair down, a dark jumper, no badge. She has a pint of something dark in one hand, and she's tapping her foot to the fiddles, and her face, lit gold by the fire, is easy and relaxed and smiling in a way you haven't seen before. She sees you. Her eyes find you through the crowd and stay there for a moment.

She lifts her pint to you.

You lift your hand back, and then someone pushes past between you, and you're at the bench.

Shiv comes back from the bar with her arms full of glasses and hands you one. "Cider," she says. "Local. You'll like it."

You do like it. It's cold and sharp and sweet and fizzing, and it goes straight to your head. You'd forgotten that. You'd forgotten you're not Alan anymore, with Alan's size and Alan's years of practice, but a small slim girl who weighs about as much as a bag of shopping. Half the glass and you can feel it: a warm, fizzing glow spreading up behind your eyes and down through your chest and into your cheeks, loosening everything, making the firelight look brighter and the music sound better and Bea's laugh beside you the best sound in the world.

"Oh," you say, to the room in general. "Oh, that's *strong*."

"It's not," says Shiv, grinning. "You're just wee."

The session swells.

The musicians go from one tune straight into the next without stopping, faster each time, and the crowd starts clapping along, and stamping, and someone near the fire gets up and starts to dance, and then someone else. Bea's eyes light up.

"Come on," she says, grabbing your hand. "Come *on*."

"I can't dance."

"I can't either! Come on!"

She drags you up off the bench and onto the bit of flagstone floor in front of the fireplace where a space has opened, and she starts to dance, if it's dancing, a wild skipping whirling thing with her elbows out and her bun coming down, and she holds both your hands and spins you round, and the cider and the fiddles take over and you're spinning too. Your hair flies out. The fire is hot on your face. Girls are whooping all round you. You're laughing so hard you can barely breathe, and Bea is laughing, and the whole low-beamed room is a blur of gold light and faces and stamping feet. Back on the bench, Lin is clapping along, fast, her cheeks flushed red from the fire and the cider, her eyes shining, watching the two of you.

You dance three tunes. Then you have to stop, gasping, and stagger off the floor, and go to the bar for water.

Shiv is there.

She's leaning on the bar with her elbows, waiting to be served, and when you squeeze in beside her she doesn't move up. She leans in instead, close, so close that her mouth is by your ear, because it's so loud in here that it's the only way to be heard.

"About the rock," she says.

Her breath is warm on your ear. You can smell cider on it, and the mere, faintly, in her damp hair.

"I know," you say.

She pulls back a little, just enough to look at you. Her sea-green eyes are on your face, and they're not laughing. Then she leans in again.

"I nearly kissed you," she says, into your ear.

You feel it go all the way through you. Down your neck and your spine, into your belly.

You turn your head, so your mouth is by her ear now, her damp hair against your cheek.

"I nearly let you," you say.

When you pull back she's grinning. Slowly, the same slow crooked grin she gave you on the rock yesterday, spreading across her freckled face.

"Still might," says Shiv.

The barman comes. Shiv orders, and leans on the bar, and the grin stays. And you, standing beside her with the cider fizzing behind your eyes and the fiddles going and your heart going faster than the fiddles, hear yourself say it.

"Shiv. I should tell you something."

She looks round.

"I want more than one girl," you say. It comes out easily. The cider makes it easy. "I always have. Not one and then another. More than one. At once. That's just — that's how I am." You swallow. "I thought you should know. Before. Anything."

Shiv looks at you for a second.

Then she laughs. Not at you: a warm, easy, delighted laugh, as if you've told her the sky is blue.

"Sure," she says. "Who doesn't?"

And she picks up her glass off the bar and touches it to yours, a little clink, and drinks.

You stand there with your glass in your hand, and something in your chest that you didn't know was clenched lets go.

When you turn back to the room with your water, you see Kit.

She's in the far corner, as far from the fire and the music as you can get in the Drowned Bell, at a tiny table under a window, alone. She's in black, of course, with her coat over the back of her chair. There's a book open in front of her, and a glass of lemonade, and she's reading. Or she has the book open, at least, and her eyes on it. You wonder how long she's been there. You wonder why she came, if she was only going to sit in the corner and read.

She looks up. Across the whole crowded room, through the bodies and the firelight, her eyes meet yours.

You lift your cider.

Kit looks at you a moment. Then she lifts her lemonade.

---

At last orders the landlord rings a real bell, a big brass one hanging over the bar, and shouts "*Time*, ladies, *time*," and somebody near you says, "It's quarter to twelve," and somebody else says, "Oh *god*, the gates," and all of a sudden everyone's grabbing coats.

"The gates lock at midnight," Amara tells you, wrapping a scarf round her neck. "Lock. Actually lock. And if you're outside, you're outside."

"A *mile*," Bea wails. "It's a *mile*."

It becomes a run. A stumbling, laughing, staggering run along the dark shore path, a whole crowd of you, scattered out in twos and threes along the shingle with torches bobbing and swinging, and someone falling over in the bracken and someone else hauling her up, and everyone shrieking with laughter. The stars are blazing. The cider is sloshing in your stomach. Your legs, which had run ten minutes on Wednesday morning, run a mile at midnight with Bea's hand in one of yours and Lin's sleeve in the other, and you don't feel it at all.

You reach the gates at two minutes to.

They're tall iron gates in the college wall, with a lamp over them, and they're still open, but only just. A man stands beside them in a long dark coat with a ring of keys in his hand, the night porter, grey-haired and long-faced, watching you all come pelting up the path with an expression of infinite patience.

He lets you through. All of you, one after another, panting and giggling and gasping apologies. And as the last of you goes past him he lets out a sigh: a long, long, deep sigh, the sigh of a man who has done this every Friday night for thirty years, and who will do it every Friday for thirty more.

Then he swings the gates shut behind you with a clang.

---

On the path to Thornfield, Bea goes ahead.

She's singing. Loudly, and very badly, something the fiddles were playing, or her version of it, with her arms out and her coat flapping, weaving a little from side to side on the path. Shiv has gone off towards her own house with a wave. Priya and Amara have gone another way. And Lin is walking beside you, close, in the dark.

Neither of you is saying anything. The quad is silver with frost under the stars. Your breath and hers go up in white clouds side by side.

"I like girls," Lin says.

She says it very quietly. Hardly louder than the crunch of your feet on the frosted path. She doesn't look at you. She's looking straight ahead, at Bea singing, with her scarf pulled up round her chin.

"I've never said it out loud before," she says. "To anyone. Ever."

You stop walking.

You turn and look at her. She's stopped too, a step ahead, and she's looking at you now, in the starlight, her dark eyes very wide and very bright over the edge of her scarf. You can see her breath going quick. You can see what it cost her.

"Me too," you say.

Lin looks at you.

"I know," she says.

And then you're both laughing. You don't know which of you starts. It just bursts out of you both at once, there on the frozen path in the middle of the quad, helpless and silly and enormously relieved, with your hands over your faces and your shoulders shaking and your breath going up in clouds. Up ahead, Bea turns round mid-song to see what's funny, and can't work it out, and laughs too, and goes on singing.

At the foot of Thornfield's stairs, Lin stops. Her floor is the next one up; yours is two.

She reaches out, quickly, and takes your hand. Squeezes it once, hard and fast. Her fingers are cold and her grip is strong.

Then she lets go, and goes up the stairs to her floor without looking back, and you hear her door close.

---

In bed, you lie in the dark, warm with cider.

The radiator knocks. The room is spinning, very slightly, very pleasantly, if you close your eyes, so you keep them open and look up at the dark ceiling and the pale shape of the window over your head. Your feet are throbbing from the dancing. Your cheeks are still hot. Your hand still feels the squeeze of Lin's cold fingers.

You count them.

In the dark, one at a time, you count them.

Bea. Asleep on your shoulder this morning. Soft in the doorway.

Yusra. Lifting her pint to you through the firelight.

Kit. Her lemonade raised, across the whole room.

Shiv. *Still might.*

Lin. *I know.*

Five. Five girls.

And you want every one of them. Each of them, warm and distinct, sitting side by side inside you without crowding each other out. You lie there and wait for it to feel like too much. Like greed, or chaos, or a problem you'll have to solve.

It feels like home.
