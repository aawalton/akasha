The last thing you remember is your back window. A book open on your knee, the evening going blue outside the glass, your eyes getting heavy.

Then hard slats under your back, cold iron at your hip, and the sound of rushing water.

You open your eyes.

Leaves overhead, broad and pale green, moving a little. Beyond them, a sky going from black to grey. Almost dawn.

You are lying on a bench. An iron bench, with wooden slats worn smooth, in a park.

You sit up, and your weight goes wrong, and you grab the armrest with a hand that closes on it too soon. You look at the hand. Small. Pale. Freckled across the back, with a narrow wrist and thin fingers.

A long fall of dark red hair slides forward over your shoulder.

Your shirt is still on you, the dark grey one, but it hangs loose now, down to your thighs, the collar slipping off one shoulder. Your black tights are there too. No shoes. Your bare feet on the gravel path are small and pale, and the gravel is cold.

You say something. It comes out in a woman's voice.

The park is small and neat. Lawns, trimmed. Tall trees with mottled, peeling bark. Gravel paths between them, and more iron benches, all empty. At the bottom of the lawn a river runs fast over a low stone weir, loud and white, and that is the rushing you heard.

Lamps line the paths on iron posts. They are lit, but not with flame. Each one holds a steady white light behind its glass, and on each pane, small and neat, is a mark. A curling, angular shape, like a letter from an alphabet you almost know.

Past the park railings there is a street. A proper street, paved, with a kerb, and cars parked along it, their shapes low and rounded and not quite any make you can name. Buildings rise behind them, six, eight, ten storeys, windows dark. A few are lit. The same steady white.

It looks like a city. It looks like almost any city, early on a weekday.

Almost.

A car goes by on the street, and it makes no engine sound at all, only a soft hum and the hiss of its tyres.

Somewhere behind you, along the gravel path, you hear footsteps. Unhurried. Heavy boots. And a man's voice, raised, not unkind, the voice of someone who says the same thing every morning.

"Right, then. Up you get. Park's not a hotel."

"Oh, yes sir," you say, and get up off the bench.

Standing is worse than sitting. Your balance sits somewhere new, lower and further forward, and the gravel is nearer than it should be, as if the world has been taken down a foot.

The man on the path is in his fifties, broad and weathered, in heavy boots and a long dark green coat. A small brass badge sits on his breast. He carries a long stick with an iron tip, the kind for spearing litter off a lawn.

He stops a few paces off. His eyes go to your bare feet, then the loose shirt, then your face.

Something in his face shifts, and the morning bark goes out of him.

Close to, you come up to about his collarbone. You have to tip your head back to meet his eyes.

"Could you help me get oriented?" you ask. "I'm not sure how I ended up here. Where am I, exactly?"

The words come out easily, in the same tongue he spoke. You didn't reach for them. They were just there when you opened your mouth, the way your own language always is, and only now that they're out do you notice they weren't English.

"This is Weir Gardens," he says. "Low Bank."

You look at him blankly.

He tips his head at the river, white and loud over the weir. "Carrowgate. On the Carrow." He says it slowly, the way you'd give directions to a tourist. "Maddox. I'm the warden. Twenty years I've walked these paths at first light."

Behind him, the park gate stands under an iron arch, and raised iron letters run along the top of it. Angular shapes, curling at the ends. You have never seen them before in your life.

They read WEIR GARDENS to you all the same.

The air is cool on your arms and on your bare feet. Above you the plane trees are in new leaf, small and pale. Over the rooftops the sky is turning from grey to a thin light.

"Someone take your things, miss?" he asks. There's no edge in it. "Shoes, bag, the lot?" He lets that sit a moment. "Or you've just had a bad night of it. I've seen both. Neither's any shame."

"There's a police post at the north end of Weir Street. Five minutes' walk. And if you're hungry, the kitchen on Tanners Row feeds whoever queues at dawn." He hooks a thumb in his coat pocket. "About now, that'd be."

"Just a hard night, I think," you say. "The gardens are lovely—"

His eyes flick to the lawns, the trimmed edges, the raked gravel, and his face eases a little.

"—but I don't think I've been here before. Would you point me the way back to the Academy?"

His brows go up.

"You'll mean the Institute," he says. "Up in Guildhall. Half an hour's walk, all uphill."

Then his eyes go down to your bare feet again. Slower, this time.

"Unless you mean the Imperial one," he says. "That's two days north of here. Past Geldor. Train, and then a bus."

He says the names a little carefully, the way a man says a place he has only ever seen on the news.

He studies your face for longer than is polite. His gaze goes from one of your eyes to the other and back, the way you'd check a child who's come off a swing.

"Did you take a knock on the head, miss?" he asks. His voice is gentle.

"If you truly belong up at the Academy," he says, "the post on Weir Street can sort it. They've a booth there that can reach anywhere."

Past the railings the street is still quiet. The sky over the rooftops is paler now, and the lamps along the paths are still lit.

"So which is it?" he says. "The Institute, or the Academy? And did you hit your head?"

"Sorry, I meant the Institute, of course," you say. "Still waking up, it seems. Point me in the right direction? I'm not afraid of the hills. Give me a chance to clear my head."

His eyes stay on yours a moment. You can see him take note that the question about your head went by without an answer.

Then he lets it go too. He turns and points his stick up the street past the railings.

"Up Weir Street," he says. "Past the police post at the top, then straight over Tanners Row. Past Tanners Row you'll find the Long Stair. Near three hundred steps, old stone. It comes out at the corner of Guildhall Square, and the Institute's on Mercer Street, just off it."

He lowers the stick. "Or there's Hill Road. Winds round the long way. The buses climb it from half past six."

His eyes drop to your feet again, and he shakes his head.

"Not up the Stair like that, you're not," he says. "Hang on."

He walks to a small tool shed by the gate, unlocks it, and rummages inside.

He comes back with a pair of men's canvas shoes, grey and scuffed, plainly too big for you. Over his arm hangs a brown wool coat, long and heavy and worn, with a button missing.

"Lost property," he says. "Folk leave all sorts in the park. These have never been claimed."

He holds them out to you.

"Thank you, that's very kind," you say, and take the shoes and the coat from him. "May I know the name of my garden knight? I'm Nala."

Maddox snorts. His ears go red.

"Knight," he mutters, and looks off at the weir. The corner of his mouth twitches, and he flattens it straight again.

"Orrin Maddox," he says. "Low Bank just calls me Maddox." He looks back at you. "Nala."

You step into the canvas shoes. They're far too long, and when you take a step your heels slide loose inside them, lifting and slapping down again.

The coat is heavier than it looks. It hangs past your knees, and the cuffs swallow your hands whole. But the wool is warm at once, and the cold you'd stopped noticing goes out of your arms.

"Turn those cuffs back," Maddox says. "And mind the shoes on the Stair. The steps are worn smooth." He tips his head at the coat. "Bring them back any morning. I'm here at first light, every day. Kitchen on Tanners Row's right on your way. Porridge'll be on now."

Then he nods once, takes up his stick, and goes on along the path toward the far benches, boots crunching on the gravel.

You're left on the path by your bench, in his coat and his shoes. The gate is a stone's throw off. Past it, Weir Street climbs away from the river, toward the police post at the top, and Tanners Row with its kitchen, and the Stair somewhere beyond. The lamps along it are still lit, all the way up.

You turn back the coat cuffs until your hands come out, and set off through the gate and up Weir Street.

The canvas shoes slap at your heels with every step. The street is brick and cobble, old and uneven, and globes of that same white light stand on iron posts along both kerbs.

At the top of Weir Street stands a small building with one lit window. The sign over its door is in the same angular, curling script as the park gate, and it says POLICE.

You walk on past it and cross Tanners Row. A queue stands along the pavement to a door halfway down, a dozen people in coats with their hands in their pockets, and steam drifts out of the doorway smelling of oats. You keep walking.

Past Tanners Row the street ends at the foot of the hill, and the Long Stair starts up it.

It's worn stone, the steps dished in the middle from long use and slick with dew. An iron rail runs up one side.

You've hiked mountains for fun. A hill doesn't scare you. You settle into it the way you would on a trail: short steps where it's steep, longer where it eases, your breath kept steady, the effort kept level.

Twice a heel slides clean out of a shoe on the wet stone. Each time your stride is short enough that your weight is already over your front foot, and you stay upright.

Your breath comes short sooner than you expect. By the first landing your thighs are burning.

You keep your pace. The body answers. It's lighter than your old one and not as strong, but it's willing, and it keeps climbing.

Millhands and bakers' boys come down the Stair past you, heading to work. Every one of them is going down. One glances at the long brown coat and the too-big shoes, and goes on down without a word.

From the middle landing the whole of Low Bank lies below you: rooftops, the white line of the weir, the grey river running on.

On the upper flights a stone top the colour of honey shows above the rooftops ahead.

The Stair comes out at the corner of a wide square paved in grey granite.

In the middle of it rises a tower of old marble gone the colour of honey, some twenty storeys tall. Two soldiers in uniform stand at its doors. Beside it a coffee cart is open for business.

Sweepers work the square with long brooms. A few clerks cross it with their heads down. Pigeons lift and settle.

Names are carved over the great doors around the square. CIVIC REGISTRY, over one. FREE LIBRARY, over a domed hall of pale stone on the west side.

Off the west corner, a street sign reads MERCER STREET. A hatch in a back wall there breathes out the smell of warm bread.

A minute along stands a building of three storeys in soot-dark brick, with tall workshop windows. A brass plate by its iron gate reads CARROWGATE INSTITUTE OF ARTIFICE. The gate is shut.

A little way along the wall is a plain side door, also closed.

By the gate a board lists courses. Each course has a row of angular marks printed beside its words. You read the words easily. The marks beside them mean nothing to you at all.

One line reads: Basic Set (evening), Mondays and Thursdays, seven o'clock.

Beneath the list: Enrol at the office, with a civic card and the term's fee.

The sun is up now. Mercer Street is empty but for the smell from the bakery, and the gate has no one at it.

You leave the gate alone. Whoever teaches here, or studies here, has to arrive somehow, and most of them will be hungry. You cross Mercer Street to watch the bakery instead.

A shop doorway across the way is set back from the pavement. You stand in it with the long coat pulled round you. From there you can see the hatch and the Institute gate both.

The hatch is kept by a round woman of sixty or so, floury to the elbows, and she talks to every customer who comes to it. Sweepers come by for rolls, and a clerk or two with a satchel. She has a word for each of them, a question about a wife, a joke about the weather, and they go off smiling or rolling their eyes.

Every time the hatch opens, the smell of warm bread comes across the street to you. Your stomach answers it.

The Institute stays shut up. Its tall windows are dark, the iron gate is closed, and the side door stays closed too. Every passer-by goes straight past it.

The sun climbs above the rooftops. The street warms a little.

Not long after, a lean man comes down Mercer Street from the square, walking quickly. His coat is dark and plain, and ink stains the cuffs and one pocket.

He stops at the hatch. The baker already has two rolls wrapped in paper for him before he opens his mouth.

"There you are, Master," she says. "And don't you let these go cold on your desk again."

He answers her shortly, counts coins into her floury hand, and turns toward the Institute's side door, a little way along the wall, a key already in his hand.

You run after him across Mercer Street. The canvas shoes slap at the cobbles, and one nearly flies off your foot; you curl your toes and keep it.

You reach him at the side door just as his key goes into the lock.

"Master, may I have a word?" you say, a little out of breath. "I have knowledge to share with a man of learning. I believe it would help you advance your position at the Institute."

He turns, the rolls in one hand, the key still in the other. His face is tired, and you can see it already closing.

"Advance my position," he says flatly. "I run the place."

His eyes go over you: the long lost-property coat, the too-big shoes, your red hair uncombed and loose down your back.

"If it's a secret sequence you're selling," he says, "or a charm that never fails, I've bought my last."

He starts to turn back to the door, and stops.

You're standing within arm's length of him. He stays there, half turned, and doesn't move.

His eyes narrow. His head tilts a fraction, the way a person's does when they're listening for a sound in the next room.

He studies your face. It goes on far longer than a stranger at a door is usually looked at.

You don't feel anything. You can't tell what he's looking at.

He draws the key back out of the lock.

"One question, then," he says. "What do you know that I don't? Show me. Don't tell me."

"I know deep mysteries of mathematics," you say. "How to chart the course of every function, including those without natural solutions. I know how to work wonders without charms or sequences, relying only on the natural world. I know my appearance speaks against me. That is because I am not from this world, and the world I come from took an independent path of development from yours. You should know better than most the profit that comes from being the bridge between two realms of knowledge. Or have you not discovered graph theory? I don't know precisely what to offer, since I don't know precisely what your world has discovered. But I have never heard of a sequence or a charm, so from your language alone I can tell there is opportunity for exchange."

The master looks at you for a moment. The rolls in his hand have gone cold.

"Not from this world," he says.

He doesn't say he believes it. What he says is that he has been lied to before, by people who looked likelier than you.

Then he turns the key and pushes the side door open, and stands back from it.

"In, then," he says. "There's a thing on my bench that wants a mind."

Inside is a short passage, then a stair, then a workshop. The lamps come up as the door swings open, in ones and twos along the benches, and the air smells of oil and cold metal and chalk dust.

Long benches run down both sides. Racks of tools hang above them, and tall windows line the wall. A locked cabinet stands against the far wall.

No one else is in yet. He lights the lamp over the bench, and it hums faintly.

He sets the two rolls on a shelf beside a cold teapot. The tea can wait.

He leads you to his own bench at the far end, under the tall north window. He lifts a slate onto it and turns the slate to face you.

Down the left runs a count in lines. Down the right, a focus in inches.

Three lines, forty inches. Four, thirty. Five, twenty-four. Six, twenty. Eight, fifteen. Ten, twelve. Twelve, ten.

The middle of the column is inked deep, black and sure. The row at the top and the row at the bottom have been scratched blank.

"A lens has a thickness," he says, "counted in lines. And a focus, counted in inches. The focus is how far the lens throws a lamp's light. You measure it out along a rule."

He says the middle of the table was measured lens by lens, over years, at the stand. The two ends were never measured. A lens that thick, or that thin, can't be ground.

"The blank rows are for two lines and fifteen," he says. "So they have to come off the filled ones."

He calls it the gap. Fourteen claimants have stood where you are standing, over eleven years.

"Every one of them left without it," he says. "Work."

On the bench beside the slate lie chalk, a ruled measure, dividers, and three ground lenses. By the window stands a testing stand, a lamp and a long rule together. A focus is read there by eye.

"Any row you write," he says, "you can check against a lens at hand."

He carries his tea and his rolls to the far end of the bench and sits down. He stays there and watches you work, with his tea going cold beside him.

Above you the building is still. Before long the apprentices will come up, and the workshop will fill.

On the slate, the two outer rows stand blank. The chalk lies on the bench within reach.

You pick up the chalk, and write under the columns while you talk.

"I'm not familiar with lenses and focuses from experience," you say. "But from the mathematics alone, either you are actively trying to trick me, or I have a lot to teach you."

Three and forty. Four and thirty. Five and twenty-four.

Under every row goes the same figure. 120.

"The obvious answer is that the product of the lines and the focus in inches is a fixed value of one hundred and twenty. I can read that off your columns."

Then the blank rows.

"So two lines would be sixty inches. And fifteen lines would be eight inches."

Some of the words you reach for have nothing waiting for them in this tongue. Product. Equation. You find what to call things as you go, and the chalk carries the rest.

"Likewise, you could solve for any case you need with a simple algebraic equation. L times F equals one hundred and twenty. If you know one, you can calculate the other through simple division."

"A lens of either thickness would settle it," you add, "if one could be ground."

Then you draw it. Two lines for the edges, crossing at the corner, and then the curve. It comes down from high on one side, dips toward the corner, and runs out along the bottom, turning away just short of both edges. It comes close to them and never touches them.

"If you were to draw the curve, it would be concave in the first quadrant, with asymptotes at zero for both the X and Y axes."

Curve. Asymptote. The chalk says what the words can't.

The master stands still. He takes the slate and reads it twice.

He looks at the curve longer than he looked at the numbers.

He sets two of the three bench lenses on the stand, one after the other. Both read what the slate and the rule say: twenty-four inches, then twenty.

Then he goes to a rack at the wall and takes down a flat wooden tray of lenses. They were ground for other jobs over the years. Most have no focus written beside them.

He picks one out, turns it in his fingers, and measures its thickness with the dividers.

"Seven and a half lines," he says.

He sets it in the stand and lights the lamp. He looks along the long rule for a while, then straightens up and looks at you.

"Sixteen inches," he says. "Sixteen."

Footsteps come down the stair. The workshop is the one lit room in the building, so the first apprentices come to its door, in grey canvas aprons. They stop, and stare at the stranger at the master's bench, and whisper behind their hands.

The master keeps his eyes on you.

"Teaching, then," he says. "Or work. Or both. As my own pupil, and the office ledger keeps out of it. Or a bench hand, at fifty crowns a day, cash, the way the repairs are paid."

He stands by the slate with his cold tea in his hand.

"Or both. There's a small room over the workshop, if you want a roof of your own."

"I would be grateful for both," you say. "I've long been accustomed to living where I work, and I love both to learn and to teach."

At "teach" the corner of the master's mouth moves. He flattens it straight again.

"Here," he says, "the learning comes first."

He sets out the terms quietly. At the workshop door the apprentices hang about in their grey aprons, and pretend they aren't listening.

"Benches from eight till four. You're paid each evening, in cash. The room comes with the wage. After four, when this lot have gone home, you're my pupil. Here, at this bench. The Basic Set first, the way every child learns it."

"And in return, I want tables. The Institute has others. You'll work them the way you worked this one."

Then he drops his voice.

"Another world," he says. "You keep that to yourself. In these walls and out of them. To the apprentices and to the office, you're a bench hand, and that's all."

He turns to the door.

"New bench hand," he tells the apprentices. "Benches."

The grey aprons scatter to the long benches. Every one of them keeps looking back at you over a shoulder.

He leads you behind the tool racks to a narrow back stair. On the half-landing is a washroom and a privy. Then the stair climbs on, steep and narrow, up under the eaves.

The room at the top is low under the roof, with one small window looking out over the Mercer Street roofs. There's a narrow iron bed with a straw mattress, a washstand, a chair, and a row of pegs on the wall.

It's warm. The heat of the workshop comes up through the floorboards under your feet.

A small globelight hangs by the bed, dark. On its glass is a mark, like the ones on the park lamps.

The washroom tap on the half-landing below was the same: just a bare spout, and a mark set into the wall above it.

"A journeyman had the room," the master says, "before the call-up took him north. His shirts are still in a crate under the bed, if you want them." He looks you up and down. "Far too big."

He tells you the benches start at eight, and goes back down the stair.

Below you, through the boards, the workshop starts up: voices, a wheel beginning to turn, the scrape of stools.
