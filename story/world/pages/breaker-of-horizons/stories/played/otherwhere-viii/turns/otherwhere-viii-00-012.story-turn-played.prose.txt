You go straight after the master, down the narrow back stair and out from behind the tool racks.

The workshop is filling. Apprentices in grey aprons are settling at the long benches, with stools scraping and voices rising. Along the east wall, one grinding wheel is already turning. The rest stand still.

The master takes you to a big, quiet man of about fifty, who walks with a lame leg.

"New bench hand," the master tells him. "Start her at the sorting table."

Then he goes back to his own bench under the north window, and bends over his work.

"Coyle," the big man says, by way of a name. He takes a grey canvas apron and a pair of cotton gloves off a peg by the door.

You hang Maddox's coat on the peg in their place. The apron is made for someone bigger. You tie it over your loose grey shirt, the strings twice round your waist.

The sorting table stands by the door. On it are trays of lens blanks, a heap of small brass parts, a tin of spirit, and a pile of rags.

Coyle shows you once. The calliper goes to each blank, and you read the thickness off it. Then you try the blank against a gauge card, and it goes into the tray the card names for that thickness. The card lists thicknesses in lines and tenths, in plain numerals. You read them at a glance.

"Brass goes in the spirit," he says. "Wipe it with rag. Then it goes back for repair."

Some of the parts come back from repair still warm. Hold one too long and it hums faintly against your glove.

"Any glyph on a repaired part," Coyle says, "you leave alone till I've checked it."

At eight the rest of the wheels start up. A rising whine first, then files and hammers under it, until the room is full of noise.

At first the calliper is clumsy in your small fingers. The jaws land a hair off every time, and you have to set them again.

By the second tray it sits right in your hand. The blanks go where the card says.

Coyle limps by. He tips out one of your trays, checks a dozen blanks with his own calliper, grunts, and moves on.

The morning goes by in blanks and brass. Your mouth is dry. There's tea by the stove, but it waits for noon.

A girl of about seventeen slides onto the stool beside you with a tray of brass to clean. There's ink on her fingertips, and she talks while she works: the call-up, her brother, her exams. She talks about the Chosen One on the news, and how everyone on the news talks round it and still can't say who it is.

Then she tips her head at you.

"I'm Tessa," she says, rag in hand, bright and curious. "Where are you from, then? Not round here, are you?"
