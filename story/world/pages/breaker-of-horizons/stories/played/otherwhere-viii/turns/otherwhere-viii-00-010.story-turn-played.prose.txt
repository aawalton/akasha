You pick up the chalk, and write under the columns while you talk.

"I'm not familiar with lenses and focuses from experience," you say. "But from the mathematics alone, either you are actively trying to trick me, or I have a lot to teach you."

Three and forty. Four and thirty. Five and twenty-four.

Under every row goes the same figure. 120.

"The obvious answer is that the product of the lines and the focus in inches is a fixed value of one hundred and twenty. I can read that off your columns."

Then the blank rows.

"So two lines would be sixty inches. And fifteen lines would be eight inches."

Some of the words you reach for have nothing waiting for them in this tongue. Product. Equation. You find what to call things as you go, and the chalk carries the rest.

"Likewise, you could solve for any case you need with a simple algebraic equation. L times F equals one hundred and twenty. If you know one, you can calculate the other through simple division."

"A lens of either thickness would settle it," you add, "if one could be ground."

Then you draw it. Two lines for the edges, crossing at the corner, and then the curve. It comes down from high on one side, dips toward the corner, and runs out along the bottom, turning away just short of both edges. It comes close to them and never touches them.

"If you were to draw the curve, it would be concave in the first quadrant, with asymptotes at zero for both the X and Y axes."

Curve. Asymptote. The chalk says what the words can't.

The master stands still. He takes the slate and reads it twice.

He looks at the curve longer than he looked at the numbers.

He sets two of the three bench lenses on the stand, one after the other. Both read what the slate and the rule say: twenty-four inches, then twenty.

Then he goes to a rack at the wall and takes down a flat wooden tray of lenses. They were ground for other jobs over the years. Most have no focus written beside them.

He picks one out, turns it in his fingers, and measures its thickness with the dividers.

"Seven and a half lines," he says.

He sets it in the stand and lights the lamp. He looks along the long rule for a while, then straightens up and looks at you.

"Sixteen inches," he says. "Sixteen."

Footsteps come down the stair. The workshop is the one lit room in the building, so the first apprentices come to its door, in grey canvas aprons. They stop, and stare at the stranger at the master's bench, and whisper behind their hands.

The master keeps his eyes on you.

"Teaching, then," he says. "Or work. Or both. As my own pupil, and the office ledger keeps out of it. Or a bench hand, at fifty crowns a day, cash, the way the repairs are paid."

He stands by the slate with his cold tea in his hand.

"Or both. There's a small room over the workshop, if you want a roof of your own."
