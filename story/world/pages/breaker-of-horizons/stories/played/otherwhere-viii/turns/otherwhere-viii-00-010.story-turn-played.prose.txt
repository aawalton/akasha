You pick up the chalk.

"Lenses are new to me," you say. "I have never ground one, or held one up to a lamp. The numbers were enough on their own. So either you set this to trick me, or I have a great deal to teach you."

You write under the columns as you talk. Three and forty. Four and thirty. Five and twenty-four.

Under every row goes the same figure. 120.

"Thickness times focus," you say. "Multiply the two and every row makes that one number. It was sitting in your columns. I only had to read it."

Then the blank rows.

"Two lines, sixty inches. Fifteen lines, eight inches."

There's no word here for the number that comes out of a multiplying, or for a rule written as marks instead of a table. You take the nearest words there are and let them stand.

"Any row from any other. Multiply up to a hundred and twenty, then divide."

You write the rule itself small in the corner, short enough to carry anywhere: a letter for the lines, a letter for the focus, the multiplying between them, and one hundred and twenty.

"A lens of either thickness would settle it," you say, "if a grinder could cut one."

Then you draw it. Two lines for the edges, crossing at the corner, and then the curve. It comes down from high on one side, dips toward the corner, and turns away just short of both edges. It comes close to them and never touches them.

The word for that shape isn't in his tongue either. The chalk says it well enough.

Hallick stands still. He takes the slate and reads it twice.

He looks at the curve longer than he looked at the numbers.

He sets two of the three bench lenses on the stand, one after the other. Both read what the slate and the rule say: twenty-four inches, then twenty.

Then he goes to a rack at the wall and takes down a flat wooden tray.

The tray is full of lenses ground for other jobs over the years. Most have no focus written beside them.

He picks one out, turns it in his fingers, and measures its thickness with the dividers.

"Seven and a half lines," he says.

He sets it in the stand and lights the lamp. He looks along the long rule for a while, then straightens up and looks at you.

"Sixteen inches," he says. "Sixteen."

The building is filling around you. Footsteps cross the floor above and come down the stair, and the workshop is the one lit room, so the first apprentices come to its door in their grey canvas aprons. They stop, and stare at the stranger at the master's bench, and whisper behind their hands.

Hallick keeps his eyes on you.

He stands by the slate with his cold tea in his hand.

"Teaching, then," he says, "or work. Or both. As my own pupil — the office ledger keeps out of it. Or a bench hand, three crowns a day, cash, the way the repairs are paid. Or both. There's a small room over the workshop, if you want a roof of your own."
