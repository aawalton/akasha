You leave the gate alone. Whoever teaches here, or studies here, has to arrive somehow, and most of them will be hungry. You cross Mercer Street to watch the bakery instead.

A shop doorway across the way is set back from the pavement. You stand in it with the long coat pulled round you. From there you can see the hatch and the Institute gate both.

The hatch is kept by a round woman of sixty or so, floury to the elbows, and she talks to every customer who comes to it. Sweepers come by for rolls, and a clerk or two with a satchel. She has a word for each of them, a question about a wife, a joke about the weather, and they go off smiling or rolling their eyes.

Every time the hatch opens, the smell of warm bread comes across the street to you. Your stomach answers it.

The Institute stays shut up. Its tall windows are dark, the iron gate is closed, and the side door stays closed too. Every passer-by goes straight past it.

The sun climbs above the rooftops. The street warms a little.

Not long after, a lean man comes down Mercer Street from the square, walking quickly. His coat is dark and plain, and ink stains the cuffs and one pocket.

He stops at the hatch. The baker already has two rolls wrapped in paper for him before he opens his mouth.

"There you are, Master," she says. "And don't you let these go cold on your desk again."

He answers her shortly, counts coins into her floury hand, and turns toward the Institute's side door.

A key is already in his hand. The side door is twenty paces from the hatch.
