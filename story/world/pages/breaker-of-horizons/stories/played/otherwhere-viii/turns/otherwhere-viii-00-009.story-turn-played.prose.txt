"I know deep mysteries of mathematics," you say. "How to chart the course of every function, including those that have no natural solutions. I know how to work wonders without charms or sequences, relying only on the natural world. I know my appearance speaks against me. That is because I am not from this world, and the world I come from took an independent path of development from yours. You should know better than most the profit that comes from being the bridge between two realms of knowledge. Or have you not discovered graph theory? I don't know precisely what to offer, since I don't know precisely what your world has discovered. But I have never heard of a sequence or a charm, so from your language alone I can tell there is opportunity for exchange."

Hallick says nothing for a moment. The rolls in his hand have gone cold, and he takes no notice of it.

"Not from this world," he says.

He doesn't say he believes it. What he says is that he has been lied to before, by people who looked likelier than you.

Then he turns the key and pushes the side door open, and stands back from it.

"In, then," he says. "There's a thing on my bench that wants a mind."

Inside is a short passage, then a stair, then a workshop that smells of oil and cold metal and chalk dust. Long benches run down both sides. Racks of tools hang above them, and tall windows line the wall. A locked cabinet stands against the far wall.

The workshop is empty. The lamps are already lit, and the one over the door hums faintly.

He sets the two rolls on a shelf beside a cold teapot. The tea can wait.

He leads you to his own bench at the far end, under the tall north window. He lifts a slate onto it and turns the slate to face you.

Down the left runs a count in lines. Down the right, a focus in inches.

Three lines, forty inches. Four, thirty. Five, twenty-four. Six, twenty. Eight, fifteen. Ten, twelve. Twelve, ten.

The middle of the column is inked deep, black and sure. The row at the top and the row at the bottom have been scratched blank.

"A lens has a thickness," he says, "counted in lines. And a focus, counted in inches. The focus is how far the lens throws a lamp's light. You measure it out along a rule."

He says the middle of the table was measured lens by lens, over years, at the stand. The two ends were never measured. A lens that thick, or that thin, can't be ground.

"The blank rows are for two lines and fifteen," he says. "So they have to come off the filled ones."

He calls it the gap. Fourteen claimants have stood where you are standing, over eleven years.

"Every one of them left without it," he says. "Work."

On the bench beside the slate lie chalk, a ruled measure, dividers, and three ground lenses. By the window stands a testing stand, a lamp and a long rule together. A focus is read there by eye.

"Any row you write," he says, "you can check against a lens at hand."

He carries his tea and his rolls to the far end of the bench and sits down. He doesn't go up to his office. He stays where he is, and watches, and says nothing more.

Above you the building is still. Before long the apprentices will come up, and the workshop will fill.

The gap is two blank rows. The chalk is in your hand.
