# A Librarian for the Library

Saturday afternoon finds you at the table by the back window, a book open flat under one hand and a mug of cocoa going cold beside it. Past the glass the valley runs west to Utah Lake, hazy with late-summer heat.

The house shakes like a wet dog.

There's no better way to put it: one hard shudder from floor to rafters, and then stillness. The cocoa trembles in its mug and settles. Out front, a man walking a spaniel keeps walking. The sprinkler next door keeps ticking round.

You turn the page. The house shakes again, and this time it doesn't stop.

The table buzzes under your palm until your teeth ache. The window crackles like cellophane. Outside, the man and his dog stutter in place, a frame forward and a frame back, black and white, gone, then there again, like a picture on an old television with the antenna knocked loose. The mug winks out and back. The book goes thin as smoke under your hand, and your hand stays solid on it, pale and ordinary and far too real. A hum rises out of everything at once, a swarm of hornets packed into the walls, and it climbs into your jaw and behind your eyes.

Shadow comes in through the walls. It reaches across the kitchen floor in long fingers, through the chairs, through the counter, toward you and you alone. You stand, the floor heaves, and you go down on one knee while the room dissolves into static.

Then it's dark, and quiet.

The quiet is total. You're kneeling on something soft and faintly springy, like the rubber matting under a playground swing. The air is still and cool and smells of old wood, a clean resin smell, the smell of a room that has never had a window opened in it.

Light comes up slowly, a dim blue-green glow with no source. It shows a floor that runs away in every direction and walls of dark wood far off, the grain all sweeping one way, curving round and up into a ceiling lost overhead. The room is round and enormous. It's like standing inside the trunk of a redwood, if a redwood were the size of a stadium.

Your kitchen is gone. Your house is gone. There isn't a window anywhere.

You get up, and the getting up is wrong.

Your shirt slides off one shoulder. The loose grey shirt you pulled on this morning hangs to the middle of your thighs like a nightdress, and the collar gapes. Your shorts have slid to your hips and are still going, and you catch them with a hand that isn't yours: small, narrow, pale, with short clean nails. Hair falls across your face, long and heavy and dark red, and when you push it back it keeps coming, down past your shoulder blades. The floor is closer than it should be. Your weight sits lower, differently balanced, and there's a new weight on your chest under the shirt that moves when you move.

Your light blue slip-ons are still on your feet, gaping at the heels.

"Hello?"

It comes out in a woman's voice, clear and a little hoarse, and it's yours. The word comes back off the far walls again and again, like a shout in a canyon. Your footsteps make no sound at all; the floor swallows them. When you take a step, the floor moves with you, carrying you further than the step should, and stops when you stop.

The echo dies away. The room stays huge and dim and empty.

Then there are words in the air in front of your eyes, on a flat panel of pale blue light that moves when you turn your head. A voice speaks them as they appear, and the voice is inside your skull and all around the room at once.

Projected energy expenditure exceeded.

Stand by for emergency protocol.

Emergency Power Mode Override Activated

The panel vanishes. The blue-green glow drains to red. Somewhere overhead an alarm starts up, a low double honk like geese going over, again and again. The floor heaves once more, hard enough to rock you on your feet, and settles.

In the red light you can see what the glow hid. At the center of the room rises a trunk, grey and smooth with age, more like stone or metal than wood, wider than a house, climbing until the dark takes it. Fine lines of blue run under its surface like veins, flickering in and out the way your street did. The hum comes from it. You feel it through the soft floor and up your spine, low and steady, and under the blare of the alarm it's the only warm thing in the room.

You let the shorts go. They slide down your legs on their own and puddle on the soft floor around your ankles, a black ring of fabric that makes no sound as it lands.

The compression tights underneath were never meant for these legs, and they hug them anyway, snug from waist to ankle, as tight on this narrower frame as on the old one. They stay put. They're easier than the shorts ever were.

You step out of the light blue slip-ons, one and then the other, and stand in your socks. The heels of the socks sag down past your own heels. The floor is cool through the cotton and gives a little under your weight, springing back as you shift.

And somehow, standing there in the red light with your hair down your back and the grey shirt hanging to your thighs, you're at ease. More at home in this body than you ever were in the old one. You couldn't say why. It just fits, the way the clothes don't.

"Okay," you say aloud, "I guess we're implementing isekai protocol. Status? Character Sheet? System?"

The chamber throws it back at you. "System... system... system..." Each one fainter, rolling away round the curve of the far walls, until the last of them is gone.

The air in front of your eyes stays empty. The pale blue panel stays gone, and the voice that spoke inside your skull keeps its silence.

You try to picture yourself, to call up the shape of this new body and see whether the box answers to it. Behind your eyes it's dark, the same dark it has always been, with your total aphantasia. There's no image to hold up.

The air before you stays as bare as it was.

Overhead the alarm keeps up its double honk, again and again, as steady as if the room never heard you at all.

Then, on the side of the grey trunk facing you, the blue veins stop flickering.

They steady first, then brighten, then hold. The lit lines run down the trunk's face toward the floor on your side and gather there, at about the height of your hand, into a knot of light under the smooth grey surface. They pulse, slow and even, in time with the hum. All round the rest of the trunk the veins still stutter in and out, but that one patch burns steady.

The hum deepens. You feel it come up through your socks, into your heels and up your shins, and the trunk's warmth reaches you across the floor like sun off a stone wall.

You walk toward it, toward the warmth, in your sagging socks. The floor takes each step and carries it too far, sliding you on further than your legs meant to go, and stops dead each time your foot comes down.

The trunk stays where it is. It ought to swell with every one of those long, carried strides, and it doesn't. The grey wall hangs out there ahead of you, the knot of light a small steady point in it, no bigger than when you set out.

You keep walking. The alarm honks on overhead, the same low double note, over and over. The red light stays exactly as red. Your feet make no sound on the soft floor. There's no clock here, no shadow to creep, no window going dark, only the honk and the hum and the next step, and then the next.

Then, all at once, it's right in front of you.

A grey wall rises before your face and curves away on both sides, so wide that it looks almost flat, climbing up into the dark until your neck aches to follow it. You stop, and the floor stops with you.

Your mouth is dry. Your tongue sticks to the roof of it. Your calves are heavy and tight, the ache of a long hike rather than a walk across a room, however big the room.

The knot of light glows just under the grey surface, at the height of your hand. Up close you can see the fine blue veins running down into it and bunching there, pulsing slow and even with the hum.

You put your hand on the trunk, over the light.

It's smooth, smoother than any bark, and hard as stone under your palm. And it's warm, warm like skin that has been lying in the sun.

The hum comes up into your palm and on into your arm, steady and deep, so strong you feel it in the bones of your wrist. Under your hand the light gathers. It draws in beneath your palm and brightens, glowing up between your small pale fingers and around their edges.

The warmth spreads up past your wrist. The hum settles, slower now, and deeper, a low note you feel in your elbow.

The air before your eyes stays clear of any panel. The voice keeps its silence.

A shoulder's width to the side of your hand, a second knot of light kindles under the grey surface.

It pulses at the same height, slow and even, in time with the one beneath your palm.

You lay your other hand on the trunk, over the second light.

Both knots flare under your palms at once, white-blue and blinding.

Then the pain comes. It burns up through your hands like hot wire drawn along your veins, into your wrists, your arms, across your chest, and up behind your eyes. Your hands stay flat on the trunk. You can't tell anymore whether you're holding on or being held.

And the chamber is gone.

Threads of blue light run out ahead of you, away from you, through a dark too big to count, and there's no end to them in any direction. They meet and cross and knot together, and where they knot there are worlds, worlds you've never seen, bright beads strung out across the black. You don't picture them. You've never pictured anything. But they're there, they're in you somehow, as sure as the pain.

Many of the threads are thin, barely there, faint as a hair in bad light. Some have gone dark entirely, cut lines trailing off into the black, and the beads at their ends are dim.

And at the center of all of it you feel something. Vast. Old. Tired, so very tired, the way a person is tired at the end of a year that should have ended long ago.

The pain ebbs, and the visions go with it, together, like a tide pulling out. The red comes back first, then the honk of the alarm, then the soft floor under your knees.

You're kneeling at the foot of the trunk. Your hands have slid down its face and rest on the grey just above the floor. Your arms are trembling. Your head swims, and you're wrung out, emptied, as if something has drunk you dry.

Your palms, where they rest against the grey, are pale and whole, with no burn on them, and nothing in you is broken. There's only the ache, draining out of your veins the way a cramp lets go.

Above you the trunk's veins have stopped flickering. They burn a steady blue, every line of them, all the way up into the dark.

The alarm still honks overhead. The red light hasn't changed.

A panel of pale blue light opens before your eyes, and the voice speaks it inside your skull.

Librarian Link: Tentative

Power Drain: Halted

Status: Emergency Power Mode

Operational: 12%

The panel stays. It hangs in the air before your eyes, and when you turn your head, it turns with you.

Still on your knees in front of the trunk, you say it out loud, your voice hoarse and small in all that space: "Okay, I'm in a magic library of some sort? Library, can you hear me?"

The chamber throws it back at you. "Hear me... hear me... hear me..." Round the far walls and up into the dark, fainter each time.

High up on the trunk, far above your head, two eyes open in the grey.

They're a bright, burning blue, the same blue as the veins, and they're far too big, each one the size of a window. They look down at you.

Then something comes pouring down the face of the trunk toward you, dark and quick and liquid, like water spilling down a rock face. It pools on the floor in front of your knees, and gathers, and stands up.

It's a lynx. A big one, bigger than any cat has a right to be, its shoulders level with your thigh if you were standing. Its fur glows a deep purple, so deep it's nearly black, lit from somewhere inside. Its stripes are black, and they aren't stripes at all. They're writing. Runes, lines of script, crawling and twisting over its flanks and down its legs even as you look at them, rearranging themselves like ants.

Its eyes are the eyes from the trunk, the same bright blue as the veins, and they're much too large for its head, like the eyes of a cartoon cat. There's nothing cute in them. They move over you, from your tangled red hair down to your sagging socks and back up again, slowly, the way someone looks at a delivery that arrived late and wrong.

"Of course I can hear you," it says, out loud, in a sharp, impatient voice. "I am the Library. Links."

Its stripes twist. "Like the cat. Spelled L-I-N-K-S."

It sits back on its haunches in front of you, and the tufts on its ears twitch.

"And not 'of some sort.' The Library. This is the basement." Those huge eyes narrow at you. "Didn't you read the packet?"

"Uh," you say. "What packet? I was sitting in my house, and then I was here..."

Links stares at you. His ear tufts go flat against his skull.

Then his eyes flicker. Blue light runs through them in fine quick lines, one after another, like text scrolling past on a screen much too fast to read.

The flicker stops. He lets out a long breath through his nose.

"It didn't load," he says flatly. "The information packet. You got none of it. Not a word."

The runes on his flanks writhe, knotting and unknotting. "All this waiting," he says, "and the packet fails."

For a moment, under the irritation, he looks very tired. It's there in the set of his shoulders and the way his big head dips, and then it's gone again.

"You're here because your magical signature is compatible with my core," he says. "The first compatible signature I've found in four hundred sixty-eight years. So I pulled you."

"And you know nothing. Not one thing."

He shuts his eyes, and the runes on him slow.

"Well," you say, "I know some things. Just not whatever was supposed to be in that packet."

"For example, I'm Nala." The name comes out in that clear, hoarse voice as if it had been waiting there. "Nice to meet you, Links."

Links opens his eyes. His ear tufts lift, just a little, and for the first time he looks at you properly: not the once-over from before, head to socks, but at your face.

"Nala," he repeats, as if he's filing it away somewhere.

You look around. The chamber runs off on every side, round and vast and red, its dark wooden walls sweeping up into shadow, the soft floor stretching away empty. There's the trunk, and the alarm honking overhead, and Links. There isn't a shelf anywhere.

"If this is a library," you say, "where are all the books? I'm a quick learner. And I've always wanted to learn absolutely everything."

His runes stir. They quicken, crawling faster over his flanks, and for a moment something shows in those huge blue eyes that looks a lot like interest.

"Everything," he says. "Well. You came to the right place." A beat. "I'm an open book."

He laughs at his own joke, a short, dry huff through his nose, and his eyes slide to you to see whether you got it.

"The books are upstairs," he says. "What's left of them."

He turns and pads off across the floor. The floor carries him, far faster than his paws are moving, a purple-black shape shrinking across the red toward the far wall.

Where the wall meets the floor, there's a dark gap in the wood you hadn't noticed before. At the bottom of it you can make out the foot of a spiral staircase. The steps wind up and round into the dark and out of sight, with no landing anywhere that you can see.

Links sets a paw on the bottom step.

You go after him. The floor takes your steps and flings them long, carrying you across the red in a handful of strides, and it sets you down at the dark gap just as the tip of his stubby tail vanishes up and round the first turn of the stair.

You follow him up.

The stairs wind round and round in the dark, tight and steep, one turn after another with no landing anywhere, no place to stop and stand. Links flows up ahead of you, more poured than climbing, and his purple glow is the only light on the steps. You keep your eyes on it and on the next step coming up out of the black.

By the second turn of the spiral your calves are burning. They were already aching from that long walk to the trunk, and now every step lights them up again. Your breath comes short. The steps go on.

You climb on. Two stories, maybe three, round and round, until at last there's a pale opening above you, and the stairs end.

You step out, breathing hard, into gold.

It's a hall, vast and dim, lit a soft old gold from somewhere you can't find. Massive wooden columns march away from you in rows, each one as thick as an old-growth redwood, their lower reaches carved deep with shapes you can't make out in this light. Between them bookshelves climb all the way to the ceiling, and above that a second gallery of shelves runs all the way round the hall, rail after carved rail.

And it's wrecked.

It has been left to rot for a very long time. Books lie everywhere, hundreds of them, thousands, their spines cracked and splayed open, lying face-down among smashed desks and broken chairs and tables knocked over on their sides. Loose pages drift across the floor, turning over and sliding on in a draught you can't feel on your skin.

The air is stale. It smells of dust and old paper, and of something sad, and under that, very faintly, of something like hope.

At the front of the hall, up on a raised platform, stands a huge desk, five feet tall at least. Its wood is carved all over with trees, and the trees are blossoming into books, their branches strung with words the way branches are strung with leaves.

Behind the desk, a long path runs back between the columns, back and back into a gloom the gold light never reaches.

Links sits down at the top of the stairs beside you and looks out over the ruin.

"Welcome to the Magical Library of Everywhere," he says quietly.

"It's..." You look round the hall, at the columns and the high carved galleries and the gold light lying over all that wreckage, and your eyes go wide. "It's beautiful."

Links turns his head and looks at you. Just looks, for a long moment, the way someone looks when the answer they got is not the one they'd braced for.

You settle yourself. "It sounded like there's work to be done. And some of those error messages downstairs were quite alarming. Where do we start?"

His ear tufts come up. The runes on his flanks quicken, and when he speaks, for once his voice has lost some of its edge.

"Bookworms," he says. "At the back of the hall. They're meant to be here. They eat the magic residue off the books and clean up the book dust. But they've gone untended for centuries. They've grown engorged. Huge. Now they eat the books themselves, and every book they eat drains more of my power."

His eyes flicker blue, fine lines of text scrolling through them too fast to read, and then they stop.

"Six of them," he says. "Clear them out of the hall. All six. That's your first task, Librarian."

He gets up and pads over to the big carved desk. Beside him, in the empty air, something takes shape: a hand, flat and broad and made of solid purple light, like his own glow pressed into a shape. It reaches behind the desk, and when it comes back it's carrying an old broom, its bristles worn down short and splayed.

The hand sets the broom down on the floor in front of you. Then it fades, and it's gone.

"Your first weapon," Links says, entirely serious.

From the dark back of the hall, somewhere past where the gold light reaches, comes a sound: wet, and slow, and chewing.

"Okay," you say. "Is this 'minutes matter' urgency, or can it wait a few hours if needed? And is there a book on bookworms I could read to prepare? I'd like to know what I'm getting into. Are they dangerous like this?"

Links considers you, his big head tilted to one side, as though a Librarian asking questions first is something new to him.

"Not minutes," he says. "Every day they're left costs me power. A few hours won't sink us."

"Dangerous? They bite. Engorged like that, their teeth are nothing to laugh at." A pause. "And one of them has grown bigger than the rest."

His eyes flicker blue, text scrolling through them. "There's a book. Should be right about..." He pads down off the platform, and into the mess of books scattered round the foot of the desk. Under a broken chair he stops, and noses out a thin volume toward you.

The cover is plain, the lettering worn but readable: Bookworm Care for Library Assistants.

You sit down on the edge of the platform with the guide in your lap, and you read.

Links settles nearby, curled on the floor with his chin on his paws. At the back of the hall the chewing goes on, slow and patient and wet.

It takes you about an hour. Somewhere in there, sitting still, the ache goes out of your legs.

A normal bookworm, you learn, is a cute little thing: a grey worm a few inches long, ringed with color at each end. They eat magic residue and book dust. Each one has an affinity of its own. They're needed to make magical quills, and their droppings feed the soil that magical herbs grow in.

They have no eyes. Their mouths are round, rimmed with rows of small, jagged teeth, and they bite.

And salt dries a bookworm out. It leaves one helpless, curled up and still, but it doesn't kill it.

You close the book. You know their habits now, and their bite, and what salt does to them.

"Salt's in the break room," Links says, before you've said a word. "Off the hall. Big box. It keeps."

You go and find the break room. Its door opens off the left side of the hall, partway back along the columns, a plain wooden door among all that carving.

Inside, a cooler stands dark in one corner, tall and silver-edged and plainly meant to be more than a fridge, with runes etched round its frame. Its door hangs open on an empty, lightless inside. Glass terrariums line the walls, one after another, and every little garden in them has run wild, pressed green and tangled against the glass.

You try the cupboards. In the third one you find the salt: a big box, plain and square and heavy, twenty pounds or near it.

You hug it to your chest in both arms and carry it out. The weight drags at your shoulders and your narrow arms, but you can carry it, and you do.

You carry it back along the columns toward the rear of the hall. With every column you pass, the chewing grows louder: wet, slow, patient, and more than one mouth at it.

Where the gold light thins out into gloom, you set the box down on the floor.

From here you can make out shapes in the dark ahead. Low, humped shapes among heaps of books, and they're moving, a slow roll and shift like something breathing. The nearest one is about the size of a small dog. The others are harder to make out, only bulk and movement further back in the gloom.

Every one of them stays bent to the books. The chewing goes on.

Links pads up beside the box. He eyes it, then eyes you, and keeps his thoughts to himself.

You go back to the break room to hunt for something to scoop and throw with. The cupboards turn up a row of chipped mugs, a small bucket, and a dented tin scoop.

You take the scoop. It fits your hand, and it's deep enough for a good fistful of salt.

You go back to the box at the edge of the light, dig the tin scoop in, and bring it up heaping full, salt spilling off the top in a thin white trickle.

Then you walk toward the nearest one, out of the gold and into the gloom, the scoop held low and ready at your side. Your socks make no sound on the floor. The chewing is loud now, wet and close.

About twenty feet out, it stops.

The thing rears up off the books. It's as long as a small dog and fat as one too, grey and soft and swollen, with rings of faded color round each end. There are no eyes anywhere on it. Its front end is just a mouth, round and open, rimmed with rows and rows of small jagged teeth.

It roars at you. It's a wet, squelching bellow, much too loud for something that size, and it rolls away through the dark between the columns.

You keep coming. Fifteen feet. Ten. Close enough.

You fling the salt.

Most of it goes wide. It fans out across the dark and pours down over the heaped books in a hiss of white. But a spray of it catches the thing along its flank.

Where the salt hits, the grey skin puckers. It shrinks and draws in on itself, like a slug's, and the bookworm thrashes on the books and shrieks, high and shrill.

It's hurt. One side of it is shrunk and wrinkled now. But it's a long way from dried out.

The shriek carries. All through the dark at the back of the hall, the chewing stops.

Other humped shapes lift up from the heaps of books, one and then another and another, blind heads turning toward the sound.

You back off fast, the empty scoop in your fist, all the way back to the box and into the gold light.

The salted bookworm comes after you. It drags itself slowly across the scattered books, one side puckered, and it doesn't stop. It's fifteen feet away and closing, its mouth open, the teeth wet.

Beside the box, Links is on his feet, fur bristling, the runes racing over his body.

You dig the scoop into the box and bring it up heaping full again.

This time you hold still. You stand your ground at the box, in the gold light, the scoop ready, and let it come to you.

It drags itself closer through the scattered books, one side still puckered and grey. Twelve feet. Ten. Eight. Its round mouth works as it comes, the rows of teeth opening and closing on nothing, wet.

At about five feet, you fling the salt straight at it.

The throw is hurried. Half the salt sprays past it in a white fan and pours away across the books behind. But the rest hits it full in the mouth.

It shrieks. Its whole front end puckers and shrinks, drawing in on itself, and the wrinkling creeps back along its body, further than before.

And in the same instant it lunges.

It's fast, far faster than that slow drag, a few feet in a single heave, teeth first. You jerk back. Its teeth snap shut on empty air a hand's width from your shin, with a wet clack you feel as much as hear.

As you jerk back, the tin scoop flies out of your hand.

It hits the floor ringing, skitters away past the bookworm, and spins to a stop out in the gloom behind it.

The bookworm drops back onto the floor. It's shrunken now along half its length, grey skin creased and drawn tight, but it's still moving. It lies between you and the scoop, four or five feet from your feet, its blind head weaving from side to side.

Further back in the dark, the other shapes stay on their heaps, heads raised, listening.

You go quietly. You circle out wide around the bookworm, toward the gloom where the scoop lies, and your socks make no sound at all on the floor.

It turns anyway. Its blind head swings round as you go, tracking you, steady and exact, as if it can feel precisely where you are without needing to hear a thing.

As you pass it, it lunges sideways, fast. But you're far enough out. Its teeth close on nothing.

You reach the scoop in the gloom and snatch it up off the floor.

On the next heap back, a second small shape lifts its head. It holds very still, listening, its round mouth toward you. It stays on its heap.

You circle wide again, back toward the box and the light, and the salted bookworm drags itself round after you, turning to keep its face to you the whole way.

At the box you fill the scoop. You set your feet. This time you take your time over it, and you aim.

At the last moment the bookworm jerks its head aside. The salt pours down onto the floor beside it in a white heap.

You're still following through when it lunges.

Its teeth rake your calf. It's a glancing bite, a quick tearing drag, and the black tights rip open under it.

It stings, sharp and hot. A line of blood beads up through the torn fabric.

You stumble back a step and stay on your feet. The bite isn't deep.

The bookworm is right at your feet now, beside the spilled salt, its mouth open and working.

You drop down and grab it, both hands, just behind its head, where the salt has already dried its skin rough.

It's easy to hold. The skin there is like coarse sandpaper under your palms, nothing slick about it at all, and you shove it over sideways, hard, onto the white heap of spilled salt.

It lands in the salt, and you lean your weight on it and pin it there.

It shrieks. It thrashes under your hands, and the salt hisses against its skin like water on a hot pan. The puckering spreads fast now, racing back along it.

Its whole body is shrinking, the wrinkled grey drawing in and in along its length.

It twists its mouth end round against your grip, and its teeth catch your forearm.

The nip is shallow, a stinging scrape along the skin, but it makes you hiss through your teeth.

You keep your weight on it. You keep it pressed down in the salt.

Its thrashing is weaker now, slower, each heave smaller than the last. But it isn't still yet.

Under your palms its skin crackles faintly as the salt goes on drying it.

You keep your weight on it and hold it down in the salt.

Its heaves come smaller and further apart. The salt crackles against its skin, a dry, papery sound, like leaves underfoot.

Then it all goes at once. The whole body draws in under your hands, shrinks, and curls itself up tight.

It goes still. What's left is a hard grey coil, dried right through, no bigger than a rolled-up newspaper.

Its mouth is shut. Through your palms you can feel that it's still alive, a faint something in it, but it's helpless now.

You let go and sit back on your heels, breathing hard. The coil stays where it is in the salt.

Overhead, the gold light in the hall brightens, only a shade, as if something somewhere has eased.

Links pads over and sniffs at the dried coil in the salt, his whiskers twitching.

"One," he says. The runes slide slowly over his flanks. "Dried, not dead."

Still sitting back on your heels, you say, "Okay. That was harder than expected. Is there an easy way here that I'm missing? Or were you really expecting me to take on a room of these with a broom?"

Links's ears go back flat.

"The broom was never for hitting them," he says, stung. "You sweep with it. You push the salt onto them with the bristles, from past where they can lunge. And they won't cross salt. Lay an unbroken line of it, and a bookworm stays on its own side. Ring one in, and it's penned. Then you can take your time."

His eyes go to the scrape on your forearm, then down to the torn tights at your calf and the line of dried blood there. The runes on his flanks slow.

"I could claw them myself," he says, more quietly. "Every swipe costs power I haven't got."

"Fine," you say, getting to your feet. "In the future, please give me the information I need, and not just what I ask for. Please."

Links goes still. His ear tufts twitch, and for a moment he doesn't answer.

"All right," he says at last, low. "That's fair."

You walk back up the hall between the columns to the platform and take up the old broom, its bristles worn short. Then you carry it back toward the salt box, meaning to tip the whole box out at the entrance and sweep it outward from there, keeping a solid line the whole way.

When you get there, Links is sitting beside the box.

"Then here's what you need," he says. His eyes flicker blue, text scrolling through them. "That box lays a line maybe forty feet long, if you keep it unbroken. The back of the hall is sixty feet across between the outer columns. It won't reach wall to wall. And the big one is heavy enough to plough straight through a salt line. It burns, but it comes."

He looks up at you. "The small ones, a line will hold."

"Okay, that's helpful," you say. "Plan B, then."

You mean to ring yourself in, keep it small and thick, and walk the whole ring down the middle of the hall, drawing the small ones in to you.

When you're done you're standing in a thick white ring, and a little under half the box is left.

Links watches from outside the ring, his head tilted, the runes on him turning slowly.

"True to my word, then," he says. "Two things. Their lunge stops dead at a salt line. But a head can still stretch about a foot over it to bite."

You look down at the ring. It's three feet across, and you're standing in the middle of it. That puts your feet only a foot and a half from the salt on every side, close enough for a head stretched over the line to reach them.

"And every time you push the ring along," Links says, "the broom opens a gap in its edge for a moment."

"Well," you say, "good thing there's a bit more salt."

You sweep the ring wider, working the salt outward a little at a time, until its edge sits two feet from you on every side, still doubly thick all the way round.

Then you tip out the rest of the box. The last of it pours out in a white hiss, and you sweep it forward, drawing the ring out in front of you into an oval about six feet long.

The box is empty now. There's just enough salt for the oval, and precious little to spare.

You sweep the tail of the oval forward, carefully, laying new salt along the front before the back ever opens. Then again. And again. The whole white oval inches down the center of the hall with you inside it, like a caterpillar made of salt.

It's slow going, only a few feet a minute. The worn bristles skip over the salt as much as they push it. Behind you a faint white trail marks the floor where every sweep scatters a little salt.

About twenty feet from the nearest heaps, two small humps lift and turn toward you.

Both come off their heaps at once. They rear up and roar, that same wet, squelching challenge, and then they crawl at you across the scattered books.

They reach the oval and stop dead at the salt, one at the front and one at your left side.

They circle along the edge, blind heads swinging. Each one stretches its head a foot out over the line and snaps its teeth on empty air.

In the center, two feet from the salt, you're just beyond their reach.

From far back in the dark comes another roar. It's much deeper, much bigger, and it rolls out between the columns and goes on rolling.

Along the oval's tail, where you've swept most, the salt has worn thin in a patch.

You reach the broom out past the salt and hook its bristles round the nearer of the two, meaning to drag it in onto the line.

It's heavier than it looks. Twenty-odd pounds of wet grey weight hang on the end of the broom, and it won't come easily.

The bookworm twists. Its round jagged mouth clamps down on the worn bristles.

It wrenches back, hard, and the handle tears out of your hands.

The handle skids across the edge of the oval as it goes, and where it drags it scuffs the salt aside, leaving a gap a hand wide.

Out on the floor, the bookworm worries the broom head, chewing and chewing at the bristles.

The second bookworm stops circling. Its blind head lifts and turns toward the gap.

You crouch by the scuffed gap, weight forward on the balls of your feet, and wait for it.

The second bookworm comes. It noses into the gap and squeezes itself through, and the salt on either side scrapes its flanks raw as it goes.

It shrieks the whole way, a thin, high sound, its skin puckering along both sides.

As it clears the line, you throw yourself on it.

Its skin is wet and slick. It slides straight out from under your arms like soap in a bath, and you hit the floor on your side, hard.

It rears over you and lunges at your face. You jerk your head aside, and its teeth clack shut on air an inch from your cheek.

It coils between you and the gap, its blind head swaying toward you.

You scoop two fistfuls of salt off the oval's edge beside the gap. It bites into the scrape on your forearm, sharp and hot, and you close your fists on it anyway.

Then you lunge, and clamp both salted hands round the bookworm just behind its mouth.

Its wet skin puckers under your fingers. You feel it change as you hold it, going dry and rough against your palms. This time your grip holds.

You bear it down onto the salt line and pin it there. Its jaws snap and snap, a hand's width from your wrist.

It shrieks and thrashes under you. Then it shudders, all along its length, and you feel it shrinking, drawing in, going grey as the salt pulls it dry.

It's nearly still now, a stiffening coil under your hands. But not quite done.

Where you scooped, the gap in the oval has opened wider than your forearm.

Outside the oval, the first bookworm lets the chewed broom fall and turns its blind head toward the wider gap.

You plant a knee on the pinned bookworm and hold it hard against the salt line.

With your hands free, you scoop up two more fistfuls of salt from the gap's edge. The gap spreads wider as you take it. You turn toward the gap with the salt in your fists, ready to catch the other one when it lunges.

Under your knee the bookworm gives one last shudder. It dries into a hard grey coil, alive and utterly still.

The one that chewed the broom pours in through the widened gap and lunges at you.

It's fast, faster than your hands. Its teeth sink into your left forearm before your fingers can close, deep and tearing.

You clamp both salted fists round its neck, just behind the mouth, and wrench it off your arm.

Its skin puckers and hisses under the salt. It thrashes in your grip, weaker now, but still strong, heaving against your hands.

Blood runs down your left arm and drips onto the white salt below. The arm is going numb, and it has begun to shake.

You throw your whole weight forward and drive the bookworm down into the salt line.

You land across it, your fists still locked behind its mouth. Your torn arm shakes, but it holds.

It bucks under you, once, twice, shrieking into the salt. Then the shriek thins to a hiss.

Its skin shrinks and greys beneath you. The thrashing slows, and slows, and stops.

You stay on it, breathing hard, until what lies under you is a hard grey coil, alive and still.

In the ruined oval, one coil lies under you and the other beside you. Your left sleeve is soaked red.

Beyond the columns, further back in the gloom, the wet chewing goes on.

Still lying across the dried coil, you lift your head and call out across the hall. "Links, is the big one the last one, or are there more?"

Links pads up to the edge of the broken oval. The runes on his flanks crawl, and his eyes flicker blue, fine lines of text scrolling through them as he searches.

"Two more small ones, and the big one," he says sharply. "Three down. Half done. The easy half."

His too-large eyes drop to your left sleeve, where the blood is dripping from the cuff, and they stay there a moment.

"You're bleeding on my floor," he says, but it comes out quieter than the rest.

"Do you have some kind of magical healing for me," you ask, "or do I need to do this the hard way?"

Links's ears flatten. "You'd need to have learned healing from a book first," he says, "and you haven't."

"The hospital wing's shut and dark until I've the power to open it. Three worms doesn't buy that."

"So, the hard way. Wash it at the break room tap, eat something, sit still for once."

"I keep roots and vegetables a human can eat," he adds, grudging, "if you're not fussy."

"Fine," you say. "We do this the hard way then."

You press your bloody sleeve against your shirt, then down along your tights, soaking the blood into the cloth.

Then you get down and roll in the broken oval's salt. It crusts to the damp cloth, gritty and white, and where it finds the bite in your arm it burns like fire.

You fetch the chewed broom. Its bristles are bitten ragged down one side, but it still sweeps. You sweep the oval's salt together into one heap, a foot and a half across and ankle-deep.

Then you walk toward the chewing.

At twenty feet, a small bookworm rears up off its heap of books and roars its wet, squelching challenge at you.

It comes off the heap after you at a slow walk. You back toward the salt, and it follows.

At the heap it stops short. Its blind head sways at the salt's edge, and it will not come on.

You lunge to drag it in. It jerks back, and you sprawl into the heap alone. Salt sprays out across the floor, and the crust flakes off your shirt in patches.

It lunges at your face. You roll aside, and its teeth snap shut on air.

It coils just past the scattered salt, head low, between you and the dark.

It takes the bait. It lunges at your outstretched arm, and you whip the arm back, and its teeth close on air.

Before it can draw back, you clamp both salted fists round its neck, just behind the mouth.

Its skin puckers dry under your grip. You throw your weight forward and drive it down into the heap.

Salt sprays up round it. It shrieks and bucks, shrinking and greying fast.

You lie across it, fists locked, as the thrashing weakens under you.

You stay on the pinned bookworm until it shudders under you and dries into a hard grey coil, alive and still.

You roll it aside, over by the other coils. What's left of the heap is thin now, barely enough for one more.

You walk out between the columns toward the last of the wet chewing, deep in the gloom.

The last small bookworm is feeding about thirty feet short of the dark back steps.

At twenty feet it rears up and roars its challenge at you. Then it leaves its heap and follows you back.

At the heap you scoop up fistfuls of salt and hold out your arm. It lunges, and you snatch the arm clear.

Your salted fists close round its neck, and you drive it down into the last of the heap.

It shrieks and bucks, its skin puckering and greying, while the salt sprays thin around the two of you.

The heap is nearly gone beneath it, bare floor showing through, and it is not yet dry.

Under your hands the last small bookworm shudders, and dries into a hard grey coil.

Around you, the hall's gold light brightens another shade.

Links pads over, his eyes flickering blue. "Salt still burns it," he says. "It just ploughs through a line anyway."

Links bristles. "I have plans. They all cost power I haven't got." His eyes flicker blue.

    Power: 37%
    Kitchen: Waking
