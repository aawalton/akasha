Saturday afternoon finds you at the table by the back window, a book open flat under one hand and a mug of cocoa going cold beside it. Past the glass the valley runs west to Utah Lake, hazy with late-summer heat.

The house shakes like a wet dog.

There's no better way to put it: one hard shudder from floor to rafters, and then stillness. The cocoa trembles in its mug and settles. Out front, a man walking a spaniel keeps walking. The sprinkler next door keeps ticking round.

You turn the page. The house shakes again, and this time it doesn't stop.

The table buzzes under your palm until your teeth ache. The window crackles like cellophane. Outside, the man and his dog stutter in place, a frame forward and a frame back, black and white, gone, then there again, like a picture on an old television with the antenna knocked loose. The mug winks out and back. The book goes thin as smoke under your hand, and your hand stays solid on it, pale and ordinary and far too real. A hum rises out of everything at once, a swarm of hornets packed into the walls, and it climbs into your jaw and behind your eyes.

Shadow comes in through the walls. It reaches across the kitchen floor in long fingers, through the chairs, through the counter, toward you and you alone. You stand, the floor heaves, and you go down on one knee while the room dissolves into static.

Then it's dark, and quiet.

The quiet is total. You're kneeling on something soft and faintly springy, like the rubber matting under a playground swing. The air is still and cool and smells of old wood, a clean resin smell, the smell of a room that has never had a window opened in it.

Light comes up slowly, a dim blue-green glow with no source. It shows a floor that runs away in every direction and walls of dark wood far off, the grain all sweeping one way, curving round and up into a ceiling lost overhead. The room is round and enormous. It's like standing inside the trunk of a redwood, if a redwood were the size of a stadium.

Your kitchen is gone. Your house is gone. There isn't a window anywhere.

You get up, and the getting up is wrong.

Your shirt slides off one shoulder. The loose grey shirt you pulled on this morning hangs to the middle of your thighs like a nightdress, and the collar gapes. Your shorts have slid to your hips and are still going, and you catch them with a hand that isn't yours: small, narrow, pale, with short clean nails. Hair falls across your face, long and heavy and dark red, and when you push it back it keeps coming, down past your shoulder blades. The floor is closer than it should be. Your weight sits lower, differently balanced, and there's a new weight on your chest under the shirt that moves when you move.

Your light blue slip-ons are still on your feet, gaping at the heels.

"Hello?"

It comes out in a woman's voice, clear and a little hoarse, and it's yours. The word comes back off the far walls again and again, like a shout in a canyon. Your footsteps make no sound at all; the floor swallows them. When you take a step, the floor moves with you, carrying you further than the step should, and stops when you stop.

The echo dies away. The room stays huge and dim and empty.

Then there are words in the air in front of your eyes, on a flat panel of pale blue light that moves when you turn your head. A voice speaks them as they appear, and the voice is inside your skull and all around the room at once.

Projected energy expenditure exceeded.

Stand by for emergency protocol.

Emergency Power Mode Override Activated

The panel vanishes. The blue-green glow drains to red. Somewhere overhead an alarm starts up, a low double honk like geese going over, again and again. The floor heaves once more, hard enough to rock you on your feet, and settles.

In the red light you can see what the glow hid. At the center of the room rises a trunk, grey and smooth with age, more like stone or metal than wood, wider than a house, climbing until the dark takes it. Fine lines of blue run under its surface like veins, flickering in and out the way your street did. The hum comes from it. You feel it through the soft floor and up your spine, low and steady, and under the blare of the alarm it's the only warm thing in the room.
