You finish the loaf. Then you start hauling: a jar of honey, and three sacks of salt, out of the pantry and across the hall to its back.

One sack at a time is easy. Two at once is a stagger. It takes you several trips.

At the edge of the gloom you strip, and fold the robe, the belt and pouch, and the slippers into a neat pile to one side.

You slather yourself in honey, from your scalp down to your toes. Then you roll in the salt and pat it on in handfuls until you're crusted all over.

The honey holds the salt thick and close to your skin, far better than your bloody shirt ever did.

Hugging a whole sack to your chest, you creep back toward the steps, as quietly as you can.

About forty feet out, the big bookworm lifts its head. It's waist high, six or seven feet long, bloated and matte grey.

It rears up and roars. Creeping quietly hasn't hidden you at all.

Its blind head swings toward you, and it surges forward, its round mouth gaping.

You charge to meet it, the sack held out in front of you.

The sack goes in whole. You ram it deep, and the mouth clamps shut on it.

The worm bucks, choking, the salt burning in its gullet, and thrashes its whole bloated length.

Its body slams into you hard and flings you across the floor. You land with the wind knocked out of you, and pain everywhere, deep and bad.

A few yards off the worm writhes, the sack lodged in its throat. It is far from finished.

Behind you, at the edge of the gloom, sit the honey jar and two more sacks.
