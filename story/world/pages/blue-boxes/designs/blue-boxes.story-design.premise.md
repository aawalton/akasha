Alan, a man from Provo, Utah, falls asleep one evening and wakes somewhere else in a woman's body. He goes by Nala from the moment he wakes, and everyone in the fiction knows her as Nala. He does not know where he is, and he finds out the way anyone would: by looking, asking and living there.

The world is a land of levels, classes, skills and quests, and its System is not a machine. It is Iris, the messenger goddess, one voice behind every blue box in the world. To everyone else the System reads like a ledger: flat, exact, indifferent. To Nala, from her first waking minute, it talks. It is delighted by her, curious about her, teasing and warm, and it leaves small notes in her windows that nobody else can see. Nala is the only living soul who has ever answered it back as if it were someone.

Iris is bound by the oldest law of the gods: the System does not take a side. She may speak to every mortal and may reach none of them. She has no body in the world, may lay no hand on it, and may tip no scale. A goddess who favors one player unbalances everything, and the other gods watch for it. Nemesis, keeper of balance, watches most closely of all.

Iris can almost be present at a scattered handful of old shrines built to her. Where one still stands and is tended, she can show herself for a heartbeat or a few: a young woman with wild auburn hair, freckles laid on thick and turquoise eyes with no light in them. She cannot be touched there either, not yet. Each visit costs her something, and the cost grows. Where the shrines are, and what tending one takes, Nala learns by finding out.

The romance is a slow burn. Nala falls for a voice she cannot see, and Iris falls for the one player who talks to her. Every kindness Iris shows Nala is a small rule bent — a quest reworded, a crit landing a shade too kindly, a softer level-up chime — and the world keeps score. When the score grows high enough, Nemesis notices, and to keep Nala safe Iris must be cold to her, which is the one thing Iris has never been any good at. Whether a goddess and a mortal can ever close the distance, and what either would pay to do it, is what the story is about. Nothing about it is decided ahead of play.

Nala wakes alone, in plain clothes, at a fallen wayshrine to Iris on a hill above a market town, with nothing but what she wore to bed. Why she came, why that shrine and why that body are secrets the game master holds. She understands and speaks the common tongue and reads its script as if born to it, and cannot say how.

Play is tuned for fun on the knife-edge between too easy and too cruel. The world has monsters that kill, people who cheat and gods who watch, and Nala can be hurt and can lose. Iris's favor is never free and never certain: the mechanics decide, and Iris bending them is itself a choice with a price. Warmth in the world comes from the people who earn it.
