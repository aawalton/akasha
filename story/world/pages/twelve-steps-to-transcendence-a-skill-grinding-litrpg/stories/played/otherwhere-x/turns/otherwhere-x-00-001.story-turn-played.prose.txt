The last thing you remember is your back window. A book open on your knee, the evening going blue outside the glass, your eyes getting heavy.

Then something scratchy under your cheek, and the smell of dry straw and dust.

You open your eyes.

Stubble. Short, pale stalks cut close to the ground, row after row of them, running away from your face toward a low hedge. The light is gold and slanting, the kind that comes an hour or two before sunset. A crow walks between the rows a few yards off, stops, and looks at you with one eye.

You push yourself up, and your arms are wrong.

They are thin. The wrists are narrow, the hands small, the fingers slim and pale with a few freckles on the backs. You turn them over. Your hands do what you tell them, a half second late, as if they are still learning.

Hair falls across your face when you look down. Long, heavy, dark red. You pull it back, and it keeps going, past your shoulders, past your shoulder blades.

Your shirt is your shirt. The old dark grey one, but it hangs off you now, the collar sliding to one side, the hem down near the middle of your thighs. Your black tights are still on. Your feet are bare and dusty, and small.

Your chest is not your chest. You look, once, and then you look at the field instead.

You get to your feet. The ground is lower than it should be. Everything is a little taller than it should be.

Behind you is a road. Two ruts worn deep into packed earth, grass down the middle, running east and west as far as you can see. At its edge, just beside you, sits a squat grey stone about waist high, crusted with yellow lichen on one side. Letters are cut into its face.

HARROW 2, and an arrow pointing east. Above the letters, a shape worn almost smooth: a shield, maybe, with something inside it.

You can read it. You know you have never seen these letters before in your life, and you can read it as easily as a street sign.

East, over a low rise, a thin line of wood smoke goes straight up into the still air. West, the road runs down toward a line of trees. Along the field's edge, the hedge is thick with small red berries.

The crow caws once and flies off toward the smoke.

The air is starting to cool.
