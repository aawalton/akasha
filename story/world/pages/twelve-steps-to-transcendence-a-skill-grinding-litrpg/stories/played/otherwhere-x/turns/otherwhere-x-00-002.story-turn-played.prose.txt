You start east, toward the smoke. It is closer than the trees, and where there is a fire there is somebody tending it.

The road is harder than it looked. The ruts are packed earth baked almost to stone, with flints set all through them, and your bare soles find every one. You try the grass strip down the middle, and it is better, until it thins out and puts you back in the rut.

Your stride is wrong. Each step lands short of where you meant it to, and when you lengthen it to make up, you overreach and stumble. Your hair swings forward into your eyes every few paces. You push it back. It swings forward again.

Along the north side of the road runs a thin ditch of still water, rainwater by the look of it, dust and a few loose stalks floating on the top. Past it, a mile off across the stubble, a forest begins: a dark edge of oak and beech, running along the whole far side of the fields.

You look back once.

From here you can see the spot where you woke. The stubble there is flattened in a ring about ten paces across, and every stalk in it is laid down pointing outward, away from the middle.

You turn and keep walking.

The road climbs a long, low rise. By the top your feet are sore, and grey with dust to the ankle.

On the far side, below you, is a village. Thatched roofs around an open green. Smoke going up from the chimneys in thin, straight lines. Off to one side, a mill wheel turns slowly in a stream.

The sun is low and red at your back. Your shadow runs out ahead of you down the road, long and thin.

Partway down the slope, something is coming up the road toward you, and it is loud. Geese. A dozen of them, waddling west in a loose crowd, honking at each other. Behind them walks a boy of about ten with a willow switch, and he is talking to them the whole way, one long stream of scolding and coaxing that never stops for breath.

He looks up and sees you, and stops dead. The geese mill around his legs.

He stares at your bare feet. Then at the shirt. Then at the black tights. His mouth hangs open.

When he speaks, the words come slow and broad, with a country burr on them, and you understand every one without trying.

"Who're you? Where's your shoes? Were you robbed? Is that a man's shirt?"

He doesn't stop for an answer.

"Did you hear the dogs howl this afternoon? All of 'em at once. Did you come from the Mile?"

He edges half a step back toward the village, the switch raised.
