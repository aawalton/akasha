You stand up at the trestle and sing "O Danny Boy", slow and low, for the room.

The voice that comes out isn't the one you left behind. You have to find it as you go, one line at a time.

The tune is new to Harrow. By the near trestle, the smith sets his cup down to listen.

The words are plain enough: a farewell, someone gone away, a promise to be there. At the far trestle a lad stops chewing. Martha's ladle hangs over the pot.

Then the song comes to "and the pipes are calling." There are no pipes in Harrow, and no word for them. Two faces crease.

"From glen to glen, and down the mountain side." There are no glens in the vale, and no mountains either.

You hold the last note and let it die out, rather than cut it short.

The room doesn't clap. It knocks the trestles with its knuckles, which is what Harrow does.

You go straight on into "Homeward Bound", a plainer song, faster, about going home.

"I'm sitting in a railway station." There's no word for that here, and this time they hear it.

A woman leans to the one beside her. "What's a railway station?"

You reach the last verse, the one about wanting to be home, and your voice scrapes on it. You finish with your throat raw and your hands not quite steady, in a body that isn't yours.

Aldous hasn't moved from the door. His face has gone still the way it went still at your name.

The room is pleased with you and uneasy about you at once, and can't settle which.

Martha breaks it. "Well. The lass can sing." She hooks a stool out from under the next trestle.

Coppers go down on the wood. Three of them: from the smith, from the woman who asked, and from a hand at the back.

The question the room has been holding comes out of the back of it. "Where'd you learn songs like that?"

Martha sets a cup in front of you. "Another. Then you'll say where a girl gets songs like that."
