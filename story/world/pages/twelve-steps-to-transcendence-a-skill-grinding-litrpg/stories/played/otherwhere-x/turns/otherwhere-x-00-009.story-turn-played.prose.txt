You start a third one: "Dulaman", a quick dancing song, and this time in its own tongue. Irish. You don't know what the words mean. You only know how they go.

The first bar is quick and bright. Under the smith's trestle, a foot starts to tap.

Then the words go out into the room. "Dúlamán na binne buí..." They aren't the common tongue, and every face in the room knows it.

The tapping foot stops.

Your throat is still raw from the last song, and it catches on the fast run of the verse. The tune slips out from under you.

You push on. The words are only sounds to you, and with no tune under them they come out ragged. To the room, you can hear it, it must sound like a stranger muttering in a tongue that isn't theirs.

At the far end, a woman draws her shawl up round her shoulders. Somebody sets a cup down very carefully.

You reach the end with your voice gone thin, and stop.

The trestles stay quiet this time. The fire is the loudest thing in the room.

"I've been singing off and on for basically my whole life," you say. "Just comes naturally. These are popular songs where I am from, but that is far, far away."

The woman who whispered earlier, the one with bright eyes, says it into the quiet. "That's no tongue of Sulon."

"There's no other tongue. Not here. Not anywhere a person comes from." She looks at you. "My gran said a skinwalker mutters in the tongue of wherever it was before."

The smith speaks once, and plain. "She sang Danny Boy well enough, Hattie."

The room lets it lie.

Aldous has come off the door. He stands at the end of your trestle now, and doesn't say a word.

Martha looks from Hattie to you, and puts both fists on the wood.

"You'll answer that in my house, lass. What tongue was that, and whose?"
