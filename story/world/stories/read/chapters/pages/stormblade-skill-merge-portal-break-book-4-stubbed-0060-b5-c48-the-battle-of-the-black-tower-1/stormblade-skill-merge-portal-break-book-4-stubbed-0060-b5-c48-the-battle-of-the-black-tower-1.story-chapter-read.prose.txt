As the God of Thunder’s portals opened across Earth and the Stormblade’s portal world, a number of the greatest powers watched.

The Crone knew of at least ten sets of eyes, each well over S-Rank, paying close attention. There’d been fewer up until Kade’s brilliant raid. Everyone knew that the God of Thunder would attempt ascension soon; this battle hadn’t been interesting. But now…now, with the storm dragon’s plans set back decades or centuries…

“Well, now it’s interesting,” the Crone murmured.

She kept an eye on the ‘lesser’ Paragons, of course. In his lonely desert fortress, Retan the Dustblade was paying attention in his own way. The Crone knew he didn’t care who won. If Kade achieved victory, his own attempt at ascension would be easier. If not, the man who’d ruined his ‘sword sect’ gimmick would be dead. Either way, Retan stood to profit.

Other Paragons had more stakes. Most had lined up behind the God of Thunder. Lightning and cloud Paragons, ones who stood to gain from whatever scraps the lightning dragon left behind.

“Mother Mine, doth we dare intervene?” the puppet who was the Crone’s closest ally and confidant asked. “The Stormblade is—“

“The Stormblade is hopelessly outgunned. I know. You’re not telling me anything I can’t see. He’ll be lucky to last five minutes with that damnable wyrm going all-out—and the God of Thunder has no choice now. If he slows down, the pressure builds. I can feel it from here, and it’s not my ascension at stake.”

“Then why don’t we watch Kade’s—“

The Crone’s hand went up. “Because ‘Eugene’ is a useless wyrm. He’s soft. Pathetic. Always has been. His battle with Kade’s nothing but a feint. The real fight’s taking place elsewhere. On Earth. That’s where we want to pay attention.”

Mind Maiden Enolda went quiet as the Crone waved her hand and cut the puppet woman’s strings. Then, alone in her tent, she stared at the battle through orbs of frozen water as both sides deployed their opening gambits.

One way or another, this was going to be an incredible battle.

The Crone gave it two minutes before she had to intervene. Maybe three.

The God of Thunder entered my portal world like the storm to end all storms.

He surged through the portal in his warrior form, Stormsteel armor covering his arc-lightning torso in whirling plates of cloud, the maelstrom almost but not quite obscuring the crackling electricity surging from his core. His spear shimmered with slicked rainwater in a permanent-looking mirror of Rain-Slicked Blade, and his helmet’s visor was down. Black cloud poured from the eye slits.

As he entered, the cloud overhead—which had already destabilized into a chaotic mess of stormwalls, anvils, and thunderheads—was shoved west by the shockwave of his aura. That rippled outward, static crackling with blue-white lightning that scoured the shadow garden of anything but scorched dirt and stone. “Kade Noelstra! You’re a brave pupil! Maybe the bravest I’ve had, aside from Tallas. But it won’t matter. This end was inevitable.”

I didn’t respond, except to step out onto the top of the Lightning Tower, feet from the storm’s massive bolts of lightning. As they crashed down around me, the heat all but blistered my skin. The clouds slowly shifted back into their vortex above the tower. I drew the Storm’s Blade and pointed it toward the God of Thunder, then dropped into Torrentborne.

He nodded and surged through the maze we’d created.

And as he did, we sprang the first part of our trap. Or more accurately, Ellen did.

She’d been waiting, hidden in the maze and wrapped in shadow, her Voidwalker form empowering the Darkness around her. Staying in her own Black Hole spell was rough, but the alternative was that she’d be detected. We couldn’t have that. Not until it was too late.

The moment the God of Thunder turned the corner and left the portal between his world and mine, she revealed herself. Instead of attacking Eugene from behind, though, her power surged toward the portal, grappling with it. Ellen’s only goal for the opening moves of the fight was to hold the portal open. If everything went the way we’d discussed, we’d need it soon enough.

And as for me?

My job was to make sure the God of Thunder didn’t notice.

And to make that happen, I had to give him a show. Cyclone Forms. Rolling Thunder. Ariette’s Thunderhead—copied as I cast Brendan’s Storm Sigil and fed the power of the Thunderhead into it. The God of Thunder moved through the maze of earthen ramparts, impossibly quickly. His armor glowed as his aura built up and his core pushed itself harder and harder. I couldn’t stand up to him alone, and I knew it.

That was fine. I wasn’t alone. And the Hand of the Thunder God I was building up to wasn’t my only move. It was merely the tip of the spear.

Rob had learned a lot from Kade.

Back in that E-Rank Glade portal, he’d learned how to fight against enemies more powerful than himself. By studying Kade’s build choices, he’d found a way to maximize his own potential, regardless of the allies around him. He’d learned—or more accurately, they’d both re-learned—honor and respect in battle from their fight during the Fallen Delver’s Tournament.

But the thing he’d learned most recently wasn’t from Kade. It was from his little sister.

He wore the swirling sand armband of the Desert Wind Guild, but he’d asked not to be assigned to a team. He was on the cusp of A-Rank—practically a minor power in Phoenix in his own right—and he’d figured he’d be better off on his own, helping stretch the city’s too-thin defenses.

As the portal opened before him and vomited out a dozen cultists, he grinned. It wasn’t a happy-go-lucky kind of grin.

Stormbreaker Cultist: B Rank

They wore robes of mottled gray, white, and black, seeming to blend in with the thick clouds that billowed out of the golden portal at their backs. Thin swords that crackled with static. Spears with liquid metal tips. Breastplates and gauntlets under their robes.

Still, Rob wasn’t worried about the cultists. Not by themselves. The larger problem was the monster that had followed them through the portal.

Stormbreaker Rolad, Paragon of the Stormwall: S-Rank

It was a rippling wall of cloud, swirling around a central spire of brackish rainwater like a miniature hurricane. Twenty feet tall—or maybe more. No weapons, but as the hurricane’s walls expanded, they started to tear into the buildings on either side of the street. Cinderblocks and concrete tore free, pummeling both cultists and asphalt as Stormbreaker Rolad advanced.

Rob braced himself. His spells rippled across his chainmail and plate, casting a golden glow across the street. His mace hammered into the first Cultist’s sword, then blew clean through it to dent her breastplate. Something cracked. It wasn’t steel. She tried to scream, but nothing came out but a puff of pink blood.

A spear skittered across Rob’s armor. Sparks flew—both the arcing electrical sparks and the metal-on-metal ones. He backstepped, both hands on the end of his mace as he turned his follow-through into a massive backswing. That cultist went flying.

But the thing he’d learned from Kade Noelstra’s sister, from the leader of the Desert Wind Guild? He’d forgotten it. Or maybe not forgotten it, but ignored it in favor of what he knew he had to do. If he’d had a team at his back, maybe he could have challenged Stormbreaker Rolad.

By himself, all he could do was give way, hammer his mace into cultists and leave them broken and shattered on the street, and retreat from the S-Ranked monster.

And he couldn’t give an inch. Every step back was more room for the Paragon’s cult to surge into Phoenix. He had to hold the portal until…until someone showed up to close it.

Who that was, Rob had no idea. He just kept fighting and, step by step, kept getting pushed back.

All across the city, the same story played out. If it wasn’t Rob, it was an entropy mage named Erik, who went down under a dozen lightning bolts in the center of a perfectly round crater of dust. Or a part of tanks with a squad behind them who found themselves cut off in the Arrowhead Mall, a few miles from a trio of golden portals that had opened around the wreckage of the Desert Wind Guild’s building. Or, a few blocks from Bob Traynor’s fortified mansion, a quartet of delvers in Traynor Guild armbands who’d been trapped when a portal opened on top of the black stone pinnacle and poured elementals down into the city from high above.

But while the city of Phoenix fought for its life against a portal surge every bit as high-pressure as the Portal Blitz, the 303 Wall’s guns were silent. Not a single Traynor Overholz Cannon fired.

Instead, they waited, second after second, as the portals pushed more and more monsters and cultists out.

Hand of the Thunder God.

The biggest one I’d ever assembled.

I’d had time. The God of Thunder had entered the Void Garden on the far side, and he’d mostly obeyed the maze’s rules—though his movements through it had been unerring thus far. So, by the time he rounded the final corner, I had not one, not two, but three separate copies of Brendan’s Storm Sigil—along with most of my Mana—drawing power from almost a dozen of Ariette’s Thunderheads.

The Hand of the Thunder God was wide enough that it cut a thirty-foot swathe through the first eight layers of the maze, and an increasingly smaller one through the next twenty. It was so bright I saw it clearly even through closed eyes. The sheer power I’d poured out could have immolated one of the Craftsman’s machines instantly, or ended a fight against a monster like Quellat in one move.

But against the God of Thunder, it was nothing but a distraction.

His spear pierced through the center of the spell. It broke around it, showering his armor with arcing sparks, and he stepped through it.

I wasn’t done, though. Far from it. The moment he broke through Hand of the Thunder God, flowers of electricity bloomed around him, and around me.

And I moved. As Stormbreak blossomed around me and my Mana plummeted to rock-bottom, I sprinted forward with all the energy I could muster. Thunderlord stance into a quick thrust. The first of the electrical pulses rippled outward, washing over the God of Thunder and me.

I attacked. The God of Thunder reacted. His spear caught the Storm’s Blade. Metal scraped on metal, and lightning arced between us as Stormbreak kept surging.

“You’ve learned well, kid.”

I didn’t say anything. I just kept attacking.

And then, when Stormbreak ended, the Lightning Tower opened fire.

We’d installed the Traynor Overholz Cannons facing the Void Garden, on every level of the Lightning Tower. One shot, loaded, and a primitive reloading mechanism. Every single cannon was aimed right at the exit to the maze. Close to fifty guns fired, filling the acrid, ozone-scented air with the strong odor of gunpowder. Explosions rippled across the God of Thunder’s armor. The shockwave slammed against his electric aura. I shifted stances a moment before the cannons’ shells hit, using Lightning Dance to be anywhere but near the SS-Plus-Ranked Paragon.

But even with all of that, the God of Thunder barely reacted. Even when Ellen’s Shadow Cell closed around him, he didn’t unleash his full strength. We simply weren’t pushing hard enough. Not yet.

And I didn’t know if we could.

The dust cleared. The God of Thunder stood, almost unscathed.

Almost.

He glanced down at the jagged, magical gaps spiderwebbing out from the center of his breastplate. Either the lightning of Stormbreak had done it, or the cannons mounted all along the Lightning Tower’s many floors had. “Interesting. You almost got through my armor. Now, let’s see. What to do about this? Ah, yes.”

Power surged from the God of Thunder. His spear glowed the same red-orange as the lightning we’d encountered in his memory portal. He swung it in a wide arc. Thunder cracked. Lightning hammered down all across the battlefield. It punched into the rods lining the dirt ramparts. Earthworks exploded outward all across the Void Garden. Dust and stone and chunks of half-buried masonry erupted into the air. It was shockingly silent. Then the pressure wave hit, like a wall of force—or like most delvers’ auras.

It pressed against me, and wherever Ellen was hiding, it had to be ripping into her, too. But she didn’t move, and the God of Thunder’s portal didn’t close behind him.

We still had him. He just didn’t know it yet.

Then the lightning knight’s spear lashed out, and I parried it. The impact jarred my wrists and elbows, and I slid back into Torrentborne stance as he started his attack in earnest.
