The morning sun climbed higher into the pale, cloudless sky, casting long, sharp shadows across the battered cobblestones of the town. For the first time in what felt like a lifetime, the air wasn't thick with the sounds of screaming, clashing steel, or the frantic barking of orders. Instead, the town was filled with the rhythmic, purposeful noises of recovery: the dull thwack of hammers against wood, the scraping of shovels clearing debris, and the low, murmuring conversations of exhausted survivors taking stock of their lives.

With Hopeless’s blessing and a day or two of guaranteed downtime ahead of them, the party naturally splintered to attend to their own specific needs.

Perberos, predictably, found the concept of 'relaxing' entirely alien. For the elven ranger, true rest was found in the quiet, methodical focus of a hunt. While the others were still blinking the sleep from their eyes, Perberos had already slipped away to the makeshift militia barracks.

The town was safe from the massive, organised wave, but the forest beyond the walls was still teeming with stragglers. Perberos sought out rangers and scouts, quickly cobbling together an ad-hoc hunting party. It was a diverse, lethal, and remarkably quiet group of rogue-class fighters. There was a grizzled, grey-bearded human tracker missing half his left ear; a lithe, hyper-vigilant feline beastkin assassin whose twin daggers were already coated in fresh poison; and a towering, remarkably light-footed half-orc scout who communicated entirely in sharp hand signals and low grunts.

Without a word of formal introduction, the four of them slipped through a gate and melted into the dense treeline. Perberos took point. The forest was a mess of trampled undergrowth, snapped branches, and the lingering, acrid stench of the Kobold escapees. Tracking them wasn't a matter of skill; a blind man could have followed the trail of devastation. The challenge lay in the execution. The hunting party spent the hours stalking through the dappled shadows, locating the small, fractured packs of Kobolds that had been scattering since their breakout. There were no huge groups left—just terrified, disorganised clusters of five to ten creatures, huddled in ravines or arguing violently over scavenged scraps of meat.

Perberos and his makeshift squad moved like ghosts. They didn't engage in pitched battles; they orchestrated silent, ruthless executions. A volley of arrows from the high branches, a blur of poisoned steel from the underbrush, and the sharp, brutal efficiency of the half-orc’s handaxes. By midday, Perberos's quiver was half-empty, his leather armour was flecked with dark blood, and he felt more centred and at peace than he had in weeks.

While Perberos sought solace in the woods, Carcan’s conscience demanded she head in the opposite direction.

Despite Hopeless’s assurance, the cleric simply couldn't justify taking personal downtime without verifying the state of the wounded with her own two eyes. She made her way back to the large, multi-roomed mercantile warehouse that had been commandeered as a triage centre.

The moment she stepped through the heavy oak doors, the scent of antiseptic herbs, clean linen, and burning incense washed over her. The atmosphere was remarkably calm. The frantic, blood-slicked chaos of the siege had been replaced by a quiet, orderly sense of convalescence. The local healers were moving gently between the rows of cots. Carcan spent an hour walking the aisles, her hands glowing with a soft, diagnostic white light as she checked bandages, inspected healing burns, and offered minor soothing prayers. But as the morning wore on, the stark reality of the situation became clear: she was a fifth wheel.

As she’d told the party, the physical injuries had all been stabilised. What the patients needed now was time, rest, and the psychological comfort that the Sisters were far better trained to provide. When a senior Sister politely but firmly patted Carcan on the arm and told her to "go and rest, child, before you collapse yourself," Carcan finally conceded defeat.

She found Josh, Bhel, and Brett loitering near the central square, watching a team of carpenters fit a massive new axle onto a heavy merchant wagon.

"Hospital all okay?" Josh asked, leaning against a stone wall, his arms crossed over his chest. He was out of his heavy plate armour for the first time in days, wearing simple, dark linen trousers and a loose-fitting tunic that did very little to hide the bruised, battered state of his musculature.

"It is," Carcan sighed, smoothing down the front of her pristine white robes. "The Sisters have everything well in hand. I felt distinctly in the way."

"Good," Bhel grunted. The dwarf was practically vibrating with pent-up anticipation. "That means we can finally get down to the serious business of the day."

"Before we do," Brett interjected, looking around with a slight frown. "Did you happen to see Bean or her entourage at the triage centre? I haven't seen the chaotic little gremlin all morning."

Carcan couldn't help the small, amused smirk that touched her lips. "I didn't see her at the hospital, no. But I did speak to Butler earlier. Apparently, Bun was deeply offended to discover that Josh has out-levelled her during the siege."

Josh blinked, a surprised laugh escaping his chest. "She’s checked my guild profile?"

"Thoroughly, it seems," Carcan nodded. "She threw a minor tantrum about the inherent unfairness of it all, vowed to surpass you before the week was out, and marched her entire party straight into the newly stabilised portal at first light. She intends to farm the dungeon until they collapse."

"Gods preserve whatever poor monsters are left in that rift," Brett muttered, shaking his head. "A competitive, heavily armed rabbit-kin is a terrifying force of nature. Still, it keeps her out of our hair."

"Right! Enough about rabbits!" Bhel declared loudly, clapping his thick hands together. The sharp crack echoed across the square. "We have a spa to find. Lead the way, Josh. You’ve got the longest legs."

Finding the establishment wasn't difficult; the town was small, and a building dedicated to luxury stood out prominently amongst the functional, soot-stained timber architecture. To Bhel's immense delight, the building had survived the invasion entirely unscathed. However, to his minor disappointment, the place was completely deserted. The beautifully carved wooden front doors were unlocked and slightly ajar, but the reception desk was abandoned, the ledger dusty, and the decorative braziers cold. The staff, like most of the town's non-combatants, were likely halfway to a city or busy packing their belongings for the impending caravan journey.

"Looks like it's self-service today, gents," Bhel announced, pushing the doors wide and marching inside with the absolute authority of a man who knew exactly what he wanted.

They navigated the quiet, echoing corridors, finding a stack of fresh, neatly folded white towels in a linen cupboard. They split off into separate changing alcoves.

When Josh emerged a few minutes later, clad only in a modest white towel wrapped securely around his waist, he found the others already gathered in the main bathing chamber. It was a beautifully designed room. The floors and walls were laid with smooth, dark slate, and the centrepiece was a massive, sunken pool, easily large enough to accommodate twenty people comfortably. Faint sunlight streamed through frosted glass skylights overhead.

There was just one glaring problem.

Bhel was standing at the edge of the pool, one thick, hairy leg dipped into the water, his face contorted in an expression of profound betrayal.

"It's cold," the dwarf whispered, sounding genuinely heartbroken.

Josh walked over and crouched down, dipping his hand into the crystal-clear water. He winced. It wasn't freezing, but it was far from the muscle-melting heat they were all desperately craving. "The heating enchantments must have run dry with no one powering them.”

"This is a tragedy," Bhel moaned, slumping onto a nearby wooden bench. "A literal crime against dwarvenkind. I survived a Kobold tide for this?"

"Oh, for goodness sake, stop whining," Brett sighed, stepping out of his own changing alcove. The mage looked impossibly thin without his layered robes, his skin a pale, aristocratic white that had rarely seen the sun. He walked to the edge of the pool, cracking his knuckles with a theatrical flourish. "Stand back, you magically inept brutes. Let a professional handle this."

Brett sat cross-legged on the slate floor right at the water's edge. He closed his eyes, taking a slow, deep breath, his hands hovering an inch above the surface of the pool.

"Now," Brett lectured, his tone instantly shifting into his preferred, highly academic professorial cadence, "the trick here is regulation. Any amateur mage can boil water. A simple spell or a crude burst of elemental fire would instantly turn this pool into a scalding geyser of steam, likely melting the slate and boiling us all alive in the process."

"Please don't boil us alive, Brett," Josh requested mildly.

"I won't, provided you remain silent and allow me to concentrate," Brett retorted, his brow furrowing in deep concentration, whispering to himself. "The challenge is thermal transference. I need to draw ambient mana, convert it into pure thermal energy, and slowly, evenly distribute it throughout the liquid volume without creating isolated pockets of super-heated steam."

A faint, deep crimson aura began to glow around Brett's hands. He didn't cast a fireball; instead, the air immediately surrounding his palms began to shimmer and distort with intense, radiating heat. Slowly, the water directly beneath his hands began to ripple.

For ten solid minutes, the room was silent save for the steady, measured sound of Brett's breathing. It was a masterful display of magical control. Josh, whose own magical abilities were limited to enhancing his physical strikes, watched with genuine respect. He could feel the ambient temperature in the room steadily rising.

A thick, luxurious curtain of white steam began to slowly rise from the surface of the water, curling lazily towards the skylights.

"Right," Brett gasped suddenly, cutting the flow of mana and slumping backwards onto the wet slate, breathing heavily. A sheen of sweat covered his pale face. "That... is remarkably taxing. It takes far more focus to gently heat a bath than it does to incinerate a charging monster."

Bhel didn't even bother offering thanks. The dwarf let out a triumphant roar and launched himself into the air, executing a surprisingly graceful cannonball directly into the centre of the pool. The splash was enormous, sending a tidal wave of hot water washing over the slate floor.

"Perfection!" Bhel bellowed, his head surfacing amidst the steam, his wet beard clinging to his face. "Get in here, you cowards!"

Josh chuckled and slid into the water. The heat was incredible. It sank immediately into his battered muscles, melting away the deep, lingering aches of the past three days. He let out a long, shuddering sigh of pure relief, leaning his head back against the smooth stone edge of the pool and closing his eyes.

A soft splash to his left announced Carcan’s arrival.

Josh opened his eyes and turned his head. Carcan had entered the water quietly. She had wrapped her towel tightly beneath her arms, forming a modest, makeshift bathing dress that fell to her knees beneath the water. Her long, silver hair had been piled up into a messy, utilitarian bun at the top of her head, secured with a pair of wooden pins, exposing the elegant line of her neck.

Without her heavy robes, her armour, or the intense, focused aura of her healing magic, she looked strikingly different. She looked softer, more relaxed, and undeniably beautiful in the diffused, steamy light of the bathhouse.

Josh felt a sudden, entirely unexpected flutter in his chest. He quickly averted his gaze, staring intently at a crack in the ceiling tiles, suddenly hyper-aware of his own size and the myriad of silver scars that crisscrossed his chest and arms from a hundred previous battles. He didn't want to stare. He wanted to be respectful.

But as the minutes ticked by, filled only with the sound of Bhel’s happy humming and Brett’s exhausted, floating sighs, Josh found his eyes drifting back.

He looked over. And, in the exact same fraction of a second, Carcan looked over at him.

Through the curling mist of the hot spring, their eyes met. It wasn't a glare, and it wasn't the clinical, assessing look she usually gave him when checking for injuries. It was a quiet, shared moment of profound vulnerability. Her golden eyes held a faint, unmistakable softness.

For a heartbeat, the rest of the world—the ruined town, the looming caravan, the threat of continent-wide rifts—simply ceased to exist.

Then, with a deafening snort, Bhel violently blew his nose into a handful of pool water, completely shattering the silence.

Both Josh and Carcan blinked, breaking eye contact simultaneously. Carcan sank a little lower into the water, a faint dusting of pink appearing on her cheeks that had absolutely nothing to do with the temperature of the bath. Josh swallowed hard, shifting his position and suddenly finding a deep, abiding interest in the architectural structure of the slate tiles opposite him.

Neither of them said a word. But the heavy, comfortable silence that settled over them for the rest of the hour felt distinctly different than before.

By mid-afternoon, all four of them were thoroughly soaked, their skin pruned and wrinkly, their muscles feeling like warm jelly. Their clothes were pristine, having been dumped in a cleaning pool, that was now stained a shade darker than it should be. They dried off, dressed in their fresh clothes, and stepped back out into the bustling town square.

The rest of the day was spent in a state of relaxed, aimless wandering. They had gold in their pockets and time to kill. They bought fresh, sweet bread from a baker who had just re-lit his ovens. They watched as the enormous caravan slowly began to take shape.

It was a staggering logistical undertaking. Over a hundred townsfolk, a mix of terrified civilians, displaced merchants, and weary adventurers, were preparing for the long trek to Ashenfall. Wagons were being lined up along the main thoroughfare, their canvas roofs patched, their heavy wooden wheels greased. Draught animals—sturdy oxen, shaggy ponies, and heavy-set workhorses—were being corralled and fed in the central plaza.

There was a palpable sense of anxiety in the air, a desperate urge to flee the site of the disaster, but also a grim, determined resilience. The town was moving on.

As the sun began to dip below the horizon, painting the sky in brilliant strokes of orange and bruised purple, the four of them retired to the tavern. It was a vastly different atmosphere from the wild, drunken chaos of the night before. The mood was subdued, quiet, and deeply companionable. They took a large table near the back, ordering a massive platter of roasted fowl and root vegetables, and a pitcher of respectable, moderately priced ale; the free-for-all of the night before vanishing as order took control again.

Just as the food arrived, the tavern doors swung open, and Perberos slipped inside.

The elf looked exhausted. His boots were caked in dark forest mud, and his cloak smelled strongly of pine needles and iron. He approached their table, sliding silently into an empty chair.

"Successful hunt?" Josh asked, passing the elf a wooden tankard of ale.

"Very," Perberos nodded, taking a long, appreciative swallow. "We swept a ten-mile radius around the western and northern palisades. Found and eliminated over three dozen Kobold groups, mostly small, broken packs. They were terrified, fighting amongst themselves. There are still more out there, deeply embedded in the deeper woods, but the local rangers will continue sweeping over the next few weeks. The immediate, cohesive threat to the town is entirely broken."

"Good work, my friend," Bhel said, tearing a leg off the roasted bird and tossing it onto Perberos’s plate. "Eat up. You look like a stiff breeze would knock you over."

The evening passed in a haze of good food, light conversation, and genuine camaraderie. They didn't talk about the rift, or Hopeless, or the terrifying implications of the broader magical fluctuations. They talked about past delves, argued over the best way to maintain plate armour, and laughed as Brett tried to explain the theoretical geometry of a teleportation spell he’d been reading about using a handful of peas and a half-eaten carrot.

It was a quiet, necessary evening. A brief respite before the storm.

When they finally retired to their rented room, the sleep that claimed them was deep, dreamless, and profoundly restorative.

The next morning, Josh awoke before the sun. The lingering fatigue of the siege was entirely gone, replaced by a thrumming, eager energy. He strapped on his heavy plate armour, the familiar weight of the steel a comforting embrace. He checked his spatial pouch, ensuring his potions were stocked and his sword was perfectly honed.

When he stepped out of the inn, the rest of the party was already waiting for him in the cool, crisp pre-dawn air. Bhel was leaning on his axe, smoking a foul-smelling pipe. Brett was adjusting his pristine robes, looking mildly annoyed at being awake so early. Carcan stood tall and serene, her staff resting lightly in her hand, offering Josh a small, fleeting smile as he approached. Perberos was perched on a low stone wall, his sharp eyes scanning the horizon.

They turned as one and looked down the main street.

The caravan was ready.

A line of nearly forty wagons and carriages, packed tight with supplies and frightened people, stretched out towards the heavy iron gates of the town. The drivers were shouting in the dim light, the draught animals stamping their hooves impatiently against the cobblestones. The sheer scale of the convoy was daunting. It would be slow, cumbersome, and a prime target for anything lurking on the long, dangerous road to Ashenfall.

Josh drew his short sword, letting the heavy blade rest casually against his shoulder. He looked at his party, feeling a surge of profound pride. They were battered, they were rough around the edges, but they were a team.

"Right then," Josh said, his voice carrying the calm, steady authority of a vanguard leader. "Let's get these people home."
