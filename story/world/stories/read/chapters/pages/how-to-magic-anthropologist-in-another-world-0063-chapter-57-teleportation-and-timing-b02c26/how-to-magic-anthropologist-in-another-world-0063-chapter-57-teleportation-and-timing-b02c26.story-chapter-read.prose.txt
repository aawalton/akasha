We returned to camp in silence, reflecting on what we had just been through.The sanctified ground welcomed us back, a home away from home, providing shelter and safety. We had left our camp set, trusting that no one would reach this far into the dead zone without a cleric on hand.

Raik reignited the fire while we dropped our packs and put our weapons aside. Shingo sat down a little too hard, the giant boy yawning deeply.

Raik waited until we were all settled before speaking.

“First,” he said, voice steady, “I want to thank Vena.”

Vena looked up, startled.

“That fight would have gone very differently without you,” Raik continued. “You held the line when the rest of us were running out of options. Your miracles broke the ghosts’ momentum again and again.”

She lowered her gaze, fingers tightening around the edge of her cloak. “I just did what I could.”

“Today, you have proved you have what it takes to make it to paladin,” said Raik.

Vena couldn’t help but beam at that. For someone who claims to know little about the holy people’s customs, Raik manages to find the best words to cheer up Vena.

He turned next to Calr.

“And Calr. If you hadn’t noticed the thinkers, we wouldn’t have understood what we were dealing with until it was too late. That call gave us the opening we needed to make our escape.”

Calr scratched the back of his head, trying to hide how happy he was with the attention. “I just connected the dots Alice already put down.”

“That still mattered,” Raik said.

He exhaled slowly, shoulders dropping a fraction.

“I think we could have retreated even without the banshee’s intervention,” he admitted. “Barely. It would have depended on Vena’s miracles.”

“I still have a few heals left,” Vena said, “but more than that and I would have had to fast for three days again.”

“And you can’t use your miracles while fasting?” I asked.

“I can, but only a little,” Vena said.

Katar sat on a rock nearby, elbows on his knees, scowling up a storm. He let out a long sigh.

“I need a better way to deal with ghosts,” he said quietly. “I don’t like being dead weight.”

“You weren’t dead weight,” Kan said.

Katar shook his head. “I was close enough.”

I thought about it for a moment before speaking.

“I can teleport to Veraden and get you a proper silver blade,” I said. “It would be better than relying on bells.”

Katar looked up. “You’d do that?”

“I was already thinking of a supply run,” I added. “We still have daylight left to burn. I might as well use it.”

I looked at Raik for permission. He nodded.

Ja’a let out an exaggerated sigh and flopped onto her back in the grass.

“If only you could take me with you,” she said. “A real city sounds amazing right now.”

I winced slightly. “I can’t take you farther than Ectamel. And even that would be mana-intensive.”

She rolled her head to look at me. “So, not worth it?”

“Not for what it would cost me,” I said. “If I take you to Ectamel, I can’t go to Veraden or Hano.”

“Too bad,” she muttered, closing her eyes again.

“Can you get me some crossbow bolts?” Calr asked. “I didn’t get to retrieve the ones I used.”

“Get us some fresh meat,” Raik added. “Just for tonight’s dinner.”

“I wouldn’t say no to some cake,” added Ja’a.

Everyone else rolled their eyes at that, but the tension bled away because of it, so I was grateful.

The bath came next. I had Shingo carry out the tub once more. Carrying the water up the hill was more of a challenge, though heating it was not, thanks to Raik’s magic.

As I stripped with the girls behind a makeshift privacy screen, I noticed the faint discoloration along the edges of my silver bell. A dull gray film clung to the surface. I guessed prolonged exposure to ghosts was tarnishing the silver. I checked the silver attached to Kan’s chain, and it was in worse condition. I expected I would need to replace the silver or find a way to fix it.

I left my armor in a heap and hopped in the bath.

It was crowded and slightly too warm, but no one complained. Steam rose into the midday air as we washed sweat, dirt, and strands of hay stuck in our hair.

For a little while, I felt relaxed, almost forgetting that this was a dead zone.

By the time I was done, the sun had begun to descend from its zenith. I changed into travel clothes, simple and light, not bothering with armor this time. I should be safe in the city.

I nodded at the boys waiting for their turn to get in the bath.

“Try not to get killed while I’m gone,” I said.

Katar snorted. “And you, don’t get scammed. A silver blade is made of alloyed silver; they are not worth their weight in silver.”

“I know that,” I said, sticking my tongue out at him.

I focused, reaching for half the star mana stored in my spear, and wished to be in the Sunless Reach.

The camp vanished.

The familiar night sky of the Sunless Reach welcomed me once more.

I was low on mana. The fighting, combined with the teleport, had taken its toll, and I decided to recharge for a bit, letting star mana slowly convert into regular mana before I started jumping around again.

I pulled out my comfortable chaise and settled in, observing what passed for my camp here. Calling it a camp was stretching it. A fire ring waiting to be lit, a few ruined buildings lit up under the starlight. That, plus my collapsible chair, which I always carried with me, was the extent of it. It was sparse, but also starting to become familiar.

My gaze drifted to the looming tower nearby. I wondered if I could bring the team here someday, deal with that elemental once and for all. If Vena sanctified the tower properly, no new monster should be able to claim it again. I could even bring Nina and have her repair the ruined ancient telescope. With some effort, I could turn this place into a real observatory.

The idea lingered in my mind, warm and tempting.

I leaned back and looked up.

The night sky here had become more familiar to me than Earth’s sky. The Crown of Laurels pointed north toward the Kindred Rift Gate in High Rock, which made sense. It was supposed to point toward the High Temple of the Holy, and the easiest way to travel to the Myth Realm was through the Kindred. The Sleeping Fox rested to the east, a constellation I named because of some ear-shaped triangles. And the Hunter Twins watched from the west, two symmetrical lines complementing each other.

I scanned the constellations carefully, checking whether any of them had shifted in a meaningful way, hoping to spot another planet.

I had already located the two closest ones, or at least I thought I had. The rest remained elusive.

A few shooting stars later, my mana reserves were full again. So was my spear.

Still, it wasn’t enough.

If only I could find a way to hold more star mana, I wouldn’t be so reliant on coming back here after every couple of jumps. I wouldn’t have to retreat just to refuel. I should check with Ki’a and see whether my spear was ready to absorb more nightmare souls.

Another shooting star streaked across the sky.

I focused on it, drawing it in, holding the star mana as carefully as I could. I tried to keep it inside me without letting it convert, guiding it slowly, resisting the familiar pull toward regular mana.

If only I could delay the conversion even more.

Delay.

The thought hit me so hard I almost laughed.

I was an idiot.

Why had I not thought of that before?

I closed my eyes and focused on a wish.

“I wish to be sitting on top of the domed building in five minutes.”

The star mana drained instantly.

But I didn’t teleport.

My breath caught.

I opened my eyes, heart pounding, and pulled my phone out, starting a timer.

Five minutes later, the world lurched.

I vanished from my chaise, reappearing sitting atop the ruined structure, both legs dangling freely over the edge, stone cold beneath my hips.

I laughed, sharp and breathless.

I should have figured this out weeks ago. I had been using conditional wishes for a long time now. Why had I never considered adding time as a condition, instead of only a destination?

I sat there on the roof, grinning into the night, already feeling the shape of a dozen new possibilities forming in my mind.

I started testing immediately.

The Sunless Reach offered an almost infinite supply of shooting stars, and with the help of my spear, I could afford to experiment without restraint. I began stacking delayed wishes, trying to see how many I could hold at once.

I started small.

I wished to teleport a few meters forward in five minutes.

Then another wish for a few meters to the left in six minutes.

A few meters to the right in seven minutes.

And finally, a few meters backward in eight minutes.

Each wish consumed a small amount of star mana, with the distance being so short that I didn’t think the timing condition added much to the cost.

Five minutes passed, and nothing happened.

On the sixth minute tick, I jumped to the left.

A minute later, I shifted to the right.

Then another minute, and I jumped backward.

I never jumped forward.

I sat there, my mind racing, replaying the sequence in my head.

I doubted it was the direction that mattered here. It was more likely about capacity.

The fourth wish had overwritten the first.

No, pushing it out of the queue would be a more accurate wording.

An hour of careful testing later confirmed it. No matter how I structured my wishes, I could keep only three active wishes at any given time.

I wondered if there was a reason behind the number three. Was it a hard limit like it is with soulbooks, or would I be able to bank more wishes once my soul grew stronger? I guessed I would find out once that happened.

Realistically, one of those slots would always be needed for immediate wishes. That left room for only two delayed ones.

Only two… Humm..

It wasn’t many, but enough if I chose wisely.

The first answer came easily.

“I wish to teleport to relative safety nearby whenever I am in any real danger.”

I had learned that flexible wishes worked best when I was digging for treasure with my trusty shovel, so I didn’t bother adding too much detail. A panic button that didn’t require thought, timing, or precision.

The second was to solve my other problem.

Returning here to recharge.

If I could anchor a delayed return, I wouldn’t have to burn the star mana stored in my spear just to come back and recharge it. I could jump freely, spend star mana aggressively, and let the delay do the work for me.

“I wish to be back here in one hour.”

That should be enough time to buy a silver sword for Katar.

And if something went wrong before then, my spear was still full of star mana.

The next shooting star had me wishing to be somewhere inconspicuous in Veraden.

To my surprise, I was dropped in the middle of a narrow street.

It wasn’t a main road, but it shouldn’t have been deserted at this hour either. The sun was still high in the mid-afternoon, and yet this street was empty.

I straightened and took stock.

The city felt tense. Back on the main street, townspeople moved with purpose, heads down, eyes flicking around before crossing open ground. Most of those outside were men running errands; there were almost no children or women lingering in the street. Shop doors were open, but the sellers looked ready for trouble.

Then I noticed them.

Rough-looking men clustered in small groups along the road, leaning against walls, sitting on crates, blocking alley mouths. Their armor was mismatched, chainmail patched with leather, and dented breastplates worn over travel-stained gambesons. They were loud, crude, laughing too hard, and making a point of being seen.

The locals watched them with open suspicion.

Every time one of those men walked past, the locals would avoid them like the plague. Whatever was happening in Veraden, these men were at the center of it.

I made my way to a bladesmith’s shop, the sign marked with a sword and an anvil. Inside, the air smelled of oil, ash, and hot iron.

Two men were already arguing with the smith.

“This is trash,” one of them snarled, slamming a short sword onto the counter. “You call this a blade? This can barely hold an edge.”

The smith’s jaw tightened. “This blade is meant to kill ghosts, not cut wood.”

“Ghosts, you say?” the man scoffed. “What need do I have for garbage that can’t kill a man?”

“Then pick a steel sword!” shouted the smith, pointing at a rack with normal blades rather than silver.

“No, I like this one. It’s shiny,” said the thug. “But since it’s a child’s toy and not a real sword, how about I pay you half price?”

“You insult my craft and demand a discount?” the smith shot back.

The argument grew louder, sharp enough to carry into the street. I noticed the way the men positioned themselves, trying to block and isolate the merchant.

Too bad for them.

Their loud voices attracted the attention of a pair of Veraden city guards who appeared at the threshold.

The guards looked tired of this bullshit and somewhat eager for a fight as well. I guessed guards were often locals too; they must hate seeing strangers mucking up their home.

The men clicked their tongues and backed off immediately, muttering curses under their breath. They snatched up the sword and left a coin purse behind, shoulders hunched and eyes cold.

The shop felt warmer and more welcoming once they were gone.

“What’s the deal with those guys?” I asked.

The smith exhaled heavily. “Mercenaries.”

He spat the word like an insult.

“The Fearless March Company is passing through Veraden,” he continued.

“Mercenaries?” I echoed.

“Sell-swords,” he snapped. “Bastards, the lot of them. They have no loyalty to anything, worse than bandits half the time.” He raised his voice toward the door as if hoping they could still hear him. “Bandits at least know they’re criminals!”

If freelancers were treated like local celebrities, mercenaries were the opposite.

They didn’t belong to any city. They didn’t protect trade routes unless paid. They brought chaos wherever they went, drank towns dry, picked fights with the locals, and when wars ended or contracts dried up, they turned to banditry without blinking.

In a world where you could strike it rich fighting monsters, specializing in killing people was seen as the coward’s path.

I browsed quietly and found what I was looking for.

A gladius-style silver blade, close in shape and balance to Katar’s own. I managed to lower the price to six silver without too much haggling.

The smith’s attitude toward me was entirely different, cordial, and almost friendly. My freelancer badge helped. So was the fact that I clearly wasn’t with the mercenaries.

As I left the shop, I walked a few blocks, letting myself absorb the atmosphere.

I still had around twenty minutes before my teleport kicked in.

“Hey,” I heard a man call out.

I ignored him and kept walking.

“Hey, pretty,” he said louder.

I smelled him before I saw him: sweat, old leather, and unwashed armor. Days of marching and camping had not improved his hygiene.

When I turned, he was too close. Chainmail clinked as he leaned toward me, eyes roaming, grin lazy and unpleasant.

“Relax,” he said. “How about we spend some fun time together, you and I?”

I tried to ignore him and keep walking forward.

But he didn’t take no for an answer. His hand closed around my arm.

I reacted without thinking.

Lightning surged over my skin, sharp and controlled.

He screamed as the current ripped through him, muscles locking, teeth chattering. The shock was strong enough for him to jolt away, but not strong enough to stun him or fry anything important.

I turned to face him as he held his hand in pain.

That was when he noticed the freelancer badge pinned to my chest.

“You godless Hano freelancer,” he snarled, pain twisting his face. “You think you’re better than me?”

His friends stepped in, two of them, eager now, hands dropping to weapons, eyes bright with the promise of violence.

I summoned my bident spear into my hand.

Lightning crackled softly between its prongs, a low, hungry sound that made a few nearby heads turn.

I weighed my options.

Fight.

Or jump away.

Either way, I wasn’t in real danger.

The three of them rushed me.

I felt a flicker of curiosity more than fear. I guessed I would see how far I could go. I wouldn’t mind testing my delayed safety wish, just to know how tight its trigger really was.

The would-be charmer led the charge, face twisted with rage and embarrassment. He came in sloppy, overcommitted, like someone used to bullying people who froze instead of fighting back.

I struck first.

The butt of my spear snapped up into his face with a sharp crack. Bone met wood, and he staggered back, blood spraying from his nose as he screamed. I stepped backward immediately, just in time to avoid a spear thrust from one of his friends, the point scraping empty air where my ribs had been a heartbeat earlier.

I twisted with the motion and drove my spear forward, not aiming for flesh. The tips rang against chainmail, force traveling through the links and into the man beneath. He gasped as the impact knocked the wind out of him.

That was when I let the lightning loose.

The spear hummed, then screamed, and electricity surged through the metal in sharp, repeating jolts, tuning my weapon into a proper taser for once. The man convulsed, muscles locking as he collapsed, twitching and howling.

The third man tried to help his comrade, charging in with a raised sword and a shouted curse. His attack was slow, telegraphed, and desperate. I stepped inside his swing and kicked him square in the torso.

He flew backward, breath exploding out of him, sword clattering uselessly to the stone as he hit the ground.

I reset my stance, spear held low and ready, expecting the first man to recover and come at me again.

He didn’t.

He was still groaning on the ground, both hands clutched to his nose, rocking slightly as blood seeped between his fingers.

I blinked.

That was… kind of pathetic.

Was I too strong? Or were they just that weak?

According to Ja’a, a normal, unevolved, unascended person peaked at around seven SB of soul strength. I was sitting at roughly double that. Maybe that explained it.

No.

This wasn’t a game with neat levels and clean numbers. Katar’s soul strength was barely five SB, and he could still wipe the floor with me if he wanted to. Soul strength alone didn’t decide a fight.

This was about experience.

I had fought monsters that wanted to tear me apart. Trolls that shrugged off blows that would pulp a man. Undead that didn’t feel pain or fear.

I had trained with Edmund, Garo, Yon, Nakera, Kan, Katar, and Raik. People who corrected every mistake the moment I made it, who punished sloppy footing and lazy timing without mercy.

I kept thinking of myself as weak because I was surrounded by strong people.

But compared to men who had only ever fought bandits, drunks, and conscripts, I wasn’t weak at all.

I was simply used to a higher standard of danger.

Which also meant my delayed safety wish remained completely untested.

Oh well. There was nothing to do about that now.

Lingering any longer would only invite more trouble, and I had already made enough of an impression for one afternoon. I lowered my spear, let the lightning bleed away, and turned down the first side street I saw, putting distance between myself and the three groaning men on the ground.

I still had a few minutes left before my return wish triggered.

I tried to make the best of them while lying low.

A trinket shop caught my eye, its windows cluttered with silver charms, cheap tokens, and polished bells hanging from strings. Inside, the air smelled faintly of polish.

I bought a few more silver bells. Katar would appreciate having spares. I purchased a few silver weights to add to Kan’s chains.

I also added a silver jewelry cleaning kit to the pile to fix the tarnish. A small tin of alchemical paste that looked like it had some kind of ash as its main ingredient, a stiff-bristled brush made from plant fiber rather than metal, and a thin polishing cloth woven from silk.

Finally, I added a simple silver necklace and a pair of bracelets, nothing ornate, just solid bands that could be worn under armor without getting in the way. Silver is silver, and you can’t have too much of it when fighting undead.

I finished paying, tucked everything into my bag of holding, and stepped back out into the street.

A few mercenaries were lingering farther down the road, leaning against a wall, watching me with narrowed eyes.

Fortunately, time was up, so it didn’t take long before the familiar pull tightened in my chest. The street blurred, and the noise of Veraden faded out, as I found myself back beneath the endless night sky of the Sunless Reach.

I leaned back on my chaise, letting the starlight wash over me.

There was still time left.

Maybe I should hop to Hano next and see how everyone is doing.
