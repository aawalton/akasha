As drops of beer spilled across the tavern floor, a man in the center reveled in the laughter of his companions. He had a thick beard and several scars on his right cheek. Like almost everyone else there, he wore heavy clothes and daggers at his waist. Medals adorned his chest, various sizes and colors explaining his conquests over the years.

Celebrating, the guards were numerous and represented the majority of the tavern's patrons; after all, who would go to a place like that on a Wednesday? In any case, despite the bartender being swamped with work, even more so with the cleanup afterward, the owner was jubilant. He always received a battalion of the Seventh Guard when they captured some famous thief or murderer.

After a few failed attempts to pick up some of the women in the bar, he contented himself with telling tasteless jokes with his companions.

In the corner, however, a hooded figure stood up and approached the commander. He wore leather goggles and a mask covering his mouth and chin. He was dressed entirely in black, almost as if he wanted to blend in with the others. He held back his impactful shoulder strikes to avoid drawing attention, and instead of surfing through the crowd, he glided like a worm, imperceptible.

He was of average height for a man, but anyone who had fought him could understand his fury in combat. As if he had an exoskeleton on his fists, each punch was the result of countless impacts at once.

"...And I grabbed him by the collar, you know?" the bearded man said between sobs, his cheeks red. "I threw him against the wall, until the wall broke on impact. He used one of his doors, but I, alone, grabbed one of them and broke it over his head... He only managed to escape because, well, he's a coward... That's the specialty of cowards, isn't it...?"

"Sounds like the Locksmith to me." The hooded figure said, his voice had a strange, almost ethereal quality.

The man spat his beer on the floor when he noticed the mysterious presence of the figure beside him. He straightened his back and returned the mugs to the counter. It wasn't respect; it was fear.

"...G-General!" He wiped his mouth and said with praise, almost as if the man's presence had sobered him up. "Sir!"

Saluting the general, the commander kept staring straight, not daring to look him in the eye. The hooded figure then looked at the guards nearby, who then scattered like crabs searching for any hint of food. Well, in this case, another mug of alcohol or a single woman. In fact, she didn't even need to be single; cadets were desperate.

"Rest," he said simply. Then he grabbed a beer from the counter. "I just want to know what happened. I tried to talk to Bertrus, but he refused to say a word about what occurred. I figured it would be easier to talk to you. Am I right?"

"W-Well..." The commander gave an embarrassed smile while scratching his forehead. "You know how they are with information leaking so easily, don't you? I was only there when the damned thing was pulled. I don't know how the ritual was done—"

"Oh, stop being silly," said the general, his tone lighter. "We've known each other for a long time, haven't we? You know how important this matter is to me. Besides, between you and me, they only leak false information to the troublemakers."

"I'm sorry, sir," the almost drunk replied, also with an embarrassed smile. "You know how much I respect you, though—"

"How's little Gwen?" the general said, his eyes invisible because of the leather glasses. "I imagine you're saving money to invest in the girl, right? Although I understand, this beer is very good. At least it was, in my day..."

"...What do you mean, General?" the commander said, frowning. "I beg you not to involve my daughter in this."

The general glanced at the bartender and saw that he was far away. Surrounded by people, he realized that if he acted quickly, he could go unnoticed. Tired of playing with the beer, even without having taken a sip, he crushed it with one grip, the shards sliding off the strong material of his glove. He approached the commander.

"Understand, Vaz, that you already involved her the moment you stepped inside the barracks," he said, his voice rough. "Your titles and badges are worthless if you wake up tomorrow with a dagger in your stomach. Your daughter keeps asking for her daddy every afternoon. Your aunt sees Gwen more than you do, I bet. A title like Arch Commander, however, gives you many more opportunities. All of that can change with a simple signature. All of that can change when you receive an Emblem."

Vaz maintained his same expression, seemingly unfazed. Inwardly, he was terrified. What had once been a conversation between two officers was now a gamble about the commander's future. What could possibly happen to little Gwen's future? Vaz didn't want to know.

"After this capture of Locksmith," he said, raising his head to maintain the same level, "I'll certainly have my chances in the next selection. I don't need your help."

"Help?" he replied, turning his head slightly. "Oh no, don't think I could help you with this. I simply know about your escapades during service and your connections with the Marshal. Nothing more."

This time, however, the commander fell silent. He looked at the shards of glass on the floor when he caught a glimpse of the hooded general's eyes. He saw nothing, almost as if the man had lost his soul.

"20 minutes. My office." He said as he turned to leave. "Make the right decision, Vaz." And he left.

He didn't like manipulating others this way, but when it came to family, trivial matters like this couldn't matter less. In situations where respect wouldn't cut it, fear would.

Beneath the cold desk, even colder fingers traced the high-quality wood of the general's office. From above, he rested his gaze on the city, his thoughts the complete opposite of those of the people in the streets. It was festival day. He thought it all nonsense, but he knew it was tradition. In the hands of the nobles, the same gifts were passed between couples and families; when you have everything, it's the gesture that matters.

Near the window, dozens of books occupied the shelves. From "The Art of Warfare" to "The Eighth War and its Strategies." He had read them all more than once. One doesn't rise in the military hierarchy so young without theoretical knowledge and expertise to put theories into practice. In his case, nepotism certainly helped.

He scanned the clouds that held rain. For a moment, a smile crossed his face as he thought about the ruined day of the young princes.

"S-Sir?" Vaz then broke his train of thought. "Is that all?"

As if he had all the time in the world, he rose from his chair, leaving behind all the documents he had to sign, and turned to his left. Opposite the window, a punching bag hung not by ropes, but by chains. Inside was a mixture of sand and crushed brick, the radius exceeding the average size of a common punching bag; it had been custom-made.

He placed his hand against the bag, almost as if apologizing for what he was about to do. In the blink of an eye, he jumped slightly back and jabbed the bag. His glove was then covered in sand that had fallen from inside the punching bag; his punch had broken the object. It vomited a reddish-yellow material while the general merely wiped his sand-filled glove.

The chains trembled all over, almost as if the impact of his punch had startled the metal. Vaz, without saying anything, kept his eyes wide, now copying the movement of the chains. He turned quickly and ran away.

The general turned to the table and began signing some documents as if nothing had happened. His handwriting was elegant and small, contrary to his nature. He held his pen with a delicacy he would still have to demonstrate in battle. Was the stunt necessary? No. He didn't feel good knowing they would have to replace the bag, but he wanted to send Vaz running in an effective way, and this did the trick well.

He quickly tired of his activities and turned again to observe the city, now rising and approaching the window, an eagle in its natural habitat. He then heard a door opening and didn't turn around, already suspecting who would come out.

"I imagine the reason for Vaz's panic was you..." A tired voice came from the direction of the door. "You know that—"

The woman then saw the pile of sand and the punching bag cut in half, swaying, its purpose ruined.

"What the hell!?" She exclaimed, looking outside and then closing the door. "Again? Seriously? Do you know how long it takes to make a bag like this? And I'm not just talking about production, but the whole process of gathering resources. I'm the one who has to handle the logistics, you know? I can already see I'm going to have to ask them to just put bricks in it and coat it with steel..."

"They imprisoned Harry in the Logos Tower," the general said.

"Oh, you heard?" She looked at the bag and then at the man. "Well, at least that makes sense. That's no reason to destroy anything you want like a child throwing a tantrum; we have rules here, Matthews."

The general then removed his hood and turned to the woman. Skinless, hairless; his face was reduced to a skull. His eyes remained in their eye sockets, buried and without any chance of escape. The rest of his face, however, was gone. Only the skeleton of a man remained.

Even after Matthews had undergone the transformation and isolated himself from the rest, and even after Eleanor had seen him dozens of times, she still couldn't get used to it. This, however, required a moment of sincerity. Since the day he received his second Emblem, the Bone Emblem, the man had hidden himself from the world. She felt sorry for Matthews, but she also knew that he had chosen this for himself; she knew the consequences of using a second Emblem as much as he did.

"Who made this decision?" Matthews asked, his voice indignant. "Who the hell thought the Locksmith was Logos material? He's such a fucking, argh...!"

He stomped his foot and turned back to the window, now pressing his arms and skeletal face against the glass.

"You may not like him," Eleanor replied, approaching him. "But you have to admit he was dangerous. He stole several documents from the King of Capul and the nobles of the capital. Remember all the hell we went through after that robbery? Weeks and weeks of tripled surveillance. Tripled, Matthews. He and that other unfortunate woman had to go. Just so you know, the order came from above. Don't put that on me."

"He's a brat, you know?" He said to himself, agitated. "From a young age he was a damn pest. He was supposed to take care of me, but no, of course not. Let's leave that responsibility to others, to..." He paused for a moment to compose himself, then continued. "It doesn't make sense for him to be older than me and for me to be the mature one. What kind of dynamic is that? Besides, he won't last there. He's always loved the spotlight. They say that's worse than death, you know? They even threw Dracno into that mess. Can you imagine him against that man? As if the Drumond Incident wasn't enough — feeding innocent man to the Tower — now they're just..."

"Calm down, Matt." She squeezed his shoulder as they both watched the city. Rain began to fall.

"And they don't even care that the moon is falling! Every decade that passes we're hours closer to death. Don't they know that the necklaces they use are made from an incoming apocalypse? Or they just don't care? I..."

She had dealt with this many times before; Eleanor had to let him compose himself alone. When Matthews decided to tell her that the Locksmith was his brother, she pondered for a long time whether or not he should reveal this information to his superiors. She thought it was just the general's nonsense and that he had other plans with the thief. This changed with each demonstration of love for Harry. Sometimes he removed guards from certain places when he thought the thief might attack. Or he left some routes that nobles used to travel unprotected. He worried about his brother, and Eleanor knew this love very well.

He leaned more tightly against the glass as he poured out his feelings. The dark-haired woman hugged the skeleton from the side as he remained silent. He can't even cry... She looked at the drops running down the glass and thought about how much had been stolen from him by a simple decision. A simple Symbol. That was one of the many reasons why she didn't use an Emblem; she was afraid of losing herself.

Matthews then burst into laughter out of nowhere. His laughter turned into guffaws as he turned and hugged Eleanor, who was visibly surprised.

"Wow..." he said while wiping invisible tears from his cheekbone. "This has given me a really funny idea."
