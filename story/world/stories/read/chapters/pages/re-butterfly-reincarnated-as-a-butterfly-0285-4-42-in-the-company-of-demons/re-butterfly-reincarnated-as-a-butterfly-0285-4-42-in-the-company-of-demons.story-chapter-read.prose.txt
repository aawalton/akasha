“You will not invite us into your settlement, then?” asked one of the demons who was not among the leaders.

Adon could feel the disapproval and outright annoyance of the more central figures, but the three in charge managed to maintain a stoic front. The butterfly sensed that the peripheral demon who had spoken was part of a separate chain of command. The General he reported to was not present here, which was why he did not remain silent in deference to the others.

Which ones? Rosslyn asked in her head.

The one who spoke and the second from the left, he sent.

The Queen pointed at the speaker and the second demon from the left in turn.

“Those two are scouts for the Empire, not diplomats,” she said in a voice that brooked no dissent. “You would ask me to bring your intelligence assets into my fortification? Be serious or begone.”

The lead demon standing in the center winced. It was a momentary change of expressions, but Adon caught it, along with the train of thought that went with it.

I should never have let the Justiciar insert his people into this expedition, he thought, annoyed. We are already beginning in a position of weakness, and the fool adds to the deficit with leaks.

Everyone on the demon side assumed that there must be some leak, rather than the simple truth that the humans had a telepath with them.

The other leaders were equally alarmed to the central demon—they began to wonder if there were spies among their number or perhaps back at the capital, somewhere highly placed—while the scouts felt like they might be killed, either by the humans or by their own superiors, and the demons who were neither scouts nor leaders gave off a feeling more akin to perplexity. These extras were in attendance mostly to project power. They seemed not to have realized that there were scouts among them.

That’s how the demons maintain secrecy, Adon thought to himself. Every piece of information is shared on a need to know basis, more so than in the Kingdom.

He conveyed everything he had observed to Rosslyn and King Janus. The butterfly and the Queen had decided to let the Parmonian ruler in on some of what Adon could do. It was likely necessary for them to continue cooperating smoothly, and it was certainly going to be helpful here.

“We will follow your lead, then,” said the lead demon after a moment. He donned a forced smile.

Rosslyn silently began to walk along the path the demons had taken up to the fortification, then quickly took a turn and moved toward the prearranged meeting place.

Adon found it fascinating that the demons neither confirmed nor denied that they had brought scouts along with them, as if it was a normal thing to be expected or perhaps simply nothing to be embarrassed at being caught doing. Or maybe it didn’t count as being caught if one didn’t admit to it. The butterfly could not easily figure out what the rationale was, because the demons didn’t dwell on it at all. As soon as the humans began leading them away, their minds were all on what came next. They didn’t even seem to worry about the possibility that the deception would change the negotiations to take place.

Perhaps they were right to become so blase so quickly. Rosslyn didn’t care much about the scouts either, and she hadn’t been surprised. That was why she was able to ask Adon in such quick fashion to identify them.

“To start us off on a better footing, I am called Duke Sebas,” said the lead demon after they had been walking for a minute.

“And I am called Duke Kastinov,” said another of the three leaders. “This is Count Fomoral.” He pointed to the third demon.

The rest of the demons, the three leaders said without saying, were beneath notice.

“You already know I am Queen Rosslyn of Claustria,” Rosslyn replied in a neutral tone.

“I am King Janus of Parmonia.”

The High Priestess remained silent and looked at the demons through slitted eyes, wearing the most unfriendly expression Adon had ever seen on her otherwise angelic features. The butterfly sensed that she deeply hated and distrusted them. She was here to represent the will of the Goddess, and she had heard of how they had desecrated Goddess statues and places of worship where they had conquered.

For Adon, this just represented an excuse for him to remain silent also. After all, the High Priestess was slightly closer to the demons than he was. He certainly wouldn’t skip over her introduction to put himself forward.

“We also have our religious counsel with us,” Rosslyn said before the silence could drag on for long enough to become truly awkward. The Queen had correctly guessed that the High Priestess, though she had agreed to attend this meeting, did not intend to act as if she and the demons were on speaking terms.

“Indeed,” Duke Sebas replied with a thin smile.

They continued the rest of the short walk in silence. A few minutes later, the two groups arrived at the clearing in the woods that had been prepared for them. Servants ringed the gathering, holding wine and other supplies of various kinds, and a long table with dozens of seats was set up.

The demons at least had the good manners—or at least the knowledge of basic formal etiquette—to let the King, Queen, High Priestess, and Adon be seated first. Then the Dukes took their seats, and after that, everyone else followed without apparently worrying overly much about precedence.

“So, what do you offer?” Rosslyn asked. “I am prepared to accept the unconditional surrender of all demon forces remaining in Claustria, or if you prefer to get into details, we can discuss war reparations and hostage exchange terms.”

The words were calculated to needle the demon representatives as well as to enforce the idea that the human forces were superior, and they had both intended effects. Adon sensed as the demons quietly seethed but refrained from openly complaining about the condescending opening words.

“War reparations are a topic for defeated powers, Your Highness,” said Duke Kastinov, trying to keep his words light. “The Empire has steadily advanced over the last several centuries. We have never truly been defeated.”

“Unless you count your aggressive advances being turned away as a defeat,” King Janus replied, smirking.

“It is splendid that you can consider keeping your own seat as a victory, King Janus,” said Duke Sebas. “You have kept your expectations appropriately sized to your capabilities, though I do not know if your ally shares your view.”

“I would certainly consider driving you out of the territory you occupied unprovoked as a victory,” Rosslyn said.

“That would be a path with a great deal of blood, Your Highness,” said Duke Kastinov. “Mainly human blood.”

“But we would win. And you would die to a man,” the Queen replied. “The alternative is that you could surrender.”

“If you have studied your history, then surely you understand that the Empire never surrenders,” said Duke Sebas. “The man who proposes a surrender in the Empire is signing his own death warrant. We fight. We are a people born and bred on battle.”

“That is your national mythology,” Rosslyn said, shrugging. “And I will concede it is your usual history. You either conquer and massacre, or you get slaughtered to a man trying.”

The demons around the table exchanged glances, while the leaders nodded in agreement. There was a vigorous feeling of cultural clash as Adon sensed that they were uncertain whether to be insulted. Within their culture, following orders from superiors that led to one’s own death was far more honorable than surviving through surrender.

The butterfly continued conveying everything he observed to Rosslyn and Janus as quickly as he could.

“So, why come here to negotiate for peace at all?” asked the Queen, shrugging again. “I was not expecting you. No one was waiting for you. We were prepared to go and storm the capital just before you arrived. Surely you realize that this delegation is, as you must have feared, a confession of the weakness of your position. If you leave without a successfully negotiated armistice, whatever forces your Empire retains in my land will be destroyed. Perhaps some hostages will be captured to be exchanged for humans that the Empire retains in its custody. But you could hardly consider that preferable to surrender and peaceful withdrawal, even if surrender is undesirable in your culture.”

I know you are weak, you know you are weak, and now you know that I know you are weak, was Rosslyn’s thought process. The message was received by the demons and even the people around the table who were there mainly as observers. Some of the servants, unschooled in military affairs, understood exactly what was happening.

“We do not accept defeat as inevitable,” said Count Fomoral, speaking for the first time.

The other two demon leaders both looked disgruntled, and Adon could feel the reason why. Count Fomoral’s phrasing was defiant, but it implicitly accepted Rosslyn’s framing: that the Empire was the underdog.

He was too green for this mission, thought Duke Kastinov.

What an idiot, thought Duke Sebas.

“The alternative to a negotiated peace by us is that your numbers will continue to dwindle and your land suffer under the weight of yet another fruitless conflict,” said Duke Kastinov, trying to salvage the conversation a bit.

Outraged expressions spread across the faces of the handful of officials Rosslyn and Janus had brought with them, and Adon could feel the same emotions running through their minds. The reactions were very real.

“A conflict that you started,” Rosslyn said. “We are within our rights to destroy every invader, and perhaps the fruit of our conflict will be territorial recovery for the Holy Kingdoms. What sort of a legacy would it be if this Emperor actually lost territory captured by his predecessors?”

“Are the Parmonians truly prepared to accept the consequences of such reckless actions by the Claustrians?” asked Duke Sebas smugly. “Starting an aggressive war rather than keeping to defense? I think you over—”

“Their allies are prepared to back them to the hilt and end the threat of the Demon Empire to both our lands once and for all,” King Janus said firmly, interrupting.

The demon envoys looked back and forth at each other, their faces clouded but expressions ambiguous.

But their mental states carried no such ambiguity.

They’re very concerned about that, Adon sent to Rosslyn and Janus. A major part of their strategy in coming here was dividing the two of you. They assumed that Rosslyn would be far more invested in recovering her people’s land than her foreign ally, and Janus would leap at the chance to ‘win’ without shedding any blood.

I am more invested than him, Rosslyn thought quietly. But that does not mean he has nothing to lose by agreeing to an unfavorable peace. You and I simply have to ensure that the negotiation either gives us back the entire country at minimal cost, or that King Janus does not try to bind us to something we do not want.

Foolish of them to think we could be set at odds so easily, Janus thought. That may have worked on generations past, but we represent a new era of diplomacy. And it would be an unprecedented victory if we could actually recover land from the Empire.

Since he had learned about Adon’s ability to read minds—which the butterfly and the Queen had framed as scraping surface thoughts—King Janus had been very careful to manage which ideas he allowed to reach the surface of his mind. He had the same sort of training against potential telepathic attack that Rosslyn had received long before she ever met Adon.

It didn’t matter, of course.

The butterfly could easily see deeper into the Parmonian King’s mind than Janus was aware of. He perceived the depth of his ambition—his greed for land and reputation, his enthusiasm at the possibility of turning this war of defense into a war of conquest. The ruler’s heart leaped when Rosslyn brought up the idea of recovering land.

The Ursabian royal line was extinct, after all. If those lands were recovered, they would have to be resettled with new noble houses. Why couldn’t some of Janus’s own family receive some of those holdings? There would be many plum positions to go around, and the land would naturally be split between the two allies, as it bordered both of them.

Since none of that caused his motives to be at odds with Rosslyn’s for the moment, Adon simply noted it for future reference. He was conveying far more pressing information to Rosslyn with every little change in the flow of the conversation.

And one of the demon leaders was ready, having been adequately pushed, to reveal the demons’ real hand.

“You clearly think us helpless,” said Count Fomoral. “Otherwise you would not speak so arrogantly, human. But we have the population of your entire capital city captive. If you besiege the city walls, we will not defend them. We will simply launch the population out to meet you by catapult, fifty at a time. Children ride first.”

The emotional reaction was immediate. On Count Fomoral’s own side, there was a feeling of dread. The two Dukes were displeased that he had spoken so rashly, with relatively little provocation. It was a threat they had always come prepared to make, but they had not intended to issue it at this juncture. This was intended as an ultimatum, a way of breaking a logjam, not part of the trash talk that frequently opened such meetings.

Since the other two had always intended to make that threat, however, neither of them could repudiate it. They sat silently.

On the human side, the words were met with disgust. Adon could feel the shock in most of the human embassy members.

The Demon Empire has never, in its entire history, shown such weakness as this, King Janus thought. Every rumor we have heard of their current difficulties must be entirely true.

The Queen rose to her feet, her lips pressed tightly together in an expression of barely contained rage.

“I require a break from you weak, pathetic cretins,” Rosslyn said icily. “I can only tolerate the stench of your cowardice for so long, and Count Fomoral has just exceeded my limits. If I stay, I may choke on my own desire to kill you people. Enjoy some of our famous hospitality while you await my return. We have wine and bread, which you may consume safely, since Claustria does not have a tradition of poisoning enemies we are too weak to fight fairly.”
