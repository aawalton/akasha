As the sun rose slowly in the East, Minghao exited his tent and, without saying anything, vanished into the forest around them.

Jianyi was tired and exhausted from the revelations of the night, but in good spirits. Even the lack of food did not dampen his mood.

A few minutes later, Minghao returned. “We will continue with presence this morning—the speed with which you learn will also influence the amount and quality of food you will get to eat over the next month, so our incentives should be aligned,” the retainer said with a wink.

Jianyi got up, ready to leave, but Minghao pointed to their campfire. “Leaving an open fire in the wilderness might be the right decision, rarely, though. It should always be a conscious decision, however, not the consequence of inattentiveness.”

He grimaced, embarrassed. Certainly, he didn’t want to be the cause of a forest fire, so he made to douse the flames with his water skin, but Minghao stopped him again. “If you do that, the steam will be quite visible. Again, it can be the right decision, but you need to be aware. In general, it would be best if you stop feeding a fire well before sunrise so that you don’t face the issue. My advice is that, given that there are stones around it and the fire is quite small, it should be fine just letting it burn out.”

“Thank you for teaching me, Minghao. I will try to do better.”

“No problem, you can’t know these things, so it is fine. Final point on fire in the wilderness: in areas with higher qi density than here, a mundane fire will usually not be able to spread into the environment, but, in turn, that also means that it is almost impossible to start a campfire with the mortal means I showed you yesterday.”

Jianyi nodded and tried to commit everything his friend told him to memory.

Minghao guided him a few hundred paces deeper into the forest and stopped once they had reached an area of dense underbrush. “Tell me what you see.”

He scratched his head, trying not to state the obvious, which was a fern.

Kneeling, he tried to see whether there were any tracks on the ground. There was something, but what it was he couldn’t tell. He took a closer look at the plants with their slightly brown fronds, likely a result of the cold.

There.

Two fronds had broken and, even without being an expert, he could tell that the break looked fresh.

Jianyi pointed at his find, and Minghao nodded with a small smile; his raised eyebrow raised the question of a ‘so what’?

Getting up and looking left and right, he found a frond that looked a bit flattened.

He followed the path.

A few paces further, he came across a couple of ferns that had clear gaps in them—fronds had been nibbled on.

He was so focused on his search that he twitched when Minghao put a hand on his shoulder and indicated for him to be quiet with a finger over his mouth.

Jianyi nodded and followed the outstretched finger of the older cultivator, pointing in the direction they had walked.

There, in a space between a couple of larger trees, too small to be called a clearing, stood a deer. A small one. Its head, covered in gray-brown fur, barely reached Jianyi’s shoulder, and it was currently eating a few frost-covered berries from a large bush.

He held his breath.

It looked so innocent—and he wondered what kind of lethal technique was hidden behind that almost cute façade.

Without hesitation, he pulled on his spirit to draw two cards. Satisfied, he saw that Bolt of Fire was among them.

Bolt of Fire

Technique

Common

1 x Fire

Shoots bolt of fire at single target

He drew on his lower dantian, passed a thread of qi through his Core Card, and a stream of red heat and flame burst into existence before him, screaming at the beast.

From the corner of his eyes, he saw Minghao trying to say something, but he had already pulled his spear, leaping forward to fight the creature. His teacher had told him that he was responsible for his food, so he would get it.

Quickly, he crossed the paces toward the creature, losing sight of it for a moment as he crossed behind a tree, but ready to deal with anything.

When he saw the deer again, it was dead, a fist-sized hole burned through its long neck.

Jianyi halted his charge and frowned. That had been easier than expected.

He shrugged and pulled his spirit needle from his storage ring, immediately reaching out to see whether he could harvest a card from it—it would be just too perfect if he could solve his food needs and the cost for the formation that Minghao would sell him in one fell swoop.

Unfortunately, he was unable to connect with anything and put the spirit needle back with a small sigh.

Noticing that Minghao hadn’t caught up with him, he turned and saw his teacher still standing where he had left him, with a slightly open mouth and a raised finger.

Shaking himself, the older man came up to him. “That was an interesting approach to hunting…” he said, face impassive.

“What do you mean? Was it incorrect to start the attack on the beast with a fire bolt?” he asked curiously. This went beyond the hunt; he needed to understand if he did something tactically wrong.

“The beast?” Minghao chuckled. “This, my friend, was a Roe deer. As common—and mortal—an animal as they come. If it had been a spirit beast, it would have been the right choice… Against a mortal creature, it was a bit of a waste of resources and a waste of an opportunity to train your presence, or stealth, as the Silent Fox would call it.”

Jianyi felt the heat rise to his face and cleared his throat. “Oh. How… How could I have known that this wasn’t a spirit beast?”

Minghao nodded, seemingly willing to move on. “Good question. There are several methods to differentiate spirit beast from mortal creature. There are techniques based on your soul, which I am sure Elder Sun will teach you later; second, you should be able to sense qi intensity; it is something that we can practice as well over the next few weeks. Lastly, you can just use your eyes. Was the creature especially large? Did it have sharp senses that would have noticed you? Did it have any indications of affinity, such as colorations or growths? Your eyes are definitely not the most reliable indicator, but they are a good start…”

Jianyi sighed. He felt like he hadn’t performed very well so far.

Minghao clasped his shoulder. “Don’t worry about it; at least you have food, and we can learn about field dressing and butchering an animal.”

He nodded. There was always something new to learn, after all.

“Good. First step, bleed the animal dry of blood. Make a cut on the throat and position the carcass so that the blood can run out.”

Jianyi wasn’t squeamish—he had killed humans after all—but seeing the blood flood out of the creature that had just a few minutes ago been eating berries made his gut clench for a moment. He took a deep breath to calm himself and inhaled the coppery scent of blood. Quickly, he took a few steps back, turned around, and controlled himself and his stomach.

Vomiting over a deer was unacceptable.

After a few moments, he was fine and returned to Minghao and the animal. The blood was steaming on the cold forest floor, but he didn’t let it affect him any further.

“Let’s wait for a few minutes until we move to the next steps.”

***

They went through how to make the first cut, take out the organs, remove potentially valuable parts like claws or teeth, skin the animal, and then, finally, butcher the remaining meat.

It was not something that Jianyi necessarily enjoyed—buying a few pieces of meat on a stick with a few vegetables and nice spices seemed superior to him—but it was also nothing he would shy away from.

And it wasn’t like there was a food stand in the middle of the wilderness.

When all was said and done, he was happy to have a few strips of deer roasted over the rekindled campfire for a late breakfast. The spices he had bought for one of their trips, based on Minghao’s advice—helped make it a quite enjoyable meal.

He even shared with Minghao, who chuckled at the gesture.

After the meal, Jianyi spent a bit of time cultivating, refilling his lower dantian after he had used qi for the Bolt of Fire, and processing more of his body cultivation art.

After the sun had reached its zenith, Jianyi ranged out from their camp, trying to find traces of other animals and beasts; without them, he wouldn’t be able to make any progress on the body cultivation side.

Hours later, he made it back to the camp, where Minghao was sitting, seemingly enjoying the few rays of sunshine that made it through the trees, and reading a scroll.

“You must be tired,” the retainer stated, looking up from his reading.

Jianyi nodded. “I am,” he said as he sat down with a small sigh.

“How were you planning to get through the night?” Minghao asked curiously.

Thinking, Jianyi looked into the forest around them. “I would try to sleep. I haven’t seen anything that I would consider dangerous—not much of anything, really. So it should be safe, I hope.”

Minghao chuckled. “That you haven’t seen anything might have more to do with the noise you make, but I agree. The immediate area around us feels like it is dominated by mortal creatures, and those are unlikely to attack a cultivator unless cornered.”

Relief flooded Jianyi’s bloodstream. He was exhausted, and if he had to stay up another night, he wasn’t sure how effective he could be the following day.

“Tomorrow, we can work on your presence some more. It should help on your hunting trips.”

***

Four days passed, and except for a couple of hares, Jianyi did not see a lot of wildlife. He did, however, make quite a bit of progress on reading tracks and being a bit more conscious of his own movements in the forest.

He was very aware that he would likely not become a master hunter and survivalist over the course of the month, but every bit of progress was welcome.

The evenings when he would sit with Minghao around the campfire were also a treat. The older man told more stories about his time hunting with his father and even an anecdote from the frontlines, though it was clear that this was not his favorite topic.

As planned, the mornings were spent under Minghao’s guidance, and afterwards Jianyi would follow his own rhythm, which included a lot of cultivating—it was so rewarding to be able to make progress again—and then applying the learnings of the mornings while roaming the wilderness on his own.

It was on the fifth day, just past noon, that Jianyi noticed a few prints on the forest floor that seemed fresh to him and were larger than anything else he had seen so far.

Very careful not to make any noise, he decided to follow them deeper into the forest.
