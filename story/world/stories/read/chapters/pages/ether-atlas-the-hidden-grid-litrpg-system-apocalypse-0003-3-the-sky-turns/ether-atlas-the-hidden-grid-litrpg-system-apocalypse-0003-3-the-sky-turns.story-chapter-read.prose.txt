CHAPTER 3

"Never reveal the Grid to a population not yet in crisis. That knowledge without the context produces madness. The context without the knowledge produces despair. Both must arrive together."

- Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

The groans of the floorboards settled for a second and then everything happened at once.

Vibration surged through the floor with enough force to send Donna's dice skittering across the wood. Through the bay window beside the table, they could see the grey London sky changing. A violet wash crept across the clouds, bleeding through the overcast sky like ink dropped into water.

Arthur's phone went black on the table in front of him and so did Donna's and Steve's. Around the room, phone screens died in a ripple of small frustrated reactions. A tap, a slap, a muttered curse. Someone at the bar said something about a storm coming, and then the wall lights guttered and went out.

The pub dropped into a murky half-dark, lit only by the ultramarine glow bleeding through the windows from outside.

Arthur kept his palms flat on the table, tracking the tremor. The frequency was too low and too uniform for seismic activity, too deep for a ruptured gas main, and far too rhythmic for any nearby construction. His brain had no category for it.

Then came the heat and caught him off guard.

It started at the base of his skull and poured down his spine, branching throughout his body. His muscles locked and his fingers clenched against the table’s edge hard enough to whiten his knuckles. For what was probably fifteen seconds but felt like an eternity, he couldn't breathe. Every muscle in his body had seized at once, recalibrating his biology into something new.

In his line of work, broken bones, slips, and heavy bruises were standard. Arthur knew pain but this had no physical location; it was all over, all at once. It was the terrifying sensation of being scanned from the inside out, a cold current running down every nerve path and taking a detailed and highly uncomfortable inventory of his cellular structure.

Steve let out a sharp, involuntary gasp beside him and Donna sucked in a ragged breath through her teeth. Both of them were staring down at their arms.

Arthur dropped his gaze and sat transfixed by what he saw.

Blue light was moving beneath his skin.

It wasn't a surface glow; his skin hadn’t taken on a new colour. Instead a faint lattice of fine geometric lines was tracing the pathways of his nerves, branching along his forearms and across the backs of his hands like a circuit diagram being etched in real time. The lines pulsed once, bright and razor-sharp, before fading back below the dermis, still faintly visible, like veins that had learned to conduct light.

Donna had pushed her sleeve up to the elbow. She studied the lattice on her own forearm and her other hand drifted towards the multitool inside her jacket before she caught up to the fact that the threat was under her own skin and there was nothing there to cut out.

“You seeing this?” Donna said in the flat, operational voice Arthur knew as her crisis mode.

Steve was staring at his own hands, turning them over slowly, his mouth slightly open. For once in his life, he wasn't speaking or putting forward a theory.

"What in the bloody hell is this?" Arthur said.

***

The man at the end of the bar fell first.

There was no cry or stagger or clutching of the chest that one associates with a sudden death. He simply dropped, sideways off his stool, his shoulder hitting the brass foot rail with a sound Arthur would remember for the rest of his life. A thud. The final sound of a human body becoming weight.

He'd heard it once before, on a site in Bermondsey where a labourer had collapsed from a heatstroke. The body makes a different noise when the person inside it had left. There is no bracing or an attempt to catch the fall. Just mass and gravity, unmediated by consciousness.

A woman at the table nearest to the fireplace went next. Her head dropped forward into her folded arms as though she'd simply decided to sleep right there in the middle of the pub. Then a young man fell mid-stride while walking towards the door. One moment he was on his way out of the pub, the next his legs buckled and he dropped before anyone could catch him.

As was expected in a pub where people were dropping for no apparent reason, someone screamed.

Arthur was on his feet before the scream had finished. He crossed to the man at the bar and dropped to his knees. The man was warm and there was no blood or any visible injury; the face was slack and utterly peaceful.

He pressed two fingers to the carotid and held them for ten seconds. Nothing. He moved to the woman and got the same result. Steve was kneeling beside the young man near the door. He looked up and shook his head. The young man had been walking towards the door and would never reach it.

Three people were dead without warning or wound. One in four of the pub's customers, Arthur's brain calculated before he could stop it. The three collapses had been simultaneous. Whatever killed them had passed through the room like a wave and found certain bodies wanting.

"Call an ambulance," someone shouted. "Someone call -"

"Phones are dead!— they're all dead, everything's—"

Arthur stood slowly and realised that his hands were shaking. He looked down and willed them to stop only for them to ignore him.

"Gas leak. It has to be a gas leak." Donna said. She was scanning the room, cataloguing exits and the threats but to her frustration there was no apparent threat to catalogue and combat.

"Gas doesn't kill selectively, Donna. It doesn't take three people and leave the rest standing with glowing blue veins." Steve said.

Donna didn't answer. She didn't have one. She was reaching for something logical, something mundane to explain whatever was happening around them and to them.

Then something appeared in Arthur's vision and from the stunned looks on the faces of the other patrons, Arthur could guess that they were seeing the same thing.

What he was seeing wasn't projected on a wall or a screen. It was simply there, floating in the space between his eyes and the world, as sharp and clear as text on a page held in front of his eyes.

SYSTEM CALIBRATION EVENT INITIATED.

PLANETARY ETHER INTEGRATION IN PROGRESS.

FENCE PROTOCOL ENGAGED.

DURATION: 1,260 DAYS.

Arthur blinked but the text stubbornly remained attached to his vision. It faded slowly after about five seconds, dissolving from his field of vision like frost evaporating from a glass.

"What the— what in the bloody hells was that?" someone said from one of the tables.

Steve walked back to the table. "Tell me that you both just saw that."

Donna's hand had dropped to her side, her fingers curling and uncurling. She nodded. "Blue floating text. Something about a calibration event."

"One thousand two hundred and sixty days. That's three and a half years. Whatever this is, it just told us it's going to last three and a half years." He shook his head. "And that's not a round number. No one picks one thousand two hundred and sixty unless the number means something."

Around them the pub was coming apart. Some were crying. Someone else was trying to force the front door open. A woman knelt beside the body near the fireplace, shaking the dead woman’s shoulders and repeating a name. Laura. She was saying Laura, over and over, and Arthur heard the name over everything else.

A man jumped off his stool and made for the door in panic, and an older fellow who’d half-risen to see what was happening took the brunt of his shoulder and went down hard against a table.

"Out of my way --"

Arthur caught his arm with just enough force to stop him.

"Steady on, mate. No need for that." He kept his voice level, the one he used on a site when somebody was losing their bottle near an edge. "We're all having the same night, mate. He's down because of you, so you can help him up first."

The man stared at him with frantic eyes, his chest heaving and Arthur prepared himself for a shove. Then the panic cracked and the young man’s eyes cleared. He reached down, grabbing the old man by his coat and hauling him back onto his feet.

“Sorry,” he muttered before rushing towards the door.

Behind the bar the landlord had an old corded receiver pressed to his ear, the one that was kept by the till for when the card machine played up, and he was saying hello into it. Hello, hello, hello. He set the receiver down then picked it up again. There was never any tone.

“Patrick, keep the fucking phone down and shut the back door.” someone said.

Meanwhile Arthur had finally managed to win the war of wills with his shaking hands. The fear was still present; a cold clammy wire strung from his throat to his stomach. But the professional in him had managed to take over. What's the immediate failure? What's the load? What's the weakest point? And the weakest point was information. The dead phones, the dead lights, the dead sky all indicated that this was city-wide. Possibly wider.

"We need to see what's happening outside," he said.

Donna and Steve nodded without hesitation and moved towards the door.

***

The world outside the Grapes had ended while they were playing Dungeons & Dragons.

Lime Street was frozen mid-motion. Cars sat at dead angles where their engines had simply quit; some had their doors hanging open. A black cab had drifted into a lamppost, its meter faintly aglow with residual charge that was fading even as Arthur watched. A bus sat crooked across the intersection, its passengers stumbling out the rear doors in ones and twos, looking around in fear and bewilderment.

Then there were the bodies lying on the pavement.

Arthur counted seven in the first thirty metres. Some had been caught mid-stride like the man in the pub. Others had been sitting at bus stops or leaning against walls and had fallen where the wave had found them. The people around them, the survivors, stood over the fallen bodies with the helpless expressions of people watching something they had no framework to process.

A man knelt in the middle of the road, cradling a woman's head in his lap and saying her name over and over. Arthur looked away before the sight unsteadied the only part of him still functioning.

After helping a few people on to the pavements, Arthur realised what was bothering him: there were no sirens. London without sirens was like a body without a heartbeat; it meant the systems that kept the organism alive had stopped or failed. The streets should have been buzzing with coppers but there were no ambulances, no lights, no helicopters. Just small human voices shouting, crying, and calling names in a city that had gone deaf.

Above the low roofs the towers of the city stood against the violet-blue sky, and every one of them was dark. The Gherkin stood reflecting the light from the sky with no lights from inside. In fifteen years Arthur had never once seen its lights off, not at four in the morning or on Christmas night. Forty-one floors of glass had nothing behind them now.

Steve had stopped a few paces behind them and was looking up at the sky with an expression caught between wonder and dread.

"Three and a half years," Steve said again, almost to himself.

Arthur was about to speak when Donna's hand closed on his forearm. Her weight shifted, one foot sliding back into a stance Arthur had never seen her use outside the gym, and her eyes locked onto the alley beside the pub.

Something was moving in the dark.
