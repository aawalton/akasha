“So we’re looking for half bear, half armadillo?” asked Samar. “Why do you call them care bears again?”

“It’s from an old kids’ show,” I explained. “And anyway, Juliette named them first.”

It was just me, Samar, and Kim Jong Un—who, as usual, was being carried around like a plushie in Samar’s arms. We’d set out to capture a care bear, but after more than an hour of walking we hadn’t seen a single one.

“Wait,” Samar said suddenly, “can I name forest creatures too? Like, if we find a new one?”

“Sure,” I said, though I wasn’t sure if that was a good idea. “By the way, are you comfortable in that leather armor?” I noticed she kept moving awkwardly.

“It’s not really comfortable,” she admitted, “but it doesn’t bother me too much. And I get it—it’s important.”

Hyun-woo had practically given me a speech before we left: keep an eye on her, don’t let her get hurt, don’t let her out of sight. Over and over. I promised him I’d keep her safe, of course. Now that I was basically a Forest Master, I also had a responsibility to keep my friends safe. Wait—let me rephrase that: With great power comes great responsibility. That’s better.

“And why do we only want a care bear?” Samar asked. “Can’t Kim sting other animals too?”

“I talked to him about it,” I explained. “Armadillo care bears are by far the best suited for scorpion suricata control. They’ve got the right temperament, they’re pack animals so training comes easier, and they’re strong enough for battle. Second best are the furry horn snakes, which also live in packs, but their temperament is unstable and they’re not as useful. You can ask Kim yourself—you talk to him all the time anyway. What do you even talk about?”

“Oh, all kinds of things!” she said brightly. “Well, mostly he talks about food, but I tell him about myself, my family, Asyut—my city back home—and a little about the Prophet’s teachings, at least what I know.”

The idea of Samar trying to convert scorpion suricatas to Islam was funny. But I knew she was just being Samar—teaching her new little friend about the things that mattered to her.

“Remember to keep it simple,” I reminded her. “He might not understand everything.”

“Sure,” she said, “but I think he’s really smart. Smarter than you think.”

“He’s a smart scorpion suricata,” I agreed.

That’s when I heard movement. Heavy steps, multiple of them. I quickly motioned for silence and pulled Samar behind a tree. But whoever it was, they’d already noticed us. A moment later, four goblins burst out of the brush, weapons raised—knives and spears barely two feet from our heads.

They didn’t look like the ragged pair I’d met before. These goblins were healthy, quick on their feet. And dangerous.

Damn. I’d promised Hyun-woo we wouldn’t get into trouble. But wait… maybe this wasn’t trouble.

“Hi, friend!” I called, “Remember me?”

One of them was definitely the short goblin I’d helped last time.

“Do you know this human?” asked a fat goblin, not lowering his spear.

The short one stared at me, his eyes narrowing. “All humans look the same to me…” He paused, then nodded. “Ah yes. I know this one. He helped me and Vra’tul in the forest.”

“Ah! So we are friends!” said Samar, clearly nervous but trying. “That’s good, good!”

“We are not friends,” snapped the short goblin. He jabbed his spear toward Samar. “And why is your underling carrying a forest monster?”

“Hey!” Samar snapped back, failing spectacularly to read the room “I’m not his underling, and he’s not a monster!”

“There are scorpion suricatas,” I said quickly. “They… can be tamed. Just be careful of the sting.”

The short goblin’s eyes stayed locked on me. “Let me ask you something,” he said. “In your tribe, do you have five more humans — three pale skin, one brown, one very dark?”

“No,” I said, intrigued. “In our group, besides us, there are only two more—a male and a female, both pale-skinned. We’ve never met the ones you describe.”

“Humans are liars,” hissed the fat goblin.

“Hmm…” the short goblin considered. “I believe him. Those others weren’t good in the forest. Not good like you. Not even as good as us.”

At that, he lowered his spear. The others followed.

“My name is Rul’vak,” he said. “Yesterday, one of our patrols was attacked by that group of humans I spoke of. Far to the east, a day’s travel. They seemed lost… but they were strong. They attacked without provocation. Injured some of us.”

“I’m sorry,” I said. “Maybe they were scared. Maybe they thought you were dangerous monsters.”

“They were laughing,” Rul’vak said, hatred burning in his voice. “Next time we meet them, we will decorate our camp with their heads.”

Okay. Not the direction I wanted this conversation to go. Quick—topic change!

“So you’re building a camp,” I said brightly. “How’s that going?”

“Slow,” he admitted, “but we work hard. Soon we will have a mighty stronghold—a power against the vile forest monsters, and against humans who come too close.”

“That’s great,” I said, trying to sound cheerful. “Looks like you’re much more confident in the woods than last time.”

“We are goblins,” he shrugged. “By nature we are creatures of the forest. Even this cursed place will be our home soon enough.”

He turned as if to leave, apparently done talking.

“If you want to meet again, human,” he added, “go west, north of your city. After two hours, our scouts will find you. Tell them you seek Rul’vak.”

“Sure,” I said, “I’d like that.” An invitation to a goblin camp? That was so damn cool.

“Oh, before you go,” I said quickly, “did you see any care bears, I mean… monsters that look like bears with scaled backs?”

“Yes,” said a vicious-looking goblin with sharp teeth. “We know them. They taste good. We saw a pack of four not far in that direction.” He pointed.

“Perfect,” I said. “Thanks! And please remember—not all humans are the same. Some of us are actually nice.”

“We will see,” said Rul’vak. And then they were gone.

“They seemed cool,” Samar said as we headed in the direction they pointed. “Too bad we didn’t talk more.”

“The cool part was where they planned to impale human heads on sticks?” I asked.

“They said those humans were bad,” she said stubbornly. “The ones who attacked them and laughed.”

“Maybe. But you can’t just believe one side. If we meet those humans, they’ll tell us a different version of the story. Who knows—maybe they’re good people. Anyway, it’s exciting to know there are more players. They probably started in another city, or maybe in Kamatou but left before we arrived.”

Even if the other players were assholes, they could still have valuable information.

“I don’t know…” said Samar. “But I kind of believe the goblins. Oh—look, there they are!”

The care bears. Four of them grazing in a clearing. Large, armored beasts, munching on grass.

One noticed us, stared for a long second, then went back to eating.

Kim Jong Un suggested a strategy. I’d be the bait.

“Of course you want me to get close,” I muttered. “What if they attack me?”

He showed me the same visual over and over: me, alive and fine. Translation: They won’t attack… probably.

I remembered Ramon’s death all too clearly. But that had been different—the beast that killed him was trained to fight, ordered to kill. These were just… animals. And I had leather armor, a spear, and I was ready.

Still. My stomach twisted.

Then Samar stepped beside me, lifting her own spear.

“Come on, Samar,” I hissed. “That’s not what we agreed. Hyun-woo will kill me if anything happens to you!”

“Not if the bears kill you first,” she said. “Don’t worry, I’m tougher than I look.”

She was certainly brave. Meanwhile, I was calculating the odds that two humans together would seem less worth attacking. So I let her stay by my side, even though she was only fourteen. God, I was such a coward.

Then came the bark-like sound—Kim’s doing his thing. The bears shifted nervously, then started moving fast, not running, but leaving.

For a terrifying second, I thought they’d trampled Kim. But then I spotted him—fine and unharmed.

“Hurry! We need to catch them!” Samar yelled, scooping him up and charging after the beasts.

“Goddammit, Samar!” I shouted, running after her. “Wait!”

The care bears moved fast, but not too fast—we could follow, but running in the forest was dangerous. I had to keep an eye on poisonous gas flowers, hidden predator nests, signs of hostile tribes, and still keep track of the road so we wouldn’t get lost.

I was seconds from calling it off when the bears stopped in another clearing, casually resuming their grazing.

“Stupid armadillo care bears,” I muttered. “What was that running around for?”

We crept closer, slowly this time.

“Wait,” Samar whispered. “How will we know which one Kim stung?”

“That won’t be a problem,” I said.

One of them turned, walked straight toward us, sniffed Kim, and then sniffed us—rubbing its snout gently against my arm.

“Uuuuuu,” cooed Samar. “He’s so friendly now! He’s cute!”

“Well, yeah,” I said grimly. “We just brainwashed him with mind-altering poison.”

“I know,” said Samar. “But… I feel a little bad, taking him away from his pack. His friends will miss him. Still… he seems happy.”

Him being happy was totally part of the drug effect, but I didn’t say that to Samar.

“Maybe Kim should sting another one,” suggested Samar, “so he will have a friend from his pack.”

Yeah, Samar was a sweet girl like that.“He can’t,” I said, “His venom gland takes two to three days to refill.

“Oh…” Samar said thoughtfully.

“Also, he can only maintain two care bears at a time, since each one needs weekly doses to stay… ah… loyal. And he can’t sting other species either. His poison gland tailors the toxin specifically for the creature he intends to control—in this case, care bears. Scorpion suricata biology really is something else.”

“Damn, you know a lot about this,” she said, impressed.

“He’s part of our party,” I explained. “We need to know what he can and can’t do. Could save our lives. Speaking of which—we should head back.”

“Yes,” Samar agreed. She waved to the rest of the pack. “Goodbye! Don’t worry—we’ll take really good care of him!”

We will take good care of him. We’d train him with a spear, make him into a powerful soldier—an obedient warrior with the strength of a wild beast.
