Chapter 35

“Note to self: academic French is not conversational French. The phrase ‘I am an archaeologist seeking information about local funerary architecture’ does not, apparently, translate well when shouted at an armed woman who wants to know why you smell like a cave.” - The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr, Marseille

Nadia didn’t flinch when they told her what the guardian was made of.

She sat behind her desk in the harbourmaster’s office, hands flat on the surface, and listened while Steve gave her an account of the fuse amalgamation they had killed in the crypts.

When Steve finished, the room was quiet. Rémy stood at the window with his back to them. His jaw was tight. Cédric had stopped writing in his ledger and had a pained look on his face.

Nadia spoke a single sentence and Steve translated.

“She says thank you for bringing them peace.”

Then she moved on. Arthur recognised the behaviour. It was a structural response, the refusal to let the load buckle the frame. Nadia had processed seven names in a ledger before. She would process them again. The grief existed but it didn’t stop the work.

“The riddle,” she said, through Steve. “Where does it point?”

“Notre-Dame de la Garde probably. The golden Madonna on the hill.” Steve laid out what he had, the sixty percent of it. Limestone height, Gilded Mother, brass-bound star, sea-winds. “The last two lines are the ones I couldn’t finish from the crypt.

“‘Touch the brass-bound star where the sea-winds blow, to wake the high beacon for the world below.’ I need the site. But the brass-bound star is the piece I’m missing entirely. Is there anything made of brass at Notre-Dame? Near the Madonna, on the terrace, anything?”

Nadia looked at Cédric. He’d stopped writing and was tapping his pen against the ledger, thinking.

“There is a table,” Cédric said. “On the terrace, north side. A big disc, set into the stone. It shows the directions to other cities, with distances. The tourists like it.” He turned his pen in a circle on the desk surface. “I do not know if it is brass. It could be. It is metal, and old.”

Steve’s eyes changed. Arthur had seen the look before and knew what was coming. “An orientation table. A compass rose.” He was already pulling threads. “A compass rose is a star. Brass-bound, set into stone, showing cardinal directions.” He looked at Nadia. “North side of the terrace?”

Cédric nodded.

“The Mistral,” Steve said, turning to Rémy. “It’s the dominant wind here, yes?”

Rémy turned from the window. “The Mistral comes from the north. It blows out to sea.”

“South.” Steve’s hands were moving now, tracing the logic in the air. “The wind blows south. ‘Touch the brass-bound star where the sea-winds blow.’ Touch the south point on the compass rose. The point where the Mistral goes. Not where it comes from. Where it blows to. South. Out to sea.”

He looked at Arthur. “One hundred percent.”

Arthur smiled and nodded. Steve in his element was a marvel. He looked at Rémy and asked, “Route.”

Rémy crossed to the harbour chart on the wall and tapped a finger on the south side of the Vieux-Port. “Quai de Rive-Neuve, then south through the Rue Fort Notre-Dame. Shortest route to the hill. Forty minutes in the old world.” His finger stopped. “But it is blocked. A pack of boars moved into the streets at the base of the hill four days ago. Twelve, maybe more. Armoured and coordinated. They don’t leave.”

“Are there alternative routes?” Donna asked.

Rémy grimaced. “East through Castellane and up from the back of the hill. The 6th arrondissement is a maze and we’ve lost contact with every group south of the Cours Julien. We don’t know what’s in there. Could be creatures. Could be people who don’t want visitors.” His finger traced a longer arc on the chart. “West along the Corniche and around from the coast. Longer. Open ground, exposed shoreline, and everything south of Endoume is crawling with things that hunt by scent. We sent a scouting party along the Corniche in the first week. Three came back out of five.”

Arthur studied the chart. The direct route was blocked by twelve armoured creatures. The eastern route meant days through unknown urban territory. The coastal route was longer still, through open ground with documented casualties.

“We might have to go through the pack,” he said. “But first we need to look at them,” Arthur said.

***

Marseille under the violet sky was nothing like London under the violet sky, and Arthur couldn’t stop cataloguing the differences.

London under the violet sky had gone silent. The electricity died and the silence was the sound of eight million people realising simultaneously that the systems they’d trusted with their lives had switched off.

Marseille hadn’t gone silent. Marseille had gotten louder. People shouted from windows. Arguments carried across the narrow streets in rapid French that Steve translated in fragments: disputes about water rations, territorial claims on rooftop gardens, a woman on the Rue Sainte threatening to throw a man’s possessions into the harbour if he didn’t return her cooking pot.

“The French apocalypse involves more shouting,” Donna observed.

“The French everything involves more shouting,” Steve said. He was reading signs as they walked, survivor notices pinned to walls and doors, scrawled in marker on cardboard, painted on shutters. His French was improving by the hour, the academic rust burning off under the pressure of actual necessity. “That one’s a water collection schedule. That one’s a warning about creatures in the storm drains. And that one -” he squinted at a spray-painted message on a pharmacy wall, “- is either a request for insulin or a very creative insult about someone’s mother.”

“Context?” Donna asked.

“The handwriting suggests sincerity. I’m going with insulin.”

Arthur was reading the buildings. He couldn’t help it. The limestone was different from London brick in ways his hands understood before his brain could articulate: warmer to the touch, more porous, the walls thicker and more forgiving of lateral movement. The construction philosophy was Mediterranean. Build heavy, build low and let the walls breathe. Where London resisted force, Marseille absorbed it. The buildings leaned into each other across the narrow streets like old friends holding each other up.

“The compressive strength of this limestone is superior to your London stock brick by a factor of approximately two point three,” Archie observed. “The Romans selected this quarry for the same reason. Your species has a persistent habit of rediscovering engineering principles and believing you invented them.”

Arthur didn’t respond to the observation. He’d been running his hand along the limestone for the last thirty metres, which meant Archie had been watching him do it, which meant the observation was less about the stone and more about letting Arthur know he’d noticed.

A rat-derived creature burst from a drain grate thirty metres ahead of them. Smaller than the harbour boar, fast, low, heading for a gap between buildings. Arthur didn’t even get his shield up before Donna’s axe intercepted it mid-stride. One strike. The creature skidded across the cobblestones and didn’t get up.

Donna cleaned the blade on her boot without breaking stride. “Small,” she said. “Drain-dweller. Low saturation.”

Rémy’s scouts, who’d been assigned as guides for the route assessment, exchanged a look. Julien muttered something to Marc. Steve caught it.

“What?” Arthur asked.

“He said she’s faster than anyone he’s seen. And that his crossbow is starting to feel like a toy.”

Donna didn’t react visibly, but Arthur saw her shoulders shift fractionally, the micro-adjustment she made when she was trying not to look pleased.

The elevated position was a car park on the Boulevard Vauban, four storeys of concrete spiral ramp that gave a clear sight line down the streets at the base of the hill.

Arthur braced against the top-floor wall. Below him, the boulevard was wide. Haussmann-style, built for carriages and commerce, now empty of everything except the things that had claimed it. He counted them. Twelve. Armoured boar, chitin-plated, spread across a two-block radius in a patrol pattern that Donna was assessing.

“Four groups of three,” she said from beside him, her voice barely above a whisper. “Rotating in a figure-eight. One group rests while three patrol. And the big one -”

Arthur had already found it.

The alpha was larger than the others by a third. Its chitin plates darker, thicker, with a ridge of bone running its spine. But it was the eyes Arthur recognised, the cluster. Eight or ten dark beads in a dense constellation where a boar’s skull should have held a pair. The same cluster that had watched him from the corner of the harbourside building three days ago, holding his gaze for a few seconds before deciding the cost of the harbour fight exceeded the reward.

Arthur was certain it was the same creature.

“The one from the harbour,” he said quietly. “The one that pulled back.”

“The eyes?” Donna said.

“The eyes.”

She studied the pack’s movement for a long moment. “The patrol pattern. Three groups, rotating, one resting. The alpha is positioned at the intersection, highest visibility point and the best sight lines.” She looked at Arthur. “These things are getting smarter. The retreat at the harbour was already unusual. Now it’s running coordinated patrols with rest rotations. That’s learned behaviour.”

“Ether-enhanced cognitive development in high-saturation fauna is an expected progression.” Archie’s geometry brightened at the edges. “Neural complexity scales with integration density.”

“How smart?” Arthur asked.

“Sufficient to identify and exploit environmental advantages. Insufficient for abstract reasoning or tool use. The gap is narrowing.”

Archie’s geometry rotated once, slowly. “The patrol structure is a simple resource-allocation model. Rest rotation, overlapping coverage, centralised command authority. Identical formations have appeared in human warfare for approximately nine thousand years. The creatures arrived at the same solution in approximately eleven days.” A pause. “I find the convergence rate noteworthy.”

Arthur watched the alpha complete a circuit. It moved with the confidence of something that had claimed this ground and had no reason to expect a challenge. The patrol was the scan. The pack was the threat.

Twelve armoured creatures, coordinated, led by something that learned from experience.

A straight fight was out of the question. Arthur’s eyes moved from the pack to the street, then to the buildings flanking it, and then to the construction site.

***

The construction site sat on the western edge of the creature territory like a gift from a universe that occasionally rewarded structural engineers.

A half-finished apartment building stood at the centre of the site. Six storeys of concrete frame with scaffolding still attached, steel tubes and coupling joints climbing the façade in a grid that Arthur’s brain read like sheet music. The scaffolding was standard Layher system, modular, interlocking, designed to be assembled and disassembled without specialist tools. The ground floor was open on two sides, creating a pass-through corridor approximately four metres wide. Enough to stop twelve boar from hitting them all at once.

Arthur’s eyes went from the scaffolding to the corridor. Something was forming in the part of his brain that designed encounter spaces. The DM’s instinct, terrain and positioning and action economy was now, improbably, the most useful skill he possessed.

Donna saw his face. “No.”

“You haven’t heard it yet.”

“You’re looking at the scaffolding in a very unsettling way.”

Arthur turned to the group. “We can’t fight twelve at once. It won’t be a fight, it will be a stampede. The only way I see us surviving this is if we limit the number we’re facing at any given moment.”

“How?” Steve asked.

“I don’t know yet. Maybe we can draw small groups away from the pack, bait them toward the choke point one group at a time. If it works, we thin the herd. If it doesn’t -” He gestured at the scaffolding. “That’s three tonnes of steel on standard Layher coupling joints. I can rig a release mechanism with a single pin. If everything goes wrong, if they all charge at once, if the pulls don’t work, we fall back through the corridor and I drop the scaffolding. We fight, if it works, if it doesn’t, we have insurance.”

“Insurance,” Donna said. “You want to build an insurance policy out of three tonnes of scaffolding.”

“I want us to have an exit that doesn’t depend on outrunning twelve armoured boar. The scaffolding buys us that.”

Steve was making his weird thinking face. “The patrol groups rotate on a four-minute cycle. When one group passes the east approach to the site, they’re sixty metres from the nearest support. If we can provoke just that group, light, sound, something that draws three without alerting twelve…”

“That’s a big if,” Donna said.

“Every plan we’ve had since the pub has been a big if,” Steve said. “This one at least has a building attached to it.”

Donna looked at the construction site for a long time. She was running it. Arthur could see the assessment cycling behind her eyes, every variable tested, every failure mode mapped. Arthur had never met anyone who could imagine more ways for something to go wrong, and she never started a job until she’d priced the cost of each one.

“You build the trap tonight,” she said finally. “I plan the fallback routes. Steve maps the patrol timing. Every cycle, every gap, every window where a group is separated from the main pack.” She paused. “We try the pulls. If they work, we keep going. If they don’t, we trigger the trap and we run. Nobody dies being clever.”

Arthur nodded. “Dawn.”

Arthur didn’t know if the plan would hold. He knew the engineering was sound, and the timing was tight, and twelve armoured creatures were circling sixty metres away without any reason to expect what was coming. That would have to be enough.
