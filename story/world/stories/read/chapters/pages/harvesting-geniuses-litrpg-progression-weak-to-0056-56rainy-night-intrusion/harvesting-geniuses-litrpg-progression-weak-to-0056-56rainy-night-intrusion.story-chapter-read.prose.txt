The treatment process moved forward without a hitch. Only those with severe injuries received the Cursed Fruit treatment—those with minor to moderate wounds chose to endure and wait for help, unwilling to gamble their lifespan on small cuts. The granary had become an emergency ward for everyone to shelter in, blocking out sun, wind, and the rain that had now begun to fall.

The sky that night was overcast and moonless. The campfires outside had been doused by the rain. Without the wooden cottage and the granary, everyone would have been soaked to the bone—and already-compromised wounds would have risked infection, worsening an already grim situation. Having a solid structure overhead did more than shield them from the elements. It steadied their minds. It felt, in some small way, like being home.

They had been lost in the Cursed Forest for days and nights on end, unable to sleep. Being inside something built by human hands—or at least something that wasn’t raw nature—comforted them in ways nothing else could. The granary Jinyo had summoned through the system’s facilities wasn’t small, but packing more than two hundred people inside made the space cramped enough to make breathing difficult.

Still, it beat standing in the midnight rain. Jinyo shared the cottage with Gretel, Esther, and Nephrin. The young woman from the capital had already been taken aback by Jinyo’s use of Domain magic—but the fact that he could deploy physical structures inside his own Domain was beyond anything she had anticipated. The gaze she fixed on him was searching and intense.

“Doctor Jinyo—have you ever formally studied magic? At an institute or a college of sorcery?”

“Not at all. You know my cultivation rate is only 0.1%. I only know magic to secondary school level.”

“You can use Domain magic but you’ve never formally studied? You’re joking. My entire college career just lost all meaning.” Nephrin refused to believe it. She had attended specialized magic schools from primary through university, and while she had some knowledge of advanced magic, Domain magic was currently beyond her reach.

“I have a decent grasp of Domain magic theory. It’s not difficult if you understand volume formulas and area calculations. I had to cram the textbook without sleeping for ten days before I could use it. Establishing the Domain itself isn’t hard—the challenge is the Planar control technique. I’m not particularly good at that.” Jinyo described the hardship of studying Domain magic—whether asleep or awake, textbook in hand at all times. It had been frustrating because he couldn’t train with the sword properly.

“Some people die without ever being able to do what you just did. You really are overflowing with talent.” Nephrin’s praise carried an undercurrent of dry sarcasm.

“Thank you—” Jinyo ducked his head, receiving the compliment with an awkwardness that suggested he didn’t quite believe he’d earned it.

“Hmph.” A dismissive sound from somewhere across the room.

“What’s that? Sore throat?” Jinyo turned to look at Esther.

“No. Never mind.”

“Actually, there’s something I’ve been curious about. During your practical exam, I saw you use two elements of Planar at once. Can you tell me what that technique was?” Nephrin shifted the topic. Her gaze locked onto Jinyo and no one else. Even Esther, who had been affecting total indifference, glanced over. Every pair of eyes in the room trained itself on Jinyo. He said nothing. Both women accepted that an answer probably wasn’t coming—advanced techniques were a sensitive matter. The better the skill, the more binding the confidentiality agreement; secrets that fell into a rival’s or enemy’s hands had a way of turning against you.

“It’s called Double Impact. It works in conjunction with Punishment Wave—you locate weak points in the enemy’s Planar circulation and strike the flaw. By channeling two opposing types of Planar, it delivers what feels like two strikes in a single slash.”

“…” Nephrin’s mouth opened, just slightly.

“Why that expression?”

“I didn’t expect you to share it so easily. Or maybe it isn’t a high-tier technique after all.”

“It’s Rank A. I just believe people shouldn’t hoard knowledge—that we should share what we know and walk forward together. If you take it to your grave, the next generation gets nothing from you. If you want to train in it, come study with me. Teaching it right now would take more time than we have.”

“Thank you for your generosity. I only asked because I once wrote my thesis on dual-element Planar use without spellcraft as an intermediary. The theory is simple, but I’ve never seen anyone execute it in practice. I don’t like taking things for free—just tell me what you want me to do in exchange.”

“I’d like you to teach me Planar control technique. Can you start right now? The moment we get out of the Hollow Gate, I’ll teach you.”

“Agreed.” Nephrin gave a single nod. Jinyo was the most dependable ally she had. The stronger he became, the more it benefited her. As the top-scoring candidate in the teacher’s licensing exam, Nephrin had teaching experience and a thorough understanding of magic and Planar control. Teaching Planar control technique was her specialty.

“Can I learn too?” Gretel stepped over and made the request himself. There was nothing in the cottage to do—no personal toys, no handheld game console. This cottage was only a magical replica, not the one Gretel used to play in. If anything, learning Planar control seemed far more interesting.

“You want to learn too? Why? Do you want to be a sorcerer when you grow up?”

“No. I want to be a swordsman like Mom. I’m going to become an officer and kill all the monsters!”

“Your father has ambitious goals. It might be a little early, but I’m glad to have a young student like you in class. Doctor Jinyo doesn’t mind if I take on one more student, does he?” Nephrin turned to give Jinyo a smile. Jinyo returned a tired smile and shrugged in answer. She shifted her smile toward Esther, who was sitting with his eyes closed near the door—but the smile she gave him was nothing like the one she’d offered the two of them.

“Right. Whoever you are—I believe the granary still needs a guard.”

“There are a hundred guards already. They can manage fine without me.”

“One hundred and one is better than one hundred. Besides, if there’s a sudden attack, you’ll be able to reach the patients in time.” Nephrin presented her reasoning as justification. Esther tried to formulate a counterargument, but his mind had gone blank. He reached out, pushed the door open, and walked outside with surprising compliance.

“You don’t like Esther much, do you?” Jinyo watched Esther through the window as he headed toward the granary.

“Doctor Jinyo, I genuinely respect you. But as far as I’m concerned, knowledge has a price—and my tuition fee is quite high. Don’t think Planar control technique is some trivial thing. It’s an absolutely essential foundation. Not just for sorcerers—it applies to swordsmen and every other type of practitioner as well.” Nephrin smiled sweetly at the father and son. The sight of Jinyo and Gretel sitting cross-legged side by side—a large person and a small one with the same face—was almost heartbreakingly adorable, like a pair of matching figurines.

Nephrin began lecturing on the principles of Planar: how to control it, how to apply it. It was content Jinyo already knew. When she finished the foundational lecture, she transitioned from curriculum-based instruction to applied technique, drawing on her experience as a sorceress and the knowledge she’d accumulated at the college of magic.

Her teaching style was remarkably easy to follow, covering every aspect of Planar control. Perhaps it made perfect sense—she had earned first place in the teacher’s licensing exam for a reason. Jinyo was an excellent student. Someone who had earned a doctorate from abroad in his early twenties was no fool. He absorbed the material and analyzed it as he went. It was a deep curriculum unlike anything taught in a textbook or at a military academy.

‘The curriculum at a college of magic really is a completely different world from what’s taught at a military school.’

Back when Jinyo and Rina had attended military academy, Planar was certainly a crucial subject—but the military curriculum focused on forms and using Planar to supplement physical strength. The body had to be strong, and teamwork and coordination were paramount. A college of magic was different. They directed all attention toward the use of magic through Planar.

Modern magic bore little resemblance to ancient magic, which had operated on mana. Modern magic centered on Planar. Planar was easier to control than traditional mana and more versatile in application. Even so, sorcerers had to specialize in magic above all else. Unlike practitioners from other disciplines, sorcerers didn’t entrust their lives to physical strength. Their lives depended on Planar control—its quality and how they wielded it.

The rain kept hammering down without pause. Jinyo and Nephrin spent nearly the entire night exchanging knowledge. Gretel lasted only the first hour before quietly drifting off to sleep.

Outside, Esther made his way through the sodden mud around the forest’s edge, collecting Cursed Fruit. He was one of the few people who could move freely without fearing a monster attack. The Cursed Fruit was a critical medical resource, and he was among the fortunate few who had never touched it—because he understood the principle: don’t eat what you don’t know.

It was the only food source available. If he hadn’t grown so accustomed to going hungry, he’d have swallowed it the very first day—regardless of Jinyo’s warning that it was dangerous and strictly off-limits. Still, there were people with sharper eyes who had secretly been stockpiling it without eating it. Not as emergency rations—as a commodity. Something valuable always had buyers somewhere.

By the time his bag was packed full of purple berries, Esther trudged through the wet mud back to the granary, now teeming with people. Most had already fallen asleep, though they startled awake when he walked past. One man raised his head to curse—and fell silent the instant he registered the Ether Blade at Esther’s hip.

The scent of the purple berries drew longing gazes from several people. Stomachs growled audibly, but all they could do was clutch at them. Esther paid no attention to the listless stares of people who couldn’t muster the will to walk two hundred meters from the granary and pick their own fruit. His ruby eyes settled on the girl sitting cross-legged beside Sven and the elderly man in butler’s attire.

“His color looks a little better.” Esther sat down beside Luciria.

“Yes.”

“You’re close with your brother.”

“I don’t know. I don’t know what it means to be close to a sibling.” The look Luciria kept on Sven was complicated. The only connection between her and him was sharing a father.

“What about you? Do you have siblings?”

“I have an older sister—more than ten years older. She vanished long ago. Whether she’s alive or dead, I don’t know, and I have no intention of finding out.”

“Why? Don’t you love her?”

“My life has more pressing things to deal with than chasing after someone who disappeared ten years ago. Not like you pampered young lords and ladies with all the time in the world.” No emotion, no feeling in his voice. Like a drinking-story anecdote told while drunk. Luciria didn’t like this man at all—because no matter how long someone had been gone, if they were family, she’d want to find them. However long it took.

Luciria didn’t understand people who placed no value on family. If Esther had a favorability rating with her, it was probably at minus one hundred by now. By comparison with Esther, she understood her own feelings clearly. To her, Sven was more than a stranger who shared her blood. She wanted to repay him the way he had always looked after her—clothing, food, finding tutors. All of it had been Sven. She wanted to care for him in return, as family.

‘I have to become strong enough to protect you. If I can’t protect you, I can’t protect my mother or anyone else.’

Esther glanced toward the door as the rain grew heavier by the moment. Raindrops hammering corrugated metal filled the air with a steady drumming that drowned out everything else. He was leaning back to rest, just like everyone else—when his shoulder met the concrete floor and in the same instant, a violent tremor ripped through the granary.

The thin metal sheets over brick and concrete were struck so hard they cracked. The granary wall nearly collapsed at once, threatening the entire structure. Esther seized his sword on instinct and spun toward the source of the movement. What he found was a two-meter monstrosity—a massively built, inhuman frame topped by enormous antlers that jutted well above its head.

“Monster! Monster attack!! AAGHH!” Screams tore through the air. The Hunters in the crowd grabbed their weapons. The Eltharok threw its head back and let out a bone-shaking roar, applying crushing psychological pressure. Many couldn’t move a muscle when confronted with that overwhelming presence.

Most of those gathered here were one-star Hunter exam candidates. They were no match for Eltharoks, whose average level was well above twenty-five. A single sweep of those massive arms had enough force to send them to the next world. The corrugated roof punched through. Pumpkin-headed humans swinging agricultural tools pounded the roof until the granary’s structure began to buckle.

People fled in panic, abandoning the wounded who couldn’t rise to whatever fate awaited them. Esther and Luciria fell into position around James the butler, who was carrying Sven in a desperate run.

“My instincts are truly gone, aren’t they? That many enemies and I didn’t sense a thing. When I get home I’m changing my resume to toilet paper.”

“Don’t be so hard on yourself, old man—a young man like me didn’t notice either. You’re practically in the coffin already. What do you have left to lose?” Esther had confidence in his enemy-detection ability, but he’d failed to notice them all the same. The rain had been deafening enough to suppress any awareness of the surrounding environment. The moment he drew his sword from its scabbard, the red blade was already drenched in rain.

“Today really must be my unlucky day.”

Jinyo and Nephrin snatched up Gretel and fled the cottage. Not just the granary—they were hit by a wave of Eltharoks as well. Beneath a sky where the rain refused to let up, what they saw in the darkness were dozens of blood-red eyes cutting through the black, fixed on their lives.

“This is a problem, Doctor Jinyo. In rain like this, I can’t use magic.” Nephrin counted the Eltharoks and Cursed Villagers—far more than any previous encounter. In conditions this brutal, even her vast reserves of Planar were meaningless. Rain was every fire-element sorcerer’s nemesis. A sorcerer who couldn’t cast was worthless.

“Nephrin—it seems like you’ll need to study self-defense techniques. If we make it out of here alive, I’ll give you a special discount.” Jinyo raised the Sonneteer into a ready stance.

“You really have time to joke right now?!”

“Salty!”

“Potato!” From beneath the rubble of the collapsed cottage, the round, stout potato-man kicked a wooden plank aside. It sprinted toward the largest Eltharok in the group. Its body began to glow—a three-second countdown—and its fingers latched onto the Eltharok with everything it had. The energy packed in its body ignited and detonated.

The blast shredded one Eltharok on the spot. Cursed Villagers nearby were swept off their feet by the shockwave. Jinyo clapped once and summoned a new Explosive Potato from the granary through the system, shaping it into a fresh Salty with a fiercer face than before.

Only one Eltharok was seriously hurt. The explosion startled and enraged the rest of them further. Jinyo looked up at the moonless sky. It seemed he wasn’t the only one heaven wasn’t smiling on tonight. Killing every Eltharok and saving everyone was practically impossible.
