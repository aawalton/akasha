It had been two days since the God of Merchants and I struck our deal. I still wanted to buy more, but V told me there was a limit on how many contracts his liege could form with a deity who had yet to enter the Pantheon. Three per month, no exceptions.

I insisted that I didn’t need contracts for things I wanted to obtain, but V explained that the God of Merchants was incapable of trading without forming one. For him, the contract seemed to be like the mechanism that allowed the transaction to exist at all.

In the end, V left. And even though I was disappointed that I couldn’t acquire more, what I had obtained was already far beyond anything I would have expected before his visit.

The Elven Core aside, which, at this point, was nothing more than a container for an impossibly powerful being I had no way of accessing, I now had two eggs of beasts who were immune to divine power, the arts of Fleshforging, Nullcraft, and Mortisophy, the knowledge of carving runes, and a book on magical engineering.

None of these gains would increase my own strength directly, but I hoped they would eventually turn my undeveloped Velmoryns into a race that excelled at magic.

Naturally, I was most interested in the eggs, but the problem was that I couldn’t use Guidance on them. My divine power couldn’t affect them in any way. Even moving them into the mortal world required me to purchase two separate artifacts from V, each costing a hundred Divinity Points. The bastard hadn’t included them in our agreement.

Still, I decided to use Guidance anyway, hoping at least for the basic information on how to hatch eggs in this world, and perhaps even something about their species based on what little I already knew.

[Guidance Advanced Feature – 10 Divinity Points]

To hatch beast eggs through unnatural means, energy must be supplied to nurture them. The amount required depends on the species. The type of energy is equally important - if it differs too greatly from the creature’s natural affinity, the developing beast may be harmed or undergo unwanted transformation.

Due to insufficient information, the species within the eggs you acquired cannot be identified with certainty. It is strongly recommended to use neutral energy for nurturing. This increases the hatching time but guarantees safety.

Alternatively, the eggs may be nurtured using the life force of another organism.

Moving the eggs into the mortal realm now made no sense. Based on what the Velthwyn leader had said, only timeless races could use formless mana without paying a price for it. The only one among my believers who qualified was Karla, and she was still in slumber.

My only other option was to use living creatures as sacrifices. I didn’t mind offering beasts to hatch the eggs, but I had no idea how the process would work without my divine power. The forest also lacked anything strong enough to fuel two extinct beings. Only one creature qualified - Kurraghal. But killing the lone specimen of its species felt wrong. Especially since, once I truly became the ruler of the forest, there was a chance it would bow to me.

Without dwelling on the eggs much longer, I shifted to what interested me even more. For the first time, I was about to learn magic. Even if I would never need to use it personally, understanding mana would allow me to deepen my grasp of divine power, and perhaps I would discover a way to create my own magical system in the future.

I brought the massive Fleshforging tome closer. Its name was direct enough to speculate what it was about, which was precisely why it intrigued me. I assumed it would describe a method of creating fiends or similar creatures through dark magic, but I was wrong.

Reading the book, which I had expected to take time, turned out to be as simple as flipping the pages. The moment my eyes passed over a line, the information surged through me and settled as if I had known it for years. It had to be one of the passive abilities I’d gained as a god, something similar to multitasking.

The opening pages explained the workings of the body and introduced the concept of the mana organ - something every living being capable of using mana possessed. Almost every sentient creature held some amount of mana, but that did not mean they could wield it. This was where Fleshforging came in.

It was an art of self-mutilation. The runes had to be carved directly into the places where mana flowed, most often the veins, and once they were etched, the body changed with them. There weren’t many runes to begin with, and their purpose was simple: they strengthened the physical attributes and nothing more. Only a rare handful enhanced the senses, granting sharper sight, hearing, or smell, and those were the hardest to engrave.

Luckily, I had also obtained the engraving manual, and by combining its instructions with the Fleshforging principles, my believers would be able to push the craft much further.

This is far more interesting than I imagined…

Once I finished flipping through the Fleshforging tome, I drew the next book forward - Nullcraft.

Unlike Fleshforging, this art covered every form of magic, because its purpose was to counter magic itself. To do that, one needed a broad understanding of all the ways mana could be used.

I already knew some - runes, diagrams, artifacts… but the book clarified that what I had been calling runes were actually diagrams. True runes came in many forms: seals, marks, and even tattoos. Magic had countless expressions, and to counter them one needed to understand where each form was weakest.

Diagrams were the easiest to break; disrupting a mage’s focus was usually enough to collapse the image they had in their mind. Nullcraft described a spell that halted a target’s thoughts for the briefest moment, yet long enough to force their diagram to shatter and send the mana rebounding through their body. A mage could protect themselves from such spells with mind-magic, but that only meant one would have to strike the protective spell first and the mind second. And the pattern repeated from there.

According to Nullcraft, runes were the most troublesome to counter. They were usually carved directly onto the mage’s body and unless the rune itself was damaged, there was no way to disrupt its effect. That was why Nullcraft relied on runic magic of its own. Fortunately, its spells were so direct in purpose and so simple that they could be safely engraved onto rings, amulets, bracelets - anything that kept them intact and ready to use without harming the user.

Lastly, I opened Mortisophy, which I had assumed would resemble Necromancy, only to be proven wrong again.

Mortisophy was the opposite of death magic. Instead of harming the living, it used death to heal them. Its wielder manipulated the thread of a target’s fate, nudging it away from wounds, sickness, or even imminent death.

But there was a problem.

Even the simplest Mortisophy spell required a diagram more intricate than anything I had ever seen. Compared to it, the diagrams Aria used looked like a child’s drawings. The difficulty made sense once I understood how the art worked.

Mortisophy didn’t affect the caster’s mind at all. Fleshforging twisted the body and caused bloodlust. Nullcraft made the mage anxious. But Mortisophy carried no such burden. Well, until the moment a spell collapsed. When that happened, the caster died instantly.

What a disappointment…

No matter how I looked at it, Mortisophy was the weakest choice among the three. The absence of side effects made it tempting at first, especially with how powerful its healing could be, but everything else worked against it. Its spells demanded a rare type of mana that locked the caster out of every other magical art. Even if someone managed to learn the diagrams without a single mistake, because otherwise even the smallest backlash would kill them, on a battlefield, the art would still be useless. The diagrams required too much time to shape. No one could stand in the middle of a fight without being cut down long before the spell took form. Any Mortisophy user would be marked as the top target the moment they began casting.

But that didn’t mean Mortisophy failed to impress me. Even if it was useless in combat and I had no intention of letting any of my believers waste their potential learning it, what if I learned it instead? Or rather, what if I shaped something new using its core principle?

Up until now, I never even assumed that manipulating fate was possible. I had never considered the idea that fate existed at all. And yet Mortisophy clearly tapped into something tied to fate, or at least something close to it. The book claimed it could delay death itself, and there was no other explanation for how such a thing could be possible. Nothing short of a force that touched both life and death could achieve that.

I need to understand how it functions.

If I could replicate that effect without directing it toward healing, something I already suspected was the most demanding form of magic, tied directly to life and death, and instead redirect it toward something simpler - minor hexes. Nothing dramatic but small twists of fate that, in the middle of a fight, could topple an army.

A stumble at the wrong moment. A foot slipping on dry soil. A hand shaking when drawing a bowstring. A sudden itch beneath armor. Harmless on their own, but deadly under pressure.

The idea excited me. Not only because of its potential, but because it meant I was finally making progress in understanding how the magic of this world truly worked. And if I could do that, then perhaps in time I would learn to wield divine power in ways far beyond simply pushing my will into the mortal realm.

This is going to take time. I have research to do…

The thought barely settled when a crimson notification window flashed before me.
