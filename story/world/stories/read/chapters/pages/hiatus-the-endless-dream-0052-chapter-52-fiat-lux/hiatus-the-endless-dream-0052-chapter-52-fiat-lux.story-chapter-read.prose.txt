They didn’t find anything on the scans. I was a perfectly healthy 20-year-old.

The doctors weren’t too happy about my parents pulling me out of the hospital against their recommendations, but they had no legal grounds to keep me there. If the nurses said anything about my outburst, maybe they’d have a stronger claim, but no one asked, and I didn’t see either of them again. With almost everyone suddenly having smudged faces, I doubt I’d even recognize them.

My parents were given recommendations on how to handle the next few days, particularly the importance of keeping an eye on me and that I should sleep on my side. A follow-up got scheduled a week out. It would be useless, but I didn’t argue that point.

Sparse music chimed from the car radio. My dad would change the station constantly. I could see him flicking his eyes to the mirror to gauge my reaction to the new song. Other than that, no one spoke before my dad’s voice softened the silence, “Want a milkshake? I was thinking Barn’s.”

It surprised me. I thought about the offer for a moment. Thinking about food, I braced for the sudden hunger pangs that came with realizing you hadn’t eaten for an entire day, but they never came. It felt unnatural. The same way, the lack of consciousness that used to come with dreaming felt unnatural. I’d bet anything it was yet another side effect of the Dream.

I was wavering on the milkshake, unsure if I wanted to agree because I liked milkshakes or because I wanted to spite the System.

We hit a stoplight.

The car purred.

My breath quickened.

I felt my mind receding.

I bit down on my thumb and tasted copper.

Pathetic. I was having a mental breakdown over a fucking milkshake. “Yeah,” I started. “That sounds nice. I’ll take a cookies and cream. I have a headache, gonna close my eyes for a bit.”

I saw my dad grip the steering wheel tighter and felt my mom’s eyes on me in the reflection of the window. An indie song, “Crowd Caffeine,” roared in the silence that came after I said that. No one moved. Neither of them breathed.

The light turned green, and the car rolled forward.

I refused to open my eyes until we turned into my parents' neighborhood. The smudged faces wouldn’t be the end of it. If I opened my eyes, I knew I’d witness an ocean of horrors. Likely ones worse than I could even imagine with my new Lucidity and Assimilation. Even when grabbing the milkshake, I didn’t dare look at anything until I was done with the drink. I kept the empty container out of my sight. Instead, I looked at the rows and rows of houses in the suburban neighborhood.

It was strange. I wasn’t sure if it was a side effect of the Dream healing poor vision I didn’t even know I had or my increased stats, but everything looked so much more vivid than before. From the grass to the trees, I could make out small details that I had never noticed before. I thought of the scene I had painted on the doorsteps of my parents’ house in the Dream and how I thought it looked so much more real than my world. I didn’t know if that was true anymore. The houses were still as cookie-cutter as ever, but now I spotted the wood grain. The flecked paint. The single plant by every door. Details I hadn’t noticed, even though I’d walked the street every day growing up.

Despite my reluctance, my parents had also gotten me a burger. We ate outside on the back patio. It tasted bland in a way I had never experienced before. Like I had developed new taste buds that were being newly disappointed by their reality. I tried not to let the disappointment show on my face.

“Have you ever noticed how the houses all look pretty similar?” I asked through a covered hand.

My dad perked up a little, “Yeah! They’re called cookie cutter houses. When the college was created a bunch of the administration pooled their money together to buy up all the land.”

“That,” I hesitated, “Sounds illegal.”

“Oh, definitely,” he responded, “but since each of them, technically, independently bought land not through a company, it was ignored. The bids were really high, though and there was a clause saying that all of the land had to have a certain level of urban development after five years or else the land would be taken back. The government thought that with the high bids that they wouldn’t have enough money to actually develop the land.”

“What does that have to do with cookie cutter houses?” I asked.

“Connect the dots. I gave you everything you need.” Was his answer.

I frowned and gave it some thought. “What constituted urban development?”

My dad looked to my mom with a shrug, she returned the gesture. “We can only assume.”

That wasn’t helpful. A moment later, I put two and two together, “Cookie cutter houses are cheaper to build because they’re all the same or at least similar. They built the houses at scale, that’s why they all look the same.”

He smiled, and I could almost hear my mom's eyes roll at the conversation. “Yep. You’ll notice small variations when they ran out of material or had to source from somewhere else. So, behold,” he gestured around at the rows and rows of houses, “nightmare suburbia.”

“Honey,” my mom cut in, voice a little chiding, “nightmare suburbia is harsh. It’s not all bad. It tells you who takes care of their houses. Like how I grow flowers in the front or how our neighbors string up lights.”

“What about snowflakes?” I asked with a laugh. I wanted to play it off as a joke.

My dad raised a brow, “What about them?”

“Why are they all the same?” I leaned back, but my heart thrummed.

He laughed and shrugged, “You’re the one in college. You tell me.”

I frowned but nodded. My mom joined the conversation, “Why? It didn’t bother you before.”

Dad shot her a look, but she and I both ignored it.

“Was just making a joke. I’m gonna get some water.” I dismissed the topic and stood.

I thought about the houses and the Dream, about the new details. More possibilities swirled around my head. Most ideas were disregarded, but I added the theory that the Dream might be overlaying those details into my world as a possibility.

I returned to the table with a glass of water and stared into the backyard garden full of growing plants. “When’d you start gardening?” I asked my mom.

“Wondering when you’d notice. Earlier this year. The tomatoes are especially tasty. The basil is really easy to grow too.” She hesitated for a second, “I could use the extra pair of hands if you’re up for it.”

I continued to stare at the garden. I nodded. “That sounds fun. Sure.”

A subtle sense of dread caressed the ridges of my brain. Something felt wrong, and I couldn’t tell what.

I didn’t want to think about it, but the thought wouldn’t go away. It kept digging at me, and I suddenly wished I had kept my eyes open during the car ride. The ever present static in the corner of my vision flared. I pulled up my interface.

[Congratulations, you have unlocked the tutorial by collecting over 5,000 Presence.]

[Parts of the tutorial are irrelevant to you and, as such, have been removed.]

I barely managed not to reel back. I had forgotten about this. I got so many rewards for what I did in the last Instance. I was so concerned with the Survivors that I hadn’t really thought about the walls of implanted information zapped straight into my brain.

The most concerning thing of them all was the System calling this the tutorial. I had already done the tutorial, hadn’t I? It was the shittiest, most bare bones tutorial I had ever seen, but I had only become a Visitor after I completed it.

So, what the hell could the System deem so important that it required a new tutorial?

I focused on that portion of the static and thought of the tutorial. My heart froze, and the ice crawled through my bloodstream. My throat felt heavy and a nauseous feeling gripped me. I thought of the fields Instance and my crowbar Peeler. I thought about the first time I had used it successfully on that crate and peered within. One second nothing, the next a box. That was it, something truly mundane compared to when I used it on one of the Chimera’s. What I saw there I hadn’t understood back then, but now I do. Looking at the tutorial, I began to understand, and I wish I hadn’t.

[Tutorial Task 1: Fiat lux - Decree the existence of a box.]
