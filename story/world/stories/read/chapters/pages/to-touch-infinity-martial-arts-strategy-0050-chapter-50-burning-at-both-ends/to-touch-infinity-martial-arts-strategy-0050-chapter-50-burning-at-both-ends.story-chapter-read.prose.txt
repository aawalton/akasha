The open-air amphitheater where the auction was taking place was suffocatingly hot, and dense with swarming bodies. Down on the stone steps that made up the spectator seats, Yan Shu casually pulled the broad brim of his bamboo hat lower, tilting his head just enough to ensure the shadow obscured his face. He had traded his official embroidered robes for the plain, gray cotton of a capital commoner. If any of his superiors from the Imperial Library spotted him moonlighting, he would spend the next three months copying shipping manifests.

The young poet kept his back pressed against a decorative stone pillar as he watched the scene unfold.

In the center of the amphitheater floor stood an imposing bronze pedestal holding a thick candle. The candle was scored with deep horizontal notches, each of which represented one hour. The very lowest notch, heavily marked with charcoal, marked the final phase of the auction. When it was reached, the open shouting would cease, and the final, secret bids would have until it fully burned out to be registered.

The Black Ink Consortium’s chief representative on the floor, a sharp-faced director with small eyes and an immaculately groomed beard, looked like he was trying to slowly swallow a piece of sharp glass. He had probably expected this auction to be all but unattended. And it probably would have been, if word of its existence hadn’t somehow made its way to the various merchant houses now crowding the stage.

“Fifty thousand taels!” a representative for the Grand Canal Maritime Guild roared, raising a small flag with their guild’s mark on it.

The Consortium’s representative flinched, his fingers wringing his silk sash anxiously as he barked a slightly higher counter-offer. The numbers were escalating fast, and the atmosphere in the arena was slowly reaching a fever pitch the capital hadn’t seen in decades.

“Seventy-five thousand taels!”

The voice belonged to the chief negotiator of the Sichuan Jiaozi Coalition, a stout man in deep purple silks who sat perfectly still, his face unreadable. The Black Ink Consortium’s representative stared daggers at him, and after registering another bid, stomped away from the arena as if on a mission.

“Now where could you be headed, my little weasel? Off to visit some nest in search of eggs?”

Slipping away from his pillar, Yan Shu followed after him, doing his best to blend in with the crowd of servants swarming the venue. He managed to keep eyes on the director’s expensive silk robes all the way until the man finally vanished into a small stone passageway. Following him became more difficult as the noise of the crowd receded into the distance, causing the young man’s heart to race faster and faster as the danger of being caught continued rising.

After several turns, the representative disappeared again, this time into a large room. The young Collator crept alongside the outer wall and approached the door, holding his breath and leaning his ear towards the gap where the hinge met the wall. He flinched back in surprise when the closeness proved unnecessary.

“This is not what we discussed!” the Consortium director’s voice hissed, clearly audible and full of anger.

“Keep your voice down, Director. Do you think I wanted this circus? I buried the paperwork, exactly as we agreed. It’s not my fault they found out. Someone must have found the posted notice.”

“You told me it was in an unused latrine! That while it was technically part of a public building, the structure had been abandoned!”

“Yes, well…someone must have entered and used it. Desperate people can be unpredictable, you know.”

“This isn’t over, Minister. When my superiors hear of this…”

Yan Shu could almost hear the sound of the Minister’s eyebrows raising. “Be very careful what you say next, young man. Before I decide to throw you and your superiors in jail for trying to bribe an Imperial official. I understand you’re upset, but you’re grossly overestimating your leverage here.”

After a moment of stunned silence, the sound of a hand firmly clapping onto a shoulder echoed out into the hallway. “But, I have good news for you, my friend! You can still get the contract. Just go out there and win the auction the old-fashioned way. Now...I think we’re done here. Get back out there and bid – or get the hell off of the auction site.”

Yan Shu smiled, a quiet, delighted chuckle welling up from inside his chest. Before either of them could come out, he straightened his back, quietly spun on his heel, and casually drifted back towards the main amphitheater.

The auction continued, and the price being bid for the contract continued to rise. By the time the flame was nearing the final notch, the leading bid had climbed to a value of one-hundred and fifty thousand taels. When the flame finally crossed the line, the sound of a herald striking the bronze gong rang out, immediately silencing the noisy room.

The open phase of the auction was officially over. It was time for the final bids to be registered.

A junior clerk – a nervous, shaking boy whose uniform was two sizes too big – stepped forwards onto the floor. He carried an ornate wooden box, which was neatly decorated, covered with lacquer, and fitted with a brass slot.

“The final notch has been reached,” the herald announced. “You have until the candle expires to register your final bids.”

The sound of the dripping wax was practically audible in the venue. Drip. Plop. Drip. The representative of the Sichuan Jiaozi Coalition was the first to finish writing and stand up. He walked to the box in the center of the floor and dropped a small slip of mulberry paper into the slot.

The Consortium director stared at the box as additional merchant groups came forwards to register their bids. His hand shook slightly as he dipped his brush into a portable inkwell, frantically scrawling a final number onto his slip. He blew on the ink to dry it before stepping forwards and thrusting his own bid firmly into the lacquer box.

When the candle finished burning, the bronze gong rang again. The box was lifted to the Minister’s dais. The junior clerk stepped up and broke the seals keeping the lacquer lid in place.

Yan Shu leaned forwards, his eyes narrowing. This was the moment they would find out who had won.

The junior clerk reached inside and pulled out the slips. He reviewed the final numbers silently. His eyes darted back and forth across each of them, a bead of sweat forming at his temple as he read. When he finished reviewing all the final bids, he looked up.

“The final bids have been verified. The contract for the toll rights to the Shandong Imperial Highway are hereby awarded to the winner of this auction: the Black Ink Consortium!”

A collective gasp rippled through the hall. The Consortium director collapsed back onto his bench, letting out a long, shaky breath. He had won. But as Yan Shu looked at the man’s hollow eyes and sweat covered face, it was clear the victory had come at a cost.

Yan Shu adjusted his bamboo hat again, a small, satisfied smile spreading across his face. This was the most fun he’d had in what seemed like a lifetime.

As the crowd began to filter out of the stone amphitheater, the young scholar’s eyes drifted towards the representative of the Sichuan Jiaozi Coalition.

The southern merchant was standing up, scowling in disappointment, and clearly unhappy about being outbid.

Yan Shu’s grin broadened. The Consortium thought the battle was over. That they had survived the flood. But had they? He glanced back at the victorious representative.

“Oh, my dear ferret-like friend. For a member of an evil syndicate, you are quite naive. This isn’t over yet – by any means. You think corruption only goes in one direction? Oh, no, no, no. The capital has more excitement in store for you yet!”

The young poet chuckled again, humming to himself as he casually strolled back towards the Imperial library. He was thoroughly enjoying every moment of being a spy.

Nearly two thousand pairs of marching feet raised a dust cloud all across the mountain pass leading away from Mt. Meng.

Riding atop a fresh stallion, Lesser Elder Wi Mo gently flicked open his fan. The air was brisk, but a cold sweat prickled at the nape of his neck. It had been weeks, yet his throat still ached with a phantom pain where the blood-stained edge of Eom Tae-seok’s blade had bit into his skin.

Humiliation. It was a bitter taste that he hadn’t been able to wash out of his mouth since the battle at Iron Creek.

On paper, it certainly seemed like they had won. The Iron Creek mine was firmly under Meng-shan control, and soon the iron it produced would be outfitting their soldiers. But what should have been a glorious victory had been tainted at the last moment. They had lost hundreds of disciplined spearmen, had their garrison at the mine butchered, and – most gallingly – had lost a High Elder. All that, just to claim a single plot of ferrous earth.

The Meng-shan could not afford to lose many High Elders. They didn’t have very many; most of their troops were mediocre, mass-produced spearmen. The High Elders were the bedrock of the sect, to whom all the regular disciples turned for guidance. To lose one was to lose a piece of the sect’s soul.

Of course, the Patriarch had been furious. The memory of standing in the grand hall, feeling the crushing weight of the Sect Leader’s disapproval, still made Wi Mo’s stomach turn. It had taken every ounce of his political cunning, a total draining of his own personal coffers, and the deliberate sacrifice of two regional logistics directors to shift enough blame to keep his own head attached to his shoulders.

Now, he was marching off to seize yet another mine. But this time, the sect had flat-out refused to supply him with a trump card. The Patriarch wasn’t about to risk losing another High Elder in the conflict.

A fact which left Wi Mo facing a massive power imbalance in the field. The Tai-shan forces were presumably still anchored by that disfigured monster; the man who had plowed through a wall of spears and cleaved a horse in half. Without a martial powerhouse of their own to check the veteran general, the losses amongst the rank and file could quickly turn catastrophic.

Fortunately, where martial might fails, technology can sometimes provide a way.

Silver opens many doors that would otherwise stay closed, even in conflicts such as this one. Their affluent allies at the Black Ink Consortium had a vested interest in continuing to strangle the Tai-shan. When Wi Mo had hinted at his predicament to them, the Consortium’s directors had used their immense capital to acquire something that would help equalize the situation.

Wi Mo pulled the reins of his stallion, turning the beast slightly to look back down the length of the marching column.

Located at the rear, behind the marching troops but in front of the supply wagons, flanked by two entire squads of elite guards, was the Consortium’s answer to the question of how to deal with the martial world.

The siege ballista was an imposing apparatus made of iron-braced oak. It was so heavy that it required its own team of six oxen just to barely drag it down the road. The device was built from three enormous composite bows, intricately linked together with a complex web of gears, pulleys, and winches in order to combine their draw strengths. Winding it up required multiple men working in parallel, each turning one in a series of enormous iron cranks.

Wi Mo had watched a trial firing back at the sect; a single bolt had blown apart a thick stone wall and torn up a long streak of ground behind it. He was looking forwards to seeing what it did to a man. And to one man, in particular.

The Lesser Elder clacked his fan shut, a grim smile slowly spreading across his face as he turned his horse back towards the front of the line.

Let the Tai-shan think relying on their Elders will save them. Let them think they have an advantage to press. Because the next time Wi Mo encountered that old monster, Eom Tae-seok was not going to survive their reunion.
