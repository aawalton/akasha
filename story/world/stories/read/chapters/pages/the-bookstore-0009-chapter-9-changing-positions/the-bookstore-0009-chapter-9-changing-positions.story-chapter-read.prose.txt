Dorgh walked leisurely along the dusty road, letting his boots carry him while his mind went through the same old numbers.

The money they had earned from that naïve northeastern girl had already vanished into a few urgent debts and another stretch of repairs on the road to the province capital. In a practical sense, the deal had been good for the town. In a broader sense, it changed nothing. Dorgh understood the mayor’s ambition and, on his better days, even respected it. Still, every time he looked at Mountainend with clear eyes, he reached the same conclusion.

Trying to revive the town was futile.

To quiet that familiar heaviness in his chest, he chose to set such thoughts aside for an afternoon and check on the bookstore instead. The young woman should already be feeling the first blows of reality by now. A store in that location, in this town, with that kind of business... despair should have set in quickly enough.

When the building came into view, he straightened his vest and prepared himself.

This time, he had come on foot. Paying for a coach just to visit a roadside store would have been an unnecessary luxury, and Dorgh prided himself on not wasting coin where coin need not be wasted. As he approached the side entrance, he was already arranging the proper words in his head. Nothing too harsh. A few sympathetic remarks. A practical suggestion or two. Something to soften the embarrassment of poor business without making the girl feel pitied.

Then he stepped inside and forgot every one of those words.

Someone was sitting at the far-left table, reading.

A customer.

Dorgh stood there for a moment like a man who had opened the wrong door. All his earlier assumptions shattered at once. She had done it. Somehow, that strange girl with the decaying craft and the absurdly named store had gotten a customer before the week was even over.

“Welcome...” Anne began automatically, then looked up and blinked. “Oh, Mr. Dorgh. It’s you. Come in, come in. Don’t stand there like that. Have a cup of tea.”

Her voice was calm, but only because she forced it to be. In truth, she was immediately alert.

Had he come for town matters? For more business? Out of curiosity? She would not dare hope for another customer so soon, but hope had a way of blooming anyway. Still, she kept her expression smooth and glanced only once toward Rick, who remained deeply engrossed in the Syllable and seemed completely unaware of the dwarf’s arrival.

Anne noticed Dorgh staring.

She also noticed that Rick did not.

Interrupting someone’s reading without cause was among the worst offenses possible inside a bookstore, so she dismissed the impulse quickly. There was no need to drag the boy from his study merely to satisfy the manager’s surprise. On the contrary, the scene itself was useful. It showed a customer absorbed enough to forget the world. That image had its own persuasive power.

And if Dorgh was here, then perhaps he could be turned into a regular too.

Tea and a little gossip, then.

The invitation broke the dwarf out of his improper staring, and Dorgh quickly recovered his usual bearing. Drawing his attention away from the reading hunter, he walked toward the counter and thanked Anne before taking a seat. A warm cup was already waiting for him, which denied him the comfortable option of refusing politely.

He was not fond of tea. Given the choice, he would rather drink a mug of beer regardless of the hour, weather, or company. Still, he knew enough not to hold dwarven drinking habits against those raised differently, and the girl’s open expression made refusal feel ungracious.

“How are you faring, Miss Anne?” he asked, still looking at the cup more than the woman who had served it. “I must admit, I’m surprised you already have customers. Our town tends to be... a little slow when it comes to attracting business.”

He was too direct to hide his surprise entirely, though experience kept the sharper edges off his words.

Anne smiled faintly.

“Well, I still have a lot of work ahead of me, but things are settling nicely enough. It’s a slow start, yes, but I’m confident it will work out.” She glanced toward Rick with the easy warmth of a satisfied proprietor. “Rick is a wonderful customer. Quiet, eager to learn... Don’t you know him? He seemed like a local.”

She asked it as though the subject meant nothing to her beyond the obvious.

It did not fool Dorgh for a moment.

Still, it was politely done, and that counted for something.

His brows rose as he matched the name to memory. Then understanding lit his face. Ah. So that was how she had managed it. His old instincts had not failed him after all. A strange business in a poor town had not magically created demand overnight. She had simply caught one of Mountainend’s eager but underused boys in her net.

“Oh, indeed, indeed. Young Rick is certainly eager,” Dorgh said kindly. “His family is one of the original settler lines. They own a carpentry shop in town. His father is one of our best builders, and his mother has a fine hand for carving. Actually, I had intended to recommend them if you needed help with any more complex work, but it seems you no longer require a referral.”

Anne missed nothing.

The condescending softness beneath the manager’s tone was clear enough once one knew to listen for it. Rick’s relationship with the town—and likely with his family—was more strained than the boy himself had said. That matched Anne’s earlier suspicions. He had the look of someone underestimated so often that he had begun believing it.

Interesting.

Still, she let the subject pass for now.

“How do you like the tea?” she asked.

The question was innocent enough on the surface, but Anne had not missed his resistance to touching the cup. Normally, making a potential customer uncomfortable went against her instincts. Yet the tea remained one of her best tools against reserve, pride, and caution. For some people, warmth and taste worked more efficiently than logic ever could.

Dorgh forced a smile and looked down at the drink again.

Had the question not been asked so directly, with such apparent innocence, he might have refused and explained his distaste for soft drinks. But doing so now would have made him seem petty. Worse, it would have made him seem afraid of a cup of tea in front of a girl young enough to be his daughter.

So he lifted it and took a sip.

The effect was immediate.

First came the scent, gentle and floral, carrying a freshness unlike any herbal drink he had tasted before. Then came the warmth, the faint bittersweetness, the soft weight of the liquid settling into his stomach with a depth that felt strangely familiar. Something in the aftertaste tugged at his memory—of youth, ambition, and handful of half-buried dreams he preferred not to revisit too often.

Confused, Dorgh took another sip.

No, it truly was good.

Excellent, even.

It reminded him, faintly, of something far more expensive. Something royal, almost. He dismissed the absurd thought quickly, yet the comparison would not leave him entirely.

Dorgh liked to think of himself as a fair judge of things. Still, admitting he enjoyed a non-alcoholic drink proved unexpectedly difficult. After a short internal struggle, fairness won.

“Good,” he said. Then, with increasing conviction, “Very good. Lass, this tea is unlike the usual sort entirely. I’ll be honest with you—besides water, I don’t usually drink anything that wasn’t fermented. But this...” He lifted the cup slightly. “What is it?”

Anne had expected approval, but not such a strong reaction.

His senses were sharper than she first assumed. That meant she would need to tread a little more carefully with him than with Ceres or Rick. Even so, the line of conversation had already bent her way. There was no point retreating now.

“It resembles a mystic drink a little,” she explained. “That is likely why it tastes this way.”

“Oh!” Dorgh said in response, his expression brightening with recognition. “No wonder. No wonder at all...”

He fell briefly into recollection, and Anne caught the spark in his eyes before it faded. Not simple appreciation. Longing.

Now that was worth far more than a compliment.

“Has Mr. Dorgh tried mystic drinks before?” she asked, letting curiosity sit naturally in her voice.

The question was fair, and Dorgh knew it. But he still cursed himself inwardly for having revealed enough to invite it. If he had kept his mouth shut, he could have enjoyed the tea and gone home without old frustrations being stirred.

Too late for that.

“I have,” he admitted. “They’re rare outside the Emperor Seat, but not in dwarven lands. We are not particularly good with potions, but mystic brewing...” He smiled despite himself. “That, you could call a dwarven craft. I tasted them a few times in my younger years. I liked them well enough, but they are expensive, difficult, and hardly the kind of thing one gets to enjoy often.”

Anne could have challenged his claim about the origins of the craft. She owned at least one or two books that would contradict that popular belief. But history, especially the kind passed along through pride and repetition, was rarely worth correcting unless correction served a purpose.

The light in his eyes, coupled with the unmistakable note of regret, told Anne more than a longer confession might have. Of all her talents with people, the one she trusted most was her ability to find traces of an old dream before the person speaking realized they had exposed it.

She chose a simple trap.

Too simple, perhaps, but the moment felt right and impatience won over elegance.

“You are right, it is difficult,” Anne said, sounding almost rueful. “I tried following the instructions for some of the easiest recipes once or twice and failed miserably. It takes talent... and serious effort.”

The effect was instant. Dorgh’s head snapped toward her.

“Miss Anne,” he said before thinking better of it, “you have instructions for brewing mystic drinks? How?” Suspicion rose just behind his excitement. “It cannot be what I’m thinking, can it? That kind of thing would be far too valuable...”

Anne cursed herself inwardly. She had pushed too fast.

A better line, a subtler line, would have been wiser. But the mistake had been made, so all that remained was to advance bravely.

“Mr. Dorgh,” she said, allowing a touch of wounded dignity into her tone, “have you forgotten my occupation already?”

He opened his mouth, but she continued before he could answer.

“I am a bookmancer. An archivist. My duty is to record and spread knowledge.” Her voice softened again, though the pride remained. “Mystic brewing may be rare, but it is still a craft like any other. That means it can be taught. I cannot claim I recorded it myself, of course, but I did come across an ancient book with some recipes.”

The dwarf stared at her.

Then his eyes widened, and something old and bright lit behind them so suddenly that Anne nearly smiled outright.

There it is, she thought.

Another customer, caught.
