“Think there’s anything above epic?” Pup asked, admiring his prize.

As luck would have it, he turned out to be the winner of the last trial, which had earned him the right to pick first. I had been fifth, which was two spots shy of earning a black curse.

“Stop gloating,” Rayle hissed.

He had come in second and was really pissed that Avon had let the kid win the prize. To a certain degree, I was pissed as well. While all of us had several Grand Arcana cards and agreed with the general idea that members of the group should develop equally, each would have preferred to become the owner of an epic.

“It’s said that there’s a rarity above it,” Sparta said as she leaned on my shoulder. “A great and special card type that has the power to change everything.”

“Bullshit.” Rayle spat on the floor. “If there was anything above epic, someone would have found it by now. Just look at him!” He pointed at Pup. “He can’t stop bragging about it to fart! Think someone with anything better would keep it inside?”

As crude as the analogy was, it made a lot of sense. Anything stronger than an epic would be able to transform a participant into a deity. Then again, boosting that card to its full potential had to be a nightmare. Of all the Grand Arcana cards in my possession, I had only managed to boost one of them once. That had come as a reward from Athena herself. The goddess was as scary as the rumors went, even if she was easy on the eyes, as Avon would say.

“Yeah, but what if there was?” Pup asked. “Just imagine what monsters we could take on.”

“We can take on some pretty big monsters even now,” Rayle said. “If lightbulb would let us.” he glanced at Avon.

“It isn’t a sprint, kid,” Avon said, ruffling Pup’s hair. “It’s a walk. A very slow, painful walk. All that sprinting does is get you killed.”

“Then why don’t you let me win all the trials?” Rayle crossed his arms. “If sprinting is death, I’ll take all the risks, while you get to crawl in peace.”

From my perspective, the scene had taken place years ago. According to reality, there were seven years until it happened. I still remembered it vividly, though. That was the first time cracks had appeared in the group. Before that, we worked together as a team, largely thanks to Avon. Sparta would come and go. We had just started sleeping together, so in a way, she too was considered an honorary member, if such a thing existed.

One spat in the preparation hall had quickly brought us back to reality. No matter how much effort was invested in taping over that single obvious fact, sooner or later, the ugliness of the grand Arcana would seep through.

I finished what was left of my cup of water and watched it refill. Thanks to the tasks I had performed for the deities during this trial, I also had a dish that gave me three slices of bread per day, but I wasn’t hungry.

Above me, the moon was almost at its zenith in a sky that had no clouds.

When I had started my second run, I had been convinced that Harlequin was the greatest monster of them all. Now, I was starting to have my doubts. A deity that broke the rules of the Grand Arcana for some reason I couldn’t even understand scared me a lot more.

Constantly going on trials without an escape in sight was soul-crushing, dangerous, and often lethal, but at least everyone seemed to know what they were doing. The tort was that all of us had been rats in the lowest level of a maze. Earning the power that I had earned me the right to peek slightly over the walls, yet I still couldn’t see what lay beyond.

It’s time, Serp said.

I looked at the moon again. There were still a few minutes until it was at its peak, though that was no reason I couldn’t start the trip now. From where I was, it was going to take me roughly twenty minutes to reach the hill within the crystal fields. Even with the new ring I had obtained, the effects were going to be considerable. Magic was out of the question, not to mention I had to keep Serp and all of my other weapons within my inventory.

“Let’s get this done with,” I said as I went forward.

The worst mistake anyone could make while crossing the Crystal Fields was to run. It was one of those counterintuitive things, like running during a downpour. The more crystal spires one walked past, the more they drained one’s life and magic. Running only made them pull more.

All the time, I felt a prickling sensation, as if ants were crawling all over my body. I ignored it, focusing on what was ahead. Soon, the forest behind me vanished, hidden by the field of spires. One could almost call them beautiful if they weren’t so deadly. Now and again, I’d come across skeletons on the ground: animals chased into the fields by greater predators, participants that had lost their senses, even criminals who had been cast out from the nearby city, condemned to a painful death.

Some of the remains had jewelry and remnants of clothes still on. I didn’t pause to pick up a single one. There would be plenty of time to collect them on the way back, assuming they were worth anything.

Nothing regarding the hill indicated that it was special. Most dream spots were like that… until someone took a nap there. The information regarding their location was always valuable. Those with the ability to walk within dreams had a chance of obtaining an interesting conversation, a pet, or a debilitating nightmare, depending on their luck. As for the rest of us, more often than not, they had never woken up. There was a time when Rayle would spend a fortune on merchants, buying up any information on the matter. I never knew what he did with it. One day, he just stopped and never discussed the matter further.

“Ready for what’s coming, Pup?” I asked.

The dream beast barked in my mind.

“You, too, Serp.”

Yeah, yeah… The saber’s annoyance was palpable. Just don’t die, old man.

I cracked a smile, then found a place to lie down among the crystals.

“Sleep,” I muttered.

When I opened my eyes again, the crystals were gone. I was in a field early on a foggy day. If I didn’t know better, I could almost think that I was back on Earth. Everything was so real: the breeze, the sounds of the birds in the distance, even the dawning rays of the sun. If I looked at the horizon, however, the mistakes became apparent. The mist formed a wide circle around the fields, continuing up into the sky where it turned into clouds. Even listening to the sounds, one could come to the conclusion that they were coming from the air itself. There were no birds as far as the eye could see, and yet if I closed my eyes, I could swear that they were a few feet away.

A drooly Dalmatian was the final proof I needed, rushing towards me as if it had known me its entire life. In a way, one could say that it did, but only because it was my dream creature.

“Yes, Pup,” I said, scratching him behind the ears. “Are they out there?”

The god suddenly turned, pointing towards a section of the distant fog. Seconds later, it began to growl.

“How many?” I asked.

All I got in response was three barks. By that, I could only assume that an entire skulk had gathered. Unlike the common dream beasts, skyfoxes liked playing with their prey. From what I knew, they would create a sense of false security in the dreamer, leaving days, even weeks, to pass without incident. Then they would storm him all together, devouring all elements of the dream along with the person’s sanity. Lucky participants woke up with traces of trauma. Others drooled out on the spot, incapable of speech or movement.

Why do the worst monsters have the most innocent-sounding names? I thought as I summoned Serp.

I bet a lot of beings out there are wondering the same about you, old man, Serp chuckled. You’re just a Greg, and look how many you’ve killed.

“Not enough,” I said out loud, then used the Thirteen of Chalice to launch a force thrust into the distance.

Initially, nothing happened. It seemed that my attack was completely wasted, flying through the air with no enemy to target. The moment it struck the fog, the entire wall of mist stirred.

“That hurt, didn’t it?” I asked, combining my next strike with a multi-attack.

Waves of strikes descended on the same spot, causing ripples in the fog like bullets in water. For three full seconds, I persisted until the skulk finally tipped their hand.

An indescribable scream filled the air: yells mixed with hissing and coughing and yelps that only foxes could combine into one sound.

An entire section of the fog ripped open, taking with it the surrounding fields and even the sky.
