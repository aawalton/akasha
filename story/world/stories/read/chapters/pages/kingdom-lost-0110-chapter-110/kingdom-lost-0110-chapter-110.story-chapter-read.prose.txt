The lock gave her the quarter turn and then it gave her nothing, the same as before.

Riley held the pressure for a count of three, the slow even pressure her father had taught her was the only kind that worked on small mechanical things, and the cylinder locked against the next motion the way a door resists against the wrong key. She eased off and tried it the other way. She tilted the blade higher, then lower. The lock did what old locks do when they have decided about you. Nothing.

"You win," she told it. "Again. Enjoy it."

She needed air. She needed to be somewhere that was not at this table.

She went down the stairs and out into the yard.

The yard at midday had no edges to it anymore. Two weeks ago she could have counted the people in it from the tower doorway. Now she could not. The soldiers had the new longhouse frame up along the eastern wall and their hammers fell out of time with one another. Women carried water from the well in a line that did not stop. Children ran between the foundations of buildings that were not buildings yet. The cookfires had doubled since the Hoshin came through the gate, and the smell of them reached her halfway across the open ground, smoke and barley and not enough meat.

She crossed the yard and went out through the gate toward the river, past the sentry who straightened when he saw her and eased when she lifted two fingers off the coil at her hip, and the noise of the settlement fell behind her and a different noise came up to meet it.

The pit kiln dug into the ground was by the south bank, where the good clay ran in a band the length of the river. It had not been here a month ago. Now it was its own small settlement: a row of forming tables under a lean-to, racks of pots drying grey in the air, carts with their beds caked in dried clay, and at the center of it the fire pit.

The pit was the size of a small room and it was working. They had started it slow. She could see that, because the ware near the edges had not cracked, and ware cracked when you brought it up too fast and let the last of the water in the clay turn to steam with nowhere to go. Over the hours the pit had climbed hot, hotter than any cookfire and well short of anything she would have called a real kiln, and the pots showed it. They came out of the ash in patches of red and brown and black and grey, the same firing giving a dozen colors depending on where a pot had sat and how much air had reached it. It was uneven, but this was a process, for something that was new. If they had thousands of bricks, they could build a proper kiln so they could do this right. For now, this was how they had to do it, the old way.

She looked at the bricks. Dozens of them, fired hard and stacked in courses at the edge of the ground, waiting. Those were the new kiln. The real one, the permanent one, the kind that held an even heat and lost fewer pots and did not care which way the wind came across the flat. Someone had been firing bricks in between the pots. The bricks were the thing they were building toward, one firing at a time, using the worse method to make the better one possible.

She stood at the edge of it and let herself not be needed for a moment.

The first time she had been near a fire in this world she had been on the wrong side of starving, jamming a door upright with two benches and talking to her own stomach to keep the fear company. She had measured that whole stretch of her life by whether she would eat and whether the thing at the door would get in. Now she stood on a riverbank with a hundred things half done behind her and a hundred people who all wanted a piece of her attention, and the only quiet she got was the quiet she stole at the edge of a fire pit where nobody had noticed her yet.

"There's never a middle ground in life," she said, almost smiling. "Starving, or swarmed."

Then a sound reached her that did not fit the rest, and the almost-smile went flat. It was not loud. It was two voices that had stopped working and started arguing, and she knew both of them.

Across the pit, on the far side where the charcoal was being raked out to cool, Tama stood with Nami and three of the Hoshin, and the work around them had slowed the way work slows when the people doing it are listening to something they should not be in the middle of.

Riley did not cross to them. She stepped back instead, into the shade of the drying racks, where she could see and hear and not be seen. The moment they saw her she would have to hear what had happened rather then watching it unfold real time.

The pit was running both ways at once. On one side the Hoshin carried finished pots to the fire and set the green ones in among the coals. On the other they raked the burned-down charcoal out of a cooled corner of the pit, shovel by shovel, spreading it to cool the rest of the way, because the charcoal went into the mixture and the mixture went into the pots and Nami had built the whole line so the pit fed itself.

Tama had a shovel in her hand. She was not using it. She was pointing with it, at the charcoal beds and the racks and the carts, and she was talking in the low even way she spoke.

"It would run better the other way," Tama said. "Cool the charcoal first, off the fire, before you carry the pots in. The pots can wait. A fire cannot be unbuilt once it is hot."

"No." Nami did not raise her voice either. She had a stillness in her, the same stillness Riley had seen across the longhouse fire the night the village chose. "That is not the way we do it. There is an order to it and the order was set before you arrived. The pots go in when the pit is hottest or they do not fire clean. The charcoal waits. We already worked this out."

"I am only saying it could be better."

"And I am saying it works."

The three Hoshin between them had stopped pretending to work. One of them was looking at Tama. Two of them were looking at Nami. That was the part that mattered, and Riley saw it from the shade and felt the heart of the problem settle in her chest.

This was not about charcoal. Tama was probably right about the charcoal, and Nami was right that the line was hers, that she had been handed it and built something that worked. Both of them stood on solid ground, and that was the problem, this line only had room for one of them.

She stepped out of the shade.

"Tama."

Both women turned. The three Hoshin suddenly found their work again.

Riley kept her face relaxed and her voice even, the voice she used when she wanted a problem to look smaller than it was. She did not look at the charcoal and she did not look at Nami's line. She looked at Tama.

"Walk with me."

Tama held her eyes a moment. Then she set the shovel against the cart, head down, and gave a single nod.

Nami did not wait to be addressed. She turned to the nearest of the three Hoshin and said something short about the next rack, and the woman moved, and Nami moved with her, and the line went back to running as though it had never stopped. She did not look at Riley as she went. Riley watched her disappear into the work.

She and Tama walked out along the bank, away from the pit, until the scrape of the shovels and the knock of the pots fell back behind them and there was room to talk openly.

Riley stopped.

"Tama," she said. "We have a problem."

Thorne stood at the edge of the cut with the foreman beside him and listened to the man tell it twice. The crew had been working the stand of timber the southern road had opened, the same timber that was going up as bunkhouse frames back home, and an hour past midday the saws had stopped on their own because the men had heard growling in the trees. Not from one place. The foreman was sure of that, and the two men behind him were sure of it too. Growling from the left of the cut, then growling from the right of it, close together, too close together to be one animal walking.

Zelgra stood a pace off with the spear resting in the crook of her arm and her head tilted, listening to the foreman the way she listened to a man quoting her a price she did not believe.

"More than one direction," Thorne said.

"Aye."

"How long since."

"Quarter hour. Maybe less.”

Thorne looked at the tree line.

The crew had done the right thing going quiet and the wrong thing staying. A stand of cut timber was open ground with the cover pushed back to arm's reach on every side, which was good if you wanted to see a thing coming and bad if the thing coming was faster than you. He read the edge of the woods the way he always read it. Not for the animal first. For the balance. The birds were not calling on the left of the cut. They were calling on the right. Something on the left had told them to be quiet, and something on the right had not gotten there yet.

He had hunted the Woodland Wolves before, the ones the system marked Level One, and they were raw, all weight and not much thought, the kind of animal that committed to a lunge and could not call it back. He had killed three of them and worn what they dropped. But growling from two sides at once, close and quick, was not how a Level One moved. A Level One was a hammer. This was something that had learned to be in two places, or something fast enough to seem like two.

"It moves faster than the ones I have taken," he said, half to Zelgra and half to the trees. "A higher one. It will not lunge the way the others did. It will wait, and it will be where you were going to be, not where you are."

"Take the men back to the road," he told the foreman. "Now. Walking, not running. Running calls it."

The foreman took the men back. Thorne watched the cover on the left.

Zelgra did not watch the left. She had turned a quarter to the right, toward the side where the birds still called, and she reached over without looking and closed her hand on his forearm.

"You mean," she said, "like that one."

It had come out of the cover on the right while he watched the left.

It stood at the edge of the cut where the brush had been pushed back, and it had not pushed the brush to get there. That was the first thing Thorne marked, because a Level One could not have come through that growth without announcing every step of it. This one had. It was the wet-slate color of the others, grey going to brown along the spine, but it was bigger, a clear hand bigger at the shoulder, and it carried the size differently. The Level Ones had worn their size like armor they could not take off. This one wore it like a thing it knew how to use.
