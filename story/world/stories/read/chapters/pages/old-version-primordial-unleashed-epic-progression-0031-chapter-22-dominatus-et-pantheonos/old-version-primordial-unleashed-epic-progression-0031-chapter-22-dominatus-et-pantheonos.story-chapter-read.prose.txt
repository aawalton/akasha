As Skippii returned to their companeight’s campfire in the dark of midnight, he found Tenoris snoozed around its embers, wrapped in his legionnaire’s cloak. He yawned and blinked awake as Skippii approached. “How fared your tempering tonight?”

“You shouldn’t have stayed up for us,” Skippii said.

“I was sleeping until you arrived,” he said. “Come now, give me the tale.”

“I’ve accessed something else,” Skippii said as he knelt beside his companion and stretched his hand over the fire, enticing the coals to take flame once more. “A deeper connection with the earth. Not like fire magia. More like what I evoked on the riverbank.”

“Seismorix’s temper?” Tenoris said.

“Perhaps,” he said, glancing at Cliae. “But, it’s mine. It’s not of the Gods.”

“Perhaps you are a God,” Tenoris smiled, though his eyes drifted in fatigue. “Tell me, Cliae. What have you named this new evocation?”

“Seismic Quake,” they said, sitting close to the fire. “But I don’t dare not evoke it strongly while the arcanus is watching.”

“Why? Would she stop you?” Tenoris asked.

“I don’t think so. But I don’t know how she’ll react, or who she will tell.”

“Are the Coven hostile?” Tenoris said.

“I haven’t seen anything of them, but the arcanus is watching me all the time.” Skippii turned and scanned the shadows between the legion’s tents for her form. “Maybe they’re planning something. I doubt they’re idle. I know they don’t like me. But while I’ve got the Imperator’s grace, they can’t act on it.”

The big legionnaire yawned. “Does it make you hungry?”

“Erm, no,” Skippii answered, a little taken aback by his change in tone. “A little thirsty if I stretch myself too thin.”

“How does it feel? Does it itch? Is it like growing pains?”

“This new ability… evocation, felt like I was stretching beyond my body. Now quite painful as it was extremely uncomfortable, and a little unnerving.”

“Do you see any of the Gods?” Tenoris’ eyes gleamed. “You may not recognise them, but I know them all. If you witness a face, or a voice, I could identify it.”

Skippii looked away, ashamed to face Tenoris’ hopeful gaze. He was a man of the Pantheon; he still hoped that one of its Gods would reveal themselves as Skippii’s acheron.

“No,” Skippii sighed. “Not since it awoke within me. No voices.”

“Has Oyaltun come to you again?” he asked.

“She hasn’t,” Skippii said. “She’s watching me just like the rest of them, waiting for me to make a mistake.”

“Her Sentiescence possesses no malice,” Tenoris said softly. “Her guidance is a blessing.”

“Her guidance is absent,” Skippii rebuked. “It sure would be easier if she came down from the heavens and explained everything to me, and to the Imperator and the Coven.”

“She speaks in visions,” Tenoris said. “And dreams.”

“Why not give the Coven visions then, and get them off my back.”

“Perhaps she does,” Tenoris said. “Ah, but they are devoted to Kylin, Stormstress, and they may not heed her Sentiescence.”

“Politics,” Skippii sighed. “We best get to sleep, then. I feel a vision brewing.”

“You do?” Tenoris said.

Skippii laughed. “Sure. I see… millet for breakfast, and a long march ahead of us, and the Octio swishing his whipping-stick.”

“How sage.” Tenoris smiled fondly and rose to retire.

***

That evening, after a long march, the Ninth Legion emerged from the damp woodlands into a forest felled of timber, and the barren fields beyond. Tall grass and weeds conquered the neglected farmland and frail orchards. A wide trampled path rolled over the low hills and onto the flatlands before Nerithon, but fresh shoots sprang up at spring’s fertile command.

No dogs barked at their coming as they traveled westward. Ivy crawled over the cold walls of abandoned farmsteads. All around were signs of the Ürkün’s passage: the charred remains of many hundreds of campfires pocked the earth; bovine creatures—too large to be cows, but too skinny for oxen—abandoned by their masters, grazed aimlessly on the wild crop; and at a junction on the path, ten stakes were raised, and ragged red cloaks fluttered hollowly in the soft wind. As his column passed, Skippii watched slaves dismantle the stakes and unstring the bodies attached to them, burying them in hasty graves.

“The Fifth,” Fulmin said. “Must have been captured in battle.”

“Looks like we’ve arrived just in time,” Orsin said, and saluted the dead. Skippii and his companeight took up the salute, and it spread to the legionnaires about them.

All day, horsemen cantered up and down the column. Each time he heard their hoofbeats, Skippii turned, expecting to see twelve cloaked warmagi advancing. But they were only messengers, or patrols of light Lacustrian cavalry. The legion did not stop for a midday break, but encamped early that evening in a field with a farmhouse at its centre.

As their slave Onionis was preparing their rations, and Cur was heating a pot above the fire, a trumpet rang from afar. Drusilla and Kaesii sprang to their feet and grabbed their weapons.

“It’s ours,” Arius said. “The Fifth.”

The horn blew again, regal and tuneful.

“Their Imperator?” Orsin said.

“That is likely,” Arius agreed.

Leaving their weapons behind, Skippii and his companeight ventured east towards the summons. All around, legionnaires filtered from their campfires until a grand procession amassed at the palisade gates. Their superiors shouted and lashed with canes to force order, and a corridor was formed, leading to the farmstead at the camp’s centre where Titus Virellix held lodgings.

Into their camp rode nine horsemen. Their iron armour was burnished, but their faces were bruised. At the fore rode the Fifth Legion’s Imperator, erect in his saddle. But many of his staff slouched, and their horses’ ribs showed.

“Where’s his crown, if he’s an Imperator?” Kaesii said.

“He won’t wear it in another Imperator’s camp,” Cur said. “He’s not the boss here.”

After the procession passed, their superiors herded them back to their camps.

“Get a move on,” their Octio snarled, flashing his cane at their backs.

“If he could wield a spear like he does that stick,” Orsin said, “the campaign would be over by now.”

“We should try it,” Arius said. “Push him to the front of the phalanx.”

“Did you see how he stayed at the back, at Erithas?” Kaesii said. “I doubt he saw any of the killing.”

“That’s where he’s supposed to stay,” Cur said. “In case the Primus dies, and he has to take over.”

“Gods forbid it,” Tenoris said. “That would doom us.”

As they returned to their camps, Skippii noticed the other legionnaires of his tonnage linger. They talked among themselves, watching him, then a few came forward into their companeight’s camp.

“Hail, Erymenes’ Hammer.” The legionnaire extended a hand, and Skippii shook it.

“Well met,” Skippii said. “Glory Auctoritas.”

“Name’s Junnius,” he said. “Tenth Companeight.”

“Skippii,” he said.

Soon, the whole of Tonnage VI was gathered around their single campfire, huddled around the adjacent tents, attracting more from the cohort at large. Whatever rift had been between him and the other legionnaires now came crumbling down. Skippii shook each of their hands and weathered their nicknames.

“Summitor’s Fist.”

“Light of the Ninth.”

“Seismorix’s Scythe.”

“Skippii the Scortcher,” Kaesii corrected them, but soon grew tired of the effort.

“Glory Imperium Auctoritas,” Skippii greeted them. Though he wanted to dispel the rumours that his powers came from the pantheon, he held his tongue and made his allegiance to the legion clear.

“Skippii, my friend,” somebody spoke and threw an arm over his shoulder, more companionably than was merited. “How are you?”

Skippii inspected his face. “Companeight six. We rescued your companion.”

“Yeah, of course, we’re friends,” he said. “And I know now, how you did that. You used this magia, right? You and your lot.” He grinned at Skippii’s companeight. “You all knew, didn’t you?”

“Of course,” Cur said, busy with his stew pot.

“Why did you keep it a secret?” the legionnaire asked.

“Because you didn’t have to know,” Drusilla said bluntly at Skippii’s side.

“Alright, alright,” the legionnaire withdrew. “I understand. Here, meet my fellows.”

Most of the legionnaires of Skippii’s tonnage were young like him. Many hailed from the capital city of Vestia, or its sister mountainous realm Summitus. However, there wasn’t a corner of the Auctoritas that wasn’t represented by at least one son amongst the Cohort II. Those from Clidus had darkened skin, the colour of sun-ripened olives; the hands of Virelius’ sons were softer, their hair fairer; the Lacustrians’ speech ran as fast as a river, their hair dark, and styled with a single, short braid which they hid from their superiors beneath their helmets. They were all his half-brothers, his distant blood and countrymen.

At the procession’s rear was Custos Maritor—the red plume of his helmet rising above their heads. And beside him, Skippii glanced at the Octio, ever plastered with a sneer. But for the time being, they allowed the informal gathering to go on.

An older legionnaire with grizzled stubble and a crooked eye shook his hand firmly. “Well met, Earth Hammer. I witnessed your deed from the front of the phalanx. Felt the tremors in my feet. Truly, the Gods have blessed you.”

“Glory Auctoritas,” Skippii said, then stammered. He had stomached enough lies and diversions for one day, and they would soon find out for themselves anyway. “When you figure out which God exactly has blessed me, tell me. I’d like to know.”

The legionnaire laughed. “It is not of my ilk to know such things. But you’ve not been visited?”

“No. I’m astray.”

Skippii was suddenly aware of the thin quiet that had spread from their conversation as the surrounding legionnaires listened intently.

“But it changes nothing. I’m still a legionnaire.” Skippii raised his chin to meet the veteran’s keen eye. “My power is in the hands of the Imperator.”

The veteran nodded slowly. “Glory Imperium Auctoritas.”

It was customary to respond with ‘Dominatus et Pantheonos’. However, Skippii opted for something less pantheonic. “Victory to the Ninth.”

“Quickly now,” somebody barked. The Octio strode into the centre of their procession, disciplinary rod in hand. “Say your greetings and be done. Return to your camps. I shan’t have you lagging on the march tomorrow.”

Many grumbled and departed. A few lagged behind to shake his hand, but before long, only his companeight and their Octio remained. The man glowered at him as though he were going to say something, but remained silent.

Skippii hailed him nervously. “Glory Auctoritas.”

The Octio’s eyes narrowed, then their gaze flickered towards their tent where Orsin stood statuesque. The two veterans glared at one another like stags across a field. The Octio’s lips twitched, as though they would say something, but only snarled. Then his eyes returned to Skippii.

“Dominatus et Pantheonos.” He spoke the words like a threat. “Custos Maritor requires your presence.”

“Yes Octio,” Skippii replied, and followed his superior to Custos Maritor’s tent. Upon entering, he found his Primus alone, washing his hands in a basin beneath a shrine. He wore a simple tunic. His bronze armour and brushed-clean cloak hung from pegs nearby. The veteran looked naked without his attire–vulnerable–but as he turned to Skippii, his eyes were as sharp as ever.

“Sit,” he said. “I mean to talk to you about something I mentioned the other day–about me performing a rite of initiation for you.”

“Oh.” Skippii could tell by the sound of his own voice that he had not hidden his apprehension well.

Drying his hands, Custos Maritor came to sit at his side, pouring a small cup of wine for them. “It’s your choice, of course. I could admit you to Maricorus’ care, if you pledge to worship the Swantide.”

He motioned towards a rustic tabletop shrine. Three candles were lit in a trident-shaped holder, lighting a slab of splintered wood, upon which the symbol of Maricorus was painted: a white swan whose wings formed waves. Decorated about it were vials of sand from different shores, seashells and a small band of pearls. A tangle of ropes represented turbulent waves, and atop them rode a small hand-carved galley.

“It may dispel some of the animosity, if you were to be made astral.”

“What does it involve?” Skippii asked. “To be initiated?”

“I will take you to the ocean and declare your presence to Maricorus. You should feel something. For me, it was a rush in my feet, and a feeling of being dragged out of my body. But it passed quickly. Sometimes I can feel it now when I pray.” Custos Maritor creased his lips. “But other than that, nothing much. You can call yourself astral, as long as you perform some basic prayers. You get out of it as much as you put in. If you pray daily, your connection with the Swantide will grow. You may commune with him. I do sometimes. I receive some answers… sometimes not. It depends on the questions you ask. Maricorus is more concerned with the oceans than our lives, you see.”

Skippii lowered his gaze. For a time, he had hidden from the others that he was astral. But recently, it had come to define him, warts and all, and he felt reluctant to give it up on a whim.

“No?” Custos Maritor asked, reading his mood. “Not a good idea?”

“It’s a great idea,” he said. “Thank you for offering. But… if I am to be initiated into the pantheon, I want it to be for a good reason–because I have a connection with one of the Gods, not because I feel coerced into doing so. Not for politics.”

Custos Maritor sat back in his chair, considering him. “Good response. I don’t think you should do it just because the Coven…” he trailed off. “Well, they don’t like you really, do they?”

Skippii laughed at his bluntness. “I don’t think being astral would make a difference. It still wouldn’t explain my magia. And they think that I’m… you know… a heretic.”

“Well, it would disprove that,” he said. “Maricorus would never accept you if your soul were blemished—which, I’m not saying it is.”

Custos Maritor paused, and it struck Skippii to see his superior seem so awkward with his words. “Perhaps it would bring you peace to know you did not walk the mortal plain alone, and unguided.”

“Not alone,” Skippii whispered to himself, then raised his voice. “The Imperator gave me his leave. And, I have my companeight.”

“That’s right.” A warm smile crossed his Primus’ lips.

“I should tell you, I have been training my magia away from camp each night.”

“The arcanus informs me so,” Custos Maritor said, expression unflinching. “As long as it does not interrupt your legionnaire’s duties.”

“It won’t,” he said, raising his chin. “Thank you for the offer, superior, but I think I will decline for now.”

“As you wish,” Maritor shrugged. “I won’t keep you any longer. I’m just as tired from today’s march as you are.”

That night, as Skippii turned in sleep, his dreams were filled with the anxious faces of legionnaires surrounding him. They merged with the faces he knew as a child, when legionnaires towered above him. He gazed up at the men he once knew and told each of them about his new gift. They acted shocked and insisted he inform his mother. But she was nowhere to be seen.

His dream turned into a murky haze. Where was she? In their wagon? But no, she had returned to Auctoritas. He had remained in Philoxenia, and joined Legion Nine.

Suddenly, the mists in his mind cleared. He was alone in a field at night. The sky bore no clouds, yet no stars. A cold finger traced his spine, and a voice breathed down his neck. He could not make out the words, but felt its presence looming over. Skippii felt frozen to the spot, too afraid to turn around. But beneath him, the fires of the earth glowed. He reached for them, and drew them into his breath, and awoke.

Skippii opened his eyes in his companeight’s tent. Beside him, Tenoris snored. His heart was racing, and he was sweating. Lying still, he waited for his nerves to calm, and waited for the morning trumpets to announce the dawn.
