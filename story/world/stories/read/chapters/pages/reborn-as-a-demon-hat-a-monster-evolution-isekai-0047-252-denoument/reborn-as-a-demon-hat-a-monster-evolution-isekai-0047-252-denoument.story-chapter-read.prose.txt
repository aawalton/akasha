“Here it comes…” Keadmon whispered. "The End."

Ethan saw that he was rapt. His eyes were basically glowing. He hadn’t seen the God this excited since the performance began. Right now, he seemed like a kid taking in his first show by himself.

Ethan looked to the stage and saw nothing. The dimmed lights had obscured even the mime that wore his form.

Then, after a few tension-filled minutes, an explosion of green flame erupted from the top of the stage.

The curtains caught fire and began to crumble away. The plagues containing each Lightborn and Archon also began to melt until the paintwork ran dry and crusty, then they simply fell from the rooftops. The searing heat was enough to make Ethan think, for a moment, that Kaedmon was finally doing what he’d planned to do all this time: set a trap for him and blow them both into oblivion.

But then the eyes of the Archon-mime – his eyes -  opened in the darkness. They shone with the same lambent green Ethan had beheld in his dreams, his worst visions of a future that brought total obliteration.

And then with a single swing of two blades, and a maniacal laugh that drowned out all sound, the Archon-mime cleaved into Kaedmon’s Cradle, broke the rock apart, and then was himself enveloped in the raging fires on stage.

Kaedmon let out a breath and closed his eys as the flames threatened to rush towards himself and Ethan.

But when the God opened his eyes again, the flames vanished in an instant.

Everything onstage was blackened as charcoal. None of the players appeared for a standing ovation. Instead, Ethan looked down to see their burnt bodies lying at the bottom of the stage.

“Hm,” Kaedmon said. “I’ve seen better.”

He then rose as nonchalantly as a child waking from their afternoon nap.

“Shall we take in the evening air?” the old God asked. “I love a stroll after a show.”

Ethan double blinked as a rectangular door opened at the far end of the theatre – similar shape and size to a fire escape.

He couldn’t quite see what was beyond it, and at this point, he couldn’t even make an educated guess.

“Are you giving me the choice or telling me to follow you?” he asked.

Kaedmon looked genuinely bemused.

“You need me to give you the choice?”

He walked away without waiting for him, ambling off with a jovial stride as though he’d just seen the most uninteresting show in the whole universe.

“No,” Ethan said before following him out.

...

The outside of the theater ended up being more of a maze than another actual room.

A maze that, like everything else in this realm, seemed to defy the laws of…anything.

Ethan followed Kaedmon through a series of black and white walkways, several converging into each other as though they were moving in real time. Yet, as he strained his eyes, he saw that this was nothing more than an illusion – a trick of sight.

He noted how Kaedmon seemed to skip between black and white spaces on each ‘checkerboard’ style walkway, like he was playing a game of hopscotch that only he was aware of.

Considering that this God seemed quite a bit madder than Ethan had expected, the reality probably wasn’t far from the truth.

As they traversed the maze together, Ethan saw that it began to open out in the middle and start to curve round a large, translucent glass bowl. The bowl was monolithic in size and scale, dominating the series of checkered stairways that began to climb upwards towards a light in the sky above. Within the bowl was a gigantic black and white fish – strikingly similar to a Japanese Koi fish, Ethan noted. It’s long, angular dorsal fins and moustache-like tendrils protruding from its mouth were twitching as it spun around in a circle, never stopping, never slowing, progressing on its pointless path seemingly without alternative.

Ethan clocked Kaedmon’s head rise to inspect the giant creature. Maybe it was what he considered a pet.

“Round and round it goes,” the old God sighed. “No will. No inertia. Just motion. Motion dictated by a cage of glass and a mind that can’t quite conceive of how great its suffering could be.”

Kaedmon stopped on the lip of the walkway to stare into the bowl.

“It can see freedom in every turn, every rotation,” he said. “Yet it knows no other life. Eventually, it learns to forget. For what does it benefit a being to exchange one cage for another?”

Kaedmon palmed the glass affectionately.

“How can you help a living thing that sees what it wants and yet chooses to remain a prisoner?”

As the hand of the God lovingly caressed his pet’s ‘prison’, he suddenly felt the entire glass case vibrate with motion. Slowly, a series of thin cracks began to spread from the right side of the bowl. The sound of crackling glass filled the air, quickly accompanied by the sounds of rushing water and the freed fish that leaped from its cage to follow the stream down into the depths of the checkerboard maze.

And as it fell, the old God noted the odd sense of excitement that he’d felt as it trailed down. That sense stayed with him as he looked to his side and saw Ethan, one sword raised and smoking, staring back at him.

“You can offer it another path,” he said.

Kaedmon smiled.

“That’s what you believe the solution is, don’t you?” he asked. “Just give the people a choice and they’ll make a world that remains solid and stable.”

“A world will exist no matter what they do,” Ethan replied, taking a step towards the God as the water from the broken glass bowl continued to cascade down beside them. “The only thing they must decide is how to live in it.”

“Oh. The world will still exist no matter what its inhabitants choose. You’re certain of that?”

“I’m certain of nothing,” Ethan said. “Nothing, except that your version of order isn’t working anymore.”

Kaedmon’s eyes darted to Ethan’s tightened grips on his blades.

“…on that, we can agree,” the God nodded before about turning and walking up the next flight of steps.

“Come,” he said. “I believe I promised you some evening air.”

Ethan followed with both swords at the ready, fairly certain at this point that the God didn’t have some kind of nefarious trick up his sleeve. If Kaedmon had wanted to resist him, he’d had more than enough chances already.

No, Ethan thought as he climbed the checkered staircase to heaven. It’s almost like he’s…testing me. This is a battle, of a sort. But not the type I’m used to.

Just as he was about ready to quit this whole charade and simply take the head from Kaedmon’s shoulders, a door opened beyond the staircase. And light – beaming and bright – shone into the checkered dimension.

“I think you’ll like this,” Kaedmon told him over his shoulder.

They emerged from the maze into a gilded observation chamber that seemed to hang in the void itself, its walls nothing but thin ribs of gold holding back an endless pane of glass. Beyond it lay Argwyll in full: a vast, turning sphere wreathed in cloud, oceans glinting, mountain chains like scars, city-lights pulsing faintly beneath the twilight band. Ethan didn’t just see it—he felt it. Every breath drawn in a thousand streets brushed his lungs. Every shout, every whispered prayer, every clash of steel and scrape of boot over stone thrummed in his bones. The tang of salt from distant coasts lay on his tongue alongside the copper stench of spilled blood in some nameless alley. Even the Gobrin Deserts, a smear of pale gold from this height, resolved into individual grains of sand in his mind’s eye—each one hot, distinct, counted. Up here, he realized, Kaedmon was letting him borrow a fraction of a god’s endless perception, asking him without words if any mortal could bear it for long. Beside him, Kaedmon sighed.

“Well?” he asked. “You’ve seen it for all its worth. All its glories, all its foibles. Now I’m vain enough to ask you: what do you think? Do you see beauty down there?”

Ethan eyed the God. “Like most things, it depends where you look.”

“Hah! A fine answer, and one which cuts right to the heart of the problem: perception. I must say, you’ve always been rather good at getting to the heart of things. Perhaps you earthlings have some worth after all.”

“We have plenty of our own foibles, too, I can assure you.”

“And yet,” Kaedmon smiled. “There is some beauty in that, is there not?”

The old God’s smile was oddly defeatist. Ethan again had the strange sense that the old man had brought him here in a vain attempt to have someone know his final words. Every step he’d taken up to this place had become more and more labored until now, at the height of his realm, he leaned against the glass pane of his observatory and sighed his deepest sigh yet.

“You wish to know why I let it go on for so long,” he finally said in his strange, sad whisper. “Why endure the Cycle? Why fabricate a Law that ensured conflict, rather than attempt to mitigate it?”

Ethan came into step with him.

“The thought had crossed my mind.”

“I was stalling.”

“Stalling?”

“Stalling for time. For more time. And – yes. I am astutely aware of how utterly childish that sounds. Judge me after our conversation is done – remember?”

Ethan blinked. “That doesn’t really answer the question. You could have changed things up any time you wanted. You could have tried a new path. But you didn’t. Instead, you kept watching your own world burn.”

“Ethan, that’s all we can ever do. No matter what we build, we have to watch it die.”

“If you think that,” Ethan replied. “Then you’re no artist at all.”

His eyes were focused on Argwyll. On Westerweald where the Hybrids had managed to build something of their own. On Eastmarch where Langley was leading a new order in his name, preparing the people for gradual integration. On the caverns that lay beneath the earth, containing Sanctum and all its new environs. On Griffon’s Watch, where the horrors of the old Doctor Haylock were being stripped away.

And on the small isle of Mistborne, where, at the top of the Umbral Order’s highest tower, his friends were waiting.

“You can’t be afraid of endings,” he said, whispering the words at first, slowly rising in pitch as he realized that now he had the full attention of the God-man. “Nothing has an end. Not really. Change is neither a force for good or ill. It simply is. Sure, everything returns to dust. But dust returns when the wind rises.”

He looked at the dejected Kaedmon, seeing for the first time that this was what the God of Argwyll had become long ago. In his mind, he’d imagined anything but a being like this. And he just couldn’t accept that the man was so ready to die like this. In a state of nihilistic detachment.

“All those people down there have managed to do – to believe – so much more than what you’ve ever given them,” he said. “They’re already making something entirely new. Gradually, each and every one of them is learning that they don’t need a God – or a universal Law – to tell them how to live. They’re finally listening to that little voice in them that asks ‘what if?’ And, for as much of an old bastard as you are, that’s at least one thing you got right when you made them.”

Kaedmon’s gaze lingered on him for a while, as though the deity were watching him for traces of hidden motive or meaning. Then, gradually, he smiled again.

“You know the moment when I realized I was done?” he asked. “When I realized that my time as a deity, ultimately, was going to end sooner rather than later?”

“Do tell.”

He leaned back, taking in the sight of his world and the infinity of space around it. When he spoke again, it was with the relief of a man who was happy to leave the world behind.

“It was the moment when I saw that my imagination had run dry,” he said. “Or, to be more precise, I had intentionally limited it. Because I stopped being able to see something that went beyond the bounds of my perception. More than that; I stopped being able to conceive of any possibility, any action, that existed outside my frame of reference. Like a voracious reader who only knows one way to read a story – who believes there is only one kind of story to tell, one way to tell it, and one way to understand it. After a while, that kind of belief becomes a festering sore for both reader and artist – a self-fulfilling prophecy of creative defeatism. A valorization of the derivative and a visceral rejection of authenticity in deference to narratological certainty. A need for objective understanding in a chaotic, uncaring universe. Those are what stories became to me, after a time. Certainty. Little blankets of clarity that I could drape over the harshness of reality. Even if they represented the certainty of nothing more than regurgitation – it didn’t matter. Those stories were mine. And mine alone.”

He raised his pale, wrinkled hands, looking at them like someone who wanted nothing more than to tear them off his arms and throw them into the void of space.

“I became a God who killed his own imagination for the promise of a few more cycles…a little bit more of the same.”

He then turned abruptly – a gesture Ethan would normally associate with the opening of an attack. But he didn’t react. He simply turned to face the old God’s tired eyes, and saw the light of Argwyll’s red sun glinting off them.

And the odd sense of joy that was welling up behind his lids.

“Outside of my control, there is nothing. There should be nothing. And yet, there Artorious was. And now, here you are. Your existence – and triumph – is proof that chaos always returns. Dark always swallows light. Your victory is proof that certainty is the greatest fantasy of all.”

He swallowed, aware that he was finally coming to the main point.

“This tells me one simple fact: this story is no longer mine. And, more than that, I no longer wish it to be. So, to cut a long story short, as you Earthlings say…”

He spread his arms wide and knelt before Ethan, in a gesture so utterly sacrificial that it made Ethan feel a little sick.

Then, all at once, he felt his limbs shift. He felt his right arm stiffen and then thrust out of its own accord. And in the next second, the point of his sword was fixed right at the old God’s wrinkled throat.

“If you must take this head to finish this tale, then my answer is: by all means!”
