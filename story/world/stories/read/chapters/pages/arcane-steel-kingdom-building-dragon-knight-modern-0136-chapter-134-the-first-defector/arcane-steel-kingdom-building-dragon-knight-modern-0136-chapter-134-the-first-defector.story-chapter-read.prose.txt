Cinderfall was drowning in its own paranoia.

I watched the live feed from the ceiling drone with grim fascination. The Hegemony war council had deteriorated into a kindergarten squabble. They were getting their asses handed to them at every turn. Every flanking maneuver was crushed. Every supply line was severed. Every shielded advance was met with precise, overwhelming artillery fire.

To their medieval minds, my tactical awareness looked like omniscience. They couldn't fathom a satellite network or thermal optics. They came to the only logical conclusion their feudal society could generate.

They had a mole.

The heavy weirwood table in the King’s redoubt was surrounded by shouting, red-faced lords. A baron from the eastern marches slammed a fist onto the map. He accused the Duke of the Southern Vales of treason, citing an unpaid debt from three generations ago as irrefutable proof of his defection. The Duke responded by drawing a dagger. Royal guards had to physically wrestle the men apart.

Old grievances flared up. Rivalries that had laid dormant for decades were suddenly weaponized. They pointed fingers over stolen land, slighted daughters, and bad trade deals. The room reeked of sweat and desperate self-preservation.

King Theron sat at the head of the table. He looked like an exhausted schoolteacher dealing with rabid toddlers. The veins in his neck pulsed.

I took a sip of my coffee in the CIC. The entertainment value was high, but the strategic implications were even better.

Theron finally snapped, he brought his gauntlet down on the weirwood table. The wood splintered with a sharp, violent crack.

The silence in the room returned instantly.

"The next man who makes a baseless accusation without ironclad proof loses his head," Theron growled. His amber eyes swept over the paralyzed nobles. "Am I clear?"

The lords nodded, swallowing hard. They sat back down. The shouting stopped.

Theron had applied a temporary bandage to a severed artery. They would no longer bicker in front of him. But the core infection remained. They didn't trust each other.

The immediate fallout was visible on the Oracle’s tactical map within the hour. The massive, cohesive block of the Cinderfall army fractured. The noble houses withdrew their personal retinues from the main camps. They established separate, heavily warded encampments. They refused to share supply lines. They refused to coordinate patrol routes. The fifty-million-strong army devolved into dozens of smaller, myopic factions fighting their own isolated wars in the mud.

They had isolated themselves. It made my job infinitely easier.

Nowhere was this fracture felt more acutely than at the absolute bottom of the hierarchy.

...

Fedric adjusted the leather straps of his oversized breastplate. The cold bit into his fingers.

He had volunteered for this.

When the Hegemony knights rode into his village three weeks ago to drag the able-bodied men away at spear-point, they had found Fedric sitting on his porch. His meager belongings were already packed in a burlap sack. The recruitment officer had stared at him, utterly baffled by a peasant willingly climbing into the transport wagon.

Fedric simply had nothing left to lose.

His village had ostracized him since birth. His father died in a mining collapse before he was born. His mother bled out on the midwife's table. In a superstitious backwater, an orphan born of tragedy was considered a curse. The villagers avoided his gaze. They crossed the street when he walked past. He tilled the rocky soil for the local alderman, receiving a quarter of the wages the other field hands got.

He was suffocating in isolation. Enlisting felt like a way out. He had grown up listening to the bards sing of the military brotherhood. He wanted to share a campfire with men who would guard his back. He wanted a place to belong.

The reality of the Cinderfall camps shattered that illusion on the first night.

There was no brotherhood. The noble retinues kept to themselves, guarding their warm tents and hoarded rations with drawn steel. The later conscripts, the dregs pulled from the dying villages, were shoved into the unwarded outer perimeter.

Fedric found himself assigned to a regiment directly under the King’s command. The veterans called it Death Row. It was a meat shield. A collection of expendable bodies meant to march ahead of the valuable knights and trigger the enemy's traps.

Today was his turn in the grinder.

The staging ground was a scene from hell. The mud was a freezing, knee-deep slurry that smelled of voided bowels and cheap wine. Officers rode up and down the lines, cracking whips against the shoulders of men too terrified to stand.

The tactical briefing was brutally simple. They were being sent into the valley in five-man clusters. They would march forward until the ground exploded or the rapid-fire magic tore them apart. They would exhaust the enemy’s traps with their bodies. Then the heavy infantry would push forward over their corpses.

Fedric stared at his boots. The mud threatened to suck him under.

A heavy hand shoved him hard between the shoulder blades. He pitched forward, his face slamming into the slush. The metallic taste of blood flooded his mouth.

"Get up, rat," a Cinderfall captain snarled. He didn't bother drawing his whip. He simply kicked Fedric in the ribs. "Find your squad. Lock shields."

Fedric scrambled to his knees. His lungs burned. He grabbed the splintered shaft of the iron halberd he had been issued and dragged himself upright. He found four other terrified men. They huddled together, raising their heavy, rusted iron shields into a crude turtle formation.

A horn blew. A long, mournful note that echoed off the distant mountains.

The line lurched forward.

Everything became a disjointed blur of noise and adrenaline. Fedric pushed his shoulder against the man in front of him. The mud sucked at his calves. He kept his head down behind the iron rim of his shield.

The ground fifty yards to his left erupted.

A geyser of black earth and shredded armor shot into the sky. A deafening crack ruptured his eardrums. A hot spray of something wet and metallic splattered against the side of Fedric’s helmet. He didn't look. He kept walking.

Another explosion rocked the earth to his right. The concussive wave knocked the breath from his lungs. The five-man squad beside him simply vanished in a cloud of supersonic steel bearings.

They were approaching the dead zone. The veterans back at camp talked about an invisible line in the ash. Once you crossed it, the rapid-fire turrets engaged.

A blinding flash of azure light illuminated the mud beneath Fedric's boots.

The world tilted.

He flew backward. The concussive force of a near-miss plasma mortar threw him ten yards through the air. He hit the ground hard. His left shoulder popped out of its socket with a sickening crunch. White-hot pain blinded him.

He lay in the freezing sludge, gasping for air. The sky above him was a chaotic crisscross of red and blue tracer fire. The roaring whine of the automated turrets chewed through the remaining squads.

Fedric clutched his halberd with his right hand. His knuckles ached. He needed to get up. He needed to crawl forward and stab the enemy.

A thought pierced the haze of pain.

Why?

He remembered the proclamations read by the Enemy's Heralds just days ago. The Lord of Wight had offered amnesty. Land. Food. In response, King Theron issued a draconian decree. Any soldier who deserted, any man who surrendered to the enemy, would condemn their entire bloodline. Their families would be branded as traitors and executed in the public squares.

It was a terrifying deterrent for a man with a wife and children.

Fedric had no one.

There was no mother to hang. No father to behead. No village that would mourn him. Cinderfall couldn't punish him. He had gambled his life on the Hegemony to find a brotherhood. He had received a boot to the ribs and a march into an abattoir.

Another memory surfaced.

Four weeks ago, silver packages rained from the sky. The villagers in his hometown had swarmed the crates, hoarding the magic food. Fedric had stayed on the outskirts, sitting on his rotting porch. He knew better than to approach the mob. They would have beaten him for trying to claim a share.

But a metal golem had walked away from the crates. It approached his porch. It didn't speak. It simply dropped a warm, silver pouch of beef stew directly into his lap before turning away.

The people of the Silver Lion had fed him when his own neighbors would have let him starve.

Fedric looked at the rusted halberd in his hand. He let go. The weapon sank into the mud.

He forced himself up onto his knees. Agony radiated from his dislocated left shoulder. It dangled uselessly at his side. He planted his right boot in the dirt and stood up.

He raised his right arm high into the air. He couldn't lift his left, so he just let it hang, a clear display of unthreatening vulnerability.

He began to walk.

The battlefield was a cacophony of slaughter. Men screamed. Explosions rattled his teeth. The air tasted of ozone and vaporized copper.

He walked straight toward the Enemy trench lines.

He crossed the invisible boundary. The automated C-RAM domes swiveled on their bases. The mechanical tracking noise whirred in his ears. A turret locked onto his position.

Fedric closed his eyes, expecting the stream of red tracer fire to tear him in half.

The whirring noise shifted. The turret pivoted ten degrees to the right. It opened fire, shredding a charging Cinderfall squad fifty yards away.

Fedric opened his eyes. He kept walking.

He passed a crater. He stepped over the mangled remains of a Cinderfall captain. The defense grid completely ignored him.

The threat-assessment algorithms programmed by Tes scanned every moving object on the field. They tracked velocity, weapon posture, and mana signatures. Fedric held no weapon. His pace was slow. His arm was raised. The logic gates flagged him as a non-combatant. The turrets sought active threats.

He waded through the final stretch of no-man's land. The noise of the battle seemed to fade, muffled by the sheer, surreal nature of his survival. He was a Shadow walking through hell.

He reached the edge of the Enemy fortifications.

The contrast was jarring. There was no mud here. The trench floor consisted of interlocking metal grates. Heavy pumps hummed quietly, draining the excess water into subterranean channels. The walls were reinforced with clean, stamped steel.

A figure in matte-black armor stepped out from behind a heavy plasma emplacement.

Fedric braced himself for the execution.

The soldier reached up. A hiss of depressurizing seals echoed in the trench. The black helmet retracted, revealing the face of a young elven man. His silver hair was clipped short. His eyes held no malice.

The elf reached over the sandbags. He grabbed Fedric by his good arm and hauled him down into the dry, secure trench.

Fedric collapsed onto the metal grating. He clutched his dislocated shoulder, his breathing ragged and shallow. He waited for the interrogations. He waited for the chains.

The elf didn't draw a weapon. He reached into a storage compartment on his chest rig. He pulled out a thick, thermal-lined blanket and draped it carefully over Fedric’s shivering shoulders.

"Hey," the elf said. His voice was calm, carrying a steady, grounding warmth. "My name is Erovos. Don't be scared."

Fedric stared at him, his mind completely blank.

"The medical automata will be here in a minute to pop that shoulder back into place," Erovos continued, checking a datapad on his wrist. "They’ll administer some painkillers. After that, we’ll get you a hot meal and a dry bunk in the holding sector."

The elf squatted down, bringing himself to eye level with the trembling conscript.

"What's your name, friend?"

Fedric’s lower lip quivered. The adrenaline crashed, leaving behind a hollow ache. He looked at the clean trench. He looked at the thick blanket. He looked at the elf who had just asked for his name instead of his blood.

Tears spilled over his soot-stained cheeks. A ragged sob tore out of his throat. He wept against the metal grating, shaking with the relief of being treated like a human being.

He was just a lonely farmhand, overwhelmed that a single soldier cared to know who he was.

He didn't know his name would outlive empires. He didn't realize illustrations of his solitary walk through the crossfire would one day fill history books, studied by scholars in the hallowed halls of Dragon Valley. Fedric only wanted a place to belong. He had no idea his boots had just pushed the first stone in a domino effect that would save millions of lives.
