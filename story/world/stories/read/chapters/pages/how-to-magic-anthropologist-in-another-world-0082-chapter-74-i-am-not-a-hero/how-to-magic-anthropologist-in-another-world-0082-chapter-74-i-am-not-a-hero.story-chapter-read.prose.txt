I watched a shooting star streak through the sky, its silver trail briefly cutting across the heavens as my body instinctively absorbed the passing Star Mana. Normally, that sensation of cool fullness felt comforting, like entering an air-conditioned room on a hot, humid day, but tonight it did nothing to fill the hollow ache sitting inside my chest.

My Mana reserves might be fuller, yet emotionally, I felt completely drained. My body was exhausted from the day, my eyes heavy with unshed tears. Surely, this was the sort of situation where crying would provide some sort of catharsis. But even that was denied to me.

Why was I even mourning them?

Those men had been besieging an innocent village. They had extorted civilians, threatened people who could not defend themselves, and were entirely willing to escalate into violence for the sake of a contract and a few gold coins. By every practical standard, these were not people I should have been sympathizing with. If anything, I should have felt relieved that the siege was over and the people responsible had either surrendered or died.

The outcome was objectively good.

And yet, all I could think about was how wasteful it felt.

I was never under the illusion that the mercenaries were innocent, believe me. I was not naïve enough to pretend they were misunderstood people forced unwillingly into evil. But I also could not quite bring myself to view them as cartoon villains who woke up each morning and kicked a puppy.

Most of them were probably products of the society that created them. They were born somewhere, raised with whatever values this world had given them, and eventually arrived at the questionable career choice of becoming armed contractors willing to besiege villages for coin. To them, this was likely just work, nothing more.

That was what unsettled me most. They probably did not think they were doing anything especially wrong. Most of them looked young, late teens, maybe early twenties. Adults, technically, they were old enough to hold weapons and yet young enough to still be profoundly ignorant.

I sighed and rubbed both hands over my face, staring upward at an alien sky that kept feeling less foreign each day. There was probably something deeply arrogant in all of this.

Shit, who am I to sit here morally evaluating an entire mercenary culture? The anthropologist part of my brain was ashamed of my own behavior.

And yet, none of that changed how senseless it all felt.

Every person felt like a path cut short, and I did not mean that in some grand destiny sense. What I meant was the collection of unrealized possibilities, the futures that would now never happen, the relationships that would not deepen, the future children that would never be born, the habits that would never evolve, and the opinions that would never change. These were people who would never get older for better or worse.

I actively stopped that train of thought. I was spiraling, so I shook my head.

What I mean is that either life matters and these deaths are therefore a tragedy, or none of it matters, so why live at all?

I let out a tired exhale and stared at the stars. The constellations reminded me I was not on Earth, though that thought, which should have been comforting, instead reminded me of my own hypocrisy.

Was this world really that different from Earth?

I remembered reading about the situation in Sudan worsening just days before taking my flight to Tunisia. I remembered feeling sad for maybe five minutes before going back to trip preparations, thinking instead about what to download onto my laptop just in case I actually managed to get myself isekai’d.

Yeah... distance makes horror digestible, I guess.

Far worse things had happened on Earth than twenty-one soldiers dying; that was for sure.

In eight years, the United States killed at least as many Iraqi civilians while “liberating” the country as their own tyrant did in twenty-four years, and that was only one of the more farcical wars that had happened on Earth. Do not even get me started on the situations in the Middle East.

Yet I was able to avert my eyes and keep living my life because I had been spared from getting blood under my fingernails while repeatedly checking pulses. Or maybe it was the memory of a man calling for his mother while bleeding into the dirt that had been absent.

No, more likely it revealed something unpleasant about me that had always been true.

In the end, I am simply a spoiled rich girl who had mistaken education for wisdom.

For all my curiosity, all my supposed adaptability, and all my increasing combat capability, I was still fundamentally a sheltered girl born into extreme comfort. I had grown up buffered from true material hardship, and I had believed, with no small amount of arrogance, that I had what it took to be like my favorite fantasy heroes.

What would they do if they were in my place, with my power set?

I already knew the answer. I was not stupid. My teleportation ability had always been absurdly optimized for one thing above all else.

So when the next shooting star crossed the sky, I found myself whispering into the cold air, “I wish I would go somewhere I could actually be useful.”

The familiar sensation of teleportation overtook me almost instantly. A moment later, I found myself standing on the muddy bank of a river.

It was dark, perhaps only minutes after dusk, the last traces of sunset still faintly staining the horizon in muted violet. That meant I was slightly east of the slime farm village. The air was damp and cool, carrying the earthy scent of wet soil and river water. Nearby, a lantern hung from the branch of a crooked tree, its warm amber glow swaying gently in the breeze and casting trembling shadows over the grass.

That was when I heard a growl followed by a frightened feminine whimper.

I turned toward the sound and immediately understood why my power had brought me here.

A large bear-like beast had crept up on a young couple while they were skinny-dipping in the river. Their discarded clothes were scattered nearby on the rocks, which made me blink in surprise despite the obvious danger.

The young man had instinctively stepped in front of the girl, placing himself between her and the beast, which was objectively brave, even if he looked completely out of his depth.

The beast had literally caught them with their pants down.

I stepped forward as stealthily as I could manage and drove my bident spear directly into the creature’s side.

As the two-pronged tips punched through fur into the muscle beneath, I activated the taser function by charging the copper rings embedded in the shaft.

Lightning crackled violently through the monster, making it spasm from the sudden shock. Its entire body locked up, which gave me enough time to yank the spear free and drive the blade into the side of its neck in one smooth motion.

The beast collapsed heavily into the mud with a wet choking sound, twitched twice, and went still.

Silence returned to the riverbank as the couple stared at me in absolute disbelief.

Both of them were dripping wet, completely naked, and visibly confused, probably wondering who I was and how I had gotten here.

I gave them both a small, awkward wave, then tapped into the remaining star mana stored in my spear, disappearing before either of them could say anything.

The world folded around me once more, and a second later, I was back in the quiet solitude of the Sunless Reach, standing beneath the stars as though none of it had happened.

After a full star mana recharge, the second jump dropped me just outside the glow of a campfire, my boots sinking slightly into soft dirt as I appeared beside a small encampment arranged in a defensive circle of wagons. The wooden carts had been pulled close together, their wheels reinforced with iron rims and their sides covered in patched canvas that had clearly seen a few years of use. A few lanterns hung from the wagon frames, adding to the warm golden light cast by the fire at the center of the camp.

It looked like some kind of merchant caravan stopping for the night. The people appeared to be a group of traveling families, with women and children accompanying the men, all of them carrying the weathered look of people who spent most of their lives on the road. A couple of dozen people sat around the flames eating from wooden bowls, their dinner appearing to be a thick stew made from barley grain, dried vegetables, and rehydrated jerky. The smell was simple but pleasant enough to remind me that I had skipped both lunch and dinner.

I barely had time to process the scene before I heard harsh coughing.

A middle-aged man seated nearest to me was hunched forward, one hand gripping his bowl while the other clawed uselessly at his throat. At first, it sounded like an ordinary coughing fit.

Then the coughing shifted into wheezing as the man started desperately trying to suck air into his lungs to no avail.

He dropped his bowl into the dirt with a clatter and started pounding weakly against his own chest.

I stepped forward before anyone else could fully process what was happening, moved behind the man, wrapped both arms around his torso, and started applying the Heimlich maneuver.

For a brief second, he stiffened in confusion. Then I positioned my hands properly and pulled sharply inward and upward.

One thrust. The wheezing continued.

A second thrust. I heard a woman nearby gasp.

A third thrust, and a wet chunk of jerky shot out of his mouth, landing unpleasantly close to the campfire.

I released my hold on the man, and he immediately doubled over, sucking in air with greedy gulps.

The entire camp had gone completely silent. Every pair of eyes was now fixed directly on me.

I checked on the rescued man, making sure he was breathing properly. He was still coughing lightly, but in a much healthier way, with air finally reaching his lungs.

Satisfied with my work, I gave the entire group a deep, dramatic bow, and before anyone could recover enough to ask questions, I tapped once again into the star mana stored in my spear.

A second later, I was gone.

The next jump took me inside a dark room.

For a brief moment, I simply stood there blinking in confusion as my eyes slowly adjusted to the sudden change in lighting. The only source of light was moonlight filtered weakly through a nearby window, its magenta rays making the room feel eerier.

The inside of the room was modest and cramped, clearly somebody’s home, with a wooden table set against one wall, a few baskets stacked on top of it, and a narrow bed positioned against the far wall.

A woman was sleeping there. Or at least, I initially assumed she was sleeping. As I drew near, it became obvious something was wrong with her.

She was breathing unevenly, and her expression was tense with obvious discomfort even in unconsciousness. Sweat clung visibly to her forehead and neck, dampening the pillow beneath her. Her blankets had been partially kicked aside, probably from alternating between chills and overheating.

Well... that explained why my magic had sent me here.

I crouched beside the bed and gently pressed the back of my hand against her forehead.

She was hot... probably running a high fever.

This was less straightforward than my previous interventions.

How could I help here? It was not like I was a healer.

I could theoretically carry her to the Temple of the Holy and dump her directly into the care of Lady Sana.

The issue was star mana. Teleporting while carrying another human would consume all my current reserves, and doing so would leave me stranded in Hano, forcing me to take the slow trip back to the Sunless Reach since I had completely forgotten to bank a return wish.

This was suboptimal. I would do it if I had no other choice, but there had to be a better solution.

I rested my chin briefly against the Y-shaped nook of my spear while thinking.

Then I remembered something Sana had told me after healing me during my first days in Hano, after I caught the red death.

“Even potions from a skilled alchemist can help you. We do not possess a monopoly on healing,” she had said then.

Right... alchemy. Not every medical problem needed divine intervention.

I moved closer to the window, peering outside.

Even in the dark, I could make out Hano’s enormous outer walls rising in the distance, their silhouette unmistakable against the night sky. I was probably somewhere in the outer cottages near the city perimeter.

I returned to the woman and checked her pulse. Using my phone, I timed her heartbeat quietly while observing her breathing. I also used the phone light to check her eyes and complexion.

Her heart was beating fast, but somewhat steadily. She did not appear to be in immediate danger of dying within the next few minutes.

Good enough. I tapped into my spear and wished to teleport back to the Sunless Reach.

Cold air replaced the stale warmth of the cottage instantly, and the stars greeted me overhead.

I spent a few minutes recharging my spear with fresh star mana, drawing from passing celestial bodies while mentally planning the next wishes.

Once my reserves were replenished, I spoke.

“I wish to return next to the sick woman in fifteen minutes.”

The spell worked as the star mana was consumed, banking a delayed teleportation.

Now for step two.

I waited for the next visible shooting star and spoke again.

“I wish to go to an open potion seller.”

The world folded, and a moment later, I appeared inside what was unmistakably an alchemist establishment, probably one of the fancier shops in Hano’s shopping district, since only those stores would remain open this late at night. Plus, the place definitely looked expensive.

My experience with alchemy was minimal. I knew Nina dabbled in it a bit, and I had bought a few cosmetics from the soap maker in the freelancer baths. Oh, and the period suppressants. Those were super convenient.

This shop, however, was something else. It had floor-to-ceiling shelves covering the walls, packed with glass vials and ceramic containers. The cosmetics I usually bought were stored in wood so they would not shatter on impact, but here, people could afford to be fancy. More telling was the fact that I could not even begin to identify half of what was on display, from bottled liquids to raw ingredients, and especially the metal tube-shaped equipment that looked suspiciously like magical craft beer machinery.

Behind the front desk sat a bored-looking teenage fox boy with orange ears poking through messy orange hair.

He had been leaning his chin against one hand while reading some kind of ledger before I appeared in front of him. His ears shot upright so fast it was almost cartoonish.

He stared at me for a long pause while I observed my surroundings before finally saying, “You can’t just do that.”

“Medical emergency,” I replied immediately. “Adult woman, high fever, sweating, and shallow breathing. She is unconscious but stable, and her skin is hot to the touch.”

He blinked several times. Then, to his credit, he switched from confusion to professionalism remarkably fast.

“How long has she been like that?”

“No idea. I only met her five minutes ago.”

“Any coughing? Rash? Vomiting?”

“Not that I observed.”

“Red eyes? Green complexion?”

“No, and no.”

He nodded thoughtfully, then disappeared briefly behind the counter.

A moment later, he returned holding a small amber vial filled with pale blue liquid.

“General fever cure. It will strengthen the body’s natural healing,” he said. “It’s not a cleric’s miracle, but it should help stabilize her.”

“How does it work?”

“It temporarily boosts the soul with kindred characteristics, making the drinker more adaptable against diseases.”

That sounded ridiculously broken. Was there a version that could make me as strong as Yon?

Do not get distracted, Alice. Focus on the task at hand.

“How much?”

“One silver, five copper.”

And that was why only Ja’a bought potions. Those cost more than most mission rewards. Relying on them regularly would put any adventurer into debt. Still, I made a mental note to come back later and investigate further.

I unclipped my bank badge and used it for payment.

The fox boy glanced at it, noting my San rank.

Right. I had a prestigious nobility title. Most of the time, I forgot about it.

“Thank you for your purchase, my lady,” the teen said.

I pocketed the potion. “How do I administer it?”

“Drinking half should work. Add the other half again in six hours if symptoms persist.”

“Excellent. You are my second favorite medical professional.”

“Who is the first?”

“A Holy girl your age who healed an army on her own.”

“Sheesh, the adage is true. No matter how good you are at something, there is always a Holy kid who is better than you.”

I chuckled at his good humor and waved him goodbye before stepping outside the shop.

The shopping streets near the noble district were unusually quiet at this hour, though there were still a few people out and about. Even now, Hano never truly slept.

I leaned casually against a wall and waited.

Five minutes later, the banked teleport activated. Reality folded around me once more.

A second later, I was back inside the dark cottage beside the sick woman.

I uncorked the potion.

“Alright,” I whispered, approaching the bed again. “Let’s see how well magical NyQuil works.”

Administering the potion was easier than expected. Despite being mostly unconscious, she still reflexively swallowed when I carefully tipped the vial to her lips, letting the pale blue liquid slide slowly into her mouth. Once she had taken roughly half, I corked the remainder and set it on the nearby wooden table where she would be able to reach it later.

For a while, nothing obvious happened.

I watched her carefully, expecting perhaps some visible magical effect. Instead, the woman simply continued breathing, still flushed and sweating heavily, though perhaps slightly less miserably than before.

Since there was little else to do, I reached into my bag of holding and pulled out one of the extra towels I kept folded inside.

I started gently wiping her face and neck dry. Her skin was damp and warm beneath my hand, but I thought she was no longer quite as hot as earlier.

I briefly considered changing her clothes, since her nightgown was visibly soaked through, but I hesitated. Even for medical reasons, undressing an unconscious stranger felt like the sort of action that required more consent than I currently possessed.

So instead, I waited.

Fortunately, the potion seemed to work faster than expected. Roughly ten minutes later, her shivering stopped completely, and a few minutes after that, her eyelids fluttered open.

She blinked slowly at the ceiling, looking disoriented and groggy, before her gaze shifted sideways and landed directly on me. For a moment, she simply stared at the strange woman sitting beside her bed in the middle of the night.

Then her brow furrowed.

“Who are you?”

“That is not especially important right now,” I said. “I used divination magic to detect who might need help, and it led me here.”

“How are you feeling?” I asked instead.

She took a slow breath, clearly assessing herself. “Weak,” she muttered hoarsely. “But... less terrible than earlier.”

That was encouraging.

“I gave you half of that potion,” I said, pointing toward the small vial on the nearby table. “An alchemist told me to leave the other half for later. If you are still sick in six hours, drink the rest.”

Her eyes followed my gesture, and her expression shifted into concern.

“I cannot afford a potion.”

Of course, that was her first thought.

“Do not worry about it,” I replied. “Consider it a gift.”

Only then did I glance more obviously at her damp clothes. “Do you need help changing?”

Her face scrunched slightly. “No, I can manage.”

She then attempted to sit upright and immediately swayed, her body disagreeing with her assessment. I stepped forward and steadied her by the shoulders before gravity could take hold.

“Easy,” I muttered, helping her toward a nearby chair.

Once she was seated, I moved around the room using my phone light for navigation, looking for fresh clothes. I eventually found a folded nightgown in a nearby closet.

While she changed, I busied myself stripping the damp sheets from her bed and replacing them with dry ones.

When I finished, she was sitting quietly in the chair watching me.

“I would have died, wouldn’t I?” she asked. Her tone was calm despite the weight of the question.

I grimaced. “Probably,” I said, choosing honesty. “You should have called for help.”

That earned me a bitter little laugh.

“I only really have my son left,” she said while adjusting the sleeves of her fresh nightgown. “But ever since he got married, I rarely see him anymore. That harpy stole him from me.”

There it was. Apparently, mother-in-law grievances were universal across worlds.

“You are still young,” I said, because she genuinely was. She could not have been much older than her early forties. “You could always start dating again.”

That made her actually laugh.

“Hah. You are funny. Who would want an old hag like me?”

I made the strategic decision not to step on that conversational landmine.

Instead, I redirected. “Are you feeling well enough to sleep again?”

She nodded.

“Do you want me to call your son?”

“Nah. I am fine. Thank you.”

She climbed back into bed with significantly less effort this time, pulling the fresh blankets around herself with visible relief.

Once she settled, she looked back at me. “What is your name, young lady?”

I hesitated before answering.

“I am Alice.”

Recognition flickered across her face.

“Oh,” she murmured softly. “So the rumors are true. There really is a new saint around here.”

That statement hit me like a gut punch.

Before I could deny the allegation, exhaustion reclaimed her, and within moments she had drifted back to sleep.

I stood there for several seconds staring at her sleeping form before activating my wish powers and teleporting back to the Sunless Reach.

Cold air greeted me as I reappeared beneath the stars. I extracted my chaise lounge from my bag of holding and collapsed onto it dramatically.

“I am not a saint,” I muttered to the empty night.

It would not have taken a true saint months to do what I just did.

That is the uncomfortable truth, isn’t it?

I had helped at least three people in what could not have been more than half an hour. Ever since discovering the absurd utility of my power, I could theoretically have been doing this nonstop. Teleport somewhere, solve a crisis, recharge, and repeat.

The thought made me groan and cover my face.

“Let us do some deeply unhealthy math,” I muttered.

On Earth, roughly two people died every second, but that was with a population of eight billion. Here, assuming my teleportation range covered a population of eight million...

Two multiplied by 3600 divided by 1000...

If I assumed people died at roughly the same rate as on Earth, that would mean around seven people died every hour.

Which meant every day, people were dying while I was busy training, reading, drinking coffee, or sleeping instead of helping.

I groaned and rubbed my face. I was being ridiculous and also mathematically dishonest.

Not every death was preventable. I was not responsible for aging, terminal illness, or people dying to monsters stronger than me.

Still, the logic was uncomfortably sound. People had been dying in situations where I could potentially have helped. I had simply chosen to live my life instead of becoming a martyr and dedicating myself entirely to others.

It was the same reason I had chosen academia rather than becoming an EMT.

Back on Earth, I also chose to study anthropology instead of biology and try to find a cure for cancer. Here in this world, it was not any different. I chose magic and adventure instead of spending every free second saving people.

I, Alice Abbott, am not a saint.

I am not a hero, and I never will be.

Still, helping people had lifted my mood from the rot I had found myself sinking into after the dead mercenaries.

I was exhausted, emotionally and physically, and I desperately needed sleep.

But despite my better judgment, I decided on one final jump, before bed.

I waited for the next shooting star.

“Take me somewhere I would be useful.”

The stars answered almost immediately.

A moment later, I reappeared on the bank of a river.

Again. Apparently, rivers were hotspots for trouble.

This time, however, instead of monsters or panicking civilians, I found a shirtless man with a rope tied around his waist, dragging an enormous boulder upstream through waist-deep water.

He was not struggling. He looked mildly inconvenienced at best.

While I stared at him in silence, he turned, noticed me, and immediately brightened.

“Alice?” he called, sounding genuinely delighted. “What are you doing here?”

I blinked.

“Yon?”

That was certainly not what I had expected.
