Something was watching them from the shadows.

Orro felt it with every fiber of his being—a malevolent presence that crawled along his skin, leaving goosebumps in its wake. He kept his expression neutral as he guided their small group through the winding crypt corridors, but his senses remained on high alert, every muscle coiled and ready to spring.

“This place smells like death bathed in sour milk,” Jer announced, wrinkling his nose as they passed another alcove filled with ancient, dusty urns. The young acrobat was walking on his hands, his feet dangling above his head as if the looming danger was merely an inconvenience to be flipped over.

“Eloquent as always,” Orro muttered, but his eyes never stopped scanning the shadows. Four lives depended on his vigilance. Five, if you counted the dragonling, which he absolutely did—Cade would never forgive him if anything happened to Bunny.

The small white dragon was currently riding on Jer’s feet, clinging to his ankles and chittering excitedly as if the handstand was the most thrilling activity imaginable. For all Bunny’s chaotic energy, the creature hadn’t strayed far from Jer since they’d entered the crypt. Orro filed that observation away—even animals sometimes knew things humans missed.

Rayka moved closer, her fingers brushing his arm. “You’re doing that thing with your jaw again,” she whispered, her voice pitched just for him.

“What thing?” he asked, though he knew exactly what she meant.

“That clenching thing that means you’re overthinking every possible way this could go wrong.” Her eyes, warm amber in the torchlight, searched his face. “We’re okay, Orro.”

He wasn’t convinced, but he nodded anyway. For her.

Chubbs, perched on Rayka’s hip, pointed a chubby finger at a carving on the wall. “That warrior looks angry,” she observed with surprising clarity. “Why is he fighting invisible monsters?”

Orro followed her gaze to what appeared to be a warrior figure, sword raised in eternal battle against unseen enemies. Something about the carving triggered a prickling sensation along his spine.

“Maybe the monsters are hiding,” Rayka suggested, adjusting Chubbs on her hip. “Like a game of hide and seek.”

The Grassblade assassin wasn’t so sure. This crypt predated even the Culling War, when the Progenitors were wiped out to make room for mortal life. A remnant of a forgotten age, shrouded in millennia of darkness. What secrets did it hold? And why did it feel like the very air was watching them?

The corridor ahead split into three branches. Orro raised a hand, signaling the group to halt.

“How much longer until we reach the bottom?” Rayka asked, adjusting Chubbs on her hip. “The others might already be there.”

“Hard to tell. These old Progenitor structures never follow sensible architectural principles.” Orro studied the passages, weighing their options. “But if my theory is right, all paths should eventually lead down.”

“I vote middle!” Jer flipped back onto his feet, catching Bunny smoothly as the dragonling tumbled through the air. “My gut says middle passage.”

“Your gut also said that meat pie from the docks market was ‘perfectly fine to eat’,” Orro reminded him, remembering the two days Jer had spent moaning in misery.

Jer grinned, undeterred. “And it was delicious for the first six hours.”

Orro’s retort died on his lips as he caught a flicker of movement in the air before the leftmost passage—a distortion almost invisible in the torchlight.

“Down!” he barked, shoving Jer aside as darts erupted from hidden compartments in the walls, whistling through the air where they’d stood moments before.

Bunny screeched, wings flaring in alarm as he darted between Jer’s legs. Chubbs watched with wide-eyed fascination, pointing at the darts embedded in the far wall.

“Sharp things!” she exclaimed, not a hint of fear in her voice. “They wanted to play with us!”

“Not the kind of game we want to join, little one,” Rayka murmured, checking Chubbs for any sign of injury. The toddler seemed entirely unperturbed, her blue eyes scanning the walls with intelligent curiosity.

Orro pulled himself to his feet, mentally cataloging the trap. Pressure-triggered, likely connected to the stone tiles beneath the archway. Sophisticated work for something so ancient. How it still functioned was its own dark miracle.

“Good reflexes,” Jer commented, brushing dust from his clothes. “Though a little warning next time might be nice.”

“If I’d warned you, you’d be full of holes,” Orro replied, moving to examine the rightmost passage. As he approached, the air before it seemed to shimmer, just like the left path had moments before.

Orro froze. That wasn’t possible. Traps were mechanical, triggered by pressure plates or tripwires. They didn’t activate just by being observed.

Unless...

“Chubbs, darling,” Rayka said suddenly, her voice tight as she followed Orro’s gaze. “What do you see in that hallway?”

The assassin turned. Chubbs was staring at the rightmost passage, her golden eyes sharp and assessing. The toddler raised a pudgy hand, pointing.

“It doesn’t like us,” she said with unusual solemnity. “Especially me. It’s very angry.”

The air distorted violently, the shimmer intensifying to visible waves. The stone around the archway began to crack, dust trickling down as if the very structure was straining against itself.

“Middle passage,” Orro decided, drawing a dagger from his belt. “Move. Now.”

They pushed forward, Jer taking the lead with surprising grace, Bunny scampering at his heels. Rayka followed with Chubbs, Orro bringing up the rear, eyes constantly scanning for new threats.

The middle passage narrowed as they progressed, the carved walls closing in until their shoulders nearly brushed stone on either side. The air grew stale, heavy with the scent of ancient dust and something else—something metallic and sharp that reminded Orro of blood.

“Somehow I don’t think Cade and the others had it this rough,” Jer whispered, his usual levity subdued. “Is it just me, or is this place particularly hostile?”

Orro felt it too—a sense of focused malevolence directed specifically at their group. Every muscle in his body tensed its touch, and he nearly drew his enchanted blade then and there if not for the omnipotence behind the sensation. Whoever or whatever it was, its hatred spilled across each member of their team.

No, Orro corrected as he let his senses sharpen on what he was feeling. It’s not on us.

His eyes landed on Chubbs.

It’s on her.

A grinding sound above was Orro’s only warning. He lunged forward, tackling Rayka as a section of the ceiling smashed to the floor where she’d been standing heartbeats before. Stone fragments exploded outward, peppering his exposed skin with stinging impacts.

Jer had already flattened himself against the wall, one arm curled protectively around Bunny. The dragonling let out a distressed chirp, wings fluttering uselessly against his injured side.

“Everyone alright?” Orro demanded, helping Rayka to her feet. Chubbs was clutching her carved bone pendant, eyes wide but remarkably composed for a child who’d nearly been crushed.

“We’re good,” Rayka confirmed, brushing stone dust from Chubbs’ hair. “That wasn’t triggered by anything. It just... fell.”

Orro examined the fallen stonework. She was right—there were no visible mechanisms, no tripwires or pressure plates. Just smooth, ancient stone that had suddenly decided to collapse.

“This place is reacting to us,” he said, his suspicion hardening into certainty. “Or to something we’re carrying.”

A scraping noise echoed from deeper in the passage—a grating sound like stone dragging across stone. It rumbled with enough ferocity to shake the very marrow within his bones.

“Please tell me that’s just the resident tomb-rat dragging off dinner,” Jer quipped, but his hand had moved to the cooking knife at his belt. “A really well-fed rat.”

The sound grew louder, accompanied by a rhythmic thudding that vibrated through the floor beneath their feet. Whatever was coming, it was large.

Orro made a swift decision. “Back. We need to find another route.”

They turned, only to discover their retreat blocked by a wall of stone that hadn’t been there moments before. The crypt had sealed them in.

“Well, shit,” Jer remarked, eyeing the new barrier.

The scraping sound grew closer, the vibrations growing in strength with each pound of his pulse. Orro positioned himself in front of the others, daggers ready. He clenched his teeth against the invading noise, though he kept his shoulders loose.

“When it appears,” he said quietly, “I’ll engage. Jer, stay back with Rayka and Chubbs. If an opening appears—”

“With all due respect,” Jer interrupted, moving to stand beside him, “you’re going to need someone watching your flank. And despite appearances, I’m actually quite good at staying alive.”

Orro hesitated, surprised by the steel in the younger man’s voice. The acrobat was usually all jokes and foolishness, but the eyes that met his now were deadly serious.

“Besides,” Jer added with a flash of his familiar grin, “I’ve outrun the Darkreach patrols since I was eight. This should be nothing.”

Before Orro could argue, the scraping stopped, leaving a silence so complete he could hear his own heartbeat. Then, from the shadows ahead, something emerged.

It moved with an awkward, lurching gait, its form a grotesque amalgamation of stone and desiccated flesh. Ancient armor clung to its massive frame in rusted fragments, and where a head should have been, there was only a jagged crystal pulsing with sickly green light.

“What in the four hells is that?” Jer breathed.

“Golem,” Orro responded, recognizing the construct from ancient texts he’d studied when he was in training. “A guardian.”

The golem stopped several paces away, its crystal head tilting as it regarded them. Then, with unexpected speed, it charged.

Orro met the attack head-on, his daggers striking the creature’s stone body with a shower of sparks. The impact jarred his arms to the shoulders, but he held firm, deflecting a massive fist as it swung toward his head. The thing was fast—impossibly fast for something made of stone.

He ducked under another swing, slashing at what might have been a knee joint. His blade scraped across stone, barely leaving a scratch. Whatever this material was, it wasn’t ordinary rock.

A flash of movement caught his eye—Jer, launching himself off the wall in a spiraling leap that carried him over the golem’s reaching arm. The acrobat landed on its shoulders, driving his cooking knife down into the junction where crystal met stone. Sparks flew, and the construct roared—a sound like grinding millstones.

The golem thrashed, one massive arm sweeping back to dislodge its attacker. Jer pushed off, executing a perfect backflip that carried him safely beyond reach. But the maneuver left Orro exposed. A stone fist caught him in the ribs, lifting him off his feet and slamming him against the wall.

Pain exploded through his chest. Ribs cracked and his vision blurred. He slid to the cold ground, fighting to stay conscious as the golem advanced.

Through the swirling ocean of red stars that swam across his sight, he saw Rayka pressing herself against the wall, shielding Chubbs with her body. The toddler’s eyes were fixed on the battle, unnervingly focused for a child so young.

“Hey, rock-head!” Jer’s voice rang out, drawing the construct’s attention. “Over here!”

The acrobat darted forward, slashing at the golem’s legs before jumping away from its counterattack. Each step was precisely calculated to keep the guardian’s focus on him and away from the others. The redhead danced through the golem’s onslaught, bending backward to avoid a swing that would’ve taken his head clean off, and then using the momentum to perform an effortless backhand spring into the air to avoid the monster’s followup attack.

Orro forced himself to his feet, ignoring the stabbing pain in his ribs. They couldn’t defeat this thing with conventional weapons. Each attack barely scratched its surface, and it moved with unnatural speed and strength.

His hand moved to his wrist, where a simple leather bracelet concealed his most dangerous tool.

He’d promised himself he wouldn’t use it again so soon. The spirit realm took its toll, each visit draining something from him that took longer to replenish with each use. But there were lives at stake—Rayka’s life.

The golem had Jer cornered now, the acrobat’s back pressed against the wall as the massive construct closed in. There was no choice.

Orro twisted the bracelet, activating the ancient magic bound within.

Reality fractured.

Colors bled away. Sound dampened to hollow echoes. The physical world became transparent, overlaid with currents of ethereal energy that flowed like luminescent rivers through the crypt.

The spirit realm.

But he wasn’t alone.

Eyes. Countless eyes watching from the deeper shadows. Ancient. Hungry. Aware. They fixed on him with predatory intensity, aware of his intrusion into their domain. Whispers slithered through his consciousness—not words, but impressions of malice, curiosity, hunger.

The walls of the crypt revealed their true nature in this half-world—not mere stone, but boundaries between realities, carved with sigils that pulsed with sickening energy. Binding spells. Containment wards. This place wasn’t just a tomb. It was a prison, forged in not one but both of these realities. Any hope that Orro had held for outsmarting this place died in his chest.

And at this place’s heart, something vast and terrible stirred, disturbed by his presence. Something that reached tendrils of awareness toward him, probing, testing.

It knew him. Or rather, it knew what he carried.

Terror threatened to overwhelm him, but Orro forced his focus back to the golem. In the spirit realm, its true nature was revealed—a construct of pulsing sigils and binding spells rather than stone and flesh. At its core, where the crystal sat in the physical world, throbbed a nexus of power—the true heart of the creature.

The crystal was a decoy, he realized.

Orro struck, his dagger’s edge augmented by his temporary existence in the spirit realm. He severed the magical bindings one by one, methodical despite the eyes watching, the whispers growing louder, the sense of something massive drawing closer.

The construct’s energy matrix collapsed. The whisperers shrieked in fury, the sound driving ice picks into Orro’s brain. The reaching presence accelerated its approach, a tidal wave of ancient malevolence bearing down on him.

He twisted the bracelet again, snapping back to physical reality just as the golem froze mid-attack.

“Move!” he shouted, grabbing Jer’s arm and pulling him away.

They barely made it to where Rayka stood when the crystal at the golem’s core flared blindingly bright, then shattered. Shards exploded outward like deadly shrapnel, slicing through the air. Orro threw himself in front of the others, his body a shield as fragments cut across his exposed skin.

Pain lanced through his shoulder as a particularly large shard embedded itself in his flesh. He staggered but remained standing, watching as the golem collapsed into inert rubble.

Then silence, broken only by their ragged breathing.

“How did you—” Jer began, staring at the fallen guardian.

“Not important,” Orro cut him off, wincing as he probed the shard in his shoulder. It was in deep, but removing it now would only worsen the bleeding. “We need to keep moving.”

Rayka approached, her eyes taking in his injuries with quiet concern. “Your ribs,” she said quietly.

“I’ll manage.” He straightened, ignoring the grinding pain. “Is Chubbs alright?”

The toddler had her face buried against Rayka’s neck, but at the sound of her name, she peered up. Her expression was solemn, far too knowing for a child her age.

“The stone man was lonely,” she said simply. “But he’s gone now. The others are coming.”

“Others?” Jer echoed, scanning the passage.

A low rumble answered his question as the crypt shuddered around them.

“We need to move,” Orro decided, pushing through the pain. “Now.”

They continued deeper into the passage, which began to slope downward—hopefully toward the central chamber. Bunny had returned to Jer’s side, the dragonling’s senses clearly on high alert as his head swiveled to track every sound.

“That was some impressive footwork back there,” Orro said to Jer as they navigated the increasingly unstable passage. “Where did you learn to fight like that?”

Jer shrugged, an uncharacteristically serious expression crossing his face. “You pick things up when you grow up in Darkreach. The trick isn’t knowing how to throw a punch—it’s knowing when to run instead.”

“You could have run back there. Should have, by any logical assessment.”

“Yeah, well,” Jer’s smile returned, though it didn’t quite reach his eyes. “Logic isn’t really my specialty. Besides, family doesn’t run when family is in trouble.”

“Family,” Orro repeated, testing the word.

“That’s what we are, isn’t it?” Jer glanced back at him. “Maybe not by blood, but by choice. That counts for more in my experience.”

Before Orro could respond, the passage opened into a small chamber. Ancient stone pedestals lined the walls, each bearing what appeared to be a puzzle box of intricate design. At the center of the chamber stood a larger pedestal with a crystal orb pulsing with ethereal light.

The only exit was a doorway on the far side, sealed by a stone slab covered in arcane symbols.

“I don’t like this,” Rayka murmured, scanning the room with wary eyes.

“Puzzles!” Chubbs exclaimed, wriggling to be put down. “I want to play!”

“Not this time, Chubbs,” Rayka replied firmly, tightening her hold. “Something tells me these aren’t ordinary games.”

Orro approached the central pedestal cautiously, examining the orb without touching it. Symbols similar to those on the sealed door swirled within its crystalline depths.

“A key,” he surmised. “But which configuration is correct?”

Jer moved to examine the puzzle boxes along the walls. “Maybe these control it somehow? Each one could represent a possible solution.”

Orro nodded, impressed by the acrobat’s insight. “Logical. But that means only one is correct. The others likely trigger traps.”

“So we need to figure out which is the right one,” Rayka concluded. “Any ideas?”

Chubbs pointed to one of the boxes—a hexagonal construct with spiraling patterns that matched those in the orb. “That one feels nice,” she declared. “The others are mean.”

Orro and Rayka exchanged glances. The child’s intuition had proven uncannily accurate thus far, but the stakes were high. A wrong choice here could be fatal.

“Wait,” Jer said suddenly, examining the sealed doorway more closely. “These symbols... they’re not random. Guys, I think they’re telling a story.”

Orro joined him, studying the ancient carvings. They depicted figures in various poses, some raising their hands toward stylized stars, others kneeling in supplication. But there was a pattern to their arrangement—a sequence that repeated with subtle variations.

“A dance,” Jer realized, tracing the figures with his finger. “Or a ritual. See how each position flows into the next?”

Orro saw it now—the carvings were instructions, showing the precise sequence of movements needed to activate the door.

“How did you notice that?” he asked, genuinely impressed.

Jer shrugged, suddenly self-conscious. “I pay attention to patterns. Have to, in acrobatics. The rhythm of movement tells you everything you need to know.”

“So we follow the pattern on one of the puzzle boxes,” Rayka suggested, joining them at the door. “But which one?”

“The nice one,” Chubbs insisted, still pointing to the hexagonal box.

With no better options, Orro approached the indicated box. Its surface was covered in movable segments, each bearing symbols that corresponded to those on the door. By rotating and sliding these segments, one could create different combinations—different versions of the ritual depicted on the doorway.

“Here goes nothing,” he muttered, beginning to manipulate the puzzle according to the sequence Jer had identified.

The segments shifted smoothly under his fingers, clicking into place as each correct position was achieved. When the final piece locked into position, the box hummed with power, and the orb on the central pedestal flared with light.

For a moment, Orro thought they’d succeeded.

Then the floor beneath their feet shuddered violently. Cracks appeared in the stone, spreading outward from the pedestal. The orb’s light changed from gentle blue to harsh, sickly green—the same color as the golem’s crystal.

“That’s not right,” Jer muttered, backing away. “The sequence was perfect.”

“The crypt changed the rules,” Orro realized with grim certainty. “It’s working against us. Against her.” He nodded toward Chubbs, who was watching the failing puzzle with a frown of concentration.

The orb pulsed faster, its light becoming painfully bright. The sealed door remained stubbornly closed as the room continued to degrade around them.

“Now what?” Rayka asked, clutching Chubbs tighter as the floor beneath them grew increasingly unstable.

Orro’s mind raced through possibilities, each more desperate than the last. They could try another puzzle box, but if the crypt was actively working against them, it would simply change those solutions too. They could attempt to break through the door, but without knowing what lay beyond, they might trap themselves more thoroughly in this unforgiving dungeon.

“Bunny, no!” Jer’s shout snapped Orro from his thoughts.

The white dragonling had darted from Jer’s side, racing toward the central pedestal. Before anyone could stop him, Bunny leaped onto the plinth, his claws scrabbling against the smooth stone as he climbed.

“Get him back!” Orro ordered, starting forward.

But Bunny had already reached the orb. The dragonling studied it for a heartbeat, head tilted in that birdlike way that suggested deep thought. Then, with decisive movement, he struck the crystal with his damaged wing.

The orb shattered.

Green light exploded outward, washing over the chamber in a blinding flash. Orro threw up an arm to shield his eyes, expecting pain, destruction—

Instead, the sealed door rumbled open, revealing a passage beyond.

Bunny chittered triumphantly, scrambling down from the pedestal to race back to Jer’s side, looking inordinately pleased with himself.

“Did he just—” Rayka began, bewildered.

“Solve the puzzle by breaking it? Yes,” Orro confirmed, equally stunned. “Apparently sometimes the direct approach works best. Nobody tell Cade I said that.”

Chubbs was giggling, clapping her hands in delight. “Bunny WINS!” she proclaimed happily. “Smart dragon!”

The floor continued to destabilize, sections beginning to collapse into darkness below. They had no time to marvel at Bunny’s unconventional solution.

“Go!” Orro commanded, ushering them toward the open doorway. “Before this whole chamber collapses!”

They raced for the exit, leaping over widening cracks as the ancient stone gave way beneath them. Jer went first, Bunny clutched to his chest. Rayka followed with Chubbs, her movements hampered by the need to protect the child.

Orro brought up the rear, ignoring the pain that lanced through his injured shoulder and cracked ribs with each step. Just as he reached the doorway, the floor behind him fell away completely, plummeting into unseen depths.

The passage beyond sloped steeply downward, twisting and turning as it descended deeper into the earth. They ran, half-sliding down the incline as tremors continued to shake the crypt. Dust and small rocks showered from the ceiling, evidence that the collapse was spreading throughout the structure.

“We must be getting close to the bottom!” Jer called back, his voice nearly lost in the rumble of failing stone. “The passage is widening!”

He was right.

The narrow corridor was opening into what appeared to be a much larger space ahead. Faint blue light filtered through the dust-filled air, suggesting a chamber of significant size.

They emerged onto a ledge overlooking an enormous cavern—easily the largest space they’d encountered in the crypt. The ceiling soared overhead, lost in darkness despite the bioluminescent fungi that clung to the walls, casting everything in an eerie blue glow.

And in the center of the cavern floor, approximately thirty feet below their position, stood a massive stone altar.

“That has to be what Cade was talking about,” Rayka said, gesturing toward the altar. “The heart of the crypt.”

“Question is, how do we get down there?” Jer peered over the edge of the ledge, assessing the drop. “Bit too far for a casual hop.”

Orro scanned the cavern, looking for a path. There—a series of stone outcroppings formed a rough staircase down the wall, though with significant gaps between some steps.

“This way,” he directed, moving toward the first outcropping. “Watch your footing. These may not be stable.”

The descent was treacherous. Each stone projection shifted slightly under their weight, threatening to break away from the wall entirely. Orro went first, testing each step before signaling the others to follow. His injured ribs protested every movement, sending sharp pains through his chest with each breath.

Halfway down, disaster struck.

The stone beneath Rayka’s foot crumbled without warning. She teetered on the edge, Chubbs clutched tightly to her chest. For one heart-stopping moment, it seemed certain they would fall.

Orro lunged upward, catching Rayka’s arm just as her balance failed completely. The sudden movement sent lightning bolts of agony through his injured shoulder and ribs, but he held fast, muscles straining as he supported both her and Chubbs’s weight with one arm.

“I’ve got you,” he gritted, pulling them toward more stable footing. “Hold on!”

Jer scrambled down to help, providing additional support until Rayka regained secure placement on a different outcropping. Chubbs, remarkably, seemed to treat the near-disaster as an exciting game, her eyes wide with exhilaration rather than fear.

“That was fun!” she declared, patting Rayka’s cheek reassuringly. “Can we do it again?”

“Let’s not,” Rayka replied weakly, her normally steady voice shaking slightly. “Once was quite enough.”

They continued more cautiously, eventually reaching the cavern floor without further incident. The altar stood before them, an imposing structure of black stone carved with symbols similar to those they’d encountered throughout the crypt. But there was something different about these markings—they seemed more... deliberate. Precise.

“I don’t see the others,” Jer noted, scanning the vast chamber. “We must be the first to arrive.”

“Or the only ones to make it this far,” Orro added grimly, the thought weighing heavily on him.

Rayka touched his arm gently. “They’ll be here. Cade always finds a way through.”

Orro nodded, hoping she was right. He approached the altar, studying the ancient structure without touching it. Its surface was unnaturally smooth, almost reflective despite the apparent age. At its center lay a depression roughly the size and shape of a human hand.

“Some kind of activation mechanism,” he observed. “But activating what?”

“Whatever it is, I doubt it’s friendly,” Jer commented, keeping Bunny close as the dragonling attempted to investigate the altar himself. “This entire place has been trying to kill us since we arrived.”

Chubbs wriggled in Rayka’s arms, pointing insistently at the altar. “Ray-Ray,” she said with a tone that sent chills down Orro’s spine. “That’s not the end.”

Before Orro could ask what she meant, a grinding sound echoed through the cavern—stone shifting against stone. But it wasn’t coming from the altar. It was coming from the walls.

Sections of the seemingly solid rock face were moving, sliding apart to reveal dark openings. From these newly formed doorways emerged figures—humanoid shapes moving with jerky, unnatural motions.

“Jer,” Orro said quietly, his hand moving to his daggers. “Get Rayka and Chubbs behind the altar. Now.”

The acrobat didn’t question the order, immediately guiding the others to the minimal shelter provided by the massive stone structure. Bunny went with them, the dragonling’s instincts clearly recognizing the approaching danger.

The figures emerged fully into the blue light, revealing their true nature. They wore the tattered remains of ceremonial robes and armor, designs that Orro recognized from ancient records—the original Arbiters, those who had first claimed to serve the gods directly. But these weren’t living beings. Their flesh had the waxy, preserved look of the long-dead, and where eyes should have been were only empty sockets filled with sickly green light.

“Undead,” Orro stated flatly, counting their numbers as more continued to emerge from the walls. Ten. Twenty. More still coming. “A lot of them.”

“Please tell me you have a plan that doesn’t involve fighting an army of walking corpses,” Jer muttered, his cooking knife looking woefully inadequate against such numbers.

Orro’s hand moved toward his back, where his true weapon was secured beneath his cloak. He’d hoped to avoid revealing it unless absolutely necessary. That moment had arrived.

With practiced motion, he drew the blade—or what remained of it. Once, it had been a magnificent sword, but now only the hilt, crossguard, and a jagged remnant of the blade remained. Yet even in this broken state, power radiated from the weapon, causing the air around it to shimmer with barely contained energy.

The reaction was immediate and alarming. Every undead Arbiter in the chamber froze, their green-lit gazes fixing on the broken sword with unmistakable recognition. A collective hissing sound filled the cavern—not breath, for these things had no need to breathe, but some primal expression of hatred.

“What’s… What’s going on?” Rayka asked quietly from behind the altar. “What is that weapon, Orro?”

“Later, I promise,” he replied, settling into a fighting stance as the undead began to advance once more. Their movements had changed—become more coordinated, more purposeful. Whatever intelligence guided them had recognized a significant threat.

The first Arbiter reached him, decayed hands grasping. Orro struck with the broken blade, and where it touched the undead flesh, power flared. The creature shrieked as its form began to disintegrate, green light spilling from widening cracks in its desiccated skin.

But there were too many. For each one he destroyed, three more pressed forward. They circled, trying to flank him, to separate him from the others.

“Jer, I need you out here,” Orro called, dispatching another undead with a precise strike. “Can’t hold them all alone.”

The acrobat vaulted over the altar, landing gracefully at Orro’s side. “Just like old times,” he quipped, though they’d never actually fought together before. “You take the fifty on the left, I’ll handle the fifty on the right.”

Despite the direness of their situation, Orro found himself appreciating Jer’s gallows humor. It was easier to face death alongside someone who could still find something to smile about.

They fought back to back, Orro’s broken blade carving through undead with supernatural efficiency while Jer used his acrobatic skills to evade and redirect their attacks. The young man might not have had a magical weapon, but his speed and agility made him surprisingly effective—kicking undead into each other, using their own momentum against them, creating chaos in their ranks.

But it was a losing battle. More undead continued to emerge from the walls, an endless stream of ancient guardians awakened to defend their domain. Orro’s movements were slowing, the pain from his injuries combining with fatigue as the fight dragged on. Even Jer’s boundless energy was flagging, his breaths coming in ragged gasps between maneuvers.

“We can’t keep this up,” Jer panted, narrowly avoiding grasping hands. “There are’s too many!”

He was right. They were being overwhelmed by sheer numbers. Soon they would be too exhausted to fight, and then...

“Rayka!” Orro called, cleaving through another undead. “Is there any other way out? Any passage we missed?”

“Nothing!” she shouted back, her own dagger flashing as she defended Chubbs from those that broke through their line. “We’re surrounded!”

A new sound cut through the chaos—a high, keening wail that seemed to vibrate through the very stones beneath their feet. Chubbs stood behind the altar, her small hands pressed against the ancient rock, her eyes blazing with an inner light that matched the blue glow of the fungi.

“Chubbs, no!” Rayka lunged for the child, but it was too late.

The altar activated. Symbols carved into its surface flared to life, pulsing with power that spread outward in concentric waves. The floor of the entire chamber shuddered violently, and new fissures opened, releasing a blinding surge of green energy that swept across the cavern.

The undead paused in their advance, seeming momentarily confused by this development. Then, as one, they turned toward Chubbs, their empty gazes fixing on the child with renewed purpose. With the altar activated, she had become their primary target.

And now there were hundreds of them, filling every corner of the vast chamber, all moving with singular intent toward the small girl behind the altar.

Orro and Jer fell back to Rayka’s position, forming a defensive perimeter around Chubbs. The situation had gone from desperate to hopeless. There was no path of escape, no strategy that could overcome such numbers.

For the second time in his life, the exiled assassin faced the absolute certainty of defeat.

His hands shook.
