Trevor and Adabelle spent their afternoon out on the balcony, sifting through Klar’s books about the ecosystem of the Wildlands below. The duo had moved to a large couch, where he was resting his head in his wife’s lap. She idly ran her fingers through his hair as they read. Despite calling it homework, they enjoyed learning new things.

“Finally found it!” she announced, holding her book in front of Trevor. He put his down on his chest to see the detailed diagrams she was showing him. “They’re called Lysume trees, named after the person who researched them. These crystalline trees actually aren’t plants at all, but animals that migrate in a very specific pattern around the Wildlands. It takes them almost a full calendar year to make the whole circuit.”

“That’s pretty neat,” Trevor replied, taking the book from her. “Are they, perhaps, herbivores?”

“They are,” Adabelle said. “We can’t see it from up here, but they have several grasping limbs that rip the bark off of the actual trees that they pass, consuming it as they go. They’re very hardy, so they aren’t hunted very often and they have a mutually beneficial relationship with the damku, which are the monkeys that are constantly clambering about. Those live in the Lysume tree’s canopy. Why do you ask?”

The courier handed her back the book she had given him and picked up his own, flipping through the pages. “There are several types of trees down there that have evolved to carry their seeds in their bark, which grows back quickly. A lot of the animals there either eat it or rub against it, which helps them to spread. This book doesn’t mention the Lysume Trees specifically, but I have to imagine that they’re probably pretty prolific pollinators.”

“That’s what the book says,” she confirmed with a nod. “They’re one of the few creatures down there with no natural predators. The damku are a different story, but that’s one of the reasons why they live atop the Lysume trees.”

“I wonder why nothing tries to eat them,” Trevor mused, looking up at his wife.

Adabelle was happy to share the information, and her tail swished on his stomach. He had to grab it to avoid getting tickled. She didn’t seem to notice.

“Their entire body is made out of the same crystal as their leaves,” she explained, flipping to the next page to show off another diagram. “So not only is their hide very tough and barely organic, but they also tend to stampede if any of them are noticeably attacked. They’re moving slow now, but they can really get going if they feel threatened. Everything else knows that you either pick off the damku for your meal or get out of their way, because they’re big and they won’t stop just because you’re in their path.”

“Sounds scary, honestly.”

“I can see where panic would set in when a herd of crystal monsters taller than anything you’d seen before started running towards you, yes,” Adabelle laughed. “Did they say how tall those trees were? The ones with the seeds in the bark?”

“They average around six hundred feet,” Trevor answered. “So they’re tall tall, and we’re seeing a lot of those Lysume trees breaching their canopy, so that’s just crazy. It makes me wonder how big everything else is in the Wildlands. I’ll be honest that I have no idea the scale of anything down there even though we were told our cliff was three miles tall. I think everything is just huge.”

“The land of the giants. It’s no wonder so many countries want to get in there so much,” the noblewoman said, casting her gaze over the side of the balcony. “Even the lumber from a handful of trees would net you several buildings. Who knows what other bountiful resources are down there?”

“It doesn’t matter when even the smallest creatures are big enough to chew you up,” he replied, shaking his head. “Like, how big do those damku get so that we can see them from here? And then those are the tasty ones most of the other animals are hunting? Yeah, I can see why Todarro wants Summoned. People like you might be the only ones able to survive down there.”

“I think you’re right, Trevor. It certainly sounds like a thrilling challenge, if I’m being honest, but I can also see where Lora is coming from.”

“Can you?” he asked with a chuckle. “She wanted to fluff feathers all day, and if these things are the size of buildings then those have got to be some pretty big feathers.”

The courier paused, contemplating his words while Adabelle waited for him to catch up.

“Actually, yeah, that does sound pretty nice,” he conceded. “Definitely not sanitary, but I can see the appeal now.”

“That, and it’s such a beautiful, untouched land that I certainly don’t want to be a part of the expedition that destroys it,” Adabelle added.

“It really is something special,” Trevor agreed with a smile, looking out through the holes in the railing so he wouldn’t have to move his head from his wife’s lap. “Alright, big question for you.”

“Go ahead.”

He set the book on Wildlands fauna onto his chest and shifted so he was looking up at her again. “What is the biggest or most impressive monster you’ve fought solo?”

“Solo?” she repeated, and he nodded. “Hmm. That’s a tough one. There were plenty that I had fought with Lailah and Griffin that were out of my league alone.”

“You went on a lot of adventures with them, huh?”

“As many as we could,” Adabelle answered with a fond smile. “We were fairly eager to travel, help people, and get stronger, and we did so quite often as a part of the adventuring guild. Whenever other countries requested help, we sometimes had to travel far away at their behest. We were good.”

“I believe it. Lailah’s now a consultant to the guildmaster and Griffin is one of the king’s personal guards,” Trevor said. “And you are the mayor of an entire town that’s proving to be one of the best places to live in the entire world according to 100% of Rashies, Waynes, and Trevors. That’s one successful adventuring party.”

The demonkin gave him a patient smile. “That survey sounds a little biased. Also, while I admit that Rashie is probably a unique enough name that not too many other people have it, I’m quite certain there are more Trevors and Waynes in our world.”

“They couldn’t be found, so the polling is completely accurate until proven otherwise.”

“Is that how it works?” she asked, raising an eyebrow.

“It’s how it works right now because it helps me win,” he answered shamelessly.

“I suppose I can allow it this time,” Adabelle replied graciously before running her tail against his stomach again. Her courier had let his guard down, and he jolted as he was tickled. He was quick to grab it again, glaring up at her with an indignant look that she found adorable. “I have a tale for you. It ends in a victory, but not because I beat the monster.”

Trevor’s eyes flicked to her tail when she said she had a tale for him, but decided he’d rather have a story than banter. He hugged the now-flailing appendage tight to his chest, where it tried and failed to tickle him further.

“Yeah, I’d like to hear that.”

Adabelle nodded. “This was about six or seven years ago. The nation of Kapsca, to the southeast of Lucent, had put out a call for help because it was time for the Asuwalla to wake and their most recent Summoned didn’t have the power to take care of it yet.”

“The Asuwalla?” Trevor asked. “What’s that, and how did you handle it by yourself?”

“In their tongue, it roughly translates to the Desert Wanderer,” she answered with a smile. “It’s a one hundred foot tall monster made up of the elements of the desert, which covers the country’s entire southern and eastern side. As in, its body is literally made of sand, rocks, cactus, and bits of whatever it can find like old wagons and tumbleweeds.”

The courier arched an eyebrow. “And it’s a hundred feet tall?”

“That’s right,” Adabelle said, nodding. “Every twenty years or so, it awakens on one side of the desert and crosses it on a random path. The priests of Kapsca created a whole set of magic divinations specifically for trying to figure out when the Asuwalla will rise and what path it will take.”

“So this is some kind of, what? Desert spirit? Is it the same one every time or different?”

“Same soul, different body,” she answered, enjoying how interested her husband was in her story. “Most times when they don’t have someone to handle it, they have to evacuate whole settlements if it would get too close. The closer to the date the monster rises, the more accurate their divinations are. If the priests are unlucky, reality could be very different from what they foresaw.”

“Uh oh.”

“That particular time, they called for help because the priest who divined its path put it well outside of their capital with a few weeks to go. It was supposed to be safe. Because of this terrible twist of fate, they weren’t going to be able to evacuate the city in time when they did the divination again and saw that it was going to go right through.”

Adabelle raised her head to stare at the sky. “We—Lailah, Griffin, and I—were relatively close by when we received the emergency quest that was sent out to all Summoned within a certain range. It was a tough ride, but wracors do really well in the desert and we were able to push them to get to the city.”

“Is this going to be a story with a happy ending?” Trevor asked.

“We’ll get there, dear,” she responded, running her fingers through his hair. “By the time we arrived, there were still several neighborhoods that needed to be evacuated and the Asuwalla was already in sight. It wasn’t as if they weren’t trying, there just wasn’t enough transportation for everyone. I went after the monster while Lailah and Griffin did what they could to help get people to safety.”

“That must have been pretty hard with your sword,” he said thoughtfully. “I imagine something that big, made of such slapdash materials, probably wouldn’t register that something stabbed it in the foot.”

“You’re right. I did not stab it. Just like you, I realized that it would be a fruitless endeavor.”

Trevor took a moment to think it over before sitting up and turning to face her. He scrambled to catch the book that fell from his chest, letting go of her tail. Luckily, he managed to snatch it before it hit the ground. With a sheepish smile, he set it aside and looked at his wife. “You used your Power, didn’t you?”

“I did,” Adabelle confirmed as she shifted to face him. “Before then, I had been relying only on my swordsmanship and the same stats every other Summoned had. It had worked out because we were mostly dungeon diving, but that wouldn’t work here. This was the first fight that made me realize that I needed to stop being scared of what I could really do when I let loose and lean into my Power.”

“That must have been hard for you,” he said, taking her hand in his.

The noblewoman smiled at his concern. “It was, but I would have been far more devastated if I failed the people still in the city.”

“So what did you do?”

“I made it rain,” Adabelle answered, leaning against Trevor. “I was fairly regular in putting points into my Mana stat because I had magical items that required it. Making torrential rain in the desert wasn’t easy, but I managed. The drought that came after was much easier.”

“Drought?” he asked, furrowing his brow in thought. His eyes widened as he understood. “Oh! Because it was probably mostly sand, you wanted to try and harden the mud you made to slow it down, right?”

“You’ve got it, Trevor,” she praised, tilting her head to kiss his cheek. “Every time the Asuwalla got used to one thing, I switched to the other. Wet it down, dry it out. Repeat. I didn’t let it get accustomed to either, and even threw a couple of tornadoes its way, though that didn’t work nearly as well.”

“Sounds like it must have been a very draining fight,” the courier remarked.

“It was, and Griffin had to come out and save me when it got too close,” she admitted, sounding a little guilty about the fact. “But I managed to stall it out long enough for the last citizens to get away to safety. The Asuwalla still went right through the city, there was no stopping that, but there wasn’t anyone to chase so there was no reason for it to wander. It traveled in a straight line that they later turned into a road.”

“A victory, but not one where you slayed the monster,” Trevor started, remembering her words from before.

“Exactly. After that, I talked to Jackson and took his advice to start exploring my Power a bit more. It might not have been something I wanted, but it was still a part of me. I needed to get over myself, as he put it, and use everything in my arsenal.”

“Wow. That’s a great story. Are you upset that you weren’t able to defeat it?”

Adabelle thought about that for a moment. “No, not really,” she answered, shaking her head. “I don’t think something like the Asuwalla is really meant to be defeated. Slowing it down was accomplishment enough. Though… we might find ourselves on a trip to the desert in the next thirteen years or so.”

“I’ll go where you go, lady, don’t ever doubt that,” he said with a chuckle. “But I’m going to complain about monsters the entire time. You’re just going to have to accept that.”

“I think I can handle that. Especially because I know how to take your mind off of it.”

“It’s going to be really hard to take my mind off of something like that.”

“We’ve been reading for a pretty long time. Why don’t we take a break and…” Adabelle trailed off, her fingers running down his chest. “I can demonstrate for you.”
