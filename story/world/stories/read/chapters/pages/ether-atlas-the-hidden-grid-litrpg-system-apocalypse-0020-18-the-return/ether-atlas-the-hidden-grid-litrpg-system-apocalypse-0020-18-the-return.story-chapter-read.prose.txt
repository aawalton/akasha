Chapter 18

"Donna told me today that my Acuity investment is 'a bet on a future that might not exist.' She's right. Every point I put into Acuity instead of Constitution is a gamble that I'll live long enough for the payoff." - The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr, London, Day 2

They crossed back through Greenwich Park in silence. The deer watched them leave with the same patient attention they'd shown on the approach, blue eyes tracking from the tree line, antlers motionless against the violet sky.

Arthur wondered how many other tests along the Curator trail had been misread by candidates who'd fought what they should have walked past.

Once they were through the gate and back on the street, Steve pulled out the guidebook, the billhook bobbing on his shoulder. He'd been turning the riddle over since the observatory.

"Temple Church," he said. "Lines one and two are settled. But 'where two share a saddle.' That's been nagging at me since the courtyard."

"You said it was a seal or a coin," Donna said. She was walking point, axe low, eyes on the street.

"It is. I know I've seen the image. Two figures on a single horse. It's iconic."

"Why would two knights share a horse?" Arthur asked, the logistics bothering him. Two armed men on a single animal were an engineering problem. The weight distribution would compromise the horse's endurance. Not to mention it was unkind to the horse.

"Maybe they were broke," Donna said. "Like that joke. Two kids sharing a stick horse because the family couldn't afford a second one."

Both men looked at her.

“What? Not complicated, is it? Two men, one horse. They didn't have the budget for two."

Steve's mouth was half-open as he made the necessary connections.

"The Knights Templar took a vow of poverty," Steve said, nodding vigorously. "And their official seal, the one stamped on every document the Order ever produced, the most famous image in Templar iconography." He closed his eyes. "Two knights on one horse. The Sigillum Militum Xpisti." Where two share a saddle.

"And the last line?" Donna asked.

Steve’s momentum stalled. "‘Where the boy king’s guardian bears the weight.’ Temple Church has knightly effigies in the Round. I cannot remember which of those men served a child king, or what the damn weight means….” He trailed off, frustrated. "I need a proper reference. A history of the effigies."

“Then we'd better find you one,” Arthur said.

They were moving through New Cross now, heading northwest towards the river. The streets carried the silence of a held breath, but in the growing daylight the city's damage was more legible and visceral. Windows shattered from inside, a car with its boot open and luggage strewn across the pavement, a pushchair overturned on a kerb with no one near it. These were stories told in objects. None of them happy.

And then, as if the universe owed them one, Steve saw a sign on a building set back from the road, half-obscured by overgrowth: GOLDSMITHS, UNIVERSITY OF LONDON. Below it, an arrow pointed towards a heavy brick building: LIBRARY.

"Arthur." Steve stopped and his voice carried the urgency of a man who'd spotted water in a desert.

"That's a university library. Not a branch library with three shelves of large-print mysteries. Archaeology, ancient history, religious studies, architectural surveys. It could have everything I need for every riddle this path throws at us."

Donna turned. She shifted her weight, testing the back that had been stiff since the temple. It seemed to be holding. "How long?"

"An hour, maybe two."

"Twenty minutes."

"You are taking the piss; I won’t even be able to browse the shelves properly in twenty minutes. Forty-five."

"Thirty, Steve."

Arthur assessed the building. It was sturdy brick, with narrow windows and one visible entrance. The door was heavy wood with a reinforced frame. If someone was inside, they'd barricaded well.

"Thirty minutes. I hold the entrance. Donna clears the ground floor. Steve gets what he needs," Arthur said.

They approached and Arthur tried the door to find it locked. He wedged the crowbar into the gap and leaned on it. The mechanism resisted a little and then gave in with a crack that echoed down the empty street.

The lobby smelled of paper, old binding glue, and of warm wood that had been holding the same books for decades. The windows let in enough violet daylight to navigate by. In front of them were rows upon rows of shelves receding into the gloom. Steve made a sound that Arthur could only describe as devotional.

"Ground floor, history and humanities," Steve read from a floor plan on the wall. "First floor, social sciences. Second floor..."

"That will do," said a voice from the dark.

Arthur raised the shield just as Donna's axe came up. Both of them moved simultaneously, Arthur forward, Donna flanking left, before the voice's owner stepped into the light from the windows.

She was an older woman of perhaps sixty-five, thin and rigidly upright, wearing a grey cardigan over a blouse that still had its top button fastened. Her hair was pinned with the severity of someone who considered loose hair a form of professional negligence.

She held a candle in a brass holder in one hand and a hardback Oxford Companion to Classical Civilisation in the other, and she looked at the three-armed strangers who had just broken into her library with an expression of measured disapproval.

"The library is closed," she said. Her voice was cut-glass Home Counties; every consonant treated as mandatory. "The university suspended all services when the emergency began. I have been maintaining the collection in the interim, but lending privileges require a valid institutional card, and I rather doubt any of you are enrolled students."

Steve adjusted his glasses, then adjusted them again.

"Hello, I'm an archaeologist, Steve Parr. I've published in the Journal of Roman Archaeology and the Proceedings of the Society of Antiquaries. I have a genuine academic need for..."

"Publications are not a library card, Mr Parr." She set the Oxford Companion down on the desk and adjusted the edge to meet the corner. "I am Mrs Hadley and I have been the senior librarian at this institution for twenty-three years. The fact that the sky has changed colour does not alter the lending policy."

Donna looked at Arthur. Arthur looked at Steve. And Steve looked like a man who had finally encountered an obstacle his charm could not navigate.

"Mrs Hadley," Steve tried. "With the greatest respect, we're in the middle of..."

"I am aware of the situation, Mr Parr. I have been aware of it since the lights failed and the rather alarming animals began appearing in the car park. I have maintained the collection, kept the doors locked, and ensured that not a single volume has been misplaced, misfiled, or removed without authorisation." She adjusted her cardigan. "The situation outside does not concern the lending policy."

"We could just take them," Donna said, in the tone of someone making a tactical observation rather than a threat.

Mrs Hadley turned to her. The look she fixed on Donna could have stripped paint from a battleship.

"You could. And I would note it."

Donna looked down. The way a schoolgirl would under a headmistress's gaze who'd caught her running in the corridor. The woman who had killed a guardian in an underground temple had just been scolded by a librarian.

Arthur suppressed something that wanted very badly to come out as a laugh. "Mrs Hadley. How many people have come through that door since the sky changed?"

“You mean, before you vandalised it?” Mrs Hadley started but paused. The question had landed somewhere uncomfortable. Her gaze moved from Arthur to the broken lock to the empty street beyond.

"You are the first," she said.

"Mr Parr, what precisely do you require?" she said with a sigh. The question was a reflex. Twenty-three years of directing lost undergraduates had wired it into her conversational structure.

Steve seized the opening before it could close. "Templar history. Specifically, anything on Temple Church and the knight effigies. Mediterranean archaeological surveys. Megalithic temple cultures. lithic engineering….And—" he hesitated before adding, “And anything you have on ancient medical traditions that describe energy channels in the body. Ayurveda, traditional Chinese medicine, that area."

Mrs Hadley studied him. Whatever she saw, whether it was the scratched glasses, or the dried blood on his sleeve, or the genuine need beneath the academic patter, was enough.

"The Templar collection is in aisle fourteen, second shelf from the top," she said. "Mediterranean archaeology is in aisle nine. Megalithic cultures and megalithic architecture are in aisle eleven. The Lehner is the standard survey, but the Renfrew is more rigorous on dating methodology. The energy channel material will be in comparative religion, aisle twenty-two. You will not find a single volume covering all traditions. You will need the Zysk for Ayurveda and the Unschuld for Chinese medicine at minimum."

Steve had drifted towards the shelves before she had finished. Mrs Hadley tracked him with the peripheral awareness of a woman who had spent two decades monitoring undergraduates.

"Mr Parr. You have not been issued borrowing privileges."

"I'm only looking." Steve's voice came from somewhere in the 930s, European history. "I'm allowed to look."

"You are allowed to consult material within the premises. You are not permitted to remove items from the building without a valid library card."

Mrs Hadley watched him disappear into the 930s, then turned to Arthur and Donna. Her composure changed by a degree. "While Mr Parr is browsing, perhaps you could tell me what is happening outside. From the state of you, it would appear to be nothing pleasant."

Arthur looked at Donna. Where did you start?

"Mrs Hadley, truth is, we're still trying to understand it ourselves," Arthur said. "What we know is that some of the rules have changed. Biology. Physics. Things that shouldn't--. We don’t really know why."

"And there are monsters," Donna said.

Mrs Hadley received this with stoic calm. "Yes. A rather dreadful fox attempted to enter the library. Considerably larger than any fox has a right to be, and aggressive in a manner I would describe as purposeful." She smoothed her cardigan then added matter-of-factly. "I struck it with an umbrella until it reconsidered. I then closed and barricaded the door."

"You fought off a mutated animal with an umbrella," Donna said, staring at her.

"I defended the collection with the tools available to me. The umbrella was by the door and the animal was not welcome."

Steve emerged from the stacks carrying eight books. His arms were full, his chin holding the top of the pile in place, the billhook was wedged awkwardly under one elbow. His face had the strained look of an historian who'd been forced to choose eight books from a university library and was already grieving the two hundred he'd left behind.

Donna stared at the pile. "You're carrying that."

"Obviously."

Mrs Hadley stood. Her gaze moved from the books to Steve and then to the door as if she was watching a crime unfold in slow motion.

"Mr Parr. Those have not been processed."

"I know, and I am genuinely sorry, I really am, but..."

"They have not been stamped, catalogued as on-loan, or assigned a return date. If you remove them from this building, they will be recorded as missing, and the next audit will reflect a deficit in the history and comparative religion sections that I will have to account for."

"Mrs Hadley, there may not be a next audit."

"There is always a next audit, Mr Parr."

Steve looked like a man being held hostage by a lending policy.

Arthur made the call. "Steve. Go."

Steve moved for the door with the books clutched to his chest, navigating the lobby at a pace that was technically walking but spiritually fleeing. Mrs Hadley rose from behind the desk.

"Mr Parr!"

"I'm sorry!" Steve called over his shoulder as he reached the entrance. "I swear on everything I have ever excavated that I will return every single volume!"

"That is what they all say!"

Donna was outside. Arthur paused at the broken door and looked back at Mrs Hadley standing in the violet half-light of her library, surrounded by tens of thousands of books.

"Deptford," he said. "The hardware shop on the high street. There are survivors and they could use someone with organisational skills. You cannot stay holed up here for long. And I’m sorry for the books."

Mrs Hadley straightened her cardigan.

"I shall consider it," she said. "After I have noted the missing volumes."

They repacked while walking. Steve redistributed the books into his pack and Donna's, over her objections, with the Templar history and the Lehner in his top compartment for immediate access. His pack was significantly heavier now, a man carrying his tools.

The riddle's last line was still unresolved. Steve would need time with the Templar volume to find what he could about Protector. But the direction was clear: Temple Church, the Inns of Court, northwest through the city.

"Tonight," Steve said. “Give me a few hours with the Templar history and I'll have the last line.”

"Then we’ll camp near the church. Find what you can and we’ll go in at dawn," Arthur said.

The streets widened as they neared the approach to London Bridge. Arthur felt the pull behind his eyes shifting and sharpening. As they crested the bridge approach, Donna slowed and looked west.

Arthur followed her gaze. South of the bridge, maybe half a mile west along the riverbank, an industrial complex squatted against the shore. A sprawling, low-rise building with a sawtooth roof, Arthur recognised the kind from a hundred surveys. A Victorian frame with twentieth-century additions. It was the old print works with its windows dark and the loading bays open.

What caught his eyes though was the geometry of the building.

Arthur's eyes were trained for structural inconsistencies. The building was one storey from the outside. But through the open loading bay, the corridor visible inside angled downward into a depth that had no business existing in London clay at this elevation. You couldn't dig a basement this close to the river without pumping it dry every time it rained. And that was just shoddy engineering.

"That's a riverside Victorian slab foundation," Arthur said. "And from the looks of it the corridor inside goes down at least two storeys. There's nowhere for it to go."

"It is not a structural error," Archie stated. "Structures saturated with concentrated Ether produce spatial distortion. In an industrial building, circuit boards, wiring, and machinery may act as unintended conduits. The System designates these structures as Saturation Anomalies."

"Spatial distortion…..so the Ether is bending space inside the building," Steve said slowly. "You are saying that the rooms are bigger on the inside than the outside."

"Considerably bigger," Archie confirmed.

Steve was quiet for a moment. Then a grin spread across his face, the terrible grin of a man who had just realised that reality now contained something he'd only ever encountered in games.

"That's a dungeon. An actual, literal dungeon."

"Steve, do try not to look excited about the building breaking physics," Donna said.

"I'm not excited. I'm terrified, but also, it's a dungeon, Donna."

Donna studied the building. "We go around."

"We go through," Steve said. "The absorption rate in there -"

"The creature density in there -"

"Both are elevated," Archie confirmed. "Risk and reward scale proportionally."

Arthur looked at the dungeon and then at his battered party, carrying books and protein bars and water that wouldn't last another day.

“We need to be stronger for what’s ahead,” he said. “Temple Church might have another guardian. We barely survived the last one at Level Two. We’re Level Three now, and that’s not enough.”

Donna looked at the loading bay. Then at Steve, who was favouring the arm that had been bitten three fights ago. Then at Arthur, whose shoulder was functional but not healed.

She held his gaze, set her jaw. After a pause she moved the axe to her other hand.

"In and out," Arthur said. "We clear what we can. We take what Ether the place gives us. And we leave before it takes more than we can afford."

He adjusted the Bullguard Shield and started walking towards the loading bay.

Archie's geometry brightened. "Saturation Anomaly density exceeds the Sanctum Zone baseline by a factor of four. Creature mutation within the distorted space will be significantly elevated. The recommended minimum party level for engagement is four."

"We're Level 3," Donna said.

"Recommendations do not require compliance.”

"Archie just told us we're underlevelled for this dungeon," Steve said grinning. "This is exactly like walking into a zone that's two levels above your character and hoping the drops are worth the corpse run."

"Are the drops worth it?" Donna asked.

Arthur looked through the loading bay, into the impossible geometry.

"Only one way to find out," he said.

The dungeon loomed before them.

THE PAPER BACKUP

I walked out of a university library with eight books and I now understand that they may be worth more than the building they came from.

Every device on the planet has died. Which means every fact humanity trusted to read off a screen has died with them. The servers are cold. The cloud is gone. Thirty years spent moving our entire memory onto silicon, and the System deleted the silicon in an afternoon.

What is left is what was printed. Paper only.

The scale of that does not shrink the longer I look at it. Medical databases, gone. Engineering standards, gone. Every language corpus, every scanned manuscript, every thesis that existed only as a file. A species that spent a generation congratulating itself on going paperless has just learned that paper was the only backup to survive the crash. We will spend the next twenty years relearning things we knew last Tuesday, and we will relearn them out of physical books or not at all.

Which reframes the woman guarding that collection. She is not an eccentric. There is a code for what she does, a professional principle her institute has held for decades under a little heading: preservation and continuity of access to knowledge. It was written to cover damp and shrinking budgets. It now describes the last defence of the recorded memory of the species, and not one of the people who drafted it ever imagined it would be read that literally.

And I should have seen it before anyone. I have spent my life reading the durable medium, clay and stone and bone, marvelling that it survived at all. Then I kept every note I ever made on a laptop in a flat I will not see again. The archaeologist trusted the cloud.

The Curators never made that mistake. They never wrote something that could be switched off. They carved it. They built it into monuments and painted it onto rock. They chose the medium that outlasts empires.
