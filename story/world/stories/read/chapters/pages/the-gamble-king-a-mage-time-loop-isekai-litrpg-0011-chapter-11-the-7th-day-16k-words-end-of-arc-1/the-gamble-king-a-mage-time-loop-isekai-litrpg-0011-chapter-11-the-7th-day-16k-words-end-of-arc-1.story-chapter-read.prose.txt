Something was wrong. Something was very wrong.

Max had expected to wake up on the morning of the sixth day transformed. The Dragon Heart's power should have been coursing through his veins by now, settling into his soul like sediment after a storm. He should have been able to feel Fanga humming beneath his skin, ready to manifest at Gregory's test. The whole plan had been built on this assumption—eat Heart, die, come back with power intact.

And by all reasonable measures, it seemed the power had transferred. Only not in the way he'd been expecting.

Maybe it was because the thing had belonged to a freaking dragon.

Max had been awake for about thirty minutes now, and he could not get up.

Not "didn't want to."

Not "felt groggy."

Could not.

His limbs felt like they'd been filled with wet concrete. Every attempt to sit upright sent waves of nausea crashing through him, followed immediately by a dizzy spell that made the tent walls spin like a child's top.

He was sweating heavily despite feeling colder than he'd ever been in his life. The kind of cold that came from the inside out, like his bones had been carved from ice. Gerth had lit a small fire near his bedroll some twenty minutes ago, and Max could barely feel its heat despite being close enough that the smoke made his eyes water.

His heart was doing interesting things too. Racing for a few beats, then skipping, then settling into a rhythm that felt suspiciously irregular. Each breath required conscious effort, as if his body had forgotten how to perform this most basic function automatically.

Max knew exactly what this was. The Dragon Heart. Had to be.

The problem was, if he died with this power still killing him, he'd be back to zero rerolls. And if he came back to this same day and died again from the same cause, well. That would be that.

"I'm fucked," Max said to the tent ceiling.

"Do not say that," Gerth's voice came from somewhere near the fire. The old healer sounded irritated, which was his default state but seemed more pronounced than usual. "Negative thinking leads to negative outcomes."

"I wasn't being negative," Max replied, his voice coming out as barely more than a whisper. "I was being accurate."

"Accuracy and pessimism aren't the same thing, though they often travel together." Gerth appeared in Max's peripheral vision, crouching beside the bedroll with his leather satchel. "Let me see your eyes."

Max tried to turn his head to look at the healer directly. The movement sent the tent spinning again, so he settled for rolling his eyes in Gerth's general direction.

"Hmm," Gerth said, peering at him closely. "Pupils are dilated. Skin's gone gray. You're sweating like a pig but shivering like it's midwinter." He pressed the back of his hand against Max's forehead. "Fever's climbing too."

"Wonderful diagnosis," Max managed. "What's the cure?"

"For what, exactly?" Gerth asked, sitting back on his heels. "You haven't told me what's wrong with you. Just woke up like this, did you?"

Max considered his options. The truth was obviously out of the question. But lying to a healer about symptoms when those symptoms might actually kill him seemed counterproductive.

"Food poisoning?" he suggested weakly.

Gerth's eyebrows climbed toward his receding hairline. "Food poisoning that makes you sweat ice water and turns your skin the color of old parchment? What exactly did you eat, boy? Poison?"

"Maybe."

"Maybe," Gerth repeated flatly. "You maybe ate poison."

"It's possible."

Gerth stared at him for a long moment. "Are you having me on?"

"Would I joke about this?"

"You once convinced three different people that you'd discovered a new species of bird, when you'd really just painted a chicken blue."

Max blinked slowly. "I did?"

"Last spring. Kept the charade going for two weeks before someone noticed the thing was laying eggs."

"That does sound like something I'd do," Max admitted, though he had no memory of it. Harek's reputation for elaborate pranks was apparently well-established.

"So yes," Gerth continued, "you would absolutely joke about this. The question is whether you're stupid enough to joke about it while you're actually dying."

"I really don't understand how this happened," Max said, which was technically true.

Gerth sighed, the sound carrying the weight of decades spent treating idiots who'd done idiotic things for idiotic reasons. "Right. Well, whatever you've done to yourself, lying here debating it won't fix it." He stood, knees popping audibly. "I'll go brew something for that fever. Willow bark, maybe some feverfew if I can find any that hasn't been trampled by horses."

"Sounds delightful."

"It'll taste like boiled leather and disappointment," Gerth said matter-of-factly. "But it might keep you from cooking your brain." He paused at the tent entrance. "Try to sleep. Your body needs rest more than it needs you lying there feeling sorry for yourself."

"I'm not feeling sorry for myself."

"No, you're feeling sorry for the situation. Which is worse, because situations can be changed." Gerth turned back, pulling a rough cloth from his satchel. He knelt beside Max again, his face softening slightly as he wiped the sweat from Max's forehead with surprising gentleness.

For a moment, the old healer's gruff exterior cracked, revealing something that looked almost paternal. The cloth was warm and smelled faintly of herbs. Gerth's movements were careful, methodical.

"I'll be back soon," he said quietly, the harsh edge gone from his voice. "Don't do anything stupid while I'm gone."

"Such as?"

"Dying, for instance."

With that cheerful encouragement, Gerth gathered his things and left, the tent flap falling closed behind him with a soft whisper.

Max was alone.

This was really not what he'd expected. But being stuck meant he had time to think. And thinking, Max realized, was probably more useful than panicking.

First observation: he wasn't bleeding. At least not yet. Not like the last time he'd consumed the Heart, when blood had poured from his nose, ears, and eyes like his body was a broken faucet. That had to be a good sign. Maybe.

Second observation: the Heart itself was still intact. He'd managed to work this into his conversation with Gerth—casual questions about whether anything had gone missing from the Prince's tent, whether anyone had mentioned the artifact being damaged or stolen. The old man had looked at him like he'd asked whether the sky was still blue.

"Why would the Heart be damaged?" Gerth had said. "Prince checks on it every morning and every night before sleeping. Thing's more secure than the crown jewels."

Which meant the Heart hadn't looped back with him. This wasn't some external poison working its way through his system. This was related to his soul, to whatever changes had occurred when he'd consumed the artifact.

Which was freaking worse.

The fever spiked, sending waves of heat and cold through his body simultaneously. His vision blurred, then cleared, then blurred again. His heart did another one of its interesting little skips.

Oh God. It's getting worse.

He thought about Sabo's words by the river. "Such shortcuts often exact their own price." Helpful in retrospect. Less so when you were already paying said price and had no idea how to make it stop.

Max tried to remember everything he'd ever read about dragon magic in Sabo's novels. Unfortunately, most of it had been frustratingly vague—poetic descriptions of "ancient power" and "primordial essence" that told him exactly nothing useful about what consuming a Dragon Heart might actually do to a person.

He thought about Fanga itself. What it was, how it worked, whether there was some way to accelerate or control whatever process was currently trying to kill him.

He thought about Gregory's test at dawn. About what would happen if he simply didn't show up. About whether anyone would come looking for him or just assume he'd fled in cowardice. About death. The final one.

He thought and thought and thought.

"Damn it all," Max managed to mutter to the tent ceiling.

The problem was, he had no idea what to do about this. At all. None whatsoever. He was flying completely blind, with no manual, no precedent, and no backup plan beyond "hope it doesn't kill me."

This had all started with that stupid suggestion from Sabo, and now here he was—dying slowly in a tent.

Wait.

Sabo.

She had said she was with the pilgrims. She could have said anything else, but affiliated herself with them. The pilgrims were here, camped somewhere nearby. They'd been weird—all that talk about Chroniclers and dream-watching—but if this was a thing related to the soul, they might have some starting point for what he should do.

Right?

Max honestly didn't know, but it was the only lead he had.

He tried to call out, but what emerged from his throat was barely more than a whisper. His vocal cords felt like they'd been replaced with wet paper. He tried again, managing something that might generously be called a croak.

On the third attempt, he produced a sound that was audible beyond the tent walls, though it couldn't accurately be described as human speech.

"Gnnngh," Max said to the world at large. "Hlllp."

This was him trying to scream. He couldn't even fucking scream properly.

"Urrrrgh," he tried again, putting all his remaining strength into it.

Footsteps approached the tent. The flap rustled, then opened to reveal Tomas's bearded face, wearing an expression of mild concern.

"Heard you were dying," Tomas said, poking his head inside.

"Yeah, no shit," Max wheezed.

Tomas studied him for a moment, taking in the sweat-soaked bedding, the gray pallor, the general air of impending doom. "You do look like you're dying," he observed. "What'd you eat? Some sort of mushroom?"

"No mushrooms."

"My grandmother taught me about mushrooms," Tomas continued, apparently settling in for a chat. "What you can eat, what you cannot. I didn't see any you can't eat around here, but there's always new ones." He leaned against the tent pole. "Could you tell me what the one you ate looked like? So I can add it to those not to eat."

Max stared at him. "I didn't eat any mushrooms."

"Right, well, if you remember what it looked like before you die, that'd be helpful. A lot of people have died for the mushrooms we now know not to eat, and I have a lot of respect for those brave souls." Tomas nodded solemnly. "If you die, I'll have a lot of respect for your sacrifice for the greater cause. Though I do hope you survive."

Do these people have no sense of empathy for a dying person?

"That's..." Max finally wheezed, "very touching."

"Well, my grandmoth-"

"Tomas," Max interrupted, using what little breath he had left. "Please. Call the pilgrims."

"The pilgrims?"

"Their chief. Elder. Whatever they call the one in charge."

Tomas blinked. "You want to see the pilgrims? While you're dying?"

"Especially while I'm dying."

"That's... peculiar. Even for you."

"Please," Max said, the word coming out more as a gasp than speech. "Just... get them."

Before Tomas could return, a young guard appeared at the tent entrance, clearing his throat nervously.

"Lord Vanheim? His Highness Prince Keiran requests an audience."

Max tried to sit up and immediately regretted it. The world tilted sideways, then righted itself with alarming reluctance. "Send him in," he managed.

The tent flap opened wider, and Prince Keiran entered, followed closely by Sir Gregory. The space, already modest, suddenly felt cramped with two additional bodies of considerable presence. The Prince's silver hair caught the firelight as he surveyed Max's condition with cool eyes.

"Your Highness," Max said, the greeting coming out as barely more than a whisper.

"Harek." The Prince's voice carried its usual measured authority, though his expression softened slightly as he took in Max's state. "I heard you were unwell."

Max glanced at Gregory, who stood silently beside the Prince, his face unreadable.

"I have no idea what happened," Max said, which was technically true in the most literal sense. "Might have eaten something that disagreed with me. Or been bitten by some sort of insect."

"Hmm." The Prince moved closer, his gaze taking in the sweat-soaked bedding and Max's obvious distress. "Well, you'll be pleased to know we're departing for Frosthold tomorrow at dawn instead of afternoon. Your condition has necessitated a change in our schedule."

Max blinked slowly. "That's... considerate."

"Do not die on me, Harek," the Prince said, though his tone held more pragmatic concern than emotional warmth. "Your father would resent me for it. And my daughter..." He paused, considering. "Well, I'm not entirely certain what Aelara would think. How are relations between you two at the moment?"

Max had absolutely no idea what Harek's relationship with his betrothed was like, but 'lying' seemed like the path of least resistance. "Wonderful," he said.

"Hmm," the Prince repeated, his tone suggesting he found this response either unexpected or suspicious. Possibly both.

"Hang in there, my boy. It will all be alright." The Prince turned to Gregory. "Perhaps we should postpone his test? He's clearly in no condition to manifest anything beyond fever sweats. This could be the result of all that endless running you've had him doing."

Gregory's expression remained neutral. "I doubt running could do this to a person, Your Highness."

"Still—"

"By the royal laws governing knighthood," Gregory continued politely but firmly, "I cannot change a date once given. Lord Vanheim will have to fail if he's unable to manifest Fanga by dawn tomorrow."

"What an asshole," Max muttered.

The Prince's eyebrows rose. "What was that?"

Max blinked in surprise. He'd thought that was in his head. Apparently talking without meaning to was another delightful new symptom. "I said I was expecting no less from such a distinguished knight. Integrity. Consistency. All that."

The Prince studied him for a moment, then nodded slowly. "Indeed. Well, rest up, Harek. We'll have Gerth look after you." He glanced around the cramped tent. "Sir Gregory, shall we leave the boy to recover in peace?"

"Of course, Your Highness."

As they prepared to leave, the Prince paused at the tent entrance. "Do try not to die, Harek. Your father and I have been friends for thirty years. It would be awkward to explain to him that his heir expired on my watch due to bad fish or whatever this is."

"I'll do my best not to inconvenience you with my death," Max replied weakly.

The Prince's mouth twitched in what might have been amusement. "See that you don't."

It didn't take long after that for the pilgrims to arrive.

"Lord Vanheim," came a voice from outside the tent. "We understand you have requested our presence."

"Come in," Max said, then immediately regretted the invitation as three white-robed figures entered the already cramped space. The elderly man with the impressive beard led the way, followed by two of the younger pilgrims with their distinctive blue star tattoos.

"You called for us?" the elder asked, settling onto his knees beside Max's bedroll with surprising grace for someone his age.

"Hey. Hi. Remember me?" Max managed, his voice barely above a whisper.

The elder's weathered face creased in polite confusion. "I'm afraid I have no memory of having spoken with you previously, Lord Vanheim. Though of course, I know who you are."

Max felt a spike of frustration cut through his fever haze. Right. The river conversation had only happened in one loop. To them, they'd never met.

"Right," he said. "I'm Harek Vanheim. Apparently you know that already."

"Indeed we do," the elder confirmed.

"How?"

The elder exchanged a glance with his companions. "Because you are the only nobleman of substantial... build... named Harek Vanheim currently present in this encampment."

One of the younger pilgrims added helpfully, "The blue star tattoos help us remember faces and names. It's quite useful for—"

"Right," Max cut him off, not wanting to enter a philosophical debate about pilgrim memory techniques while his body was slowly dissolving from the inside out. "Look, I believe this illness I have originates from the soul. I need help."

The elder's eyebrows rose slightly. "How do you know it originates from the soul?"

Max glanced meaningfully at Tomas, who was still standing near the tent entrance, taking in every word with obvious fascination.

Max looked at him. Then kept looking.

Tomas beamed back, completely missing the hint. "Oh, don't mind me," he said cheerfully. "Please continue. I'm quite curious to know what sort of mushroom attacks the soul directly. My grandmother always said—"

"Tomas," Max interrupted. "Could you please leave?"

"Leave?"

"Yes. Go away. Somewhere else."

Tomas looked genuinely confused. "But this is fascinating. Soul mushrooms? I've never heard of such a thing."

"Tomas."

"Right, right." The bearded soldier finally took the hint, backing toward the tent entrance. "I'll just... check on the horses. Or something."

Once Tomas had departed, Max turned back to the pilgrims.

What followed was essentially the same conversation he'd had by the river, condensed and edited for his current state of near-death. Max explained about being from another world, about knowing their lives as stories, about the entity calling itself Sabo who might be a Chronicler. The pilgrims listened with the same expressions of growing wonder and reverence they'd shown before.

"You have been honored," the elder said finally, his voice hushed with awe. "Few receive direct visitation from the Watchers. To be known by a Chronicler is to be marked by fate itself."

"Wonderful," Max croaked. "Very flattering. Now can you help me not die from whatever this honor is doing to my soul?"

"There is a way," the elder said thoughtfully, stroking his beard.

"Great," Max croaked.

"Though you could die."

"Boooo," Max said flatly.

One of the younger pilgrims actually chuckled at this, quickly covering his mouth when the elder shot him a reproving look.

"We would need to put you in a ritual trance," the elder explained, settling more comfortably beside Max's bedroll. "If you have indeed consumed a dragon's heart, then there are essentially two souls occupying your body at present. Your own, and a fragment of the dragon's essence."

"Two souls," Max repeated. "That explains the fever dreams and the inability to move without feeling like I'm dying."

"Precisely. Dragon hearts are repositories of immense power because they contain the dragon's soul and accumulated magical essence. To truly kill a dragon, one must destroy its heart. Otherwise, the creature can regenerate from even the smallest remaining fragment."

The younger pilgrim with the star tattoo added, "Right now, part of the dragon's soul is attempting to assert dominance within your spiritual essence. You would need to enter your inner world and chase the intruder away."

"Okay," Max said slowly. "Okay. And how exactly do we do this?"

"It is a ritual of the old ways," the elder explained. "We would guide your consciousness inward while your body remains in deep trance. The process could take hours, possibly longer. And if you lose the spiritual battle..."

"I'd be evicted from my own body," Max finished.

"In the most charitable interpretation, yes."

Max considered this. On one hand, the idea of engaging in metaphysical combat with a dragon's soul while his body lay unconscious seemed like exactly the sort of insane gamble that would get him permanently killed. On the other hand, he was already dying slowly, and at least this way offered a chance of survival.

"I'd like to do it," he said finally. "I'd like to try."

The elder nodded solemnly. "Then we shall prepare the necessary components. We would request that you be brought to our encampment, where the ritual circles will be established."

"That can be arranged."

As if summoned by the mention of moving Max, Gerth chose that moment to return, carrying a steaming bowl that smelled like boiled tree bark and disappointment. He took one look at the assembled pilgrims and immediately scowled.

"What are you people doing to my patient?"

"Offering assistance," the elder replied calmly.

"We're going to their camp," Max explained. "For a ritual."

"Absolutely not," Gerth said, setting down the bowl with enough force to slosh liquid onto the ground. "Too much motion could kill you in this state. You're staying right here until this fever breaks."

"Gerth, I'll be alright—"

"Don't you 'Gerth' me, boy." The old healer turned to face the pilgrims, his weathered face set in hard lines. "This is Harek of House Vanheim, heir to Frosthold, son of Lord Tredor and Lady Elsa. If you kill him with your mystical nonsense, they'll have your heads. All of them."

The elder regarded Gerth with serene patience. "We do not hold another person's life in our hands. That belongs to higher powers than ourselves."

Gerth spat on the ground. "Fuck your higher powers. I've been keeping this boy alive since he was in swaddling clothes, and I'm not about to let a bunch of star-tattooed lunatics finish him off with ritual dancing."

The pilgrims exchanged glances, their expressions remaining infuriatingly calm in the face of Gerth's hostility.

"The choice is his to make," the elder said simply.

"It is," Max agreed weakly. "And I'm making it."

Gerth stared at him for a long moment, then threw up his hands in exasperation. "Fine. But when you die from this foolishness, don't expect me to explain it to your father."

"I'll try to include that in my last words," Max said.

Gerth muttered something under his breath that sounded distinctly uncomplimentary about pilgrims, their gods, and their collective intelligence, but he stepped aside as two soldiers were called to help transport Max to the pilgrim encampment.

The wooden stretcher was about as comfortable as lying on a dining table, which was to say not at all.

Two soldiers carried Max through the camp with the sort of careful efficiency usually reserved for transporting valuable but fragile cargo. Which, Max supposed, was essentially what he'd become.

The procession attracted attention immediately. Conversations stopped mid-sentence as people turned to watch the heir to Frosthold being carried past on what looked suspiciously like a makeshift bier.

"There goes Lord Harek," someone muttered nearby. "Heard he's dying."

"About time," came a voice from Max's left. "Never was fit to inherit anyway."

Max turned his head slightly to see who'd spoken, but the effort sent waves of dizziness through him, so he settled for staring at the sky and pretending he hadn't heard.

Near the sentinel camp, Torsten raised his hand in what might have been a salute or simply acknowledgment. Skeld, standing beside him, made a quick gesture—two fingers to his forehead, then pointed skyward. Some sort of blessing, perhaps, or just the northern equivalent of "good luck, you'll need it."

Varn simply nodded and went back to sharpening his knife.

"Courage, Lord Vanheim!" called a voice from the cook fires. "The gods favor the bold!"

"His little brother's probably celebrating," another voice said, clearly not intended for Max to hear but perfectly audible nonetheless. "Finn always was the smarter one. Better suited for lordship anyway."

"Harsh but fair," someone else agreed. "At least the boy can add his own gambling debts without help."

Max closed his eyes and tried to pretend he was somewhere else. Anywhere else. A beach in the Caribbean, maybe. Or his old apartment, with its reliable heating and distinct lack of people discussing his imminent demise.

The stretcher jostled as they navigated around a supply wagon, and Max had to grip the sides to keep from sliding off. His bearers muttered apologies and adjusted their pace.

"Easy there," came Tomas's familiar voice. "He's fragile cargo."

Max opened his eyes to find Tomas walking alongside the stretcher, leading Flash by the reins. The warhorse seemed unusually subdued, its dark eyes fixed on Max with what looked almost like concern.

"How are you holding up?" Tomas asked.

"Like I'm being carried on a plank toward my own funeral," Max replied weakly.

"That's the spirit. Keep that sense of humor." Tomas reached over to pat Max's shoulder. "This horse of yours has been restless all morning. Keeps looking toward your tent. Animals know things, they say."

Flash snorted softly and nudged closer to the stretcher, close enough that Max could feel the animal's warm breath on his face. For a moment, the simple presence of the horse was oddly comforting.

"Take care of him if I don't make it back," Max said. He wasn't sure why he said that. It was cheesy. Defeatist. Unlike him.

"You'll make it back," Tomas said firmly. "You're too stubborn to die from whatever mushroom this is."

"Is that your professional medical opinion?"

"That's my opinion as someone who's watched you survive things that should have killed you three times over. I was with you at Eastwatch, remember?"

They passed a group of younger soldiers who'd clearly been discussing Max's condition. One of them called out, "Don't let the pilgrims sacrifice you to their tree gods, my lord!"

"Different religion entirely," Max called back, then immediately regretted the effort as his throat burned.

"Save your strength," Tomas advised. "You'll need it for whatever they're planning."

The camp's edge was approaching, marked by the transition from military order to the more organic arrangement of the pilgrim encampment. White tents formed their perfect circle around the central fire, which burned with that same unnaturally steady flame Max remembered.

"Well," he said to no one in particular, "this should be interesting."

"That's one word for it," Tomas replied. "Terrifying is another."

"I prefer 'character building,'" Max said.

"You have plenty of character already. What you need is to not die."

"I'll take that under advisement."

The stretcher bearers slowed as they approached the pilgrim circle, and Max caught a glimpse of robed figures emerging from their tents to greet them. Time to find out whether ancient mystical rituals were any more reliable than modern medicine.

Probably not, but at this point, he was willing to try just about anything.

The preparations took longer than Max had expected. The pilgrims arranged stones in intricate patterns around the central fire, sprinkled what looked like sand or salt in careful lines, and muttered in a language that sounded nothing like the common tongue he'd grown accustomed to.

One of the younger pilgrims approached with a clay bowl filled with a liquid that smelled like fermented flowers. "Drink this," she said, offering it to Max. "All of it."

"What is it?"

"Something to thin the barriers between worlds," the elder explained from across the circle. "Your consciousness needs to move freely if you're to navigate the inner realm."

Max took the bowl and sniffed it again. "Does it taste better than it smells?"

"No," the pilgrim said matter-of-factly.

"Fantastic."

Two other pilgrims helped him remove his shirt, the movement sending fresh waves of nausea through him. The air was cool against his fevered skin, and he found himself shivering despite the warmth radiating from the central fire.

Before the ritual could begin in earnest, the elder approached and knelt beside Max, his face serious.

"You said you are not the original owner of this body, did you not?"

"Indeed," Max confirmed.

The elder nodded slowly. "Then you would not have this problem in the first place if you were. No one can simply take another's body. It is one's soul's dwelling and rightful property."

Max frowned. "What are you saying?"

"The battle will not be as you think—you fighting the dragon itself. You will both be incorporeal entities in the inner realm. But there is a third presence there, one you must find." The elder's eyes were grave. "The true master of this body. You will need to convince them to let you have the body for yourself, to let go of this world. Or..." He paused. "They could reclaim it entirely."

The implications hit Max like a bucket of cold water. "Harek. The original Harek's soul is still in there."

"Indeed. And until he grants you true ownership, you remain a... guest. Which is why the dragon's essence can take hold as it has. The body recognizes no clear master."

Max stared at the elder, processing this information. "So I need to convince a dead man to let me keep his life."

"Not dead. Displaced. There is a difference."

"Right." Max looked down at the bowl in his hands. "And if he says no?"

"Then you will find yourself in the void between worlds, and this body will either return to its rightful owner or be claimed by the dragon entirely."

"Fantastic." Max lifted the bowl. "Well, here's to persuasive conversation."

The liquid tasted like someone had fermented flowers in dirty socks, then added a hint of bitter herbs for good measure. Max forced himself to drink it all, grimacing with each swallow.

Almost immediately, the world began to shift around the edges. Colors became more vivid, shadows deeper. The central fire seemed to pulse in rhythm with his heartbeat.

The pilgrims began to chant, their voices weaving together in harmonies that seemed to bypass his ears entirely and resonate directly in his bones.

That didn't make sense at all, but it was the best description he could find.

The sound grew louder, or perhaps his perception of it did, until it felt like the very air was singing.

Lights began appearing in his peripheral vision—small, dancing points of illumination that moved independently of any visible source. When he tried to focus on them directly, they vanished, only to reappear somewhere else.

"The fuck?" Max muttered, then immediately regretted speaking as the words seemed to echo strangely in the air around him.

Voices joined the lights—whispers in languages he didn't recognize, conversations that seemed to be happening just out of reach. The boundary between the physical world and whatever lay beyond it was dissolving like sugar in rain.

The central fire suddenly flared higher, and Max could swear he saw something in the flames. A shape. Human-like but made entirely of flickering light and heat. It was looking at him with what might have been eyes, though they were more like concentrated points of burning intensity.

"Okay, that's new," Max said, his voice sounding distant even to himself.

More figures appeared around the fire—dancing shapes of flame and shadow that moved with grace. They seemed to be celebrating something, or perhaps mourning. It was impossible to tell which.

Max's consciousness began to drift, like he was floating just above his own body. He could see himself lying on the ground, surrounded by chanting pilgrims, but he felt increasingly disconnected from the physical form.

The elder's voice cut through the growing haze, urgent and clear: "Remember—find the true master. Convince him. And beware the dragon's whispers. It will try to claim you both."

"Noted," Max tried to say, but wasn't sure if the words actually left his lips.

The last thing he saw before consciousness slipped away entirely was the fire-figure stepping out of the flames and walking directly toward him, its burning eyes fixed on his with unmistakable intent.

Then everything dissolved into darkness and motion and the sensation of falling through infinite space.

Light.

Pure, blinding light washed over Max like a breaking wave, followed immediately by absolute silence so complete it felt like being wrapped in cotton. He could feel himself walking, though his feet made no sound and there was no sensation of actual contact with anything solid.

He looked down and found himself standing on what appeared to be still water—so clear and pure he could see his own reflection staring back at him with perfect clarity.

Max was back in his original body. His own face, his own hair, his own everything. The first thing he noticed, naturally, was that he was wearing his job clothes. The same khakis and button-down shirt he'd worn to Pinnacle Digital Marketing on the day he'd been fired.

"Seriously?" he said to his reflection. "This is what my subconscious chooses? The outfit from the worst day of my professional life?"

His voice carried strangely in the space, echoing without any visible surfaces to bounce off.

He looked up from his reflection. The light had dimmed to something manageable, revealing a perfectly blue sky dotted with a few lazy white clouds. There was no visible sun, yet everything was illuminated with that ideal natural brightness you only saw in paintings or dreams.

The temperature was exactly perfect—neither warm nor cool, just the precise point where you forgot your body had needs.

Max turned in a slow circle, trying to orient himself in this space. In one direction, far on the horizon, stood a single tower. It looked medieval, the kind of thing you'd expect to see housing a princess or a wizard. In the opposite direction, the world grew progressively darker, storm clouds gathering with flashes of fire and lightning dancing between them.

The darkness was approaching. Slowly but steadily, like a tide of shadow creeping across the pristine landscape.

"Right," Max said to himself. "Dragon's influence, moving this way. Tower's probably where Harek is. Simple enough."

He started running across the water's surface, his feet somehow finding purchase on what should have been liquid. The sensation was like jogging on the world's most perfectly calibrated treadmill—effortless forward motion without any of the usual strain or fatigue.

The tower grew larger as he approached, revealing intricate stonework and narrow windows that suggested considerable age. When he reached the base, Max glanced back over his shoulder. The dark influence had gained ground, but not enough to be an immediate threat. He still had time.

The tower's door was massive, carved from dark wood and fitted with iron hinges that belonged in a museum. Max pushed it open and stepped inside.

He was immediately hit by a cart.

Or rather, a cart passed directly through him without slowing, followed by the horse pulling it, which also failed to acknowledge his existence in any meaningful way. Max stumbled backward, more from surprise than impact, and found himself in the middle of what could only be described as a medieval parade.

The space inside the tower was impossibly large—not a tower interior at all, but a broad stone plaza surrounded by cheering crowds. Banners flew from every visible surface, displaying heraldry Max didn't recognize but which felt familiar nonetheless. The air buzzed with celebration, voices raised in joy and excitement.

"Long live Lady Elsa!" someone shouted nearby.

"Hail to House Solaryon!" called another voice.

"Long live Lord Harek!" the crowd chanted. "Happy birthday to the heir of Frosthold!"

Max stepped carefully through the crowd, people passing through him like he was made of mist. The detail was incredible—he could see individual threads in clothing, smell roasted meat from food vendors, hear the jingle of coins in purses and the creak of leather armor.

"Holy shit," he breathed, spotting his first elf.

The woman was tall and graceful in a way that seemed almost choreographed, with pointed ears and features so perfectly symmetrical they were nearly alien. Her hair was silver-white and braided with small gems that caught the light as she moved. Everything about her radiated an otherworldly elegance that made Max understand why every fantasy author in history had been obsessed with elves.

Sabo's descriptions in the novels hadn't done them justice. Not even close.

More fantastic beings moved through the crowd—dwarves with elaborately braided beards and axes strapped to their backs, humans in the livery of various kingdoms, even a few creatures Max couldn't immediately identify but which looked like they'd stepped out of the most ambitious fantasy films ever made.

Through the celebratory chaos, Max spotted the focus of the crowd's attention. A ornate cart was making its way slowly through the plaza, decorated with flowers and pulled by white horses. On the cart sat an elven woman with long dark hair and the same otherworldly beauty Max had noticed in the crowd, though hers was tempered by something warmer, more human.

Beside her sat a child—a boy of maybe ten or eleven with black curly hair and bright blue eyes. He was waving enthusiastically at the crowd, his face lit up with pure joy. The boy was leaner than Harek had been when Max first inhabited his body, but the resemblance was unmistakable.

"Lord Harek and Lady Elsa!" the crowd chanted. "Long live the future of Frosthold!"

Max stared at the scene, understanding flooding through him.

Banners stretched between buildings proclaimed "Happy 11th Birthday, Lord Harek Vanheim!" in elaborate script. Another read "Eleven Years of Joy - Many More to Come!" The heraldry of House Vanheim flew alongside House Solaryon banners, celebrating not just political alliance but the child who embodied both bloodlines.

This was a memory. Harek's memory.

The happiness on young Harek's face was infectious, even viewed through the strange lens of spiritual possession. This was before the gambling, before the drinking, before whatever had turned the joyful child into the disappointing young man Max had replaced.

"Huh."

Max pushed through the crowd, trying to reach the ornate cart. The celebration continued around him, oblivious to his presence.

"Harek!" he called out, waving his arms. "Hey, kid!"

The boy continued waving at the crowd, his bright smile never wavering. Max might as well have been shouting at a movie screen.

"Right," Max muttered, weaving between people who passed through him like he was made of air. "Memory, not reality. Got it."

He moved closer to the cart, studying young Harek's face then reached the edge of the cart's path and extended his hand toward the boy, curious whether he could make any contact in this memory-space.

The first arrow took Lady Elsa in the throat.

Blood sprayed across young Harek's face as his mother jerked backward, the elegant smile transforming into shock and pain. The second arrow punched through her chest with a wet thud that carried clearly over the crowd's cheers. The third struck her forehead with surgical precision, snapping her head back and sending her tumbling from the cart.

"My God," Max breathed, stumbling backward as blood splattered across the white horses and flower decorations.

Young Harek fell from the cart as his mother's body hit the ground, landing hard on the stone plaza. His birthday clothes were soaked with blood—not his own, but hers. The child's joyful expression had vanished, replaced by confusion and terror as he crawled toward his mother's still form.

"Mother?" he whispered, his small hands shaking as he reached for her. "Mother, wake up!"

The crowd's celebration turned to screams. People ran in every direction, trampling banners and overturning vendor stalls in their panic. Knights appeared from somewhere, shouting orders and drawing weapons.

Max's eyes snapped to the rooftop where the arrows had come from. An elven archer stood silhouetted against the sky, bow still raised. Even from this distance, Max could see the cold satisfaction on the assassin's face.

"Death to the traitors!" the archer shouted, his voice carrying clearly over the chaos. "Death to those who would sully our name with filth!"

One of the knights had spotted the archer. Light began gathering around the knight's raised sword. But the archer was already reaching for something at his belt.

A crystalline orb appeared in the assassin's free hand. He struck it against his bow, and the crystal burst into flames that seemed to burn without heat or smoke.

"For the Úlûvain!" the archer cried. "Remember this day!"

The orb flared brilliant white, and the archer vanished just as the knight's attack struck the rooftop. The magical energy tore through stone and timber, collapsing half the building in a shower of debris and dust.

But Max's attention had already returned to young Harek, who was still kneeling beside his mother's body.

"Mother, please," the child sobbed, blood streaking his face as tears cut clean lines through the crimson. "Please wake up. It's my birthday."

A younger version of Gerth appeared through the chaos, his beard less gray and his face less weathered, but unmistakably the same man. He moved through the panicking crowd, his healer's satchel bouncing against his hip.

"Harek," Gerth said gently, kneeling beside the boy. "We need to go. It's not safe here."

"No!" Harek cried, clutching at his mother's bloodied dress. "I won't leave her! She needs to wake up!"

"She can't wake up, lad," Gerth said, his voice breaking slightly. "I'm sorry, but she can't."

More knights arrived, forming a protective circle around the scene. One of them, wearing the distinctive armor of House Vanheim, approached Gerth urgently.

"The plaza isn't secure," the knight reported. "There could be more assassins. We need to evacuate the heir immediately."

Gerth nodded grimly and reached for Harek, who fought against him with the desperate strength of a child refusing to accept the impossible.

"No! No, I won't go!" Harek screamed, his small fists beating ineffectively against Gerth's chest. "She needs me! Mother needs me!"

"Your mother is gone, lad," Gerth said, tears streaming down his own face as he lifted the struggling child. "But she'd want you safe. That's what mothers do—they keep their children safe."

The crowd had mostly dispersed, leaving behind a plaza littered with abandoned celebration banners now stained with blood. The white horses stood perfectly still, as if understanding the magnitude of what had just occurred.

Max stood frozen in the middle of it all, watching eight-year-old Harek being carried away from his mother's body while the boy's cries echoed off the stone walls.

"Damn," Max said quietly to himself, understanding flooding through him. "This is so fucked up."

Max followed their path through the dispersing crowd, stepping over trampled flowers and abandoned celebration banners. The plaza that had been filled with joy moments before now looked like a battlefield.

As they neared the edge of the square, the world shifted around him like water.

Max blinked and found himself standing knee-deep in a slow-moving river. The water was clear and cold, flowing past his legs with gentle persistence. The sound of it was oddly soothing after the chaos of the plaza.

Chanting filled the air—low, melodious voices singing in a language that sounded like wind through trees.

A boat appeared upstream, moving with the current toward where Max stood.

It was unlike anything he'd ever seen. The vessel was carved from what looked like a single piece of white wood, its sides decorated with intricate designs that seemed to shift and move in the flickering torchlight. Silver trim caught the light along its edges, and flowers—so many flowers—covered every available surface.

Lady Elsa lay in the center of the boat, dressed in a gown of midnight blue that made her skin look like porcelain. Her hands were folded over her chest, and her dark hair had been braided with silver threads and tiny white flowers. She looked peaceful, as if she were simply sleeping.

The boat was surrounded by swimmers—elves moving through the water, their voices rising and falling in harmonious waves. Some carried torches, others held flowers which they cast into the water as the boat passed. Humans lined both banks of the river, adding their voices to the elvish chant.

He watched until it disappeared around a bend in the river, taking Elsa on her final journey.

When he looked back toward the riverbank, the scene had changed again.

The crowd was dispersing slowly, people approaching Lord Tredor and his children to offer condolences. Max could see the formal courtesies of medieval society playing out—the careful words, the respectful distances, the way grief was acknowledged but not dwelt upon.

"My deepest sympathies, Lord Tredor," a well-dressed man was saying. "Lady Elsa was beloved by all who knew her."

"House Blackmoor stands with House Vanheim in this dark hour," added a woman in elaborate robes. "If there is anything we can do..."

Tredor accepted the words with the politeness of someone going through motions while his mind was elsewhere. Haldor clung to his father's leg, while baby Finn had been taken by the maids to spare the crowd his continued wailing. Max immediately recognized them.

But where was Harek?

Max scanned the gathering until he spotted the boy at the edge of the crowd, sitting alone on a fallen log near a small grove of trees. No one seemed to have noticed his absence from the formal proceedings.

Harek sat perfectly still, staring at the spot where the boat had disappeared. His face was blank, emotionless—not the expression of a grieving child, but of someone who had simply... stopped.

As Max watched, a figure approached the isolated boy. An old man in ragged clothes, bent with age and carrying a walking stick. A beggar, by all appearances, though what he was doing at a noble funeral was unclear.

"Terrible thing, young master," the beggar said, settling himself on the ground near Harek's log. "Terrible thing indeed."

Harek didn't respond. He continued staring at the river.

"Lost my own mother when I was about your age," the beggar continued. "Hurt so bad I thought I might die from it."

Still no response from Harek.

The beggar studied the boy for a moment, then reached into his tattered pouch. "You know what helped me?"

This time Harek's eyes flicked toward him, just for a second.

"Finding ways to think about other things. Happy things. Things that made me smile when everything else made me sad." The beggar's voice was gentle. "Do you like toys, young master?"

Harek nodded slightly.

"I thought you might." The beggar smiled, revealing several missing teeth. "Tell you what—I've got a little game here. Might help take your mind off... things."

He pulled out three small wooden cups and a tiny carved ball. "Simple game. I put the ball under one cup, move them around, you tell me where it ended up. If you win, you get to keep this."

The beggar produced a small wooden horse from his pouch. It was crudely carved but painted in bright colors—red and blue and gold—the kind of toy that would catch any child's eye.

"What if I lose?" Harek asked, his voice barely above a whisper.

"Oh, you won't lose," the beggar said with a wink. "I can tell you're a smart lad. Sharp eyes like your mother's."

Max felt a chill that had nothing to do with the river water.

The beggar placed the ball under the middle cup and began moving them slowly—so slowly that a blind person could have followed the ball's location. His movements were deliberately clumsy, exaggerated, ensuring that Harek could easily track where the ball ended up.

"Where is it, young master?"

Harek pointed to the correct cup without hesitation.

"Wonderful!" the beggar exclaimed, lifting the cup to reveal the ball. "I knew you were clever. Here's your prize."

He handed Harek the wooden horse, and for the first time since his mother's death, the boy's face showed a flicker of something other than emptiness. Not quite happiness, but... engagement. Interest.

"That was easy," Harek said, turning the toy over in his small hands.

"You're a natural!" the beggar agreed. "Want to try again? I've got other toys..."

"Harek!"

Lord Tredor's voice cut through the air like a blade. Max turned to see the man striding toward them, his face dark with anger.

"What are you doing?" Tredor demanded, his grief temporarily replaced by protective fury. "Who are you?"

The beggar scrambled to his feet, cups and ball disappearing into his pouch. "Just offering comfort to the young lord, sir. No harm meant."

"Comfort?" Tredor's voice rose. "You're running games on my son at his mother's funeral?"

"Father, he gave me a horse," Harek said, holding up the toy.

Tredor looked at the carved animal, then at his child, and his expression shifted from anger to something much more dangerous.

"Get away from my son," he said quietly. "Get away from my family. If I see you near any of my children again, I'll have you flogged."

The beggar backed away, hands raised in surrender. "Meant no offense, my lord. Just trying to help a grieving boy..."

"Help?" Tredor stepped forward. "You were teaching him to gamble. At his mother's funeral."

The beggar's mask slipped for just a moment, revealing something cold and calculating beneath the grandfatherly facade. Then it was back in place, all innocence and wounded feelings.

"A simple game, nothing more—"

"Leave. Now."

The beggar left, melting back into the crowd.

Tredor knelt beside his son, his anger fading back into grief as he took in Harek's appearance.

"We need to get you cleaned up," he said gently, though his voice was thick with exhaustion. "And changed into clean clothes. You have moss all over you."

"I like the horse," Harek said, clutching the toy against his chest.

Tredor looked at the carved animal—bright paint over rough wood, clearly made by someone with more enthusiasm than skill. The kind of cheap trinket sold to children at markets.

"Keep it if you want," he said finally. "But no more games with strangers. Do you understand?"

"I understand, father."

But Max could see something in young Harek's eyes that Lord Tredor missed. The boy had won. He'd felt clever, successful, in control—everything he hadn't felt while watching his mother die.

For just a few minutes, the pain had stopped.

And now he knew that games could do that.

Max wanted to scream at the memory, to shake young Harek and explain that the beggar had let him win, that it was a trick designed to create exactly this feeling. But he was powerless to change what had already happened.

He could only watch as the seeds of Harek's eventual destruction were planted in the soil of his grief, disguised as kindness.

The world shifted again, and Max found himself standing in what was obviously Frosthold's great hall. Stone walls stretched high overhead, decorated with banners displaying the Frostfang bear sigil of House Vanheim. A massive fireplace dominated one wall, its flames casting dancing shadows across the assembled crowd.

This was clearly some sort of formal gathering. Lords and ladies in fine clothing clustered in conversation groups, while servants moved between them carrying trays of wine and delicate foods that probably cost more than most people made in a month.

Max spotted young Harek immediately. The boy was a bit taller than he'd been at his mother's funeral but still carrying that hollow look around his eyes. He sat alone at a table near the wall, working his way through what appeared to be his third cup of wine.

"Already drinking at this age," Max muttered. "This is going well."

Across the hall, two younger boys were engaged in some sort of elaborate game involving wooden knights and a miniature castle. Max recognized them immediately—Haldor and Finn, Harek's younger brothers. They were laughing, completely absorbed in their play.

Harek watched them with an expression that wasn't quite jealousy and wasn't quite disdain. More like... distance. As if he were looking at strangers through a window.

"Harek!" Haldor called out, spotting his older brother. "Come play with us! We're recreating the Battle of Silver Falls!"

Harek took another sip of wine and shook his head. "I'm busy."

"Doing what?" Finn asked, abandoning the game to approach his brother. "You're just sitting there."

"I'm thinking."

"About what?"

"Important things," Harek said, in the tone of someone who thought they sounded very mature and worldly. "You wouldn't understand."

Finn's face fell. The kid couldn't have been more than eight, and the dismissal hit him like a physical blow.

"We could understand if you explained it," Haldor tried, ever the peacemaker.

"No, you couldn't," Harek replied, not even looking at them. "You're children."

"You're only five years older than me," Haldor pointed out.

"Five years might as well be fifty," Harek said.

The two younger boys exchanged glances.

"Father said we should include you more," Finn said quietly. "He said you've been sad since—"

"Don't," Harek cut him off sharply. "Just... don't."

An uncomfortable silence fell between them. Haldor fidgeted with one of his wooden knights while Finn stared at his feet.

"We miss her too," Haldor said finally. "Mother, I mean."

"You barely remember her," Harek replied, his voice flat. "Finn doesn't remember her at all."

"That's not true—"

"It is true. You remember what people have told you about her. That's not the same thing."

Haldor's face flushed red. "That's a horrible thing to say."

"It's honest."

"It's mean."

Harek shrugged and took another drink. "The world's mean. Better to learn that early."

Finn had started crying, though he was trying to hide it. Eight-year-olds weren't very good at hiding anything.

"Come on, Finn," Haldor said, putting an arm around his younger brother. "Let's go back to our game."

They left, and Harek continued drinking alone.

"You're an idiot," Max said to the memory. "They're trying to help you."

But young Harek couldn't hear him. And even if he could, Max doubted it would have made a difference.

The scene dissolved, reforming into what looked like a training yard. Harek had grown fat. He was older now—maybe fifteen—and sporting a spectacular black eye. His clothes were muddy and torn, and his lip was split and bleeding.

Standing over him were three boys who looked to be around seventeen or eighteen.

All bigger than Harek.

"Had enough?" the largest one asked. He was built like a brick wall and had the kind of face that suggested thinking wasn't his strong suit.

Harek spat blood and struggled to his feet. "Hardly started."

The second boy laughed. "Look at him, Roderick. Still thinks he's tough."

"I don't think I'm tough," Harek replied, wiping blood from his mouth. "I know I'm tougher than you three idiots."

This was clearly not the response they'd been expecting. Roderick's face darkened.

"What did you call us?"

"Idiots," Harek repeated slowly, as if speaking to someone who had trouble understanding words. "You know—stupid people. The kind who need three of them to handle one of me."

Max winced. Harek might have been developing a talent for insults, but he clearly hadn't developed any talent for knowing when to shut up.

"You little shit," the third boy snarled, stepping forward.

"Careful, Marcus," Harek said with a crooked grin. "Don't use up all your big words at once."

Marcus threw a punch that caught Harek square in the stomach. The boy doubled over, gasping, but somehow managed to stay upright.

"You done?" Harek wheezed.

"Not even close," Roderick said, cracking his knuckles.

What followed was less a fight than a demonstration of what happened when one out-of-shape fifteen-year-old tried to take on three older, stronger opponents. Harek got in a few good hits—including one that left Marcus with a bloody nose—but the outcome was never really in doubt.

When it was over, Harek lay in the mud, breathing heavily through what was probably a broken nose. The three older boys stood over him, looking satisfied with their work.

"Next time, watch your mouth," Roderick advised. "There's only so much disrespect we'll take from Vanheim's little disappointment."

They left, and Harek slowly, painfully, pushed himself up to sitting position.

"Could've gone better," he said to no one in particular, then started laughing.

It wasn't a happy laugh. It was the kind of laugh that came from someone who'd given up expecting things to go well and found it all darkly amusing instead.

Max realized what he was watching. Harek was self-destructing on purpose. The insults, the refusal to back down, the almost deliberate provocation—this wasn't stupidity or poor impulse control. This was someone who wanted to get hurt.

"Goddamnit," Max muttered. "You're punishing yourself."

The scene shifted again. Harek was older again, stumbling through what looked like Frosthold's corridors in the middle of the night. He was drunk—not tipsy, not slightly impaired, but properly, thoroughly drunk. The kind of drunk where standing upright required conscious effort and walls became essential navigational aids.

He ricocheted off one wall, overcorrected, and bounced off the opposite one. His coordination was shot, but his determination was intact. He was heading somewhere specific.

Max followed as Harek made his unsteady way through the castle, past servants who pretended not to see him and guards who looked the other way. Apparently everyone had gotten used to the heir's nocturnal wanderings.

Harek's destination turned out to be Lord Tredor's study. The door was closed, but light leaked out from beneath it. Voices could be heard inside—his father in conversation with someone Max didn't recognize.

Harek didn't knock. He grabbed the door handle, turned it, and stumbled inside.

"Father!" he announced loudly. "We need to talk!"

Lord Tredor looked up from his desk, where he'd been reviewing documents with a man in noble robes. Tredor's face went through several expressions in quick succession—surprise, embarrassment, and finally resignation.

"Harek," he said calmly. "You're drunk."

"Very," Harek agreed, swaying slightly. "But I had an important... important... thought!"

"I'm sure you did." Tredor glanced at the man. "Lord Peyter, perhaps we could continue this tomorrow?"

The man gathered his papers quickly. "Of course, my lord. Good evening."

He left, giving Harek a wide berth. The boy had managed to find a chair and was attempting to sit in it, though this proved more challenging than expected.

"What's so urgent that it couldn't wait until morning?" Tredor asked, his voice carefully neutral.

"I figured it out," Harek said, finally succeeding in sitting down. "The answer to everything."

"And what's that?"

"Nothing matters," Harek announced with the satisfied air of someone who'd solved a complex puzzle. "Nothing at all. We're all going to die anyway, so why bother pretending any of it means anything?"

Tredor studied his son for a long moment. "That's a very bleak philosophy for someone your age."

"It's honest," Harek replied. "Like you always taught me to be. Honest."

"I taught you to be truthful. That's not the same as believing the world holds no meaning."

"What?"

"Thinking that nothing in life has purpose or worth."

Harek considered this, his alcohol-addled brain working slowly. "Yeah. That's what I said. Nothing matters."

"Your brothers matter," Tredor said quietly.

"Do they? They barely talk to me anymore."

"Because you push them away."

"Because they're better off without me," Harek corrected. "Everyone is."

The pain in Tredor's voice was carefully controlled but unmistakable. "That's not true."

"Isn't it?" Harek laughed bitterly. "When's the last time anyone was happy to see me? When's the last time I did anything that made you proud instead of disappointed?"

Tredor was quiet for a moment. "You saved that merchant's daughter from the river bandits last month."

"I was drunk then too," Harek pointed out. "Probably wouldn't have done it sober. Too smart to take those odds."

"But you did do it."

"Lucky accident."

"Harek—"

"Face it, Father," Harek interrupted, his words slurring slightly. "I'm not the son you wanted. I'm not the heir Frosthold needs. I'm just... here. Taking up space until I die or disappear."

The silence that followed was heavy.

"Go to bed," Tredor said finally. "Sleep this off."

"In a minute." Harek struggled to his feet, using the chair for support. "Just wanted you to know I figured it out. Why Mother died, why everything's gone to shit since then. It's because I wasn't worth saving."

"That's enough."

"She died protecting something worthless," Harek continued, as if he hadn't heard. "Must've been embarrassing for her, realizing that in those last moments—"

The slap came fast and hard, snapping Harek's head to the side and sending him stumbling backward into the wall. For a moment, the only sound in the room was heavy breathing.

Tredor's hand was still raised, his face mirroring only shock and regret. He'd probably never struck any of his children before.

"I..." he started, then stopped.

"There it is," Harek said quietly, touching his cheek where a red handprint was already forming. "The honesty you're always talking about."

"I am sorry. I shouldn't have—"

"Don't apologize," Harek said, pushing himself off the wall. "It felt good, didn't it? Finally acting on what you've been thinking for years."

"I don't think—"

"You don't think I'm worthless?" Harek laughed. "Then why does everyone else?"

He headed for the door, his steps slightly steadier now that adrenaline was mixing with the alcohol.

"Get out," Tredor said quietly.

Harek paused at the door. "What?"

"Get out of my sight. I don't want to see you again until you're sober and ready to apologize for what you just said about your mother."

"And if I'm never ready?"

"Then stay away."

Harek nodded slowly. "Understood."

He left, and Tredor sank into his chair, putting his head in his hands.

Max watched the scene dissolve, feeling sick. That had been brutal to witness.

The next memory showed Harek probably several months later, standing on the battlements of Frosthold in the middle of the night. He was drunk again, but this time there was something different about him. Something more purposeful.

He climbed up onto the stone parapet, testing his balance. It was a long drop to the courtyard below—easily far enough to kill someone.

"Let's see if this works," Harek muttered to himself.

Max felt a chill of understanding. Harek was about to test his power. His reroll ability. And he'd chosen the most dramatic method possible.

"You absolute idiot," Max said. "There are easier ways to—"

Harek jumped.

The fall took maybe three seconds. Max watched the boy's body hit the cobblestones with a wet crunch that echoed off the castle walls. There was no doubt about the result—Harek's neck was bent at an impossible angle, and blood was already pooling beneath his head.

For a moment, nothing happened. Then the world shimmered, and suddenly Harek was back on the battlements, alive and uninjured.

"Well," he said, looking down at the now-empty courtyard. "That's something."

The scene shifted rapidly through a series of increasingly reckless moments. Harek picking fights with armed men. Harek diving off cliffs into unknown water. Harek challenging obvious cheaters to games of chance, then refusing to pay when he lost.

Each death was followed by a return, and each return seemed to drain a little more humanity from him. The knowledge that death wasn't permanent was making him careless with life—both his own and others'.

Max watched in horror as Harek's gambling addiction escalated. What had started as simple card games became elaborate schemes. Betting on fights, on weather, on whether birds would fly left or right. The amounts grew larger, the stakes more ridiculous, the consequences more severe.

"Fifty gold says I can drink Yann under the table," Harek announced in one memory, gesturing to a man twice his size.

"You're half his weight," someone pointed out.

"Then it should be easy money for you," Harek replied with a grin that was all teeth and no warmth.

He lost that bet, along with dozens of others. But it didn't matter, because he could always come back and try a different approach. Death had become just another type of loss—annoying but temporary.

There were girls too—encounters that started with charm and ended with disappointment. Harek had learned to be entertaining, learned to make people laugh, but he'd never learned how to connect with them. Every relationship was just another gamble, another opportunity to lose something he'd convinced himself he didn't really want.

The scene settled on what looked like a formal garden party. Harek was probably around sixteen now, and the years of excess had started to show even more. His face was much puffier, his clothes expensive but carelessly worn. He stood near a fountain, nursing a cup of wine and surveying the assembled nobles with the expression of someone who found the whole thing beneath his notice.

Across the garden, Max spotted a young woman who could only be Princess Aelara. She was around his age, with long reddish hair and her father's sharp features softened by youth. She wore a simple but elegant dress in pale blue, and she was looking directly at Harek with something that might have been curiosity.

As Max watched, she approached Harek with the confident stride of someone used to being welcomed wherever she went.

"Harek," she said, offering a slight curtsy that was perfectly polite but not particularly deferential.

"Your Highness," Harek replied, his bow equally correct and equally indifferent.

"I was wondering if you'd like to dance," Aelara said. "The musicians are quite good."

Harek glanced at the musicians, then at the couples already dancing, then back at Aelara. His expression suggested he'd rather clean stables.

"I don't dance," he said flatly.

"Everyone dances," Aelara replied with a smile. "It's just a matter of whether they're good at it."

"I don't dance well, then."

"I could teach you."

"I don't want to learn."

Aelara's smile faltered slightly. She'd clearly expected a different response.

"It's just one dance," she said, trying again.

"And then you'd want another, and another, and eventually I'd be trapped in conversation about music and poetry and whatever else noble ladies find fascinating." Harek took a sip of wine. "I'd rather skip to the end and admit we have nothing to talk about."

The rejection was brutal in its casual indifference. Aelara's face went through several expressions—surprise, hurt, and finally anger.

"I see," she said coolly. "Well, thank you for your honesty."

"You're welcome."

She turned to leave, then paused. "You know, Lord Vanheim, people say you're rude and selfish and completely unsuitable for marriage. Knowing you since we were children, I thought they might be exaggerating."

"They're not," Harek confirmed. "If anything, they're being charitable."

"Clearly."

She left, her spine straight with offended dignity. Harek watched her go with no apparent regret, then finished his wine and helped himself to another cup.

Max felt embarrassed just watching it. Aelara had been trying to be kind, trying to include him in the social fabric of court life. And Harek had essentially told her to fuck off as rudely as possible while maintaining technical politeness.

"You're an asshole," Max muttered. "She was being nice to you."

But watching Harek's face, Max caught something the princess had missed. A flicker of something—regret? self-loathing?—that passed across his features when he thought no one was looking.

He'd wanted to dance with her. Max was sure of it. But Harek had convinced himself that he didn't deserve nice things, that every connection he made would inevitably turn to disappointment and pain. So he'd cut it off before it could begin.

The coward's way out, disguised as honesty.

The memories continued, growing darker and more sordid. Harek's adventures in brothels, where he paid for the illusion of affection while maintaining the emotional distance that kept him safe. His escalating drinking, which had progressed from social lubrication to necessary medication for getting through each day.

There were fights—not the structured duels of nobility, but desperate brawls in taverns and back alleys. Harek seeking out violence like a drug, needing the pain to feel anything at all.

Max watched him lose fight after fight, die in increasingly creative ways, and come back each time slightly more hollow than before. The reroll ability that should have been a gift had become a curse, enabling behaviors that would have killed anyone else.

One memory showed Harek at his current state, standing in his father's study again. But this time he was sober, and Lord Tredor looked older, more tired. The years of disappointment had carved lines around his eyes and silvered his hair.

"The expedition to retrieve the Dragon Heart from Dragonmeet leaves in a fortnight," Tredor said without preamble. "You'll be going with them."

"Will I?" Harek asked, settling into a chair uninvited. "That's news to me."

"Prince Keiran requested your presence specifically. As heir to Frosthold, your participation would demonstrate our house's commitment to the realm's security."

"How thrilling."

"This is not a request, Harek. This is a command."

Harek studied his father's face, noting the hard set of his jaw and the determined glint in his eyes. "And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll no longer be the heir."

A silence.

"You'd disown me?" Harek asked, his voice carefully neutral.

"Yes," Tredor replied. "For seven years, I've watched you waste every opportunity, squander every gift, and shame our family name at every turn. I've made excuses, offered second chances, and held out hope that you'd find your way back to the man I know you could be."

"And now?"

"Now I'm tired." Tredor said. "Tired of defending you to other lords. Tired of explaining your behavior to your brothers. Tired of watching you destroy yourself and take our family's reputation down with you."

There was a pause, as if he hesitated to say it, then...

"I am tired of you, Harek."

Harek was quiet for a moment. "So this is an ultimatum."

"This is a final chance," Tredor corrected. "Prove to me—prove to yourself—that you're capable of serving something larger than your own self-pity. Show me that my son still exists somewhere beneath the drunk and the gambler and the disappointment."

"And if I can't?"

"Then you're free to continue your decline as a landless commoner. Without our name, without our resources, and without my protection."

The silence stretched between them. Harek stared at his hands while Tredor waited for an answer.

"The Dragon Heart expedition," Harek said finally. "Dangerous work."

"Very."

"Men will die."

"Probably."

"Including me, possibly."

"That would depend on how seriously you take your responsibilities."

Harek looked up, meeting his father's eyes for the first time in the conversation. "Would you mourn me? If I died out there?"

Tredor's face softened slightly. "Every day for the rest of my life."

"Even though I'm a disappointment?"

"You... you are my son first," Tredor said quietly. "My disappointment second."

Harek nodded slowly. "I'll go."

"Good."

"But I want something in return."

"Harek," Tredor's expression hardened again. "You're hardly in a position to make demands."

"Not a demand. A request." Harek stood, moving to the window that looked out over Frosthold's courtyard. "If I die on this expedition, don't lie about it. Don't make me out to be something I wasn't. Tell people I died as I lived—carelessly and without much consideration for consequences."

"Son—"

"Promise me," Harek insisted, still looking out the window. "No heroic speeches, no noble sacrifices. Just the truth. 'Harek Vanheim died because he was an idiot who made poor choices.' That's the legacy I've earned."

"You're not dead yet," Tredor said. "There's still time to earn a better one."

"Is there?" Harek turned back to face his father. "Look at me, Father. Really look at me. Do you see someone who's going to turn his life around and become the heir Frosthold deserves?"

Tredor looked, and Max could see the exact moment when hope died in the older man's eyes. Whatever he saw in his son's face was enough to convince him that redemption was no longer possible.

"I see someone who's given up," Tredor said quietly.

"Exactly."

Harek moved toward the door.

"Farewell, Father."

Tredor didn’t move. "I will see you again."

Harek chuckled, bitter and dry, then walked out.

The memory dissolved, and Max found himself on the battlefields of Eastwatch. He recognized the scene immediately—the same chaos, the same desperate fighting, the same inevitable outcome he'd experienced firsthand.

But this time he was watching from the sidelines as Harek fought his final battle.

The young man moved like someone who'd given up on survival. He took unnecessary risks, made careless mistakes, and seemed almost relieved when the goblin's knife found its mark.

As Harek lay dying in the mud, his eyes stared sightlessly at the blood-streaked sky. There was no regret in his expression, no fear. Just quiet acceptance of an ending he'd been moving toward for years.

"NUMBER OF REROLLS REMAINING: 0," appeared briefly above his head, then faded as the light left his eyes.

Max stood in the sudden silence, processing what he'd witnessed. The memories had stopped, leaving him alone with the weight of Harek's entire miserable, wasted life.

"Well," he said to the empty air. "That was fucking depressing."

A voice answered from behind him. "I know, right?"

Max spun around to find himself face to face with Harek Vanheim. Not the child from the memories, not the broken young man from the later scenes, but something in between. Maybe seventeen or eighteen, old enough to have experienced life's disappointments but not yet completely hollowed out by them.

Harek looked exactly as Max remembered from inhabiting his body, but cleaner somehow. Less puffy around the face, less slouched in posture. This was Harek as he might have been if he'd made better choices.

"Hello, Max," Harek said with a rueful smile. "Thanks for watching the highlight reel. Really brings back the memories." He paused. "Fair warning—I've been picking up your vocabulary while you've been in my head. Don't be surprised if I start saying things are 'cool' or 'fucked up.'"

"You're him," Max said. "The real Harek."

"Real as anything gets in here," Harek confirmed, gesturing vaguely at the mental landscape around them. "Sorry about the dramatic presentation. Didn't have much choice in how my life story got told."

"You've been watching me," Max realized. "This whole time, you've been in here while I was using your body."

"Had a front row seat to the whole thing," Harek agreed. "Have to say, you've done more with it in a few days than I managed in seven years."

Max stared at him, trying to process the implications. "You're not angry? That I... took over?"

Harek laughed, and it was the first genuinely happy sound Max had heard from him in all the memories. "Angry? Max, you've been everything I should have been. Brave, decisive, actually useful in a fight. You'd make my father proud, impress Gregory, and even start to repair my relationship with my brothers."

"I've also gotten you—us—killed multiple times."

"But you kept getting back up," Harek pointed out. "That's the difference between us. When I failed, I gave up. When you fail, you try again."

The two of them stood in uncomfortable silence for a moment.

"So what happens now?" Max asked finally.

"Now we have a conversation about the future," Harek said. "Specifically, whether you're going to keep my body or if I'm going to reclaim it."

"And?"

Harek grinned. "Well, that depends on what you're planning to do with it."

Max stared at Harek, trying to process what he'd just heard. "What do you mean, 'what I'm planning to do with it'?"

"Your body. My body. Whatever we're calling it now." Harek shrugged. "I mean, I was dead, Max. Like, properly dead. Zero rerolls, lights out, game over. Even if I wanted to come back—which, honestly, I couldn't—the system doesn't exactly have a resurrection clause for people who've used up all their lives."

"So when I took over..."

"I knew I was never getting back in the driver's seat," Harek confirmed. "The moment you showed up, that was it for me. One-way ticket to the afterlife, do not pass go, do not collect two hundred gold."

Max felt a weird mix of relief and guilt. "Then why are we having this conversation?"

"Because just because I can't reclaim my body doesn't mean I'm going to hand it over without conditions." Harek's expression grew serious. "This isn't some charity case, Max. You want to keep living my life? There's a price."

The world around them suddenly dissolved. Max found himself back on the pristine lake under the perfect sky, standing face to face with Harek on the impossibly clear surface.

Except now the horizon was much darker. The storm clouds had advanced significantly, crackling with fire and lightning the color of old blood split the sky, and Max could swear he heard something roaring in the distance.

"Oh shit," Max said, instinctively stepping backward. "The dragon's getting closer."

"Hmm?" Harek glanced over his shoulder at the approaching storm. "Oh, that thing."

He snapped his fingers.

The entire storm front froze mid-advance, lightning suspended in the air like a photograph. Then, with the sound of a giant wave receding, the darkness was pushed back to its original position on the horizon.

"There," Harek said, turning back to Max as if he'd just adjusted a thermostat. "Better?"

Max stared at him. "What the hell was that?"

"Basic reality management," Harek replied matter-of-factly. "This is my inner world, Max. I'm the landlord here. That dragon can throw all the tantrums it wants, but it's still just a guest."

"You make it sound easy."

"It is easy. For me." Harek gestured vaguely at the restored blue sky. "I've been hanging out in here for days while you've been running around in my body. Had plenty of time to listen to that thing's whole spiel."

"What did it say?"

"Mostly boring conquest nonsense. 'I am the eternal flame,' 'bow before my ancient power,' 'I will devour your essence and remake this vessel in my image.' Real standard dragon rhetoric." Harek rolled his eyes. "Honestly, for something that's supposedly thousands of years old, it's remarkably unimaginative."

Max found himself grinning despite the circumstances. "You're mocking a dragon's conquest speech."

"Someone has to. The thing's been going on about its magnificent presence for three straight hours. I was ready to let it win just to make it shut up." Harek paused. "But then you showed up, and I got curious. Why does Max Stalinsky want to keep my body badly enough to risk everything?"

"Seriously?" Max stared at him. "You watched everything I've been doing and you have to ask?"

"Humor me."

Max gestured broadly at the inner world around them. "Because I've spent the last week living more than I did in twenty-six years on Earth. Because your world is real and mine was just... waiting. Waiting for something interesting to happen, waiting for someone to notice me, waiting to feel like I mattered."

"And now you matter?"

"Now I'm trying to matter. There's a difference."

Harek nodded slowly. "Fair point. Though you picked a hell of a life to matter in. Most people would've run screaming after the first goblin tried to disembowel them."

"Most people don't get do-overs when they screw up."

"True." Harek paused. "You know, we're remarkably similar, personality-wise. It's actually kind of disturbing."

Max had been thinking the same thing. The dry humor, the self-deprecating comments, the tendency to crack jokes in terrible situations—it was like talking to a reflection.

"Yeah, I noticed that too. Apparently we both deal with existential dread through sarcasm."

"Great minds think alike. Or in our case, mediocre minds think alike."

"Speak for yourself. I got your body killed significantly fewer times than you did."

"That's because you cheat," Harek pointed out. "You had rerolls to work with. I was playing on hardcore mode."

They stood in comfortable silence for a moment, the absurdity of the situation settling around them.

"So," Max said finally. "What do you want?"

Harek's expression grew serious again. "I want you to do your best."

"That's it? That's your condition?"

"No, that's the bare minimum. Let me be more specific." Harek began counting on his fingers. "I want you to put a smile on my father's face. I want you to fix things with my brothers—Haldor and Finn deserve better than the distant asshole I became. I want you to make amends to everyone I wronged, which is going to be a long list. I want you to be worthy of what the Vanheim name is supposed to represent."

Max felt the weight of those expectations settling on his shoulders. "Anything else?"

"Don't let my mother's death be meaningless," Harek said quietly. "She died because she had children—because she loved us enough to protect us. All three of us. I spent years making that sacrifice pointless, turning myself into something she would've been ashamed of. Don't do that."

The simple honesty in Harek's voice hit Max harder than any dramatic speech could have. This wasn't about grand gestures or heroic destinies. This was about a son who'd given up and a mother who'd died believing in him.

"Harek," Max started, then stopped. "I don't know if I can—"

"I know this isn't your problem," Harek interrupted. "I know you didn't sign up to fix my family's dysfunction or carry the weight of my mistakes. You just wanted to live in a fantasy world where magic is real and adventures happen. I get that."

"But?"

"But I'm asking anyway." Harek's voice was steady, matter-of-fact. "Because I don't have anyone else to ask. And because, despite everything, I still care about them. Maybe that makes me selfish, asking you to clean up my mess. But that's the deal."

Max stared at the young man in front of him, seeing not the broken drunk from the memories but someone who'd found a purpose in his final moments.

"You're right," Max said finally. "It is selfish."

"I know. I just said it."

"It's also unfair to dump a lifetime of responsibilities on someone who just wanted to escape his own problems."

"I know that too."

"And despite all of that," Max continued, "I'll do it. Not because I have to, but because... hell, because they deserve better than what you gave them."

Harek's face broke into a broad, warm smile. "Thank you."

They stood facing each other on the crystal-clear water, and Max extended his hand for a handshake.

Then Harek held up a finger. "Oh, one more thing."

Max's hand froze mid-extension. "What now?"

"Find the people who killed my mother and make them pay for it." Harek's tone was casual, as if he were asking Max to pick up groceries. "The Úlûvain—the elven purists who considered her marriage to my father a betrayal of their bloodline. They're still out there."

Max stared at him. "You're a real asshole, you know that?"

"Hey, you can't shame the shameless," Harek replied with a grin. "We got a deal?"

Max looked at the extended hand, then at Harek's expectant face, then at the peaceful inner world around them. In the distance, the dragon's storm clouds waited still.

"You're going to saddle me with a revenge quest on top of everything else?"

"Think of it as a bonus objective."

"A bonus objective that could get me killed."

"Everything could get you killed. That's what makes it interesting."

Max shook his head, but he was smiling despite himself. "Fine. We have a deal."

He grasped Harek's hand, and the moment their skin made contact, the world around them began to dissolve.

The world rippled like disturbed water, colors bleeding and shifting until the pristine lake dissolved completely. In its place, Max found himself standing in a small, cluttered apartment.

His apartment.

The same worn couch where he'd spent countless hours reading Bjorn's adventures. The same scratched coffee table where his laptop had lived. The same kitchen where he'd heated up mediocre takeout while wondering if this was all there was to life.

"Huh," Max said, looking around. "That's unexpected."

"Well," Harek said, his voice already sounding more distant, "you're the new owner now. Guess it reflects your inner world instead of mine."

Max turned to look at him and immediately felt his stomach drop. Harek was dissolving. Not dramatically, not with pain or fanfare, but quietly. Motes of light were drifting away from his form like dandelion seeds on a gentle breeze.

"Kinda sad, isn't it?" Harek observed, holding up his hand and watching pieces of himself float away. "All that effort to redecorate and I don't even get to enjoy the new place."

"So this is it, huh?"

"Yep." More light drifted from Harek's shoulders. "I'm counting on you to keep that promise, by the way. Don't make me haunt you."

"And when I do fulfill it? What then?"

"Who the fuck cares?" Harek laughed, the sound growing fainter. "It's your body. Do whatever you want with it. Get married, have kids, become a legendary knight, open a bakery. I'll be elsewhere."

Max watched more pieces of his companion fade away. "Where do you think you're going?"

"No idea." Harek's legs were becoming translucent. "I saw your memories while you were using my body. All those religions you guys had on Earth. Seemed like everyone had different ideas about what comes next."

"That's because none of us actually knew."

"Right, so this is an adventure all by itself, isn't it?" Only Harek's torso remained solid now. "Maybe I'll go inhabit some other poor bastard's body in another world. Really make the rounds."

"That would be cool."

"I'd make sure to make it matter," Harek said. His arms were fading now, dissolving into sparkles of warm light. "Hey, if I do end up in this world again, I'll come find you. I feel like we could be good friends."

"I'd like that," Max said. "Yeah."

"Be careful with the Shards," Harek's voice was barely a whisper now. Only his head remained, floating in the growing darkness of Max's apartment. "And don't trust anyone who offers you free gambling lessons."

"Noted."

"Good luck, Max. I know you'll do better than I did."

The last motes of light drifted away, leaving Max alone in his old apartment. In the distance—somewhere beyond his kitchen window, which definitely hadn't looked out onto a storm-lashed horizon before—the dragon's influence still waited.

Max stood in the quiet space, feeling the weight of promises made and responsibilities accepted. The apartment felt different now. Not like a prison of mediocrity, but like a starting point. A place he'd outgrown.

Time to get to work.

Max took a deep breath.

"OI!" he shouted toward the storm clouds beyond his kitchen window. "YOU OVERGROWN LIZARD! PAY THE RENT OR GET THE FUCK OUT OF MY BODY!"

The response was immediate and dramatic. The storm clouds began swirling, condensing, taking shape. What emerged was a dragon that redefined the concept of "massive"—easily the size of a city block, with scales that gleamed like molten gold and eyes like burning suns. Its wings could have covered a football stadium, and when it opened its mouth, Max could see rows of teeth the size of subway cars.

Max smiled.

"I have always wanted to try this," he said, raising his hands and spreading his feet in what he hoped looked like a proper fighting stance. "Ka... me..."

Energy began gathering between his palms—actual, visible energy that crackled with blue-white light and made the air around his hands shimmer like heat waves.

" ...ha me..."

The dragon's eyes widened in what might have been genuine alarm.

Max felt a wild grin spread across his face. This was actually working. He could feel the energy wanting to explode outward, could sense its potential to completely obliterate whatever he pointed it at. For the first time since arriving in this world, he felt genuinely, overwhelmingly powerful.

"MERCY!" the dragon bellowed. "I invoke the sacred right of parley! Stay your hand, sovereign of this realm!"

The energy dispersed immediately as Max's concentration broke. He blinked at the massive creature, slightly disappointed.

"Seriously? I didn't even get to the good part."

"Forgive the interruption," the dragon continued, lowering its massive head in what appeared to be deference. "But I recognize the ascendancy of a new master and would seek terms of peaceful departure rather than face annihilation."

Max raised an eyebrow. "Did you know what I was about to do?"

"I confess ignorance of your technique," the dragon replied, its tone respectful but wary. "Yet the gathering of such primal forces within your inner sanctum suggests capabilities that I would prefer not to test firsthand."

"Smart guy." Max lowered his hands completely. "So what's the deal? You just... leave?"

"Indeed, noble master. I shall depart your vessel and return to the ethereal currents as a free spirit. The power you have already claimed through rightful consumption—it remains yours whether I will it or not."

"And you won't try to possess anyone else?"

"I have learned the wisdom of choosing one's dwelling with greater care," the dragon said solemnly. "Perhaps a host less versed in the destructive arts would prove... safer."

"Alright then." Max nodded. "We have an understanding. Try not to let the door hit you on the way out."

The dragon inclined its massive head in what might have been a bow. "Farewell, Max Stalinsky of the Distant Realm. May your reign prove more prosperous than my brief tenancy."

"I'll do my best not to blow anything up accidentally."

The dragon dissolved back into storm clouds, which dispersed rapidly until nothing remained but clear sky and the peaceful interior of Max's old apartment.

Max felt reality beginning to tug at the edges of his consciousness, like someone gently shaking him awake.

"Well," he said to his empty living room, "that was easier than expected."

The world faded to black.

Max's eyes snapped open to find himself staring at a circle of very concerned pilgrim faces. The first thing he noticed was that breathing no longer felt like drowning in molten lead. The second thing he noticed was that he was absolutely soaked in sweat. The third thing he noticed was that his limbs felt like they'd been filled with wet sand.

He tried to sit up and immediately gave up on that particular ambition.

"How..." Max croaked, his voice barely a whisper. Speaking felt like dragging words through broken glass. "How long was I out?"

The elder leaned closer, studying Max's face. "Who am I speaking with?"

Max considered this question. The honest answer would probably cause more problems than it solved.

"The guy who just had a very interesting conversation with a dragon and a dead person," he managed. "Still me. Just... with new management."

The pilgrims exchanged glances, then broke into relieved smiles. Two of the younger ones actually hugged each other, which was probably the most emotion Max had seen from any of them.

"All day," the elder said, settling back on his heels. "You've been under for all day. This is dawn."

Before Max could ask "Dawn of the seventh day?" he heard the unmistakable sound of military boots approaching at pace.

"Lord Vanheim!" called a voice that sounded both official and slightly out of breath. "Sir Gregory requests your immediate presence!"

Max tried to turn his head toward the voice and discovered that his neck muscles had apparently gone on strike. "Could you perhaps help me get there? I seem to be experiencing some technical difficulties with basic movement."

Two soldiers appeared in his field of vision, looking down at him with expressions that suggested they weren't entirely sure what they were supposed to do with a lord who couldn't stand up.

"Just... grab me under the arms and drag me if you have to," Max said. "I'm not exactly in a position to complain about dignity at this point."

The walk to wherever Gregory was waiting proved to be less a walk and more a barely controlled stumble punctuated by frequent pauses for Max to remember how breathing worked. The camp was fully awake now, the rising sun casting everything in that particular golden light that made even military encampments look almost pleasant.

Max kept his head down and tried to filter out the voices around him. He could sense it now—the power the dragon had left behind, sitting in his chest like a banked fire. Whether he could actually manifest Fanga on command was another question entirely, but theoretically, yes. The capability was definitely there.

"Harek!"

Max looked up to see Gerth pushing through the small crowd that had gathered to watch the proceedings.

"You're alive," Gerth said, reaching them and immediately checking Max's pulse at his wrist.

"Yeah," Max replied, and found himself smiling despite everything. Harek's memories had given him a much clearer picture of who Gerth really was—probably the only person in Harek's life who'd never fully given up on him. "Thank you for that. For never giving up, I mean."

Gerth blinked in surprise. "Are you still delirious? Do yo—"

"Sir Gregory is waiting," one of the soldiers interrupted, clearly uncomfortable with the delay.

They resumed their awkward procession, Max's feet occasionally remembering how to support weight. The gathering that came into view was substantial—easily hundreds of people arranged in a loose semicircle around a clear space where Gregory stood waiting.

The sentinels were there, Torsten giving Max a slight nod. Tomas stood with a cluster of soldiers, anxious. Prince Keiran waited with the other nobles, his expression neutral but attentive. Captain Rhen watched with his usual detachment.

And at the center of it all, Sir Gregory stood with his hands clasped behind his back, the morning sun catching the worn edges of his armor.

The murmurs of the crowd died away as Max was half-carried, half-dragged into the circle. Gregory studied him for a moment, taking in his obvious physical state.

"Lord Harek Vanheim," Gregory said, his voice carrying clearly across the assembled crowd. "Seven days ago, I set you a challenge. Manifest Fanga by dawn of the seventh day, or be found unworthy of squireship."

Max nodded, not trusting his voice.

"I told these witnesses," Gregory continued, gesturing to the crowd, "that should you succeed, I would take you as my squire. Should you fail, I would not. The sun has risen. The seventh day has begun."

Gregory's eyes fixed on Max with the intensity of a man who'd spent his life measuring the worth of others.

"Prove yourself worthy, Lord Vanheim. Or accept your failure with whatever grace you can muster."

Max looked at the two soldiers supporting him, then at Gregory, then at the expectant crowd. The intelligent thing to do would be to admit defeat gracefully and avoid the public humiliation that was about to unfold.

Naturally, he did the opposite.

"I can stand," Max said, pulling away from the soldiers' supporting hands.

He immediately crashed to the ground like a felled tree.

The crowd's reaction was a symphony of mixed emotions. Some shook their heads in resignation—this was exactly what they'd expected. Others murmured appreciatively about his perseverance, which was more than most had given him credit for. A few looked moved by the sight of someone refusing to quit despite obvious physical limitations.

"Do you admit failure?" Gregory asked, his voice carrying no judgment, just professional assessment.

Max braced his hands against the ground and tried to push himself up. His arms trembled like overloaded bridge cables. He made it halfway to vertical before his legs betrayed him completely and he collapsed again.

Several people in the crowd looked away. This was becoming difficult to watch.

Max lay panting in the dirt, his entire body shaking from the effort. Every muscle felt like it had been replaced with jelly. But giving up now would mean disappointing not just himself, but Harek's memory and all the promises he'd made.

"Do you—" Gregory began.

"No," Max interrupted, still gasping for breath. "No, I am not giving up. Just give me a moment, please?"

Gregory nodded silently.

Max used the pause to catch what little breath he could manage. In the distance, he could hear Flash making excited noises, the warhorse apparently sensing something the humans couldn't. The sun continued its relentless climb over the eastern horizon.

On his third attempt, Max screamed with effort as he forced himself upright. His legs shook violently, but they held. He stood swaying like a drunk sailor, but he stood.

"Supposing one does have Fanga," Max said between labored breaths, "how do you make it manifest?"

Gregory studied him for a moment. "You feel for the core of energy in your chest. Like a flame that burns without consuming. You coax it outward, let it flow through your limbs, your entire being. It should feel as natural as breathing once you find it."

Max closed his eyes, blocking out the weight of hundreds of gazes fixed on him. The crowd, the nobles, the soldiers, Gregory himself—all waiting to see if the notorious Harek could manage this feat.

Alright, he thought, dragon power, don't fail me now.

He reached inward, searching for what Gregory had described. There—a warmth in his chest that definitely hadn't been there a week ago. It felt like holding a small sun inside his ribcage.

Come on, Max urged silently. You're supposed to be ancient magical power. Start acting like it.

The warmth responded, spreading outward from his chest through his arms and down to his legs. It felt like fire flowing through his veins, but pleasant fire, the kind that made you feel invincible rather than incinerated.

Gasps erupted from the crowd.

Max opened his eyes to find himself surrounded by a translucent whitish energy that flickered and danced around his body like aurora borealis made personal. His heart thundered in his ears, each beat seeming to pump more power through his limbs.

Gregory's eyes had gone wide with surprise. The crowd stood frozen, mouths agape. Even the prince made that satisfying face.

"...So?" Max asked.

"You pass," Gregory said, his face was already back in its normal expression. Which was mostly neutral. That emotionless bastard.

"Oh yeah," Max said, grinning like an idiot.

Then consciousness abandoned him entirely, and he collapsed into darkness.
