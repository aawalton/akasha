Instinctively, I darted between the cars lining the road beside me, ducking out of sight. Fear surged through me as my heart began to race and my thoughts swirled. I was terrified that it was too late, that whoever was in the building ahead had already spotted me, and it was stupid. I should have been more careful, but I’d just strolled around the corner without even checking whether there was anyone there, and I knew better than to do that. I’d been in the world for long enough to know how dangerous it was, so I should have thought. I should have been more cautious.

The sudden panic had made my breathing rapid and shallow. I struggled to catch my breath as I crouched there, wedged between two cars and praying that no one had seen me. One of the cars was black, I told myself. The paint was cracked and flaky, but hopefully, that meant my leather jacket would help me blend in with it. As long as someone just glanced down without looking too closely, they might not see me.

I needed to check. I knew that. I had to lift my head and look around, but I was too scared to move. I was worried that, if I did, it would catch someone’s eye. That the movement would be enough for them to spot me, and that anxiety kept my head bowed as I strained my ears to pick up any hint of footsteps or someone approaching.

But I heard nothing. The only sound was the faint whistling of the wind through the ruined buildings around me, and after a couple of seconds, the fear of attracting someone’s attention was overpowered by my fear of the unknown. Anxiety forced me to look up. I slowly lifted my head, trying not to make sudden movements, and scanned the street around me.

Thankfully, I saw no one sneaking up on me, and I examined the street again before my eyes returned to the seemingly occupied building I’d noticed before. I was still a distance away from it. It was at the end of the street, but a thin yet constant stream of smoke floating upwards.

There had to be people in there. I couldn’t see them, though. The front of the building had collapsed, becoming nothing more than a pile of rubble, and it shielded whoever was inside from view. I couldn’t tell how many people were in there, whether there was just one lone survivor or an entire group.

My gaze lingered on it for a moment longer before moving to the glass-fronted towers on either side. They were both clearly abandoned, and most of the glass had been shattered. The little that remained was covered in some kind of moss that spread across the surface like bacteria in a petri dish. The thought turned my stomach, and I had to look away, checking the rest of the buildings around me for signs of patrols or people on watch, but I saw none.

It seemed like it would be impossible to access most of the buildings around me, I decided. I wouldn’t feel safe doing it, anyway. They were much more run-down than the ones on the main street I’d turned off, which was strangely impressive. I thought they had looked bad when I first saw them, and although most of the windows had been smashed, at least the actual buildings themselves seemed fine. The structures were still standing, but that was not the case on the side street I had found myself on.

A few of the buildings had been entirely destroyed. There was one slightly further along from where I was huddled that was nothing more than a mountain of stone and concrete, and vines were creeping across almost every office building or block of flats or whatever they were. They snaked around the exposed pillars, wrapped tightly and burrowing into the stone, and I assumed that wasn’t helping. It was probably making them even more unstable and likely to collapse.

But surely it couldn’t all have happened after the bomb dropped and everything that followed, I thought as I glanced at the occupied building before letting my gaze return to the pile of rubble. It seemed strange that it was so entirely destroyed whilst the ones on either side were still standing, so maybe it had happened before. Perhaps it had been demolished intentionally or in the fights and unease that tore across the globe.

I swallowed down the emotions that rose within me, torn between longing to know more and not wanting to think about some of the darker stories that I heard whispered around that campfire. They always sent a chill down my spine. I knew people could be bad, that they could be evil. I’d been warned of it since I was young and witnessed far more than I should have, but I didn’t think they could be so cruel. Maybe it was my naivety, but I didn’t think people would knowingly target and manipulate the young and desperate quite so openly.

Shaking my head in an attempt to push the memories aside, I scanned the street once more. Satisfied that I couldn’t see anyone looking out from the ruined buildings, I edged back onto the pavement and began to sneak along the street. I remained crouched and stayed as close to the car as I could whilst avoiding the glass and weeds that littered the ground.

It didn’t take long before my injured leg started burning from the awkward squat I was doing, and the fear of catching some of my exposed skin on the rusty metal from the car pulled at me. I was pretty sure I’d had a tetanus shot at some point in my reality. I couldn’t remember when exactly, but I’d probably had one. However, it seemed unlikely that I would have in that world. Vaccines were probably impossible to get ahold of, and that concerned me.

How bad was tetanus? If it was something that people needed to be protected against, it had to be pretty awful and life-threatening, I reasoned, eyeing the orange spots that were visible through the cracks of paint on the car. I didn’t know any of the symptoms or signs, but I assumed it would probably cause an agonising, slow death, and I couldn’t stop myself from moving slightly further away from the rusty metal.

Despite my anxiety, I couldn’t keep my eyes away from the building at the end of the street for long, and regret pulled at me. I didn’t know what to expect from the people inside, but I wished I had better things to trade with them. I couldn’t remember everything that was in my backpack, but I knew I had at least a little bit of meat from the fox I’d killed that I’d managed to cook before sneaking into the city, a few weapons, and some supplies. They might be interested in them, but it wasn’t enough.

Hesitation lapped at me, and I chewed my lip as I tried to work out whether to turn and hurry towards the outskirts of the city or not. If I did, I should have been able to take down a few animals fairly quickly. There were plenty of foxes, rabbits, and deer, and I’d probably be able to get cleaner kills now that I had the fishing wire. I could set some traps and steer the animals into them. Then I’d actually have something of value to trade.

But the people might be gone before I got back. That was a concern, but I could be quick, I decided. More and more wilderness was starting to encroach on the city, so I would probably find some animals within a couple of miles, and I hoped it wouldn’t take long for me to get a couple. I could probably do it before night fell properly, and then I should have still had enough time to get away from the traders again and find somewhere relatively safe to sleep until sunrise.

Nodding to myself, I started to turn away before a smell reached my nostrils, and I froze. My knees became weak, and I had to lean on the car for balance. The paint and rust crunched as my shoulder hit it before raining to the ground with a soft, high-pitched tinkling sound, but I barely noticed. I was too distracted.

They were cooking something. That was the smoke that I could see. They hadn’t lit a fire just for warmth or to dry clothes or something. It was for their food, and it smelled delicious. I had no idea what it was, but I was pretty sure it was something I’d not eaten for a very long time, and I wanted to eat it again.

I needed to. The desire was so strong that I took a few steps forward without even noticing. I only realised when the sound of glass shattering beneath my feet caught my attention, and I glanced down before quickly moving off the broken pane I’d walked on. Luckily, the glass had not pierced my shoe, and I forced myself to stand still, swallowing down the saliva that had gathered in my mouth, as I stared hungrily at the building once more.

The thought of hunting was pushed from my mind. I couldn’t risk the people leaving and taking whatever it was that they were cooking with them before I got back. I needed to go to them immediately and see if they’d be willing to trade. I could offer them the last of my food, half the spool of fishing wire, my spare knife. Whatever they wanted in exchange for even just a bite of that deliciously rich, meaty smell.

Conflicting emotions crashed into me. I felt my eyebrows draw together as I inhaled deeply again, and my stomach growled loudly, making its desire known. There was no doubt in my mind that they were cooking meat, and I knew that I couldn’t be a vegetarian in every world, like I was in my reality.

Even though the real version of me had made the decision, it seemed unlikely that every version had, and in a world like the one I was in, it didn’t make sense not to eat meat. Survival was hard enough already, and there was no point in making it even harder, but it wasn’t just the knowledge that I regularly consumed it that made me uncomfortable. It was how good and tempting the scent was.

I didn’t normally feel that way about meat. I hadn’t for years. It usually just smelt fine to me, not particularly enticing or appetising, but in that moment, I felt like I might even trade away my jacket for some, despite how much it meant to me.

No. I probably wouldn’t go that far, but it was tempting.

Checking the street for people once more, I started to move closer again. My steps were more cautious that time. I actually looked where I was walking, and I was glad. If I’d been completely enticed by the smell like I had before, I probably would have tripped over the thick tree root that had caused the pavement to split open.

But I only made it a little further before something stopped me in my tracks again, and my heart catapulted into my throat. Laughter. I could hear laughter coming from the building ahead, but I wasn’t even that close to it. The sound was distant, and it didn’t instinctively strike fear in my heart, which felt like a nice change. The person didn’t sound as though they were mocking me or laughing triumphantly because they’d caught me. They just seemed… happy.

That realisation made my chest hurt, and the sounds that followed only made the ache worse. There were others there, a few at least, and they were all laughing. I had no idea what had caused it, but it seemed like they were having a good time.

I couldn’t remember the last time I’d laughed, a quiet voice at the back of my head pointed out. There wasn’t much cause for it in that world, but it still made me sad. I knew that I used to do it often back when I lived at camp, but it was easier then. The others were with me, and they filled my heart with love, joy, and happiness.

And I longed to have that again. I wished I could feel so safe and happy and carefree, that I could laugh just as loudly as the people in the building were laughing, and I felt myself take a step closer before a shout stopped me.

Dropping lower, I pressed myself against the car again before realising that the shout had also come from the building. Fear burst in my heart, and the urge to run towards it, to protect whoever was inside, filled me, but there was no need. More laughter exploded from the building, almost drowning out another shout.

The voice didn’t sound scared, I realised as confusion grew within me. It didn’t seem as though they’d raised their voice because they’d been attacked or were trying to scare someone or an animal off, and their tone wasn’t angry. It seemed almost… playful?

Did they not know how dangerous that was, I couldn’t help but think as I huddled even closer to the car, scanning the street again. Surely, they must be aware. Everyone in that world had to know how risky it was to do anything to alert people to their presence or draw attention to themselves. Even if they had no one to teach them, they must have learnt from experience that doing so only leads to being attacked or ambushed. If they hadn’t, there was no way they could have survived that long.

But no one shushed them. Their voices remained loud, clearly floating down the street towards me, and I knew I was not alone in the city. Even though I hadn’t seen anyone else yet, there were always people in cities, so there had to be others. They must have heard the people too, and I didn’t know what to do about that.

Part of me was tempted to rush towards them and warn them of how loud they were being. Perhaps they were unaware for some reason, but it seemed unlikely that anyone could be so stupid. If they were, they must have survived so long out of sheer luck, and I was not lucky. Bad luck followed me around, and I was scared of inflicting that upon them.

But there was another reason that kept me frozen and unable to help the people. It was the fear that they did not care, and that filled me with terror. If they had no concerns about being overheard, they must have a reason for that. It was probably because they knew they would win in a fight if someone were to attack them, so they were either well-armed or there were loads of them. Potentially both. Then, if anyone tried to attack them, it wouldn’t be a bad thing. They’d be able to kill the person, and they’d just have more supplies.

I stepped off the pavement, slipping between two cars again as I took a deep breath and tried to slow my racing thoughts. If that was the case, and it did seem likely, approaching them was an even bigger risk than normal. As much as I yearned to have some of the food, I doubted they’d want to trade with me. They probably had more than enough supplies already, so what could I offer them?

Once they realised that, I doubted they’d just let me go, I realised. There was nothing stopping them from just murdering me and stealing everything I had, and perhaps that was the real reason they were being so loud. They could have been trying to lure people close so they could pick them off more easily.

The urge to run away seized me, but another idea came to me, keeping me from fleeing. Perhaps there was something I could tempt them with, I realised. If there were truly as many of them as it sounded like, they might have enough people to take down whoever was looking for me. I could coax them in, tell them about the reward and how if the people were willing to offer so much, they must have even more in their compound. Then maybe they’d be willing to help me. I’d let them keep everything, all of the food and supplies. Knowing that I was no longer being hunted would have been enough for me.

But that was dangerous too. It was possible that they were not greedy enough, that they were too sensible to risk it. They could have easily decided to capture me and hand me over to the group that wanted me in exchange for the reward.

Or maybe they were part of the group. I didn’t know anything about them, not really. I’d never seen the people who plastered posters with my face on around the country, and I had seen one in the city. It was before I’d entered the world, but I could remember seeing it on the side of one of the crumbling buildings, so I knew they must have reached the city.

My stomach grumbled, mourning the delicious food that I would never get to taste, as I turned away and hurried back to the main road. Even once I reached it, I couldn’t stop myself from glancing over my shoulder almost constantly. I was so scared that they’d spotted me and were following me, and I could feel eyes tracking my movements. They burrowed into my skin, making me feel even more on edge than before, but I couldn’t see anyone. No matter where I looked, I didn’t even catch a glimpse of life.

It was just my paranoia, I told myself. That was all it was. I was just being paranoid, and no one was watching me, but still, my steps grew faster. The throbbing ache in my shoulder and shin grew more noticeable. Every time my foot hit the unsteady, glass-littered pavement, pain burned down my leg and radiated through my torso, but I refused to let myself slow until I reached a junction at the end of the road.

Peering around the corner, I scanned both directions before darting across the road. I needed to move away from the building with those people in it. Even if they weren’t the ones who were looking for me, it still made sense to get away from them. I needed to get away from everyone.

A piece of paper fluttering in the wind ahead of me caught my attention as I rushed along the street, and I felt my heart lurch. My chest felt tight as I drew closer and closer before bending down to snatch it up. It danced to the side as my hand neared, seemingly trying to evade me, but I grabbed it and straightened up.

Anxiety simmered within me as I turned the paper over, and my breath caught in my chest. It was strange to see the drawing staring back at me. It looked both so similar and so different to how I remembered myself looking, but I had no doubt that it was me. Somehow, I just knew it.

I crumbled the paper into a ball, taking another few steps before throwing it beneath the nearest car. It would have been so much better to burn it or destroy it in some other way so no one else would find it, but I couldn’t exactly start a fire, and it seemed stupid to carry it with me until I could. If someone were to catch me and searched my stuff, they’d find the picture. They probably would have seen it already by that point; it was displayed in most cities I’d been to, but it would make it impossible to convince them that I was not the one they were looking for.

But the fact that the poster was there made me feel a bit better in a strange way. Although it scared me, it also felt like confirmation that I’d made the right decision. The people in that building were probably related to the group, so I’d been smart not to try to trade with them.

I did need to get out of the city, though. That much was clear. I had to get out without anyone seeing or recognising me, I told myself as I unwound the bandanna from my wrist and secured it around the bottom half of my face again. It was in dire need of a wash; that was why I hadn’t been wearing it, but I could put up with the smell if it meant I survived.

A poster caught my eye as I neared the outskirts of the city. It had been stuck to a wall inside what appeared to have been a bank in the past. Half of the image was concealed by shadows, and I couldn’t help but do a double-take.

From what I could see, the person in the drawing didn’t look like me, and I wasn’t sure what to make of that. It felt like a good thing. If they were hunting for someone else, that meant they’d probably put up fewer pictures of me, and I’d be safer. Perhaps they’d even get bored of hunting for me or assume that I’d died long ago, and they’d just give up, focusing on the new girl instead.

But that relief was quickly drowned out by fear. What if it were someone else that I knew? They could have turned their sights on my camp, on the people who raised me. Maybe they’d decided that they might know something about my whereabouts. If that was the case, they were in danger. I had to warn them.

I looked around before stepping closer, needing to get a better look at the poster. My heart hammered in my ears, my knees felt unsteady, and my fingers trembled, and I had to move far too close before I could see the face properly.

A relieved sigh slipped from my lips, and I felt tears sting my eyes. It wasn’t them. I didn’t recognise the poor girl on the paper, but it wasn’t me or anyone from my camp. They were safe, and that was all that mattered, I realised. My lips pulled into a grin beneath my bandanna, and I turned away from the poster again, just as a noise came from the street outside.
