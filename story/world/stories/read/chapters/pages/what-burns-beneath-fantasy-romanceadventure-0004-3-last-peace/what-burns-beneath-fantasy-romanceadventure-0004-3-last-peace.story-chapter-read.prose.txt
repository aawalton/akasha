Fellbrook in autumn was a collage of scarlet, gold, and umber. I was enjoying the scenery as I walked through town, a large basket cradled under one arm, collecting goods for Brother Clem.

He was becoming notably frail in his old age, and the townsfolk regularly made sure his every need was met. And, since I liked to help the old cleric with apothecary work, it was my weekly task to bring deliveries to his cottage.

Probably due to the excitement for tonight’s Harvesttide festivities, today’s donations were almost too much. By early afternoon I was inching along with a large heap of candles, vegetables, soaps, herbs, and baked goods precariously balanced in front of me.

“Brin!”

I looked up, which was a mistake. One boot hit an upturned root. I stumbled forwards with a yelp, my bounty scattering across the grass.

Royce came loping up. He wore a pale linen tunic, sweat trickling down the sides of his face. A woodcutter’s axe was propped against a stack of split logs nearby.

“You okay? Anything injured?” He sounded like he was trying not to laugh.

“Only my dignity,” I puffed. “Your fault, you know.”

He stooped, grinning, and helped salvage my delivery. “Sorry. You’re gonna hate me even more in a minute.”

“Uh-oh… why?”

Royce snickered, nodding towards a bulging brown sack beside the woodpile. “Mom wanted Clem to have some stuff from the garden.” He laughed at my expression. “I can carry it.”

“Salt, I hope so! I wasn’t sure I’d make it there as is!”

“She packed this for you, too.” He produced a linen-wrapped bundle.

My gift was a large butter-colored biscuit, a seedcake glazed with honey, and a fragrant red apple. I settled happily onto the grass, asking Royce to thank his mother for me, then munched on my impromptu lunch.

Royce flopped down beside me, elbows propped beneath him and long legs stretched out. He produced another biscuit, somewhat squashed, from one pocket, and took a large bite.

I couldn’t resist. Two fingers traced, and I murmured my very first rune. I imagined the taste of lemons.

Royce’s face screwed up into a comical pucker. “Not funny!” he declared, smacking his lips together. Then his blue eyes glinted, and he snatched my biscuit away.

“Hey!” I tried unsuccessfully to wrestle his arm back.

He offered me the now-sour lump in his other hand. “Nuh-uh, magic lady, you can have this one.”

I huffed a laugh, releasing his arm. “I’ll just change them again. You can’t win, here.”

He snorted. “I can if I eat them both. Texture’s still great.”

“I’ll make them taste like fish,” I threatened.

“I like fish.”

“No, you don’t. You hate fish.”

“I hate touching fish. Too slimy. But eating them? Nah, that’s fine.”

I snorted. “Fine, then it’ll be spoiled milk. And onions. And dung.”

Royce’s broad shoulders shook with laughter. “Salt, alright, have mercy!” One tan hand stretched back out. “How about we each get one, you make them taste like bacon, and we call a truce?”

I accepted my food, tracing. “Ulen. Truce. Mine’s sausage, though.”

He shook his head, sandy hair swaying in the cool air. “You’re insane.”

I gave another exaggerated eye-roll and we munched in happy silence. After several minutes the clatter of a wooden ladder sounded from nearby. I glanced towards the Fellbrook inn.

Nightmare’s End. Dellin Cole had renamed the establishment twenty years ago. Durst said that Fellbrook had briefly become a spring of trade and activity; paladins had come to scatter sacred silver seeds, and people had made pilgrimages from across the realm to rebuild the town and see the divine scar.

Then the witchwood had flourished, choking out the roads and wreathing Fellbrook in isolation. And so the Nightmare’s End had gradually become less of a waypoint for travelers and more of a local fest hall.

Today the building was decorated in a slew of vibrantly colored ribbons; burnt oranges, rose reds, and cheerful golds. The front door was propped open. Dellin Cole and his two sons moved tables and chairs around inside, doubtlessly clearing an area for tonight’s festivities. His wife, Miss Alaine, was climbing a ladder to hang strings of colorful beads along the windows.

I smiled at the sight.

“I’m taking Sevalla to the party tonight.”

My smile froze.

Royce and I had shared many stolen kisses and lingering looks as we flourished into adulthood. And one day the realization hit me, quite like a hammer, that I loved my best friend like a brother… and nothing more. I’d told him as much.

And now… well. We had a routine.

Royce was very handsome. Fellbrook’s unpromised young women looked at him with obvious interest whenever we visited the inn for an evening meal. He would sometimes flirt back- and more later on, I was pretty sure, though that wasn’t a topic I ever broached- all dimples and blue eyes and broad shoulders. And then, after a young lady had walked away, he’d look at me with an expression that made my insides twist.

I’d always respond with bright enthusiasm, asking if he wanted me to leave so he could turn his attention elsewhere.

And my cheerful best friend would turn sullen.

Sometimes the change in his demeanor would last for moments. Sometimes for days. Eventually my unassuming friend would return, with a warm smile and an expression that held no expectations, and things would go on as usual.

Our routine.

“That’s great.” I meant it. I didn’t know the baker’s daughter very well, but she seemed nice and usually smelled like freshly baked bread. By my reckoning, those were two excellent qualities. “I hope you two have a nice time.”

Sometimes I felt guilty during these moments. I knew how he felt about me. And yet here I was, day after day, tromping back into town to be an inescapable presence that he could hardly ignore.

I hadn’t ever grown close to anyone else my own age. I hadn’t needed to. I had Royce.

Royce, with his infectious laughter and steadfast friendship.

Royce, who was now looking at me in a way that made me want to flee.

I cleared my throat and hopped up, errant crumbs falling from my frock. “I’d better get all this to Clem.” I piled the day’s bounty back onto my too-small basket, gaze studiously fixed on the grass.

Silence snaked between us.

Then one strong hand reached up, gently prying the basket from my fingers. He offered a lopsided grin. “Can I help?”

I hesitated.

As usual, Royce seemed to read into my silence all too well. “I didn’t mean… I’m sorry. I shouldn’t’ve said anything.”

And just like that, my inside unclenched. His clear blue eyes asked for nothing but my company.

“Alright. But only if you carry all the heavy stuff.”

“That’s what I’m here for!”

Clem’s cottage was nestled on the southeastern edge of town. I knocked, received no answer, and gingerly prodded the door open.

“Clem? Brother Clem?” I poked my head inside. There was no response. I inched through the doorway, ushering Royce in behind me. “He naps a lot, these days,” I murmured as we crept into the small kitchen. Its two windows were open, letting in brisk autumn air and warm sunlight.

“Lucky,” my friend chuckled, heaving the brown sack onto a countertop.

We restocked the room’s cupboards. Once everything was in its place, I dusted off my hands, then turned towards Royce and nearly jumped out of my skin.

Clem stood behind him, quiet as the grave. He was staring at me with wide, glassy eyes. “I think he died.”

My heart skipped a beat as the cleric shuffled forwards, face deathly pale. I reached out to take one of his hands. It felt like dry, brittle parchment. He was shaking terribly.

“What? Who?!” Royce’s baritone jumped several octaves.

“It was like watching the sun go out. How can something like that die?” Clem’s crackling voice broke.

I looped my arm around one of his, ushering him towards a chair. My mind raced. No one in town is ill, or has been injured lately. At least, not that I know of…

The cleric turned towards me, tears dripping down his cheeks. “Fia?”

I stopped midstep, breath gusting out in relief. This had happened a few times; not often, but enough to no longer be terribly alarming. I steadied myself and said, in the most soothing voice I could manage, “It’s Brin, Brother Clem. Brin Damelle. Fia’s daughter. Do you remember me?”

He peered through clouded eyes. “Brin? Oh, I… yes. Yes. Fia’s daughter.” He looked around, blinking rapidly, as if he’d just woken from a deep sleep. “I’m sorry, dear girl. I must have… forgotten myself.” One trembling hand lifted to touch, seemingly in shock, the moisture on his cheeks.

“It’s alright.” I hesitated, keeping one hand over his.

Royce gaped at me, clearly at a loss.

I winced. This was probably the first of Clem’s ‘confused moments,’ as the townsfolk gently called them, that my friend had seen. “He’ll be okay,” I mouthed.

Royce crept forwards, hands outstretched. “Can I, er, do anything?”

Clem shuffled towards the kitchen chair, floorboards creaking with every slow step. “Fia’s daughter. Fia’s…”

I gnawed on my lower lip and, after a moment’s thought, grabbed a small black kettle from one of the cupboards. “I’ll make you some tea, okay?” He didn’t respond. “Were you sleeping, sir?” Perhaps he’d had a particularly vivid dream. His ‘confused moments’ usually passed quickly, but he still seemed… off. Lost, almost.

“Always,” Clem whispered.

Royce and I exchanged worried glances.

“I woke up, once. Just for a moment. And then he…” his voice cracked again and he hid his face.

I wrung my hands, wondering how I could possibly help.

Royce paced, clearly unnerved by the old cleric’s behavior. Then he snatched the kettle away, hurrying towards the fireplace.

I grabbed one of Clem’s recipe books. He always seemed more clear-headed when he had a task to focus on. “Brother Clem, do you think… um, could you help me mix a fever tonic?”

He was quiet for a long, breathless moment. Then he rose, looking slightly dazed. “Is someone ill, Brin?”

I glanced towards Royce, teeth sinking into my lower lip. “Oh, n-no, just… with the weather getting colder I thought… it would be good to have some on hand, right?”

Clem shuffled forwards. Gnarled, trembling hands began pulling herbs out of the cupboards. “Of course. Yes, of course. We’ll start with the soaking. Grab a few drops of rum from the pantry, if you please.”

“Yes, sir.” I breathed a sigh of relief.

Our afternoon devolved into grinding, measuring, muddling, and mixing, until Clem and I- and Royce, who joined in with clearly feigned enthusiasm- had filled several glass jars. Once the work was done, Clem declared himself ‘old and entitled to a nap’ and retreated to his bedroom.

I opted to stay, at least until sunset, in case he woke up distraught again.

Royce helped me clean up the kitchen, then headed out into the chilly evening air.

“Have fun tonight!” I called from the front door.

“Be more fun if you were there!” He turned and winked.

I just smiled. When dusk eventually fell and I shut the sleeping cleric’s door, distant music and dancing firelight were visible from Nightmare’s End. For a moment I considered going to join the doubtlessly merry townsfolk.

But the nearby witchwood leaves were hissing, seemingly more restless than usual. And Royce’s blue eyes had been bright and unbothered and I wanted them to stay that way.

So I ducked my head against the cold air and walked home through the trees, murmuring soothing words as they reached out for me.
