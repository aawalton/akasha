Far away in a labyrinth of mirrors otherwise known as the Sect of Illusions, a certain transmigrant stood before a massive mirror, staring blankly at her reflection.

It was perfect. She was perfect—with skin as smooth as glass, eyes as bright as stars, and a face so symmetrical it could cut. Platinum gold hair fell past her shoulders, as soft as feathers, yet not quite as soft as her skin itself—supple like milk, her bosom ample, her waist practically nonexistent.

It was beautiful. Exceedingly so.

Yet Lysandra hated it.

She scowled, and the illusion cracked beneath the weight of her emotions, peeling back to reveal her true features.

A face so terribly scarred by fire that her once-soft skin was now a web of puckered ruin—red and raw, as if the flames had leached every trace of her past beauty and replaced it with ash.

It was not a recent injury. Not in the least.

Had that been the case, she would have found a way to heal it. To restore it.

She would have paid any price.

But this was not an injury that could be healed. It was the face she had borne at the moment of her awakening, and there was no going back, no matter how fiercely she wished otherwise.

Her scowl deepened, hatred for her own reflection bubbling to the surface. Before her emotions could fully escalate, however, a knock resounded against her bedroom door.

"Lysandra? Are you in there?"

It was elder Malverin’s voice, and at the sound of it, the illusion snapped back into place.

Beautiful. Perfect.

"Come in," she called, her voice twisted to sound brighter. Happier.

Elder Malverin pushed the door open, and Lysandra put on a wide smile. "How are you, Elder Malverin? It has been too long."

Elder Malverin smiled back—genuinely, warmly. "I'm doing well. As well as I can." Her smile turned knowing. "You seem to be doing well too."

"Oh, I'm perfect," Lysandra said, brushing a lock of golden curls from her face.

Elder Malverin chuckled, shaking her head as she lowered herself into a seat. "You never change."

Lysandra shrugged. "There's no reason to. I'm perfect."

"Hmm." Elder Malverin studied her for a moment, her expression sobering. After a beat, she sighed. "I fear I bring terrible news, Lysa."

Lysandra stilled, delicate hands clasped above her belly.

A beat of silence. Another.

Then she spoke. "You're recruiting me for the war, aren't you?"

Elder Malverin's expression turned helpless. "I'm sorry. I tried to keep you out of it, but we had no other choice."

Another beat of silence. Then—

"I understand," Lysandra said, turning to glance at her reflection. "Tell me what I need to know."

"Lysandra—" Elder Malverin began, helplessness rising in her voice, but Lysandra cut her off firmly.

"Tell me what I need to know. What is this mission?"

Elder Malverin swallowed, took a moment to gather herself, then began to speak. She laid out the core of the mission—what it entailed, and why they had chosen this plan at all.

When she finished, she exhaled. "I'm sorry I couldn't keep you out of this—"

"I'm not." Lysandra shook her head. "I've been dormant far too long. It's high time I stretched myself."

Elder Malverin paused, visibly startled. "Are you sure?"

"Yes." Lysandra smiled at her reflection. "Now show me clips of the battle so far. I need to assess my opponents." She glanced at Malverin, smile widening. "The last thing you need is for me to die."

"Do not joke, Lysa." Malverin's glare sharpened. "This is not a—"

"Game. I know." Lysandra waved a hand. "Now show me the clips."

Elder Malverin hesitated before producing a memory construct—a crystallized record of highlights from every battle fought so far. She activated it, and in the air above them, a montage of blood and violence began to play.

Lysandra watched the floating projection impassively, unmoved by the blood or the gore. She was a transmigrant nearing the Mythic class. She had seen her share of both.

Occasionally, Elder Malverin offered commentary—names, context, particulars about a given scene—but for the most part she stayed silent, watching Lysandra's face as the footage grew progressively brutal.

Lysandra said nothing. Felt nothing.

Until the transmigrants clashed.

For the first time, something flickered in her perfect gaze.

Intrigue.

She leaned forward slightly, eyes narrowing as she watched Nymera tear Kael Varros to shreds in minutes. As Seraphine carved Sophran apart continuously, finding fault lines within his very path. As Ashscar screamed within Volgrim's grip—only for Volgrim to be ripped apart by Nymera seconds later.

But it wasn't until Aodhán's battle against Alaric Thorneveil that something resembling caution crossed her face.

“What is it?” Malverin asked, latching onto the emotion immediately.

For a moment, Lysandra didn't answer, her brow creasing in a tell Malverin had long since learned to read as trepidation. Then she pointed at Aodhán. "This boy. He's different."

Malverin frowned. "How? He's the weakest of the four."

"I'm not talking about his strength." Lysandra's frown deepened. "I'm talking about his perception. Look at the way he moves—anticipating, tracking. He can sense his enemies even when he cannot see them. He's instinctively aware of their position at all times." She glanced at Malverin, eyes sharp. "He has core sense."

Malverin's eyes widened, and when she looked back at the projection, she couldn't unsee it. It was there in the way he moved—the way he turned before an opponent appeared, the way he dodged before an attack arrived. He was seamless. So utterly in tune with the battle around him that it seemed impossible to catch him by surprise.

Malverin shuddered. He might have struggled against Alaric, but against an illusion cultivator, the fight would be over in seconds, their entire arsenal rendered useless against him.

She mentally cursed herself for not seeing it earlier.

It was fortunate Lysandra had caught it. If she was to join this war, Malverin wanted her matched against the weakest possible opponent—and Aodhán was no longer it. Her gaze roamed the screen, searching for someone better. Someone less perceptive. Someone she could reasonably survive.

But it seemed Lysandra had other plans.

"I want him," she said, pointing at Aodhán's image. Her words hit Malverin like a bucket of cold water.

"What!" Malverin blurted. "Do you want to get yourself killed?"

"Malverin—" Lysandra began with a sigh, but Malverin wasn't listening.

"Go with Ashscar," she said, jabbing a finger at the injured boy Nymera had pulled from the brink. "He's the weakest link. The easiest possible opponent."

"I don't want the easiest opponent." Lysandra's voice sharpened. "I want the most interesting one. And this"—she nodded toward Aodhán’s image on the screen, her smile spreading—"this may be the most interesting battle I'll ever fight in my life. Two cultivators with the gift of core sense. Can you imagine it?"

Malverin sputtered, dread settling in her chest like a bag of stones at the sheer magnitude of Lysandra's foolishness. Oh, why had she agreed to this stupid plan?

"Lysandra." Her voice came out quiet. Careful. The tone she used when she needed someone to actually listen. "This is not the time to chase perfection. This is the time to be strategic—to find the easiest path to victory and take it.”

She pointed at the screen, her gaze hardening. "That boy will tear through every illusion you throw at him before you've finished casting it. You know this."

Lysandra said nothing.

Malverin pressed on. "If you won't take Ashscar, then look at the spatial transmigrants. Either of them is a far more manageable—"

"I don't want manageable." The word left Lysandra's mouth like something distasteful. She turned from the screen to face Malverin fully, her expression composed, unbothered, and just slightly insulted. "I want Aodhán. And you need to have a little more faith in my abilities, Malverin—I haven't survived this long by being careless."

She smiled then, slow and certain, her teeth perfectly white. “It’s going to be perfect. The most beautiful battle you’ll ever see.”

Malverin stared at her for a long moment. Then she closed her eyes and prayed.

Lysandra had used the word perfect.

There was no going back now. One battle pair was already decided.

Back in the Storm Sect, Aodhán stood before a fire rift, a contemplative frown on his face.

"Do you have any experience with fire rifts?" he asked, sensing Crixis's unease without looking at him.

"I… I've been in one before," Crixis said. "With my father."

"After or before you awakened?" Aodhán pressed, his gaze fixed on the swirling orange portal.

Crixis frowned. “Uh… after. Is that relevant?”

"Yes." Aodhán nodded, then finally glanced at him. "I want to put you in a position you're completely unfamiliar with. Up against an affinity you have no experience with. I want you to learn something about inevitability within that space. And the only affinity I can see here that fits that bill is,” He glanced around the Verge, completely ignoring the ring of disciples watching him as he pointed at a swirling gray rift, ringed with a line of crimson.

“Ash.”

Crixis stared at the menacing rift and swallowed. "I've never delved an ash rift before."

"I figured." Aodhán smiled. "Which is exactly why it's perfect. It's unpredictable. Unfamiliar. And it's a horde rift, so you'll barely have time to think, let alone control your attacks."

Crixis massaged his temple. "So I gain insight into my path by surrendering control."

"You should." Aodhán nodded. "And even if you don't, it's inevitable that you'll come out with something."

Crixis laughed faintly at that, and Aodhán smiled. "Don't worry. I won't let you die."

The tension bled from Crixis's shoulders in one long exhale. "Than—"

"I will, however, let you get very close to it."

"What?"

Crixis's eyes went wide, but Aodhán's smile didn't waver. "You said you learn best when you're in actual danger. I'm only trying to help you."

Crixis sputtered. "I— that's not— I didn't mean—"

"Don't worry," Aodhán chuckled, dropping a hand onto Crixis's tense shoulders. "Like I said—I won't let you die." He turned them both toward the rift, enjoying Crixis's fear just a tad more than he should.

A small smile pulled at his lips. "Are you ready?"

Crixis stared at the churning rift, fear and tension coiling tightly within him. But his emotions only held him rooted for a moment before slowly firming into resolve.

He forced a nod. "I am."

“Good. Let’s go.” Aodhán smiled, and without hesitation, they stepped into the rift.

The choking, acrid scent of ash was the first thing Crixis smelled the moment they arrived within the rift. It engulfed him like a cloud of suffocating stillness—thin, dry, sharp.

The air itself was thick, almost sticky, making every breath feel like work. Around him, the world stretched in dull, lifeless gray, fine ash coating every surface like powder. It fell perpetually from the pale, featureless sky, as though the sun itself had been smothered beneath endless layers of smoke.

One flake landed on his nose, and he sneezed. “Uh… maybe this was a bad idea, elder brother.”

Aodhán chuckled, seemingly unaffected by the hostile environment. “It’s not. Remember, I chose it because it’s unfamiliar, and unpredictable.” His smile curled into a smirk. “And because you leaned away from it when we passed by earlier.”

Crixis stilled. “I did?”

He recalled nothing of the sort, but Aodhán's smirk only widened. "It was instinctive. I doubt you even noticed."

“But you did.”

Aodhán nodded sagely. “I did. And I think it’s perfect.”

Crixis took a slow, steadying breath, forcing his arms to relax as he studied the rift more carefully. There were no monsters in sight, but he knew it was only a matter of time before they began showing up.

In tens. In hundreds.

Crixis wasn’t sure he could handle it.

“You can,” Aodhán responded, reading his emotions clearly. His smile softened. “I know you can. And I’ll be here in case things go south. I’ll be invisible, but I’ll be here.”

Crixis nodded shakily, forcing himself to push through the fear clawing at his chest. But then—like a phantom swallowed by the wind, Aodhán vanished, leaving him alone within the rift.

The silence that followed was immediate and total.

Crixis glanced at the endless expanse of ash and fear gripped him by the throat almost instantly, but he forced himself to stand tall, muttering hastily under his breath.

"I can do this. I am inevitable. I can do this…"

The mantra continued for a few more seconds, but just when he thought he’d gained a bit more courage, a sound like grinding bones split through the air.

One. Three. Ten.

They echoed all around him like charred wood splitting under pressure, drawing closer with each moment. And then—

The ash shifted. Not from the wind, but from beneath. Small mounds formed around him, rising and collapsing in uneven pulses as something pushed upward from below.

Crixis’s breath caught. He stumbled backward a step.

His leg sank into the ash.

He froze.

Less than a meter away, a chitinous limb broke through the surface. Black. Jointed. Charred at the edges like burnt wood.

It twitched.

A second punched through beside it. Then a third.

Terror seized him immediately—cold and unforgiving— as the ash caved inward and the creature hauled itself free.

It was a hulking mass of hardened chitin and cracked exoskeleton, its body laced with ember-lines and smoldering fissures that pulsed like dying coals.

Nearly five feet tall, the creature was huge, with eyes like burning coals and more limbs than Crixis could count in his frozen shock.

His gaze found the floating text above its head, and the terror in his chest tightened.

[Ash Skitterling – Tier 21]

For a moment neither man nor beast moved.

Then another chitinous limb broke through the ash. And another. And another. And…

Crixis lost count.

Within seconds he was surrounded by dozens of Skitterlings all glaring at him, the silence between them stretched taut like a bowstring at full draw.

Then, like a match dropped into gasoline, they lunged. All at once.

Crixis barely had time to think. Much less summon a storm.

He was absolutely, thoroughly, catastrophically fucked.
