“No, there is no need. Thank you again, Mr. Dorgh. And if you could please forward my message to that contact, I would be very grateful.”

Anne answered with calm confidence, as though she had not spent the entire morning pretending reluctance and carefully dragging a town asset manager toward ruinous generosity. Now that the sale was done, there was no reason to maintain the earlier uncertainty. The building was hers. And so was the rift.

Dorgh still offered to take her back to town.

The place was barely habitable, the road long, the air harsh, and the thought of a young woman staying alone in an abandoned roadside inn seemed to trouble him more than he tried to show. But Anne refused with such firmness that he eventually gave up on insisting. In his eyes, it was likely just the stubborn drive of youth. A reckless determination to get started quickly.

He was underestimating her.

Anne had no intention of wasting coin on comfort, transport, or delays when she had a building to secure and less than two weeks before her books arrived. Saving her ancestral craft would depend on more than her ability as a bookmancer. The bookstore had to survive as a business too, and businesses did not thrive on romantic speeches and hopeful sighs. They thrived on preparation.

So she watched Dorgh climb into the coach, heard the wheels creak, saw the small cloud of dust trailing behind him as he left, and only then allowed herself a long, slow breath.

Silence settled around the building.

The inn looked even larger now. Large enough to swallow mistakes whole.

She reached into her coat, took out a folded scroll, and opened it over one of the less filthy tables. It contained her preliminary plans for the bookstore, sketched long before she had ever seen the property in person. Useful, but now outdated. The size was different. The entrances were different. The shape of the place, the rooms, the restrictions, the opportunities—everything had to be adapted.

Two weeks.

That was the time she had. Roughly that long before the mercenary company from the province capital arrived with her books. By then, this building had to stop being an abandoned inn and start becoming a bookstore.

Anne looked around and began reordering the work in her mind.

Some things came before everything else.

Basic cleaning. Essential resources. A functioning core. Then the layout, repairs, furniture, and the sign. If she worked well, rested little, and wasted nothing, it could be done.

First, she had to decide what part of the building would actually be used.

That was easy enough in theory. The size, which might become a great advantage later, was currently a burden. She could not afford to leave four entrances open. She could not watch that much space. She could not clean all of it, repair all of it, and certainly not secure all of it alone with only Moruh to help.

The back door that opened toward the fenced courtyard could remain useful, but the stable entrance and the former main entrance would have to be sealed for now. As for the public side of the store, the best option was obvious. The tavern area was broad, practical, and already arranged for people to sit, read, and gather.

She would close the normal entrance and operate through the tavern side instead.

Yes. That would work.

The public room was big enough to receive far more people than she expected to see on any ordinary day. Fifty, perhaps, if she truly filled it, though even ten regular customers would be enough to make the place feel alive. For now, the arrangement would limit her services to normal research, reading, and study. That was acceptable. A bookstore did not need to reveal everything at once. It only needed a start.

After sealing the unnecessary entrances and restricted areas, she would repair the damaged walls, windows, and furniture in the public section. Other crafters could do such work faster and better, of course, but other crafters expected payment, and coin was a privilege currently denied to her.

Fortunately, Anne owned more than enough books and scrolls on masonry, carpentry, repairs, wood treatment, practical layout, and even decorative work.

Theory was not practice.

Still, everyone had started somewhere.

The last stage would be the bookshelves and the sign. The sign especially mattered. A store without a proper name would not prosper. As for the shelves, she had been around them all her life. She knew their measurements, their strengths, the proper depth for various formats, the dangerous shortcuts, the elegant ones, and all the little choices that made the difference between storing books and respecting them.

Two weeks, she thought again.

Then she rolled up the planning scroll and tucked it away.

Before any of that, she needed the heart of the place.

The ideal location for a bookstore circle was hidden, central, and difficult for outsiders to tamper with, preferably below the structure. That part, at least, fortune had granted her.

The basement.

While Moruh fetched water from the backyard well, Anne made her way down the narrow basement stairs.

They clacked and complained under her weight. The air below was colder than above, damp with the trapped smell of stone, old dust, and long-settled neglect.

“Illuminare!”

A sphere of light condensed into existence before her, first no larger than a marble, then swelling into a steady bright orb that hovered above her shoulder. For a basic spell like this, she did not need to summon the grimoire. She only needed the familiar inner connection, the memory of the page, and enough will to pull the effect through.

The basement room was medium-sized, cluttered with broken chairs, cracked tiles, and the useless remains of storage. Two smaller side rooms opened from it, both too narrow to matter much in her current plans. Anne considered knocking their walls down in the future, but after a quick inspection, decided there was enough space already.

This would do.

Per tradition, every bookmancer’s circle was personal.

No two were exactly alike. The broad principles remained the same, but the arrangement, emphasis, flow, and chosen outer arrays all reflected the bookmancer’s training, temperament, style, and recorded repertoire. In that sense, the circle was not merely a tool. It was a declaration.

Anne had never had peers to compare hers to. That was another consequence of belonging to a fading craft. Still, she intended to leave behind something worthy of her lineage, even if the craft died with her.

She had completed her formal training ten years ago.

Since then, she had spent years gathering books, scrolls, notes, old formulas, lost references, costly materials, and fragments of older designs, all to prepare for this. A proper bookstore circle had three foundational functions. It had to establish a domain. It had to absorb environmental energy. And it had to move that energy through the domain wherever needed.

With a proper foundation beneath the building, she would be able to strengthen spells cast within the store, support linked arrays elsewhere on the property, and eventually affect other rooms without standing in them. It was one of the great principles of bookmancy. The foundation made everything else possible.

First, however, she had to clean.

The work was miserable.

Dust rose in thick choking waves each time she swept. Old grime clung stubbornly to the floor and walls. Moruh helped with offended competence, floating water where needed, shifting lighter debris, and occasionally grumbling beneath his breath when Anne pushed him toward a new corner of filth. By the time they finished, several hours had passed, and Anne was beginning to regret not having recorded some cleaning spell years ago.

Only beginning.

The regret was not yet strong enough to justify wasting grimoire pages on it.

After a brief pause and a bread loaf eaten with very little grace, Anne finally began the real work.

The first lines came in the center.

She knelt on the cleaned stone and drew the initial array carefully, building from within outward. Every section was enclosed by a circle, each line exact, each curve delicate. In bookmancy, circles carried more than shape. They spoke of boundary, force, beginning, ending, and the power of containment. Nothing sloppy belonged in a foundation.

At the heart of the design, she placed a crystal prepared to store the energy absorbed by the circle.

Then she moved outward.

The central array followed defined principles. The outer rings were hers.

Wisdom tree ash. Dragon blood. Other prepared materials reduced into ink. Highest quality crystals she could afford. Adapted ancient patterns whose study had stolen months, then years, from her life. Anne used them all with steady hands and absolute focus.

Drawing arrays was not like writing words.

It demanded energy, precision, and control. There was no room for laziness, and very little room for recovery once an error had been made. Several times the work drained her badly enough that she had to stop, sit back, breathe, and force the trembling from her fingers before continuing.

Still, she pressed on.

Slowly, steadily, ring after ring expanded across the stone floor, the design growing more intricate as it widened. The light from her floating orb caught in the still-wet lines and made them seem like veins of dim power winding through the basement.

At last, she drew the final connection.

For a heartbeat, nothing happened.

Then the crystal at the center emitted a faint glow.

The entire circle answered with a quiet pulse, subtle but unmistakable, as though the basement had just taken its first breath.

Anne let out all the air in her lungs and dropped back onto the cold floor.

It worked.

Exhaustion swept through her so quickly that her body seemed to remember its limits all at once. But even through the fatigue, she turned her head and let her eyes travel over the completed circle.

Not a single known mistake.

Not one.

Everything had been done as well as she could possibly do it. The materials, preparation, adapted designs, the flow of the outer arrays, the storage crystal, the placements, all of it, perfect. If there was any flaw in this foundation, then it was one she did not yet have the ability to perceive.

A small smile found its way onto her lips.

The expression pulled a memory with it.

Her grandfather, old, stern, and patient, standing over a younger Anne with folded arms as she fought through another lesson.

“In our craft,” he had told her, “it does not matter how far you want to go. The foundation will always come first. A weak bookmancer can survive much with a strong foundation behind them. A strong one may wither before reaching their potential without it.”

At the time, she had thought the lesson repetitive. But now she could see how important it was.

The old man would be proud of this.

He would deny it, naturally. But he would be.

The thought tightened her throat.

Anne shut her eyes for a moment, forced the threatening tears back into place, and pushed herself upright. Sentiment could come later. Perhaps years later. For now, there was work.

Next came resources.

Clean water, staple food, and the kinds of necessities that turned a building into a place where a person could actually live.

When Anne stepped out into the backyard, her movements slowed considerably. A part of her had been waiting for this moment ever since she bought the place, though she had refused to give that worry full shape in her mind. The bookstore circle should cover the whole property. It should.

Theory, unfortunately, had a habit of becoming less reliable when put to the test.

She walked toward the fence marking the edge of her territory, stopped a couple of steps from it, and felt for the answer in the linked foundation beneath the house.

Then she exhaled sharply.

“Thank the Gods.”

The coverage reached. Not just the building, but the stretch of yard as well.

Anne let herself enjoy that relief for a moment before looking toward the well. It sat about ten steps from the back door, plain and practical, its stone rim weathered but serviceable.

“Moruh, is the water clean? Can it be consumed?”

The familiar froze.

Then he slowly turned his head toward the well with a look of betrayal so dramatic it would work if Anne had been inclined to mercy.

She was not.

Moruh swallowed, padded toward the well, and drew a small sign in the air with one paw. A bead of water rose from the depths, gathered itself into a clear floating sphere, and hovered before him. He stared at it with deep resentment, then gave it a cautious lick.

“It is clean,” he said after a pained pause. “Though there are some energy traces. Likely groundwater running down from the mountains and picking up mild magical properties along the way.”

“Excellent,” Anne said, ignoring his tone entirely. “Another advantage of being close to the Wall.”

The grimoire appeared again, floating at her side while she turned to the necessary page. Then came another moment of pain—not physical this time, but financial. She drew out a vial of the ink made from wisdom tree ash and dragon blood and stared at it in silence.

Using something so precious for a well was painful.

Still, water was not something to treat cheaply. If she relied on lesser materials, she might have to redraw the connected array far too often, losing time and energy in the long run. Better to spend wisely now than foolishly later.

“Ianua Annectit!”

An invisible array formed above her palm as she cast. She tipped the vial carefully, letting five drops fall into the air. The ink struck the spell-structure and revealed it in fine glowing lines. Anne guided the completed array downward, lowering it into the well until it vanished from sight.

Then came the harder part.

With slow, precise effort, she traced an unseen line of power back from the well toward the basement, connecting the new array to the bookstore circle below the building.

The strain hit immediately.

Even with the support of the foundation, this level of spatial linking touched near the edge of what she could currently handle. By the time it settled into place, Anne was leaning against the stone rim of the well, breathing hard, sweat running down the side of her face.

Still, it was done. Water was secured.

That left only the final survival measure for the day.

Fields.

Fortunately, the town had kept the weeds under some control to make the property look less disgraceful to potential buyers, so clearing a basic planting space took only an hour instead of the nightmare it might have been. Once the ground was ready, Anne took six small leaf-wrapped bags from her magic pouch and laid them out carefully.

Blessed seeds.

Not blessed by gods, of course. Names had a way of improving market value. These were the work of herbmancers—real labor, craft, and price. Their worth came from the ability to grow faster and healthier than normal crops, which made them a luxury Anne had only barely been able to justify.

Price had forced her choices.

Two tree seeds and four common crops, nothing more.

For the trees, she had chosen pink apple and soul tea. The latter had been painfully expensive, but worth it. For the crops, she had prepared wheat, sunflower, parsnip, and longleaves. Except for the soul tea, the rest were ordinary enough to be found in countless household plots. That was exactly the point. She was not planning comfort, but survival.

If the bookstore failed to earn quickly, these plants would help carry her through until it did.

Anne planted them all with care, pressing each seed into the loosened soil with more reverence than most people would give a shrine offering. When she finished, the light had already shifted toward evening.

She stood in the yard, tired to the bone, dirty, hungry again, and yet unable to suppress the small fierce satisfaction rising in her chest.

Tomorrow, she would begin repairs and start shaping the building into something worthy of receiving books.

Soon—if she worked hard enough—this place would stop being an abandoned inn and become a real bookstore.

A real bookmancer’s bookstore.

Anne allowed herself that thought.

Then she looked down at her hands, her coat, her boots, and the film of basement dust and sweat covering nearly every part of her.

First, she needed a bath.
