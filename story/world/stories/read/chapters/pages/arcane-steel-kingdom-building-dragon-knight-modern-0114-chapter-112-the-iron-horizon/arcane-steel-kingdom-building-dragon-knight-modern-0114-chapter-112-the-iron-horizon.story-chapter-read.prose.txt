The sea air tasted entirely different when you were standing on a hundred thousand tons of nuclear-powered steel.

It didn't taste of rotting kelp, or splintering pine, or the desperate, unwashed sweat of men hauling on brine-soaked ropes. It tasted clean. It tasted like ozone, heavy machine grease, and absolute, undisputed dominance.

Acting-Commodore Theramore leaned his forearms against the reinforced railing of The Axiom’s bridge. The wind tore at his crisp, midnight-blue uniform, snapping the fabric against his boots. He ran a calloused, age-spotted thumb over the single obsidian star pinned to his collar. He was sixty-two years old. His knees popped like dry twigs when the weather turned, his lower back carried a lattice of jagged scars from a Cinderfall slaver’s whip, and his left eye had a permanent milky haze from a piece of pirate shrapnel three decades gone.

But right now, staring out through the polarized glass at the churning, grey expanse of the Azure Sea, he felt like a twenty-year-old midshipman on his first deployment. His blood was singing.

Four years ago, the courts of old Aerthos had known him as the Tragic Admiral. It was a title whispered behind feathered fans with a mix of pity and aristocratic mockery. He had been the highest-ranking naval officer of a kingdom that didn't give a single, solitary damn about the ocean.

He remembered standing in the grand, velvet-draped halls of the capital, turning his hat in his hands, literally begging for coin. He had practically wept in front of the council, pleading for pitch, for good oak, for brass fittings that weren't already green with corrosion.

And every time, the silk-robed lords of House Black had sneered down their noses at him. “The shores of Aerthos are defended by the Mage Towers, Admiral,” they would drawl, waving away his reports of corsair incursions. “Let the land-forts cast their spells. Your little wooden toys are just there to collect customs taxes from fishing barges. Do not bore us with the sea.”

House Wight had managed the land borders to the north. House Black guarded the inland capital. And Theramore? Theramore had been tossed into the ocean, armed with nothing but a handful of leaky tubs held together by rusted nails, sheer stubbornness, and spit.

Yet, for a decade, not a single enemy vessel had breached his coastline. He had fought off raiders using blind reefs, weather traps, and suicide-decoy tactics that made other captains sick to their stomachs. He had squeezed blood from sea-stones. Other nations had tried to poach him—offering him galleons of ironwood and crews of hundreds—but he had spat on their gold. He was a man of Aerthos. He was loyal to the Silver Lion.

He had traveled to Wighthelm on that final, fateful day to try one last time. He had heard the young Lord Alarion was returning from the Academy. Theramore had planned to corner the boy, to drill the absolute, vital importance of coastal defense into his young, genius head before the politicians could ruin him.

Instead, the sky tore open. The green fire fell.

Theramore closed his good eye, the phantom memory of the ash still burning in his lungs. He remembered the smell of vaporized flesh. He remembered the heavy, suffocating weight of the iron collar snapping shut around his neck. They had offered him a commission in the Cinderfall navy as he knelt in the dirt, bleeding from a head wound. He had told the Cinderfall captain exactly where he could shove that commission, using terminology that made the surrounding soldiers blush.

So, he became a slave. He hauled marble for a monument to his own kingdom's destruction. He starved. He waited to die.

Until the day the sky turned black with metal, and a giant made of iron spoke with the voice of the boy he had come to see.

When Alarion Wight announced the formation of the Aegis Legionary Academy, Theramore didn't hesitate. He was an old, battered human surrounded by sharp, agile Dark Elves who looked at him like he was a relic of a dead age. He didn't care. He sat in those sterile, glowing crystal classrooms, staring at holographic interfaces that gave him migraines, and he swallowed the curriculum whole.

He learned about hydrodynamics applied to adamantium. He learned about nuclear propulsion, radar horizons, and kinetic shielding. The young elves struggled with the advanced math; Theramore looked at the tactical simulations and saw the playground he had prayed for his entire life.

He drove Mirelle, the Academy head, absolutely insane. He invoked obscure challenge protocols established by the first graduating class to skip modules. He barged into her office at three in the morning to argue with the AI instructors about the turning radius of a destroyer-class hull in rough seas. He didn't just break the grading curves; he shattered them, leaving the tactical instructors scrambling to rewrite their own test scenarios.

He had one goal. He had heard of the young prodigy, Valen, the boy who currently held the highest naval scores. Valen, who favored the dark, silent depths of submarine warfare.

Theramore wanted the surface.

Alarion had created a new structure for this impossible navy. A One-Star Warlord was the equivalent of a Commodore. Valen had been given a star and command of the Abyssal Fleet—the submarines. He was the One-Star of the Deep.

Theramore took the surface. He became the Commodore of the Iron Horizon.

But one star wasn't enough. The old sailor’s ambition burned hotter than the fission core humming five decks below him. Two stars meant Rear Admiral. That would put him on equal footing with Mirelle, who controlled the home defense. Three stars were Fleet Admiral. The absolute peak. He wanted that third star. He wanted to own the oceans of this world so completely that not even a seagull could drop a feather in the water without his written permission.

"Commodore."

The voice snapped him back to the present. He turned from the reinforced glass.

Commander Trisa stood at attention, a data-slate tucked neatly under her arm. She was a Dark Elf, sharp-featured, efficient to a fault, and currently wearing an expression of deep, suffering patience. Mirelle had assigned Trisa to him specifically because she was the only officer who wouldn't flinch when Theramore started swearing in old-world nautical slang.

"Spit it out, Trisa," Theramore grunted, pacing toward the central holographic tactical table. "What are the blind bastards doing now?"

"Sensors confirm the three enemy fleets have fully converged at the outer marker, sir," Trisa reported, her voice calm and analytical. "They have dropped anchor. They are holding a position. No aggressive maneuvers detected."

Theramore snorted, a harsh, grating sound. He leaned over the glowing blue table, his eye tracing the clusters of red icons hovering at the edge of the display.

It was a ridiculous sight. Three of the world's most powerful navies gathered together like a flock of nervous ducks waiting for a breadcrumb.

To the east, the Beastkin. He could practically smell the wet fur from here. Their flagship was a massive, ugly brute of a ship, carved from the hollowed-out trunk of a petrified tree and bristling with bone plating. To the west, the Lumina Imperium. Fifty ships radiating a sickeningly bright, consecrated light, their sails woven from mana. And in the center, the Cinderfall coastal defense fleet. Heavy, lumbering galleons packed to the brim with fire siphons and heavy ballistae.

They were waiting for a fight. They were waiting for a target.

What they got was the 3rd Fleet. The Invasion Force.

Theramore stood at the center of a formation that defied all traditional naval logic. Beneath his boots was The Axiom, a Sovereign-class Supercarrier. Five hundred meters of flat, grey deck space, housing enough Wyvern fighters and Phantom bombers to level a small country.

Flanking him were the two Crusader-class Light Carriers, The Templar and The Paladin. They were smaller, faster, and designed to screen the flagship and launch rapid, localized airstrikes.

And ahead of them all, a wall of grey iron. Twenty-seven Destroyers, their radar dishes spinning silently, their missile tubes closed but humming with lethal potential. Deep below the churning surface, a pack of ten Nuclear Attack Submarines loitered in the dark, waiting for a single word.

It was the largest concentration of Alarion's naval might outside of the Obsidian Fang’s hidden drydocks.

Theramore chuckled, rubbing his bearded chin. "Look at them, Trisa. Bobbing in the water like rotten corks. They don't have a damn clue what they're looking at. No sails. No oars. No glowing magic rocks strapped to the masts. They probably think we're a mirage."

He slammed his fist onto the edge of the holotable. "Lock the main batteries. Give me firing solutions on the Cinderfall flagship. Aim right below the waterline. I want to snap their keel like a dry twig."

Trisa didn't move to the weapons console. She stood perfectly still, her posture rigid. She cleared her throat.

"Sir. We cannot."

Theramore’s good eye narrowed. "Did your ears suddenly fill with bilge water, Commander? I gave you a direct order."

"The Lord Commander's orders supersede yours, Commodore," Trisa replied, entirely unfazed by his glare. She tapped her slate. "We are under strict directives to hold position. We do not fire until the enemy engages, or until Lady Nyxia has formally delivered the declaration of war to the Steward of Dragon Valley. We are currently acting as a deterrent, not an execution squad."

Theramore groaned, throwing his hands up in the air in an exaggerated display of frustration. "By the salty breath of the abyss! This instant communication nonsense is going to be the death of naval warfare!"

He paced the length of the bridge, his boots clacking heavily. "In the old days, a captain at sea was a king! You sailed over the horizon, out of sight of the politicians, and you made the calls. You trusted your gut. You read the wind. Now? Now I've got a nineteen-year-old kid sitting in a flying metal mountain halfway across the world, holding me on a leash like a misbehaving hound!"

Trisa sighed, a tiny fraction of her professionalism slipping. "Sir, you yourself said the logistical coordination provided by The Oracle network is the greatest tactical advantage in maritime history. I believe your exact words were 'it's like playing cards while seeing everyone else's hand.'"

"I know what I said!" Theramore barked, pointing a finger at her. "It’s brilliant! It’s flawless! And it completely ruins the fun! Where’s the fog of war? Where’s the mystery of what's waiting in the fog bank? It’s all just... math now."

He stopped pacing and looked out the reinforced windows again. The grey hull of The Templar cut through the waves to starboard, a sleek, utterly emotionless machine of death.

"The young Lord," Theramore murmured, his anger fading into a grudging, profound respect. "He understands it, though. I'll give him that. He didn't build us ships of the line to stand side-by-side and trade broadsides until someone runs out of wood. He built us, predators."

He looked back at the holographic display, staring at the red icons of the enemy.

"Those fools out there," he said, his voice dropping to a gravelly whisper. "They rely on the wind. They pray to spirits and angels to fill their sails. If the air goes dead, they sit there and rot. But us?"

He patted the cold steel console of The Axiom.

"We don't need the wind. We don't need the tide. We have a caged star burning in the belly of this beast. We sail where we want, when we want, and God help whatever barnacle-brained bastard gets in our way."

The tension on the bridge was a physical weight. The crew of Dark Elves, clad in their neat uniforms, sat at their stations, fingers hovering over keypads, waiting for the word. They were disciplined, but they were hungry. They knew what was at stake.

"Any word from the capital?" Theramore asked, keeping his eyes on the sea.

"Nothing yet, sir," Trisa replied. "The Cinderfall communication lines are a mess. Our Specter units on the mainland have reported severe panic in the high command. They are struggling to organize a cohesive response."

"Good. Let them bleed from a thousand cuts before we bring the hammer down."

Theramore walked to the comms station. He picked up the heavy, wired receiver—a tactile redundancy built into the advanced magitech systems—and pressed the broadcast button, speaking to the entire 3rd Fleet.

"This is the Commodore. All vessels, maintain Condition One. Keep your reactor outputs steady. Ensure your missile tubes are warm. The enemy is confused, but confusion breeds panic, and panic breeds stupidity. Do not blink. Do not break formation. We are the iron wall they crash against."

He hung up the receiver and looked at Trisa.

"I want a direct line to Valen. Ping his flagship."

Trisa tapped a sequence on her board. A moment later, the audio feed crackled to life.

"Commodore Theramore," the smooth, eerily calm voice of Valen echoed on the bridge. Even through the encrypted channel, the young general sounded like he was lounging in a library rather than commanding a pack of nuclear submarines in hostile waters. "To what do I owe the pleasure? I am currently charting the thermoclines beneath the Cinderfall coastal shelf."

"Just making sure you haven't flooded your tubes down there in the dark, boy," Theramore grunted. "You keeping your eyes open?"

"Always, Commodore," Valen replied, a hint of amusement in his tone. "Leviathan and her sisters are holding at sixty fathoms. The enemy fleet above us is entirely unaware. Their acoustic signatures are deafeningly loud. Wood creaking, men shouting. It is terribly archaic."

"Don't get cocky," Theramore warned. "Those Imperial ships have heavy wards. And the Beastkin shamans can sometimes smell magic in the water. Keep your distance until the order comes."

"I assure you, my screens are clear," Valen said. "We await your signal from the surface, Commodore. Happy hunting."

The line clicked dead.

Theramore huffed. "Arrogant pup. Thinks because he swims in the dark, he's invisible." But there was a spark of pride in the old admiral's eye. The kid was good. Too good. It pushed Theramore to be better. If he wanted that third star, he couldn't let a submarine captain outshine him.

He walked back to the front of the bridge. The sky was beginning to grey with the true dawn. The standoff had lasted through the night.

"Sir," Trisa's voice was suddenly sharp, stripped of its usual calm patience.

Theramore spun around. "What is it?"

"We are receiving a priority transmission from The Aegis. It's a fleet-wide broadcast."

Theramore felt his heart kick against his ribs. He gripped the railing. "Put it on the main speakers."

A burst of static cleared, replaced by the cold, absolute voice of Alarion Wight.

"All fleet commanders. The declaration has been delivered to Dragon Valley. The period of grace has ended."

A profound silence swept over the bridge of The Axiom. The waiting was over.

"Cinderfall is now a hostile entity," Alarion's voice continued, ringing with the weight of impending judgment. "You are authorized to engage. Break their lines. Sink their pride. Show them the new world."

The transmission cut out.

Theramore took a deep breath, filling his scarred lungs with the recycled air of the bridge. A feral, terrible grin split his weathered face. He had waited four years for this moment. Four years of slavery, ash, and relentless, grueling study.

He looked at Commander Trisa. She was already hovering her hand over the weapons console, her eyes locked on his.

"Well, Commander," Theramore growled, his voice carrying across the silent bridge. "The Lord Commander has slipped the leash."

He pointed a finger at the holographic display, straight at the massive icon representing the Cinderfall flagship.

"Spin up the railguns," the Commodore roared. The old sailor finally let loose. "Target the lead galleons. Blow those scurvy, wood-hugging bastards out of my ocean!"
