Daily Life

Mai had underestimated the complexity of the job from the very beginning. When Miho first explained the tasks she’d be handling, Mai thought it would be simple—routine work that wouldn’t demand much from her. But she quickly discovered that nothing in that house was ever as simple as it looked.

It wasn’t just the dust and cobwebs, the rooms were filled with objects she wasn’t supposed to touch without meticulous care. There were far more grimoires—ancient and dangerous ones—that Miho had mentioned, with their pages guarded by more than simple locks and talismans. They radiated a threatening aura Mai preferred not to acknowledge. In her wisdom, Miho kept those books secured behind sealed bookcases, places Mai was meant to avoid or just handle very careful in case she was cleaning, though the temptation to peek sometimes lingered like a whisper behind her ear.

Beyond the books, the storage room behind the house was another source of mystery and danger. OOPArts, artifacts impossible to classify, relics that looked like they belonged to another era—or another reality entirely—crowded the space in a chaotic collection that Miho protected with strict care. The place was a kind of sanctuary for objects of unknown origin, and Mai quickly learned she shouldn’t touch anything without explicit instructions.

Many of them were magical and had caused problems for other people. Miho had collected them from many places and kept them there because she said that destroying them would only cause more problems by releasing the forces they contained, which could jump to something else and get lost in the streets. Miho even avoided them, and when she taught Mai some things about them and the shelves where they were kept, she made it clear that if she saw any seals in the house that were half broken, she should let her know so she could replace them without fail.

Although she felt intimidated at first, she soon realized the work wasn’t as bad as she’d imagined. She didn’t dislike cleaning, nor did she mind the meticulous tasks. Over time, she adapted to the routines—the small rituals that kept the house in order. The work didn’t overwhelm her, it became something to focus on, a way to keep her mind from wandering into confusing, painful thoughts.

But the visitors… that was another story altogether.

The people who arrived with unsettling regularity always carried an air of secrecy or urgency. From yakuza to politicians to frightened housewives, Mai was quickly learning that this wasn’t just cleaning—she had to blend into the place. Miho greeted each of them with a captivating smile and guided them to her office with a confidence Mai envied.

Many times, Miho handled her clients’ problems without ever leaving the house, speaking with a calm, firm voice while Mai listened from a distance. But other times, she scheduled appointments and went out to investigate something personally, leaving Mai in charge of the house—alone with the familiars, but never at ease, knowing something could happen at any moment. Still with the passing of weeks was getting more and more acustomed to the place.

The house became a crucible of strange characters. Mai kept a posture of cold politeness so as not to interfere with the problems of Miho’s clients—many of whom required absolute discretion. Still, she couldn’t shake the feeling of being watched from time to time, as visitors eyed her with curiosity. Other customers came more often and had gotten used to seeing her in the house. Another stray that Miho had taken in, they surely thought, and it was true.

Miho, with her psychic and mediumistic abilities, dealt with far more than mundane issues. Her work stretched well beyond simple consultations or finding misplaced items. She was an expert at locating things lost—objects that had slipped into the fog of memory or vanished under mysterious circumstances. For those who knew her, this was her main source of income: a special talent no one else seemed able to grasp, yet one Miho wielded with unsettling ease.

Mai didn’t fully understand how it worked, but she saw the results. Miho merely listened to a client’s request, closed her eyes for a moment, and then—just like that—the issue was resolved. The missing object appeared where Miho said it would. The forgotten clue resurfaced. It was as if her mind could pierce through the seams of reality and retrieve what had been lost forever.

Sometimes, in the rear study, Miho used other techniques when her first attempt yielded nothing: pendulum work, or a strange form of meditation she performed behind a sealed door, inhaling incense that seemed to sharpen her perception.

But that wasn’t all.

Miho possessed another ability—something more ethereal, a talent she used to interact with the people who visited her. Often, clients didn’t come only for mundane advice or problem solving, they sought something deeper. Miho had the capacity to read emotions, to perceive things beyond words and gestures.

What Mai eventually understood, after observing for some time, was that this wasn’t simple analysis. It was closer to an interpretation of the client’s aura, their dreams, their unspoken burdens.

Miho could pull their deepest thoughts into the open—their hidden desires, their darkest fears. For some, it was a blessing. For others, a curse.

Mai couldn’t imagine how anyone could do something so… invasive at times, so personal. But it wasn’t her place to ask questions. Miho’s house was full of strange practices, and Mai was there only to work. After all the clients came for a reason.

What truly sent chills down Mai’s spine, however, were the mediumistic rituals. Miho didn’t limit herself to mind reading or object tracking—she also performed as a priestess, using abilities tied to ceremonies Mai could barely understand. She carried out exorcisms, sealed spirits, or performed rituals that seemed to rip something out from the other side—freeing or binding forces Mai couldn’t begin to imagine.

Miho always said she could talk to the dead and even lend them her voice—something that terrified Mai deeply. Although she never admitted it out loud, the idea of the dead communicating with the living chilled her to the bone. It wasn’t something Mai wanted to imagine, much less witness. The notion of the dead walking among the living felt strange to her for some reason, that she preferred not to think about it at all.

Miho had told her that technically, she too was a ghost in society’s eyes, just like the rest of the feys. In a way, the hidden world was more connected to the astral and spectral than to the everyday reality of people commuting to work.

There had been an attempt by Miho to access Mai’s memories. Something she had brought up once with the intention of helping Mai uncover more of her past—those empty spaces and fragmented recollections she could never retrieve. But when Miho tried, something inside Mai’s mind shut her out. Mai was relieved, in a way, that she had failed. Miho had told her that feys seemed to harbor a terror of remembering, something she already suspected because of her dream.

It was as if an invisible wall had risen between them, preventing any deep memory from surfacing—only the recent ones. Miho tried several times, her expression focused, her mind working like a needle searching for a lost thread of knowledge. Yet she failed every time.

“Looks like there’s something that doesn’t want feys to remember,” Miho told her once, with a sad smile, as if she understood that there was something deeper within Mai that could not be touched.

Despite those attempts, Mai knew that even though the pieces of her past remained hidden, something had returned when she arrived at the house. The memories she had seen—those scattered fragments of her life—were all she had.

***

In her free time, aside of walking around the neighborhood and nearby stores, Mai explored the house with renewed curiosity, slowly discovering details and oddities she had overlooked before. In one of those moments, she stumbled upon several weapons carefully stored in a corner of the basement. Swords, daggers, and—what caught her attention the most—an ancient but well-preserved bow.

The wood was smoothly polished, and the horse hair string, though old, still held perfect tension. Without thinking too much, she decided to ask Miho for permission to use it in the backyard, it had been gathering dust there.

Miho, after a doubtful glance, nodded.

“Do what you want, but replace the string. I never did because it was a gift. Who the hell gives a bow to a blind woman?” she said with a mischievous smile curling her lips.

Considering Miho could see with her glasses and had razor-sharp hearing, Mai only hoped—nervously smiling—that she really hadn’t used it on anyone.

Mai took the measurements and took a train to go find a new string in the direction Miho had indicated. The shop owner was an acquaintance of Miho's and called him to let him know. It was a small shop two districts away that sold sporting goods. The old owner had been watching Mai with concern as he prepared her order, and no wonder: Mai had been staring in awe at the archery section and the different types of bows. A compound bow had made her eyes light up like a little kid in a toy store. She bought the strings, practice arrows, some things for bow maintenance, and gloves, and took the train back.

After replacing the string, that afternoon, as she stood before the practice target, Mai felt a strange connection to the bow, as if it were a natural extension of her body. At first, the arrows flew erratically, but little by little she gained more control. With each shot, something inside her awakened.

It wasn’t just strength or dexterity—there was something else, some kind of hidden memory guiding her movements.

She remembered how to use it perfectly.

Her father.

She sensed it. She couldn’t clearly recall him, but deep within her she felt that the arrows flew because of knowledge she had learned long ago. Something from that past, from those moments of instruction, remained intact in her muscles. Every shot she loosed seemed to confirm that it was muscle memory—something deep and ancient within her, a remnant of a time she could no longer reach, but that still was inside her.

Seeing Mai’s progress, Miho offered more than just permission to train.

“If you’re good with that, let’s see how you do with something more physical,” she said one day, watching her shoot with a critical gaze.

“Huh? For what?”

“Because it’ll be useful. You’re small, but you’ve got good muscles.”

“Sorry for being small...”

“Use it in your favor. A bit of self-defense will do you good. If someone points a gun at you, do you think you’ll be fast enough to pull out a bow? Good luck explaining to the police why you were carrying that in the middle of the street. I may be able to assist the police, but that does not mean I can carry a weapon with bullets. You might be able to, if you join the Inoue Section as a special investigator, but for now, it is best that you learn to defend yourself, just as I had to do.”

Miho had a point on that… but she had see some feys that visited Miho carrying concealed weapons. There was even a monk that once appeared at the door carrying a cursed sword.

And so began her self-defense lessons.

Mai wasn’t stupid, she knew she wasn’t just training her body but learning how to use her short stature as a weapon. But adapting was difficult at first. Their first session was chaotic, with Mai trying to attack Miho without much control or technique. Miho was tall and long-armed—two things Mai had to account for.

However, instead of scolding her, Miho remained patient, encouraging her to find her own rhythm.

What began as a series of failures and stumbles slowly turned into something more fluid. Miho was surprised by what Mai was capable of. It wasn’t just physical strength—Mai seemed to possess an instinctive understanding of how to defend herself, as if her body had learned to fight long before her mind could comprehend it.

Maybe in the Other Side she was fighting all the time? Against what?

What surprised Mai even more was Miho’s revelation. Despite being blind, Miho had impressive skill without her glasses, relying solely on her hearing and touch. She didn’t need her abilities for that.

During their training sessions, Mai watched with astonishment as Miho evaded and blocked her attacks with a precision that seemed impossible for someone without sight. Miho didn’t just rely on her other senses—she seemed to have a deeper connection to her surroundings. She often said that the loss of her vision had sharpened everything else, but Mai wasn’t sure how much of that was true. It didn’t matter. Each encounter with her, each lesson, peeled away another layer of the enigma that was Miho, while Mai slowly began to feel more secure in her own skin, more capable of managing the chaos inside her whenever she used her own abilities.

***

And so months started to pass and summer arrived.

Mornings in Miho’s house quickly became a routine that Mai grew surprisingly fond of. Over time, training, work, and the small chores of daily life blended into a natural rhythm. Mai woke early—before the sun fully rose over the horizon—and began practicing with the bow in the backyard, perfecting each shot, each stance. The sound of the arrow cutting through the air became a constant melody for her.

She didn’t know whether it was the exercise that pushed her to continue, but something inside her insisted she had to. Handling that bow felt natural.

Though she did have to apologize to the neighbors once or twice, when an practice arrow drifted off and lodged itself in the tree in their yard.

After an hour of practice, she headed to the kitchen to prepare breakfast. Always something simple, but well made: toast, eggs, fish, or some recipe she had found online that Miho could enjoy before the long hours of work began. But green tea could never be absent from the morning table. Once they finished eating, daily tasks began—books, clients, and constant attention to the small details of the house. Miho had started teaching her some things about her job too, perhaps she could help her with something else.

At midday, they always took a break for lunch, and if the afternoon was calm, they continued training in the yard until dusk. Miho wasn’t strict about schedules, but when work demanded it, she was punctual. The routine gave both of them a sense of balance.

Sometimes, after lunch, the hours of work stretched until the sun began to fade, and during those quiet moments when no one came to the house, Miho would take the habit of going for a walk around the neighborhood with Mai. Sometimes they ended up in an izakaya whose owner knew Miho. Mai learned there that despite she liked beer she was weak to alcohol.

Miho’s blindness was no obstacle to enjoying the world outside. On those walks, Mai noticed Miho’s distant gaze, as if she were listening to something beyond the simple noise of the street. But on some days, when Miho went out alone or met with a friend, Mai found time to think about herself—about everything she still didn’t understand about her life, and about the questions that kept piling up.

Besides working at the house and training, Mai had grown accustomed to going out more often. In the early hours of the day, she slipped through the city streets with quiet steps, always careful not to draw attention.

Although her hair, once silvery, had darkened over the weeks, she still felt the need to be more cautious than necessary. The dark color of her skin lightened in the first days of her arrival as if it had only been a tan. Her pointed ears remained an unmistakable sign of her true nature. Mai often styled her hair to cover them just enough to avoid being noticed. Sometimes her moth-like antennae would peek out, but she hid them quickly beneath her hair—almost as quickly as she tried to forget her past.

One day, as she walked toward the market, a woman approached her. She looked like one of those housewives who often came to Miho seeking help with some spiritual or emotional issue.

The woman studied her for a moment, and before Mai could react, she said, “Are you Miho’s assistant? I’ve seen you before.”

Mai, somewhat taken aback, could only nod.

“Yes… I work with her.”

The woman smiled, but her gaze lingered for a second on Mai, as if trying to sense something within her.

“Be careful, dear. Not everyone is what they seem.”

A chill ran down Mai’s spine. Was she just a worried neighbor, or something more? She couldn’t help glancing over her shoulder as she walked away, half expecting to see someone lurking around the corner.

Later that day, she realized the woman wasn’t a client but a local neighbor, a friend of the one who often peeked from her window. Mai hadn’t interacted much with the people living nearby, but it was true that the constant flow of visitors to Miho’s business was more than enough to stir up rumors.

***

The August sun beat down hard, but Mai—already accustomed to Tokyo’s suffocating heat—found refuge in the shadows cast by the narrow streets leading toward the Nakano district. During her first visits there for residency procedures, she had felt a strange sense of familiarity, as if every corner of that neighborhood had already been etched somewhere in her memory.

Nakano housed the registration offices for feys in Tokyo, as well as the bureaus handling everything related to the hidden world. The Inoue Section had several departments tied to security, housing, health, and registry, forming part of a broader division that extended across all of Japan—along with the Asagiri Section in Kyoto, The South Bureau in Okinawa and the Gembu Kumadori Office in the north.

The bureaucracy of the Inoue Section was tiring on its own, but it wasn’t the paperwork that had captured her attention—it was the distric itself.

She still didn’t fully understand the why, but the feeling lingered inside, resonating deep within her senses like a song long unheard. The district, held a particular charm for her. The small shops and low buildings, some old houses stubbornly resisting time—all of it felt more genuine, more real, than the bustling, neon-heavy Shibuya where Miho lived. Although the district where they both were was more residential.

Once she grew comfortable moving around the Tokyo alone, Mai began taking the train to Nakano more often, especially in the late afternoon, when the sun dipped low and the streets were drenched in a warm golden glow.

She liked losing herself in that maze of alleyways—watching people, recognizing now familiar faces of the Inoue Section, or observing strangers who drifted past her.

One of her favorite places was Tetsugakudo Park.

Every time she visited, something stirred within her, as if that place had once been part of her life in another age. But the fragmented memories of her past kept her from understanding why she felt that way.

The park was surrounded by thick trees that offered cool shade amidst the summer heat. Mai often sat on a bench near the pond, watching the water ripple softly under the touch of the wind. In her mind, the images of the park blended with distant, blurry visions—yet something refused to fall into place. Sometimes she would close her eyes and feel, in a strange and quiet way, that she had already walked along those paths, long before her rebirth. But her memories remained scattered, like pieces of a puzzle she still didn’t know how to assemble.

As the months passed, Mai began to understand her new world better. Life with Miho—the work, the training—had become a routine that gave her a sense of stability.

But when she was alone, wandering through Nakano’s streets, she felt something deeper calling to her. As if the neighborhood itself—its buildings, its parks—was part of a story she didn’t yet understand. Sometimes she wondered if she had truly lived there once, on those very streets, in a time so distant it blurred into the mist of her broken memories. But like the pond’s water in the park, everything seemed to flow and dissolve into the warm summer air, leaving her with more questions than answers.
