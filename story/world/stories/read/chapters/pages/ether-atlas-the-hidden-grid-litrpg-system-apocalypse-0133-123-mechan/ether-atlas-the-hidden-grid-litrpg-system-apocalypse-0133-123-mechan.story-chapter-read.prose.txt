Arthur sat against the dressed stone with his hands on his knees. He had been looking at the door from across the passage for the better part of an hour, and not one angle had told him anything useful.

Steve was crouched at the base of the right-hand slab with his fingernail in the lowest course of the frame, tracing the seam for the fourth time. Donna stood with her arms folded and her weight on one heel. She had tried everything twice and was waiting for someone to produce an idea worth unfolding her arms for.

The mechanism behind this door is intact, Archie said.

All three of them looked up. Sarah, sitting on her heels a few metres back with her hands in her lap, tilted her head.

Intact and functional. It is not seized, not fused, and not barred from the far side. It is waiting for an input it has not received.

“You could have mentioned that an hour ago,” Steve said.

You did not ask whether the door was functional. You attempted to open it by force, then by inspection, then by force a second time. The door is not the problem.

“Then what is the input?” Arthur asked.

Archie did not respond.

“So force isn’t opening this, and our inspection’s come up with bugger all,” Steve said. “What’s left? Magic? Ether?”

Steve stood up, held his arms out and said, “Abracadabra.” He waited a while, and when nothing happened said, “Tu Én Énu Ru.” Nothing. “Sator Arepo Tenet Opera Rotas.” Still nothing.

“Worth a shot,” he said, shaking his head.

“Really? Abracadabra? What is this, a fairy tale?” Donna said.

“I would have you know, Abracadabra’s first known mention goes back to the second century AD in a book called Liber Medicinalis.”

“Shouldn’t you be using some Greek word of power then? You know, Greek magic door, Greek magic word,” Arthur said with a grin. “Sarah, do you know a Greek magic word?”

“I was…I am a doctor, Arthur. Why would I know magic words?” She stretched her legs and said in a small voice, “Askion, probably.”

“Steve, use that. It won’t work, but it’s at least entertaining,” Arthur said, looking back at Steve.

Steve had a faraway look on his face. He was chewing his lips and muttering something to himself.

“Hmm. A Greek magic door,” he said to himself.

“You know what, there is a reference to an actual Greek magic door.” Steve was now tapping his fingers on the door slabs. “Well, not magic obviously, but it was looked at as such.”

“Heron of Alexandria. First century. He wrote a book called the Pneumatica that describes a set of automatic temple doors. You light a fire on the altar and the fire heats the air trapped inside a sealed chamber underneath it.”

“The hot air expands and pushes water out through a bent pipe into a hanging bucket. The bucket fills and the weight pulls ropes that are wound round two vertical posts. The posts turn and the doors swing open. Put the fire out, the air contracts, the water drains back and the doors close.”

“So the priest lights a fire at the altar and the temple opens by itself, in front of a couple of hundred people who would walk a long way to see something like that happen. Two thousand years ago that was a god answering. Magic door.”

“So we have spent an hour,” Donna said, “shoving at a door that a Greek priest used to open with a match.”

“He would have used coal, but essentially yes,” Steve nodded. “Heron also made a dispenser that gives you holy water for a five-drachma coin, which is the first vending machine in the world.”

“Why is the vending machine relevant here?”

“It’s not, I just thought you would appreciate the information,” Steve said matter-of-factly.

“Thank you. Coming back to the actual problem, I don’t see an altar anywhere here,” Donna said.

“There would not be,” Sarah said, joining the conversation. “A Greek altar does not go inside the temple. It stands outside, in front of the building, in the open air. The sacrifice happens outside and the smoke goes up.”

“So we might have walked past it,” Steve said.

Arthur looked down at the floor under his boots. The pivots were under the stone. All the working parts of the door were below the floor.

“The pivots are under the floor,” he said. “That is why I found nothing. I spent an hour reading the face of a door whose entire mechanism is underneath us.”

He stood and faced the passage they had come through. “We want a hollow stone with a pipe coming out of the bottom or something similar. If it is there, it is behind us. Spread out. Do not go past the den.”

Donna found it a few minutes later. It sat in a recess about twelve metres back. It was cut deep enough to be obscured by shadows and read as shadow itself from the middle of the floor.

The recess held a stone block. It stood at about waist height. The top had a shallow lip around its edge forming a broad dish. Arthur ran his hand across the lid. The horizontal seam where the lid met the body of the block was tight and clean. A sealed box with a fire on the lid was a pressure vessel.

Steve crouched by the base and followed the block down to where it met the floor. A bronze pipe ran from the stone into the passage floor at a shallow angle. The bronze was bright and looked as though it had been cast last month.

“Right now we are lighting a fire,” Arthur said.

Steve stepped back from the altar and raised Harrow. He aimed the tip at the shallow dish and sent a lance of heat across the surface.

The fire hit the stone and scattered. A burst of heat washed over the lid, leaving a small scorch mark, and the passage lit up for a second with the flare of it. Down the corridor, the door did not move.

“No, this is too quick and violent,” Arthur said, shaking his head. “A burst across the surface will not heat the stone.”

Steve looked at the scorched lid.

“The air is inside the box,” Arthur said. “The heat has to go through the stone and into the sealed chamber underneath. We need a steady flame sitting in the dish. You said the priests used burning coal in the dish. We need to do the same thing here.”

Steve adjusted. He brought the tip of the rod down to the centre of the dish and held it there. The flame that appeared was the size of a fist and steady.

The four of them stood around the stone altar waiting for something to happen.

“I softened the hinge plate in Marseille with my bare hands and blistered both of them while doing it. This is easier, though I am not enjoying it anymore.”

“How long?” Donna said eventually.

“Heron does not say. He gives you the drawing and then goes off to write about a whistling bird.” Steve’s voice was thin with concentration.

Sarah moved to his left side and put two fingers on his wrist. “Your pulse is already elevated. I’ll ease you through it.”

“Thanks,” Steve said, nodding in relief as Ether passed through him.

The minutes passed in the cold passage with Steve holding the flame. Arthur watched the stone around the dish darken by degrees, heat conducting through the block from the point of input outwards, losing intensity as it went. After about ten minutes, the warmth had reached the base of the block where the bronze pipe met the stone.

Donna held the corridor behind them with Ravener in her hand and her eyes on the dark.

Some fifteen minutes later, the sound came.

A deep gurgling moved through the floor beneath their feet, water finding a channel that had been dry for a very long time. Then came a heavier sound, the grinding protest of stone pivots turning inside stone housings.

The medium is pneumatic displacement. The water volume is consistent with a suspended counterweight system, Archie said.

The doors looked about a quarter open when Donna said, “Right. That would do for now. Stop.”

Steve looked up with confusion on his face. “What?”

“Let it go.”

Steve cut the flame and sat back on his heels. His hands were trembling and he pressed them flat against his thighs. Arthur and Sarah were looking at Donna with incomprehension on their faces.

“We are not carrying unbound weapons through that door,” Donna said. “We have three cores and we have three unbound weapons. The mace, the messer and the bracelet. Three vessels, three cores. We bind, then we go forward. Now we know the heat trick works for the door. We can go in as prepared as we can be.”

“We will risk the binding, if we get interrupted,” Steve contested. “If we get attacked from the dungeon main entrance, before the binding is done, we will lose the core.”

“Three cores against three people going in carrying blunt iron,” Donna said. “I will take that trade.”

“By the time we are done, the altar will be cold again and we will have to start over,” Steve protested.

“She is right.” Arthur made the call. “We do this one at a time, on cleared ground. Steve holds the lane with the rod up until the last one is finished.”

Steve pulled himself to his feet and took position facing the entrance with Harrow raised and the tip faintly glowing.

Behind them, they heard the stone doors closing shut again.

Donna went first. She set the flanged mace on the floor and took one of the three cores from Steve’s pouch. She placed it against the central hub of the mace head, wrapped her fingers around both and closed her eyes.

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

Piraeus

THE GRAIN CULTS

I have spent a career being right about James Frazer, and I am about to stop enjoying it.

The Golden Bough is built around the dying and returning vegetation god. The corn king is cut down, buried and reborn. The same figure under a hundred names across every continent Frazer could get a missionary’s letter out of. The method is atrocious. He flattens cultures into one another, takes whichever version of a myth suits him and ignores the four that do not, and treats the planet as a long argument he had already won. Modern scholarship took him apart for it and modern scholarship was right.

The corpus will not go away, though.

Osiris is cut into pieces and put back together. Egyptians filled moulds shaped like him with soil and barley, then watered them until grain grew from the body of the god, and the rites at Abydos were closed ones.

Inanna descends into the underworld, is killed and hung on a hook. She returns only after Dumuzi is sent down in her place. From then on, the two divide the year between them. The Maya Maize God is decapitated and rises again through the cracked shell of the earth. After the Hero Twins defeat the lords of Xibalba, the gods make human beings from ground maize.

The pattern appears elsewhere too. Hou Ji survives being abandoned as an infant, teaches people how to grow grain and becomes the ancestor of a dynasty. Sita’s name means furrow. She is found in a ploughed field and returns to the earth at the end of her story. Then there is Hainuwele, from Seram. She is killed during a dance, cut into pieces and buried. The edible plants of the island grow from what was buried. That account was recorded from living people in the 1930s. It was not invented by a Victorian scholar looking for evidence.

So here is my correction. Frazer was wrong about the why and he may have been right about the what, which is the most humiliating way for a scholar to be corrected, because you cannot even take the credit for the disagreement.

Most grain myths can be explained as public stories. Osiris is carved on walls. The Maize God is painted on pottery. Hou Ji appears in the Shijing. People told these stories openly because they wanted them to be remembered.

Eleusis was different. Its mysteries lasted for nearly two thousand years. Revealing them could be punished by death. One family guarded the central rite across generations. On the final night, the hierophant showed the initiates a harvested ear of grain and said nothing. The rites of Osiris at Abydos were also kept inside parts of the temple most people could never enter. And that changes the question.

An open story spreads because people understand it. A sealed rite does not need to be understood. It only needs to be repeated correctly. That makes it less like a story and more like a delivery mechanism. Meaning can disappear over the generations but the performance survives. As Archie put it yesterday, a rite can survive the death of understanding. A text cannot.

For three thousand years, men walked into a building and watched a priest hold up a piece of wheat. They did not know what they were being shown.
