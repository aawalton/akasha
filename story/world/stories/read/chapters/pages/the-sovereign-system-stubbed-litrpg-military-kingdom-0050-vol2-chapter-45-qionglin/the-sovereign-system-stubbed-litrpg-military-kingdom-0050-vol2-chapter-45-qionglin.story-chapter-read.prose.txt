A lone figure waltzed down a wide street, idly waving a fan in his left hand as smoke coiled behind him. He wore a pencil-thin mustache, and his piercing blue eyes gleamed with satisfaction. A small, smug smile clung to his lips.

His crimson robes stood out starkly against his pale skin, giving him a sinister, almost spectral presence.

“General, Qionglin is being destroyed per your orders,” a deep voice reported from his side.

The speaker was slightly taller than average, clad in a flashy robe lined with ornate armor. He held a plumed helm under one arm, revealing tousled blond hair and a youthful but tense expression.

Hayden Hart shot him a brief glance. That piercing gaze made the other man flinch.

“I can see that, Crown Prince,” Hayden said coolly.

“Where is General Lowe?”

At the mention of the name, the prince’s expression wavered.

“We’ve brought him to your tent for questioning, as you requested.”

“Excellent,” Hayden replied, his smile deepening.

Without another word, he strode toward a temporary tent pitched just outside the burning fortress. The wind carried the charred stench of flesh—thick enough to turn a stomach.

Hayden seemed immune, his eyes alight with amusement as he inhaled the ruin like perfume.

“You may confer with the other officers while I speak with the General,” he said to Prince Leo, waving his fan in a lazy, dismissive arc.

The gesture might have been seen as disrespectful—flippant even—toward the crown prince of the kingdom. But Leo didn’t seem to care. If anything, he looked relieved to be dismissed. With a stiff salute, he turned on his heel and left without a word.

Hayden paid him no mind. He waved off the two soldiers guarding the tent entrance and stepped inside.

The interior was cramped and plain. A small table stood at the center with two stools on either side. A flickering lantern hung from a low beam, casting erratic shadows across the canvas walls. At the far end sat a pale man, barely upright, his posture held more by pride than strength.

The air was thick with the scent of decay and medicinal herbs—a pungent blend reminiscent of a battlefield infirmary.

“General Lowe,” Hayden said, taking the remaining stool. He smiled like an old friend. “How are you feeling?”

The man across from him looked terrible. Sweat clung to his wide brow, and his skin had the waxy pallor of someone losing a slow battle. Still, his brown eyes held a flicker of intelligence—dull, but alert.

“Like shit,” Lowe muttered with a wince. “The wound’s gone bad. I’m a dead man, and you know it.”

Even weakened, his voice carried weight. One could still imagine him bellowing orders from horseback.

Hayden didn’t reply. He simply fanned himself, the same smile etched into his face.

“What do you want?” Lowe scoffed. “Here to gloat about your victory?”

He let out a raspy chuckle, which quickly turned into a fit of coughing.

“You think I couldn’t have beaten them if I hadn’t been reduced to this?”

Hayden’s eyes danced with amusement as he watched the dying man struggle. His smile remained untouched by the General’s bitterness, as if Lowe’s words were nothing more than hot air.

“Tell me, General Lowe,” he said smoothly, “you managed to claim Qionglin Fortress in under two weeks, correct? What sort of resistance did you encounter? How many men were stationed here?”

The General raised an eyebrow, clearly not expecting the line of questioning.

He shifted upright on the stool with a wince, carefully adjusting the blood-soaked bandages wrapped around his side. His movements were slow but deliberate. When he finally looked up, his brown eyes held a renewed glint of lucidity.

The man’s face was a roadmap of scars and wrinkles. His broad brow faded into a bald scalp, the last vestiges of youth long erased by decades of battle. Perhaps, once, he might’ve been considered handsome—before war had claimed him.

“Ten thousand men. Many were still green,” he answered plainly. “We spent the first week sending out probing attacks. The defenses were... underwhelming. Once we secured a foothold on the ramparts, they collapsed under pressure.”

Hayden nodded, folding his fan and pressing it to his chin in thought.

“Is that the sort of defensive effort you'd expect from a nation that just sent assassins across the border?” he asked, tone light, almost idle—tapping the fan softly against his chin.

General Lowe frowned.

“What are you implying? That Qin didn’t send the assassins?”

“Xiu Fortress fell in less than a day and a half,” Hayden replied, not answering directly. “And it was taken by a seventeen-year-old from the military academy.”

“A seventeen-year-old?” Lowe echoed, incredulous. “What noble house does he belong to?”

At that, Hayden’s expression shifted for the first time. His ever-present smile stretched wider, finally touching his eyes—eyes that now shimmered with something manic. The look was enough to make a lesser man shudder.

“None,” he said softly. “He’s a commoner... a very interesting one at that.”

As if suddenly aware of his own excitement, Hayden cleared his throat. His smile shrank slightly, his composure returning.

“I imagine our forces faced similar situations in Longtian, Yunxi, even Xiandong. There’s no doubt in my mind—Qin wasn’t behind the assassinations that started this war.”

Lowe didn’t respond immediately. His pale, furrowed face twisted in thought.

“So then who was?” he muttered. “The only state that would benefit from a war between the Marxx Kingdom and the Empire would be Lhair... but as far as I know, they haven’t made any moves. And they’re on our western border, where our forces are still strong.”

Lowe straightened a fraction, eyes narrowing as if the pattern finally made sense. He’d narrowed down the likely culprit—but he was missing the final piece of the puzzle.

Lhair had already infiltrated the kingdom.

Hayden relayed the information calmly. But instead of reacting with outrage, the old General let out a deep, weary sigh. He turned quiet, eyes drifting into the shadows.

“How could this happen?” he whispered, staring into nothing. The weight of the revelation seemed heavier than the certainty of his own death. “Has the king truly allowed this to happen? After three centuries carving our place into the world... will it all end here?”

Unmoved by the man’s introspection, Hayden spoke again.

“You were present when the assassinations and declaration of war was discussed in court, yes? Tell me everything you remember—no matter how small.”

Lowe gave him a long, suspicious look, as if truly evaluating the man before him for the first time.

“Hayden Hart, descendant of the famed Blood General,” he said flatly, no reverence in his tone. “I’ll admit, your ancestor was instrumental in defining the Marxx Kingdom’s borders. Without him, we may have been crushed before we ever spread our wings.”

He shifted uncomfortably, wincing as pain lanced through his side.

“But he was hardly a man. He had no values, no compassion, no sense of integrity. He was a devil in human skin.”

Hayden didn’t react. His faint smile remained painted across his lips. Only the quickened fluttering of his fan betrayed the shift beneath the surface.

“Perhaps this is fate,” Lowe added. “On the eve of the kingdom’s ruin, the devil’s descendant sits before me. Those sharp, cold eyes of yours… they tell me everything. You’re more like him than you care to admit.”

The sudden snap of Hayden’s fan echoed sharply within the tent.

Lowe’s eyes widened. Just for a moment, Hayden’s expression twisted—gone was the polished calm. What emerged was raw, unfiltered loathing. His eyes blazed with fury.

And just as quickly, it vanished. He composed himself, though the fan now waved with noticeably more force.

“Consider yourself fortunate that you’re already a dying man, General Lowe,” he said coldly. His voice carried an edge that cut like a blade. “But make no mistake—if you ever speak of my ancestry again, your surviving family will join you in the afterlife.”

The words hung heavy in the air.

Lowe’s already pale face drained further of color. Lowe’s face tightened; he seemed to believe every word.

It wasn’t an empty threat.

Lowe let out a dry chuckle—one that quickly turned into a violent coughing fit. Pain racked his body, his face contorted in agony as his injuries flared.

It took nearly a full minute for the fit to subside. When it did, he was visibly paler, his breaths shallow.

“I don’t have long,” he said, emotionless now. “Tell me your theory, and I’ll give you everything I know. You won’t need to worry—I’ll take it to the grave with me.”

The thick air remained as the two locked eyes.

Only when he opened his mouth again did the choking tension ease, if only slightly.

“I suppose there’s no harm in sharing my thoughts.” Hayden said lightly, his usual attitude returning, “After all, dead men tell no tales.”

He folded his fan and leaned forward slightly.

“There is a traitor within the kingdom—someone in a high enough position to influence troop movements.”

It was a shocking claim, bordering on absurd. Yet the confidence in Hayden’s voice made it sound less like a theory and more like a certainty.

Such talk bordered on treason. If overheard, it could earn him execution—or a slow rot in the dungeons. But Hayden wasn’t worried. And Lowe understood why.

He had an hour left, maybe two. And even then, lucidity wasn’t guaranteed.

“It’s a bold claim,” Lowe replied, his tone measured. He didn’t question the implications—he simply acknowledged the truth behind the suspicion. “But… you may be right. Too many things have gone wrong to blame it all on incompetence.”

He shifted, trying to stay upright as he spoke.

“King Julius was a capable ruler—especially in the early years of his reign. The kingdom stabilized quickly after he ascended the throne. We regained lost lands, pushed back both the Qin Empire and the Lhair incursions. For five years, we flourished.”

His eyes gleamed briefly, filled with the light of old victories.

“We reestablished our borders. We were strong. It felt like nothing could touch us.”

He paused, exhaling.

“Then came the wedding ceremony… and the birth of the crown prince. The kingdom rejoiced. Julius had an heir—a strong son, destined to follow in his footsteps.”

Hayden listened in silence, his fan waving gently. He didn’t interrupt the old man’s nostalgia. Whether it was out of compassion or calculation was anyone’s guess.

Lowe winced, shifting again. His body was failing.

“Where was I? Ah, yes… the marriage.”

He wet his lips and continued, voice weaker now.

“Things began to change after she came—his first concubine. At first, it was subtle. The king started skipping court sessions. Alexander took over most of the duties. Julius grew sluggish, put on weight, and snapped at his advisors more often.”

“At first, I thought it was age catching up to him—but the man was barely forty.”

Lowe shook his head, pain flickering across his face.

“He started forgetting things… and spending all his time in the inner palace—a place where only his harem and the eunuchs could reach him.”

Hayden’s eyes narrowed, taking in the new information with interest.

Lowe went on, breath hitching.

“Then… Prince Lyon was born…”

Before he could finish, the old General’s eyes glazed over and his body slumped forward. His weight crashed into the small table between them, knocking it over with a loud crack.

Hayden stepped back instinctively, avoiding the splintered wood as it scattered across the floor.

Lowe’s limbs twitched violently. He collapsed onto the ground, convulsing as foam spilled from his lips. His eyes rolled back into his head, his body wracked with violent spasms.

Hayden sighed.

He watched the scene with all the concern of a man inconvenienced by the weather.

Then, without a word, he stepped forward and brought his heel down. A sharp crack echoed through the tent—and just like that, the shaking stopped.

Silence fell once more.

“If I’d known you were about to die,” Hayden muttered, brushing dust off his robes, “I would’ve told you to stop prattling on.”

He turned without a backward glance, pushing through the tent flap with a few idle words on his breath.

“The king’s concubine, huh…”
