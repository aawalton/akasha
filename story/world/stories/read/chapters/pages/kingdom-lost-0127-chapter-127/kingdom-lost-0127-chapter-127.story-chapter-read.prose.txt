Chapter 127

The wagon groaned back through the depot gate, empty, the wheels marked with fresh mud from the road. The team pulled it in a slow arc and brought it to a stop near the resource building, where the pile of remaining ore had been sorted and the last of the gold sat in stacked crates ready to move. Riley watched from the yard as the driver stepped down and the loaders came out to meet him. The Clawborn wagon was bigger than anything her people had, deep-bedded, iron-banded at the wheels, and it had made the run out to the treeline and back without a wheel going soft. It would carry the rest of the gold on this run, and as much ore as the frame would take.

She opened the HUD.

The waypoint she had set outside the Hoshin village sat green on her map, and the icons for her cavalry and the first infantry group were closing on it from the south. She watched them a moment, moving in the small clean steps the HUD used to show pace. The larger group would take the fallen and the resources into the treeline and post the guard. The second group, about fifty of the infantry, had already broken off from the column and swung wide onto the depot road. They would be here inside the hour.

Riley closed the map and turned toward the resource building.

Inside the treeline where the road curved and the forest curved with it, the ground was flat and dry under the pines. Her men had chosen it because the road bent in there and the trees came down almost to the ruts, and a body laid a few paces past the last trunk was invisible from the road unless you were looking for it. That was where the fallen were placed. Not on the ground. On beds of cut boughs the cavalry had laid down first, soft green cushions to keep them off the wet earth, arranged in two neat rows with a walking lane between them. Each man was straightened. Cloaks were pulled across their faces. Their weapons were laid at their sides.

Four soldiers stood guard. Two at the road side, two at the deeper side where the forest went dark. They stood with their backs to the fallen watching in every direction. The order Riley had given was simple, protect them from all threats.

She stood a moment at the resource building's door and watched the icons on her HUD do the work of the operation while her hands stayed at her sides. The fallen laid. The guards posted. The wagon back. The infantry inbound.

It was the strangest command she had ever run. She was standing in a yard in the middle of a captured depot and moving a hundred and some men through a battlefield with her eyes.

She let out a breath and stepped down into the yard.

Thorne came around the corner of the resource building with his cloak thrown back over one shoulder and his hand on the horse he had been leading. He stopped when he saw her.

Riley walked to meet him at the depot gate.

"Ride out and check on the scouts," she said.

"At once."

He turned to the horse. Riley caught his arm.

Thorne stopped and looked at her.

She kept her hand where it was for a beat, holding his eye. She wanted the next thing to land and not be lost in the movement of getting to the saddle.

"Give them this," she said. "If they see anything more than a single wagon and a small guard, they slow it down. Stealth. Not a fight. Anything they can do without being seen. Broken traces. A lame horse. A tree across the road. I want minutes, Thorne. Every one they can buy me."

Thorne nodded. "A single wagon and a small guard they let through."

"Let it come. We can handle a single wagon."

"And more than that."

"They slow it, and they get word back the moment they see it. Not after they slow it. When they see it."

"Understood."

She let go of his arm. He mounted and touched the horse into a trot and rode out through the gate.

The loaders had the wagon backed up to the resource building's front doors, and the gold was coming out in the small heavy crates the Clawborn had used to store it. Valrik stood at the tail of the wagon with a wax board he had found in the Clawborn office, in his hand, marking each crate as it went up. He did it the way Riley had seen him do everything since the gate had come down. Head lowered. Shoulders forward. Not rushing.

She came up beside him.

"How's it running?"

"Well. The wagon is holding the weight. We'll have the gold aboard in the next quarter hour, and the ore stacked in behind it until the frame won't take another piece."

"Good."

He kept marking, then he stopped and looked at her.

"Warden."

"Say it."

"The depot. When the wagon rolls, we'll have what we can move. The rest is inside these walls and it's not coming with us. Do we use the black powder?"

Riley looked past him at the resource building, at the storehouses beyond it with their doors still open and their shadows still full of the piles she and Valrik had stood inside earlier. She thought about the four charges of black powder in the packs. She thought about what they would do to those walls. She thought about the walls themselves, cut stone and set timber, work someone had done in the north over a long time.

"No," she said.

Valrik waited.

"We're going to take this depot," she said. "Not today. When we've broken them. When their citadel isn't sending wagons anymore. This will be ours. The walls, the storehouses, the towers we didn't burn. We repurpose it. Not just for our gain, the meaning of what it had stood for before.”

Valrik nodded slowly.

"Then we hold the powder."

"We hold."

He wrote a note on the wax board and lifted his head to the crew, and the next crate went up onto the wagon.

Riley stood beside him a moment longer.

"There's one more thing."

"Warden?"

"We need to bury the Clawborn dead."

Valrik's hand stopped on the wax board. His head came up and he turned to face her fully. His eyes hardened.

"We will show them this respect? After what they have done to our troops, to the Hoshin."

Riley looked down.

She let the weight of what he was saying settle onto her before she said anything back. The mat in the square at Hoshin. The children who had been quiet in the doorways when she had first walked through that gate. The man with the tongue cut out of him. What that community had lost, and how long it had been losing it, and how ordinary a thing that loss had become to the Clawborn who had built this depot to hold what they had taken. Valrik had walked through the same village. He had seen the same things. He knew.

She let him have the argument. He was right on every part of it that was about respect.

She raised her head.

"I know, we bury them," Riley said, "not out of respect."

Valrik waited.

"So, they cannot be brought back. So, the Clawborn can't come out here and collect these bodies off the field and bring them back. These agents of theirs, these men who did what you and I have both seen done, don't get to walk out onto another road and do it to another village. We put them in the ground and we don't mark it. Every soldier of theirs we leave standing is one more we fight later."

Valrik's face did not soften. It settled.

"Deny them the return."

He nodded once. "It should be done before the second wagon comes."

"Get it started. Deep enough that a shovel wouldn't find them in a week. Away from the walls. In the trees, where the ground is soft."

"At once."

He turned and called two of the loaders off the line and pointed them toward the far side of the yard, and they went. He walked after them.

A shadow moved across the yard.

Riley looked up.

The sky over the depot had changed while they had been talking. The clear high blue of the morning had gone. Cloud had come in low from the north, dark at the belly, moving faster than the wind at the ground would explain. The light had flattened. The color had gone out of the walls and the timber. The air had the cold, iron-tasting weight in it that came before hard rain.

Riley watched it move.

The same weather the citadel had woken up under. The same bruised sky she had not seen from here but had felt was coming from the moment Thorne had ridden out. It had reached her now.

She thought about the wagon and the ore still to load. She thought about the fallen in the treeline and the boughs under them, and how boughs did nothing against a hard rain. She thought about the men digging on the far side of the yard, and the ground going to mud under them, and the road going to mud under the second wagon when it came.

She thought about Thorne, out on the road, and her scouts ahead of him.

She took a slow breath.

"Alright," she said, quietly, to no one, to the yard, to the sky. "Come on, then."

The first drops hit the packed dirt at her feet and left small dark stars in the dust.

She lowered her head, opened the HUD, and got back to work.
