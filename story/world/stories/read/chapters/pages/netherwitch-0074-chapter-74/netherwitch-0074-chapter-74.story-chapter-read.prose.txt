Aidonopol could be split into three major regions.

The first was on the second layer of Tartarus, Frourio. This segment of the city sat on a fork of the Cocytus, guarded by tall walls and a colossal castle. Beneath Frourio was Laimos. Laimos was a broad cavern connecting the second and third layers of Tartarus. The tunnel was 250 meters wide at its thinnest and ran for a full three kilometers. Airships often passed through the central part, carrying goods from the second layer to the third.

On the third layer could be found the last region of the city, Ektasi. Here, unlike Frourio and Laimos, the city could not be contained. The bulk sprawled out into the hills and the plains surrounding the great cavern. Ektasi was the most vibrant part of the city, having a complex series of subdistricts serving residents, commerce, and industry alike.

Sylvia was in Laimos, better known as The Neck. To protect her identity, she wore the form of the light elf, Frida.

Laimos was a beautiful rot.

An ugly slum crawled up the walls of the cavern, so poor and ramshackle it made Viridian City’s urban blight look like paradise. It was a hideous fungus, a reflection of greed and indifference.

It was difficult to properly capture the size and complexity of those caverns. Demons did not require food, air, or drink. “I believe so as well,” Sylvester agreed. His crimson eyes shone. “You mean to have me invited into the Obsidian Tower as an esteemed guest.”

The old witch cracked a smile. “Well then, boy, can you make it happen?”

“At the risk of sounding arrogant, my victory is assured,” the silver-haired gentleman returned. “But, it won’t be enough if I enter alone. With so many demons, fey, and celestials gathered in this city for the International Exposition of Magic, the Obsidian Tower will surely be well-armed. And they will likely have other guests of power. So, tell me, how much support can the two of you bring to bear?”

Aidonopol was flooded with immortals. Demons, celestials, and fey formed thick throngs in the streets, the crowd gazing in wonder at the great works of magic around them. Lords, companies, and independent mages had thrown together grand displays to show off their inventions. On some stages, mages revealed new spells. On others, new enchantments and alchemical processes were shown.

One in particular caught Sylvester’s eye.

“Imagine, a world of golems!” a boyish voice shouted excitedly. “Golems cooking meals! Golems tending the fields! Golems for every labor of every kind!”

Wynn fluttered over a stage, his iridescent wings shedding pale-blue sparks. A score of humanoid statues formed a line behind him. Half the golems were fake, Sylvester could tell from the dullness of their essence. They only existed to add gravity to the tiny faerie’s form.

“Some say: ‘why own a golem when you can have a slave?’” Wynn continued, delivering his speech to the gathered crowd. “My answer? Loyalty and commitment! A slave is only yours for one hundred years. In that same century, a golem’s value will hardly depreciate. A golem will never disobey orders. In fact, the golem I have behind me has been tending that furnace for 48 hours straight without slacking even once!”

Golems were a kind of artificial phantasm. Like a phantasmal beast, they had a blood core which provided life and energy. In addition, they had a psychic core which supplied the golem’s ‘consciousness’ along with its instruction set. Designs varied at this point. Some golems projected their body as a figment. Others solidified that figment with essence. Then there was a third type of golem, whose body was a fully enchanted construct.

The biggest flaw of a golem was its price. The cheapest golems started at around 15,000 soli, but constructs that simple couldn’t be used for anything but brain-dead labor. Never mind tending a furnace, even cutting a tree or carving a rock was impossible. Those actions demanded special skills and ki manipulation.

By comparison, tamed low-level phantasms were many times cheaper. Shamblers sold for around 500 to 600 soli. An entire Helheim legion was priced at a mere 3 million soli. It was only when soul stones or high-level phantasms were added to the equation that the price of golems became competitive.

Except, soul stones granted immortality. Golem cores did not.

“Slavery is a curse upon the economic system,” Wynn continued to the crowd. “Why waste time on slaves when a golem is better in every way? We all know the answer: price. Golems are too expensive. But that’s where my company, Wynnderous Servants, comes in! Let me present to you our grand innovation! The Wynnder Core!”

With great pizzazz and the sounds of trumpets, two golems marched onto the stage carrying between them a blue-colored crystal.

A smile on his lips, Sylvester tipped his hat in the faerie’s direction, then turned away. Soon he melted into the crowd. Wynn’s shouts were quickly drowned out by the voices of those surrounding. On the right, a bashful swamp hag tried to explain the features of her new sighting goggles. These devices purported not only to let a scout see further, but were also resistant to many illusions and anti-divinations. Sylvester wrote himself a digital note to take another look at them later.

A bit further along, a spriggan introduced an improved meditation pad. The design could coagulate various primordial ethers, allowing an individual to digest the energies faster. Another interesting device. Once upon a time, Sylvester had believed that cultivation arts could merely speed up the rate at which the soul collected energy. He’d been wrong. The reason his second skill book was garbage was because of his lack of supporting information at the time.

Advanced cultivation methods demanded the user be in the manifestation realm or higher. Also, superior cultivation methods for asteri had never been developed. A few elemental arts, however, had made their way into the Cloud Island Wilderness.

“We should devote resources to developing advanced meditation pads of our own and to creating manifestation realm cultivation arts,” Sylvester mused, writing another note.

Things were fine now, but when their population neared a million, most of the gamers would have to cultivate through meditation. There simply weren’t enough materials on a minor plane. Even the introduction of dungeons and renewable veins wouldn’t change the math. Especially as gamers grew in level and thus in their digestion rate.

“I’ll discuss it with Emmy later,” he decided.

The International Exposition of Magic was on the second layer of Tartarus, filling a park in the Frourio district. Originally, the space had been too small. So Demon King Zagan had mercilessly bulldozed the surrounding buildings to make way for the 30 square kilometer display. Even so, there wasn’t enough room for everyone. Ten million guests had arrived so far. The projections Sylvester had seen expected fifteen million at the Exposition’s peak.

The result was a mad scramble for places to stay.

Virtually all the hotels and apartments in Aidonopol had been filled by travelers. The price of the remaining rooms had been driven sky high. The deluge didn’t just affect Tartarus’s capital, it’d spilled out into the surrounding towns and cities. To service the huge volume of traffic, dozens of airships plied the skies, continuously bringing guests in and out of the city.

Fortunately, he was a man of wealth. High prices were of no trouble to him. The less well-endowed denizens, however, had been forced to live in tent cities. Sylvester had even seen a few who’d dared to rent rooms in the ramshackle dwellings of The Tentacles.

Finally, he came to a stop in front of a large stone building.

On either side of the entrance were obelisks, the surfaces carved with sigils. Dozens of demons were entering or leaving, with plenty of fey and celestials to be seen. Many wore robes bearing stars in combination with an ankh wrapped by an ouroboros.

“And here I am, at the Collegium Magicae,” Sylvester said.

The college was the most prestigious magical organization in the netherworld. The school’s origins dated back twelve thousand years. The school had started when teachers and scholars gathered around the legendary stele on which Marduk wrote the Great Codex. Even today, the campus on Duat guarded that ancient wonder from the Age of Magic.

But it wasn’t history that granted the Collegium Magicae its grand esteem. Nor was it the depth of its arcane studies. Instead, it was the school’s steadfast beliefs and commitments. Knowledge knows no nationality. This was the motto the college bore on its walls. An adage to which it’d held from the Golden Age all the way into modern times.

The college’s doors were open to the denizens of the netherworld. Demons and fey were welcome to Duat, one of the thirty-three heavens. Their affiliation didn’t matter. All magisters were granted special visas to visit whenever they liked. The secrets of magic were to be shared with all.

To encourage this ideal, the college had created the Laureate of Magic. A laureate was broadly seen as the greatest honor to which a mage could aspire. Lady Vallenfelt had obtained hers by discovering the rune yithmafar. The meaning of this honor, however, varied among mages. Some saw laureates as greater than master magisters. Others viewed laureates like Esmeralda as high school students lucky enough to grab a Nobel Prize.

Which was to say: worthy of respect, but not worth listening to on any subject beyond their narrow niche of study.

“Emmy will be thrilled when she learns I’m a magister,” Sylvester chuckled.

He entered the building, his cane ringing on the polished stone floor. The structure was two stories tall, with each level large enough to fit a cyclops. Glimmering runes had been shaped into the walls. They were not an enchantment. Instead, they were a beautiful depiction of every glyph publicly known.

Lines of demons, celestials, and fey were gathered. The immortals waited to be served by the various assistants provided by the college. Here, participants would register their creations with the college, file complaints, or handle other bureaucratic matters. If Sylvester wanted to join them, he’d have to wait a full hour.

Being a shameless man, he unleashed his cultivation instead.

It didn’t take more than a minute for a girl in brown robes to whisk him over to a private booth. On the other side of the kiosk, a muki offered a smile.

It was good to be a member of the upper class.

“What can I help you with, sir?”

Muki were goblins of the bloodline Kategaris Xotiko Metallos. Even when compared to a redcap or a faerie, the man was quite short. Like most muki, the goblin had red-brown skin and a heavy-set frame. If it weren’t for his size and the two stubby horns on his head, Sylvester might’ve mistaken him for a dwarf.

Which was of little surprise. Some insisted muki were dwarfs. Denizens from both bloodlines loved mines and tunnels. Both were heavy-set. Actual dwarfs, however, hated the comparison. Not because they despised the goblins. Rather, because dwarfs were proud, burly men with bulging muscles while the muki were magic-wielding tricksters. Why, any good dwarf could drink a muki into the ground. A point they famously tried to prove whenever a muki was met.

Legend said, no muki had ever refused their challenge.

“I intend to register for the magister test,” Sylvester asserted. “It is my understanding that the college will provide this service during the exposition.”

The muki’s brown eyes widened.

“Your understanding is correct, sir,” the horned goblin replied. “The magister test is an affair the Collegium Magicae takes very seriously. So, before we begin, I must ask if your esteemed self has ever taken this test before.”

“I have not,” the warlock answered firmly.

“Then you must register your information,” the muki replied. The goblin lifted a short rod. Runes lit up along its length, catalyzing a simple spell. In the back of the room, a cabinet opened, papers flying to land on the kiosk’s desk. Tap. Tap. Tap. Three touches of the instrument copied the form onto some blank papers. “Additionally, the Collegium Magicae requires a retainer of fifty thousand soli from all participants. This money will be returned should you pass the test. Otherwise, it is forfeit.”

“Fifty thousand is no problem,” Sylvester waved off, casually placing 90 glints on the table.

The muki looked down at the gold, skeptically. Ah, yes. Gold was not favored in polite society.

“I’ve been traveling a lot lately,” Sylvester explained with an easy smile. “As a denizen of the Federation, I always preferred the Kubera Credit Union over Mammon. Unfortunately, they don’t have any branches here on Tartarus. Gold works well enough to avoid the hassles. Though, it does mean I lose a few soli on every trade. The trials of being a tourist.”

The muki gave a sudden nod of understanding. With a small sweep of his hand, the goblin drew the coins into his soul.

“You’re from the Federation too, then? What parts?” the muki questioned.

“Recently, Jeolsung,” Sylvester answered, leaning against the counter. He wore a friendly smile. “Before that, Alfheim.”

“Ah! I thought I recognized your bloodline,” the horned man said, raising a hand excitedly. “You’re a sofos, right? They’re all blondes and platinums. Rare, but hard to forget if you’ve seen one.”

“Warlocks are rarer,” Sylvester sighed in lament. “There are far too few of us.”

“There are rarer bloodlines,” the muki laughed. “I’m from Uku Pacha, by the way. My master brought me here for the International Exposition of Magic along with his other apprentices.”

“And they went and stuck you behind a kiosk,” Sylvester commiserated. “I do hope you get a chance to view the Exposition.”

“Luckily, I’m only stuck here half the day every day,” the muki replied. “A word of advice, if you’re looking for a good bank in the area, I’d suggest Bǎo Yín. They have a branch in Aidonopol.” He pushed the papers toward the warlock. “Now, if you’d fill these out, I’ll see if I can find a magister who can administer your test. What was your name again, sir?”

“Call me Swift,” Sylvester answered. A sly smile spread on his lips. “Sylvester Swift.”

Phantasmal Organs

Just as biological creatures have organs that provide specialized functions, so too do phantasmal beasts and the denizens of the netherworld. For demons and the like, these organs are the physical manifestation of traits. Thus, they can represent many specialized powers and passive benefits.

Not all organs are discrete objects, however. Some encompass the whole body, like veins and lymph nodes. Such organs usually cannot be harvested or transferred. It is the discrete organs, therefore, that make up most of the phantasmal organ market.

Organs can be used as materials in forging or they may be implanted in living beings as part of an alchemical process. The standards for harvesting a ‘living organ’ are very different from those used by the common hunter. Consequently, when organs are used in alchemy, it is often better to capture the target alive.

Organs as Materials

Material Tier

Generally speaking, the grade of a material taken from a phantasm beast will match its class. Therefore, a Class I phantasm will produce Tier I materials and a Class III phantasm will produce Tier III materials.

However, this presumes that the essence of the harvested creature is concentrated into the organ. When done perfectly, with little to no essence spilled into the atmosphere, 2 to 3 such high-quality parts can be collected. Alternatively, a hunter may choose to extract many more droms’ worth of lower tier parts.

Demons and other soul-bearing creatures may also be harvested for their organs. However, the tier of such materials is usually two ranks lower. That is because the phantasmal body is supported by the soul. This issue can be resolved if the demon willingly sacrifices soul essence during extraction.

Thus, a Class III demon commonly produces Tier I materials and a Class V demon produces Tier III materials. Despite the grade loss, for Tier VIII and greater materials, finding a Transcendent or an arch-level immortal is often the easiest solution. Phantasmal beasts and ores of that grade are extraordinarily rare.

Material Age

The age of any organ taken from a beast or an immortal determines its stability. This is important as most organs cannot be used raw. They must instead be modified, purified, forged, or smelted. Young organs will easily dissipate when worked, or quickly lose grades of quality.

Typically speaking, materials with less than 1 year of life are considered too unstable for serious production. Most smiths will refuse to touch them, not wanting to waste their labor. For more complex modifications, materials with an age of 10 years or even 100 years are preferred. Materials with an age greater than 1000 years can be problematic for opposite reasons: they are too stable and strongly resist change.

This doesn’t mean such young materials have no value. In certain types of alchemy, the object is purely a fuel or consumable resource. Thus age has little bearing. Young materials can also be smelted into older materials to raise the core material’s Tier. However, if a smith is to engage in such a practice, the core material must be at least 10 years old.

Phantasmal Parts in Equipment

The primary market for high-tier phantasmal organs is the equipment manufacturing industry. Large-scale construction, like airships, may use some phantasmal parts during production but for the most part, they make use of more easily sourced bulk materials.

Weapons, armor, and other custom goods, however, make heavy use of the highest tier materials that can be found. That means very high-level equipment is often unique. Many of the items cannot be replicated ever again, because they used materials which were unique or required multiple arch-level beings to make a personal sacrifice.

Transplanted Organs

Organs and other biological parts can also be implanted into other beings. Doing so can add traits or special abilities. In some cases, it may even add attribute points, effectively raising a creature’s level. Similarly, organs from other phantasms can exercise power beyond a normal creature’s class and rank.

The most natural and sustainable way to add parts to the body is mutation and consolidation. The next best way is through transformation realm ki arts. Both of these processes form organs naturally. This ensures the highest compatibility. However, it is also possible to directly splice organs into the body. The result of doing so is that the parts will not scale with the host’s strength and ability. Further, they will also put a higher burden on the soul and the body.
