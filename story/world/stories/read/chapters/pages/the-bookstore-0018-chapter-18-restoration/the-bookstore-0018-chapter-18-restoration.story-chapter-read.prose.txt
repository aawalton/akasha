Anne stared at the thick cut of boar meat on the table as if it had personally offended her. Cleaning it properly would take time. Seasoning, portioning, and preserving it would take more. Storing it well would be the worst part of all, because she lacked enough earth essence materials to do the work the way it ought to be done. Of course, she could settle for ordinary processing and eat through it quickly, but that would waste quality, shorten its useful life, and force boar onto every meal until she began resenting Rick for being successful.

That, she decided, would be unreasonable.

The ten kilograms she had taken in trade were not beyond what she and Moruh could consume even without special preservation. Still, Anne had no desire to live on boar every day, and wasting essence meat ranked somewhere near sacrilege in her personal scale of bad decisions. Life at the border was truly impractical. In a proper district of the Emperor Seat, she could have crossed the street and bought half the supplies needed to solve the matter in an hour.

Then again, in the Emperor Seat she would also have neighbors, noise, interruptions, and the kind of constant human clutter that slowly eroded a person’s will to exist.

Out here, she slept soundly.

Her grandfather had always been fond of saying that life was a balance of losses. As a child, Anne had found him intolerable when he said it. Now she was forced to admit the old man had not always been wrong.

“Pros and cons,” she muttered.

Against her better judgment, she left the meat for later.

There were times when household needs had to yield before a rarer opportunity, and this was one of them. Among the books Ceres had recently acquired for her was a compendium of papermancy. That would have been enough to brighten Anne’s week. Papermancy was not one of the great dominant crafts of the age, but it was old, subtle, and dangerously compatible with several branches of bookmancy.

Unfortunately, the book had arrived damaged. Not completely ruined, or beyond saving. But damaged enough that most sellers would have written it off, and most buyers would have called it a pity and moved on.

Anne was not most buyers. Repairing it would be difficult. It would demand more precision and conceptual reach than anything she had shown so far at the bookstore. It would require research, patience, and a degree of controlled audacity she had learned to respect in herself. Yet it was possible. More than possible. She could already feel the shape of the work.

Better still, just touching the project and considering the right method had stirred that familiar sensation deep in her spirit—a taut, invigorating strain, like a muscle being pushed toward growth rather than torn by it. If the restoration went well, her grimoire would almost certainly answer. Perhaps with several new pages.

That certainly made the meat less urgent.

Anne carried the damaged compendium upstairs to the large room she had reserved for bookmancy work. Two weeks earlier, she had cleaned, organized, and claimed it for precisely this kind of project. The table near the double window was wide enough to hold reference texts, notes, tools, and delicate materials without crowding her hands, while the light was good for close work even before magical assistance.

Spring had made the expansion possible.

With the little wooden helper taking over more of the cleaning and routine labor below, Anne had finally been able to reclaim parts of the building for more specialized work. That nearly justified the six pages spent on the construct, though Anne still maintained that the result had exceeded the category of a mere construct in ways that deserved future study.

The ruined compendium already lay where she wanted it, surrounded by note sheets filled with restoration attempts, historical references, papermancy fragments, and three separate lists of possible approaches she had already rejected.

The first stage of restoration was never spellwork. It was preparation.

For this book, she would need enough contextual knowledge to reconstruct damaged passages, enough conceptual control to bridge what could not be fully recovered, suitable replacement paper for the missing or weakened sections, proper ink to stabilize the restored text, and sufficient energy to hold the whole thing together without distorting its meaning. The thread itself she could produce through bookmancy. The rest required thought, research, and materials.

The paper used in the volume was fortunately not obscure. Standard wood-essence plant fiber, common to its era and region. Anne retrieved a blank sheet of comparable quality from her stores, trimmed it carefully to size, and laid it over the first damaged page.

Then she moistened the new sheet with a fine, even layer of wood essence oil.

The spell came next.

“Apograph, Transfero, Apio.”

Power flowed from her fingertips in a smooth, directed pulse. The new page shimmered first, then softened into a pale glow as it settled against the damaged original. The oil spread the connection through the fibers. For a few breaths, the two sheets remained visibly separate, one old and wounded, the other fresh and blank. Then the boundaries blurred. The new material sank into the old, reinforcing it without overwriting it, strengthening structure where age and damage had made it fragile.

Anne lifted the page and checked the reverse side.

Intact, and acceptable.

That had been the easy part. The true difficulty lay in the text.

The compendium had been written in ancient Solarium, the mother language of papermancy. So she had to proceed carefully. Ancient Solarium was not impossible for Anne, but restoring a text demanded more than reading it. It required following the logic beneath it, understanding where a phrase should lead, sensing when a gap could be rebuilt faithfully and when it had to be bridged more cautiously.

The first chapter covered the introduction and a brief history of the craft. That was fortunate. Definitions were dangerous to restore without certainty. History, by contrast, at least offered some structure. Anne’s research had already given her enough clues to move through the opening section with some confidence, and the surviving portions of the chapter still held enough context to guide her.

She summoned her grimoire.

It floated open beside her, pages turning with that dry whisper she would always find reassuring. Anne selected the needed reference and began.

Her pen moved across the page without touching her hand. Ink gathered at the tip and wrote in measured strokes, as though an invisible scholar were seated beside her. At the same time, Anne extended her free hand over the damaged portions and drew out a fine strand of pale blue magic.

A cohesion thread.

It trembled between the old words and the new ones, not physically, but conceptually—linking what remained to what must be restored. Where Anne knew the text, the work proceeded quickly. The pen wrote, the thread sealed, and the page settled into a stronger whole. Where she was uncertain, she slowed, considered, rejected one phrasing, chose another, and only then allowed the connection to hold.

The worst sections were the true gaps.

There, nothing remained to copy properly. Only absence, torn knowledge, and broken sequence.

Those places demanded the real craft.

Anne treated each like a road cut short by an abyss. She could not pretend the missing ground still existed. She had to cast a path across the break—one anchored firmly enough on both sides that the original meaning would not collapse under the crossing.

Sweat gathered slowly at the back of her neck.

Her breathing thinned. Not from panic, but from concentration. Restoration of this level demanded both precision and nerve. Too loose, and the page became a patched lie. Too rigid, and the gaps refused her. The work required judgment, and judgment was always expensive.

At some point voices drifted up faintly from downstairs. Anne noticed them without truly hearing them. A customer, perhaps. More than one. Moruh could handle known visitors well enough, and if the situation required her personally, he would come.

So she did not stop.

The pen kept writing. The thread kept trembling between damaged meaning and recovered structure. Ancient Solarium reformed line by line beneath her gaze, not perfectly, but with increasing integrity. By the time she reached the end of the chapter, her shoulders ached and her head felt mildly hollow from effort.

Still, when she leaned back and studied the result, satisfaction came immediately.

The first chapter was whole again. Not entirely original. No honest restorer would claim that after damage this severe. But it was faithful, stable, and most importantly, readable.

Anne exhaled slowly and let some of the tension leave her hands.

Then she felt it.

The shift was subtle, but unmistakable. Something within her grimoire had loosened and widened. She had expected growth by the end of the restoration if the work went well enough. Instead, it had come after the first completed chapter.

Her eyes sharpened. She did not interrupt herself to verify the exact number of new pages, but she was almost certain it had gained two, perhaps three. That was better than expected. Very much better.

Which meant the compendium was even more valuable than she had hoped.

Downstairs, Moruh’s voice drifted upward again—clearer this time, dressed into the artificial dignity he liked to adopt when trying to sound wiser than he was.

Anne smiled faintly and let the grimoire settle.

A short break, then.

“Welcome, dear guests,” Moruh said, forcing himself into a grave, professional tone. “How may I help you?”

He had been lazily batting at his own tail a moment earlier, but the instant the door opened, he sat straighter and adjusted his hat with the solemnity of a count magistrate receiving petitioners.

Hakon regarded him with immediate suspicion. Iris and Cyrus, by contrast, looked interested.

“Well,” Iris said quickly, before Cyrus could seize control of the conversation and ruin whatever dignity they had left, “we wanted to consult the storeowner about something.”

“I understand.” Moruh folded his paws together in a manner he believed looked impressive. “The mistress is occupied for the moment. Is it something I may resolve instead? May I assume it concerns your current shortage of resources for paying future lending fees?”

There were creatures gifted with magical instinct, creatures gifted with intelligence, and creatures gifted with neither basic tact nor the faintest sense of conversational softness.

Moruh belonged undoubtedly to the third group.

Iris’s mouth twitched.

Cyrus lowered his eyes at once, suddenly fascinated by the floorboards. Their first visit had gone far better than any of them had expected. Anne had counseled them seriously, let them begin studying immediately, and even given Cyrus the Syllable. But generosity on a first visit did not mean the store could run forever on goodwill. They had been told directly enough that continued reading would have to be paid for.

Which was fair.

The trouble was that Mountainend offered almost nothing for a party like theirs. When they left the bookstore the first time, all three had returned to town full of renewed energy and very little sense. They had thought they would take a few local jobs, gather enough coin, and come back in no time.

That had lasted until reality reminded them where they lived. There were no guild boards worth the name. No proper adventuring contracts. No line of waiting employers in need of warriors, mages, and healers. After that, Riverend had become the obvious answer, and none of them liked it.

Going there again meant expense, delay, and the familiar humiliation of arriving poor in a town that seemed built to notice and punish poverty.

Then the rumors began. A local hunter—Rick, that half-failed, badly regarded one—had apparently come back from the forbidden forest with a real bounty after spending time at the bookstore. Not just game, or scraps, but a proper haul. Enough that people in the market were still talking about it.

At first, the story sounded absurd. Then it kept repeating. So the three of them had asked questions.

Common sense said the forest near Mountainend was no place for an adventuring party trying to advance. However, common sense was beginning to look less reliable than before. If there was truly something here—some opening, route, or way to profit without crawling back to Riverend—then they would rather find it than leave.

Food and lodging in Mountainend were free, or close enough to it. While humiliation in Riverend was expensive.

That was why they had come. Not for books this time.

For the truth.
