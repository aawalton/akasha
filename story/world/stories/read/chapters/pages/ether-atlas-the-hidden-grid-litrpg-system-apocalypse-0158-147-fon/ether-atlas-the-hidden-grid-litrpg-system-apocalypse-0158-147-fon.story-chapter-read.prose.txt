Chapter 147

A carpenter's hands move the same over a cradle and a coffin. From the doorway, the work is identical.— Codex of the Long Watch, Watcher's Commentary

Dionysis repeated the phrase in his head.

He had said it back to his mother four times this morning before she was satisfied, once he had said it too fast, once too loud, once with his eyes on the door as though someone might already be listening.

He said it once more under his breath, walking the cart down towards the checkpoint. Thanasis waved him through without a second look. At the eastern stores Dionysis found a different man with the sign-in sheet, an older man with a clipboard and no interest in conversation. Dionysis overheard that the usual man had been sent down to check inventory at the quay. It was a short-handed week.

Dionysis unloaded, signed where he was told, and walked the empty cart down the hill with the sentence still in his mouth, undelivered.

"Tomorrow, then," he said to himself.

* * *

Past the harbour the road climbed in long switchbacks through the residential streets above Monastiraki, past shuttered balconies and walls marked with the garrison's chalk symbols. Dionysis had learned to read the chalk over the last few weeks. An arrow pointing uphill meant a cleared route. A crossed circle meant a checkpoint ahead. Two parallel lines meant watched but not manned.

They reached the outer checkpoint to find two sacks open on the inspection table.

A soldier had his arm buried to the elbow in beans. Another stood over a row of men's belongings: a comb, a tobacco pouch, a bit of wire and a spoon with a bent handle.

The officer under the awning was shouting about lamp oil.

"Two jars short in six days. Either the lamps have learned to drink or one of you is selling it below," he said.

The checkpoint sat at the junction where the old tourist road met the Acropolis approach. Thanasis was there, leaning easily against the barrier post with a clipboard.

"Not my men. Supply detail, from municipal stores," the corporal with Dionysis's team said, and handed over the inventory sheet.

They were carrying oil, flour and beans up the hill today.

Thanasis checked the wax on the oil stoppers, ran a thumb round the rim of each jar and showed the officer that it came away dry.

The officer jerked his chin towards the men and said, "Search them."

Two soldiers stepped forward and started patting down the men.

Dionysis raised his arms in compliance. The soldier's hands pressed along his ribs, under his belt and down both thighs. He checked Dionysis's coat pockets and removed the bread bundle his mother had given.

"What is this?"

"Bread."

The soldier stared at him long enough trying to decide if he believed him, then he opened the cloth, broke the bread in half and examined the middle.

His mother had put olives inside it and one fell on the table and rolled around.

The soldier gave the pieces back. "Next time tell her to use less oil."

"You tell her, if you have a death wish," Dionysis said with a smile and almost sighed in relief when he got a smile in return.

"Enough talk," the officer at the checkpoint barked. Thanasis counted the heads one more time, wrote something in his clipboard and waved them through.

* * *

By the time their detail reached the eastern barracks, sweat had soaked through the back of Dionysis's shirt despite the morning chill.

The marked unloading strip was occupied by a stack of damaged sacks. The corporal ordered them to stand against the wall while a stores man found space. Two soldiers came out carrying a crate between them.

Petros was near the far wall supervising the stacking of water containers with a clipboard under one arm. His sleeves were rolled, and there was a charcoal or ink mark along the side of one finger. He wore the same easy face Dionysis remembered from the boatyard, though there were shadows beneath his eyes and his beard had grown unevenly along one cheek. He had a ledger open under one hand and a soldier stood at his shoulder and read each entry aloud after Petros made it. Petros was listening to another corporal complain.

"Six sacks were entered," the man said.

"And six sacks were sent from here," Petros said.

"Five arrived."

"Then one became tired on the way."

The men around the corporal snickered.

"I write everything down and my ledger is checked every evening. If a sack went missing it was not here. If you don't believe me, take it up with Captain Karras," Petros said.

"Kala, kala," the corporal said and backed away, clearly in no mood to talk to Karras about anything.

Petros looked up when Dionysis's group brought the first sack through. His eyes passed over Dionysis, moved to the corporal and then returned to the ledger.

"Eight flour, two beans and four oil," the corporal said.

"You were meant to bring six flour, four beans and four oil," Petros said, flipping a page.

"We were told eight flour and two beans."

"Then the men will have no stew to dip their bread in."

"Oil there. Not beside the flour," Petros said, taking a step forward and pointing at a space by the counter as one of the supply details brought in the oil.

"You want the sacks or not?" the corporal asked.

"Yes, yes, a little less stew never killed anyone," Petros said and glanced at the soldier standing behind him who gave a nod. "Stack the oil first."

Dionysis felt clammy and he was sure the others around him could feel his heart thump in his chest. There was no way he was going to be able to give the message with so many people, and for a shameful second, he realised that he was relieved.

"Wolf boy, don't just stand there like you are on your first date," Petros said, looking at Dionysis.

Before Dionysis fully realised it, he found himself saying, "How do you know of Foteini?"

"Who?" Petros asked, puzzled.

"The first date thing you said. I took Foteini out last week, she looked so pretty in her white linen jacket that I couldn't say anything when I saw her. And then I clumsily spilled coffee on her sleeve. She will never talk to me again, I'm sure."

The men in the store were listening to Dionysis act like a fool but their faces were amused, not suspicious.

"Idiot, he was just making a statement, he doesn't know about your love life," the corporal said.

"No love life anymore, maybe I should find out who this Foteini is," a younger man from Dionysis's detail said.

Petros was smiling along, making an easy joke here and a slap on the back there, but his eyes had changed.

Foteini was the name of a girl Petros had dated for two years. That in itself was nothing; Foteini was a common enough name. The part that got him was the linen jacket and the coffee. The linen jacket was Sarah's, something she had got from Santorini or Mykonos. This was before their father had passed away and before MSF.

Foteini had borrowed her jacket, forgot to return it for three months, and when she did Sarah had found a coffee stain on the left cuff. She had hated Foteini ever since.

"Lover boy, you will not get another chance if you don't finish stacking the sacks," Petros said and moved to inspect the flour. The soldier standing behind him followed.

The men quickly placed the oil jars in their designated place and started with the flour sacks. The corporals stood around sharing a cigarette and complaining about the rations. Petros moved from one sack to another checking the seal and the stitching.

Dionysis was slowly losing hope; only the bean sacks were left. As he placed a sack down, Petros came over to inspect it and the soldier followed. Dionysis wasn't sure how it happened, but the next moment the base of the sack was spilling beans onto the floor. He could have sworn that Petros had twisted and pulled at the stitching cord when he had bent down to inspect the seal.

"Gamóto," Petros swore and bunched up the base of the sack to keep the beans from spilling. "Get the needle and the cord," he said to the soldier and motioned at the counter.

The moment the soldier left, Dionysis blurted out the memorised lines.

"Sarah sends message. All four back, working to free everyone. They need to know where people are kept, council status, weak points and garrison schedule."

Petros made no reaction, in fact he was not even looking at Dionysis. His face was turned towards the soldier as he came back with the needle and the stitching cord.

"Lover boy is so lovesick that he can't even put a sack down properly, re," Petros said, starting to stitch the base of the sack.

"All sacks are stacked, sign the paper and let us go. I have another delivery today," the corporal with Dionysis's detail said.

"Siga siga. I stitch this bad and rats get into the beans," Petros replied.

It took another few minutes of Petros unhurriedly stitching and the corporal swearing under his breath before Petros got up, straightened his clothes and picked up the clipboard from where he had dropped it.

"Oil, yes, flour, yes, beans, yes," he muttered to himself as he looked around.

"Fasolada takes time, you know," Petros said, signing the delivery papers.

"What?"

"The beans, they reminded me of Fasolada, a good Fasolada takes time. Wash and soak the beans overnight, slowly cook them for hours and then wait another two days for the flavour to mature. You can't hurry if you want the good thing."

"Why are we talking about soup?" the soldier behind Petros asked.

"Because we have beans but no time to make anything good with them," Petros said.

"I always hated Fasolada," the corporal said.

"You hated Fasolada when it was common," Petros said, motioning at the sky above. "Now you will take a bath in it, if someone made it properly."

"Okay, done here. I have to go to the maintenance yard next," Petros said and started towards the back door. "Lover boy, give my regards to Foteini."
