Sedgewick Vale was… overwhelming. There was no other word for it.

We passed through dense wooden gates after brief introductions and statements of intent. Renner gave the guards descriptions of the pacted from Snowmelt, but no one matching any of them had been seen coming into the city.

Then we walked in behind the Thalessians, straight onto a wide road. It was paved. This was, first and foremost, what made me goggle. I’d spent my entire life with the roughshod charm of Fellbrook’s narrow, uneven cobblestones, but Sedgewick Vale boasted roads wide enough to run two wagons down and smooth enough to never damage a wheel.

I walked along in a bit of a daze, Royce at my side. The two of us spun slow circles, heads lifting and jaws dropping at the sights, sounds, and smells.

One-story cottages, two-story shops, market stalls wide enough to fit my entire kitchen inside. The steady hammer of metal on metal from a smithy, the calls of folks manning bins of fish, meat, spices, vegetables, soaps, candles, woodworks, and cloth. Men and women trotting along to one another or even to us, touting goods for sale. One bent-back woman trailed the street pulling a small cart full of hand-carved witchwood ornaments; bracelets, necklaces, strung chimes, and shining broaches. I looked at her wares, lost in thought and tracing the skin of a now-bare wrist, as she eagerly chatted about all the ways her charms would protect me from monsters.

Eventually Teela noticed me lagging. She trotted back, looped one arm through mine, and tugged me away from the cart with a roll of her eyes. The woman gave her an ugly look, harrumphed, and rolled her wares away.

I might’ve stopped to peruse more, but I was too busy gaping at everything to actually choose a set direction. Teela made comments on the people, the streets, and some interesting-looking shops as we walked. Royce paced along, taking everything in with obvious shock, and announced that by two streets in he’d already seen more people than in his entire life.

Jareth marched down the streets like he owned them. Teeth sparkling, head high, booming out greetings to anyone who turned his way. Quite a few peddlers rushed up, offering him and Joselia various goods alongside deep bows. Everything was rejected with grace, save for two apples that came sailing through the air from a young boy in a stall. Jareth beamed at the lad, saluted him, and tossed his apple to Teela with a wink.

She turned bright pink. I bit down on a grin, nudging her with one elbow and wiggling my eyebrows. Her flushed deepened and she hissed at me to hush.

Renner strolled some distance ahead, side-by-side with Dame Joselia. The two were engaged in quiet conversation. More than once, my gaze lingered on the set of his shoulders; they were, for the first time in several days, not stiff. Even the way he moved seemed absent of its usual wariness. No coiled muscles, no sharp eyes scanning the shadows. Whenever he turned to speak, the set of his jaw was softened.

I’ve never seen him so relaxed. So… not on edge. He must really trust the two of them. Jareth… I don’t quite know what to make of him. He’s certainly very charming. Joselia seems lovely. She’s so calm and sure of herself. I wonder how long she’s known Renner?

The woman walked beside him, cloak sweeping at her heels. Shoulders back and hair glinting gold. The picture of grace and dignity. Quite often she turned back, green eyes bright as they searched out Teela, Royce, and myself. Silently ensuring we were all accounted for before turning her warm gaze back to Renner.

She’s so happy to see him. It’s plain as day. It reminds me a bit of Marion; how she looks when I come into town after being away for a while.

Joselia wouldn’t hurt me. She wouldn’t rip my clothes off or torture me.

The thought, accompanied by the deluge of images Renner had so callously instilled last night, stopped me midstep. Teela, arm linked with mine, tripped and nearly face-planted. I apologized quickly, helped her up, and hurried along. My heart pounded with every step and I found myself even more lost in thought.

Every street we turned down led to even more bustling expanse, and I soon grew dizzy at the sheer size of everything. Fortunately, Joselia knew exactly how to reach the city Watch. She led us through busy roads until we came to a broad, two-story building made from gray stone. A fenced-in training yard lay spread out behind it. A dozen men with sweat-shined faces moved within the space, going through drills with swords, shields, and spears. Metal clanged alongside shouts, oaths, and raucous laughter.

The building’s interior was an immediate, welcome reprieve from all the noise. Jareth held the door as we all filed into a vast space set with desks, tables, racks of weapons, barrels, and heavy wooden trunks. Everything was very tidily spaced throughout the room. The far wall held a wide stone hearth with four figures gathered around it.

The first was a burly older man with dark features, thick leathers, and a belted sword. A young red-haired woman sat at a desk nearby. She was bent over, quill in hand, scribbling on a length of unfurled parchment.

An older man with sparse hair and blotchy, tear-streaked cheeks knelt on a pallet of wolf fur before the hearth. He wore a dirty, long tunic, rumpled trousers, and slippers.

Beside him, supine but alert, was a white-haired woman with a narrow jaw and trembling hands. Her nightdress was torn and stained with blood in several places. One arm and leg were tightly wound with bandages. Hints of crimson peeked from beneath the white cloth.

I raised a hand to my mouth, gaze transfixed on the injured woman, as the burly figure straightened.

“Dame, Sir!” He snapped a salute. “Captain Gelmire, head of the Sedgewick Watch. Glad to have you.”

“Under unhappy circumstances,” Joselia answered smoothly, striding to the hearth. Jareth followed at her heels. “Dame Joselia Deron. At your service, captain.” She knelt before the older pair, voice softening, and stretched one hand towards the woman. “And at yours. May I?”

The woman’s gray eyes were watery. “Are you a healer, lady knight?”

“Of urgent battlefield wounds only, your ladyship. But I’d still like to see the damage, get a sense of what I’m up against. I have a light touch, I give you my word.”

The woman extended her injured arm and leg. Joselia didn’t unwind the bandages, but she spent a while testing bones, checking the woman’s pulse, examining her eyes, and feeling her forehead.

“I tended the wounds myself, lady knight,” the man said. He had a thin, rattling voice. “Brother Ilim. My wife and I tend the temple in the city’s south quarter.”

“You seem to be in good health, considering your encounter,” Joselia sat back on her heels, then glanced at the captain. “Which I’m eager to hear about. You’ve taken their statements, sir?”

He nodded. “Aye. All’s been scribed. I thought it best to hold them here, in case… ah.” He swiped one large hand through his hair, wincing at the elderly couple. “Well. I admit it’s a strange tale. I wanted to let someone with more… expertise… in these matters take charge.”

Brother Ilim gave his head a vicious shake. “Nothing was done willingly! On my word! We made no deals, no bargains-!”

“Peace, Brother.” Joselia settled herself near the hearth. “With your leave, captain, I’d like to hear this tale unfettered.”

He gave a curt nod, looking quite relieved at the dismissal, and exited out the back door. The red-haired woman trotted at his heels.

Ilim’s eyes shone in the firelight. Sweat dappled his wrinkled brow as he clutched his wife’s hand. “On my life, please, we made no pacts-”

“Tell us everything, Brother,” the paladin interjected calmly. “I’m not here to cast accusations.”

The cleric’s voice grew thick. “It looked at me. Those eyes, lady knight, those eyes. I didn’t want to obey, I swear on my life-”

“Sir,” her tone sharpened. Not to a pointed edge, but to one of steady command. “Collect yourself. My compatriot and I are here to aid, not to condemn, and I know full well the guile of such monsters. Your fear of us is misplaced.”

He trembled, gaze fixed on his wife, but the statement seemed to instill some measure of calm. “He told me to be still. So I was still. Even when that thing grabbed her.”

Joselia gripped his shoulder. “Take a breath, my friend. Be easy. Start from the beginning, please.”

“Resa… Resa woke me in the night.” Brother Ilid’s chest swelled. He stared into his wife’s face, drinking her in as she returned the look solemnly. “The moon was high, and I could see her face in the glow. She was frightened. She’d heard a noise.”

“What kind of noise?”

“A growl, lady knight,” Resa whispered. “We thought maybe a stray dog had wandered in.”

Her husband nodded. “I lit a candle and we stepped out together, her with the candle and me with my cane. Went out to the temple proper. It’s a large area, with a shrine dedicated to each god and one to the blessed hero. A statue, with a relic from that terrible battle down south. A real shard of the divine sword. And of course it’s very dark in there, so we… we didn’t see… him. Not at first.”

Joselia leaned forwards. “Describe him, please.”

“Like a man, but… off. Tall, pale, with black hair down to his shoulders. Eyes full of stars, and you just… by the saints, I couldn’t look away from them.” Ilid pressed a shaking hand to his head. “He told us to be calm and quiet while he ripped the statue apart, and it was like… like being in a dream. Like a fog in my head. I just stood there!”

Joselia folded both hands across one knee. “It sounds like you were put under a glamour. There’s no shame in falling prey to such dark work. I’ve seen magic of that kind first-hand, and few indeed can resist it.”

Ilid shuddered. “He snapped the stones. Like it was nothing! Then he came over and stared with those awful eyes. I couldn’t look away. He told us to answer his questions honestly, and we did.”

The paladin frowned. “Detail the Fae’s questions, please.”

He spread both hands apart. “He asked if we were runekeepers! Us! Imagine such a thing. He wanted to know if Resa or I had ever performed any magic. Of course we said no, and then he asked how long we’d been working at the temple. Decades, we told him. He asked if we knew of any runekeepers in Sedgewick and we said no, not for years. Then he seemed put out, and asked it all again. Our answers stayed the same, and he just stood there for a while. Lost in thought.”

“Curious. What happened next?”

The man paled. “He… he looked between us for a bit. Then he walked to Resa and… and…” he turned away, looking ill, and my stomach churned.

“He told me to hold onto it,” Resa groaned. Her voice was strained. “The sword piece. Put it right in my hands! Told me to be still, said it would be worse if I struggled, and then this… black, snarling beast appeared!”

“From the shadows,” her husband gasped, clutching his wife’s hand. “It was a shadow, and then it was like a great hound! All in one breath!”

“A leshar.” Joselia was the very picture of calm. “A mindless creature. One whose sole purpose is to obey a master.”

“It bit me,” Resa indicated her leg, where I was certain a pattern of wounds similar to Royce’s lay underneath the bandages. “Took hold of my leg, and of course I screamed and tried to fight it off-“

“Pardon,” Joselia interrupted smoothly, one blonde brow tilting upwards, “Were you not still under the Fae’s spell? Glamoured?”

Ilid shook his head. “We were each, but one at a time. Never both at once, not even when he asked his questions. We took turns answering, and the other stood terrified. When the dog… the leshar, came, he told me not to move. He made me… I just watched.” His voice broke. “I stood there, just stood there, while it hurt her!”

His wife curled one hand against his cheek. Her own voice hardened. “And we’re surely alive because of it. The creature… I don’t even understand what happened. One moment I was in the temple, and the next I was in a garden. The most beautiful place I’ve ever seen. Smells and sights like you wouldn’t… and the stars! Salt, if I hadn’t been so frightened I might’ve…” she gave a helpless grimace, head dipping down. “I got dizzy and very ill. The monster was there. The dog, not the man. It let me go, and of course I ran. Not well, nor quickly, but I made it to a huge white house. I tried to open the doors, but they were locked. Then the dog came after me again, snarling and snapping. I’ve never been so terrified! I thought it would tear me to pieces! It knocked me down, took hold of my arm, and I got dizzy again. Then everything went black.”

Ilid traced the red-stained cloth around his wife’s upper arm. “My story’s a bit different. They vanished- Resa and the leshar. Like all the darkness in the room twisted around them, and then… gone. Just gone. Just her blood on the floor beneath the relic. It fell when she disappeared.”

“Curious.” Jareth paced circles, hands clasped behind his back. “It sounds like this creature had a very specific goal in mind.”

“He stared at it for a while. He looked very angry. I’d been told to be still, to be silent, so I just… I… stood there and wept. Couldn’t make a sound, couldn’t even beg him to bring her back.” He covered his face, shoulders shaking, and my own throat grew thick. After a moment the cleric dried his cheeks and continued, “Finally he just… snapped his fingers. The dog reappeared, dragging Resa with it. She was unconscious. He picked up the relic and walked right out the front door, and a few breaths later my head cleared.”

“A strange tale, indeed.” Joselia seemed lost in thought. Finally, measuring each word as if weighing them for logic, “And his goal was clear, if not the reason behind it. It does sound as though this creature was… attempting an experiment, of sorts. Perhaps he needed a mortal to bring the relic to his realm. That would, of course, indicate that he himself was unable to, and would mean it’s hidden somewhere in the city.”

“Or the outskirts,” Jareth supplied.

She nodded. “Perhaps. Or perhaps he found another means of transportation.”

I felt sick. Sweat dripped down the back of my neck as I looked between the two Thalessians helplessly. Far too aware of the studiously neutral looks Renner, Teela, and Royce were all aiming my way.

He wasn’t bluffing. He wasn’t. I pictured Jareth’s broad smile and Joselia’s creased forehead, each with a bloody strip of gold embedded in them. He meant it. He’d do it. He’d murder them without a thought and then hand me their heads. I bit my tongue so hard I tasted blood.

Ilid wiped his eyes. “There’s one more thing, lady knight. He said something, right at the end. When he picked up the shard.”

Joselia’s fingers tented. “Oh?”

“I barely heard it. Barely even noticed, I was so distraught over poor Resa. But he held the relic up and stared at it for a long while, and he seemed… very confused.”

She frowned. “Curious. And what did he say?”

The cleric spread his hands wide. His answer nearly stopped my heart. “‘Why her?’”
