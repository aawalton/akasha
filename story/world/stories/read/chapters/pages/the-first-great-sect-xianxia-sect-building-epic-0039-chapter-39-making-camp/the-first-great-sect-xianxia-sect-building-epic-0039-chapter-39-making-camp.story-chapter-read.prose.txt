The first day of the journey ended with relief and grumbling in equal measure. Servants were grateful to finally escape the rain, finally, as though half the convoy hadn’t been covered by carriages and carts, unlike Hua and the other riders on horseback who had to make do with travelling cloaks and wide hats.

A scout had found a hill with a flat top that possessed clear sightlines in all eight directions. They were not attacked by bandits on the way up the gentle incline though signs of prior camps were found. Latrines and fire pits already dug. Bones from a large animal loosely buried. Likely travelling merchants reaching the same conclusions for safety.

They made camp as the mist was fading and the sun was setting. Frigid evening winds made it difficult for anything to dry. Camp was laid out efficiently at the direction of Captain Yao while her cousin wasted time arguing with Liu Xin in the rain.

Tents were laid out in an easily expanded grid. Had this been a war party, these neat rows would have extended to the horizon. Her tent was what anchored the pattern, the very centre of the camp with those nearest belonging to her closest underlings, those being Captain Yao, Cousin Weiji, and Liu Xiu. Further out was the general mess area where the servants were already cooking. Beside that were their stores being guarded by a handful of soldiers. A stabling pen was created for the animals who were placidly eating straw and grain. And finally, the soldier’s barracks were neatly aligned to face the direction of the road. In a fuller party, their tents would have been laid out in concentric circles that stretched out across the horizon and, from her father’s stories, a full mobilisation was more a town than anything else.

Her tent was an entire production. Her father’s travelling tent took four attendants an hour to raise. It had shocked her to see it. A great kingfisher was embroidered on the flaps that served as the entryway of the tan tent. Banners were being staked into the ground, joining the pair of great torches already lit and burning away beneath an entrance canopy. The only thing that could make it worse would be a decorative carpet.

Hua left them to do that and went looking for her cousin. He was working logistics with a servant who scurried away the moment he saw Hua.

“Please let me do my job in peace,” Weiji said, watching the servant flee away from them.

“How is your Cultivation coming along? Still at three stars?”

“That hasn’t changed in the hours since you saw me. It couldn’t possibly have changed. Why?”

“Meditate with me.”

“Why does your meditation involve a bucket?”

“In case you have a deviation,” she said with a toothy grin.

“Thanking the Lord for his faith in this unworthy servant,” Weiji replied with an insincere smile.

They made their way through camp, Weiji informing Captain Yao of the general direction they would be heading. The captain nodded. He was at the first star of Qi Gathering and from a vassal clan beside. He knew the importance of meditation. They left camp in quick order with no one following them.

A shimmer of bright green caught Hua’s attention twenty minutes into their exploration of the quiet forest. The persistent drizzle dampened sound and the lightening mist stole some visibility. A green that bright was noteworthy as it was pervasive, but it did not grow thicker in any direction Hua observed.

Weiji made a considering sound as they walked through the haze, tilting his head.

“This way,” he said, veering off to the right.

Hua followed. It was a few minutes more before they found it. A grassy meadow awaited them. Unremarkable but for the shade of green she was seeing. Grass so vibrantly green it looked to be painted. She strolled around it slowly, following the shimmer in the air that was the same colour as the grass. She observed the flows of energy in a way she had never been able to. Potent Qi had coalesced in this clearing drawn from across the forest. Attracted to a spot that was the dead centre of the clearing. The same way water collected down a slope.

“This isn’t a dragon vein but there’s a lot of Qi I can sense beneath us. Far too much of it.”

Hua waited a while for her cousin to add to that. He didn’t.

“How certain are you that this is no dragon vein?” she prompted.

“Completely,” Weiji said with no hesitation despite how cautiously he paced the centre of the meadow, flattening grass that sprung back immediately. He kept clenching and unclenching his fists. “Even if it was just a dragon claw, I’d be able to sense it. This is… there just happens to be a lot of Qi here. It happens sometimes, but never this much. Never this pure. I don’t like it.”

She cracked open an eye and looked at her cousin, evaluating him again. His sensitivity might genuinely be better than hers.

“There happens to be a lot you mislike. Is it pure enough for us to claim it as a future pilgrimage site?”

“Without a doubt.” He nodded. “I’ll mark it down on the map and have it added to the clan’s records of cultivation sites.”

“Good. Then let us Cultivate.”

They sat across from each other on a tarp, the bucket between them. The grass was springy beneath her knees. Weiji sat cross-legged, a stance Hua found uncomfortable. To her left was Qing, lying across the tarp, her head supported by her palm, watching Hua.

“Why do we utilise elemental lightning?” she asked just as he fell into a meditative trance.

Weiji cracked open his eyes. “Because our scripture is the one for Five Thunders. The Thunder Agency wields divine lightning to fulfil their duties, and we wield an imitation borne from the records of their deeds. To saturate our standard meridians with lightning Qi converts them to the Fulmination Meridians and, with them, we can better utilise our Clan techniques without the strain of constant conversion in our bodies. Knowledge that’s widely spread since our Patriarch reached the peak of the Foundation Establishment.” He scowled at her. “There, are you satisfied that I’m not a complete fool?”

“Only partially. If the method is so well known, why are there so few who make it through Qi Gathering?”

“Assuming you have an outward-facing dantian, you run into the reality that Qi is thin, and everyone guards locations with high density. Like this one will be guarded soon. Even if you do find one, Cultivators are vessels still defined by the limits of our flesh. We can only absorb so much and there are many bottlenecks: the thickness of our dantian, the size of our spiritual channels, how easily our meridians take to elemental lightning. In saturating the body with Qi, you create more areas for it to escape. It seeps out as our skin breathes, is lost to blood and urine and ejaculate. Even if you unlock a meridian, you’re faced with constantly trying to stop your Qi leaking back into the world and that takes great control.”

“That’s one of the easiest things to do.”

“Hua, you’re a prodigy. You do with instinct what takes others years to learn. I struggle with it and my advantage is my Qi sense. I know you are perfect, there is no need to force it down my throat every time we talk.”

“Since when do you talk back so much?”

“Since I became too valuable for you to dismiss. Now, stop distracting me, and breathe.”

Hua closed her eyes and opened herself to the world as she breathed.

A barrier covered every Cultivator in their own Qi. A border that was skin deep and defined the space between the inner world and the outer world, between Jing, the essence of the outer world, and Qi, the essence of the inner world. Hua allowed hers to loosen. Fade away as her inner self melded with the world.

The world’s essence was myriad and multifaceted. There was rarely a singular type of essence that could be easily absorbed. The mountain her clan controlled was filled with magnetic stone and naturally produced slightly more lightning qi than everywhere else. Here, in the middle of the forest, wood was dominant, yes, but most of it was a medley of different aspects. Water and earth melding together. Vital energy from the colony of ants waging a war beneath them. Roots absorbing water and food from the soil to feed the rest of the tree. The distant light of the stars upon the world. She and Weiji, mirroring the canopy of the cosmos, constellations upon the earth.

When she felt at one with the rich medley of external essence, Hua set her dantian to rotate. Slowly at first. It took more effort than usual when there was a layer of Earth Qi at her base. It was like trying to sail over sandy shallows. You risked being beached. The process took more than double the effort Hua remembered it needing just to force the initial rotation.

Rotation created suction. Qi tended to move from areas of high concentration to lower ones. Artificially creating that effect by spinning the dantian and forcing an absence to exist where there had just been stationary Qi. But Hua was good at the process. Spinning one droplet of Qi, leaving an absence, created a relative difference in density.

It was a foundational technique and a double-edged sword. As Weiji said, Qi had the inclination to leave the body if one couldn’t control it. If the rotational speed wasn’t constant, the relative absence might occur outside her body, and Qi would seep away and join the external world.

When you had many absences from the rotational effect of spinning a dantian, it made it easier to breathe in Qi. To pull on that external essence and then to strip it of its desire to leave. Hua did this by converting raw Qi to lightning. Her body had been designed by generations of work to conduct lightning Qi.

And yet, today it was supremely easy to breathe in Qi. If all she had known was the mist spray, now she had a full glass to gulp down. It filled her spiritual channels, building and building until she had something like a great mass of water clogging her spiritual channels. Carefully, Hua thickened the barrier separating her from the world, just enough that her Qi wouldn’t flee her body. Pulled that new medley of watery essence to her dantian and there, she applied the force of her Qi to strip away layer after layer of the external essence. Some of it escaped, that couldn’t be helped, but what did not escape, she churned into sparks of lightning.

She layered those sparks within her dantian, allowing them to cling to the walls. Only a small amount could be kept, but relative to what she was used to, it was a deep amount. The dantian became more unstable as Qi was forced into it. It took time to integrate. But the rest would not go to waste as they could be used on her meridians.

Time to face the abyss.

Sending Qi through her first standard meridian was a simple enough thing to do. The lightning she pushed through it topped off the fraction that had been lost exposing herself to the outside world. Weiji was wrong. Even Hua wasn’t perfect; the dead boy in the city was more talented than her. She too lost Qi. Maybe not enough to lose her Cultivation but enough to require maintenance.

She found no issue with the second and third. The fourth was particularly degraded but the fifth was filled to the brim. Balancing both took little effort. The difficulty lay in being aware of it.

Her sixth meridian, unlocked when divine lightning struck her, repelled the elemental Qi she was pushing forward. Unlike the others, it was truly saturated, with not a hint of loss to it at all, and could accept no more. The excess had no choice but to squeeze past, straining her spiritual channel painfully. And then, it just vanished beyond her senses.

No, not vanished. It was just a greater void than she had ever dealt with.

She was afraid to reach past. From the sixth to the seventh, that gaping emptiness beckoned. And Hua did not know what would happen if she plunged into the darkness.

Careful. You know it’s there. Just stay calm and fill it with a bit of Qi. You’ve done this before. This is light work for you.

“Slow down,” Weiji murmured, pulling her slightly from her own trance.

He’d felt that hesitancy in her Qi. Right now, Weiji was at his most exposed to the world, and Hua existed in it, even if she tried to close herself off. But he should not have been able to feel the Qi in her meridians from a distance.

Hua might have cursed him out but she was too distracted by the Qi she was pushing to her seventh meridian. Where her first five had felt like gazing into a well, this felt like she was plummeting into the same darkness that stole Qing. The Qi she had brought forward vanished into a well so deep it seemed to have no end.

But just as with the darkness from the broken world, Hua hit the ground. Her Qi hit a wall and shot back in the wrong direction. It burned. Fire engulfed her meridian, her Qi lighting up. Spreading down her channel. Igniting her Qi. All her Qi.

Hua stopped spinning her Meridian and broke the barrier separating her from the world before a bundle of burning Qi struck her sixth meridian and set off a cascading wildfire inside her. She forged a barrier in her spiritual channel, built a damn in her channels, and gave the hot Qi no choice but to escape.

The difference in density forced her Qi through her skin. Her arm tore apart. Hua shouted. She knew she did. But it was so quickly cut off by her stomach rebelling, her gorge rising so violently it hurt worse than any punch Grandmother ever inflicted upon her.

She tipped forward, grabbing the rim of the bucket. Hua lost the contents of her stomach to the bucket. That, and the vile sludge that had formed in her body.

“Easy, Hua, easy,” Weiji soothed, holding back her hair. Oh, she hadn’t noticed him moving. “Just breathe with me, alright.”

Weiji made it easy to focus on him. His presence was a familiar sort of jittery. It wasn’t solid or grounding, but like a ship, you learnt to adjust for the rolling waves. Her sixth meridian ached from the backsplash of her attempt. But she breathed through the fiery pain, breathed in time with her cousin.

When she was done expelling the putrid Qi along with the contents of her stomach, he pulled her against him, her back to his chest. He had always been big, bigger even than Brother Jiang. She felt it now, his warmth despite the barriers separating them. He was twitchy, nervous, she could tell it in how his fingers aimlessly tapped against her, the foot in front of her thudding against the ground repetitively. The erratic heartbeat that drowned hers.

“I’m fine.”

Her voice cracked. It was barely above a whisper.

“Drink first,” he ordered, forcing a gourd in her hands. She hadn’t noticed him carrying it.

Hua’s first sip she swirled in her mouth and then spat into the bucket. She did with the second and third until the water wasn’t tinged with rotting flesh and ash. It was warm, and yet, more refreshing than the first snowmelt they received each year; that frigid water that set your teeth chattering and tasted only of spring, of life, and of hope for a new year.

Then he held out a hand with fresh persimmon, dried meat, and nuts in wax cloth. Hua didn’t even know how he could fit all of that.

“Do you have an expanding storage pouch in there?”

“Those are myths,” he said softly, without the usual jagged edges. He gestured with his large hand. “Now eat.”

Hua did so with her uninjured hand.

When she was done eating, he pulled back her sleeve. It was a motley mix of purples and greens, deep bruises that had come from the inside, and minor burns banding like a helix across her flesh. Blood wept from her arm, dark sludge dripping down. It smelled terrible after being exposed to something as fragrant as a persimmon.

Weiji sacrificed the wax cloth to wipe her arm down. She felt very strange at the care he was showing. The last time she remembered someone taking care of her, slow and gentle like this, she was maybe nine. The first time she got sent to Old Ren for lightning burns. Her brother had been beside himself with worry, rightfully guilty.

“No pills?”

“Don’t be ridiculous,” he said, tying the bandage around her arm. He had those as well from his never-ending supply of things. “The last thing you want is anything to destabilise your system further.”

“Do you have a full kitchen and hospital in your cloak?”

“Don’t be silly, just an oven and a clinic,” he said steadily despite the fast beat of his heart. There was sweat dripping down his forehead into her hair. And still, he tried to be calm for her. “I learnt to always be prepared because of your brother. He got into so much trouble whenever we left.”

“Brother? He’s always been a stickler for the rules.”

“Only around you. I’m older than you both. I’ve seen you both at your worst,” he said, absently squeezing her arm. “I think this might have been one of those worse moments for you.”

“It was.”

“That was a Qi deviation you just had.”

“Nearly one. Nearly.”

She shivered, thinking of her father. A legend brought low by their own Qi. Was Hua going to be his legacy in all the ways that truly mattered?

“Fine, a near-deviation. You’re a prodigy and prodigies aren’t meant to have deviations. Have you ever had one before?”

“Once.”

“Recently?” Hua did not answer. “Can I check your meridians?”

“Weiji, you don’t ask an unmarried woman to see her meridians.”

“Luckily, I’m asking to see my lord’s meridians,” he countered, squeezing her arm again and again. Hua didn’t say anything about the pain he was causing her. He didn’t even notice he was doing it.

“Fine. Do as you please.”

She let him prod at her wrist with his Qi. His was that lightning bolt you thought you saw on a sunny day, a sheet of clouds that was just brighter than the others for a moment.

Weiji was precise in his inspection, his Qi relaxing. It soothed the jagged feel of her spiritual channels.

“Your sixth meridian is deep. Very deep. This much Qi is… Hua, what exactly did you do? This doesn’t even feel like elemental lightning. There’s very different about it.”

“I just got struck by lightning.”

“That day in the city, you asked me what I saw in the sky. Then when we offered our respects to our ancestors, the dead approached you. During the purification in the city, you were looking at something no one else could see. You with your golden eyes. Will you tell me what happened?”

“You told me not to trust you.”

“And yet you said you did.”

“Would you believe me if I said heaven came after me? Struck me with divine lightning.”

Took Qing from me and left her to haunt me, she thought but did not say of the ghost just watching her curiously. Took all I loved out of malice.

“Yes.”

She startled in his lap. “That easily.”

“Never seen eyes like yours before. I can believe in strange things after all the strangeness we’ve been experiencing. And I know you don’t lie often.”

“It needs more Qi than I’ve Cultivated,” she admitted breathlessly, disbelieving how easily Weiji trusted her. “I made a Trigram between lightning and earth with me in the middle. Divine lightning broke open my sixth Meridian. Filled it with more Qi than I can imagine. And then it left the next one needing more. Lots of it. Maybe all of them. More than I can get just from the cycling we usually do. I might… Weiji, I might be stuck at this stage forever.”

“No, you won’t.”

“You can’t know that,” she said, speaking doubts she hadn’t even known existed. “I used the entire stockpile from Qiqi and Shenhe and it did nothing. Nothing. Didn’t even stop my earlier stars from degrading. Weiji, I need to get stronger. If I don’t advance fast enough, I won’t be able to keep the Elders in check.”

“Weilong, Hua, listen to me. You always find a way. That’s what you do, you keep walking forward without worrying about the rest of us. I know how much work you put in to make it effortless. You survived divine lightning. I know you’ll find a way to make it work.”

“What if you’re wrong?”

“I’m not.”

Beneath the starless sky, Hua found herself believing, if only for a moment.
