Chapter 64

“Their architecture has nothing to do with shelter. The chamber is a sieve. What walks out is what was meant to walk out.” — Codex of the Long Watch, Selection Protocols

The farmhouse smelled of dust and dry stone when Arthur woke. Donna was already up, sharpening the axe with the intense focus of honing a blade for imminent bloodshed.

“How long was I out?” Arthur asked, cracking his back.

“Seven hours. Steve and Sarah both managed nine.” She didn’t look up. “Don’t get used to it.”

“How long did you sleep?”

“Enough. I took a stroll around the perimeter, early morning.”

Sarah was crouched beside Steve in the corner, both palms flat on his forearms, eyes half-closed. Steve was sitting very still, and looking intently. Whatever she was doing didn’t look like healing. It seemed she was reading.

“He’s clear. The channels have recovered well,” she said, and moved across to Donna.

Donna looked at her. “I’m fine.”

“You’re functional. That’s a different word.” Sarah took her wrists and held them. “Hold still.”

The warmth ran through, and Donna’s shoulders eased by a fraction. Sarah gave herself a nod and moved to Arthur next.

“You’re going to walk a Seeder dungeon today. You can’t be just fine. You need to be optimal.”

She ran the warmth through Arthur, and he felt the morning soreness lift out of him in a single clean draw. He had not realised how heavy yesterday had been until it was gone.

“This is better than any energy drink,” he said. “You could make a fortune handing these out.”

“Except that I can’t bottle it,” Sarah said with a smile.

“If she could, you’d be her first dependency case with how you made a habit of getting crushed, skewered, bitten.” Donna ran a piece of leather along the axe edge.

Steve looked up from the corner. “Two doses a day. Plus a top-up post-engagement.”

* * *

The walk into Paola took twenty minutes. Cities since Day Zero had each emptied differently. London panicked. Marseille had settled. Paola had emptied in a hurry, doors open, a bicycle on its side in a courtyard, washing still strung between buildings, but nothing in the chaos suggested flight. People had walked away from this place to somewhere else. Quickly, but not in panic.

“Moved to Azzopardi’s compound,” Donna said. “Or the coast maybe.”

“Probably both.”

The Hypogeum entrance was a stairwell in the side of an unremarkable limestone building. Steel railings around an opening that had been a museum entrance a few weeks ago. The ticket booth was empty, and a printed sign in three languages said the first tour started at eleven.

Steve crouched at the entrance.

“It’s a Neolithic burial complex. Three levels carved into solid limestone with antler picks over fifteen hundred years, if you could believe that. Seven thousand bodies were excavated in the early nineteen hundreds. A third of the skulls showed cranial deformation nobody could fully explain. That’s the textbook account.”

“And the part the textbook left out?”

“We’ll see in a minute.”

Stairs descended into limestone walls washed in a faint blue light. Ether saturation here ran thicker than the surface plateau, the kind he had last felt at temple nodes.

He went first, shield up.

The chamber at the foot of the stairs was small, about five metres by four, and the walls made his step falter.

Niches had been cut into the limestone at varying heights, dozens of them, alcoves no larger than a man’s torso. Most held long bones in parallel rows or skulls stacked low with orbital sockets facing outward.

Above them, the corbelled ceiling rose to a shallow dome, antler-pick striations still legible after fifteen hundred years of carving. Faint red ochre clung in the smoothed sections of wall. Underfoot, the floor crunched with what Arthur did not look down to identify.

Sarah crossed to the lowest niche on the left wall and crouched at it.

“Some of these are children,” she said quietly.

“How can you tell?”

“There’s a pelvic fragment in the deposit.” She pointed, not touching. “The iliac crest hasn’t fused. That happens between fifteen and twenty-five. This one didn’t get there.” Her finger moved. “Juvenile femur next to it. Growth plate still distinct. Maybe nine years old.”

“About seven thousand individuals’ worth of bones were excavated from this place over the early nineteen hundreds. Most disintegrated when the site was opened. Eleven skulls survived in measurable condition and were classified as dolichocephalic. Long-headed,” Steve said, looking around at the niches.

“Archaeologists have always been very firm about what this place is. A Neolithic burial complex of religious nature. Standard funerary practice.”

“Your tone says you think that is BS,” Arthur said.

“Seven thousand bodies, Art. A Neolithic population on a small Mediterranean island, working stone with deer antler, spent fifteen hundred years carving a three-level subterranean structure to put them in. The labour required for it, the population required to support that labour. None of it scales for what we’re told it is. So yeah, BS.”

Donna looked at the niches.

“So what we’re told isn’t what this is.”

“As much as I dislike it, it often is the case with history and archaeology.”

They found a painting on the back wall, in red ochre, faded but legible. Two figures. The taller of them wore a long robe, leading the smaller figure forward by the hand. The smaller figure was also in robes, walking. The two of them faced a spiral.

“A priestess,” Steve said.

“Leading an initiate?” Sarah asked.

“No idea,” Steve said, looking at the spiral.

* * *

A passage opened off the antechamber’s western corner. Arthur moved into it, shield up, and the edge clipped the limestone of the passage roof. A short, hard chime of metal on stone rang out, and a creature dropped from the wall.

It came out of a corbelled niche he hadn’t seen, a horrifying mix of dog and deer, bone-pale and calcified, about a metre long with four legs ending in blunt pads. The triangular face had no eyes and no ears. From either side of the skull, two slim prongs angled forward, vibrating on a frequency Arthur felt rather than heard. Two sharp antlers jutted from the head.

It hit his shield from above. Arthur took the impact on the shield boss and rotated to absorb. As it bounced off the shield, Donna’s axe took it across the spine where the prongs joined the skull. Arthur stomped on the severed neck to be sure.

The body dropped and clattered onto a pile of bones at the base of the corridor wall, prongs still vibrating. The sound rang through the rock.

Before the sound could settle, three more creatures dropped from niches further down the corridor, some five metres ahead. They came towards the party at a sprint, antlers aimed forward.

Arthur planted his feet and triggered Contact Reinforcement. The blue ran out of him into the floor, and his frame locked.

The first reached him a body-length ahead of the others and hit the shield at full velocity. He absorbed the impact on the boss and pinned the creature against the corridor wall, its forelegs crumpling under the angle.

Donna moved past his right shoulder, the axe slashing across the second creature’s throat where it tried to scramble over its companion. A line of red blossomed across its throat. Arthur’s hammer came down on the pinned one’s skull while he moved his shield to intercept the falling body of the second creature.

Arthur shoved the body back, closing the room the third creature had to lunge in, and Steve’s thermal lance dropped it where it stood. This was a thin, controlled line of Ether that exploded the spine outward.

Arthur held Contact Reinforcement for another two seconds, watching the corridor for movement. Then he let the technique go.

Sarah was cataloguing damage. Donna had a shallow cut along the back of her left forearm where a prong had grazed her. Arthur’s shield had two long score marks across the boss where the first impact had landed.

That’s when they saw the fifth creature. It was about fifteen metres further down the corridor. It was moving back and forth, its prongs sweeping the air in long horizontal arcs. It walked five paces left. Stopped, swept, turned, and walked back.

It was hunting and finding nothing.

Donna put her arm up and said in a whisper, “Back. Ten steps back.”

They regrouped at the bottom of the stairs. Steve had been muttering to himself, trying to crystallize whatever theory was forming in his head.

“These things seem to hunt by sound. The first one came when your shield scraped the roof. The other three came when the first went down and clattered into the bones,” Donna said.

“Yes, that’s my theory as well, but then why didn’t the fifth charge us? We made enough noise while killing the three,” Arthur said, brushing bone from the hammer head.

“Yes and no.” Steve had finally reached a conclusion. “Yes, we made enough noise. No, it did not hear us.”

“It has to be the technique. Contact Reinforcement isn’t just hardening you and the floor under your feet. It’s making the substrate dense enough to be acoustically different from the rest of the chamber. Sound generated inside the radius of the technique hits the boundary and reflects back. It doesn’t propagate.” Steve’s hand was speeding up. “These things hunt on substrate vibration.”

“So when you’re channelling, the room can’t hear us.” Donna’s axe was still ready in her hand.

“Yes, but it also means I can’t move. I can hold it for about twelve to fifteen seconds at a time. Maybe four times before my reserves go critical. Less if I take hits.”

“The theory should be easy enough to test,” Donna said, looking ahead. “You trigger the technique, and we create a small noise. If it hears, Steve gives his PhD back. If it doesn’t, you drop the technique, and we create the noise again.”

“Let me remind you that I have two PhDs while you have none.”

“Yes, doctor smartypants, let’s go test your theory,” Donna said, moving into position.

* * *

The party positioned themselves about ten metres from the loitering creature, and Arthur triggered Contact Reinforcement. As his body and the ground around him hardened, Donna threw a small stone at a wall, some three metres from Arthur. The stone pinged off the wall, creating a sharp noise. The creature kept to its back and forth, giving no indication that it had heard anything.

Arthur dropped Contact Reinforcement, and Donna picked up another stone. As this one struck the wall, the creature reacted immediately. The prongs locked onto the wall, and it charged. As it reached the wall, Arthur rushed forward and shield-tackled it into the wall. Donna followed up a second later with an overhead chop on its skull. The chamber fell silent.

Donna scowled at him but didn’t argue.

Sarah, who had kept quiet during the first chamber, finally said, “How can you guys joke in such a situation? My stomach is in knots, and I haven’t even done anything yet.”

“Be in near-fatal situations enough times, and it stops being fatally serious after a while.” Arthur smiled.

“I doubt it,” Sarah said as she crouched near a creature, prying its core out.

“Trust me, there are only so many times you can come close to death before it stops being all dire and glum.”

“Let’s get going. Now we have a way to fight in here that doesn’t draw the room,” Donna said.

Steve snapped his ledger closed and stowed it in his backpack. “Welcome to the new fight,” he said quietly.

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

Hypogeum, Paola

Acoustics

The acoustic principle held under stress, which is what I want to write down before I lose the thread.

Contact Reinforcement densifies the substrate under the channeller’s feet. That part we knew. What we did not know is that the densification produces an impedance boundary with the surrounding rock. Sound generated inside the radius does not cross that boundary. It reflects. For as long as the channel runs, the bubble is acoustically silent to the outside world.

This is consistent with how impedance mismatch works in any acoustic context. Sound moving from one medium into another loses energy proportional to the difference in their acoustic impedance. A loud noise on land barely registers underwater. Densify a column of limestone enough relative to the surrounding limestone, and you get the same effect at smaller scale.

What this implies: the same trick should work in reverse. If we ever need to broadcast through rock, to draw something out, to lure a hunter past us, Arthur should be able to anchor a bubble adjacent to where we want the noise heard. The boundary would amplify rather than dampen. I have not tested it. I want to.

Now the part I keep coming back to. The Hypogeum has a documented acoustic anomaly. The Oracle Chamber on Level 2 resonates at approximately 110 hertz, a frequency that has been measured independently by several research teams, and that produces observable neurological effects on people who stand inside it. This is published research, not fringe.

Five thousand years ago, with deer-antler tools, a Neolithic population on a small island carved a subterranean chamber tuned to a frequency with measurable neurological consequences. By accident, allegedly. I do not believe it was an accident. The frequency is too clean. The chamber is too purposeful.
