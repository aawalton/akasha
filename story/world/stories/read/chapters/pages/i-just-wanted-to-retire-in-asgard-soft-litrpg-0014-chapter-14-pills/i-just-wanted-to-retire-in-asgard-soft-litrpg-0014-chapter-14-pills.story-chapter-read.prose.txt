Barry stood in the wreckage of the lab and studied the five of them: proud, mostly, with a little doubt tucked in behind it.

The clones stood in a tidy line, eyes open, awareness arriving by degrees. Shoulders squared, hands loose, heads tipped slightly forward. They had Bela's pale undertone, her sharp jaw, the same set to the eyes. Just none of the sad parts.

He tapped one on the forehead. Faint ticking answered from beneath the skin at their temples and along their collarbones, a soft mechanical pulse that kept time with no heartbeat.

"Standing orders." His voice was flat, the voice he used when he was already halfway out the door. "Conquer this planet and rule it through the plans I've put in place. Then start answering prayers by priority matrix. Keep the world stable. Don't escalate. Don't drown in sin. And above all, don't come looking for me. If something breaks and you can't fix it without expanding the system, contain it and wait. That's all."

They didn't salute. They didn't even nod. They took in his orders and went back to scanning inward. The system was live now: raw prayer traffic, priority tags, emotional-weight scores, all of it pouring into them in real time. He could hear the ticking steady as they locked onto the feed from the town above.

He crossed to the medical bay, where Bela still lay. The restraints were off. Her breathing was even, her vitals steady on the monitor. He checked the numbers once and straightened. Twenty minutes, tops, before she woke. The gun he'd held to her temple sat on the side table, unloaded, next to the empty vial. He left both there.

"System."

[Yes, ready for orders.]

"I just finished a task. Aren't you going to reward me? Points, maybe? A level-up?" His hands were already on the remote he'd used to hack the system.

[No. No such code or data is available.]

Barry smiled and set the remote down. He glanced around. No Thor. No Loki. Good. Whatever game those two had been playing, they'd pulled their pieces off the board for now. His eyes went back to Bela.

"If only you were less like me..." he said. He pulled the portal gun from his coat, dialed the coordinates without looking, and fired. A blue oval of light cut the air open. Warm air spilled through from the other side, carrying the sharp smell of his distilled chemicals and, underneath it, alcohol. The dwarves, probably.

"Mission complete."

He stepped through before the weight of what he'd built could catch up with him.

The portal collapsed. Silence. The lab doors locked with a heavy magnetic thud, and behind them the clones began to move, glancing at one another, blinking.

"So, should we start?" the one in the middle asked.

"Let's start with... wearing some clothes," another said.

Bela woke slowly. The first wrong thing was the surface under her: soft. The lab had never had a bed like this. Warm air, clean sheets, in a facility that smelled of coolant and sterilized metal. The light was low and even, nothing like the harsh white arrays she remembered. And her head felt clear, as if whatever she'd been carrying had simply lifted, leaving every sensation sharp.

"Barry?" She scrubbed at her eyes. When she opened them, she flinched back. "Who... what?"

Five figures stood in a loose half-circle around the bed, still and attentive. Their simple dark clothes fit as though they'd been measured at the exact moment of activation.

Their faces were variations on her own, same bones, same eyes, but the expressions sat differently on each. One leaned slightly forward. Another let his hands hang loose, fingers flexing now and then as if testing their range. A third stood perfectly square with his gaze fixed on her, which Bela found more than a little creepy.

The last thing she remembered was Barry's gun against her temple. The cold circle of the muzzle. His flat voice explaining the compound already in her blood. She scrambled back until her shoulders hit the wall, fingers clamped on the edge of the mattress hard enough to hurt, pulse hammering in her throat.

The clones didn't advance. They spoke, five voices overlapping just enough to sound like one.

"Mom."

It wasn't robotic. It was gentle, even sincere, and still inhuman in a dozen small ways.

"Mom?" The fear didn't drop. It went colder. She couldn't say why, but these things already loved her. She could see it in the set of their shoulders, in the way their eyes followed every small shift in her face, waiting for an answer she had no idea how to give.

"Okay," she said, looking around for any trace of him. "Where's Barry?"

Silence. The one nearest the foot of the bed shifted his weight, as if testing whether stepping closer would help or make things worse, then seemed to decide against it.

"I'm guessing you're the clones? Then you must know where he is, right?"

A life-sized hologram of Barry flickered into being at the foot of the bed. A tray appeared beside it that hadn't been there a moment earlier, plain metal, holding two pills, one blue and one red, identical in size.

"I deliberately seeded emotional and relational data into them," the hologram said, dry and almost gentle, the way only Barry could manage. "I imagine that was already clear. They recognize you as their mother. Don't worry. It wasn't an accident. It was a design choice."

Again Bela listened for empathy in his tone, and again she couldn't find any. Just facts and science. The hologram held perfectly steady, no flicker, no distortion. Same coat he'd worn when he left. Same expression he used when the explanation was finished and he was only confirming parameters.

"You're probably wondering about the pills. Hang tight and listen. The blue one triggers the residual compound I injected into your blood. Everything since the day you met me gets erased. The intelligence, the empathy, what you now understand about the prayer network and what it costs. Gone. You wake up ordinary, a simple frost giantess, the way I found you. Safe."

A half-second pause. His eyes stayed on hers.

"The red pill keeps all of it. It raises your intelligence further, preserves the EQ I gave you, and hands you full administrative control over the five of them and the network they run. You become the permanent interface between the people and the thing that answers them now."

Then, quieter, almost soft: "It's the most ethical thing I can do for you, so it's your call. And don't search for me. I'm already gone."

The hologram lingered on his face a beat too long, then vanished. The pills stayed. The room went quiet again.

"Ha! Piece of shit, right to the end." Bela scoffed, but her eyes were going wet as she stared at the pills. "I wanted to say it to his face."

The clones didn't try to fill the silence. They watched, patient and filial, and let her sit inside the quiet they'd been built to give her. One lifted a hand as if to pat her shoulder, then stopped, still working out the world in front of him, the only world he had.

She looked at their faces, her own jaw and her own eyes on five towering men. She looked at the crystals, the data scrolling across the screens, the tools Barry had left behind. With all of it at her fingertips, she could do anything, be anything.

The smartest person in the realm. That was his parting gift, in his own cryptic way.

But he'd been right. Being this smart didn't feel like a gift. It felt like a floor that kept dropping out from under her. Every new layer of understanding opened three more problems she could no longer ignore, and her heart, her empathy, only made it worse.

One of the clones couldn't hold back any longer. He stepped closer, head tilted. "Mom, you look distressed. We can wait, you know. There's no time pressure on this decision."

"But we have orders to stabilize this planet," another added, already half turned toward a readout only he could see.

He tapped his temple, and the status reports bloomed into view like a hologram, all the data Barry and Bela had fed in, laid out in the air.

"I see," she muttered. The affection under all that flat language was real, or as real as anything built to feel it could be. That made it worse, not better. They would keep reporting. They would keep watching. They would keep calling her Mom whether she answered or not.

She looked down at the tray. Blue on the left. Red on the right.

Blue took everything back: the intelligence, the empathy that turned every prayer into something with physical weight, the knowledge of how the machine actually measured faith and rationed miracles to keep a town from tearing itself apart. She'd wake up small again. Barry would be a stranger. So would these five. The network would fade into noise she never had to hear, and the gun at her temple would shrink to a half-remembered nightmare with no context. She'd walk out of this room and into a life measured in ordinary limits.

Red locked her in. Full control, higher intelligence, the EQ still lit. She'd decide which prayers rose and which sank. In some ways she'd be a goddess behind the scenes, with no off switch.

She couldn't stop thinking about the town above, about both kinds of faces at once: the ones who still prayed because it worked, and the ones who'd stopped because it never had. She didn't let herself linger. She already knew both by heart, and that was the whole problem. Knowing them this clearly made the choice heavier, not clearer.

Her hand moved before she'd fully decided to let it.

The clones tracked it and said nothing. None reached for her. None leaned in. Five pairs of eyes were fixed on the tray and on her fingers hovering over it.

Her hand drifted slowly toward the blue.
