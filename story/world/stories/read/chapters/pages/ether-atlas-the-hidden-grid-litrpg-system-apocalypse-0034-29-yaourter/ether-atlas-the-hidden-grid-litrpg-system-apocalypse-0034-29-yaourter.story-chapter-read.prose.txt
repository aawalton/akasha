Chapter 29

"The Watchers of Massalia maintained the southern conduit for twenty-three centuries. They did not know where it connected. They did not need to know. The infrastructure requires maintenance, not comprehension." - Codex of the Long Watch, Field Observation, 3rd Century BC, Massalia

The dark spat the party out onto warm stone, and Arthur's first thought was that the air tasted dodgy.

The metallic edge of Ether saturation was the same, had been since the pub, but beneath it was salt and brine and impossibly lavender.

His ears were ringing and his stomach was trying to decide whether it still belonged inside his body. The transit had been exactly what Archie had described and nothing like what Arthur had expected. The experience was of having his body squeezed into a space that had no interest in accommodating him and then released.

He pressed his palms flat against the floor and waited for the room to stop spinning.

Donna on the other hand was already on her feet. Because apparently high Dexterity made portal travel easier or some other such malarky that Arthur had no interest in considering while he was trying very hard not to throw up.

Arthur heard her boots scrape on stone a few metres to his left and then the soft rasp of the axe leaving its loop. She'd landed, assessed, armed, and oriented herself in the time it had taken him to remember which direction was vertical. Damn her.

"Chamber," she said, her voice flat. "Circular. Forty metres across, maybe forty-five. Two passages, one left, one right. No movement. Almost identical to where we came from."

Arthur got his feet under him and stood up gingerly.

Steve meanwhile was still on the ground, lying on his back with his arms spread, staring at the ceiling with a shocked expression on his face. His mouth moved but no sound came out. After a while he tried again.

"We just teleported," he said in awe.

"Steve." Donna said without looking at him. She was watching the two passages. "Get up. We don’t know what’s out there."

"I'm processing."

"Well, process vertically."

Steve mumbled and then reluctantly got up.

“Pretty sure Archie called it a transition, not teleportation,” Arthur said, turning to Archie for confirmation.

The geometry was there, hovering at the edge of the platform, the familiar lattice of interlocking shapes that had been their constant companion since the London stone. But the light that usually pulsed through the structure in response to their proximity had faded to a faint, static glow, and there was no rotation or processing changes to his geometrical change.

"Archie," Arthur said.

Nothing.

"Archie. Status."

Silence.

Steve stared. "He's never not responded. Not once. Even when he's withholding, he acknowledges the question."

"Archie." Donna's voice was harder. "Can you hear us?"

The dim lattice pulsed faintly. Then settled back to its static glow.

"He's there," Arthur said. "He's just not....available."

"Did the transit break him?" Steve asked.

Nobody had an answer. Archie drifted beside them like a lantern with the wick turned down. Arthur realised that he was feeling a sense of loss. The alien construct was probably not a friend, but he had become a companion. He didn’t know what had happened but knew that he couldn't fix it. The only thing he could do was to keep moving forward.

"We need to figure out where we are. He's been about longer than civilisation. One bumpy ride isn't going to finish him off. He'll be back. In the meantime, we can't stand about here."

***

The chamber was similar to the one beneath Temple Church and yet entirely different.

The shape was similar. Circular, domed, made from the same seamless Seeder stone, and with the same concentric rings on the platform they'd arrived on.

But the stone around the original work was different. Arthur ran his hand along the wall and felt the texture change three times in the space of a metre. Seeder engineering at the base, smooth and machine-precise. Above it was rougher work, hand-cut and careful but still rough. And above that, the work was cruder still, medieval repair work patching a gap where the centuries had found a weakness.

Three civilisations had touched this wall. The Seeders built it. Someone maintained it. Someone else repaired the maintenance.

Steve had reached the wall too. The transit nausea had burned off and his brain was running at full capacity, which meant he was talking.

"The substrate is Seeder obviously. Sub-millimetre precision, no tool marks, material I can't identify. But this middle layer-" he tapped the Hellenistic stonework, "-this is Greek. Second or third century BC. Someone was maintaining Seeder infrastructure during the classical period. And this top layer-" he ran his fingers across the cruder repair work and frowning, "-is much later. I can't place the tradition. The style isn't Roman or Greek. Somebody else, centuries after was patching what the Greek builders left."

He looked at Arthur.

"Three layers and three different eras of maintenance. The Curators weren’t just maintaining the Grid in London. They must be maintaining it everywhere. Different agents, different centuries, same mission. Can you imagine the scope?”

"Steve," Donna said from the mouth of the left-hand passage. "History lesson later. Where are we?"

Steve sniffed the air. "You can smell the brine, surely. There is salt in the air and warm limestone above us. So somewhere coastal, and somewhere warm, warmer than London at least."

He looked at the Hellenistic masonry again. "Greek maintenance layer puts us somewhere in the Greek cultural sphere but that's a lot of coastline. Southern France, Italy, North Africa, Greece itself, Turkey." He spread his hands. "I can't narrow it further from down here. We need to get to the surface."

"We could be anywhere," Donna said, shaking her head. “Fifteen minutes ago we were in London. Now we're somewhere on the bloody Mediterranean.

"The Templars deposited money in London and withdrew it in Jerusalem. We just deposited ourselves in London and withdrew somewhere."

They turned away from the wall and stopped short. Hanging above the platform in the same glowing blue letters that they had become familiar with another riddle was waiting.

Steve made a sound that Arthur had previously associated with dogs seeing squirrels.

"Don't touch it," Donna said.

"I'm not going to touch it. I'm going to read it." Steve dropped to his knees at the platform edge, his face centimetres from the letters. "This is, this …. It's Koine Greek. Common Greek, the lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean from about the third century BC. The same Greek the New Testament was written in. And the works of-"

"Steve. Read it."

After what felt like twenty minutes of Steve slowly reading, then shaking his head to correct himself, then reading again, he finally sat back.

Leave the Lion's Port for the soldier's stone,

to the deep-set crypts, silent and alone.

Under the hand of the Lady in Black,

Touch the anchor where the shadows fall back.

He looked up at Arthur. "'Lion's Port' is probably the name of a place and we need to go from there to a soldier's tomb. A crypt or something resembling one, probably underground. The 'Lady in Black' could be a statue, a painting, a funerary marker."

"Can you solve it?" Arthur asked.

Steve chewed his lip. "Not from here. Anywhere with a Greek past and a harbour, which narrows it down to rather a lot of places."

***

They took the left passage and climbed upward.

Arthur went first with his shield up. Donna behind him, axe in her right hand and the pry bar in her left. Steve at the rear with the Reacher, still muttering the riddle under his breath in a loop that would have been annoying if Arthur hadn't known it was how his brain processed things.

The passage climbed in a gentle spiral as the walls transitioned from Seeder stone to rough-cut limestone to what looked like natural cave rock. The air was considerably warmer and the salt smell had intensified. The exit when it came, was nothing but a crack in a rock face. A fissure, narrow enough that Arthur had to turn sideways with the shield flat against his chest. Steve got his pack caught. "Bugger," he whispered.

And then they were outside.

The first thing they saw was a harbour below them; it was a long rectangle of water cut into the heart of a city, walled in by wide paved quays and packed from end to end with boats. At the far end, where the city began, a broad road ran straight inland between tall buildings, and over the head of the quay hung a vast flat canopy of polished steel, holding a mirror image of the violet sky above.

Apartment blocks in cream and honey, which would have picturesque under different circumstance looked ominous instead with shuttered and boarded door and windows. To the south, above the rooftops, a church sat on the highest hill in sight with a gold figure on its tower catching the light.

Smoke rose from a dozen points across the hillsides. Cook fires rose from different points below them, the evidence of a city that had lost its infrastructure but not its people.

The smell was overwhelming. Fish, brine and lavender from the hillsides. Arthur stood very still taking it all in. He was somewhere. Somewhere that wasn't London, somewhere warm, somewhere with a harbour and ochre buildings and twenty minutes ago he'd been standing on the Thames. His brain was finding it difficult to build a bridge across that distance.

Then the engineer part of him took over, when nothing makes sense, look at the infrastructure. The harbour buildings were limestone, not brick and the walls were thicker than London construction, the window were narrower, the rooflines were lower.

Limestone was compressive but brittle under lateral stress. These buildings would flex in an earthquake where London brick would crack, but a hard enough impact would shatter them where brick might hold. Different failure modes and different structural philosophies inspired by different geographies.

"Marseille," Steve said behind him. His voice had changed, the speculative uncertainty from the chamber below replaced by the certainty of a man recognising something "That's the Vieux-Port. The Old Port. We’re looking at a harbour the Phocaeans founded in 600 BC." He grabbed Arthur's shoulder. "We're in Marseille. We're in southern France."

“Could be worse. Could've been Croydon.”

Steve went quiet as his eyes looked at the various landmarks and Arthur could almost hear the riddle turning behind his eyes, the new data being fed into the machine that was Steve’s brain.

"Soldier's stone," Steve said slowly "Could be a soldier buried in Marseille. There are several Roman martyrs associated with the city, but the most significant—"

He stopped speaking while his face went through a whole gamut of expressions. "There's a Saint Victor. Roman soldier, third century,” he said finally, “He was martyred here. There's an abbey built over his tomb, on the south side of the harbour. That could be the soldier's stone…..could be."

"Or could be someone else entirely." Donna said.

"Well, the deep-set crypts fits. The abbey has a crypt. A big one, built over a Hellenistic necropolis." He was chewing his lip, wanting to commit but not trusting himself yet. "The Lion's Port, though, I don't know what that refers to. And the Lady in Black could be a dozen things. I need more context…..someone local to talk to or find a reference I can check."

"I'm seventy per cent on Saint-Victor. I need the other thirty before we walk into a crypt in a foreign city based on my academic French and a hunch."

Donna half turned looking at the quayside below them, where figures were moving along a line of fishing boats hauled up out of the water onto the quay

"People," she said. "Armed and organised."

Arthur followed her gaze, where he could see six, no, seven people on the quay, spaced in a loose cordon that covered the approaches from the water's edge. Most carried tools repurposed as weapons. Gaff hooks, boat poles, a fire axe. One even had a crossbow.

They weren't hiding and it didn’t look like they wanted to ambush them. They had positioned themselves deliberately to limit access to the quay.

Donna's hand tightened on her axe. "Someone trained them. The spacing is deliberate. Overlapping sight lines. Those are not survivor’s huddling……I bet that's a patrol."

A woman stepped forward from the group. The gaff hook she carried was three feet of steel with a curved blade at the end, and she held it with the comfort of someone who'd been carrying it long before the world ended. Her face was weathered and watchful and if she was surprised by three strangers emerging from a crack in a cliff face, she didn’t show it.

She called out. "Oh! Vous, là!"

Steve stepped forward. Cleared his throat. And delivered the worst French Arthur had ever heard from a man with a doctorate.

The woman stared at him.

Steve tried again, slower this time, with hand gestures that suggested he was either introducing himself or attempting to describe a particularly complex fish.

The woman turned to the man beside her and said something that made him snort.

“What did she say?” Arthur asked.

Steve’s face was rigid. “She said my French sounds like a priest reading a ransom note.”

Steve looked back at Arthur and Donna. "She wants to know where we came from. Apparently, there's no road from the east that isn't blocked."

"That's what she said?" Donna asked.

"That's the substance. The actual phrasing was closer to 'How the hell did you get here, and why do you smell like a cave.'"

The woman called again and her tone was sharper this time. "Alors?"

Arthur slung his shield onto his back, raised both hands palm-out, and started walking towards the quay.

"Smile," he said over his shoulder. "We're diplomats now."

Donna did not smile. But she lowered her axe.

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARRMarseille,THE RITUAL CHECKSUM

Archaeologists have a word for repeated human behaviour when the original purpose cannot be recovered. Ritual.

Sometimes that is the correct answer. Sometimes it is the academically respectable way of writing: They kept doing this and we do not know why.

The repaired wall beneath Marseille has made that word considerably more dangerous.

A technical manual is fragile. It survives only while someone can read its language, understand its terminology and recognise the machine it describes. Remove any one of those things and the instructions become useless.

Ritual on the other hand survives differently.

Do not move this stone. Keep this chamber dry. Repair any crack before winter. Walk the boundary in the same direction. Open the door only on the appointed day. Teach the sequence to the next keeper exactly as it was taught to you.

The explanation can change completely while the action remains intact. Engineering becomes obligation. A safety restriction becomes taboo. Scheduled inspection becomes ceremony. The machine is forgotten, but the maintenance continues.

Ritual is a checksum.

Exact repetition makes mistakes visible. Sacred authority discourages improvisation. A child can inherit a procedure whose technical vocabulary died ten generations before they were born. For infrastructure expected to survive thirty-five thousand years, that may be more reliable than written instructions.

The Greek masons may (and it is a big may) have understood what they were repairing. The people who patched their work centuries later may have known only that the wall must not be allowed to fail.

The Curators would not have needed an unbroken line of engineers. They needed stewards. Priests, masons, families, military orders. Each generation could supply its own explanation, provided it preserved the correct behaviour.

I have written the phrase ritual function unknown more times than I can count. I used it whenever the evidence showed careful repetition without an obvious practical reason or result, and I treated the absence of an explanation as evidence that no practical explanation existed.

Looking back at it now, it feels less like professional caution and more like failure of imagination.

How many ceremonies have we catalogued without asking whether something beneath them was being inspected? How many sacred boundaries follow buried infrastructure? How many pointless traditions only looked so because the thing they protected remained asleep?

Not every ritual is a maintenance protocol. Obviously. But I am no longer comfortable assuming that none of them are.

✎ Ritual may not be the opposite of technology. It may be the form technology takes when the explanation is allowed to die but the task is not.
