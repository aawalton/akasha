The meeting room of the ship felt smaller now.

It was the same room. The same oak table with the copper spiral inlay. The same brass-rimmed portholes. The same four cups of water that Pelka had refilled twice without anyone noticing. But the air inside it had thickened, somehow, the way a room thickens after a funeral, when nobody quite wants to be the first to leave.

Aiven sat with his hands folded on the table.

Virelle sat beside him, very still. Pelka had taken the seat across from them, her clipboard hugged against her chest. Vane stood by the door, where he had stood through most of the morning, silent as a column.

Aiven let out a long breath.

“One week,” he said.

One week.

That was the window Lucien had given him. Seven mornings. Seven evenings. And then the man would come back, and ask the same question, and expect a different answer.

Aiven’s answer was not going to change.

Which meant a fight. He was sure of that part. Lucien had said it himself, in the Fold over a cup of tea, said it the way a man might mention the weather. He could not allow a power like Aiven’s to exist outside his equation. He would not tolerate insubordination. The choice was partnership or removal, and the removal would not be merciful.

There was something else, too, something Aiven turned over slowly like a smooth stone.

The window itself was an act of confidence.

A week was a generous span of time. A week was the kind of time you offered an opponent you did not consider dangerous. If Lucien had thought Aiven might actually become a threat in seven days, he would have come back tomorrow. Or tonight. Or never left at all.

He had given the week because he was certain. Certain that even with the seal undone, even with the Loom-Breaker spent, even with the Starweave at his side, Aiven could not become enough in seven days to matter.

He was probably right.

But Aiven did not have the luxury of agreeing with him.

Because this was no longer about the world.

It was about Lyra.

Lyra, who was alive. Lyra, who had walked into the crater on stiff legs and not looked back. Lyra, who had knelt in the grass and said yes, Master, in a voice he had never heard from her before, in a voice that did not belong to the girl who used to laugh in the back of her parents’ shop.

She was alive, and she was suffering, and she was inside Lucien’s reach.

The questions could wait. How she had survived Hearthport. How she had become Lucien’s…person. How, in only a month, she had learned to place seals on a power even she had not understood. All of those questions could wait. All of them.

What could not wait was getting her out.

In one week, he needed to be strong enough to take her back.

One week to learn an unprecedented power he had only just unsealed.

One week.

And yet.

Something pulled at the edges of his resolve.

Would Lyra still be safe in seven days?

He did not know. He had no way to know. Lucien had asked her if she understood what was in store for her when they returned, and she had said yes, in that small voice, the way a person says yes to a thing they cannot avoid.

Should he have risked it all before Lucien took her? Should he have fired a bolt, a flare, anything at all? Should he have torn the regulator open right there in the clearing and taken his chances?

He had been holding the stirring of his own mana, had been one breath from doing it, and Virelle’s hand had closed around his wrist and stopped him.

It had been the right call.

It had been the right call, it was what Lyra wanted, and the regret still stung anyway.

It was the same trick his mind always pulled. He would resolve to live without regret, and then his mind would turn around at the next crossroads and tell him, very calmly, that he had picked the wrong path again. The clerk in him kept a ledger. The ledger never balanced.

He pressed the heel of his hand against his temple.

Lucien would not discard Lyra. He told himself this carefully. If she was on his team, it was because Lucien needed her for something, and a man like Lucien did not throw away tools that were still useful. She would be alive in a week. She would be alive.

Some quieter part of him knew that this was a thing he was telling himself in order to keep functioning, and not a thing he had any actual evidence for.

He let the thought finish, and then he set it down.

There was no more time for thinking. He had spent the entire morning thinking. He had spent the past month thinking, in some sense, every time the world had handed him a new horror and asked him what he was going to do about it. Thinking was a clerk’s reflex, and thinking was useful right up until the moment it became a substitute for action.

Now was the time to act.

He lifted his head.

“Pelka.”

She straightened so fast her clipboard nearly went over her shoulder. “Y-yes, Sir Aiven?”

“I need to stay here. For the full week.”

Pelka blinked. “Here, sir? On this island?”

“Here,” Aiven said. “I need to train. I need open space and somewhere I can fire mana without flattening half a city block, and the islet outside the porthole will do for that. The living quarters on this ship are small but they are enough. I just need rations sent. Water. Whatever else makes a week practical.”

He paused.

“Will you ask Cyria for me. Tell her what we need. Tell her why.”

Pelka had already begun scribbling.

“I can certainly file the request, sir,” she said, her voice settling into the brisk register she always reached for when there was an actual task to perform. “It will require Lady Cyria’s personal sign-off, of course. Resource allocation outside Vulpine’s perimeter is not something I can authorize on my own. But I can have a courier on the wing within the hour.”

“Good,” Aiven said. “Tell her exactly what happened. The whole conversation. She should approve. It is in Vulpine’s interest as much as ours.”

Pelka nodded again, several times, her bangs flopping. She was already half out of her chair. Vane followed her.

He turned his head.

Virelle was sitting where she had been sitting all morning. Her hands were folded in her lap. Her prismatic orb hovered close to her shoulder, dim and quiet. Her eyes were fixed on a point somewhere on the table that did not have anything in particular on it.

She had been quiet.

He was noticing that, now. Noticing the way the noticing arrived late, in pieces, the way important things sometimes did. She had been quiet in the meeting room when Lyra had first explained about the seals and Lucien. She had been quiet in the clearing while Lucien spoke. She had been quiet on the walk back up the ramp, and quiet through the morning, and quiet now.

Not the kind of quiet that meant she was sulking. He knew what Virelle’s sulking sounded like. Virelle’s sulking sounded like a stage actress projecting to the back row of an empty theatre.

This was a different quiet.

He thought he might know why.

He thought he might need to address it, at some point soon.

But not yet. Not before he had even lifted a finger to begin keeping the promise he had just made to himself.

“Virelle,” he said softly.

Her eyes lifted to his.

“I need you,” he said. “For this week. I need you to help me become stronger. I cannot do this without you.”

She looked at him for a long moment.

Whatever she had been holding inside her loosened, just slightly, just enough that the corner of her mouth lifted. It was a small smile. Not the bright, theatrical thing she wore when she was performing herself. A smaller, more honest version.

“Yes, Master,” she said. “Of course. I will always be by your side.”

Aiven let out the breath he had not known he was holding.

The following morning, Cyria had sent her answer.

It came in the form of a smaller scout-class supply ship, painted in the matte ochre of Vulpine’s logistics fleet, which docked alongside their scout ship at first light. Crates of dried rations were unloaded onto the deck. Two casks of fresh water. A medical kit larger than strictly necessary. A small crate of polished mana-stones, the kind used for training drills, with a handwritten note from Cyria tucked into the top.

Aiven unfolded the note.

Train hard. Try not to break the islet. — C.

He almost smiled.

He folded the note and put it in his pocket.

By midmorning, Virelle had set up the islet for him.

She had chosen a stretch of grassy ground a hundred paces from the airship, where the cliff dropped away into a long, empty curve of cloud.

“First,” Virelle said, drifting in front of him, her hands clasped behind her back, “we will revisit the basics. Mana bolt.”

Aiven nodded.

“You will recall the principle,” Virelle continued. “The mana is drawn from your core, not your extremities. The hand merely shapes it. Previously, Master, you failed in conjuring anything, and you were very distraught over it.”

“Thanks, Virelle.”

“You are welcome, Master.”

She raised a finger.

“However. Now there is no longer a wall around your core. There is a valve. A controlled aperture. You will begin small. You will begin with what feels like a candle and we will increase from there. The aim is precision, not destruction. Anyone can break a wall. It takes a craftsman to break the correct brick.”

Aiven raised his palm—the human palm— toward the open sky.

He breathed in.

He felt for the place inside his chest where the white roar lived. The new throttle was easier to find than he expected. It was like locating a faucet by feel in the dark. He turned it, just barely, the smallest possible crack.

Light gathered at his palm.

It was clean, white, and steady. A small bolt, no larger than a marble.

He fired.

WHUMP.

It crossed the sky in a straight, unhurried line and dissipated harmlessly into the cloudbank beyond the cliff. There was no recoil. No headache. No staggering, no nosebleed, no near-fainting.

He stared at his hand.

“Huh,” he said quietly.

“Huh, indeed,” Virelle said. “Again. Open the valve a quarter-turn further.”

He fired.

And again.

And again.

By the fifth attempt, the marble was a peach. By the eighth, the peach was an apple. By the twelfth, the apple was the size of a man's skull, and when it dispersed into the cloudbank, the cloudbank parted, briefly, to show the sun.

He paused.

He looked at his palm.

He thought, distantly, that he should be feeling something.

Exhilaration, perhaps. Or joy. Or some small, private excitement at what was happening between his hand and the sky. Not so long ago, he had been a clerk who could not summon a candle-flame on the tip of his finger without sweating through his shirt. He had spent entire evenings trying to coax a single wavering spark out of his own mana, only to give up and go to bed feeling like the world had been built for a kind of person he was not.

Then even that had been taken from him. The small magical aptitude he had clung to, the sad little flicker that had been the only proof he was a magic user at all, had quietly stopped working one day.

And now, here he stood, on the edge of a cliff above the cloudbank, firing apple-sized bolts of clean white mana into the open air without so much as an incantation.

A younger version of him would have wept. A younger version of him would have laughed and run a victory lap around the islet. A younger version of him would have written to Lyra about it, in long, careful sentences, in the small notebook he used to keep for things he was proud of.

He felt none of that.

He felt the weight of seven days. He felt the small, frantic ledger his mind kept running, the one that tallied up the things he could do today versus the things he would need to do in a week. He felt Lyra kneeling in the grass. He felt Lucien's hand resting briefly on her shoulder.

There was no room in him for delight. There was barely room in him for breath.

He closed his fist. The white light gathered there again.

He fired, and Virelle corrected him.

She was, in every measurable way, an excellent teacher.

And yet.

Something was missing.

Aiven could not have written down what it was. There was no specific gesture absent, no particular line he could point to and say, here, this is where the gap is. It was a quality. A texture of how she usually was around him. The theatrical sniff that should have followed his first wobbly stance. The dramatic eye-roll when his bolt wobbled left. The little remark she always made under her breath, the kind of remark that pretended to be insulting but was really an excuse to remind him she was paying close attention.

All of it was muted today. Trimmed back. Replaced with something quieter and more correct.

It was as if she had built a thin wall of glass between them and was speaking to him politely through it.

He noticed it.

He filed it.

He kept training.

There would be time, later. There had to be time later. He could not afford to look at it too closely now, because if he did, he would have to stop, and stopping was not on the schedule for today.

He fired the next bolt.

The light changed slowly.

By late afternoon, the cloudbank below the cliff had turned the soft orange of a kiln. Aiven’s shoulders ached. His palms were warm. The grass in front of him was scorched in a wide, uneven half-circle from where his stray bolts had dipped low. Sweat had dried on his forehead and dried again.

He could fire half a dozen bolts in succession now without losing his stance.

It was not enough. It was nowhere near enough. But it was a beginning.

Virelle drifted down beside him, her feet not quite touching the grass.

“That is sufficient for today, Master,” she said. “Your core is fatiguing. Pushing further would only entrench bad habits.”

Aiven nodded. He was too tired to argue.

They walked back to the ship together in silence. It was not a comfortable silence. It was not an uncomfortable silence either. It was the silence of two people who were both being very careful not to say a particular thing.

The living quarters were small.

It was a scout-class ship, after all, and the cabins on its lower deck had not been designed with two long-term occupants in mind. Theirs was perhaps four paces across at its widest, with a single round porthole, a small writing desk bolted to the wall, a narrow wardrobe, and one bed.

One bed.

Aiven did not bother asking Virelle if she would prefer a separate cabin.

He knew exactly how she would answer. The performance she would have given at the question alone, the gasp, the hand to the chest, the elaborate pronouncement that of course they would share, that anything else was unthinkable. He could practically write the speech for her.

He thought, briefly, that he would have liked to hear that speech today.

But he still did not ask the question.

Dinner was simple.

Pelka had brought them two trays of warm stew and a half-loaf of dark bread and excused herself the moment they had thanked her. Aiven ate slowly. Virelle ate even more slowly.

Afterward, he had taken a short, hot bath in the cramped lower-deck washroom, and come back to the cabin in a clean shirt and loose trousers, his hair still damp.

The lantern on the writing desk had been lit.

Virelle was at the porthole.

She was standing, not floating. Her hands were resting on the lower curve of the brass rim, and her forehead was almost touching the glass. Her hair caught the lantern light along its edges. Her prismatic orb hovered beside her shoulder, low and slow, the way it always did when she was thinking and did not want to be interrupted.

She was looking out at the dark.

The cloud-cover beyond the cliff had gone deep blue. There was nothing in particular to look at. A few stars above the cloudbank. The faint glow of the islet’s residual mana. The black silhouette of a far ridge.

She had not noticed him come in.

Or she had noticed, and had decided not to turn.

Aiven sat on the edge of the bed.

The mattress was firm. The frame creaked. He rested his elbows on his knees and let his hands hang between them. He looked at her back for a long moment.

The thin glass wall was still there.

He could feel it from across the room.

He drew in a breath.

“Virelle.”

Her shoulders tensed, just slightly. She did not turn.

“Come here for a moment.”

She held still long enough that he thought, briefly, that she might not come at all. Then her shoulders eased, and she drifted from the porthole, slowly, the orb trailing after her, until she was standing in front of him.

She did not sit.

Aiven looked up at her.

“Hey,” he said. His voice was very quiet. “What’s wrong?”
