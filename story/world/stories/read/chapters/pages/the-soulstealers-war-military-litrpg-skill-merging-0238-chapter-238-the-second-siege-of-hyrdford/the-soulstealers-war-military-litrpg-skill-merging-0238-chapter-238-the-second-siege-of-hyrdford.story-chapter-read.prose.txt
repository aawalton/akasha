I was expecting the Hangman to take part in the initial siege. I braced myself for another devastating in the middle of the city. Some kind of mushroom construct, except this time, it would slaughter civilians instead of soldiers.

Nothing came. The orcs continued down the slope, approaching as a wave. As they marched, our own forces gathered behind the gate, forming ranks for when the gate fell, or for if any orcs breached the wall elsewhere.

While they approached, I replaced my shield. It wasn’t my personal shield, but it was better than nothing. And then I waited.

When the orcs entered the range of the trebuchets, Lord Ceonwin gave the signal. The artillerymen lit pottery balls of oil on fire, then flung them into the orc ranks. They seared across the sky, leaving trails of burning smoke in front of Welkinmere. When they crashed to the surface, they left burning streaks in the orc ranks, but the beasts just kept advancing in an orderly formation, unperturbed by the burning and screeching comrade beside them.

“That’s the power of a Black Captain?” I asked. “If they had it their way, they would be rushing to the wall as fast as they could.”

“Indeed, they would,” Lord Ceonwin replied. “But this is no ordinary Black Captain. This is a sorcerer who would be weak in a one-on-one fight, but excels at battle formations and controlling his lesser orcs.”

“Any idea if they’ve got suppression collars?”

“Only a suspicion,” he said.

“Would they have retreated on their own, if they saw our numbers and have such a powerful Black Captain who can compel them like this?”

“Perhaps…” Lord Ceonwin said. “We won’t know until we see the aftermath of the battle.”

I tapped my foot. Soon, the orcs pulled in range of the catapults, and we fired smaller stones at them. The payloads were still heavy enough to take out an ogre in a single hit, though, so I counted it as a win.

But the orcs kept marching. Their footsteps pounded over and over, causing the ground to tremble, even on the stability of the wall. As they approached, I readied my crossbow. I picked the strongest targets I could find. Ogres and trolls were easy, but it was the Black Captains and shamans that we needed to worry about. They wouldn’t be as obvious to the regular archers, and being smaller, they were harder to hit with trebuchets.

As soon as the orcs drew within range of our arrows, we loosed as large of a barrage as we could. They were within two-hundred paces of the wall, and any moment, the Hangman could attack.

There was so much to focus on, but I managed it. I let my field of view go wide, and I let my mind take in everything I could.

And when I spotted a shaman, I took him down. He was pointing his staff at the wall, preparing to blast our men off the ramparts. We couldn’t let him.

I triggered a resonance Skill for good measure, and it was necessary. The shaman raised a shield, but my bolt pierced right through it and nailed him in the forehead. He collapsed on the spot, and his beam of rot died where it began.

“How much Presence do you have left?” Lord Ceonwin asked me. He drew his great warbrand and gave it a whirl.

“About half my supply,” I replied. “I used a lot of it to get our men to safety.”

“Save the rest for the Hangman. Shamans’ shields will fall eventually, and if not, Saeglaf and his apprentice will handle them.”

I bowed my head. “What about that?”

A battering ram approached behind the first wave of orcs. Ogres pulled it, their tusks swaying, beige skin glistening with sweat. It was utilitarian, except for the tip of the ram, which had been carved with spikes. Then again, those probably had a purpose as well—doing more damage to the wall.

“They won’t bring it in range of the wall until the trebuchets are destroyed. When it gets closer, I will use other sappers against it. But we may have to accept that the gate will, eventually, be breached.”

I hated it, but it looked like he was right. The battering ram was holding back as the rest of the orcs drew closer. They moved to surround the wall.

“Where’s the Hangman?” I hissed as I reloaded my crossbow and aimed at another shaman. He already had a shield up to protect himself and the nearby orcs from arrows. A mesh of arcane black mist hung over their heads, protecting them.

“Waiting for his army to cut off our escape. The last thing he wants is for us to flee out the back gate.”

I waited for the shaman’s shield to fall. Arrows fell on him, and he stopped them in their tracks. But, to aim his attack properly, he had to lift his shield. He cancelled the Art, and as he blasted a beam of darkness from his staff, I loosed a bolt and sent it piercing through his forehead—without the help of resonance Skill.

The shaman collapsed on the spot.

“Ladders!” Lord Ceonwin yelled. “They are going for the trebuchets! Do not let them!”

I slung my crossbow over my shoulder and turned my attention to the nearest tower. Saeglaf was already protecting it, so instead, I searched for a tower farther from the gate. Since the orcs had fully enveloped the wall, they could put up ladders wherever.

There was no point on the wall where our defenses were less dense. Since I couldn’t help everywhere, I needed to protect the towers.

I stayed low, holding my spear out, until I found where I was needed the most. As tall ladders ascended up toward the nearest tower, Lord Ceonwin rushed to intercept. He used an Art—a basic pulse of raw energy that swept off his blade, shattering the ladders halfway and sending the orcs plummeting back to the ground.

More ladders ascended toward the tower, but he could keep handling them. The third tower over, however, was open.

I sprinted along the back of the ramparts, standing on the very back ledge, as I approached the tower. The Dupes and militiamen on the wall had left more than enough room for us to run along the wall behind them, if we needed to reposition, however, I as ladders swung up to the lower segment of the wall as well, and the soldiers scrambled to repel the ascending orcs, they spread out, blocking the foot path.

I couldn’t just leave them to fight off the orcs on their own. As I passed, when I could, I pushed into the fray and kicked orcs back, or jabbed with my spear to take out heavy targets. It wasn’t so easy to put ladders up to the regular wall, given its construction, and they had to lean over at an angle because of the wall’s slope, exposing climbers to more arrows as they tried to ascend.

Even better, it made it easier to knock the ladders over. Instead of pushing them all the way back and over, they were at the perfect angle to give a little nudge backward, and the ladder’s base would collapse out from beneath it, like a person slipping on ice. The top of the ladder scratched down the wall with ease.

When I’d first fought orcs, almost two years ago now, they’d had weak, mismatched armour. Today, their armour was far heavier. They wore primitive scale mail, which was just misshapen chunks of metal stacked overtop each other and bound together by wires. Sometimes, they wore heavier plates.

Today, my armour was nowhere near as strong as my regular armour. It gave some general attribute boosts, but nothing near as strong as my regular stuff. Still, with how far my Strength had come, and how durable my spear was, I could stab through most of the basic orc armours without even needing a resonance Skill.

Progress along the wall was slower than I wanted, but I kept the embattled trebuchet tower locked in my vision. The trebuchet kept throwing burning payloads into the incoming stream of orcs.

Despite their earlier formation, most of it had broken up upon reaching the wall. Now, leftover orcs streamed from the camp, rushing to meet us.

After I passed the second tower over, something whistled through the air. “Get down!” I yelled.

But it didn’t do any good. Ahead of me, a stone blasted over the top of the wall, shattering the merlons and flinging debris back into the city. The thatched roof of a house inside the wall collapsed, and people evacuated it.

I grimaced. The inner wall must’ve been completely full if we still had people hiding in the outer wall. We had nowhere to retreat. If the outer wall didn’t hold, the civilian casualties would be terrible.

Pushing through the dust, I continued on toward the tower. Other dupes rushed to fill the hole that the orcs’ siege weapons had made.

I squinted out across the battlefield as I rushed along the top of the wall, but it didn’t take long to spot the orcs’ weapons. They’d created a line of catapults behind their main army, and they launched debris into the city. Some covered rocks in tar and set them aflame and threw them.

When I reached the third trebuchet tower, the one without any sappers or Path-walkers to protect it, I jumped up to the top.

It was about twenty paces wide on either side at the top—a perfect square—and the trebuchet sat in the center. Artillerists operated it, using it to fling rubble and oil-filled burning jugs into the orc ranks. Up at the top, the guards weren’t as densely-packed. Only a thin line circled the edge, and a few guards laid on their backs, arrows sticking out of their throats as they gasped for air. There was nowhere to drag them to, and the civilians trying to save the dead were already too busy.

As soon as I made it to the top, a sergeant turned to me. “My lord!” he exclaimed. “We barely survived the first wave of ladders, and they’re already putting them back up.”

Though I had to save my Presence for later, it didn’t mean I couldn’t keep protecting the wall from where I stood right now. As soon as the orcs leaned another ladder up against the tower, I kicked it down.

However, a few of the ladders had hooks at their ends, and were perfectly sized for the towers—that was what they’d been building in the weeks they’d been waiting on the ridge. Sizing the ladders perfectly, adding heavy hooks to their tips.

I could’ve pushed them down still, given time, but the orcs didn’t let us have any. Orcs clung to the ladders as they rose, and I had no choice but to fight them as soon as they reached the top.

The orcs that led the charge were Steels. They wielded axes and clubs, and they swung wildly. Even if they were weaker, they forced me back. One landed a hit on my shoulder, and it was strong enough to push me to the side. It took three separate retaliatory stabs to kill him.

I fought off the first wave, spinning my spear and sweeping it, or stabbing from above with the precise movements Moonhand taught me. All around, orcs fell.

But with the ladders attached to the wall and the orcs constantly streaming up, there was no opening in sight.

Worse, where was the Hangman? Why was he holding back?
