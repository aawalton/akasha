Fringe didn’t understand the gravity of their situation, and he didn’t think it was important. What was important was staying alive at the present, and at present, his team was being led by a cocky know-it-all eighteen-year-old who took breaks to talk about plants every five fuckin’ minutes.

He was even more annoyed that the team just followed him around obsequiously, listening like dogs as he spoke. It was driving him up the wall.

So he kept focused, blocking out Hayden the best he could—but it was impossible. He always heard the first sentence before he suppressed it.

“Blue plants are a sign of…”

“Orange plants with that whorl pattern…”

“Do you see that?”

About the eighth time that they stopped, Fringe couldn’t stand it anymore. He whirled around. “Can you stop talking about plants? This is a mission, not a foragin’ expedition.”

The group looked at him with their seeing spirits activated.

“What?” Fringe asked.

The group’s sniper, Yaka, frowned. “He’s explaining the indicators for poisonous plants. The one at your feet’s an example.”

Fringe looked down and saw a green plant crunched underneath his foot. “Example? Example of what? An alien plant no one knows shit about? This is another world—no one has ever seen one of these plants.”

“That’s true, but cora’s the same no matter where you go,” Kalia said calmly. “That’s what he was explaining. Look at that plant with your seer.”

Fringe felt anxiety as he looked at the innocuous plant underneath his foot. He activated his “Life Vision” spirit—Eylia. She flooded his brain with colors, helping him see cora. Sure enough, the plant lit up an unnatural bright purple. He stumbled backward.

“W-What the hell is that?”

Photel and Yaka exchanged some laughs, but Kalia wasn’t amused.

“Pay attention and you’d know,” she said. “Now let’s go.”

The group was ten minutes out from the meadow when they picked up Lent’s signature. He was being chased by a pack of beasts that were something akin to a jackal, but they were as large as a bear and had toxic green fur. Lent was professional, cutting them down with smooth wind blades that cut through the beasts without making much noise.

Hayden was visibly impressed by the man—as was natural. Lent was a dancer, spinning and cutting through two at a time, always curving the blades around trees like boomerangs to prevent trees from falling. He was powerful—but he was severely outnumbered.

“I’m going in,” Hayden said.

“The hell you are,” Fringe said.

“This isn’t up for discussion.”

“Okay, let me spell this out. If you die, we all—”

A unique jackal with red fur turned to them. It was far faster than the others, catching them off guard. The group reacted instantly, but Hayden had already sent clear lines at the beast.

Perhaps out of curiosity, Kalia, Fringe, and the rest watched, ready to attack if necessary. It wasn’t. Since their seeing spirits were activated, they got to witness the surreal moment that the lines touched the jackal—and pulsed with erratic energy. That energy spread through the beast’s coran channels like electricity, and when it hit the jackal’s core, its core pulsed like a beating heart.

The beast spasmed and fell onto one leg.

Hayden lifted his hand, raising the chaotic lines like a whip. They then connected, formed blades, and he whipped his hand with terrorizing strength. It cracked down on the creature’s spine, making it drop and convulse on the ground before dropping dead.

It all happened within five seconds.

Then, the beast erupted with tora, the refined cora that made spirits and their hosts stronger, and one hundred percent of it entered Hayden’s chest—proving it had died to Hayden’s hands.

Hayden took down a mutant C-rank jackal by himself.

“Hey, guys?” Lent cried. “Are you going to help or what?”

Fringe snapped out of his daze when he remembered that Lent was fighting a dozen of the ugly bastards. He jumped into the fray, killing the beasts with his bare fists to avoid making too much noise. Minimal input for maximum output—that’s how A-ranks fought. It’s how he fought. Yet when he caught a glimpse of Hayden killing beasts with surreal efficiency, he felt a pang of inferiority. So without thinking, he activated Gravity Well, and the beasts near him flew to him like a magnet.

One, two, three. He threw three punches as the beasts flew through them, and all three blew the skulls out of the beasts. It was the fourth one that was the problem. He was showing off, so he didn’t use the Bubble spirit to create a barrier. As a result, he had to release Gravity Well and dodge.

He did both skillfully, but the momentum flung the fourth beast into a tree, leading to an aggressive snap that rang out in the forest.

Photel scoffed. “So much for stealth.”

Fringe winced. It was an accident, but accidents are a matter of life or death, so everyone was glaring at him. He opened his mouth to apologize, but Lent was already speaking to Kalia.

“What’s done is done,” Tibbi said coldly, using a rock to scrape mud off her boot.

Lent did the same before looking at Kalia. “Okay… what now?”

Kalia nudged her head at Hayden, who was examining the mutant jackal. “Ask him.”

“So he’s actually the leader?”

“Yeah, but it’s actually not that bad. You’d be surprised.”

“Huh…” Lent turned toward Hayden. “I guess he did just kill a C… Or was I seein’ things?”

“You weren't seeing things,” Photel said. “He killed the thing—and did it in a messed up way.”

“What’d he do?”

“I don’t know. We should ask.”

As the group approached, Hayden set to work processing the beast. He didn’t do it with a physical blade. Instead, he lifted his hand, and five formless, centimeter-thick lines extended from his fingers. In a hypnotizing demonstration, he fanned his fingers closed, and the five lines twisted together and melded into a single object—a beautiful dagger. He crouched in front of the mutant creature and started skinning it with the blade.

Lent was right confused about the ordeal. He wanted to tell the rucker that it wasn’t gonna cook, but he was too shined by the display to even speak.

“Did you find a good spot?” Hayden asked.

Lent snapped back into the present. “Uh… yeah. Right by the tree. Did the dig and then brushed it.” He lifted his hand, and a sphere of wind formed in his hand. “It’s nice ‘n natural. Doubt a tracker would do jack to find it.”

“Good work,” Hayden said expressionlessly. “Landmark?”

“Nasty bitta groundcover; yellow as mustard and reeks of poison.”

“Excellent.” Hayden continued to skin the leg.

Lent and Kalia exchanged glances. They were caught between asking what he was doing and about how he killed the beasts. Tibbi jumped in and asked the latter.

“What spirit do you really have?”

“The Clear Line spirit.”

“That doesn’t look like a Clear Line, friend,” Yaka said.

“I doubt it looks like anything else you’ve heard of either,” Hayden countered.

“Well, you got me there…” Yaka said.

“Me, too…” Photel said. “And to be honest, that’s unnerving. I hate to pry, but I think we all gotta know—what the hell did you do to that thing? I don’t think anyone’s going to sleep a wink if you don’t tell us.”

“Then don’t sleep,” Hayden said, cutting the hide up to the ankle. “I’m not going to disclose my secrets to you.”

“The hell you won’t,” Fringe said. “If you—”

Tibbi stood in front of him. “Shut the hell up. You’re the fuck up here—and I’m done with it. Now, if you’ll kindly fuck off, I have a man to yell at.”

Fringe’s nose scrunched, but he turned away. Then, Tibbi turned to Hayden. “Look, guy—that’s extremely uncomfortable. That’s not some blade or an element. That’s disturbing.”

“As opposed to what?” Hayden asked, cutting the ankle to release the pelt. “Burning me alive? Shearing through my limbs? Blowing my brains out of my skull?” He looked up for the first time. “Do you understand how absurd you all sound right now?”

“Ey, no need for callin’,” Lent said. “We’re just curious is all. I’m damn near seventy, and I still haven’t seen a clear spirit be used for anythin’ but training.”

Tibbi stared at him with wide eyes. Lent didn’t look a day above thirty. He must have accumulated a fortune early on.
