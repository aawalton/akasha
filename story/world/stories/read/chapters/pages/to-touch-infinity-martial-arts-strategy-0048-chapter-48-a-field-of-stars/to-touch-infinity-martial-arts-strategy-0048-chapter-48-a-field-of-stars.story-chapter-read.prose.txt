“If it’s just some caltrops, the boy can handle it,” came a voice from the forge, as one of the men looked up from a nearby station. He was completely bald, with a short black beard that was singed around the edges. Although older than the apprentices, he still appeared surprisingly young – especially for a person who was presumably a master smith.

Mu-cheon looked back down at the shop assistant in front of him. The boy was beaming a bright smile back at him, but the Young Master shook his head.

“These aren’t normal caltrops. And we’re willing to pay extra for quality.”

Tak let out a gruff sigh. He laid down his hammer, dusted off his leather apron, and grudgingly tromped over.

“‘Quality’ and ‘caltrops’ aren’t two words you often hear used together. What’re you thinking?”

The Young Master retrieved one of Chae-ha’s bookbinding needles from his belt, and held it up so the triangular facets could catch the forge light.

“Smaller than usual…and made to match one of these. The spikes only need to be a finger’s width in length, but we’ll want them thin and light-weight, like a leatherworking needle. Made from hardened steel.”

The smith’s eyebrows shot up. “You want me to make fancy toebiters out of perfectly good tool steel?”

“Yeah, more or less. Oil-blackened, and with the same three-way points you see here. Can you do it?”

Master Tak scratched his bald head idly. “We have some nail stock I guess I could repurpose. Draw it out, cut it into wires two fingers long, forge the edges on, and then heat ‘em up and twist ‘em together. It’s a lot of work for something we’d normally just cast, though...”

“How much?”

The smith’s nose twitched slightly. “For how many?”

“Let’s say…five hundred.”

Tak’s nose twitched again as he thought. “That’s something like four or five days of work. I suppose we could do it for three taels…” he began, as Mu-cheon reached for the pouch on his belt.

“…half up front, and half on completion. And I’m not interested in bargaining, young man! Don’t try and haggle with me for coppers on this. It’s fully custom work…”

His words dried up as Mu-cheon pushed two small ingots into his palm. “You got it. Half up front, half on completion.” The Inner Disciple grinned at him. “Like I said, we’re willing to pay for quality.”

After a moment of initial shock, the smith grinned back at him. The man raised the silver bars to his ear and casually clinked them together, hoping to hear the clear ring of pure silver. Once he was satisfied they were real, his eyes met Mu-cheon’s again, and his grin broadened.

“Oh, don’t you worry. I guarantee these’ll be the nicest toebiters any of us have ever seen.”

The next day, the morning sun was peeking through the window of Chae-ha’s room in the infirmary, causing her to wince as the painfully bright light fell over her face. She sat up, rubbing the sleep out of her bleary eyes, and ignored the remnant pain in her side as she reached for the materials left by her bed. It was time to get to work.

Propping herself up again with some pillows, the young Inspector took out her sewing kit. Her fingers ran along the length of the silk panels that made up her scholar’s robes, as she silently thanked them for everything they had endured on her behalf. They were torn where she had been stabbed in the side, and despite having been cleaned, visible spots remained from where drops of blood had splattered in places and left small, stubborn stains. The young girl held up a sewing needle; these silks had a few more surgeries to endure.

With deft movements, Chae-ha began stitching small square patches of garment leather along the sides of each panel. One end of each patch was left open, creating a series of small pockets that faced outwards. Finally, she added two small holes to each of the openings so a piece of linen thread could pass through. That would allow her to temporarily seal them off.

Short of breath, the young girl looked down at her work. One line of a dozen pockets had taken her nearly an hour to stitch, and there were still eleven more to go. Her injured side was already radiating a constant, dull ache, and she could feel a bead of sweat beginning to form by her temple. But despite the difficulty, she found herself pursing her lips in resolve. The result would definitely be worth the effort.

Chae-ha closed her eyes. Something had to change. No matter what, she refused to remain a liability.

Taking a deep breath, she opened her eyes and got back to work on sewing the next set. This project would take several days to fully complete, but then again – she had nowhere else to be.

Her self-reflection was interrupted by the sound of thumping boots, followed by a polite knock on the door. She raised her voice in answer.

“It’s okay, senior brother. You can come on in.”

Mu-cheon opened the door, his eyes scanning the organized piles of fabric and thread. He coughed slightly.

“I just, uh…wanted to check in, and make sure you were doing okay. See if you needed anything else.”

Chae-ha ran her hand over the outside of one of her panels again, her fingers pausing atop a small blood stain. After a moment, she looked back up at her friend.

“Would it be too much to ask for some silver embroidery thread?”

The young man scratched his head. “Of course not. It does seem a bit…strange, though, coming from you. Since when did you care about embroidery, little sister?”

“I don’t. But I’ll draw a lot of attention if I go out there covered in blood stains.” She patted the small stain on the silk panel under her hand. “So…I should probably cover these up with something.”

Mu-cheon nodded slowly. “I see. What were you thinking of using?”

Her expression barely changed, but Chae-ha couldn’t help herself. Her mind went racing back to a night five years prior, when the two of them had laid down after training, stared up at the night sky together, and declared their intentions to change the world. It was a rare, happy memory. She shook her head to clear it before replying.

“Given that I have a bunch of small spots to cover up, I was thinking of using…a field of stars.”

She forced her expression to remain perfectly neutral.

“You know, to represent the caltrops.”

The better part of a thousand li away, Yan Shu was sitting at his work desk in the Imperial Library in Bianjing. His personal brush, the handle worn totally smooth by near constant use, lay off to one side. Next to it rested his inkwell. Sparkles of reflected candlelight glinted off the murky liquid inside, almost as if it was clamoring to work with the young prodigy again on some new masterpiece.

Yan Shu chuckled as he addressed the eager pot. “I’m afraid there will be no poetry today, my friend. But don’t worry; I’m sure there will still be plenty of excitement.”

The young Collator stretched his stiff shoulders, a wry grin spreading across his face as he reached for the stack of papers in front of him.

“And now, my old comrades, to battle!” Yan Shu whispered to his writing implements. “Let’s see what glory awaits us in this stack of tax filings.”

He pulled the first sheet towards himself and began to review it.

“Ah, a declaration of intent to purchase…an empty granary! On the very first try, we have struck gold, my dear compatriots.”

Yan Shu continued working his way through the pile, filing papers, making copies, and double-checking that procedure had been correctly followed for each. It was dreary work, but it was also his job; he had studied for years to get this position, and now he was stuck with it.

Half-way through the stack, with his boredom level approaching full saturation, the poet paused.

The document in front of him was an Imperial auction notice. It stated that, in order to fund infrastructure improvements for the Feng-shan sacrifices, the future toll rights to the Imperial Highways leading to Shandong were to be auctioned off.

“That’s strange,” the young man muttered under his breath. “The Imperial court has no shortage of coin.”

Yan Shu’s eyes continued down the document. The auction listing was backdated to three weeks ago, and public bidding was supposed to take place at an amphitheater near the Imperial Palace in less than five days. He trailed his finger down to the bottom of the page, searching for the registry of bidders. To his surprise, there was only one group listed: the Rising Sun Mercantile.

“What’s this – only a single bidder? And one I’ve never heard of? Now, that hardly seems reasonable. Did the rest of the merchant clans all simultaneously experience a shortage of ink?”

Standing up and dusting himself off, the young prodigy went to look up the company’s previous filing information. He pulled out a dusty drawer full of small registration cards, each with basic information about some business that had registered with the tax office. When he found the one for Rising Sun Mercantile, he rolled his eyes and chuckled softly.

“Rising Sun Mercantile. A newly registered holding of…who else? The Black Ink Consortium. Of course.”

Yan Shu returned to his desk and drummed his fingers on its surface as he considered the paper in front of him. Someone high within the Ministry of Finance, and possibly the Minister himself, was trying to keep the existence of this ‘public’ auction a secret. Imperial law required that the dates for such auctions be publicly posted, so there was definitely a notice hanging somewhere. Probably in the most inconspicuous location they could find.

The young man looked over at the blank sheets of high-grade mulberry paper resting on the corner of his desk, a smug smile growing on his face.

“Well now, the gentlemen at the Consortium have gone to such trouble to set up a mouth-watering table. Wouldn’t it be dreadfully bad manners if no one else showed up to eat?”

The Collator dipped his brush into the inkwell, before lowering it onto a blank piece of paper and beginning to write. His hand flew across the page with savage grace, writing characters with clear, bold strokes. Within moments, he had manufactured a set of professional looking auction invitations. Which were suspiciously lacking any direct attribution.

“It is time for us to part, but do not weep, my inanimate companions,” Yan Shu whispered, as he blew on the ink to help it dry.

“We will surely meet again tomorrow. But for now…I have some doors I must go and slip these under!”
