Liao Wen dreamed of stairs.

Valeria landed below me, still dealing with her shock from earlier, before she quickly took the shape of an ordinary servant with black hair. She was used to stuff like this, quickly finding her role in this specific dream.

As soon as she stepped among the clerks, Liao Wen’s mind decided she belonged there and supplied the recognition for her. Watching his dream remember someone who didn’t exist made my skin crawl.

I didn’t try to look for it, and instead just stuck to her. She clicked her tongue and did her work. I mostly just observed her work.

The first pages really did hold grain numbers. Deeper in the stack, those same columns turned into troop movements and lists of villages that had become tax districts after Baolian “stabilization.” More clerks came out of the dimness with fresh ledgers while the stairs kept rising. Valeria turned another page before stopping. “Here, look at the title.”

Advisory Integration Protocol.

Trade advisors were listed first. Cultural representatives and military observers followed, along with a list of reasons the local government might need Baolian help. The reasons ranged from religious conflict and illegal weapons to tax fraud and noble resistance. On paper, Baolian officials had simply arrived and discovered every problem.

The notes attached to those incidents showed how the problems were made. Yes, made. A border patrol had been ordered to search a village until somebody fought back, then a provincial lord received anonymous letters accusing a Baolian officer of abusing his daughter, followed by weapons appearing beneath a temple without anyone saying who put them there.

Once the local court started arguing about blame, Baolian advisors offered to help.

Soldiers entered to restore peace, then officials who remained to make sure the trouble didn’t return. Fang had already done this twice.

Valeria tapped the third section with one finger. “There. Stabilization.”

It made me frown. I had seen that trick on Earth. Call it stabilization, keep the soldiers out of the first paragraph, and argue over the wording until they were already in place. Fang only needed each step to look temporary just long enough for the court to agree.

The worst part was they didn't need to do all this. Any of this. They did it anyway. Because the Emperor of Pride enjoyed it. Such a theatre boy.

On the seventh evening, it left three hours without a location or stated purpose. Liao Wen had recorded where Fang would sit everywhere else.
