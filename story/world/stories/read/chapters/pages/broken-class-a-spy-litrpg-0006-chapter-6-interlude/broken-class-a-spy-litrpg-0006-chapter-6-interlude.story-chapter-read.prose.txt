Beyond the blood-stained walls of Darvaza. Where Arthur lost everything, lay the true architects of power and cruelty.

Far across the wilderness, in the heart of the kingdom, the capital city shimmered like a jeweled crown atop a rotting corpse. Within its spires and marble halls, the upper echelon of Guardians - those born into noble bloodlines, trained in magic prepared for a hunt. Not for survival. For legacy.

Every four years, they called it tradition.

The System called it the Great Hunt.

A brutal spectacle where noble scions under the age of twenty were sent into monster-infested wilds with handpicked companions and enchanted steel. What began as a rite of passage had grown into a ritual of power. Where bloodlines were measured not in pedigree, but in predator kills.

Victory brought prestige, rare artifacts, and political leverage. Defeat brought shame. And death? Death was common enough that betting on it was both a vice and an industry.

In a smoky tavern tucked inside the merchant quarter of the capital, four men hunched over a scarred table, tankards in hand, voices low but animated.

“You're mad if you don’t back the princess,” grunted one, jabbing a finger at a paper listing the odds. “Ysara’s team has the best odds for a reason. Half her guard used to be battlefield knights.”

“Yeah, but they fight like they’ve got a healer watching their backs,” another said, nodding. “And they do.”

“Still...” a third man chimed in, swirling his drink, “don’t sleep on Rowlar. That Baron Drelmor’s boy commands ice like it’s a limb. He’s only E-rank officially, but you wouldn’t know it by the way he flattened that troll last spring.”

“He’s cold as his element,” muttered the fourth. “But if you want real money, bet on the wild card. Knight Garran Drax. Count Alveron’s son. Madman in the field. Doesn’t follow formations, just wades into beasts like he's trying to impress death itself.”

They laughed, traded coin, and argued over whose house would rise or fall with the coming Hunt. Unseen by them, behind gilded walls and enchanted curtains, the game was already being played.

In a forgotten hovel on the outer edge of the capital, the air reeked of rot, sickness, and despair.

A cloaked woman knelt beside a frail child sprawled atop a straw mat. His skin was pale, his breath shallow - a fluttering thing barely clinging to life. The woman’s gloved hand moved to his chest. A soft glow bloomed beneath her palm, golden and steady.

The color slowly returned to the boy’s cheeks. His breathing evened out. Ribs that had heaved like bellows now rose with quiet rhythm. The spell continued for several minutes, drawing on her strength. Mana pouring from her like water from a cracked jar, until she felt the worst had passed.

Only then did Princess Ysara of House Valen exhale and rise to her feet, her shoulders heavy with exhaustion.

She turned to the kneeling woman beside the child, a slave with hollow eyes and a threadbare tunic.

“He’ll wake within the hour,” Ysara said, voice soft. “Keep him warm. He’ll need water, and broth if you can find it.”

The slave fell to the ground in a full kowtow, forehead pressed to the dirt. “Thank you, Highborn. Thank you. Thank you...”

Ysara didn’t wait for more gratitude. She gave a tired nod, pulled her cloak tighter, and stepped outside into the dim light of dusk.

Four figures in travel-stained armor were already waiting - her royal guards, silent and tense. They flanked her without a word and began the walk back toward the palace, their eyes constantly scanning the alleys and rooftops. This part of the city was no place for a royal, not even a healer beloved by the common folk. Especially not her.

They reached a vine-choked wall near the palace’s outer ring. A concealed door opened with a whispered passphrase and a flick of a rune-stone. Moments later, they emerged into the quiet opulence of Ysara’s chambers, breathing easier at last.

As the secret panel closed behind them, one of the guards - tall, broad, and perpetually exasperated, spoke first.

“Ysara,” Yudursh said, voice laced with frustration, “why do you risk your life for a sick slave child? Again? You know how dangerous this is.”

She removed her cloak and hung it carefully. “Because he needed healing.”

“There are nobles who could use that healing,” he said, crossing his arms. “Real allies. Powerful ones. A single healing session could earn you a future duke’s favor.”

She gave him a tired glance. “I’m not interested in trading my mana for political capital.”

Yudursh barked a laugh. “And that’s why they call you mad. You’re the only noble I know who thinks compassion is a currency.”

“Maybe that’s why it’s valuable,” Ysara said.

He shook his head. “You can’t save them all. You know that, right? The slaves exist to carry burdens so we don’t have to. That’s the system. It works.”

Her gaze hardened, calm but unyielding. “I know I can’t save them all, Yudursh. But that doesn’t mean I won’t try.”

A heavy silence followed, thick with tension and unspoken history.

Outside, monsters stirred in the wilds. Inside the palace, another kind of monster smiled behind a desk.

And in between stood Ysara - idealistic enough to light the path... and just naïve enough to become the bait.

In a hall of polished marble and silence, a man in embroidered velvet strode past columns and whispering servants. The palace air was heavy with incense and secrets.

He reached a sealed door, waited until it locked behind him, then whispered a spell. Wards shimmered faintly before snapping into place.

Inside, another man waited. Older, sharp-eyed, draped in noble robes behind a desk of carved obsidian.

“Is it done?” the seated man asked.

“Yes, my lord,” the other replied. “The arrangements were made through third channels. No trail.”

The lord nodded once, satisfied. “Good. If luck holds, that sanctimonious girl won’t survive the Hunt. If she dies, no one can strengthen their claim through marriage. We remove a pawn and deny the other houses a queen.”

His voice was calm, almost bored.

“If we can’t control her,” he said, eyes narrowing, “then no one will.”
