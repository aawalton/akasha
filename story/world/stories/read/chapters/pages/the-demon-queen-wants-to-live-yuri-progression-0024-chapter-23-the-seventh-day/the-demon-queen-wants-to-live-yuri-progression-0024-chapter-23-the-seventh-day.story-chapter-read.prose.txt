The seventh day came as all endings did. The 
sun had barely touched the sky when we had left the village behind and 
made for the forest. Ash carried a shovel she had borrowed from Tom the 
night before. I carried the Darkness Binding folded across my arms, the 
way I had seen others carry the dead.

We did not wander far from Hamel, or wander 
for long. A clearing past the treeline, close enough to find again. It 
was far from the river, and far from the road. No idle wanderer would 
stumble upon this place. We looked around, and Ash found the place. She 
chose a spot beneath a broad oak with a strangely split trunk. One that 
had clearly been there for many years, and might stay there for many 
more. It was a landmark, one we could find again.

"This place will do," Ash said. She drove the
 shovel into the earth. The ground was soft from the week's rain, and it
 gave easily. She dug in silence, and I stood and watched the hole 
deepen.

This was a tactical consideration. That was 
the reason for this. Our backgrounds and our marks were already 
suspicious, the Inker's unease had been plain last time. My Darkness 
Binding was a marvel amongst marvels. Ash's green armor was also fine, I
 could admit. Arriving in armor as extraordinary as ours would only 
invite questions that had no safe answers. We would retrieve these when 
the time was right. Someday.

That was the reason. It was a sound one.

Eventually, the hole was deep enough.

Ash set the shovel aside and reached for her 
own armor first. She placed the pieces inside the hole one by one -the 
green plate, the greaves, the pauldron. Each piece she set into the 
earth with great care. The Hero's Sword she kept. That much I expected. A
 Hero without a sword was simply a woman, and even stripped of her 
Class, I did not think Ash was ready to be simply that. Besides, the 
sword no longer had its power or its glow. Without them, it was just 
fine steel.

She stepped back and looked at me. It was my 
turn now. I stood at the edge of the hole. I stood there for a long 
moment. The Darkness Binding lay across my arms. I had worn it for 
centuries, and in that time, it had done everything from turn aside the 
blows of Heroes to the wrath of the Gods themselves. It had been my 
signature. The thing so many had seen before their end. It was one of 
the finest garments to have ever existed. Was.

It was just armor now. Its special functions 
were all but gone. Functions that had let it match every one of my needs
 no longer worked, and would likely never work again. Without them, all I
 held was a very durable garment of black laced with red. I had not worn
 it for some time. I suspected that if I did, it would be as 
uncomfortable as Martha's garish clothes had once been. I took a breath 
and set it into the hole in the earth, beside the Hero's armor. The 
black now lay against the green.

Ash picked up the shovel. She paused. "Are you alright?"

"I am fine. Most of the ache is gone. I can draw in ambient mana-"

"That is not what I asked," Ash said softly.

I did not look at her. "Fill it," I said.

The Hero looked at me and said nothing. A 
moment later, she began to do as I had asked. The hole filled slowly. 
The dirt covered the green first, then the black. It took minutes before
 the last of my Darkness Binding finally disappeared beneath the earth. 
Something stirred in my chest, just for a moment.

Was this what others called grief? No. It 
could not be, for I did not feel such a thing. And even if I did, I 
would not feel it for a mere cloth. I was simply lighter, that was all. 
As if a weight I had been carrying for a very long time had finally been
 set down. Ash patted the earth flat. We stood over the unmarked packed 
earth and said nothing, because there was nothing to say.

I was dressed in the clothes Martha had set 
out for me today. A brown dress, simple and worn. Ash wore a similar 
thing in a faded blue. They were not bad garments. Certainly not garish.
 We walked back to the village in silence.

Hamel was awake when we returned.

There were more villagers walking through the
 dirt paths of Hamel than I had ever seen before. There was an urgency 
to all of them I had only seen for the Phoenix. They knew what day it 
was then. I saw him from across the square.

Marin sat on the low wall near the well, his 
legs dangling from the edge of it. Sara sat beside him, and she was 
talking. Her smile was bright and wide. She was saying something I could
 not hear, and when she spoke, her hands moved and mimicked some gesture
 I did not understand. Marin was slowly nodding and listening. I watched
 the two of them for three seconds. Perhaps four. I turned away before 
either of them could notice my gaze.

I made for Martha's hut. The woman herself 
caught me before I could enter. And when she did, she pressed a small 
satchel into my hands. I peered inside. There was the egg, nestled in a 
layer of white cloth. There were several changes of clothing, all of 
them folded neatly. I had come only to retrieve the egg.

"I did not ask for this," I said.

"No, you didn't." Martha pressed the satchel 
into my hands. Her grip was firm for a woman her age. "But you're takin'
 all of this anyway. There's a dress for warmer weather at the bottom 
and one for when it's cold. You'll want both, where you're going…and 
where you might go after that."

"You do not know where I am going."

"I know you'll be cold eventually, lass." She
 smoothed the strap of the satchel against my shoulder. "You just eat 
when hungry…don't skip meals. Think I taught ya a little, at least 
enough to make something that'll pass for food. Ya eat properly, you 
hear me?"

I heard her. "That is…noted," I managed.

Martha looked at me. It was the same look she
 had given me every day since I had first sat at her table and eaten 
four bowls of stew. I still did not have a name for it, but now I was 
starting to recognize its shape, somewhat. "You're a kind girl, Lily," 
she said. Her gaze went past me, finding Marin and Sara where I had left
 them. It returned to me again.

I had returned to the hut last night in…an 
unfortunate state. Ash had carried me to our room. I had assumed that 
Martha and Tom would both have been asleep. I had assumed wrong. Martha 
had been waiting for us. I opened my mouth. There were many things I 
could have said -many corrections, many objections. The words that came 
were not any of them. "Your stew was adequate," I said. "I will remember
 it." I will not miss it.

Martha smiled, and her eyes were wet. She 
touched my shoulder one last time. Then she turned and went inside, and 
the door closed behind her. I stood there for a moment longer than I 
should have.

The sound of boots reached us before I saw 
them. Then the creak of armor. Then the Inker appeared at the northern 
edge of the village, his soldiers arrayed behind him in the same 
formation as before. He had brought almost twice as many men as he had 
before, that much I could tell from a distance. This was no escort. Or 
at least, this was an escort that could become something very different,
 given the chance.

Many of those men and women in silver stood 
at the village's gate. The others formed another line, twenty paces 
behind the first group. I stood at the center of the square. Ash stood 
beside me. The satchel hung from my shoulder. The Inker looked at us as 
he approached, stopping halfway between us and the men he had brought. 
He gazed at the scorched earth that had not yet fully healed. Something 
flickered across his face. He smothered it. If he had any questions, he 
did not ask.

"You said one week," I said. "It has been seven days."

The Inker composed himself and straightened his robes. "So it has, and here you both are. You are both ready, I trust?"

"We are," Ash said. She stepped forward. "We gave our word and we mean to keep it. We'll come willingly."

The Inker stared at us and he nodded slowly. 
Something drained from his soldiers, and there was that greed in his 
eyes again. He smiled widely, turned to his soldiers and raised a hand.

That was when Tom stepped between them.

The old merchant planted himself in the dirt 
path with his arms folded across his chest, his round belly jutting 
forward. He was shaking. "Now hold on," Tom said.

The Inker's hand stopped. His eyes moved to 
the merchant, and there was a sneer across his face. He looked at Tom as
 if the man was no more than the merest of insects. One that had decided
 to announce itself by biting at his skin.

"T-these two saved this village," Tom said. 
His voice had stumbled on the first word, but it had steadied. By the 
end of the sentence, it was louder and firmer than I had ever heard it. 
"Some beast came down on us from the sky, and your soldiers weren't 
here. They were, and they saved us folk." Tom did not stutter once.

"Tom. What the hells are you doing?" Martha's voice was sharp as she stood behind him.

Tom just faced the Inker. "You want to drag 
them off like criminals? After what they did for us? That's just not 
right. That's not right at all."

There was sound. The sound of a plated boot 
on the dirt. One of the soldiers had taken a single step forward. None 
of the others had. Tom looked at the line of soldiers, and his shaking 
only grew worse. Still, his feet stayed exactly where they were. Then 
Martha was beside him. She put her hand on his arm. I thought she meant 
to pull him away. She stood next to her husband instead. "The man's a 
fool," she said. "But he's not wrong this time. These two lasses ain't 
no criminals. Don't take 'em like they are."

Then others joined them. The first was one of
 Hamel's two guards -the woman who had tried one attack against the 
phoenix and had thought better of it. She stepped forward and joined the
 two. Then came the man whose fence I had mended. Then two of the women I
 had seen hauling water from the well. More joined them. Faces I 
recognized, and faces I did not. Before long, there were a dozen of 
them. Then two dozen. Then half the village stood in a single terrified 
line between the soldiers and us.

None of these people were warriors. All of 
these people were afraid. I could see all of their arms, and I saw no 
mark greater than a measly Spark.  I had seen men face insurmountable 
odds before. They had always been facing me. I had never understood why 
they fought battles they knew were pointless. I did not understand it 
now either, but now it made something inside me ache.

The Inker stared at this display. His lip curled. He seemed to be weighing scales only he could see.

One of the village men stepped forward and 
unsheathed a pathetic wooden sword. The Inker raised his hands, and I 
saw both of his marks start to glow. His mouth opened.

"You fools!" I said. Everyone in the village 
turned towards me. "You absolute blithering and suicidal fools." My 
voice was loud. Loud enough to carry across the square.  I looked at 
Tom, who was still shaking. At Martha, whose hand had not left his arm. I
 had a glare for everyone. Even the ones I did not recognize. I took 
them all in with one scathing look. "Step aside. Step aside now. Do not 
leave the children with-" Something caught in my throat. I talked past 
it. "You would die for nothing. We have already agreed to go, and so now
 we will go. You will all stand aside, before I lose what little respect
 I have for this miserable village." My breathing was hard. Harder than 
it should have been.

Still, nobody moved from that line. They all 
stared at me, but not one had moved. Ash stepped forward by my side, and
 her hand found my arm. It was a brief touch. "Please. We'll be alright.
 We chose this. Let us go." Her voice was gentle.

The line had not parted for me. It had hardly
 even stirred. But for Ash, the villagers began to move aside, one by 
one. Tom was the last to step aside. He looked like he wanted to say 
something. He opened his mouth, closed it, opened it again.

"You come back, you h-hear?" His voice 
cracked on the last word. "Both of you…Ash, Lily. You come back and you 
eat my wife's cooking and you tell us how it went. Don't matter how long
 it takes, just come back someday."

I did not answer. I did not trust myself to. Ash dipped her head. "We will."

The Inker watched all of this in silence. 
When we approached, he glanced at our clothing and said nothing. 
Whatever the man had thought of what had just transpired, he buried it. 
He gave a signal, and the soldiers started to move. I walked past him 
and past his first line of soldiers. I walked past the last hut at the 
edge of the village, until I was almost out of the gate.

"Lys!" There were small footsteps behind me.

I stopped and turned at the sound. Sara raced
 towards me. She slowed when she came close and skidded to a halt, 
nearly losing her balance in the dirt. The girl's face was flushed red, 
and her breathing was quick. She thrust her right hand towards me before
 I could say a word. Sara was holding something.

It was…some kind of cord, made of thin 
leather. I had seen its like used by the villagers to tie their bundles.
 It was more than just a cord. Threaded at the end of the cord was what 
looked to be a single bead of clay. It was small, barely more than a 
chip. I took it. It was rough in my fingers and lopsided besides. The 
bead had been shaped to resemble something that tried to be, but was not
 quite round. Two small points jutted from its top, curving slightly 
inward. They were horns.

They were horns.

The girl had shaped the bead to look like 
horns. My horns. They were uneven, and the left was thicker than the 
right. The size of them compared to the roundness beneath made them look
 more like the ears of those rabbits. Still, I knew what they were meant
 to be.

"Is this…ade-quit?" Sara asked slowly. The 
last word came out half-broken, as if she had been practicing but had 
not yet mastered it.

I regarded her in the light. The Cradle on my
 left arm was almost humming. There was a huff from the Inker, and I saw
 his soldiers shifting. They were restless. I did not care for them. 
Slowly, I crouched down. The way I had seen Ash do with some of the 
other children in the village. My hand trembled as it rose towards the 
girl's head.

I placed my hand on her hair. It was soft 
beneath my fingers. The girl leaned forward into the touch, the way 
those small creatures by the river had leaned into each other. "It is 
more than adequate," I said, and meant it. My voice was soft. Had it 
ever been so soft?

Briefly, I feared I had not said enough. The 
fear died when the girl smiled. Her shoulders straightened, as if she 
had expected me to refuse this, and was now relieved that I had not.

I lifted the thing and wound it over my head,
 until the bead itself tucked beneath the collar of my dress. It felt 
warm against my skin from where Sara had been holding it. The clay had 
felt rough in my hands. Now it was only soft. I looked at Sara. There 
was more to say, and I did not know how to say most of it. I looked at 
the green Spark on her arm. It was small and faint.

"You will practice," I said. "That mark of yours -you will grow it. You will be stronger when I see you next."

Sara nodded fiercely, so hard her whole head was bobbing, her jaw set with a seriousness that looked absurd on her young face.

I chose each word carefully. "I dub you my 
first retainer in this new world. Do you know what that means? What I am
 giving you?" Sara shook her head. "It means you serve at my side. That 
you carry my name and my…trust." I held her gaze. "This is not something
 given lightly."

Sara's eyes were wide. She was a mere child, 
and so she likely understood none of it. Her back still straightened as 
she puffed out her small chest. "I'm a re-tainer," she said, testing the
 word. Then, louder, "I'm Lys's retainer!"

I should have corrected her. Should have 
explained the weight of the title, the centuries of tradition behind it,
 the blood that had been spilled to earn such a position in my court. 
"Yes," I said instead. "You should be proud."

Sara beamed. Then she threw her arms around 
me so suddenly and with enough force that it nearly toppled me. Her grip
 was ferocious for her size. I did not embrace her back. I placed my 
hand on her head again, and I let it rest there.

"Lys?" I turned my head. Ash stood there with an odd expression on her face, watching me with the girl.

Ash nodded seriously as Sara let go. The girl stepped back and wiped her nose with her sleeve. I rose to my full height.

"Wait for me," I said. "I will return someday. No Queen would ever abandon her retainer and deserve the name."

The girl was crying. I saw that now. She was 
crying, and trying very hard not to make any sound at all. Perhaps 
because of me. I could not face her then. I turned and walked south. My 
hand found the bead beneath my collar, just for a moment. Then it fell 
to my side. Ash fell into step beside me. The Inker and his soldiers led
 the way ahead, and behind us, Hamel grew small. All of it.

The tiny huts with their doors that barely 
deserved the name. The low wall I had sat beside so often. The villagers
 who stood between us and a small army. I left Hamel carrying a satchel 
with a golden egg lined with black. Around my neck, I wore a single clay
 bead shaped like horns by a strange child who thought I was pretty. 
These were the possessions of Lysanthia, formerly the Demon Queen, the 
strongest to have ever lived. Meager things, nothing compared to what I 
had once accessed at a thought. A collection that would inspire no fear 
at all. I found I did not mind.

The Hero walked beside me. The wide road 
stretched and widened again further ahead. I looked at her once, and 
found the trace of a smile. She said nothing, and neither did I. The 
silence between us now was one that needed no filling. There was a 
warmth in my chest that had nothing to do with the Cradle, or the egg, 
or the afternoon sun. It sat behind my ribs and would not leave. I did 
not know its name.

Fools. Every last one of them.

I did not look back, but I wanted to. That was new. At some point, Hamel had turned from nothing into something.
