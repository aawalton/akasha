Chapter 60 — The Saint of Bloom

ARC IV: THE THINGS THAT STAY

SYNOPSIS: This chapter belongs to no one but Alondra. She has known what this morning would ask of her since the morning she felt the central cluster turn toward her like a room full of people who had been waiting. She has not told anyone. She walks to the edge of the war alone, and she does the one thing her entire career as a healer never taught her how to do honestly: she gives everything, on purpose, with her eyes open, and lets it cost exactly what it costs.

Alondra had walked the Verdant Expanse alone since before dawn.

Not toward the central cluster this time — toward its edge, the perimeter line where the old growth gave way to open terrain, where the trees thinned and the war, if you stood still enough and let the wind carry sound the right direction, could already be heard as a distant, ragged static of impact and shouting. She had walked the long way, the way she always walked it, because the long way gave her time to arrive at herself before she arrived at anything else.

She had not needed the time today. She had known what today was since the morning weeks ago when the cluster had turned toward her like a room full of people who had been waiting, and she had not stopped knowing it since.

I have known the shape of this morning for a long time. I just didn’t know it would be this one.

The smoke reached her before the battlefield did — a gray smear against the sky, low and heavy, the unmistakable signature of a war that had been burning for hours. She stood at the tree line and looked out at it: the scorched ground, the broken formations, the small distant shapes of people she had healed and failed to heal and was now, six years too late by some measures and exactly on time by others, walking toward.

No one had asked her to come.

Six years ago, someone needed help, and I never realized it until it was too late. This time... no one is asking. I'm going anyway.

She walked into the contested zone without armor configured for combat, without a guild tag that registered her as a combatant, without anything that should have let her cross the boundary unchallenged.

A coalition scout intercepted her two hundred meters from the field’s edge. Young, alert, doing exactly what a scout should do when an unidentified player approached an active war zone.

"This area is restricted under Protocol Seven. State your affiliation."

"None," Alondra said. It was, in the specific and complicated way that mattered today, true.

The scout hesitated, clearly running the same calculation Alondra had spent six years failing to run on time: was this worth escalating, was this a threat, was a single unaffiliated player worth the resources to stop.

She did not wait for the calculation to finish. She kept walking.

Caelric saw her before his scouts finished reporting her.

He had been standing at the command rise since the air changed, watching the western tree line with the specific stillness of a man who had learned, three years ago, that the things which mattered arrived as small deviations and that paying attention to small deviations was the entire discipline his career had been built on. He saw a single figure separate from the tree line and walk, unhurried, directly toward the heart of the engagement.

He met her at the perimeter of the coalition’s rear formation, close enough now that he could see her face clearly — green robes, the Saint of Bloom emblem at her collar, an expression that held none of the urgency he would have expected from someone walking into an active war zone alone.

"You don’t need to be here," Caelric said. He said it as fact, not threat. He had know her guild history. Their relationship on the past. Former B&B. No current affiliation. No tactical reason to be standing on this ground.

"I know," Alondra said.

"This isn’t your fight."

"I know that too."

Caelric studied her for a long moment. He had spent years learning to read the difference between people who said things and people who meant them, and what he saw in her face was not bravado, not desperation, not any of the predictable emotional states he had catalogued for moments like this.

The decision was made before she got here.

"Then why?" he said.

Alondra looked past him, toward the scorched field, toward the distant figures of people who could no longer stand under their own strength. Her voice, when it came, was quiet, entirely without drama.

"Because I walked away once." she said.

"I was wrong."

A beat.

"I'm not making that mistake again."

Caelric didn't move.

"I don't know what you intend to do," he said. "But I have an engagement to win. I won't hold my fire for sentiment."

"I'm not asking you to."

Something in his expression shifted—not softening, exactly, but into the particular kind of respect one professional sometimes extends another, even across enemy lines, when the other person has made a choice with their eyes fully open.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I hope it works."

"It will," Alondra said. "It just won't be free."

Caelric turned away from her without another word.

He walked back toward the coalition formation with the unhurried, measured pace of a man who had made a decision he did not particularly want to have made, and raised one hand — not in triumph, not in command-voice urgency, but in the simple, weighted gesture of someone authorizing something because the war required it and his feelings about the war were not a variable his role allowed him to weight.

Somewhere behind him, the siege constructs’ heartbeat-rhythm began to climb.

Alondra did not watch him go. She was already walking past the coalition’s outer line, toward open ground, toward the center of everything, and nobody stopped her, because nobody quite knew what she was or why a war that operated on guild affiliations and tactical rosters should make room for a single woman in green robes walking calmly into the middle of it.

She stopped at the center of the contested zone.

Around her: the wreckage of two hours of war. Scorched ground. Glassy craters where siege fire had landed clean. The distant, ragged shapes of B&B’s line, what was left of it, people on the ground who were trying to stand and not managing it, people who had stopped trying to stand at all and were simply breathing, conserving whatever fraction of strength remained for whatever came next.

This is what it costs when nobody comes.

This is also what it will cost if I do this. The only difference is which version I can live with.

She planted her staff in the scorched earth.

A barrier bloomed outward from the point of contact — not large, not dramatic, just enough, a thin shell of warded light that wrapped around her in a thirty-foot radius, the kind of defensive casting that any Saint of Bloom learned in their first year. It would not stop a direct siege hit. It did not need to. It needed to buy her the handful of seconds the next part required.

She spread both arms.

The wind found her first — not a wind that came from any weather system, but the wind of something enormous waking up and exhaling for the first time in longer than the server had existed. Her robes caught it, lifted, the fabric snapping taut and then loose in a rhythm that had nothing to do with any breeze a person standing on open ground should have felt.

Her feet left the ground.

Slowly. Inches, at first, the absorption skill building beneath her like a tide coming in too gently to notice until it had already claimed the shore. Her hair lifted around her face. The green light gathering at her palms deepened, thickened, became something closer to a physical substance than a glow.

Two and a half kilometers west, in the heart of the central cluster, the oldest tree in the Verdant Expanse shuddered.

Not violently. Not with the visual drama of something breaking. It shuddered the way something enormous shuddered when it finally, after a very long wait, was asked the question it had been prepared to answer for longer than anyone currently alive had existed.

And it answered.

Alondra felt the network open beneath her like a held breath finally released.

This was nothing like the awareness she had touched weeks ago, that first cautious reaching that had made the cluster turn toward her in startled recognition. This was the entire thing, all at once, three hundred square kilometers of root and canopy and water-table and soil-memory opening to her at a scale her Saint of Bloom training had never been built to hold and that she held anyway, because some part of her had apparently always known this morning was coming and had been quietly preparing for it without telling the rest of her.

It isn’t being pulled from me. It’s being asked. The whole forest is asking, all at once, and saying yes to itself before I even finish forming the request.

She felt the mountains at the Expanse’s northern edge give up something that lived in their stone. She felt the lake’s deep cold water release warmth it had been holding since before the server had a name. She felt the dungeons beneath the western ridges — places no quest had ever sent a player, places that existed in the map data and nowhere else — contribute something that didn’t have a clean name but felt, distantly, like permission.

It moved through her and out of her, toward the battlefield, toward fifty-four people lying broken on scorched ground, and she felt every one of them the instant the energy reached them — not as numbers, not as HP bars climbing, but as small, individual returns. Breath coming easier. Pain receding. Strength arriving in the specific, irreplaceable shape of a body remembering it was allowed to keep going.

Bron. Up on both knees now. Not standing yet. Soon.

Nyx. Breathing deeper. The elbow holding now instead of shaking.

Phina, and the small light in her arms, brightening.

Thorne. The crow, settling, the wing already knitting back toward true.

Alaric’s hand, opening. The dice catching light again.

Morty. And the rest of B&B members looking up.

Miles—

And behind her, two and a half kilometers west, the Verdant Expanse began to die.

Not all at once. The way a held breath released didn’t end all at once, but unspooled, gave everything it had in a long, continuous exhale that left nothing behind for itself. The old growth canopy paled, leaf by leaf, the deep green going first dull and then gray and then simply absent. The lake’s water clouded. The mountains’ stone lost whatever quiet warmth had lived in it. The forest gave itself, completely, on purpose, to the people two and a half kilometers away who needed it more than it needed to keep being a forest.

Alondra felt all of it. The absorption ran both directions — she was not a pipe the energy passed through cleanly, she was the place where three hundred square kilometers of dying life and fifty-four people’s desperate need met and converted from one thing into the other, and the conversion happened inside her, in the only vessel built to hold something this large and this final.

It hurts. It is supposed to hurt. If it didn’t hurt this much, it wouldn’t be enough.

In the last moment before the energy completed its transfer, in the single heartbeat before release, Alondra’s awareness brushed against one final point in the network — a single plant, near the heart of the central cluster, that was not giving anything at all.

The pre-server plant.

It stood untouched amid the dying forest around it, neither participating in the great exhale nor resisting it, simply present, the way it had always been present — older than the server, older than the war, older than whatever question the rest of the forest had just finished answering. It was, if anything, taller than she remembered.

You’re not part of this. You’re something else. I don’t know what yet.

There was no time to wonder further. The energy reached its peak.

Alondra screamed.

It was not a sound she chose. It tore out of her involuntarily, the body’s last honest response to a weight that exceeded anything a single nervous system was designed to carry, three hundred square kilometers of life converting through her chest in the span of seconds that felt, from inside them, like they lasted considerably longer. Her arms, still spread, shook violently. The green light at her palms flared blinding-bright.

Then she released it.

It left her all at once — a final pulse of light and warmth that washed outward across the entire contested zone, touching every B&B member still on that field simultaneously, the last and largest portion of everything the forest had given arriving in one decisive wave.

And Alondra fell.

She did not fall gently. The barrier she had cast at the start had long since dissolved, spent on holding her upright through the casting, and her body dropped from a height of several feet with nothing beneath it but scorched earth.

Phina caught her before the ground did.

Not physically — Phina was forty meters away, on her knees, Glimmer cradled against her chest, with nothing left in her own reserves for anything as demanding as a full catch spell. But she had enough. The barest minimum, the dregs of mana too small for anything else, and she used every drop of it on a single, simple casting: the earth beneath Alondra softened, just before impact, a cushion of suddenly-yielding soil that turned a fall that should have shattered bone into a fall that merely hurt.

Alondra landed in dirt that had become, for just long enough to matter, something closer to a held hand than ground.

She did not move after that.

Not dead — the system would have flagged that immediately, clearly, with the cold finality reserved for actual loss. Simply empty. A Saint of Bloom with nothing left to bloom, every reserve she had spent six years building drained in a single act she had chosen with her eyes open and would, if asked in this exact moment, choose again.

CONTESTED ZONE, CENTER — 15:52

Miles opened his eyes.

He could move. That was the first thing he understood — strength returning to limbs that had been past the point of strength five minutes ago, the burn along his side fading from agony to ache, Emberwing flaring back to full brightness against his collarbone with a small, indignant heat that felt almost like relief expressing itself the only way fire could.

He sat up.

Across the scorched field, he saw her — a small green shape on the ground, unmoving, Phina crawling the last few meters toward her on hands that didn’t have the strength left to crawl and doing it anyway.

He closed his eyes again. Briefly. Not in relief.

I told you. I told you this wasn’t something I wanted from you.

Then he opened them, and stood, because there was a war still technically in progress and a woman on the ground who needed someone to reach her, and grief — whatever this was, whatever name belonged to watching someone give everything for people who had never asked them to — was going to have to wait its turn behind the immediate fact of getting to her.
