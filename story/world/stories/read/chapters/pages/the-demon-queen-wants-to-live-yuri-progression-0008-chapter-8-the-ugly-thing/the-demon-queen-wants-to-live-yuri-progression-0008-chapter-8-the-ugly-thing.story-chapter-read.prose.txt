I knelt over the broken pieces.

The girl's gift had broken into five pieces. 
The largest of the five was no bigger than my palm. The smallest was a 
small chip, so small I might have missed it entirely. I took two of the 
largest pieces and pressed them together. Their edges did not match, not
 until I turned the second and pressed them again. The two shards matched, but 
the pieces came apart the moment I let go.

Mana. If I could fuse them with mana, then it
 would take. It was such a simple trick, one I had never thought to 
need. I reached for my power and called the mana to my fingertips and 
tried to push it into the clay. Even without the [System] I should be 
able to do this. The mana swirled against the surface and slipped away, 
the same way it had slipped from every technique I'd tried since 
arriving in this world. The pieces sat in my hands, still broken.

Why the hell was I doing this? I resisted the urge to punch the ground again, though it was strong.

I tried again. Again, it failed. I tried one 
more time, and failed one more time. I pocketed the pieces and stood. 
This was beneath me -wholly, thoroughly beneath me. I had conquered 
nations. I had burned a tree older than any flag. I had broken the spine
 of the world itself. It was beneath me to sit in the dirt and mourn a 
lump of mud.

I kept the pieces as I walked away. I had taken them on a whim, nothing more. There was only one thing that mattered. The marks.

I took a place at the edge of the village, my
 back against the low wall. It let me see all of Hamel's pitiful square 
in one look. If I needed to watch these humans to understand their 
strange powers, then I would.

The first was a woman who hauled water from a
 village well. The bucket was large, and water tipped from it as she 
walked. The weight of it should have been enough to strain her. She 
showed none of it. A large green dot on her forearm pulsed as she hauled
 the weight. That power was clearly physical reinforcement of some kind.

But when the woman tried to move the door of 
her hut, it did not give. She strained against it. The door had swollen 
shut in last night's rain, and it took the woman throwing her shoulder 
against it to finally budge it.

This reinforcement of hers was rather limited then. Was that her own failing, or a failing of her mark?

Next, I saw a boy standing next to a cooking 
pit. A flame flicked from his wrist at a snap of his fingers, and he 
used it to kindle the wood. This was a power I knew well. The elemental 
powers had always been the most obvious.

But his power had something strange. Smoke 
rose from the flame, and the boy had to wave it away with his hand. He 
did not move the air away, which meant that he could not. In my time, 
fire and air had always gone together for the classes that had them. It 
seemed his mark granted him only one of two conjoined powers.

Strange, to think of it as 'my time'.

That was two categories of these powers I now
 knew. Both of them simple. It was small progress, but it was progress. 
This was what mattered.

My eyes drifted to a girl who ran across the 
village square. She was chasing a small cat. The girl's face turned. It 
was not Sara, but one roughly her age. The girl was laughing. And in her
 I saw another girl, one whose lip had trembled, and whose voice had 
echoed, though it should have done neither. I forced my attention away.

It fell on a man who stood by a post at the 
edge of the village. He pressed his palm against the cracked post. The 
brown dot on his hand glowed and the split in the wood sealed as 
something from his hand projected outward.

He had given mana a form outside of the body. 
It was a kind of projection. Not an impossible power in my time, but 
certainly a rare one. One that even I had not possessed.

There were three categories then. Physical, 
elemental, and this...projection? I made these observations, but I did 
not examine them as deeply as I could and should have.

The pieces were still in my pocket. They felt
 like they stabbed me through the cloth. I rose and moved to a different
 spot and tried to focus.

It was like slowly filling a map, my generals
 getting the lay of the land before the marching of the horde. Each of 
their reports drove back the darkness of ignorance. I should have been 
fascinated.

These were powers unlike the ones I had spent
 so long mastering. I should have been fascinated. Curiosity had always 
been my strongest hunger...it should have swallowed everything else 
whole.

I still saw a girl's face in my mind, between
 every blink. I pressed my back against the wall and closed my eyes, 
trying to will the image away. It did not leave.

I returned to the room.

The bed was made and Ash's pillow no longer 
sat by the far wall. The single lantern was out. Light came in from the 
window. I sat on the edge of the bed and stared at the opposite wall. I 
had ordered villages burned. Not once, not twice. I had done it dozens 
of times, across the breadth of many wars. I had watched entire armies 
die beneath my magic and felt nothing.

The Dwarven capital had fallen in a single 
afternoon. There had been eighty thousand souls, by the lowest estimate.
 I had slept well that night. I had killed a God and slept well that 
night. Killed his sister, and slept well that night too.

One child cried, and I could not concentrate. The child was irrelevant.

The marks were what mattered. Power was what 
mattered. This world was dangerous, and in ways I did not understand. There had been proof of that already in the form of a corrupted Divine Beast. Who knew what else was out there? I could not afford distraction. It did not work.

It was not the scale that made it different. I
 knew that, even as I tried to pretend otherwise. It was simpler than 
that and far more infuriating. Before, there had been a wall between the
 act and the feeling. I had always assumed it was mine -a distance 
between me and petty things like guilt. Why would the Dragon feel guilt 
over the worm?

The Class. That wall had been the 
Class, and it was gone now. Strange, that I hardly felt a thing for the 
orders given a thousand years past.

For the Elven people of Osyrus, whom I'd had 
cut down to the last man simply for not prostrating themselves before 
me. For the Dwarven race, who would forge their masterworks no longer. 
For the land of Ashia, whose Gods I had killed, and which was, I had no 
doubt, still shrouded in perpetual darkness. Even a thousand years 
later.

I pulled the broken pieces from my pocket and
 laid them out on the bed. I would remake the vase. Not for the girl 
-that would be absurd. Simply to clear my head, so that I could return 
to studying the marks without this insipid distraction clawing at me. 
That was the reason.

I gathered the pieces and went to find mud. The path down to the river was muddy from the rain.

The riverbank was quiet. It was a place not 
far from the river, the same river from the forest, I was sure, just 
further downstream. What meandering brought it this close to the village
 I could not say. The villager I had asked had been only too eager to 
point me to it.

I laid the broken pieces out in a row on a 
flat stone, arranged by size. They were a reference. If I was going to 
make a new one, it should at least resemble the original.

I gathered a fistful of mud from the bank and
 sat cross-legged on the grass. The mud was cool and thick between my 
fingers and it caught under my nails. I used both hands together to form
 the base. There was too much force. My fingers tore through the mud.

I tried again.

This time, I gathered more mud and tried to 
press lighter. The shape slumped sideways before I'd even finished the 
base. It was too wet. I tried again. I took mud from further up the 
bank, where the ground was drier. This time the clay held its shape for a
 few moments before cracking apart in my hands -it was too dry.

I tried again.

I mixed the two types of mud, working them 
together with my palms the way I had seen potters do in my throne room, a
 lifetime ago. I had been bored, and one of my generals had brought me 
slaves. I had asked the men for their talents, promising them safety if 
they could keep my interest, even for an hour. Those potters had made it
 look effortless. It was not effortless.

This time, the mixture seemed right as I 
began to shape it. My hands were the problem -they were made for magic, 
made to swat down griffins, made to wield power that could reshape the 
world. They did not have the skill for this. They had never needed it. No matter how much care I gave, each press was either too hard or
 too soft, every attempt to thin the walls left them uneven.

"Damn it." I started over as the sun moved overhead. I did not track it.

There were more failed attempts. Before long,
 there was a pile of lopsided lumps beside me. Only one of them might 
have been passable. If only it had not developed a crack that split the 
entire thing in half when I set it down. I made many attempts. By the 
seventh, I forgot about the marks entirely. By the ninth, I stopped 
thinking about the girl.

At some point I noticed the dress Martha had 
given me. It was ruined. There was mud streaking up my arms. I felt it 
dot my chin where I'd wiped sweat without thinking. There was mud on my 
face, on a face whose pale beauty had struck terror and awe as far as 
there were people to think.

The tenth attempt was the closest of them 
all. The thing did not crack when I put it down. There were flaws. Its 
walls were slightly uneven, but the shape was clearly still a vase. I 
tried to smooth the rim with my thumb. The rim caved inward, and then 
the rest of it buckled in on itself. I set what remained on the ground 
and pressed both palms flat against my knees.

Then I slammed my fist into the ground. 
Footsteps made me turn. It was only Ash. The Hero walked to the bank 
carrying a boot in one hand and a knife in the other. She glanced at me 
once, and I felt her take in all of me. The mud covering my hands, my 
arm, my face. She had clearly come to find me. Who had told her? The 
hero sat a few paces away, on a large stone. She began cutting at the 
boot's sole. I ignored her and began my eleventh attempt.

Neither of us said anything, and so the 
scraping of the knife filled the clearing. My eleventh attempt was 
perhaps one of my worst yet. I had not even managed to form the base. I 
cursed and started again. The next attempt's base fared no better. I 
cursed louder.

If I had been even a fraction of what I was, I
 would have laid waste to this entire forest. But I was not a fraction 
of myself. I could reach for no spell. I could not even shape mud with 
my hands.

Then there was something in the clearing 
other than the scraping of a knife. "You need to wet your hands first" I
 froze and turned to her. "Wet your hands first. You keep trying with 
the clay, that's why it keeps fighting you…just wet your hands first."

She had offered me help I had not asked for. I
 could have reminded her of that. Reminding her would not make the shape
 take. So I turned back and dipped my hands into the river, letting the 
cool water run between my fingers.

I took some time to gather myself, and then I
 gathered the clay. On my next attempt, I felt the difference. The clay 
obeyed my hands. I formed a base that did not immediately give in when I set it down. When I formed the walls, one layer at a time, they all held.

I did not sigh, but the sound I made might 
have been close to one. I offered the Hero no thanks. She had continued 
her scraping.  It took some time before I finally stared at a vase that 
was close to adequate. It was still terrible by any measure.

It was lopsided. One of its sides was too 
thick, the other was clearly too thin. I had made the base too wide on 
the left, and it made the whole thing slightly lean in one direction. It
 might topple if the wind changed. Then there were the many cracks along
 the base. It would leak within seconds if I ever dared fill it with 
anything.

The girl's creation had been better. That vase had been far more symmetrical. A child had outperformed the Demon Queen.

I gingerly held the vase up and turned it. I 
paid more attention to the vase than I had to those humans' strange 
marks. This vase was the worst thing I had ever made.

It was the only thing I had ever made.

I turned to Ash. The Hero was watching the 
river. She had not looked away in some time. "Sara's usually by the big 
tree at this hour," Ash said. "The big oak near the west side of the 
wall. I'm sure you've seen it."

I had seen it. I stood and was careful to cup the vase with both hands. "This is not for her," I said. Ash said nothing.

By the oak near the west side of Hamel, I 
found the girl. It was broad enough that four men could have hidden 
behind it. Sara sat at its base, drawing shapes in the dirt with a 
pointed stick. She looked up when my shadow fell over her.

Her body went rigid in an instant. Something 
tightened around her eyes. Then she pulled back, and pressed her back 
against the tree.

I had spent centuries reading the fear on faces. I had rarely seen it so plainly.

For a time, I stood in place as she sat in 
hers. There were only ten paces between us. It might as well have been 
ten thousand. I had crossed the flaming river, once. A place of black 
fire, stretching endlessly in all directions. I could not approach the 
child sitting in front of me.

So I did the only thing left to me. I took 
one step forward and held the vase out, arms extended full forward. Men 
had given me tribute with the same rigidity.

I spoke, and I did not recognize my own 
voice. "I broke yours. This is its replacement." The girl was still 
frozen, and she said nothing. The silence stretched long enough that I 
considered simply setting the thing on the ground and leaving. There was
 no need for whatever this was.

The girl's eyes slowly moved from my face to 
the vase. They moved from the vase to my hands. To my skin and my palms 
covered in the mud. The girl stared for a long time.

Then the tightening in her eyes softened just
 a fraction. She got up and walked forward. She reached out with both 
hands and took the vase from me. Then she held it up in the light and 
turned it over as she examined it.

The vase had not changed. The rim was still 
lopsided. There were more cracks in it than I remembered. She still held
 it against her chest. "Thank you," she said. The words were quiet, no 
more than a whisper. Her smile was not quiet.

It made no sense. The scales did not balance.
 Her vase had been better. In every measurable way, her vase had been 
the superior creation. This one was uglier and more uneven. It could 
hold water even less than hers might have. It could not hold anything.

The girl turned and ran, clutching the vase 
to her chest with both hands. She rounded the corner of the nearest hut,
 and then she was gone. I stood beneath the oak, and did not move for 
some time.

I found Ash where I'd left her.

She was still by the river. She had repaired 
her boot, and now she sat with her bare legs stretched out in front of 
her. I sat down beside her and joined her in her river gazing.

"It was worse than hers," I said after a 
time. "By any standard of measurement I can imagine, it was inferior. 
And she still took it. She took it as if it were something precious." My
 voice was quieter than I had ever heard it. "Why?"

The river moved past us. Somewhere downstream, a fish broke past the surface.

"Because you made it yourself."

I looked at her. It still made no sense. "It 
is a physical object. It has measurable qualities. It has functions, and
 it either fulfills them, or it does not."

"It does function," Ash said. "Just not the way you're measuring."

I opened my mouth. Nothing came out. My old 
world had been so simple. A spell that couldn't kill was discarded, a 
vase that couldn't hold water was nothing. And yet.

"You don't have to understand it," Ash said. "Many never do."

I did not argue with her. But I saw it again,
 later, when I passed by the window of the hut nearest to the oak. It 
sat on a shelf, next to a wilting flower. The ugliest vase in Hamel, and
 likely the ugliest in the world.

It had already been given a place.
