Chapter 11

"Observation: the Nadi Matrix uses RPG terminology because the Curators spent fifty years embedding RPG mechanics into human culture. We're not playing a game that looks like reality. We're living a reality that games were designed to look like. The implications of this are either terrifying or hilarious and I haven't decided which."- The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr, London

The Bloomberg building rose from a wide stone plaza that should have been full of commuters heading for the Tube. Tonight it was a dark glass monolith reflecting the sky. Somewhere below it was the Mithraeum.

If that was what the riddle was pointing to. Whatever it was, it was close enough that the frequency in his skull had stopped pulling and started humming like a tensioned cable.

Donna slowed at the edge of the plaza, her hand snapping up to signal a halt.

"Movement. North side. By the van." She said, her voice dropping.

Beneath the dead silence of the city, a sharp skittering sound echoed off the stone. Multiple sets of claws moved in a loose, shifting pattern that was deliberate and coordinated. The sound of animals that knew exactly where their prey was and were positioning themselves accordingly.

Then three shapes separated from the shadows.

They had been London foxes once. The kind that screamed at three am, raided bins with impunity, and occasionally locked eyes with you on a tube platform with the supreme indifference of animals that had stopped fearing humans generations ago.

These were no longer those foxes.

They were the size of medium-large dogs, their bodies distorted by muscle growth that pushed against skin not equipped to contain it. Their fur had thinned to coarse bristles and their eyes produced a faint sick blue light that pulsed in rhythm with something inside them.

They moved together, one holding the centre and two flanking, with the discipline of a hunting unit that had been working as a pack long before the sky changed and since then had simply become much better at it.

Donna read the formation instantly. "The centre one will drive the attack while the flankers cut off retreat. They have done this before."

Arthur raised the shield. "Steve, behind me. Donna, take whichever one commits first."

He was thinking in D&D encounters. Three hostiles, tight formation, open ground. The plaza offered no chokepoints or terrain to funnel them. It was terrible ground for defenders. The smartest play was to anchor at the building entrance where the doorway would limit the approach to a single creature.

"Back up towards the doors. Slowly. Do not turn around." He said, in a low voice.

They'd made it four steps when the centre fox charged.

It came straight at the shield. Arthur braced and caught it square, the impact rang through the bin lid and up into his shoulder like a bell struck with a hammer. Claws raked the corrugated steel in a shrieking scrape. Jaws snapped at the rim, searching for fingers, for anything soft. Arthur shoved forward, driving the creature back, and brought the crowbar down across its flank. The fox yelped and twisted away.

The flankers hit them simultaneously.

The one on the left went for Donna. She'd been waiting for it, her weight low, the axe cocked, and her body was rotating into the strike even before the animal had fully sprung. The blade caught it mid-air in the ribs and the fox crumpled sideways onto the stone with a wet, truncated sound.

The one on the right went for Steve.

He jabbed the billhook forward, thrust first, like Donna had told him. The curved blade caught the fox across the shoulder, opening a bloody line through the bristled hide. But the animal was faster than the pole and Steve’s ability to wield it, the fox twisted inside the billhook's reach and Steve's advantage vanished in an instant. Jaws closed on his forearm below the elbow.

The sound Steve made was the involuntary, guttural noise a body produces when it registers damage before the brain could process it. He staggered sideways, the fox hanging from his arm, its weight dragging him off balance.

Arthur couldn't help him. The centre fox had recovered and was coming again; low and fast, aiming for his legs this time. He dropped the shield edge downward and caught the animal against the paving stones. It thrashed beneath the metal as Arthur brought the crowbar down onto the skull. The thrashing stopped.

Donna had moved to Steve. She had to be careful, the fox was clamped onto his hand and a missed strike could take his hand off instead. She reversed the axe and drove the flat of the head into the fox's spine. The jaws loosened just enough for Steve to wrench his arm free. The billhook came down a second later, the strike, messy and desperate, the curved blade hooked behind the creature's neck and pulled sideways. The fox dropped.

The first fox, the one Donna had hit in the ribs, was trying to drag itself away across the plaza, leaving a dark smear on the stone. Donna crossed to it and finished it with a single downward stroke.

***

Steve slumped down. His legs folded and the bollard behind him caught his weight. His face was the colour of candle wax and his left arm hung at his side, blood running from the crescent of puncture wounds and dripping off his fingertips onto the stone.

Donna knelt beside him and pulled a compression bandage from her pack. Her hands were steady, her jaw was not. Arthur could see the muscle working, the adrenaline she was processing through clenched teeth rather than letting it show on her face.

Arthur's own hands were trembling. His shoulder ached from the shield impacts and the scratches on his arm from the alley fight had opened further during this one, blood mixed with sweat and stung beneath his torn sleeve. The crowbar felt like it weighed twice what it had ten minutes ago.

Three foxes. Forty seconds. And they were wrecked.

"I would like it noted," Steve said, his voice thin but steady, "that my billhook successfully wounded the creature before it wounded me."

"Noted," Donna said, wrapping the bandage tight. "Also noted that it bit you anyway."

"The two observations are—" Steve hissed as the fabric pressed into the punctures. "—are not mutually exclusive, are they?”

“Arthur, tell her, it was a tactical trade-off. Proper historical vanguard work, that was."

"Looked more like you were trying to feed it your jacket. Next time, remember the reach on that pole, you are supposed to keep them at arm's length." Arthur said, wiping a mix of sweat and grime from his forehead with his sleeve.

"Brilliant. Critical analysis from the bloke who fought a mutant with a modified bin lid," Steve muttered, though his waxen face twitched with a faint, shaky grin.

The construct's geometry assembled beside the nearest bollard, the lines compressed and turning slowly. It had not appeared during the fight.

"Threat response coordination exceeded initial projections," it stated. "Marginally."

Arthur looked at Steve bleeding through the bandage, then down at his own torn sleeve. "Marginally," he said. "I'd hate to see what a bad night looks like."

Then the shift came, but it was stronger this time. As though something inside his nervous system had just crossed a threshold it had been approaching and the entire network had rearranged itself in response.

Blue text assembled in his vision.

THREAT NEUTRALISED

ETHER ABSORBED: +45

LEVEL UP: LEVEL 2

INTEGRATION: 15 / 119

ATTRIBUTE POINTS AVAILABLE: 3

The text faded and the slow healing began.

Arthur saw it happen on Steve's arm first, because Steve's wound was the worst, and he was staring at it with the fixed, unblinking attention of a man watching his own biology do something his education said was impossible.

The puncture wounds were closing, very slowly, but visibly. The edges of torn skin pulled together with a faint purple luminescence.

Donna looked at her own shoulder, where a claw had opened a shallow gash during the fight and saw the same thing happening. She watched it for a few seconds, then pulled her sleeve down and said nothing.

Arthur checked his own scratches, the skin was warm, the luminescence faint but unmistakable. His body was repairing itself with an energy that hadn't been there an hour ago.

"Regenerative response within projected parameters," the construct noted without elaborating on what those parameters were, or who had projected them.

“That,” Steve said, still staring at his arm, “is profoundly unsettling.” He looked at the construct. “How? The puncture wounds are closing. What’s providing the energy for tissue regeneration at this rate?”

“Ether integration has reached a threshold that enables baseline cellular repair. The mechanism is autonomous.”

"So our wounds mend with Ether," Donna said, her voice dropping into a dangerous, level tone. "And you didn’t think that was worth a bloody mention before the fight."

The construct’s geometry rotated once, unhurried. It did not elaborate.

Arthur flexed his fingers. The trembling had stopped. His shoulder still ached, but the ache was receding. Something had changed inside him, he felt it as a shift in the baseline.

He felt slightly denser, as though his body had just been tightened by a fraction of a turn, a bolt brought from loose to firm. He looked at his hands. Nothing had changed visibly, the calluses from fifteen years of site work were still there, so was the scar across his left knuckle from a chisel slip in his second year.

But the skin beneath was warm, humming at a frequency he could almost hear. A few hours ago, these hands had been holding a pint glass. He’d been laughing at something Steve said about some sewage drain. Now they were covered in fox blood and repairing themselves.

He didn’t say anything. There wasn’t a sentence that fit this absurdity.

"Three attribute points," Steve said. He'd noticed the notification. It was not the sort of thing he would miss even with a bleeding arm. "The system just gave us three attribute points."

Donna stood slowly, testing her weight on her legs. "I can feel something, sitting unspent. Like potential energy that hasn't been told where to go yet."

"Same," Arthur said. It wasn't a sensation he could describe precisely. Just an awareness that something inside him was available and uncommitted. Waiting for a decision.

Donna was watching her own forearm where the claw gash had been. She prodded the new skin with her thumb and watched it give and resettle.

"The attributes, we hold the allocation. All three of us." She said.

Steve's head came up sharply. "What?"

"Our bodies just healed without permission. I didn't authorise that and I don't know what it cost me or will cost me." She looked at her hand. "Now this system is offering us another change and it is voluntary this time. Which means we get to decide. And I am not deciding in a plaza full of dead foxes with a pull dragging us towards something we can't see."

"It's points, Donna. It's..."

"It's biology." She looked down at him. "You don't know if allocating Strength puts you on your back for an hour. You don't know if it spikes a fever. You don't know if the system needs you sitting still while it restructures whatever it restructures. We don't modify biology in environments we can't control. That's the rule."

Arthur was nodding. The same instinct kept him from load-testing a beam that was carrying a roof.

"She's right, Steve. Inside a secure building. Or somewhere we can lock a door. Whichever comes first."

"For the record, this is the part of the campaign where the DM watches the wizard sulk." Steve sighed.

"You always sulk," Donna said.

Arthur walked towards the Bloomberg building's entrance, the shield on his arm, the crowbar in his hand, and three unspent attribute points sitting in his nervous system like a charge waiting to be directed.

Below the lobby, below the concrete and the service corridors and two thousand years of accumulated London, the Temple of Mithras was waiting. The pull in his skull said so. The riddle said so. And whatever was down there, he was walking towards it with a team of three who had just levelled up, were bleeding through their bandages, and hadn't eaten since the pub.
