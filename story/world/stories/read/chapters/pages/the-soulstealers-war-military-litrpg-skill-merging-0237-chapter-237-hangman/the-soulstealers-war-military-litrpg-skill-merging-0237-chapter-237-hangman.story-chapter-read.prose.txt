The orc archers got off one more volley before we crashed into them. This time, the arrows flew nearly horizontally as we approached.

Since I had time to prepare, I created a pair of reversed wells with my Art, both aiming at a single point ahead of me.

When the arrows passed through it, my Art caught it and flung them back the opposite direction, sending them scattershot into the orc lines. The beasts crumbled, collapsing onto their backs, or flung off their feet with how much I’d accelerated the arrows.

The volley didn’t get all of them, though. Our riders charged through their exposed lines, and they crumpled in front of us. Who would put archers in front of a spear formation, all exposed like that?

Unless the goal was to force us to deal with one last threat, keeping us here just a little longer and sacrificing a few pawns to destroy a far more important unit.

And we had no choice, because if we left the archers alive, they’d skewer us from behind, taking out our horses and killing more of us than otherwise necessary.

I hated being forced into a situation I couldn’t get out of, but it wasn’t over yet. We just had to kill the archers before whatever trap they had in mind could spring.

We blasted out the other side of what remained of the orc archer formation. I pointed my spear toward Hyrdford, then shouted, “Back to the city! Now!”

Before we could move, the ground exploded in front of us. A massive fungal growth blasted out of the ground, throwing chunks of soil and stones everywhere. Burr reared up on her hind legs, and a horse beside me threw itself onto its back, tossing off its rider. I could barely see the fungal growth through the shroud of dirt.

I used my Perception Skill. Ahead, there was nothing, but behind us—aside from the regular Iron-tier orcs—was a Gold.

A Hangman. It had to be.

He must’ve been a druid-type, with his ability to create massive fungal growths like that.

As the dust cleared, I set a hand on Burr’s neck, calming her down and trying to take stock of the situation. Massive gray stems had blasted out of the ground in front of us, covered in hair and dripping slimy spores. The mushrooms had spear-shaped caps, and dark vines stretched out from beneath the caps, reaching toward my riders. They reminded me of the segmented folds beneath a mushroom’s cap—don’t ask me what those were actually called; biology wasn’t my strongest subject.

I swatted one away with my spear before it could wrap around me, but another grabbed a Dupe beside me, dragging him off his horse by his neck. The tentacles pulled him in toward the epicenter of the blast, but his body disintegrated before he ever arrived. It decayed while he was still alive, turning into black and brown sludge.

Without hesitation, I triggered my Art above the fungal formation. It was just a basic Art, aiming straight down but it was strong enough to rip apart the head of a few mushrooms. The others collapsed around it, and their tentacles flailed.

“Go!” I yelled. “Ride as fast as you can! There aren’t enough to catch all of us!”

I led the way to the left, navigating around the side where the tentacles were the thickest still. If I could prevent any of them from hitting my men, I would.

As we charged, I deflected three tentacles that raced for my neck. They tried to rip me off my horse like they were nooses.

When I passed the fungal structure, I looked back, making sure nothing was about to grab me from behind. I watched it rip a pair of Dupes off their horses, grabbing them by their necks. One died in its grasp. His throat rotted into a wet, brown slurry, and his head broke off while still gasping for air. The second cut off his tentacle with a sword, but it had been holding him for too long, and he died regardless, still begging for air.

Behind us, and through the dust, a silhouette hovered over the main orc army. It was an orc itself, but it had to be three heads taller than the others, and its flesh was so pale I could see the black veins beneath its skin. It wore nothing but a set of tattered pants—its entire upper body was bare.

The beast had a bald head, and crimson mushrooms grew off the side of its face. It held a heavy executioner’s sword in one hand—a blade without a completely flat tip. It was a foot wide, and at least five feet long, and it was chipped and rusty all along its length.

I didn’t know how many times I was going to think it tonight, but that had to be the Hangman.

We weren’t here to fight it, though.

“Keep riding!” I yelled.

The Hangman pointed its sword, and another patch of giant mushrooms erupted in front of us, blasting out of the dirt. I steered Burr to the right, leading the entire army around them. This time, we had more room to maneuver, but we’d sped up, and not every horse could turn as tightly as Burr could. Some toppled over, and others charged straight through the center of the cluster.

I used one more art, this time hitting the cluster from the side. It was far more effective, tearing off most of the mushrooms’ heads and sending them flying out over the horizon. The rest only managed to grab two more riders before we passed.

After that, no more clusters appeared. The Hangman wasn’t chasing us, and we had an open route to Hyrdford. The gate was still open, but the wall was complete. They’d attached the last merlons and finished the ramparts around the back, even if the mortar was still wet.

Most of the civilians had made it inside the wall. A few wagons raced for the gate. When they saw us, they picked up their pace even more.

Behind us, orc horns sounded. Footsteps thudded, armour thumped, and the main orc army was on the move. Fell-fox riders whooped, and their mounts yipped, as they raced ahead to catch us.

“Faster!” I yelled. We needed to get back through the gate, and in time for them to close the heavy doors.

I pulled to the side as we approached, letting the others through, then circled around to intercept any other attacks. A fell-fox rider loosed an arrow from horseback, but I swatted it out of the air with my spear as it fell, sending it harmlessly into the grass.

From the wall, a shout rang out. A commander yelled, “Archers, draw!”

Hundreds of archers stepped forward, and the wall’s torches flared to light, revealing the true numbers within the wall.

“Loose!”

Bowstrings softly thwupped, and orange-feathered arrows whistled across the sky. They struck fell-fox riders off their mounts, or killed the mounts themselves. After the first volley, a few foxes made it through, but after the second volley, nothing was moving.

The last of my riders trotted through the gate, alongside the last of the wagons, and I followed behind. As soon as I passed through, a pair of Dupes on each side began pushing the doors shut. They were simple wooden gates, but far thicker than any other door I’d seen. Despite the lack of ornaments on their exterior, their size and sturdiness alone impressed me. Was it still going to be the weakest part of the fortress?

As soon as I passed the gates, I stopped in a plaza of packed mud and dismounted, then handed Burr’s reins to a regular Dupe. “Bring her to the stable,” I instructed.

“Yes, my lord,” the Dupe whispered.

“Where’s Lord Ceonwin?”

“He’s above the gate, overseeing the defense.”

I nodded, then sprinted to the stairs beside the gate as the Dupe led Burr back to the stables.

I found Lord Ceonwin standing at the very center of the ramparts above the gate, wearing his full armour, though with his helmet tucked under his arm. A Dupe commander stood beside him, carrying a signal standard for the archers.

“My lord!” I called. “They’re marching!”

“You did well,” Lord Ceonwin replied.

“Maybe,” I said. “We lost about a quarter of our riders.”

“That’s war. Their sacrifice will not be forgotten.”

I scowled, but he was right. Still, the callousness that he spoke with…well, it was probably necessary. It was the voice of a man putting aside his feelings for a battle, so he could do what necessary.

“We found the Hangman,” I reported. “It’s a druid…ish. Massive mushroom structures, that sort of thing.”

“If we close the distance, his main Art will be weaker. He won’t be able to use it against us.”

“Yeah, but if he can grow them in the city…”

“How was his range?”

I chewed my lip, trying to estimate in my head. “About two-hundred paces.”

“Then if he attacks the city, we’ll know he is close. You and I, Yesman’s squad, and Saeglaf and his apprentice will engage him.”

I raised my eyebrows. “Outside the wall?”

“We’ll drive him away from our men and from his own army.”

I nodded.

“Lord Levi…” Ceonwin whispered. He coughed, then added, “Soon, you must begin making your own judgements about battles. I will not be here forever.”

“When I reveal myself, I’ll be ready,” I promised, keeping my voice just as low as his. I leaned forward on the ramparts, squinting at the approaching orc army. With the sound of clomping hooves and whistling arrows quieted, all I could hear were the distant howls of the orc force and the whistling wind. They snuffed their torches and advanced across the pale-purple lit ground as a shadow.

“Understood,” Lord Ceonwin replied. “You had best reveal yourself sooner than later, however.”

He was only giving a suggestion this time. We were equals in rank.

“Sir, with the Hangmen…” I began. “Are they all like that?”

“Not all of them. There are wicca and sorcerers as well.”

“I mean… do they all go for the necks? Because I can see how they got their name. But it just seems like a very odd strategy.”

“Typically, yes. We don’t know why, except perhaps as a symbolic measure. Their goal, above all, is to demoralize. Do not let them.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You don’t have to speak like that anymore. Stand by my side, and help me hold the gate. The men will follow your commands as if they were my own.”
