“What do you need me to do?” Patrice asked.

Eola hunched over the table. Her fingers flew over the unfinished modifying mark and the others she’d learned, trying desperately to find similarities. Next to her, Colin traced the same marks with his eyes. He was reading along, his shoulder bumping into hers and his brow furrowed as he tried to see the patterns in her notes.

They’d been working for only a handful of minutes, but even that short time was more than they could afford. She needed an answer now. Not later. Now. And that—and her pure, unadulturated—

A squeak interrupted her as Roth missed a note.

Colin glanced over his shoulder at Roth. Eola didn’t. She knew what she’d see, and the motion would take time—time Roth didn’t have. She wanted to kick herself for not figuring this out sooner and for letting him start the Soul Bleeding process, but that wouldn’t help them, either.

“S’cuse me, but I want to know how to help. Or, if you don’t need me, I’m going to take a nap.”

“You’re seriously thinking about sleep right now, with everything that’s happening?” Colin locked eyes with Patrice.

“Well, yes.” Patrice shifted, lying on the floor and somehow managing to look comfortable in her armor. “Just wake me up when we’re ready, Colin. Or if Eola needs help. Then again, I am the worst mage in Varin’s, and you probably need someone with talent to figure out all of that.”

“You’re unbelievable, Patrice,” Colin muttered.

“And yet you’re still friends with me,” the tall girl shot back, then snored dramatically before he could say anything else. Her hand wrapped around the haft of her hammer, though, and it all felt…faked. Patrice was just as tense as everyone else.

As Colin grumbled to himself and soft flute music wafted from where Roth sat against the door, Eola focused. She’d had plenty of time to think about the nature of modifying marks in Ordered Magic and her spell chains. Days and days of studying. This should have been done already. If she wanted to help her friends and prove herself any sort of mage, she’d have to learn it now. And she’d use her pure, unfiltered fear of failure to make herself do it.

Modifying marks in Ordered Magic were the domain of battle mages and spellswords. They were only used to change the pacing, punchiness, or effect of Child’s Magic—and even then, only mages on the dueling circuit, those who fought other mages, and the ones who ventured into dungeons routinely needed that flexibility. For most mages, modifying marks were a waste of Mana. They could afford the extra time for a First or Second Order spell. One quirk of Ordered Magic was that casting a First Order spell was less Mana-intensive than casting more than one modifying mark to a cantrip.

Of course, this meant that all of Eola’s research had turned up nothing. No one had researched modifying marks since Varin’s discovery of Ordered Magic. They were redundant and unnecessary, so if they weren’t useful for combat, no one drew them, and even fewer mages remembered them.

But Eola needed them. Those marks still existed. They were still folded into Ordered Magic runes. This one had to be no different, but she hadn’t found it in any of the runes she knew, or in any sketches she’d borrowed from Instructor Tarik.

Colin touched Eola’s hand, and her attention snapped onto him. “Look at this pattern and tell me what you think.”

She stared at his finger as it touched a half-dozen different marks, one after another, then shook her head. “I have no idea what you’re trying to—“

“Okay. Great. That’s what I was hoping.”

Patrice cleared her throat from the corner, and Colin glanced over his shoulder, then kept explaining. “These marks all have the same patterns near their bases. It’s like an empty space, but that empty space is…it’s hard to see.”

Eola nodded, touching the same spot on the rune. Her finger bumped Colin’s. “Okay. And…?”

“And your half-finished mark doesn’t have that emptiness. It can’t attach to a rune the way it’s constructed. That’s why you’re not finding it in any rune arrays, and why your spells keep backlashing when you add it. It can’t fit in them.”

“That’s great, but can you tone it down?” Patrice asked.

“Aren’t you supposed to be sleeping?” Colin replied without looking.

“You two are being loud.”

“Sorry,” Eola said. “We’re trying to reconstruct magic from nothing here.”

As Patrice huffed and muttered to herself, Eola ignored her as best she could. All she cared about was figuring out this strange mark before Eddarn broke in or Roth’s Soul Bleeding got out of control. They had to find a solution—and like Varin’s mistress had said, they had to find the best one.

But right now, Eola would settle for one that worked.

Eddarn’s voice cut through Colin and Eola’s muttered conversation.

“Alright, girlie, you’re a sneaky rat. You ent going to come out, and I ent going to leave. I know you and your little friends are here, though.” He hadn’t moved from his chair, and the book monster stalked overhead, somewhere on the room’s second or third story walkway. “Eventually, you’ll run out of patience, Tabby Cat, and when you do, I’ll be here. The cult needs fresh blood, and you’re exactly who we’re hungry for. You and that cursed cat.”

Eola did her best to ignore him. Her throat was try, but she couldn’t stop for a sip of water. It’d be one thing if they’d broken the code, or even if they’d made any progress.

But they hadn’t.

After going in a circle for the third time, Eola stared at the ceiling and groaned. “This isn’t working, Colin. This mark doesn’t have any patterns in common with modifying marks. I can’t figure out how to use it.”

“Make it easy on yourselves. I’ll even let your friends go, girlie,” Eddarn said.

Eola swallowed. Colin didn’t have any answers, and Roth was running out of time. Soul Bleeding wasn’t meant to be used for long-term casting. Madame Reyanna had said it was an emergency measure, but right now, Roth was using it as his main source of Mana. The impact on his body was already becoming all too apparent; his face was a caricature of pain, and his veins stood out across his exposed hands and wrists.

She didn’t have a solution.

Or, more accurately, she did have one. It just…wasn’t a good one.

“I’m going to surrender,” she said after a moment.

“No!” Colin gasped.

“You can’t!” Patrice said. Her eyes snapped open, and she glared at Eola. “We’re not giving up on this.”

“It’s the best move we’ve got. Roth can’t do this much longer, and he needs to get out of here and to the hospital. You three can go, and I’ll…figure something out.” Eola didn’t have any idea what that something would be, but at least her friends would be safe. She’d failed them over and over since they started the Library Exam; this was the least she could do for them.

She cast Voice Throw—a piece of Child’s Magic kids used for hide and seek—and started talking. Her voice echoed from the floating room’s shelves. “You’ll really let them go?”

“Even better than that, girlie,” Eddarn said. “I’ll point them toward the exit. Your friend ent burning pure Mana anymore, is he? I can smell the Soul Bleeding. You can get him the help he needs. Just give this farce up.”

Eola opened her mouth, but before she could say anything else, Colin shook his head and pointed at the mark on the page. His eyes were wide, but he wouldn’t say anything. Instead, he tapped the mark over and over.

But the moment Eola dismissed her spell, he started talking and wouldn’t stop. “Eola, I don’t think this is a modifying mark at all. I’ve been trying to find the pattern for one in it, and there’s not even part of one. This rune has nothing in common with modifying marks, except that it’s in Forsooth Script.”

“So what are you saying, then?” Eola asked.

“That this doesn’t fit the pattern for a modifying mark, but it does fit a different one. Where did you learn this from again?”

Atta’s eyes locked on Eola’s. The cat stared for a moment, then licked her paw and curled into a ball on the dust-covered table. Eola had no idea what that meant. It’d be so much easier if the cat would just talk when other people were around; she clearly knew something.

Not that she’d be able to tell Eola anything useful.

“I need Varin’s journal,” Eola said quickly.

“Why?” Colin asked, but he dug through his satchel and handed her the empty binding with only half a page in it. “Where did you learn this mark?”

“Magecraft is about finding the best solutions, not the most realistic ones. Cut away the fetters of preconception, believe the world is what you can make it, and magic will meet you there,” Eola read. “That’s the answer. We’re looking for realistic solutions, not the best ones. We’ve been doing this wrong. I’ve been doing this wrong.”

“Are you surrendering or not, girlie?” Eddarn asked from outside.

“Where did you learn the mark?” Colin asked, voice almost frantic.

“I learned it from the trog shaman. But that doesn’t make sense. Monsters use magic, but it’s not properly Ordered Magic. It’s not the same as what I do, either, so this…this couldn’t be a modifying mark,” Eola said.

“Ow!” Colin said as he stood up quickly enough to knock his knees against the table. Eola winced in sympathy, but he kept talking through it. “Ow. Okay. That explains the patterns thing. It doesn’t look like a modifier, doesn’t behave like a modifier, so it’s not one. What is it?”

Eola nodded slowly. Colin was right. This didn’t fit into a pattern. Forsooth Script was a simple language, with clear rules that it never violated. Modifying marks all connected to the words they modified in specific ways. The letters and words became one, and meanings shifted, like prefixes and suffixes in Alemic. The same thing happened in magic. Four marks, one Old Alemic rune. The modifying marks changed the ideogram. And the same thing also happened in Ordered Magic.

But this symbol didn’t fit like a modifying mark.

She’d been trying to make it connect to an Old Alemic rune. It had looked like a modifying mark, and all modifying marks were in Forsooth Script. The dozen she’d learned all were, and she knew more than probably anyone. And it looked like an incomplete sketch of one.

But it wasn’t one.

Eola’s eyes widened. “What is a modifying mark, anyway? Does it have to modify a cantrip, or could it modify other things It can’t modify Ordered Magic, but what if this…modifies a modifying mark?”

Atta’s eyes snapped open. Her head flicked over to stare at Eola, and just like that, she knew. She had it. Colin—that too-caring, too-precise Colin—may have inadvertantly handed her the answer to the riddle, and most likely, their way to save Roth, themselves, and just maybe, their chance of passing the Library Exam.

Now all she had to do was prove it.

Patrice glared sleeplessly at Eola from the corner as Colin sketched his healing ritual. He was working as quickly as he could, but even so, Eola was very aware of every second passing. His perfectionism was agnonizing, frustrating for everyone in the room, and absolutely necessary.

Eola was pretty sure she was right. She’d been ready to burst out of the door right then and there, but Roth had stopped her. Through gritted teeth, he’d insisted that he could hold on a little longer, and that they had to know this would work—and that meant a test run. His magic had almost faded in the pause, and he’d had to pick up the flute again right after.

Patrice’s magic wouldn’t work for this, and Roth was busy—which left Colin’s rituals.

The healing ritual was a beautiful thing.

He’d drawn it at three times its usual size so Eola could see what he was doing—a concession she hadn’t gotten the first time she’d seen it, when she’d felt the need to steal it from him instead. It wouldn’t affect the amount of Mana needed, but it did let Eola find the Echoing mark hidden inside of the nearly-perfect rune array. He’d finished it first, and right now, he was finishing up the Peacebonded mark’s connections to the Old Alemic rune in the middle.

He didn’t know that, but to Eola, it was painfully obvious.

Teachers usually drew larger than necessary when lecturing, and Colin had picked up the skill naturally. It was all just patterns, after all, and the patterns didn’t care how large they were—only that the spacing stayed proportional.

“Okay, finished. Filling in now,” Colin said.

Mana poured into the ritual array, and Eola saw it.

The answer. Right where she thought it would be.

It wasn’t in the marks’ connections. Those were always the same: two points of contact, whether they crossed the rune, swirled through it, or touched without intersecting. Never more. Never less. Her new mark couldn’t do that. It had the wrong number of possible crossing points, and they didn’t fit right on the Old Alemic.

They weren’t designed to modify Old Alemic at all. They were designed to modify Forsooth Script.

Eola sketched her mark. But she didn’t sketch it as part of her own spell. Instead, she layered it directly on top of the Forsooth mark for Echoing.

The mark forced its way onto the symbol. The entire ritual ideogram shook in mid-air, and Colin’s forehead broke out in sweat as the rune started to fracture. “Eola, what in Y’aer’s name are you—“

“Shut it, Colin,” she half-shouted. “I’m testing my—“

Echoing vanished. Her new mark appeared where it had been, disconnected from the rest of the healing ritual’s structure but filling the space. The ritual’s shaking stopped. Colin’s Mana stalled, and the entire pattern drained and started to disintegrate.

Then Eola’s Mana poured into it, and the spell finished.

Healing energy poured from the symbol as it hung mid-air for a few seconds before vanishing. Eola waited for it to repeat, but it didn’t. Whatever she’d done, it had cut the Echoing out of it.

And with that cut, Colin’s powerful, First Order healing ritual had regressed. When the spell had cast, it had cast as Child’s Magic. As a cantrip. A modified cantrip, with three marks—which was why Colin’s Mana had faltered. He hadn’t been ready for the cost.

Eola pulled up her shirt sleeve. Her bruises were still very much present. They’d yellowed, like they’d been there for a few days, but they certainly weren’t healed, and her side and chest were still sore from the beating she’d taken. The spell Colin had cast should have left her feeling better, and it should have persisted and kept healing her until the ritual stopped.

It shouldn’t have ended after a single pulse. That was the behavior of Child’s Magic, not of a First Order ritual. What she’d done had forced Colin’s spell to cast as a weaker version of itself. It was unbelievable. Impossible. But, like Varin’s mistress had said, by cutting away her preconceptions, she’d found a new, powerful solution.

“What did you do?” Colin asked, eyes narrowing and fists balled.

Eola just shook her head slowly. She’d done it. She’d figured out the mark the trog shaman had used—and she’d figured out why she hadn’t been able to decipher it. It wasn’t a traditional modifying mark at all. It was another layer of magic. And now, it was hers.

“Did you take over my spell?” Colin asked. “How? What…?”

“Not exactly. I think I Cut part of your spell off,” Eola mumbled. “Yes. That’s exactly what I did. I cut a modifying mark off of your First Order ritual, and forced it to cast as a cantrip.”

“You forced me to cast Child’s Magic?” Colin stared at her, eyes wide. “That sounds like countermagic, and that shouldn’t be possible. Does that mean…?”

It did sound like countermagic. Like the real kind. The kind that everyone from Atta all the way to Colin had insisted wasn’t possible, but that every spellsword fantasized about for a few training sessions before they settled on oppositional cantrips instead. Those lacked the flexibility of what Eola had just done, though. If she could see a modifying mark, her new mark was fast enough to add to a spell—and if she could Cut a spell apart fast enough, even First Order magic would be just as weak as her own.

And she doubted that Eddarn or the monster could cast Second Order magic consistently. An eight-bar mage would have a few tricks, and they’d have more endurance than any of her friends, but he wouldn’t be on Maestro Yarrowbloom or Instructor Tarik’s level.

“Does that mean we can win?” Colin asked.

“I think so,” Eola said. Before she could say anything else, he wrapped her in a hug, and she had to struggle free from it. “Yes. Yes, we can win this. Wake up Patrice. We’ve got a way to beat that cultist and his impossible familiar. We’re going to have to get lucky, but we can still survive this. And if we’re very lucky, we can get back on track, find those books, and pass the Library Exam.”
