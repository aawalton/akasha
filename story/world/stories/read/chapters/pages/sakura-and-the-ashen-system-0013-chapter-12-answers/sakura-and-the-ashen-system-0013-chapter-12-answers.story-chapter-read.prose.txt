They did not stop right away. Pampli carried them through the forest as if he were racing the wind. Branches lashed at their shoulders, roots flashed beneath the beast’s paws too fast for Sakura to follow them properly. She had stopped looking back a long time ago. There was no point. If the pursuit was still on their trail, she would never see it in that wet blackness between the trunks. All she could do was press herself against Pampli, catch the rhythm of his run, and hope he himself knew where it was best to take them, farther away from the city.

Sakura did not notice at once when the beast began to slow down. His stride grew heavier. Only when water flashed ahead between the trees did Sakura realize they had apparently reached some hidden place by the river.

Pampli stepped out from under the dense branches onto a narrow rocky strip of shore and stopped. The river was dark, with a cold metallic sheen. The forest stood around them like a solid wall. Mebrel was far behind.

Sakura was the first to slide off Pampli. Her legs nearly gave way from exhaustion, but she managed to stay upright by bracing one hand against his hot side. Gilbert jumped down after her much more easily than she did, though he did not look especially lively either. His cloak was damp, his hair stuck to his face, and the bone bracelets on his wrists still caught the faintest light even in that darkness. For a few seconds, neither of them spoke.

Then Pampli let out a heavy breath, took two more steps toward the water, and simply tipped over onto his side. He did not collapse. He really just rolled down, drained by everything he had been through. The enormous black body sank into the wet grass and loose stones. The crimson glow in his stripes dimmed. The beast closed his eyes and made it very clear, without words: that was it. No farther. Now he was going to lie here and sleep.

Sakura smiled without meaning to. After the arena, after everything... Pampli suddenly did not look frightening at all. Just an exhausted beast who had done the impossible for her and now wanted to lie down by the river and go nowhere else.

Sakura stepped closer and gently touched the back of his head with her fingertips.

“Thank you,” she said quietly. “Seriously. You were incredible.”

Pampli did not open his eyes. He only snorted softly through his nose, almost contentedly, and flicked what might have been an ear. Sakura stood beside him for another second, then looked back into the dark forest. She heard no sound of pursuit now, no snapping branches or voices moving through the wet blackness between the trees. It seemed they really had broken away, or at least won a little breathing room. That alone was already a lot.

Sakura washed up a little in the cold water.

“Looks like they’re not catching up to us yet,” she said to Gilbert afterward.

“Yet,” he corrected dryly.

Gilbert crouched by the water and silently pulled up his pant leg. At first Sakura had no idea why, but then she saw the narrow metal bracelet around his ankle. It was nothing like the bone bracelets on his wrists. This one looked much cruder: cast from darkened metal, rusty in places, with unknown runes scratched into it. The clasp was crooked and ugly.

“What’s that supposed to be?” she asked.

Gilbert looked at her as if the question was offensively obvious.

“Inventory blocker.”

He picked up a flat stone from the riverbank, bent down, and fixed the bracelet in place with his free hand, pressing his leg against a boulder so the clasp rested against the stone. Then he struck it. The blows were hard, clearly painful, but the metal did not give on the first try. The edge of the bracelet scraped the mage’s leg, and then the clasp cracked. Gilbert clenched his teeth and struck one last time. At last, the clasp broke. The bracelet slipped off his leg and fell into the wet grass.

“They put those on all prisoners?” Sakura asked, looking at the broken piece.

“All of them. But this is a sorry imitation of what a proper dungeon should have. In Mebrel, as you can see, they decided this was good enough.”

Sakura looked at the deformed metal again and thought it really did look pathetic. As if this place cut corners even on basic safety rules. Judging by Gilbert’s skeptical look, that was exactly the case.

The mage washed the blood from his ankle, then picked up the bracelet and threw it into the river.

“But even that piece of junk completely blocks access to an Inventory.”

Sakura frowned, remembering how easily she had pulled out Lir’s knife before the fight.

“They didn’t put one on me. I used my Inventory just fine in the cell and in the arena.”

Gilbert gave her a brief look.

“Maybe because they didn’t plan to keep you long. Before the arena, they might simply have decided it wasn’t worth the trouble. Or they forgot.”

He gave a dry smirk.

“So Mebrel isn’t exactly a bastion of security?” Sakura asked.

“Not all of Mebrel. Only the part you had the fortune to end up in,” Gilbert answered.

He looked at her carefully, his head tipped slightly to one side.

“And now,” the mage said, “maybe you’ll finally explain yourself?”

Sakura let out a short breath. Yes. That would be fair. They moved a little farther from Pampli, toward a large tree whose roots pushed out of the ground above the riverbank, and sat beneath it. Sakura stretched out her legs, feeling her knees and shoulders ache. Gilbert sat across from her with his back against the trunk, and in the dark his face looked even paler than usual.

“They decided I was a mage,” she said at last. “Or rather, they only suspected it at first. Then Merren decided to arrange a test and sent me into the arena. As a trial.”

Gilbert narrowed his eyes slightly, but did not interrupt. He already knew about the arena, but not what exactly had happened there.

“And yes,” Sakura added after a pause. “I guess he wasn’t entirely wrong. Only I’m... not exactly a mage. Or not a normal one. I don’t know what the right word is. I was able to tame an obsidian monster.” She glanced toward Pampli. “Which, as I understand it, is not supposed to happen at all.”

Gilbert nodded slowly, as if that was exactly what he had been afraid of hearing, though he did not seem surprised.

“I knew something was wrong with you back in the dungeon,” he said. “There was a strange energy coming off you. Wrong for a mage. Wrong for any class I would know.”

Sakura clasped her hands tighter.

“The truth is, I don’t understand any of this at all,” she admitted. “Not this world. Not what happened to me. Not what I am. I need answers.”

For one brief moment, she wondered if she should tell him the whole truth. That she was not from Veyr at all. That she had come from another world, from a train car that had suddenly caught fire. That she had died there before waking up among Crowlis’s ruins.

But no, that would be too much. Especially now, when she barely knew him, and he already had more than enough reasons to look at her like a walking disaster. In truth, Sakura was not sure she would ever want to tell anyone that part of her life unless there was no other choice.

Gilbert looked at her for a long time, his eyes narrowed. Sakura did not feel as if he truly distrusted her, but it seemed as though the mage was simply trying to fit her words together in his head. It was obvious he had questions of his own too, and sooner or later he would ask them.

“Then let’s start with something simple,” he said at last. “There has been a hunt for mages in Veyr for about fifty years now.”

Sakura frowned.

“Because of Crowlis?”

“Formally, yes. According to the official version, it all began after the tragedy at Crowlis’s castle, after something so terrible happened on his lands that it gave them the perfect excuse to remake half the world. Mages were declared the main threat. Not the only one, of course, but the easiest one to point at.”

He spoke calmly, too calmly, as if trying to keep his real feelings buried under the words.

“Veyr is ruled now by the chroniclers,” Gilbert went on. “A class of their own. They had long stood close to power, at court, around the laws. But after the Crowlis tragedy, one of the chief chroniclers managed to do what many nobles before him never could. He became king. And ever since then, truth here is written the way the throne finds convenient. The chroniclers rewrite history almost every day.”

Sakura listened without moving.

“The laws against mages were put in quickly,” Gilbert said. “First as a temporary measure. Then as a sacred necessity. Old records were seized, rewritten, burned. New generations were given a ready-made version of the world, one where a mage is almost always the same thing as a threat, magic almost always leads to disaster, and any other past is either heresy or a story for drunks. Children are raised on that lie. It makes ruling easier. A person who is afraid asks fewer questions.”

Sakura gave a dark little smile.

“How convenient.”

“Exactly,” Gilbert said.

He turned his gaze toward the river and continued more quietly.

“Beyond the capital, Veyr lives badly. You’ve already seen that. Poverty, local tyrants like Merren, obsidian beasts. In words, the king protects the people. In reality, he clings to a policy of... preserving the problem. He does not solve it. He walls it off. There is no great war against the beasts.”

Sakura was silent for a long moment.

“The southern lands,” Gilbert said, “suffer the most. The beasts rage there in greater numbers. They were driven there about forty years ago. Settlements in the south die out faster, but no one cancels the taxes. And while mages in the north are still sometimes hidden or bought, used under someone else’s supervision, in the south they stopped bothering with that a long time ago. They gathered them.”

Sakura raised her eyes to him.

“Gathered them?”

Gilbert nodded once.

“In colonies. Closed settlements. Officially, for control, registration, and ‘safe containment of individuals with dangerous abilities.’ In reality, just cages. People were kept there for generations, broken, used as proof that a mage without chains is not a person but a disaster. Then, when they were no longer useful, or when fear demanded fresh blood, the colony was simply cleared out.”

He said it so evenly that a chill ran down Sakura’s back.

“I’m from there,” he added. “From the south. From one of those colonies. As far as I know, I’m the last mage from the south still alive.”

Sakura looked at him and could not find words right away. Now his calm gaze and even his sarcastic half-smile no longer seemed like simple parts of his character, but something built over a very old, very dark pain.

“I’m sorry,” she said quietly.

Gilbert answered with the faintest smirk, more habit than mockery.

“Oh, don’t waste pity on me, girl.”

“I mean it.”

“I know.”

The river murmured beside them, Pampli breathed heavily off to the side, and Sakura tried to fit everything she had just heard into her head. Veyr had not been broken by monsters alone. The government had gone on breaking it further.

“How did you escape?” she asked after a pause.

Gilbert looked at her, and then that more familiar smirk came back.

“I’ll tell you that story a little later. Another time.”

Sakura let out a breath through her nose. Curiosity jumped inside her at once, but there was no point arguing. He had already told her more than anyone else in his place probably would have.

“All right,” she said. “Later, then.”

They sat in silence for a while. Two hunted people who might now be able to join forces and help each other. Sakura wanted to believe that. Their trouble was shared now.

“Did all of this really begin with the tragedy at Crowlis’s castle?” she asked. “Just like that?”

Gilbert shrugged.

“There are no simple answers here. I don’t know what it really began with. Maybe Crowlis. Maybe much earlier. Maybe he was simply turned into a convenient point of reference. But one thing I know for certain: the obsidian beasts,” he said, giving a short nod toward Pampli, “did not appear out of nowhere.”

Sakura looked at the beast too. Pampli was still lying there, deeply asleep.

“Pampli is different,” she said. “Now he’s with us, not against us.”

“Today, yes,” Gilbert answered calmly. “What that will mean tomorrow is something we still have to find out.”

Sakura accepted that. There was truth in Gilbert’s words, and no arguing with it.

She ran her palms over the wet fabric of her cloak and said slowly, “I need help. With all of this...”

Gilbert nodded.

“I already figured that out.”

“I need to understand what I am. Why I can do... this. What all of it means. And preferably before someone decides to either sell me or cut my throat in the name of royal truth.”

“A fine plan,” he said. “Almost without flaws.”

“Very funny.”

“I’m not joking.”

Sakura glanced at his hands.

“Then help,” she said.

He lifted his wrists a little higher. The pale bone bracelets, carved with runes, looked almost like part of him in the damp forest dimness.

“As long as these are on me,” Gilbert said, “I won’t be able to help you with anything except conversation. And, annoyingly enough, I know less than you and I would both like.”

Sakura frowned.

“So how do we get them off?”

“The people who know that are the ones with access to rune magic,” he answered. “Rare enchanters in the cities. They are officially allowed to use minor magic, but only under supervision, within the bounds of a license and all the government nonsense that comes with it. Everything is under strict control. Step sideways once, and you stop being a useful specialist and become a threat to order.”

Sakura leaned her head back wearily against the wet branches above.

“So we need someone who knows rune work, lives in a city, is under government control, and is very unlikely to be happy about removing restraints from a wanted fugitive mage.”

“In general, yes.”

Sakura was quiet for a moment. Then she said it in a tone that even she herself was not entirely sure was a joke or not:

“So we’ll have to kidnap one.”

Gilbert looked at her for several seconds. And then he laughed.
