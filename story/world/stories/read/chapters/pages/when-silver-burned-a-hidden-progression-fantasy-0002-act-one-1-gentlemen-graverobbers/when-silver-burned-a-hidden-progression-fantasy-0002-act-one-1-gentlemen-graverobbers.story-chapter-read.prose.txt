HUNTER

"A trap-maker, I thought, the way he roved over things with eerie efficacy. Rare in one so young—a'know, rich, coming from me? Still. Had you been there, you'd intuit the same in my stead.

"He scoured, pried, always that something. Even long before I met him...

"I wonder if he's still looking for it."

- The late recountings of Noémi Slipfall

The door slowly swayed back and forth, eliciting a creak with each turn. The sound stopped when Kalsin steadied it for a moment, then pushed it fully open. He stepped forward carefully, gripping the hilt of his sword still snug inside the scabbard strapped under his arm. The creaking resumed the moment the door fell closed behind him.

Faint silver light shone through the dirty window panes, setting the stage for humble furnishings. A three-legged table huddled in one corner with only a seemingly untouched bowl of soup resting on top. Dried apples, pears lined a shelf above the window with an empty stove yawning near. A woodsman's axe hung from a pair of thick nails on the wall.

He approached the table, stepping with the rhythm of the creaks. He dipped a finger into the soup. Cold. The table was clean, no dust on its surface. Good craftsmanship, too. Sturdy looking thing, sturdy enough to break a tooth or two if he'd set up a meeting between them. Assuming the teeth in question were of human origin. He eased his fingers from the hilt, stretching them out, then into a fist, then stretching again. Frowning at the sweat that coated his palm, he wiped them dry in his trousers.

He burst into the connecting room, trading subtlety for haste. His sword wasn't halfway out by the time he assessed everything in front of him. Not a difficult job, that. Save for the man lying in a narrow bed, asleep or dead, there wasn't much to note.

Kalsin readjusted his sword sling so the scabbard hung at the small of his back, the finicky thing having slipped. He then sat on the side of the bed to check the man's pulse.

Dead. But dead how? His clothes that of a Clenn, albeit a poor one, didn't have a single spot of blood on them. Kalsin rolled him on his side, but couldn't see injuries. Calloused hands. A man well into his years, but too early for time to take its final toll on him. Sickness? Folk should've been tending to him. Poison in the soup? No, the bowl was filled to the brim. Must've been something else entirely.

A thin veil of fog had descended on the village by the time Kalsin got back outside. Sap, his bahten, gave a soft nicker as he walked up to him. He opened his saddlebag to stash away the coins he'd looted. "Don’t balk, now." Kalsin said as he patted Sap's neck, the bahten’s blue skin cold and slimy under his palm.

"About making a living, or speaking as if our gilled companions could cognizance your words?" The words came from behind him.

Kalsin took his time finishing up with the bag. "Deathbeds everywhere?"

"Yes. That, and the valuables townspeople left behind." Kalsin turned just in time to catch the sack thrown at him, one considerably larger than his own haul. He lifted it, gauging its weight, then arched a brow at his sister.

Cinder was leaning on the hay bale sprawled at the roadside, her arms folded in a way that hid her stump. A mischievous face, blonde hair unkept and wind-tossed, she looked half too charming for their grim surroundings. But looks were deceptive. Kalsin's sister could only ever hope to charm shadows, and they both knew it.

Kalsin nodded. "All dead, no injuries. Not even the faintest sign of struggle. It's like they just decided to lie down for the evening, and forgot about the small matter of getting back up."

"Rattles the mind, doesn't it?" Cinder said. Kalsin could hear the excitement in her tone. A sort of excitement you only get when sniffing the trail of a mythos.

"What are you thinking?"

"I've heard of such a thing before. Nocnitsa. Night maiden. Dream eater. They're called many things throughout many stories, but it's always the same way of killing, with sleep and nightmares." She looked up, as if the shrouded night sky held the answer. "Sometimes the victims die the moment they close their eyes. In other tales, it's a conundrum. Those caught in her nightmares appear as if they're dead, unable to wake as the maiden feeds, but they're very much alive. You kill the maiden, and the dreamers die with her. You interrupt her feeding, and she might as well put you to sleep with them. A cautionary tale that sometimes there's no right choice to make."

"An invention of tales is hardly required to prove something as obvious."

"Something as obvious is hardly expected to go unembellished by poets, writer-downs and their pens."

They stared at one another for a hanging moment.

"Well. You had me on the academic part," Kalsin said at last, feeling the wash of bahten-breath upon his neck. He ducked out of the way before Sap started sampling his hair. "What about the pragmatic one?"

"Heh, not bad. Some pay generously for a live nocnitsa, ‘specially kept for their tears," Cinder said, pushing herself from the hay. "And just so we’re rectilinear, I have you on all parts, be it academic, pragmatic, or protoaesthetic."

Kalsin turned toward the road when staccato clamps reached them, the sounds drawing closer. The fog parted to a woman on bahten-back, her plated armor dark and glinting with dew. She pulled on the reins, hardened fins sliding on the stone as the reptilian horse came to a sharp halt.

"I'll scout around," Cinder said, already walking in the other direction. Good ears, Kalsin’s sister had, and even better sense. She quickly disappeared into the fog.

Kalsin waited for the woman to dismount. She was breathing hard with sweat beading on her sidecuts, several locks having escaped the bun at the back of her head. Her Scar was a small thing, a black incision sitting on her jawline.

One look at Jack's face was enough to tell she was irate. Kalsin folded his arms. In his battle to hold back a smile—as well as trying to avoid looking at her Scar—he failed to notice she wasn't interested in calming down this time around.

One moment Kalsin was standing, the next an armored foot crushed down on his, making him buckle. Before he could even as much as fall, Jack got around and behind him, her muscled arms firmly locking around his neck. It kept him from hitting the ground. Unfortunately, it kept him from drawing breaths as well. At first Kalsin didn't struggle, but lacking the vital ability to replenish his lungs quickly changed his mind. The headlock tightened and he wheezed. He tapped at the iron wrist rapidly.

As if to make a point, it took one more second for Jack to let go. Kalsin fell forward, sputtering. He shot a half apologetic, half mad look at her. Jack snarled in response, raising a middle finger. A gesture he's become quite familiar with the past years. He probably deserved worse, but after that one time when she'd bashed him with her shield, she had likely come to the conclusion that inflicting permanent brain damage as his bodyguard wasn't the wisest way to go about her obligation.

She stalked up and down as Kalsin caught his breath. The violence having calmed her down just a notch, she shook her head, crossing her arms in front of her and pointing an accusatory finger at him.

"I know," Kalsin said, aware his words were a false mockery of his intentions. "But we have to move fast if there are lives at stake."

She shook her head, though less certain this time, raising a fist and spreading her fingers.

Kalsin snored. "Horseshit. You care as much as I do. But we're not hauling around a boulder's weight in armor to slow us down." It might've been a low blow, but he had spoken true. Jack rode Hollow, a midnight-black warlander bahten with a bulky frame and more stamina than most of their kind, but considerably slower in short bursts than Sap. Kalsin didn't like leaving his bodyguard behind, but on those rare occasions when they got word before, and not after the mythos started their slaughtering rampage, time was a currency weighed with the lifeblood of innocents.

It was rusted and irony that, in their line of profession, the already-spilled contents of said lifeblood usually resulted in better pay. Hurry to save people, or stall to fatten the pickings? The forever warring question they had to deal with.

Jack hooked her kite shield off of Hollow's saddle and looped her arm into the enarme straps. The shield was long enough to cover her from chest to toe, and she was a tall woman. The unpainted metal gleamed wet in the night, a dark layer of wood coating the edges with nicks and slashes marring it by the dozens. The weight didn't seem to bother her, though Kalsin had hefted the thing plenty of times, and it had bothered him plenty.

Kalsin glanced back at the house, unease creeping back in. The unnatural slumber reeked of mythos, but this power to put an entire village under permanent sleep—he'd never seen the like. He shifted his scabbard under his arm in preparedness for likely conflicts.

With Jack at his side he started down the road, delving deeper into the village. They kept an eye out, peering through the fog that revealed buildings ahead of them just to let them sink behind, like a magician's cloak during a particularly vaporous stage performance. The rattle of Jack's armor, as well as her éj-glass lantern dangling from her hip, would give away their position faster than a drum beating procession on celebration day, but there was nothing to do about it. She'd proven time and time again the tradeoff between adequate protection and the element of surprise was one worth the barter.

"Your shoulder plate is rusting," Kalsin said. Jack spared a glance at the piece of armor, but when she didn't acknowledge it otherwise, he started fishing around in his pockets for a piece of sandpaper. She slowed her pace to let him scrub at the discolored patch.

"You'll have to get your steel in shape once at the capital. We’ll make time."

Jack frowned, but Kalsin continued. "This haul nets us some spare coin to spend. The plan remains unchanged—every lead is pointing to Remallon—and you might as well be productive as I make my turns through the underworld." Her frown remained in place, but she made no protests otherwise. An agreement, albeit a grudging one.

They only took a few more steps into the town when Jack froze. A second later Kalsin heard as well, voices from ahead. Talking, yelling, a human commotion. Kalsin raised a brow at Jack, but she only shrugged. Cinder had fast disappeared, as expected. He and his sister always made sure not to be seen together.

With Cinder being a Clenn, keeping a pair of Scarrd company would push her below the lowest pariahs of society, and lower still for being a cripple.

The sounds hushed, then came a shout. "Who goes there?" A man's voice.

"Vultures," Kalsin replied, using a common, though somewhat stigmatized name for their profession. "Heard mythos nested here. We came to investigate truth from rumor."

The mist thinned to unveil the road widening into an intersection large enough to hide the buildings on the far end. A low wooden platform occupied a large area in the center, the sort they ready for special occasions, whether they be village festivals or executions. A man wearing an army uniform stood at the base, looking their way. He waved a gloved hand and the soldiers around him resumed their activities. Men and women moved with purpose, some rolling kegs, others hoarding sacks of grain and crates packed with cabbages. Their uniform was a rare combination of grey and blue. Grey meant they were part of a Scarrd division, but the blue marked them as soldiers outside the standard military ranks and beholden only to the Arches. The forcing hand of the greater side of politics, if you will.

"A ghostly eve, is it not?" the leader asked, tracking their arrival as the pair made their way to him. His face was clean shaven, free of blemishes. Did he value it as treasure? How would it feel to have the luxury of hiding his hideous scar under his uniform instead of having to wear it on his face? Lucky bastard.

Kalsin replied with—despite his thoughts—no trace of venom in his voice, "Evening. Ghostly's an apt description. With respect, sir, men of the Arches were the last thing I expected to cross tonight."

"That so? No fault to that, I suppose. For one chasing word of mouth from civilians, you likely note half and half again of what they say, and believe a quarter of it. An Arch lifts a finger, and, hm..." he trailed off as one of his men approached him with a ledger in hand. He looked it over, then passed it back with an absent nod. "Logistics. I've no head for them." Even as he talked, he never let Jack out of his field of vision. If his mind was intent on deciphering which of them posed more threat, he was successful. Jack might've had the powers of a dirt only, but she had the strongest physical surge Kalsin had ever seen.

The man said, "You arrived through the fractures? The waters calm these days?"

Kalsin nodded. The fractures were narrow, bottomless rivers splitting the Republic like an intricate system of spiderwebs, creating an archipelago of a thousand islands shuffled so close that you could jump from one piece of land to another with little effort. Some used spear-like boats to traverse the fractures, but creatures such as bahten were natural at navigating the thin channels as well. The rivers provided a convenient way of transportation, though their narrowness also created complications aplenty. A fallen tree blocking the way. Mythos surfacing from the endless depths. Bandits lying in wait, safe behind a rise. The list went on.

Their passage has been calm, however, and Kalsin said as much. The man grunted in response. Kalsin asked, "Is it true, then? Arch Belamina is taking a sixth husband? The Civil House and the Arches are gathering in Remallon to celebrate the wedding?"

"True as it gets." He spit to the side. Clearly, the man enjoyed the sound of his voice. "When everyone has their eyes on the sparks and rifts, and the Clenn delegation escorting the Arches to form pretty lines when they parade through the city, they always forget the dirtier side of the coin. The Scarrd Arch Militia. Ain't that a mouthful? With the high-profile marriage right around the corner—may her Archness and her husbands live long—we've been double timing it towards the capital for the past month, covering the tails of our betters as per usual."

"The Clenn delegation came through the village as well?"

"They had no need for it. We, however, have been requisitioning supplies from settlements along the way to keep us from going hungry. Perks of the job," he said, matter-of-fact about his legal robbery.

A quality that would get him far in Kalsin’s profession.

"I see," Kalsin said. "Everything's in order in the Republic, then?"

"Yes. As far as you hunters are concerned." His voice turned rankled, a twitch of annoyance on his lips. He glanced at Jack standing beside Kalsin like an unmovable tower, the tip of her shield resting on the ground, then back to him. "This all common knowledge. Don't think yourself special. In fact, I think we might've just taken care of your mythos problem."

He stepped aside, drawing their gaze to an unmoving body behind him. It was a woman, her head lifelessly drooping from the edge of the platform with a puddle of recently dried blood gathered underneath. Kalsin stepped closer, lifting her head. The throat was slashed open. Save from the white and wooden textured hair, the nocnitsa looked just like a human. He saw her back had been lashed brutally, causing only a ragged field of torn skin and meat to remain. Kalsin’s eyes fluttered in a barely noticeable expression, trying to quell his rising anger.

"We found this one alone, chained up and delirious. At first we thought she was just a common criminal driven mad by the pain of the lash, but then we heard the chanting, the sacrilegious wails. She was in a trance, convulsing, unnatural. Two greets two makes four, it didn't take much to figure her magic was somehow responsible for all the dead."

Kalsin listened, pretending to inspect the mythos whilst holding back words and curses he knew he would regret immediately. Killing the nocnitsa would've been Kalsin's last resort. Not for the sake of the mythos, but for the village folk. According to the stories Cinder had mentioned, there might've been a way to save these people, a faint one, but still worth trying. That's why you call for hunters. That's why you don't let soldiers to their own devices.

Calm, keep calm. They're common footmen. They don't know better.

Jack gave her opinion with an almost unnoticeable shift of her leg, pulling it backward. The message was brisk even for her, but Kalsin could figure the meaning. Leave it alone. What's done is done.

"Didn't put up a fight, this one, but we did save you the trouble of getting blood all over your hands," the leader said, concluding a matter that probably made his requisitioning duties less burdensome. What with all the dead people lying in their beds.

Kalsin stared at him, silent. He knew his strange, bright silver eyes unnerved people. Or his Scar stretching between his sockets made their stomach heave with disgust. One of the two.

"Anyway. We're done here." The man turned to address one of his soldiers, unwilling or unable to maintain his gaze. "I doubt you'll find much of value left, but feel free to sniff around."

A trifling victory, but a victory nonetheless.

The walk back to their horses was somber. The thinning mist revealed Cinder halfway back as she joined them.

Before Kalsin could speak, Cinder said, "I heard."

Kalsin made a noncommittal hmph in reply. Jack shifted uneasily. He didn't need to look at his bodyguard; she always wore the same expression around Cinder. He knew it by heart. Her brow crinkled, her lips hung slightly downturned. She never gave a definitive answer why she treated his sister the way she did. Kalsin could easily guess it, though. A Clenn had no business living her life alongside a pair of Scarrd. Jack never acknowledged Cinder directly, always communicating through Kalsin instead. Treating his sister like she was a pariah among pariahs.

And it rested on Kalsin to make it so that Cinder wouldn’t deserve such a treatment. The solution was leading them to Remallon.

"Stop it, Kal," Cinder said. "Don't tangle yourself into a mess over a few idiots who trained all their lives only to follow orders. All that warfare, it makes for some colorful tales, but they've had it easy. They never learned to think for themselves."

"When you notice a crevice splitting your porch, you call a carpenter. When you find a hole in your shirt, you visit a tailor. All they had to do was send for us," Kalsin said. "Their lack of ability to handle mythos isn't the issue. Their lack of ability to admit their shortcomings is."

The silence was absolute. Kalsin brushed through his hair. He shouldn't vent his anger at those who don't deserve it.

"It's just..."

"I know," Cinder said. "I know. It's a hunter's lot. Always one step behind disaster—and profiting from it. Killing the mythos doesn't bring back the victims, and to take their belongings and all they've tolled for throughout their lives as payment? It's unthinkable. But there's no other way. You think ordinary folk would pay handsomely for the death of some beast they might or might not have seen from a distance? Some baseless rumor they've heard? No. Like everyone else, they barely spare it a thought. Accidents happen to other people. Always others. It's never them. But come face-to-face with a mythos with their life about to be snuffed out, as they would give their body's worth in gold to be saved, by then it's too late.

"People see the bodies, people hear the carnage. There's been a killing, they say. Misfortune befell other people again. Word spreads and reaches our ears. We come and we kill the beasts and we take our toll from the dead."

"Just to save people for once, you know? To make a difference for the better. I'd like that." Kalsin said quietly. Was he genuine, or just deluding himself to ease something? Immaterial, maybe. Only the end result mattered.

Cinder put a hand on his arm, squeezing. "We put an end to monsters that would sooner or later carve another bloody path in a rampage. We do make a difference."

"Maybe," Kalsin shrugged off her hand, giving a smile he didn’t feel. "Some days I wish it felt that way."
