CHAPTER 6

"Every Watcher assigned to a bonded pair has noted the same phenomenon: the protected party member becomes the candidate's primary vulnerability. This is not a flaw in the selection criteria. It is the criteria. A candidate who protects nothing will guard nothing." - Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

Three streets had never felt so far.

The route from the Grapes to Fenchurch Street was one Arthur had walked a hundred times. Down Lime Street, left at the junction, past the sandwich shop and the accountancy firm with the cracked window that he’d noticed six months ago and that nobody had fixed. Seven minutes on a normal evening, maybe eight if the traffic lights were against you.

There were no traffic lights now and there was no traffic. Arthur had cursed the London traffic more times than he could count and today he would take another honking lunatic behind him, in a heartbeat. The city had stopped mid-breath, and the three of them were advancing through it as hostile territory.

Arthur kept to the centre of the road. The pavement was cluttered with bodies and people kneeling beside them, but the road, choked with dead vehicles though it was, at least gave them sightlines. He could see ahead and he could see the angles where side streets fed into the main road.

The details of the collapse assembled themselves as they walked. A bus sat sideways across an intersection, its passengers long gone, the driver still slumped against the wheel. A row of Boris bikes lay toppled against their docking station like dominoes. A woman sat on a kerb with a toddler in her lap, rocking slowly and staring into empty space. A man stood in the doorway of a Pret, eating a sandwich mechanically, his mind somewhere it could not come back from.

Arthur watched him for two steps longer than necessary. A few hours ago, he’d been eating lunch and checking if the pub had their usual table available.

Steve, who had been quiet for nearly a full minute, said: “The Boris bikes. They’ve all fallen in the same direction.”

“Not now, Steve,” Donna said without turning around.

“East to west. Consistent with a concussive wave propagating from—”

“Steve.”

He went quiet.

The bodies were the worst. All of them looked peaceful, and that was the sodding problem. Asleep on pavements, slumped in doorways, curled on benches. Violence at least the mind can understand, not the abrupt, silent absence of the person who’d been inside the body moments before.

Twenty-five per cent of the people he could see were dead, and the consistency gave the catastrophe the cold precision of a specification. As though something had passed through the population and selected against a variable with the efficiency of an engineer culling defective components from a production line.

He hated that thought. He hated that his brain had produced it. But the pattern was undeniable.

Steve noticed it too, which was not surprising.

“It’s not random,” he said quietly, his eyes tracking the bodies on the pavement as they walked. “The distribution is too clean, too even. One in four, roughly, everywhere I look. That’s not a bomb or a gas or a pathogen, those don’t select with this kind of precision. It’s more like...” He frowned and then added, “Like a biological filter. Something in the population is being selected for.”

“Steve, not now.” Donna’s voice was tight. Right now she could see Emma as one of these bodies.

Steve went quiet. But Arthur could see his lips moving as he walked. Counting, correlating and filing the data into whatever internal framework his brain was building to make sense of this new horror of a world.

***

They were halfway to Fenchurch when Arthur heard the hissing.

A hatchback had drifted down the slight decline of the street and had come to rest against a bollard, its front crumpled just enough to split a coolant line. Steam rose from beneath the bonnet in a thin, persistent plume. The driver hung against the seatbelt with a shallow cut on her forehead and her eyes closed. Then Arthur saw the face twitch.

Arthur had changed direction before the decision had fully formed. Donna grabbed his sleeve.

“Arthur...”

“Ten seconds. No more,” he said, moving towards the car.

He reached the car and saw that the frame had bent enough to bind the hinges. He wrenched the door open and reached across the driver to release the belt. The woman groaned as he eased her out and carried her to the pavement, setting her against the wall of an estate agent’s office with her legs straight and her head supported.

Her eyes fluttered. "What— what happened? Is this— did you see the sky?"

“You're all right. Stay where you are until you've got your legs back, then find somewhere safe and keep your head down,” Arthur said.

The woman blinked at him, then at the dead car, then at the wrong sky, and her face crumpled into the expression of someone for whom reality had exceeded the bandwidth of comprehension.

As he ran back, he saw Steve was watching him with an expression which was somewhere between admiration and clinical concern. “You realise,” he said, falling into step, “that you’ve now run towards danger three times in the last hour. At a certain point, that stops being altruism and starts being a diagnosable condition.”

"It's not a condition," Donna said, her eyes moving over the parked cars and the dark shopfronts. "It's a liability, and one that I've apparently decided to keep."

"That was almost affectionate," Steve said.

"It wasn't. Now keep moving."

Arthur didn’t answer, there was nothing to say and the damn gash beneath his jacket was itching, the maddening itch of tissue knitting together at a speed his body had no precedent for. He pressed his hand against it, rubbing it gently and kept walking.

A man across the road had stopped mid-stride turning to look at Arthur as he crossed to the car. The man had tried the door himself a minute ago. The door was bent into the frame and hadn't moved. Arthur had torn it open like the hinges had deeply offended him. The man looked at his own hands and then at Arthur again before walking away.

***

The rats came out of a skip near the junction with Fenchurch.

Two of them, both were smaller than the alley creature, maybe three times the size of a normal rat, but wrong in the same fundamental way as the spider in the alley. Their fur had thinned to coarse bristles and their eyes caught the violet light with a sinister oily gleam that no rodent eye should have produced. They moved in tandem towards them with the coordination of animals that had stopped being scavengers and started being predators.

Donna saw them first and her hand was on the multitool before Arthur had finished processing what he was looking at.

The first rat lunged at Steve’s ankle. Steve swung the pipe downward in a short, ugly arc that caught the animal across the skull and it crumpled. The second came at Arthur from the left, he drove the heel of his boot into its side and Donna’s blade finished it a second later.

The encounter had lasted perhaps five seconds. No floating text or notification followed. Just two dead rats that were not quite rats.

“Too small for experience points, apparently,” Steve muttered, shaking the pipe clean.

“Good, if the apocalypse starts rewarding us for killing rats, I’m going to have concerns about the incentive structure.” Donna said, pulling her blade free and wiping it on her jeans.

“At least we won’t be stuck killing rats in a cellar.” Arthur said, caught Donna’s expression, and decided to inspect the dead Vauxhall Corsa next to him instead.

"Same sort of mutation as the spider in the alley. On a smaller scale." Steve crouched, turning a dead rat's head with the pipe. "The zoo would be a right problem."

“We deal with what is in front of us instead of worrying about things we can do nothing about right now.” Her eyes were back on the street ahead, scanning for the restaurant. “We've got enough problems of our own.”

***

The restaurant was a flashy steakhouse on the ground floor of an office building. The kind of place where they charge you thirty quid for a steak just so you can listen to a bloke named Hugo explain cryptocurrency over lunch. Its front door stood partially open and they could hear tense, overlapping voices from inside. The sound of people arguing about what to do when none of the options were any good.

“Emma!” Donna said, pushing through the door.

A woman behind the counter looked up sharply. She was younger than Donna by a few years, with the same dark hair cut shorter and the same sharp jawline, and for a half-second her face held the terror of someone who had been waiting for some terrible news. Then the terror broke into relief, as she saw Donna moving towards her.

Donna crossed the room in four quick strides and pulled Emma into an embrace that was brief and hard and contained. Emma’s hands gripped the back of Donna’s jacket. Neither of them said a word.

Arthur scanned the restaurant, giving them a moment alone. There were half a dozen survivors and a body near the bar, covered with a tablecloth. One in four, the ratio holding. It was not all panic inside the restaurant though, someone had been working. The front tables had been dragged sideways to narrow the entrance. The staff stood behind the counter in a tight group, and every one of them was holding a knife.

This was not the panicked scavenge of people who’d grabbed the nearest sharp object. The blades were distributed by size, a cleaver for the biggest man, a boning knife for the woman near the door, and paring knives for the rest. Someone had assessed the inventory, matched it to the hands, and given instructions. It was what Donna would have done. Arthur had watched her narrow a room like this before, turn furniture into funnels and hands into a plan. Whoever had set this up had the same instinct.

Then he realised that everyone seemed to be looking at him. Their eyes searched his face hoping for news that made sense of it all. “The streets aren’t safe.” Arthur said it the way he’d say a beam was compromised. “Stay inside. Lock the door. Barricade it with whatever’s heavy and don’t go out unless you absolutely have to.”

Someone asked the question. The question everyone on every street in London was asking. “What the fuck is happening out there, mate?”

Arthur met the man’s eyes. He could have lied or he could have given some platitude. Both would have been kinder.

“We are not really sure ourselves. World's different now,” he said instead. “And there are things outside that weren’t there this morning. Dangerous things.”

To him it was better to be scared shitless and prepared than to be comfortable and unprepared. They would have seen the text as well, Arthur was sure of it.

Arthur gave the people in the restaurant a smile that he hoped was reassuring. “Just stay together. Someone somewhere will be trying to sort this mess out. Until they do, keep an eye on each other.”

Donna released her sister and held her at arm’s length, scanning her. The same quick, professional sweep she’d use on a building after a breach. Checking for damage.

Donna’s gaze moved over Emma once. “You’re okay.”

“I’m okay,” Emma said. “Donna, what is...”

“We don’t know. We’re still working on that.” Donna looked around the room. The narrowed entrance, the distributed knives, the covered body. She looked at Emma and asked, “You did this?”

“We had an hour before you got here. I wasn't going to spend it sitting about crying.” Emma reached beneath the counter and produced three chef's knives. Heavy carbon-steel things, the sort made for taking apart a side of beef. “Kept these back. Thought you might want the good ones.”

Donna took them without a word. Handed the largest to Arthur, kept one and gave the third to Steve, who accepted it with obvious reluctance. “Didn’t imagine Tuesday evening ending with a kitchen knife in my belt.” He said.

Donna looked past her sister at the room. "Back door?"

"Bolted, and I've dragged the chest freezer across it. John is watching it…with a cleaver." Emma shrugged. "Anyone coming through there would have problems."

Donna nodded. "Good. Keep the rest close and don't open the door for anyone you don't know. I’ll come back when we know more." She said and turned towards the door.

Emma watched her go with a resigned expression. She didn’t argue or ask to come with them. She simply picked up the largest remaining knife from the counter and stood near the door with a quiet resolve.

Steve caught Arthur’s eye on the way out and tilted his head slightly towards Donna. Arthur watched her and understood, in the limited way available to a man who'd never had family to leave behind, what walking away from her sister was costing her. Steve wouldn't understand it either. They’d walk beside her and carry what they could.

They had just exited the restaurant when the air besides them bent. It was a distortion of air, like the shimmer above hot tarmac, except the tarmac was cold and the shimmer was assembling itself into geometry. Thin lines of pale blue light intersected in mid-air, connecting and folding into a structure that rotated slowly into coherence.

The three of them froze, Donna’s hand had found her blade.

The structure in front of them stabilised. It was a rotating geometric form, roughly the size of a human head, hovering at eye level. Its internal lines were shifting and reconnecting with precision at impossible angles.

Arthur stared at it and it stared back. It was more a feeling than any actual staring back, considering the thing didn’t have eyes. But several of its angles aligned towards him, before it spoke.

“Candidate evaluation complete.”
