Chapter 46 – The Square

ARC IV: THE THINGS THAT STAY

She gets Yuki, a frog, and the beginning of something she did not plan. Also: somewhere outside the server, someone who has never played a video game in her life is creating a character.

She closed the forum feed at 07:43 and did not open it again.

The guild hall had a particular quality on war-preparation days. Not loud, exactly. Concentrated. Alaric had claimed the main table entirely, his tablet surrounded by three empty mugs and a notepad with tabs color-coded by urgency. Bron was in the training yard with Boris, running the same drill sequence he had run yesterday and the day before, and Boris participated with the quiet intensity of something that had found its purpose and was not going to be distracted from it. Thorne had posted seventeen field research notes on the guild board before breakfast. Rigor had annotated four of them in red ink. Thorne had not removed the annotations, which was its own kind of conversation.

Livia stood in the doorway for a moment, notepad in hand. Someone had left a mug of tea on the windowsill for her — the right kind, already cooled to the right temperature. She picked it up. Nobody claimed it. She took her notepad and left before anyone could add her to a planning meeting.

The Square sat where it had always sat, at the intersection of Aethelgard’s four main districts. Players crossed it constantly on their way somewhere else. The fountain at its center was decorative — no buffs, no quest triggers, no mechanical function — and the water moved the way water moved when someone had rendered it carefully and then forgotten about it, which gave it a quality that most of the server’s designed features didn’t have. It looked like it had been running for a long time regardless of whether anyone was watching.

Livia sat on the fountain’s edge, set her mug on the stone beside her, and opened her notepad. Not for session notes. The other kind of writing.

Two days since the declaration. Miles’ planning room light was on when she walked past at 02:00 last night. She hadn’t knocked. She had stood in the corridor for a moment, long enough to hear the sound of pages turning, and then walked on.

She wrote: ‘Nobody is saying anything about the light. Nobody will. That’s its own kind of care.’

Then she stopped writing and looked at the square. Near the south entrance, a vendor had appeared overnight with a new banner: “War Preparedness Bundles — 12 Potions, 1 Tactical Map, 1 Emotional Fortitude Tincture (Results Not Guaranteed).” A player in mid-tier armor was examining the tincture with the expression of someone who had run out of better options.

She decided not to have a feeling about that.

The fountain made its sound. Someone’s mount — a small fire-salamander — had wandered into the fountain basin and was sitting there with visible satisfaction while its owner stood at the edge looking resigned. The server’s birds, rendered in clusters of three or four, moved through the square in their usual patterns. Above the civic hall, the community board had a new category she hadn’t seen before. She made a note to check it later.

She was refilling her notepad margin with small observations when Yuki sat down on the fountain’s edge.

Not next to her. About a meter away, on the curved stone edge, with two drinks she’d brought from somewhere. Standard gear, not stream gear — the layered mage equipment she wore when she was actually playing rather than performing. Her Voidpetal staff rested across her knees. She was not live. Livia could tell because Yuki’s posture when she was live had a particular quality of awareness, a slight forward lean, an orientation toward the invisible audience. Right now she was just sitting.

She set one of the drinks in the space between them on the stone. “Green tea extract. The vendor calls it something else.”

“You knew I was here,” Livia said.

“I saw you leave the guild hall. I was in the area.”

“You’ve been in the area for two days.”

Yuki picked up her own drink. “I like this square.”

Livia looked at her. Then picked up the tea. It was the right temperature. Yuki had timed it.

“Thank you,” Livia said.

“Discount for two.”

The fire-salamander was still in the fountain. Its owner had given up standing and was now sitting on the edge, scrolling their HUD. The salamander had its eyes closed.

“How long do you think he waits?” Yuki said. “Before he starts.”

“Alaric’s middle estimate is eleven days. Low is four, high is eighteen. He presented all three to Miles with full methodology. Miles said thank you and filed them.”

“And Miles’ estimate?”

“He hasn’t said.”

“He won’t,” Yuki said. “Because the number isn’t the point. He knows Caelric better than the model does.” She turned her drink in her hands. “He’s not going to share the number because sharing it would make it feel like that’s what matters. And it isn’t.”

“You’ve been thinking about this,” Livia said.

“Since the declaration.” Yuki looked at the square. “The way Caelric built it — no threats, no performance, just: here is a fact, here is a choice, here is what follows from each option. Very clean.” She paused. “Too clean. That’s the tell. It’s the structure of someone who has already decided what happens and is walking everyone else through the documentation.”

“He’s not trying to win the war,” Yuki said. “He wants the Contract. The war is just the mechanism that gets it for him.”

“Yes,” Livia said.

“So what does he actually want it for.” Not a question. A problem she was working out loud, like she was thinking on stream except without the stream.

“That’s what I don’t have yet.”

“But you’re looking.”

“I’m looking.”

The silence between them had the quality of something being decided.

“I’m not joining B&B,” Yuki said.

“I know.”

“Not switching sides either. But I have the same question you do. And you don’t have a combat stats.” She looked at the fountain. “I’d like to know the answer. To what he actually wants it for.”

“Next Tuesday. I have a location I want to check. I’d need someone who can handle themselves if it’s not what I expect.”

“Tuesday works.”

“I haven’t told you where yet.”

“Tuesday works,” Yuki said again, in the exact same tone.

Livia wrote in her notepad: ‘Yuki — Tuesday. Wants the answer. Same question.’ Then she looked up.

“Do you write everything down?”

“Everything that matters.”

“How do you know what matters?”

Livia considered that. “You don’t, always. Sometimes you write it down first and find out later.”

Yuki was quiet. Then: “My chat would lose it if they knew I was sitting here.”

“Why aren’t you live?”

“Some things aren’t content.” She said it simply. Not a principle, just a fact about this particular moment.

The frog appeared seventeen minutes into the silence.

It came from the fountain. Not with a splash or an announcement or any of the usual signals that something significant was about to happen. It surfaced from the decorative water, climbed onto the stone edge between them, and directed its full attention at Yuki.

Medium-sized. Green, with a slight iridescence that the server’s rendering had given it. Pixelated glasses, slightly askew on its actual frog face, the frames catching the afternoon light. It sat with the posture of something that had arrived at a destination.

It inflated its throat sac. The server rendered the sound as somewhere between a croak and something that, charitably, could be described as melodic.

“Hello,” it said.

Yuki leaned back about six inches. Not in alarm. In the specific way of someone recalibrating their expectations for the next several minutes. “what the hell.”

The frog hopped three inches closer. Its glasses shifted slightly with the movement. “I have been rendered by ninety-eight thousand and seven votes. I am Blorbo. I have been waiting to introduce myself to someone suitable.”

“Suitable.”

“Your focus when you’re concentrating is compelling. I noticed it during the Championship. The way you read a situation before you move — very efficient. Also your posture.”

“You were watching my posture during the Championship.”

“I notice many things,” Blorbo said, with the complete serenity of someone who saw nothing problematic about this.

Yuki looked at Livia. Livia had her pen hovering over the notepad. She was writing something.

“Are you writing this down?” Yuki said.

“Everything that matters.”

“I thought you said you didn’t always know what matters.”

“I have a strong feeling about this one.”

Blorbo produced a daisy from somewhere the rendering engine did not fully account for. Slightly wilted. He extended it toward Yuki with one foreleg, with the confident ease of someone who had considered whether this was a good idea and concluded that it was.

“Ugh,” Yuki said. “Gross.”

“I understand this is sudden. I am patient.”

“You are a frog.”

“I am an entity of significant server presence who chose a frog rendering. Small profile. Aquatic access. Excellent low-angle observation. Also I find the aesthetic appealing.” He adjusted his glasses with the same foreleg that was holding the daisy. “Primarily the sightlines, though.”

“Primarily.”

“Yes.”

Livia kept writing.

Blorbo turned to her. His head tilted at the angle of something doing a genuine assessment. “You’re the therapist.”

“Yes.”

“I’ve been meaning to speak with you. I know things about this server. Places that aren’t on the map. Things that were here before the map existed.” A glance at Yuki. “But that can wait. I am currently otherwise engaged.”

Yuki put her drink down. “You said places that aren’t on the map.”

Blorbo’s iridescent patches shifted slightly brighter. He set the daisy on the fountain stone, carefully, stem-side down, as if expecting it to stay. “Yes. I have existed in this server in various forms since before the current rendering architecture. I have passed through zones that were deprecated, paths that were closed. And one place that was neither — it exists between the zones players use and the zones the system uses. I passed through it once, while the votes were accumulating and I was not yet fixed. Not yet… this.” He gestured at himself: the glasses, the iridescence, the daisy on the stone. “It felt like waiting. Like a room that had been ready for a very long time.”

“Could you find it again?” Livia asked.

“Yes,” Blorbo said. Then: “Mostly.”

“Mostly.”

“The general direction is certain. East, past where the map ends. After that…” he considered, “…the path responds to who is looking. I believe multiple people with different kinds of attention would be more effective than one.” He looked at Yuki. “Particularly someone who finds things that prefer not to be found.”

“What does it feel like?” Yuki said. She had gone still in the specific way she went still when something was worth paying attention to.

Blorbo considered this with visible effort. “Between things,” he said. “Like it is waiting. The rest of the server has texture — sound, movement, the usual noise of a place that is being used. This place has none of that. It is very old and very quiet and it knows you are there.”

“Does it have a name?” Livia asked.

“Not one I know,” Blorbo said. “I never entered. I approached it once and then did not approach again. It did not feel like the right time.” A pause. “I think I was waiting for the right time.”

Yuki had pulled out her HUD. Not her stream overlay — the map interface, the one she used for navigation. She was looking at something.

“East past the rendered zones,” she said. “The Cradle of Aethon. It’s on the map — official name, been there since launch. No active quests. No content.” She paused. “Players call it The Maw. Every client that enters crashes. No warning. Just: in, then desktop.”

Blorbo looked at her. “I have never crashed,” he said. “When I approached.”

Yuki looked at Blorbo for a long moment. Then at Livia.

Neither of them said anything.

The fire-salamander opened one eye, looked at all three of them, and closed it again.

“Tuesday,” Livia said. “All three of us.”

Blorbo picked the daisy back up. “I will bring a better one.”

Yuki stared at the space where the daisy had been sitting. Then at Blorbo. Then at Livia.

“This is going to be a thing,” she said.

“Almost certainly.”

Somewhere outside the server, a laptop that smelled like instant coffee and three weeks of anxiety had been open to the Aethelgard character creation screen for forty-seven minutes.

The name field: ‘lumiere_27.’

It meant something specific to exactly two people in the world. One of them was in a hospital bed in a city that was not this one. The other was sitting in a borrowed apartment, in a chair that was not hers, in front of a game she had never played, trying to find a way in.

The name had taken eleven minutes. She’d typed her usual account name first, then deleted it. Then typed ‘a_normal_player’ and stared at it for four minutes before deleting that too. ‘lumiere_27’ had come to her the way the right words sometimes came — without announcement, already finished. She’d typed it and left it.

Class selection: Mage, Rogue, Warrior.

She read the descriptions twice. Mage: complex skill system, requires understanding of mana resource management. Rogue: mobility and stealth, requires map familiarity. Warrior: direct combat, high resilience, straightforward mechanics.

She picked Warrior. It was the most honest description of what she was doing here.

Appearance: six minutes of adjusting sliders before she clicked ‘randomize’ and accepted whatever came up. Tall, red hair, serious eyebrows that were not her eyebrows, armor that was slightly too large. The character looked like someone with no particular plan who was going to do the thing anyway. She kept it.

She clicked: Create Character.

The screen dissolved into a sunlit field. An NPC in cheerful armor materialized in front of her. “Welcome, brave adventurer! I will guide your first steps in Aethelgard!”

She typed into the chat bar: ‘how do I find a specific person in this game’

The NPC said: “Let’s start with the basics! Press W to walk forward!”

She pressed W. Her character walked forward three steps and collided with a decorative fence post. Stopped. She pressed W again. The character continued walking against the fence post with patient futility.

She typed: ‘I am stuck in a fence’

The NPC said: “Excellent movement! Now let’s try your first combat encounter!”

Six players responded to the global chat message within ninety seconds. Two of them were helpful.

She got herself out of the fence. She started walking again, in what felt like the right direction, and discovered that the apples she kept passing were collectible if you pressed E on them, and pressed E on all of them, and by the time she had figured out how to open her inventory she had forty-seven apples and was walking steadily away from everything she was trying to reach.
