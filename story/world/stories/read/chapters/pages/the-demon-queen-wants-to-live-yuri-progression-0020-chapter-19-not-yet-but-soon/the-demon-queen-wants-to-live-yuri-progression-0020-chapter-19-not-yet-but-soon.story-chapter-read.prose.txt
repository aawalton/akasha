A Demon Queen does not lie. Some people might call this a virtue. Those people are fools.

A lie is a concession. It is an admission that one is too weak for the truth. I have faced Gods and found them wanting. I will not flinch before something as feeble as truth.

The girl had asked me to save Marin. I had not agreed to her request nor had I denied it, though I had tried to say at least one of those two things. For a Queen, even a request left unanswered is a promise. It was now beneath me to let it stand unfulfilled.

I walked through the village with my jaw set. People moved out of my way. Men had always said I looked especially frightening when I was determined. Right now, I was. Almost as much as I had been that day, so very long ago. I passed my spot by the low wall. I did not stop. Small footsteps trailed behind me, I did not turn.

“Lys! Where are we going?”

The girl used that diminishing name again. Perhaps no more could be expected of a child. “We are not going anywhere. I am going somewhere and you are following me without permission.” The footsteps quickened until the girl was practically at my heels.

My destination was near the edge of the village. The quietest place in Hamel, and it had been for some time now.

I found the mother outside the hut, where she usually was. The boy was in her arms, swaddled in a blanket, though it was not cold. His eyes looked upon me, but they saw nothing. His chest rose and fell, but only in a mechanical way.

And there was the Hero, standing near the hut, stacking firewood. She had told me she did these tasks, that which the mother no longer did for herself. The Hero spotted me immediately, and she stiffened. For a moment I almost thought she might bar my path. She did not.

I stopped in front of the mother. The woman looked up at me slowly, and her face was a face I had seen before. It was in the aftermath of a dozen villages like this, long since razed by the horde. A face that proved that the fighting was done, and it had not gone the way one had hoped.

“I will see the child,” I said. “Marin, I believe his name is.” I pointed at him. “I have the power to heal things, and I will heal him.” My red eyes bored into the woman’s darker ones.

Her arms tightened around her son. I did not think she was trying to defy me. She did not look capable of it, not any longer. “They…they’ve all already tried,” she said. Her voice was hoarse and thin. “Trisha's cousin came from two villages over yesterday. Sh- she had a healing mark, a strong one…it was a Line. I thought it might do….” She trailed off, and then shook herself before continuing, “The herbalist mixed something he said would help. Been giving him that every day...forcing him to take it but….” She shook her head again, and this time the gesture was so small her head barely moved. “Please...just let him be.”

“I am not some village woman, and I am no mere herbalist with some petty cure,” I said, sleeve pulled back and left arm raised, so that she might see the white Line. “I carry a Sovereign mark, one that allows me to fix things. You say other people have tried and failed. I say that none of those people are me.”

I did not understand the issue. A Queen stood before her, offering help. Men had committed great sacrifices all in the hope that I may look their way and grant them a hundredth of what I was offering this woman now. My favor had been rarely given, but it had never been considered, let alone spurned.

The woman stared up at me. It seemed my words did little and less for her -I could see that plainly. Perhaps this woman simply did not understand the being that stood before her and the power she possessed. To this woman, all power was equal, and all of it had fallen short. That was a common mistake the powerless -and even many of the powerful- made.

“Please,” The woman said again. The word was barely above a whisper. “I can’t.”

I opened my mouth to push further. To assert, to do what I had always done. My authority was something I had wielded for a very long time, and not once had it failed me. I met the woman’s gaze.

No…perhaps I was wrong. Perhaps having hope itself was the issue. I had broken armies, and yet I did not know how to break this woman’s pessimism. There was no purchase here. I did not know how to break through whatever shell surrounded this woman, not without breaking her with it. The old world had been much simpler.

I stood there, arms crossed. Very well. I sat down.

I sat down, cross-legged, in the dirt in front of the hut with my arms folded. I faced the woman, and did not speak. I had seen someone do this before, in front of this very hut. I did not fully understand why it had worked then. I suspected it had something to do with the act itself. It was not a thing I would have thought to do on my own, and I was not entirely sure I was doing it correctly.

I tried it anyway. Perhaps Ash had spoken to this woman. If she had, and if that was necessary for this particular gambit, then I was at a distinct disadvantage.

The silence stretched. I saw Ash staring at me, but she did not intervene. I took that to mean that I was doing this correctly. Sara had stopped some distance behind me. I could hear her fidgeting with a stick, scratching shapes into the dirt.

Minutes passed as the sun moved overhead. A bird called from somewhere above us, and another answered it. The smoke from a nearby hearth rose into the air and thinned.

The mother watched me sit there. Her expression did not change. She tried to look away, which was rather difficult, considering I blocked much of her vision. Perhaps that had been the point. The minutes stretched more and the mother shot me worried glances that she might have hoped I didn’t see. I did. Then, she looked at me. Slowly, the expression on her face shifted.

Something about the woman’s shell cracked. I would admit the woman was a rather fierce opponent. Few had resisted any of my attacks for this long, but the result had always been inevitable.

Without a word, she shifted Marin in her arms and held him out -just slightly towards me. The boy was no longer being shielded. It was the smallest permission I had ever received. I rose.

“Sara.” I turned to the girl. She was ten paces behind me, watching with those very wide eyes. It was impressive she was still there, and she had been remarkably silent. “Stay there. Do not come closer until I allow you to.”

“But-”

“I do not know what will happen, and so you will stay there. That is not a request.” I tried to make my voice…gentle. The way I had heard Ash speak to children. It sounded different coming from my mouth than it had Ash's. The girl’s lower lip jutted out, but she stayed. It seemed this attack was rather effective against human children. I would remember that.

I knelt beside the mother and placed my left hand on Marin’s arm. His skin was warm beneath my fingers. That surprised me, though it should not have. Living bodies were warm. Even broken ones. “My power looks like a flame. Regardless of appearance, it is not one. It will not harm him.” I said. Better to warn the woman now, than have her interrupt needlessly. The woman faintly nodded.

I took a deep breath and pushed trickles of white Bloom into him. The impression came immediately, and it was wrong. Or perhaps it was more accurate to say that there had been an absence in the fish, some hollow I could fill with Bloom. There was no hollow inside the boy. He was still there.

I could feel him distant and walled off. The Ink’s rejection had not destroyed what was inside him. There was a…barrier of a kind, something smooth, and behind it I could feel the faintest pulse of what the boy's soul must have been like. I had occasionally used Spells that could see inside the minds of others before, and so this sensation was not entirely alien to me. This was the child’s mind, and it was still whole.

This was not what I had expected to find. I had expected to find nothing at all. Some thing that looked alive on the outside, but was dead on the inside. Perhaps that would have been better. Would have been more merciful than whatever the Ink had done to him here. The Cradle’s warmth hit the barrier and slid off without any resistance at all.

I pushed harder, with more mana, and even more focus. The brilliant white shone until my hand blazed against the boy’s small arm, and the barrier did not budge. The issue was not one of force. I knew that no matter how much force I had, it would not change the outcome -would not force the Cradle through.

I knew exactly what this was, for I had seen this before. This was a decision, one made by the System onto the boy. It had declared the boy unworthy. My hands were shaking. I reached out with my right hand. There might still be-

I forced my right hand back, and channeled only the Cradle. The white flared so brightly that the mother flinched beside me and even my eyes took a second to adjust. And with this much of the Cradle, I could almost feel the boy. I could almost hear him, past the barrier. The impression was faint, but there was one. The boy had been excited at the Inking. When he had knelt before the Inker, he had trusted the world to give him something good. It had given him this instead.

The light died as I pulled my mana back. There was no point in wasting it against a barrier that would not yield. I thought I had felt something, near the very end when I had pulled the Cradle back. The boy had hoped.

Damn it.

The mother was watching me with an expression even I could understand. She had hoped too, at some point. I braced myself for the accusation, for surely one must come now. “I’m sorry,” she said instead. I blinked, it was an apology. She was apologizing to me. As if my failure merited her comfort instead of her fury. It was one of the most absurd things I had ever heard.

There were footsteps, and then Sara was by my side. She moved forward and took the boy's other hand in her own. Green light pulsed from her palm. There was a Spark there, so small and faint that I had never noticed it at all. It was clearly some manner of healing power, one that did nothing.

“Child….” The mother swallowed. “I told you last time…it won’t take.” Her voice was very low. I heard it anyway.

The girl had done this many times before then. She had not told me this. A child’s Spark against the System. It was the most futile thing I had ever witnessed.

Sara’s hand dropped after a handful of seconds. The green faded and she panted hard. She looked at me and I felt something I hadn’t in a very long time: fear. Fear to meet her gaze, and what I might find inside it. There was no accusation in her eyes. She looked at me, the way she always did. The hope was still there. That was the worst part. I had failed, and this girl still hoped.

I rose to my feet. My legs were unsteady beneath me, though I kept my back straight. I walked away from that quiet hut, and made for the nearest gate. Ash fell into step beside me as we walked to the point where the village gave way to the wilderness beyond.

“Lysanthia. The Ink is tied to the System in some way,” Ash said quietly. “It is not a shame that you couldn’t heal him. I doubt there is anyone who-" She paused, but she kept moving. "You…you don’t look like you’ve given up.”

I kept walking. The forest was ten paces away. Then five. “It’s because I have not.”

“Giving hope where there is none,” Ash continued, and her voice was careful, “can be its own kind of cruelty. To that girl and to that mother.”

I stopped in place. Ash took a few more steps before she stopped too. “The System...” I said. “Yes, I felt the System inside that child. It has judged him unworthy, as it sometimes did in our time. That is the reason why he will not be whole again.”

“So-”

I cut her off. I knew the objection she was about to make. “If the System has passed its judgement on that boy,” I said, “then the System and I have a disagreement. It would not be our first. I will make it accept my verdict, rather than its own. I have done this before.”

Ash stared at me for a long time. “And…you are sure about this?”

I raised my right hand in the air. A small trickle of black fire rose into the sky. “Yes. It is beneath me to boast about something I cannot do. I can do this. It is simply a matter of practice.”

Ash looked at me for a minute longer, and then the tension in her shoulders left her. Then, strangely, she smiled. “Alright. I’ll come with you then.”

I had not asked for her presence, though I did not mind it. I moved towards the forest-

“Lys!”

I turned back. Sara stood at the edge of the village, a dozen paces behind us. I did not know how long she had been there, though I was sure she had not overheard. “Are you….” Her voice was small. She looked at me, and then looked at the ground.

A Demon Queen had to make many great speeches. Had to announce every great campaign with an even grander address. It was the way of the Class as it was me. I reached for the words, and did not find them. Instead, I simply said the first thing on my mind. “Not yet, but soon.” I said. “You will wait for me.”

The girl blinked, looked back up at me. I walked into the trees, and Ash followed beside me.
