Arthur pushed his frail body like it was a dying machine with one last job to do. Every movement hurt, but he turned pain into data. Observation into advantage. Efficiency into survival.

He noticed it early in the shift, certain zones of the mine yielded more copper than others. Small details. The shimmer of ore in the rock, the way other workers clustered around certain pits when the guards weren’t paying attention. More interestingly, the yield from each spot seemed to degrade over time. The longer they worked an area, the less they got out of it. That meant movement mattered. Adaptation mattered.

So Arthur led his father in a quiet dance across the pit, shifting from one copper-rich patch to the next before the others caught on. He didn’t draw attention. Just enough to stay ahead of the curve. By sunset, their cart was full.

And then another.

By the time the bells rang for evening withdrawal, they had filled five carts.

One cart over quota.

Arthur kept his face blank as they rolled the last load into the collection point. The Guardian counting carts barely looked up, just grunted and scratched a mark in the ledger.

Good. No fanfare. No suspicion.

Just survival.

The climb back to the hut was slow, the kind of weary trudge that didn’t need conversation. Dust clung to Arthur’s skin like regret - heavy, stubborn, impossible to shake. His muscles felt like shredded rope. But inside his mind, he was already moving again.

Today, the goal had been productivity. Tomorrow, it would be information.

He waited until they were alone again, firelight casting long shadows on the dirt walls.

“Dad,” he said quietly, “what kind of monsters live outside the wall? You talk like they’re worse than death.”

Paul took a long moment to answer. His eyes stared into the flame like it might reveal a memory he’d rather forget.

“They come in waves, son. Not often, every few years. But when they do... it’s hell. I only saw them during one of the attacks, back when I was young. Back when they needed runners to carry supplies to the wall.”

He paused. Shook his head.

“They’re not beasts. Not really. They move in packs. Most walk on four legs, some on two. Hulking things with claws longer than your arm and mouths that can bite through stone. When they come, it’s not one or two, it’s dozens, sometimes hundreds. We just call them the tide.”

Arthur stayed quiet.

“They hit the walls hard,” Paul continued. “But the walls hold. Always. Doesn’t matter how many try or how strong they are, they haven’t so much as cracked the stone. It's the only reason the Guardians survive, because the monsters can’t get through.”

“And the slaves?” Arthur asked. “You’ve never tried to fight back?”

His father gave a sad chuckle. “We’ve tried. Tools, rocks, fists. It’s useless. Doesn’t leave a mark. Even the Guardians don’t fight alone. If one of the beasts gets over somehow, they all gang up on it. Five, six at once. Even then, they bleed.”

Arthur absorbed the information silently. A full assault of monsters. Guardians struggling to win even in groups. A wall that was somehow unbreakable from the outside.

And yet...

“Then how do the oxen get out?” he asked softly. “The ones pulling ore. They leave through the gates, right? If monsters are everywhere, those animals are just walking bait.”

Paul blinked, thrown by the question. “I... don’t know. Never thought about it. Most of us don’t. The outside isn’t our concern. It’s not survival.”

Arthur nodded slowly.

That wasn’t a flaw in Paul’s thinking. It was conditioning. Willful blindness born of desperation. Don’t ask questions that won’t keep you alive.

But Arthur had no intention of surviving by accident.

Paul sighed. “You’ve been different lately, son. You used to be so quiet. Kept to yourself. But today... the way you worked, the way you talk now... it’s good. I like it. These silly questions give us something to talk about.”

He gave Arthur a tired smile. “You should ask your mother sometime. She’s the smart one between the two of us.”

Arthur allowed a small nod. He’d expected the comment. Counted on it, even. A slight shift in behavior could be overlooked, especially when it came after a head injury. Especially when that change meant hitting quota instead of missing it.

Still, he kept his voice neutral. “I’ll ask her when she is back.”

Because questions were dangerous things in a world like this.

But ignorance? That was fatal.

Mary returned to the hut well after dusk, her face smeared with exhaustion and the faint stench of whatever chemicals were used in the waste shed. She didn’t complain. None of them did. Complaints didn’t feed you here.

Arthur didn’t press her with questions right away. He played his part, settling into the rhythm of the home, if you could call it that. The three of them exchanged simple words as Mary began preparing their meager meal, but to Mary and Paul, the conversation meant more than food. It was routine. Familiarity. A small ritual of survival disguised as a family dinner.

Halfway through, Mary moved to the corner to stir the same bland gruel they’d eaten that morning. Arthur could smell the ash in it. The desperation. But when Paul casually mentioned they’d managed five carts that day, her face lit up like someone had whispered a miracle.

“Five?” she echoed, barely able to believe it. “Truly?”

Paul gave a tired smile. Arthur just nodded.

For a moment, she looked like someone had removed the weight from her shoulders, even if just a little. She knew exactly what failure at the end of this moon meant. But one extra cart today? That was hope. Hope bought in blood and sweat, but hope nonetheless.

When the food was ready, she served Arthur first and gave him the lion’s share again. This time he hesitated. “Mom… you need to eat too.”

She waved him off without missing a beat. “You need it more. Your body’s still healing. We couldn’t afford a healer, so food will have to do.”

“But...”

“No buts. We already feel bad enough, son. Don’t make me feel worse.”

He stopped arguing. Not because she won. But because he couldn’t afford to break the moment. Not when the warmth in her voice pierced something hard inside his chest. He was raised in steel rooms under fluorescent lights. Programmed to obey, survive, kill. But this? This cracked him open in a way nothing else had.

He would regret this bond. He knew it.

But he wasn’t strong enough to push it away.

Later, as they lay huddled in the dark, Arthur brought it up. The oxen. The open gates. The question that had been gnawing at him all day.

Mary didn’t hesitate. “I’ve heard the Guardians talk,” she said. “They say the monsters hate the smell of oxen dung. That it drives them away. The beasts won’t come near it.”

Arthur blinked in the dark.

She kept going. “That’s why oxen are so valuable. Their dung is used to make a kind of potion. It doesn’t stop the monsters, but it keeps them from getting too close to the supply routes. It’s not perfect… but it helps.”

Arthur said nothing. But inside, a new line formed on his mental checklist.

Monster-repelling potion. Find it. Steal it. Use it.

A few minutes later, Mary drifted into sleep beside Paul, their breathing slow and rhythmic. Arthur lay awake a little longer, staring at the cracked ceiling.

Tomorrow, he’d start learning more about the Guardians - the monsters who lived inside the walls, wore human faces, and smiled while they broke you.
