At the base of the Divine Temple, thirty soldiers stood in a tight line before the twin gates, barring entry to the end of the civil war.

In response, Zora thumped his staff once more.

“Open the gates, please,” he called.

The men didn’t move. Armour creaked and rifle stocks tightened against shoulders. Their formation was neat, but Zora heard all the small betraying sounds: a swallow half-stuck in the throat, a scuff of heel, and the stiff whisper of fabric shifting where a hand trembled around the trigger.

They were afraid.

Of course they were.

But one of them—a captain by the weight of insignia on his chest and the way his men oriented around his breath—stepped half a stride forward, rifle raised.

“Halt your advance!” he shouted. “For your crimes against the Divine Empress, we have shoot-on-sight orders from Her Four Households! To go against the words of Her Four Households is to go against—”

Zora cocked his head. “In the Plagueplain Front,” he said mildly, “there is a proverb: ‘When a king’s mouth is stolen, his words are no longer his.’ Tell me, captain, do you even know who you are really protecting?”

The captain faltered. Zora heard it in the brief static between heartbeats.

“The Divine Empress is tainted,” Zora said. “A witch stands in her shadow, whispering in her crown. Up in that temple…” He tilted his staff towards the height above. “Up there resides not the heart of the Attini Empire, but the heart of the Swarm itself.”

A murmur rippled along the line of rifles, quickly strangled.

“You are not Royal Guards,” Zora continued. “You are merely regular soldiers of the Divine Capital. You are men with wives and brothers and old mothers, perhaps in the Outer Regions as well, and you are not bound to Her Four Households by oath and blood. And, at this point…” He smiled faintly. “Surely you have heard of me.”

Silence.

The captain swallowed, then raised his voice again—though it wavered on the first syllable.

“You are… the Thousand Tongue,” he said. “Zora Fabre.”

“Correct. And what have you heard I have done, captain?”

“You… have destroyed fifteen strongholds along the Northeastern Road,” he began. “Eleven hijackings of heavy armament caravans that were supposed to reach our Forward Armies. Illegal redistribution of those same heavy armaments to untrained commoners in the northeast and northwest. Four thousand four hundred and thirty-two wounded soldiers, seven decommissioned War Commanders, one hundred and seven broken fungus mortars, and over twenty thousand anti-chitin bullets expended… and you also cleared the Swarm nests in the eastern valleys.”

“Correct.”

“You taught villagers how to fight. You saved Nohoch Ik’Balam before a train full of Mutant-Classes could reach the station. You destroyed an underground laboratory where children were being experimented on, and you… killed a Magicicada Witch.”

“Correct,” Zora said, when the captain finally ran out of breath. “And throughout all of those deeds, captain, what is the one thing that I have never done?”

The courtyard held its breath.

“... You do not kill humans,” the captain muttered under his breath, so quiet only Zora could hear it. “You are a man on the side of humanity.”

Then he stepped back, lifted one arm, and cut it sideways.

“Make way,” he said, louder. “Let them pass!”

Boots shuffled. Rifles dipped. The line split cleanly down the middle, opening a straight path to the gates.

Kita let out a breath that sounded a little like a scoff and a little like relief. Baya’s armour sang as he stepped past Zora and led the elite team forward, giving the captain a grave nod as he passed.

“Will you accompany us,” Baya asked, “while we cut off the head of the Magicicada Witch all men have been suffering under?”

The captain didn’t even hesitate. “Of course, my lord.”

Zora inclined his head at the gates. “Then, captain, will you open them for us?”

“We… cannot,” the captain admitted. “We were only told to guard the Divine Temple from the outside. The inner mechanisms are controlled by the Royal Guards within. We do not have the keys.”

“Fair enough.” Zora rolled his shoulders once and took a deep breath, feeling his lungs expand and contract. “I should warm up my voice anyways.”

He thumped his staff once more—lightly, almost politely—and then his voice cracked through the air.

“Open the gates.”

Wind obeyed.

A brutal pressure roared out from his mouth, bounced to his staff, then rippled outwards ten times stronger as a shockwave so powerful it struck the giant wooden doors—and tore them clean off their hinges, flung inwards like scraps splintering across marble.

Shouts burst from within—startled, panicked—as the Royal Guards scrambled for cover behind pillars and banners as dust billowed in clouds.

Gunfire answered an instant later.

Bullets shrieked across the vast base level of the Divine Temple. One round sliced toward Zora’s skull—he tilted his head a breath to the right, letting it pass. He’d heard the trigger tighten before the rifle even barked.

“Ah,” he murmured, stepping forward slowly as the elite team charged in before him, “they shoot faster than they think. That is good. A nation must begin somewhere.”

He raised his staff as Kita, Enki, Machi, and the elite soldiers scattered into the Divine Temple. The base level was a ring of columns and balconies, galleries stacked three-high around a central hall, and the Royal Guards had nested along the railings, rifles braced over carved stone.

Statues of past Divine Empresses loomed around them, silent witnesses to the mess their descendants had allowed.

Zora craned his ears at one of the statues for a moment.

“... In the red-dust south, where the wind scrapes the bones of the earth, a small girl trained with sawtooth blades as the dark stars fell,” he began. “She swung until dust rose in circles around her, and the wind itself learned to follow her will.”

The wind around him spiralled upwards in tight rings around all of them, deflecting bullets, pushing Royal Guards back, and creating a moving shield around them as they advanced.

“And when the first Insect God crawled from the canyon fire of the dark stars, she did not bow to it. She met its burning breath with her own heat. Her blades struck its chitined arms, and sparks leapt like embers fleeing a forge.”

A controlled wave of sparks burst forward, fanning out like a low fireline. It didn’t burn flesh, but it scorched the ground, making the Royal Guards shout and forcing them to retreat as they advanced up the first stairwell to the second floor. Kita disarmed and knocked out two enemies at once. Machi fired a rifle burst into the shadows. Enki opened silent wormholes that redirected spears into walls and bullets into ceilings.

“And when the beast fell, she stood upon its iron shell. The earth trembled under her feet, for she now carried the weight of the village she saved from the dark star.”

Just beneath the Royal Guards on the third, fourth, and fifth floors, the floors quaked violently, making their footing unreliable and slowing their charge.

No one fell to harm, but every movement apart from theirs became staggered.

“She marched north for three years, and fifty-six regions marched with her. Their feet beat the earth like thunder. Dust rose, wind followed, and roads straightened beneath their unity as they marched back south as one force—and at the southernmost end of the continent, she planted her flag and stomped, sending a ripple across the endless Crawling Sea.”

He thumped his staff once as they reached the sixth floor, knocking every ambushing Royal Guard there onto their backs before they could even fire shots at Baya and Kita.

“... And there, the First Divine Empress declared the end of the continent as the southernmost border of the Attini Empire, never to be tainted by the bug of a blood again,” Zora finished as they reached the topmost seventh floor, nearing the second set of wooden gates at the end of the hall. “What would she think, seeing those gates she built with her two hands tainted now?”

A final gust of wind knocked the gates off their hinges, throwing them back as Zora and the rest of his team stepped through together—into the Divine Hall.

Beyond the splintered doors spread a space so vast Zora could feel the air losing its nerve before it reached the far walls. Sound went out and came back thin, stretched over high ceilings, polished stone, and banners that hung like still waves. At the end of the hall rose an elevated dais, and upon it sat a throne welded from ant heads—mandibles interlocked, chitin polished and lacquered until it gleamed like an effigy of conquest.

To both sides of that throne, four raised platforms jutted out at staggered heights. On each plateau sat a more conventional throne: four seats for the Lords of Her Four Households. Their arrangements made a kind of crown around the central figure on the ant-head throne.

The Divine Empress herself sat there now.

Her posture was straight, but everything else about her sagged. Her chin was propped on one hand. Her gaze—at least from the way silence sat around it—was turned vaguely toward them, disinterested, bored, as if a warlord kicking down her gates were no more than a change in the day’s procession.

On the four flanking thrones, the Lords of Her Four Households and their noble retainers sat like mannequins brought in to fill a script, while thirty Spore Knights stood in heavy armour around the hall, each wielding a giant sawtooth blade at rest. Their breaths were shallow. Their pulses ticked faint but steady in Zora’s ears, but their bodies didn’t carry alertness. Their energy was dim; their attention didn’t bite at the intruders at all.

Here, not a single visor pivoted.

Everyone behind Zora tightened instinctively, hands drifting toward hilts and triggers, breaths held in the seams of their armour. It was wrong. It was unnatural. The Spore Knights didn’t so much as turn their heads towards the intruders, and that just wasn’t right—but Zora himself barely spared them a thought.

His staff was humming, and the reason for that stood at the Divine Empress’ right hand.

A lone attendant with her veiled head bowed, hands folded, still as lacquered wood.

Too still.

“... What is the plan here?” Zora asked. “Is this where ‘Reverberator’ Decima chooses to make her last stand?”

The veiled attendant finally moved. Slowly—deliberately—she set one hand upon the Divine Empress’s shoulder. Zora’s brows pulled tight. That gesture alone was wrong. No attendant would dare touch the Empress so familiarly, let alone at a time like this… but the Empress herself didn’t flinch. She didn’t startle. She didn’t even blink.

His frown deepened.

Then the ‘Divine Attendant’ stepped away from the throne and began down the stairs with a measured, regal glide.

The regality didn’t last long.

With each step down, her human disguise peeled off with it—literally peeled, flesh-coloured skin tearing like paper. Beneath, chitin plates gleamed like the sun. Four arms unfolded from her sides, and braided brown hair—grafted unnaturally onto her scalp—flowed off her skull. Between her brows, three small eyes blinked open like embers fanning to life, and by the time she reached the bottom of the dais, a five-eyed humanoid cicada draped in jewellery and noble robes stood where the Divine Attendant should’ve been.

Small curses tore out of the team behind Zora. Machi tightened her throat, Kita’s breath hitched, and even Baya looked slightly uncomfortable looking at the Magicicada Witch. Only Enki didn’t react. The boy merely raised his rifle a degree, as if he’d expected this exact moment.

Zora only scowled harder, because the Divine Empress still hadn’t moved. Not a gasp, not a tremor, not the smallest sign of horror or revulsion. She simply stared blankly ahead with hollow eyes, posture slack. Even more alarming, her Four Households seated at her sides were exactly the same, empty and unbreathing like dolls set on display.

No mental manipulation spell should’ve held through this. Seeing a Magicicada Witch in the flesh should've made them come to their senses no matter what, so…

Why?

What did she do to them?

In response, Decima reached into her mouth with her upper right hand.

Zora heard cartilage shift. He heard chitin scrape—and then she pulled something out with a wet, choking gargle.

A small chitin wand slid out of her throat, trailing a slimy membrane before the air dried it clean.

… There it is.

She held her wand with eerie poise, all four arms aligning around her like the petals of a poisonous flower preparing to bloom.

All five of her eyes fixed upon Zora.

“On that train, the first time we ever spoke, I told you I would strive to make history repeat for you,” she murmured, her voice now doubled—human cadence atop insect resonance. Then she grinned, readying her wand in front of her. “Come, Thousand Tongue. We shall have our spellcasting duel.”
