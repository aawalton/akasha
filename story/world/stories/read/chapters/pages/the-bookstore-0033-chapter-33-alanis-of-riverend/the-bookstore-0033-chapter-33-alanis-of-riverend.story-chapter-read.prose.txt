Another kick drove dirt across the alley and slammed into the unprotected back of the curled-up half-elf.

The previous one had struck her stomach hard enough to empty her lungs, so Alanis could only clutch her knees and try to make herself smaller. It did little good. Pain never stayed where the blow landed. It spread fast—through ribs, spine, arms, pride, and memory.

And in that moment, she could not honestly say what hurt more. The beating itself was nothing new. Alanis had long since grown used to sneers, contempt, and the cruelty aimed at half-elves. She had endured humiliation before. She had been shoved, mocked, cornered, and made to feel lesser for the crime of existing between races. Even being beaten three against one was not, by itself, enough to break her spirit.

What she could not endure calmly was the expression on the pure-blood elf’s face. He stood just beyond the reach of the kicking, clean and composed while his lackeys did the dirty work. His golden hair and fine clothes marked him as someone used to being obeyed. His eyes held no rage, only cold satisfaction. He looked at her as if she were filth on the street, something unworthy of sharing the same air.

That was what made her chest ache. Elven disdain toward half-elves was no secret. Alanis had known it all her life. But she had never expected one of them to take such personal interest in ruining her. The theft would have been enough. The threats had made it worse—promises to have her fired from the guild, to force her and Dorothy out of their house, to press his family’s influence until there was nowhere left in Riverend for her to stand upright.

That was what frightened her. Not the pain, or the insults, but the power behind them.

So when someone interfered—when a group suddenly forced the attackers back and broke the rhythm of the beating—Alanis did not waste the chance. She rolled quickly, ignoring the pain in her side, and reached for her bow where it had fallen beside the wall.

Her hands shook violently as she came up into a crouch.

The three who had stepped in were not city guards, nor mercenaries, nor anyone Riverend would fear. The big one had already grabbed the largest of her attackers. The foxkin held another back with a wooden staff. The robed girl stood tense and ready, caught between spell and judgment.

The pure-blood elf had just been shoved aside and was turning back with disbelief and fury, as though he could not understand how anyone had dared put hands on him.

Alanis drew. There was no time to think properly. Pain, fear, humiliation, and something deeper all crashed together inside her. The alley narrowed around that feeling until there was room for only one thing: him.

Her gift answered. Woodmancy surged through her more eager than ever before, without hesitation, without muddle, without the half-formed grasping she had known in practice and frustration. Bow, hand, wood, arrow, and path—they aligned in one violent purpose.

She released. The arrow flew like it already knew where it belonged and struck the elf in the chest.

No one moved. Everyone in the alley just stared.

The attackers had not expected the battered girl on the ground to respond with a killing shot. The adventurers had not expected the rescue to become murder before they could even establish control. Alanis herself looked from the fallen elf to her own shaking hands as if they belonged to someone else.

Cyrus released the man he had been grappling with and took a step back.

Hakon dropped his staff and ran to the fallen elf immediately.

Iris’s spell fizzled before it fully formed. She turned and glared at Alanis with naked alarm.

Then Hakon touched the wound, checked for breath, and went still.

“He’s...” The foxkin swallowed. “I can’t save him. He’s already dead.”

The words fell into the alley like a death sentence. The two surviving attackers exchanged one horrified glance.

Then they ran.

“You’re all dead!” one of them shouted over his shoulder.

The threat echoed after them into the street.

Cyrus began to panic.

“This wasn’t our fault,” he said, too loudly. “This wasn’t—we didn’t do this, we were helping—”

“Shut up,” Iris snapped, though her own voice shook. “Do you want the whole street to hear?”

She looked from the corpse to Alanis and then to the corner of the alley, thinking too fast and not fast enough.

“Why?” she demanded at last, the fear in her turning sharp. “We could have handled them. You could have shot his leg, his arm, anything. Why did you kill him?”

Alanis opened her mouth, but nothing useful came out. There was no defense she could offer that would not sound like madness or guilt. She had killed him. The act stood in front of all of them, impossible to soften. Whatever anger or terror had driven the shot no longer mattered beside the body at their feet.

Hakon recovered first. He looked toward the street, then deeper into the alley, scanning for witnesses, anything dropped, anything that could tie them to the scene.

“We need to move,” he said. “Now.”

“Where?” Cyrus asked. “Back to the inn? No—we leave town. We leave right now. If we get far enough—”

“They’ll notice us faster if we panic,” Iris said, though the words lacked full conviction.

Then all three turned to Alanis. The half-elf felt suddenly colder than the evening air.

They were right to look at her that way. The mess had come from her. Their danger had come from her. Dorothy’s danger too, now that the thought reached properly into her mind.

That last realization cut through the worst of the shock.

“You won’t get out easily,” Alanis said quickly. “Not now. Not if it’s him. His family has influence in town. They’ll hear about this soon.”

Iris’s expression hardened further.

“Then what?”

Alanis forced herself up despite the pain.

“Come with me,” she said. “This is my fault, but I’ll do everything I can to get you out of town. I think... I think I have a way.”

She did not wait for permission. There was too little time, and if she stopped to think too long, fear would root her in place. So she turned and ran—or rather limped quickly—out of the alley and into the poorer streets to the south.

After a moment’s hesitation, they followed. Relief nearly buckled her knees. She led them through dark, narrower roads, turning where habit and desperation guided her, until ten minutes later she reached the small shabby building she and Dorothy called home. It looked barely better than a shack from the outside, but to Alanis it had always meant safety.

She threw open the door.

“Dorothy!”

“Aye, girl, what are you fussing about?” came the answer from the back. “It’s too early for supper to be—”

Alanis did not wait for the rest. She rushed into the kitchen and stopped only when she saw the dwarf. Dorothy stood over a clay pot at the stove, long bright red braid hanging down her back, one hand stirring with practiced force. The sight of her lifted one terrible weight from Alanis only to make the others feel heavier.

Dorothy turned quickly. The irritation in her face vanished the moment she saw Alanis properly.

For a second, Alanis wanted to collapse into the dwarf’s arms and let her decide everything. Dorothy had always been there. From the first day she took in a starving, frightened child, she had been the one fixed point Alanis trusted. The broad hands that could strike a table hard enough to crack sense into a fool had also always been there to catch her when she fell.

But this was too large to dump into Dorothy’s hands without trying to stand first.

Tears came anyway.

She hated that.

“Dorothy, I...” She swallowed and tried again. “I messed up badly.”

Dorothy’s face softened into the sort of reassuring smile she used whenever she thought the world had frightened Alanis more than it deserved.

“No, this time I really...” Alanis shook with the effort of saying it plainly. “Dorothy, I killed someone. I killed that damned elf.”

The words tore out of her.

Whatever remained of her control went with them. Alanis stumbled forward and buried herself in Dorothy’s embrace, crying in great, ugly sobs she could no longer contain. Fear, guilt, pain, and a tiny shameful pulse of satisfaction at the elf’s death all twisted together until she could barely tell one feeling from another.

Dorothy stiffened. Her arms still came around Alanis immediately, but the change in her body was unmistakable. She understood at once that this was not some tavern scuffle or broken cart or guild insult. This was dangerous, potentially deadly.

And still she held her. From the day she brought Alanis into the house, Dorothy had promised in her own silent way that the girl would never face the world alone again. Now that promise had become heavier than either of them could bear easily.

“I’m sorry,” Alanis choked out. “Dorothy, I’m so sorry—”

“No,” Dorothy said, firm at once. “Now is not the time for that. And believe me, girl, we’ll talk about it later.”

She pulled Alanis back just far enough to look her in the face.

“Tell me exactly what happened. Everything.”

So Alanis did. Between breaths and tears and broken starts, she told the story as clearly as she could. Dorothy listened without interrupting, her face settling into a hard stillness that frightened Alanis more than shouting would have.

When the telling was done, Dorothy pulled her into one more strong, steady embrace. Then she asked an important question.

“You brought those adventurers here?”

Alanis nodded weakly.

“Go call them in.”

“Dorothy...” Alanis started, suddenly afraid of what the next words might be.

“Go,” Dorothy said. “Quickly. We leave town now. It won’t be long before they come looking for you.”

There was no room left for argument. Alanis wiped at her face, turned, and went back toward the front room.

Sorry or not, regret would not turn time around. Reality had no mercy for truthful tears.
