The Inker had an expression I knew well. It was a mix of fear and reverence -the kind that came from seeing something truly overwhelming and almost beyond comprehending, and finding the beauty in it anyway.

"T-this cannot be," the Inker finally said. It was the first thing he had said in some time.

"The Ink merely finds. Is that not what you said?"

One of the soldiers had taken a step forward now and his friends looked like they might follow. The village people shifted nervously, trying to stare everywhere but at me. Only Ash looked directly, her hand reaching at her side for a sword she had left back at the hut.

The Inker slowly straightened and tried to look tall. A feeble gesture after the weakness he had shown. "S-show me your arms," the Inker said, voice carrying. "The Ink does not lie, but perhaps I merely misspoke."

Almost, I did not oblige. I extended my arms out toward him. The two marks did not look any different from the others given today, just two small dots. The Requiem was black and the Cradle was white.

He stared at my arms and swallowed. "C-consecration," he said slowly, and then louder. "Consecration!" He shouted it, despite the silence. "Two today have been blessed. Chosen for Consecration!"

Chosen?

That finally made the crowd stir, a little.

"Hamel! You have been blessed. You have been graced by good fortune!"

"What is this Consecration?" I demanded.

The Inker's gaze met mine, and this time there was none of the fear. There was something else -greed. "You." He gestured past me and toward Ash. "You two will be Consecrated. You will be taken in to an Academy. Your marks will be nurtured, so that your talents can be put to good use." He paused. "It is a great privilege, and a greater honor."

"No."

The Inker stared at me. His confusion lasted long. "This is not an honor you can choose not to accept." This came out in a whisper, the sound not carrying past me. "It is an esteemed position. Even as a half-demon you will be-"

"No," I said, louder this time. "Whatever small position you seek to bestow upon me is unbefitting one such as I."

There were footsteps. Ash came and stood beside me. "Apologies, Inker," the Hero said. "But I must decline as well. You honor me, but I am unfit for it."

"Consecration," the Inker said, his gaze moving past us and sweeping the crowd, "is one of the highest distinctions one can receive. To reject it…" The man cocked his head to the side, slightly. "Would only bring misfortune not only to those who denied the privilege, but all those surrounding them. Bad fortune."

There was a clicking sound -the sound of several gauntleted boots taking a single step forward in unison. The soldiers all had blank, stoic faces. Their gazes were steady. It was a threat, and a thinly veiled one.

Another set of chains, far more obvious than the last. Those cuffs had been placed upon me by the Pantheon. This fool thought to replace them. This small man? Ash shifted and something in her face looked stricken. She was looking back over her shoulder.

Damn it.

No doubt this man would make an example out of Hamel if we did not comply. Would he go so far as to raze the entire village? I did not know. All I knew was that many of my generals certainly would have, and did.

This submission did not bear thinking about. I looked behind my shoulder, found the girl: Sara. Saw her mother put a hand on the girl's shoulder. The hand was white.

I tried to reach for the marks. It was a desperate, instinctive thing. My mana surged toward the two small dots. They were Sovereign, weren't they? I willed them to answer, willed them to give me something. Anything.

They did not answer. The marks sat beneath my skin. I could feel them -the budding shapes of something along my mana pathways. My power washed over them and found no purchase at all. There was more there, but even this examination told me I would not have access to this new power here and now. They were newborn things.

The soldiers took another step. The Inker watched me with patient eyes.

My overwhelming power was long since lost to me, and in its place I had something I did not even understand. I had broken the world, and now I stood in a village square, powerless, while a middling priest with a middling mark threatened to cage me.

The fury nearly took me. It would have been so easy. Even without my marks, even with this thin and feeble mana, I could kill this man. Perhaps several of his soldiers before the rest brought me down.

And then Hamel would burn for it.

I took a breath and forced the anger away. And without the anger, I could see the board clearly. We were playing a game, this Inker and I. I had invented some of its rules.

I had seen this man before, in the shape of many of my Generals. When I had been Queen, they had fought over every morsel of recognition I deigned to throw their way. The size of the achievement mattered little. The conquest of a single border fort would see three of them claiming credit, each more loudly than the last. Zarvok, especially. The fool would trip over his own sister to be the first to kneel before me with good news.

This man was the same. He was no true leader, wielded no true power. He was a tool that channeled his master's will. He lived and died by the will of another. I did not yet know the value of my marks, or the Hero's, but this Inker had spoken of them with reverence and had then reacted with complete awe.

There was a solution here.

"Inker," I said. He paused. Whatever he had been about to say next died on his tongue. "You intend to drag us before your Archon." The word tasted strange on my lips. "Two unwilling women, bound and resentful. Two women with great potential."

The Inker's jaw tightened. He did not answer.

"It should be good news, and yet any man not a fool will know of the risk…of the meat tasting bitter simply because the hunter was too eager." I dropped my voice low. "Tell me, do you know what happens to men who bring back complications in place of gifts?"

"Is that a threat?" the Inker asked. There was less sharpness in him than there had been.

This was working. I took a single step forward and stared at the man's eyes. "There is no threat, Inker. I am merely explaining your position to you, since you seem unable to see it yourself. You can drag us to your master in chains, and do not doubt that you will have to use chains. Your Archon's first words will not be 'well done.' He will take one look at us and he will say 'why couldn't you manage two women on your own?'"

Ash squirmed uneasily at my side. It was good that she did not speak. This was no conversation for a Hero. The Inker looked at me, and I saw him hesitate. Good.

"Or," I said, "you can return to your master and tell him you discovered something extraordinary. Two women with valuable marks. You assessed these women and earned their cooperation. And that we now come willingly, as allies, because you were wise enough to see the value in that. That makes you the man who found and cultivated something rare. Not the man who lost control of his own ceremony. The difference is everything."

There was silence.

"You think very highly of yourself," he said quietly.

"I think accurately of myself."

He held my gaze for a long time and I held his. "I can simply kill you both here. No complications at all, that way."

"Indeed you can." I nodded. "But then you won't be able to bring back anything but two useless bodies. We might not be worth bringing back at all." I smiled, looked around. "An easy choice, if you like spending your life in these backwaters."

This was still a gamble, but I thought I had the right of it. It had been long since I had used my intellect for anything but crushing my enemies with elaborate spells. A long time since I'd had to use the pen rather than the sword.

The Inker slowly nodded and looked at Ash. "And does this hold true for you as well?"

"It does," Ash said simply.

The Inker looked at us for a very long time. And then he looked past us, at the line of soldiers. He was still weighing scales, even when the answer was obvious. He finally looked back at us. His jaw worked. "One week," he said through his teeth.

"What?"

"You will have one week before I return. And when I do return, the two of you will come with me willingly, as you say. As allies." He straightened his robes. And now the fear was gone. "And if I were to return here and find the two of you missing…I will not come looking for you. But there will be people who answer for you. I am sure you understand my meaning."

He was showing the blade behind the glove. At least this one had the decency to let me see it.

"We will be here," Ash said, voice steady.

"Very well then." The Inker took one step and closed his case. The latch clicked once, and then he turned to his soldiers and gestured. And the men began to move.

They moved as one, and for a few moments the only sound was from boots on dirt and the creak of armor. We watched them and the Inker head out onto the road and into the dark. Their steps carried for some time. Nobody spoke until the sound of their steps had faded entirely.

Tom let out a very deep breath behind us. "Well," Tom said weakly. "That's- that's that, then."

I finally turned and looked at the village. My gaze first fell on Sara, before it moved to Martha. "Thank you," Martha said.

I turned from her before I could do something foolish with my face. This had not been for them.

It took some time for the village to go back to what it was. For most of the people to return back to their huts.

I walked through Hamel in the dark. The ceremony had long since ended but the many lanterns still hung. Most still burned, but a few had gone out. Those that remained cast a warm glow over the mud paths.

There was no destination. I simply walked where my feet took me. And there were gazes on me.

I passed by a hut and the man standing outside met my eyes and nodded once. I moved around a corner, and the woman standing there lingered instead of hurrying away. Her gaze followed me back. A boy I had seen splitting wood two days prior raised his hand in a wave. I did not wave back. I did not know how.

There was no fear. Though perhaps there never had been any. Fear was a thing I knew what to do with. I had been feared for centuries. I had been fear for centuries. It made people predictable and useful. The looks I got now carried something else, something that sat unevenly on my shoulders, like cloth cut for someone else.

Gratitude. I thought that was its name. I had rarely been its subject before, especially not without doing anything at all.

Eventually, I stopped my wandering and moved back to the hut -Martha and Tom's hut, though they had given over their son's room to us without a second thought, as if anyone would invite strangers into their home.

The hut was empty. So the two had not returned then. I cast one look around and moved to our room. The door was open half a crack, enough for the light inside to peer out. I reached for it and stopped. Sound came from within, so quiet that without the silence of the village around me, I would not have heard it at all.

I knew the sound of weeping. I had caused enough of it.

Lantern light peaked out of the door as I peered in. There was Ash, sitting on the floor with her back against the far wall, knees drawn to her chest. Her shoulders shook and her face was buried in her arms. The two marks on her forearms were faintly visible in the lantern light.

The door was already cracked open. One push, the barest of force, and I could step inside. I did not know what I would say, only that I would have to say something. Something inside of me stung. It made me want to go inside and do something I'd so rarely done: ask, instead of demand.

I almost did it. I did not know how. My hand fell to my side.

I took two steps back and turned. I walked back out into the night air. The air was cool, and Hamel was quiet now. Somewhere, a dog barked once and then fell silent. I walked to a spot by the low wall, one where I had sat and failed at twenty different techniques. I sat there now, in the quiet, and did not try anything at all.

The Inker had given us time. Seven days of it. Whether that would be enough, for anything that mattered, I did not know.
