Kivamus looked at the captain.

Hudan continued, “Feroy was telling me earlier that adzees do not stay in one place too long if they do not find food, that is, good hunting grounds. Either they will attack tonight or tomorrow at the latest, or they will move on to another area. So I think we should keep everyone inside the village walls today. Then we'll decide what to do tomorrow based on what happens tonight.”

Kivamus did not answer immediately.

A full day without work outside the walls would hurt. The road diggers would lose a day. The workers at the dam site would lose a day. The farms outside would not be tended properly. The hunters would not go out. The coal miners would lose a full day of production too. That would cost the village. Literally.

But then he remembered the dark tree line and the reports from the guards.

Two adzees in the southwest. Three in the northwest. One or more near the southeast.

They had come far too close for his comfort.

“If the beasts came that near last night,” he said slowly, “then they are not afraid of the village right now. They might be hiding just beyond the tree line even now. That is too close.”

Hudan nodded. “Exactly.”

Kivamus exhaled. “Then yes. No one leaves the village today.”

Duvas rubbed his forehead. “That will delay several projects.”

“I know,” Kivamus said. “But the workers inside the village can still keep working. The barracks construction, its palisade, the tenement block, Darora’s workshop, Cedoron’s smithy, the soap work, the road marking inside the walls. There is still plenty to do.”

“And those outside?”

“We can afford one day of lost work if it keeps my people safe,” Kivamus said. “In fact, we can allot those workers to all the tasks going on within the village. I know Finn would be happy to have more laborers helping him with the tenement, even if just for a day. So will the other foremen.”

Duvas yawned again, then nodded. “I will send a few servants to the foremen. Pinoto, Yeden, and the others whose workers go outside. They will tell their people not to leave today and to help out where they can.”

“I’ll send mounted guards to all three gates,” Hudan said. “The gate guards are not to let anyone go out. Anyone coming in can be allowed inside, like visiting merchants, but no villagers leave until we say otherwise.”

“That works,” Kivamus said.

Hudan pushed himself to his feet. The movement looked slower than usual, but his voice was steady. “Then I’ll go speak with the guards.”

Duvas closed the ledger he had not really been reading. “I will meet with the servants.”

“Alright,” Kivamus said. “Do that. I’ll wait here for now. I’ll try to get something else done until then.”

Hudan paused and looked at him.

“No,” the captain said.

Kivamus raised an eyebrow.

“You do not need to wait for us or work today,” Hudan said. “You need to sleep. None of us slept last night because of the threat, but there will not be an attack during the day. We need to use this time to rest, since we might not get any sleep tonight either. Feroy understood that. He went to sleep so he will be ready when he is needed. The rest of us need to follow his example.”

Kivamus wanted to argue out of habit. That he couldn't afford a wasted day, where he could work on one of the various blueprints that were half finished, especially when the threats surrounding the village never ended.

Then he stopped.

Hudan was right.

“Fine,” he said after a moment. “You are probably right. I am not sure I could focus on work right now anyway.”

Duvas looked relieved to hear that.

Kivamus rubbed the side of his face and stood slowly. “It is better if all of us take some rest during the day. Tonight may be difficult again.”

Hudan nodded. “It will.”

No one said more after that.

The three of them left the table, each going toward his own task, while the manor hall remained quiet behind them.

***

By the time the sun went down, the manor yard was already crowded.

Kivamus walked slowly across the yard with Hudan and Duvas beside him. The light had faded quickly after sunset, and braziers had been placed in many corners to keep the manor properly lit. The extra heat was not welcome. It was already peak summer, and even though the rains had not started yet and the air was not muggy, the sweat on his back never really dried. There was barely any wind inside the manor walls.

At least the guards looked more rested than they had in the morning. Since no workers had been allowed to leave the village during the day, none of the guards had needed to patrol the farms, protect the road workers, or stand guard at the dam site. Hudan had ordered every guard to rest as much as possible before nightfall. It showed. Their faces were still tense, but most of them looked ready to do whatever was needed of them.

The servants and maids were busy too. A pair of servants carried a sack of grain out toward the miller’s place in the village, while two others came back with a freshly ground sack of flour, their hands white with it. Maids went in and out of the kitchen near the servants’ hall, helping with cooking and baking bread in the clay oven. Others carried already cooked food out to the waiting guards.

Yesterday, the guards who lived outside the manor had come only after the howls were heard. Tonight, everyone had gathered in advance. That made the manor feel smaller than usual. While the latest round of recruitment of twenty more guards was already over, taking the total number of guards to 120, around 50 of them were out of the village to escort the merchants, at the losuvil encampment, or out hunting. Even then, there were around 60 guards inside the manor now, including the women, apart from those who were posted at the watchtower. Most had already taken their swords and shields, with spears leaning nearby against walls or sheds. They sat or stood in small groups, eating early, speaking in low voices, and looking toward the gate more often than they needed to. The recruits would stay back to protect the manor if it came to that, while the experienced guards would go out to fight the beasts if needed.

They had all agreed that eating early was better. Once it grew fully dark, the adzees could come at any time. If the horns sounded, no one would get time for a proper meal after that.

Lucem and Clarisa were helping distribute food too, which was unusual enough that Kivamus noticed it at once. Lucem carried bowls with a seriousness he rarely showed during chores, while Clarisa moved beside him with a tray of cups. For once, she was not scolding him for doing something foolish, and he was not trying to escape the work.

On another side of the yard, Kerel and Nesser stood near Nurobo. The medic was giving instructions to Savomi and two other medics in training. A few pots of boiled water had been set nearby, and Nurobo kept pointing at the bandages and clean cloths as if the placement of each one might decide whether someone lived or died. Syrene had already brought out a full jar of their precious losuvil powder and handed it to him earlier. Nurobo had only grunted in reply before returning to work.

Some of the guards were already at the village gates though. Hudan had told Kivamus that each watchtower would have four or five guards on it tonight, most of them women because they could operate both crossbows and scorpions. The male guards would be posted below the towers near the gates, shields and spears close by. It was a practical arrangement. If anything came close to the walls, the tower guards could shoot from above while the men below prepared to hold the gates, or even to go out and take the fight to the beasts.

Kivamus still wished Tesyb and Trevalo had returned during the day. They were already overdue to return Tiranat by now, and having those eight trained guards here would have helped.

But now that evening had come, he hoped they did not arrive tonight. The forest was not safe in the dark, and if the adzees were still circling the village, then a tired caravan returning in the dark would be an easy target. Tesyb and the others would already be exhausted from travel. Better that they stayed away until morning.

The manor gate opened then, and Darora came in at a fast walk.

Kivamus turned in surprise. The carpenter looked dusty and tired, but his eyes were bright. A couple of his apprentices were behind him, both carrying tools.

“Darora?” Kivamus asked. “What are you doing here?”

The young carpenter stopped in front of him and bowed quickly. “Milord, we finished the third scorpion.”

Kivamus stared at him.

Duvas blinked. Hudan’s head turned sharply.

“You said it would not be ready until tomorrow,” Kivamus said. “And that was your earliest estimate.”

“I know,” Darora replied. “But we knew how important it was for the village’s safety, so none of us got much sleep last night. We had already worked late yesterday to finish as much work on the scorpion as we could. Then after the howls started last night, I could not sleep, so I went back to the workshop around four bells in the morning.”

He gave a quick, tired smile.

“When I arrived, many of the apprentices were already there. Layita too. They had come back because they could not sleep at home either. Each of them had started doing some part of the work they had been assigned.”

Kivamus felt something warm settle in his chest.

These were not guards. They would not be standing at the gates with shields and spears. Yet they had still found their own way to help defend Tiranat.

Darora continued, “Once I came, I stopped everything except the scorpion work. No crossbows. No other crafting. We worked on it all day, with everyone doing their best so that each part got crafted in a single try, to prevent wasting any time from having to repeat it. I started assembling it in the afternoon and I just tightened the last bolt." He grinned. “The third scorpion is ready to use now. It has not been tested yet, but I checked everything twice. There should not be any problem.”

He glanced toward the village wall. “If you allow it, we can mount it on the southeast watchtower now. It is already dark, but Layita had built the mount during the day. We only need to fix the scorpion in place. It should take an hour at most. I will need space cleared on the platform, though. Too many guards up there will get in the way.”

Kivamus looked at the captain. “Can we risk moving guards from the watchtower?”

Hudan nodded at once. “It is risky but it will be worth it. I will still keep one guard up there to keep a watch, and clear the rest of the platform for the carpenters. If they can finish within an hour, having a third scorpion tonight could make a big difference.”

Kivamus turned back to Darora. “You heard the captain. And you have done excellent work. I will remember this when the time comes for your next payment.”

Darora shook his head. “I am not doing this for money, milord. Tiranat is my home too. I may not be a guard but I still want to do my part to protect it however I can.”

Kivamus looked at him for a moment.

Not long ago, Darora had been a slave in Nokozal’s limestone quarry. He had never even been to Tiranat before that. Now he spoke of the village as his home, the same way Hyola did. Kivamus was not sure there was any reward that would mean more than that.

He nodded. “Then we should not waste time. Go ahead and mount it. Use any guards there if you need help lifting it up.”

Darora straightened. “You can trust me. I will have it done by seven bells.”

Then he turned and hurried back toward the gate, his apprentices rushing after him.

Duvas watched him go. “He really is talented. And motivated.”

“That he is,” Kivamus agreed.

The majordomo folded his hands behind his back. “I am glad we brought him here. I know I opposed you when you said we should send guards and free those slaves from the quarry, especially since we were so short of food at the time. But you were right, as usual. It turned out for the better in the end, even if that was the very first batch of slaves we had liberated. Hyola is already one of our best guards, her temper aside. And Darora has become invaluable for all these new weapons you keep making.”

Kivamus smiled, then looked around the manor yard, searching for the tall redhead. He did not see her.

“Where is Hyola anyway?” he asked.

Hudan huffed softly. “She was off duty last night, and on duty during the day. But she said she did not want to miss the fun of shooting a scorpion at the beasts tonight. So she took a couple hours of rest earlier, ate dinner, and went back to the watchtower to help.”

Kivamus laughed despite the tension in the yard. “Of course she did.”

Hyola was competitive enough that he should have expected it. During another short test yesterday, she had proven surprisingly good with the scorpion, just as she was with a crossbow. Isomi, who had been present during the first test, had done well too. Knowing both of them were on watch tonight made him feel a little better. If they got a chance to shoot at the adzees, the village would have a real chance of hurting them badly.

They kept walking toward the manor gate, and before long Feroy came in from outside.

The former mercenary looked tired, but alert. He had gone out to speak with the watchtower guards and tell them what to do if the beasts were seen while no senior guard was nearby to give orders.

Earlier in the day, Feroy had suggested setting small fires around the village. Not too close to the walls, but around 50 meters out from each corner tower where a scorpion was or would be mounted. A large log had been placed in each fire so it would burn steadily through the night. The idea was simple. The beasts might be drawn by the light or become curious about it. If they came close, the fire would make them easier to see.

At that distance, the scorpions could shoot very accurately. The sizable fire would also increase visibility for another 50 meters or so beyond it, giving the tower guards a better chance of judging where the beasts were even if they were 100 meters away. If the adzees did not come even that close, then it was unlikely that they would attack immediately anyway.

Since they had set fires at all four corners, the adzees would not see one dark corner and decide it was safer to attack there.

The ex-mercenary’s planning looked even better now, since Darora had finished the third scorpion ahead of time. Once it was mounted on the southeast tower, only the northwest tower would remain without one—the only corner without a gate next to it. That could not be helped for tonight. The fourth scorpion would take around four or five more days, and by then the adzees would have already moved on. Kivamus could only hope the northwest corner would not be where the attack came tonight.

Feroy walked up to them. “The tower guards know what to do now.”

“How are they?” Hudan asked.

“A little scared,” Feroy said plainly. “But ready. The scorpions help a lot. So do the archers.”

The village did not have many true archers. Yufim was out on a hunting trip, and Nurobo was now needed as a medic. But a few former hunters had enough basic skill with bows, that after being recruited as guards, they had all been trained properly by the self-proclaimed best archer of Tiranat. They also got more than enough practice whenever they went out on hunting trips. No one could create a good archer in less than a year, not really, but they still had enough experienced bowmen to add support where needed. Hopefully, more scorpions could be mounted on the watchtowers in the coming months so that it would not be necessary to post archers there after that, and they could keep hunting.

But that was for the future.

Feroy continued, “Most of them understand that they are not to shoot just because they see movement. If the beasts stay near the tree line, they watch. If they approach the fires, they warn the others, and blow the horn. If they come toward the wall or gates, then they shoot.”

“Good thinking,” Kivamus said.

They reached the manor gate and after looking outside for a while, they turned back toward the yard. Kivamus’s stomach reminded him that he had barely eaten today. The captain had once again forbidden him from leaving the manor if the howls started, so he decided he should eat now before he got too tense to have the dinner.

He had taken only a few steps back toward the manor house when the first howl came.
