The shift had started at 08:00 AM.

It was now 10:12 PM.

Sofia Rossi had developed, somewhere around hour six, the strange clinical clarity that came after sleep stopped being an option and started being a memory. She'd cycled through coffee. Cycled through tea. Was now drinking water, because her hands had started doing a small interesting thing when she held the mug.

The room had passed through a number of phases.

Phase one had been the realization. That realization had pulled the entire nexus out of whatever its analysts had been doing and arranged them in a tight, sweating crescent around the main display. Phase two had been the briefing, which had been short, because nobody had anything useful to say. Phase three had been the predictions.

The predictions had been the worst part.

Seong Jin-hwan stood at the front of the room in a suit that had been clean fourteen hours ago. His coffee machine, which he understood mechanically and used compulsively, sat half a floor down. He had stopped going for refills two hours in because the trip cost him three minutes of monitor time and he couldn't afford the three minutes.

"What can he learn from this?"

The voice belonged to Park, one of the systems analysts who had been at his station for the entire shift and had developed an audible squeak in his chair. Seong turned. Park flinched.

"Sir. Most of the class is locked. The real stuff, the abilities require Resonance thresholds. He hasn't even passed the first tier. The Lexicon barely opens." Park gestured vaguely at his screen. "Half the passives gate on level. He's level twenty-five. Whatever he is now. So, can he actually take anything out of this? Mechanically?"

The room considered this.

"Storyteller's Intuition," said Vasquez, the QA lead.

A beat.

"...Right," said Park.

"Lore Echoes on historic items and locations," Vasquez continued, reading from her screen because she had stopped trusting her memory three hours ago. "Provides psychic impressions, clues, or reveals hidden Conceptual Keywords. Passive. Always on. He's standing inside the abandoned draft of the entity his class is descended from. The whole room qualifies as a historic location."

"So he can," Park said.

"He can," Vasquez agreed. "If the circumstances allow."

"The circumstances," Seong said dryly, "have been allowing rather a lot."

A muted laugh moved through the room. It died fast.

Dr. Aris Thorne, who had been sitting calmly with a cooled cup of something herbal, finally spoke. She had not removed her reading glasses for two hours.

"Let's calibrate," she said. Her voice had its usual unbothered cadence, and the room re-organized itself around it like compass needles finding north. "The Shifting Galleries is one Stanza. A failed one. Listed in the original genesis documentation as The Fourth Revision - meaning it was discarded three times before being discarded a fourth. It contains no functional cosmology. It's a graveyard of an idea."

"So the lore payload is limited," said Park, hopefully.

"The lore payload is limited," Thorne confirmed. "With one exception."

"The Curator," Sofia said.

"The Curator." Thorne tapped a fingernail against her cup. "An Axiom Entity. Personally written, per Morpheus's own documentation, by the First Maker. He holds, at best, a fractured understanding of his Master's actual project. A guard dog left at an abandoned house. He doesn't have anything concrete. He has the idea of the man limited to what the man was inside that stanza, and a list of grievances against intruders."

"That's still a lot," Vasquez said.

"That's still a lot," Thorne agreed.

Seong rubbed his eye with the heel of his hand. The fluorescent overheads on the upper tier had started doing the thing where they appeared to flicker without actually flickering.

"Kage is currently blind on the First Maker thread," he said. "Functionally. He has the class. He knows he has the class. He knows it's legacy. He doesn't know whose hand he's wearing, doesn't know what the hand built, doesn't know what the hand was trying to build. If this goes well—"

"Or wrong," Sofia murmured.

"—or wrong," Seong continued, "he leaves this dungeon with context. A lot of context. Possibly a new quest thread."

"Which would be his second active genesis thread," Vasquez said. "Alongside Valerius."

"Two," Seong said.

He let the word sit on the table like a chess piece.

The previous timeline had been a train. They had known the schedule. They had known the stops. Now the timeline was not a train. The timeline was an X-wing skimming a few feet above a six-lane highway at light speeds, and somewhere in the cockpit was a man with a sentient sword.

"All right," Seong said. "Worst case. He leaves with more information, a new thread, and the dungeon loot. That's the ceiling. Yes?"

He looked around the room.

Nobody answered.

"Yes?" he repeated.

Park said, slowly, "Probably."

"Probably," Seong said. "That's the most we can say."

"That's the most we can say," Vasquez agreed.

Someone in the back tried to add an assumption and lost it halfway through the sentence. Someone else tried to add a different assumption and gave up before forming the verb. The hall went quiet, an organism running out of suggestions.

Dr. Thorne, very gently, turned her head.

She looked at Seong. She raised her eyebrows. She tilted her chin, perhaps four degrees, in the direction of Kenji Tanaka.

Kenji Tanaka was doing his absolute best impression of a professional adult. He had clasped his hands in his lap. He had centered his weight evenly on his chair. His face was doing nothing in particular. His left foot, however, was bouncing at approximately three Hertz, and he had been making small humming sounds for the past ten minutes that he believed were inaudible.

They were not inaudible.

Seong looked at Kenji.

A picture came up unbidden in his mind. Grey snow. Two figures. A bell. The tightness in his chest that he had carried home from the Ashenvale fight, the thing that had refused categorization, that had followed him into his elevator and his apartment and his shower. He had spent the most recent time treating it like an unexplained line item in a spreadsheet. Eventually he had stopped looking at it.

He looked at it now.

It was still there.

He exhaled.

"All right," he said. "There's nothing more we can do."

A few heads turned.

"There was never anything we could do, apparently," he continued, "because trying to predict what this player will do has never been productive. We've established that. Repeatedly. The model breaks every time we run it." He paused. "It's Morpheus's job from here. We are observers."

He looked at the wall clock. The shift was long past due.

He sat down.

He sat in the rolling chair in the center of the deck, the same chair from Ashenvale, which someone had since formally moved next to his usual standing position and never moved away. He crossed his legs. He stared at the main screen. He breathed.

The room watched him.

He was not bothered.

Without looking at anyone in particular, he took out his department card. He held it between his index and middle finger, in the air, in the direction of Kenji Tanaka, and said nothing.

Kenji's professional composure, which had been holding by a single thread for an hour, snapped.

He beamed. He stood. He took the card, said "yes sir thank you sir," and ran.

He came back six minutes later with both arms full of plastic bags. Shrimp chips. Rice crackers. Three kinds of cheese-flavored snack with names that did not legally translate. A bag of dried squid that nobody asked for and that Vasquez immediately claimed. Two trays of canned coffee. Four melon sodas. A box of chocolate-coated almonds. A small bag of preserved plums that Thorne accepted with a quiet "thank you" and tucked into her pocket without opening.

The chairs were already in formation. The mood, despite the hour, had risen, because nothing on earth restored a tired engineer faster than the suggestion of permission to stop being an engineer.

Kenji distributed snacks like a wedding usher.

The main screen showed Kage, on a bench in Korros, between an apothecary and a paint supply shop. The party was assembled soon after.

"They're starting," Sofia said.

"They are not starting yet," Seong said. "They are forming up. There's a difference. I would like everyone to please find their seats."

Everyone found their seats.

The pre-dungeon puzzle ran on the main display.

The party gathered before the projected doorway. The painted field. The crystal lens. Zherdev's key transforming, the lens being aligned through a combination of false shadows and a sun nobody could see.

Three rows back, a developer named Han leaned forward and opened his mouth.

"That puzzle was actually my id—"

"Shh," said three people at once.

Han closed his mouth. He chewed a rice cracker with dignity.

The signature mechanic activated, the painted landscape draining of color and the doorway thickening with weight. The whiteboard at the side of the nexus, which someone had earlier filled with rarity weights and threshold predictions, came alive with green checkmarks as each prediction landed correctly.

Then Kage stepped forward.

Black script spiraled off his hand and the meter slammed past one hundred percent and kept climbing.

"There it is," Sofia said, voice flat.

The sky tore. The breaking voice spoke. Master.

A junior analyst at the back made a small sound that was the polite version of oh shit.

The platform dropped.

The party fell into the void.

The main display caught up at the obsidian platform, and the floating frames came into view, and the crumpled parchment moons turned in their slow lazy rotation over the abyss, and the entire room exhaled, because regardless of how this ended, what they were looking at was not on the syllabus.

"Aesthetic Score: Eleven out of ten," said Vasquez, who managed the design QA pipeline. "We didn't even build half of that."

"Morpheus is rendering on the fly," Thorne said quietly.

"For one party."

"For this party."

The party figured out the marks.

The room watched them figure it out. The common marks stabilizing geometry. The rare marks pointing through the void. Julie's geometric ink doing nothing yet. Kage's floating script doing nothing yet. It was, in the way good systems design always was, beautiful to watch a problem unfold inside the minds of ten players who had no documentation.

"They're going to get it in two minutes," Kenji whispered, on his second melon soda.

They got it in faster than that.

The party crossed the bridge in formation. Anchor, passenger, anchor, passenger.

"They got it," Kenji breathed.

"Don't narrate," said Park, who was completely transfixed.

The Storm and Water frame opened. The party defeated the first reef mobs in a series of clean coordinated engagements. The room ate snacks. Kenji's leg had stopped bouncing. He had reached the stage where the data was so good that his body finally surrendered the hype and went calm.

Then Kage edited the Glasscoil out of the sky.

A small orange alert chimed at Sofia's station.

She looked at it.

She looked at it again.

"Director."

"Yes."

"A world boss has just spawned inside Korros city limits."

Seong did not look away from the screen. He simply tilted his head a fraction.

"In Korros."

"In Korros, sir."

"Which world boss."

"Designation reads," Sofia said slowly, "The Glasscoil - Untranscribed. Level thirty-six. Hostile. Currently active in the Artisan's Quarter."

"...Inside the city."

"Inside the city."

Seong looked at the screen, where Kage, fully unaware, was standing on a reef beneath an unfinished storm in a separate plane of existence, his Author's Ink reduced and his Syllable Debt rising.

A laugh escaped Seong. Quick, sudden, gone, the way a sneeze escapes.

The party defeated the Mawbound Draft-Behemoth. The room cheered, quietly. Han allowed himself a small fist-pump. Sofia clapped twice and then realized she had clapped and stopped.

They moved into the deep.

The Leviathan of the Unwritten Deep awoke. The room ate snacks. The fight resolved in fifteen minutes with the throat door opening on a yielded boss, and the first key fragment dropped, and someone in the back muttered "one of five," and the room settled in for the long haul.

That was when Ren spoke.

Klaid, Ren said, on the screen.

The nexus went very quiet.

Master Jin's, Ren said.

Seong said, "Stop."

The system, which had no instruction to stop and had been queued to follow the narrative, did not stop. Seong did not mean the system. He meant the room.

The room stopped.

"Sofia."

"Sir."

"Pull everything we can legally see on a Klaid. Kendo. Disciple of a Master Jin." He paused. "Public records only. News. Tournament archives. Don't touch the user database."

"Sir," Sofia said. Her hands were already moving.

"Don't even look at the user database."

"Sir."

A file came up on the secondary display thirty seconds later, because some search problems were easy and this one was very easy.

A face filled the screen.

A young man, photographed at a tournament, helmet under one arm, in a clean white keikogi, his hair cropped short, his expression utterly composed.

Someone in the back row whistled, low and slow.

"Yamamoto," Vasquez said warningly.

"What. He's handsome. I'm allowed to notice."

"Focus."

The next year of articles tracked his trajectory. Profile pieces. Interview requests.

Articles bloomed across the secondary display. The thumbnails arranged themselves in a chronological rail. Tournament wins at fifteen. Tournament wins at sixteen. A photograph with Master Jin, both men in plain samue, the old master's hand resting briefly on the boy's shoulder in what the caption described as the closest he has ever come to a public smile. The national championship at seventeen, with the famous still image of the moment - a young man, mid-cut, his face empty of every expression a face was supposed to hold, his opponent already half-turned away in acknowledgment of a strike that hadn't formally landed yet.

Mushin, the headlines all said. The Boy Who Reached Mushin.

Then the accident.

A single article, dated four years ago. Tragedy strikes the family of national kendo champion. A photograph of a car. A photograph of a hospital. Father, deceased. Mother, in critical care.

Then they stopped following the boy.

Then, two years after the accident—Where is Klaid? The kendo community waits. And one year after that—Another year passes. The world remembers. The world waits.

Thorne, who had been holding her preserved plums forgotten in one hand, set them down on the console without looking.

"That," she said, "would explain a great deal."

"We don't know it's him," Thorne agreed.

Seong looked at the file. He looked at the main screen. He looked at the file again.

The Yggdrasil user database was on a server thirty meters from where he stood. He had administrative authorization for it. There was a chair he could sit in, an authentication he could pass, a name he could type into a search field, and the answer would be on the screen within a second.

The current data isolation policy required a judge's order.

He had been part of the committee that wrote that policy.

He breathed out through his nose.
