Chapter 132

I shod thirty-six horses for a column marching west and entered the count in my book, because a farrier counts shoes. When the column did not return, a man came to my shop and asked how many had gone. I told him I was a farrier, not a census. He took the book from the bench and counted the entries himself. A man who counts shoes has counted horses, he said, and a man who has counted horses has armed anyone who reads the page. I keep the book locked now. — Codex of the Long Watch, Field Observation, 5th Century BCE, the Isthmus

Arthur rested his palms against the hull to feel the structure. It was not something he did consciously; his hands moved to survey whether it was a building or a boat.

The caïque was out of the water and stood on the slip, canted over on a cradle of oiled timber props. The underside of it smelled of hot tar and the green rot of weed drying off in the sun. Someone had scraped the growth from the planking that morning. The clean strakes were pale against the darker wood that sat above the waterline.

The planks were set edge to edge and fastened onto sawn frames. It was old work and it was good work. What he did not know was how well it would float. A building told you when it was tired. It cracked, it sagged, it dropped plaster where the stress ran. A boat told the sea, only the sea, and the sea did not leave a lot of time for repair work.

“You are feeling my boat like a doctor,” Spyros said from above him.

The captain sat on an upturned crate softening a pot of pitch over a small brazier. He was past fifty, burnt to the colour of the tar he worked and with the forearms of a man who had spent forty years pulling on wet rope. He pushed his caulking iron into the pot and drew it out trailing a thread of black tar.

“I am trying to work out if it is sound,” Arthur admitted.

“It is sound. I built half of it myself. Twice.” Spyros laid the pitch into a seam and pressed it with the flat of the iron.

“The engine, that is dead, and good riddance. An engine is a stranger you have to feed in oil. The hull I know. The hull and I have an understanding.”

Petros came round the bow with a coil of line over one shoulder and his sleeves pushed up. He dropped the coil on the slip beside Spyros and toed it.

“Your understanding would have parted three pulls from the deadeye. I found it before you tore it in the middle of the sea. Again,” Petros said.

“The shroud was fine.”

“The shroud was a museum piece.” Petros crouched and spread a length of the old rope across his knee, showing where the strands were furred and pale inside the rope. “You look at it, Arthur. You are the engineer. Him and his understanding.”

Arthur took the rope, and the outer surface looked decent enough. When he opened the lay with his thumbs the core was dust; the fibres had worn through from the inside where nobody had thought to look. It was the sort of failure that gave no warning on the surface and then buggered you when you were least expecting it.

“He is right, it would have snapped the moment you needed it,” he said, handing the rope back.

Spyros took the rope, looked at it a while and shrugged at Petros.

“We change it. Good thing you do not sail tomorrow. A man who crosses to Aegina with old rope, he makes it sea. All of it, sea,” Petros said.

“He means he makes a mess of it,” Sarah said. She had come down the slip with Donna behind her, stepping over the props.

“A total mess. That is the idiom.”

“Sea,” Petros agreed, satisfied, and went back to his rope.

Donna crouched at the waterline and looked past the harbour wall to where the water started. Arthur watched her run all the failure points that she could do nothing about. Twenty-seven kilometres of the sea, with no radio, no engine, nothing under the keel for the whole crossing but open water and whatever the open water hid.

“How long, once we are out?” Donna asked.

“With a wind like they have been giving us, four hours or five. Becalmed, then we row, and we do not talk about becalmed because that is much longer.”

Spyros set another rope of pitch into the seam. “I put the boat on the slip and I work every seam because out there, there is no shallow to run the boat into. You spring a plank in the near water, you swim to a rock. You spring a plank halfway across.” He pressed the iron and left the sentence hanging.

“And there is company. The octopus in the Mikrolimano is the size of a car now. So. We go over the boat twice,” Petros added.

Arthur looked at the clean seams, the fresh caulking and the one frayed shroud on the slip, and found that he trusted the work more for having watched them argue over it. A man who fought about his rigging was a man who checked his rigging. He would rather cross with these two bickering over a deadeye than with anyone who told him it was all fine.

Maria held the overcoat up by the shoulders, turned it once to check the fall of the hem and hung it on the nail above the Singer without ceremony.

“That is the last one,” she said, and pulled the bobbin out with the practised snap of a woman who had threaded ten thousand bobbins.

Arthur had seen the hides hanging here before they had gone to deal with Kastella. Laid out and finished it looked like nothing you could buy in a shop. It looked like something the world had grown in answer to the apocalypse.

He picked up his trousers from the bench and turned them over. The leather was heavier in the leg panels than the joints, and the stitching ran in clean parallel lines from waist to cuff without a single wobble. The deep violet tint of the grain sat evenly through the full thickness of the hide. Maria had doubled the panel across the thighs, calves and buttocks where the plate pockets were stitched.

“These are good, Maria,” he said, and meant it in the way he would have talked about competent steelwork. The seam allowance was uniform. The tension on every stitch was identical. She had made armour that looked like clothing, and she had done it on a treadle sewing machine with a child asleep in a basket at her feet.

“Efharisto. Try them,” Maria said, clearing the bench.

Donna picked up her vest first and held it at arm’s length. It was close-cut through the torso, with pockets for the iron plates stitched over the chest, back and shoulders.

“Maria, this is extremely well fitted,” Donna said, which from Donna was a standing ovation.

“I have your measurements. I have hands. It is not difficult,” Maria said with a smile.

Steve picked up one of the two overcoats and held it against his chest. His was the longer coat, cut below the knee, with deep interior pockets that Maria had placed without being asked. She had noticed what he carried and had planned accordingly. There was a pocket the right width for a notebook, one the right depth for the leather pouch and one that would hold pencils rather well.

“Now this is what they call bespoke. Maria, you are a genius,” Steve said with a wide smile.

“Only a seamstress. A genius would have done it faster.”

What Sarah picked up matched no other piece in form, function or aesthetics.

Her coat fell to mid-thigh and had a fitted waist. It had a proper standing collar, a belt drawn through flat loops and a shape to it the other four did not have. It looked like a coat. It looked, in fact, like a coat a woman would choose to wear for a posh evening out.

Donna studied the coat for a long moment, then looked at Sarah. “Is yours tailored?”

“All of the gear is tailored. Maria tailored it.”

“You know what I mean,” Donna said.

“Maria and I discussed the cut,” Sarah said, smoothing the front panel with both hands.

“I had her cut it so it would close over the chest and not flap open the first time I bend over to heal. That is called fit. It is not vanity, it is function.”

“It has a belt.”

“Belts are functional.”

“You discussed it for three fittings. The collar, the waist, the way the back falls when you walk. She also wanted the pockets moved twice,” Maria said from behind the Singer.

“You had fittings? When?” Arthur asked.

“I did not want to spend the apocalypse dressed as a bin bag. Yes. I asked her to make it nice. Sue me,” Sarah said, and gave up defending the coat.

“And does it stop a claw?” Donna asked.

“Two layers front and back, iron plates in the chest and the kidneys. The collar sits properly and it would stop a claw. Petros wanted to put six plates across the back. I told him a woman is not a cupboard,” Maria said.

Petros was leaning against the yard wall with his arms folded, watching the gear with keen attention. “These are worth something. On the market. Good leather, good iron, good hands. People would trade a great deal for one of these,” he said.

They did the binding in the courtyard under the lemon tree. Sarah did not want armour laid out on the kitchen table where her mother would have to walk past it and think about what it was for and where it would go.

Steve laid the five Hunter cores in a row on the canvas. The light in them was steadier and deeper than anything they had bound before, bar the guardian cores and the progenitor core that, according to Archie, they did not have a suitable vessel for.

Beside the cores, wrapped in oiled cloth, sat the iron plates Stavros had cut to Maria’s dimensions. Forty uniform squares of transmuted Ether-iron, each the size of a flat hand and curved to follow the contour of the body. Arthur counted out twenty-six and they slotted them into the gear pockets while Maria supervised. Eight into his trousers, four across each thigh panel, the rough side of the iron gripping the leather lining. Four into Donna’s vest and four into her trousers. Five into each overcoat. The remaining fourteen went back into the cloth.

“Better vessel, better result. The iron gives the binding something more substantial to hold on to and remake,” Steve said, picking his coat up.

Steve started the binding. He spread his coat now with plates in it across his knees and pressed a core against the leather. “I want it to keep out the weather and turn a claw, and I do not want it to restrain my arms when I lift them.”

He held the intent, and the core dissolved into the leather without any resistance. The coat was already what it was meant to be. The overcoat changed. The iron plates rippled and flowed over the leather, settling into the grain. The deep violet tint darkened until the coat was nearly black, and then the grain restructured itself across the full length of the garment in fine directional lines that followed the fall of the cloth.

Steve put the coat on. The leather sat snugly against his frame and the fine directional lines in the grain caught the morning light. He worked his arms around and found everything to his satisfaction.

“Palisade. Refined. A proper name this time, none of your Field Pack nonsense. Plated, keeps out the weather and has space for my notebooks. Exactly what I asked for. I love it.” he said, holding his notebook up against the inner pocket.

“You look like a country parson who moonlights as an assassin,” Donna said.

“Oh hush,” Steve said and pulled up his collar.

Donna went next. She set a Hunter core against the chest panel of the vest and went still. The stone dissolved into the leather in a pulse of deep violet that ran through the stitching and the plates and settled into the grain.

“The binding respected the cut,” Arthur said with a smile.

The maker’s precision left no structural ambiguity for the binding to resolve. When the vessel is already correct, the core reinforces and refines rather than restructures. This is the distinction between competent and exceptional craft.

“Sanctum-Grade stones, Ether-infused and that did bugger all. Every piece is still Refined. The hide must be the ceiling,” Steve grumbled.

They were dressed in dark leather that had been living animals on a hill a month ago and was armour now, and it had names. He let the plain fact of it sit in him.

Yiayia said something in Greek from her chair against the wall.

“She says we look like soldiers. And she asks whether we are going to war or a dinner, because we are dressed for the one and invited to the other,” Sarah translated.

Arthur had forgotten the dinner at Markopoulos.

“We will leave the new coats at home. No reason to travel through the city in gear no one else can afford,” Donna agreed.
