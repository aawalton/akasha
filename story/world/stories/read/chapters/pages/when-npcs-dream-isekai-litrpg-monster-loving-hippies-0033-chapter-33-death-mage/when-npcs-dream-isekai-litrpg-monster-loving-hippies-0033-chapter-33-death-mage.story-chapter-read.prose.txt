I practiced with general gas only because I could actually see it as smoke. But the time had come. I was ready to take my gas magic to the next level.

For today’s setup I got:

First, I checked all windows and doors, making sure the room was properly ventilated. Then I set the brazier, lit the charcoal, and half-covered it with the metal lid so it would burn low.

Next, I filled the glass bottle with water and set it mouth-down in the water basin. I fitted the clay tube: one end positioned in the brazier above the glowing coals, the other passing through a hole in the water basin (sealed with tar) and into the mouth of the inverted bottle - Because the bottle was initially filled with water, no common air could enter.

Soon, bubbles rose through the pipe, replacing the water inside. What remained was not smoke, or air, but something else entirely. Something deadly: CO—carbon monoxide gas.

Carbon monoxide was invisible, odorless, and extremely poisonous. It came from the incomplete burning of carbon-based fuels like wood.

It sometimes called the invisible killer. It’s how people kill themselves using car exhaust fumes, or how families die when a faulty gas heater fills an unventilated room—they go to sleep warm and comfortable, and never wake up again.

Even small amounts of CO in the air can quickly build up to dangerous levels inside the body, because CO binds to blood far more strongly than oxygen. At high concentration it causes headaches, dizziness, blurred vision, disorientation—and within minutes, unconsciousness, and death.

How did I know all this?I used to internet.

And that was my grand plan. The reason I chose gas magic in the first place.

I was going to be a carbon monoxide mage.

Magic here was hard. Big, flashy spells were impossible for someone like me. But a small trick—like shifting a sphere of gas—could be deadlier than any fireball or lightning bolt.”

Once I had the bottle safely filled with deadly gas and corked, it was time for the magic part.

This was where things got a little weird. The chemical effects of different magic types clearly required modern knowledge of chemistry, but as Miguel explained, they were disguised in medieval terms. Luckily, I had his scribbled notes in the margins of some books—they helped me a lot.

The base gas circle was the same, but the two inner circles were different. I practiced dry runs until I got the hang of it.

Since this was a circle specifically tailored for carbon monoxide, it was supposed to be considerably more powerful and effective compared to general gas. Still, it was my first attempt—so I started small.

I targeted a small sphere of influence around the bottle, uncorked it, and moved the invisible gas slowly into the moth cage.

The furry insects beat their four wings frantically, but after five seconds they slowed. After ten seconds, they stopped moving altogether. I held the spell for about a minute. I couldn’t tell the concentration level over time—since the gas was invisible.

When I finally opened the cage and touched them, the moths all tumbled to the floor. Lifeless.

Success.

I was now a carbon monoxide mage.
