THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

Marseille

THE CITY AS TEXT

I’m standing where Pytheas stood before he sailed to Britain. The same stones, the same water and the same harbour, give or take twenty-six centuries of silting and renovation. But under a different sky.

The Phocaeans founded Massalia in 600 BC. They chose this harbour because the horseshoe shape created a natural breakwater and the limestone cliffs provided defensive elevation. I know this because I wrote a paper on Phocaean colonial harbour selection in my second year. I got a B+. Professor Haddon said my conclusions were “enthusiastic but undersourced.” I’m standing in my source.

The city is a text I’ve been reading my entire career without knowing it was written in a language I’d one day need to speak.

I can date a quay from its masonry. I can identify the point where one building phase replaced another. I can explain why the harbour was founded here rather than two miles along the coast.

What I cannot tell is which warehouse has drinking water, which alley remains passable or what two whistles from a rooftop mean. The woman with the gaff hook can. She knows which boats can be moved without opening the defensive line, where an attack is likely to come from and which people will hold when it does.

None of that knowledge is written in the stone.

Archaeology is what remains after the living annotations have disappeared. The jokes, warnings, shortcuts, habits and arguments die with the people who carried them. We study the durable fraction and mistake it for the whole. A barricade built yesterday becomes an archaeological feature the moment its builders are gone. We examine the stone, the furniture, and the tool marks, then reconstruct what the people must have been trying to do.

I have told colleagues that all of it would be so much easier if the people in the past had explained themselves. Now we are doing the same thing. Every camp is inventing signals, routes, rules and methods that exist only in memory. If the people carrying that knowledge die, the meaning dies before the objects have even gathered dust.

Perhaps that is what these notes are becoming. Not a record of the stones, but an attempt to preserve the missing annotations.

✎ Stone preserves the nouns. People carry the verbs. If we want civilisation to survive, we need to save both.
