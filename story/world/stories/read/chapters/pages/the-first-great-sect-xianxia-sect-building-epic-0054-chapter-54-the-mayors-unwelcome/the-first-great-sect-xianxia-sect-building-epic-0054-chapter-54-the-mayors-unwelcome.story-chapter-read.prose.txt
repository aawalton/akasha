Death descended upon the city of Coaltown in the Western Reaches of the Amber Sea Province. The Lord Liao Weilong entered Coaltown with a convoy headed by, well, heads. A whole panoply of decapitated heads. Dozens of them spiked and lashed to wooden boards raised atop a wagon. With each turn of the wheel, each bump and hump, they rattled, cascading chatters that spread as though they were a singular creature, from one end to the other.

A gruesome sight, truly. Sockets plucked empty by crows and vultures. Decaying and fetid strips of flesh sluicing away. Clouds of flies grown fat on the feast that never tried to flee. Maggots sprouted from some skulls and would one day become another fly in the swarm. It was a breeding ground for death, and pestilence, and it was a trophy to herald one Lord Liao.

Coaltown, Hua suspected, would not be ready for her arrival.

The name Coaltown was an artefact of its early life when coal had been discovered. Since then, it had become a mining hub for precious metals like copper and zinc for bronze, deposits of iron, silver, and maybe even some gold, but that ignoble, common name remained.

Perhaps the name was deserved. Squat buildings sprawled down a strip of land bordered on one side by a plateau that rose and rose and kept rising, keeping the town in its shadow cool whilst the other side was a great lake terminated, fed by runoff from the mountains in the distance. Days and weeks of journeying had brought the mountains into greater view and Hua could see more than floating, frozen peaks.

She got a sense of the range, the immensity of the mountains that shielded the Amber Sea Province from the more Imperial provinces, and was awed. It was hard crossing this mountain range and threading a path through the northern plains involved crossing through the vast Jurchen territories and whatever lay to the far north. This difficult crossing was partly the reason that three Great Clans were the de facto rulers of a massive province, true powers instead of subordinate ones. One could make the journey south via the sea, but one would need to contend with all the leviathans lurking beneath the surface.

And so the Amber Sea was almost a separate nation, officially, legally, and logistically. It was unfortunately bracketed between the endless Jurchen hordes to the north and the east held old enemies of the dragon throne. Despite this distance, the Dragon Throne had flooded the province with Imperial forces. They could spare a captain in Qi Gathering to protect a scribe, which spoke to the defences the Governor himself might have.

They were forced to ride three abreast as the pass narrowed in the shadow of the northern plateau, sheer stone walls giving Hua a sense of vertigo. Tough, solid rock, it did not notice the travel of their convoy. This was a beautifully defensible location. Hua would not have attempted a siege here. Either you came through a killing zone, scaled the impossible height of the plateau, suffered over the mountains west, or built an armada on the far side of the ocean which would likely be set alight on the first night by saboteurs.

Maybe a small force from stealth could cross the southward lake, assuming it was deeper than it was wide, but that meant swimming through yellow waters that, even from a remove, looked poisonous. Runoff from the mines and refineries, Hua assumed. The work of industry. Grains, meat, and fish were brought into Coaltown whilst it sent back metals. Did it lack the ability to irrigate crops and rear animals with sweet waters? Or was Hua simply assuming mortals were incompetent?

As they approached, the pass again widened. Coaltown came fully in view. A crowd gathering, watching, soot on their hardy clothes. The city possessed two sets of walls, the outer ones half again the height of the inner ones.

Her outriders carried with them tall banners of her kingfisher. The white backgrounds were already greying. Give it another day and they might be more coal dust than ink and fabric. The city’s name proving itself true.

“Never seen a place so miserable,” Weiji said, riding beside her. “And I’ve been places you couldn’t imagine. This is really where our wealth comes from?”

“You’ve never been before?”

He shook his head. “Never had a reason. I would have joined him for his first tour to the west. It was being planned before he left. Funny, I’m still here, still serving a Lord Liao on their first trip. The future has a way of rhyming unpleasantly.”

“Unpleasant, am I?”

Past the brim of his wide hat, he gave her a haunted look. “Nothing to do with you. Just time, just thoughts.”

It soon became clear that the darkness of the squat, ugly buildings was neither a function of the shadow the plateau cast nor an unfortunate proclivity for dark colours, but a result of layers upon layer of coal and soot clinging to walls, fabric and windows, tinting retaining wooden support till they looked like unusually wide beams of charcoal. Civilians wore very wide hats, their clothes sturdy leathers and strangely woven hemp instead of silk or rare cotton. Veils and fabric masks were a common sight and Hua understood why as a gust surged across the yellowed lake and blew a cloud south to north, a shadowy fog that would have left her choking if not for her veil and the simple fact that she could hold her breath for long periods.

The mortals in her convoy hacked out their lungs. Weiji had simply tipped his hat to the side. Hua imagined the Shaman Yan and the Jolly Ranger were perfectly fine in one of the covered wagons.

Her horse sneezed violently and nearly bucked Hua off with the force of it. She stayed on with her strong thighs and soothed his neck, murmuring apologies. It took some time for him to calm. Hua forgave him. It was rare for her horse to misbehave; the worst he had done was eat some of her dried fruit while Hua had been distracted.

“We’ll get you a mask and a hat,” she promised. “Or get you somewhere that’s covered. I promise you won’t deal with this again.”

“You care about that horse more than the mortals.”

“Yes,” she agreed as they crossed through the outer walls.

The outer walls did not seem to have any defensive interest beyond making it vaguely inconvenient for an army to line up. They were tall and thin, angled slightly, barely obstructing the view of the walls The inner walls were taller, possessed of an outward curve. Ash and dust would blow against the wall and spread up until momentum forced it to curl back. That was the idea. Whether it worked or was just mortal superstition was another matter. Atop the wall, soldiers and guards manned the walls. Spears and arrows and ballistae.

Her horse sneezed again. Hua patted him consolingly. If her horse got sick because of the coal dust, she was killing everyone in this forsaken city in the middle of nowhere. It was a shame that so beautiful a locale was suffering the effects of industry. The nearest mountain, distant, seemingly impassable, was a hive of activity—artificial shapes, some tall and thin, others wide and squat and giving the impression of curving around the mountain, buildings connected by bridges of wood and steel and paths worn in by decades if not centuries of effort, gaping voids where mine shafts began like the mountain possessed many mouths, some which bellowed out dark clouds, others seemed to suck air like a sharp inhale, many more seeming to spew white smoke as mist rolled down. It was a mountain of contradiction and colour, industry carving through nature, men taming the rugged wilderness.

Hua would never live here, that much was certain. Not with the smoke, not with the disinteresting architecture that proved painfully functional and not at all aesthetically pleasing. Too much grime, too much dirty, and far, far too much Fire Qi bubbling beneath the mountain.

With how painfully hot the ground burned to her senses, Hua was surprised no one’s soot-stained clothes had ignited. Surprised the dry grass hadn’t been set ablaze already.

“Weiji,” she asked lowly, expecting him to understand.

He shook his head, still in that strange mood. “Later. We’ll worry about it later. Too many other things first.”

Once they had fully crossed into the outer walls, teams of masked men pulled ropes Hua had not noticed. The tall slats that made the outer wall rotated to seal shut. Hua understood now that they served to block out the first assault of smoke and coal dust, defence against the environment, not against people.

As Hua had expected, they were received poorly.

“Most honoured Lord Liao, we greet you with joy in our hearts and awe in our souls to be graced by your venerable presence. Let all who call this city home know the power and glory of our honourable protectors. It is this unworthy servant’s greatest honour to welcome you to the city under this mayor’s stewardship…”

He prattled on for a while longer, the mayor in the vestments of his office. She most certainly did not feel honoured by the welcome. Yes, she understood that she had given them barely any time to prepare, which was intentional, but she thought they could do more than a crowd of this size. Was she not meant to receive gifts and tribute as any arriving Lord should? They could have at least done with more banners and streamers.

She didn’t ask where the cheers were since, well, she had what amounted to a small mountain of skulls behind her.

“This Lord Weilong greets you,” she said, staying upon her horse, offering the mayor minimal face.

He did not react to that, no emotion showing past his grey eyes the same shade as used water from a washtub. She expected him to comment on her name, on her brother or the Patriarch. As mayor, he would know the name of her father’s previous heir. But he was smart enough to ignore any such incongruencies.

“When your outriders announced your arrival, I could scarcely imagine you would bring such a large convoy. May this loyal servant know why such a large convoy was needed?”

Weiji would usually have answered for her, but he was still in that odd mood of his. He was looking about curiously, watching the city, the plateau, the lake, and the mountain looming in the distance. You warned me to behave and here you are flying between the clouds.

But it did not matter. Something as minor as this she could deal with.

“As a loyal servant, perhaps you would be interested in hearing the accounts of hundreds of villagers displaced by the acts of a bandit within your purview,” Hua suggested lightly.

She gestured at the gruesome display of skulls, wondering how many more skulls she would be adding to her monument.

“Witness the work this Lord has accomplished since arriving in the Western Reaches with only fifty cavalry soldiers.” She made sure the mayor was looking at them. Empty sockets were easier than her burning gaze. “Bandits menaced this region under your watch. Under your watch, the name Liao became a mockery.”

“My Lord—"

Lightning sparked, bright as the white fields of her banner, bluer than the kingfisher’s plumage. A lash of lightning that retorted with thunder soon after. The Mayor flinched. His guards and servants, those meant to give him status, they all flinched.

Her horse just sneezed.

She petted him errantly, stroking the long hairs on his neck. He deserved better than this. When she finally returned home, Hua would make sure he got the finest feed, the freshest water, and the sweetest air.

“There will be a reckoning,” she announced to all who could hear and knew her words would spread across the full extent of the sprawling city. “All will be called to account. Those who aided and abetted the rampage of Kang the Serpent will suffer the same fate. Those who turned away from justice will know the judgement of lightning.”

“My lord, I promise you, we are all loyal servants. Not a one would aid a bandit. Against such a threat, every loyal subject would send messengers to the honoured Liao Patriarch. Chaos has made it difficult to send information, so perhaps that is what you refer to.”

Hua smiled. “Ah, yes, the chaos has made it difficult, I agree. My journey here was a story fit for legend. This Lord had forgotten the difficulty mortals experienced.”

She gestured behind her and from the convoy emerged a figure in yellow robes and a furred cap, a triple-ringed staff that jangled with each step.

“I believe you know my latest travelling companion.”

Hua watched the mayor’s face lose all colour as Wu Yan lifted her veil and bared her face to the world.

“After all, you did hear out her request.”
