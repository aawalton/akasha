"I feel strange."

She was wobbly. She took a tentative step but lurched forward and then back, trying to correct it, arms snapping out for balance that wasn't there. Clive got to her elbow before she could go down into the leaf litter. She grabbed his arm and held it and stood very still, like she was waiting for the ground to stop being a problem. After a moment she looked down at her feet.

"Those are toes," she said.

"Yes."

"They're very small."

She wiggled them experimentally, then tried a step. Unsteady, but she stayed up on her own this time.

"How do you people do this," she said. "You only have two of them."

"Billions of us manage."

"Billions of you have had practice." She took another step, more cautious this time. It held. She looked down at herself.

"How do I look?"

Clive recognized the trap immediately. There was no good answer to that question. Azura was pretty, there was no doubt about that. But if he said she looked good, Jill sitting three feet to his left would have opinions about that. If he said anything less than good, he would upset a creature that was, until very recently, capable of biting him in half and might still be, depending on how the transformation worked.

"Honest opinion," he said. "You look like yourself. Just upright."

“Like myself…” Azura frowned. She looked down at her arms, turned her hands over, studied her fingers like she expected claws and was mildly offended by what she'd gotten instead.

"This feels so unfamiliar. I expected to be taller," she said.

"You were."

"I'd like to be again."

She looked at Jill, who returned her gaze with a sigh.

"You look fine," Jill said.

“Are you sure?”

“Here, you can see for yourself,” Clive said. He pulled out his sketchbook. A few strokes, and a mirror materialized in his palm, still warm from the page. He held it out to her.

Azura took it carefully, raising it to her face.

Her free hand came up and touched her cheek, then her jaw, then the bridge of her nose. She watched her reflection do the same, her amber eyes tracking every movement.

"The eyes are right," she said. "Those are mine."

She tilted the mirror. Studied her hair — dark, falling just past her shoulders, catching the last of the grey light. She pulled a strand forward and examined it between her fingers, rubbing it like she was testing the weave of something unfamiliar.

She pressed two fingers into her forearm and watched the skin sink in.

"It's so soft," she said. "Everything about this form is soft."

She poked her own cheek. Then her shoulder. Then, with growing alarm, the back of her hand.

"All of it," she said. "All of it is like that."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"That's just what skin does."

She pressed her fingers against her collarbone, apparently relieved to find something more solid underneath, then moved upward to the cartilage of her ear and immediately recoiled.

"That one is wrong," she said.

"It's an ear."

"It bends." She touched it again, gingerly, like she expected it to bite back. "Why does it bend? A useful appendage would not bend."

"It helps with sound collection."

"My ears did not bend."

"Your ears were attached to a skull the size of a cart."

She considered this, found it insufficient, and moved on. She pressed a finger into her own bicep, frowned at the give, then clenched her fist and tried again. Slightly less give. She seemed marginally satisfied by this.

Then she pinched the skin at her elbow.

Her expression went flat.

"What is this," she said.

Clive looked. "Elbow skin."

"It doesn't—" She pulled it gently. Let it go. "It doesn't do anything. It just— sits there. Extra."

"Everyone has it."

Jill, from her log, spoke without looking up. "It's called the wenis."

A pause.

"The what," Azura said.

"The wenis. The skin on your elbow."

Azura turned to Clive with an expression that demanded he explain why this information existed.

"She's right," was all he said.

Azura looked back at her elbow. She pulled the skin again, very deliberately, then released it.

"Humans," she said, "are so strange."

"You'll get used to it."

"I don't think I want to."

She lowered the mirror. Held it against her chest with both hands, the way a child held something it had been given and wasn't sure it was allowed to keep.

"Can I have this?" she asked.

"It's yours."

She looked down at it. Then at him. Then she took another wobbly step forward and almost immediately overcorrected, grabbing his arm for the second time.

"I still hate the feet," she said.

“You’ll find your footing.”

Jill watched with her arms folded. "Your dragon," she said, "is going to be exhausting. We should make a move now. The village isn’t getting closer , and your dragon is going to need time to learn to walk before we put her in front of people."

"I can walk," Azura said. She took a demonstrative step. It was, technically, a step.

Jill's gaze moved over her. Then, unexpectedly: "Chin up. You're dropping your head when you move. Your balance will be better if you lead from the chest."

Azura straightened. Tried another step. Wobbled less.

"Your posture is good," Azura said, studying Jill with her amber eyes. "For a human."

Jill blinked. "What?"

"Clive slouches."

"I don't slouch."

"You do when you draw. You round your left shoulder. It will cause problems eventually."

Jill looked at him. A hint of a smile formed at the corner of her mouth.

"She's not wrong," she said.

"You always said it gave me character."

"I said it made you look like you were guarding something." She fell into step beside Azura, steadying her briefly when the path turned uneven. "Didn't make it not slouching."

Azura glanced at Jill. Jill glanced back.

Some silent accord passed between them. Clive watched it happen and understood, with the particular resignation of a man who had been outnumbered, that this was going to become a pattern.

The road east was flat at least, which helped. Azura managed the first ten minutes by keeping her eyes on her feet, which did not help her balance but seemed to help her confidence, and Clive decided not to mention this.

She fell twice.

The first time she went down on one knee in the dirt and was back up before either of them could move. The second time she caught Jill's arm on the way, and Jill caught her without comment, and they continued.

"How do you not think about it constantly," Azura said.

"About what?" Clive asked.

"Walking. Every step is a decision. You have to decide, continuously, not to fall." She watched her feet. "How do humans accomplish anything else."

"You stop noticing eventually."

"I refuse to believe that."

"You rode a horse once," Jill said. "First time. Do you remember thinking about it?"

Azura looked at her. "I have never ridden a horse."

"I meant— do you remember the first time you flew."

A pause. "No."

"Same principle."

Azura considered this, which bought them another hundred yards of quiet before she tried a slightly longer stride and nearly overbalanced again.

"Different principle," she said.

The village announced itself by smell. From a distance, Clive was able to detect it with his [Apothecary’s Nose]. The woodsmoke, livestock, and damp warmth of a settlement.

But when they arrived, it was unlike any town in San Dioral. The entire village was covered in an ethereal fog. It moved with the slow deliberateness of something alive, curling around the base of buildings and pooling in the spaces between them. The rooflines surfaced above it like islands.

The buildings were real enough. Timber-framed and stone-footed. But the fog gave them a quality of impermanence, blurring their edges, making the furthest ones look like suggestions of buildings rather than buildings themselves. As if the village might, given the right conditions, simply decide to disappear.

"Does the fog ever lift?" Clive asked.

"No," Jill said. “The fog protects the village. Hides it from invaders.”

Clive looked out at it and thought that if he were an invading army, he would find it deeply unsettling. Then he noticed what was moving through it.

The lights between the buildings weren't lanterns. Or not only lanterns. Small shadow-shapes drifted at ankle height, trailing dim luminescence behind them the way deep-water fish did. They pooled around doorsteps and beneath eaves. Where they gathered, the mist parted slightly, as if giving them room.

"What are those," Azura said.

"Shade-lings," Jill said. "Harmless. They come off the Veil when it thins. The villages out here have had them long enough that nobody notices anymore."

One bumped gently against Clive's boot. It had no real shape — more the suggestion of one, a smear of darker dark, roughly the size of a terrier. It nosed at his heel and moved on.

"They're attracted to warmth," Jill added. "Body heat, mostly. Firelight. They don't bite."

"Do they do anything?" Clive asked.

"They eat the vermin. Keep the grain stores clean." She paused. "The children keep them as pets sometimes. The older ones say they remember things. Carry impressions of the people they've bonded with. Whether that's true or just sentiment, I couldn't tell you."

She gestured to a building ahead. “Maelus will be waiting. Lets go.”
