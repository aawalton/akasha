The Eye of the Storm slowly finished opening, and I could only imagine its confusion at the sight below it.

Gigantic mauls of shadow and green-black lightning cracked the portal world’s earthen floor as they slammed down around the God of Thunder. His body might’ve been broken, and he bled lightning from dozens of cuts, but he wove through the hammer-pillars, dodging and rolling as he avoided what felt like the entire world crushing down on him. Behind him, red-orange columns of electricity scoured the earth, the beams slicing down from an angry sky and leaving behind twisted columns of glass in their wake—columns Ellen’s Stormshade Hammer shattered moments later.

The Storm’s Blade lashed out faster than my eye could follow. It sliced another deep cut into the dragon’s body—and this time, the Eye of the Storm empowered the attack. Where it had been a lightning-wreathed rapier, an echoing blade of pure stormlight and shadow followed. The crack of thunder echoed through the breaking portal world. A chunk of the God of Thunder’s electric body came loose, crackling as it hit the ground.

Laminar Blade. Stormfall. I stayed close, slicing and cutting even as claws closed around me and jets of hypercompressed rainwater sliced into my maelstrom armor. It held. Barely. But it held.

Cyclone Forms. Rolling Thunder and Windfall. The God of Thunder slowed, but didn’t stop. A Black Hole opened behind him, slowing him further as my Hand of the Thunder God blasted across him. I followed it with a Lightning Chain, throwing myself back into the fight as he fled and cast spell after spell right back at me. I twisted in the air to dodge beams of lightning that rained down around me, cutting winds, and sonic blasts of thunder. Lightning Dance pulled me out of danger, then away from the dragon’s follow-up strikes.

We fought back and forth, the shattered dirt ramparts turning to mud in the rain and the rain evaporating as lightning shot back and forth between us and shadow erupted from the strobing darkness. The three of us fought all-out—the God of Thunder givng way before our assault, and both Ellen and I keeping the gap closed so the Forge, Hammer, and Anvil could do their jobs.

Then, something shifted.

“Enough!” the God of Thunder…thundered.

Lightning cracked down in dozens of different places all around us—so much lightning, sustained for so long, that the very idea of shadow disappeared, and the only reason I knew where the Paragon even was was that his own body was darker than the columns of crackling, pulsing electricity. It went on and on, obliterating the Stormshade Hammer and punching a hole through the Eye of the Storm’s clouds. The dirt melted. The stink of burning brick filled the air. And still his attack kept firing.

For almost fifteen seconds, he unleashed the full strength of his S-Plus-Ranked build. The wind ripped at my skin, driving superheated sand against it until I used Mist Trance to avoid it. Ellen’s Voidwalker form took the hits as best it could, but a line of steam sliced across her stomach. Blood spurted upward.

“Enough. Kade Noelstra, you’ve been an interesting opponent. The best prey I’ve ever hunted. But the hunt is over. You’ve cost me too much. I can’t stop now. You must die.”

“You’re right, Eugene. The hunt is over,” I said.

Then I excluded Ellen and used Stormbreak.

The first time I’d used Stormbreak with the Eye of the Storm, it’d been a desperation move against Queen Mother Yalerox. We’d both been connected to the storm then, and her Mana had failed to drain, resulting in a long, endless explosion as my Unique skill tried and failed to consume what felt like endless Mana. It had consumed her—and it had almost killed me. My C-Rank body, with a shattered core held together only by my fury and my desire to see my friends survive, hadn’t been enough to handle it.

When I’d used it against the Placid King, things had been different. The many-limbed, octopus-like Paragon hadn’t been attuned to the Eye. Stormbreak had been working with near-infinite Mana then, too, but only through me. I’d had control—and I’d been working with a much stronger body, core, and Stormbreak. That had given me the power to use it to turn the tides of the fight, and I never gave back the initiative from there.

This time, I wasn’t up against an A-Ranker who was grappling with powers beyond her comprehension, and I wasn’t up against a Paragon with no connection to the storm.

Even so, Stormbreak would not be denied.

The zones appeared, a negative around him and a positive around me. My hair—where it hadn’t been scorched to nothing from the sheer heat and fury of our battle—stood on end, and the static ran across my skin. And I threw myself into battle.

Stormbreak detonated. My Mana tried to drain.

It failed.

I’d spent the entire fight waiting for the right moment to open the Eye of the Storm—an energy battery that, unlike the pillars of Mana-dense stone in the God of Thunder’s world or the glowing crystal at the center of the Crone’s, didn’t contain energy so much as it recycled it. Now, it was active.

Back in that portal in the desert outside of Carlsbad, Queen Mother Yalerox had boasted that the Eye of the Storm would let her kill any delver Earth cared to send against her, up to and including the Light of Dawn. And she’d been right. With its full strength backing me, and with the power of SS-Rank, the reality set in.

At the time, I’d thought that even the God of Thunder might’ve struggled against her.

And I was far, far beyond her.

My vision went white. Both flowers of lightning bloomed. The dark spot that was the God of Thunder stood out clearly against the pulsing flower around not him—his vanished after only a few seconds—but me. My Mana tried to drain. It failed; the Eye of the Storm poured more and more into me every time it drained. The Eye of the Storm shorted out the God of Thunder’s electric blossom in seconds.

“What…what is this?”

“You should know.” I gritted my teeth against the sheer energy pouring through me and threw myself into battle. There was no time to be subtle. No time to poke and prod with thrusts and lunges. I grabbed the Storm’s Blade in both hands and slashed it into the God of Thunder’s flank. An arc of lightning followed, slamming into the monster’s body hard enough to send him flying.

Then I shifted stances. Cyclone Forms. This time, I didn’t bother with Ariette’s Thunderhead. Brendan’s Storm Sigil syphoned off a torrent of energy from the Eye of the Storm—more than it had ever pulled from the Thunderheads, but still a tiny fraction of the total. The multiple Hand of the Thunder Gods I cast, then echoed with Rolling Thunder, went on and on, boring into the God of Thunder’s body like lightning drills.

“You haven’t been watching? I’ve been working hard. Putting together as much power as I could. The Eye of the Storm. The Lightning Tower. The maze of shadow you destroyed in your second visit to my world. All of it was here to fight you. I prepared as hard as I could, Eugene, and you know what I found?”

The light flickered. One. Three. Six. Nine. Ten. Thunder followed each burst, the sensation like being caught inside a bass drum. Lightning flowed through my veins. Singed my nerves.

Then it stopped. Blackness settled across my portal world, and I looked on what I’d wrought.

Glass. The world’s dirt had been churned into hundreds of jagged glass sculptures by the God of Thunder’s earlier attacks, then crushed and reformed into a single sheet of the stuff by Stormbreak and the Hand of the Thunder Gods I’d echoed across the wreckage of the Void Garden. A perfectly flat, perfectly smooth surface. Brick and portal metal, copper and earth, and all melted down to nothing.

Across the glass from me, the God of Thunder lay. His lightning body twisted and thrashed, sparks arcing across the glass. I’d hit him with almost everything I had—with the combined powers of SS-Rank and  the Eye of the Storm—and it hadn’t been enough.

I walked up to him, my body twitching from the sheer lightning that had poured through it. Not a drop of Mana remained; any weaker, and I’d be suffering from Mana Burn.

Then I pushed my sword against his body and pushed. It sank in, the watery coating of Laminar Blade slipping past the God of Thunder’s defenses and sinking in until the blade stopped suddenly, a foot from the hilt. I pushed with everything I had, but it didn’t budge.

“It didn’t matter how hard I fought you. Three days wouldn’t be enough. You’re already healing, and the Eye of the Storm’s closed. I can’t do that again. Not for a while—and in that time, you’d kill me.” I smiled as the gigantic dragon glared down at me.

“Then you admit defeat?”

“No. Not at all, Eugene. You see, I realized that all the preparation in the universe would only get me to this point, right here.  For the rest of it, I’d need help.”

Ellen emerged from the Lightning Tower, into the darkness-covered world that, until a moment ago, had been lit so brightly by our lightning-and-thunder battle that not a single shadow had remained.

“His core’s right here,” I said.

She nodded. Shadow magic welled around her—and in a separate spot on the God of Thunder’s far side.

The sword slipped deeper, punching through the Stormsteel it had stuck on, as Ellen’s Black Hole spell grabbed both the God of Thunder and the Storm’s Blade, pulling it deeper as I pushed.

Something cracked.

And the God of Thunder screamed in agony as his core shattered under the blow.

We’d won.

Even more than the Placid King, the victory over the God of Thunder shocked me.

But unlike the Placid King, whose core had given way under the Storm’s Blade’s assault, the God of Thunder’s body didn’t move—didn’t so much as twitch. The Placid King had died in fury and anger, trying desperately to take me with him. The God of Thunder, by contrast, seemed to die silently.

I knelt, one hand on the Storm’s Blade’s grip and trying to steady my breathing, my spirit ready to continue fighting even as my body’s fresh and re-opened wounds screamed in protest. I had nothing left to give. No Mana, no Stamina, and no more energy. Exhaustion washed over me, fighting with the shock of how sudden—and how close—our victory had been.

I couldn’t help but think about how much power Ellen and I had brought to bear against the God of Thunder in the final few minutes, from the moment Ellen’s trap had sprung in the dragon’s portal world, scouring his body and severing wing and tail, until Stormbreak ended. But at the same time, the God of Thunder had been close to winning, too. His power had been all but overwhelming, and there’d been a good dozen or more times when he could have brought it to bear on me directly. Instead, he’d seemed to first be too arrogant, and later, too panicked. And the portal worlds—both of them—and Earth had suffered for it.

The world around us had glassed completely over; waves of molten, shadow-pushed sand had even washed over the Lightning Tower in the God of Thunder’s rampage, coating it with shimmering, purple-white glass. I focused and opened a small crack in the bottom floor. Steam poured out, along with a molten, nose-drying stink.

“It’s done,” Ellen said.

“It is,” I agreed. But I wasn’t so sure. What we’d done raised questions—questions about the nature of ascension, the Dual Skill Progression system we’d shared, and the nature of power in the universe. I’d need to find answers. At some point, Ellen would probably want—

“I think we should keep our cores like this,” Ellen said softly.

Or not.

“Why?”

“Two reasons. First, it feels right.” She bumped her shoulder into mine, and I winced. “Oh, sorry.”

“It’s fine. I caught a gigantic flying lightning snake’s claw there. Probably bruised, maybe broken. Not a big deal, though. Sarah and Sophia can patch me up later.”

“Right. Yeah, it feels right. We’ve been binding ourselves more closely with every step on this journey. It makes sense that the last one would link our cores.”

I nodded slowly. “That makes sense. And the second one?”

“That one’s more practical. Right now, that shared core might be all that’s…preventing us from feeling the pressure to ascend. Until we can confirm that’s not it, it’s better if we—“

I nodded, throat tight. “Yeah, for sure. We’ll keep it like this for now.” I reached out to touch her back—carefully, because while she hadn’t taken the same hits I had, she was hurting, too. This fight had been far too close for comfort, and we were both lying about it—and about something else.

But it was the last one we’d have to face like it. No one in their right mind would come after either of us now that we’d killed the God of Thunder. And my people were safe.

Granted, Phoenix had taken hits. People had certainly died—maybe even delvers I knew. But with the Placid King defeated and the God of Thunder dead, there were no powers left who were either on the cusp of ascension or had an eye on Earth.

Save one.

“I’ll have to confront the Crone eventually.”

Ellen nodded. “She’s the last threat, and the greatest. But that doesn’t have to be today, and that fight doesn’t have to come to blows. She seems aligned with us.”

“In some ways. In others…” I trailed off, then shrugged. “We’ll cross that bridge when we get to it, though. For now, I need a—“

But before I could finish my sentence, the God of Thunder twitched. Electricity surged through his body, and he turned over so his eyes locked on to mine. I drew the Storm’s Blade and dropped into a defensive Torrentborne stance as Ellen’s Voidwalker form erupted over her skin.

Even as I reacted, though, the dragon slumped, and his eyes drooped shut. He stared, lightning arcing weakly off his shattered body and pain rippling across his massive, battered face. He coughed, brow tightening, and spoke, voice tight and pained.

“Interesting, kid. You really know how to…how to go for the kill.”

Then he laughed quietly as I unsummoned the Storm’s Blade. Ellen stared at me, then shook her head. “What are you doing, Kade?”

I didn’t know. But I did know one thing. Eugene, for all that he’d been a monster—for all that he’d threatened my city, my world, my people, and me—had also been my mentor, ally, and friend. The God of Thunder had been nothing but an enemy for the last three days, but Eugene deserved more. And I deserved one last chance to talk with my mentor.
