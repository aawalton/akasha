A reddish oak-covered book lay on the large worktable while strings of golden light drifted above it, sometimes sinking through the cover and pages before rising again. The magic binding had been inlaid into the wood through invisible runes so fine that even Anne had needed to check them repeatedly to trust her own work.

So she checked them repeatedly. Only after the twelfth inspection did she finally allow herself to declare the restoration finished.

Months after Ceres had brought it to her, the papermancy compendium would at last leave the workshop and enter the archives.

It had been the most demanding restoration Anne had attempted since opening the bookstore. The pages had needed rebuilding, the text had required reconstruction, and even the cover had been damaged badly enough to demand near replacement. Yet the effort had been worth it. Not only had she recovered a whole new craft for her collection, but the work had also expanded her grimoire by four pages.

Anne rested her fingertips on the cover for a brief moment, letting herself enjoy the result before lifting the volume and carrying it downstairs.

The last few weeks had followed a rhythm she was only beginning to understand. The mornings were often quieter now, allowing her to finish the more exhausting part of the work—restoration, research, cataloging, and the endless tasks that made the bookstore more than a room full of books. The afternoons, by contrast, had grown lively. Rick and the golden trio still came regularly to study, take notes, argue over conclusions, and drink tea in quantities that Anne was trying not to find financially alarming.

Since Asha’s abrupt departure, the others had shifted more of their effort toward practical training in the mornings, which left Anne with the uninterrupted hours she needed. She did not object to the arrangement. Study should always lead to application. Otherwise, a bookstore became little more than a polished coffin for knowledge.

Rick, in particular, had begun taking his studies more seriously. Or rather, more ambitiously.

A hunter’s path, much like a warrior’s, depended on the body being strengthened and guided by essence. But where warriors favored direct confrontation and battlefield techniques, hunters leaned toward mobility, survival, and skills that let them fight ingeniously. Speed, tracking, trapping, evasion—those mattered more to a hunter than a glorious exchange of blows.

Rick understood that now. Sadly for him, his richer days had ended almost as quickly as they had begun. After the boar and the wolves, he had enough coin to improve, but not enough to behave carelessly. He had only saved two days of reading credit, and even Anne had to admit that he spent them wisely.

First, he had used the beastiarium to strengthen his understanding of local beasts until he felt confident identifying them and acting around them. Then he had turned to skills. He chose running and evasion—not really offensive, but easier to learn, essential for survival, and far more suitable for someone in his position than fantasies of killing stronger beasts head-on.

Two weeks earlier, after learning from the mayor’s butler that Asha had left on a long trip, Rick had moved to the next stage of his plan. Anne had not missed the timing.

Fortunately for him, the bookstore held training books for both skills.

Training books were more expensive than ordinary texts, but basic skill volumes still remained within reach of a determined poor customer. Anne charged six silver for a day of study with one. It was not cheap, but neither was proper instruction, and in this case the book offered something far closer to a trainer than a mere explanation.

Rick chose running first. With his first day of credit, he opened the book and began exactly where Anne expected he would—at the foundation.

The first chapter focused on breathing. That often disappointed people. They wanted speed and power, not lungs and rhythm. But a poorly understood body ruined every later step. The opening pages read like an ordinary compendium, explaining the major breathing patterns, what each one preserved or sacrificed, how movement altered them, and how essence interacted with strain and recovery. Only after the reader built a proper mental image of those patterns did the book begin its true work.

Virtual simulation. It was one of the most exquisite techniques in bookmancy and one of the hardest to craft properly. First, the bookmancer had to build a virtual master model of the skill, whether from personal comprehension or from the understanding of a true practitioner. Then they had to reproduce the structure of that mastery without simply copying experience itself. Finally, they added points of interaction, places where the reader’s own effort, judgment, and sensation could interact with the model.

During training, the student would repeatedly perform the skill inside that guided mental framework until their execution approached the minimum standard of the master model.

The book itself became a kind of patient instructor. It corrected, demonstrated, pressed, rejected, and redirected. But all of that happened in the mind. The body still had to catch up afterward through repetition, physical effort, and enough discomfort to make the lesson real.

Compared to a magitech skill scroll, the training book was obviously less convenient.

That was why most preferred scrolls. A skill scroll could pour functional knowledge into a user’s mind in minutes. One activated it, endured the transfer, and then began practicing with a ready-made template already in the head. A training book demanded study, repetition, and the real possibility of failure. A scroll gave a fast, reliable, shallow gain. The book offered a slower, riskier, but stronger one.

Someone using a scroll might perform the skill within minutes. While those using a training book might take a week, perhaps a month, pushing through it properly.

But if both continued afterward, the one trained through deeper comprehension would almost always understand more, and eventually surpass the other in mastery. That was why the old noble lines had never truly surrendered themselves to easy gains. Even when they no longer relied on books, they still preferred masters and trainers over shortcuts for their heirs.

Rick, for all his limitations, had sensed the value of that immediately.

On his first day, he reached the second chapter, which focused on conserving stamina while running. Then he spent the next week physically training what he had learned. He ran between town, the bookstore, and the forest, then began choosing rougher paths and uneven ground so the skill would not become dependent on comfort. Just by integrating the breathing patterns and energy-saving methods, he felt the difference. So he decided to return—somewhat reluctantly—to spend his second day of reading credit and finish the rest.

One more day proved sufficient. He completed the last three chapters—Irregular Paths, Sprint Techniques, and Persecutions—and then spent another week working them into his body. By the end of it, he had become confident to attempt something more ambitious.

So he left with the Mapping Book on his second solo expedition.

Winter was only three weeks away. That fact pressed on all of them, though Rick seemed the only one willing to convert urgency into action without complaint. He wanted better passages before snow and cold forced caution onto everyone.

The golden trio had objected. Partly because they were worried for him, and partly because they were bored.

They wanted to go with him. All three asked in their own way, but Rick refused them all. Mapping demanded quietness, attention, and mobility. A group would only make the work more difficult.

Anne, watching their disappointment settle into the room over the following day, made a practical decision. She gave them a commission.

Originally, she had meant to wait for Ceres’s return and hire the mercenary to handle the matter, but the task was too simple to reserve for someone of Ceres’s expense. The trio knew the road to Riverend, they had spent time there before, and desperate though they currently were, Anne judged them capable and trustworthy enough for a supply run.

Naturally, she did not make the decision out of pity. She genuinely needed more supplies and disliked letting the stock fall too low while depending on uncertain return dates. At the same time, having the trio drift in every afternoon only to reread old notes and drink tea without spending anything was not a promising direction for the bookstore’s finances.

So she solved both problems at once.

Usually, escorting, transport, and retrieval were done more by mercenaries than adventurers. Adventurers were expected to chase beasts, ruins, and danger. Mercenaries carried things, guarded roads, and handled practical contracts. But circumstances had a way of bluring the distinctions, especially when coin grew short. The trio needed a job, and Anne needed goods.

Dorgh, of course, had warned her against building trade ties with Riverend. In his view, it risked earning the mayor’s displeasure, and in Mountainend displeasing the mayor was often treated as breaking the law. Anne had listened politely and dismissed the advice just as politely.

She could not cripple her business because of a provincial rivalry that had nothing to do with books.

What, exactly, could the mayor do to her?

The town had done almost nothing to support the bookstore’s actual operation. Its improvements did not reach her stretch of road. Its public comfort did not keep her shelves stocked. Its officials had sold her a building and collected their satisfaction from that bargain already. Anne paid taxes—or would, when spring came and they were due—and the deed to the property was firmly in her possession.

So unless the mayor intended to ban a legal bookstore for daring to purchase supplies where supplies were available, his irritation would remain just that. Irritation.

And if he did intend to do it, well, that would be ridiculous. Wouldn’t it?
