Currently, Ning was traveling with a merchant caravan introduced by Old Zhou.

As someone who had lived within the sect for over forty years and survived countless missions beyond its borders, Old Zhou’s recommendations carried real weight. If he said a caravan was reliable, then it was at least competent enough not to get its passengers killed on the first stretch of road.

As for why Ning was in a merchant caravan in the first place?

The reason was simple.

He was hitchhiking to his mission location: Redhorn Village.

Ning had learned one thing very clearly during his time in the sect: the outside world was dangerous.

Not “occasionally risky,” not “slightly inconvenient,” but genuinely lethal. The kind of place where a moment of carelessness could mean death.

Since this was his first time traveling so far beyond sect territory, Ning had no intention of charging out blindly. He wanted experience first.

That was where merchant caravans came in.

They weren’t particularly strong, but they were experienced. They had traveled these roads countless times and knew how to avoid most of the trouble that came with them.

Convincing the caravan leader had taken a bit of effort, but nothing too difficult.

The caravan itself belonged to Hengyuan Trading Company, a mid-sized merchant group that ran routes between Blackthorn City and the outlying settlements. Since their base of operations was Blackthorn City, all he had to do was reveal his status as an outer-sect disciple.

And, quite accidentally, let slip the jade token of Elder Shen, a “temporary loan” he had swindled from the old man for this very journey.

That sealed the deal immediately.

So yes, the hitchhiking process had gone smoothly. Not only because of the token, but also because Ning’s cultivation wasn’t low. At the Fifth Stage of Qi Condensation, he was already considered a respectable combatant.

Among the caravan, only the leader was stronger.

That man sat atop a supply crate near the center wagons, eyes half-closed. His name was Zhen Lei, and he was a late-stage Qi Condensation cultivator.

Ning chose not to bother him directly as the guy was busy aura farming.

Instead, he mingled with the guards.

By midday, the heat had grown noticeably worse. One of the guards walking beside a wagon had wiped the sweat from his forehead several times already, so Ning eventually took out a small ceramic flask from his storage pouch and offered it over.

“Want something cold?”

The guard, Sun He, according to the name stitched onto his leather vest, looked curiously at the thin layer of frost covering the flask.

“What is it?”

“Frozen Breath Rice Wine. I made it myself.”

The moment Ning loosened the stopper, a faint fragrance spread through the air. Sun He took the cup Ning poured for him, tasted it, and immediately gave an approving nod.

“Not bad at all.”

That was apparently enough advertisement.

The nearby guards had already noticed the smell, and soon another two wandered over looking for a taste of their own. Even Zhen Lei eventually opened one eye from where he sat atop the supply crate.

Ning caught the movement and raised the flask toward him. “Senior Zhen?”

Zhen Lei hesitated for all of a second before holding out his cup. After taking a sip, he gave a quiet grunt of approval and finished the rest.

“Good stuff.”

Coming from a man who had barely spoken since they left Blackthorn City, that seemed to count as high praise.

After that, Ning’s supply began disappearing at an alarming rate.

By the time the caravan stopped for a short rest, he had somehow ended up sitting among the guards while cups of Frozen Breath Rice Wine were passed around. Ning himself drank very little. The wine had done what he wanted it to do anyway, and conversation came much more easily afterward.

When Sun He learned that Ning was heading toward Redhorn Village for his first mission this far outside sect territory, he finally understood why Ning had been paying so much attention to everything along the road.

Ning didn’t deny it.

“I’ve read plenty about traveling outside the sect, but reading and actually doing it are different. What do you usually watch out for?”

That question quickly got the older guards talking.

Bao Lin, a broad-shouldered man sitting nearby, told him not to put too much faith in maps. A route that had been perfectly safe several months ago might have a collapsed bridge, a new beast territory, or some other problem by the time you passed through again.

Sun He agreed, then pulled a yellow talisman from inside his vest.

“And keep a few of these somewhere easy to reach.”

Ning recognized the evil-exorcising talisman immediately.

If someone had shown him one in his past life, Ning would probably have told them to put their faith in science.

Unfortunately, science had never needed to account for an angry dead cultivator crawling out of an abandoned well.

In this world, spirits were very much real.

From what Ning had read, they were formed from a convergence of malevolent energy between heaven and earth, sometimes intertwined with unresolved obsessions or lingering regrets left behind by the dead.

He had bought several talismans before leaving the sect precisely because of that, though he had never actually seen one used.

“You’ve needed one before?” Ning asked.

Sun He’s face immediately soured.

“Once. That was enough.”

He didn’t seem particularly eager to elaborate.

Zhen Lei did it for him. Still sitting nearby with a cup in hand, he explained that ordinary evil-exorcising talismans weren’t meant to destroy anything truly dangerous. Against weaker spirits, however, they could repel them or interfere with them long enough for someone to escape.

“Sometimes a few breaths are all you need,” he added.

Ning quietly committed that to memory. At least the spirit stones he’d spent preparing for the trip hadn’t gone to waste.

From there, the subject naturally turned toward places better avoided at night. Bao Lin mentioned abandoned shrines and old wells, while another guard added crossroads to the list.

Ning had to ask, “All because of spirits?”

Bao Lin looked at him as though the answer should have been obvious.

“What did you think lived there?”

...Fair enough.

The stories only became worse from there.

One guard spoke about hearing someone call his name from an empty field after sunset. Another mentioned a roadside shrine where three travelers had disappeared within the same month. Someone else had apparently once woken up to find muddy footprints circling his bedroll despite the ground around their camp being completely dry.

Ning listened carefully.

Honestly, it sounded exactly like a ghost story.

And the first people to die in ghost stories were always the ones who disregarded the set rules.

Eventually, the topic shifted to more ordinary dangers, and Ning asked about bandits.

Bao Lin snorted. “Bandits? I’d rather meet them.”

Sun He nodded in agreement. “At least you usually know what they want.”

Zhen Lei gave a quiet grunt before adding, “Rogue cultivators are worse.”

That opened another can of worms.

The guards began trading stories about false caravans, poisoned food, inns with formations hidden beneath the floorboards, and cultivators who pretended to be injured just to lure travelers close enough to attack.

It got worse the more Ning heard.

He mostly listened, occasionally asking about something that caught his attention. There was no need to interrogate anyone when the guards were perfectly willing to scare one another with old travel stories.

By the time the caravan resumed its journey, they were already treating him much more casually.

Zhen Lei pointed toward one of the middle wagons.

“Ride there. No point wasting your qi walking the whole way.”

“Thank you.”

Ning accepted immediately.

That was exactly what he wanted. Preserving his spiritual energy in case something happened was far better than spending it just to prove he could keep pace with a wagon.

Fortunately, the rest of the journey passed without incident.

Ning spent most of it listening to the guards and keeping an eye on the road. By the time the low stone walls of Redhorn Village appeared in the distance, he had at least picked up a decent list of dos and don’ts for traveling outside the sect.

He rose from his seat and adjusted his storage pouch.

“This is my stop.”

Sun He reminded him to stay sharp, while Bao Lin told him not to trust cheap inns. Someone farther back shouted that the next time Ning joined their caravan, he was expected to bring more wine.

Ning laughed, cupped his fists toward them, and stepped down from the wagon.

The caravan slowly continued down the road.

Ning watched it leave for a moment before turning toward the village ahead.

Seems my social skills haven’t rusted.

With that thought, Ning walked forward into Redhorn Village.

[Mission Notice]

Recently, a group of early-stage Red-Eyed Spirit Rabbits, led by a mid-stage Red-Eyed Spirit Rabbit, has been harassing the farmlands of the Mi Family. Eliminate the rabbits and prevent further damage.

Reward: 25 spirit stones and one kilogram of Red-Striped Barley.

This was the mission Ning had chosen.

Every outer-sect disciple was required to complete at least one red-paper mission every three years. Failure meant punishment, loss of contribution points, or, in more serious cases, demotion to peripheral rank.

Ning, however, had chosen this mission deliberately.

The reason was simple.

The Mi Family.

By all accounts, the Mi Family had been remarkably fortunate.

Originally, they had been little more than an ordinary cultivation household: three early-stage Qi Condensation cultivators, around twenty mortal members, and no real standing to speak of.

That changed when one of their younger members revealed outstanding talent and successfully entered the inner sect of the Pure Qi Sect.

There was a saying that when one person became immortal, even the chickens and dogs around them would ascend.

The Mi Family was a rather good example of that saying.

With an inner-sect disciple backing them, their influence expanded rapidly. Through their connection to the sect, they secured land and protection near Redhorn Village, eventually growing from a forgettable rural family into a minor but respected local power.

Ning had been planning to visit them for quite some time.

The reason was the Pure Qi Sutra.

Only two years remained before the outer-sect competition. If Ning could push the Pure Qi Sutra to its final third level before then, both his cultivation speed and combat strength would improve considerably.

For that, raw effort alone wasn’t enough.

He needed insight.

Ning had spent quite some time trying to deepen his understanding of the Pure Qi Sutra, but there was only so much he could figure out by himself.

Unfortunately, Elder Shen had practiced a different primary technique during his early years. His explanations were still useful, but when it came to the finer details of the Pure Qi Sutra, there were limits to how much he could help.

However, the elder had once casually mentioned something important.

Before entering the inner sect, the Mi Family’s genius had left behind written insights on the Pure Qi Sutra. The family occasionally sold or shared copies of those notes, both for income and to maintain their connection with nearby cultivators.

It wasn’t Ning’s only option, but it was certainly the cheapest one available to him.

He didn’t have the standing to casually consult high-level cultivators, nor did he have enough spirit stones to purchase some rare annotated inheritance.

The Mi Family occupied a much more convenient middle ground.

Originally, Ning had only asked Si Sihua to keep an eye out for missions near Redhorn Village.

The fact that she had found one involving the Mi Family itself was simply a pleasant surprise.

“I really did get lucky,” Ning muttered.

He could finish his red-paper mission and visit the Mi Family at the same time.

Two birds with one stone.

The first thing Ning noticed upon reaching Redhorn Village was the enormous red stone standing near the entrance.

It curved upward from the ground almost exactly like a horn.

So that’s where the name came from.

At least one mystery solved.

Ning spent the next hour wandering around the village, casually asking about the Mi Family and the recent rabbit problem.

Normally, a tavern would have made this kind of information gathering much easier. Unfortunately, the village tavern was closed, so Ning made do with shopkeepers, farmers, and whoever else seemed willing to talk.

By the end of it, he hadn’t found anything particularly suspicious.

The rabbit problem was real, the Mi Family’s reputation mostly matched what he had heard, and the villagers appeared more confused than alarmed by the whole situation.

Most of the strange looks were probably because a sect disciple had spent the better part of an hour wandering around asking questions.

Ignoring them, Ning finally made his way toward the Mi Family residence.

Compared with the other homes in Redhorn Village, it was noticeably larger, surrounded by a proper courtyard wall with two guards stationed near the entrance.

Ning approached one of them.

“Excuse me. I’m an outer-sect disciple from the Pure Qi Sect. I came regarding the spirit-rabbit mission.”

He showed his outer-sect token as he spoke.

The guard was only a mortal, but Ning still spoke to him politely. There was no reason not to.

The moment the man saw the token, his expression changed.

“I-I see. Please wait here. I’ll inform the patriarch immediately.”

He hurried through the gate.

Ning waited outside.

It wasn’t long before a middle-aged man emerged from the residence, walking quickly toward him. His clothes were considerably finer than those of the guards, and the faint spiritual fluctuations around him marked him as a cultivator.

The man took one look at Ning and the outer-sect token hanging from his waist.

Then, for some reason, his expression became strange.

“Excuse me... who are you?”

Ning blinked.

“Hm?”
