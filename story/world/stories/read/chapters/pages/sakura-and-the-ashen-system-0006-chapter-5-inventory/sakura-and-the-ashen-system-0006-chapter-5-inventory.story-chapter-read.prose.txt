Sakura swallowed and finally forced herself to answer before her silence became even more suspicious.

“Something was chasing me,” she said, trying to make her voice sound tired rather than too even. “I was walking through the forest. It caught up to me too suddenly... I think I hit my head pretty badly, and now... I’m not really orienting myself very well.”

The older fisherman narrowed his eyes even more, as if he was not so much listening to her words as measuring them against her face, her scraped palms, and the dirt on her clothes.

“So, a monster,” he drawled with such irony that Sakura immediately understood this was a man who had made a habit of not believing people from the first word. “And what kind exactly? There are plenty of them around here, you know.”

The boy beside him shifted awkwardly from foot to foot, still glancing between Sakura and his father.

“You can tell she’s not lying,” he said uncertainly. “Just look at her.”

“I am looking,” the older man grunted. “That’s why I’m asking.”

Sakura quickly ran through everything she could say without exposing herself even more.

“Black,” she answered, a little more firmly now. “Big. Crimson light in the cracks. Huge sharp spikes. It drove me toward the ruins, and then I got lucky...”

“Lucky?” the man repeated.

“Another monster attacked it,” Sakura said, trying to stay close to the truth, but not too close. “They went at each other, and I ran.”

The older fisherman grunted, but without the same pressure as before. Apparently, the explanation sounded wild enough to be believable in this world.

“So you’re lucky, then.”

“Not completely,” Sakura answered quietly, and before he could start another round of questions, she added, “I need water. Just water. I’ll leave as soon as I can walk properly again.”

The boy immediately turned to his father.

“We should help her. You can see she’s barely standing.”

“I can see you’ve already decided to drag every miserable soul into the house,” the man said dryly, though the sharp hostility was gone from his voice now. He looked Sakura over again, letting his gaze linger on her hands, her cloak, her face, then waved a hand shortly, as if he was too lazy to keep arguing. “Fine. The nets can wait. You’ve already ruined them anyway.”

“I didn’t ruin them,” the boy protested at once.

“Of course not. They floated downriver out of shame all by themselves.”

Sakura was not entirely sure whether that had been a joke or simply the normal way they spoke to each other, but the older man’s tone had softened a little, and that was already good. She stepped closer slowly, making sure to keep her hands visible.

“I’m unarmed,” she said. “And I won’t cause trouble.”

At that, the older man snorted openly.

“In this world, you can die walking around unarmed like that. And fast, too. Come on then, unarmed girl.”

The house by the river turned out to be very close, a little higher up the slope, behind low bushes and a leaning wattle fence. It was small, squat, and dark from damp and time, with a gray shingle roof and stones set on top here and there to keep the wind from tearing it away. Wet fishing nets hung by the entrance. Under the awning stood barrels, clay jugs, and bundles of thin rods, probably for mending gear. The whole house smelled of smoke, dried herbs, fish, and something warm and homely, so simple and earthly that for a second everything inside Sakura tightened painfully.

Inside it was cramped, but not poor. A low ceiling with darkened beams, a rough wooden table by the window, two benches, an old chest, fishing gear stacked by the wall, bunches of dried herbs hanging overhead, and a hearth with a pot still hanging above it. In one corner stood a heavy crude armchair with a sagging seat, once covered in dark fabric, now almost completely faded. Fish bones and some gray roots were drying on the windowsill. Everything looked like people had lived here for a long time.

“Sit,” the older man said, nodding toward the chair. “Before you drop on my floor.”

Sakura sat down carefully. The chair was hard and uncomfortable, but after stone, dust, and the basement, even that felt almost luxurious. The boy was already fussing by the table, pouring water into a heavy clay mug. He handed it to Sakura too quickly, nearly spilling half of it, and smiled with such earnest awkwardness that his intention became obvious at once.

“Here,” he said. “Drink. The water’s clean. I drew it this morning.”

Sakura took the mug with both hands and drank several large gulps. The water was cool, and only after the third swallow did she realize how dry her throat had become. The boy was still looking at her with that same awkward, almost boyish half-smile, and it made her feel strange and a little uncomfortable.

“Thank you,” Sakura said, lowering the mug.

“Want some stew?” the older man asked, already taking the pot off the fire. “Fish stew. Not exactly a delicacy, but it’s not poison.”

“I do,” she answered at once, without trying to preserve her dignity.

“Well then. A living person always eats more willingly than a dead one.”

The boy snorted as if he had just heard an excellent joke, though Sakura was not at all sure the older man had actually meant it as one. A minute later, a clay bowl of thick fish stew was already sitting in front of her, smelling of river fish, onions, and some kind of herbs. Sakura picked up the spoon and forced herself not to eat too greedily, though her body practically lunged at the hot food. After the very first swallow, a heavy, almost painful relief spread through her. She did not even notice herself closing her eyes.

“There,” the boy said with satisfaction. “See, Father? I told you we should help her.”

“What I’m seeing so far is that you’ve already married the first girl you’ve met in your head,” the older man replied, setting his own mug on the table.

The boy flushed so fast that Sakura had to lower her eyes, suddenly embarrassed too.

“I didn’t do anything...”

“Sure.”

At last the older man sat down across from her and looked at her more calmly now, without the earlier squint, though still closely.

“All right. Since you’re in my house eating my stew, let’s introduce ourselves properly. I’m Brein. This is my son, Lir.”

Sakura lowered her gaze to the bowl, hiding the smile that had nearly slipped out.

“Sakura,” she said after a short pause.

Both fishermen exchanged a look.

“That’s a strange name,” Brein said without the slightest attempt at delicacy. “Not from around here.”

“It’s pretty,” Lir put in quickly.

“I didn’t ask for your opinion.”

Sakura shrugged as if she did not care.

“Maybe it isn’t from here,” she said cautiously. “I’m not really sure where I’m from.”

Brein caught that uncertainty at once.

“What do you mean, not sure?”

Sakura set her spoon down quickly, as if she were just tired of eating and not buying herself time.

“I hit my head harder than I thought,” she said with light, almost self-ironic weariness. “Some things I remember... and some are like fog.”

“Convenient,” Brein remarked.

“I wouldn’t call it that.”

Luckily, Lir stepped in again before the questioning got too sharp.

“She already said a monster chased her. After something like that, people forget worse things.”

“After something like that, people usually remember a lot very fast,” Brein answered dryly, but did not press further for the moment.

Taking advantage of the pause, Sakura had another spoonful of stew and said, almost casually,

“I saw guards at Crowlis’s castle.”

Brein tensed at once. It showed not only in his face, but in the way he stopped moving the mug in his fingers.

“Go on.”

“There were a few armed men by the ruins, looking over the carcass,” Sakura said. “They were talking about Crowlis.”

“Go on.”

Sakura lowered her eyes to the bowl.

“I guess I really did hit my head harder than I thought. Please, tell me about Crowlis.”

Brein leaned back in his chair and snorted.

“Sure. Either you hit your head, or you lie very badly.”

“Father,” Lir said, though without real reproach now.

“What? If someone doesn’t know who made half the world live in a nightmare for fifty years, then either she’s lying, or there’s one big hole where her memory’s supposed to be.”

Sakura stayed silent, letting them argue while catching every word.

“Crowlis was a mage,” Brein said at last. “Or a scholar. Or a madman. Depends who you ask. Some say he meddled where no human should meddle. Some say he tried to reach the gods and picked the wrong door. And some think it was even worse than that.”

“And because of him, the monsters appeared?” Sakura asked quietly.

Lir shrugged.

“That’s one of the theories.”

“The most popular one,” Brein muttered. “After his experiments, the world seemed to twist sideways. The gods cursed these lands and filled them with obsidian beasts. Or maybe not the gods. Or maybe they didn’t curse anything and just turned away. Around here, everyone tells it differently, especially after they’ve had a drink.”

“You sound like you’re in a tavern yourself right now,” Lir remarked.

“And you can keep quiet and eat.”

“I already ate.”

“Then be quiet on a full stomach.”

Sakura lowered her eyes, hiding her tension. So the world connected the obsidian beasts to Crowlis. So the rumors about him were still alive. And if anyone ever found out what exactly she had become in that basement, nothing good would come of it.

“Fifty years,” she repeated quietly. “That’s a long time.”

“For disaster, not that long,” Brein said. “For human memory, long enough. Half the people don’t remember facts anymore, only stories. But people still avoid Crowlis’s ruins. And not for no reason.”

“And you really don’t remember where you’re from?” Lir asked, looking at Sakura with curiosity. “Not even roughly?”

Sakura froze for a second, then answered, trying to keep her voice even.

“The nearest city, I think.”

“From Mebrel?” Lir asked at once.

She nodded.

“Yes. From Mebrel.”

Brein narrowed his eyes. Sakura let out a tired breath and lowered her gaze to the bowl.

“I’m from there.”

“Well, all right,” Brein repeated with dry irony.

“I remembered that,” Sakura said quietly.

Luckily, Lir decided to smooth things over again.

“Come on, Father. If she’s from Mebrel, it’s not that strange she ended up by the river. People from there cut through the forest all the time.”

“Smart people don’t cut through the forest,” Brein muttered.

“I wasn’t out after dark,” Sakura said quickly. “Things just... went bad before I understood how bad.”

Brein stared at her for a few more seconds, then grunted and reached for his mug.

“All right. Sit, eat, and get yourself together, since you made it here alive. After that, we’ll see what to do with you.”

Lir gave her an encouraging smile, as if that was already close to hospitality. Sakura gave the slightest nod, but nothing inside her relaxed. Mebrel. Now she at least had a name to hold on to if she had to keep lying.

Brein stayed with them for a while longer, then muttered something about the nets, got up, and went back out toward the river. The door creaked behind him, cold air slipped into the house for a moment, and then it was quiet again, almost cozy, if not for the fact that Sakura was still sitting in a stranger’s house. Lir moved closer almost immediately, as if he had been waiting for his father to leave.

“Don’t take him too seriously,” he said in a low voice, nodding toward the door. “He’s like that with everyone. Even with me.”

Sakura gave a faint smile and nodded.

“Oh, I noticed.”

Lir smiled too, visibly encouraged that she had answered him at all.

“If you want, I can take you to Mebrel tomorrow. You really shouldn’t go alone. Not after everything that happened today... Well, you know.”

Sakura slowly raised her eyes to him.

“Take me?”

“Yeah.” He shrugged as if he were offering something completely simple. “I need to go there in a couple of days anyway, but I could go tomorrow. If Father lets me.”

Sakura did not answer right away. She had too much to think about. Mebrel was still only a name she had snatched out of the air, a random point on the map of a foreign world that she had grabbed onto so she would not look completely lost. But a city meant people, directions, possible answers, and at least some kind of foothold.

“Thank you,” she said carefully. “That... could help.”

Lir lit up at once, then suddenly frowned as he looked at her hands. Fortunately, the cloak’s sleeves were a little too long on her and hid the mark on her left wrist.

“Hey, why aren’t you drinking a potion?”

Sakura did not understand the question.

“What potion?”

Lir blinked.

“A healing one. So your injuries close up. Your palms are scraped, and I think your face got clipped too. Don’t tell me you really don’t have any?”

Sakura froze for a second, but immediately forced herself to lower her eyes as if she were simply looking at her scratches.

“No,” she said as evenly as she could. “I... don’t think I do.”

“How do you not?” Lir sounded almost genuinely shocked now. “Everyone should have at least one little vial. Or two, unless they’ve got no head at all. No one goes into the forest without them.”

Sakura exhaled slowly. Apparently, this was yet another thing every child in this world knew and she did not.

“Looks like I did an especially good job today of seeming strange,” she said, trying to joke.

Lir huffed, but did not let it go.

“Haven’t you opened your inventory at all?”

Sakura lifted her eyes to him.

“Inventory,” he repeated more slowly now, as if he were talking to someone who had hit their head harder than even they realized. “Your personal storage. You don’t carry everything in your hands, do you?”

Sakura felt everything inside her tense, but kept only tired confusion on her face. She lowered her gaze to the table, not wanting him to see too much in her expression. Inventory. Of course it was another obvious thing she had not known. She thought carefully about the word, almost distrustfully, like a door that might open onto absolutely anything.

[Inventory]

A translucent window immediately flashed before her eyes. Sakura almost jerked, but managed not to. The panel opened quietly, cleanly, in cold System light, and the first thing she understood was that Lir could not see any of it. He was looking at her exactly as before, simply waiting for a reaction. His eyes did not catch on the text, did not shift, did not narrow. To him, the air in front of her was still empty.

That was good. Very good.

Sakura quickly ran her eyes over the text.

INVENTORYSlots Used: 5/12Minor Healing Potion x2Dry Ration x1Water Flask x1Linen Bandage x2Flint and Steel x1

Coins:Copper: 11Silver: 2Gold: 0

For a few seconds, Sakura simply stared at the list, feeling both relief and absurd irritation at the same time. So she really had had things all this time. Not much, a pathetic little survival set, but not total emptiness either. And the little vials were there too. Two of them. She tightened her fingers a little on the edge of the table.

“Well?” Lir asked. “Got anything?”

Sakura raised her eyes to him, trying to look not shocked, only a little embarrassed.

“I do,” she admitted. “Two small potions.”

“There.” He sounded as if he had just rescued her from a rare form of idiocy. “I told you. Your head really is all scrambled.”

“That’s true,” Sakura said quietly.

She looked back at the translucent window. Minor Healing Potion. The moment she focused on the line, a short note flashed beside it.

MINOR HEALING POTIONAccelerates the healing of light and moderate injuries.Quantity: 2

Sakura reached for it mentally, still not fully understanding how it worked, and in the next second a small glass vial with cloudy reddish liquid was actually in her hand. She almost flinched when the vial appeared, but caught herself in time. Lir only nodded, as if everything was completely normal.

“There you go. Drink it. At least half,” he said. “It won’t do much for serious wounds, but it’ll pull the skin together faster.”

Sakura turned the vial slowly in her fingers. The glass felt cool and real, which meant the inventory was real too.

She drank about half, stopping only when she remembered Lir’s warning. Almost at once she felt a faint warmth under her skin. Not that terrible foreign thing that had been coiling inside her after the altar, but something simpler and easier to understand. Her palms started stinging harder, the cut across her left palm burning the worst, and then the pain went duller. The smaller scratches on her hands and face pulled unpleasantly, as if they were being drawn together from the inside.

Sakura quickly put the half-empty vial back into the inventory, not wanting to keep holding something whose existence she had not even known about a minute ago.

This world was far more complicated than she had imagined.

“So,” Lir said more softly, “are we going to Mebrel together tomorrow?”

Sakura slowly raised her head.

“We’re going.”
