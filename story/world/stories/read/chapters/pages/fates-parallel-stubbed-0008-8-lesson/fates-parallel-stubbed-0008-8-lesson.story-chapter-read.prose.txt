“Mages, Martial Artists, and Spiritual Cultivators, collectively known as immortal practitioners or sometimes simply cultivators. They share many things in common—they all benefit from greatly increased longevity, they all manipulate mana in some form or another—ki and qi are simply mana stored within the body or soul respectively—and they can all use mana to create magical phenomena.”

Lee Jia saw Dae taking notes studiously and couldn’t help but wonder why. Didn’t he know this stuff already?

“Now then, the differences between the disciplines comes down to how and what they cultivate and the method by which they produce their magical effects. To begin with the example most familiar to me, mages cultivate the mind. Through insights gained into the nature of existence by studying our own aura, we expand our awareness and mental acuity. This increases the size and strength of our aura, as well as the precision with which we control it.”

Another student—one of the disciples from Qin by the looks of it—stood to ask a question and Hwang gestured for them to speak.

“Pardon my rudeness, Elder, but are we going to be learning magic techniques at any point in this lecture?”

“Hoho, have patience. I do apologize for my blathering, but I promise you that this information will be valuable to you. You may even learn a thing or two about your own discipline! In fact, why don’t I speak about it now, hmm?

“The spiritual cultivators of Qin focus on the cultivation of the soul. They draw mana in through their meridians and store it within a part of the soul called the dantian. If you were to follow in the footsteps of the God Emperor Qin, the ultimate goal would be to refine qi within the dantian into divine energy, craft from that divine energy a spiritual body, and then finally ascend to true immortality.

“Finally—and this might be obvious at this point—martial artists cultivate the body. Like spiritual cultivators, they draw mana into themselves. However, instead of their meridians, they allow the ki to permeate through their body by following the natural pathways of the blood vessels. In doing so, their very flesh and blood is enhanced in a manner not unlike the natural state of being for half-spirits—though a skilled martial artist has a great deal more ki in their body than an unawakened half-spirit. We’ve actually only just recently discovered that half-spirits cannot survive without either their natural ki reserves or the mana within their aura, so martial arts training should be a priority for us! Hohoho!”

Lee Jia saw Magus Hwang glance at her briefly as he laughed. He was talking about her, wasn’t he? How her body had stopped working entirely when she ran out of mana before. So nobody had known that would happen? But then how did Ienaga know how to fix it so quickly?

“Now then, onto the benefits of interdisciplinary cultivation—well, this part is mostly theoretical at the moment. However, it is the belief of many of my contemporaries as well as myself that there are undiscovered benefits to cultivating in two or even all three disciplines simultaneously.

“The most basic synergy theorized is as follows—the mana that gets converted into qi or ki most likely comes directly from one’s aura. Even non-mages have one, they simply aren’t aware of it. A larger aura should allow for longer effective training sessions, a more dense aura for more efficient training, and improved aura control should make such training easier. In theory, just by being a mage, training in other disciplines becomes faster, easier, and more effective.”

This time, Lee Jia couldn’t help but ask a question herself.

“Magus Hwang, if that’s true then why haven’t all mages already started practicing these techniques?”

“Hoho, an excellent question! The reason is twofold—First, until the founding of this academy, our three nations have guarded our techniques rather jealously. Even now, the exchange of information has been somewhat limited. Second, there is a reason this academy has been filled with the brightest young disciples our nations could find.

“Over time, the natural mana within a mortal becomes corrupt, eventually leading to natural death. I myself am so old that I am sustained entirely by the mana in my aura—a second awakening has long since become impossible for me. It may theoretically be possible to do so after forming a spiritual body and discarding the old, corrupt form. Unfortunately, the God Emperor has declined to respond to my inquiries on the matter.”

Lee Jia wondered if he was joking, or if Magus Hwang really had attempted to ask the most powerful person on the continent—maybe in the entire world—to test his theory.

“Now, before I instruct you on the methods to awaken your mana senses, I’d like to touch briefly on the nature of the techniques employed by each discipline. As with the cultivation methods themselves, there are fascinating similarities and differences in the implementation of their techniques.

“Beginning again with Mages—and I must admit to some academic bias here—all techniques are essentially different ways of applying the basic principles of magic. At its core, magic can be split into two forms—talismans and formations. There are also artifacts, enchantments, blessings, and so on, but they are all simply different applications of those two essentials.

“Talismans and formations are both sets of instructions which transform elemental mana into physical phenomena. The main difference between the two is reusability—a talisman generally creates an immediate explosive effect, while a formation’s effect is ongoing, or even permanent. This practice is not limited to mages, but we have two distinct advantages over other immortal practitioners when it comes to creating and using them.

“First, creating talismans and formations usually requires rare and elementally attuned materials. The jade slips used by Qin scholars are one such example. White jade is extremely well attuned to all six primary elements, making it an excellent conduit for complex talismans.”

Another student rose to ask a question, and this time Lee Jia recognized the speaker as Xin Wei.

“I thought there were five elements? What’s this about a sixth?”

“Ah, you are referring to the wuxing elemental theory. That is a reasonably practical elemental model which encompasses some of the most common naturally occurring elements. Many of Qin’s great sects still use it simply because it has been the basis for their techniques and traditions for millennia. However, modern scholars generally agree that the six pointed octahedral elemental model—or simply ‘mana theory’—is more accurate.”

“The—what?”

Lee Jia shared Xin Wei’s confusion, and judging from the susurrus that went through the room she wasn’t alone. In fact, Hyeong Daesung seemed to be the only one who wasn’t confused.

“Hoho, I see I’ll need to explain a bit further. The old wuxing model describes the five elements of Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water, each of which is inherently oriented towards Yin or Yang. In mana theory there are an uncountable number of elements, which are composites between the six primary elements: Earth, Fire, Air, Water, Yin, and Yang. Imagine an octahedron—a diamond—with each of the six primary elements at a point across from its opposite. Earth and Air, Fire and Water, Yin and Yang; each element is a combination of these.”

It sounded quite complicated, and Lee Jia wasn’t sure if she understood it entirely. At the very least she would remember those six elements and their opposites.

“Now where was I? Oh yes! Talismans and their materials. Mages have the unique ability to control the mana outside of our bodies in the form of our auras. With sufficient aura control, we can attune regular materials with mana in order to allow them to conduct spells as efficiently as jade. A powerful and skilled mage—or a martial artist, but we’ll get to that—can even draw talismans on thin air to create simple spell effects at will.

“The other advantage mages have stems from our control of elements. A spell to produce fire requires either fire-attuned materials or fire-attuned mana. With enough practice, a mage can freely adjust the elemental attunement of their aura to suit their needs. Early on this requires a great deal of focus, and would be used to create specific elemental talismans, but a skilled mage can create generic talismans which change their effects dynamically based on the type of element channeled through it.

“This versatility comes at a cost, of course. A mage is only as powerful as he is prepared! Once exhausted of talismans, a mage’s impact on a battle is severely limited. Only the most powerful of us can cast spells from thin air, and even then they are a pale comparison to a properly prepared talisman. We can, of course, operate combat formations. Such formations are static and require advance preparation, however. This—along with our shield formations—is why it is said that Goryeo has peerless defense, but is weak on the attack.”

Lee Jia had wondered about that a bit. If Goryeo was so small, why hadn’t the other nations overwhelmed it by now? How had it survived after centuries of conflict? Now it made a bit more sense—her country was a turtle with an impenetrable shell.

“Now let us compare the other disciplines in their applications of similar techniques. Naturally, this is not my area of expertise, so my descriptions may not be entirely accurate. You should direct more detailed questions to your other instructors as appropriate.

“First, martial artists. As I alluded to earlier, martial artists are also able to cast spells from thin air. They are, in fact, much better at it than we mages are. You see, a martial artist uses their entire body as a talisman. Infused by ki as it is, their flesh becomes the conduit and their movements become the instructions. This—in combination with their physical prowess, sturdy physique, and martial techniques—makes them formidable warriors.

“Finally, the spiritual cultivators. To the untrained eye, a cultivator appears to be simply a better mage! The truth is, of course, much more nuanced. A cultivator’s techniques are created by inscribing formations within the complex pathways of their meridians. By directing qi through a certain pathway, the meridians act as formation instructions and craft the qi into the desired phenomenon. This gives them the advantage of being able to freely cast spells through the formations created by these pathways.

“There are a few limitations to this. First, once a pathway is opened through the meridians, it is permanent. Some techniques skirt around this problem by building on the same pathways to develop more complex techniques out of the simpler ones, but ultimately this means that a cultivator has a hard limit on the number of techniques they can learn in their lifetime. Unlike a mage, who can develop numerous spells and formations for any occasion, a cultivator must carefully choose which techniques to devote themselves to.

“The other primary limitation is elemental balance. Channeling elemental energies attunes you in much the same way that a mage might attune a talisman towards a certain element. For example, the more fire techniques a cultivator practices, the more attuned towards fire they become. A strong enough attunement can make it difficult or even impossible to use techniques of an opposing element.

“Heavily attuned practitioners are known to advance in power quickly, but suffer extraordinary bottlenecks, and may even plateau completely. Martial artists are also known to align their ki towards the body’s natural elemental affinity, which has a similar effect. This is just a theory, but it may be possible to offset a heavily attuned soul though an oppositely attuned body, or vice versa. And, though it is usually balanced by nature, the elemental makeup of one’s aura could also be used to correct an imbalance—though permanently altering it isn’t an option I can recommend in good faith.”

It was...a lot to take in. A few days ago, Lee Jia’s entire knowledge of combat could be summarized as ‘claw at their eyes and try to run away’. Now she had to consider talismans, elements, techniques, balance—it was enough to make her dizzy. For now, she decided to focus on the absolute basics. She’d learn how to draw talismans and formations, learn how to control ki and qi, and worry about the rest later.

While Lee Jia was trying to commit as much of the lesson as she could to memory, Magus Hwang proceeded to explain the breathing and meditation techniques she had already learned to awaken her mana sense. He also described how to refine her mana sense. Currently, her awareness was like a little mote of light floating inside of her aura. Her goal while meditating would be to focus more mana into that mote of light, expanding her awareness within her aura.

According to Dae—who helpfully answered her questions so she didn’t have to interrupt Hwang’s lesson—she would break through to the next stage of magedom when her awareness took up her entire aura, like his. This was apparently called the mana gathering stage, and would be parallelled by the other disciplines similarly gathering spiritual energy until their respective vessels—the soul and body—were full.

Once the vast majority of the class was busy meditating to awaken their mana sense, Hwang gathered the remaining group together. The mages in the class consisted of Lee Jia, An Eui, Hyeong Daesung, and the tiger-headed boy Lee Jia had seen before. Seong Eunae was either not in this class, or not as interested in rehashing the basics as Dae and the tiger guy were.

“As you four have already awakened, you can feel free to take this opportunity to meditate, or I can teach you some basic talismans and formations.”

The tiger-headed boy decided to go meditate while Dae predictably chose to stay and study. For Lee Jia and An Eui, it wasn’t really a choice. Mana sense was currently their only tool, and it was apparently quite useless without talismans. They needed this more than anyone else.

Lee Jia paid close attention to the lesson, absorbing the information like a sponge as Magus Hwang showed them some basic offensive and defensive talismans. The process of creating a talisman was fairly simple. You drew the characters onto a sheet of paper or parchment, then infused the talisman with mana by just pushing mana from your aura into it until the mana started to flow around it instead of through it. It would take a long time, hours or even days depending on the mage’s aura control and the complexity of the talisman.

Formations were the same, except instead of characters you drew a complex ritual circle—sometimes even multiple overlapping circles for particularly advanced formations. Magus Hwang noted that formations could be broken, and as such it was best to create them from engravings or inlays rather than simply drawing them. As Hwang described the methods, an unexpected problem came up for Lee Jia, albeit not a new one.

“Part of what goes into a talisman is the intent of the creator. It is paramount for each glyph to have a strong meaning to the inscriber—it must be understood fully, and replicated perfectly. Regular writing will work, of course, but some mages like to use their own unique codes in order to obfuscate the purpose of their talismans and formations. Either way, you’re going to want to work on your calligraphy!”

Indeed, Lee Jia didn’t know how to read or write—never mind perfectly inscribing a talisman. Even if every brushstroke was absolutely perfect, Lee Jia’s talismans would fail because she didn’t understand the characters. The ritual circles were just larger groups of characters, so she wouldn’t have any luck there, either. She’d have to either invent her own form of writing or, more realistically, learn how to read and write.

Lee Jia looked towards An Eui, but the nervous look on her face was not promising. An Eui could read well enough, but she hadn’t had a chance to practice calligraphy in a long time. Nothing for it, then—Lee Jia approached Dae.

“Hey, Dae. Could I ask a favor?”

“Of course! What can I do for you Miss Lee?”

Dae answered a bit too eagerly and—was his tail wagging?

“I...um, I don’t really know how to put this but, uh—oh ancestors, this is embarrassing. I don’t know how to read!”

Lee Jia stared down at her feet in shame and blushed.

“Oh? Oh! Right, yes, I can see how that would be a problem, hehe.”

Lee Jia pressed her index fingers together and looked up at Dae. Wait, why was he blushing?

“If—if it’s not too much to ask, do you suppose you could teach me? Just when you’re free. You seemed pretty studious and your notes from before helped a lot so I thought you’d be a good tutor.”

“O-of course! Hehe, I’d um, I’d be happy to! Though, how did you read my notes before?”

Dae’s tail was starting to become a hazard as it wagged back and forth excitedly. Odd—she knew he was into studying, but wasn’t he getting a bit too excited?

“Oh, my roommate helped me. So you will, then?”

“Certainly, Miss Lee. How about tom—the day after tomorrow? We should be free, then.”

Lee Jia agreed. Tomorrow would have been no good since neither of them was likely to be up for much of anything after one of Ienaga’s sessions, but afterward sounded fine. There were no more classes scheduled for the rest of the week aside from Ienaga’s, though Lee Jia wondered once again what they were supposed to do with all that free time.

“Alright then! I’ll look forward to it!”

“Yes! I m-mean, uh, me too! Hehe.”

Lee Jia looked askance at Dae. She didn’t get it—who got that excited about reading?
