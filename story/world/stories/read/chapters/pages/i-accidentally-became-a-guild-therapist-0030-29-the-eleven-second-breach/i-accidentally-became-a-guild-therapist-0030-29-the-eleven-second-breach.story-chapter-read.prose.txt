I ACCIDENTALLY BECAME A GUILD THERAPIST

Chapter 29 – The Eleven-Second Breach

ARC III: THE WORLD REMEMBERS

SYNOPSIS: Day 4. Outside, the guild is busy organizing highly absurd "Fishing Event." Inside the planning room, Livia's second session with the enigmatic T.S. takes a chilling turn. An unprecedented system anomaly breaches containment, leaving behind a fleeting echo that fractures the boundary between game and reality.

The event had been running for four days, and the server had done what servers do during major events: it had become itself, but louder.

Every zone was active. Every guild had a strategy. The Verdant Expanse had become a contested field for World Activity points, with three guilds running simultaneous botanical surveys—none of them aware that the flora was now emotionally aware within two hundred meters of Livia, which Thorne had filed as a potential scoring advantage in his research access paperwork, which Alaric had approved same-day with a note that said "unprecedented, good."

Livia had sessions in the morning. In the afternoon she was watching the leaderboard update in real time when Morty appeared in the planning room doorway with his recording crystal and the expression of a skull that was trying very hard to contain something.

“I need to show you something,” he said. “I filmed it this morning. I was not planning to film it. I was trying to film the river for ambient World Activity footage. Then Bron happened.”

He played the crystal.

The Silvermash Zone, according to the event patch notes, had "dynamic aquatic challenge content" for World Activity scoring. What the patch notes had not fully communicated was that the fish in the Silver River had organized.

Not figuratively. Literally.

They had raid parties.

Morty’s footage opened on a wide shot of the riverbank, silver water catching afternoon light, looking peaceful. Then Bron waded in with a fishing rod the size of a small siege weapon, and the water—moved.

Not with current. With intent.

[World Activity Zone: Silvermash River.]

[Aquatic Formation Detected. Fish Raid Party Alpha: Tank Fish (1), DPS Fish (3), Healer Fish (1). Difficulty: Scaled to player level. Current player: Bron Ironslam, Lv.95. Difficulty: Maximum.]

The fish have a Tank.

The fish scaled to Lv.95.

The fish are raiding Bron.

The Tank Fish was, by any reasonable assessment, absurd. It was large. It had the stance—insofar as a fish could have a stance—of something that had absorbed many hits and was prepared to absorb many more. It positioned itself directly between Bron’s hook and the rest of the formation. When Bron cast, the Tank Fish took the hook. Shook it off. Reset position.

Bron stared at it.

“Did that fish just—” he said.

The DPS Fish attacked the line.

Three of them. In rotation. Bron yanked the rod. They disengaged and let the tension release before he could capitalize. The Healer Fish circled the back of the formation, doing something with water pressure that appeared to be buffing the others—their scales were brighter after it passed, their movements sharper.

Bron tried a different cast angle. The Tank Fish read it and adjusted.

Bron tried a fake cast. The Tank Fish did not move. It had seen this before.

Bron tried using his shield as a distraction. The fish ignored the shield.

This went on for twenty-three minutes.

In the footage, Morty’s camera occasionally panned to show his own reaction, which was a skull trying very hard to document professionally while a laugh built in his chest cavity. The crystal picked up faint sounds of him pressing his hand against his mouth. Three times.

At minute twenty-four, Bron sat down on a river rock. He looked at the fish. The fish looked at him. The Healer Fish circled slowly. The DPS Fish held their positions. The Tank Fish was still.

Bron set down the fishing rod.

“Okay,” he said. To the fish. Sincerely. “I respect this.”

The fish did not respond. Fish did not respond. This was a fundamental rule of biology and game design both.

Bron did not appear to require a response.

“I’ve been doing this a long time,” he said. “The raiding. Fighting things. I’m immortal, which sounds great until you realize it means the stakes are always the same and nothing ever surprises you.”

The Healer Fish circled again. Its buff pulse was slower now. Attentive, somehow, in the way that things without faces could be attentive.

“You surprised me,” Bron said. “I didn’t expect to have a strategy problem. I’ve fought dragons. I’ve tanked siege weapons. And a fish—” he looked at the Tank Fish specifically, —“a fish gave me a real problem today.”

He picked up the rod again. But differently. Not setting up for a cast—just holding it, the way you hold something when you’re not sure what you’re going to do with it yet.

“I hope you have a good team,” he said. “You’ve got a good healer. Whoever put that formation together knows what they’re doing.”

A long pause.

Then Bron cast. Clean line, perfect angle, the kind of cast that came from muscle memory accumulated over years of doing something badly until you did it right. The hook hit the water exactly where the Tank Fish was not. One of the DPS Fish, rotating out of position, hit instead.

The other DPS Fish broke formation to support it. A gap opened.

Bron moved fast for someone that size.

[Authenticity Check: PASSED.]

[Genuine strategic engagement. Emotional resonance: detected. World Activity Score +340. Resonance Score +180.]

[The System Note: emotional resonance source is bilateral. Both parties registered.]

Bilateral.

Both parties registered resonance.

Bron and the fish.

The fish resonated.

Bron caught it. He held it up for a moment—a large, silvery fish that was looking at him with the eyes of something that had fought hard and knew it.

Then he put it back.

He watched it swim away. Back to the formation, which had regrouped and was now regarding him from the center of the river. The Tank Fish had resumed its position. The Healer Fish pulsed.

“Good fight,” Bron said.

He stood at the riverbank for a while. Then said, to nobody Morty could see:

“I think I helped it.”

Morty’s camera shook slightly. Just once. Then steadied.

The video had two-point-one million views.

Morty had titled it: "immortal warrior has a real problem for the first time in years (it is a fish)"

The top comment, forty-seven thousand likes:

“I came here for gaming content and left having a conversation with my dad for the first time in four years. What is this server.”

Livia sat with the crystal after it finished playing.

Bron talked to a fish about purpose and surprise.

Two million people watched.

Someone called their dad.

The fish resonated back.

She could not tell Bron any of this. If he found out, he would try to replicate it on purpose. The Authenticity Check would catch it. Forced intent backfired. She would tell him after the event.

After the event, she was going to tell Bron that he made two million people feel something by having a real problem for the first time in years.

She was looking forward to that conversation.

T.S. arrived at 10:00 exactly. Three knocks, evenly spaced.

They sat in the armchair. It went moderate blue—the specific shade Livia had started to associate with someone who had decided to be somewhere and was still calibrating what that meant. The watching-efficiently eyes were the same. But there was something less careful in how they held themselves. Precision, in Livia’s experience, was sometimes armor. When someone let the precision loosen slightly, that was usually the session that mattered.

They started with the event. Livia told them about the Ironfall player who had replied to her message two days ago: "I logged in. I’m not ready for a session yet. But I logged in."

“That counts,” T.S. said.

“I know.”

A small pause. T.S. was looking at the lamp.

“The fishing video,” they said. “The bilateral resonance. Did you know that was possible?”

“I knew Bron was possible,” Livia said. “I didn’t know about the fish.”

Something at the corner of T.S.’s expression that might have been amusement. “The Authenticity Check measures genuine emotional engagement. It doesn’t filter by species.”

The Authenticity Check doesn’t filter by species.

Aethelgard is something else.

Then T.S. looked at their hands, and back at Livia, with the expression of someone who had arrived at the thing they had been building toward.

“The Resonance Score category,” they said. “It did not exist before you arrived.”

Livia’s pen stopped.

“It was added to the Championship structure after you came,” T.S. continued. “The event was planned before you arrived. The standard categories were always going to be there. The Resonance Score was not. It was added after the system changed. By someone who knew exactly what they were building and why.”

The category was designed around what I do.

Specifically.

After I arrived.

The whole event has a category that exists because of me.

And someone built it in without a trace.

“Who?”

T.S. was quiet for a moment.

“I can see the architecture of it,” they said. “I know when something was built and roughly how. I cannot see who authorized it. The chain is very clean. The kind of clean that means someone who knows this system from the inside, not just the surface.”

“The company that develops Aethelgard,” Livia said. Carefully.

T.S. did not move. The armchair was a slightly deeper blue.

“Many people there would have the access,” T.S. said. “The question is not who could have done it. It’s who would have reason to.”

Who would have reason to design a scoring category around what I do.

Build it into a server-wide event.

Leave no trace.

And do all of this after I arrived.

She wrote in her notepad. T.S. waited, patient in the way of something that had been waiting for seven years and had learned the skill thoroughly.

“There’s something else,” T.S. said. “The event has made you visible beyond this server in a way nothing else has. Do you understand what it means for someone from outside to look in and recognize you? Not as an NPC. As you.”

Recognize me.

Not the robe.

Not the Mental Supporter classification.

Me.

“Has that happened?” Livia asked. Quiet.

T.S. met her eyes.

“I watch what I can,” they said. “The system monitors external interaction. It catches most things.”

Most.

Not all.

Most.

At the door, leaving, T.S. said: “Next session. The real reason I was shelved. Not the parameters answer. The official reason and the actual reason are not the same.”

“How different?”

A pause. Then:

“Different enough that someone spent considerable effort making sure only the official one was on record,” T.S. said.

They left.

Seven years unregistered.

Shelved for asking too many questions.

Someone made sure only the official reason was on record.

The real reason.

Next session.

Two things happened that evening close together.

First: a thread appeared in the external community forum. Yuki versus Morty, the polished campaign versus the accidental documentary, building traction in the healthy way that good rivalries did. But buried at the bottom, one comment from an account called DevWatcher:

“has anyone noticed the resonance score seems specifically calibrated to reward exactly what livia does? the metrics map directly onto therapeutic outcomes. that seems intentional”

Thread locked. Four minutes after the post.

Standard moderation time: forty to eighty minutes.

Four minutes.

Someone was watching that thread.

Second: Miles.

He came to the planning room at 9pm, the hour that had become his default check-in on event days. He saw the T.S. notes on the table. Sat down without being invited.

“The Resonance Score,” he said. Not a question.

“Someone built it specifically around what I do,” Livia said. “After I arrived. Clean authorization chain. No trace.”

Miles turned his coffee mug once. She had catalogued this habit: the mug-turning meant he was deciding something.

“There are people who know this system very well,” he said, slowly. “Who have been involved from the beginning. Who understand what it’s built on and what it’s capable of. Not just as players.”

“Would any of them have reason?”

He looked at the lamp. The warm Goldmere lamp that she had placed because it didn’t intrude. The one he had tested the position of four months before she arrived.

When he spoke, the words came out with the specific care of someone who was saying something true and was choosing exactly how much of the truth to say:

“Someone who believed this server was missing something it needed,” he said. “And had the access to try to provide it.”

Had.

Not might have had.

Had.

He is looking at the lamp.

The lamp he tested.

Before I arrived.

The question that was in the room sat between them.

Neither of them asked it.

Both of them knew it was there.

That was its own kind of answer.
