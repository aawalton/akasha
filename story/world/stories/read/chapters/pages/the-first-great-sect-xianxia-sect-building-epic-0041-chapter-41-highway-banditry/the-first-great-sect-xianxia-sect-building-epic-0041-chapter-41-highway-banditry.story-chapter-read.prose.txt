Six li away, at the top of a ridge, a group of bandits laid in wait to kill Liao Hua and her cousin. The bandits had taken advantage of the tree cover and foliage shielding the ridge to hide themselves. The way to the ridge was a steady incline with pockets of wheat crops that had survived a turbulent earth, violent rains, and fires from lightning strikes, before fading into shrubs and trees past the boundaries of the farm.

“It could just be a random camp of travellers that are wary after all the chaos. There is no guarantee these are bandits that you can kill,” her cousin Weiji argued from his horse, watching the ridge out of the corner of his eye. His body weight was directed fully to her, not giving away information to the bandits.

It was unlikely they could see Hua the same way she could see them. That was the twelfth piece of green shifting strangely in the distance, not following the patterns of cold wind or an animal rustling through the thicket. Hua would commend them for their stillness otherwise. And the fog was deep enough that normal eyes would not notice it.

“Weiji, this is a classic bandit ambush. I’ve heard enough stories to know it. A singular path for your prey to travel. An advantaged position. Taking advantage of local chaos.”

“Fine, perhaps they are bandits. How many are there?”

“Probably twenty. Maybe thirty.” She aggressively raised her brow. “Is this a matter of you wanting to deal with the bandits personally? Weiji, you can’t keep all the murder to yourself.”

“My Lord Liao Weilong, that is three times more than ten, which was already pushing the limit of what I would accept. What happens if there are more a li or two away? This is a needless risk, picking a fight at a disadvantage. Let’s wait till we have numbers and then run them down.”

“They’ll be gone the moment they realise we mean to press the numbers advantage. If they’re smart, they’ve guessed at least one of us is in Qi Gathering and are betting they have the numbers to gun us down.”

“I do not like any of this. You want the two of us to storm an entrenched position to give cover for a smaller force.”

“Exactly. I don’t even need to tell you my thoughts before you figure them out. We’ll pretend to take a break. You,” she said, gesturing to a scout. “Return to the good captain at a relaxed pace. Tell him I want only eight other soldiers to deal with this. Have the rest kept three li back. Make the convoy look as tempting as possible.”

Hua dismounted as the scout turned about and led his horse away from them. She stretched out her shoulders, stroking her horse’s neck. It was a calm beast. Didn’t ever surprise her. Soon, Weiji joined her, retrieving the tarp from last night from his saddlebag. He had a pouch of peanuts that he was picking at nervously. The horses meandered off to graze with the other scout watching over them.

“You’ll be fine. Just remember to count to ten,” she said lowly, sitting with her legs stretched out. “Count to ten and then strike. You’ll be just fine.”

“I have no idea what the even means.”

“Trust me for once.”

“I mislike this plan. Risking the convoy is needless. All of this is needless.” Hua reached across and stole a handful of peanuts. Weiji sighed. “These are major concerns you will have to consider, my Lord. Fun and humour can be had, but if they bring about the destruction of your reign, will they have been worth it?”

“You worry overly much.”

“I hope I am. But as your second, I must offer my counsel over and over until you listen. Whether you accept it is a different matter, but you must listen. If a day comes when we are at war, and the lives of our clan are at stake, will you make a choice for more than your satisfaction, or shall we be casualties to your carelessness?”

She clenched her fists, fighting to stay calm. “Always.”

“When the day comes that you order us to our deaths for a greater goal, I hope you will have made us believe in that goal.”

“This isn’t purely for my entertainment. There is a reason for my actions.”

“Enlighten this unworthy servant.”

“Mortals get weak quickly. Their muscles cramp, their bodies grow weak quickly, and they become stiff if they stay in one position for too long. We’ll wait here, comfortable, while they grow weary and anxious unable to move.”

Weiji leaned back, his narrowed eyes loosening from stony disapproval. “You’re doing what your father did at the Battle of the Plains. He sat his army down overnight and simply waited whilst his enemy exhausted itself.”

“Yes. When the most powerful man in the province stands across from you, you can’t risk resting. Even with the numbers disadvantage, he made his victory look trivial.”

“My apologies, it seemed I saw you incorrectly.” He smiled, reaching out with a handful of dried fruits. Where he got them, Hua would never know. “I assumed you hadn’t given any tactical considerations to your actions.”

She didn’t accept his apology, but she did take the fruits. As the minutes rolled by like the cold winds rolled through the amber fields, Hua watched her enemies. Just as she’d hoped, they were forced to stay still. Even when the convoy was moving, they would have to stay in hiding for even longer. So, she ate and drank and hashed out a plan with her cousin.

It would be another hour before Captain Yao arrived with the convoy. Even Hua could not see the bulk of her soldiers, so far were they, hidden by the fog the bandits took advantage of as well.

“My Lords Liao,” he greeted without a hint of warmth, “this Captain Yao has arrived with your compliment of soldiers.”

She nodded to Weiji and allowed him to explain the situation. The captain’s frown deepened with each word.

Finally, Captain Yao nodded. “And so, I am meant to accept that my liege lord and his second will head into battle at the vanguard of an intentionally understaffed force against an entrenched enemy in an advantaged position?”

“Yes. Will that be an issue?”

“No, as we will either win this foolish battle or I will be dead in a ditch within the hour. As my lord wishes, I will die in battle with him.”

“Your sense of duty does not extend to politeness,” Weiji snarled, standing to his full, looming height.

“This lowly captain only speaks what he grasps. If there is a greater merit to this assault plan than simply running down a pack of bandits with our superior cavalry, then I am eager to learn it. While you think on the matter, excuse me, I will ready my men.”

And just like that, he turned to convoy the seemingly suicidal orders to his soldiers.

“I hope he takes an arrow to the knee,” Weiji muttered. “Insulting his lord. Madness. I should have him whipped for that.”

“I’d rather a soldier who speaks to me plainly than one who never questions me. I’ll listen to good counsel no matter how it is said,” she said, throwing back his words. “So long as he does his duty, he can spend his nights insulting my memory. And last I checked, you approved his presence here.”

Weiji shook his head furiously and walked away from her in a huff. His horse responded to his anger by neighing and backing away. It might have had something to do with the lightning running down his hands.

***

Hua and her advanced squad of ten rode through many li of fog and wheat separating her from the bandits. With the sun growing stronger, the fog had been dispelled slightly. Enough so that Captain Yao cursed as he picked out the first bandit, still a li away. The bandits were hidden well for mortals. They kept themselves still with great discipline. Not distorting their silhouette. Even a bright colour that stood out could be ignored if it did not shift and change.

Below the ridge was the outermost edge of a wheat field, perhaps three or four horse widths wide. Anyone trying to avoid the crevasse that took out the road without spending hours or days building a bridge would have to travel through this path. And the moment they did, they would be assaulted from their exposed flank.

“Remember to count to ten,” she said lowly so that only her cousin could hear.

At the last moment before they entered kill range, Hua raised her hand and made a fist. Her soldiers came to a halt as she stood in her stirrups, making a production of her suspicions. Would they take the bait, or would they flee instead?

Hua waited, watching with unblinking eyes. Moments passed by, her breathing slowing. Just as she thought they would choose discretion, there was a movement amidst the foliage.

Then, the sharp whistle of something fast approaching reached her.

Hua’s hand snaked out to cover her face. There was a loud thwack as her gloved palm was struck. Her horse neighed, leaning away. She steadied it with her shins, examining the projectile.

She held a rock in her hand. This is going to be less fun than I hoped.

The slinger had revealed his group, eager to get a quick kill on Hua. It forced the rest into action. They were standing slowly, limbs stiff. Weakened by hours of inaction and stillness.

“Weiji, on me,” Hua hissed before leaping out of her stirrups. Her Qi flowed slowly, slower than anything she was used to. Still irritated by her near-deviations.

That was fine. Hua had Tempered her body. Taken it to the very edge of human capability and then pushed it past that. There came a point where the best way to move, to advance, were things written down within your bones and muscles, within instinct long before intent was involved. In a moment, she’d covered thirty paces, barely having thought about it.

Half a second behind her, Weiji landed. Another rock cut through the air, heading straight to him. She glanced back and saw him fling his arm to deflect it. The smooth stone flew back, faster than the original slinger had managed. On the ridge, red coloured the fog.

She ducked beneath a rock seeking her head. It whizzed far over the heads of everyone behind her. They were being fools, attacking the obvious Cultivators, instead of the horsemen. Avoiding a battle with a kingfisher banner was the right choice, but if they were going to force a defeat, they should have focused on her backup, and then overwhelmed her and Weiji with raw numbers.

Her cousin passed her by, his longer strides and more physical Cultivation giving him an edge.

She saw a sling be loosed. Before she could hear it whistling towards her cousin, Weiji shifted a step to the right. The shot went wide and struck where he had been a moment ago, a distance a mortal would cover in twenty strides.

There was a danger to charging a line of enemies with ranged weapons. A projectile could remove their head or cause them critical injury. But that danger lowered with higher Cultivation. It assumed they could land their hits.

And besides, there was nothing so terrifying as an angry Cultivator in your ranks.

Hua leapt and cleared the peak of the ridge. Beneath her, wide-eyed bandits too slow to orient their weapons. She crashed through branches and landed with a great crash on one of the bandits, crushing his rib cage. Before any of his friends could react, she unsheathed her blade and swept out a crescent moon as silver dragged through flesh.

A duet of screams cut the air surer than any blade. The tip had caught an eye. The other, she had carved deep through the arm. Torn off a piece of his ratty shoulder armour and bit through flesh and then bone.

Hua pulled out her stained blade, ignoring his screams. She raised a leg and slammed her boot into his chest. He went flying and struck a tree with a boom. Protected by a ratty tunic, neither his spine nor chest endured. His arm, barely hanging on, sheared off and found a new home in the bushes.

A grunt beneath her reminded her of the man she’d landed on. She brought her foot down and stomped his throat flat, bones crackling as his neck was crushed. Hua had honestly thought he’d died from her landing on him. Great constitution wasted on a bandit. Likely someone in the early phases of Body Tempering.

“You bitch,” snarled the man clutching his bloody eye socket. He had a sabre in hand and swung it—

Hua ignored him, assessing the enemies dotted between trees and bushes, some trying to form a line whilst the rest were firing off stones at the men riding hard up the ridge. She’d deal with them next.

—but before the sabre could hit her, Weiji’s giant body barreled into the single-eyed bandit. He tackled him with his shoulder and kept going. Weiji put his physical cultivation to good use and slammed the one-eyed bandit into a boulder.

Red splattered across that boulder.

Hua left Weiji to handle whoever was left behind her and dashed forward to the three bandits forming a line. They carried spears with firm grips, readying themselves to meet her. Good, practical weapons, spears were. You pointed them and enemies tended to die if they came too close. Speed did nothing but ease the spear’s path through your enemy.

Which is why Hua dropped into a slide at the last moment, cold stones poking through her robes. Dust billowing as she slid beneath steel spear tips. Hua reached out once her face was past the central spearman’s crotch, getting an unfortunate glimpse of his underthings, and snagged his ankle. The momentum of her slide dragged him down. He landed hard on his front.

Sadly, his fate was not quite done.

Hua rose, still gripping his ankle, and spun him about. He shouted bloody murder as he hit his ally to the left. But Hua was not done, and, with all her strength, she flung him at the slingers. He bowled over two slingers. The third had been too far to reach and yelped, scrambling away from the confusing mess of his allies.

Hua reached back to the last bandit. Grabbed her spear and tugged it out of her hands. Hua held it around the middle, drew her arm back, and with a strong step forward, threw the spear as a javelin.

It caught the startled slinger in the jaw and sheared it clean off in a shower of teeth, bone, and viscera. Hua frowned. She’d been aiming for the neck, hoping it would leave it partly hanging to make a crimson smile. Not to leave a lolling tongue and a mortal who could still… calling it a scream was wrong, it was more a croaking wail, the sound a dying animal made but, also the sound of human bewilderment.

Hua rotated her shoulder, sighing when she felt a small pop. Ah, just a little bit of stiffness. How silly. She should have stretched.

Hua looked to the woman whose spear she had stolen who was watching with wide eyes, an animal fear capturing her features. She was not a beautiful figure, but Hua found a certain nobility to her animal fear.

“This lord asks that you stay where you are.”

Two slingers and the man she had used as a projectile remained. There were others making the smart choice to flee, recognising how utterly outmatched they were. But that still gave her three more mortals to deal with.

Giving them time to get up and retrieve their weapons seemed the best way to extract entertainment from this, and so she did. When they saw her standing there, merely waiting, one of them pissed his garments. Hua sighed and took a single step forward if they would not meet her.

They broke.

I did not want to chase you down, she thought as they fled, appearing alongside the first in a moment, her sword snaking out and puncturing through his armpit, possibly his heart, and out the other shoulder.

He had a knife sheathed at his waist that she plucked with her nimble fingers before he understood he was dead. A quick flick saw the knife lodged in the skull of the man she had originally used as a projectile. He went down mid-stride, dead instantly. Before blood could stain her gloves, she withdrew her sword and flicked it clean.

The last of the three was making his way at a dead sprint. Hua left him be. A horse was going to trample him very soon. She turned about and found the woman she had commanded to stay trying to flee. Mortals truly were a tiresome lot.

Liao horsemen had finally made it up the ridge. The bandit she’d left alone barely had time to raise his weapon before indifferent cavalry cut him down.

She caught up to the woman and grabbed her by the back of the neck, squeezing tight.

“This lord asked you not to move. Now, I have some questions.” Perhaps not the most appropriate place to interrogate a mortal, what with the pounding of horses, the screams of dying bandits, and the caw of distant crows seeing their next feast. “What is the status of the roads to Coaltown? Are they equally as problematic as this section?”

Hua loosened her grip on the mortal so she might answer.

“Fuck you—”

Hua sighed at that very insightful answer. Of course the mortal would choose to be difficult. At least she knew someone good with them. She carried the woman aloft, stepping over her dead comrades. Her cousin had always been decent with mortals barring Liu Xin, but the Liao Clan tracked their lineage extensively and had never wed so far east. Distrusting foreign blood when their rivals, the Yu Clan, intermingled often with foreigners, wasn’t much of a surprise.

Weiji was working efficiently to strike down his enemies. There was something very rote and mechanical about his violence. He reached out, grabbed a man before he could flee, and snapped his neck cleanly.

“Cousin—"

He turned on a dime, blade lashing out. Hua raised the woman and let her stomach handle absorbing Weiji’s wild swing.

“Pay attention,” she hissed as guts spewed forth from the woman she was holding. “You nearly hit me. And you killed my hostage!”

“Then stop hiding your presence on a battlefield,” he snapped, turning for a moment to punch a woman’s face in. “And drop that body before you get guts on yourself. They have acid and shit in them. Do you have any idea how difficult that is to wash out?”

Hua did so, rather irritated to have both her captive and her kill denied. She’d be getting her revenge against Weiji at some point.

“I’m going to kill the rest now that you’ve stolen my kill.”

Hua gave chase, her heart pumping. Eager for another kill.
