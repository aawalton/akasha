Chapter 111

A measure keeps no shrines and answers to no name. It will let a clever man walk half a day towards the wrong headland before it corrects him, and even then it does not correct him. It simply declines to be where he expected, and leaves the walking to him. — Codex of the Long Watch, Watcher's Commentary

Arthur set his boot against the old quay stone and hauled the bow of the varka up until the keel ground on the shingle and held. He looped the painter twice round a rusted bollard that had not moored anything in a year, tested it with a short pull, and only then let go. The shield rode heavy against his back, the new weight of it a thing his shoulders were still learning.

Above the waterline the ground rose in cracked terraces towards a low headland, and on the headland stood what was left of a sanctuary. Three courses of a wall, a scatter of drums where columns had stood, a threshold slab worn to a shallow dish by feet that had stopped coming. It faced east across the strait, back towards the smudge of the mainland they had rowed from. Everything that had mattered here had looked at Athens.

Donna was already ten paces up the slope, low, the Ravener loose in her hand. She had not gone up to admire the view.

She crouched at the edge of the first terrace and put two fingers against the dust without touching whatever she was reading in it.

"Something lives here," she said. "More than one of them. They came down this line to the water and went back up carrying." She tipped her chin at a shape in the weeds that resolved, once he looked at it properly, into the picked cage of a goat's ribs. "That is a week of eating, in one place. They den in the ruin."

"How many is more than one," Arthur said.

"Enough that they were confident about the goat." She stood. "Cats, going by the tracks. Big ones. I would rather meet them before they meet us."

Steve came up the shingle with the map folded under one arm and his ledger open on the other, already looking past the bones to the stones beyond them. "That is the Skiras sanctuary, or it was. East-facing, on the strait, exactly where Herodotus put it." He sounded like a man arriving at an address he had written down correctly. "Ninety percent."

"You said." Donna moved off towards the wall. "Weapons out. We clear the den first and argue about Herodotus after."

Donna smelled the danger first, as was the case most times.

Arthur unslung the shield and brought it across his body. The blue-tinted iron of Breakwater caught the light filtering through the canopy.

"Inside," Donna murmured, pointing with her axe. "I can hear them. We take out this den before we go to the sanctuary. I don't want a threat behind us."

The cats were waiting in the shadow under a fallen architrave, and they were not cats any more in any way that mattered.

The first one out was the size of a hunting dog, its coat grey, skull too broad behind the eyes. It did not yowl or bristle as a cornered animal should. It flowed out low and fast and silent and went straight for the gap between Arthur and Donna.

Arthur brought the shield across and the animal hit the rolled rim and slid off it, its claws finding nothing to catch, and Donna severed its spine on the backswing before it landed. Two more came out behind it.

"Left," Donna said, and moved to position herself.

Steve was at the back. "Three more in the architrave, they are pushing you towards the drop," he said. Arthur shifted his feet off the crumbling lip going down to the sea and put his back to sound stone, and the pincer the animals had been building closed on nothing.

One of them broke off towards Steve and Sarah. It lunged at the Mender and then dropped twitching at her feet with an overloaded Matrix. Steve ended it with a quick fire lance through the skull.

After that it was work rather than danger. Arthur was a wall a house-cat mutation could not get behind, and Donna was faster than anything else on the island. It took her two strokes on the big one and one on each of the others. Arthur crushed two with the hammer and Steve took the last one with a fire lance.

Nine of them. Under two minutes.

Sarah knelt beside the bodies with the knife and began cutting. It was routine by now, the small incision behind the sternum and the two-fingered extraction that she performed with clinical efficiency. The four larger cats yielded two small, dull cores. The runts had nothing worth taking.

"Three hundred and fifteen, of nine creatures; that can't be right," Steve said. "These are node-adjacent creatures; they should be giving a lot more Ether."

Sarah brought the cores to Steve. "Cores are small as well. Same size as the fox ones."

"Weird," Steve said, brushing pine needles off his sleeves and examining a scratch across his forearm. "I believe the accepted taxonomy would be Felis Infernus Skiradensis."

"You cannot keep naming everything," Donna said, wiping her axe on a handful of dry grass.

"It is the sacred duty of the natural philosopher to classify and record." Steve pocketed the pencil with the expression of a man engaged in profoundly serious work. "I shall enter it in the ledger alongside Serpens Sarahvorus and the rest. We are building a body of literature, Donna. Posterity demands it."

"You are building a body of nonsense," Donna said, already moving past the den toward the open ground beyond.

"Serpens Sarahvorus was your finest work and you have been coasting since," Sarah said, smiling. "Now where is this node?"

The sanctuary, or what remained of it, sat on a flat shelf of limestone overlooking the strait. Two courses of a retaining wall, a scatter of worked blocks, and the faint trace of a rectangular platform cut into the rock. The scrub had claimed most of it. Without Steve pointing at the ground geometry Arthur would have walked straight across it and kept going.

He walked the platform as he read every site, reading the stone against the story, and the story held up. The orientation was right. The old cult of Athena Skiras had sat exactly here, facing the city, and if you stood on the worn threshold and sighted back across the water you could put the line straight up the flank of the distant hill where a small temple to Victory had once hung out over the drop. He closed the ledger and held it against his chest.

"The heart of the sanctuary would have been the platform centre," he said. "Where the cella stood. The node should be here somewhere."

"Except I feel no pull," Arthur said, walking to the middle of the platform and standing on the bare rock.

He waited. He let his breathing settle and waited for the pull. It never came.

There was no pull, no directional pressure, no sense of anything sleeping underneath. He had always felt the pull of the node when near it. Here the stone felt like stone.

He laid his palm flat on the slab to be certain, as you press a wall you already know is not load-bearing. Nothing answered him: no pull, no waking, no keeper rising out of the ground to be killed. Just old marble and the smell of cat.

"There's nothing here," he said. "It's dead, Steve. This isn't a node."

The party spent the next thirty minutes turning over rocks and walking the perimeter for signs and clues they could have missed. They found nothing.

"It has to be it," Steve said, frustrated. "Every reason points at it. The cult, the battle, the temple aimed straight at this shore for two and a half thousand years."

"Grid null," Archie stated. "This location does not register on the node array. It is structurally inert."

Steve's jaw tightened, but he did not speak. He stood at the platform edge with the ledger pressed against his chest, staring out at the strait for a long ten seconds.

"What did I miss?" Steve asked, almost to himself.

"Come now, Doctor Parr, this is a minor hiccup," Arthur said, clasping Steve's shoulder. "Aren't you fond of reciting the tale of Heinrich Schliemann and Troy?"

Steve looked up with a smile and his eyes lost some of the despair.

"Yes, yes. You are correct. All we need to do is retrace the steps," he said. "So what do we know with absolute certainty?"

"We know you broke the first corner riddle correctly, since Sounion was a node," Sarah said.

"And we know of one definitive measure," Arthur added. "The phi triangle we measured beneath the Acropolis."

"Right. So the golden triangle we saw under the Acropolis had two corners and we know one of those is definitely Sounion," Steve mused. "The second corner I obviously got wrong, since there is no node here."

"Then stop staring at the island and read the verse again," Arthur said, not unkindly. "Properly, this time. You solved it too fast to solve it right, Steve."

Steve went still, the pencil stalled against his thigh.

"There we go," Arthur said with a small smile. "The machine has started back up."

"The riddle said let the measure, not the name, run true," Steve said, starting to pace. "But there is no measure."

"I thought phi was the measure," Donna said.

"It could be. But a ratio needs two fixed points before it means anything, and we have one. Sounion. The apex is a mark on a chamber wall I cannot place anywhere yet, and the second corner is the thing we came to find. One point and a shape can be made to stand anywhere you like. There is nothing to run yet."

"So it never pointed at Salamis at all," Arthur said.

"It never pointed anywhere. I could not place the triangle, so I stopped measuring and started arguing. A sightline, a clean goddess-name, a good story. The verse told me the story would betray me. The measure, not the name. I had no measure to run, so I ran the name instead."

The air was still on the platform. The pine canopy rustled at the margins, and across the strait the cranes of Piraeus held their positions like sentries waiting for ships that would never arrive.

Steve set his jaw and said nothing for a moment. Then he crouched, laid the ledger on the warm stone, and pulled the folded map of Greece from the inside pocket of his jacket. He spread it flat with both hands, weighing the corners with loose rubble.

He traced his finger across the gulf on the map, past Salamis, out into the open water to where Aegina sat in the middle of the Saronic basin.

"The maiden who vanished," he said. "Line one of the verse. The isle of the vanished. Aphaia. Aegina." He let the name sit in the air. "I had it in my hand from the first line, and I threw it away because the god-label was muddled and a cleaner one was sitting closer and sounded more clever."

He laughed, though the sound had no pleasure in it. "The riddle told me in four lines of plain verse not to trust the name. I followed the name."

"Too clever for my own good," Steve said, shaking his head.

"You did say Aegina," Sarah said, trying to cheer him up. "Back at the cape. Before the rest of it."

Donna folded her arms. "So this is the wrong rock."

"This is the wrong rock," Steve confirmed. He pressed his thumb and forefinger against the bridge of his nose and held them there.

Arthur looked at him across the dead platform. "I did not help. The sightline argument was mine. I talked you into it."

"You offered a reading," Steve said. He folded the map slowly and put it back in his jacket. "I took it because it confirmed the conclusion I had already reached. That is my mistake, not yours." He picked up the ledger and stood. "Aegina."

Donna had already turned toward the path back to the quay. The light through the pines had gone amber, the shadows lengthening across the broken sanctuary floor.

"Then we have the wrong island, a borrowed boat, and about two hours of daylight left," she said. "Let's go."

Arthur rowed them back across the strait without speaking. The water was calm, the oar strokes punching dark circles into the surface that healed behind them before the next pull. Steve sat in the bow with the ledger open on his knees, staring at nothing. Sarah watched the Perama shore grow larger. Donna watched everything else.

They beached the varka on the same cracked slipway where they had found it, shipped the oars, and dragged the hull back to its resting position. Arthur replaced the stiff tarpaulin, and Donna arranged the folds until they matched the creases that had been there when she first peeled the cover back. When they stepped away, the boat looked exactly as abandoned as it had that morning.

The picnic basket was empty. Eleni's bread and olives and tomatoes had been eaten on the platform of a dead sanctuary on the wrong island, and there was nothing left in it except the cloth and the knife.

They walked up through the lower streets of Piraeus as the shadows ate the last of the light. They had reached the lower slope of the Kastella district, where the streets ran between old warehouses and the corrugated walls of the container-yard market, when they heard sounds coming from the market.

Donna stopped and loosed her axe.

Arthur heard it a half-second after she did: claws on concrete and a low snarling. This was mixed with human shouts, screams and cries.

They rounded the corner into the yard.

Five wolves stood among the steel boxes. They were larger than the one from the previous day, their shoulders level with Arthur's hip. As large as the ones they had fought in the wilds.

One of them was standing over something, and as Arthur looked closer that something became a body in Council grey. Two more guards lay in the narrow gap between containers. One was face down on the concrete. The other had managed to get his back against the corrugated steel and was sitting upright, but his head had dropped forward and he was still.

There were what looked like ten, maybe twelve, Council soldiers taking positions across the yard.

The largest wolf lifted its head from the body and looked directly at them and then reared up on its hind legs. Its muzzle was dark and wet; it held their gaze and did not flinch.

Arthur brought Breakwater up across his body and set his boots.

The Tidy Label The thesis was simple, and I would have marked an undergraduate down for stating it so badly: follow the cleaner attribution. Athena Skiras belonged to Salamis without ambiguity, without syncretism, without the Aphaia mess. The name was tidy. The site was famous. Herodotus named the sanctuary. The Athena Nike sightline from the Acropolis ran straight at it across the water, and I took that alignment as confirmation because it had the shape of evidence and I wanted it to be.

It was not evidence. It was a coincidence dressed in a good suit.

The verse told me, in language a child could parse, to follow the measure and not the name. I followed the name. I built a three-stage argument for why the cleaner god-claim was the better guide, and at every stage I was doing exactly the thing the riddle warned against.

The Curators used geometry because geometry does not drift. A name gets syncretised, re-attributed, merged with whatever political patron needs a local goddess to wear a different face. Aphaia becomes Athena becomes a confusion that sends you to the wrong island. But phi is phi in the fourth century before Christ and on the afternoon I wasted on Salamis, and the ratio does not care which priesthood swept the floor.

The bitter part is not that I was wrong. Being wrong is the mechanism by which I am occasionally right, and I have made my peace with that transaction. The bitter part is that I had Aegina in my hand from the first line of the verse. The maiden who leapt and fled. Aphaia. The isle of the vanished. I knew the myth, I knew the island, I knew the temple on the pine hill. And I put it down because the label was untidy, and I went looking for a neater one.

I spent twenty years training myself to distrust the tidy answer in every other context. Apparently I kept a private exemption for mythology.
