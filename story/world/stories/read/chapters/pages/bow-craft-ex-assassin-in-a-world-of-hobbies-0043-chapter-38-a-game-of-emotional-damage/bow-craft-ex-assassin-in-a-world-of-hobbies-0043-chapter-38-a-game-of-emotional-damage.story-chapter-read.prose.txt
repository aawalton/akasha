Chapter 38A Game of Emotional Damage

Elsewhere, an angel hovered far above a gorge and a ruined bridge, looking every bit like one except for the silver ponytail. When the winds of the lower troposphere proved too strong, she put on a yellow hard hat, and the winds died down, just as advertised. When she’d first been given form by Her, she’d been told that her reason for being was to watch over a certain lost soul and keep her from straying from her path, but now she had to keep an I-beam from being pushed around by the upper troposphere’s jet streams.

“Crane operator! Hold the descent! Girder team, fly heading one-one-zero! Metro team, descend flight level two-five-oh and get the wind direction!”

It amazed her how the cable lowering it was practically S-shaped. There was nothing more she could from down here, though, at least not until the winds changed, so she ended up thinking about what had happened to that girl again. After the disbandment, she’d never phoned back, and the angel herself wasn’t allowed to initiate any interaction. Just as now and before, she was always just the one standing around, waiting for something to happen before she was allowed to act.

And now, for whatever reason, the team in charge of the twisting girder stopped their work. A few of their members were keeping the girder still while others started waving at her.

Something’s wrong. She could see their mouths moving. Why can’t I—she heard a ping in her head—oh, I left it on mute. She willed forth the telepathy they angels normally used to talk to each other, even from the farthest poles of the three Realms. Hearing thousands of voices at once wasn’t nice, though, hence by Her grace and superior experience in UX, there were options to organize channels into vox rooms and manage timeouts and permissions, and all it took was a mere thought.

One such thought later, the flight wing leader shouted straight into her brain: “Ma’am Grace! Behind you!”

She was annoyed and alarmed at once, but when she spun around, a spaghetti monster-like distortion had opened up into a maw. Before she realized it, its tendrils had already entrapped her, and she was elated—and puzzled; concerned; left wondering.

To those outside, the spaghetti monster ate her, but to her, the constant press of the wind had simply disappeared, and where there had been a hundred meters and the ruins of a bridge below her, there were only ten and a forest floor instead.

She looked down at the child praying on her knees. She took off her hard hat, and released her hair. “Lei-rei,” she said.

***

“Craft, my guy,” Pruned said. He didn’t sound half as amused as a while ago. “You really wanna go there?”

There was a ball in Craft’s throat. His shoulders tensed up, and he couldn’t stop himself from angrily looking at the ground. It was already too late for him to take his words back, and he believed in them too much, besides. He looked up at Pruned. “I’m not doing it any other way.”

Pruned snickered in a way a crackling speaker might. “Listen, bro, we both know you won’t even last a minute in a straight fight”—and Craft was under no illusion that he would—“so lemme give you a deal. How about we play a game of Emotional Damage?“

Huh.

“C’mon.” Pruned inched closer. “It’s really simple, bro.”

…So, he turned out to be a huge, yappy nerd, but here’s the summary:

He was proposing a turn-based card game. At the start of the match, two or more players each held at least ten cards. Each would write down as many of their insecurities and weaknesses on as many cards as possible, and if they used up the starter set of ten cards, they would draw ten more blank cards, but it’s not required to fill up all cards.

Each time you correctly guessed what was written on an opponent’s card, they’d have to discard it. Each time you correctly guessed an opponent’s unwritten weakness or insecurity, the opponent must discard a blank card; if they didn’t have a blank card, they must reveal one of their written weaknesses.

Once you’d run out of cards, you’d lose. If you break down in the middle of the game, you lose. The last man standing or holding back their tears wins.

The secret, though, was if you’d correctly guessed an unwritten weakness, you got to have another free turn—and this could be chained, but only for unwritten weaknesses. Secondly, if you were to reveal one of your written cards, you were allowed to draw one from your opponent’s active deck, and if you’d picked out a written card, you were allowed just one other free pull (no chaining); of course, it would be a complete guess, and if you were to draw a blank card, your opponent would just keep holding onto it, and your own card would have to be discarded.

“So it’s a Johari card game,” Craft said, cupping his chin. It’s more strategic than it looks. Psychologizing one’s opponent was the underlying gameplay, but it wasn’t just psychologizing: one had to know what the opponent didn’t know about themselves. On top of that, strong awareness of one’s own weaknesses, together with brute-force vulnerability, seemed to be another possible strategy. That wasn’t his strong suit, though.

“You look like you’re raring to go, bro,” Pruned said.

“There’s one thing I don’t get.” Craft shrugged. “What’s keeping either of us from just lying?”

***

The process of forming a contract had involved very sparkly legal documents (just four pages in numbered items, don’t worry), but now, neither of them were allowed to lie, whether by commission, omission, or paltering.

And now, Craft sat in front of Pruned like a fortune-teller would a crystal ball. Between them was a slab of rock which served as the playing table. Craft held his own cards up in front of him, while Pruned splayed his out like a command deck—thirty cards floated in front of him in two rows. It didn’t help that a fairy was flying around collecting shiny rocks from the others, and based on the two pouches the little guy was carrying, the odds were 32:1.

Game start. Two fairies worked together to flip a coin, and it landed on the table with the relief of a tree’s crown facing up. Pruned was first.

“You’re unlovable.”

“Fuck.”

Craft flicked one of his cards to the graveyard pile. To be fair, that one was low-hanging fruit. Across him, Pruned was keeping quiet—but damn it, I can tell he’s smirking internally.

It was fine. With “unlovable” out of commission, Craft still had nine cards: “coward,” “afraid to hurt,” and “burden” for written ones, and the rest were unwritten. It was a risky strategy, but against Pruned’s deck, he had to hedge against the vulnerability strategy somehow.

He glanced up from his cards. Pruned still hadn’t so much as shifted. The fact that he didn’t have any facial features left Craft unable to read his emotions.

However, micro-expressions were only a small part of the story. The thick deck and lack of expression meant Pruned was cautious by default, but that, in itself, could also be a smokescreen to cause his opponents to let their guard down, and he was still the same spirit-core who was capable of summoning incredible legalese from thin air, legalese which had zero loopholes. Compressed into four pages—Craft still couldn’t shake off how incredible it had been.

Well, in the end, Craft was faced with a highly intelligent, analytical, and cautious opponent.

“You second guess yourself when you don’t have to,” he said…and Pruned discarded one to the pile. There was a tingle on Craft’s skin as he watched the card float from the deck and onto the discard pile. Telekinesis?

“You’re just as good as I thought, bro.”

Craft chuckled nervously. Being correctly estimated by an opponent was hella terrifying.

“You punish yourself when you fail,” Pruned said.

Craft shook his head. His turn now. “Back at you.”

Pruned discarded one. “I’m surprised, bro. This sort of stuff’s usually a package deal.”

It wasn’t that he didn’t punish himself. He simply did while continuing to act. How could it be a weakness if it didn’t actually drag him down, and how could it be an insecurity if he had long accepted the fact?

On the other hand, he’d figured out Pruned by now. He was someone “still working through it,” just like anyone else, and that easygoing attitude of his was just a cover for whatever was simmering beneath the surface.

“Let’s just say I’m built different,” he said with a smile.

“I see, I see.” Pruned laid down a card that said: it takes ages for me to make up my mind. “Man, I hope I pick something good…”

One by one, a card in Craft’s deck would lift slightly, then slide back again. If Pruned was hoping to get a reaction out of Craft by doing this, he was out of luck; Craft knew how to play poker with a mob boss.

Finally, Pruned picked one out, and he laid down a card that said: I’m a coward to my own emotions. “Classic, but oof.”

Great, he gets another pull. Hopefully, he’d draw a blank, but after another minute, Pruned pulled a card that said: I’m afraid to hurt others through my own failures.

“Bro, I’m taking secondhand damage here.”

Craft took none. These were just things he had long known about himself, after all.

Still, he was down to one written card and six blanks. On one hand, Pruned would burn through cards if he were to go with the vulnerability strategy, but with the number of cards he still had left, it was still possible for him to win by brute force alone.

It was looking grim, but there was another win condition. If Craft were to run out of written cards—thus, he’d be left with only blank cards—and no other player could correctly guess his unwritten weaknesses, then he’d automatically win after the same number of turns passed as he held blank cards.

And no one knew him better than himself; the four written cards he’d started with were the only written cards possible. Pruned would continue trying to guess his unwritten weaknesses, but it would be futile, and after six turns, Craft would win.

“You wonder if you can even follow up on your own goals,” Craft said. Pruned added another one to the discard pile.

“Not bad.” Pruned put down a card that said: I can’t let go of my ambitions. He picked out one of Craft’s cards, but drew a blank. “Really, not bad. Looks like you really get me, huh, bro?”

Craft chuckled. “Do I?” He didn’t think so, himself, but Pruned laughed.

“I ain’t that deep! Sometimes whatchya see is whatchya get, and y’know, bro, this is actually all I’ve got.”

Craft found that hard to believe. Sure, there were people who were lucky enough to be able to live life without the burden of a hundred-layered ego, but someone like Pruned? It was impossible for him not to have ridden an inner rollercoaster at least once.

“You still think you need to achieve something in life,” Craft said, and Pruned lost another card.

“Case in point,” the spirit said, “it’s way too easy to figure me out, ain’t it, bro? It’s like, duh, I’m this kinda guy—but, bro, y’know, I’m only obvious coz I’m not trying to hide anything.” He laid down a card: I never know if my choices are right. “Painful stuff is painful, but it only becomes deep if you try hard to hide it.”

He doesn’t sound like he’s saying that randomly. Was he trying to tease out more intel? As Pruned drew another blank, Craft realized Pruned’s angle of attack—and that he, himself, had realized too late.

“You plan, but don’t act,” Craft said, and he gulped when Pruned didn’t lose a card.

“Oh, yeah, I’m on a name basis with that edgy chick you came with.”

He’s going to do something. He chuckled. “This is the first time I’ve ever heard anyone describe her that way.”

“What’s the deal with you two, anyway?”

Is he trying to use her as leverage against me? A lot of things went through Craft’s mind the moment he’d thought those words. The fact that someone could be used as leverage against him sent a chill up his spine, but it also made him glad to feel such fear for the first time, and something within him seethed that someone would even try to pull that on him—on him, with Lei-rei, of all people.

“I’m not sure what you’re trying to get at,” Craft said. “She’s not part of the game.”

Pruned stayed quiet a split-second too long, and Craft realized he’d revealed too much defensiveness. No matter how subtle he’d been about it, avoidance was avoidance, and dodging a question was the same as admitting a weakness.

“Not sure about that, bro.” Pruned paused again. He’s doing that on purpose. “Coz you don’t want your burdens to mix with hers, right?”

Hesitantly, Craft flicked away the last written card he had. The victory countdown was on, but it didn’t feel like he was winning at all.

“You imagine yourself succeeding, but you’re not willing to fail,” Craft said. Still, Pruned didn’t lose a card, and that’s when Craft realized his estimation of Pruned had been wrong.

The spirit-core wasn’t someone stuck in the past. He wasn’t someone “still working through it,” and he wasn’t just some guy who veiled his problems with a teaspoonful of frat bro flippancy. It wasn’t as if he’d “solved it,” either; he’d admitted to a few counts of weakness already.

Pruned had no qualms looking at his weaknesses, showing them, and throwing them away. What was he other than a veteran in this emotional game? And the fact that he’d read Craft this well—just how much experience did this guy have? Years? Centuries? Lifetimes? This world proved one could live as many lives as they needed to, and Pruned had had all that time to be better and eventually be the best version of himself—to be the endgame Craft was chasing.

Yet, even after becoming the endgame, Pruned still suffered. He sounded as if he had never actually escaped anything, and what he was doing now was just a rough simulation of being ‘better’—that if he continued doing what was ‘right,’ then eventually, things would magically fall into place.

But after lifetimes, all Pruned showed was there was no ‘better,’ only ‘right.’

Craft couldn’t accept that, because if it were true, then he was doomed never to rest. Just what was the point, in that case?

“It’s a little problematic how you think, bro.”

“Assuming you really get me.”

“Not sure I do, but this ain’t the first time I’d had a run-in with that sushi fanatic, y’know—”

“I think I just learned something new.”

“—and it seriously bothers me she hasn’t just broken you guys out of this place yet.”

What? Craft was carefl not to change his expression. “Explain.”

“I was about to lol,” Pruned laid down a card—I can’t stop myself from being stubborn— and he drew another blank from Craft’s deck. “Ah.”

Craft was tempted to throw his deck down on the table. “Do you like dancing around the point?”

Pruned flicked away a card. “Do you like thinking no one could possibly understand you?”

Craft tensed and averted his gaze, and he didn’t know why. He knew he had to let go of a card, but why? There was no real reason for him to do so, yet deep inside, he could feel something hemorrhaging.

“What’s this about Lei-rei?” he pressed. He had to focus on his cheeks to keep them from tensing.

“You ever seen her do that weird un-cooking thing, bro?”

“The one where entropy’s just a suggestion?”

“Yeah! That, that!” He paused. “Oh, you know about entropy? So, you like to think you shouldn’t rely on a god, even if they exist, coz what a man can do with their own strength is what a man should do after all—ain’t that right?”

“How the hell did you make that jump?”

“Put that card down, bro.”

There was that tingle on Craft’s skin again, and he leaned away, dodging Pruned’s telekinesis.

“Wh— You can dodge that?—”

“No, hold on—how’s that even a weakness?”

Pruned paused, considering how to reply. “Coz you’re trying to control things you actually can’t. I bet that hasn’t gone well for ya.”

It hadn’t, and a wince broke his poker face. “I don’t think it ever has,” he muttered, and he didn’t mind if Pruned had heard. It was all in the past, after all. He wouldn’t cry over it now, but it was just so true until now. If it was still true, then how could he say he had changed? Has anything changed?

Am I still turning away from the people trying to care about me?

He let a card slip from his fingers, and it drifted onto the playing table. Why did he have that thought? No, why was he surprised? There was only one person he could turn away from now, and it haunted him how rare she was—how close she was to him—and yet he couldn’t completely turn towards her. She was opening a door for him, yet he still doubted whether that door truly had something good on the other side; only death came the first and last time he’d tried.

But, she was a good person. She deserved good things, and that only made him doubt whether he was any good at all. Until now, he’d made himself out to be a man who could still do better, and who, by his persistence, would eventually become the best version of himself. After all, there were less reasons to walk away from someone who was at least trying, and for now, it seemed to be working.

But, she was smart. One day, she would figure out the full picture of all his cute defects and realize how grotesque he actually was, for if the CAZ still existed in this world, and they ordered him to kill her, he would still do it.

That was why, he would thank Enty every night that the CAZ wasn’t allowed to exist here. With no force to bind him, the outcome of whether someone left him or stayed—all hinged on him.

“On the other hand, that chick’s uncontrollable,” Pruned continued. He leaned closer. “Bro, she’s like, the antithesis of a control freak. I swear, it’s super weird why she hasn’t broken you two out of here…”

Craft shook his head. If that were true, then Lei-rei had her own agenda to keep him here. What would that be other than a betrayal? “That’s impossible. Even a nuke wouldn’t breach the walls of this place.”

“Not sure what a nuke is, bro, but that sounds kinda like a compliment, so it is a compliment.” Pruned slinked back behind the table. “Anyway, she can basically just start cooking and this place would fall apart.”

“That doesn’t make any sense.” Craft cupped his mouth. “Subspaces should have lower entropy than realspace. If Lei-rei can only lower entropy—”

“Yeah, nah. Bro, her cooking doesn’t affect entropy.” Pruned stopped talking, letting Craft come to the conclusion himself.

Time travel. It should be impossible, but—wait, if the black-fire were made of exponents’ worth of tiny black holes… A chill crawled up his spine. Lei-rei’s reverse cooking was nothing short of a controlled burn in the fabric of reality, and that would be more than enough to destroy a subspace from the inside.

Then—then why was she keeping them here?

Had she…betrayed him? For what cause? For what gain? If they had the same thought process, then she would also know that he’d figure it out sooner or later. What, so she just needed to delay for something? For what? They were just getting some damn grass for Nightshade!

His head spun. Nothing made sense. Nothing was right. No matter how much he rubbed his head, this doom-filled throbbing just didn’t want to go away.

“You’re totally incapable of imagining someone having good intentions for you, huh, bro”—Craft flicked out a card—“whoa, I didn’t mean that, but okay.”

“Do you have a Hobby of telling people what they don’t want to hear?” He chuckled. “Seems like you figured me out, anyway. Just fire away and end the game already.”

***

At this point, Pruned knew that Craft only had blank cards left. By one of the exceptional win conditions, he could call it out and immediately win the game, but that was not to Pruned’s creed.

Brotherhood did not mean glossing over these things.
