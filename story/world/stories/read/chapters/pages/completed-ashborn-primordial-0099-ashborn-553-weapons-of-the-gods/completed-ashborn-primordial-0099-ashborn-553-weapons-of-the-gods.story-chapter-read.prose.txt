Maiya arrived on the bridge to find a grinning Saunak waiting for her.

She’d seen that grin before, and it never meant anything good.

Nearly two dozen Asura were in the Vimana’s command center, eyeing the demon like Shrikes. They’d been given orders by Vir to keep an eye on Saunak, and as was usual for Asuras, they went above and beyond.

Maiya almost felt sorry for Saunak. Almost.

The projected display in the center of the room showed the rogue Vimana ahead of them, its weapon emplacements still not repaired as it ambled back to Matali.

Unfortunately for them, their voyage was about to be cut permanently short.

“Are we ready?” Maiya asked.

“Anytime you like!” Saunak beamed, practically salivating at the opportunity.

“Can’t believe they haven’t surrendered yet,” Maiya muttered. “You’re sure they received our hails?”

Without Vir and Siya to unlock the other Vimana, Maiya was hoping she’d at least be able to gain the use of one more. It would be a crying shame to destroy it.

“Absolutely,” came Saunak’s reply. “They’re ignoring us. Grakking fools want to die. I say we oblige! So? Can I blow them out of the sky?”

“Do it,” Maiya replied, cringing at the sheer waste.

Saunak’s hands moved across the projection table without another word and the Vimana’s primary weapon came online with a hum Maiya felt through the floor. The hum grew louder and louder, amassing gods knew how much prana.

Then it fired.

A column of visible, ultra-dense Ash Prana crossed the distance between the two vessels in less time than it took to blink, penetrating the rogue Vimana’s shielding in a flash of light so bright it whited out the projection.

When it cleared, the Vimana was no longer floating lazily through the sky. The entire top of the structure had been gouged through, vaporizing its bridge and killing its crew well before their minds had any chance to register they were even under attack.

Secondary explosions wracked the superstructure in a cascade and Maiya watched with great sadness as the marvelous contraption came apart mid-air.

What had been a Vimana only moments prior was now a vast cloud of burning debris falling toward an empty field.

She realized belatedly that she might be labeled by the history books as the ‘Destroyer of Vimanas’. Cursed as the one who recklessly erased a piece of history.

But the deed was done, and Maiya did not have the time to negotiate with the Matali general with everything else going on. At the very least, it was one less crisis for her to worry about, and there certainly was no shortage of those.

The wreckage struck the ground ponderously, as though it refused to accept its own demise. The larger chunks made craters that sent soil and rocks flying high into the sky, while the the burning fragments ignited the dry vegetation almost immediately.

“Deploy the suppression airships,” Maiya commanded.

“Already underway, Rajni,” an Asura replied. “We’ve airships en route with Asura proficient in Water arts. We’ll have those fires doused before long.”

Maiya didn’t doubt it. She’d yet to see the Asura fail to complete a mission with anything less than total perfection.

She watched the projection for another moment before turning on her heel.

“Leaving so soon?” Saunak asked. “Why not take a moment to celebrate this momentous occasion?”

“I’ll celebrate when I’ve solved all the crises in the realm,” she said, entering the lift and flipping its lever.

“So, never, then,” Saunak said with a wry smile as the doors slid shut in front of her.

“Pretty much,” she said with a taut smile of her own.

Not even an hour into the Tribunal’s emergency meeting, and Disanna knew she had lost.

Though Lord Reth was trying to hide his satisfaction, he was failing miserably at it, and Zora was as hunched over as always, her pale ghael skin almost luminous under the orb light. Orbs, here in Balindam’s upper city. Who would have guessed such a thing possible mere days ago?

It was one of the few arguments she’d won as of late. Reth and Zora had argued to keep their great Artifact active, sucking prana from the region as they always had even in light of the bounty that now blessed their lands.

She’d argued that with all this energy, the days of needing the Artifact to dissuade their neighbors from attacking were long gone. That in the new age Imperatrix Ira heralded, such precautions had been unwarranted in the first place, well before the Akh Nara reunified the realms and banished the blight of the Ash.

She’d prevailed, and now all citizens of the Pagan Order benefited from that prana, not just the demons they sequestered in the Undercity.

It had all been going so well… and then this had to happen.

It all started when she received Maiya’s report of the Rajas’ meeting. Of Camar Gadin and the Vimana’s attack. Of the miraculous mass resurrection the Akh Nara had worked. She still didn’t know if she believed half of it, but it hardly mattered.

Others believe it. The masses believed it, and that was enough.

It was why she’d hailed Maiya on the Tribunal’s emergency Communications orb right after without informing the others.

She saw an opportunity to broker an agreement, and she’d seized it, summarizing the details to Tribunal after.

As per their agreement, the Order would take a lead role in facilitating cooperation across the new realm in light of the recent Vimana attack.

“You did this...” Zora rasped. “Without consulting us? Without a vote? You struck a bargain with the red-haired devil. You subverted the Order’s purpose.”

“On the contrary, I positioned us as the only body trusted by both sides to manage the integration,” Disanna bristled. “That is what we have been working toward for the past five years, is it not? Do you not see the opportunity here?”

“The Akh Nara’s vision has never been our own,” Zora said. “If the rumors are true, he no longer holds sway over us.”

Reth lifted his head. “He spoke for every human who supports demon integration. None of that has been undone by Camar Gadin.”

“Three hundred and seventeen demons died in that city, Reth,” Zora said. “A Matali general fired the weapon of the Gods at demons with no repercussions whatsoever. The dead revived, yes, but that does not make the humans innocent.”

“They did die,” Reth said quietly. “I do not make light of that.”

“Then we must act.”

“Whatever response we consider must be weighed against everything we’ve slogged to build,” Disanna said. “The Order has spent five years earning the trust of demon Rajas and human courts alike. We destroy all of that if we retaliate. We’ll become an enemy to the realm overnight.”

“Wrong,” Zora said. “We become the enemy of humans. Humans who hate us. Our bond with demonkind will only grow. The threat that held us in line is incapacitated. Yet you propose we cower as if he were not.”

“I propose we behave as if his return is a possibility,” Disanna said, losing her patience.

“Irrelevant, once we act. Demons will be forced to fight. And demons will win.”

“You are proposing all-out war, Zora,” Reth said, looking more than a little concerned.

Zora’s eyes turned slowly toward him.

“No, Reth. I propose the Pagan Order respond to attempted genocide. I propose that Matali pay. I propose we show the realm that demons can defend themselves without the Akh Nara.”

“And if Matali responds in kind?” Reth asked.

Zora shrugged, which was an awkward, ungainly motion on a ghael. “Then it is war. I tire of humans pretending to put up with demons. It is time we stake our place in the new realm.”

Disanna squeezed her eyes shut. She’d known, of course, that the only thing holding the radicals of the Pagan Order in check had been the Akh Nara’s overwhelming power.

There was no doubt he would return. Zora knew that as well as any. But if they acted now… if they sowed the seeds of war before he did, not even he might stop it.

Their decision here today would determine the Fate of the realm for years to come, and Disanna could not, in good conscience, be a part of that.

She opened her eyes at last. “I will not vote for war. I am, however, not deaf to the wants of our people. If we must absolutely mount a response, then I will only support actions that are measured and limited. If Matali is to be held responsible, our actions must be aimed at the Matali military specifically.”

“We strike their grain reserves. Caravans. Trade infrastructure,” Zora rasped.

“What? Why?” Disanna blurted. “What would such a misguided attack do but provoke them?”

“The alternative is war,” Zora said. “Which you are so keen to avoid.”

Their argument was put on hold when the audience chamber door suddenly opened and a red demon walked through.

Disanna recognized him immediately. Karav had been a senior officer serving in the Pagan Order’s intelligence division for years, and one of their foremost hardliners.

He was followed by a smaller figure carrying a stack of intelligence documents.

“Tribunal, forgive the interruption. We have updated intelligence on Matali and Sai.”

Reth’s eyes narrowed. “Sai? Why are we receiving intelligence on Sai?”

“Because, my lord, our analysts have examined events in the broader human realm, and what we see is most concerning,” Karav said, motioning for his assistant to set the documents on the table before them.

“The Saian coastal city of Kaia has seen three separate attacks on demon merchants just in the past day. And yet, none of the perpetrators have been arrested. None of the local authorities have made any move to protect demon residents, and King Riyan Savar has entirely ignored the problem.”

Disanna looked over at Zora, a bitter expression on her face. How convenient for this intelligence to arrive at exactly the moment the ghael could most benefit from it.

“Our strong sentiment,” Karav continued, “is that Matali alone is an inadequate target. The Pagan Order has always protected demons throughout the realm, and now is no different. Saian failure to protect demon civilians constitutes its own form of complicity. A coordinated response, targeting both, signals that no human territory is exempt from accountability.”

Reth leaned forward to pick up the stack of papers. “You are proposing striking both nations?” he asked, flipping through it.

“I am merely suggesting the Tribunal consider whether such a strike is warranted. The decision is, of course, yours to make.”

Indeed it was, in theory. In reality, the decision had already been made.

With this much evidence, it would be suicide not to act. Disanna might very well find herself mobbed in the streets of Balindam should news get out. And it surely would. Zora would not let slip such a golden opportunity to shore up her political power.

It wasn’t the first time Disanna had seen this sort of tactic, after all. She had no doubt that Karav's intelligence was correct, if cherrypicked. The timing of its arrival, however, and the framing of its recommendations made it abundantly clear that Karav had been preparing this argument well before Camar Gadin. Someone in the Order had been ready, and it didn’t take much to guess who. Demon supremacists were everywhere among their ranks. Always itching for the opportunity to lash out at humans in any way they could.

“What you are suggesting is slaughtering innocent civilians,” Disanna said. “Many men, women, and children will die.”

“I assure you, Lady Disanna, I merely wish to produce the political effect we desire,” Karav replied, smiling diplomatically. “Nothing more and nothing less.”

“Cities, in other words,” Disanna replied dryly.

“In Matali, we recommend secondary urban centers where any government response would be slow. In Sai, Kaia is a good contender. Despite being a coastal trade hub, it lacks any significant military presence.”

“Unbelievable! Are you listening to yourself?” Disanna cried. “‘Secondary urban centers.’ ‘Good contenders.’ You are condemning hundreds to die, Karav. Possibly more. Innocent people who had nothing to do with any of this. Who might very well be demonic sympathizers! All this will do is ensure that any humans who might’ve sided with us or sat on the sidelines will take up arms against demons. It will taint the reputation we’ve slogged so hard to build.”

“Need I remind you that was the Akh Nara’s wish, not our own,” Karav replied bitterly. “And I assure you, my goals are not nearly so sinister. I am merely talking about sending a message. The consequences must be visible, and, while unfortunate, that visibility may well require casualties. I will not pretend otherwise, and I do not deny the risk to our reputation. However, I argue that letting humans trample over demons with no consequence is far worse. As many demonic sympathizers among their ranks as there are, I assure you there are many more who wish nothing more than to see every last one of us burned to ash.”

Disanna felt like she’d just swallowed ash. The most infuriating part was that he was right. A message did need to be sent. Just not like this…

“Disanna,” Zora rasped. “You have spent five years building trust with these people. What did they do the moment they had a chance? They assaulted us.”

“They aimed a Vimana at Bairan children,” Karav continued. “Our trust did not stop them. Our integration did not stop them, and the Akh Nara himself could not stop them without first allowing so many demons to die. It was only a miracle that saved those poor souls..”

Disanna could only grind her teeth in frustration. They all knew that those were not the actions of ‘humankind’ as a whole, nor even the actions of the Matali government. It was a rogue human… but such arguments were useless against radicals like them.

Reth cleared his throat. “Swear to me that you will do everything within your power to minimize civilian casualties.”

Karav placed his palm upon his heart. “You have my word, my lord,” he said, bowing deeply.

“Then you have my vote,” Reth replied. “I agree that we must send a message, and soon.”

Disanna gripped the handle of her armchair so hard it splintered under her strength.

What a farce! Karav could promise anything he liked, and when his soldiers stormed those cities and torched their buildings, he would simply apologize and sow some tall tale of how it was a tragic and unavoidable turn of Fate, ensuring he appeared deeply distraught.

“I’m disappointed in you, Reth,” Disanna seethed. “If the human among us does not stand up for his people, then who will?”

“Please,” Reth replied. “We both know the sorts of feelings I harbor for my kind. I would not shed a single tear were demons to wipe humanity off the map. The world would be better for it.”

Disanna sighed. She could vote no. The outcome wouldn’t matter, now that two of the three had voted in favor. It would be noted that she was the lone vote against it, however. Her approval would decline, and she would have to fight for some other win to regain that favor.

Or she could lobby for conditions to be attached. To lessen this evil as much as she could, before voting in favor.

A unified council vote was always more appealing, after all. It showed unity of leadership, and unity was strength.

She’d hate herself for it, but she knew what she had to do. Luckily, Disanna couldn’t care less about endangering her position, and she would gladly sell her heart if it meant minimizing casualties.

“Matali alone,” she said, meeting Zora’s stare. “We attack only Matali, not Sai, and only military installations. If you agree to this, you’ll have my vote.”

Zora’s eyes did not leave her face. “I find this agreeable, though we will strike targets of opportunity. Military or otherwise.”

“Then simply find an appropriately valuable military target,” Disanna said. “Karav is a capable intelligence officer. I’m sure he can find a suitable location.”

Both her and Zora looked to Karav, and despite the aura of supreme confidence she projected, internally, she was entirely unsure of her gamble.

“Striking military targets would only provoke a stronger response, I’m afraid,” Karav replied, shaking his head. “And I would rather not strike at all if we cannot strike at both. Our message needs to be sent, loud and clear. Nobody is safe. Not just the perpetrators.”

Disanna tasted blood in her mouth. “If that is the case, then I shall not endorse this, nor any strike that endangers civilians. I will not endorse a war that demonkind will pay for in blood for generations. My vote is no.”

“Then it’s settled,” Reth announced. “With two in favor and one against, the Tribunal approves this operation against Sai and Matali. Go, Karav, with our blessing, and may all the Gods be with us.”

“Thank you, my lords,” Karav replied, bowing deeply. “I shall not let you down.”

Disanna pushed her chair back, utterly disgusted with her peers. “This is the end of what we have built. I want it noted that I opposed this action. When the war comes, as it will, I will be remembered as the only one who tried to stop it. I will not pretend to support what you are doing.”

“Noted,” was Zora’s only reply.

Karav cleared his throat, bowing deeply. “With the Tribunal’s permission, I will begin coordinating with our regional commanders. I will, of course, require authorization to deploy assets from the Aindri and the Chitran rebel networks. Some of these assets are not formally Pagan Order, but they have indicated a willingness to participate.”

Disanna spun to face him. “The Aindri? What do they have to do with this?”

“Raja Jagath has been ill-disposed toward these integration efforts for some time. While he has not, as far as I am aware, sanctioned an attack, at least thus far, he has not opposed it either. His personal forces are at our disposal.”

So that was where Jagath had gone, Disanna thought. Maiya’s report had mentioned the Raja had been unresponsive.

It seemed that the Rajas had a traitor in their midst.

I must inform Maiya, she thought, being careful to mask her shock at this turn of events. She only hoped humanity’s patron saint received the information early enough to counter it.

“Permission granted,” Zora said.

Karav bowed and left, his assistant hurriedly scooping up the stack of papers before following him out.

“I know how you feel, Disanna,” Reth said after a moment of silence.

“Like a failure?” Disanna scoffed.

Reth shook his head. “There has never been a moment in the past five thousand years when demonkind and humanity were going to coexist. There were only delusions. The Akh Nara’s reunification effort was impressive, no doubt, but it went against the natural order of things. What we are doing tonight is simply letting nature’s balance play out. You will see this clearly when the morning comes.”

“Demons will prevail,” Zora proclaimed before sinking into the shadows. A new trick, that. She’d been welcomed into the Iksana with open arms, and while they’d inscribed Dance of the Shadow Demon upon her years ago, it was only recently that she had mastered the ability.

Reth took his leave as well, leaving Disanna to sit alone at the table. Not half a day had passed since she had promised Maiya that the Pagan Order would facilitate cooperation across the realm.

There was no other choice now.

She’d have to go behind the Tribunal’s back and inform her that they were about to send strike teams into Matali and Sai. To warn her that they intended to slaughter civilians, likely igniting a war none of them would be able to prevent.

Disanna remained at the table until the braziers burned down, then rose and walked to her quarters to initiate a call she so very much wished she did not have to make.

Only, she would never be allowed the opportunity.

Disanna almost laughed at the sight of the two operatives who barred her path.

“And?” she asked. “What is the meaning of this?”

“My apologies, Lady Disanna. By the order of Lord Reth and Lord Zora, you are to be detained until the operation is complete.”

Disanna bit down so hard she drew blood.

Fate, it seemed, intended to deny her even this small victory.

She raised her arms in defeat. “Lead the way, then.”
