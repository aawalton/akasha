“What do you think?”

Anne asked the question without looking up from the note scroll spread across the counter.

Moruh, who had been lounging there with the careless dignity only a well-fed familiar could maintain, rolled onto his side, yawned, and dragged himself closer. He knew perfectly well that his mistress rarely wanted his real opinion in such moments, but failing to answer would only invite her irritation.

For a bookmancer, choosing new spells for the grimoire was no small matter. The grimoire was tied to the soul, and every new blank page gained with spiritual growth was both an opportunity and a burden. One chapter for the essential functions of the craft, one for self-defense, half reserved for emergencies, and the rest distributed carefully according to one’s long-term path. That had been the rule passed down by her grandfather and master, and in Anne’s experience, it was a sensible one.

As far as she knew, an ordinary archivist might reach one hundred and twenty pages in a lifetime. Her grandfather, who had lived through both the height and decline of the craft, had reached two hundred and ten, placing himself as the fourth strongest bookmancer in their line’s recorded history. Anne, by contrast, had possessed only thirty-two pages before opening the bookstore, just three more than when she finished her apprenticeship.

That slow growth had not worried her. Real progress was expected only after a bookmancer established a proper domain and began practicing the craft in truth.

Now she had proof.

Three weeks. Three customers. And six new pages.

Her current total had jumped so suddenly that the old theory no longer felt theoretical at all. The bookstore was working. The domain too. The craft itself was answering her efforts. Which, naturally, brought her straight into a more immediate problem.

Six blank pages, and an infinity of possible ways to waste them.

“Mistress,” Moruh said, straightening his hat with exaggerated seriousness, “being practical is always the right choice. I would never dare comment on your sacred craft, of course, but in the matter of your personal needs, I can point out that food, cleaning, and organization require a great deal of time and effort. Anything that eases those burdens might be considered worthy.”

“Is that so?” Anne replied absently.

The answer was perfunctory. She had never intended to hand such a decision to anyone else. Even so, something in his words caught at her mind and stayed there. Not the whole response, of course. Moruh’s speeches tended to be longer than their value justified. But three words remained.

Food. Cleaning. And organization.

She leaned back slightly and let the implications settle.

Housekeeping was mundane work, and her grandfather would likely have been appalled by the direction of her thoughts. He had despised the squandering of grimoire pages on what he considered lesser conveniences. But Anne was not her grandfather, and she had no intention of walking the exact same road just because it had once been a magnificent path.

Housekeeping might be mundane, but it was not meaningless.

Every hour spent cooking, cleaning, carrying, sorting, repairing, and keeping the place in order was an hour stolen from research, customers, and growth. In the long run, that theft would accumulate. Her success depended not only on preserving knowledge, but on making efficient use of her own time.

A rare nostalgic smile touched her lips.

He would hate this idea, she thought. Which made it all the more satisfying.

With that in mind, Anne pulled a fresh note scroll toward herself and began listing possibilities, requirements, and useful references. Ten minutes later, three titles had been circled.

The House Witch.

Homunculus Codex.

Summons.

Moruh, watching from the side, began to sweat.

As always, his mistress had taken the simplest possible suggestion and transformed it into the most complicated path available. When he had mentioned food, cleaning, and organization, he had imagined a spell or two. Something for washing floors, perhaps. Maybe one to knead dough or carry dishes.

He should have known better.

Anne had never been content with workable solutions if there was a more perfect one hidden behind a week of excessive research and a dangerous amount of creativity.

That was simply how she existed.

By the time she returned with the books, she had already begun explaining her reasoning aloud, partly to organize it for herself and partly because Moruh, despite his frequent uselessness, was still the closest thing she had to a second mind.

The first volume, The House Witch, was a biography of the mage Negil Housemaster, written by a bookmancer from an earlier age. Negil had founded housemancy, a practical magical discipline dedicated to every possible household chore. The path had never become truly popular, and by modern times it had grown almost as rare as bookmancy itself.

The second, Homunculus Codex, was less friendly reading. It gathered some of the most successful rituals ever used to create autonomous artificial beings. The practice had been banned in many places throughout history, sometimes for sacrilege, sometimes for danger, and sometimes for reasons that shifted depending on who held power. In the present age, only the Lyn Theocracy still fully prohibited it, though distrust remained common everywhere else.

The last book, Summons, was not a normal book at all.

It was an enthon.

To retrieve it, Anne had to unseal the golden-wooden chest where she kept her enthons stored. The contents of that chest were among the most precious parts of her collection. Enthons were not texts meant for study and rereading, but books containing one-use magical drawings, arrays, and ritual forms. Once expended, their value vanished. That made every page within them costly in a more painful way than coin alone could measure.

After checking the contents carefully, she selected one bound in pale ancient wood and resealed the chest.

Review came first. Then comparison, rejection, and more reading.

The more she studied the available rituals, the less satisfied she became. The codex offered excellent work, but not what she wanted. The biography was useful, but too limited in direct application. The enthon gave her a piece of what she needed, though only as part of a greater structure.

So she did what she always did when existing knowledge proved insufficient.

She began combining it.

The work consumed a week.

A full week of notes, diagrams, corrections, discarded drafts, five additional reference books, and more concentration than any sensible person would waste on domestic assistance. Yet the result justified every hour. Even Moruh, who had begun the process deeply worried and ended it even more worried for entirely different reasons, could not deny that the design was brilliant.

No, more than brilliant. It was audacious.

The thing Anne designed could barely be called a mere spell arrangement. It was closer to an artifact framework recorded directly into the grimoire, and it would require all six new pages.

The first two pages would shape the body.

Since the creation’s work would center on household tasks, Anne chose woodmancy as the most suitable physical basis. It would be vulnerable to fire, yes, but she had no intention of letting it near ovens or open flame if that could be helped. One page would define and hold the wooden structure. Another would grant movement and proper articulation.

The next two pages would be devoted to the summoned spirit.

One to bind it.

One to sustain and empower it.

Anne selected a Spring spirit for the role. Its bright nature, affinity for water and plants, and clean, refreshing energy made it ideal for the kind of household companion she envisioned. Better still, by tying its power core to the bookstore’s domain circle, she could prevent future energy shortage problems and avoid many of the limitations that plagued more traditional constructs.

The final two pages would establish control and grant a minor dual affinity over water and wind.

The so-called control was, in truth, Anne’s proudest compromise. Normal command structures over summoned beings degraded over time, becoming less efficient, less precise, and less trustworthy. Her workaround would not eliminate that issue entirely, but it would stabilize and extend it far beyond what most would expect.

To improve the body further, Anne carved symbols of power into each wooden piece before assembly. At the core, she placed a young soul tea seedling as the chosen vessel for the Spring spirit.

Initially, the result looked underwhelming.

A crude child-sized wooden doll without a face stood in the center of the working space while the ritual components waited around it. Moruh had tried not to show his disappointment, but he failed.

Then Anne completed the binding.

The moment the spirit settled into the body, the whole thing changed.

Vines burst gently from the core and wound themselves over the carved wood like living ornament. Fine tendrils wrapped the limbs, softening the crude lines without hiding them. Small flowers bloomed along the shoulders and crown, and the whole creation took on a fresh, bright presence that made the room feel cleaner simply by standing inside.

Anne stared at it with open delight.

The result had exceeded even her expectations.

What stood before her was no longer a wooden helper animated by a clever construction. It felt... alive was too strong a word, perhaps, yet not entirely wrong either. It had presence. A pleasant one. A small thing of wood, vine, bloom, and borrowed spring.

A satisfied smile spread across her lips.

Those six pages had been spent magnificently.

“Mistress is truly...” Moruh said, eyes wide with reverence and disbelief, “the mistress.”
