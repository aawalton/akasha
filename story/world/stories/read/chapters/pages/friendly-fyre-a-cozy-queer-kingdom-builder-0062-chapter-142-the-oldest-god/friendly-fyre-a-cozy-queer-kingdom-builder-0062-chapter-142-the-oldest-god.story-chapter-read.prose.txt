“Lisari!” I hurry over to her. She extends her hands in greeting, and I clasp them. “I’m so glad to see you’re alright. I was worried when you left so abruptly.”

“Pleasure to see you, dear.” Lisari lets go, adjusting her glasses with a smile. “Apologies for my untimely departure. You see, I had just received a broken nose.”

“Oh,” I quickly say. “Of course. I didn’t mean to imply—”

She laughs. “No no, don’t apologize. It was quite fun. And painful! I haven’t had an injury like that in centuries!”

“I, um. Good?” I suggest.

“Yes, quite good indeed! Now come.” She takes my arm. “Walk with me. I’m in desperate need of intelligent conversation.”

Her grip is gentle—not as if she’s trying to be careful with me, but as if she actually might be that frail. I think back to the time I was cornered by her and Blair in the throne room and how helpless I felt. I’m not sure if it’s the protection of Fyreneth’s spell, or simply the fact that I know Lisari better now, but in this moment I feel entirely at ease in her company.

“I won’t be able to stay long,” she tells me as we stroll through the streets. The fallout from the fight with the gods has already been cleaned up, no rubble or stains to give any indication of a conflict—it helps to have a Dungeon Core that likes to eat any spare rubble. “This city is no longer good for my health.”

My grip on her arm tightens, recalling what Blair had said about the risk to her remnant. “Are you in danger?”

“Not immediately.” She says it as casually as remarking about the weather. “But it seems you do understand the danger my being here now poses. My refiner is much older than most, but I have my own tricks to keep it subdued for a time. Or I would not have made it to this day and age in the first place!” She cackles.

I don’t find the topic nearly so entertaining. “Is this the last time I’ll see you?”

“Why?” she asks. “Are you trying to get rid of me?”

“No!” I object. “I only—”

She pats my arm with a laugh. “You’re far too easy, child. No, I hope this won’t be my last visit, though they will need to be far more infrequent than before.” That, I’m sure, will make Mirzayael happy. “Of course, you’re always welcome to come visit me outside the city, though the danger we’ll be in will be reversed.”

Even so, I do plan to leave the Fortress eventually. When I can find a way to do so safely. Perhaps one day, if I can find a way to expand Fyreneth’s spell beyond the Drifting Isles.

“How are things unfolding in the Heavens?” I ask.

She brightens. “Thank you for asking. It’s going marvelously! Well, for me at any rate. Everyone is in quite a tizzy. Many gods have soured on the notion of targeting Travelers and Fyrethians, and even the gods on Lorata’s side are reluctant to start a war, given your ability to strip them of their immortality. Many that forgot the fear of death were quite starkly reminded. To say the least, Lorata is upset at the division you’ve caused, but there isn’t terribly much she can do about it. Only time will tell, but the tides seem to be shifting in favor of ‘pretend this particular thorn in our side doesn’t exist and hope it goes away on its own.’”

I suppose this was the best outcome I could have hoped for, given the circumstances. “Would an olive branch help? We have two gods in our infirmary being tended to. If you brought them back to the Heavens, do you think that might score a point in our favor?”

Lisari laughs. “Always full of surprises. Which gods do you have?”

“Kero and Relona.”

Lisari hums, rubbing her chin. “Kero tends to keep to himself. Hard to say what his return would do. Relona, however, is liked by many. If you can win her over, and she vouches for you, that will certainly help tilt the scales in your favor.” She waggles a finger at me. “Tilt the scale, note. I can guarantee nothing.”

“Of course,” I agree with a small sigh. “I understand. I just wish there was more I could do.”

Lisari turns her face toward the sun, letting out a content sigh of her own. “Don’t be so hard on yourself. You’ve achieved more in the last year than I have in the last several hundred. Why, if I’d known Fyreneth had such spells buried in her fortress, I would have activated them centuries ago! I’m just glad someone finally dug them up.”

I muse on this as we reach the staircase leading up to the first floor of the palace. Despite Lisari’s declaration of her frailness, she seems happy to continue the trek.

“Did you ever meet Fyreneth?” I ask.

“To my extreme regret, I did not,” Lisari says quietly. “Our timelines do overlap. Unfortunately, I didn’t have the chance to learn much about her until she was no longer with us. A pity. I suspect she was a genius. It’s a shame she is remembered for everything except the revolutionary spell networks she designed.”

“Her narrative is not set in stone,” I say. “Goodness knows my world has its own share of revisionist history. Now that Fyrethians are back in the world, we can begin to spread the word. We can show everyone who our founder truly was.”

She pats my arm. “I’d like that.”

We reach one of the pavilions that is alternately Ollie’s nap space or the children’s playground. The kids have currently claimed it, playing another one of their gravity-defying games with cloudstone. A breeze tickles past me and ruffles the hair and clothes of the children running about the square.

“I think my champions would like this place,” Lisari muses.

The remark catches me off guard. “Your champions?”

It abruptly strikes me as odd that I’ve never met her champions before now. In fact, I don’t think she’s ever even mentioned them. During our research we found she did have one named champion—Piria, a dracid—but I hadn’t read anything about a second champion. Did she take a new one when all the other gods were holding tournaments?

“Indeed,” Lisari says, letting go of my arm to go lean against a pillar. She folds her arms casually, but I get the feeling it might not entirely be for show. “They’re opposites in many respects, but both would enjoy a home such as this. Something peaceful. Perhaps I’ll send them your way, one day.”

“I’d love to meet them,” I say truthfully. What sort of people would Lisari take as champions?

Lisari chuckles. “You’d love to meet anyone!”

“Not true,” I object, smiling slyly myself. “I think I’m quite content never meeting Lorata again.”

Lisari cackles, and the sound turns abruptly into a coughing fit. She claws at the robes above her chest, but before I can lift a hand to help, she gets her coughing under control. “Ah. I suppose I should be taking my leave before long.”

I frown in worry, hovering helplessly over her. “I wish you could stay longer.”

She smiles sadly. “So do I, dear. But all things must come to an end. Do not fret, however.” She sweeps her hand around the city. “The end of one thing is the beginning of another.”

I think about my transition from Earth to Lusio. From human to harpy. Caves to clouds. She’s right; for how frightening and strange every new beginning felt, they always brought me to a better place.

“You know,” I tell Lisari, “I don’t think you’re nearly as chaotic as you pretend to be.”

She puts a hand over her heart. “I’m wounded! I have an image to uphold, thank you very much.”

“Why do you want the Heavens to fall?” I press her. “The truth, this time.”

“I always tell the truth,” she says with a grin. “Though rarely all at once.”

I fold my arms. “You’re dodging the question.”

“What? My other explanations haven’t been enough?” she continues to tease. “It’s not enough for you to accept that Lorata has been deceiving mortals for centuries, and I’d like to put a stop to it? You can’t believe that stagnation is boring and I’m eager to usher in a new age and hierarchy?”

“Lisari.”

She chuckles once more, leaning her head back against the pillar. For a moment she doesn’t say anything, and the sounds of children playing wash over us.

“No one is meant to live this long.” She says it so quietly, the breeze almost carries her words away. “To lose everyone you’ve ever known. Anyone you’ve ever cared for. It’s lonely, being a god.”

A lump settles in my throat, summoning memories from Earth.

“Took me much too long to learn that,” she says. “I’ve spent a lot longer in the mortal realm than most. The other gods have had a taste of what awaits them, but they don’t understand. Not really.”

I swallow down the painful knot of sympathy. “I’m sorry.”

She shakes her head. “Don’t be. You’re the first to give me hope in a long while. When a remnant breaks free, gods don’t die peacefully. But maybe in this city, under your guidance, we can start to pave the way toward a gentler future.”

I laugh quietly. “That’s a lot of pressure to put on one person.”

“Well, then.” She turns her head to the side, and seconds later Ollie flies by, spiraling through the air. “It’s a good thing you’re not doing it alone.”

[Two months later]

“MOM, MUM, HURRY UP!”

“We’re coming,” I assure Ollie as I sort the scrolls and charcoal in my bag. “Just a minute more.”

Mirzayael is waiting for me at the door. “Fyre, you’ve already double and triple checked. If you forget a piece of chalk we can always send for it.”

“Oh, yes. Chalk!” I ask the Dungeon Core to deposit a rod of the material into my hand, which it happily retches up. “Good thinking!”

She snorts. “You could have the Dungeon Core do that anywhere.”

“Yes but it’s the principle of the thing.” I shoulder the bag on and hurry to meet her at our chamber doors. “I haven’t gotten to do this since grad school!”

Mirzayael shakes her head with a faint smile. “At least one of us is excited for more drudgery.”

“It’s not drudgery!” I place a hand over my chest in mock offense. “Education is the most noble of pursuits.”

“I’ve yet to be convinced.”

“Then let me convince you.” I crook my finger for Mirzayael to lean down, as if to whisper something in her ear. She obliges, and I instead take the opportunity to give her a quick kiss.

Mirzayael hums thoughtfully. “You make a strong case.”

I laugh as I open the door, pulling Mirzayael along. For all her objections, I think she’s relieved to take a day off ruling as well. The Council will handle things in our absence; after all, it’s only one day a week.

Ollie is waiting outside for us in a pavilion, his tail swishing impatiently as we join him. “FINALLY! WE’RE GOING TO BE LATE!”

“Inside voices,” I remind Ollie, reaching up to tap his translator. “And what’s the charge on Dizzi’s spell?”

“I DUNNO,” Ollie says, looking down at his translator. Then he seems to register what I just said. “I dunno. I can’t use Echo to tell me anymore.”

“No, but you can use your magic,” I remind him. “Just like everyone else. Remember, focus on it and try to imagine it like a cup full of water.”

Ollie grumbles, but he closes his eyes, tapping a claw to the second amulet he’s wearing—this one is green instead of his blue translator. (Dizzi let him pick the color.)

“It feels mostly full,” he reports, opening his eyes. “I think.”

“Good!” I say. “That should be plenty to last the day. Want to give it a try?”

He wrinkles his muzzle. “I like being big.”

I shrug. “That’s fine. You can stay outside if you like. If you don’t want to sit by Meritis—”

“NO WAIT, WAIT! I mean, wait!” He taps a claw to the emerald, and it illuminates with the glow of his magic. It takes a moment for the change to become noticeable, but gradually Ollie begins to shrink.

This time, he stops when he’s eye-level with me. Still a sizable dragon, it’s much more practical than the cat-size he’d been before. Dizzi incorporated a bit of Kanin’s anti-gravity magic as well, so Ollie doesn’t crush the ground he walks on.

Ollie giggles, looking himself over. “It feels weird to be so small.” As if he’s not still the size (and weight) of a car.

A flurry of blue and green feathers greets us as Meritis lands nearby.

The boy grins at the sight of Ollie. “I bet I can beat you in a race while you’re like this.”

“No fair!” Ollie objects. “Also, I’ll definitely still win.”

“Prove it.” Meritis crouches, spreading his wings.

“Last one there is a rotten egg!” Ollie says, bolting into the sky. The ground cracks where he pushes off.

“Hey! Cheater!” Meritis flies after him.

I chuckle, prompting the Dungeon Core to repair the cratered ground. Dizzi still has a couple bugs to work out in that anti-gravity spell, it seems.

“That kid is a dangerous influence,” Mirzayael says as we follow after them at a much more leisurely rate. We start down a set of the palace’s steps.

“Which one?” I ask.

“Both.”

We can hear the room before we see it; a cacophony of voices all talking over one another, from the lower tones of adults to the shrieks and laughs of children. It brings back memories of school, when everyone was chatting before the bell rang and the teacher started class. I’d mostly spent that time tucked into a corner with a book.

The amphitheater is fairly small, as far as amphitheaters go, and sunk into the layers of the city like progressive steps downward. At the bottom is where the teachers have assembled, while each layer is occupied with students of various ages and species, clustered together in excited chatter. There are perhaps a hundred people all told, and a significant portion of them are Travelers. The rest are made up of various visiting scholars and adult Fyrethians, though no small part is also composed of Fyrethian youth. They were the original audience, after all, before we decided to widen the scope of this venture.

Dizzi’s eagle-eyes catch sight of us first, and she waves frantically until I (and Mirzayael, after I elbow her) wave back and descend to greet her. Familiar faces such as Gardi, Blair, and Captain Marlow are also present. A more recent addition is Attiru, the cambion map-maker who led us to the Drifting Isles in the first place. I was especially pleased they replied to my letter. I reached out to Kanin as well, but he’s difficult to pin down without a physical address, and I can no longer reach him via the System’s Contact interface. I have faith he’ll find his way back here one day; he certainly knows where to find me.

“I thought you were going to be late to your first day of class,” Dizzi teases as we reach the bottom.

“Very unbecoming of a teacher,” Captain Marlow agrees. His expression is serious, but I share enough of his dry humor to know he’s joking.

“I don’t suppose it makes it better than I’ll be a student, too?” I ask.

Attiru chuckles softly. “I think that makes it worse.”

I look up at the class, who has grown a little quieter since their queens’ arrived. Ollie is sitting with Meritis, of course, and I’m pleased to see they’re joined by both a handful of Fyrethian children, and our youngest Travelers, Yuna, Zhiyong, and Aliz. There’s an electric air of anticipation in the room—or perhaps I’m merely projecting my own eagerness.

“Well,” Blair says, “I believe everyone is accounted for.”

Dizzi’s wings flutter in excitement. “Then what are we waiting for?”

I step forward and clap my hands for the amphitheater's attention. The talking dies down to murmurs but doesn’t entirely peter out.

“Silence!” Mirzayael shouts.

The hall goes silent.

Captain Marlow gives a hearty chuckle.

I look around at the others, but no one takes the lead. Dizzi animatedly gestures me forward. I don’t need to be told twice.

“Welcome, everyone,” I call, raising my voice for the room to hear. Dizzi created a modified translator I could use that would amplify my voice, but it’s slightly delayed and creates an echo I find distracting, so I’m more than happy to speak for myself.

“Thank you for joining us,” I continue. “Today marks one month since regular classes began. It certainly wasn’t without its wrinkles, but overall it seems to be a resounding success. Thank you again to our wonderful teachers, whom we couldn’t have done this without!”

There is a smattering of applause, mostly from the adults in the room. The kids already look bored.

“Today we’ll be trying something new. These weekly elective courses will be optional, of course, but we hope you will all find something to enjoy. We hope to expand the scope of our classes in the future, but for now we will be heading the supplementary lessons. Let’s introduce each of our teachers and their subjects, shall we?”

Dizzi hops forward before I can usher anyone up, bless her.

“Heya!” she cries, waving to the room. “I’ll be teaching arcane theory. I know y’all have been working with Gardi on magical basics, but I’m here to really dig into the nitty gritty details!”

“And your name,” I prompt.

“Oh come on,” she says. “Everyone already knows my name.”

“Not everyone in the audience lives here,” I remind her.

Dizzi sighs dramatically before raising her voice. “And I’m Dizzi, the court artificer, or whatever.” She gives an exaggerated bow before stepping back.

Attiru steps up next, nodding to me. “If I may?”

“Of course,” I say, gesturing them on.

The cambion surveys the hall with apparent reverence. “It’s a pleasure to be back in this fascinating city. My name is Attiru, and I am a cartographer by trade. Fyre has invited me to teach courses on world history, and perhaps arcane geography, if things go well. Thank you for having me.”

We introduce Blair, Gardi, and Captain Marlow, next, each of whom will be teaching one of the world’s primary three languages; Valenian, Common Dunmorish, and Common Signs, respectively. I still have translators that allow me to understand Valenian and Common Signs, but without Echo in my head I no longer can understand most of them without an aid device. I’d love to rectify that, and I’m excited to finally have dedicated time set aside for me to begin learning.

Eventually it comes to me. “I will be teaching advanced mathematics and a handful of other scientific fields.” There is decidedly less enthusiasm for my announcement, but the handful of scholars and Fyrethians who lean forward eagerly make it all worth it. I turn to Mirzayael.

“Physical combat,” she says. There is a pause.

“Mir,” I think.

She mentally sighs. “Including weapons training and self defense.”

I suppose that will do for now.

Dizzi is hopping from foot to foot. “Which do we start with first?”

“Magic!” Ollie thinks into my mind. “Magic, magic!”

That shouldn’t surprise me. “Ollie, other people can’t hear you.”

“MAGIC!” His translator calls, and several people around him flinch from the volume. “Magic,” he repeats, much quieter.

Dizzi cackles. “The people have spoken!”

I can’t say I’m upset by this development, though I expect Dizzi’s sort of magical theory might not be exactly what Ollie is looking for. Luckily, he still has Gardi’s daily classes to attend.

Then again, I would have been excited no matter which class we did first.

It’s been such a long time since I’ve had the opportunity to study in a structured environment, and I’ve been desperate to learn everything I can about this world since the day I first arrived. It’s already been a year, but it feels like hardly any time has passed at all. Perhaps living two lives does that to one’s perception of time.

Mirzayael grumbles something about no one wanting to learn practical skills anymore, but she takes a seat with the rest of us as Dizzi flies up to a spot on the podium.

I excitedly take out a scroll and nub of charcoal in anticipation of the lecture. There’s still so much about the basics of magical theory I want to learn!

Mirzayael chuckles into my mind. “You’re acting like a kid on their soul day.”

“I can’t help it!” I grin. “Who wouldn’t be excited to learn about such things?”

“Plenty,” Mirzayael thinks. “But that’s what I love about you.”

Her affection warms me like I’m bathing in the sun. Even if she can’t understand what I get excited over, she’s simply happy to see me excited; and that’s what I love about her.

Not for the first time, I wonder how I came to find myself here, in this wonderful world at the side of a wonderful woman. Surrounded by so many friends, empowered by people who see me for who I am and what I strive to be; I’m not sure I deserve such a life.

But I suppose it isn’t about what we do or don’t deserve. It’s about what we make of the life we’re given, and I wouldn’t trade this one for the world.

The End
