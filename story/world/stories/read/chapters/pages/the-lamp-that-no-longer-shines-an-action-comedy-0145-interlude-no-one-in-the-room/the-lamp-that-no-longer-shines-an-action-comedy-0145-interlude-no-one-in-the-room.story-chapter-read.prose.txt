[Time]: Shortly after Cecilia Wellington's 18th birthday[Location]: Holheim · Wellington Estate · Private Clinical Wing

The clinic was a bespoke installation within the Wellington Estate, designed with weaponized tranquility. Acoustic dampening wards absorbed all echoes. Ambient mana diffusers prevented accidental resonance. The furniture was heavy enough to withstand a sudden kinetic outburst without rearranging. The lighting was calibrated to the forgiving warmth of late afternoon.

Dr. Aris had treated witches who survived other worlds and made it home, only to find themselves mourning the lives they had burned to escape them. She had mapped the paranoid architectures of ancient immortals.

She had built, over years, a clinical architecture capable of holding the weight of an apex predator's inner collapse.

Then, Cecilia Wellington walked in and sat down.

The newly designated Heir of House Wellington was eighteen years old. She wore a high-collared, immaculate dark dress. Over her right eye, she wore a simple, unadorned black silk patch.

The family’s Grand Healers had forcibly reconstructed the socket while she was unconscious, leaving her looking perfectly, terrifyingly intact.

She rested her hands on her lap. She did not fidget.

Dr. Aris felt the air pressure in the room alter.

It wasn't aggression. It wasn't volatile mana. It was weight: oceanic, pressing inward over the furniture, the walls, and the doctor's own chest.

She opened her notepad. She kept her voice a calm, practiced invitation.

"What brings you here today, Cecilia?"

Cecilia looked at her. Her single, uncovered blue eye held the light of a dying star: still burning, patient with its own extinction.

"My sister wanted to give me a gift," Cecilia said. Her voice was perfectly level. "She left before it was finished."

Dr. Aris's pen paused. A gift. Not my sister attacked me. Not my sister maimed me. The structure was still intact, but she could already feel the masonry groan.

"The medical report described significant trauma," Dr. Aris said, guiding carefully. "What arcane focus did she use? A cursed blade? A ritual artifact designed to bypass passive mana defense?"

Cecilia's gaze remained placid. "A silver spoon. From the dining room."

Dr. Aris's pen went still.

A mundane silver spoon. No enchantments. No runic edge. The physiological logic of a Witch's body made this impossible: passive mana would have shattered the silver on contact. The flesh would have rejected the intrusion instantly.

For an un-enchanted spoon to breach a top-tier Witch's eye, Evangeline couldn't just push.

Cecilia had to pull.

"You suppressed your regeneration," Dr. Aris said, the professional boundary in her voice slipping for the first time. "While it was happening. You actively forced your body to accept the damage, second by second, or the wound would have closed before it could form."

"Yes," Cecilia said. "She asked for them. It was more convenient that way."

Dr. Aris stared at the eighteen-year-old girl.

The therapeutic framework of victim and perpetrator did not apply here.

Evangeline had wielded a harmless piece of silverware. She had bypassed the impenetrable defenses of a Wellington prodigy entirely through Cecilia's bottomless, unconditional permission. Cecilia had built the altar, lay down upon it, and actively held her own wounds open so her sister would not be inconvenienced.

"Can you tell me," Dr. Aris said carefully, "how the evening began?"

"It was my eighteenth birthday," Cecilia said. Her voice carried the quiet cadence of a story told from memory rather than pain. "Midnight. Evangeline came into my room. Her footsteps were light. It felt like she was coming in for a pajama party. I asked her where my present was.

"She sat on the edge of my bed. She kissed my forehead. She said she was going to give me my gift right now."

Cecilia's eye was fixed somewhere past the doctor's shoulder.

"I sat up. I hugged her. I put my face against her shoulder and asked what it was. She asked me to be the Heir." A pause. "I thought she was joking. There was no reason to change it. But her tone wasn't a joke. It sounded like she was complaining about a trinket she no longer wanted.

"She said: 'They say only the blind are fit to lead the Wellington family. Only when the physical eyes are gone can the Mystic Eyes see the truth.'"

"A family superstition," Dr. Aris offered.

"Gossip," Cecilia corrected. "I told her so. I spoke to her the way you would coax a child. I told her no one had ever made that rule. It was a flawless counter-argument."

"How did she respond?"

"She smiled. She didn't answer."

Cecilia looked back at the doctor. Something infinitely small shifted in the stillness of her eye.

"She sat in the silence for a long time. Then she said: 'Cici. Give me your eyes. Just treat it as a gift to your older sister, once you become Head.'"

A beat."So I agreed."

Dr. Aris felt a cold that had nothing to do with temperature.

Evangeline hadn't offered a real reason. She had offered the shape of an excuse, and then abandoned it the moment Cecilia pushed back. She had allowed Cecilia to believe she was arguing against a logical motive, only to respond with a silence that proved logic was never part of the equation.

"If she wanted your eyes," Dr. Aris said, navigating carefully, "why is only one covered? Why did it stop?"

"She got halfway through. Then she dropped the spoon." Cecilia touched the edge of the patch. "The spoon was covered in blood. She stood up. She looked at me and said: 'This is boring, Cici. I don't want your eyes, and I don't want this family anymore.' She kissed my forehead. And then she left."

The clock ticked.

The cruelty was so absolute, so devoid of malice, that it defied every framework Dr. Aris had ever used.

Evangeline hadn't stopped out of mercy or guilt. She had stopped because her attention had expired. Her most devastating strike wasn't the mutilation. It was dropping the tool because she found it dull.

"Cecilia," Dr. Aris said, leaning forward. "Are you waiting for her to come back?"

Cecilia looked at her.

"The gift isn't finished," Cecilia said.

Evangeline had started a sentence with a silver spoon and walked away before placing the period.

Dr. Aris felt her footing give way entirely.

She retreated to the last reliable stronghold: cognitive reframing. "Cecilia, listen to me. When she gave you that reason, the gossip about the blind heir, she gave you a door to push against. You pushed it. When you answered her, she didn't argue. She just continued. And then she abandoned you.

"That is not an unfinished gift waiting for a conclusion. That is the erratic cruelty of a deeply broken mind. You are trying to find logical closure in an inherently irrational act of abuse. You must release yourself from the burden of her sickness."

Cecilia looked at the doctor. No defensiveness. No denial. Only the fathomless, quiet patience of the ocean floor looking at a cup.

"You speak of her as if she were a person," Cecilia said softly.

"She is a person who hurt you."

"A person has a motive. A person reacts to the world. If she had left because something in our family wronged her, I could extract a lesson. I could tell myself: Be fair, and those you love will not leave. If she had been testing my devotion, I could tell myself: Love harder, and she will stay."

Cecilia's blue eye was completely dry.

"Evangeline is not a person. She is the weather. She does not arrive because you deserve it, and she does not leave because you made a mistake. She left because that is what she is. I am not holding onto a delusion, Doctor. I am accurately describing my coordinates. I am wearing the climate she left behind."

Dr. Aris opened her mouth. Nothing came.

"She gave me a door," Cecilia continued, her voice dropping. The composure held, but something microscopically thin shifted in the glass of her expression. "She gave me the gossip as a reason. When I pushed against it, she didn't hold the door shut.

"She stood there and smiled, because the door was never attached to any walls. She let me believe that if I gave her a good enough argument, she would be persuaded. That we were having a human conversation, for one second."

Cecilia's eye found the doctor's and held it.

"Then she said she was bored and she closed the door. You are asking me to walk away from the frame, Doctor. But I cannot. That crack, that one moment she tried to be a person with a reason, is the only evidence she ever was."

Dr. Aris looked at the eighteen-year-old across from her. At the silk patch. At the perfectly folded hands. At the face that had accepted all of this and called it an unfinished gift.

Empathy did not flow from patient to doctor. It flooded out of the doctor, drowning her in the ambient weight of the abyss Cecilia was peacefully inhabiting.

A hot tear slipped over Dr. Aris's lower lash line. Then another.

She lowered her pen. Her hands were shaking. Decades of professional armor. Gone.

"I'm so sorry," Dr. Aris choked out, her clinical voice completely gone. "I don't have the tools. I cannot heal you."

Cecilia Wellington looked at the weeping woman across from her. She did not move. She did not look away.

Then she took a tissue from the box on the desk between them and held it across the space.

"It's alright," Cecilia said softly.

[Time]: 1998 · Shortly After the Babylon Wedding[Location]: Wellington Estate · Private Containment Wing

The grandfather clock at the far end of the corridor had run down sometime after the second doctor left. No one had gone to wind it. The pendulum sat motionless. The absence of ticking was worse than the sound had been.

Victoria Wellington was twelve years old.

She sat on the velvet bench outside the containment room. The bench was wide enough for five. Her mothers occupied the far opposite ends, six feet of upholstery between them.

They had ended their marriage two years ago, the shared grief of Evangeline's departure curdling into something that made existing in the same room intolerable. Yet here they were. They did not touch. They did not speak.

When one drew a shaking breath, the other stared at the molding above the door as if something architectural and neutral could help. Victoria's birth mother was running her thumb over the empty space on her ring finger until the skin turned raw.

Victoria's patent leather shoes did not reach the floor. She had stopped swinging them an hour ago.

She watched the heavy oak door.

The first specialist had walked in that morning with a ledger of therapeutic anchors and the confident stride of a woman who repaired broken minds the way engineers repaired bridges.

She came out twenty minutes later without the ledger. It had stayed on the other side of the door. Her face was the color of old ash, her hands trembling too badly to work the clasp on her satchel. She walked past Victoria's mothers and did not slow down.

The second was a High-Order Empath, a woman whose presence felt like a warm room. She lasted forty minutes. When the door opened, she didn't walk out.

She pressed herself against the corridor wall and slid down it to the carpet, her face in her hands. She wept with a silent, catastrophic despair, the particular grief of a person who had poured everything into a vessel with no bottom, and poured, and poured, and the bottom had never come.

Then the final doctor.

She was ancient. A legendary figure, someone who had treated phenomena that predated the modern calendar. Victoria's mothers had spent weeks and an enormous sum of political capital to bring her here. She was the absolute, final measure.

She had entered without ceremony. The door closed.

The corridor temperature had been dropping for an hour. By the time the second hour passed, frost was beginning to rim the edges of the door frame, thin and white, creeping outward along the grain of the oak.

Three hours.

The brass handle turned.

The ancient doctor stepped out. She had gone in like a granite pillar. She came out as something softer and more hollow, a form in the right shape with the weight removed. Her eyes, previously sharp as a surveyor's, were entirely vacant.

Victoria's mothers stood simultaneously.

"Doctor." One of them couldn't hold her voice steady. "Please. Is she—"

"I have nothing to report," the doctor said.

"What does that mean? Did the containment fail? Tell us what is broken so we can fix it—"

"There is no failure." The ancient doctor's voice was stripped of its clinical cadence, rough with something she hadn't brought into the room. She looked at the frosted door. "To fail, you must have a patient who is fighting you, or a patient who is in pain. But she is not in pain."

A long pause.

"I spoke to her for three hours. I offered every anchor, every memory, every fragment of warmth I possess. I did not fail to save your daughter." The doctor looked back at them. "I simply could not find anyone in the room to save."

One of Victoria's mothers made a sound with no name in any language. She sank to her knees on the carpet. The other staggered back against the wall, her hand over her mouth, her shoulders beginning to heave.

The ancient doctor turned and walked down the corridor. She did not look back.

Victoria sat on the bench.

She looked at the frosted door for a moment. Then she slid off the velvet, and her shoes made a small, quiet sound as they hit the floor.

Her mothers didn't notice. They were folding in on themselves, two women who had nothing left to reach for in each other.

Victoria walked to the door.

A hand caught her arm. "Victoria—no."

She pulled free and pushed the door open and let it swing shut behind her.

The acoustic wards swallowed the corridor.

The room was white and featureless and freezing. The ambient diffusers on the walls had gone dark, overcome and silent. The air tasted of ozone and something deeper than cold.

Cecilia sat in the center of the room.

She wore the wedding dress. It was immaculate, undamaged, as though the Hanging Gardens had never fallen and she had simply come home. Her eyes were open, fixed on the blank wall ahead.

But the edges of her silhouette were wrong: soft, blurred, as though she were dissolving into the surrounding air. The sublimation was advanced. She was letting the world go. The water was closing over her head, and she was simply allowing her lungs to fill.

Victoria stopped a few feet away.

"Cecilia," she said.Her eyes did not move."Look at me."

Nothing. The frost on the walls extended another centimeter toward the ceiling.

Victoria took a step forward. The cold intensified: not the ordinary cold of a room without heat, but a dense, pressurized cold, the cold of very deep water pressing inward from all directions.

"Look at me."

Nothing. The breath continued, quiet and automated, maintaining minimum function while the person inhabiting the body let go.

Victoria felt the tears break.

She had been holding herself together for three hours on that bench, and then she walked into this room and saw her sister dissolving, and the structure simply gave way.

She crossed the remaining distance and grabbed Cecilia's hands.

Cecilia's skin was wrong. Cold and rigid, barely kinetic. The sensation of something that had almost finished the process of becoming an object.

"Look at me!" Her voice cracked and tore, the volume wrenching out of her without permission.

Her eyes did not blink.

Victoria's tears hit Cecilia's hands, hot against the cold, entirely undignified. She didn't wipe them away. She squeezed as hard as her twelve-year-old hands could manage.

"Evangeline left," Victoria choked. "She left and she threw this family away because she was bored. And now you're doing it too."

She dug her fingers in.

"You are doing exactly what she did. You are sitting in this room and you are leaving me alone."

The mana diffusers flickered.

"I'm twelve years old." Victoria's voice was shaking so hard the words barely held together. "I don't know how to run this house. Our mothers aren't even looking at each other anymore. If you leave, they will make me the Heir."

A sob tore out of her, ugly and uncontrolled.

"I'm scared, Cecilia. I'm scared and I can't do it and there is no one else."

She dropped her forehead against Cecilia's frozen knees. The tears came without any shape now, hot and relentless, soaking into white fabric.

"If you sink," Victoria whispered into the white dress, "you have to drown me too. I am holding your hands. You don't get to go somewhere I can't follow."

The frost on the walls stopped advancing.The temperature held.

Slowly, so slowly it barely registered as motion, the frozen fingers beneath Victoria's grip curled inward.

Victoria lifted her head.Cecilia's eyes had moved.

The vacant, drowning depth of them was still there. But something had shifted across their surface: a tectonic, agonizing refocus, like a world resuming rotation after a very long stillness. The edges of Cecilia's silhouette had snapped back into sharp, physical focus.

Cecilia was looking at her.

At the tears tracking down her cheeks. At the white gloves she hadn't thought to remove before she grabbed her. At the shape of her face and the red rims of her eyes and the twelve-year-old who had walked through the door no doctor had been able to come back out of.

The silence was different now. Less like a void. More like a held breath.

Cecilia looked at Victoria.

And she did not look away.
