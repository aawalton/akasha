Chapter 72

After the Arrow

The world returned like a claw strike.

There were no dreams, no distant voices, not even the warm flicker of pain that usually heralds the return to consciousness.

Only a blunt impact: air forcing itself into her lungs like crushed ice, an invisible needle piercing her chest, and a brutal yank upward, as if her soul had been dragged back into her body against its will.

And so Mai opened her eyes, and pain jabbed through them.

The ceiling was white—too white— and unknown. There were monitors around her, displaying readouts of her vitals.

She looked down. She was in a hospital gown. She blinked, letting her eyes adjust to the brightness. Something pressed against her right wrist.

Then, the metallic sound. Clink.

She was handcuffed to the bed. She looked at her hands and arms; her skin was darkened as if she had spent a long time in the sun, but it wasn't wrinkled or sunburned. Her silvery hair fell haphazardly over her shoulders. She had a small wireless sensor on her chest with a red light that was flickering.

A second passed. Then another. Her breathing steadied. And then the voice arrived.

Finally awake, a whisper inside her skull. You had me worried, little one.

Mai closed her eyes, not to rest, but to understand.

Azusa.

Not a fever dream. Not a battlefield hallucination.

She was still there.

Like a handshake that squeezes far too tightly. Mai had given her the name. Mai had given permission to that entity in a state closer to death than life. She’d thought maybe it had all been a dream born from dying.

She could almost have sworn that it had all been just a hallucination—the last flickers of her fading consciousness.

Her mind had conjured up the fantastical illusion that, at the very last moment, someone had appeared to save her.It hadn’t been a prince or a princess on a white steed.

But it wasn’t. She was alive. Everything had happened.

The shrikes, the Xe-res.

Sasha. Ludovic. Eun-ji. Jiho. Soo-ah.

It hadn’t been a nightmare. She knew. They were gone.

In her mind, she saw once again the scenes of her companions being torn to pieces, absorbed, or simply vanishing from the face of the Earth, without leaving a single trace.

Her lips trembled.

It had been true. It had all been true. And now she had to live with the burden of remembering those final moments of the people with whom she had shared so many moments over the past few months.

She had survived.

She was alive.

But where?

“Where…?” Her throat scraped like she’d swallowed sand. What did they do to me?

Do you want the gentle version, or the true one?

The voice of Azusa wasn’t mocking, it carried almost no tone at all. No emotion.

Mai clenched her jaw.

The gesture was new in her, before she would have felt fear, confusion, maybe even a flicker of hope.

Now there was only a thin, severe line—sharp as a scalpel.

“The true one”

A brief silence. They were curious. Not the innocent kind. The dangerous kind.

“Who?”

She looked around again.

The room was small, rectangular, contained. Not a hospital, but a laboratory disguised as one.

The walls had that false smoothness of reinforced fireproof material, and the floor gave off the sterile coldness of facilities where no one ever walks barefoot.

To her right, a camera drone hovered, pointed directly at her face.

Seeing her awake, a bitter spark rose in her throat.

“What happened?”

They recorded everything, Azusa said, with neutral calm. From the moment you were left alone on the battlefield… until the portal vanished.

Of course, Mai thought. The drones with camera systems must have captured it all.

Silence. A silence weighing tons.

Memory returned in fragments again: red snow, Sasha’s eyes, Jiho being swallowed, Ludovic falling. Soo-ah. How would she tell Nikolai? Maybe he knew already?

Mai swallowed.

The sound was rough.

“You could have saved them,” she whispered, not raising her voice, but her tone dry. It wasn’t an accusation. It was a fact.

Azusa didn’t hesitate. I could have, she admitted with the calm of someone stating a mathematical equation. But it wasn’t the right moment.

Mai closed her eyes—this time because it hurt.

The pain didn’t cry. It burned—slow, deep, like a coal buried under the tongue.

“They…” She couldn’t finish.

Azusa did it for her.

They died before I could intervene without destroying you as well. And you, Mai… you were the only one who could do it. The only one capable of breaking the sphere.

It was a logical explanation. Clean.

Too clean.

Mai clenched her teeth.

Like hell! Why me?!

That new edge in her mind—dry, sharp—came from knowing the world not only betrays you: it uses you. But of course, she know that. Why surprised her?

Don't lose your temper, don't yell, Azusa said in a softer tone, as if trying to calm her down.

And that was the truth.

She wanted to lose her temper. She wanted to yell. She wanted to cry.

Get a hold of yourself. Right now, throwing a tantrum would only put you—and a lot of other people—in a tight spot.

When she lifted her head, she saw it:

Across the room, a mirror set into the wall. She thought it was polarized glass, an observation window. But no.

It reflected.

Mai looked at herself.

The image hit her like the recoil of a poorly-calibrated weapon: pronounced cheekbones, sunken eyes, silver hair falling in disordered strands around her shoulders.

A thin, almost fragile figure… yet strangely taut, like a drawn wire about to snap—or cut. She truly looked like an evil witch from some old tale.

“I hadn’t seen myself since… I don’t even know when,” she murmured.

You’ve spent a long time on the battlefield.

“I don’t need you to remind me.”

But I do anyway.

The voice sounded close—almost at her ear.

An earring on her right ear glimmered for an instant, as if it had breathed.

Mai brought her hand to her right ear. She had never been one to wear earrings, but now she had one in her ear. The sensation of that voice in her head seemed to spread to every part of her body. She didn’t need to imagine it. If it could take the shape of a bow, then maybe she could do that, too.

“You’re there…? Inside that?”

It’s my anchor. My body, for now. Though I can extend beyond it if I deem it necessary—or when you want to use me. I'll explain it later, but it's complicated. The earring represents the bow, one half, the other half is inside you.

Use me.

Exactly what Azusa had done.

“It’s not over? Why do you say when I’m able to use you?”

It is over. At least against the ones you call Fractus.

“Then?” His voice trembled as she asked. Azusa’s answer implied that it could happen again. And the answer only confirmed it, as a chill ran down her spine...

Other anomalies exist. The fabric of reality was broken for too long. More distortions will appear.

“Can I close them with the bow?”

Yes. By the way, you can talk to me without moving your lips. Try it. Just think clearly. There's no need for words.

Mai sighed. “Why am I handcuffed?”

Why do you think? I told you—they recorded everything. You’re in a research facility.

It really worked. Mai didn’t ask aloud, but Azusa added anyway:

And yes. I was the one who broke the experimental needles. Who diverted the biopsy attempts. Who increased your density when they tried to move your bed with new reinforced restraints.

“They used me as an experiment?”

They tried.

Mai looked back at the mirror.

Saw herself again: thin, exhausted, dark-eyed… but holding a posture she didn’t recognize.

Her back was straight.

Her eyes, though sunken, shone with a razor-sharp clarity.

“Who are they?” She asked

The army.

“How much time passed?”

Five days since the sphere collapsed. Three since they brought you here. Two since they gave up trying to pierce your skin.

“And no one plans on letting me go.”

No...

“Figured… By the way… what's going on with my skin?”

It's a side effect of using me. The radiation emitted when you summon me causes your body to take on that hue for a while. But it should fade in a few days.

“Radiation?”

That’s another of the many reasons why you were the only one suitable to be my vessel. No other human or fey could have withstood the radiation required to contain my form without perishing.

“There are other fey like me.”

Not of your kind.

“There’s nothing special about me…”

She inhaled.

Her ribs and groin hurt. With her free hand she lifted the sheet slightly. Tubes. That explained the pain. As if her bladder wasn’t small enough, now they had stuck tubes into her urethra and her rear.

I didn't let them perform experiments on you, but I did allow that for your care, Azusa said.

“Have you been able to communicate with anyone?”

No. I didn’t need to. But I revealed my presence through static and by causing physical effects around you. That was more than enough for them to just give you the necessary care without having to put you under anesthesia to dissect you.

She let out a tired groan.

“What do they want?” she asked.

To know how you did it.

“I didn’t.”

…

You did. I only opened the door.

A shiver crawled across her skin.

She stared at her hand, cuffed to the bed.

The skin around the restraints was untouched: no bruising, no friction marks.

“Azusa... What am I now?”

The entity hesitated—a heartbeat, nothing more. But Mai felt the weight of the response like a hammer. You are Mai. The same as before. But also something else. Much more than you were.

“That tells me nothing.”

It means the war is over for you… but what comes next won’t be peace.

Mai let her head fall back into the pillow. A massive exhaustion wrapped her like a damp shroud.

“I’m alone,” she said in a voice stripped of everything—pretense, shield, hope.

Azusa answered without softness, without pity, without pretending:

Not exactly.

Mai closed her eyes.

The true weight of the team… collapsed over her like a delayed avalanche.

She knew—with a grim certainty—that no one else remained in that field of snow.

She had been the only witness, the only survivor, the only one who heard the last sounds of her companions fading into the world.

A tremor pressed against her throat.

She didn’t cry. But her body did, silently, inwardly, like every cell curled in on itself to grieve. She wasn't going to give whoever was behind that mirror the satisfaction of seeing her cry.

And at that moment, something happened that, had it not been for the situation she was in, she would have noticed more clearly. That entity was speaking to her mind in a tone very different from the one it had used before. Azusa’s tone almost sounded calmer, almost concerned.

Hang in there. You were on the verge of death and you're still alive, you can handle this, girl. You know full well what’s coming. People are already putting two and two together in many places.

“What two and two?”

The story. Or at least the story that’s reaching everyone’s ears and eyes.

Mai's eyes flew open. She knew what Azusa was saying, and only a great effort kept her from speaking the words that were already in her mind. “What story? Everyone must know what happened.”

That depends on something else: the perception of the truth and who benefits from one version versus the other. People decide the rest.

“Justice should have only one truth.”

Justice has had many meanings, just as what is considered the truth has. Depending on manipulation and semantic shifts, that changes. In the end, it’s the law of self-interest that prevails, little one. You’ve always been an idealist. That’s one of your virtues—and one of your weaknesses, too. You can't save everyone with both hands in front of you.

“I’ve only wanted a place where those of us who are different can live in peace without being persecuted. I want to help. Is that so wrong?”

That depends on who you ask and the methods used. You tried them according to your circumstances. That’s what led you here.

Mai looked at her hands. One was free, and the other was chained to the bed.

If you truly want to protect something in the end, what you need is power and a position—even if it’s in the shadows. Even if you don’t like having authority, if you want to protect, you need to take up the necessary weapons to do so. You need to surround yourself with your friends—and even more so with your enemies. And you need to know which information is necessary to protect what you want.

“I’ve never liked lies.”

But you know they’re necessary. Lies and truth are both necessary, you just need to know when one is more appropriate than the other. If you rely on the law, all that awaits you is a chain on your other hand. You won’t find justice there, because you are playing with both hands tied in a rigged game. The only truth is that, in the end, if you’re brave enough, you’ll navigate between the two. Sometimes, even a lie can bring about justice.

She knew it. She knew it from the beginning.

Ever since she lived with Miho, she knew it. The secret was always there, even if its nature changed.

In the end, the world knew that the feys existed; it knew that magic and powers existed. But in the end, it always seemed as though there was a necessary step where, in order to protect everyone, it was necessary to lie about something.

Then the voice returned—dry as a snapped twig.

Mai…

“What?”

They’re watching you. And they’re coming in.

Mai looked at her reflection. She used her antennae. She could barely see across the room, but she could sense soldiers walking nearby. They were the Joint Forces. They truly regarded her as a laboratory specimen.

She knew what she had to do.

The war had truly ended. Years of combat behind her—but a new one could begin at any moment.

She knew that.

It was the price of what she had done.

The drones had recorded a girl suddenly summoning a bow and using it to erase the sphere. Something even thousands of mages failed to accomplish during the magic attack. She had simply risen into the sky and, with a single arrow, ended a conflict that had dragged on for years.

She knew what that meant.

She would have a lot to explain.

Now that the war against the extradimensional invader was over, something almost as dangerous could begin: a conflict between humanity, the feys, and the aeon.

In the worst case, she would be used as proof that the Fractus invasion had been orchestrated by the hidden world. It wouldn’t be surprising. After all, in the twentieth century, an immense global operation had been launched to erase signs of the hidden world following the Second World War.

The feys and magic could easily become the next target. She had no evidence of this… but based on her experience, she had no doubt either.

What will you do? Azusa asked.

“Can you detach from me?”

No. I’m bound to you. To manifest.

“I mean if the earring can be at a distance. If I can take it off.”

Yes. That’s not a problem. But if you summon me, I can appear at your side.

“They can’t hurt you?”

No. But they can hurt you.

Mai stared at her reflection.

She knew what she had to do.

They will do horrible things to you. You know that.

Mai remained silent.

She did know.

She knew exactly what they would do. Tests. Experiments. Interrogations. She had learned enough during her time in Wishper to recognize the signs. The Tripartite Treaty would be ravenous for her testimony about what happened, though the footage spoke for itself. The only thing those images showed was her using a weapon to end the war. She would have to give many explanations.

“What should I say about you?”

You may tell the truth. It doesn’t change the fact that they can’t harm me, and that I am here to help—so long as it is through you.

“You won’t intervene unless I ask.”

I can obey if that is what you want. I will not argue.

The metal door began to open with a hydraulic groan.

Mai lifted her head, resigned.

She would take that bullet for everyone if she had to.

She wouldn’t do it for the entire hidden world—her ambitions were not larger than her small body, but she knew her limits. She wouldn’t do it for all the feys, nor for all humans or aeon. She would do it to protect the fragile coexistence they had spent decades trying to build. Her friends out there, living in secrecy, in the shadows.

The war had ended.

And if she could do something—anything—she wanted to try.

There would be tests, interrogations.

But she only needed to answer with what she knew.

She didn’t need to lie. Azusa had come to her in a moment of absolute need. She would not lie about that.

She carried too many deaths on her hands, deaths she had caused over the decades. Even if they had been scum, it was still her responsibility.

She had to face what was coming.

“So be it,” she murmured.

And for the first time in a very long while, she wasn’t afraid.

***

The war had ended, but the silence was more deafening than the explosions that came before it.

The world had changed, and Mai—the last survivor of a battle that consumed millions on that Siberian battlefield—was now a shadow in history.

A shadow that carried on her shoulders a secret no one could ever discover, far greater than her small frame could justify.

Just as Azusa had warned, the security cameras on several drones installed across the distortion had recorded every second of her struggle. The image of Mai—wounded, dying, collapsing into the snow, only to rise again and loose an arrow as the distortion sphere vanished—had been transmitted like a final gasp of hope and unease to the command centers of the Joint Forces across Siberia and beyond.

The official narrative that emerged from that event spoke of a mixed team of feys, humans, and aeons who, in unison, fought to their last breath to defeat the threat. The sphere had supposedly collapsed from expelling so many enemies.

A theory had been published suggesting that a collapse had occurred within the matter comprising the sphere and that, by expelling too many fractus, this had led to a systematic collapse of the bridge connecting the Fractus dimension with Earth. It was basically an imbalance between positive and negative energy. Some people objected, but their voices weren't really heard. That theory had only grown more popular after renowned scientists from around the globe explained it in prime time on TV. Since data from the Fractus dimension had never been obtained, that theory could well be considered true given what had happened to the invaders after the sphere collapsed.

All the fractus around the world had disappeared, leaving the cores behind.

There was talks of sacrifice, of unwavering courage, of a titanic effort to save the world. People wept, united under the banner of that heroic victory…but the truth was different.

There had been sacrifice; there had been courage. But hiding the truth was like spitting in the faces of all those who had died over the years.

However, to prevent another conflict, it was now necessary. And a small, middle-sized fey could only hope that perhaps, in the future, history would give each of them their rightful place—whether that was good or bad, depending on who wrote it.

Mai lived.

But now hidden behind a curtain of lies, very carefully woven by those tasked with maintaining the balance of power.

Her name was never mentioned.

It was a controlled story, a fabrication serving the interests of the three great blocs: humans, feys, and aeons. The media celebrated the end of the war, the fall of the fractus… but Mai was not part of that celebration.

She was isolated in a white bubble, answering questions over and over again and being the subject of observation and investigation.

It’s going to be hard to let the world flow with this, Miho. She thought to herself.

And that was enough.

She had already realized that many times.

But now she was tired of it.

Maybe Azusa was right.

Whatever it was, she would have to choose between doing the right thing and doing what was necessary to protect the little bit of calm the world was currently enjoying.
