Morning came grey to the islet.

The cloudbank below the cliff had not turned gold yet. The sun was somewhere behind a long, thin sheet of haze that thinned the light to the colour of old parchment.

Aiven stood at the small deck of the scout-ship.

His hands rested on the brass rail. He was wearing his coat. The obsidian arm caught the dull light along its etchings, slow cyan pulses moving across the back of his knuckles like the breathing of a thing not quite asleep. Beside him, Virelle stood very still, her prismatic orb hovering low and quiet at her shoulder. Her eyes were on the middle of the islet, where the grass had been worn flat by a week of impact.

Neither of them had spoken in some time.

He had not slept.

He had tried. He had been lying down for most of the night, in the small cabin, with Virelle curled silently against his side. The lantern had been out for hours. The ship had rocked gently in the soft updrafts of the islet. By every measure of a person who should be able to sleep, he had been a person who should have been able to sleep.

His head had refused to cooperate.

He had been very confident in front of Cyria yesterday. He could see that, now, in the dawn light. He had laid out the plan in the meeting room of the Cinder-Fox with the calm of a clerk filing a manifest. Get Lyra. Break the compulsion. Survive. He had said it cleanly. Cyria had teased him about thinking like the protagonist of a story. He had said the world had given him second chances and he was not going to fumble this one.

He even had Cyria bring in a teleportation artifact and one that can break spell-links. One was needed for escape, and another one to free Lyra from Lucien’s spell.

And Cyria had wished him good luck.

Then the thoughts had begun.

Can you make it out of this alive?

The question had been the first one. It had arrived in his head with the bluntness of an officer at a door.

He had not answered it. He had let it stand at the door.

Then the other questions had come.

Will Lyra even be present?

That was the harder one. The whole rescue depended on it, and he had been so focused on the survival half of the plan that he had not, until last night, sat with the possibility that the rescue half might not even have a target in the room.

He had run through the cases the way his clerk-brain still ran through cases on instinct. He had three.

Case one. Lucien brings Lyra with him to the islet. Best case. Aiven and Virelle hold ground in familiar terrain. Cyria’s compulsion-breaking artifact stays close. Lyra is within reach, the fight is bounded, the geometry of the room is something Aiven has spent a week learning. He had liked case one. Case one was almost optimistic.

Case two. Lucien brings Aiven and Virelle to the Fold, where Lyra is. Second-best. Enemy terrain, but at least Lyra is somewhere in the same space. Cyria’s teleportation artifact, the one she had quietly arranged for him yesterday afternoon, would extract them from the Fold if it came to it. Case two was workable. Tight, but workable.

Case three. Lucien comes alone, refuses to take Aiven anywhere, conducts the entire confrontation here, on the islet, while Lyra remains in the Fold and out of reach.

Case three was the one his mind had circled all night.

Because case three required Aiven to talk Lucien into bringing him to the Fold. To convince him. To negotiate. And Aiven had no idea what currency Lucien might accept for that transaction. He had thought of three approaches. He had rehearsed two of them. He had been on the third when the sky began to lighten and he had given up on sleep entirely.

If case three played out, he would have to extemporize. He would have to lean on Lucien’s curiosity, on the man’s appetite for theatrical resolution, on the small academic vanity that had made him personally explain the world over a cup of tea the first time they had met.

It was a thin plan.

He had spent the last hour of the night staring at the ceiling, trying to make it less thin, and failing.

His coat pocket weighed gently against his hip.

Inside it, wrapped in a square of dark silk, was a small object Cyria had passed him with a small bow yesterday afternoon. A higher-tier teleportation talisman, the kind that could be used across folded spaces. Single-use. Vulpine had three of them in total. He was wearing one of those three, and Cyria had not said what it had cost her to give it to him, but Cyria did not need to say a thing like that out loud.

If it came to it, he could use it. From inside the Fold.

If it came to it.

His heart was pacing up.

He could feel it in his teeth. In the small skin behind his ears. In the slight tremor in the fingers of his human hand where they rested on the brass rail. He drew a slow breath through his nose and let it out slowly through his mouth, the way Virelle had taught him for the lightning, but the rhythm did not steady him quite the way it had on the islet.

He was not, after all, about to fire a bolt.

A small hand closed around his.

It was his human hand, the right one. Virelle’s fingers were cool. She did not squeeze. She simply rested her palm against his, fingers interlaced, the way a person reaches for the hand of someone they are about to stand beside through something they cannot do alone.

He looked at her.

She was looking at the islet, not at him. But there was a very faint flush on her cheeks, the colour she sometimes got when she had decided in advance to do something that would have, on any other day, mortified her to be seen doing.

“It will be all right, Master,” she said.

Her voice was quiet.

“You are much stronger now than you were a week ago. You must not forget that. And you must not forget the other thing. You are not in this alone. I am here. I will be here through whatever happens. You will not be alone in any room you walk into today.”

Aiven’s mouth lifted, very slightly, at the corner.

“Thank you,” he said.

Virelle sniffed, then, the small sniff she used when she was about to follow tenderness with theatre, as if to balance the books.

“In truth,” she added, with a small lift of her chin, “I alone would likely be sufficient to handle this. The last time we faced him, I was caught off-guard. He arrived without warning. He spoke to you in his calmest voice and I had no time to prepare. That will not happen today. I have spent the past week preparing certain countermeasures. He will find me considerably more inconvenient this time.”

Her thumb brushed once, very briefly, across the back of his hand.

“So you may relax, Master. I have, as it were, taken several liberties on your behalf.”

Aiven looked at her for a long moment.

The nervousness in him did not vanish. But it grew smaller, the way a fire grows smaller when someone has put a lid on the kettle. He gave her hand a careful squeeze. He did not say anything. He did not need to.

The air shifted.

It was not a sound. It was a small, cold change in pressure, the kind a person feels in their sinuses before a storm. Virelle’s prismatic orb gave a sharp, attentive pulse and rose to her shoulder. Aiven’s hand tightened on the rail.

In the middle of the islet, a long jagged seam opened in the empty air.

The seam was violet, the same violet as the portal Lucien had used to leave the clearing six days ago. It hung in the air for a moment, edge-on, like a tear in a piece of paper. Then it widened.

Lucien stepped through.

He was alone.

His hood was back. His golden eyes were exactly where Aiven had been expecting to find them. He was dressed in the same long dark robe, the same neat collar, the same precise calm. He looked, in every measurable respect, as if he had simply walked out of a study and across a hallway and into a room he expected to find them in.

Lyra was not with him.

Aiven’s stomach dropped, quietly, the way a stone drops in deep water.

Case three.

He drew a breath.

He let go of Virelle’s hand, just long enough to take the rail with both of his. Then he pushed off, and they both lifted.

He was not flying gracefully. He was still wobbling at the edges. But he had been practicing this exact descent for two days now, the smooth glide from the deck of the scout-ship down to the open grass, and he could perform it without falling. Virelle drifted alongside him with the practiced ease of a person who had been flying since the day she was made.

They came down softly, perhaps ten paces from where Lucien stood.

Aiven’s boots touched the grass.

Lucien tilted his head, very slightly. His mouth had taken on a small, considering shape.

“My,” he said. “You have been spending your time wisely.”

He took a small, measuring breath in.

“Although I notice the wisdom is the kind that prepares for consequences, rather than the kind that avoids them. That is, I will admit, the kind I had quietly hoped to encounter today.”

Aiven did not bother with pleasantries.

“Where is Lyra."

Lucien smiled.

It was the same gentle, paternal smile Aiven remembered. The smile of a teacher who had been waiting for the correct question, and was now able to deliver the planned response.

“I thought you might wish to see her,” he said. “Follow me into the Fold, Aiven. I would not deprive you of her company today.”

He half-turned. The violet seam at his back widened slightly.

Aiven’s mind moved very quickly.

Case three had just become case two, in the space of a single breath. He did not have to convince Lucien of anything. Lucien was already opening the door. Which meant Lucien wanted him in the Fold. Which meant, almost certainly, that Lucien had a reason for wanting him there.

A fight on enemy terrain. With Lyra somewhere in that terrain. With one teleportation talisman in his coat pocket, and one Virelle at his side, and one obsidian arm on his shoulder, and one ocean behind a regulator that had agreed to anything he had asked of it for the past six days.

It was not a great hand.

But it was the hand he had.

He stepped forward.

Virelle stepped with him.

They walked through the seam.

The Fold looked exactly as it had the first time.

The same long, vaulted hall, ribbed with pillars of pale stone that seemed to extend further upward than any roof would have allowed. The same diffuse, sourceless light, neither warm nor cool. The same soft, ambient hum of a space that was not entirely inside the ordinary geometry of the world.

There was no one in it.

Lucien walked ahead of them.

His pace was unhurried. The cadence of a man giving a tour of a museum at his own pace, without bothering to check whether the visitors were keeping up. The Fold accommodated him. The floor beneath his feet sounded, very faintly, the way a wooden gallery floor sounds under a tutor’s shoes.

Aiven walked behind him. Virelle drifted at his side.

After perhaps thirty paces, Lucien spoke without turning.

“Have you truly thought things through, Aiven? Over the past week. Properly?”

Aiven kept his voice even.

“I have. I had already thought things through before our first meeting.”

Lucien made a small sound. It was not quite a sigh.

“That is a pity,” he said. “I must confess, I had hoped a week would soften the answer. People do tend to grow more reasonable when they have been left alone with the weight of a decision for a few days. You are, I see, the unusual sort whom the weight makes more decided rather than less.”

He paused.

“It is genuinely unfortunate. I do not like to see a talent of your kind set itself against me and die so very young.”

The last word landed in the hall like a small pebble in water.

Aiven felt Virelle move.

It was a small motion, smaller than the casual eye would have caught, but he was tuned to her now in a way he had not been tuned to anyone before. He felt her draw a sharp breath. He felt her body shift forward by perhaps half an inch. He felt her hand, at her side, gather the first quick wisps of lavender heat.

She was about to strike Lucien from behind.

Aiven’s hand closed around her wrist.

He did not look at her. He kept his eyes on Lucien’s back. He simply held her wrist gently, the way he had been held in the clearing six days ago, and she stilled.

Lucien turned.

His golden eyes settled on her hand, on Aiven’s grip on her wrist, on the small dissipating wisp of lavender at her fingertips, with the unhurried curiosity of a man examining a footnote.

“It is quite all right, Starweave,” he said. “I bear you no ill will for it. Although I will note, for the historical record, that it would not have done very much. You are, at this point, not in a position to do meaningful damage to me.”

Virelle’s chin lifted.

“Would you,” she said, low and cool, “like to find out?”

Lucien smiled.

“I know exactly what you are capable of. I gave you your shape. I gave you the language in which you think about yourself. There is nothing inside you that I have not, at some level, written.”

He turned away again.

“But we are wandering off-topic. Come.”

The hall opened.

Where there had been long vaulted stone a moment before, the space ahead simply widened, the way a room widens in a dream, and a low dais rose from the floor to meet the new geometry. On the dais was a chair. The chair was not, strictly, a throne. It was too plain for that. But it was raised, and it was central, and the architecture of the hall arranged itself around it the way architecture arranges itself around the place a tutor stands in a hall of students.

Beside the dais stood Lyra.

She was in the same outfit Aiven had last seen her in. The traveler’s robe. The brown scarf at her shoulder. Her honey-blonde hair was loose and clean.

Aiven’s breath caught.

Lyra turned at the sound of their footsteps.

Her face was uncertain.

It was not the empty, dissociated stillness of a person under heavy compulsion. It was not the soft, broken warmth of the woman in the clearing six days ago either. It was something in between, a careful, wary uncertainty, the expression of a person who had been told to wait somewhere and was not yet sure what she was supposed to do when the people she had been waiting for arrived.

Her deep blue eyes found Aiven’s.

They held him for a moment.

She did not smile. She did not speak. Aiven did not know what to read in her face, and he did not have time to try.

Lucien turned, halfway up the dais, and looked at them.

“Let us be efficient, Aiven. I know what you want. You want her.”

He gestured, lightly, toward Lyra.

“Therefore, let us discuss a trade.”

He let the word trade hang in the air for a polite second.

“Lyra goes with you. You take her back to your scout-ship. You set up whatever small protagonist’s life you wish to lead. You decline to interfere in my work. In exchange, Starweave remains here. With me.”

He spread his hands.

“It is a clean exchange. One human, of unique gifts, in return for the safe delivery of another. You walk away with the person you have been looking for. I retain the asset whose creation, you will remember, was a great deal of work for me. Everyone is paid. Everyone goes home. No one needs to die today.”

He looked at Aiven.

“It is a generous offer, considering. I do not, as a rule, make generous offers. I am making this one because I would prefer not to have to kill a talent of your kind, and because I am sentimental about my keysmith, and because Starweave belongs, in any honest accounting, with me.”

Virelle did not say a word.

Beside him, Aiven felt her go very still. Her hand, which he had been holding at her wrist, did not pull away. It did not curl into a fist. It did not flare with lavender. It simply rested, perfectly still, against his palm.

She had drawn breath to retort. He had felt it in her shoulders.

And then she had not retorted.

Because some quiet part of her wanted to hear his answer, too.

She had told him, in a small cabin on a small ship in the small lantern-light, that there was a wicked thing inside her that wanted Lyra gone. She had told him so honestly that she had cried about it. He had said he did not blame her. He had said he would think things through after, and tell her, and be honest.

Aiven did not look at Virelle when he answered.

“No.”

His voice was quiet. He did not raise it. He did not embellish it. The word was simply, neatly, set down on the floor of the hall.

“Virelle is not a tool to be exchanged.”

Lucien tilted his head.

“You speak as if she belongs to you.”

He said it without heat, the way a man might correct a small factual mistake.

“You must remember, Aiven. There would be no Virelle without me. I made her. From a dungeon. From years of preparation. I gave her her form, her thought. She is, in any meaningful sense of the word, my work. You did not summon her so much as accept her into your home. The credit, the cost, the authorship — all of it is mine.”

Aiven did not look away.

“Regardless.”

He let the word sit.

“Virelle has made it clear to me that she does not wish to stay with you. She has told me, in her own words, that this is what she wants. And whatever the question of authorship is, whatever the question of who-made-whom is, she gets to decide what happens to her now. I am going to respect that. I would expect you to respect that too, but I do not, in fact, expect you to.”

Beside him, Virelle’s hand had begun to tremble, very faintly.

Not in fear.

Lucien was quiet for a long moment.

Then he gave a small, almost regretful sigh. The same small, almost regretful sigh he had given at the end of their conversation in the clearing.

“I see,” he said.

He turned, slowly, until he was facing them fully. His golden eyes settled on Aiven’s with the gentle attention of a tutor at the end of an oral examination that had not gone the way he had hoped.

“Then, I suppose, there will be no deal today.”

He let out a small breath.

He raised one pale hand.

He did not bother with a gesture for theatre. He did not raise his voice. He did not name the spell. He simply curled his fingers, slightly, the way a tutor might curl a finger to dismiss a class.

“Aiven Roan,” he said.

His voice was almost kind.

“Die.”
