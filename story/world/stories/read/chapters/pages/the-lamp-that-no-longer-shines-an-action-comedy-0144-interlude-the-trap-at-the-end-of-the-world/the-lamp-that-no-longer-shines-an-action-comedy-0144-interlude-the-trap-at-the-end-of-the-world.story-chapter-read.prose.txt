[Time Span]: 1995 – 1998[Location]: White City · Tulip Club / The Witch's House / Milan'thirskaya Vacant Penthouse

The dust from the Tulip Club's demolished VIP lounge was still settling around them.

Where the floor used to be, there was now a structural void several stories deep, the result of a Wellington heir casting an obliteration spell directly at her own feet. The air smelled violently of pulverized marble and residual Tier-5 Aegis magic.

Rhode von Ludwig stood in the wreckage, her eyes burning in the dark. She had won the exchange. It tasted like nothing. The Heir had only lost because she had split her processing power mid-free-fall to calculate a safety corridor for a weeping cousin.

Rhode raised one hand, halting the advance of the Ludwig kin behind her.

"That time doesn't count," Rhode said, adjusting her collar with contemptuous precision. "You lost on a technicality. Next time, Wellington, you won't have a cousin to catch."

Cecilia stood a few paces away. Her clothes were, inexplicably, completely immaculate. She looked at Rhode with eyes the color of deep water at the bottom of a trench: still, pressurized, and entirely without performance.

"If the conclusion displeases you," Cecilia said, "we can correct it now. One on one."

The mana in the air spiked. Rhode's smile went feral. Cecilia's fingers shifted to the geometry of a counter-array.

Then, slow footsteps echoed from the shattered corridor.

"If you are quite finished redecorating my city."

Liandra Milan'thirskaya stepped into the light.

A pair of white branching antlers rose from her brow, catching the light with the unhurried certainty of something that had never once considered being small. Her tail moved behind her with its own quiet gravity. Her pupils were vertical slits of shattered grey glass.

She moved with a slow, predatory ease, exuding the particular decadence of a youngest daughter who had never once been asked to be responsible for anything and had dedicated the resulting freedom to wasting herself as thoroughly as possible.

She planted herself between the two factions and looked at Rhode with the flat, crushing boredom of someone who found the entire situation beneath comment. "Alucard is going to be here in ten minutes to deal with the property damage. Go play elsewhere, Ludwig. She's already going to make me file three years' worth of incident reports."

Rhode scowled. But she lowered her hands and stepped back into the ruins.

Liandra turned.

Cecilia's fingers had not relaxed. The historical bad blood between their houses was its own pull. Cecilia had every reason to expect the second blow to come from a different direction.

But Liandra didn't project a single thread of killing intent. She looked at the eighteen-year-old Wellington heir for a long, quiet moment, and then gave a faint, almost imperceptible nod.

[The Witch's House · Six Months Later]

The Witch's House carried its usual amber warmth: low conversation, the measured strains of whatever was playing on the stage, and the particular charge of a room full of high-tier mana. Cecilia sat at the center of it like a soundproofed vault.

She offered the room nothing. She kept her eyes fixed on the slow, deliberate collapse of an ice cube in her amber liquor. She didn't adjust her hair, didn't trace the rim of her glass, didn't shift her weight to acknowledge the music from the stage.

She just sat there, sealed and still, holding her posture with the taut, fragile geometry of someone actively bleeding out from a wound nobody else could see.

And it was quietly tearing the social fabric of the room apart.

The bartender had walked past her section three times to wipe down an already immaculate counter, stealing brief, helpless glances at her profile.

A few stools down, a heated debate had died, the patrons unconsciously angling their shoulders toward her like plants leaning toward a dying sun. The sheer density of her quiet, uncomplaining damage had a pull all its own. It bypassed etiquette entirely and triggered a sudden, almost violent panic in whoever looked too long.

People looked at the devastating stillness of the Wellington heir and immediately wanted to throw their own lives on the bar counter. Let me put a wall around you. Take everything I have. Just look up at me.

A Witch from a minor noble house had already crossed the event horizon.

Her eyes were bright with that exact, feverish devotion. She was hovering inches from Cecilia's shoulder, leaning in far too close, having completely abandoned her own aristocratic dignity after three failed openers.

Cecilia had declined each one with the same quiet, Antarctic courtesy, never once lifting her eyes from the melting ice.

A hand came down on the minor Witch's shoulder.

Liandra sat onto the empty stool, not looking at either of them, and merely allowed a fraction of the Milan'thirskaya bloodline's ambient pressure to leak into the immediate air. The Witch went white and evaporated. Liandra signaled the bartender for a bottle.

"If the crowd irritates you," Liandra said, not turning, her voice carrying the rasp of expensive alcohol and chronic sleeplessness, "why sit at the open bar? Get a private booth."

Cecilia watched the last sliver of ice complete its dissolution.

"Because I do not want to be alone," she said.

Liandra's hand paused on the neck of the bottle.

She looked at the Wellington heir's profile, the amber light moving across the perfect architecture of her face, the deep stillness in her eyes, and recognized the frequency of it. The specific, load-bearing solitude of someone for whom being surrounded by people and being alone had ceased to be meaningfully distinct categories.

She filled her glass. They sat shoulder-to-shoulder in the low murmur of the bar and shared a silence quieter than anything inside it.

[The Witch's House · One Year After That]

The story had been circulating all day.

Liandra had purchased an independent garden planet for the sole stated purpose of wanting to see fireworks. Over the course of one night, she had gotten blackout drunk, picked a duel over a perceived slight, and engaged in high-tier combat on the surface.

The crossfire had obliterated her opponent, sent three scions of prominent noble houses to the Underworld, and reduced the planet to a cooling rock in the space of four hours. Alucard had spent a week in parliamentary chambers, burying the incident under mountains of gold and political leverage.

When it was finally settled, Alucard had said nothing. She had simply walked into the room and slapped her youngest sister across the face.

With the passive regeneration of an Arch-Witch, the physical mark was gone from Liandra's cheek before she reached the street.

When she pushed open the door of the Witch's House that night, the bar went quiet by degrees as people clocked her arrival. They looked at her with the usual suffocating cocktail: mockery, fear, fawning greed, poorly concealed pity.

"All of you," Liandra said, her voice low and flat and carrying the specific, venomous calm that precedes something irreversible, "get out of my sight."

People scattered.

Cecilia was two stools down. She knew exactly what Liandra had done: the lives extinguished, the political wreckage, the slap that had left no physical mark. She did not flinch or scoot away or adjust her posture to signal moral distance.

Liandra dropped into the seat beside her. She stared at the bar surface, her breathing irregular.

"Ash."

The name came out barely above a whisper.

Then Liandra began to speak, not to Cecilia, not to the room, but to some internal corridor where time had frozen. Her words came out broken and directionless, slipping between tenses, between subjects.

She spoke about Ash as if the Ovelia War had never happened, as if Ash weren't the entity the Inner Sea called the Third Seat of the High Council, weren't the one the Inner Sea called Death. She spoke about her sister the way you speak about someone standing right beside you.

She talked about exiled mothers. She talked about long, quiet evenings in a house that kept getting larger as the people in it kept leaving. She talked about a law student who frowned at flies and baked cookies the night before she shipped out, and then—

She talked about a ticket to an amusement park.

"She promised," Liandra said. Her voice had lost all its decadent sharpness and acquired the small, terrified cadence of a child. "When the war was over. She tucked the tickets into the front cover of her law book. She promised she would take me."

Liandra's grip on the glass had gone white-knuckled.

"The park was demolished." She choked on the words. "They razed it. Built over it. She promised she would take me, and she promised, and now there's nothing left, and she promised—"

The tears came without warning. They slid silently into her liquor, heavy and absolute.

Cecilia sat for a moment in the quiet. Then she reached across and placed her hand over Liandra's where it gripped the glass.

"Yes," Cecilia said. Her voice sounded like it was coming from the far side of something: water, or time. "I know. She promised."

At those words, the last thread holding Liandra's composure snapped.

She buried her face in her free arm and wept, not the beautiful, tragic kind, but the raw, hyperventilating, agonizing kind, the kind that tears out of the throat and doesn't care who hears it.

Cecilia didn't let go. She kept her hand exactly where it was and stayed with her until the bar closed and the street outside went quiet and the sky over the White City began, at last, to turn grey.

When the tears finally stopped, Liandra sat up. Her eyes were bloodshot and her hair was a wreck and her coat was completely destroyed, but something in her spine clicked back into place. Old and unhurried, the aristocrat surfaced.

She stood, extended her arm to Cecilia with a studied, gentlemanly grace, and walked her to a waiting Milan'thirskaya griffon carriage.

They went to the nearest vacant property. The family had always owned more of the White City than they could inhabit.

The next morning, before she left, Liandra tossed a heavy brass key onto the kitchen counter without meeting Cecilia's eyes.

"I've never lived here," she said, her voice still hoarse. "It's yours."

[The Shared Penthouse · Two Years In]

Liandra stopped visiting the underground clubs.

Instead, she began returning to the penthouse in the early hours of the morning smelling of ozone, ruptured dimensional fabric, and the deep-space vacuum of high-risk hunting grounds. She would drop a bag of harvested arcane cores onto the floor, bleeding from minor spatial lacerations, and find Cecilia sitting by the window in unbothered silence.

Cecilia never asked where she had been. She marked her page in her textbook, stood up, and tossed Liandra a clean towel and a healing ampoule.

They didn't decorate. They occupied the vast, empty rooms of the Milan'thirskaya property like two soldiers sharing a foxhole after the rest of their battalion had been wiped out.

One evening, after Cecilia had recounted a story about something Evangeline had done, another multiversal warrant, another brilliant and unreachable piece of wreckage, Liandra sat for a long time in silence.

"What does she actually mean to you?" Liandra finally asked.

Cecilia looked at the city lights through the floor-to-ceiling window. She considered the question the way she considered all serious questions: with genuine, quiet weight.

"To be close to her," Cecilia said, "is to be close to pain." She paused. "To be away from her is to be away from happiness."

Liandra took a long, burning pull directly from the bottle. She set it down, propped her elbows on her knees, and pressed her forehead against the heel of her hand.

"I wish Ash were the younger sister," Liandra murmured. "I want her to know what it feels like. To be the one left standing."

Cecilia let out a soft, hollow laugh."A pity there are no ifs."

[The Same Penthouse · Three Years After the Tulip Club]

Cecilia was preparing to file her early graduation paperwork.

She was twenty-one. She could have graduated two years earlier. In her first year at Yggdrasil, in strict academic terms, she had possessed the knowledge for it. She hadn't. She had followed the standard timeline with methodical, clockwork precision, completing exactly the required credits for each year, no more, no less.

Because Cecilia had needed a schedule. She had needed a fixed track that would allow her to keep moving without having to stop and look at what she was moving through.

But the tracks had run out.

Several empty bottles sat on the penthouse floor. Cecilia was sitting directly on the hardwood, her back against the glass of the floor-to-ceiling window, watching starlight refract through the pane. Tears fell silently down her face.

"Liandra," Cecilia said.Liandra looked up from the armchair. Her glass was empty."What does it feel like to die?"

"An Underworld civil servant processes your paperwork," Liandra answered, the phrasing automatic. "You take a number, wait in a very nice spa, and eventually your slot opens up."

Cecilia's mouth curved. "No." She turned to look at Liandra directly. Her ocean-blue eyes were drunk and perfectly lucid at the same time. "I mean true death."

The room went still.Liandra looked at her. She set down her glass.

"Cecilia," Liandra said, her voice stripped of its usual languid architecture. "Let's get married."

Cecilia didn't answer immediately. Her eyes widened fractionally. She looked at Liandra the way one looks at a sentence that doesn't parse.

And then she laughed.

It came out soft, and then it didn't. It built, unstoppable, helpless: she laughed until she was gasping, until she was flat on the hardwood with her hands over her stomach, until the tears on her face shifted from the other kind entirely.

When she finally caught her breath, she looked up.

"Who," Cecilia managed, "proposes like that?"

Liandra didn't smile. She waited.

The laughter drained. What replaced it was something Liandra had never seen on the Wellington heir's face in three years of proximity, the specific, electric clarity of a person who has just identified a frame they are willing to inhabit.

The composure had not returned. What was there instead was more honest and considerably more dangerous.

"Tell me your plan," Cecilia said softly.

Liandra sat forward. She described it plainly, without embellishment.

A wedding at the maximum feasible scale: the border between Holheim and Milan'thir, the Hanging Gardens of Babylon, neutral territory built into the coordinates, the kind of spectacle that the entire Inner Sea would be required to register. The two oldest houses of the world's aristocracy, their combined political weight demanding attendance, demanding coverage, demanding witness.

A stage so loud it could not be ignored.

And then: a hostage situation. Two younger sisters holding the knife to their own throats. Come back and stop this, or watch us ruin our lives with your worst enemies. A trap with two pieces of bait and two intended targets, designed to do the one thing that waiting had failed to accomplish.

The final layer of the trap went unspoken.

It occupied the space between them as clearly as anything either of them had said in the last three years: the mechanism at the bottom of the plan, the last contingency, the thing that would happen if the trap worked and the sisters still didn't come.

Liandra finished. She looked at Cecilia. Cecilia looked back.

Cecilia smiled. It was a slow, gentle smile, sweeter than anything Liandra had seen her wear in three years, and completely, structurally insane.

"Alright," Cecilia said. "Let's do it."

Liandra felt something surge through her, not hope, exactly. Something older and more reckless. She smiled back. It was a cruel expression, the kind that didn't bother disguising what it was: the deep, foundational ecstasy of a person who has spent years in freefall and has finally, at last, been given permission to fall.

Two bodies at the edge of the world, holding hands.
