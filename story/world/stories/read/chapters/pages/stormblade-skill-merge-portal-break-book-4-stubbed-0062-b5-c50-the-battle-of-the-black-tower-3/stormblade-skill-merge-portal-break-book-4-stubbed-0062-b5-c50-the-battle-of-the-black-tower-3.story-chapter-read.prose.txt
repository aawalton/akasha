The dragon soared overhead.

As he passed, columns of lightning arced up from the lightning towers atop the packed earth walls, seeming to walk under his long bulk. Everywhere they made contact, a second wave of electricity rippled out, this one pushed from him and into the portal metal spikes.

Explosions hammered my ears, the pressure wave trying to knock me over—but I’d stood on the walls as Angelo Lawrence went all-out, and I’d ran across the desert outside of Carlsbad when he made a second sun rise. Compared to that, the God of Thunder’s passing was bearable, for me. But not for the earthwork ramparts. Any lightning rod his electricity touched detonated like a bomb. So did the conduits hidden in the earth. All around the Lightning Tower, the Void Garden’s carefully constructed maze turned to so much piled earth, superheated glass, and red-hot portal metal.

And this was only his aura.

The Traynor-Overholz Cannons bristled outward from the Lightning Tower. They fired, filling the air with round shot that slammed into the God of Thunder’s lightning-scaled body. He roared—but I didn’t sense an ounce of pain or frustration in his voice. “You’re fighting well, Kade Noelstra. Let’s see what—“

It was at this point that the Stormshade Avatar hit him.

The column of crackling green-black lightning slammed into the God of Thunder like a missile. I hadn’t realized it could fly, but it sliced through the air almost like it had been launched; when it hit, thunder rippled across my portal world, and the shockwave knocked Ellen off her feet. Darkness welled all around the God of Thunder’s midsection. Blue-white lightning fought with green-black.

Ellen rolled. Lightning arced around her—the green-black kind—as she used Voidwalker Form again and poured out an Abyssal Storm, then layered it with Echoing Black. She rarely used that skill. It worked best against enemy casters. But right now, we needed every advantage we could get, and echoing the God of Thunder’s attacks while blinding him was a big advantage. If it could hold.

My job was to let it hold.

Stormrunner threw me through the air. My own lightning arced down just like the God of Thunder’s, and I sprinted forward on it. The gigantic dragon was, for a moment, blinded. Lightning Chain jerked me toward him. I landed sword-first; the Storm’s Blade ripped into him, punching into his static-lined flesh.

He roared. Lightning welled, a massive orb of blue-white fury, in front of his jaws.

It fired toward Ellen. She ducked behind the portal—the last thing in the Void Garden still standing after just thirty seconds of the God of Thunder’s true presence—and fired off another spell. Black Hole. This one ripped the Paragon downward. He crashed into the earth with another roar. All three of us poured everything we had into him. Shadowstorm Battery pulled at my Mana, and I pulled at Ellen’s.

Eugene righted himself. “Enough of this.” His jaws closed around the Stormkin Avatar’s column of shadow, ripping it in half, and my aura collapsed.

A second Eugene slammed into him, this one weaker and smaller and formed of pure shadow. His tail slammed into it. Thunder boomed. The shockwave tore earth apart below it and filled the air with dust. Sunbeams and lightning columns poured down from above as Cheddar and Pepperoni engaged.

And I hacked away at the God of Thunder. Laminar Blade. Stormfall. Everything in my kit. My Mana crept upward, getting me closer to being able to cast my offensive spells again. Shadowstorm Battery fed into me.

But everything we were doing—the entire fight—hinged on two small details.

First, that the God of Thunder stayed occupied enough not to notice the storm brewing over the Lightning Tower as the Eye of the Storm opened.

And second, that the portal to his world stayed open.

If either of those failed, we’d fail, too.

Ophelia St. Vrain had no ego.

She never had. She was exactly as good as she thought she was, exactly as talented. Ego only caused problems. It made no sense to have one when it only got in the way of her goals. People so often let themselves think they were smarter, more powerful, or more important than they actually were. Not her. She was exactly who she thought she was—someone who didn’t understand people, someone who was incredibly powerful by any normal standard, and someone who, in the grand scheme of things, wasn’t currently all that important.

But because she had no ego, she was very good at reassessing her earlier misconceptions. And her current assessment of other people was that, under the right circumstances, they could be very simple, and they could make a lot of sense.

For example, consider the fireball currently swerving through Phoenix’s sky, on a collision course for Sky Harbor, trailing spikes of ice meters long in its wake.

That was a unifying, logic-enforcing phenomenon.

Intellectually, Ophelia St. Vrain knew that the ball of fire and ice was not, in itself, supernatural. It was the end result of the Phoenix Reborn fighting an S-Rank monster. But she could understand her charges’ reaction to it. They were all screaming, pointing, and panicking.

Understandable. Much to Ophelia’s surprise. Still, she had a job to do, and she couldn’t do it if she was being swarmed by hundreds of terrified people. That simply wouldn’t work.

So, as the frostfireball zoomed toward Sky Harbor—setting palm trees and cacti ablaze in its wake before freezing them solid—Ophelia started with the simplest solution first.

She threw an exclusion zone around her. A weak one—E-Rank—but more than enough to hold against a bunch of scared, pathetic, unawakened humans. She pushed it slowly out until she’d forced the mob of refugees back a few hundred feet from the control tower, then hopped out the shattered window and landed on the runway below.

Then she got to work. For something like what was coming, she’d need the strongest exclusion zone she’d ever created, in the smallest space she’d ever made one. Her Mana dropped precipitously as she crafted layer after layer of exclusion into a tiny package no more than a foot across and six inches long. A dozen layers. Two dozen. The most condensed exclusion zone, built around a grain of sand.

Then she used her A-Rank Unique Skill upgrade and pushed the packet of ‘Nope. You can’t be here’ directly into the fireball.

After all, her job was simple. Do not, under any circumstances, allow the civilians at Sky Harbor to get hurt. And a pair of S-Rank juggernauts crashing down on the airport-turned-refugee camp certainly qualified.

Nathan was losing.

It was no shame for him to admit it. He wasn’t Angelo Lawrence. No one in Phoenix was. That man had been a legend. He was just the Phoenix Reborn, a fire mage of greater-than-average skill and much greater-than-average resilience.

That was the only reason he hadn’t lost immediately. Anterox, Storm’s Sentinel, battered at him with lances of ice so sharp they would have cut through any armor he wore if he hadn’t micro-melted their blades into dull edges. The temperature rocketed between hot enough to melt steel and cold enough to freeze the air into solid ice. They grappled and hammered at each other, magic crackling and popping.

And the whole time, they pushed inevitably closer to Sky Harbor.

There was shame in losing if it meant wiping out the civilians there. And while Nathan Anders knew about the Lonely Mage’s power, he was confident that she wouldn’t be able to stop two S-Rankers going all-out. But he couldn’t stop, either. He had to keep fighting, because his job was to keep the S-Rank Paragons occupied, and he had to do his job.

The packet of exclusion layers hit him in the chest. Half of his ribcage was excluded. The rest stayed in motion, falling out of the sky. He watched as it slammed into Anterox and obliterated the monster’s pale blue body in a column a foot across and almost fifteen feet long. Blood poured down out of the sky as both he and the monster slammed into the ground.

The second-to-last thing that went through Nathan Anders’s head was a gloating thought. He’d won. The Paragon had lost. After all, he wasn’t called the Phoenix Reborn for—

The last thing was the runway’s tarmac.

“Oops,” Ophelia said as she turned her attention from the smoking, freezing mass she’d destroyed and focused on the next threat. After all, she had a job to do.

All across the wreckage of the Void Garden, the battle raged.

The God of Thunder moved impossibly quickly. Ellen’s spells only snared him for a fraction of a second at a time. Her shadows washed over him, digging into his flesh, only to be torn apart by the furious dragon’s movements.

Mine weren’t doing any better.

I only stayed attached to the SS-Plus-Ranked dragon by sheer force of will. The Storm’s Blade had sunk deep into his shoulder, and I hung on, lashed in place by a Lightning Chain. The God of Thunder’s lightning coursed through my nerves. Pain followed, but I ignored it. I’d gotten good at ignoring pain, after all. The skills I’d merged, the Laws I’d learned, the training I’d had at the God of Thunder’s hands and my desperate battles against powerhouses like Yalerox, Deborah Callahan, and the Craftsman—every one of them had hurt.

A little lightning was nothing.

My Stamina dropped like a rock as I tried to ignore the electricity pushing through me. It hurt, but I could tough through it. I knew I could.

And as long as I was here, stabbing the God of Thunder’s flank and back while I summoned clouds to fill the portal world’s skies and make up for the ruined lightning farms below, he couldn’t turn his full attention toward Ellen.

Toward what Ellen was doing.

Shadows poured into his portal world. They found purchase at the base of the massive pillars of stone, far below the blue-red sky. Tore at the monoliths, slowly wearing them down. A crack appeared. Then another.

I couldn’t see any of this. But she’d been fighting at half-strength this entire time as she stood on the border between two worlds. Most of her concentration wasn’t on the God of Thunder at all.

Hand of the Thunder God fired. The beam of lightning ripped, point-blank, into the monster’s flank. Rain poured out of the sky. It hammered both me and the Paragon I’d tied myself to, hitting like bullets. Every impact opened a tiny wound or bruise on my exposed skin, and even more Stamina vanished. But I couldn’t stop. Couldn’t hold back. Everything I had—the time had come to give it all.

So I did. The Storm’s Blade ripped from the dragon’s flesh, a gout of lightning trying and failing to cauterize the wound. It simply couldn’t burn a being made of itself. Slipstream cut dozens of chunks from the massive dragon’s body. They healed instantly. No matter what I did, the God of Thunder’s rank was simply too high.

I couldn’t hurt him. Not permanently. It felt like a fly biting a horse. An annoyance.

“Is this all you’ve got?” I ground out through clenched teeth. “If it is, you’re going to lose.”

“Oh, Kade Noelstra, I haven’t even begun to fight.” The God of Thunder stopped, his wings spreading wider and wider until they encompassed my entire portal world. Much like the Placid King had seemed to fill the space he’d been given with tentacles, the God of Thunder’s wings covered the sky overhead.

Chain Lightning broke.

I used Lightning Dance. The first jump brought me to the ground. The second dodged a burst of lightning thicker than a tree. The third and fourth kept me out of the God of Thunder’s talons and jaws, respectively. And the fifth brought me to the Lightning Tower’s threshold.

“Checkmate,” I said. The aftereffects of the God of Thunder’s lightning rippled through my veins. I spasmed and looked at my hand. Every nerve had burnt a bright red. I couldn’t loosen my death grip on the Storm’s Blade. “I win, Eugene. You lose.”

“Eugene? You’re still calling me that?” The God of Thunder hesitated. For a moment, I did, too. Then he continued. “And what do you mean, checkmate? Oh, that?”

He looked up at the still-unopened Eye of the Storm. It was close. Another few seconds, and I’d be able to open it. “That’s nothing. Even with it, you cannot match my power. Besides, kid, I’ve got another move to make.”

Far across the wreckage of the Void Garden, Ellen threw herself to the ground on the wrong side of the portal as , despite her best efforts, it slammed shut. A second one opened, right in front of me. I peered into it, blood running cold. “No.”

“Yes. Kade Noelstra, you’ve relied on your allies for far too long. The Eye of the Storm is powerful, but I don’t see any reason to sit here and let you blast away at me with Queen Mother Yalerox’s invention. Not when we can simply…fight elsewhere.

“Of course, you’re welcome to ignore me. I’d estimate that, from the time I enter your world, it will take forty-five seconds to start doing significant damage to your city. A minute and a half to level it and the desert around it. Two minutes—give or take a few seconds—to cause enough damage to render your world uninhabitable within a week or two. Any longer than that will be truly catastrophic.

“So, Kade Noelstra, I believe the check belongs to me, and you have only one move.”

Then, before I could stop him, the God of Thunder surged through the portal, and I followed as it slammed closed behind me.

The Earth shuddered.

Jessie was halfway across the city from the location she and Kade had talked about briefly, in her shock-absorbing wheelchair, and she still felt it. The world felt like it was collapsing.

“The God of Thunder shouldn’t be here,” she said. “Put him back where he belongs.”

Councilwoman Myers didn’t argue. She gave the same order.

All around Phoenix, every Script and Binding flared all at once as every core left in the Governing Council’s storage poured its energy into the 303 Wall. The Traynor-Overholz guns shifted to their new assignments almost automatically, every crew ready and waiting for this moment.

The ground shook all around Jessie and the others deep in their bunker as the God of Thunder turned to face an attacker that slammed into him like a ball of pure thunder and fury. The portal closed behind them. And Phoenix’s defenses got to work against the strongest portal monster ever to break into Earth.
