Kalsin gasped like a man pulled from deep water. His eyes snapped open, breath catching in his throat, and found himself staring at the roof with something soft pressing against his ears and keeping his head locked in place.

Ringtail’s face came into view and blocked out the roof, looking down at him. The soft things keeping him in place were her thighs. Covered thighs. She had dressed, Kalsin absently noted to himself.

“How long was I out?” Kalsin croaked.

She just kept staring. There was a hint of redness around her eyes.

Kalsin slowly pushed himself to a sitting position, her headlock letting him go with a reluctant smush. He rubbed his ears. They were still warm.

“You were hurting,” she said finally, voice low. “Not asleep. Not daydreaming. Convulsing, like, like you were locked in a nightmare.”

Kalsin scrubbed a hand down his face. The vision lingered in his mind—Ringtail, a younger Ringtail, running rooftops, her brother’s scream, the sound of a body breaking somewhere far below. And he felt everything that she did as it happened.

The imprint of her despair clung to him, still.

“How long?” he asked again.

“Hours,” she said. “I don’t know. Noon has passed, probably. I didn’t go outside. I thought you were dying.”

“I wasn’t.”

Ringtail slammed a hand against the pillow. “Thanks, Kalsin! That’s real fucking comforting!”

Yeah. She had every right to be upset, he figured. It would have been better if he actually told her what to expect. But as it stood, Ringtail wasn’t the only one around here with a talent for reckless decisions.

Kalsin opened his mouth to speak, then stopped, wincing as another flicker of her pain passed through him. Scar absorbing powers or not, he had no interest in repeating this experience anytime soon. Maybe it was time to set a new rule: one fresh trauma per month. Two, if he was feeling in a particularly heroic mood.

“I’m sorry, Rin.”

She answered with silence. Her frown didn’t ease up one bit.

“...I like it when you call me Ringtail,” she muttered eventually.

“Ringtail, then.”

Kalsin looked down at his stomach. His new scar sat exactly where hers had been. He suppressed the shiver of disgust that wanted to run across his back, then bent down to retrieve his shirt.

It is what it is. He had made his choice. If he wanted to walk this path, a tiny scar like that would be the least of his problems.

“What are you doing?” Ringtail asked, watching him swing off the bed and start pulling on his boots.

“Hm?”

“You were— You just—” she stuttered. “You can’t just perform a… a miracle like that and decide to leave without a word!”

Kalsin decided to play dumb. “What miracle?”

Ringtail scoffed, grabbed the hem of her shirt, and yanked it up with an aggressive move. “This miracle, Kalsin! This fucking miracle.” Her belly was nothing but a smooth expanse of toned skin.

Huh. In retrospect, there was not much point playing dumb.

He just shrugged, pulling on the strings and starting on his other boot. “It’s a long story, and not all that interesting. What you need to know is that you’re Clenn now.” He pointed with his chin toward the door. “I recommend you join the Clenn camp and disappear before the company finds out your scar has mysteriously popped out of existence. I think they have a Fény with them. They’ll chalk it up to some administrative error on their part, I’d bet.”

Ringtail stared at him, shirt still hiked halfway up her chest, like he’d just suggested she grow a second head and take it for a stroll in the town square. She then slowly let it drop and scooted closer to him on the bed.

“Are you a god?” she asked quietly.

Kalsin lurched back. “What? No!”

“Then, then what…”

“What am I? Uh,” he muttered, tightening the second boot. “Been asking myself the same question for a long time. Since it became apparent that I would never come into my…”

My surge, he bit off the sentence, finishing silently. There had always been something off about him. He just never realized to what extent.

Kalsin stood up and gave the room a once-over. His scabbard had landed against the far wall, belt coiled next to it in a heap. He walked over and began strapping it on. His fingers fumbled with the buckle at the back.

He heard paper crinkling, sheets ruffling, and then boots on the floor before someone batted his hands away and started fastening the strap. Kalsin stood with his head down, letting Ringtail help finish the loop. A cold breeze blew from outside. The sounds of faint chatter drifted through the thin overhead window.

“Thank you,” Ringtail said, finally. She leaned into him from behind, hugging his waist. “You gave me my life back. Thank you.”

Kalsin swallowed something heavy in his throat. “I… I’m not a good person, Ringtail.”

“You were good to me. Even when I didn’t deserve it.”

He let out a small laugh. “Liar. We were awful to each other.”

She smiled into his back. “Well, you just evened the score on your part, miracle boy. Which means that I owe you now.” Her hands started wandering southward. “And I think I know juuuust the way to make a small part of it up to you…”

Kalsin grabbed her hand. He wanted to speak, but didn’t know what to say. That healing her had not been part of the plan, that it had been foolish and impulsive? That they were likely never going to see each other again? None if it felt right to say. So he just stood there, relishing the sensation of someone holding him in the moment.

“You’re leaving, aren’t you,” she whispered.

Kalsin remained silent.

“I don’t want you to go.”

He didn’t want to go either. But sometimes, you had to choose a bad option to avoid a worse one. He raised her fist clenched around him to his mouth, kissing her knuckle. He didn’t know what Jack had planned. But it sure as hell didn’t involve another woman coming along with them.

“Goodbye, Ringtail.”

She let him go, and he pushed through the door without looking back.

The air had grown colder since early morning, the gray clouds gathering in strength on the horizon. Sudden gusts of wind tossed Kalsin’s hair, coming and going in short tosses and carrying a scent of salt with them. Men jogged around the edges of the training grounds, carrying baskets and rolling up straw mats to protect them from the looming storm.

Kalsin pulled his collar tight around his neck and started toward the storehouses. First thing’s first—he needed to fetch his éj-lantern. No traveler worth a damn wandered into the wilds without protection from Blindlight, ‘specially with the end of the month creeping closer. He’d also need rations, a sturdy bedroll, and oil for his steel. Came with the territory now, being a proper soldier with shield and chainmail and all.

He nodded to Crux as he walked past, the man in the process of disassembling the cooking station. There was still some broth left in the pot, and Kalsin slowed his steps as the smell hit him. He could grab a late lunch first, maybe.

“Sir.”

A voice pulled Kalsin from his thoughts. He looked up to see a woman standing before him. Her silver hair was woven into a single braid that ran down the front of her shoulder, and the armor she wore was less army-issue plate and more a hunter’s flexible leather gear. She stood ramrod straight, holding a spear nearly as long as her planted in the mud.

“Reporting for duty, sir,” she said, smashing a fist against her chest. For some absurd reason, her armor had a cleavage. And just a size too small to contain her prodigious proportions.

“Um,” Kalsin said. He glanced over his shoulder, wondering if a lieutenant was standing behind him. But no such luck.

“Permission to be at ease, sir?”

Kalsin’s hand fidgeted with his scabbard. “Er… yes?”

The woman’s stance relaxed, her foot set lightly apart and her free hand coming to rest behind her. Her eyes quickly assessed him from head to toe, gave herself a private nod, then focused back on him. “You are taking steps to arm yourself. That is good, sir, and I respectfully ask you to keep this habit. I can’t be with you at all times, and the Scout can only do so much in a scuffle.”

What the hell was she on about?

Kalsin looked to the side at Crux questioningly, but the man only shrugged and resumed scraping leftover broth into the next bowl.

“Sir, are you listening? Do you not…” the woman trailed off, tilting her head. She watched him for a moment, searching his eyes in a way that was more than a little awkward.

Kalsin shifted uncomfortably. “I’m sorry, miss, but… do I know you?”

Her eyes widened for a moment. She then snapped her heels back together, coming to attention once again. “I apologize, sir. I see this might not be the most opportune moment for debrief.” She thumped her chest again. “May I take leave for now?”

“Uhuh. Sure, lady. You do just that.”

“Thank you, sir. Your will is my desire.”

The woman then turned on her heels, her braid flipping around like a flag, and marched away in lockstep with the butt of her spear hitting the ground.

Kalsin watched her retreating form disappear behind one of the distant clay huts.

“You saw that too, right, Crux?” he said.

“Why the hell would I have not?” Crux said. He scratched his nose with his arm and then extended a bowl of steaming broth toward him. “Heated some for you, Ghost.”

Kalsin accepted it with an absent nod. “Huh. ‘Preciate it.”

“Maybe she’s an older gal from somewhere down the wall? It’s not uncommon for folk to get a bit shuffled around after an assault. And you do sorta look officier-like,” Crux said, dangling a spoon idly.

“Yeah. Yeah, maybe.”

Kalsin stared into the distance for another second before he took the spoon, blew on the broth, and started shoveling.

At any rate, older boy business was no longer his problem. He would get his stuff, he would wait till midnight, and then he would be off.

It was time to stop playing soldier, and start whatever the next chapter held for him.

Together—with Jack by his side.
