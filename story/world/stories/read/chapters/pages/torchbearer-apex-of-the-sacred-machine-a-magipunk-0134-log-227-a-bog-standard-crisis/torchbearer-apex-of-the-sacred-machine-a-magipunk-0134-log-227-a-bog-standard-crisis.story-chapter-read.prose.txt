As the clouds broke away under the unerring assault of the red beam of light, a ghastly wail began to waver through the streets. It took me a second before I recognized the air raid sirens howling their message. It had an oddly musical note to it, more desperate lament than monotone warning.

I already had one leg over the saddle before the first note died down. Scar and Swyne, however, hadn’t moved an inch. They eyed me with tempered curiosity and open distrust, respectively, and it was clear that they wouldn’t be along for the journey.

“Not sure this is the best idea,” Scar said with a pained smile.

“It is a stupid idea.” Swyne had crossed his arms again, still staring toward the beam of light. “They are not even going to let you inside, and even if you do get in, they are going to rough you up after.”

“That why you’re leaving?” I asked, fingers flexing around Pharus’ holster.

“Oh, they certainly tried,” the old man said, and suddenly, the wind felt a little colder. “Though they wouldn’t even have spotted us if someone hadn’t been flashy with their skills.”

“Look, I’m sorry, alright?” Scar said.

“Not your fault. You did what you had to. But no, the reason we’re leaving is because of the damn ‘tax’. That’s why they are so aggressive, you see. Because they want Cash. They hoard it, and no one knows what they do with it, but they sure as hell won’t let anyone else have it.” Swyne looked as through he wanted to spit on the ground, but he took a look at Brin and stopped himself.

“The Cracked control the… Cash?” I was still balancing the bike with one foot on the ground and one on the footrest. It was getting uncomfortable.

Scar scoffed, his wistful derision made manifest in sound. “Cracked, Police, what’s the difference?”

“Hey! Don’t talk about them like that, alright?” Swyne said to Scar. “This used to be a great place for kids to grow up!”

For his part, Scar just shrugged. “It’s going to hell now.”

“Been going to hell for years. At least it didn’t have any witches.” Swyne grumbled as he turned back to me. “Anyway, the kid is right. You got nothing to gain from going up there, and a lot to lose.”

Not breaking eye contact, I pushed the bike upright and slid into the saddle. “Maybe. But I have a debt to pay for the power I got.”

The words came to my lips as easy as breathing, and for a second, I was back in Peruti with a sword on my hip urging me forward, and Chris’ hand on my shoulder urging me to stay. So, halfway lost in my daydream, I didn’t have to think about what to say next.

“Seems to me that you aren’t exactly powerless, either. And I could use the help.”

But then the similarities shattered and the memory faded, blown away like dead leaves. Back in reality, or what I called reality these days, Scar watched me, searching for something in my eyes. I didn’t know what he was looking for or if he found it, but before long, he turned away, abashed.

Swyne scoffed.

“Cute. But I paid my dues a long time ago.” Then, as if something had just occurred to him, he tilted his head. His eyes narrowed, not in anger, but instead glinting with an intense curiosity that turned his gaze into a sterner mirror to Scar’s.

“Maybe you have, too,” Swyne said. Underneath the rough cynicism, there flowed a surprising gentleness. “Maybe you shouldn’t keep setting yourself on fire just to keep others warm.”

A cold autumn wind blew through the void left by everything he didn’t say because he did not know.

“I guess I will catch you later.” I said eventually, turning the bike around with a gentle squeeze of the throttle.

“Maybe,” Swyne said when I came to a brief halt at the end of my loop.

In lieu of goodbye, I nodded to him (grunt) and Scar (a crooked smile and a wave), then raced out of the parking lot and toward the crimson pillar.

Every second wasted was a second in which the Ferals closed in on the center of the Domain and thus, the kids. The cold, rational side of me made me obey traffic rules, anyway. It would be fine.

The power radiating from that grim beacon left no doubt that there would be some Ferals invading at the very least, but even considering the time I’d spent advancing Nexus and coming to this town, there hadn’t been enough time for the kids to travel far. Logically, there couldn’t be that many Ferals top answer Latirae’s call. It would be fine.

With the restrictions in this Domain, Nexus wasn’t able to calculate a route for me, so it took longer to weave through the streets than I would have liked. I didn’t have time to worry about it, though, and the pillar of red was all the destination marker I needed. It would probably be fine. Cold autumn air buffeted by face as I drove, and I had to be careful to avoid slicks of wet leaves on the road, which added to my travel time. It should be fine, though. Wouldn’t it?

As seconds stretched into minutes, other worries started scratching at my cold detachment. How could this have happened? What was Latirae’s plan? She was a goddamn Feral, so how could she even have a plan? And why, oh why, could she not leave me the fuck alone?

As I raced toward the center of town, Red brick walls rose on either side of the street, standalone houses making way to terraces, then condos. I had to skirt around the hill at the center of town because of some road closures, which cost me precious time. I leaned deeper into the turns.

A few minutes later, I finally arrived at the barricade and came to a sliding halt. Despite its prodigious size, Barricade certainly was the right word for it. Cars, trash cans, telephone poles, shopping carts, literally anything that was sturdy or sharp had been jammed together to blockade the street from wall to wall. The place was full of people, too, more than I had seen in one place since the Palace courtyard. Some scurried about, carrying supplies or making last-minute repairs, but most just stood atop the wall and stared toward the crimson pillar with apprehension.

When the crowd got too thick to continue driving, I swung myself off of the bike without a second thought and pressed on. Several people eyed me when I shouldered past them, and I earned several angry shouts by others—most of them smoking cigarettes—but I ignored them as best I could.

Latirae’s beacon crackled in the distance like the judgment of an angry god, called on me to soothe its rage. Finally, I reached the foot of the wall. It was about two times as tall as me, with dozens of defenders atop its crown, and no gate in sight. I didn’t have time to look for one, either. With one last check of Pharus and Manus, I wrapped Arx tighter, crouched, and funneled all my processing power into my legs.

Then, in one single leap, I flew over the barricade, and at the apex of my rise, finally caught a glimpse of the breach. All I saw on my way down was devastation. The myriad floodlights all around the perimeter somehow only seemed to darken the shadows in the valley of death that lay beyond it. Burned-out buildings, broken streetlamps, shattered cars, and far too many traces of dried blood. I thought I saw another, older barricade in the middle distance, broken to pieces by thousands of savage attacks.

Another dose of shame and anger lanced through my heart, but now was not the time to contemplate.

I landed on the cold, ash-covered asphalt in a roll, and wasted no time breaking into a run. Before the first screams of fear and annoyance could rise behind me, I’d already turned a corner and was halfway through summoning another iteration of Nexus.

I got on the bike as soon as it materialized, and headed for the red beacon still thundering in the distance. My priority had to be keeping the kids safe, but if Latirae kept broadcasting her location like that, why not pull out the problem at the root? My eyes narrowed as I cold-forged hazy blueprints into hyperrealistic plans.

As I approached the source of the light, however, the streets became increasingly hard to navigate. I had to slow down, which gave me more and more time to take in my surroundings, outlined as they were in the color of neon blood. Somehow, it made the real bloodstains only stand out more, and there were many of them. Eventually I had to get off the bike and walk, and with each broken door, each shattered window, and each charred playground crossing my path, my contempt for myself rose until it threatened to shatter my composure like ice in spring.

I never should have let this happen. Dashing through a charred park and dodging twisted metal benches, I grabbed Pharus to stop it from bouncing against my leg. This was all my fault, because I didn’t put two and two together, focused as I had been on myself, and myself alone. Like a distant specter, my anger rose until it became but a physical force that threatened to snap me in half, but I pushed it down with all the cold determination I could muster. Down that path lay the graves of far too many friends.

No, hating myself wouldn’t help.

Cold logic would.

As I wove my way through rubble and destroyed cars, I kept my eyes locked on the beam in the distance. The fact that I hadn’t seen any Ferals so far relieved me, especially after I double-checked to make sure Ardor’s main functions were running at nominal levels, and I hadn’t been blinded like on the palace square. Then I remembered that for every Feral I wasn’t seeing, Latirae had just gotten stronger.

Despite my frustration, I couldn’t rush. On one hand, my surroundings were ideal for sneaking up on unsuspecting foes. On the other, the same could happen to me. The destruction only became worse as I progressed, and soon I found myself in a maze of broken bricks, writhing electric cables, and twisted metal that slowed my pace to a crawl.

To call it frustrating was an understatement. While I had to hoard my Logic and keep my head on a swivel, Latirae was getting stronger by the second, feeding on the Ferals she was dominating. I briefly debated throwing caution to the wind and committing to a full charge, but that was the old Sam speaking, the one I no longer wanted to be. If it had just been me, alone in my Domain, and safe in blissful ignorance, perhaps I would have said ‘fuck it’ and gone wild. Now though, I knew people’s lives were at stake, and the risk of Ferals slipping past me was far more terrifying than any threat to my own life. If they got to the rest of the city, it would be a slaughter, and there was a real chance that they would make it all the way to Pina, and I’d never hear the end of it. If she even survived.

No. Way.

What good was all the Logic in the world if I laid in some cave, lost and forgotten, with the blood of children spattered over my case?

So I took a deep breath, and willed the torrent of Logic inside me to stir. There were many options I could have chosen, but in the end, there was only one that all but guaranteed killing three Ferals with one handcannon, so to speak.

A cold smile cracked through my gloomy mood.

I focused on the determination, reassuring and calming my mind. I remembered Zephyro’s even-headedness in the face of extreme danger, and the cold, soothing light that accompanied his abilities. I reminded myself of feeling safe, and in control.

Then I exhaled.

The world froze, shifted, realigned and folded in on me. Then, with the tolling of a temple bell, the Logic exploded upward in a blazing pillar of cyan.
