The voices of the song-girls in the teahouse did nothing to soothe the ringing in Patriarch Yeon’s ears.

Across the table, his long-time friend, a private broker named Gi-taek, stared down at a single sheet of paper bearing the official stamp of the Black Ink Consortium. The man’s fingers were shaking slightly as he set his pipe down in an iron tray.

“Five hundred taels,” Yeon rasped at him. He slammed a calloused hand onto the table, rattling the cups. “Gi-taek, I’m telling you there was nothing in that damn box! We completed the mission successfully, and no one stole anything. The box was verified and sealed before we even picked it up!”

“My friend, please, lower your voice,” Gi-taek hissed, glancing nervously towards the bouncers at the door.

“I will not lower my voice! They hired us to carry an empty box – and then blamed us for theft when we delivered it!” Patriarch Yeon leaned forwards as he raised his voice, his hands supporting his weight on the table. “This so-called appraisal of damages...it’s madness. It’s fantasy! There never was any jade statue!”

Gi-taek rubbed his temples, refusing to meet his friend’s eyes. “It doesn’t matter what you say – the sender was a prominent antiquities dealer in Jinan, and the cargo was verified by a registered notary. The evidence is iron-clad, and regardless…the Consortium has paid them out already.”

“You think I don’t know that?! I’m saying they should have fought back, not just…paid him!” the Patriarch almost screamed, his face turning red. He shrank back a little when he noticed the dozens of heads turning to stare at him from around the teahouse.

“It’s too late! The money’s already changed hands. Legally, you are liable…”

But the Patriarch had already stopped listening. All they have to do is hire your agency through a proxy, she’d said. Then, they also hire the bandits who ambush you on the road. Suddenly, you owe them ten thousand taels.

It wasn’t ten thousand. But five hundred was more than enough to achieve the same result.

He was ruined.

“You brokered this deal, Gi-taek,” Yeon pleaded, his anger struggling against a growing sense of despair. He reached across the table and grabbed his friend’s sleeve. “You know people at the local Black Ink branch. Tell them the whole thing was a fraud! If I have to pay back five-hundred taels, with or without interest…the 72 Springs escort agency is finished. Everything I’ve built over the last twenty years will be gone!”

The song-girls chose that moment to finish their routine, causing a round of applause to break out. As it did, Gi-taek slowly pulled his sleeve from Patriarch Yeon’s grasp. He looked across the table with a deep sadness in his eyes. “I’m sorry, my friend. But there’s really nothing I can do.”

“What do you mean, there’s nothing you can do?!”

“I’m just a broker! The contract was signed, all the correct legal steps were followed, and the Consortium already paid out the money. Now, according to the contract, it’s your turn to pay them back. There is simply no judge in the Middle Kingdom that would take your side on this. Some might even order you beheaded, convinced that you did steal the statue. If anything, you should be grateful that it’s only silver they’re demanding.”

Patriarch Yeon slumped back in his chair. “If I sell all the gear we bought, I can raise…sixty taels. If I also sell all the horses and the wagons, I could get...maybe another eighty. That’s all I have, Gi-taek! It’s all I have.”

“That still leaves…three-hundred and sixty taels. And that’s before interest,” his friend murmured, looking away. “You might be able to keep up for a few weeks, but by the end of the second month, you’ll miss a payment. When that happens, they won’t wait to see if you can scrape any more coppers together. They’ll declare you to be in default…and they’ll come to seize your estate.”

The Patriarch’s eyes widened, his breath catching in his throat. “My family’s…estate?”

Gi-taek nodded. “They’ll take everything they can get their hands on. Land, heirlooms, women, children. I’m sorry, but the 72 Springs escort agency isn’t going bankrupt. It’s going to be repossessed.”

Yeon stared at his friend, his mouth falling open as the sheer cruelty of the trap began to sink in. He looked down at his immaculate, polished iron bracers, realizing with a sickening jolt of despair that they were just part of a costume he was wearing to his own funeral.

Patriarch Yeon was a senior disciple, a capable martial artist, and a veteran experienced in combat. He would be confident standing his own against half a dozen thugs. But there was no fighting his way out of this.

“They planned this,” the Patriarch whispered, his voice shaking as he stared into the murky liquid in his cup. “From the very moment they offered me that contract...they knew this was how it would end.”

And so did she.

Gi-taek sighed, picked up his pipe, and puffed out a thick cloud of grey smoke.

“There’s only one thing we can do now, my friend,” he said, as he motioned for a serving girl to approach, and a new round of songs began.

“And that is for us both to get very, very drunk.”

Chae-ha’s slow and methodical recovery in the infirmary had finally given a bit of her mobility back. Grimacing slightly, she paced awkwardly around the perimeter of her room, pushing the limits of her ability to move around but glad for any opportunity to be out of bed.

It wasn’t long before her side began to twinge. With a small sigh she returned to the foot of her bed, sitting down on it with abundant caution. As she felt the panels of her scholar’s robes settle against her legs, she ran her fingertips over the edges, feeling the faint ridges she’d stitched into the fabric. Master Tak had delivered her order of steel caltrops three days ago, and she had spent her mornings carefully slotting them into place. They now lined both sides of the bottom halves of the panels, safely out of the way so she wouldn’t accidentally sit on them.

A noise filtered in from the hallway, and Chae-ha’s ears perked up. It was the thud of a familiar pair of boots, which usually foreshadowed Mu-cheon’s arrival. This time, however, the sound was accompanied by several other, lighter footsteps. She invited them in; the door swung open, and the Young Master stepped inside. Just after him followed four men dressed in outer disciple robes.

“Good morning, little sister,” Mu-cheon said, offering a bright, easy grin. “I brought some people I’d like you to meet.”

The man leading the small detachment stepped forwards, his posture perfectly military. He had the weathered, sun-baked look of a man who had lived on campaign rations and slept in the dirt. He seemed to be in his late twenties, with a serious, no-nonsense expression, a small, flat nose, and a neat topknot that reined in what looked to be surprisingly curly hair. His gaze was sharp and discerning, sizing up the slight girl in scholar’s robes in front of him with the quiet caution of a career soldier.

“These are the guards I promised you,” Mu-cheon said, gesturing to the group. “They were a five-man unit until they lost a member during the battle for the Iron Creek mine. They’ve all seen combat; they know how to hold a line, and how to work together.”

The leader nodded, striking his fist proudly against his chest in a practiced military salute that was aimed directly at Mu-cheon.

“The one in front is Sergeant Noh Gu-shim,” Mu-cheon said, gesturing to the man with the curly hair. “He and his men will be your personal guard from here on out.”

Chae-ha picked up on the name immediately – Noh. She glanced up at Mu-cheon, who gave her a very brief, knowing nod. Gu-shim was a younger cousin of High Elder Noh Gwan-ho, the master of the Disciplinary Hall, whose life Chae-ha had once saved by unmasking a traitor. Of course, the soldier standing in front of her knew nothing of that.

“Young Master,” Gu-shim called out eagerly, maintaining his salute, “it’s an honor to serve you. If you hadn’t held the rear, not once, but twice, our unit would have lost a lot more than one member. If this young lady is someone important to you…we’ll gladly protect her with our lives.”

Chae-ha looked over the four veterans, her expression calm but a hint of amusement dancing in her eyes. “I am grateful to you all for agreeing to work with me, and for your dedication to the Young Master.” She took a deep breath.

“I’m sorry, but I’m still confined to this room for the time being, so I won’t be of much use for a while. Once I’ve healed, however, we can schedule some time to train together. If we’re going to work as a team on the battlefield, it’s important that you get used to my fighting style.”

Gu-shim’s eyebrow twitched, his gaze finally shifting down to her thin body, her scholar’s robes, and the delicate silver star embroidery decorating them. He looked back at Mu-cheon, his expression a mix of confusion and polite skepticism.

“I’m sorry miss, but…I think you should leave the fighting to the professionals. You just stay back and follow our orders; we’ll make sure you’re safe.”

Mu-cheon let out a booming laugh, clapping the burly veteran hard on his shoulder.

“I think you’ve got things a bit backwards, Gu-shim,” Mu-cheon informed him, a wry grin splitting his face. “You’re going to follow her orders, not the other way around. And she’s not staying behind, she’s coming to the battlefield with me. That’s why we need you to protect her.”

A look of bewilderment crossed all four of their faces. “What…I mean, Young Master, why…why would we…”

Mu-cheon folded his arms. “If I have to follow her orders, so do you.”

The squad leader looked stunned, his mouth hanging open. The Young Master rolled his eyes.

“It’s a joke, sergeant. Look, this isn’t public knowledge, but she’s my strategist. And she’s the one who came up with the plan that saved your hides two weeks ago. I’m just the loudmouth who implemented it. Anyways, just do the training she’s talking about. You’ll understand quickly enough.”

The squad leader did his best to collect himself, before eventually offering another salute. “Whatever you say, Young Master. We all owe you our li–”

Gu-shim stopped his own sentence mid-word. The man’s head slowly turned towards the closed door, his hand instinctively rising to the handle of the sword on his back. Behind him, the other three guards also shifted their positions, moving to block-off access to Chae-ha’s cot.

A moment later, she heard it, too: the sound of footfalls sprinting down the wooden corridor, in complete disregard for the infirmary’s strict rules.

Mu-cheon’s grin vanished. His muscles tensed as the running footsteps skidded to a clumsy halt right outside the door.

Almost immediately, there was a series of urgent knocks. Then – without waiting for an answer – the latch clicked open. The door swung inwards to reveal a disheveled figure leaning heavily against the jamb, his shoulders rising and falling rapidly as he struggled to catch his breath.

“Lieutenant Shin,” Chae-ha observed, her voice cutting apart the tension in the room. “No, it’s Commander Shin, now. Is everything alright? What brings you here?”

The haggard officer faced the two of them, gulping down air to calm his racing heart. Beads of sweat ran down the sides of his face, his uniform was rumpled, and his topknot was coming undone.

“It’s the Meng-shan,” he panted, his voice cracking slightly.

“One of our scouts sent a bird. They’ve started their march on the Deer Valley mine!”
