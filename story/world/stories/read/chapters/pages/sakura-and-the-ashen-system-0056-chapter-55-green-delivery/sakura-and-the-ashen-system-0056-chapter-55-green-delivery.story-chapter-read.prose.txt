The wagon they had hidden in advance had been standing in the forest for more than a day. Sakura had left it under a canopy of branches, in a damp lowland between two hills, where the road could not be seen even from the nearest rise. The wagon was old, a work wagon, with worn sides and creaking wheels.

Sakura stood ankle-deep in wet grass and pulled the green tarp over the wagon. The fabric was dense and heavy. Dealing with it was as inconvenient as it got. In ordinary light, the cloth looked dark green, almost swampy, but if turned at the right angle, thin protective threads appeared in the fibers. Something like woven traces of a treatment Sakura still did not fully understand. Not that understanding it was necessary. The important thing was that the fabric worked. This was the same green tarp that was supposed to keep out green mist.

Sakura threw the edge of the fabric over the top frame of the wagon, pulled it down, fastened it to the hook, and checked the strap. Then another one, and another. Gilbert probably would have done it faster and more deftly, with the face of a man who had been doing nothing else since childhood. Sakura, meanwhile, managed to pinch her finger twice, drop the tarp, fasten it too crookedly, and once almost fall off the wheel.

But in the end, she managed. Now the tarp held. Sakura checked several times to make sure she had left no gaps. She needed room for improvisation in their plan, so if she suddenly had to put people without suits inside, she needed to be sure the mist would not get in and poison them.

Sakura had also attached the stolen metal tags from the workshop to the side of the wagon. She had grabbed all of them from the storeroom while the workers argued by the table and did not look her way. She had seen how the tags were attached to official delivery wagons and fixed them to hers in exactly the same way.

There were already bundles inside. Some were real protective fabric, but most of it was ordinary old rags stuffed in for volume. Separately, Sakura had placed several green sets, spare filters, two heavy cloaks, and a bundle of gloves. She did not know what condition Gilbert and Lir would be in if they reached her at all. So she had taken everything she could carry and hide.

After that, Sakura harnessed the horses. Both work mares stood calmly. Over the past few days, they had already managed to get used to the fact that their lives had become part of someone else’s suspicious plan. Sakura checked the harness, tightened the straps, and ran her palm over the damp neck of the nearest mare. The mare snorted quietly.

Of course, the horses had not been standing in this forest lowland all this time. On one of the days before the arrest, Gilbert had simply brought two work mares to a small inn outside the city. Early in the morning, without unnecessary explanations and with a face as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world.

Where he had gotten them, the mage, in his usual manner, had not bothered to clarify. Sakura was not sure she wanted to know either. She even suspected the horses might have been stolen, but Gilbert had paid the innkeeper too well to keep them, feed them, clean them, and ask no questions.

All this time, no one had come to demand the mares back. So everything was fine. Probably.

Sakura had taken them before dawn and led them to the wagon by a long detour. Now they stood in front of the old wagon under the green tarp and were ready for the road.

Sakura put on the green suit for herself last. It was too wide in the shoulders and too long in the sleeves. The heavy fabric rustled with every movement, and the mask hid her face well enough, though it slightly distorted the world before her eyes. Breathing through the filter was unpleasant, as if the air first passed through wet earth, then through a rag, and only then entered her lungs. But that was still better than inhaling mist and dying.

Sakura tightened the strap on the mask, then checked everything necessary one more time.

Pampli sat beside the wagon and watched her very carefully. On his obsidian muzzle, it was not as easy to read expression as on a human face, but Sakura still felt it: he understood that something important was happening. And he truly was an important part of the new plan.

Sakura took from the bag a worn leather strip of harness with the sharp smell of horse sweat and the workshop. She had removed it from a real service harness while escaping the storeroom through the back exit and circling around the workshop yard.

Sakura crouched in front of Pampli and held out the strap.

"Smell."

Pampli tilted his head and carefully drew in the air. Then again, deeper. His nostrils twitched.

"Find a green wagon like this. With this smell. It will go from the workshop to the Castle. Understand?"

He simply looked at her. Sakura sighed and tried to explain more simply, gesturing at the same time.

"Big wagon. Horses. People. Green fabric. It needs to be stopped."

Pampli growled softly.

"Do not eat the people."

He froze, puzzled.

"And do not kill them," Sakura added strictly. "Scaring them is allowed. Scaring the horses is allowed. Breaking the harness is allowed. Putting the wheels into a ditch is allowed too. Do not touch the people if they run away. Clear?"

Pampli lowered his muzzle to the strap again, as if trying to properly memorize the smell. Sakura placed her palm on his head. The cold, smooth surface under her fingers was unexpectedly calming.

"Make sure they don’t reach the Castle. I need to get there first."

Pampli lifted his eyes.

"And then go into the forest. Don’t wait for me on the road. If you see guards, run."

He snorted as if the last rule was offensive.

"Yes, I know you’re smart."

Sakura hugged him around the neck for a few seconds, and Pampli stood motionless. Only his tail twitched once in the wet grass. Then she let him go and pointed toward the road.

"Go."

Pampli shot forward almost silently. His black body slipped between the trees, lowered, and vanished behind the bushes. After a few moments, Sakura could no longer see him. She only heard the distant crack of a branch.

She was alone. The plan had many doubts and risks. She did not even want to think about how many. If Pampli failed, if time ran out too early, if something went wrong... The worst would be if Gilbert did not come out to the agreed meeting point at the right time. Then all of this would have been done for nothing. And the risk of exposure would grow with every minute.

Of course, she had tomorrow, the blue cloth, and the suits. She had left part of it in the forest on purpose, in case something happened to the wagon on the road. But tomorrow, she would no longer be able to enter the prison in a wagon. Tomorrow, the risks would become ten times greater, and their plan would start breaking the way the foreign core under Gilbert’s skin was surely breaking, slowly killing him.

Sakura prayed to every god she could remember that the plan would work today. That today, she would get both Lir and Gilbert out. And that they would finally return home. More precisely, not home. The three of them no longer had a home. Maybe they themselves could become a home for one another.

Sakura climbed onto the driver’s bench, took the reins, and forced herself to inhale slowly through the mask, as much as the filter allowed. Right now, the real green delivery should be traveling along another road, the one where she had sent Pampli. Pampli was supposed to stop the wagon midway, far from the city, according to the time Sakura had calculated. Then, before all of this raised an alarm, she would have half an hour, maybe an hour.

Right now, Gilbert inside the Castle should begin his part of the plan, without even knowing whether hers had worked.

"Come on," Sakura said quietly.

She snapped the reins. The wagon jerked, the wheels creaked, and the green tarp over her head gave a heavy sigh from the movement.

Gilbert woke before the footsteps in the corridor changed. Sleep, if it could be called sleep at all, came in brief drops. His body lay on the stone bunk, but his mind still caught on every sound beyond the door.

Today was the green day. Gilbert could not see the mist from the cell or confirm it with his own eyes, but thanks to his last bit of reconnaissance, he knew the sequence. First red, then green today. And tomorrow would be blue mist.

Would the foreign core under his skin last until tomorrow? It twitched just then, as if it had decided to remind him that it was under no obligation to last. Pain had settled so tightly into his body that it had become like another layer of clothing. His ribs ached after the interrogations, his split lip stung, and under his collarbone it was hot and wrong. It was hard to count the parts of his body that did not hurt yet. But Gilbert wore pain like armor necessary for survival. Over the years of his life, he had long since grown used to it. Pain reminded him that he was still alive and was only a symptom of the growth of his inner strength.

And still, waiting any longer was impossible. Gilbert could quietly endure a little more pain, but he knew the foreign core did not simply hurt. In the end, it could kill him if it was not pulled out in time.

He sat up. The neighbor by the door lifted his head. The large man slept badly and nervously, like an animal constantly expecting a blow. His injured hand, with its swollen fingers, was pressed to his chest. After their first clash, he no longer went after Gilbert, no longer tried to take his food, and even breathed more quietly when Gilbert looked at him. In the narrow sense, this big man was trainable.

"Get up," Gilbert said.

The neighbor froze.

"What?"

"Get up."

"Why?"

Gilbert looked at him more intently, and that was enough for the man to stop asking questions and slowly rise. He was taller than Gilbert, broader in the shoulders, and when they first met, he had considered that an advantage. He had realized the mistake some time ago.

"Do you want to leave the cell?" Gilbert asked.

The neighbor stared at him in surprise.

"What?"

"The question is simple."

The man swallowed. Several feelings changed across his face at once: distrust, fear, greed, and fear again.

"Is this a trap?"

"Yes," Gilbert said. "But not for you. For the guard."

The neighbor fell silent. Gilbert rose from the bunk. The world swayed, but he did not allow himself to lean on the wall.

"You will help me open the door," he said. "After that, you will get a chance to run."

"And if I don’t help?"

Gilbert looked at his injured hand.

"Then I’ll break the other one."

The neighbor understood.

"What do I do?" he asked hoarsely.

Gilbert stepped closer. The neighbor tensed, but there was nowhere to retreat. The cell was too small to escape this forced coexistence.

"You need blood on your face," Gilbert said.

"What?"

"For believability."

The neighbor managed to open his mouth, but Gilbert did not give him time to say anything. He had already struck. Not hard, but precisely. Knuckles to the nose, already broken before. The man jerked, cursed, and grabbed his face. Blood came at once.

"Are you sick?" he hissed.

"Right now, we need it."

"I’ll..."

Gilbert stepped closer, and the man instantly cut himself off.

"Go to the door. Pound on it and shout that Gilven is gone. Make noise for me and force the guard to come inside. If he doesn’t want to, say you need the healer urgently. Make him come in. Understand?"

The neighbor wiped blood with his sleeve, smearing it across his face.

"They won’t believe me."

"It doesn’t matter. The main thing is for a guard to enter the cell. And be ready to help if something goes wrong."

Gilbert went to the door and briefly checked the viewing slit. It was quiet outside right now. The shift was not exactly sleepy, but already irritated. Good. Irritated people always wanted to deal with problems faster.

He showed the neighbor the place.

"Stand here. So you are clearly visible. Don’t look at me."

"And where will you be?"

Gilbert pointed to the corner beside the door, in the part that could not be seen. The neighbor frowned, then understood. It seemed he already regretted asking about getting out of the cell, but the desire to leave was still stronger.

Gilbert took his place in the blind spot. He did not know exactly how the guard would behave. In the worst case, he would call someone else with him, raise the alarm, and grab a crossbow. If two came in, Gilbert was already calculating how to knock them out without catching a crossbow bolt with his body. The strength of his not-very-bright neighbor would be useful here too.

The neighbor struck the door with his fist.

"Hey!" he shouted. "Open up!"

Gilbert closed his eyes for half a breath, calming his pulse. The neighbor struck again.

"Open up, you idiots! He hit me!"

Someone cursed outside.

"Shut up."

"Open it!" The neighbor slapped the door again. "Gilven is gone! He smashed my face and disappeared!"

Footsteps approached. Gilbert opened his eyes. The viewing hatch clicked. The neighbor stood by the door, large and sweaty, with blood on his face. Gilbert’s bunk, clearly visible, was empty. The guard could not see Gilbert.

For a moment, it went quiet behind the door.

"What do you mean, disappeared?" the guard asked in a different voice.

"How would I know?" the neighbor barked. "I woke up, he threw himself at me, hit me, and then he was gone! Look for yourself!"

"Step back."

"Just open already!"

"Back, I said!"

The neighbor took a step back, exactly as Gilbert had told him in advance. Not too far, but enough for the guard to feel that he still controlled the situation.

A key jingled behind the door. Gilbert slowly shifted his weight onto his front foot.

The first lock opened, then the second. The guard did not call a runic mage, and that was expected too. The cell was inside the general suppressing block, but the door itself was ordinary. There were runes on the main passages. Here, there was only a lock. Still, when Gilbert made his way to the main exits, he would have to remember the runes.

The door swung open.

"To the wall," the guard barked, stepping one pace inside. "Hands..."

He did not finish. Gilbert struck from the side with all the strength he had left, hitting under the ear. The guard jerked. The neighbor, fortunately, also did his part: he lunged forward and slammed into him shoulder-first. They both almost fell.

The guard opened his mouth to shout, and Gilbert was already behind him. He pulled the belt from the neighbor’s hand, looped it around the guard’s throat, and tightened it. The guard wheezed, dug his fingers into the belt, and slammed his head backward. Pain flashed through Gilbert’s cheekbone, but he did not let go.

At that moment, the core under his skin began pulsing wildly again, and black spots swam before his eyes. Gilbert cursed inwardly, but did not let go. The guard kicked against the floor, while the neighbor held his hands and swore through clenched teeth. Then the guard’s body went limp. Gilbert held for one more heartbeat and let go.

Gilbert nodded to the neighbor without saying anything. The man had done his part of the plan better than Gilbert had expected from him.

"Strip him," Gilbert said.

The neighbor looked surprised.

"What?"

"Jacket. Boots. Quickly."

They worked awkwardly. The guard was heavier than was convenient. The neighbor pulled at the cloth too roughly. Gilbert moved too carefully, because every sharp movement sent pain under his collarbone. But after a few moments, another man’s uniform already lay on the floor: jacket, pants, boots, belt, cloak, keys, baton, and a short service knife.

Gilbert pulled off his prison shirt and threw it to the neighbor.

"Put it on him."

"Why?"

"So they won’t immediately understand who is lying here."

While the neighbor pulled the prison shirt onto the guard, Gilbert dressed himself. The uniform fit badly. The jacket pulled at the shoulders, the pants were a little short, and the boots pinched. But the cloak hid most of the mismatches, the belt gave the silhouette the right weight, the baton in his hand added threat, and the keys at his waist made him look like someone who was allowed to open any door here.

The neighbor stared at the open door with wild eyes.

"Now?" he asked.

Gilbert stepped aside.

"Run."

The neighbor hesitated, because he did not believe it.

"Run. Just not after me," Gilbert said. "Run left. Toward the dining hall. There is a kitchen there. In the kitchen, there is a back service entrance. Maybe you’ll get lucky."

That was enough. The large man darted into the corridor without even trying to move quietly. He was a bad escapee and an excellent lure for distracting eyes. A couple of seconds later, someone shouted from the far end of the corridor:

"Hey! Stop!"

Because of the escaped neighbor, noise and pounding steps were already rising through the prison. Gilbert waited a little, making sure the noise was moving away from their cell, not toward it. Then he stepped out and closed the door behind him. He looked exactly like all the other guards moving through the prison. There were flaws in the disguise, but Gilbert also had a head start and a chance to avoid the traps if he was careful, lucky, and convincing enough. That was exactly what he intended to be.
