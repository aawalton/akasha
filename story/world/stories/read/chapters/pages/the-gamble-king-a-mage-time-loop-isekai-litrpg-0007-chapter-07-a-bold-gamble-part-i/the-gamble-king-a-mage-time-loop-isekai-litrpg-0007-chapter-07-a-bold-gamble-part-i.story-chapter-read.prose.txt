The convoy had halted for the day, an unusual respite from their relentless march toward Frosthold. The pilgrims of Thael needed time for their rituals, or so the soldiers muttered around their fires. Max wasn't complaining. A day without running himself into exhaustion was a gift, and one he intended to use wisely.

His time in this world had taught him that religion here wasn't merely ceremony—it had power. Real, tangible power. The pilgrims' white robes and synchronized movements weren't just for show. The Prince respected them enough to delay the journey, which told Max all he needed to know about their importance.

The pause also gave him time to learn more about the Dragon Heart—the artifact they'd recovered from Dragonmeet, and the prize that enemy forces had tried to capture at Eastwatch. Max had gathered bits and pieces from casual conversations over the past days. The Heart remained with Prince Keiran at all times, whether they were moving or stationary like today. It never left the royal pavilion, guarded day and night by rotating shifts of the Prince's most trusted men.

All except during the deepest hours, when Sir Gregory himself took over the watch. Unlike ordinary men, the legendary knight apparently didn't need sleep—just a few hours of meditation with eyes half-open, alert to the slightest threat. His tent stood closest to the Prince's pavilion, ensuring he would be first to respond if anything happened.

If Max wanted to learn more about the Heart's security—and he desperately needed to—there was only one approach that made sense. As future son-in-law to the Prince, he had at least some claim to an audience.

Max straightened his tunic and approached the massive royal pavilion that stood at the center of camp, easily three times larger than any other tent. Two guards flanked the entrance, standing rigidly at attention despite the early hour. Four more patrolled the perimeter, moving with the practiced precision of men who took their duty seriously.

Max straightened his tunic and approached with confidence.

"Evening," he nodded to the guards. "Is the Prince still awake?"

The guards exchanged glances. "He is, Lord Vanheim," answered the one on the left, a tall man with a carefully trimmed beard. "He's reviewing tomorrow's march with Sir Gregory and Captain Rhen."

Perfect. All three men he needed to observe in one place.

"Excellent," Max said, stepping forward as if entry was a foregone conclusion. "I'd like to speak with them."

The guard shifted slightly, blocking his path. "The Prince left orders not to be disturbed, my lord."

Max forced a small, entitled smile—the kind he imagined Harek would use when pulling rank. "I'm certain those orders don't apply to family."

The guard's expression remained unchanged. "All due respect, Lord Vanheim, but the orders were specific."

"Very well." Max sighed dramatically. "Then please inform the Prince that his future son-in-law wishes to discuss an important matter regarding tomorrow's training. Tell him it cannot wait."

The second guard, younger and visibly less comfortable with confrontation, nodded. "I'll convey your message, my lord."

Max waited, studying the tent's structure while trying to appear casual. The pavilion was divided into sections, with what appeared to be a more private chamber toward the rear. That would likely be where they kept the Heart—not in the main strategy area where dozens of officers might come and go.

The young guard returned moments later. "The Prince will see you, Lord Vanheim."

"Thank you," Max said, slipping past them into the tent.

The interior was warmer than the night air outside, heated by braziers placed strategically around a large central table covered with maps. Oil lamps hung from the ceiling poles, casting the space in golden light that made the shadows in the corners even deeper.

Prince Keiran stood at the head of the table, his silver hair gleaming in the lamplight. To his right was Captain Rhen, finger tracing a route on the map. And at the far side, standing perfectly still with his back to a hanging tapestry, was Sir Gregory.

All three looked up as Max entered.

"Harek," the Prince acknowledged. "What brings you to us at this hour?"

"I apologize for the interruption, Your Highness," Max said, bowing slightly. His eyes darted around the tent, cataloging details. Six support poles. Two entrances to the rear chamber—one directly behind the Prince, currently closed by a heavy flap, and another off to the side, partially obscured by a weapons rack. "I've been considering Sir Gregory's challenge, and I wanted to ask about techniques that might help me manifest Fanga before the deadline."

Gregory's face remained impassive, but Rhen's eyebrow raised slightly.

"At this hour?" the Prince asked.

"Time is a luxury I don't have," Max explained, moving further into the tent as if to better address Gregory. This gave him a clearer view of the side entrance to the rear chamber. Two more guards stood there, barely visible in the shadows. "Six days seemed impossible when Sir Gregory set the terms. Now I have until sunrise."

"Sit," the Prince commanded, gesturing to an empty chair. Not a dismissal, but a clear indication that this would be a proper conversation. "Wine?"

"No, thank you," Max replied, taking the offered seat. He noticed a flicker of surprise cross the Prince's face at his refusal.

"You've shown remarkable dedication these past days," the Prince observed. "I must admit, it's not what I expected when Sir Gregory set his terms."

Max seized the opportunity to appear earnest while continuing his survey of the tent. "I don't want to fail, Your Highness."

"Few men do," Prince Keiran replied. "Yet failure is the most common outcome in life. Wouldn't you agree, Sir Gregory?"

Gregory stepped forward into the light.

"Failure teaches more than success," the knight said calmly. "But only to those wise enough to learn from it."

"I'd prefer to succeed," Max said, angling his chair slightly to keep both the rear entrances in view.

The Prince smiled—a small, controlled expression that never reached his eyes. "Another surprise. The Harek I knew seemed more comfortable with failure than ambition."

"Eastwatch changed me," he said simply.

"So it seems," the Prince nodded. "Then let us address your question. Sir Gregory, what advice might you offer your potential squire?"

Gregory studied Max for an uncomfortably long moment.

"Fanga comes when body and spirit are unified," he finally said. "You've been training the body. That's only half the equation."

"And the spirit?" Max asked, genuinely curious despite his ulterior motives.

"Clarity of purpose," Gregory replied. "Why do you seek this power?"

It was a deeper question than Max had anticipated. He took a moment to consider, aware that a shallow answer would only confirm their low expectations.

"Initially, to prove myself worthy," Max admitted. "Now... to be of service. To protect what matters."

Gregory's expression didn't change.

"Better," he said. "But still external motivations. Fanga comes from within, not from what others think of you."

Movement at the rear entrance caught Max's eye. A servant emerged carrying an empty tray, the flap briefly open enough to reveal the inner chamber. Max glimpsed a pedestal in the center of the room, and atop it, a silver chest inlaid with what looked like iron bands.

That had to be it. The Dragon Heart.

"Is there nothing more concrete?" Max asked, forcing himself to focus on the conversation. "Some exercise for the mind as well as the body?"

"Meditation," Gregory answered. "Find stillness within chaos. Like the eye of a storm."

Captain Rhen, who had been silent until now, spoke up. "Waste of time, if you ask me. Either you have the gift or you don't."

"And if I don't?" Max asked.

"Then you fail," Rhen said bluntly.

"That remains to be seen," the Prince interjected smoothly. "Many warriors discover their potential only at the moment of greatest need." His tone suggested this was merely an observation, not a recommendation.

Gregory nodded. "True. But manufactured crisis rarely produces lasting results."

Max watched as another guard emerged from the side entrance, confirming his count. Two stationed outside the tent, two at the inner chamber, plus whatever security might be inside with the Heart itself.

"I appreciate the advice," Max said, knowing he'd pushed his reconnaissance as far as was prudent. "I'll try the meditation tonight."

"An earnest effort, even in failure, has its own worth," the Prince said. "House Vanheim has always valued perseverance."

Max stood, recognizing the polite dismissal. "Thank you for your time, Your Highness. Sir Gregory."

"One moment, Harek," the Prince said as Max turned to leave. "Curiosity compels me to ask—what prompted this sudden dedication? After years of... different pursuits."

It was a test. Max could feel all three men watching him, measuring his response.

"Necessity," Max replied, deciding honesty—or something close to it—was his best option. "At Eastwatch, I realized how unprepared I was. How much I'd wasted. That's not a comfortable realization."

The Prince's expression softened almost imperceptibly. "Indeed. Growth often begins with discomfort."

"Good night, Your Highness," Max said, bowing slightly before making his exit.

Outside, the night air felt like a blessing after the tension of that encounter. Max walked casually back towards his own tent, processing everything he'd observed.

He now had the basic layout of the Prince's pavilion. The Dragon Heart was kept in a silver chest with iron bands, placed on a pedestal in the private chamber. Security consisted of two guards at the main entrance, four patrolling the perimeter, and two more stationed at the inner chamber where the Heart was kept.

Most importantly, he'd confirmed that Gregory would be the one guarding it alone from midnight until dawn. The knight who supposedly didn't sleep, just meditated with eyes half-open, remaining alert to any threat.

Max couldn't shake the Guide's words from his mind. She'd seemed to know more than she'd revealed. Maybe she could give him pointers?

"Worth a shot," Max muttered, changing direction toward the outer edges of camp.

He spotted a cluster of soldiers sharing a meal around a small fire, familiar faces from earlier marches.

"Evening," Max nodded, trying to appear casual. "Quick question—those pilgrims who joined us yesterday, where are they camped?"

One soldier—Brant, if Max remembered correctly—gestured vaguely eastward. "Near the stream, my lord. Set up separate from us. Religious folk, keep to themselves mostly."

"Any idea what they're doing out here?" Max asked. "Seems dangerous to travel with all the fighting."

Brant shrugged. "Pilgrims go where their faith takes them. These ones worship Thael, I think. The Dreaming God."

"Thael," Max repeated, testing the unfamiliar name. Another soldier nodded.

"Thanks," Max said with a nod. "Appreciate the help."

"My lord," Brant called as Max turned to leave, his tone suddenly more serious. "Best not wander too far beyond the camp perimeter. The sentries spotted lycanthrope tracks not an hour ago."

Another soldier nodded grimly. "Full moon's tomorrow night. They get bolder when it's near."

"I'll be careful," Max assured them, though the warning sent a chill down his spine. Lycanthropes. Not just werewolves from Earth mythology, but something potentially far worse if Sabo's novels were accurate. "Just going to the pilgrim camp and back."

"Still," Brant insisted, "take a torch. They don't like fire."

The pilgrim camp came into view as he crested a small rise. Unlike the military encampment with its organized rows and watchfires, this gathering had an entirely different energy. About twenty simple tents formed a perfect circle around a central fire that burned with unusual steadiness—no popping or crackling, just a constant, unwavering flame.

Around this fire sat twelve figures in identical white robes, motionless as statues. They faced the flames, hoods drawn up to conceal their features, hands resting palm-up on their knees. Not a single one moved or spoke. Even their breathing seemed synchronized, barely perceptible in the stillness.

Max approached hesitantly, unsure of the proper protocol for interrupting whatever ritual this might be. As he drew closer, he noticed smaller details—the robes weren't actually identical after all. Each bore subtle, unique embroidery along the hems in pale silver thread. Patterns of stars, moons, what looked like running water.

He cleared his throat.

Nothing. Not a single head turned.

"Uh, hello?" Max tried, feeling increasingly awkward.

Still nothing.

He took another step forward, and suddenly—without any visible signal—all twelve heads turned toward him simultaneously. The movement was so perfectly coordinated it sent a chill down Max's spine.

"Oh, uh... hi," Max stammered, raising a hand in awkward greeting.

For several uncomfortable seconds, no one spoke. Then one figure at the far side of the circle rose with fluid grace and approached. As they drew closer, the hood fell back to reveal an elderly man with a face etched by deep lines. His beard was indeed impressive—pure white and reaching almost to his waist, braided with thin silver chains that caught the firelight.

"Welcome, seeker," the old man said, his voice surprisingly strong. "Thael's dreams encompass all who wander."

"Thanks," Max replied, unsure how to respond to the religious greeting. "I'm Ha—"

"Lord Harek Vanheim," the elder finished for him, inclining his head slightly. "Your presence in the waking world is known to us."

Creepy.

"Right," Max said, glancing at the still-motionless figures by the fire. "Sorry to interrupt your... whatever this is."

"The Twilight Meditation ends with moonrise," the elder explained, gesturing toward the sky where the moon was indeed beginning to peek above the distant mountains. "You arrive at the hour of transition. Some might call that fortunate."

As if on cue, the other robed figures rose in perfect unison and dispersed to their tents without a word, leaving only the elder standing with Max.

"Come," the old man said, "share tea with us."

Before Max could respond, the elder turned and walked toward the largest tent at the circle's edge. With nowhere else to go and his curiosity piqued, Max followed.

The tent's interior was sparse but oddly comfortable—sheepskin rugs layered over the ground, simple cushions arranged in a small circle, a low table in the center bearing an iron kettle. Three other robed figures already sat inside, their hoods now lowered.

To Max's surprise, they were young—two men and a woman, none older than twenty-five. Their heads were shaved completely bald, and each had a small blue star tattooed at the center of their forehead.

"Sit," the elder gestured to an empty cushion. "The water dreams of becoming tea."

Max lowered himself onto the cushion, trying not to stare at the strange tattoos. The younger pilgrims watched him with calm, curious expressions but offered no greeting.

One of the young men poured steaming water from the kettle into five simple clay cups, then added dried leaves from a small pouch. The scent that rose was unfamiliar—herbal and slightly sweet, with an underlying earthiness.

"So," Max started, accepting the offered cup, "I'm looking for someone who might be traveling with your group. A woman called the Guide?"

The four exchanged glances.

"We have no guide," the elder said. "Thael leads us through dreams."

"She's about this tall," Max tried again, gesturing. "Blonde hair, green eyes. Travels with a... distinctive-looking man. Very tall, unusual features."

"No one matching this description travels among us," the woman said, her voice soft but clear. "We number twenty-three exactly, and have since leaving the Summer Temple."

Max frowned. "But she said she arrived with the pilgrims yesterday. That the Prince permitted you to join our company."

"This much is true," the elder nodded. "We did join your military band yesterday, with the Prince's blessing. But no woman such as you describe walks our path."

"That's... weird," Max said, taking a cautious sip of the tea. It was surprisingly good—warm and soothing, with a hint of something that reminded him of honey. "She specifically mentioned being a pilgrim seeking the waters of Frosthold."

The younger pilgrims exchanged another look.

"The Waters of Reflection do draw many seekers," one of the young men acknowledged. "Though few find what they truly seek there."

"Could she have been traveling separately? Maybe joined the camp when you did but isn't part of your order?"

The elder shook his head. "No outsiders have approached our circle. The Prince's men keep careful count of all who travel under their protection."

Max sat back, genuinely confused. "So either she lied about being with you, or..."

"Or what, seeker?" the elder prompted.

"Or she wasn't... real?" Max suggested, feeling slightly ridiculous.

To his surprise, none of the pilgrims laughed or dismissed the idea. Instead, they looked to their elder with expressions of renewed interest.

"Describe this meeting," the old man said, leaning forward slightly.

Max recounted the encounter by the river—the woman emerging from the water, their conversation about Fanga, her cryptic comments about shortcuts and burdens. He described both her and the misshapen guardian in detail, watching as the pilgrims' expressions grew increasingly intent.

When he finished, the tent was silent except for the gentle hiss of the fire outside.

"You have been honored," the elder finally said, his voice hushed.

"Honored?"

"Few receive direct visitation from the Watchers," the woman explained, her eyes wide. "Especially in these broken days."

"Watchers?"

"Those who observe," the elder explained. "They walk between worlds, recording events as they unfold. Neither gods nor mortals, but something... adjacent."

"A Chronicler," one of the young men added reverently. "One who sees and remembers, so that nothing is truly lost to time."

The word hit Max like a physical blow. Chronicler. The exact term Sabo had used in his message.

"I'm sorry, did you say 'Chronicler'?" Max asked, his voice suddenly tight.

The elder nodded. "Few know of them. They are as powerful as the Patrons in their way, though they seldom interfere directly. They observe, they record—"

"They write stories," Max finished, a cold anger beginning to build in his chest.

"Yes," the elder confirmed, looking slightly surprised. "The greatest events in our world are preserved in their chronicles. How do you know of this?"

Max set his tea down carefully, trying to keep his hand from shaking. "Lucky guess."

It all made sense now.

The woman by the river wasn't some random guide or minor deity.

She was Sabo.

"These Chroniclers," Max said, keeping his voice even, "can they move people between worlds?"

The pilgrims exchanged uneasy glances.

"There are... stories," the elder said carefully. "Ancient tales of souls brought from elsewhere to fulfill roles no native could perform. But such accounts are considered myth by most scholars."

"Not a myth," Max muttered under his breath.

"You seem troubled," the woman observed. "Has the Chronicler's appearance disturbed you?"

"You could say that," Max replied, struggling to contain his anger. "Let's just say we have... history."

The elder's eyes narrowed slightly. "You speak as if you know this Watcher personally."

"In a manner of speaking," Max said, unwilling to explain further. How exactly did you tell people that their entire world was a story written by someone from another reality? That you'd spent fourteen years reading about their lives as entertainment?

"Then you are more honored than we realized," the elder said, bowing his head slightly. "To be known by a Chronicler is to be marked by fate itself."

"Or just really unlucky," Max countered.

The elder studied him for a moment. "The Watchers do not appear without purpose. If one has shown herself to you, there is meaning in it."

"Oh, I'm sure there is," Max agreed bitterly. "The question is whether that meaning benefits me or just makes a better story."

His words clearly confused the pilgrims, but Max was beyond caring. Sabo—the woman, the Guide, the Chronicler, whatever name fit—had put him here, in this body, in this world. And now she was playing games, dropping cryptic hints instead of actual help.

"I should go," Max said abruptly, standing up. "Thank you for the tea and the... information."

"Wait," the elder said, rising as well. "You have been touched by powers beyond ordinary understanding. The path before you may be dangerous, but it is clearly significant. We could offer guidance—"

"I've had enough 'guidance' for one day," Max interrupted. "Thanks all the same."

The pilgrims watched him with expressions of confusion and concern as he ducked out of the tent. Outside, the camp was quiet, the central fire still burning with that unnaturally steady flame.

As Max strode away, the elder followed him to the edge of their circle.

"Lord Vanheim," he called softly. "Whatever your history with the Watcher, remember that they rarely intervene without grave cause. The balance of this world may depend on your choices."

"No pressure," Max muttered, not slowing his pace.

The elder's voice followed him into the darkness. "Return if you seek wisdom. Thael's dreams are open to all who wish to understand."

Max didn't look back, his torch cutting a bright path through the night as he made his way back to the main camp. His mind raced with implications. If Sabo was a Chronicler who could move between worlds, who could bring people like Max into this reality, what did that mean? Was this all just some elaborate game? Some twisted form of entertainment?

And more importantly—if Sabo could bring him here, could Sabo also send him home?

The thought stopped Max in his tracks. Home. Back to his apartment, his life, his world. Away from magic and monsters and the constant threat of death.

But did he even want to go back? To his fired-from-his-job, shot-by-robbers existence? At least here he had purpose, even if it was thrust upon him by a manipulative author-deity.

"Hey, Sabo," Max muttered. "If you're watching me right now, the least you could do is give me a straight answer."

He found himself at the eastern edge of the main camp, where supply wagons formed a rough half-circle. It was quieter here, away from the soldiers' fires and revelry. Max climbed onto an empty wagon bed and sat with his back against wooden planks, staring up at unfamiliar stars.

The night sky took his breath away. Without light pollution, the stars blazed with an intensity Max had never witnessed on Earth – thousands upon thousands of them, crowding the darkness until it seemed more silver than black. The constellations were strange, patterns he couldn't recognize.

"Would Earth's sky look like this even without cities?" Max wondered aloud. He'd been camping once or twice as a kid, thought he'd seen a "real" night sky, but nothing like this. This was like standing in space itself, or inside one of those high-budget anime films where the backgrounds were painfully beautiful.

A shooting star streaked across the darkness, leaving a momentary silver trail that seemed to hang in the air before fading. Max watched it disappear, too jaded to make a wish but still captivated by its beauty.

The silence seemed to press in around him as thoughts he'd been avoiding for days suddenly demanded attention.

Home. Earth. His father.

Dad.

Max had been so caught up in survival, in learning how to navigate this world, that he'd pushed aside the implications of his departure from Earth. Had he actually died on his apartment floor? Was his father sitting right now in a hospital somewhere, mourning a son who had simply vanished from existence? Or worse, attending his funeral?

The thought made his chest tighten.

His dad was 67, stubborn as hell, and living alone since Mom abandoned them ten years ago for another life in another city. Who was making sure he took his heart medication? Was he eating properly, or surviving on those frozen dinners Max was always lecturing him about? The two of them spoke three times a week without fail. His father would maybe even blaming himself somehow.

"Shit," Max muttered into the darkness, pressing the heels of his hands against his eyes.

Dad had already lost Mom. Now his only son had vanished too. The unfairness of it made Max's throat constrict. His father didn't deserve that kind of pain. No one did.

And yet...

There was another emotion bubbling beneath the surface, one that brought with it a sharp stab of guilt: excitement.

Because for all the dangers and difficulties, for all the confusion and pain, part of him—a significant part—was thrilled to be here.

This was the world of Bjorn, the setting of stories he'd followed obsessively for over a decade. The breathtaking sky, the magic, the monsters, the grand conflicts that shaped nations—all of it was real, and he was in the middle of it. Not just reading about it, but living it.

He had power here. Potential. Not just Harek's status as a noble's son, but the reroll ability—a gift that made him uniquely equipped to take risks others couldn't. To attempt feats others wouldn't dare.

And nobody knew what Harek could do. Nobody expected anything from him other than what he chose to show them.

"I can be anyone here," he realized aloud. "Do anything."

The contradiction of his feelings was almost painful. Grief for what he'd lost, for the father who must be mourning him. Excitement for the possibilities this new existence offered. Guilt for feeling that excitement at all.

"What kind of son feels like this?" he asked himself. "What kind of person gets dumped into another world and starts thinking about what he can gain from it?"

But the answer came readily enough: a person who'd spent years escaping into fantasy because reality had disappointed him. One who'd always felt like he was meant for something more than quarterly marketing reports and office politics.

A person like Max.

He sighed, leaning his head back against the wagon. The wood was rough against his scalp, but the sensation grounded him, reminded him that whatever inner conflicts he felt, he was still here. Still alive, if in a different body and a different world.

And wasn't that exactly the kind of internal conflict that Sabo loved to write? Characters torn between duty and desire, between the comfortable known and the dangerous unknown?

"Is that what this is?" Max asked the darkness. "Just another plot point in your story?"

No answer came, of course. Just the distant sounds of the camp settling down for the night and the occasional snap of a watchfire.

Max closed his eyes, trying to quiet his mind. As the challenges threatened to drown him, his anchor thought was a simple request to whatever powers might be listening: Let my father be okay. Even if I never make it back, let him be okay.

A howl pierced the night, jolting Max from his thoughts.

He straightened on the wagon bed, listening. The sound had come from somewhere beyond the camp's perimeter, echoing across the valley before fading into silence.

Unlike the soldiers who tensed at such noises, Max felt oddly detached from the threat. Lycanthropes were dangerous, certainly—in the Chronicles, they were intelligent predators that maintained their human cunning even in beast form. But with his reroll ability, even they represented a temporary setback rather than true danger.

"Thanks for the reality check," Max muttered to the darkness, as if the howl had been a deliberate reminder to focus on his problems.

The sound had pulled him from his philosophical wandering and back to the immediate challenge: the Dragon Heart and Gregory's test. As he considered his options with renewed clarity, a realization struck him.

He couldn't steal the Dragon Heart. Not tonight.

The plan was half-formed at best. The security was too tight. And most damning of all, Sir Gregory would be guarding it personally—the knight who supposedly never slept, whose reflexes were supernatural even by the standards of this world.

Max's shoulders slumped. Without the Heart, he had no way to manifest Fanga by dawn. Without Fanga, he would fail Gregory's test. Without becoming Gregory's squire...

"I'd just go back to being Harek the useless," he said aloud. "Back to square one."

Another howl, this one more distant, drew his gaze toward the mountains.

Then a thought occurred to him—a possibility so obvious he was surprised it had taken him this long to consider it.

What if he just... died?

Not permanently, of course. Just temporarily. He had rerolls to spare, after all. Ten of them, according to his last count.

If he died now, he could respawn earlier in the day, before his failed attempt to question the guards, before his reconnaissance of the Prince's tent. He could start over with a better plan, maybe even find an opportunity that he'd missed.

"It's not like I haven't died before."

But how to do it? He wasn't keen on another spear through the chest or goblin knife to the throat. If he was going to die deliberately, he'd prefer something less traumatic.

Something controlled. Painless, if possible.

As he slid off the wagon, another memory surfaced—Gerth, the healer, lecturing him just that morning.

"Touch those blue berries and you'll be dead before you know it," the old man had scolded. "Nightshade's cousin, we call it. Doesn't even hurt. Makes you sleep, then never wake."

Now that knowledge seemed providential.

The blue berries. He'd seen them growing near the stream where he'd met the Guide—Sabo—that morning.

"Doesn't even hurt," Max repeated, weighing the idea. "Just sleep, then respawn. Clean. Simple."

It was better than any alternative he could think of. Better than failing Gregory's test and watching his one chance at redemption evaporate. Better than stealing the Heart and potentially being caught, executed as a thief.

Decision made, Max started walking toward the eastern edge of camp where the stream cut through the valley.

The stream gurgled softly as Max approached, its surface reflecting the moonlight that filtered through scattered clouds. The camp noises faded behind him, replaced by the gentle sounds of flowing water and night insects.

He scanned the bank, eyes straining in the darkness. There—a low shrub with dark leaves, dotted with clusters of small, round berries. They looked black in the moonlight, but Max knew they'd appear blue in proper light.

Kneeling beside the plant, he plucked a handful of berries, rolling them between his fingers. They were firm, glossy, almost attractive. Nothing about them screamed "deadly poison."

"Death by berry," Max murmured. "Not exactly the heroic end I imagined when I read the Chronicles."

He raised the berries to his nose, inhaling their scent. Surprisingly pleasant—sweet with an underlying tartness, like blueberries with a hint of something else. Something not quite familiar.

For a moment, doubt crept in. What if Gerth had been exaggerating? What if the berries weren't deadly, just mildly toxic? Then he'd be stuck with a night of vomiting instead of a clean reset.

"Only one way to find out," Max sighed.

He popped the berries into his mouth.

The flavor burst across his tongue—intensely sweet at first, then developing into something more complex. Nothing like anything he'd tasted on Earth. The closest comparison might be blackberries mixed with honey and something spicy, almost like cinnamon but sharper.

"Not bad for certain death," he observed, swallowing.

He gathered another handful, eating them more slowly this time, savoring the strange flavor. If these were his last moments before respawning, they might as well be pleasant ones.

With his task completed, Max made his way back to camp, finding a quiet spot between supply wagons where he could sit with his back against a wheel. No sense in making a spectacle of his death.

"And now we wait," he said to the night air, settling in.

He'd expected to feel something immediately—pain, nausea, dizziness. But minutes passed, and Max felt completely normal. Had Gerth been wrong? Or worse, had Max picked the wrong berries entirely?

Wait, how was that worse?

Just as he was considering a return trip to the stream for more berries—possibly the actual poisonous ones this time—he noticed it. A subtle numbness in his tongue, as if he'd burned it on hot coffee.

"Finally," he murmured, the word slightly slurred.

The numbness spread rapidly, moving from his tongue to his lips, then down his throat. Not unpleasant, exactly—just a gentle fading of sensation. Like novocaine but spreading throughout his body.

His limbs grew heavy, and a pleasant warmth suffused him. This wasn't so bad. Almost cozy, really. Like being wrapped in a blanket on a cold day.

"Gerth knew his stuff," Max observed to nobody.

His eyelids grew heavy. The night sounds became muffled, distant. The stars above seemed to pulse gently, keeping time with his slowing heartbeat.

A thought drifted lazily through his mind: What if the reroll doesn't work for deliberate suicide? What if there are rules he didn't know?

Too late to worry about that now.

The warmth had become a burning heat, sweat beading on his forehead despite the cool night air. Fever—a symptom Gerth hadn't mentioned. But still no pain, just increasing disconnection from his body.

How strange that something so deadly could feel so gentle. Max's breathing slowed, each inhalation requiring more effort than the last. His vision blurred, the stars above merging into smears of light.

Maybe he should be frightened, but he wasn't. He'd done this before, after all. Died. Come back. It was almost routine by now. Just a temporary inconvenience on the way to a fresh start.

His head lolled to the side, no longer supported by muscles that had ceased to obey his commands. The camp was silent now, or perhaps he simply couldn't hear it anymore. The world had narrowed to the small space he occupied, everything beyond fading into insignificance.

"S'ee youu yeshturdayyy," Max slurred, his tongue heavy, the words tumbling out in a syrupy mess before consciousness slipped away entirely.

His last thought, trailing into darkness: This really was the easiest way to go.

Soon enough, the In-Between formed around him.

"Harek's journal," Max thought, his awareness expanding through the formless space. "He said he could choose an anchor point when dying."

The glowing number 10 pulsed before him, unchanged by his deliberate death. Self-inflicted or not, the system counted it the same.

Max concentrated, trying to visualize what Harek had described—doors leading to moments he'd lived. At first, nothing happened. The void remained empty save for the floating numeral.
