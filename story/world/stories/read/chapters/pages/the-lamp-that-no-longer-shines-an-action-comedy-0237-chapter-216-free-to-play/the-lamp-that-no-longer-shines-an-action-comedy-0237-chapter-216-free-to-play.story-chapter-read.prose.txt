[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 49 — 2:00 PM[Location]: High-Altitude Research District · Sector 6 · Lab 606

Hathaway had fully intended to go native. She had sat down on Day 43 with the reading list open and twelve thousand words of budget, turned to the glossary at the back of her notebook where the first rule she had ever written about witch academia was still waiting, and resolved to name and shame every theorist on it.

Rule #1 of Witch Academia: "Technically Adequate" translates to "You literally killed me in the gunfight, but I will continue hating your math from the afterlife."

Then the schedule took over, and after the schedule, her hands.

None of it got written sitting down. It went into the gaps, and the largest gap she owned was the commute: eleven minutes each way at altitude, wings holding a glide that cost nothing, both hands free and the navigation running somewhere below the level of decision, dictating to a quill while the messenger hawks got out of the way.

It was, when the last of it landed on the desk, an APA-standard dissertation.

Title page. Author, affiliation, and contributing assisting researchers. Table of contents. Abstract and keywords. A structurally elegant, cleanly paragraphed, scientifically dispassionate body. Then a meticulously alphabetized reference list, an appendix, and a section of acknowledgments.

Clear. Legible. Categorized.

Nino read it standing, all twelve thousand words, and set it down on the terminal with the flat exhaustion of a woman looking at a beautiful disaster.

"Your content," she said, "is an incoherent slurry. You have misread the void-exchange literature so comprehensively that I am going to have to check whether you were reading the same monographs I handed you. Four of your central claims are wrong. The other two are not wrong, which I attribute to volume."

Hathaway's shoulders came down.

"The formatting," Nino continued, and the hoarse voice moved into a register nobody in that laboratory had ever heard it occupy, "is sublime. It is elegant. It is arrogant past excusing. Every witch who ever intends to touch a quill should be required by statute to look at it."

Hathaway blinked. "Arrogant?"

Victoria had come over from the secondary terminal without being asked, and was already reading. Her gloved finger stopped at the top of the first page.

"You summarize your findings before the argument begins," she said. "You have handed the enemy your conclusion before she earned the right to know it. You are showing your hand on turn one, because you do not believe she can stop you even knowing what is coming."

The finger moved down.

"By mapping your own structure you imply the reader may wish to skip portions of it, which is a written admission that your paper contains filler." She turned the page without hurry. "And here you publicly confess to having required assistance. There is no precedent for that in print. Advertising that you needed help is either suicide, or a flex so total it dares the reader to come and test the claim. I have allies, I needed them, and you are welcome to try it."

Then her finger came to rest on the reference list, and stayed there.

"But this," Victoria said, "is the killing stroke."

"It's a bibliography," Hathaway said. "It lists the books I read."

"A witch omits an author's name to strip her of the record. You omitted no one." The pale blue eyes came up. "You have listed forty-one full names, the year each of them published, the volume, and the exact page on which each of them was wrong. It is an itemized challenge with coordinates attached. It reads: I know precisely what you said, I know when you said it, and I know where you live."

Hathaway looked down at her own formatting.

I wrote a term paper. Her developer brain watched the localization layer finish its pass. And the cultural build shipped it as a ballistic threat to the entire discipline.

Nino had already pulled a blank administrative form across the desk and begun filling it out in cramped block capitals.

"Registry of academic convention, naming rights, filed on your behalf," she said, without looking up. "The Hathaway Format. It will clear by evening." The stylus kept moving. "Victoria, port yours into it. I am porting the primary paper as well."

Hathaway's careerist thread put both hands on the table. My name. On a formatting standard. Every ambitious witch on the continent, for as long as anyone is publishing.

Her accountant thread, which was slower and considerably less fun at parties, arrived about four seconds later.

And every paper in this civilization is somebody shooting at somebody.

She had acquired a permanent, prominently displayed position at the head of every academic firefight for the next century, at no charge. Her own standard printed the author's affiliation directly beneath the title, so anyone who wanted a word already had the building.

Great. She filed it under liabilities and declined to look at it again. Come and discuss the margins, then. The arsenal is fully stocked.

Then Nino pulled the manuscript back across the desk, uncapped a red quill, and went through it a second time without saying anything at all. She marked the exact line where each of the four claims came apart, wrote underneath it the question Hathaway had failed to ask, and named the monograph and the page on which the answer had been sitting the entire time, until there was more red in the margins than there was black in the body.

Hathaway carried it back to Room 302 and rewrote the section that night. The clean sheets went into the stack in the morning.

The submission was going to Sophistry.

Marianne Horton, Fourth Seat of the High Council, had founded the Ultra-Modernist school and named her journal after the word the establishment had chosen to insult it with. It reviewed in weeks, and it would print a claim that half the discipline wanted dead on arrival.

A verified miracle sitting in a sealed laboratory counted for nothing at the Axiom of the Water Dragon without a public, peer-reviewed record behind it, and all three papers were going in together as a dedicated feature, one primary and two supplements, which meant the journal would be running a symposium on nightmare conversion whether it had planned one or not.

Hathaway watched Nino square the stack, and her spectator thread came politely to attention.

Six weeks ago the Tenth Seat had stood in a lecture hall and filed the Ultra-Modernists under foolish. Professor Nino Lucent was that woman's sister, and she was submitting to that woman's opposition, with that woman's own student on the author line.

Beside her, Victoria lifted her own pages off the bench and squared them against the steel.

Hathaway was still enjoying it thoroughly when her scheduling thread cleared its throat and produced the item she had been successfully not looking at for nine days.

Emilia Lucas's final. One wondrous item, one client, fourteen thousand nine hundred and two entries of prior art, and a blueprint that was still a featureless rectangle of clean parchment.

[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 55 — 4:30 PM[Location]: Yggdrasil Academy · Dormitory [Golden Bough] · Room 302

"Evangeline is coming to consult on the assignment."

Hathaway choked on her tea, put the cup down hard enough to ring the saucer, and coughed into her sleeve until her eyes streamed.

"Why? Who authorized that? Can't she just answer on the terminal?"

Victoria stood at the window with her posture intact.

"She reported my previous inquiry to Cecilia herself," she said. "Cecilia determined that if Evangeline is capable of being useful, she should be made to be useful, under supervision, on a schedule."

She adjusted one cuff.

"Furthermore. Intent is easier to read across a table."

Hathaway ran the logic twice looking for the seam. A slave disclosing her own unsanctioned contact was the most obedient act available to her, and a channel that already existed was safer supervised than open. Every step was the correct call, made by the right person, for the right reason, and at the end of it Evangeline had a time and a place.

She caught Victoria's hand, lacing her fingers through the leather.

"I'm coming with you," she muttered.

Victoria's gaze came down to their hands and stayed there a moment, and something at the glacial edge of her softened by a fraction of a degree.

She nodded once.

[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 55 — 6:00 PM[Location]: White City · The Witch's House · Private Booth

Evangeline had been there a while.

She sat back in the velvet with a glass of luminous Blue Sophora on the table in front of her, and the level in the glass had barely moved. Her posture was the picture of unhurried leisure, every line of it effortless, dashing, charming, and instantly punchable.

Victoria crossed the booth in three clipped strides, sat down directly opposite, and folded her hands.

"If you have something useful, say it. When you are finished you may leave."

Evangeline's eyes glinted over the rim of the glass. "If you will not ask, how am I to know what you find useful?"

"What is your relationship with the Fifth Seat." Victoria's hands stayed folded. "And why did you commit something as staggeringly rude as arriving uninvited and departing without a word."

Evangeline sighed, set the glass down, and took her time.

"Taking Berenice at her literal word is the first step toward becoming a fool," she said. "I merely went to her house for a buffet."

She smiled. "You should ask your ghost witch friend about it."

Hathaway's cross-reference thread pulled the file and briefly forgot to be afraid.

Ghost Witches phased through every ward in commercial production and spent the gift almost exclusively on the private dessert vaults of apex witches, leaving equivalent gold on the empty trays, which satisfied nobody. Hathaway had eaten a box out of Irene's, handed to her by the Eighth Seat with the awkwardness of an adult offloading candy on a child to end a conversation.

"I was tagging along with a raiding party," Evangeline said, entertained by the memory. "One does not present a calling card under those circumstances, nor linger for goodbyes."

Victoria's shoulders held exactly where they were.

"You stated that she hates cats. Your evidence."

"There is not a single living cat on her estate."

"Impossible." The rebuttal came fast and flat. "It is public record that the Fifth Seat holds one of the largest collections of rare Lantern Cats in the Inner Sea."

"Oh, yes." Evangeline's smile widened. "Collected. And every one of them is housed in a folded-space habitat calibrated precisely to its preservation."

Hathaway could not hold it any longer.

"Then why did she write the manual?"

Evangeline turned her head, and the amethyst eyes came to rest on her with the fond patience of a tutor who has watched a promising student walk into the same trench three times running and is prepared to watch it happen again.

"A manual of that rigor requires only that someone watched, very closely, for a very long time."

"Neutral research interest would explain it," Hathaway said, pushing. "But nobody spends years observing something they hate."

"You have the causality reversed." Evangeline did not raise her voice. "She did not watch them because she hated them. She hates them because she watched them that long."

The booth went quiet until Victoria broke it. "What is it you want."

Evangeline spread her hands, empty.

"You asked a question. I answered it."

Victoria let the answer sit exactly as long as it deserved. "She likes sunflowers. Is that true."

Evangeline settled further back into the velvet, and her posture went from relaxed to something a shade past it.

"Indeed it is."

Victoria sat with that for a long moment, pale eyes tracking a calculation nobody else could see. Then she looked up.

"What is your assessment of Professor Lucas."

Evangeline picked up the Blue Sophora, swirled it once, and drank. When she lowered the glass her expression had gone somewhere quiet and mocking.

"I am very grateful to her."

A cold line traced itself down Hathaway's spine for no reason she could produce on request.

Victoria stood, aristocratic armor fully restored, and walked out.

Outside, the evening had come down cool over the Commercial Ring, and Hathaway floated most of the way to the corner.

Because the brief had cracked. Forty-four other students in that cohort were right now competing to build the best possible object, and the client had fourteen thousand nine hundred and two of those already, boxed and logged and never mentioned again.

Stop shipping stats. Her inner producer had the whole design document open and was writing fast. Ship the one thing in that collection that's actually about her.

And she had exactly one personal fact about the client, forty minutes old: a species Irene Habsburg could not stand, catalogued and preserved and studied to the point of contempt by a woman who had never once stopped taking notes.

I know what I'm building.

Beside her, Victoria walked with her chin level and her jaw under load, and said nothing at all for two full blocks, and did not let go of her hand.
