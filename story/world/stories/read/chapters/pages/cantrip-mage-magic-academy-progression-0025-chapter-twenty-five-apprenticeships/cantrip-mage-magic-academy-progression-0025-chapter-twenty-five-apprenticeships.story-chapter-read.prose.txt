Two days later, Eola was getting frustrated with her new apprenticeship.

She sat in one corner of an empty classroom, tinkering away at a spell-chain that should mimic Reap and Scythe. The spell wasn’t a combat spell—it was something grain-attuned mages used to help with the harvests—but that was the point. Instructor Tarik had encouraged her to try a spell she never thought she’d need.

That was fine, but nothing Colin had done seemed to have changed. He was two desks over, mumbling to himself as he worked through the steps to one of his First Order rituals. And Roth had a harp. An entire harp. The angry-looking boy glared at them from across the room as his fingers danced across the strings, almost like he was daring them to say something about it, and magic welled around him. A ball of glowing, yellow-orange light appeared.

Instructor Tarik clapped. So did Patrice.

Roth’s glare only grew deeper.

Of the five, Eola was the only one who seemed to be trying to take their mission seriously. Patrice certainly wasn’t. She hadn’t cast a single spell, drawn a single rune, or demonstrated any magical aptitude at all.

As her frustration grew, she snapped her copy of Evander’s Guide to Battle-Marks shut and turned in her seat. “Master Tarik, what are we supposed to be doing?”

“What?” The professor tore himself away from the harp and raised an eyebrow.

“I know we’re supposed to be working toward something. What is it?”

“I honestly don’t know. My goal for the first few weeks of your apprenticeships is simply to facilitate as you confront your problems with Ordered Magic. If I can do that, my hope is that I’ll start seeing how Ordered Magic works as well.”

“You don’t know how Ordered Magic works?” Roth asked accusatorily. Colin didn’t even look up from his chanting.

Instructor Tarik sighed. “Of course I know how Ordered Magic works. Every professor knows how it works—on a surface level, and deeply enough to handle the questions we get in classes. But as I tried to explain, the mechanics of attunements, Mana, and soul are a mystery. Miss Lemiene, you’ve had experiences with Mana lines. What do you think of them?”

“They’re like a walker-roller for a child before they can safely walk on their own,” Eola said promptly. “But they’re not necessary.”

“And yet, Madame Reyanna teaches them as if they’re gospel from Y’aer themselves. She was trained with them, and they help her, so she’s training another generation of mages in them without stopping to consider what thinking in terms of Mana lines actually accomplishes. I don’t believe that they’re a learning lie, personally. If they were, they wouldn’t work for so many mages. But I don’t know that. I don’t know much of anything.”

Atta jumped onto Eola’s lap, then climbed onto the desk to curl on top of her rune dissection. She sighed; the cat familiar had started to manifest in the classroom as well as her bedroom, and she was vaguely inconvenient at best.

Roth’s fingers strolled across the strings, and his glare deepened.

“Do you play anything else, Mr. Gerr?” Instructor Tarik asked.

“Yes.” Instead of elaborating, his fingers started to waltz from string to string, letting the notes hang in the air. They moved faster and faster, and after perhaps ten seconds, a glowing shield appeared in the air. It was wider, but thinner, than Eola’s own cast of Safe Shield.

“Runeless magic?” Eola asked after a moment.

“No. It’s not runeless. It’s a different language. Musical notation. It wouldn’t work for anyone with a normal attunement, but I’ve always been a freak,” Roth said. It was the most he’d said in one go, and he shut his mouth right after.

“Fascinating. So, you’re still following the same steps as Ordered Magic?” Tarik asked.

“Yes. My dad wasn’t happy about it. I ended up with my uncle most of the time.”

Eola swallowed painfully. “I was also with my uncle. That’s something we have in common.”

“We don’t have anything in common,” Roth snapped. He stood, picked up the harp, and slowly, gently set it into its padded case. Then he picked it up with one hand—the boy was strong—and stomped to the door, kicked it open, and vanished. Tarik made no move to stop him.

Eola stared as the door swung shut. She hadn’t talked about her uncle or parents with anyone, and she wouldn’t have done it with Roth either, but it had been two days since Evelyn and Bannoque had moved out and Roth and Patrice had moved in. Most of the time, students got shuffled around after their apprenticeships began, but with both Colin and Eola in the same suite, it had been easier to leave them both in place.

Two days with Roth Gerr. Two days with a brooding, angry boy who was constantly playing on his harp, or a flute, or just singing in his room, and who only left it for classes. Just another commonality with her, Eola decided—but it was a frustrating one even so.

“Well,” Patrice said, then let the silence hang.

“Well, what?” Colin asked, finally looking up from his ritual. “Are you going to do some magic? Show us your attunement?”

Eola nodded. “You’ve never cast a single spell when I could see it, and you had Colin cast that hangover charm after Y’aer’s Day. You have to be able to do some magic—otherwise, you couldn’t have come here.” Even as she said it, she shivered, vaguely aware of who she sounded like: Catrine Andrese.

“Nope. This is just another class for me, and I never do anything in classes.” Patrice smiled widely. “I’m the worst mage at Varin’s Academy for a reason, Eola.”

“I’m going to have to see your spellwork eventually, Miss Clerk,” Instructor Tarik said.

“If you insist on it, it’ll just be you and me, Prof, and it’ll be in a demonstration circle, with full protective runes up—or even better, in the cells, if you can swing it for me. Otherwise, I’m out.”

Eola perked up at that, and even Colin leaned in to listen. But Instructor Tarik only sighed and nodded. “Very well. If you really find the cells necessary, we can make that happen. Miss Lemiene, Mr. Tremory, you are dismissed for the afternoon. Miss Clerk, it’s time for me to see what you can do.”

Patrice grimaced, and Eola winced in sympathy as the red-haired girl mumbled under her breath. “And what I can’t.”

As far as Eola was concerned, her new roommates were an improvement over Evelyn and Bannoque. Sure, Roth looked like he was ready to attack someone almost all the time, and his harp, flute, and beautiful tenor voice meant the suite’s common room was even less quiet than it had been when her old roommates had used it for make-out sessions. And yes, Patrice was loud, talkative, and prone to barging through her door during a study session—or to yelling through it when Eola tried to lock her out.

But at least Roth left her alone, and at least Patrice understood—after some explaining—what she was trying to do. And they weren’t interested in exploring each other’s mouths and bodies on the couch either, thank Y’aer. It was…nice…having four people in Varin’s Academy who knew her struggle with unattuned Mana, and what she was trying to do with her spell-chains.

She had eight figured out now.

Call and Calm had been first; the familiar-bonding spell that had linked Eola and Atta could also be used to summon a mage’s familiar to them, but in their case, it was all but worthless. Atta simply didn’t need it, since any time Eola left the room, she followed along in her stone figurine form.

Chromite Cloth was a good combat spell-chain for casting before a fight, but the temporary armor was less flexible in combat than a Safe Shield. It was simply too slow to cast. In fact, that was a problem with all of Eola’s First Order equivalents. She still hadn’t cracked the speed problem. On the other hand, it had been her fifth spell—a major milestone on her path to Journeymage.

Her other spells included five from the list of modifiable, general First Order spells Instructor Tarik had provided. None of them were combat spells at all. She was happy with her version of Ward Watch, though. The spell normally created a barrier in a door, window, or similar structure, but by swapping the Silenced mark out for a Reaching mark, Eola found that she could adjust the width and length of the spell effect, letting her seal off entire sections of a room instead. That had some combat potential.

More importantly, it wasn’t really Ward Watch anymore, either. Eola was looking for other marks that she could substitute in her spells, but so far, all she’d gotten was a lot of minor backlash. There was only one exception: Lighting Wing.

It had been the first spell she’d encountered in Theory of Magick: A Guide to Ordered Spellcraft. The spell-chain was based on Bright Ball, but where the Child’s Magic cantrip threw a long-lasting orb of light into the air, Lighting Wing produced a glaring line of light that cut across space. It had several advantages over most of Eola’s other spells.

First, it was fast. It took just the blink of an eye from the time it filled until the time it hit. It took longer to cast, of course, but the modifying marks she’d slowly found included Held. She could pre-cast it just like Chromite Cloth.

Second, it actually hurt her opponents. The glare was enough to blind and dazzle, and a direct hit produced nasty-looking burns. They weren’t lethal—far from it—but they were painful. That gave her an offensive option she could use against other mages.

But the last feature was the most important for her. It was the second modifying mark. Peacebonded. If she removed it and replaced it with Echoing, the Lighting Wing turned into a much, much brighter layered light blade, and it did a lot more damage. A single Safe Shield still beat it, of course, but if she beat an opponent’s guard, it could end a fight.

The trouble was still speed. It was always speed.

It took a Journeymage between five and nine seconds to cast most First Order spells. That was why most duelists and spellswords rarely cast anything higher than a First Order spell, and most stuck to Child’s Magic and the simplest of Ordered Magic. Even five seconds was an eternity in most fights—only the fastest and most forgiving First Order runes were useful to them.

Most of her spell-chains took her twenty or more.

And she couldn’t reduce that at all.

After a few hours, she’d had enough of theorizing. Lighting Wing was plenty fast as a Held spell, and she couldn’t accelerate its sketch any more than it already was. With a sigh, she set her notes in their spot on her desk and stood up.

The common room was…loud. But harp music was a better variety of loud than what her old roommates had been up to every night.

“House Clerk’s out on the Lake of Shards, jammed between it and the northern Spines,” Patrice said. The sound of harp music and Roth’s tenor accompanying it was nice, at least. The two girls sat on the couch, Patrice’s notes and books conspicuously closed. Eola wasn’t sure she’d ever seen them open. Then again, the redhead clearly had something going on with her mystery attunement, and Eola wanted to find out what.

Besides, research into her own attunement was difficult, and until she could access the third floor’s books, she didn’t see a good way to speed it up. Colin knew something was wrong with her mark arrangements, but he couldn’t explain what.

So, here she was, listening to ‘Lady’ Patrice Clerk talk about her family.

“Foglight’s cold. Most of the time, the river through it’s iced over, and we’ve got these docks on skis that go out on the Lake of Shards when it starts freezing up in the coldest stretches of winter, too. But the city exports fresh vegetables to nearby towns, even in the winter. House Clerk’s family attunement takes care of half of that, and the other half is the greenhouses.”

It was obvious from Patrice’s grin that she wanted Eola to ask, so Eola did. “What’s the attunement?”

“That’s our secret. It’s actually two! My brother’s a heat-attuned mage, and my mother’s a soil mage.” Patrice smiled even wider. “Between them, the greenhouses, and the harvest mages we hire, it’s two or three weeks from planting to harvesting in the Foglight greenhouses. We use Bright Ball and other light spells to grow through the nights.”

Then her smile cracked slightly. “I was supposed to be a soil mage.”

“But instead, you’re…?”

Patrice shook her head, not looking at the books on the table. “The worst mage at Varin’s Academy.”

Eola’s hand balled into a fist in her lap. “The four of us are supposed to be helping each other figure this out. We’re apprenticed to Master Tarik now, and if he’s right and no one understands Ordered Magic, your attunement could be hugely important for learning why—and…I know you want to make it work.”

“My attunement is…” Patrice hesitated, then shook her head again, red hair flying every which way. She looked, if anything, suddenly embarrassed. “It’s not part of this mess. Tarik made a mistake picking me. Your attunements all have rules. They might not be Ordered Magic’s rules, but they’re rules. Mine…when you were ten or so, your attunement came in, right?”

“Right.” Eola shut her eyes. The room was quiet, but she could hear her Dad’s voice even now, explaining to her that she’d never cast most of the spells in her book, that even Journeymage would be beyond her. Ink and chalk would be her only sketching tools for Ordered magic, not wands or staves or even her fingers.

“Mine came in while I was helping in one of the Clerk greenhouses. I…sort of blew out the glass. Not intentionally, it just kind of happened. After that, I got the usual treatment. An endless string of tutors, a few more blown-out windows and scorched rooms, and eventually, an arrangement with Varin’s Academy. If I didn’t try to do magic until I came here, they’d try to figure me out.”

“So, you didn’t do magic at all for how long?” Eola asked.

Patrice shrugged, but pain flashed across her face for a moment. “Five years. Since I was thirteen.”

Eola shuddered.

“Not even Child’s Magic. When my attunement came in, that got to be too dangerous, too.”

Roth’s door opened, and his dark eyes glared out from under a shock of messy, near-black hair. A pure white songbird echoed the look from his shoulder. The door clicked shut a moment later, and he pushed by the couch and out their suite’s door. As he went, Eola caught the armor under his robes. It wasn’t a metal breastplate, or even metal at all. Leather gloves, a thick jacket that looked like padded cloth overtopped with deer hide, and boots. And, as far as she could tell, he didn’t carry a sword. Instead, a hatchet hung from his belt, opposite a flute.

The door shut behind him, and Patrice stared at it for a moment. “What do you think his problem is? His attunement works, at least.”

Eola didn’t have an answer, and after a few more minutes of sitting, Patrice stood, gathered her books, and said her goodbyes, leaving Eola by herself in the suite. She shrugged, went back to her room, and cracked open Components. She’d almost finished her translation, and when she did, she’d be able to—finally—read the book for information instead of interpretation.
