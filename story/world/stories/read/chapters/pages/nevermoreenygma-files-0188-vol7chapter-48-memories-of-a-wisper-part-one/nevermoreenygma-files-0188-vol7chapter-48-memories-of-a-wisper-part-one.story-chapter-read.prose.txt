The first year in Wishper was more observation than action. It wasn’t that they doubted her ability, on the contrary, they wanted to make sure she knew how to operate within the team before throwing her into high-risk missions.

After the welcome in Kyoto, she and Jinat took a small plane that dropped them off in some fog-shrouded mountains somewhere in northern China.

From what Mai had gathered, the entire place must have once been a monastery at some very distant point in time, but it had been refurbished almost like a modern military base, while still retaining a certain ancient air among the buildings, walls, and winding passages scattered throughout the mountains. The entire site had been donated to Wishper by Fu Xiang, the alchemist.

The truth was, however, that the place didn’t even have an owner to begin with. It had vanished from history hundreds of years ago, never to be found again. Fu Xiang had discovered it decades earlier with his wife, and no one had visited it since—until he donated it to Wishper to serve as a base of operations. Despite the renovation, Fu made it clear that the forests—let alone the peach trees—would remain untouched. A forest at that altitude was a miracle, but the presence of peach trees was more than just unusual. Mai could only think of a certain mountain legend and its connection to peaches, but she decided it was best to save that for her own research in the future.

And this was now where she would spend her time until her deployment began.

Wishper was no ordinary group, it was a small army without allegiance to any flag. Its ranks were mostly composed of feys and humans with special abilities. There were people without any of these traits, but their roles were more focused on logistics, intelligence, and analysis. They were the ones who made sure operations had the necessary information, planned escape routes, and prepared resources when needed. However, on the battlefield, the special teams were the ones calling the shots.

Mai realized this in her first month within the organization. She wasn’t the only newcomer, but she was the most closely watched. Jinat had been clear in the initial assessment:

“Like I told you, we don’t want you to be just another soldier, Mai. We want you to know what to do when you’re alone in the worst possible place and how to work in a team.”

That “worst place” was something she still didn’t fully understand. She thought her years in Japan had prepared her enough, but it didn’t take long for her to discover there were many things she wasn’t ready for.

The trainings weren’t only physical. Wishper worked on infiltration tactics, undercover operations, tracking, and close-quarters combat. They knew how to move in the most dark and strange corners of the hidden world, how to avoid detection by magic or technology, and how to adapt to any situation on the field.

The first month was quite tough. Mai was fast more than strong, but also she was skilled, but that meant nothing if she didn’t understand the whole team’s flow and each member movement. Every mistake she can made translated into a hit or an insufficient reaction time. They didn’t treat her harshly out of disdain, but because they needed her to be the best she could be.

It didn’t take long before that lesson stopped being theoretical.

The exercises were simple on the manual. The practical side of things was another matter.

Jinat had designated specific areas for different types of training, and in Mai’s case, so that the others could observe her skills. Mai simply failed to observe the others’ movements.

A small structure built along the east mountainside in the training area, with three levels, and multiple entry points. That was a hostage scenario. One target to extract, three hostiles inside, one roaming outside. A standard drill designed to test coordination under pressure.

Mai and the others had studied the layout beforehand. Memorized the routes, the blind spots, the likely positions.

She moved before the signal.

Her body reacted faster than thought—slipping through a side entrance, silent, precise. One of the “hostiles” turned the corner and didn’t even have time to raise his weapon before she struck, disarming him and pinning him to the ground.

“I’m dead,” said the hostile.

Clean. Efficient.

She advanced.

Another target ahead—she sensed him before she saw him. Her antennae vibrated faintly, mapping the space. She cut through the corridor, taking him down just as quickly.

Two down. Too easy. Good.

And then there was a sound behind her.

Mai froze.

Not part of her path.

Not part of her plan.

She turned.

Through the open corridor, she saw it—one of her teammates, a boy she barely knew, collapsing against the wall. A mark burned through his chest armor, smoke rising from the impact point.

“I’m dead.”

The third and fourth hostile stepped out from cover, weapon still raised.

She hadn't taken into account the possibility of a fourth person, even though the exercise was supposed to involve three adversaries. Her tendency to improvise rather than always follow the information provided had let her down. It was true. She'd been through that more than once in Tokyo. But here, she'd relied solely on the exercise instructions.

Mai moved, but it was already over.

The instructor’s voice echoed through the structure.

“Stop. Reset.”

The world snapped back into silence.

The “hostile” lowered his weapon. The boy on the ground let out a breath, still conscious, but not getting up immediately.

Simulation.

But the damage had been real enough.

Mai stood there for a moment, the echo of the shot lingering in her ears.

They regrouped outside. No one spoke at first. The wind moved softly through the mountains, as if nothing had happened.

Jinat approached her, hands in her pockets, expression unreadable.

“You cleared your route,” she said.

Mai didn’t answer.

“You neutralized two targets in under thirty seconds.”

From the corner of her eye, she saw the boy being helped to his feet. He avoided looking at her.

Jinat stopped in front of her.

“You succeeded,” she continued. “That’s the problem.”

Mai clenched her jaw.

“You left your sector. You didn’t wait for the team to close the perimeter. You created a gap.”

“I could handle them,” Mai replied quietly.

“I know.”

That answer came too fast.

“And while you were proving that,” Jinat added, her voice calm, almost detached, “someone else took the hit.”

Mai nodded. “I know, sorry.”

The words settled slowly.

“You don’t get to win alone here.”

Mai looked down at her hands.

They were steady.

Too steady.

“Again,” Jinat said, already turning away. “Same scenario.”

No lecture. No punishment.

Just repetition.

Mai took a breath and nodded.

This time, when the signal came, she didn’t move first.

“You’re good alone, but here you can’t just be an individualist.” Jinat told her after the drill, there was three successes, and in the variation only two failed attempts, one where she had reacted too late. “If you act like this is only your mission, someone else is going to die because of you. These aren't just street fights.”

Mai didn’t argue. She knew it was true.

Street fights.

But that stung a little. Mai felt like she’d taken her job in Tokyo seriously. The underworld had its own tactics and sometimes military training, but it was true that, in the end, she’d ended up in a fistfight with someone on several occasions. So calling it a “street fight” wasn’t exactly a lie. But it did sound a little insulting; she couldn’t deny it.

The training was spartan but tailored. Not everyone received the same program. Since feys usually had different abilities and most had none at all, sometimes small adjustments had to be made to the training.

The trainers had actually been part of armies in the past: United States, China, Russia, Israel. After many wars, some became disillusioned with military life and moved to the private sector, while others simply switched fields. Wishper had tapped into the latter to hire and train generations of feys, espers, and mages. In the case of the last two, there was no need to hire soldiers, but specialists in magic and psychic powers. This even required more control depending on the mage or esper.

Mai was surprised when she was informed that even she would have to take ESP and magic classes in the study halls of the enormous library at the base. But she understood. Even without using magic herself, knowing it had saved her life more than once, whenever she had to face someone who could wield it and evade them.

These classes were held in another part of the temple that had once been a study library but had since been renovated.

Symbols were drawn on the walls, from which scrolls with magical lessons hung. The library floor was a gigantic mandala with a study area arranged like an amphitheater. In the central table, a quartz sphere was suspended on a copper stand, surrounded by a series of crystals that emitted a faint, almost imperceptible hum.

The class consisted of about sixty students at that time, all mixed together. Feys, humans, one couldn’t tell who had magic or who didn’t, or who could use abilities.

In front of her, a thin black man with round glasses and the air of a chemistry professor who had taken a detour into the occult introduced himself in a slightly tired voice. It was Anton, and as she later learned, his specialty was control magic—truly dangerous since it was linked to voodoo.

Obviously, she introduced herself as the newest member and heard a few whistles.

“How old are these people?” Mai thought. Apart from two hobbit-like figures off to the side, who looked like children, almost everyone else appeared to be young adults feys or more old human people. She was sure no one in that room could be younger than their mid-twenties. Although, well, it was impossible to know the age of feys only by the looking.

Mai sat at the back, with a notebook full of schematic drawings that had been given to her by one of the instructors: summoning circles, diagrams of etheric flow, notes in Japanese, Chinese, and an ancient dialect she didn’t fully understand.

“Miss Kanda Mai, right?” Anton said, checking a tablet.

Who? Mai thought.

“Sorry… Kanzaki Mai.” Mai crossed her arms, raising an eyebrow. “No, that’s crossed out… sorry. It’s Izumi Mai.”

Mai glanced to the side as she heard some laughter. Identities are masks, after all, she thought gloomily. At least he got the name right. Who the hell had written her name wrong and then crossed it out? Had anyone heard of her identities in Tokyo?

“I would like to know your understanding of basic magical systems.”

“Yes, although I am not a mage,” Mai replied, crossing her arms.

“No one is entirely, until they try.”

Mai looked at him with the same expression a cat gives a running blender.

“Alright,” the instructor said, turning on a voice recorder. “Tell me, in your own words, how you distinguish between a magician, a sorcerer, and a witcher.”

“Really?”

“Really. Think of it as if I’m conducting a survey for your grandmother.”

Mai sighed.

“I never met her, professor.”

“Then for mine.”

There was a silence. She raised her gaze, resigned.

“Very well. Magicians, in the broad sense, channel external energy through symbols, rituals or conduits. They use rules, formulas, study. They are like engineers of miracles and approach magic as a hard science, using materials to create rituals and spells. Many cannot control magic on their own, but they can learn to use it through study. Part of alchemy also comes from that magic, since it was a system designed by people without magic. Philosophical systems can also be created among magicians.”

“Sorcerers use internal energy—the ki, the will, or the soul—blood inheritance, it depends on the manual you read. And witchers… they make deals. They bargain. They steal power, or borrow it from entities with which their spirituality aligns.”

Mai swallowed. She knew this part very well. “It was through animism that magic evolved into shamanism in ancient times. All current branches of magic are a more complex systematization of shamanism, including all religions, as maps to understand the world. The difference lies in the cultural environment, but it is believed that originally they all came from the same source.”

The religions that followed were an attempt to gain greater knowledge of the forces existing in nature and were imbued with powers and forms people could recognize, creating belief systems that over time evolved into religions for humans. From there, it branched into exoteric knowledge, which includes the practices of ordinary people: rituals, liturgical practices like prayer, study, and group practices. Then there is the esoteric, which are the branches that attempt to delve deeper and create new branches of knowledge in specific areas of those religions or by creating new philosophical systems.”

Mai took a breath after the explanation. Thanks to Miho, she had learned the fundamentals of shamanism quite well. Globally, it was the oldest form, but being isolated in cultural environments, it was also restrictive in some ways. Although, at the same time, there were branches like the dream walkers and those who used the energy of ley lines, which were exceptions.

As she spoke, Mai opened her case and took out a metal pendant with a small obsidian talisman. She placed it on the table.

“This doesn’t cast magic,” she said. “It only amplifies what I already have. It’s like an antenna. But I cannot use magic myself. My case is just a fey skill.”

“Yes. Or rather, a kind of spiritual sonar. I can sense surfaces, presences, vibrations, but I cannot see the future or shoot fireballs.”

The professor jotted down a few more notes, then looked up with curiosity.

"Interesting. Your ability falls within restrictive skills."

Mai nodded. "Exactly, like those who use specialized magic and cannot use either sorcery or witcher powers, but can still learn magic. In my case, I don’t know much magic, I just learned a little of the basics to help with my work. My echolocation ability has been more useful."

“Because my fey core is very small.”

“Something like that.”

Is this a nursery for big kids? Mai thought anxiously.

“She really is a moth,” “Her cuteness level has gone up 100 percent.”

I can hear you, Mai thought, blushing but tried to focus in the sphere.

Then everything stopped.

The word fey itself encompasses many creatures from the hidden world, but one of its roots is ‘fee.’ One of the most common creatures, yet also most connected to the primary elements of water, earth, nature, and air. Your only weakness is fire… ironic, since moths are drawn to the light,” he said, glancing at Mai’s antennae, which had stopped vibrating.

Miho had told her something similar a long time ago, but only about fire. She hadn’t mentioned the connection to so many elements, just nature. Perhaps she had generalized it. Although it was true that Miho always told her to spend time in nature because it was good for her. Was that the reason? Well, almost everyone feels better in nature unless they have a phobia of insects or are germophobic.

Either way, Mai had learned something a bit more specific about her nature that she hadn’t found in Miho’s books. Perhaps it was due to different approaches? If so, what did the hidden Chinese and western literature say about her abilities as a fey?

The instructor smiled, noticing Mai’s expression as if she were searching her mind for answers. “I have high expectations of you… Miss Izumi. You may return to your seat. I’ll come by and indicate where you should start your studies to catch up.”

Mai nodded. “Thank you.”

And so she began to get accustomed to life there.

Morning training, magic classes, and specialized training.

In the afternoons, depending on the day, she could dedicate herself to base activities such as cleaning, equipment maintenance, or even special classes. Jinat was one of those who taught these classes alongside other instructors, covering global activities and news from the hidden world. They conducted practice and strategy exercises, posing potential combat, rescue, and infiltration scenarios. Mai wondered about the resources, but it was obvious that not everything came from Fu.

ZAIEN and R&R always supplied resources—ships, equipment. Weapons were obtained through other means, and many parts were assembled directly on base. Beyond that, Jinat was somewhat displeased with ZAIEN, and more specifically, with its leader.

Since the early days of the war on terrorism in the Middle East, and later the global conflict against Octogonus, a strange rumor had circulated: that ZAIEN had been involved in a certain project within the Kingdom of the United States. A kind of predictive program—supposedly the reason behind so many successful strikes against the enemy. Apparently it was something tied to the DoD of the kingdom and ZAIEN, but few details were known, and the meetings with ZAIEN’s shadowed leader had never revealed whether such a technology truly existed.

It might have been nothing more than misinformation spread by Octogonus. Still, the doubt lingered.

Although that was a matter for the higher-ranking officers. As for Mai, she had nothing to do with it, but she still heard the rumors circulating around the base all the time.

As the months passed, she learned to trust her teammates, to read their movements before they even spoke. In Japan, she had always worked alone or in small units, but here every mission could mean the life or death of dozens. She also ran into some familiar faces—though what pleased her most was finding Miria, the helicorn.

Miria was the real jewel of the base, even the dragons patrolling the mountains paused when they saw her galloping through the sky with her wings unfolded, the helix-shaped horn gleaming on her forehead. Being the only one of her kind on Earth made her something extraordinary. A centaur with iridescent wings and a spiraling, prism-like horn was not something one saw every day. Added to that, she occasionally walked around bare-chested, so it was no surprise she drew many stares.

Among all the beings inhabiting the base, she was one of the most singular.

Standing, Miria reached nearly two meters in her equine half form—pearl-white with bluish reflections—and filled almost the entire length of her room.

But she usually took her humanoid form to fit in places, so there was no problem. The mystery of her biomass was somewhat of a secret, but apparently shifting forms was a state that involved Miria’s own magic. It seemed that every part of her was a version in a different state that she could switch between at will. This made her almost a kind of shapeshifter, though with a limitation: she always retained her humanoid features from the waist up, except for her wings.

It was Miria who invited her to share her barracks. To the envy of many, she accepted, and that was when Mai learned her secret of hiding her form, as well as a little bit about Miria's personal life. Miria could be considered a girl who was interested in many things, and she was quite fun when she was not working.

In her room were books, flight instruments, CDs, star maps, and—curiously—a collection of bottles filled with sands in different hues. A hobby with no apparent reason behind it.

“From places I’ve already visited,” Miria explained one night. “When I fly, I like remembering that not everything that disappears is forgotten.”

Mai, though she didn’t say it, understood the metaphor all too well.

Living with Miria turned out easier than expected. They shared schedules, humor, and a strange sort of complicity in thei rmischief at the base with some of the other members. Sometimes Mai helped her braid her mane—a task requiring sometimes patience and really steady hands—and other times Miria prepared impossible infusions, brewed from herbs that grew only at altitudes where the air tasted faintly of ozone. The air in those mountains was nothing like the smog-choked industrial centers of China, and even different from Japan.

At night, when the wind rolled down from the higher mountains and the base fell quiet, they talked about everything and nothing: the hidden world, the rumors of war, old myths that, in some cases, weren’t so old after all. Miria’s laughter was deep and far gentler than when they’d first met during operations in 2009. Despite the horrors she’d endured as the only one her kind, she hadn’t allowed them to ruin her spirit.

“I belong to the sky. But sometimes… I like landing too,” she often said.

From then on, they shared a roof, conversations, and a routine which, though disciplined, became far more bearable. Between training sessions, classes, and simulated missions, that corner of the base turned into their refuge—a small world suspended between the noise of the day and the stillness of the night. Unfortunately, Miria often disappeared as well.

She was already a Wisper—meaning she was on active missions—while Mai still had more training to complete.

But eventually, the deployment missions came in 2013.

The first real assignment was in Europe—Spain—together with a mixed team of ten. Though Mai assumed she would be operating mainly in Asia, some of the commanders wanted to test her adaptability in the field by placing her with a different unit. Jinat hadn’t been particularly pleased, but Mai accepted.

The objective was to infiltrate a network trafficking from Africa into the black markets of central Europe. Her task wasn’t to burst in guns blazing, but to move unseen, collect information, and ensure the leaders were eliminated without alerting the rest of the organization.

Mai understood it well. They moved through the night, tracking the traffickers’ movements, intercepting messages, rerouting transport lines so they would arrive exactly where a larger team of wispers waited. In the end, the operation concluded in absolute silence: the leaders vanished one by one, unnoticed until it was far too late. The rest of the network collapsed within a week.

In the months that followed, more operations came. Some were extraction missions, others elimination, and many were dedicated solely to gathering information. She moved across Europe, the Middle East, and parts of South America, witnessing with her own eyes what Jinat had warned her about.

Japan was a paradise compared to other places. In many countries, feys were hunted like animals or used as tools in rituals and experiments. In others, they simply disappeared into the shadows, sold to organizations that drained them dry and sold their essence to the highest bidder.

Mai learned quickly that they could not save everyone.

And that was what frustrated her the most.

The Star Worshippers’ cult had splinter sects now that their leaders were gone—small collectives that were better eliminated before their threat could grow. Yet the main heads never reappeared.

Octogonus, pressured by war in the Middle East from both the Empire and Russia, had also lost much of its power. Most of those who remained had faded away, leaving only small cells. Rumors claimed that the missing ones had abandoned their cause entirely, disappearing into the lucrative world of digital currencies, amassing fortunes and trading activism for anonymous comfort. But the seeds of discord they had planted over the years became inspiration for new organizations.

Between missions, thanks to the contacts she had made in her early years, she decided to investigate something. Wishper had an extensive network, and through a few acquaintances, Mai sought answers to a question that had lingered in her mind ever since her conversations with Aya: her father’s story.

During her time in Tokyo she had heard the stories about her mother, Izumi Hisui, a fey mercenary whose name had left behind scattered stories and really, really, strange rumors throughout the twentieth century. Little was known about those years, for Izumi had tried to live quietly before her death—or disappearance.

But her father remained a mystery.

And Mai wanted to know if anything, anyone, any trace of family remained.

The truth: the last recorded member of her lineage had died in the 1990s.

But in her search, she found far more than names.

The records spoke of an old family, rooted in the south of France—the Quironellé.

A surname that, she discovered, came from a Hellenic branch that had migrated centuries earlier from Epirus, carrying with them a particular devotion to Chiron, the healer-centaur, from whom they claimed descent.

They had served as archers and field surgeons in various European wars, a minor house of rural nobles whose crest portrayed a centaur with a bow and a bandaged arm.

Par la main et la flèche, nous soignons. —By the hand and the arrow, we heal— read the motto carved beneath the shield.

Her father, Quincy Quironellé, had been one of the last direct descendants, though over time the family had faded through marriages, migrations, and modern disinterest.

A small lineage, but one that left its mark by producing notable archers in both France and England, where the surname gradually transformed—first into Acheron, then into Acherson, until disappearing entirely in the 18th century.

Quincy had been the younger of two brothers, and had fled home at a young age to become a mercenary. The story cut off there: years of silence, until he reappeared in Japan—Quincy Hisui—after taking his wife’s surname.

Mai smiled.

Perhaps that explained why she felt so naturally drawn to bows. On her father’s side, an entire history had been etched into her blood.

And even if she was a fey now, she was also its last survivor.

By 2017, Mai was regarded as one of the best in covert operations. She wasn’t the strongest or the fastest among the members, but her ability to act, infiltrate and extract information was unmatched. Her physical skills, combined with her instinctive ability to sense danger, made her one of the unit’s most valuable agents. And although she had finally been assigned to the Asia-Pacific region, she was still occasionally called for missions elsewhere.

However, the work wasn’t only infiltration.

She had to learn to negotiate with unexpected allies, and move in circles where the betrayal was the rule rather than the exception. She encountered former traffickers who had switched sides, corrupt politicians who used their influence to hide atrocities, and people from the hidden world who weren’t sure whether to trust her—or fear her.

Sometimes, she had to act as a hunter.

Other times, as a diplomat.

And in certain situations, just as a shadow slipping through the darkness, leaving no trace at all.

On one of her missions in 2017, the Wispers intercepted a group of mercenaries transporting containers of feys on a ship in the Red Sea. And was an operation that occured in the middle of the night in the water. It was the first time Mai had faced such a large-scale open confrontation since the Korea incident years earlier.

Bullets flew around her as saltwater splashed across the deck. Her teammates moved with almost surgical precision in that chaos of bullets, magic and blood mixed with salt water, eliminating enemies with terrifying efficiency. Mai leapt onto containers, dodged shots, and fired her own weapon—long overdue, it had been, since she had last faced so many opponents. When she ran out of ammunition, she had to use the weapons of fallen enemies and grenades. But after a half-hour battle, it all came to an end.

When they finally opened the containers, they found two dozens of feys in varying states of deterioration. Some were barely alive. Others were already dead, and a few, according to the list , had simply vanished.

That night, Mai could not sleep. The smell from that container lingered in her nose for days, and the image haunted her mind for months.
