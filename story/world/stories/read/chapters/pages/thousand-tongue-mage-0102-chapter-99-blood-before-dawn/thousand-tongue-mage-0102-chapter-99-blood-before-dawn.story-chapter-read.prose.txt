Dawn of the full-frontal assault came like a blade wrapped in cloth—soft at first glance, sharp if you touched it.

The eastern horizon hadn’t yet broken into full light, but the Salaqa Region had already decided it was morning. Troops tramped across the earth. Axles complained along harsh roads. Siege machines cleared with low, animal groans as crews hauled them south, and all of it ran towards the end of the continent, where—farther than sight—sound struck an invisible wall and echoed back thinner and weaker.

Zora didn’t need eyes to see the walls of the Divine Capital on the southern horizon. The wall’s northern faces were so colossal no sound could roll over them, which left a big ‘blank’ in his senses when it came to figuring out just what was going on behind them.

He supposed he’d find out soon enough.

Early in the morning, the Salaqa Manor’s grand balcony—facing the courtyard—was already crowded with men. Himself aside, Enki stood quietly to his left, Kita stood to her right, while Ifas and Machi stood behind them both respectively. Save for Enki and Ifas, the two ladies were moderately armoured. Even further behind them stood Sora and the heavily armed soldiers: forty-three of them were Salaqa retainers, handpicked elites from the west, east, and central. They were the men who’d be following Enki into the Divine Temple, and they were the strongest infiltrators the Reunification Army had to offer.

Behind the railings, Lord Baya alone faced the courtyard, where a dense field of hundreds of officers, couriers, engineers, and strategists made a living storm. Dozens of tents had already yawned open with orders beginning to multiply. Chalk rasped over slate. Relays of voices threw new information up the chain and pulled new instructions down. Runners sprinted to and from the steps, delivering messages and new reports, and tubes of rolled maps passed from hand to hand.

The first mortar would be fired soon. The Reunification Army would launch their attack in less than thirty minutes, Zora assumed, so if there was any time to make a speech, it would have to be now.

And as the manor’s bell struck the seventh morning hour, Baya let his chest become a bellows.

“Attention!”

The word hit like a flat palm on a drum. Hundreds of heads turned, posture reassembled. Zora heard joints taken back into order, straps tightened, a dozen little resolves being hammered—even the siege crews out on the road felt the command in their bones and glanced back at the Salaqa Lord.

What a voice.

“... For ten years,” Baya began, “the Magicicada Witch of Adults, ‘Reverberator’ Decima, has sat in the shadow of the Divine Empress and Her Four Households. For ten years, she has whispered poison into our laws. For ten years, she has guided the reins of the empire away from its duty and bled the Outer Regions as if they were nothing but fodder for rot.”

A runner stopped running to hear. A scribe forgot his page. The word ‘ten’ picked up a decade’s worth of salt and suffering and set it down on the stone.

“She stole our people,” Baya continued. "She stole our grain. Our mines. Our mountains. Our very rivers. And now she seeks to sever the Divine Capital from the Outer Regions entirely. The Divine Capital—the first and final bastion against the Swarm, and the heart that once beat for all of us.”

Around the forecourt, men stood taller without moving. Zora heard armor decide not to rattle, and heard a hundred mouths forget to wet themselves.

“But the blame for the suffering of the Outer Regions does not rest on Decima alone.” Baya’s tone softened. “In the far eastern highlands, there is a saying: ‘The river does not flow because it is ordered, but because it chooses its own path’. There is no such thing as a man who only follows orders. Every man has a will. Every man has a choice. And we—” He swept his hand not towards the common men, but toward himself and those like him, “—we who bear the title of ‘Noble-Blood’… we had chosen silence.”

The words cut clean. Even the ink on the scribes’ brushes paused, as if shame could thicken liquid.

“The Noble-Blood lived cushioned in comfort while our people starved. They warmed their feet while the old froze; they whispered dreams of the future while the young bled; they listened to the Witch’s voices and declared them law. And even I—the Lord of Salaqa—am not exempt for the crimes I have committed towards my people. I knew it was the Witch. I did nothing. There is no difference between being unable to fight and being unwilling to fight, for a closed hand is a closed hand.”

A small wind ran across the forecourt, sliding over helmets.

“... But today,” he said, and the morning light finally caught the edge of his sawtooth blade as he drew it, “is the day we correct our shame. “Today, we will end the Magicicada Witch. Today, we will cut out the infestation at the empire’s heart. Today, the Attini Empire will remember what it was built for.”

He raised the blade to the sky.

“Sixty years ago when the First Divine Empress struck down her first Insect God and began her long march to unite all fifty-six regions under a common banner, she paid for her victory with her left hand.” He lifted his blade higher. “In that moment, her flesh fell to the dirt, and her blood ran red across the southern soil. The ground drank her sacrifice, and it remembered that blood spilt was not blood lost. For every drop she gave the earth, she would take back ten more from the Swarm, and it was with that conviction that she went forth and built the Attini Empire.”

And he didn’t hesitate.

Baya dragged his blade across his left palm, and his fist opened over the railings. Droplets fell—plip, plop—and Zora heard every last one of them sinking into the earth, like little seeds being planted in a very old field.

Zora smiled.

“This,” Baya bellowed, “is my apology to the men of the Attini Empire whom I have failed! I have worn Noble-Blood and not borne noble duty, so this is my declaration of a new beginning! The blood I lose today will be repaid tenfold by the Swarm, and the Magicicada Witch will pay for our suffering!”

With that, he gripped his bloody hand into a fist and saluted, crossing his arm over his chest.

“For the Attini Empire!”

The shout hit the manor’s face and ricocheted back, ran down the steps into the street, shouldered into the waiting ranks, and went further.

What began as a mouthful of air from the grand balcony became a traveling shout that found throats ready to be found.

Companies already marching on the southern roads took it up without breaking stride. Engineers laying the last timber on a siege bed took it up mid-swing. Cooks in field kitchens took it up with ladles in their hands. The shout ran post to post, cart to cart, ladder to ladder.

“For the Attini Empire!”

It leapt across neighborhoods the way, leapt across courtyards, and jumped from gate to gate. Watchtowers threw it on like a cloak and hurled it down to the long columned roads. Men waiting along canal paths gave it to the water, and the water carried it as a hum.

“For the Attini Empire!”

It climbed walls, poured from rooftops, skittered along tiles, sank into courtyards, and ran down the broad roads like a river that’d reach the sea on its own terms. In the fields outside the city, oxen flicked their ears at it. In the barracks, a man who’d planned to be afraid lost his plan and took up a better one. The sound gathered itself into a single tone so large it forgot to end—and the Salaqa Region resonated with the empire’s chant, born from a single dream to avenge his family’s deaths.

“FOR THE ATTINI EMPIRE!”

The chant rolled and rolled until it seemed like it’d never stop. Zora could certainly hear it, still.

Morale’s turned on its heel, I see.

Last night may have been a solemn table set for grief, but now the same table had been upended for breakfast, and everyone ate standing. He supposed that was the nature of a powerful speech. ‘A right word is a drawn bow’ was a saying from the Mori Masif Front, and Baya had loosed his arrow clean.

Baya finally turned away from the railings, and Kita met him with a grin.

“That was cool,” she said.

Baya smiled back as he patted her on the shoulder. “A good speech falls upon the shoulders of all Noble-Blood. It is our duty to hold it and to be held to it.”

While Kita wound bandages around her father’s hand, Zora listened to Baya’s breath settle back into itself. The Salaqa Lord didn’t wince. The man had pain tolerance, and he could give a good speech.

Zora supposed if the Attini Empire hadn’t bound itself to the tradition of Divine Empresses instead of Divine Emperors, Baya would be a decently good candidate for the throne.

Some men can command armies, but very few can command belief.

Once the last bandage knot was tied, Baya turned from his daughter, nodded, and faced the elite team assembled across the balcony.

“This mission will be dangerous,” he said. His voice did not need to rise to be heard. “There is no guarantee any of you will return. Even so, are you ready?”

Forty-three voices answered lightning-quick, unwavering:

“For the Attini Empire.”

With that, Baya and Kita both turned towards Zora. Zora turned to Enki, who’d been leaning against the railings with his diamond rifle resting against his shoulder.

“Do it,” Zora said.

Enki dipped his head.

“All of you face the railings,” he said coldly. “Face them straight and close your eyes. When I tell you to run forward, you run forward, and you do not open your eyes until I say stop.”

Kita frowned. “Why are we closing our eyes again?”

Enki stepped forward, cracking a finger. “I was once on the receiving end of wormholes opened by others. It is violently disorienting for people who have not gone through them, and we will be going through hundreds of them in sequence. Zora will not be affected because he is blind, but the rest of you will be sick if you keep your eyes open—so just run straight ahead and trust my wormholes will take us where we have to go.”

The boy’s tone didn’t invite argument, so a slow ripple of uneasy nods followed as everyone but Zora and Enki closed their eyes. Sora was the last one to do so, but Zora supposed she was just too busy jotting every single moment down in her journal to be on time.

Then space folded in front of them, and the first large wormhole opened in front of them.

Enki sprinted forward.

The rest of them followed.

As Zora plunged through wormhole after wormhole, the world broke into slices around him—thin, flashing impressions carved into sound and pressure. One moment, the air was wide and cold. In the next, artillery boomed from siege platforms, the first cannons firing at the walls. Then he heard soldiers shouting to advance, others dragging the wounded, rifles cracking, dirt erupting under shrapnel. Then there was stone. Then it was open sky again.

The wormholes brought them from the Salaqa Manor to the open battlefield, and they kept running.

Suddenly, the battlefield was behind them. The air changed. The scent changed. They’d sprinted past the giant walls, and now they were inside the Divine Capital.

Still running, they passed shuttered market arcades, avenues lined with statues, narrow residential corridors where entire families hunkered behind closed doors. Breath and footsteps echoed between stone storefronts. The city held its fear quiet, the way prey did when predators watched, but Zora wasn’t even keeping track of the wormholes anymore. Even for him, they were disorienting to run through. He couldn’t even begin to imagine how dizzy it’d be for everyone else if their eyes were open—

“Stop,” Enki said.

Every boot struck stone and went still. Everyone’s eyes opened a second later.

They’d only been running for about ten minutes, but now they stood at the base of the Divine Temple deep within the city—a colossal square pyramid whose steps rose like a throne carved for the sky—and two giant gates waited before them, guarded by a small line of soldiers who immediately stiffened at the sight of them.

It sure must be a strange sight, seeing fifty men show up at the gates without so much as a warning, but Zora’s attention was purely on the Divine Temple’s summit.

He tilted his staff towards the top, and it shook even harder.

… You really are up there, aren’t you?
