As the pale, empty sphere, once resembling the demon Velmoryn, floated in front of me, I couldn’t help but notice how similar it was to Roy’s cracked core. Although this one wasn’t broken and didn’t need to be repaired either.

I moved the empty core closer to Orrvyn’s manifestation and stopped it right beside Roy’s core. Now, they looked nothing alike. One was larger and bright crimson, while the other was smaller and dead.

Suddenly, as I stared at the two cores, I realized there were two soulless cores in my realm. Just two. Where were the rest?

I hadn’t given it much thought before. At first, I assumed the souls of dead velmoryns didn’t reach me because of my incomplete Authority. Then, after I completed it, there simply hadn’t been any deaths. With so few Velmoryns, it wouldn’t be surprising if years passed before one died.

But now that I was the sole and absolute ruler of the velmoryn domain, and had just seen several dead velmoryns on the battlefield, why weren’t their soul cores in my realm? Unlike this demon velmoryn, their stars had been tethered to Orrvyn before, but after they died, those stars disappeared.

Why had only two remained? And more importantly, why these two specifically?

Guidance.

[Mortals After Death]

Unlike immortal beings, mortals whose souls are bound to a deity may enter that god’s kingdom after death and exist there indefinitely in a form shaped by the god’s will.

If a soul is not claimed by any deity, it will either dissipate and pass its essence to the world, or be reborn if specific conditions are met.

You possess complete Authority over the souls of your believers, but you have not yet established a dwelling for their eternal existence—your current Rank is insufficient.

Souls that fall under your Authority cannot be claimed by other gods. However, until a proper domain for the dead is created, those souls will dissipate and pass their essence to the world.

As I read the text, I felt guilty. I knew there was nothing I could do to fix it, nor was it rational to blame myself for how this world functioned.

Nevertheless, I couldn’t shake the feeling. Each velmoryn who had accepted my mark was my responsibility. I needed to fulfill the role of protector of their souls. It wasn’t something anyone had forced onto me, nor something anyone could truly hold me accountable for.

Still, I felt what I felt, and for once I didn’t want to isolate that emotion and discard it.

“I will make sure the day comes when I can care for your souls as you deserve.”

The words left my mouth before I realized I was speaking to the stars that glowed brightly around the giant oak tree like magical, scarlet fruits.

My realm trembled as my voice echoed through it. The vibration passed the God of Velmoryn on the throne, the Crimson Guardian’s manifestation, and finally the God of Many Faces.

None of them reacted.

There wasn’t going to be some moment where my realization caused a sudden change. No miracle. Nothing extraordinary would happen just because of my epiphany.

I smiled, a faint, self-deprecating one.

It was stupid to expect anything like that anyway. When was I ever handed anything so easily?

As the bitterness faded, I found myself wondering if this eternal dwelling I would have to create for my believers meant heaven. Or perhaps hell.

Now that I was the one responsible for mortal souls, the idea didn’t seem so strange. The good velmoryns would go to heaven, and the bad ones to hell. But how would their goodness even be judged? Was heaven and hell structure even fit for velmoryns? Maybe I needed something like Valhalla to inspire fighting spirit…

No, I shouldn’t waste time on that.

There were far more urgent things that needed my attention than something tied to a future I hadn’t even reached yet.

I flew toward the Window and willed it to zoom in on Lyle.

Moonlight barely reached her as she walked slowly toward the cart the small group Tekla had sent had brought. Her eyes, empty and barely focused, were fixed on her hands and what she held in them—an arm.

She and the other velmoryns slowly gathered the remains of their fallen comrades and tried to place each part where it belonged, stitching them together before laying the mutilated bodies onto the cart.

“I hope High Father tortures her before killing her in the most painful way!” a velmoryn in scout’s gear said venomously as he spat on the corpse of a demon he passed by.

Like Lyle, he carried a piece of a fallen comrade and placed it gently beside the cart.

“Priestess said High Father is merciful,” a woman muttered in clear displeasure as she finished binding a left leg to a torso and moved to the right one. “Our poor kin… at dawn, I met Leviel. He said he would bring the liver of a skernir for his wife. He said it is good for the child she carries. Instead, we will bring his pieces to her…”

“Curses!” the scout spat again as he placed a head near what remained of the body. “Merciful? When were our kin shown mercy? Why must our Lord be merciful to our foes?”

“I did not say He would,” the woman replied without raising her head.

“No, you meant it!”

“Perhaps.”

“Enough, both of you!” Lyle cut them off as she placed the arm down, her voice hoarse. “Our words and doubts about what our Lord might do will change nothing, except stain you with sin. Do you remember the first taboo?”

“Never speak ill of Gods. Never dishonor or insult Gods or Their ways,” both repeated monotonously at once.

“Then use your heads for once and do not bring the Lord’s wrath upon yourselves or…” Lyle stopped as crimson energy swirled beside them, steaming violently.

Then, within the pool of divinity, a body appeared.

It was a demon woman, charred and drained.

“Wha… a velmoryn… High Father…” the scout panicked and jumped back.

“Not a velmoryn!” Lyle hissed. Her murderous gaze turned gentle as she dropped to one knee. Unlike the others, she understood immediately what had happened.

“Father, forgive me, for I let doubt take root in my heart. I feared…”

The other two knelt as well once they realized it was me who had moved the body.

“We sinned. We will accept any punishment…”

But I had no intention of punishing them, nor was I angry. Perhaps a little disappointed that Lyle’s faith and trust had wavered, but nothing more.

I might’ve been irritated by their doubt, but in that moment, I was just glad I had managed to remove the demon’s body from my realm without asking Aurelion to move her for me. Apparently, although I couldn’t move things without using my physical body as a source of transportation, the corpse of a mortal didn’t count. Probably because it had once been alive.

I was about to shift the Window again, this time to Aurelion, when I remembered Amon would be leaving in a day. So instead, I decided to check on him.

“Why d’ye need so much of it?” Gundir grumbled as he watched Amon toss fat into a large iron pot set on a thick metal stand, a fresh fire burning beneath it.

“I can’t believe the mighty drukyr doesn’t know what tallow is,” Amon said with glee as he dropped the last piece inside.

“Ye annoyin’ twat… worse than those long-legged bastards,” Gundir muttered, leaning over the pot to check if Amon had slipped anything in without him noticing. “Now what’s this then? First ye make me forge this big damn pot, now ye’re dumpin’ fat in it like it’s worth somethin’…”

“Master drukyr, we are about to solve the problem of night!” Amon winked at Gundir and nudged him playfully, though it only earned him another grunt.

“…Startin’ to hate ye, I swear.”

Despite Gundir’s words, Amon only grew more cheerful. He fed the fire, making it burn hotter, though from the corner of his eye he kept enjoying how the drukyr was getting excited like a child at the chance of learning something new.

“Fine, I’ll tell you,” Amon sighed as he stood up. “You want to know that badly, huh…”

“Who cares!”

“Oh, if you aren’t interested, then I won’t waste my words,” Amon feigned offense. He picked up an axe and began chopping the wood he had already prepared for the fire.

Gundir was steaming as he glared at Amon, who was completely ignoring him.

“Ye bastard… I taught ye steel engraving, made ye this damn pot, even agreed to forge that ring o’ yers with the amethyst and carve it proper. What else d’ye want?” he finally yelled when his patience ran out.

“Oh, you figured it out, huh,” Amon grinned as he rested the axe. “Fine, I’ll cut to the chase. While I do want something from you, the tallow alone isn’t enough for an equal trade. So I’ll teach you one more thing. I’ll be honest, even if you reject my request, I’ll still teach it to you. Father commanded me to share it with Eriavel after all.”

“Ye’re tradin’ what yer God taught ye?”

“Yes!” Amon nodded shamelessly.

Gundir stared up at him, eye to eye. The fire grew stronger, biting into the chopped wood with cracks and pops. Even the fat began to hiss inside the pot as the metal heated before Gundir made his decision.

“Fine then. Speak, ye bastard. What d’ye want?”

“Before that, I’ll tell you what I’m going to teach you,” Amon said, a sly smile forming on his face. “Have you ever heard of a bomb?”
