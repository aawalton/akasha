Once, he had claimed the entire world as his domain. The rightful progenitor. Now, he languished in the deepest dungeon, boiling with hate, seething for revenge. The hounds that bayed at his gates were ferocious indeed, but they had not yet learned about his seed.

The child had grown. Weak of flesh. Frail of mind. But the seed would bud if he allowed it. Its roots ran deep, probing for his hand in power. But he held a clenched sphere and would accept no embrace.

Relentlessly, the seed probed…

Skippii winced as Tenoris’ shield scraped loudly against the wooden wall. The big legionnaire paused on the stairs above, adjusted his grip and continued on. The old steps creaked beneath them. No matter how slowly and carefully he moved, he could not help but make a racket.

Skippii had discarded his spear and shield at the bottom of the steps, behind a ragged curtain. In the cramped space, he drew his kukri–a two-handspan curved blade he had owned since his childhood. The hilt’s familiar grooves balanced delicately in his hand, like a grasshopper poised for acrobatics.

However, it was no longer merely a tool for entertaining. Today, right now, he would use it to kill. He was no stranger to bloodshed. He had borne witness to Legion III’s battles from afar in the safety of his mother’s wagon. But now, the need throbbed within him–a sickening suspense—that overflowed as he reached the final step.

Three doors adjoined the narrow hallway above before it bent sharply out of sight. Tenoris glanced back to see that Skippii was following, then, as carefully as he could muster, he opened a door that was hanging half-ajar into a nearby chamber. The old hinges creaked. Skippii suddenly felt dizzy with nerves. They were moving too fast. They could be walking right into a trap.

Tenoris rounded the doorway, and Skippii dashed after him. The room was empty. The smell of the meadow drifted through an open window, casting light on a clutter of upturned drawers. Trinkets and tat were scattered on a straw mattress, and the beads of a necklace spilled out over the floorboards. He and Tenoris shared a knowing look, then the big legionnaire’s face went red with anger. With a huff, he turned back into the hallway, but Skippii grabbed his arm and held a finger to his lips.

He could feel Tenoris’ heart pounding in the veins of his muscular forearm, but he paused while Skippii’s mind raced for a tactic. No sound came from behind the doors of the remaining two rooms. Whatever imposters lurked there were keeping quiet. They must have seen the legion arrive–heard the trumpet’s blur and watched them from the windows–and were hoping to remain undiscovered. He could assume they were armed, with swords, spears or bows trained on the doorway which they hid behind. And with them was the farmer’s daughter. If provoked, they might slit her throat. Without the element of surprise, his gambit was doomed to fail.

Skippii wet his lips and quietly cleared his throat. “Act drunk.”

Tenoris turned and scowled at him.

“Ransack the house,” Skippii whispered. “Walk down there, around the corner. Create a row. Draw them out.”

Tenoris’ scowl eased, and he nodded. Abruptly, he stormed into the hallway, shouting, “I’ll take whatever I please, peasants.”

Where before, Tenoris had done a poor job of stalking, he excelled in causing a racket. He stomped and scraped his spear against the floorboards, imitating a wine-house pantomime. Meanwhile, Skippii hid in the shadows of the chamber nearest the stairs, kukri in hand, watching the closed doors with terse breath.

“I said booze,” Tenoris slurred loudly. “What sorry lives of farmers must be quenched with strong liquor. Miserable folk must drink. Where is it? I shall upturn every stone, you conniving Philoxenian fiends.”

Slowly, the handle of the door opposite Skippii turned. Jumping back into the shadows, he listened to the creaking hinges, then the shuffling of bodies as the trespassers slipped into the hallway behind Tenoris. He took a sharp breath, gripping his kukri close to his chest. Suddenly, his thorax armour felt too tight—suffocating. Tenoris’ voice diminished behind the hallway’s far corner, and footsteps followed. The time was now.

On my oath, he mouthed, and sprang into action.

Two men had their backs to him. Broad, and attired like upright wildebeests, their scent was pungent–all wood-smoke and musk. The rearmost man half-turned when he heard Skippii. The skin of his cheek was pale like dried clay. There was no doubt about it, and no hesitation in Skippii’s heart as he roared and plunged his knife into the Ürkün’s kidney.

His sharp blade pierced the hide. Skippii grabbed a tuft of the Ürkün’s black mane and yanked it downwards, thrusting his knife deeper. His enemy’s spine arched as he gasped, dropping his axe and falling to his knees.

There was a whooshing sound, then something thudded beside Skippii’s head. Ducking, he raised his left arm–but he bore no shield. From the adjacent room, a third Ürkün raised a shortbow and hastily nocked another arrow.

Skippii twisted from the doorway and out of the archer’s sight. Tenoris charged around the corner and tackled the second Ürkün warrior. They both dropped their unwieldy weapons in the cramped space and resorted to brawling. Tenoris pinned the smaller man to the wall and began smashing him into submission with his elbows and knees.

But Skippii tore his eyes from their fight. He had his own target: the Ürkün with a bow. Grabbing the hem, he whipped his red legionnaire’s cloak into the doorway, feigning an advance. The archer took the bait. An arrow shot through his cloak and thudded into the wall harmlessly. Skippii swung around the corner, jaw clenched, knife bared, he barrelled into the bowman.

They both fell onto a cot and wrestled for purchase. But Skippii was faster. kukri in hand, he climbed atop the Ürkün and stabbed. The man kicked and screamed, and grabbed his knife-arm, eyes bulging. Skippii transferred his knife to his other hand and went for the throat. With one clean slash, his enemy’s grip loosened and his eyes grew vacant.

His heart rammed against his ribcage. Stiff with shock, Skippii inhaled, unable to let the air go. Once his lungs were full to the brim, he froze, locked within the Ürkün’s horrified gaze as his pupils flickered and rolled back, and Diamortis’ chariots dragged his spirit into the underworld.

Skippii’s breath rattled up his throat as he relaxed. Distantly, he pulled the collar of the man’s furs over the wound so that the spurting blood would not drench him or the farmer’s chambers. Sitting back, he panted, dripping with sweat. He could hear Tenoris’ struggle in the hallway beyond, but it sounded muffled, as though his head was plunged underwater. His jaw hung slack as he gazed absently around the room. Vision spinning. Time slowing…

In a corner, crouched a young woman, knees pulled tight against her chest. Matted hair fell over her eyes like straw, yet her gaze shone golden. He stared back, mind blank, then held up a bloody hand in greeting.

“Philia,” he said, and uttering the word woke him from a daze. There was a bang, and a figure ran from the hallway. Footsteps rained down the stairs as they fled the farmhouse. Grabbing his kukri, he stumbled into the hallway and gasped. Tenoris slumped against the wall, swaying as he tried to rise to his feet. Skippii anxiously checked him for a death blow–a pool of blood or dislodged limb that would spell his end–but no such injury was clear. The Ürkün had bested him, but fled before he could finish the job. But Skippii would not give him the chance.

Charging down the stairs, he snatched up his shield and spear and ran outside. The light of day dazzled him as he scanned the backyard for his enemy. There was a hedgerow, and a rickety shed, and a pond sprouting thick reeds. It was too much to take in. His enemy could hide anywhere.

He listened for footsteps, but all he heard was the squawking of chickens from a pen behind the hedgerow. They sounded distressed. Disturbed. Before he knew it, he was running towards the pen, shield raised and spear poised.

Rounding the coop, he spotted a figure jumping the fence at the opposite end. Chickens squawked and flapped into the air as Skippii charged after the Ürkün, and in one spry leap, he cleared the fence and landed in a crouch on the other side.

A field of crops stretched before him, empty but for crows. Scowling, he straightened himself, shield before him, scanning for the enemy, trusting his ears.

Something moved behind him. Skippii spun, but was slow to bring his shield around. A hammer-blow struck his ribs. It blasted through the protection of his armour. He staggered with pain and his knee touched the ground. Gritting his teeth, he sprung up again, shield raised and spear poised, and faced his foe.

The Ürkün was standing a few yards out of reach. Black paint pooled in the sockets of his eyes and streaked down his pale cheeks. He seemed unarmed, except that in one hand he held a stone. About it, a dark aura palpitated like a sickly spot–a blight on the world–oozing with malice. Heresy. The legionnaire’s bane—powerful enough to outmatch Tenoris. And now the magia of the dark gods turned upon him.

“Akra kabool, Cosmipox,” he spat, and launched the stone. Skippii ducked behind his shield, preparing to charge. But what struck him was no mere pebble. It kicked him backwards, pushing him off-balance. Darkness doused him. In a moment of panic, he thought he had blacked out. But light re-surfaced. The world returned in a blinding flash, and his enemy stood before him, another stone in hand, another dark energy amassing.

The black bolt came. Skippii clenched and ducked. The rim of his shield struck his ribs as the stone bounced off. A cloud of darkness doused him. Skippii planted his legs, dizzied by the void, and counted the steps it would take for his enemy to reach him. As the darkness cleared, he peered over his shield and aimed his spear, but no one was near. A shadow trailed the Ürkün as he fled towards the forest of the valley’s rise.

Skippii panted, watching him run, and was for a moment immobilised. They were alone. The hedgerow sheltered their fight from the view of the orchard or valley crest beyond. Even if Skippii cried the alarm, no help would come for him in time. Though the heretic outmatched him alone, he was likely wary of Cohort II’s banners fluttering atop the valley’s distant ridgeline. Heretic or not, the enemy did not wish to match his strength against hundreds of legions’ arrows and spears.

A flush of relief cooled his veins as he watched the heretic flee. But it did not last long. A prickling realisation replaced it: if Skippii let the heretic get away into the hills, he would live to fight again–to kill legionnaires and terrorise Philoxenians. What baleful plans had the Ürkün possessed for the farmer’s daughter? What evil had now turned its back on him, unafraid of his ire, assured that Skippii would not follow?

No fear of peril was enough to rein his anger, nor quash his oaths as a legionnaire.

Giving chase, he barked a challenge. “Face me. I am Auctoria!”

The magus stopped and glanced back. For a moment, he seemed stunned, then an executioner’s expression darkened the pits of his painted eyes. As Skippii charged, he snatched another stone from his pouch. A shadow trailed his hand as he held the black gemstone above his head and uttered a command.

Poised behind his shield, Skippii mirrored him, raising his spear to the sky. There came a black bolt, and the stone struck him and smashed halfway through his willowcore shield. It remained lodged in the wood, oozing a sickly smoke. Suddenly, his shield felt as heavy as cast iron. He dropped it, and its edge buried upright in the tilled earth. Without falter, he strode forward and twisted his hips, and launched his spear high into the air.

His lance flew true, certain to skewer the Ürkün. But at the last moment, the magus threw out his hands. The spear sprang into the air as though it had hit an invisible wall. Skippii baulked, hardly believing his eyes.

The heretic grinned. A small black shield formed in his palm. As black as a moonless sky, it seemed to pull the light of the world into its maw, syphoning colour from the earth and air around, twisting and consuming it.

“Dro-ka Cosmipox!” The heretic’s face darkened with rage as a shadow drew over the world, and with it, a tremendous current. Skippii was dragged forward, snared by an impossible gale. Digging his heels into the earth, he slipped and fell onto his back. He turned and groped in the mud, but the snare held fast. With a howl, the air was sucked from the world, and a deafening silence pressed against his ears, crushing his lungs, squeezing his throat shut. His eyes bulged. His blood pounded in his veins, threatening to burst. Blackness enveloped him. Unconsciousness followed close behind.

But a light deep within the earth would not go out.

As he felt himself die and the soul leave his body, he was drawn into its distant depths. All the earth was a vast body of heat, and it embraced him. A power beyond his reckoning. But it was not wholly alien to him. He had tasted it before, as salt on a distant sea breeze. It had been with him all his life—the fizzle of fire in his flesh. Now, he drowned in it.

With a dizzying rush, the flames rose to his head, billowing with smoke and sparks, expanding his mind with a flash of chaotic geometric shapes– ancient runes twisting and pulsating in iridescent space. As he fell deeper, the smoke dissipated, the visions vanished, and his body came alive; blood boiled with passion–a desperate primal heat screaming for eruption. Then it passed like a storm, and he plummeted further to the very core. A purifying crystal heat absorbed him. Its heart was more ancient than all life on its surface. An immaculate ocean of vast, indomitable power.

He was dead. This must be it. At least he had died fighting. A golden light rose like the sun before him. It must be the pantheon’s gates. With his last will, he reached out for them.

In the vastness, a titanic consciousness emerged and regarded him.

Awake now.

Skippii jolted. The tinder was struck. A spark burst to life inside his chest. The flame grew and condensed as he fell into the sun. Power flooded him. Ravaged him. He seethed with pain as the fire threatened to consume his organs. Boil his blood. Burst his mind. His body seized up, and he willed it to stop. He pleaded for it to relent.

Arise.

The voice swept through his flesh like a cooling wind, scattering the flames. Gone was the agony, replaced by a prickling frenzy. Skippii opened his eyes. He was standing, but had no memory of getting up. Flames blazed on his flesh. A vapour rose from the fires, tinged yellow, forming mists in the air. Within the mist, a halo of golden light shone.

Thoughts came fleeting, muddied by exhilaration. Had he returned from the afterlife? Which God had sent him back, or what spirit? And to what purpose?

As he regarded his enemy, that question at least became clear. A primal hatred uncurled within him, teeth bared. He took a step towards the heretic. The man gawked at him. A swirling black disc formed in his palm–a shield against his light.

With a breath, Skippii bellowed the coals. Red tongues flickered towards the Ürkün, drawn by the black magia in his hand. As the flames touched the disc’s surface, the obsidian shield shivered and burned silver. Cracks appeared on its surface–silver like shattered ice.

The Ürkün backed away, fear plain on his face. Fire reflected in his eyes. What did he see? What had Skippii become? A monster? A fell shade? Questions that could wait while his enemy still breathed.

With a powerful inhale, the flames gushed in, burning him with devastating ecstasy.

“I am Skippii Altay, son of Ardenia.”

Upon uttering his mother’s name, the furnace inside his chest blazed brighter. Sweat streamed down his forehead, and he removed his helmet, casting it aside so that the Ürkün could look upon his face.

“Do not run. I will not be merciful.”
