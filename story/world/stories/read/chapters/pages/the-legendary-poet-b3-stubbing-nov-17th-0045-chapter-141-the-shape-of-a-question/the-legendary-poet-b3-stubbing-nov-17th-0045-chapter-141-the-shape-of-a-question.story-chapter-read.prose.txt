Renshi woke before his alarm, which was normal, and lay there for three full minutes staring at the ceiling, which was not.

The ceiling was white. His apartment was mostly white, and mostly empty, and both of those things were decisions he had made on purpose. A low bed, a small kitchen, a stand by the door for the shinai-bukuro. A single shelf with five books on it, four of them about kendo and one a novel Lily had given him two years ago that he kept meaning to read. No decorations, aside from a narrow scroll with a single calligraphy character on it that his first teacher had brushed for him when he turned twelve. Mizu. Water. His teacher had laughed when he'd given it to him, and said, you'll understand it later, and Renshi had spent the eight years since quietly suspecting that later had not yet arrived.

He got up. He folded the blanket. He made tea and drank it standing at the window, and that was where the thing that had been sitting on his chest since last night finally got its first full attention of the day.

Klaid is alive.

Not that anyone had thought he was dead. That was the strange part. Nobody in the kendo world had ever said the word dead. They said vanished. They said went quiet. They said after what happened with his family, you know, and then lowered their voices. Four years of that. Four years of a name that came up at tournaments the way certain techniques came up, with reverence and a little fear, spoken by coaches who had been there, at that Nationals final, who had watched a seventeen-year-old step into a state that veterans twice—thrice—his age chased their entire lives and never touched.

Renshi had been in the bleachers. He remembered the sound the hall made.

And yesterday… or, today, considering the time, on a white page inside a written arena, in a video game, he had watched a gray-faced man with a sword do it again. Feet in the old pattern. Weight in the old shape. The exact footwork people had spent years trying to reverse-engineer from competition footage. Master Jin's school. The one that didn't exist anymore because the only student who ever truly inherited it had put down the sword and walked out of the world.

And I said his name in front of everyone.

He set the tea down. That part kept coming back to him at random angles, like a splinter you couldn't find with your thumb. Lily, Zara, Jax, Vami, all of them turning. His own voice, too loud, too certain, delivered to a party of people who had spent a whole night raiding beside a man who had clearly, deliberately, painstakingly arranged to be nobody. Kage. The gray man. A man who wore the invisibility the way Renshi wore ma-ai, constantly, automatically, at exactly the right distance from everyone.

You didn't build that by accident. You built it because you wanted it. And Renshi, in one breath, had taken a hammer to it.

He didn't say anything, Renshi thought. Somehow that was the worst part.

He dressed, did his morning stretches out of habit more than need, and slid the shinai into the bag. The bag was scuffed at one end, old canvas, the single least futuristic object he owned. He slung it over his shoulder, checked that the door locked behind him, and stepped out into the morning.

The walk to the dojo took twenty minutes. Renshi had lived in this district for three years and had never once taken a car to practice. The walk was part of the practice, in a way he would have had trouble explaining to a journalist. Twenty minutes of moving through the world at human speed before spending hours moving through it at inhuman speed. A margin. A breath held between the two.

It was a good morning for it. Cool, dry, the kind of blue sky that looked washed. The district spread out around him in low green hills, houses set far apart with actual distance between them, trees that had been left where they'd grown instead of being replaced with optimized shrubbery. There was a pond at the bottom of the first hill with two herons standing in it, motionless, doing their entire job description. A road ran alongside the path, and every few minutes a car slid past on it, silent, driverless, usually empty or carrying one person staring into a screen held six inches from their face.

Renshi watched one go by. The passenger was a man about his own age, glasses, blank expression, light from the screen pooling on his chin. He did not look up at the herons. He did not look up at anything.

It was the ordinary sight of the century, and Renshi only noticed it at all because he'd spent the last week somewhere that sight didn't exist.

The world had gotten very good at the small things, people said, and it was true. The AI acceleration had arrived so fast that nobody had gotten the chance to decide what it was for. So it had been aimed at everything convenient. Cars drove themselves. Deliveries flew. Half the service industry was machines now, and the other half was people training the machines. You could go a full week in a city without another human being touching your food, your package, your door. The screens had gotten better and better, and the worlds inside the screens had gotten better and better, and the world outside them had quietly become a place people passed through on the way back to their pods.

It hadn't made anyone's life worse, exactly. That was the strange, soft shape of it. It had just made life optional.

Out here, far from the stacked districts where buildings grew on top of buildings like cells dividing, it was gentler. Green. Quiet. People chose places like this precisely because the quiet was still available. Renshi's coach had once called the district a museum of how things used to feel, and Renshi, who had grown up in it, had never been sure whether that was praise or an obituary.

A bird crossed the sky overhead, alone, high enough that it was just a moving speck with wings. Renshi stopped walking and watched it until it disappeared over the hill line.

In Crown, he thought, the sky has things in it too.

That was the thought that kept surfacing, in different costumes. Last night he had fought a leviathan under a black dome of floating text. He had stood on a reef made of crystalized idea and heard words in the air announce attacks before they happened, watched a man with a poet's class rewrite a dungeon with a sword. He had done things with his body that his body had never done, in a place that had no business existing, and it had felt, from the first minute, less like using technology and more like being allowed somewhere.

He started walking again.

He hadn't been a gamer. That was worth admitting honestly. From nine years old his life had been a straight line, dojo, school, dojo, tournaments, and games had been the thing other kids did with the hours he spent on footwork drills. Lily had changed that. Lily, who he had known longer than almost anyone, who had worn him down over the course of an entire winter with the specific stubbornness she usually reserved for keeping their party from killing each other, until he'd agreed to try Dominion's Fall.

He'd expected to tolerate it. Instead he'd found something that had quietly rearranged his understanding of what a game was. The neural dive didn't animate an avatar for you. It read you. Your synapses, your proprioception, your actual trained movement, translated into the world. When Renshi swung a sword in Dominion's Fall, it was Renshi swinging. Sloppy footwork produced sloppy results. Good structure produced good structure. The carry-over to real training had been small, a few percent at most, the difference between shadowboxing in water and shadowboxing in air. But it had been real, and it had let him enjoy the game without the guilt of knowing those hours were stolen from the person he was trying to become.

Then Crown of Destiny had launched, and a few percent had become a bad joke.

He'd missed launch day because of the Qualifier finals, which he had won, which the commentators had spent the entire broadcast calling the arrival of a new prodigy. He'd smiled for the cameras. He'd taken the medal. He'd gone home, logged in a day and a half late, made Lily's party look for their fifth member, and within an hour of playing he had understood that everything had changed.

Crown didn't approximate his movement. Crown used it. Full control, full range, the entire vocabulary of his body available in a world that felt, at times, more real than the one he was walking through now. No hum of servers. No latency in the soul. Grass that bent. Wind that had temperature. People, everywhere, people, looking at each other, shouting at each other, arguing over prices and quest routes and whose fault the wipe was, gloriously, stupidly, magnetically present in a way the screen-glued passengers of the real world had stopped being years ago.

He could practice in there. That was the absurd, beautiful secret of it. He could drill suburi against a training dummy for four hours and his real shoulders wouldn't tear, his real knees wouldn't swell, and his brain, the part that actually mattered, the part that owned the timing, would keep every repetition. Crown was, among other things, the greatest training facility ever built, disguised as a fantasy novel.

And somewhere inside it, playing, was the man his entire sport had been waiting four years for. The thought arrived on schedule, right at the top of the second hill, as if the walk had a marked place for it.

Why is he there?

Renshi had turned it over all night, in the dark, and he still had nothing that held weight. Klaid had quit kendo at seventeen, at the absolute summit, after the accident that had taken his father and, from what little Renshi had been able to piece together over the years from hushed conversations, left his mother badly hurt. That part, people understood, or thought they did. Grief. Family. A son stepping back to hold what was left of his household together. It was tragic and it made sense and the kendo world had filed it under tragedy and kept his name warm in case he ever came back.

But if you could hold a sword again, if your body and your mind still did that... and yesterday proved, beyond any argument, that they did...

Why a game? Why that game, why now?

And under that, the question that had actually kept him awake, the one with edges:

Why has no one ever heard of him?

Because here was the thing Renshi knew that the public didn't, couldn't. If Klaid had wanted to be a professional gamer, he wouldn't be an anonymous gray man. He would be a name. The same way he would have been a name in kendo. That level of perception, that timing, that mind, it didn't have a ceiling it couldn't reach, and the pro gaming scene was a pyramid with a very visible top. Renshi had checked, last night, lying in the dark with his phone glowing above his face. He'd searched the old Dominion's Fall ladders, the tournament archives, the streaming charts, everything, for anyone matching the profile. Nothing. No trace. Either Klaid had spent years playing under names nobody connected to him, at a level nobody noticed, which was absurd...

Or he hadn't been playing to be seen at all.

He'd been playing for something else. Money, maybe, the thought surfaced, remembering the raid, remembering the flat transactional way Kage had spoken, the way the gray man's eyes had moved over loot tables like a man reading invoices. His mother was hurt. Care cost money. The math assembled itself coldly in Renshi's head and he didn't like how easily it fit.

Four years, he thought. That's what four years of that does to a person. You become the gray man.

And yet. And yet, in the white, when the duel had started and the world had narrowed to two combatants in a box of white paper, the gray man had burned off like fog. What was underneath had been so bright that Renshi, standing in a different fight entirely, had stopped mid-swing to stare.

Mushin, his own voice said again in memory, helpless with recognition.

The kendo world was still waiting for that. Coaches still showed that final to students. Commentators still used Klaid's name as the unit of measurement for potential, the way physicists used constants. He could be the next Klaid. Renshi had heard it said about himself, more than once, and every time it had landed like a small, precise insult, because he was older now than Klaid had been in that footage, and he had never once touched what that seventeen-year-old had touched. They were all chasing a ghost's shadow, drilling toward a summit one man had stood on and then casually walked away from, and none of them knew the ghost was alive, and training, training, inside a game, in the dark, alone, for reasons Renshi couldn't see the bottom of.

And I know.

That was the weight under all the other weights. He knew. As of yesterday, he was, as far as he could tell, the only person in the world who knew both halves of the sentence: that Klaid was alive, and where Klaid was.

What was he supposed to do with that?

Tell his coach? The thought lasted half a second. Excuse me, sensei, I found the vanished prodigy, he's a legendary poet in Crown of Destiny, I watched him enter mushin in a dungeon owned by a godlike AI. Beyond the fact that it sounded insane, beyond the fact that he had no right, there was the part that sat heaviest in his stomach: Klaid had hidden. Whatever the reason, however much the kendo world missed him, the man had built a locked door and lived behind it for years, and Renshi had already cracked it once, in front of eight people, in a single careless breath.

He wasn't going to swing the door wider. That much, at least, he could control.

I should apologize, he realized, and then immediately: for what, exactly? For recognizing the most important footwork in modern kendo while standing next to people?

Yes, answered the part of him that kept perfect ma-ai even in safe zones. For exactly that.

The dojo appeared at the end of the road, low and wide and new, its clean lines half sunk into the hill behind it.

Anyone expecting tradition would have been disappointed, and many visitors were. The exterior was glass and pale composite. Inside, the floors were smart-mats that measured force distribution across every step and fed the data to a wall display. Training partners waited along one wall in charging cradles, humanoid forms with articulated arms and blunted shinai-shaped strikers, programmable to replicate any recorded fighter's patterns at any speed. Resistance rigs hung from the ceiling. A recovery suite hummed behind frosted doors. The air smelled faintly of the mats' polymer and, underneath it, stubbornly, of sweat, because some things no engineering firm had managed to optimize away.

Renshi loved it, and felt vaguely guilty about loving it, and had made peace with the guilt through a simple arrangement: he would use every tool the century offered, and he would carry the old shape inside himself where the tools couldn't reach. Perfect kamae on a smart-mat was still perfect kamae. The mat didn't do it for you.

"Renshi." The facilities manager raised a hand from the front desk. "Early again. You've already qualified, you know?"

"I know, Brendon. Hello." He bowed slightly, out of reflex. "Is the east floor open?"

"It is now." A grin. "Hiroshi's in the west with the striker rigs. He's been telling everyone your VR game is going to soften your feet."

"Hiroshi can test that theory whenever he likes."

"That's exactly what I told him you'd say."

There were five or six others already inside, all of them high-level, all of them here for the same reason - the next World Qualifiers, all of them people he had fought and would fight again. They greeted him the way competitors greet each other, warmly and watchfully, and he moved through it on autopilot, bowing, exchanging the small words, storing his bag, beginning his warm-up.

And underneath all of it, steady as a heartbeat, the thing he'd carried through the door.

None of them know.

He looked around the floor, at Hiroshi arguing with the striker rig's calibration, at Mina doing footwork drills with her eyes closed, at the wall display painting everyone's force patterns in soft blue light. Five weeks from now, these people would stand in an arena, and commentators would say prodigy, and somewhere in the building someone would show that old footage to a rookie, and say the name.

They don't know he's out there. They don't know he's better than ever. They don't know he was three meters away from me last night, cutting open a boss with a sword that has no weight, inside a… poem.

Renshi finished his warm-up and picked up his shinai. The weight of it settled into his hands the way it had ten thousand times before, familiar as his own pulse. He stepped onto the east floor. The smart-mat woke beneath his feet, drew a soft ring of light around him, and waited, patient and precise, for data.

He raised the shinai into chudan.

For a moment he just stood there, breathing, looking down the line of the bamboo at the empty far wall. In his mind, uninvited and perfectly clear, a gray-faced man shifted his weight off a back foot that had no business holding that much balance, and became water.

That's the shape, Renshi thought. That's the shape I've been chasing.

And now I know it still exists in the world. Somewhere above a dive pod, in an apartment I've never seen, the man who owns it is probably, right now, logging back in.

His first cut came down clean, and the mat recorded it, and the second followed, and the third, each one a small exact question asked of the morning. He settled into the rhythm, feet and breath and bamboo, and let the repetition carry him forward the way it always had, toward a summit he had finally, after four years, actually seen with his own eyes.
