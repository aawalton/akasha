Sometimes the best plans came from divine toddlers with cosmic identity issues.

Cade adjusted his mask—the gleaming porcelain curves and intricate golden filigree fitting his face like it had been crafted specifically for him—which, he supposed, it had. The crystal filters hummed softly against his skin, and the marbled textures of the metal were warm despite the predawn chill. Around him, his crew made final preparations, each adorned with their own divine handicraft.

“How do I look?” Jer asked, striking a dramatic pose with his pearly mask secured. “Dashing? Mysterious? Ready to seduce monsters in a plague marsh?”

Elena smacked her twin on the back of the head. “You look like an idiot with fancy headgear.”

“An attractive idiot,” Jer corrected, adjusting his mask with exaggerated dignity. “There’s a difference.”

Cade allowed himself a small smile. Their banter felt normal, grounding—a welcome counterpoint to the swirling chaos of the past twenty-four hours. Death’s voice still echoed in his mind, perfect and terrible: When you need me, simply ask.

Not fucking likely.

His unease continued to build as he reflected over his encounter with the well-dressed deity. He had proposed they rob the Arbiter fortress, and yet moments later, Chubbs had solved the main reason they would need to undergo such a dangerous operation. Had Death been testing him? Did he not know?

He was missing something. And he hated it.

“Everyone clear on the plan?” he asked, surveying his team. His family.

Gav nodded, his mask glinting in the fading moonlight. The lycanthrope stood tall now, burns still visible but no longer life-threatening. Whatever Chubbs had done to that mask, it had pulled Gav back from the edge of death itself.

“Sneak past murderous Arbiters, enter toxic marshes, chase maniacal mask-thieves, recover a mystical crown before said thieves useuses it to create a bloodthirsty crime syndicate bent on our destruction. Oh, and don’t die horribly in the process.” Gav ticked each point off on his fingers. “Am I missing anything, Stormhollow?”

“You forgot ‘keep adorable toddler away from her magma-sister,’” added Rayka, adjusting the straps on Chubbs’ miniature pack.

Cade heard the implication in her tone. She’d already switched sides to favor Chubbs, and not the super murdery goddess who actually razed Elysia to the ground, current rumors notwithstanding. The thief wanted to agree. He wanted to raise his middle fingers to the heavens and spirit Chubbs away from all of this divine trouble. But his recent history with the gods had already proven he was really, really, bad at hiding from them right now.

If they were really serious about protecting Chubbs, Cade needed to change the game they were currently stuck in.

He caught Nora’s eye over Chubbs’ head. The ex-paladin’s gaze was sharp, questioning. She’d been watching him with increased intensity since his return from the forest, and that simmering curiosity had only been heightened after Chubbs’ miraculous mask creation.

Cade didn’t know how, but she knew something had happened out there in the forest—something more than a simple walk to clear his head.

They needed to talk. But not now, not with everyone listening.

“All right,” Cade announced, straightening his leather jerkin. “Arbiter checkpoint is half a mile ahead. Remember—we’re pilgrims on our way to the Weeping Shrine. Stick to the script, keep your heads down, and for gods’ sake, don’t let Chubbs speak to anyone.”

“I can be quiet!” Chubbs protested loudly.

“Exhibit A,” Cade sighed, a tired smile creasing his lips. He turned to Orro. “You’re on point with me. Nora, you’ve got our six. Everyone else, middle formation.” His eyes found Evie. “Anything we should know about what’s coming?”

The siren shrugged delicately, her silver-blue hair catching the last glimpses of starlight. “The stars are unusually quiet tonight,” she said, her musical voice tinged with unease. “Which is rarely a good sign.”

“Fantastic,” Cade muttered. “The silent treatment from the cosmic fortune tellers. Just what we needed.”

Bunny nudged his leg, intelligent eyes catching the first rays of dawn. The dragonling’s scales shimmered with an iridescence that hadn’t been there before Chubbs’ light show. Another mystery for another time.

“Let’s move,” Cade ordered, touching the dagger at his hip for reassurance. “Stay sharp, stay together. Bernard’s head start ends today.”

***

The Arbiter checkpoint wasn’t just a checkpoint—it was a fortress.

Cade assessed the structure with a thief’s practiced eye as they approached. Twenty-foot walls of polished stone. Watchtowers rose from each corner, manned by archers and figures in the most obvious mage robes he’d ever seen. Still, the messaging was clear: If our arrows miss, our missiles of ice and fire won’t.

Subconsciously, the Son of Ruin started to ready his Destruction core for action. The massive iron gate stood partially open, allowing careful inspection of all who wished to pass. A line of travelers—merchants, pilgrims, and locals—stretched back from the entrance.

And that was just what was visible.

“There’s a ward line,” Nora murmured, falling into step beside him. “Can you feel it?”

He could. Like the buzz of a thousand angry wasps just below the threshold of hearing, the magical barrier hummed at the edge of his awareness. His newly awakened core responded to it, a faint pulse of heat in his chest.

“Detection ward?” he asked quietly.

“Worse, I think,” she replied, voice grim. “It feels like the identification ward back at our old temple. If it works the same, then it will readreads your core signature and comparecompares it to their records.”

Cade’s stomach dropped. “So they’ll know exactly who we are the moment we cross it.”

“Not necessarily,” Nora said, eyes fixed on the gate ahead. “The ward identifies your core type, not you personally. Unless...”

“Unless they’re specifically looking for someone with Destruction magic,” Cade finished. “Someone like me.”

“Precisely.”

“Fuck me sideways with a rusty sword,” Cade muttered. “Any chance the masks might help with that?”

Nora’s expression was unreadable behind her mask. “Given who made them? It’s possible. But I wouldn’t bet our lives on it.”

“Too late for that,” Cade replied with a grim smile. “We’re already all-in.”

They joined the queue of travelers, doing their best to look unremarkable. While they did, the young thief used hand signals to his crew, wary even to use their focal stones for communication in such a heavily monitored zone.

Cade pulled his hood lower, mentally rehearsing their cover story. Pilgrims to the Weeping Shrine, seeking blessings for a sick relative back home. Simple, forgettable.

Chubbs bounced on her toes between Rayka and Evie, her blonde curls bobbing beneath her hood. “Are we there yet?” she stage-whispered, loud enough to draw glances from nearby travelers.

“Soon, honey,” Rayka soothed, squeezing the child’s hand. “Remember what we talked about? The quiet game?”

“I’m winning!” Chubbs announced proudly.

Cade exchanged pained looks with Orro, who merely shrugged. The assassin’s hand never strayed far from the concealed blade at his side.

As they inched closer to the gate, the tension in Cade’s shoulders built to a near-painful level. The guards were performing thorough inspections, checking papers, questioning travelers. One woman was turned away, her protests falling on deaf ears as she was escorted firmly back the way she’d come.

“Next!” barked an Arbiter guard with a captain’s insignia on his armor.

Cade stepped forward, forcing his face into a mask of humble deference. “Morning, sir. We’re pilgrims bound for the Weeping Shrine.” He gestured to his companions. “My family and I seek blessings for our ailing grandmother.”

The guard’s eyes narrowed. “Papers.”

Cade produced the forged documents he had prepared the night before. The guard scrutinized them, frowning slightly. The young thief was confident in this part of their disguises, at least. Those documents were masterpieces. Just the right amount of aging on the parchment, with the perfect switchback of ink types to emulate a bored government scribe from the north.

“Masks,” the guard barked, pointing to their faces. “Where did you get them?”

“Family heirlooms,” Cade replied smoothly. “My father was a marsh guide before the quarantine. Passed them down when he... passed on.”

The guard grunted noncommittally, continuing his long scan of their papers. “Lot of masks for one family. Starling, was it?”

“Big family,” Cade shrugged. “My father was... prolific.”

Behind him, Elena snorted, quickly disguising it as a cough.

The guard studied each of them in turn, lingering on Chubbs’ small form. “The child too? She’s yours?”

“My daughter,” Rayka stepped forward, placing protective hands on Chubbs’ shoulders. “She was born with weak lungs. The mask helps her breathe.”

Chubbs, bless her chaotic little heart, chose that moment to give an unconvincing cough.

The guard’s suspicion was palpable. “Remove your masks. All of you.”

Cade’s pulse spiked. “Sir, with respect, the air here is—”

“That’s an order, pilgrim,” the guard snapped. “Remove them now, or turn back.”

The Son of Ruin felt rather than saw his crew shift subtly into fighting stances. Orro’s presence at his right seemed to grow darker, more dangerous.

Just as Cade’s fingers reluctantly moved toward his mask, a cry rose from the guardhouse.

“It’s them! The ones from the village fire!”

Cade’s heart plummeted as a younger Arbiter pointed directly at them, recognition blazing in his eyes. “The Destroyer! The fugitives! That’s the same hilt of the blade that cut that demonic fire! It’s them!!!”

The thief realized it was the same Arbiter whose best friend had burned. The shorter of the two that had blocked his passage when they’d spotted Nash in the tavern.

And now, the same man had blocked their way forward.

Time seemed to crystallize into razor-sharp fragments. Cade could see everything with painful clarity: the guard captain’s hand dropping to his sword; archers and mages on the watchtowers swiveling toward them; Rayka’s fingers tightening protectively around Chubbs; Orro’s body coiling like a viper about to strike. Frustration coiled around Cade’s gut.

Why did his luck keep running out on him? He really thought with how horrible the past few days had gone, the winds would finally shift in their favor.

Not for the first time that day, Cade was wrong.

In that fractured moment between breaths, Cade made his decision.

“RUN! For the gate!” he roared, shoving Rayka and Chubbs ahead of him.

The courtyard erupted into chaos.

Arbiters converged from every direction, swords drawn, boots thundering against the cobblestones. Orro moved in front, his cloak flowing back to reveal his traveling armory of daggers and knives. In a flash, blades were suddenly in his hands as he cut a path forward. Three guards fell to his exotic twists and flips, each weapon finding a throat or a slit between their plated armor.

Behind them, Nora’s massive sword sang from its sheath across her back, sweeping an arc that forced the nearest guards to leap backward. The obsidian weapon cleaved through the pre-dawn air, shimmering started her deadly sword techniques of water and fear magic.

The Dreadcaller was waking up.

But now they were trapped in a killbox.

“Barricade the exit!” the captain bellowed. “Archers, mages, take position! No one leaves!”

The massive iron gate at the far end of the courtyard—the entrance to the Blightmarshes—began to creak closed. Metal screamed against stone as Arbiters hurried to seal their only escape route.

“Split up and circle back!” Elena shouted, eyes wild with panic.

“No exits,” Cade growled. “Forward is the only way.” His eyes found Gav. “Shield Chubbs! Everyone else, weapons ready!”

Arbiters poured into the courtyard from side passages and barracks, at least twenty strong now. Armored and disciplined, they formed a semicircle of steel before them, cutting off any retreat. Above, archers notchcked arrows, their aim steady. Next to them, mages called upon all sorts of elements, readying orbs and shafts of arcane energy.

Then the worst happened. From the ranks of soldiers, the air thickened further with the unmistakable pressure of multiple core users preparing to unleash their power. Blue light shimmered around several Arbiters’ hands—water and ice cores. Others’ palms glowed red with fire magic.

The mages lining the ramparts were just a distraction. These were the true threats.

And here stood Cade’s crew—eight against an army, with the only exit rapidly closing.

“Surrender now!” the captain demanded, his voice ringing across the courtyard. “Lay down your weapons and you’ll receive fair judgment!”

Cade’s eyes met Orro’s, then Nora’s—a silent conversation in a fraction of a second. A plan formed, unspoken but understood by those who had fought together, survived together.

At least, he was pretty sure they were all thinking the same thing. Gods, he really hoped so, otherwise this would be the most embarrassing story to share in the afterlife.

“You want fair?” Cade called back, stepping forward. Silver light began to dance across his fingertips, thin threads of gold weaving through the destructive magic. “Then how about you all face me one at a time?”

“Umm… No,” the captain replied, gesturing at all of his warriors. “We’ve got you.”

“Fine,” Cade replied softly. “Then let’s make this unfair.”

Power surged up from his core, no longer a trickle but a flood. The gold-threaded silver light engulfed his arms, spiraling up to his shoulders. Every instinct screamed at him to unleash it, to let the destruction magic tear through everything in its path.

But control—control was the difference between power and mastery.

“Cade, the gate!” Rayka cried, pointing toward the now half-closed entrance to the Blightmarshes.

With surgical precision, Cade channeled a portion of the raging energy into a concentrated beam that struck the massive gate mechanism. Metal shrieked, twisted, and then shattered as the destructive force unmade its molecular structure. The gate juddered to a halt, leaving a narrow gap—just wide enough for them to pass through single file.

“GO!” he roared to his crew. “Through the gate! NOW!”

The Arbiters surged forward, realizing his intent. Arrows rained down from above, forcing Cade to throw up a hasty shield of fiery energy that disintegrated the projectiles on contact. The mages’ projectiles rained down next, and he was forced to dive out of the way as a barrage of ice and sharpened stones dove for his location.

Jer was first to move, his acrobatic form flipping over the front line of guards, landing in a roll that carried him through the stunned ranks. Elena followed, her whip blade flashing as she cut a path behind her brother. Gav sprinted behind them, one arm around Chubbs, his other hand extended as he shoved Arbiters to the ground by their heads.

More arrows and magic shot toward his friends, and Cade unleashed a torrent of wind and flames to block them. His spell created an overhang between them and the gate, but he could feel his core drain at a prestigious rate.

“Kill the Destroyer! He must not be allowed to escape! Capture the others, but kill if you must!” the captain roared to his troops. “For honor!”

“FOR HONOR!” came the zealous reply.

They weren’t going to make it—not all of them. The Arbiters were closing ranks, and the energy required to maintain the shield was draining Cade rapidly.

“I can’t hold it,” he gritted through clenched teeth, the shield flickering.

“Then don’t hold,” Nora said beside him, her voice roaring over the clash of magic and steel. “Strike.”

She was right. There was no clean way out of this. No solution that didn’t involve blood and broken bodies. His hesitation was born not of fear, but of knowing what came next would only cement his reputation as the Destroyer.

The thought burned like acid: every life he took pushed him further from redemption, cemented the world’s perception of him as a monster.

But his crew—his family—had no other chance.

“Get ready to run,” he told Nora, Orro, Rayka, and Evie. Then, to the shields of energy around his arms, he whispered: “Let’s show them what destruction really means.”

The power responded eagerly, hungrily. The gold threads within the silver magic brightened until they were painful to look at directly. Cade brought his hands together, fingers splayed, and then thrust them outward with a primal roar that came from somewhere beyond conscious thought.

Silver-gold energy exploded outward in a perfect hemisphere, a wave of pure destructive force that swept across the courtyard. It didn’t discriminate, didn’t choose targets—it simply ravaged everything in its path.

Stone cobblestones cracked and disintegrated. Metal weapons shattered like glass. Wooden structures splintered into dust.

The Arbiters directly in the wave’s path were thrown backward with bone-breaking force, their armor crumpling under the impact. Those on the periphery scrambled for cover, faces twisted in terror and awe.

In the sudden, ringing silence that followed, Cade stood at the epicenter of devastation, the silvery threads in his magic pulsing like living things. The courtyard was transformed—a thirty-foot radius around him reduced to rubble and ruin.

“Go!” he repeated to his stunned companions, voice ragged with effort. “I’ll cover you.”

They ran, darting through the newly cleared path toward the half-open gate. Cade followed, maintaining a smaller shield behind them to deflect the arrows that resumed their deadly rain once the shock wore off.

One Arbiter—older, his face lined with experience and conviction—struggled to his feet directly in their path. His sword was gone, armor dented, but determination burned in his eyes.

“Stop!” he called, blood trickling from a gash on his forehead. “You think that way leads to safety, Destroyer? That way leads only to death!” Something changed in his expression—not just determination, but a desperate kind of honor. “You’ll kill everyone you love by entering that hell!”

Cade hesitated for a heartbeat, struck by the man’s sincerity.

“He’s trying to help,” Evie murmured, her siren’s perception cutting through deception. “He believes what he’s saying.”

“We have no choice,” Cade replied grimly, then called to the Arbiter: “Better the devil we don’t know than the one at our backs.”

They pushed past the man, who made no move to stop them. The rest of the Arbiters were regrouping, but slower now, warier after witnessing Cade’s display of power.

The gate loomed before them, the narrow opening beckoning like the maw of some ancient beast. Beyond, mist swirled—the toxic atmosphere of the Blightmarshes. Jer, Elena, Gav, and Chubbs waited just inside, their masked faces peering anxiously back at them.

“Hurry,” Cade urged, feeling his energy flagging. “I can’t maintain this much longer.”

One by one, they squeezed through the gap—Rayka, then Evie, then Orro. Nora paused at the threshold, massive blade still at the ready.

“Go,” Cade told her. “I’m right behind you.”

The ex-paladin gave him a skeptical look but complied, sliding through the opening.

Cade backed toward the gate, maintaining his shield against the renewed Arbiter assault. Arrows and the occasional bolt of elemental magic splashed harmlessly against the barrier, but each impact drained him further. The gold threads in his silver magic were fading, his advancement threatening to slip away as quickly as it had come.

He had one last task.

Reaching the gate, he gathered the remnants of his power into his palm. With a final, defiant gesture, he slammed his fist against the massive iron hinges. Silver-gold energy poured into the metal, eating through it inch by inch until the entire structure groaned, twisted, and then collapsed inward with a thunderous crash.

Tons of iron and stone sealed the entrance behind him, cutting off pursuit. The dust hadn’t even settled before Cade staggered backward, exhaustion crashing over him in a suffocating wave.

“Shit,” he wheezed, the world swimming before his eyes. “That looked... expensive.”

Rayka caught him before he could fall, her arm steady around his waist. “Easy, big brother. I’ve got you.”

The rest of the crew crowded around, faces a mixture of awe, concern, and relief behind their masks. They’d made it—all of them—but the cost was etched in Cade’s trembling limbs and the deathly pallor of his skin.

“That was the most terrifying thing I’ve ever seen,” Jer said, eyes wide. “And also the most amazing. You just—” he made an explosive gesture with his hands, “—unmade everything!”

“Congratulations, Cade,” Gav observed quietly. Everyone, including the thief, stared up at him while he held Chubbs close. “What? He reached Middle stage with his core just now. I’ve never even heard of a silver ranker rising from Early to Middle Sstage in a few months, much less a few weeks.”

“Yes, well,” Cade managed a weak grin, “I’ve always been an overachiever.”

Chubbs tugged at his pant leg, her golden eyes solemn beneath her tiny mask. “You did good, Cadie. The gold looks pretty with the silver.”

Cade ruffled her hair with a shaking hand. “Thanks, squirt.”

Orro surveyed their surroundings, ever vigilant. The mist swirled around them, ethereal and threatening, but for the moment, they were alone. No Blightwalkers, no Arbiters—just the twisted landscape of the marsh stretching before them.

“We should move,” the assassin advised. “They’ll find another way in, eventually.”

“How are we going to get back out?” Elena demanded, gesturing to the collapsed gate. “You just sealed our only exit.”

Cade straightened, drawing on reserves of strength he didn’t know he possessed. “Tomorrow’s problems, today’s survival. One impossible thing at a time.”

As they gathered themselves to move deeper into the marsh, Jer suddenly pointed upward, his face paling behind his mask. “Uh, Cade? Did you do that too?”

Above them, where the sky should have been visible, roiling black clouds had gathered. Not ordinary storm clouds—these pulsed with an internal light that was neither lightning nor flame, but something more primal. The darkness spread like spilled ink across parchment, swallowing the sun. Thunder rumbled, but it wasn’t the crack of electricity—it was deeper, more resonant, like the grinding of cosmic gears.

Cade felt his core respond to the storm, recognizing its nature on an instinctual level. “Not me,” he said quietly. “That’s my core’s step-mom throwing a fit.”

“Your what?” Elena demanded.

“Destruction,” Nora clarified, her voice hushed with concern. “The goddess is stirring.”

The storm intensified, the clouds spiraling into a vortex directly above them. The thunder grew louder, more insistent, almost like laughter.

Whatever game the gods were playing, the board was being set. And Cade and his crew were pieces moving deeper into danger with every step.
