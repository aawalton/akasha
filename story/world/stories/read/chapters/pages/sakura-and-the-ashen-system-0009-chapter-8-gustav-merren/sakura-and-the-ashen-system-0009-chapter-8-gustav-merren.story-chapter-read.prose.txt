The head of Mebrel burst into loud, delighted laughter, as if he had just made a genuinely brilliant joke, and Sakura’s mouth twitched with disgust before she could stop it. She winced and clenched her fingers beneath the folds of her cloak, just barely. The man noticed at once and, if anything, seemed even more amused by it.

“Oh, I see your sense of humor is pretty weak,” he said, wiping his thick damp fingers on the edge of the cloth lying on the table. “A shame. A sense of humor can help you live longer. Sometimes.”

He reached for the glass goblet of water standing on his right, took several greedy gulps, swallowed loudly, and only then spread into an unpleasant, self-satisfied smile.

“Since we’ve started off on such a pleasant note, I should introduce myself,” he said. “Gustav Merren, head of Mebrel, keeper of order, guardian of peace, and in recent years also the man forced to clean up the consequences of idiots having been in power.”

Sakura looked at him straight on, and everything inside her went colder still. Gustav Merren. A deeply unpleasant man, and not someone from whom anything good should be expected.

Merren set the goblet back on the table with a noisy clack, almost missing the coaster, leaned back in his chair, then suddenly pitched forward again so sharply it was as if two different men lived inside him at once: one soft, lazy, and constantly drinking water, the other twitchy, vicious, and always on the verge of shouting.

“So,” he said, his voice much harder now. “Let me repeat this for clarity. You were detained on the basis of testimony indicating that you were present at the ruins of Crowlis’s castle at the moment of recorded anomalous activity, after which you left the scene and attempted to enter the city without notifying the guard, without identifying yourself, and without offering any explanation of your own accord. Under the current circumstances, in case you somehow failed to understand, that looks very, very bad.”

Sakura drew a slow breath.

“I am guilty of nothing,” she said evenly. “This is slander.”

Merren slammed his palm down on the table so abruptly that the goblet jumped, and Sakura flinched in spite of herself. The city head’s face went red with anger in an instant, his cheeks flushed, and his small eyes glittered even more nastily.

“Lies!” he barked so loudly the sound bounced off the walls. “You lie to my face, girl! I do not like being lied to.”

He grabbed for the water again, took several quick gulps, exhaled through his mouth, and stared at her now not mockingly, but with that irritated, sharpened focus that was somehow even worse.

“I have a witness,” he said. “A reliable one. Not a drunk from the market, not some town idiot, not a child. A person saw you at the ruins. Saw you hiding among the stones, and then saw you running away afterward. So let’s begin simply. What. Were. You. Doing. At Crowlis’s castle?”

Sakura felt a slow chill run down her spine. A witness. Someone had seen more than she would have liked. But she could not let that show.

“I already told you,” she answered. “I am guilty of nothing. I’m a refugee. I was walking through the forest. A monster attacked me. I ended up near the ruins against my will.”

Merren twisted his mouth as if she had given him not an answer, but a cheap parody of one.

“A refugee,” he repeated with exaggerated amazement, and then burst into laughter again, shorter this time and meaner. “Of course. How lovely. How convenient. Catch someone near a cursed place and suddenly they become either an innocent orphan, or a refugee, or concussed.”

He dug his fingers into the edge of the table and leaned toward her again.

“Where exactly are you a refugee from?”

“The south,” Sakura said after a short pause. She stood straight, not stepping back, taking the questions head-on.

“The south is not an answer. The south is large. From where?”

She held his gaze.

“From a small town called Sarge,” she improvised on the spot. It was unlikely Gustav knew the name of every town in the south.

“Sarge?” he repeated in a tone that made the word sound like a personal insult.

He struck the table again, though not as hard this time, more to underline his own importance than out of real fury. But the fury was still in him. Sakura could feel it too clearly. This man did not simply love authority. He loved seeing people tremble in front of him. And the longer the conversation went on, the more he likely hoped for exactly that.

“Is there anyone who can confirm your status?” he shot back. “Relatives? Letters? Seals? People who traveled with you? Any living soul who could say, yes, this girl came from there?”

“No,” Sakura said.

“No?” he repeated sweetly, with almost painful pleasure. “How unfortunate. And where are your family?”

Sakura was silent a second longer than she should have been, then answered anyway.

“They died.”

The word hung in the room heavier than the others. Merren narrowed his eyes. For a brief moment she almost thought he might lose that disgusting smile, but no. He simply reached for the water again, drank, wiped his lips, and continued in a quieter voice that somehow sounded even worse.

“All of them?”

“All of them. The South showed no mercy to my family.”

“And you made it here alone?”

“Yes.”

“Without escort?”

“Without one, sir.”

“Through the forest?”

“Yes.”

“So, a lone refugee with no proof, no family, no witnesses, and no proper explanation walks through a forest full of obsidian beasts and somehow ends up at Crowlis’s ruins on the very day a red glow flared up there?” He shook his head and clicked his tongue loudly. “You do hear how that sounds, don’t you?”

Sakura did hear it. And that was exactly why she kept holding her ground, because any new word could only make things worse.

“Lir the fisherman’s son escorted me to Mebrel. He only helped because he saw the state I was in after I escaped the beast. I did nothing at the ruins,” she said. “I did not go there on purpose. I was trying to survive.”

“Everyone is trying to survive,” he roared so suddenly that Sakura nearly flinched again. “And that is not an excuse!”

His mood changed too fast. One moment he exploded, the next he settled again, then reached for water, then laughed like a man who took a kind of pleasure in his own temper. Sakura looked at him and thought that this was not merely a hot-headed official, but a dangerous tyrant who had grown used to everyone around him adjusting themselves to his fits.

“You were hiding among the stones,” he continued, more controlled now. “The witness saw you. Then you ran from the guard. Why? If you are innocent, why did you run?”

“Because the guard is not always noble and eager to help a lone girl,” Sakura answered. “I’m not an idiot.”

“And before that? What were you doing there before that?”

“Hiding.”

“From whom?”

“From the beast, obviously.”

Merren stared at her for a long moment, then rose from his chair so abruptly it gave a pitiful creak beneath him. He was shorter than he had looked while sitting, but broader and rougher. He took several steps across the office, turned, took another swallow of water, and looked at her again.

“You are either the most unfortunate fool I have ever seen,” he said, “or you are pretending very, very well.”

“I am not guilty,” Sakura repeated. “And that is the truth.”

“The truth? The truth?” He suddenly laughed again, darkly this time, then cut himself off so sharply it was as if someone had strangled the sound in his throat. “The truth, girl, is that you came to Mebrel on the day something happened at that cursed castle for the first time in decades, something bad enough to send my men scurrying through the city like rats in a fire. The truth is that you were seen there. The truth is that you are alone.”

He sat back down, but this time not sprawled, rather coiled, like a predator about to spring. For several seconds he just stared at her, and Sakura had already begun to think he was about to circle back to the same questions again when he suddenly asked in a completely different tone, dark and almost cold:

“What is your class? Mage?”

The question hit harder than she expected. Sakura caught the way even the guard by the door tensed at those words. So yes, there was clearly something raw here where mages were concerned. She remembered Gilbert, with his strange bracelets.

She answered at once, giving herself no time to hesitate.

“No.”

Merren looked at her without blinking.

“No?” he asked quietly.

“No,” Sakura repeated. “I’m not a mage.”

He was silent for a long moment.

“I don’t believe you,” he said.

Then Merren suddenly came alive, as if he had finally reached the part he actually wanted to tear open.

“Do you know what the problem with mages is?” he asked, not waiting for an answer. “They always think they’re smarter than everyone else. One hides power, another hides knowledge, and a third tears everything down in his path. The way Crowlis did. And then my city gets chaos, confusion, and death. So no, girl. The word ‘no’ does not satisfy me.”

He leaned back and suddenly smiled almost gently, which made Sakura feel even worse.

“But I am a fair man,” he said. “I like clarity. I dislike speculation when proof is available.”

Sakura already knew she was about to hear something bad.

“You will undergo a test that shows your class,” Merren said. “And then we’ll see whether you are lying to me or not.”

A long, sticky silence followed.

Sakura stood utterly still, but deep beneath her ribs, in the place where that wary foreign force had lived ever since recently, something stirred again. A sharp surge, like a warning. Cold ran over her skin at once. Sakura tensed her shoulders just slightly and crushed that motion down by force of will, not letting it rise any higher, not letting anything unnecessary show on her face. Not now. If anything strange happened in this office, if even the smallest hint of what she had become slipped out of her, Gustav Merren would no longer want explanations, interrogation, or proof. He would simply order her killed.

She looked at him and suddenly understood one more thing with painful clarity. He could have started with the test immediately. From the moment they shoved her into the office, he could have skipped the cheap spectacle with the accusations, the tantrums, the pounding on the desk, and the sudden swings in mood. If he really had a way to check her class, then none of this scene had been necessary except for one thing. He enjoyed it. He enjoyed watching the person across from him hesitate and justify themselves while being cornered step by step.

Sakura felt such sharp disgust toward him that she was almost surprised she managed not to show it in her eyes. Or maybe she did not manage it at all. Gustav Merren was not merely an unpleasant man. There was something especially slick, dangerous, and petty in him. And Sakura already understood that men like him were often the ones who became somebody’s ruin.

But for the moment, she waited.

Merren seemed to enjoy the pause almost physically, and then finally began to speak again. His voice was calm now, but the threat in it had not gone anywhere.

“In Mebrel, girl,” he said, rolling the goblet between his thick fingers, “not everything rests on honest words alone. Words, you know, are unreliable things. Especially someone else’s. But fortunately for me and unfortunately for you, people turned out not to be as stupid as the System apparently expected.”

Sakura said nothing, but now she listened very carefully.

“The System gives us a great deal, of course,” Merren continued in a lazy, almost lecturing tone. “Levels, classes, inventory, special skills. But it does not allow us to read one another directly. In other words, live as you like, sort it out yourselves, trust words or cut each other’s throats at random. Charming. And very sophisticated. But people, fortunately, learned long ago to look for loopholes.”

He lifted one finger like a lecturer who particularly enjoys the subject of his own speech.

“Artifacts,” he said with near pleasure. “Invented long before you and even before me. Some show traces of residual magic, others sense lies, some open sealed doors, and others help bypass certain limitations of the System. And there are those that let us see what the System would especially prefer to keep hidden from other people’s eyes. A class, for example. And a level.”

At those words, Sakura felt that dark, patient thing move inside her again. Not stronger than before, but closer now. As if the very idea that someone was about to reach under her skin with a foreign tool, a foreign artifact, and tear away what even she herself did not yet fully understand had provoked an immediate, displeased reaction from the force. Sakura crushed it down again at once.

“There is,” Merren repeated, watching her so closely it was as if he hoped to see that struggle with his own eyes, “an artifact that would very quickly help us understand who you are.”

Sakura inhaled slowly.

“Then why haven’t you simply used it yet?” she asked.

Merren stared at her, then suddenly spread into a satisfied smile.

“Because I do not like things to be too simple. Simplicity is for bakers, millers, and very lucky widows. And I may have in my hands someone connected to Crowlis’s castle, to an anomalous flare, and to who knows what else. In moments like these, I prefer not only to get the answer, but to see what shape it takes under the right conditions. The test will not be done with the artifact. It will be done another way.”

Sakura said nothing, but swore inwardly. Of course. Of course it was not enough for him simply to know. He wanted a spectacle.

Merren turned sharply to the guard at the door.

“You,” he snapped. “Prepare the arena. And gather people. Let them come. Since we have such an interesting case, I see no reason to keep it to a small audience.”

The guard gave a short nod.

“Yes, sir.”

“And...” Merren paused for a second, then smiled so nastily Sakura disliked the rest of the sentence before it had even come. “Wake Pampli.”

The guard hesitated just slightly, as if he wanted to make sure he had heard correctly, but after catching the city head’s look, he bowed his head again at once.

“It will be done.”

The door shut behind him, and for the first time in the whole conversation Sakura let one brow lift just a little. Pampli. The name was so strange, almost ridiculous, that in any other setting she might have smirked. But now, for some reason, it only made her more uneasy. An arena. She would be taken into an arena. Her heart began hammering in her chest.

Sakura looked at Merren and felt a hollow emptiness tighten unpleasantly in her stomach. All of this was becoming worse and worse. They wanted to drag her into the light, force her to show herself, drive her into conditions where fear and desperation would almost certainly give Merren the answers he wanted most.
