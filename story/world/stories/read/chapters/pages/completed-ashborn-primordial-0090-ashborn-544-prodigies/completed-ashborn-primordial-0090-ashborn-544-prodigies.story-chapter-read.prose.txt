Throughout all its long and undoubtedly storied history, Cirayus guessed that the Vimana’s workshop had never witnessed the sort of heated argument it was seeing now.

The Ravager simply watched in silence as Siya and Saunak went at each other over what appeared to be a projection of the Vimana's levitation system, though the glowing inscriptions might as well have been written in a foreign language for as much Cirayus understood of them.

He’d been fully prepared himself to guard the girl when the Akh Nara had asked him. To defend her from Saunak, both physically and verbally, certain that the old demon would lord over her.

Cirayus assumed his role would involve a great deal of careful conversation with Siya and quite a lot of admonishment and threats toward Saunak. Given that Cirayus had personally seen him reduce accomplished Thaumaturges to tears with his words, he fully expected the same here. Size and age were completely irrelevant to a demon like Saunak, and Cirayus wondered if he even registered children as different from adults.

Truth be told, Cirayus had been looking forward to intimidating the Thaumaturge.

Yet it soon became clear that the girl had been through more in her short life than most demons experienced in a hundred years, and he certainly had not been prepared for the small Imperium goddess to utterly dismantle Saunak’s understanding of his own field.

“This is not how the levitation systems work!” Siya’s voice rang across the workshop, sharp and entirely without deference. “Not at all! Your theories are completely incorrect! The gravitic field is generated entirely by the phase-matrix amplifier, not as a result of resonance with Affinity prana.”

Cirayus would not have believed until that moment that anything could leave terrifying Mad Thaumaturge standing frozen with his mouth hanging open and his eyes wide.

“I… see,” the old demon said at length. “Yes, I think you must be right.”

He clearly did not see. Cirayus might not have a clue about the topic at hand, but he had known Saunak long enough to know when the demon was entirely out of his depth. It happened so rarely that seeing it was, frankly, one of the most entertaining experiences of Cirayus’s life.

The girl was already moving on to her next admonishment, gesturing at a different cluster of inscriptions and rapidly explaining why the assumption underlying the entire Imperium magic field theory Saunak prided himself on was wrong in a half-dozen ways.

No, it was patently obvious that Siya did not require coddling. So much so that she seemed to actively resent it during their initial introduction, when he attempted his usual approach with frightened children.

She’d simply given him a small smile that suggested she understood exactly what he was doing and appreciated the intent, but that it wasn’t necessary. She’d then gone on to ask him several pointed questions about the Vimana’s structural composition that he had no hope of answering.

She was a child in height only. Cirayus had served Rajas less composed than her.

And yet, for all the intellectual debates and the brilliance on display, the two had yet to come even close to a solution.

It was understandable, of course. Their foe was not the Matali band of humans, who could be dispatched with a blade or a Chakra attack.

No, these two were up against one of the greatest workings of the most advanced civilization ever to exist. Designed to be all but impervious, and the longer Cirayus listened to the two minds in this room, the more he began to suspect that even brilliance had limits.

He cleared his throat, making the two pause their debate to look at him.

“Might I suggest,” he said, acutely aware of his standing in any conversation about Imperium magic, “that the two of you make use of the facilities of our own Vimana? Its schematics, perhaps? Or the fact that no Imperium magic the Akh Nara has ever encountered has a single defense against Chakra attacks? I know little of such systems, but it seems to me that there might be some hint lurking there. At the very least, it would serve you better than your current bickering.”

The looks on their faces were worth the entire trip.

Saunak looked like he’d just sustained a Warrior Chakra attack. The old demon’s mouth opened in righteous indignation before closing and opening again as the demon considered his words, staring at Cirayus as though he’d grown an extra head.

Siya, for her part, had gone red from the collar of her robe to the tips of her ears.

“Yes, uncle Cirayus,” she said, clearing her throat and composing herself. “That is a most excellent idea. Saunak? Come.”

Siya walked out of the workshop with all the dignity of an Empress while Saunak scurried behind her, like some lost animal.

Cirayus followed behind, a warm smile spreading across his face that he didn’t bother to hide, given that neither of them was looking at him.

Uncle Cirayus, eh?

He was old enough that it ought to have meant nothing to him. He’d been an uncle and a grand uncle and a great uncle to more of his progeny than he could count, yet this one landed differently. But not because of her status or what she represented.

No, the girl was simply special. A jewel, regardless of her background. And if Cirayus had learned anything in his long life, it was that jewels were to be protected. Protected and polished.

He remained silent as they discussed their options, some ideas sounding quite promising to Cirayus’s layman’s ears.

The enemy they faced was beyond anything they’d ever fought, but even so, Cirayus found it all but impossible not to hope.

Vir was aboard the Asura’s lead airship discussing the Vimana’s current route when his communications orb lit up.

The moment he pulled it out and saw the captain’s grim expression, he knew to expect bad news.

“It just changed course, Akh Nara. Their new heading is due southeast.” The Asura hesitated a moment before continuing, as though it wasn’t his place to say whatever he was about to.

“Akh Nara, this course leads directly to Camar Gadin.”

Vir cursed under his breath.

Beside him, Balagra stopped scrawling over the map he’d been using to plot the Vimana’s course.

Camar Gadin had two hundred thousand demons. It was one of the oldest demonic cities.

“We believe their Vimana’s sensors must have picked up the city and altered course.”

“How long do we have?” Vir asked, his mind already coming up with evacuation plans.

“Perhaps half a day at its current pace, Akh Nara. Maybe slightly more, it is difficult to say.”

“Understood,” Vir replied. Continue tracking them, and maintain your distance. Do not engage under any circumstances.”

“Your will be done, Akh Nara,” the captain responded with the traditional Asuran acknowledgement, ending the communication.

“Mobilize every asset we have,” Vir said, turning to Balagra. “Every available skyship, airship, and every Asura we can muster. I want them in the air and headed to Camar Gadin at once.”

“Of course,” Balagra said, shuffling off to bark out orders to the crew.

He’d barely left when Vir reactivated the orb. Where earlier communications orbs were keyed to a single destination, recent advances had allowed the option to call anyone whose pranic destination address was known.

Vir entered the sequence for Raja Thaman’s emergency communications orb and initiated the call.

All Rajas were required to carry these precharged orbs on their person, and the Bairan Raja answered within seconds.

“Akh Nara,” he said gravely. “I would say well met, but I trust you have news I will not like to hear.”

“Unfortunately so,” Vir said, keeping his voice as neutral as possible. “The rogue Vimana has changed course. It is heading directly for Camar Gadin. At its current speed, we estimate half a day until it arrives at your clan’s capital.”

“You are certain of this?” Thaman asked.

“As certain as I can be. It’s possible they will redirect, but I wouldn’t hold my breath. I am advising an immediate mass evacuation. We’ve already mobilized every airship in the Asuran fleet to assist. I intend to contact Tara to help establish emergency camps outside the city. Your people need not go far. A dozen miles should suffice. We need only to get them outside the walls.”

Thaman scowled as he considered the implications, the silence stretching longer this time.

“Akh Nara,” Thaman said slowly. “You must know what you suggest is impossible. Forget half a day… even with an entire week, this would be a monumental task. The logistics of moving two hundred thousand souls, to say nothing of the many who will undoubtedly refuse to leave their homes regardless of what we tell them… And what of the young? Or the elderly? Many will die merely attempting this.”

“I know,” Vir replied grimly.

“Then you must also know that what you are describing is not an evacuation. It is full-on panic.”

“I’m aware,” Vir said, gripping deck’s railing. “But the alternative is to do nothing. Tell me you prefer the alternative when that Vimana levels Camar Gadin. Panic will be the least of your worries. There won’t be anyone left to panic.”

Thaman scowled, though Vir knew his anger wasn’t directed at him.

“You are certain they bear us ill will?”

“The Vimana is commanded by a Matali general who bears a deep-seated hatred of demons over the slaughter of his wife and child. The only hope we have thus far is that they have yet to bring the weapons systems online, but I’ve no way to predict how long that situation will last. We have a Vimana of our own en route, but it will not reach Camar Gadin in time. Not until it is too late.”

“A Vimana, you say?” Thaman replied, clearly taken aback. “That is good. That is very good. What of our own forces? Do you have anything that might deter or delay them?”

“Believe me, I tested everything I could think of, and I have weapons the likes of which you couldn’t hope to match. I have the best minds in the realm working on a solution as we speak, but I can’t promise they’ll find one in time.”

“This will cause more chaos than the city has ever known,” Thaman said quietly. “Just as things were beginning to settle down, too. It seems we are fated to be thrown from one catastrophe to the next.”

“It certainly seems that way,” Vir said, pursing his lips, wondering if they’d ever get a reprieve. He somehow thought not. Not until the Fateweavers were dealt with once and for all. If such a thing was even possible.

“I will issue the order,” Thaman replied. “Arrangements will begin immediately. Send your airships and Tara’s people too. Send anyone you can spare. And, Akh Nara?”

“Yes?”

“May the gods be with you.”

Vir suppressed the twisted smile that almost rose to his face.

For the gods, he knew, were all dead. Demonkind was entirely on its own, and that terrified Vir more than anything.

“Thank you, Raja,” he said, betraying none of his dark thoughts.

The orb went dark.

Balagra was surrounded by a half-dozen officers, shouting orders, redirecting the fleet, and giving a half-dozen other commands.

Vir stood at the railing for a moment longer, looking across the new continent at all the land that had yet to be mapped, under a bright blue sky that betrayed none of the chaos that was about to descend upon them.

They had twelve hours.

They’d better make every second of it count.
