Chapter 85

We count the work in lintels raised and lines laid true. The villages count it differently: a house that ate this morning, a son home before dark, a daughter who did not have to be brave. I’m no longer certain which ledger matters more. — Codex of the Long Watch, Field Observation, 7th Century BCE, Thessaly

Arthur woke without reflexively reaching for his hammer for the first time since the System. His subconscious had registered this space as safe.

The room was small and clean. The bed under him was a little too short and his feet dangled off the end of it. He did not mind. The fact that he was in a bed was more than enough. Grey sea-light came through the window, and the house smelled of old plaster and lemon oil. He took in the smells and the early-morning sounds without moving, because for once he was not bothered about anything trying to kill him.

Steve was awake on the other bed, sitting up against the wall with the ledger open on his knees. The first thing Arthur noticed was that he was not writing. For weeks the man had filled every spare minute writing and drawing, his pencil going like a metronome.

“You’ve been up a while,” Arthur said.

“I have.” Steve closed the ledger over his thumb. “The name your future mother-in-law dropped last night. Markopoulos.”

“She’s not my mother-in-law.”

“She thinks she might be. Same thing, for our purposes.” Steve rubbed his eyes. “I know him. Knew him. We met at a megalithic conference five years ago, the kind forty people attend and twelve of them are asleep. Afterwards we exchanged a few emails about Maltese trilithon construction, the corbelling at Tarxien.”

“And you want to talk to him.”

“I want to talk to him very much,” he said. “He’s spent over three decades on Greek megaliths. There are maybe a handful of people alive who know what he knows, and one of them is apparently arguing about a chickpea recipe with your...” He caught Arthur’s look. “...with Yiayia.”

Arthur sat up, and his left side did not protest. He pressed his fingers to his ribs and there was no spike of pain. Sarah was getting pretty damn good with her healing.

“The family knows him. I’m sure Eleni can put you in a room with him and nobody official will hear about it,” he said. “We don’t need to go knocking on an archaeologist’s door as four strangers.”

Steve nodded and closed the ledger properly.

“Get dressed. I can hear someone cooking, and I’ve already learned not to keep that woman waiting,” Arthur said with a smile.

The kitchen was the largest room in the house, and Eleni ran it as she must once have run her café: a long scrubbed table, a single gas ring feeding off what was left of the café’s gas bottles, and Eleni over the ring with the focus of a woman who fed people on principle and had taken one look at a man Arthur’s size and decided he was the morning’s main project.

“Sit,” she said, before anyone had managed a good morning. “Sit, sit. Sarah, the bread.”

Arthur sat where she pointed as Sarah looked him over and gave a smile.

“How’s the rib?” she asked.

“All good, thanks to you,” he replied.

Breakfast was bread, strapatsada, olives and a wheel of white cheese. A pot of coffee was going on the stove and filling the room with a smell Arthur had feared he would never wake to again.

Steve sat down, looked at the table like a man checking whether it was real, and then ate with the focused dedication that he usually reserved for research papers.

“Slow,” Sarah told him, in a voice Arthur had been noticing since last night’s dinner.

Her voice had changed in this house. It ran faster and warmer and folded little Greek words into the gaps, an ela here, a sound of fond exasperation there. She was more herself here and more theirs at the same time.

Yiayia, at the head of the table, said something short in Greek, and Sarah laughed despite herself.

“What?” Arthur said.

“She says a man who eats like that has been somewhere without women.” Sarah was still smiling, looking at Steve, who let the observation pass.

The door off the yard banged and Petros came in smelling of salt and the sea. He kissed his mother on both cheeks and his grandmother’s hand and dropped into the chair beside Sarah.

“So,” he said, scooping some eggs. “The wanderers wake. Re, Sarah, you let them sleep until the sun is up? In this house? Mama, you allowed this?”

“Did you not hear? They walked from Nafplio,” Eleni said with a laugh.

“Oh, yes, yes. Nafplio. Sarah took Arthur to see Nafplio,” Petros said, joining his mother in the charade.

“The story is I took all of them to Nafplio,” Sarah said, gritting her teeth.

The rest of breakfast passed in easy conversation and the warm comfort of family.

***

When the table was clear, Donna brought up the gear question.

“Do you know about crafting things?” she asked Petros.

“Yes, many people are doing it with the cores. The fighters are using them on armour and weapons. Soon we will have hoplites again,” he replied with a smile. “People are also using the cores for more practical things. A farmer made a barrel that stores ten times the water it used to.”

“Good, so you are already doing it here. We need better gear,” Donna said. “And we need to sort that out before we do anything else.”

“Our weapons are not as effective as they were before,” Arthur explained.

“So you get new metal,” Petros said. “Athens is full of metal. Half of it is behind glass in the museums.”

“That is exactly where the good material is.” Steve leaned in. “The National Archaeological Museum has bronze that has been sitting in those halls for a month with everything switched on. Properly tempered, the real thing. There are blades in those cases that would...”

“No,” Donna said.

“It’s the single best collection of—”

“That is exactly the reason we steer clear of it. You think that place won’t be under watch? You don’t lift the crown jewels and stay invisible. What else is there?”

Petros had been watching the exchange and he nodded at Donna’s point. “She’s right, the museum is a bad idea. The academics took it the second week. There are men with weapons on the steps.”

“But you don’t need a museum. Ela. Every boat in Piraeus carries knives. Rigging knives. Gaff hooks. The long spikes for the rope. Fish blades my grandfather used. My yard is full of metal that has soaked in the violet stuff, and nobody guards a boatyard.”

“We are looking at something more substantial,” Arthur said.

“There is the market,” Petros went on. “It sits four mornings a week, down by the old container yard. Shops have moved their stock there. Anything you want, if you have something to trade. Food. Tools.” He rubbed two fingers together. “Tobacco, mostly, or cores. Nobody wants Council paper, so the city runs on cigarettes now. A man will sell you his grandfather’s sword for two packets and feel he robbed you.”

“There’s Stavros too, with the forge under the bridge,” Maria put in from the doorway, a child asleep on her hip. “Slow, but good. If you want something made rather than found, he’s the one.”

“Or you can deal with the Council or the academics directly,” Petros finished.

Arthur filed all of it. The material was the easy bit. None of what Petros was describing would do them any good without the other thing, and you did not find cores in a drawer or buy them with cigarettes. You took them off something that would fight to keep them. They only had two between the four of them.

“What about the islands?” Donna asked casually. “If we ever needed to get out on the water. Does that still happen, or is everyone landlocked now?”

“It happens, with the Council’s agreement.” Petros shrugged. “Me, I keep the boats running. There’s a man, Spyros, sails a caïque out of the next harbour over. I have known him for twenty years. He’s the reason half the families on this street have laid eyes on their people across the water since the lights went out. I keep his rigging working, he keeps me in fish I’m too lazy to catch.”

“If you ever want a boat, you come to me first before you go to Spyros. Not the other way. He’s a good man but he’ll still skin you on the price.”

Donna set her cup down and asked the question that was probably on all of their minds. “You mentioned a Council. Tell us about them.”

The room changed slightly. Petros looked over to his wife and then his mother. Arthur caught the faint unease.

“What is there to tell,” Eleni said. “They set themselves up early in the old Parliament, on Syntagma. They send patrols and keep order, more or less. It’s better than nothing.”

“Who is ‘they’?” Donna said. “One man? A committee? The army?”

“All of them, fighting in a nice building instead of the street,” Petros said. “The army generals, the academics from the university, what is left of the old politicians and the Church.”

“But who actually decides?” Donna asked.

Petros turned his palms up. “Re, if I knew that I would be a rich man. We keep our heads down. I sit on my own eggs and let them get on with their bickering.”

“You sit on your...” Steve said.

“He means he keeps to himself,” Sarah explained. “Minds his own business. Doesn’t ask questions. It’s an expression. We have a great many of them about eggs.”

“We do,” Petros agreed, untroubled.

***

The plan came together over the next few minutes.

“Material’s no good to us on its own,” Arthur said. “We need cores first.”

“So we go and earn them,” Donna said. “Same as always. Somewhere with enough wildlife to be worth the trip and few enough people to keep it quiet.”

“The wolves,” Petros said at once, all levity gone from his voice. “A few kilometres past the container yard there is nothing living now but the packs. And they are not wolves anymore. Much bigger, much stronger. Some of them stand up on two legs when they attack. People have started calling them lykanthropoi.”

“Wolf-men,” Steve said quietly, to nobody.

“The Council patrols the container yard, but it takes four of their men to put one of these down. I lost a man from the yard to them in the first month.” He said it plainly.

“Tomorrow, then,” Donna said. “We go at first light and see what these things are. Nobody swings unless something swings first.”

Eleni had crossed the kitchen without a sound and was standing over the table, looking down at all of them.

“What are you planning?” she said, looking at Sarah.

“Mama, we need more cores. Everybody needs them now.”

“You are home now. Why do you need weapons and the stones? I did not know if you were dead or alive until last night, and now you want to walk into danger willingly?” She turned on Petros. “Vlaka. You fill their heads with nonsense.”

“Mama, look at them.” Petros had his hands up before she finished. “Do they look like they need coddling?”

“Petros, talk to your friends at the dock. Find them some work, agapi mou,” Maria said. “They can help with the boats and the fishing.”

In every system I have monitored, no candidate has ever been reassigned to fishing, Archie said in their heads. I would not object to logging the precedent.

Eleni had learned her daughter was alive one night ago, and this morning that daughter wanted to walk towards something it took four armed men to kill. There was no cover story that made that reasonable to a mother, and Arthur suspected the whole truth would have done worse.

Sarah looked at the others, then got up and took her mother’s arm.

“Mama, I need to talk to you,” she said, motioning towards the stairs.

Eleni looked at her daughter for a long while before she let Sarah guide her up.

“What if she doesn’t allow it?” Donna asked Arthur in a low voice.
