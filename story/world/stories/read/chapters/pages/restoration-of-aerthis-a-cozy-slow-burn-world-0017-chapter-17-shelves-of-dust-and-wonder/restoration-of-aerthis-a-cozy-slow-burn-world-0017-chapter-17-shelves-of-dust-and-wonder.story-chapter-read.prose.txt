Over the next two days, Elynara didn’t miss a step.

Morning meant shrine time. Offerings first; fresh and simple. Mana feeding came after. The spirits took it like clockwork now, drifting closer before she even knelt down.

With little reason to speak, she just shaped, fed, and played with the sprites. Noting the small changes each time. The areas around the shrines were turning a deeper, healthier green. And even the ground seemed more alive somehow. There were sprouts where there were none yesterday.

Even over such a short few days her offerings were having an effect.

And then came training.

She walked loops around the eastern fields, drawing light lines of mana into one hand, sometimes two. Let them hover atop her staff. Pulled them longer. Shortened the weave mid-step. Starpuff snapped one apart with its face and spun away like it had proven something.

“You’re not helping,” she muttered, grabbing the thread again.

The tension sat higher in her arms now. Longer holds meant more strain, but it stayed clean. Even when she tripped on uneven ground, her shaping didn’t scatter.

And keeping it up for long didn’t waste as much mana as before either.

Progress!

After an hour, she would shift her focus. Mana sensing came next. She wasn’t meditating in place, as that wasn’t the point, but rather walking, watching, feeling the flow of mana and pulse of the ground around her.

The better she got at sensing while moving, the sooner would her goal of being a mana-and-monster sensing master come true!

It was slow going for now. But improving nonetheless.

She could see how patches of mana held shape for longer. Noted how some small dips near the orchard felt... off. She tracked that feeling until it gave her a partial outline of a mole tunnel she couldn’t see.

She smiled at that. And then nearly laughed when Starpuff zoomed in to chase that mole around its hole.

Really, her spirit companion antics were something else.

Patrols stayed quiet. One Bonefang on the first day, none on the second. She dismantled it on the spot, but didn’t drag the corpse. The meat was stringy, and the hide wasn’t worth the effort, so she only took the best cuts. Rina didn’t ask, just taking the offered bundle. While Theren just glanced at her hands when she came back and went back to work.

The herbs list filled steadily. She knew where they grew now, including ridge ends, root splits, shaded hollows and places that didn’t dry too fast. She found three full patches on her last loop and left two untouched, already planning the harvest order.

Her mana capacity hit sixty-seven. She felt the expected spike in difficulty at sixty-four, and each increase was taking even longer now. But she didn’t mind too much.

Evening meant one last pass by the shrine. Spirits flickered gently, more alert than before. They didn’t react to her, simply waiting. And when she gave them mana, they pulled it in without rush.

Starpuff flicked past her head, clipped the frog carving again, then vanished into the grass.

“Still not a fan of that one?”

No answer, of course. But the glow trailed off smug.

The wind stayed mild that night. The fields were quiet. And no movement she could detect near the woods.

Tomorrow she would finish the herb request, do one final patrol pass, and head back.

On the third day, Elynara woke to muffled chatter and the clatter of bowls, the kind of morning noise that meant the kitchen had been awake longer than she had.

Light filtered in through the shutter slats, slanted and gold, and somewhere near her toes, Starpuff was very busy trying to wiggle underneath the blanket.

She exhaled and flopped an arm across her eyes.

“Just five more minutes.”

The blanket twitched, then the lump of invisible smugness bumped against her ribs like it had very strong feelings about that.

Another bump.

Another.

She groaned, sat up, and flung the blanket aside before she could change her mind. Starpuff pulsed once in triumph and zipped past her cheek, trailing green sparks that flickered through the beam of sunlight cutting across the floor.

“Menace,” she muttered, pushing hair out of her eyes.

Her shirt hung slightly loose. The belt fit on a tighter notch again. Her pack, still slumped by the door, leaned lighter than she remembered.

Right. Last day.

She had things to finish - and not just for the guild.

Her boots hit the floor with a soft thunk, and a few minutes later she stepped into the hall already thinking about what to bring back for Mina. Something fun. Something weird, maybe. Probably not a frog carving, though that one certainly had personality.

The kitchen was full. Rina held court near the hearth, hair tied back, tail swinging behind her with every gesture. The youngest two kids darted between chairs. The third was carefully not making eye contact with a bowl he clearly didn’t want to finish.

“Elynara! There you are!” Rina waved a spoon like a command baton. “Quick, before everything gets cold!”

She slipped into her usual spot at the corner, just in time for Kaela to slide a plate her way.

“We made the good stuff today,” Kaela said with a wink. “Also the weird jam. No one warned Dad which jar was which.”

From the other side of the table, Theren grunted and calmly took another bite of toast without comment. Elynara bit back a smile and focused on eating before someone tried to pile a third helping onto her plate.

Conversation flowed around her like it always did: light, chaotic, and half of it consisted of things she only half understood about farm life. But she didn’t drift off this time. Once her bowl was empty and the last of her tea cooled, she cleared her throat.

“I wanted to tell you some things about the shrines.”

Three heads turned. Then four. Even Aren looked up from where he’d been tying a sack closed.

“About the offerings. I’ve been watching how the spirits react. Dried food doesn’t do anything, they don’t care for it at all. But fresh fruit, especially anything just harvested? That gets the spirits happy.”

Kaela’s mouth made a small ‘o.’ Rina tilted her head, ears twitching.

“As for the charms… They’re good. Really good. Especially the rounder ones seem to hold mana longer. If you pour a little more mana into them while carving, even better.”

“They hold mana even if we don’t use magic?” Kaela asked.

“Doesn’t matter. Focus and intention seem to be enough for some of your mana to end up in the carvings. If nothing else just holding them close and focusing should work. And if you do learn a bit of mana handling, even basic, you can charge pieces after making them too, so you could even reuse old ones.”

Rina’s smile spread like someone had just handed her the whole planting schedule two weeks early.

“Well,” she said, standing to scoop another pile of vegetables into a sack, “we’ll just have to make a whole box of the things, won’t we?”

Kaela nodded eagerly. Finnie launched into a flurry of questions about which shapes held the best mana and whether carving a frog-shaped charm made it stronger or cursed forever. Theren raised a brow but didn’t object. Even Aren gave a small grunt of agreement.

“We’ll tell the others too,” Rina said. “The whole village, if we can help it. No reason to keep it to ourselves, now that we know.”

Elynara flushed, brushing her hand down the side of her tunic.

“It’s nothing special. I just noticed a few things. But, um, could you help me prepare a gift for my friend Mina? I promised I would bring her a nice little something from the farm.”

Rina was already waving toward the back room before she even finished speaking.

“Aren, grab the box! And the jars! We’re not letting her leave empty-handed. She’s got a little girlfriend to spoil, after all.”

Elynara choked on air.

“It’s not–she’s not–we’re not–!”

Kaela looked far too gleeful. Theren smirked without lifting his eyes from the tool blade he was cleaning. Finnie whispered something scandalous and was promptly shushed by no one at all.

Even Aren let out a single, very low chuckle.

“The lot of you,” Elynara said, rubbing her forehead. “Unbelievable.”

“Absolutely,” Rina said brightly. “Now here are your love gifts, and I want to hear nothing about any payments! You helped us lots already with your hunts and spirits stuff!”

There was no winning with that chatterbox of a woman. But she still accepted the wrapped bundle when it was offered, and made sure to thank each of them properly before slinging her satchel into place.

Wishing them all a good day she went on to do her now almost routine pass.

The shrine path waited just past the fence, and her boots found the familiar trail without effort.

One last patrol before her return to town.

Elynara stepped over the last ridge of grass and into the familiar quiet of the shrine clearing. The morning light hadn’t fully broken through the trees yet, but the air was already warm enough to carry the scent of wet leaves, old moss, and a faint tang of wood still warming from the night.

The spirits stirred before she got within reach.

She paused, just for a moment, watching them drift. One circled the bowl, the other hovered near the stones, pulsing slow and even. Neither waited for her to sit. They moved with purpose now, recognizing her form.

“Eager today, aren’t you?”

No carved offerings this time. She had something else in mind.

She knelt at the usual spot, staff across her knees, and extended one hand.

A slow thread of mana pooled into her palm, steady and unshaped. The spirits surged immediately with no hesitation or delay whatsoever. They pressed into the offered flow like children crowding a treat bowl, each drawing just enough before darting off to the edges of the clearing.

Elynara let her awareness stretch.

She caught the moment they pressed their own energy back into the land.

The mana they returned wasn’t hers. Not directly. It had shifted a little. Perhaps filtered, processed somehow. Smaller in volume than what she gave, but cleaner and more tailored? Her senses barely caught the difference now. That subtle, reformed trace. Like comparing raw wool to spun thread.

Perhaps not the best comparison, but one that felt right to her.

They circled back. Waited.

She gave more.

Again they fed, again they pulsed outward. The second pass spread wider. Barely. But it reached beyond the previous circle marked by the stones now, touching into the thinner grass beyond the path.

The cycle repeated once more.

By the third return, they slowed. One circled, hesitant. The other lowered toward the ground and hovered still.

They were full.

Elynara exhaled, blinking once. Even though a third of her mana was gone, easily measured by the familiar pull in her chest, it was worth every drop.

She rose, walking the edge of the clearing while humming to herself.

And perhaps to Starpuff too, the little menace seemed to enjoy it too.

The effect wasn’t dramatic, barely an improvement really. Nothing had burst into bloom, but the colors felt a little more vibrant now. And where the land had held a dull haze of mana before, she now felt threads forming. Distant and weak, but still there when there were none before.

Mana moving with the growth.

It reached a small distance beyond the shrine ring now. The furthest tips barely grazed the nearby root cluster, but they were there.

That was enough to confirm her suspicions.

She turned back to the stones and sat again, letting her hands fall to her lap. Opting to simply replenish her mana this time around. No training for once.

The spirits hovered nearby but didn’t crowd her now. Their glow dimmed slightly, gentle and satisfied. Starpuff floated down from above and landed on her shoulder with the kind of smug weight only it could manage.

Elynara didn’t shoo it.

The proof was there. Restoration was possible.

Even if it would take more mana than any single mage could offer… even if the patience, time, and resources required bordered on absurd...

It could be done. That was the difference.

And now she could carry that truth forward.

Half an hour later, Elynara tugged the satchel strap tighter across her chest and set off toward the forest path, boots pressing into dew-damp grass with a familiar rhythm.

Starpuff had taken up its usual post just ahead, flickering with all the importance of a lead scout, only to fall back and tug at her cheek the moment she didn’t match its pace.

“I fed you already,” she muttered, batting gently at the puffball as it looped behind her head like that proved something.

It trilled in protest. She sighed, fed it a short thread of mana, and kept walking.

The edge of the woods came into view by the time her fingers had fully dried. She slowed slightly out of caution, and swept the outer brush with her senses.

Still nothing.

Four full days of sweeps had cleared most of the nearby threats. The ground held signs of life, but nothing larger or dangerous. Still, she made a full pass around the known routes, checking each path to make sure. Not because she expected to find anything, but because it was what she had trained herself to do.

Only after the last bend felt clear did she step into the treeline.

Light filtered down in sharp cuts, scattering gold across the moss. She moved quickly, following the ridges she had marked days ago. Her herb list had narrowed to just two final targets; they would be easy finds if the weather held, and it had so far.

The first patch sat beneath a sloped root system, right where the water likely pooled after last week’s rain. Leaves curled open as she approached, clean and undisturbed. She took what she needed, trimming carefully and tucking the stems into the cloth wrap at her side.

Second came from a hollowed stump, shaded and dry. The stems there looked brittle but still usable.

The last took her higher up the ridge, where the earth thinned and the wind carried more dust than scent. It bloomed under stone cover, just enough for a handful.

By the time she stepped off the ridge and looped back toward the lower trail, the satchel sat fuller at her hip.

Done.

She could have turned back then. No reason not to. But her legs carried her forward, already tracing the wider loop she had walked each day. One last patrol. For habit. For her own peace of mind.

Half an hour passed with nothing but wind in the brush. Birds chattered, but too high to be of interest.

Then a rustle.

She shifted without pause, staff in hand, and shaped the spell mid-turn.

The Bonefang lunged from the side brush, mouth wide, but she was already moving. The fire caught it mid-leap, and collapsed in a thud of limbs and smoke before its claws even touched ground.

She didn’t stumble, or need to correct her spell. It was all just a single clean motion.

Elynara blinked once, lowering her hand.

That was it?

The first time she had seen one, her core had nearly locked up from fear. Now she didn’t even need to stop walking. Her mana didn’t waver. Her pulse barely shifted.

She crouched beside the body, checking for movement, then reached to extract the fangs, the only things worth the effort.

When she stood again, Starpuff looped lazily overhead, spinning once before nestling behind her hood like it knew exactly how smug it looked.

That nosey troublemaker.

She didn’t bother to argue.

Her skills had grown quickly and quietly. And now the threats of yesterday were just a nuisance of today.

It felt good to feel strong. But she knew there was much to improve upon still.

Returning to her patrol, little else stood out on her trip. Since she didn’t need to look out for herbs anymore, sightseeing and testing the limits of her mana sense became her new priority.

And it wasn’t until nearly an hour later, just when she was about to start on her way back, when something shifted.

The stillness of the area was sudden, and unexpected. Far too silent to be normal.

Elynara paused halfway through a step, one boot pressed into moss, the other hovering mid-air as her ears caught up with what her body had already noticed.

No birds. No breeze. Not even the faint rustle of leaves she had come to expect. It was like the world had forgotten how to breathe.

She let her raised foot fall gently, then glanced over her shoulder.

Starpuff had stopped.

No loops, no lazy flickers. Just a dim-green pulse, steady and low, hovering barely above her shoulder like it wanted to vanish into the folds of her hood.

A tightness wound into her chest, but she didn’t panic yet. Just that quiet thread of warning that only ever came when things broke the pattern hard enough to silence even the trees.

She turned, slow and cautious, and swept her eyes along the brush line.

The path ahead dipped, but the slope beyond looked… wrong. Deeper than it should be. No wind to stir the undergrowth. No chittering things darting between roots. Just a hollow weight clinging to the edges of her senses.

Starpuff pressed closer, its glow flickering once, like it was considering bolting. It never pressed this close before.

“I know,” she whispered.

But still, her feet moved forward. Because if the land was quiet, then something had made it quiet. And if she didn’t look, someone else might not get the chance.

The trees grew thicker along the left bank. She followed the right, stepping from root to root without touching the loose soil. Her staff shifted silently from shoulder to hand.

When the clearing came into view, she didn’t step into it.

She didn’t need to.

Because even from behind the old knotted trunk she’d half-hidden behind, the shape in the hollow below made her breath catch.

It was eating.

A Bristleboar carcass ripped down the middle, its spine snapped like kindling, with blood pooling into the moss in slow, soaking rings. Another lay nearby, barely recognizable.

And crouched above it all, feeding with slow, deliberate movements, was something huge and wrong.

It had the shape of a bear. Mostly. But the bones jutted in unnatural angles. Plates of dull ivory ran along its back in jagged ridges. Horns twisted out from its skull like the roots of a storm-struck tree. Each claw was the length of her forearm. And the steady, guttural, wet sound it made scraped at the back of her teeth.

She stayed still, not daring to even twitch or whisper.

And just watched the thing.

The creature hadn’t noticed her. Not yet. Its attention stayed fixed on the meat, ripping through muscle and snapping bone like it was clearing debris.

Even so, she didn’t breathe until she had counted eight full seconds without it turning.

Then she ducked lower, heart thudding slow and deliberate, brain catching up with what her eyes had already accepted.

That thing was big.

Big enough that the Bristleboars looked like prey animals beside it. Big enough that even the usual surge of fight-or-flight stayed locked down, like her body knew there wasn’t a right answer.

She swallowed, the inside of her mouth dry.

That wasn’t just another strong monster. That was a problem. A threat. A predator expanding its territory.

And it was far too close to the village.

Her mind ran the math before she could stop it. A creature that size wouldn’t stay hidden. If it was here because it was hungry, which it must have been with how little meat remained on the first corpse, that meant the smaller monsters had likely already fled.

Which explained the surge. The Bristleboars, the Bonefangs. They hadn’t just shown up by chance.

They had been running.

And this was what they’d been running from.

Her stomach turned. Not from fear, but from the cold, creeping clarity that told her what she didn’t want to admit.

This wasn’t a one-off sighting. It wasn’t a rare spawn.

It was a displacement.

A predator pushed deep into safer territory. Which meant it wouldn’t stop here. Not once it finished its meal.

And the farms were just a few hills away.

She bit the inside of her cheek, grounding herself. Her hand hadn’t left her staff. Her fingers twitched slightly as she calculated again.

Going back to town was an option. Report it. Get a hunt party. Wait.

But how long would that take? A day? Two? What if they didn’t find it again? What if it moved? What if it reached the farms first?

She couldn’t risk that. Not with how it moved. Not with how silent the land had become.

And worse, she had already seen it.

She couldn’t just ignore that.

And idle thought passed through her mind.

This must be how even veteran adventures might sometimes die. Capable enough to run, but knowing they couldn’t just run. Just leave the problem be.

Starpuff hadn’t moved from her shoulder, pulsing gently in time with her thoughts. Its glow flickered nervously, then steadied.

She exhaled once.

Not running, then.

If she was going to strike, it had to count.

A flicker of thought brought her hand to her satchel. She crouched lower, circling slowly through the trees until she found a small rise. It had an angled, clear sightline, with a boulder to her left and two trees to her right.

No deadfall. No brambles.

Perfect.

Her fingers tightened around her staff, her feet settling naturally into the slope. Every motion felt familiar now. Known. Practiced.

And that was important, because she only had one shot.

The spell would shine.

She had used it a hundred times. More. But never like this, never empowered before what she needed. What she felt was safe.

She drew deep.

Deeper than she had even during her training runs. Mana surged up from her core, curling hot against her ribs. The spell began to form: heavy, dense, compacted between layers of pressure. She didn’t add anything new. Didn’t risk changes. Just built it stronger. Tighter. More precise and condensed.

She felt the air around her ripple.

The creature twitched.

Its ears turned. Then its head.

The eyes were black. Not deep, but flat. Like stone soaked in oil.

It stared directly toward the trees, unblinking.

Her shaping finished just as it shifted its weight, bones cracking beneath its shoulders as it turned.

The spell flared in her hands, almost done.

One chance at a clear shot.

And it had just seen her, but it was too late for it.

The spell exploded.

Not just in sound, but in shape, in pressure, in consequence.

Elynara’s staff bucked in her grip as the cast reached full compression, mana flaring outward with a force that cracked the very air. The impact slammed into the clearing like a hammer dropped from the sky. The Bristleboar remains launched backward violently, with one sheared apart midair, and the other tumbling end over end through a thicket before slamming into a tree trunk with a sound like wet stone.

She couldn’t see the monster. Not through the smoke. Not through the light.

But it didn’t matter.

Her feet had already shifted, grounding her weight. Her hands drew again.

She didn’t hesitate, and knew better to celebrate too early. Simply prepared spell two as fast as she could.

The blast had rocked the earth, thrown soil high and carved deep into the underbrush. Leaves tore free from branches. The air was heat and shrapnel. Her ears rang.

Still, she shaped.

No thought for her own hearing, no time for distractions now.

Only the next cast mattered.

Because she knew.

That hadn’t finished it, not yet.

The answer to her unasked question came fast.

A painful roar split the aftermath, low and full of hate. It rolled over her like a wall of broken glass.

Full of nothing but pure fury.

The smoke tore open.

The beast surged forward through the cratered path, its entire side mangled and blackened, skin cracked and bleeding where the fire had landed. Its eyes burned with deep aggression. Only one thought in its skull now.

Kill.

It leapt.

Forward and up. Over the boulder she had picked for cover. Its claws scraped deep gouges into the stone as it vaulted, landing hard enough to send another shockwave through the ground.

Tree branches snapped. Bark rained down. Her balance rocked…

…but she was still casting.

Spell two bloomed in her hands, compressed tighter than the first, her mana shaping under pressure like a second heartbeat. Her core screamed under the weight of it.

She didn’t flinch.

The beast tore through the first tree, shoulder-checking the trunk as it barreled forward. Wood shattered. Splinters flew. And it hardly even slowed.

It saw her.

So close. It was so, so close now.

Its jaw opened, and the noise it made hit her like a hammer.

She didn’t blink, just aimed.

And fired.

The spell launched almost point-blank, straight into its open mouth.

She didn’t wait for the explosion.

Didn’t see it. Didn’t need to.

The force hit her next.

Fire and pressure cracked the world around her. The shockwave ripped through the clearing and threw her back like a doll. She tumbled over roots, her staff flung loose, her body skidding against the moss-streaked ground until she hit a slope and rolled once, twice, before landing flat.

Silence rang louder than the blast.

She lay there. Dazed. Cold air pressed into her lungs in a gasp she didn’t remember taking.

Then the pain arrived.

It was dull, and nothing felt broken. It was just every limb protesting the very notion of movement.

Still, her arms moved.

Her fingers curled, grasped empty air before closing around the staff. She pulled herself up.

Shaky. Slow.

Another cast?

She tried.

Mana responded, but weakly. Friction bled into the shaping. Her core flickered, the draw inconsistent.

One more empowered spell. Just strong enough for a Bristleboar. With no more mana to give, that was the best she could prepare.

She breathed through her nose, vision swimming at the edges.

But no roar followed. No charge happened. No branches breaking.

Just wind. And ash. Some fire crackling. And the distant creak of trees shifting in place.

The forest hadn’t gone quiet again.

She had.

Elynara forced her weight back under her. Leaned into the staff. Legs trembling.

No movement ahead.

Cautious, and focused, she limped forward, every step measured. Her left foot dragged slightly. The smoke thinned.

And there it was.

The monster sprawled across the ruined clearing, half its face missing. One horn gone. Blood soaked the soil beneath it in a wide, ugly smear. Its chest didn’t rise.

No tension left in the limbs.

Eyes glassed over.

It was dead.

She stared for five full breaths.

It didn’t twitch, or move. It just lay there, still.

Then her spine released its tension. Shoulders dropped, knees buckling from under her..

Kneeling not by choice, her body simply gave out.

Just needed to be low. Just needed her breath back.

Starpuff returned after a long delay, flickering dim, then curling beside her ear like it wasn’t sure whether to flick her nose or rest against her temple.

She didn’t move.

Didn’t speak.

But she was glad her little spirit companion was there. Its presence reassuring to her in ways she didn’t expect.

Eventually, she stood back up. Gritted her teeth and checked her limbs. Nothing broken. A shallow cut across her upper arm. Burnt palm. And a long scratch along her thigh where she’d rolled through a bramble. Probably some bruises too, she couldn’t tell yet.

But it could have been worse.

Should have been worse.

But she had won.

She had faced something that shouldn’t have been here, that would have killed anything it reached, and stopped it alone.

She blinked once, and the stinging hit behind her eyes. Starpuff tried getting her attention, but she was too busy reeling and just swiped it away.

The shock of the event had just now reached her.

Still, she walked.

The way out felt longer now. She took the clearest path she could, staff in hand, her pace uneven but steady. Every part of her throbbed with tiredness, but the forest no longer felt oppressive.

Just heavy.

By the time the trees thinned and the first fencepost came into view, the sun had shifted again. Afternoon light angled through the fields, scattering gold across the brush.

And by the fence line, there stood Theren, axe in hand. Aren beside him with a hook. Rina near the gate, sleeves rolled and worry carved into every line of her face.

The moment they saw her, they moved.

Rina reached her first.

“Stars, girl–what happened? Are you hurt? What were those explosions about?”

Theren’s gaze stayed fixed on the tree line, jaw tense.

Aren didn’t speak, but the hook in his hand was tight in his grip.

Elynara tried to answer. Her mouth opened once, then twice. Until she managed to mutter out:

“There was a big monster in there.”

Then the rest.

“I found it in the eastern hollow. Past the old slope. Dunno what it was, but it was treating Bristleboars like snacks. It’s dead now.”

She didn’t remember finishing the sentence. Didn’t remember when her legs finally failed her.

Just Rina’s arms catching her. The warmth of the apron. The faint scent of herbs and kitchen woodsmoke.

And the sound of her own breath finally catching up to her.

The tears came next.

Not because of fear, or pain.

Just the kind of exhausted relief that came from knowing it was over.

Rina pressed a hand to the back of her head, rocking her like a child.

Aren stood nearby, eyes wide.

Theren said nothing.

But the slow, assessing, and proud look in his eyes said plenty.

And then Elynara slept. Right there. No more magic, no more plans. Just the loving embrace of dreams.
