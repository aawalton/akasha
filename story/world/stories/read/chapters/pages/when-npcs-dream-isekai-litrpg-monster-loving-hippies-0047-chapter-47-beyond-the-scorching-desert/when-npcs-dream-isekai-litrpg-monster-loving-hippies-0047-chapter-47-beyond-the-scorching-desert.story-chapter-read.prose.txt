I am one from a species of bamboo-like beings—tall and hardy, rooted by nature, evolved not to move, but to command.

We do not walk. We do not build.And yet, we have endured for thousands of years—because we never needed to.

Instead, we formed deep, ancient symbioses with many fungal genera.Certain fungi produce spores that colonize animal hosts; once established, they can influence behavior.

Over evolutionary time, my kind learned to wield precise biochemical control over these partners.By adjusting fungal strains, spore doses, and chemical cues, we can tune a host’s behavior along countless axes—compliance, affection, aggression, even task orientation.

We mastered the balance between control and autonomy—granting our hosts enough freedom to thrive, while ensuring their loyalty to our will.

About five thousand years ago, we discovered a bipedal species: master builders, brilliant and cooperative.

We lived in perfect mutualism and harmony.They walked, built, and crafted.We dreamed. We guided. We controlled.

We helped them live fuller, more purposeful lives. They were happy. We were happy.

Together, we conquered our world.

We built cities, networks, prosperity beyond measure.Our numbers swelled into the tens of billions.We even built ships—and reached the stars.

We were unstoppable.

But five years ago, something happened.

In the middle of an important task—guiding a space pilot host through a delicate cargo landing—I simply vanished.Ripped from my duties.Thrown here.To this cursed land.

Perhaps dark magic brought us.Perhaps some advanced alien technology.I do not know.

What I do know is this:This land lacks what we need most. It lacks builder species.

Only animal-like creatures—things of nursery tales and wild lore—dwell here, and they can barely meet our simplest needs.Our kind evolved to depend on intelligent builder hosts. Without them, we cannot sustain our societies, manufacture complex goods, or reproduce properly at scale.

We can survive for a time—scavenging, adapting—but not forever.Our only hope is that somewhere, on another continent of this world, a suitable builder species exists—one compatible with our fungal symbiosis.

But we were trapped.Trapped on a continent not ringed by ocean, but by a vast, scorching desert—a hellish barrier of heat and death.

But we were not ready to give up.We are not a species that yields.We are survivors—warriors of the patient kind.

We found a bird-like species we could use for exploration.Not intelligent, but mobile.

We sent them to scout for continents beyond the desert.Many died.But some returned.

Through countless painful, costly missions, we discovered two other continents:Help support creative writers by finding and reading their stories on the original site.one to the west, one to the south.

The birds were not intelligent enough to gather meaningful data.But we had other tools—information spores.

We embedded trillions of them within the birds.When the spores found new hosts, they grew fungi that produced more spores—each carrying fragments of biological data.Some of those spores made their way back with the returning birds.

One by one, we absorbed their reports.It was a slow, incomplete, agonizing process.But after many months, a picture began to form.

The western continent held a highly intelligent species—but not a builder.

The southern continent, however…There were three.Three intelligent builder species!

One was arthropodic—like intelligent insects.Their biology proved incompatible with our fungi, so we could not control them.

But the other two were perfect.A race of large, flightless builder birds…And another: a naked species, strikingly similar to our original builder hosts from the old world—full of promise.

We rejoiced.There was hope.

But there was a problem.Three thousand kilometers of lethal desert stood between us—a wasteland with no shade, no water, and temperatures reaching sixty degrees celsius by midday.

We could not cross.

We could send small cargo by bird, but transporting an adult bamboo individual was impossible—an adult can weigh over a hundred kilograms.

We could send a sapling, but it could not survive the arid journey.And even if it did… we cannot control ourselves.We are selfish creatures by nature.A single sapling might not want to help the rest.

So a new plan formed:Control the builders from afar.Have them build airships.Have them come to us.Have them carry us across the desert to their fertile continent.Have us take root there—and take control.

But control spores are not like information spores.They are larger. More complex.Harder to grow.Harder to transport.

And unlike information spores, control spores require active tuning.They do not simply infect and obey—they must be maintained, guided, reinforced.We could not send control spores embedded with specific commands through the birds and expect results.

So we made a plan.We would use the birds to send forty infant Scorpion-Cats—controller creatures from our own lore—to the southern continent.We embedded them with intricate fungal strains designed to compel them to dominate the builder communities.Once they succeeded, we would send additional control spores to the Scorpion-Cats and have them command the builder to construct airships to retrieve us.

But the plan failed.

After three years, our information spores revealed the truth:The Scorpion-Cats had formed tribes, fought among themselves, and enslaved monsters instead of builders.

Years of planning, resources, and sacrifice—lost.

But we did not give up.We never give up.

We shifted strategy and made a new investment: the small, winged, fluff-tailed species from the western continent.They were not builders, but they were highly intelligent—and had another key advantage: they were small and light.We could import them.

We developed sedative spores and manipulated the birds to retrieve one hundred of them.Once here, we began our work in earnest.We trained them. Controlled them. Refined the fungi for loyalty and stability.

The fluff-ball creatures had no natural ability to command other species—but that was something we could fix.

Through a complex process, we embedded them with controller fungi, granting them the ability, through subtle biochemical signaling, to direct the fungi and thus control other creatures themselves.We made them into controllers.

It wasn’t perfect.It wasn’t like us, or even like the Scorpion-Cats.But it was something.

We had created a true agent—a loyal and intelligent being with partial control abilities.

The agent could give basic commands to animals, even to builder species.Not full control—but enough to influence.Enough to manipulate.

It could infiltrate builder societies, identify key individuals—engineers, craftsmen, leaders—and guide them toward a single goal: the construction of airships.

If it succeeded, the builders would come with airships and carry our kind to safety.

If it failed, the desert would remain our prison—and our species would slowly fade away.

So I continue my work: tuning fungal strains, sending birds with fragile orders, refining the agent’s plans.I gaze across the blazing desert and remember the old world—our shining cities, our starships, the harmony between builder and bamboo.

Evolution gave us the tools.Now I must use them—and hope they are enough to change our fate in this harsh new world.
