2099

The year 2099 unfolded like a gray canvas—streaked with ozone, blood, and melting snow crushed beneath boots and new combat exoskeletons carrying bodies made old by the years of war.

At the start of the year, Stepnye Volki was more than a squad—it was a core of trust and affection, forged after months of joint operations, shared battles, the occasional quarrel, and nights around improvised campfires with the entire unit.

Mai had learned to read each member: Sasha always pacing back and forth with new orders, always ready for a fight and sometimes cracking an occasional joke. The silent Ludovic, who spoke little about himself but was prepared for anything. The comforting calm of Eun-ji, ever willing to pick up a rifle or the bandages needed for the wounded. Soo-ah’s subtle humor. Natalya’s calculated agility, and Jiho’s almost superhuman intuition—always studying, meditating, or crafting new magic in her moments of rest. Nikolai, too, had gradually started to open up to the whole team. The squad had the feeling that he and Soo-ah were always a little close, but they let them be.

Even if the world outside strained under everything the war had unleashed—waves of refugees, fractus-core trafficking, the rise of colossal trees across parts of the planet, political tensions, and the Dark Events—the unit felt like a tiny microcosm of what Mai hoped the world might one day become once the conflict ended.

Amid the conflict, news began arriving of unrest in the United States. Apparently, after so many years of fighting, society had begun demanding results, while several incidents had occurred between feys and different political factions. There were conflicts at universities, particularly in departments related to history and the humanities, some of which had even ended with bonfires where books related to history were burned.

All of it seemed to stem from something that had been brewing for years, ever since the existence of feys and magic had been revealed. Numerous youth organizations were demanding answers, claiming that the entire history of humanity over the previous two hundred years had been a lie.

Those incidents also spread to other countries, creating divisions between more conservative generations, more open-minded generations, and the younger generations who were now living through the conflict themselves.

Other problems arose from interactions between fey communities and humans. In some countries, changes in government during the years of the conflict had led to shifts in political agendas. Some governments refused to accept fey communities, while certain provincial governments in other countries established laws prohibiting personal relationships with feys. Some went so far as to declare that intimate relationships, romantic partnerships, or even friendships with feys—or with certain types of feys—were violations of the law. Some kinds of feys, particularly those with animal characteristics, were forbidden from establishing relationships with humans, with penalties of two to three years in prison under charges of engaging in interspecies sexual relations.

Meanwhile, the Council and the factions that had established the Pact were doing everything they could to navigate the treacherous and dangerous waters of international geopolitics.

Mai had also begun to change her view of the Council. Aside from Leon, Fu, Travis, and a few others, many of its members had once been morally gray figures, their votes often determined by whichever side offered them the greater advantage. Apparently, over the previous three years, the Council had changed its membership, removing those whose only interests were personal gain.

Although the main conflict for her remained the Fractus, battles were being fought all over the world, even if some of them involved votes, laws, or economic interests instead of weapons.

Whatever happened, that did not change the fact that the next morning she and the others would have to get up, grab a quick breakfast, put on their equipment, and head out to repel attacks wherever the higher command sent them.

The winter of the new year for them began in the Norilsk region with clashes against scattered waves of fractus, primarily the Rollers and the Grivens.

Each time she saw soldiers from others teams deploying behind improvised barricades or shielding wounded comrades, a strange warmth blossomed inside her: pride, fear, and something far harder to name.

She had no doubt that she has changed over in the last few years.

Her life had been a series of comings and goings, full of action. The early years with Miho, then Whisper, her time as a researcher and consultant in Tokyo, and finally joining Nevermore. Many winters had passed since then. Even so, the feeling of seeing someone die always hit her hard. It didn’t matter if she was over ninety years old. That feeling was still there. Masked in the battlefield, but beating as loudly as her heart.

That feeling deepened when she saw young soldiers fall in the early engagements, they were replaced the next day by others, trained and ready. Even if occasional tensions surfaced for them too—after all, the revelation of the hidden world hadn’t come without complications in Russia too—the truth was that the joint forces existed for that very purpose.

Combat the enemy.

The war against the Fractus would never have begun without that revelation, otherwise the world would have already been destroyed, or only the strongest would have survived.

But even the strongest didn’t always survive.

Mai sometimes caught herself marveling at the fact that she was still alive. A part of her knew that living her life from conflict to conflict gave her an advantage in this war—but still, she couldn’t help being surprised. She had made it through while some of her friends had been lost along the way.

She had lost far too many in the conflict—comrades she had met during the war, and others she had known throughout her long life. The ones she knew best were still safe, but every day she and Nikolai woke up to check that the others hadn’t joined the list of those lost on the battlefield.

And yet she had to keep going.

Spring brought missions in central Siberia, circling the exclusion zone of the largest anomaly and the Mongolian border, where the Aurex began flying over territories that had once been safe.

Mai found herself reacting faster than she thought possible, detecting flight routes and anticipating attacks as if her body itself remembered the movements of every fractus. Yet what impressed her most was how each squad member had adapted—to her, and to one another: Ludovic adjusting his position to cover flanks without anyone asking, Natalya moving with a rhythm that felt almost choreographed, Jiho preparing fractium-paper barriers while smiling faintly, Eun-ji appearing just in time to stop hemorrhages that could cost lives—or regrowing missing limbs thanks to the new organic-cultivation techniques developed during the war—and Soo-ah tossing comments that broke tension into shared laughter.

Beyond all that, a part of them moved almost automatically during missions. Sometimes they joked about whether their exoskeletons would eventually meld with their bodies, until one day they’d no longer be able to take them off.

In the engagements of April and May, abandoned factories and industrial zones turned into lethal labyrinths. Mai remembered one night when her unit entered a complex filled with Roller fractus that had assimilated tons of metal scrap. Every charge echoed like a hammer blow in her chest, and the crystal claws sprouting from them slashed through air and steel alike.

The tetanus vaccine cocktail could be just as annoying as cleaning the equipment afterward to remove any rusty metal shards. Although she couldn't deny that messing around with the magnetic cleaning gloves was sometimes fun, even though it was forbidden to use them for betting on fights in the barracks.

One same night, as they checked the wounded and secured the perimeter, Mai felt an unexpected relief when she spotted Nikolai resting beside the fire, breathing evenly—and for the first time since weeks, she saw him smile faintly again. She suspected that Nikolai and Soo-ah had something going on, since they were always together, and she was able to confirm it with her own senses a few days later.

Mai was inspecting the forest perimeter one night eating chocolate, when she noticed movement between the trees. At first she thought it might be an animal or, worse, a stray small fractus—but it only took a second to understand what she was seeing: Nikolai, in his lupine form, and Soo-ah leaning back against a snow-dusted trunk. She welcomed him with a low, breathy laugh, barely contained as soft moans escaped her lips.

There was desire there, yes—but also a strange kind of tenderness, the kind soft warmth the war could not entirely extinguish.

Mai stepped back, cheeks burning with an odd, old-fashioned sense of modesty. She hid behind a birch tree and continued her patrol as if she had seen nothing at all. She only hoped Nikolai wouldn’t start howling or anything of the sort. Sasha definitely didn’t need more surprises that day. When she came back from her patrol, she had a little blood coming from her nose, but she blamed it on the chocolate.

Sasha and Natalya looked at her suspiciously but didn't ask any further questions.

***

The following months became a mosaic of clashes: ambushes along transport routes, quick incursions to shut down fractus hives, rescue missions to evacuate civilians trapped in chaos, and containment operations where the fractus seemed to multiply by pure instinct. Even if each mission strengthened the bonds within the squad, so was the danger.

Mai discovered she could sense when Ludovic hesitated before giving an order, could predict Soo-ah’s joke seconds before she spoke it, and could tell Natalya was coordinating flanking maneuvers the moment she bit her own tail—seriously, what was that behavior supposed to mean?

Even the temporary replacements were absorbed into the group’s dynamic with surprising speed, though the memory of those who fell never faded. Every name, every silly joke, every shared meal amid fire and chaos etched itself into Mai’s consciousness.

In July, after an especially brutal fight on the outskirts of Krasnoyarsk, Mai sat alone on the ice, watching smoke coil upward into the gray sky. Her hands were covered in burn ointments from her overheated weapon, and her clothes smelled of metal and melted polymers. It had been a sudden equipment malfunction, but that sort of thing could happen. If it had been systematic, it would have been a rather rare error, and she was relieved that the helmet had a special antistatic coating; otherwise, she would have gone bald if the problem had spread to the exoskeleton’s nervous system, which was connected to the helmet.

While Eun-ji was treating her, she had realized that the squadron’s doctor was almost like a mother to the team. Even though Mai might have been the oldest there, she saw her as a girl who had been given a weapon and told to do her best on the front lines.

It might have been a misguided feeling, but given Eun-ji’s history, it might also have been a source of comfort that she had lost her family at the start of the war.

Not a replacement. That was clear from the fact that every day she prayed for the souls of her family. But at least the squadron had given her a reason to keep going.

She thought of every loss, every victory, every gesture that had turned that year of war into something more than a combat log: it was a testament to connection—a record of how they had learned to trust not just one another’s skills, but each other’s hearts.

Some might have thought it was nice, but that didn't change the fact that they were in a conflict. Those moments of relaxation were just a temporary distraction—though sometimes a necessary one—from that never-ending war.

How much longer could it last? Would it ever end?

They, along with hundred of thousand military units, were drawing closer and closer to the central core, but even if they managed to finally build a full encirclement around the central anomaly… would that really end everything happening around it?

The atomic charges dropped years earlier had only reshaped the terrain. The sphere—the ten-kilometer-wide gateway—remained protected by fractus pouring out through land, sky, and the rivers themselves.

***

In August 2099, Stepnye Volki advanced through the vast stretches of Siberia, closing in on the epicenter of the distortion near Vanavara. In Irkutsk—a city now converted into a fortress after their mission months earlier—the squad received a critical assignment. A network of automated defenses and geothermal power systems was under attack by a horde of Shrikes and Xe-res which, as always, reassembled and multiplied the moment they were hit .

The two types no one ever wanted to face.

Mai didn’t admit it out loud, but every time she heard that sound in the past—like ice cracking inside the air, or metal bending from within—her throat tightened. It was the announcement of the Xe-res slipping between layers of fractured geometry, and the Shrikes stalking from impossible angles in the sky before revealing themselves. Even for someone like her, who had witnessed more battles born and buried than she could recall without trembling, that sound carried something like a verdict—an omen.

As they approached the geothermal generators, the distortion became visible across the landscape like an open wound. The creatures were truly terraforming the terrain, and the team had to wear oxygen masks. Columns of air vibrated, bending in incompatible directions and creating pressure vortexs. Some portions of the environment were anomalous, refusing to cast shadows even under clear sunlight. As if the rocks, plants, and early snowfalls couldn't agree on the correct way to exist in that place.

Mai felt that familiar tremor run down her spine—as through her blood recognized that they were the ones crossing into territory that no longer obeyed the laws of the Earth.

Sasha walked a few steps ahead, shoulders tense, jaw clenched. Even he, who always kept a level head, looked at the warped landscape with something close to reverence. Soo-ah checked the combat drones with stubborn concentration, trying—and failing—to hide the tremor that shook her fingers every so often. Nikolai and Natalya sometimes took their mask off and sniffed the air, restless, as if whatever was coming carried a scent only they could detect.

Eun-ji walked in silence, her eyes as still as a lake before a storm, holding a serenity that Mai knew was far more fragile than it appeared. The soldiers in each of the other units said nothing, but their exchanged glances spoke for them.

The first Shrikes, in varying sizes, appeared like stings across the sky—filaments stretching and contracting as they dove with aerodynamic shapes, leaving trails of fractured mist behind them. Mai raised her rifle sight, but the target constantly warped: the angles didn’t match, the distances shifted. It was like trying to aim inside a shattered mirror. When the first Shrike swept past, the air split with a visceral shriek, the ground trembled, snow lifted in a dirty spiral, and reality itself seemed to flinch out of the way.

Shrikes earned their name from the bird, but the resemblance ended there. These fractus, like many others, sought out energy centers or telluric forces, but they destroyed anything in their path. If some unfortunate soul was even brushed by one, the body erupted with transparent spines that tore it apart. Moments later, the spines would retract, absorbing the victim. The body would then reappear impaled on outer spines along the creature’s surface—a kind of teleportation, but in the worst sense imaginable. After a few moments, the Shrike would absorb the body again, this time inward, without visibly changing its size. One could only assume the corpse was being sent into some higher-dimensional cavity within the creature. That way, there wasn't even a single fragment left to return to the families.

Shrikes attempted to grotesquely imitate local fauna but never quite succeeded. Their “skin” resembled a skeletal mesh of nodes rather than flesh. Though capable of transport or aerial dives, they could also stretch parts of their bodies across the terrain like tentacles tipped with pincers, probing the environment.

Then came the Xe-res, crawling out of spatial fissures like geometric shadows. They didn’t advance—they displaced the world beneath them. Each movement made the landscape tilt in ways that defied comprehension. According to scientists and analysts, it wasn't the usual Fractus-type pattern, it seemed that this was due to the area they were targeting at that moment. Mai sensed that the geothermal citadel wouldn’t hold if the swarm completed its movement pattern.

The only option was to cut through the chaos and destroy them before they fused into a single entity with the terrain. A plan so reckless it felt pulled straight from Sasha’s dark humor… yet it was real.

As the teams deployed, Mai felt that strange inner heat that always rose before a decisive battle. The wind bit at her face, the sky screamed under the Shrikes’ dives, and the sky folded over itself as if trying to swallow the light when they appear in the middle of the air.

Under the gray sky, Mai felt the air vibrate with that bitter feel on her antennae that only meant one thing: fractus in mass.

Each unit stood ready—knuckles white around weapons that crackled like portable storms. Their task was to defend one of the geothermal entrances, now unprotected after the security systems had been destroyed by a fractus the night before. There was no time for replacements, and all that remained were the reinforcements that the teams had set up to strengthen the towers.

The first Shrike that tried to ambush them burst from the blizzard with that metallic scream that always crawled down her spine—a reminder that no number of missions could make her immune to that sound. To her senses, it was like dragging nails across an ancient chalkboard.

Far off, the drone support units were already engaged alongside and they quickly opened fire at something lurking among the twisted trees—trees bent at angles no forest should possess.

The Xe-res followed soon after: bodies of impossible geometry folding over themselves, and unfolding like deranged origami.

The battle began with an almost musical clarity.

Mai raised her rifle, the recoil was minimal, the blue discharge perfect. The first Xe-res imploded inward, as if sucked into a tiny singularity, leaving its fractium core crackling on the snow.

To her left other team coordinated the electro-artillery:

[Fan fire, maintain distance!]

The reinforced automated towers of the place spewed electric blasts that lit the fortified city like a joyless festival. Shrikes dove from the sky and ruined buildings, multiplying as they advanced, but the electric beams tore them apart before they could reform.

Each unit moved as if they were a single creature protecting the objective. Like ants or bees protecting a colony:

The thought, “Who are the bugs now?” crossed many minds, even though no one said it out loud at the time.

Stepnye Volki charged toward a cluster of Xe-res threatening to break through to the underground heat conduits. The fractus had learned to prioritize critical infrastructure, as if guided by a shared intelligence while looking for the heat signals. Other defenses were still holding, but this was the point where a retreat could turn into a breach. Thankfully, the drones were invaluable—tireless little angels of death with fat asses to deal with the cooling. They needed no recharging, only the heat of the surrounding air to power their flight and plasma discharges. They all prayed none of them malfunctioned if the fight dragged on too long.

A Shrike slammed into Mai with the fury of a beast pushed past desperation. The debris around struck her, but at the same time saved her from being touched by the creature. Its filaments vibrated as they struggled to reconstitute, but she jammed her electric knife into the creature’s illogical shoulder joint—or what she decided qualified as a shoulder. She had never been this close to one. The blade struck home, and the shrike recoiled violently. Mai jumped back and fired, though the impact threw her body off-balance, her exoskeleton whining as it tried to compensate. She’d taken some damage from the collision, no question, but at least she wasn’t skewered on its spines.

The creature burst in a blink of black light, spraying droplets of fractium that tinkled across her armor. The core lay on the ground—jet-black teardrops like obsidian, almost resembling jewelry.

[Mai, still alive?!] Nikolai’s voice crackled through static, buried beneath snarls and detonations.

“Disappointingly alive,” she answered, hearing the werewolf’s rough laughter even through the chaos.

A few meters away, the squad huddled behind improvised cover, firing relentlessly. Soo-ah, her hands fast and almost elegant, slapped a new condenser into place and tossed it toward one of the advancing fey teams moving to seal the breach. From her vantage point, Mai saw a larger Xe-res—tall, warped, almost luminous—attempt to split itself into three simultaneous shadows.

[Cannon 6, ready!] someone shouted.

The discharge fell like a caged thunderclap. The Xe-res shrank, folded inside-out, and vanished, leaving behind a dense core pulsing with violet light.

The operation lasted forty-two minutes.

Forty-two goddamn eternities.

When the final Shrike dissolved, the snow was stained with hundreds of tiny black and purple shards, as if the battlefield had been sown with shattered stars. Although Soo-ah compared it to a choco-chip slushie and Ludovic compared it to Natalya's tail, she didn't find it funny.

Mai lowered her rifle, feeling the weight of the silence that always followed the slaughter: a silence that wasn’t peace, but a kind of debt.

Some were injured. Others missing.

She felt it first, even before they said the name.

The hollow always arrives like that: a soft, almost polite knock before driving itself in.

Mai breathed in deeply, letting the cold wrap around her again.

It was August, 2099. And there was still so much left to go… and so much left to protect.

But it was her place: to walk toward the geometry trying to devour everything, her heart steadied by those who marched beside her.

Even if they were only shadows of humans and feys surviving in a world that no longer respected any known form of war.

At this stage, missions became more complex, and the space-time distortions grew stronger. The team, understood that the key wasn’t only to fight, but to manage their tactics with precision—to remain efficient without burning out.

Sasha decided to launch a joint operation with several nearby units. The Stepnye Volki squad allied with Unitas, a team specializing in plasma weaponry and advanced drones. Joining them as well was Raven, an espionage-and-sabotage squad that had been dealing with fractium-core trafficking cases until recently, now returning to the front lines against the creatures.

These alliances weren’t uncommon, but frictions persisted—each squad clinging to its own protocols and philosophies of war. Because, really, what better time to reflect on the meaning of existence than during that war? After hundreds of thousands of years of fighting with sticks, stones, and and some magic and abilities, what better situation than that one, where something completely unknown was finally trying to take over the world?

Still, the situation was dire enough that cooperation was mandatory, even when personalities clashed. This region held the worst threats due to the anomaly, and the AIs coordinating the defense needed peak efficiency from units hardened by long exposure to the terrain. To put them to the test, the aeons sent them to recapture a small village that had been taken months earlier and had previously been a Russian army stronghold.

Raven infiltrated the enemy base using invisible drones to weave a network of interference communications, scattering a type of fractium particles that jammed the chemical signals the fractus used to coordinate.

Unitas, meanwhile, used their long-range plasma weapons to thin out the Shrike hordes from afar.

Stepnye Volki advanced on foot, using their railguns to eliminate the Xe-res—their bodies constantly shifting shape, trying to disorient anyone who dared shoot at them.

The distortion had reached its peak, and space itself seemed to warp before the squad’s eyes. The Xe-res clustered into a single shape—an immense geometric structure that swallowed the horizon—while the Shrikes launched themselves from the shadows with lethal agility. The sound of their bodies breaking and reforming filled the air, thickening the atmosphere with a suffocating sense of terror.

Nikolai, in his wolf form with a new armor, charged ahead, tearing through Shrikes with inhuman speed. Ludovic and Mai secured the right flank, using their experience and energy weapons to keep the Xe-res at bay. Coordination between the squads was flawless, though the frictions between Raven and Unitas were obvious in their exchanges—orders delivered with a sharper edge than necessary.

After hours of combat, the team finally secured the fortress, but the cost was steep. Sasha and Mai stayed behind to disable small fractus that came along the big ones, while Nikolai and Ludovic performed a final sweep for remaining Xe-res. Yet the conflict wasn’t merely physical, members of Unitas and Raven were already arguing over how effective the collaboration had actually been. And although it wasn’t the time for internal fights, tensions surfaced with predictable speed.

That night, in an improvised camp, Stepnye Volki and the other assembled squads shared stories and mission evaluations. The tensions didn’t fade, but there was a quiet, unspoken understanding that the situation was far too dire—any disagreement would have to wait until after the war.
