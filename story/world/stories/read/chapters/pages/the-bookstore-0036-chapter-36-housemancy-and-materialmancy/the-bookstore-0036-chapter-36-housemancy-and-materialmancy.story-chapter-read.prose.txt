To ease the heaviness hanging over the study room, Anne invited Dorothy into the kitchen to help prepare food.

The dwarf accepted immediately, and the moment she stepped inside, her eyes caught on the array-work. The room had already become one more example of the bookstore’s strange internal logic: a large old building filled with improvised furniture and careful patchwork, yet fitted here and there with magic too refined for such humble surroundings. A powerful, elegant being spent her time cleaning floors. A young woman ran the place with the composure of someone far older. Now even the kitchen held enchantments laid over rough stone and weathered wood.

Dorothy clearly noticed the contradiction.

Anne noticed Dorothy noticing it and chose, for now, not to explain anything.

She was still not comfortable exposing the more creative stretches of her craft unless necessity demanded it. So instead of inviting questions, she simply gathered what they needed: some resting dough, the last portion of stored meat, and a basket holding parsnips and longleaves. Then she began setting the ingredients in order.

Dorothy did not remain still for long. A kitchen, enchanted or not, was evidently a place where she felt at ease. She took over the meat without asking, portioned it neatly, and began seasoning it with practiced hands. After that she prepared a flat stone slab, spread only a trace of oil across it, and moved toward the stove.

Anne relaxed a little when she saw the choice. Earth-essence meat was better grilled, and someone who understood that without discussion already earned a great deal of respect in her eyes. She was almost ready to leave the entire matter to Dorothy when she noticed the woman standing in front of the stove with narrowed attention.

“Oh,” Anne said, slightly embarrassed, “I forgot to mention it. The stove array still lacks a proper fire core. The trio brought one back, but I have not had the chance to install and test it.”

“I see,” Dorothy said. “I was wondering how you intended it to work.”

Then she snapped her fingers and muttered something under her breath. A few tiny sparks leapt into the kindling and caught.

Anne nearly stepped back.

“Housemancy?” she asked before she could stop herself.

Dorothy did not even look up from the stove.

“Nothing so grand,” she said. “Just a few tricks I picked up for cooking. My actual craft is materialmancy.”

The wood caught properly then. A soft heat began to rise. A moment later the stone slab started to hiss faintly, announcing its readiness for the meat.

Trick or not, Anne thought, it was still housemancy.

She would not hold that against the woman. The craft was not fashionable unless someone wished to spend their life managing a noble household, and Anne could easily imagine small-minded people using it to dismiss Dorothy’s worth as a materialmancer. That sort of stupidity had no place in her bookstore.

In truth, Anne admired housemancy more than most. Had it not sat so awkwardly from bookmancy’s natural structure, she might have tried to learn several spells from it and perhaps even create one herself. It was not an inherited or naturally aligned power like so many other crafts. It had been built from the margins of them, from cleverness, observation, and the stubborn refusal to keep doing household labor the hard way if magic could do it better.

Dorothy’s spark was a fine example. A pyromancer could have lit the stove more elegantly, certainly. But housemancy did not ask for affinity or that degree of control. Somewhere in the past, some practical sorcerer had built a tiny bit of arcanistry around friction and placement, turning a problem into a trick. The challenge was not power but precision—creating the spark in the right place and at the right moment.

That pleased Anne deeply. Even better, housemancy could often be learned beside another craft without demanding the same kind of sacrifice or focus. Materialmancy, though less mocked, was not especially fashionable either. It required keen observation and an understanding of matter that many people underestimated because the work looked passive from the outside. Yet guilds across the continent could not function without appraisers. A materialmancer could feel quality, tier, and sometimes deeper properties in what passed through their hands. Loot, goods, weapons, stones—someone had to know what they truly were.

Dorothy laid the seasoned meat on the slab, letting it sizzle. Then she turned to the broth as if the kitchen already belonged to her. Anne watched the swift efficiency of her movements while shaping the dough and cutting the vegetables. A thought came to her then—bright, dangerous, and immediately attractive. She pushed it aside just as quickly.

Matters concerning the bookstore could never be decided on first impulse, however tempting the impulse might be. So they cooked.

Dorothy focused on the meal, as if trying to repay kindness through competence. Anne moved beside her, partly helping and partly thinking. The room filled with useful sounds: the soft crackle of flame, the hiss of meat, the knife working through parsnip, the quiet thud of dough being shaped for bread.

By the time they finished, nearly an hour had passed. They carried broth, bread, and meat back into the study room.

Alanis had finished bathing and now sat with the trio in subdued silence. She looked cleaner, calmer, and somehow more fragile. The distress remained clear, but without the road’s grime and the alley’s panic clinging to her, she looked younger. More guilty too. The borrowed clothes only deepened that feeling. The tall half-elf lowered her face even further when Anne came in, clearly aware that she was sitting in another woman’s garments while bringing trouble into another woman’s house.

“I suspect all of you are hungry,” Anne said, setting the food down before any of them could begin apologizing for existing. “So let us solve that first.”

The others did not object. There was a little awkwardness in the beginning, of course. The trio were used to Anne’s tea and books, not to being fed. Alanis seemed uncertain whether she even had the right to touch the food. Dorothy, at least, knew good sense when she saw it and began serving portions before anyone could make the matter more complicated.

After that, silence gave way to appetite. It was a simple meal, but a good one. The broth carried warmth into the bones. The essence meat had been handled well enough to show its richness without becoming heavy, and the bread made from Spring’s blessed wheat gave the whole thing a quiet magnificence no one at the table had expected. Even Anne, who knew the ingredients, found herself enjoying the result with fresh appreciation.

Dorothy, she decided, truly was excellent.

The food worked small wonders. It eased the group’s exhaustion enough that they could feel the full extent of it. It softened the ache in their bodies, which only made clear how much rest they truly needed. The trio, who still had not properly washed, looked half-dead now that they were no longer running on fear and urgency. Alanis seemed as though she might fall asleep and burst into tears in the same moment.

And beneath all of that lay the real problem. The two women had nowhere safe to go.

Without adventurer cards, Dorothy and Alanis could not rely on the usual cheap lodging or easy tolerance extended to working travelers. More importantly, remaining anywhere too near Riverend was dangerous. Mountainend did not trade much with that town, but news still traveled through.

The sensible choice would be to leave for another region entirely. But thaat required coin that they clearly did not have. Anne had noticed the problem when the five of them entered the bookstore. She had simply delayed deciding whether to interfere.

Helping would be in character. More than that, it might even fit the bookstore’s growing needs. But if her first assessment of Dorothy and Alanis proved wrong, the cost of extending her hand could become painfully high.

Follow your intuition, her grandfather would have said. Then, naturally, he would have ruined the advice by adding that intuition was useless when it broke their rules.

Anne had always disliked how much of life fit into the grey area when it came to such warnings.

She drew a breath and made her choice. Then she looked at Dorothy and Alanis directly, no longer pretending her attention was elsewhere.

“Would you two like to stay here for a while,” she asked, “until you decide your next move?”

The room went still. Anne smiled before either woman could refuse out of reflex.

“I have the space,” she continued. “I could also use the company. And the cooking.”

That last part lightened the air just enough. Since she had decided to help, she saw no point in doing it timidly.
