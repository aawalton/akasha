Training the songs was different than thrusting the spear all day. It demanded total and complete devotion. Even Gary, who was used to spending every waking moment training, started feeling the fatigue. His mind wandered as the days passed. He caught himself distracted. Getting up in the morning felt heavier.

He would have loved to take a break, but his life was on the line.

Leaving the arena on the seventh day—he still had no losses—he could still hear the cheers of his nickname, but it rang heavy in his ears. He was tired. So, so tired. At the same time, his body was full of energy.

The main street led to the clan. It bustled with carriages, carts, mounts, pedestrians, strong and weaker individuals. It was a tapestry of aura in all colors of the rainbow. Suddenly, even the thought of crossing it filled Gary with exhaustion. He entered a side street. The crowd was thinner here. Turn after turn, Gary dove into less populous districts, until the street held only the occasional passer-by or playing child. He looked at the sun to orient himself, always heading north—the direction of the clan.

There was a certain romanticism to an empty street mid-day—to overflowing energy with no clear expression. Clamor rose from everywhere in the city, reaching Gary’s ears like the crickets of summer, yet the scene he saw was barren. Asleep. Every person who crossed his path felt intimate, a real presence in Gary’s world. He looked at them. He felt an almost intimate connection, far different to the total indifference with which he’d treat a passer-by in a busier street. There was no crowd, only people.

“Hello,” he said. An old lady dipped her head with a smile.

A ball rolled to Gary’s feet. It was made of wrapped leather, mismatched in its shape and distribution, yet a ball nonetheless. Two children waved at him from an alley.

Gary raised his foot to kick the ball, then was struck by an existential divide so massive it rocked his sense of identity. He’d almost forgotten about the before. The world that died. He’d been a child on Earth, once. He’d played ball on the streets, laughed with friends, fought another boy and had his nose broken, giggled with a girl he liked. She used to have twin buns. Nick, his brother, had beaten up the boy who beat Gary. He’d also gotten the girl.

Gary stared at the ball. He wore robes now. Carried a spear and wore soft shoes. How long had it been?

How had he changed?

“Big brother! The ball!” a child called out.

Gary looked up at them. He grinned. He picked up the ball with his feet, bouncing it thrice, then kicked it at them. It slammed into a wall harder than intended. The boys ogled for a moment. Gary hesitated. They cheered. He laughed.

On the alley’s stone walls, a window opened up. “How many times must I tell you to stop kicking that damn ball?” a woman said, reaching half her body out the window with a plaster in hand. She followed their gaze to Gary. Her eyes turned sheepish.

“A thousand apologies, great climber! This lowly one didn’t see you! If my children offended you, please take my life instead!”

“It’s fine,” Gary replied. “You should let your children play. It’s good for them.”

“Of course, great climber,” the woman said, bowing deeply. Gary waved at the children as he walked away.

Then again, what do I know? I’m new to this world. How could I know those children better than their mother?

“That was cool! Were you poor in your world?” Goldie asked, manifesting next to Gary’s head.

“I wasn’t poor, but I wasn’t rich either,” he replied absent-mindedly.

“You look poor.”

“How do I look poor?”

“You’re a bit simple. I like that, though. It’s part of your tough guy charisma.”

“I don’t have tough guy charisma.”

“Yes, you do! That’s why all the girls like you!”

Gary rolled his eyes even as he chuckled. “Do you think she’ll take my advice?” he asked.

“Who?”

“The mother.”

“She might. You’re a climber. You know stuff.”

“About the spear, maybe. Not about raising children.”

“Wisdom is communicating vessels.”

“What’s that supposed to mean?”

“How should I know? I’m just a snake.”

Gary shook his head. “Is this the temptation of power?” he asked. “I shouldn’t speak just because I can.”

“If you can, you should.”

“I don’t think so.”

“It’s the weight of power, Gary. People will look at you to speak. Your silence will rattle them. A bad answer can be better than no answer at all.”

“Maybe someone else has the answer.”

“People don’t need answers. They need certainty. The strongest person must speak, and if they lack wisdom, they should find someone wiser to whisper in their ear.”

Gary turned the words over in his head. He was into the alleys now, a weaving maze he followed ever north. Stone walls alternated with clay ones, then wooden. Bricks appeared. The back of a skyscraper buried among huts. In a corner, Gary saw a light wooden door. It invited him, somehow. The sign above it read, “The Placating Place,” and the calmness it promised was exactly what he needed right now.

The door chimed pleasantly. He scanned the small place, five tables spread in a shop the size of his cabin. Three were empty. One held a young couple whispering sweet nothings. On the final table sat a familiar face.

“Hey!” Gary said.

Lin Xin’s face fell. She let go of her straw. “What are you doing here?”

“Believe it or not, I just wandered in.”

“Really?”

“Really.”

“It’s true. Hi, Lin Xin!” Goldie said. “That milkshake looks delicious! Can I try?”

Gary was stunned by the sight of the ever-cold Lin Xin sipping on a chocolate milkshake. Her slender form rested on the chair like it belonged to her, with her long legs crossed under the table. A book lay next to her, where she’d placed it when Gary entered.

Her irritated gaze contained a hint of embarrassment. Goldie dove for the straw. Gary grabbed her tail and pulled her back. “Don’t steal people’s drinks,” he said.

“But she didn’t reply!”

“That’s a no, Goldie.”

“Says who?”

“Says common sense.”

“Well, I say that Lin Xin and I are friends, and friends share their milkshakes,” Goldie declared.

The couple, along with the only waiter present, glanced at Gary and Goldie with mirth. The girl covered her mouth and chuckled.

Gary was aware he must have looked ridiculous, arguing with a flying snake in a cafe, but then again, so what? “What if I get you your own milkshake?” he asked Goldie.

Her black eyes widened. “You’d do that?”

“Of course.”

“Thank you, Gary! You’re the best!”

She span around herself in joy. Gary eyed the table farthest from Lin Xin, then Lin Xin herself. They crossed uncertain gazes. Gary turned away.

“Hey, where are you going? Her table is right there!” Goldie said.

Gary scratched his head. “Only if it’s fine with you,” he said.

“If it’s fate,” Lin Xin replied.

He pulled a chair back while Goldie coiled on the table between the two. “So,” Gary said after a while.

“So,” Lin Xin replied.

“Anxious?”

“Not really.”

“For the tournament, I mean.”

“I know.”

There was a bout of silence. Lin Xin sipped on her milkshake—the sucking sound seemed deafening.

“How can I help you?” the waiter asked. It was a man in his late twenties, confident in the face of Gary’s cultivation. He, too, was at Birth of Aura. The couple at the other table were also cultivators.

“A chocolate milkshake,” Gary said.

“I suppose that’s for your snake?” the man asked, good-natured teasing clear in his voice.

Goldie harrumphed before Gary could reply. “There are two kinds of people in this world. The people who do things, and the people who laugh with the people who do things. If Gary wants to buy me a milkshake, what’s your problem?”

“No problem at all. One milkshake coming up. Anything for you, sir?”

“Just water,” Gary replied. “Actually, make it another milkshake. Why not?”

“Of course,” the waiter said with a tone of professional discouragement. Gary frowned at his departing back.

“You do know this place is expensive,” Lin Xin said.

“It’s a milkshake. How expensive could it be?”

“This is a climber shop. They drive up their prices so common people can’t afford it.”

“Oh. I hate that, actually, but it’s fine for today. I came across some money.”

“As people do,” she replied.

The milkshakes arrived quickly. Gary tried his and was pleasantly surprised. He ignored the existential confusion to stare at Goldie, who was sniffing her straw. “Aren’t you going to drink it?” he asked.

“Drink it?”

“Yeah.”

“I can’t drink it, Gary. I’m incorporeal.”

His gaze fell. The waiter’s doubt suddenly made sense. “Then, why did you want Lin Xin’s milkshake?”

“To bond with her!”

A peal of laughter escaped Lin Xin. “I apologize,” she said.

“You knew,” Gary said.

“Of course I knew.”

“And you let me buy her a milkshake.”

“I thought it was a warm symbolic gesture.”

“Bullshit.”

“And, I wanted to see how long it would take you to figure it out.”

He rolled his eyes, sipping on his milkshake. “I haven’t seen you since the expedition,” he said.

“It’s busy times.”

“Tell me about it.”

“Do I need to?”

“You never do. Do you think Sui Duang will kill me?”

“Yes,” she replied.

“Really? No hesitation at all?”

“I’ve already considered it, Gary Steel. I know my answer. Your chances aren’t zero, but they’re low.”

“Hmm.” He leaned onto the table. “Do you think we can beat Feng Shen?”

This time, she really did burst out laughing. “We could team up, Gary, and we’d still fall short.”

“I thought you were a genius.”

“I’ll catch up eventually. Probably. Climbing a mountain takes time.”

“We have a saying on my planet. Shoot for the moon; even if you miss, you’ll land among the stars.”

Lin Xin circled her straw in the milkshake. She watched him. “My grandfather warned me to stay away from you,” she said. “He says you carry disastrous fate, and that even acquaintances can be affected.”

“Well then, thank God we aren’t acquaintances.”

She fought to keep her grin down. “You’re the most boyish cultivator I’ve ever talked with.”

“But you have talked with me.”

“Unfortunately.”

“Your smile says otherwise.”

“It’s a smile of ridicule.”

“I’m sure it is. You don’t seem like the type to ignore your grandfather’s advice.”

“Why’s that?”

“You seem obedient.”

She frowned. “Nobody controls me, Gary Steel. Plus, I didn’t choose this. You just sat at my table.”

“You invited me.”

“I did not.”

“Well, you didn’t say no.”

“Is it too late to say it now?”

“Way too late.”

She shook her head. “You vex me, Gary Steel.”

“Thanks. You’re just so uptight I can’t help but tease you a little.”

“There is a thin line between teasing and offending.”

“That’s exactly the point.” He leaned back, picking up the milkshake. “I’m worried about the invasion,” he said.

“Because of the puppeteer?”

He nodded. Looking around, he leaned in again. “I don’t know if I can join. If I do, I will need to be on the Gymnasium’s side, but that might pit me against the clan… Do you know which side they’ll choose?”

“They’re still discussing it. But, I know for sure that they’ll forbid us from picking the opposite side.”

“I don’t have a choice.”

“The clan doesn’t care, Gary. Our disciples fighting each other and creating enmities benefits no one.”

He sighed. The milkshake rested empty on the table.

“Don’t join the event,” Lin Xin said.

“Because of the puppeteer?”

“It’s too dangerous. The leader of the invasion will be hunting you down, and you’re nothing to an army. They’ll find you, they’ll kill you, and there will be nothing you can do about it.”

Gary snorted. “They can try.”

“I mean it, Gary. Even if the clan sides with the Gymnasium, it will be too dangerous. People from different armies will still be able to talk to each other, so tracking your location will be easy. If they send a sector boss to hunt you down, what will you do?”

“I’ll fight it.”

“And die pointlessly.”

“I fought a sector boss during the expedition.”

“They won’t be capped at Birth of Aura anymore. The fourth floor has Core Formation monsters. We’ll be small fries.”

“I can play it safe.”

“Sure, you can choose the easiest missions deep in our territory, but then what’s the point of even joining?” She shrugged. “It’s your funeral, Gary. I’m just stating the facts.”

“Excuse me? My funeral? If you don’t give a shit, then why are you speaking?”

“I’m trying to help, asshole.”

“But you achieve the opposite. Excuse me, how much do I owe you?” Gary asked the waiter.

“It’s five aura stones per milkshake, sir.”

Gary shook his head at the sky-high price, but thankfully, he was rich now. He had thousands of aura stones. He left twenty on the table and rose. “Let’s go, Goldie. Everyone wants us down, and we can’t give them the satisfaction.”

“That’s a weird way to say it but okay!” She floated up. “Bye, Lin Xin!”

“Bye,” Lin Xin replied.

Gary stormed out of the cafe. He was hurt but also rested. Both pointed at the same thing: grueling, endless, necessary training.
