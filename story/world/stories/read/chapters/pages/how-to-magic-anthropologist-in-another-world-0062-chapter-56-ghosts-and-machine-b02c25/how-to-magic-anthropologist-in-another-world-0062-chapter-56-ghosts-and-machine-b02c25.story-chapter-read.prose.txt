Most of the buildings were half-standing at best. Stone walls were cracked and leaning, wooden roofs collapsed inward, and beams blackened by old fire. Whatever had happened here hadn’t been a single event. The place looked like it had been a ruin for thousands of years.

Worse than that, some kind of tall, wheat-like plant had overrun the entire settlement.

The stalks were nearly chest-high, pale gold with thick, fibrous stems and heavy seed heads that swayed evenly in the wind. “This is terrible terrain,” Katar said, pushing one stalk aside with the tip of his sword. “You could hide a whole army in here.”

Raik raised a hand, signaling us to stop.

We froze instantly.

He crouched, brushing aside the wheat with two fingers. They had some kind of dark grains inside them.

“Ja’a, are there any soul abnormalities?” he asked.

Nothing was living there.

There weren’t even skeletons, undead, rats, insects, or even the usual signs of rot. The buildings stood empty, hollow shells picked clean by the ravages of time. There were no wooden doors, furniture, cloth, or leather. Nothing organic, only stone and metal remained.

That alone was unsettling.

I stopped in front of one of the larger structures and stared at the wall. Something about it felt wrong. I reached out and touched the surface.

It was uniformly gray.

A nearby support pillar caught my attention. The outer layer had cracked, exposing metal rods embedded deep inside its core, rusted but still intact.

“I thought this was stone fused together using an earth bloodline power,” Raik said, crouching near the pillar.

Vena stepped closer, studying the wall with new interest. “So you’re saying this three-thousand-year-old village used some kind of advanced building technique?” she asked. She was the only one here who knew I wasn’t from the Seven Realms, and her tone reflected that.

“It can’t be that secret if Alice knows about it,” Ja’a said dismissively.

“I only know it exists,” I shot back. “Theoretically. It’s not like I can make it or anything.”

We continued deeper into the village, Katar cutting a narrow path through the wheat ahead of us.

The stalks grew denser the farther west we went, tall enough that they brushed my shoulders and obscured anything beyond a few meters. I kept my Stormshark’s Whisper Soulbook active in short bursts, just enough to give me an early warning if something tried to ambush us.

That was how I noticed it.

Something solid broke the uniform sway of the wheat.

“What is that?” I said, raising a hand.

Everyone froze.

From where I stood, it looked like a dull metallic shape half-buried among the stalks. Roughly cube-shaped, but not perfectly so, its surface caught the light.

Raik looked at Ja’a. “Is that thing alive? A golem, perhaps?” he asked.

“If it is, it has been dead for a long time,” she replied. “I can’t detect a soul coming from that thing.”

We approached carefully.

Up close, it was clear that it wasn’t a solid block.

The outer shell was metal, pale and slightly oxidized, but thin enough that it didn’t feel indestructible. Panels overlapped each other like plates, and through narrow seams I could see darkness inside. Gears, maybe, something mechanical, I thought.

“It’s hollow,” Katar said, tapping it lightly with the flat of his sword.

“Careful,” Raik warned. “If it’s metal, it could be enchanted.”

On one side, partially hidden by the wheat, there was a lever. It was thick, reinforced, and connected to an internal mechanism. When I nudged it experimentally, it resisted, then shifted slightly with a dull click.

“That feels like a winding lever,” I murmured, more to myself than to anyone else.

“Winding what?” Calr asked.

“I don’t know yet.”

I pressed my shoulder against the shell to get a better angle, and the metal gave slightly under the pressure. Not much, but enough to leave a small dent.

The reaction was immediate.

Five figures rose from a nearby structure, drifting up through the collapsed stone wall as if it wasn’t there at all.

Ghosts.

They were humanoid in shape, translucent and pale, their outlines flickering in and out of reality. They looked like factory workers in gray uniforms, and I could see they had blood-red hair even when transparent.

My eyes shifted toward Calr’s hair for a second before turning back toward the new threat.

Their faces were indistinct, but I could feel their attention lock onto us the moment they appeared.

“Back,” Raik ordered sharply.

We retreated several steps, weapons raised and magic ready.

But the ghosts didn’t attack.

They drifted past us, completely ignoring the team, and gathered around the dented metal shell. One of them reached out with a hammer and lightly tapped near the dent, slowly smoothing it out, the metal flowing back into shape.

When the shell looked whole again, the ghosts faded, dissolving into nothing, and silence returned.

“…That’s weird," Ja’a muttered. “They’re gone. I can’t detect them anymore.”

We waited for a minute, just in case, but nothing else happened.

“They reacted to the damage,” Vena said quietly. “Not to us.”

Cautiously, we approached again.

This time, no one touched the shell directly. I circled it, studying the seams. I noticed wheels and some kind of circular saw-like component.

“This isn’t a weapon,” I said slowly.

I grasped the lever and tried to crank it.

It moved stiffly, but whatever mechanism it powered failed after a partial turn, the resistance going slack in a way that felt wrong.

The ghosts appeared again.

This time, their forms were sharper and more defined.

Their eyes burned red the moment they saw me at the lever.

“Back,” Raik said again, more urgently.

I stepped away.

The ghosts surged forward, not at us, but at the machine. One of them reached inside one of the panels, checking the gears. After a few moments, they faded again.

“I don’t think they’re guarding it,” I said. “They’re maintaining it.”

We approached a third time, slowly.

“Can I try something reckless?” I asked, looking at Raik.

“How strong are the ghosts?” he asked Ja’a.

“Not too strong,” she replied. “Around twelve SB each.”

Two gears near the lever weren’t connected.

They should have been.

The ghosts appeared again.

This time, they didn’t ignore me.

Their red eyes locked onto my hands.

The air went cold as the ghost almost reached me.

I backed away immediately, heart pounding.

They moved in, hands passing through the open casing, checking my work. One ghost lingered longer than the others, staring at the leather belt, then at me.

Slowly, they reassembled the shell, slotting the panels back into place as if guided by memory.

When they were done, they withdrew again, dissipating from sight.

I stepped forward once more and gripped the lever.

I cranked it. Once, twice, then three times. It was much heavier than earlier now that the lever was connected to the rest of the apparatus.

The machine came to life.

With a low mechanical hum, internal gears began to turn. Moving slowly forward, blades extended beneath the shell, cutting through the wheat in a straight, efficient line. Chaff and hay were expelled out the back in neat piles, while the grain was nowhere to be seen, likely stored inside.

It was a harvesting machine.

The field began to clear.

I blinked. This made no sense; the amount of force I exerted through the leather isn’t equal to the machine's own output.

“This thing is breaking the law of conservation of energy,” I muttered.

After 30 seconds, the machine began to slow, but it was still more than my miserly 3 crank should have provided.

To my surprise, one of the ghosts appeared beside the machine, took hold of the lever, and cranked it. The machine continued clearing the wheat in a straight line. Each time the mechanism slowed, the ghost would crank again.

They are just doing normal work.

The other ghosts drifted into the field, moving with purpose, gathering the hay into neat piles, repeating motions they must have performed countless times in life.

They were workers, finishing a task that had never been completed.“So we are definitely stealing that device,” said Calr, scratching his head.“That goes without saying,” Ja’a grinned. “The dwarfs would pay its weight in gold.”“Wanna bet that we get attacked if we take it?” asked Calr.“A sucker’s bet,” Ja’a shook her head. “Of course, we will get attacked. I bet there will be more than just the initial five.”“How are we going to take that thing?” asked Kan.“It could fit in my bag of holding if you could carry it up at least half a meter,” Ja’a suggested.“Maybe,” said Raik. “If Shingo is on one side and Kan, Katar, and me on the other, I bet we could lift it long enough for you to slip it into your bag.”“First, let me empty my bag into yours,” Ja’a said. “We don't want my things to get damaged.”We watched the ghosts clear the field as Ja’a emptied her bag.
