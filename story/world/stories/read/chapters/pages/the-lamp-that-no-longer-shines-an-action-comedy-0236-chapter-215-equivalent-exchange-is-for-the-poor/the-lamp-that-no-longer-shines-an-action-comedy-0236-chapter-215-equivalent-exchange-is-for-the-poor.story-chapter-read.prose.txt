[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 42 — 8:40 AM[Location]: The Stratus Layer · Above the High-Altitude Research District

The [Scarlet-Valkyrie] had been wrapped in velvet, laid at the bottom of a spatially expanded trunk, and not discussed since.

Hathaway came down through the cloud deck at a shallow angle with the morning behind her, two enormous articulated wings of matte-black scale holding a lazy glide that cost her nothing, and somewhere below her right wingtip a flock of commuting messenger hawks decided collectively to be elsewhere. The horns swept back from her temples in jagged obsidian, and the passive Spell Penetration bleeding off them made every warded doorway on campus feel like a curtain of wet paper. Deep under her sternum the dragon heart kept its own time, slow and furnace-heavy, idling like industrial plant on a Sunday.

Sunday. Two o'clock. Her scheduling thread produced the reminder with unusual care, because the last time it had failed to, the consequences had been itemized.

Twenty-one days ago the two of them had gone up through a cloud deck and had not come down for Lab 606, and Nino Lucent had not raised her voice about it, or referenced it, or acknowledged that a thing had occurred. She had simply issued a revised schedule with eleven additional hours distributed across it in increments of forty minutes, positioned so that no single one of them could be traded for anything a person might otherwise want to do with a Tuesday.

She had promised the heirloom a month. The month had arrived and been spent, and there was still no number to write down.

What she had instead was a hole with a shape. Somewhere around the eleventh revision the envelope had begun specifying its own contents, and every pass since had sharpened the same absence: a parameter the containment layer required, that the cat was supposed to supply. She still could not fill it. She could, by now, describe its edges to four decimal places.

Which was not a number. It was closer to a question.

She snapped both wings open ten meters off the balcony grating of Lab 606, and the backdraft rattled the observation glass, and she came down on the metal with the heavy percussive finality of ordnance arriving where it had been sent.

The wings folded away into her back and the horns dissolved, and she keyed the airlock and walked in with the flat professional stride of a woman arriving to check the overnight logs.

[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 42 — 9:00 AM[Location]: High-Altitude Research District · Sector 6 · Lab 606

The lab held its usual shivering, ozone-scrubbed cold, and Victoria was already at the secondary terminal with a cascade of raw telemetry scrolling past her gloved fingers.

"Sector Four is nominal," she said, without looking up. "Caloric intake, housing density, working hours, leisure allocation. The ambient baseline has come up one and a half points since deployment, which is the coils paying for their own maintenance." Her hand paused on the slate. "Loss column."

Hathaway leaned over the back of the chair.

The suicide rate inside a two-mile radius of the coil grid had gone up four point two percent against the decadal average. Beneath it sat the rest of the attrition: night terrors, acute paranoia, a small persistent bloom of unprompted cardiac events among the over-fifties.

Node burnout under sustained processing load. Her logic engine filed the shape of it and moved on to the distribution.

She reached past Victoria's shoulder and pulled the demographic overlay.

"It's the ones with the least," she said. "Look at the spread. Single households, no dependents, bottom quintile of leisure allocation. Everyone with something to go home to is metabolizing it fine."

Victoria's pale eyes moved to the graph and stayed there. "Which is consistent."

"A subject in distress produces static. You said that on day one." Hathaway tapped the low end of the curve, where the line dropped away. "So don't touch the hex. Raise the floor. Give the bottom quintile something to lose and the loss column closes on its own."

"Approved," Nino said, from the far end of the lab.

The professor came out of the sterilization chamber looking like a woman who had substituted sleep with grievance and caffeine in a ratio she declined to publish, carrying a star-silver tray in both hands. She set it down on the central bench hard enough to ring.

"Draft the intervention with your paper. Housing subsidy, family formation incentive, whichever prices lower per unit of retained yield." She rubbed the bridge of her nose. "Continue. The refinement problem is solved."

Hathaway and Victoria stepped up to the bench.

The tray held a loose scatter of jagged pitch-black fragments. Light went into that pile and did not come back out. There were perhaps forty pieces on it, the largest the size of a thumbnail, and the cold coming off the steel had a weight to it that made the fine hairs on Hathaway's forearms stand up under her sleeve.

[Nightmare Crystals].

Five weeks of a functioning metropolis sleeping badly, dragged through the extractor every Sunday, boiled down through however many failed filtration passes it had taken, and this was what had survived the process as a solid.

"So we have the fuel," Hathaway said. "What does it run?"

Nino reached into the pocket of her oversized coat and produced an index card.

It was, aggressively, an index card. Unlaminated, unenchanted, ruled in faint blue, filled out by hand in the cramped block capitals of a woman who wrote everything as though it were going into a log.

MALTESE HAMBURGERLove this story? Find the genuine version on the author's preferred platform and support their work!Oversized bun.Three Angus patties, heavy.Tomato ketchup, generous.Melted cheddar.Cucumber, thick-cut.

"Professor," Hathaway said carefully. "Is this a grocery list?"

Nino ignored her, raised her stylus, and drew an alchemical crucible directly onto the air above the bench in three unhurried strokes, laying the interlocking geometry of a Holy Triangle through the middle of it until the whole construction locked with a hard bright hum. She set the index card dead centre, turning it deliberately so that it lay reversed. Then she lifted one fragment off the tray with warded tongs and set it down on top.

Then Nino Lucent, a woman whose entire architecture was built on the rejection of theatre, raised both hands above her head.

Her face went flat, the expression of an employee reciting a corporate slogan at an all-hands, on camera, with a performance review pending.

"Come forth," Nino recited. "Power of nightmares, purified moonlight."

Hathaway's quill stopped somewhere in the vicinity of her own hand.

"Come forth. Spirit and power of the tangible, blood and flesh of the intangible." A muscle moved once at the corner of Nino's eye. "Shape this hamburger. Shape it."

She said hamburger. Hathaway clamped her back teeth together and held her face in a rigidly neutral position that was costing her most of her available processing. She is standing in a certified cleanroom performing an invocation at a sandwich.

"Echo six times, from moon to earth." Nino's hands came down. "Forge this hamburger. Forge it. [Food Summoning Art]."

The array took.

The fragment at the centre inverted in a single frame, the light-eating black flashing over into a pure and blinding moon-white, and then it came apart with a soft concussive pop into a rolling cloud of silver mist that flooded the bench and spilled over the edges.

Hathaway threw an arm across her nose and mouth and had a kinetic filter up before she finished the motion, her threat arrays screaming for a necrotic shockwave, an abyssal vapour, anything.

The mist hit her face and registered as nothing at all. Then her olfactory receptors were mugged, without warning and without dignity, by seared beef and melted cheese and toasted bread, and the whole sterile chamber smelled like a high-end drive-through at two in the morning.

The fog thinned into the ventilation scrubbers, and the array was gone, and the card with it.

Sitting in the exact centre of the steel bench, on a perfectly ordinary paper napkin, was a hamburger.

It was enormous. The bun had a proper glaze on it. Three thick patties were rendering hot grease into the paper, and a bead of cheddar came off the side and hit the napkin with a small greasy hiss, and the ketchup situation could fairly be described as a structural decision.

Hathaway lowered her arm.

That's it? She looked at the tray, at the thirty-nine fragments still sitting on it. I was braced for an ingot. A core. Something you bolt into a reactor.

Beside her, Victoria had stepped flush to the bench with both hands braced on the steel, and her pupils had blown wide to the edge of the iris. She swept a diagnostic lattice across the napkin, and then a second one, deeper.

"There is no mana residue," Victoria said, and her voice had lost every gram of its silk. "It is not conjured."

"There is no leyline residue. It was not transmuted from anything."

She raised her head, and did not put a hand back on the bench.

"It is real."

Hathaway's spine came up.

"Alchemy is the science of equivalent exchange," Hathaway quoted, and heard her own voice come out thin. She looked at her professor. "Physics always takes its cut. That's a Forbidden Art."

Nino picked the corner of the napkin up between two fingers, examined the grease on it, and put it down again.

"Not quite," she said. "It looks like creation from nothing. It is a purchase."

She tapped the tray.

"Nightmare energy is a currency. We filed a specification." A flick of the stylus at the space where the card had been. "We paid. Delivery was made to spec. Nothing in that sequence had to negotiate with a conservation law, because nothing in that sequence was converted."

The exhaustion in the grey eyes had gone somewhere else entirely.

"We have found a wishing machine. A weak one. But it is a wishing machine, and the mechanism is verified."

Hathaway looked at the tray.

Materials go into the item. Her developer brain took the correction, turned it over twice, and filed it somewhere cold. Currency doesn't.

One fragment. One thumbnail chip off the top of a tray of forty, and reality had accepted it as legal tender and delivered a physical object that would still be a physical object in a hundred years.

Her inner producer started building the model. One partitioned city, one month, one filtration line that only started working last week. Twelve districts on the map. Something bright and awful went up the back of her neck. The rate is good. Somebody could build a supply chain on this.

And underneath the arithmetic, unhelpfully, something else.

A purchase has two sides.

Somebody out there had taken the payment and shipped the goods, and nobody in this room had asked who, or what they wanted the crystals for.

Hathaway looked at the burger, and did not ask it either.

"So the next step," she said instead.

"We expand." Nino was already turning to the terminal. "The pilot rolls out across additional Lucent holdings, and we escalate the fear content in the coils. Baseline dread is thin. Concentrated dread is not, and the refinement line no longer loses anything worth mentioning."

Scale the region, tune the difficulty curve up, monetise harder. Hathaway found herself nodding along. There is nothing in this plan I have not personally shipped.

The printer beside the desk chattered and produced a stack of parchment thick enough to have an opinion about it. Nino tore it off and dropped it on the bench between them.

"Reading list. Metaphysical currency, conceptual materialisation, void-exchange dynamics. Ten thousand words on the structural limits of nightmare conversion. Each of you. On my desk next Sunday before the shift."

Hathaway's face performed a slow controlled demolition. Ten thousand words. On top of Deiner's final, Mephisto's yield gate, and a wondrous item nobody in my cohort has a single workable idea for.

Nino caught it, and turned all the way around, and looked at her.

"I am submitting this project," she said, "for this year's Axiom of the Water Dragon. Do you understand me."

The cooling vents filled the silence and did not do a good job of it.

Hathaway looked at her professor. Thirty-two days from a dead research line to a verified miracle. She submits every year. Every year it has been good enough to qualify and not good enough to beat whatever the monsters brought that season, and every year she has gone back to the bench and started again, and every evening there is a table she has to sit down at.

And Hathaway's name was going on the paper.

Her inner careerist kicked the door in and took the microphone. My principal investigator is submitting a world-breaking exploit for a seat on the Axiom, and she is carrying me onto the author line in her backpack. Every trace of bitterness about the ten thousand words evacuated the building. Second-year. Named contributor. This is a free ride to tenure with the reagents paid for.

Hathaway snapped upright hard enough to rattle the bench, nodding with the frantic, shameless devotion of an unpaid intern who has just been shown the door to the partner track.

"Professor!" she declared. "Ten thousand is a floor! Your faith in me is not misplaced!"

"Twelve thousand, then," Nino said, and went back to her terminal.

Beside her, Victoria had already taken half the stack off the bench and squared it against the steel.

"Whose work am I permitted to name," she said.

Nino did not turn around.

"All of it."

Something moved at the corner of Victoria's mouth that had not been there a second earlier, and she opened the first monograph.

Hathaway looked at her own share of the pile and felt the assignment change shape in her hands. Every author on this list got it wrong. I get to say so for ten thousand words. And most of them are still alive to read it.

The burger sat on the bench in the ensuing quiet, steaming gently, being real.

Victoria considered it. Then she produced a set of silver cutlery from somewhere inside her jacket, laid the napkin flat, and partitioned the thing into three exact portions with two cuts.

She passed the first across without looking up.

It was very good.
