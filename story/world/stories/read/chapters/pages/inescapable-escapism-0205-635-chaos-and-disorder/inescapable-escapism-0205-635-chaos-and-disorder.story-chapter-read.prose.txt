My steps were careful as I made my way through the city, trying to avoid walking on the broken glass. It was impossible not to step on some of it, though. It was strewn across the pavement, and it wasn’t just coming from the cars that had been left along the street. The windows of the offices, or whatever they had been, next to me had also been destroyed, leaving gaping holes that filled me with anxiety. I was terrified that people could be hiding within the shadows of any of the buildings, waiting to pounce the moment my back was turned.

A particularly loud crunch caught my attention, and my body tensed. I had been trying so hard to pick my way around the largest shards, the ones that could easily go through the thin, worn soles of my trainers and embed themselves in my foot, but I hadn’t been paying enough attention. I’d gotten distracted scanning the building beside me, and that could have been the end for me.

I held my breath as I looked down, almost expecting to see blood pooling around the shattered shards of glass beneath my shoe, but thankfully, there was none. Anxiety thrummed within me as I walked forward a little further, waiting until there was a clearing in the glass, before lifting my foot.

Thankfully, the sole was still in one piece. A few pieces of glass clung to the rubber, but none had penetrated it, and the relief I felt at that realisation nearly brought me to my knees. I could handle the throbbing pain in my shoulder and the dull ache in the front of my calf that grew more irritating with each step, but I was pretty sure my journey would have been over if I’d managed to impale myself on one of the shards of glass.

My eyes lingered on a piece that was almost the length of my leg as I carefully stepped over it. It was thin, I mused. Barely the width of my arm. If I had some leather or something, I could wrap that around the end and use it as a weapon, kind of like a sword. It would be fragile, easily broken, but sharp. In a pinch, it might be useful.

But I didn’t have any leather. Not really. I mean, there was my jacket, but I needed that, and the thought of destroying it to create such a delicate weapon that could shatter before I even had the chance to use it made my heart hurt.

I was strangely attached to it, I realised. It held some sort of sentimental value. It was close to my heart, and that felt dangerous in that world, but I wasn’t sure why. I didn’t know enough about the place I’d found myself to work that out. I had no idea what had happened in that world or even what city I was in.

Pausing for just a second, I looked around. I was in a big city. That much was clear. There were too many skyscrapers along the street for it not to be. Hundreds of thousands of people must have lived there back in the day, before whatever happened to leave it in such a state.

It didn’t look like the UK, though. As far as I was aware, the only place to have so many giant buildings was London, and I had been there a few times. I didn’t know it too well. I hadn’t spent that much time there, but something about that place didn’t feel like London.

The faded remains of a sign above a door on the other side of the street caught my eye, and I hesitated before stopping again and peering at it. The colours had been bleached by the sun, but a faint strip of green at the top and blue at the bottom could just about be seen. It seemed like there should have been something in the middle, though. A letter was still hanging there, but the rest of the world was missing. Or, at least, I assumed the rest of the word was missing. Perhaps the shop was actually just called ‘a’, but that didn’t seem likely.

I could see something else at the far end of the sign, though. Some letters or something, in a much smaller font. I couldn’t quite make out what they were from where I was standing, especially with the setting sun shining directly into my eyes, but curiosity forced me to look around before stepping off the pavement.

Carefully, I edged between two abandoned cars, almost catching my leg on a jagged piece of metal that stuck out from the front of one of them. My eyes turned wide as I stared down at it, realising just how close my thigh had come to the sharp-looking spike. It was just a few centimetres from stabbing me, just as it appeared to have done to countless others, based on the tufts of bloodstained fabric and hairs caught in the tiny barbs.

If it had even touched my skin, I was terrified that I would have contracted a list of diseases so long I wasn’t sure I’d even be able to finish reading them before I died, and I couldn’t exactly go to a doctor if anything happened to me. They weren’t particularly easy to find anymore.

I did have some iodine in my bag, though. The small, brown glass bottle was wrapped in a sock and tucked into one of the inner pockets, but I didn’t want to waste that. I couldn’t. I was already running far lower than I was comfortable with, and I needed it to disinfect water.

My gaze flitted between the metal and my surroundings as I edged around it, moving as far into the road as I dared. It felt risky. I was too exposed out there, but I needed to know what the smaller writing on the sign said.

Frustration washed over me as I peered up at it. Although I could see it better from where I was, it didn’t really help me. I still didn’t know what it said, and I didn’t even recognise the language it was written in. The version of me that lived in the world normally probably knew, but that information didn’t present itself to me.

With an irritated sigh, I turned back towards the pavement I’d come from, moving between the cars again, and stepped over the wispy weeds that had broken through the tarmac at the edge of the road. They reached up, brushing against my feet as I passed and continued walking away from the building I’d either jumped or fallen out of.

I could feel my curiosity growing stronger as I walked, picking my way over the broken glass that had been trampled to dust in some places. The urge to know what happened in the world was pulling at me, demanding my attention, and as much as I longed to give in, I forced myself to ignore it.

Fear, that’s what was motivating me. I was worried that if I gave in and allowed myself to think about it, the answers would never reveal themselves to me. They’d remain locked in my mind, out of reach, forever, and I didn’t want to risk that. Plus, I’d already decided that I was going to stop pushing to find out everything about the world and just let it come to me. That had worked best before, and I needed to try it again, but it was so hard when every atom of my body was filled with such desperate desire. I yearned to know whether the city had been destroyed by a natural disaster or…

A bomb. And natural disasters, I realised, my eyebrows pulling together and hope leaping in my heart. A bomb was dropped… somewhere in the world, I wasn’t sure where, but it triggered a chain of natural disasters that rippled across the entire globe.

People had run, I remembered someone telling me as we sat around a crackling campfire with no fear of the light or smoke attracting scavengers. They took what they could and travelled as far as possible. Some headed inland to avoid the tsunamis, raging storms, and radiation fallout, only to be chased away by typhoons or blizzards.

But it didn’t end there. Those who survived the first couple of months were forced to navigate bitter winters, followed by punishing summers, and few managed that. The seasons had calmed a little since then, apparently, but they were still pretty bad. The sun scorched the plants every summer, causing wildfires to blaze far too often, and we barely had a chance to recover before the frost rolled in. I couldn’t imagine it being any worse than that.

That was before my time, though. I’m pretty sure the bomb was dropped before I was born or not long after. I’m not certain. I have no recollection of what the world was like before. I only remember the chaos and disorder, and my earlier memory is of running. I sprinted across an open field, the grass taller than I was, and bullets tore into the dried dirt around me, but I couldn’t recall anything else about that memory. I had no idea who I was running away from or to, or who was shooting at me.

And I had no recollection of my parents.

They must have existed. Someone must have given birth to me, but I couldn’t even picture their faces. I didn’t know whether they’d died or abandoned me willingly, leaving me to navigate the world alone.

Not alone. Although there was no one with me anymore, it hadn’t always been that way. Someone used to look after me. A few people, actually. They were the ones who told me the stories of the world before everything went wrong. I could remember the smile on someone’s face as they ruffled my hair and the way someone else would lean forward as they paused mid-story to make sure I was just as entranced as they were. Another person used to ruffle my hair, stopping only when I grew taller than them, and they could no longer reach.

They were nothing more than memories, but I could still feel the love radiating from each one, and it was quickly followed by pain. That time, it was not coming from my shin or my shoulder. The phantom ache came from my heart.

It was so hard to leave them behind, part of me lamented, and I hoped they’d understand. Once they realised I wasn’t coming back from the hunt I’d gone on, they’d go looking for me, but I left them a note in a place I knew they’d look. It should have been protected from the elements, so hopefully they’d know I hadn’t gone missing or been murdered, and they’d know my reasons. They would understand why I had to leave, even if they didn’t like it.

But I wished I could go back. Ever since I’d slipped away from the camp we created, the only place of love and safety that I’d ever known, I thought that often. I’d travelled far since then, and each step took me further away, but the longing to turn back never faded.

And that was why I couldn’t do it. If I went back, I wouldn’t be able to leave again, and that was the problem. Being there put everyone in danger, and they didn’t mind. No one had ever suggested exiling me, but that didn’t matter. I had done it to myself.

Bad luck followed me. For as long as I could remember, it always had. As a kid, I’d assumed it was a coincidence. The crops I helped with always withered, animals managed to get out of the traps I set despite someone else checking them, and the people I loved got sick whilst I stayed healthy. I was unlucky, some had said. Nature was harsh, and there was no way to know which plants would survive, others had told me, but as I grew older, I saw the truth. I was the one factor linking everything together, and I couldn’t continue to cause their suffering.

But that wasn’t the only reason I’d left. The poster I’d seen when I accompanied someone to a trade had been the final straw. Paper was hard to find, despite how little demand there was for it. It wasn’t produced anymore, and it did not age well, but someone had managed to gather some. A lot, actually.

The person they were trading with had noticed the poster too. They’d seen the similarities between my face and the crude drawing, but the person I was with shrugged them off. I couldn’t remember how exactly, but she was a far more convincing liar than I’d ever been. Somehow, she had managed to convince them that the image looked nothing like me, but I’d seen the tension in her posture and the speed with which we returned to camp, taking more diversions and a longer route than usual.

At first, I’d hope it was a mistake, that it wasn’t really my face or that they were mistaken, but the posters started cropping up more and more. When the rain came, it washed them away, turning the drawings into nothing more than a smear, but they were replaced once the rain stopped.

I was asked to remain at the camp more often than usual after that. They tried to hide me away, but I knew it was inevitable. Someone would see me or remember where I had been at some point, and I was worried about what would happen when they did.

For some unknown reason, someone wanted me. The posters with my face were plastered all over the country, and I hadn’t seen any since I’d arrived in the world, but I had a vague memory of seeing one as I traipsed through the city before then. Obviously, I’d ripped it from the wall it had been nailed to, but I didn’t know how many people had seen it before then.

I didn’t understand, though. Who was putting them up, what did they want from me, and why were they offering such a ridiculous reward? It was enough to feed a person, a family, for a lifetime, and it had to be a trap. Surely no one had that much food anymore, and even if they did, I doubted they would give it away in exchange for me.

Still, it had been tempting to turn myself in at times. Last winter, when the chill made my hands turn blue and my movements clumsy, I almost did it. The cold had been impossible to shake. I’d caved and lit a fire, knowing the risks but also that I’d die if I didn’t, but still, it did little to help. The flames had licked at my fingers, almost scorching the flesh, but I had felt no warmth, and fear had set in. If I hadn’t drifted into unconsciousness, I think I would have done it.

I’m not sure how much time passed whilst I lay there, huddled in the jacket I’d been given long ago and crying softly to myself whenever I roused, but by the time I woke fully, the ice had begun to thaw again. I didn’t know whether it was luck or divine intervention that protected me whilst I was unconscious, but somehow, I was still alive and alone when I woke up. Hunger had left me weak, and the fire I’d lit had long since burnt out, but I was alive and at least relatively safe. That was all that mattered.

The thought of surviving another winter scared me. I wasn’t sure I could do it again, and the fear was almost enough to force me to turn around and walk towards the coordinates at the bottom of the poster, but I refused. Whatever awaited me there may have been even worse than being at the mercy of the weather.

But part of me still wanted to flee. I longed to pull back, to disappear from the world and never look back, but that also worried me. Although I’d found myself in another reality where I was struggling to survive and potentially being hunted, at least I was alone. That might not be the case if I went to a different world, and I couldn’t guarantee that I would be able to return home just yet. It still felt too distant.

Plus, I could feel the determination filling me. I wanted to stay there, to continue fighting and surviving. I wanted to wait out the people who were looking for me, and one day, I truly believed I’d be able to find my way back to my family, the ones who loved me despite not being related by blood. That was enough to keep me anchored in there, and all I needed to do was keep walking and avoiding people.

Of course, I couldn’t avoid them forever. There had been a couple of times since I’d left camp where I’d had to interact with a stranger. I’d stumbled across them, gotten too close before I noticed their presence or needed to trade. That had happened when I ran out of fishing wire a little while ago, towards the end of spring.

I hadn’t taken much with me when I’d left, obviously. I didn’t want the people I’d left behind to go without because of me, and whatever I’d hooked had been too powerful. Either that or I’d just reused the wire too much, and it had worn out. I wasn’t sure, but I didn’t have enough left to fish, and I didn’t have much else to help me hunt. I’d grown so hungry that I’d used the wire from my makeshift bow to catch that fish anyway, so I had nothing but a single knife.

Perhaps I should have taken more weapons when I left, but it was too late for that, and at least the person I’d traded with had been kind. I’d kept my face as hidden as possible; I didn’t want them to recognise me from the posters that seemed to be haunting me, but they seemed aware that I was young.

I was pretty sure they took pity on me. There was no other reason why they would have traded my spare fire starter and the pelt of a fox I’d practically fallen onto the night before for half a spool of fishing wire and some first aid supplies. It wasn’t a clean kill. The pelt was full of holes from my knife, and I wasn’t sure that the woman would have been able to use it for anything, but she didn’t seem to care too much.

She didn’t have any iodine, though. I had enough to last me a few more days, maybe a week or so if I was careful, but not much longer than that. I couldn’t risk drinking unclean water. The dangers of that had been drilled into me many times throughout my life, and I knew boiling the water would help purify it, but a fire felt too dangerous in the city. There were too many places where people could be hiding, and they might see the smoke. They’d have the advantage, be able to sneak up on me before I even knew they were there.

That meant I’d most likely need to find someone to trade with again. They might still try to attack me, but I doubted I had much they’d want, and I could defend myself well. That was a necessity. I would have died long ago if I couldn’t.

But I didn’t have anything to trade. I’d already parted ways with most of the things I took when I left camp, and I had very little food left. I needed that for myself, so I’d need to hunt again before I found someone. Then I’d have meat and a pelt or two, but carrying an abundance of food was risky. It could attract predators if I wasn’t careful, but I knew how to deal with that. It would be fine.

I’d leave the city first, I decided. Some wildlife had found its way into the city, but there was more in the forest at the outskirts. I could go there, catch a few deer or something, and then return to the city and find someone. That was the best plan.

My mind made up, I slowed again and looked around. The buildings around me were just as dense as they’d been before, but they seemed shorter. There were still a few reaching up into the clouds, but they were more sporadic.

For once, luck was on my side. I must have been moving further towards the outskirts of the city without even realising, and a smile pulled at my lips as I turned a corner. Immediately, my eyes found the building at the far end of the street. Half of it had crumbled into nothing. The shell still stood, but there was little more than that, and smoke drifted lazily away from it. A fire was burning inside.
