“So, it is Cyrus the warrior, Iris the mage, and Hakon the healer?” Anne confirmed politely while serving them more tea.

The guests had finally relaxed enough for proper counseling, and now she could move beyond pleasantries. From what they had already shared, the structure of the party was clear enough. The three came from the same town, had known each other since childhood, and cooperated well on a personal level. That alone placed them above many low-tier groups she had heard of.

If Anne had to reduce their problem to a single cause, it would be access to resources.

Not merely coin, though coin was certainly part of it. Weapons, information, proper guidance, suitable texts, good routes, better training methods—everything had been scarce or locked behind prices they could not pay. Of course, they were not the most talented young adventurers on the continent. Had they been, they would likely have advanced in spite of poor conditions. But Anne had never thought talent deserved as much worship as people gave it. She would choose the prepared and determined over the merely gifted almost every time.

At this point, she already felt comfortable enough to engage their problems directly. It would sting a little, certainly, but that was still better than circling around the issue while pretending not to see it.

“What challenges are you facing in your advancement?” she asked. “I may be able to help.”

The three adventurers looked at one another without knowing who should answer first. Even Hakon, who had so far seemed the calmest of them, looked a little unsettled. Anne did not press. She only drank her tea and waited.

It did not take long.

Just as when they first entered the bookstore, Cyrus took the lead.

“Eh...” He scratched his cheek awkwardly. “I did not get proper access to the guild information, so I only know rumors. If they are true, the next step for a warrior should be absorbing energy. I can feel it, I think, but I do not know how to control it. Do I need to eat more food rich in essence? Train harder? Or just keep trying until something happens?”

He said everything at once, not bothering to hide his confusion. Anne rather liked that. It was much easier to help someone who laid out their ignorance directly than someone who covered it with pride.

She acknowledged his doubts with a slow nod but did not hurry to answer. Lack of knowledge would always become a wall in advancement. Without it, a person searched in the dark and mistook effort for direction.

“Yes,” she said at last. “From what I know, the second step of the warrior path is absorbing energy to consolidate strength. The right method can vary from person to person, but the general principle is the same. Essence-rich food should help, though it is not enough by itself.” She paused, then asked the mos important question with as much kindness as possible. “Mr. Cyrus, how much can you read?”

There was no way around that.

“I...” Cyrus began, already embarrassed.

“He’s bad at it,” Iris said at once, cutting in with far too much ease. “He can barely read signs unless they are related to food.”

The joke was clearly meant to soften the moment, and Cyrus, to his credit, accepted it with only a pained look instead of real offense.

Anne sighed inwardly.

There was nothing unusual about it. Reading had become far too uncommon outside the classes and professions that still required it. Even so, she never quite stopped resenting that fact.

“There is no problem with that,” she said, easing the warrior before his embarrassment could deepen. “I also have a book that can help you learn to read fast enough.” Then she turned to the other two. “What about you, Ms. Iris? Mr. Hakon?”

How many days or weeks counted as fast enough was a question Rick would probably answer very differently than Anne, but that did not make the statement false.

“I can read the Common Tongue and some Crohnish,” Iris said with immediate pride. “I also know a few words in ancient Solarium, but only for spells.”

“The Common Tongue and Kohr,” Hakon answered simply.

Anne was not surprised that the foxkin knew Kohr. It was the mother language of many beastkin races, though unfortunately there were not many books written in it. Iris, however, did surprise her. Crohnish had long since passed out of ordinary use. Only scholars, old families, and the more stubborn keepers of knowledge still paid it much attention. For a girl of humble origins to know even some of it suggested an effort she had not yet boasted about.

Interesting and promising.

After directing Cyrus toward an empty table with a Syllable, Anne turned to the other two.

The warrior went obediently, though with the same confused expression as before, as if he still could not believe that reading had suddenly become part of his advancement path. Anne let him go without further explanation. Understanding would come once he started.

Iris, meanwhile, proved harder to crack than Cyrus had been.

The girl was too proud to admit her real difficulty, which forced Anne into a careful back-and-forth of questions, clarifications, and observations. It took some effort, but the shadow of the problem eventually emerged.

As a copper-tier primancer, Iris could cast cantrips, but not a complete spell. Primancy granted access to the four primal elements—fire, wind, water, and earth—but because of her financial limits and the way most mages specialized early, Iris had focused only on pyromancy.

That was not unusual. The trouble was in the transition.

The first stage of a mage’s path was crucial because it taught the basic relationship between the caster and magical energy. Cantrips relied mostly on chanting and channeling. Full spells demanded something more. Visualization. A complete internal image of what the spell was and how it should behave.

Iris already possessed decent control. What she lacked was guidance through the act of visualization itself.

Since bookmancy often overlapped with spellcasting disciplines, Anne could have guided her directly. In fact, she likely could have done so well enough. But Anne was a bookmancer, and whenever possible, she preferred to let the book do the heavy lifting. A reader who walked part of the path themselves always gained something more stable from it.

She counted the possibilities in her head and found that none suited Iris better than one particular volume. Unfortunately, that volume was also somewhat embarrassing.

“The most appropriate book I have for your current obstacle,” Anne said, keeping her tone as neutral as possible, “is The Tricks of Visualization.”

Iris leaned forward.

“It is both a compendium and a training book for novice mages,” Anne continued. “It focuses exactly on what the title suggests—developing the internal structure needed for visualization. It should help you move from cantrips toward complete spells.”

She did not mention at first that it was written by Anne Wordkeeper herself.

There was something deeply awkward about recommending one’s own work, no matter how justified the recommendation might be. Still, honesty won out a moment later.

“It is my book,” she admitted. “Or rather, the first one I wrote.”

To Anne’s surprise, Iris did not hesitate even a little.

Instead, the girl’s face brightened with uncomplicated interest.

“That’s even better,” she said, and accepted the book almost immediately after hearing what it contained.

Then, with no trace of ceremony, the primancer hurried off to another empty table and started going through the material at once.

Anne watched her with mild astonishment.

Well. That had gone more smoothly than expected.

Which left only Hakon.

The foxkin healer had been quiet through all of it, but not absent. He listened with focused reserve. If extracting Cyrus’s problem had been easy and pulling Iris’s had felt like tugging on something stubbornly rooted, dealing with Hakon promised to be like searching for hidden ore in random ground.

So Anne chose a different approach.

Instead of trying to pry information from him directly, she retrieved several biomancy-related books and placed them between them on the counter. Then she began discussing the topics one by one, letting his reactions and answers show her where his real level stood.

Biomancy, or healing, was less mystical and more practical than many magical crafts. That made it harder to judge quickly. A healer’s actual worth depended on a web of abilities rather than one obvious benchmark, and many could reasonably be placed between tiers depending on what exactly was being measured. Often only a guild professional could make the final call.

By convention, copper-tier biomancers should know basic treatment and healing herbology—disinfecting wounds, preparing salves, managing fevers, treating colds, and stabilizing injuries. The next major step required actual manipulation of healing essence.

From what they discussed, Anne concluded that Hakon could already use healing essence, but only in a blunt, imprecise way.

The easiest way to explain it came to her quickly.

“If a wound is bleeding,” she said, “what you are doing now is like pressing down on it. That helps, and for smaller injuries it may even be enough. But a larger wound must be closed properly. Pressure alone cannot replace that.”

Hakon considered the analogy in silence.

Anne took that as permission to continue.

“In other words,” she said, “you can already apply healing force. But to advance, you need to learn how to shape it properly.”

She was not entirely certain of the best next step, but a safe foundation seemed obvious enough.

Magic and Lifeforce.

That book should strengthen his understanding of healing essence itself before moving him on to more precise and specialized texts.

Satisfied with her judgment, Anne finally leaned back and allowed herself a moment to observe the three adventurers spread around the bookstore.

Cyrus sat bent over the Syllable with visible effort and stubborn concentration. Iris had already immersed herself in the training book with the greedy intensity of a girl who knew she had found something precious. Hakon remained near the counter, quieter than the others, but clearly going through Anne’s explanation in his mind.

A lovely sight.

Good days lay ahead.
