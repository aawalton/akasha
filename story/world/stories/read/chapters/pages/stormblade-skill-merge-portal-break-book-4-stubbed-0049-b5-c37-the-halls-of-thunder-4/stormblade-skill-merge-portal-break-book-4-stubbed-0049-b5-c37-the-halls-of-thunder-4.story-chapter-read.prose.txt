A Few Months Ago

The God of Thunder—it had been a long time since Tallas had called him Rodenick—had found a new pupil.

He’d seeded his lowest cultists—those who could barely claim the title of Paragon—in the weakest dungeons that could support them. D-Rank monsters with no prospect of growing, no potential to become more than themselves, but even so, valuable, these monsters carried a small portion of the God of Thunder’s incredible Mana reserves. They carried two cores. One, a core similar to that of any other boss that might roam a D-Rank dungeon.

The second, of course, was a fragment of the Stormsteel Path. A Stormsteel Core.

Hundreds of Stormsteel Cores had been scattered across every D-Rank dungeon the God of Thunder could co-opt. They had a two-fold purpose.

Thunder-King Yalagan: D-Rank

A pretentious, meaningless title, and one that should have offended the former Thunder King. The God of Thunder laughed.

That was the monster’s name and title, though carrying the Stormsteel Core without being able to use it had stripped his Paragon title and concealed it. The hobgoblin summoner was on a suicide mission in the name of his object of worship. He’d die in some goblin warren, and his two cores would be taken. Then he’d be reborn, given enough power to progress a single rank, and be cut loose. A second chance. But to get another, he’d need to achieve something he never had.

Victory.

The God of Thunder didn’t care if Yalagan lived or died. He only cared that he’d pass on the core, and what would happen after. Typically, the Stormsteel Core would end up powering a city or a superweapon, then be thrown away. Other times, an unworthy delver would attempt to use it and destroy it in the process. That was fine. His Path wasn’t for the uninteresting.

But today? Today, Thunder-King Yalagan’s Stormsteel Core had found itself in the hands of a very interesting person. The God of Thunder watched carefully as the kid, barely old enough to be a man, wrestled with the Stormsteel Core and forced it to merge with his own. Then he smiled.

“Kade Noelstra, welcome to the Stormsteel Path.”

The kid’s fate had been decided. Every step he took, he’d take under the God of Thunder’s watchful eye. He’d walk the Path up to the end, and then he’d add his own Law to it.

And then…

And then, the God of Thunder would hunt once again.

Magda, the Paragon of the Sliding Surge, had been through the wringer.

The Stormblade, then the Placid King, then the Stormblade a second time, and then the God of Thunder…it was almost enough to make her flee for safer waters. And then to have to confront the Stormblade and the Shadow Witch a third time, and without the ability to even fight back properly? That was just adding insult to injury.

Even so, her plan was proceeding apace.

She waited outside the shimmering, golden portal with the jet-black core and flashing, red-orange lightning. Patiently. Like she didn’t have anything to hide. A half-dozen A and B-Rankers surrounded her, weapons and spells at the ready. She didn’t have to be a genius to know that the moment something went wrong, they’d try to kill her—and she’d have to be an idiot to think she could do anything about it in her current, wounded state. So, the best thing she could do was act the part of a contrite, scared being whose only goal was to get back to safety and heal up.

The portal hiccuped for a split second. Not enough for the assembled delvers to notice, but enough for her to bring her full attention to the S-Rank gateway. The Shadow Witch fell through it, followed by the one they called the Spark of Life. Then the Portal Tyrant, and finally—though she’d hoped he’d die there—the Stormblade.

“There. You see? I’ve kept my end of the bargain. Now, let me return to my world,” Magda said, desperation creeping into her voice. “I want to be free. I want to be done with this nightmare so I can rest.”

Kade’s eyes narrowed.

“I did what I said I’d do. I got you inside. I waited for you to finish. I’ve been nothing but truthful,” Magda whined. She couldn’t help it. She was so scared.

Not of Kade. But of the God of Thunder.

“And your instinct to kill? To find life and destroy it until there’s nothing left?” the Shadow Witch asked.

“Obviously, that hasn’t vanished. But to do that, I need to recover. I have very little energy left, and all of it is going into staying awake here so I can talk to you two.” That was true. Magda had been through the wringer.

“Fine,” Kade said. “We found what we wanted. You’ll be escorted back to Wickenberg by the Portal Tyrant—assuming he’s willing—and from there, you’ll be able to return to your portal world.”

Magda held back her grin. It had been a long slog over the last couple of days. She’d been on the verge of death a half-dozen times, betrayed her core-bond partner at least once, and lost her master. But she was getting away with it. She was getting away with all of it.

The timer crept down as Magda left.

Even with the Spark of Life’s healing, it took time to recover from a running battle. My nerves were fried from using Stormbreak, and I’d taken blows without even noticing them. Cuts to my arms and legs, a small square of missing muscle from my back—probably Ellen’s handiwork, though I didn’t say anything about it to her—and a bruise that covered a good portion of my chest. They all took healing, and they all pulled at my Stamina.

So, I waited.

So did Ellen. She’d stripped down to her sports bra and shorts, and she sat across from me, hissing in pain as Sarah Cullman ran her fingers over her ribs and hips. She’d taken a big hit, too, but she’d toughed it out. It was impressive how well she was starting to handle pain—at least when she had to. And, even covered in bruises, she was beautiful.

I cleared my throat, and she raised her eyebrow. “Don’t even say it. I’m hideous.”

“Nah. You’re as pretty as a sunflower. The full-body bruise isn’t a good look, that’s all.”

She snorted.

Sarah’s eyes narrowed, and she looked over her shoulder at me. “The more Delver Traynor moves, the harder this is going to be. She’s got some internal damage that’ll take actual work to repair. Please don’t make her laugh.”

“Sorry.” I glanced at the floor and, after a moment, Sarah nodded and went back to examining my partner’s sternum.

We stayed quiet for a few minutes. Then I cleared my throat. “So, it’s about the batteries.”

“We’ve known that, though. You found the same thing in Retan’s portal world, and you heard as much from the Crone. What else did we learn?” Ellen asked, careful not to move a muscle as she talked.

I grinned cheekily. “Guess.”

“You’re really going to make me guess right now, when we’re sitting here, waiting for your timer to run out? This is outrageous, Kade.” She sighed. Sarah’s head popped up, glaring. “Sorry. Fine. Here’s my guess. The batteries are part of the God of Thunder’s weakness, but they’re not special. There’s something about what he’s doing with them that is.”

“Half right.” I stood and pulled my shirt back on. It was in tatters, but they always were after a delve. “The God of Thunder went through an incredibly traumatic moment back there. He suffered, and he watched his peers die. It was only pure luck that let him escape—luck, and an opportunity for him to forge his own Path in that exact moment. He’s never forgotten that, and I’d be willing to bet it affects everything he does.”

“How so?” Ellen asked.

“Well, he doesn’t take multiple pupils. He’s got a cult, but I don’t think he interacts with them all that much. If I’m a typical disciple—“

“I doubt it.”

“—then he only chooses those who pique his interest. He’s a naturally curious being, and that’s almost as much of a drive as his need to ascend. He’s…” I stopped, unable to explain exactly what I was thinking.

“You don’t think he’s Bob.” Ellen’s words weren’t a question.

“No. Stormlord Nalthelas was Bob. He was willing to lie, cheat, manipulate, betray, or whatever else he needed to do to ascend. His weakness was that he viewed all those A and S-Rank Paragons as tools. As living batteries.” I took a breath and started pacing the little room. “The God of Thunder had to have reacted to that betrayal.”

“How?” Sarah asked. She was still working on Ellen’s chest and side—the bruise had already faded noticeably.

I sighed. “I think…the God of Thunder is two conflicting things. First, he’s desperately lonely. I think that’s true of any of the SS-Plus-Ranked Paragons. They all take steps to mitigate that loneliness. The Placid King had an army of minions. Stormlord Nalthelas had a band of Paragons until he betrayed them. Even the Crone has the Ghostmarket.

“And the God of Thunder has an occasional pupil who interests him. Any more than that, and he’d risk becoming the Stormlord. Any less, and he wouldn’t move forward.”

“And his nature as a predator?” Ellen stood and buttoned her Portal Tyrants uniform.

“He gave me three days. He wants to hunt.”

“And how is that a weakness?” Sarah asked. “You’re both good to go, by the way. Try not to roughhouse for a couple of hours. And tell your sister hi from me.”

“Thanks, Sarah. You’re the best. He’s not Bob. He doesn’t have allies or hunting partners. Yes, he’s got a cult, but he hardly cares about them. They’re uninteresting. So, he’s concentrated all his power in himself, and in his portal world. That makes it easy to attack and hard to keep hidden.”

“So, his weakness is his inability to foster meaningful alliances?”

I nodded at Ellen’s assessment. “Yes. It means that we have an advantage he can’t replicate. We have friends and allies. But in the end, it’s going to come down to him and me.”

“And me,” Ellen said. “You’re not ditching me that easily. We’re in it together, for the long haul.”

“I was hoping you’d say that.”

The timer ticked down.

It hit zero.

The God of Thunder’s hunt began. And…nothing happened. Eugene didn’t appear in the sky over Phoenix. Lightning didn’t pour from the heavens to incinerate the Portal Tyrant’s stronghold. The earth didn’t shake.

What was going on? It had been three days. He should have been attacking.

Unless…

The God of Thunder was a predator. And predators didn’t attack the herd. They hunted for those separate from it.

The Storm’s Blade flickered to life in my hand. My armor covered my body in a sheet of swirling storms. I broke into a sprint, Ellen right behind me, as Sarah yelled something about not roughhousing.

Tanat was in my portal world, and she was alone.

And the God of Thunder was on the hunt.

Even as Kade Noelstra rampaged through his most intimate memory, the God of Thunder waited.

The three days weren’t up. Not yet.

Besides, the kid was still being interesting, and even as the last hour of sand drained from the hourglass—far too quickly for the God of Thunder’s liking—he was entertained. After all, no one had tried to assault the Hall of Thunder in a long, long time. The last being to do so had been Arkeroz. He’d been an S-ranker. That had been…twelve thousand years ago.

He hadn’t even known anyone knew about the Hall of Thunder.

It was an open wound. He couldn’t stop tearing the scab off, couldn’t let the memory fade to nothingness and the mental dungeon fall apart. In that way, he wasn’t that different from the Crone. They both had their weaknesses.

But Kade. Kade was playing things as well as any pupil he’d ever stalked. He’d remember the kid when this was over, much like he remembered Tallas. That man…he’d died in the most interesting way. He hadn’t raised his sword, or even stood. They’d been talking one moment, and then Tallas had nodded. “It’s time,” he’d said.

“So it is,” the God of Thunder had replied.

That had been all. The most interesting of his hunts so far.

So far. Because Kade was doing something new. He’d deviated, but hadn’t given up like Tallas. Instead, he seemed to be drawing on that deviation from the Stormsteel Path for strength. It was a brilliant move, if the God of Thunder said so himself. It was also the same move he’d made to fight Stormlord Nalthalas—which only added to that brilliance.

Not that it’d matter. The Stormblade Path was new. Untested. When he’d forged the Stormsteel Path and started hunting Stormlord Nalthelas, he’d needed a thousand years to exact his revenge. Three hundred sixty-five thousand days. Kade had three.

Even the allies he’d brought into the fight wouldn’t matter.

It was tragic. A small part of the God of Thunder—a part he thought of as Eugene, or as Rodenick, or a dozen other names—wanted to call off the hunt. Kade Noelstra and his friends were far too interesting to throw away. Besides, he could admit the truth to himself. Kade Noelstra was beautiful. His swordwork was unconventional, yet functional. His magic gave him enough striking power to fight back against the Placid King. And then there was Stormbreak. The kid was still ranking it up, but it was the most powerful skill on the Stormsteel Path.

Still, though…

The rest of the God of Thunder was under constant assault from two voices. The first wanted only to kill and kill, until he’d killed everything. And the second yearned to escape.

Luckily, in this instance, he’d be able to do both.

The last grains of sand fell through the narrow point in the hourglass, and the God of Thunder entered the Lightning Tower and Void Garden.

It was time, as Kade would say, to remove some pieces from the board. The opening moves had started.
