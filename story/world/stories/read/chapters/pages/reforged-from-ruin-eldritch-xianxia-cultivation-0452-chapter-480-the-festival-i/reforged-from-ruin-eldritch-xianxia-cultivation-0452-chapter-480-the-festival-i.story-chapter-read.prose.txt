The story of our Lake is a long and historied one, full of twists and turns, spirals and loops, monsters and heroes!

Once, long ago, there were great and terrible beasts that stalked the land, hunting the good people of the hills and valleys as a cat hunts a mouse. Hungry, born rife with teeth and claws, the monsters of the wilds hunted without restriction and without mercy, seeking the lives of the poor, defenseless homesteads!

But one day, there was one born of the hills and valleys that sought more than the life of hiding and hunting. Lao Bushi, strong and brave, a man of but a few years, saw what was, and decided it was not what should be. One day, when out hunting with his parents, Lao Bushi found a magic fruit, which, when consumed, made his body match the strength of his heart.

When he returned home, he told his brothers to seek out the homes of the other peoples of the hills and valleys, and to bring them together to a single hillside, where they all might settle. The tales of his brothers are long and powerful stories of their own, but in the end, each of his three brothers found the people they sought, and after many battles and many losses, they managed to bring together the peoples of the wilds, and placed them at the north, east, south and west of the highest point of that hill.

But then, what the peoples came to fear also came to pass, for the presence of so much food could not help but to draw out the great and hungering things of the dark!

But Lao Bushi was counting on this, for he was as strong as he was brave, and he was as brave as he was clever. When the many beasts came to hunt, they found their rival predators surrounding their would-be prey, and knew that the first of them to move would be the first of them to be pounced upon by their fellows. For hungry predators are allies to none but themselves, while the people of the hill could know each other, and love each other, and join together, hand in hand.

And so the hillside grew rich with farmland and new homes, and the people flourished as the beasts circled, each waiting for another to turn and strike first. And so we lived and we prospered, and over time, came to be known as the villages of east, west, north and south.

Until one day, smelling all that food and knowing that no others would dare to strike first, a greater beast, a horrid demon of the woods, came to take part in this feast prepared before it.

– The first part of the tale of Lao Bushi, performed during the festival of the Lake

The sounds of life can be overwhelming, even at the best of times. The sounds of life, when intended to be overwhelming, can be a rather dramatic thing indeed.

Children are yelling, parents laughing, singers singing songs, drunks adding their sounds to all of the above. Small, handheld fireworks are launched into the air, things of colored smoke and powder thrown as high as possible and decorating the air above the town’s streets. Drums and music play on every corner, from nearly every house, as songs are sung and games are played. The smells of cooking food of all kind, from sickly sweet to burning with spice, from meat and vegetables to pastries and candies.

Everyone participates, and everyone celebrates, and the sounds of festivities and the smells of community are only added to by the hundreds of banners, one fluttering from every building in the village, many of them incredibly bright and detailed with fresh dyes and paints.

“You’re late! I started the rub hours ago!”

Raika laughs, a bag slung over her shoulder bulging with materials for the festivities as she approaches the cooking pit in the town center. She’s followed by all four of her kids, each staring at something different, each one at varying levels of proximity. Dozens of different meals are being prepared, but there are several whole pigs being roasted over a large fire, each one arrayed at a different point around the flames, each one carrying a unique blend of herbs and spices.

La Ru and La Gao are both glaring at her pointedly, the elder of the two with considerably more force than his nephew. She just laughs, grinning at the both of them.

“And deprive you of the chance to put your own mark upon the meat? My strongest spices are too strong for you, old man! How cruel it would be to remove any hope for your cooking to shine through!”

He rolls his eyes, grumbling something, and she chuckles again, thoroughly enjoying the way he doesn’t deny her.

Still, she does put her bag down on the ground, grunting as she kneels down and starts rummaging through it. She emerges with a small clay jar, planting it on the ground next to the smoking pig.

“There,” she says, holding out a note to the duo. “My full recipe, and a jar of something special. Turns out, if you put all of it into some thick honey, mix it up for a few days, you get a wonderful blend together. I even have a brush you can use, if you like, to help you slather it on.”

“Honey? I’m making a savory mix here, mixing it with honey is just going to mess with the whole thing.”

“You think I can’t smell that?” she asks, cocking an eyebrow. “Think I wouldn’t plan it through? I promise, the sweetness will only enhance the savoryness. I added enough spices in it to put properly burn through most palates, and plenty of salt besides. I even cooked an oil out of chillies and peppers to mix in. What your roast really needs is something to balance and blend with it, and that’s what I offer. Unless you don’t trust my taste?”

“Perhaps he’s tasted your teas before, master,” Jin offers sarcastically, the closest of her students.

“My teas are delicious. It’s not my fault if your own palate is lacking, kid.”

La Gao grunts, taking in the back-and-forth with some annoyance. “You’re sure this is right? Not sure I can trust it without trying it first.”

“Then try it,” she says with a shrug. “I offer it freely. Recompense for your service in building my kiddos a home.”

“I guess you really are a hundred years old, then. Must be, to have such wildly different kids, all cultivators.”

She snorts. “Technically, only three of them are cultivators. More technically, only two and a half. Also, fuck you old man, I’m literally thirty-seven.”

La Gao and La Ru both blink at that. La Ru in particular seems a little stunned, looking down at himself as if counting something.

“Wait, you’re only eight years older than me?”

“If you’re twenty nine, then yes.”

“...Ok, so I guess you’re, like, nine years older than me.”

“You were pretty close.”

He laughs, rubbing at the back of his head sheepishly. “My wife is always telling me I have to get better with my numbers, but it’s hard to find time.”

“Hey, I’m the same way. Took me years to get any good at that sort of thing, and even longer relative to like, how time works for me.”

“Wait, does time work differently for you?”

“Anyways! Enjoy the honey-glaze, tell me when you’re ready to admit that I’m a genius chef. I’m going to show the kids around the party for a bit, make sure they don’t get into anything too stupid too quickly.”

“With respect, master, I do think I will be the one ensuring you don’t get into anything too stupid too quickly.”

She laughs at that, flicking Jin in the forehead. “In your dreams, kid. Now come on.”

She turns to Geng He and Chen Tai next, both of the ones she is, admittedly, the most worried about. She shouldn’t have bothered, though– both of them seem so thoroughly overwhelmed that they’re sticking way closer to her than she expected.

“Ready to show your stuff, kid?” She asks of Geng He.

“I’m… I’m not really sure if that’s the right call. I don’t–”

“Geng He, you’ve spent all week working on this. If you don’t at least try to show them off to people, I will be very disappointed in you.”

He grunts, which is his normal response, really. He crosses his arms, which is also pretty normal. Then he grumbles something under his breath, which is a bit new.

“Oh? What was that, oh student of mine?” she asks, even though her hearing picked it up as clear as day.

“I’m not sure where I should put it,” he grumbles.

She snorts, then starts her walking stick clacking against the dirt to walk over to another section of the festivities.

Younger kids run underfoot as their older siblings are put in charge of watching them, though most of them are, in turn, enjoying their own versions of the fun. The kids run by with small sparklers and bright little flags that they wave as they run, painting the streets in dazzling colors as they go, while their elders stroll along a bit slower, trying to sneak bits of food from the cooking stations and enjoying slightly more complex little fireworks. There are people putting up little tents, from which hang trinkets and hand-carved toys aplenty, with snacks and other treats scattered about.

She takes her motley crew to the side of the main town square, finding an empty space not quite bit enough to hold a fresh tent and not quite small enough to just be a place to stand.

“Here. You can set up next to your neighbors.”

Lai Shu is amongst the many who have set up their artist supplies around the cooking stations in the middle, her flag and the little space around her holding a mixture of beverages she’s been enjoying and carved soaps she’s made. Her daughter is by her side, and seems rather grumpy about it, staring enviously at all the other kids playing, but is dutifully helping her mother set up.

“You sure you’ll fit in that little spot?” she asks, looking at the much smaller space. “If you need to, I’m happy to move, open up some room?”

Raika doesn’t answer, letting Geng He be the one to engage.

“No, that’s… this should be adequate,” he says. He seems reluctant to speak to a mortal, though only some of it is elitism this time, rather than all of it. He also seems the tiniest bit embarrassed about it.

“Well alright then. Got any ideas what you’ll be putting out? I can keep an eye on it for you if you want to go and explore?”

He shakes his head. “No, I… mmh.”

He stands there, staring for a bit. Long enough that Wei Zin actually leans forward, wondering at him.

“Brother? You alright?”

“I’m fine! I’m… I’m fine.”

“He’s scared,” Chen Tai says with a snort.

“I am not!” He growls, almost whirling, but for Raika’s presence. Chen Tai hides behind her, poking her head out from the side and grinning at her senior brother.

“Scaaaared, scaaaared, smells so very–”

Raika’s arm comes around to put her in a chokehold, stopping her from annoying her sibling with a “gack!” sound.

“Geng He,” she says, calmly, “you don’t have to show anyone if you don’t want to.”

By this point, multiple people around them have started staring over, wondering at the commotion. People’s eyes have been on the five of them since they arrived, of course, but Geng He’s reluctance only adds to the attention, the curiosity, even the concern.

But she keeps her eyes on him, and tells him as clearly as she can that it’s alright.

He grumbles something, so far under his breath that it barely makes any words.

Then, he reaches into his storage ring, and pulls out a boat.

He places it standing upright, leaning against a building at the edge of the town square, but even still, it draws in quite a bit of attention. It’s no larger than a one-person canoe, designed to be rowed by hand and holding only a single seat, no more impressive than most.

Except for the carvings.

The wood itself is slender, artfully sculpted with clear effort and consistent, gentle cuts, until the exterior of the boat is worn almost smooth. It makes it too thin to truly be safe, but the shape of it holds firm, only the barest hint of uneven depth from where too much attention was placed on it.

But more than the design, there are the words and images stenciled in.

Raika isn’t sure when Geng He paid enough attention to the goings-on around him to hear about the town, but there’s very clearly some scenes depicted from the children’s stories that she’s heard echoing up the hill to her cabin. They’re amateurish, but not bad, not by any stretch, showing clear signs of genuine effort alongside artistic ignorance. Images of dark forests, of flowing rivers filling the land with growing stalks of rice and barley, of a great lake filling up with waters, all of it integrated into patterns along the seat, the bow and stern, and the rim of the canoe’s interior..

Even Chen Tai shuts up at that, stopping her squirming and joining her siblings as everyone turns to stare at his creation. Several sounds of “oooh” and “woah” echo as other villagers catch sight of it, and almost immediately, it starts building up a little audience of its own. Several kids even stop their playing, coming by to stare for a bit.

“You made this?” Lai Mi asks, her little eyes wide and staring at the boat from beside her mother’s skirt.

“It’s not like– yes. It’s…”

Wei Zin gently approaches, patting his junior brother on the shoulder. “This… this is an impressive piece of work.”

“How long have you been carving?” comes a brunt voice from behind them. Raika senses Qin Jia, the old woman from the skewer stall, hobbling up and almost glaring at the boat.

“It… not long.”

Raika snorts. “He started this last week.”

Geng He whirls on her, absolutely glaring. “Master! I didn’t– It’s simple. It’s amateur, I know, it’s not–”

He very nearly leaps into the air when a shriveled old hand slaps him on the arm. “Shut yer trap, ya little idgit, it’s a lovely boat. Wouldn’t trust it with any proper haul, but as canoes go, she’s damn pretty.”

Geng He is entirely silent for a little. Raika almost laughs, but she manages to keep it to just a smile. Jin joins her, bowing politely and slightly to his fellow disciple, while Wei Zin politely offers a little clap. Even Chen Tai gives a little grunting sound, staring at Geng He like she’s outright confused by him.

Raika doesn’t blame her. He does smell scared. The stress rolling off of him is borderline impressive.

And yet, he did it anyways. He carved something, and for a first attempt, it was clearly a genuine effort.

And as more and more people come by, complimenting or commenting on the work, the stress slowly abates.

“Maybe you can ask around for some advice?” she offers, noticing some of the fishermen eyeing the boat a little more critically. “I’m sure there are plenty of people who would love to give you some hints about technique, right?”

Five years of glaring at fishermen shooting her mean glances and fixing up their wounds and medications has earned her a bit of a reputation, and more than one of them pause in their criticisms long enough to join Geng He in crossing their arms and grumbling.

She laughs, especially as Geng He’s cortisol levels start spiking again. But, once again, when presented by a challenge, he squares his shoulders, swallows his fears, and faces it head on.

“It… it’s an early attempt,” he says, addressing it more to the crowd than to her. “And I’ve… never rowed a boat before.”

That changes some attitudes real fast. Several of the fishermen now approach him, multiple old hats and folks around his own age both joining up to start giving him pointers. If there’s one thing everyone likes to do, it’s talk about their passions, and multiple people seem to start catching on that Geng He is being more awkward than he is aggressive (at least for now).

A drink gets pushed in his hand as an older fisher starts telling him about how they “used to do it in the old days, back when every canoe had to hold at least ten nets!”, and she leaves him to it, turning around to–

Aaaaaand Chen Tai’s gone. Somehow.

Alright then. Good start. Now to keep it all from getting too messy.
