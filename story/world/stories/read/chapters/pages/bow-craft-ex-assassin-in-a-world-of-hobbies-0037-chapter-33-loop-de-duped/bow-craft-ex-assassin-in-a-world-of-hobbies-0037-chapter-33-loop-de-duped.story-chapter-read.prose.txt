Chapter 33Loop-de-duped

Tar black was the night. Someone limped through the forest, uncaring of how loud the twigs and glass-like leaves snapped under their feet. They knelt at the roots of each tree, praying and begging to whichever one might accept their request.

Well, that ain’t good, the eldest tree said. I’ll tell my cousin, too.

With this, World’s Origin would be safe.

***

Craft and Lei-rei were in front of the Petrifying Forest. It was a depressing place with chalky trunks and grayscale soil. The canopy itself, a basket weave of bare branches, seemed to suck the color out of the sky.

Should they really be going through here? Craft was nervous with how they were the only color moving through the forest. Sticking out like a sore thumb like this, they were asking to be ambushed, but that wasn’t even the worst part.

Deep inside, his reason for being here was already starting to fizzle out. Maybe—he refused to think—he didn’t really want to go this far for Nightshade.

“Don’t touch the trees,” Lei-rei said. She crossed the boundary, and when Craft followed suit, they were officially in Monstera’s territory.

“Hey”—he stopped at that word. His voice sounded unreasonably loud, but he realized it wasn’t because he’d gotten louder, but because everything else was dead silent. “Hey,” he started again, hushed this time. He caught up beside her and matched her pace. “Don’t just say something ominous and keep walking without an explanation—though I can kinda guess why.”

“Hm? What, wait, no. The forest itself is safe. It’s just that these trees exude petrinin sap over their bark. If you touch it, it’ll instantly cement. We’ll have to cut away the bark and buy some solvent in town to get you unstuck.”

The heck is this, superglue? That explains the chalky appearance of the trees around here, at least. Ecologically, he didn’t get why these trees were like this, but the skeletons of raptor-like birds glued to some of the fallen branches on the ground suggested self-defense. Perhaps it wasn’t the trees, but a type of fungi that grew on its bark that made the petrinin? That would explain the mushrooms growing out of some of the skeletons.

“I’m surprised there’s an ecosystem here at all,” he absently remarked.

“There has to be. Our ecology-loving friends wouldn’t be able to go on field trips otherwise.”

You can do anything you want. That was Enthusia’s implicit claim in making a paradise where Hobbies were life. She had to keep every possibility open, and to that end, a certain amount of complexity would be inevitable.

Still, quiet forests were always unsettling. Even the overhead rustling of leaves seemed so loud—

Hold on. This forest doesn’t have leaves.

He yanked Lei-rei back by the collar, and they fell on top of each other. She quickly rolled away and got into a fighting stance, but…nothing happened.

She looked at him. “I didn’t think you were another Nightshade.”

He got up and looked at her funny. “What?”

She sheathed her sword. “You…didn’t want a hug?”

He looked at her funnier. “She hugs you like that? ”

She shook her head. “Never mind. What was that for?”

He got up. “I heard something—”

The moment he looked up, he saw it: a sharpened log, thicker than a man’s torso, suspended from a rope and pointed straight down.

He pointed at it. “That.”

Lei-rei looked up. “That’s not right.”

“Anyone chance our monster friends likes to set up traps?”

Lei-rei bowed her head and thought about it. “There should only be one trap master among them, but she prefers to present official trap houses so she can invite people to test her latest contraptions.” She grimaced. “If this place has been trapped, a third party did it.”

Craft gulped. They weren’t just taking a walk in the forest anymore. One wrong move and they’ll be having tea with Nightshade.

On looking around, he could see more and more tripwires, only visible because of a talc-like sheen of dust from the forest’s natural shedding. He suspected the wires themselves were thinner than spider’s silk, or else they should have at least reflected some sunlight.

A complex look developed on his face. This was going to be a pain in the ass.

“C-Craft?” Lei-rei called. He turned, and she was blinking rapidly, mouth opening to say something, but she couldn’t find the words yet.

“H-how?” she finally said.

It didn’t mean anything to him. He shrugged. “What’s up?”

“I’ve been watching you counting things I can’t even see.”

Suddenly, their number one attacker’s mobility was gone. The moment she’d dodge in any direction, she was nearly guaranteed to trip something.

“Maybe I should teach you, then?”

She slowly, dubiously nodded. “...I suppose I can follow along.”

He looked around for an easy example, catching sight of a thin dotted line and pointing at it. “How about that one?”

Lei-rei stared at his finger’s general direction. “I can’t see it.”

“You can’t see the wire itself, but the white specks give it away.”

“White specks—where? ” She narrowed her eyes and scanned every little thing around her, but try as she might, there was nothing for her to see. She sighed.

“I’m surprised,” Craft said. “Your kinetic vision’s so good, though.”

“I suppose I’m not fit for the conceptual opposite of kinetic vision—or whatever it is you have.”

Ah, go figure. Craft cope-chuckled. “Alright, guess I’m leading the way from now on.”

***

They encountered an upsetting number of traps since then.

Lei-rei lowered her knees and dashed forwards, tripping a hundred traps across just ten meters of jungle—and she cut through every single one. Be it spikes, man-traps, falling pianos, or swinging landmines, she disabled them all with a single swipe of the blade.

Even if she sucked at spotting them, why not just trigger them on purpose and take advantage of her kinetic vision that way? Easy.

Craft joined up with her, stepping over snapped branches, broken wires, and maybe/maybe-not defused bombs. “This is the third time, isn’t it?” he said.

Lei-rei sighed as she looked at the scars she had left in the trees. “There’s no doubt about it. It’s the exact same area.”

They had run the full course of the trap trail three times. Lei-rei had only managed the hundred-trap dash because she’d learned its idiosyncrasies after the last two.

“This place… I don’t like it,” Lei-rei remarked.

Craft groaned. He’d been in these sorts of things before. “I think we’ve been caught in a spatial loop,” he said. “This seems to be pretty advanced, though. If the traps are resetting each time we make the loop, then it should be loading them in from a template space—”

“Wait.” Lei-rei had put up a hand and bowed her head, then slowly brought her hands to her head and massaged her temples. The fact that they were caught in a spatial loop wasn’t the concerning part, but rather the fact that it was in monster territory.

Those guys who lived for a good one-on-one brawl were being oddly roundabout if they’d resorted to spatial loops of all things. Just what was going on?

Once her headache subsided, she raised her head, sighing out a half-percent of the stress she’d been accumulating since yesterday. She looked at Craft. “At least you’re calm.”

“I’m used to these things,” he said with a shrug. “As far as petrified forests go, this place is actually pretty calm, too. No constant attacks, I can still hear some insects, and it doesn’t look like the sun has moved, so there’s no day-night cycle—still a little creepy, overall, but manageable.”

By Craft’s personal rating, this was a D-class anomalous zone. The traps were a little abnormal—ordinary people would absolutely get killed in a few seconds here—but they were predictable. Anyone with a smidgen of experience would run through this place in ten minutes flat had it not been for the spatial loop.

“So how do we get out of here?” Lei-rei asked.

“There should be a core.” Craft raised his thumb. “About this size.”

“That’s slightly difficult, but I can handle it.”

“It’s going to be flying around.”

“Alright.”

“And it’s going to be fast, even for you.”

“Really?” Lei-rei squinted at him—no, at something past him. “Hey, is that”—

Craft turned around. There was a distortion hovering among the branches, sucking in the light around it like a magnifying glass without form—a point distortion.

He pointed at it like a boy scout seeing a colorful bird for the first time. “That’s it!”

Just as fast as he’d pointed his finger, Lei-rei sprung and jumped up onto the closest branch. She bent her knees as her feet found perch, poised to spring again a final time, but when she did, the point distortion zipped out of the way.

She landed on a different branch, her sword merely halfway out of its sheathe.

She dropped down and met with Craft. “I think my pride has a crack in it now,” she said with a conflicted look. “Do you have any ideas?”

“Well, it’s not faster than the speed of sound, that’s for sure,” Craft said. “We usually had a sharpshooter for these situations, but I didn’t come here with a gun, so…”

“A sharpshooter? Will a bow suffice?”

“Huh? I don’t think an arrow”—is fast enough, he was supposed to say, but he remembered Lei-rei wasn’t a normal archer. He nodded. “If it’s you, it might work.”

“Are you trying to score points with me?”

He chuckled. “I wasn’t trying.”

That just leaves the bow. He had a knife, so that just left the wood. Unfortunately, all around him were petrified trees, but thanks to his experience working with glasswood, he wasn’t afraid of making something usable from it.

When he reached out towards a nearby tree, however, Lei-rei pulled him back. “Petrifying Forest, remember?”

He happily stepped back, though maybe a little mortified. “I was about to do a stupid there, huh.” He chuckled—and he froze. He normally wasn’t like this. Wait, I just casually got fixated on making a bow like that? He hadn’t even noticed it. W-wait—did Hobbies have a psychological overwrite effect?

“Don’t mind yourself,” Lei-rei replied. “Being a little stupid for your Hobby happens sometimes.”

...by Occam’s Razor, she might be right. She was the same person who did reverse-cooking.

“Aren’t there any usable parts?” he asked, looking up at the tree.

“Well, besides the sap, the fruit also contains petrinin. Once exposed to air, it cements in seconds.”

“I hope no one’s got a Hobby of eating that.”

Lei-rei paused.

No way.

“Other than that,” she continued, “I don’t think they’re usable. An arborist had developed these strains over a bet with a civil engineer. Something along the lines of, ‘Cement doesn’t grow on trees!’ ” She lazily gestured at the result. “Maybe you’ll want to make a bow out of cement. I don’t know.”

“Yeah, no, I’ll pass.”

Without any obvious way out, they ended up just standing around. Lei-rei sighed louder than he did.

“Is it safe to sit on the ground, at least?” Craft asked. There were random twigs and branches littering the forest floor from the surrounding trees. Who knew if they still had the petrifying effect?

Lei-rei sat down as her answer. He sat after her, relieved that they didn’t have to push on anymore, even though he knew they had to.

“As long as the wood’s been dead for a while, the petrinin would’ve solidified,” she explained. She sighed again.

“You’ve been like that since before we left,” Craft said. “You okay?”

“No.”

“Appreciate the honesty.” He hesitated for a moment. “Well, do you wanna talk about the reason?”

Lei-rei licked her lip and gulped, casting her gaze downward. “No.”

There went that. He gazed into the distance, hoping that the silence would let Lei-rei collect herself.

It should. At least one of them should be rested. With silence his natural enemy, he didn’t expect to be the rested one.

“How about you?” Lei-rei asked.

He looked at her. “What?”

“You’ve been more gung-ho about helping Nightshade than I would have expected. Any reason for that?”

There it was. He couldn’t tell her he was doing this just because it was the “right thing” to do—that it had been eating him alive, all this time, how he felt no satisfaction, no real reward, from doing anything “good.”

One day, he might. It was vague. All he could do was glance at Lei-rei.

“May I ask about your past?” she asked instead.

He looked at her again. He could tell she was shifting gears for his sake. Still, the past was the past, and few things in it could be considered small talk. “Like?” he said.

“How about your favorite weapons?”

Oh? He chuckled. “Here I was wondering what it’d be.”

“Well?”

“I don’t know.”

She raised an eyebrow. “Don’t tell me there’s nothing about the feel of a weapon that gets you?”

Perhaps in a world of sword and magic, he’d share such a sentiment. Modern weaponry, however, tended to be loud, obnoxious, and kick like a mule. Oh, he was good with them—just not on good terms.

“Well, if we expand the definition of ‘weapon’ a bit”—he scratched his chin—“wire cutters.”

Lei-rei eyed him dubiously. She made a snipping motion with her fingers. “For…piano wire?”

“Ah, no, you see, in my world, we run on a lot of electronic technology.”

“Is that some kind of magic?”

He was about to say no, but then again, if one squinted at the physics of electromagnetic forces, they’d end up in the quantum realm—modern sorcery.

“Probably,” Craft said. “It’s related to the radio waves I mentioned back then, but in reality, we use metallic wires more often than radio waves in most applications.”

“I see, but why wire cutters? I would have thought leveraging magic would be more enticing.”

It tickled his funny bone somewhat to hear someone refer to something so normal and known as electricity as ‘magic.’

“I was usually tasked to get in and out of tricky places. Sometimes it’s a gold suit’s villa, other times it’s an anomaly.”

“Anomaly? The terminology is throwing me off, but are you talking about dungeons and magic zones?”

Craft narrowed his eyes and shook his head. He smiled and chuckled. “We’re having a terminological breakdown, are we? Translation’s lost just as we are.”

Lei-rei quietly chuckled. “Hey, so, wire cutters.”

“Right. Because I’m the guy they go to when the team needs a creative entrance and exit, let’s just say, I’ve gotten creative with how to use wire cutters.” Craft looked down at the ground. His smile receded somewhat. “I actually hate killing, you know, but wire cutters, they’re stupid powerful, and they don’t kill. If I snip the right places, I can black out entire areas and take down fortresses, and all the effort needed is just a little retraction of my fingers.”

He extended them and pulled. “Like this.” He put his hand down, and he looked at Lei-rei with a smile. “I can do my job guilt-free. I ended up pretty good at it, and apparently, even anomalies had wires of their own.”

In the corner of his eye, the point distortion zipped by. He sighed.

“Can’t really snip the core, though. Funny it’s one letter off from the tactic that actually works.”

Lei-rei furrowed her brows as she processed that. Snip… Snip(e). She looked at him a little disappointed, and he laughed.

He laughed, but he was guilty. Lei-rei smiled—without teeth, but he knew it was real—as if she genuinely enjoyed his company. Yet, here he was covering up his angst and everything that, intuitively, he knew should see the light, or else he would corrode from the inside.

His smile disappeared, and he flipped up his hand as if giving up. “If I give up here, I’m just afraid nothing might change.”

It was part of the truth, but not enough for her to help him. He tried to remind himself of his victories instead, but they turned out too small to celebrate.

No matter how much he knew he had actually changed, it all just felt the same.

“You don’t feel much, do you?” Lei-rei said.

He was like a deer in headlights when he looked at her. Shit. He quickly turned away, but there was no hiding it anymore.

Then again, she had been the same as him. Of course she knew how it was like. He faced her again, not quite managing to look her in the eye, but showing this much should be alright.

“Is it wrong?” he asked. The moment he did, the tightness in his chest lifted.

“No.”

That was enough of an answer for him.

“I do want to be Nightshade’s friend,” he added, “but I’m incomplete. I can’t even do her a favor without meaning it. It doesn’t seem right.”

“Do you intend it?”

“How can I if—”

“You thought it, wanted it, said it, and even while you’re tortured over it, you’re still doing it. The seed’s still there.”

He looked at her with somber surprise. Her words had brought him sobriety, and he felt an unsettling clarity in his mind that matched the silence of the forest.

There were few things that could be said in times like these. Once again, he owed her, and he could see a future where he kept on owing her. The weight of all that future debt was enough for him to give up on repaying it all.

“How about you?” he said.

“What?”

“What’s your favorite weapon? That iron on your hip?”

“Knives, actually. They’re versatile.” She sighed. “If only my stats were higher, I’d be able to throw them, then I wouldn’t worry about lacking range.”

Craft chuckled…but he had an odd sense that there was a word he wasn’t fully understanding.

“ ‘Stats’ ?”

They turned their heads and met gazes, furrowing their brows as each felt out the other’s confusion.

“Have you not been briefed about your Status?” Lei-rei said—froze, and covered her mouth, looking away. “You might have something useful in there.”

Craft tilted his head. “ ‘Status’ ?”
