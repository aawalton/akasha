“Hello, you’re through to Bracken Berry Farm animal rescue. This is Surina speaking. How can I help you?” the cheerful voice on the other end of the phone said.

The greeting was familiar. It was the exact same one that I’d heard when I’d called up every single day for the last six days. It didn’t matter who answered the phone. They all said the same thing. The only thing that ever changed was the name and their tone. Sally was slightly less cheerful than Surina, and Norman sounded much less happy, but Phillipa was always the most perky, no matter how early I called.

“Hi, this is Grace,” I said, almost tripping over my words in my eagerness to speak.

“Ah, hello again, Grace,” Surina replied. “How are you today?”

“I’m good, thanks. How are you?”

That wasn’t the question I wanted to ask, but I knew I had to say that before I could get on to the real reason I’d called. Otherwise, it would have been rude, and I didn’t want to be rude to one of the few people who could stand in the way of me getting the one thing I’d been thinking about all week.

It had been so hard. I’d barely been able to focus on anything else. No matter what I was doing, my thoughts kept wandering back to the dog that I hoped was waiting for me at the animal rescue. Even when I was at the Academy with Seth or when I’d snuck off to Duncan’s house, he was in the back of my mind.

“I’m very well, thank you. I assume I know why you’re calling this morning?” Surina said, the smile audible in her voice.

“Yes!” I blurted out. “Is he still there?”

There was a pause, and I couldn’t help but hold my breath. My heart pounded in my ears, and fear surged through me with each pulse. I was terrified that Surina was about to tell me that the dog we’d brought to the vet the week before had been picked up by his old owner, and the urge to cry built within me as the silence stretched on.

We were so close. We’d gotten so close to being able to adopt him, and I was so worried that it wasn’t enough. The vet had told us we needed to wait seven days after finding him to adopt him in case his old owners got in contact and claimed him.

He’d spent one day at the vets and then six days at the animal rescue centre, and I’d called up every single day to ask if he was still there. They’d said they’d let me know if his owner got in contact or they had any news. I didn’t need to keep calling, but I couldn’t stop myself from doing it.

I was worried it would annoy them, though. I was scared that if I called up too often, they’d get irritated and tell me to stop. They could block my number or lie and say someone had claimed him. They never seemed too annoyed by me, but I still tried not to bother them too much.

“He’s still here,” Surina announced.

I had to press a hand to my lips to stifle the cry that threatened to escape. Immediately, tears welled in my eyes, and I could feel myself shaking. I was trembling with relief and excitement.

“He is?”

“Yes!” Surina confirmed happily. “And, as I’m sure you’re well aware, the required seven days have now passed, so he can officially be adopted if you and your parents are still interested in him?”

“We are!” I cried.

I hadn’t asked my mom. In fact, I hadn’t even mentioned the dog to her since last week, and Mom hadn’t brought him up either. She’d made a few comments when I was tidying the house in preparation for the dog, just a couple of mutters about how I must be blind because I’d missed some things, or did I not realise that my room also needed to be spotless, but that was it. I’d expected more.

Mom still wanted to adopt the dog, though. I knew that for certain. I’d been trying to resist the urge to call the rescue multiple times a day, restricting myself to just twice, but when I spoke to Sally the other evening, she’d mentioned that she’d just gotten off the phone to someone else who’d asked about the dog.

That had terrified me at first. My mind had automatically jumped to the worse-case scenario, and I assumed it was his owner, but Sally confirmed it was Mom. She’d also been asking about the dog that had been brought in last week. Apparently, she wanted to check his injuries were healing alright.

“Oh, great!”

“So…” I started. “Can we come get him?”

A kind laugh came from the other end of the phone.

“Give me a minute. Let me just pull up your file and check whereabouts you are in that process,” she said.

“Okay.”

I waited anxiously as the clack of fingers on a keyboard sounded loudly through the speaker. The urge to say something more, to try and hurry her along, pulled at me, but I forced myself to remain quiet.

“Remind me of your surname, Grace,” Surina requested.

“Barker.”

“Barker, Barker, Barker,” she muttered as she searched for my name. “Okay, here we go! You already filled in and submitted the form online for us, didn’t you?”

“Yes!”

I did that last week. One of the receptionists had mentioned that we could do it before the dog was technically available for adoption to help speed things up, so I’d filled it out immediately. There were a bunch of weird questions on there, things like how important was it that my perfect dog liked travelling in the car, and I had no idea how to answer that, so I just added a note that it didn’t really matter to me. I just wanted the dog we’d brought in last week.

“Great, I’ve got that here. Thanks for doing it,” Surina said. “It looks pretty good, I think…”

My heart felt like it skipped a beat as she trailed off, and questions immediately raced through my mind. Had I made a mistake on the form? Was that what she’d noticed? Or maybe I’d missed a question. Or perhaps there was a whole page I’d skipped straight over. I could have sworn I’d checked it before I’d submitted it, but I was worried nonetheless.

“Is there a problem with it?” I couldn’t help but ask.

“No, no,” the receptionist was quick to say. “Sorry, I was just reading the head handler’s comments.”

That scared me even more.

“Oh. Is it… is everything okay?”

“Yes, it’s fine! I can confirm that we’ve got all of the information we need from you, which is fantastic,” she said. “Your house appears suitable for a dog. The garden is a nice size, and your dad finished fixing the fence, right?”

“Yeah. He sorted that last week,” I told her. “The dog shouldn’t be able to get under it again.”

He’d started working on it pretty much straight away when I’d told him about what had happened, and I’d helped up. We spent ages in the garden that night, getting rid of the old chicken wire and replacing it with plastic wrapped wiring that we buried beneath a thick layer of dirt to make sure the dog wouldn’t be able to get underneath it again.

Mom didn’t even complain about the fact that we’d had to dig up the grass to do it. She came out to watch us work for a little bit, and she even brought us some water, which was a nice surprise. I’d asked her if she wanted to join us and help, but she’d refused, claiming the smell of the sheep in the field behind the house gave her a headache.

“Wonderful,” Surina said.

I waited for her to continue, barely able to breathe. I could hear her tapping something on the other end of the phone, but it didn’t sound like she was typing. The rhythm was too regular, too steady.

“So… what happens next?” I asked, unable to stand the silence for any longer.

“Next, we need to organise an appointment for you to come and meet your potential dog again so you can socialise with them a little bit,” she said before pausing. “Just to confirm, you don’t have any other pets in your household already, do you?”

That question had been on the form I’d filled in. She should have known the answer already, and I had no clue why she’d asked it again, but it made me a little nervous. I hoped it was just policy or something.

“No, we don’t.”

“Okay, fantastic.”

There was another pause, and I could feel a question bubbling up within me. I tried to hold it back; I pressed my lips together in an attempt to keep it in, but it still managed to get out.

“Can we take him home today?”

I’d asked that question a couple of days ago, and I hadn’t gotten a straight answer to it. Norman had said he’d need to double-check and let me know, but I hadn’t heard from him again, and when I’d called up that evening, Sally had answered the phone. I was too scared to ask her in case it was bad news, but I couldn’t stop myself from asking Surina.

“Normally, we require a couple of visits with you and your potential dog, overseen by at least one of our animal handlers,” Surina explained. “It’s just to make sure you’re both comfortable with each other, and there are no obvious problems or concerns, but I know your situation is a little different because you’ve already met before, right?”

“Yeah. We found him and brought him into the vets last week,” I said.

“Of course. There is a note here that explains that. So, as long as your visit today goes well, and the head handler signs it off, you should be able to take him home.” Surina paused before adding, “I assume you want to come in and see Rex today?”

Happiness soared within me, and I almost sobbed, but I managed to clamp my lips together just in time to hold it back. It was tough, though. I was just so happy and relieved. It felt like I’d been waiting so long to see the dog again, and it was finally happening. We could go and get him today, and then I’d never have to wait so long to see him again.

“Yes!” I cried before my mind caught up with the rest of what she’d said. “Wait… Rex?”

There was a slight pause before the receptionist spoke again.

“Oh, I am so sorry! That’s just what we’ve taken to calling him here. I’m not sure where the name came from or who gave it to him, but he seems to respond to it,” Surina explained, her tone apologetic. “Our handlers can advise you on renaming him, if you’d prefer.”

Before I could speak, I had to swallow down the lump that had formed in my throat, blocking anything from escaping.

“No, no. Rex is fine,” I heard myself say, but the words sounded distant and far away.

I recognised the name. I’d heard it before. No. It was more than that. I’d said it before, screamed it desperately as people lunged towards me, prepared to attack. I could see the anger that twisted their faces, the viciousness that burned in their eyes before I’d cried out for him to come and save me, but I didn’t know it was him.

It felt like all of the oxygen had been sucked out of the room. I was gasping for air, swallowing mouthfuls of it, but it wasn’t enough to chase away the lightheadedness that pulled at me and made my vision hazy as the name Rex ricocheted through my mind.

Surely, it wasn’t the same Rex, though. It couldn’t be. I thought the Rex from that world, the one where I had been trapped in a hospital where they constantly experimented on me for however long, was a person. It was a weird name for a person to have. I just assumed it must have been a nickname or something, but what if it wasn’t?

Screams echoed in my ears, but it wasn’t my own voice crying out for him anymore. It was the ones that had followed. I’d heard the shouts of terror and pain that had rang through the hospital corridors and seen the terror on Andrew’s face as he asked me what I had done, but I hadn’t done anything. Not really. I’d just called out for Rex.

That Rex was not a dog, I told myself. He couldn’t be. But even as I tried to convince myself of that, other questions slipped insidiously into my mind. If it were him, if that Rex had been a dog the whole time, did that mean he was the same dog as from the other world? The one where I’d been cast out of the village as a sacrifice to the monsters that waited beyond the gate? That dog, if that’s even what it was, was certainly large and scary enough to instil terror in anyone who saw it.

But he didn’t scare me. He’d never done anything to hurt me in any of the worlds. In fact, it was the opposite. That dog seemed to always show up when I needed him most, and he protected me. He chased others away from me and helped me escape. Perhaps he was just doing that again.

I was being ridiculous, I thought before shaking my head to clear it. My Rex, the one I’d saved from the fence at the back of my garden, was not big. He was not scary. He was just a puppy, and there was no way he could be related to the Rex from any of the other worlds. It was just a weird coincidence, and there was nothing more to it than that.

“How does that sound?” Surina asked.

Panic swelled within me as I realised she had been talking the entire time I’d been thinking about Rex, and I had not heard a word. I had no clue what she’d said or what she’d asked me, but I couldn’t ask her to repeat herself. That would be rude, and I didn’t want her to know I hadn’t been listening to her.

“Yeah, sounds great,” I said, hoping that was the correct answer.

“Okay, fantastic!” Surina replied, her tone cheerful. “Well, in that case, we’ll see you at twelve. Oh, and just to let you know so you can warn your mom. Before we can let you take Rex home, you need to have an ID tag for his collar. All dogs need to have an ID tag with their owner’s contact details whenever they’re out in public, but you can buy one here if you don’t have one already, and we have a machine that engraves it, so that’s not a problem at all.”

“Oh, okay. Good.”

“We also have a selection of beds, collars, leads, toys, and food that you can purchase today, but if you’d prefer to shop elsewhere, we can also advise you on what would suit Rex best when you come in today,” Surina told me. “Or do you plan on going shopping before you come in?”

“Uhhh…” I said, unable to come up with a response. “I’m not sure.”

I hadn’t even asked Mom whether we could pick him up yet, so I had no clue what to say to Surina. I assumed we’d go shopping for stuff for Rex at some point, and it made sense to do that before we went to get him, but I wasn’t sure whether Mom would want to do that.

“That’s completely fine. You or your mom can always give us a call back if you need anything, and I can grab one of the vets or handlers to give you guys some info. How does that sound?”

“Yeah, great,” I said before adding, “thank you.”

“Not at all!” Surina replied. “Well, I think that’s everything I need to tell you for now, and we’ve already spoken to your mom about the fees and everything, so I think we’re good! I’ll give you or your mom a call back if I do think of anything else, but if not, I’ll see you in a few hours, Grace!”

“Thanks! See you then!”

The phone went silent as Surina hung up, and I slowly lowered it away from my ear, staring at it in shock. I felt the need to check the number. I wanted to make sure I was talking to the right person and that there hadn’t been any mix-ups or mistakes, because it felt too good to be true.

I just… couldn’t believe it was happening. It was what I wanted. I hoped I’d be able to adopt the dog, but I was almost certain it wouldn’t happen. Every time I called the animal rescue, I was convinced they were going to tell me someone else had claimed Rex, that he was already gone, and I’d never see him again.

Rex.

That was his name. Surina had said we could change it. We could call him something else, but I didn’t want to. That was his name, and I knew it was. It wouldn’t be right to change it to something else just because even thinking the name filled me with uncertainty and fear.

Those weren’t the only emotions it made me feel, though. There was another one simmering beneath the surface, one that was less unpleasant than the others. Relief. A sense of relief and calmness that I didn’t expect had settled over me, and for some reason, I just felt safe. Perhaps Rex really was there to protect me, I thought with a slight smile, but it fell from my face quickly, chased away by anxiety.

I needed to go and speak to my mom. I’d agreed to see Rex at twelve, and I had to tell Mom and make sure she was okay with it. I assumed she would be. She seemed eager to adopt him, just like I was, but I was worried she’d get mad at me for agreeing to it without speaking to her first.

Throwing my duvet off me, I sat up and swung my legs over the side of my bed before freezing in confusion. I looked down at my pyjama shirt in confusion, trying to work out why I’d felt something wet landing on it, before raising a hand to my face.

Tears, I realised. I was crying. I lifted my phone, swiping across on it to pull up my camera and stared at my reflection. Tears spilled down my cheeks, and I hadn’t even noticed before. I had no idea I was crying, but I had to stop. I couldn’t go speak to Mom whilst I was still crying. If she knew how happy and excited I was, it might make her change her mind, and I couldn’t risk that.

I had to go to the bathroom first. Then, I could splash some cold water on my face, and that should help hide the blotchiness that was spreading over my cheeks. It normally did. And I should probably brush my teeth. I hadn’t had a chance to do it yet. I’d called the rescue as soon as I’d woken up, but I couldn’t go speak to Mom whilst I still had morning breath. Avoiding anything that could potentially put her in a bad mood was the best idea.

Slowly, I rose from the bed. My legs shook, and my knees threatened to give way and send me tumbling back onto my mattress, but I managed to take a stumbling step towards the door before taking another. Within seconds, I’d reached my bedroom door, but I stopped there and took a deep breath.

Wiping the tears from my cheeks, I held my breath and listened as hard as I could, trying to work out whether it was safe for me to emerge from my room. The television was playing loudly in the distance, but that didn’t guarantee that Mom was in the lounge. Sometimes she left it on whilst she wandered aimlessly around the house, and that worried me, but I couldn’t hear any other indicator of where she was.

Taking another deep breath, I reached out, my fingers closing around the door handle. I twisted carefully, making sure not to let it make any noise, before pulling it open just a tiny bit and peering through the crack. Mom wasn’t in the hallway. I couldn’t see her, and I strained my ears, listening out for the faint rustle of fabric in the room across from mine or the soft creak of the floorboards beneath her feet, but I heard no hint that Mom was nearby.

My heart fluttered in my chest as I waited a moment longer before pulling the door open just wide enough for me to fit through and stepping out into the hall. No noise came. Mom’s voice didn’t ring out, demanding to know why I was sneaking about. I was just met with the distant echo of canned laughter. A smile came over my face as I padded silently towards the bathroom and slipped inside.
