Gil told us everything.

Agnes had been the first. Her husband woke one night, skin hued blue and every breath a wet rattle. She’d run for Snowmelt beneath a moonless sky and found something sinister prowling through the forest.

When the deal was made and she returned, her beloved slept peacefully. None the wiser. And on some nights afterwards, when a lone traveler stayed the night and her husband lay fast asleep, Agnes hung a lantern in her garden. Baltha would stalk to an unlocked inn door and a victim fast asleep beneath mumbled magic.

Josiah and Gil were next. She’d come upon them in the mountains, after an evening spent lost amidst looming crags. The price of their service had been Baltha’s oath to harm no one in town. And in return, they’d feed her tirelessly. The two men spent years hunting game in frozen silence. Delivering it to the fetid mouth of the cave. Cornering town livestock when wildlife became scarce.

Aveline and Ward had been out for a tryst. Crept into the cave, hands and mouths too distracted to see what slouched out of the darkness. Begged for their lives. Granted, but the animals were too scarce. There weren’t enough solitary visitors to Agnes’ inn.

And the hag was always hungry.

She supplied the group with herbs. Hissed wicked words over each batch. Trembling hands slipped them into drinks here and there, when the hag was restless and the game had gone utterly scarce. The men would take turns or work in tandem, smuggling a sleeping victim out to a cage, and anyone else at the inn would question the fogginess of their memory but be none the wiser.

And then Thom. He’d been drinking in an alley. Saw the others carry a young man from the inn. Should’ve run and raised an alarm. Instead, he followed. Tried to stop them. And they’d brought him to the cage, where Baltha had loomed and grinned and cackled and he’d sworn his service a thousand times over.

Gil wept, cursing himself throughout the tale. There were no other pacted. He swore it.

Captain Rell listened in stony silence. Told Lewen to gather the other Watch. He returned with leather-clad, bleary-eyed men and a spare sword and jerkin for Renner. One guard helped Gil stand and led him slowly into the night. Lewen found boots and warm cloaks for Renner and I, along with Teela’s clothes.

At some point during Gil’s tale, I started feeling numb. From exhaustion, from dread, from cold. From the memories of blows against my face, of an impossibly strong hand wrapped around my throat, of my body ground into warm earth as everything went black. Of a cruel smile and hands slick with blood.

The inn was too small. I couldn’t breathe. I stared without seeing. Flinched back whenever someone came too close. Wondered what would happen to Gil. Wondered what would happen to me. Lewen tried to draw me towards the fire. We got close and I jerked violently away. Burnt flesh, screaming, too hot too close-

Someone said something. Warmth against my side. Thin arms sliding around my shoulders. Guiding me to a seat. Teela’s forehead nuzzled against mine. I leaned into her. Breathed.

Lewen put food in front of me. I stared at that, too. Grim voices talked and planned. They all seemed very far away.

More sounds. The hiss of leather fastened over cloth. Booted feet on wood. A cool rush of air as the inn door opened.

A rough voice telling me to stay awake.

And they left. Teela and I remained pressed together, alone save for the innkeeper. Master Lewen fussed over us, asking what he could do and how he could help. I offered no answers, sinking into my numbness.

Teela asked him for blankets and tea. When they arrived she draped us in soft cloth and began humming, smoothing bony fingers through my matted hair.

We stayed like that for hours. Lewen sat, then paced, then fussed, then did it all again and again.

When the sky outside began to lighten, I finally stirred. “Parchment.”

The innkeeper had been half-dozing in a chair. He bolted upright at my voice, rubbing red-rimmed eyelids. “Wha-? Did you say something, Miss?”

I cleared my throat. “Do you have any parchment? And ink? I… I need to write a letter.” I swallowed. Clenched my fists. “Two. Two letters. Please.”

He looked relieved at being given a task. “I’ll find you some, Miss. Quick as you please.” He practically ran out the front door.

Teela yawned beside me. “Letters?”

I nodded stiffly.

She stretched. “Okay. Eat.”

The porridge before me was long-cold, but I obediently took a few bites. It didn’t seem to have any flavor, but the mere action of eating urged some strength back into my body.

“Are you scared?” my friend whispered. Lashes down, shoulders drooping, hair in disarray. It framed her angular face like a manic red halo.

I took another bite. “Yes.”

“I’m not… gonna tell anyone. You know that, right?” Garnet eyes peered at me intently. “Of course I’m not.”

I managed a faint smile, but it faded almost immediately. “Thanks. I… I don’t know what Renner’s going to do.”

She swallowed. “Yeah. He seems… really mad.”

We pressed into each other again, settling into the silence. When Lewen opened the door again and approached me with several pieces of parchment, a glass bottle of viscous black, and a quill, Teela was snoring quietly.

“Thank you,” I murmured, and got to work.

The first page was easy.

Well. Relatively.

I stared at it for a very long time before dipping my feathered quill into the ink. Pressed down with deliberation and furrowed brows, tongue trapped between my teeth.

My name is Brin Damelle. I have been pulled, through my dreams, into the Fae realm. This is what I have learned.

I filled the parchment with every scrap of knowledge I thought might be useful. What he’d said about Courts and High Fae, about witchwood and the sword, about some of his kind wanting to come back to the mortal realm. Every detail I thought might be important, and a hundred things that probably were not.

I filled both sides with tidy script. Signed it and added, at the bottom, a wry caveat.

All of this information was given to me by a self-proclaimed High Fae of the Night Court. So… perhaps everything was a lie. Perhaps none of it is actually true. Sorry I can’t be more helpful.

The next letter was more difficult. The sky was a haze of orange and gold when I wrote, Dear Durst at the top. Then I tapped and traced my fingertips over the smooth parchment, shifted and sniffled and bit back tears and shoved it away and pulled it back and sighed.

I spent a while looking at Teela. Memorized the visage of her peaceful face. Whispered that I was so sorry for the nightmare she’d been through. Next I crept towards the innkeeper. Murmured my destination. Asked him to tell Renner where to find me. And Teela, if she woke before I returned.

Then I opened the door, wrapped the cloak around my shoulders, and started for the woods.

Chilly air and rustling pines kept me alert as I walked. And the promise of what lay ahead. I found it easily enough, following my trail of snapped twigs.

The rot had faded. There were still scattered patches of withered green and decayed leaves, but the awful smells were replaced with crisp pine and nutty earth. The oily black smears along the ground and trees were just patches of brown bark and damp soil, now.

And there it was. My witchwood.

Exactly how I’d left it. Silver trunk split in two, ravaged and gouged. Black leaves drooping in dark dirt.

“I’m back.” I knelt before the trunk. Threads of stark crimson gleamed within the scored gray flesh. “I promised, remember?”

I bit my lip, waiting, and after a moment the familiar rustling began. Feather-soft black stretched up. Joyful, weary tears slipped down my cheeks as shaking fingers tangled into the leaves. “There you are. I’m here. I’m going to help you. Soon, alright? Really soon.”

I folded the first letter and slid it into a pocket. Then I produced the second, braced it against my knees, and wrote.

It took a while, but I found words that were adequate. Not perfect, but perhaps goodbyes never were.

Thoroughly exhausted, I leaned my head back against the bark. Let my eyes drift shut. “Don’t let me fall asleep yet, okay?”

Velvet brushed at my hair. My wet cheeks. Murmured.

“Thanks,” I whispered.

I was so lulled by the feeling, I hardly registered the sudden sounds ahead.

Boots crunching on dead leaves. Fast. Running. The hiss of steel pulled from a sheath.

My eyes snapped open.

Renner was coming at me. Sword drawn.

Oh saints, he changed his mind he’s going to kill me what do I-

“Wait!” I screamed. I raised both hands palm-up, ducking my head behind them. “Wait, at least until we get back to town!”

He seized one wrist, yanking me forwards. His face was white. After three steps his grip loosened. “Are you insane?!”

I staggered away, gaze fixed on the sword. “L-look, if you really can’t forgive… just wait, alright? So we can get you to a cleric.”

He was panting. “...What?” He followed my gaze and, after a moment’s pause, sheathed the weapon. “Why do I need a cleric?”

My shoulders sagged in relief even as confusion knit my brows together. Did he change his mind again? Just like that? “Well, for when… look, if I don’t fulfill my end of the deal, what the Fae did for you and Teela gets undone. So if you’ve decided to kill me, we should wait until you two are somewhere safe. Where people can help.” I massaged my temples wearily. “I don’t know what they can do for Teela. But I think with immediate help, you might be alright. I… I don’t know for sure, though. It looked bad, and I never got much practice tending to head wounds. The clerics in town seemed very capable, though.”

Renner had gone very still. He was staring at me.

I swallowed, shifting from foot to foot and feeling suddenly quite nervous under the weight of his gaze. “O-oh. And, here, if you… you should take this.” I delved into my pocket, withdrawing the carefully folded letters. I held the first out, fingers trembling.

He looked at the parchment like it might bite him. “What is that?”

“It’s for you. It’s…” Frosty wind ruffled my hair, making the pines above us hiss. “Look, it’s not quite as good. As me, I mean, or as the shard. But I… I thought if you decided to turn me in here in Snowmelt, or if you… well,” I gestured to his sword, wincing, “Y-you know. Or if I… if I don’t… w-wake up after I…”

Get it together. Breathe. This’ll all be over soon, one way or another. I just need to stay strong for a little bit longer. I inhaled deeply, grounding myself with the scents of crisp, fresh firs and damp earth.

I squared my shoulders, offering the parchment again. “Take it. It’s everything I thought might be useful. Everything he told me, everything I saw and heard. I don’t know if it’ll be enough, but I thought you could take it to the Order. If it is valuable information, maybe it’ll be enough for you to get their help.”

Renner searched my face intently as he took the letter, shaking his head back and forth ever so slightly.

Hot tears welled, blurring my vision as I held out the other piece of parchment. “A-and if you… if it’s not too much trouble, c-could you give this to captain Rell? I wrote instructions.”

“What’s this one?” He sounded uncharacteristically subdued.

“It’s f-for Durst. I was hoping the next time someone here heads south, they could deliver it. It’s not enough, not nearly, but I thought… h-he doesn’t have anyone, and I’m w-worried… I just want him to know I tried my best, and that I l-love him and I’m so grateful to h-have had him and it’s not his f-fault and…”

Breathe. Breathe. Just a little bit longer.

“Really think you’re not gonna wake up, huh?” Renner’s voice was soft. Almost hoarse.

“I… I don’t know. He’ll get what he wants, and you saw… you s-saw what he was l-like.” My throat closed. What he was really like.

His expression hardened. “Yeah. Yeah, I saw. And you know, I did some thinking on my walk up. You said you’d give it to him, right? Well, shoving the damn thing in his throat probably counts.”

The words startled me into a weary, defeated laugh. “I don’t think I have it in me to-”

“I think you do,” he growled, voice dropping to a husky pitch. The sudden intensity made a shiver run down my spine. “And… look, ladyship, I’m done telling you what to do. Keeps backfiring on me. But I think you should try.” He drew closer. “And if that breaks your deal, fine. Teela’s asleep at the inn, they’ll take care of her.”

“You can’t mean that. Your head-”

“There’s better men than me all over the salted realm.” He pulled the little sword fragment from a pocket and thrust it towards me, eyes simmering. “If the back of my skull falling out means you make that thing bleed, I’ll take the trade.”

I sniffled, wrapping my fingers around the metal. It felt oddly cool. “I’d rather your skull stay in place, Renner.”

“Same. Still, though. Don’t hesitate on my account.”

My lashes lowered. “I… mm.”

“Yeah?” He moved closer, very slowly.

“N-nothing. Just… thanks. Thank you. For getting this. I didn’t… I was afraid, earlier. Well. I still am. Kind of just how things are, lately.” My chest tightened with a bitter laugh. “B-but, going back to the inn… I couldn’t do it. I couldn’t.”

He grimaced, tousling his blood-matted hair. “Wasn’t pretty.”

“Oh, salt, I didn’t even think about… is everyone alright? The captain and the guards?”

“There wasn’t any danger. Seems like they all felt it when the hag dropped. Like Gil. Ward and Aveline took off in the middle of the night. Rell’s going after them as we speak.”

“And the others?”

“The older two were sitting outside when we got there. Surrendered right away.” He shook his head, mouth warping into a scowl. “Had the bodies laid out, wanted help burying them. Didn’t want the kid’s parents to see… well. You know.”

I wrapped my arms around my middle, shivering. “How awful.”

“Yeah. I didn’t stick around.”

“Thank you,” I whispered again. “For going. And coming back. And… for a lot of things, really. For changing your mind.”

“About what?”

I gestured to the sheathed blade. “Y-you know. A moment ago you were ready to kill me, and I know you’re angry at me but I think being killed by a friend would be a really terrible way to die, so… thanks.”

Brown eyebrows shot up. “What… ladyship, this wasn’t for you.” He tilted his head towards the ravaged, fallen trunk, eyeing it warily. “It was for that.”

“What? A witchwood? That’s a crime, Renner!” I turned, stepping defensively towards the piteous tree. “And why would you-”

“Don’t.” His hand snapped out, wrapping around my wrist.

I blinked as he pulled me closer. “Don’t what?”

“Don’t touch the tree.”

“I thought you were done telling me what to do?”

He scowled. “Fine, please don’t touch the tree. This one’s barely alive, but you're bound to a Fae. Something in there wants to kill you.”

The declaration gave me pause, but only for a moment. “No… no, that can’t be right. I walked through the whole grove after what happened in Fellbrook, and none of the trees acted any differently.”

He slashed his free hand towards the limp branches. “Are you serious? Those things were all over you!”

“Renner, it’s nothing to be afraid of!” I disentangled my wrist, lips curving as I returned to the trunk. “They’ve always done that. Since I was a baby.”

Renner followed so closely that his breath ruffled the back of my hair. “Well, I’ve only seen them do that to things they kill.”

“It won’t hurt me. Watch.”

His only answer was an unintelligible grumble. Leaves crunched as he prowled in a restless half-circle just behind me.

“It’s time,” I murmured, kneeling before the silver bark. “I promised I’d try to help, remember? I’m sorry I couldn’t do it sooner.” Chilly fingertips traced jagged wood. “And I’m so sorry if you’re in pain. I’m going to do my best, alright?”

The leaves rustled. Reached. Behind me, metal hissed.

“It’s safe,” I promised again, glancing over one shoulder.

His face was a stone. The sword gleamed, half-drawn.

Always on edge. Always ready to fight. The thought came unbidden, and with it a fresh pang of sorrow. “Hey… Renner?” I turned, biting down on my lower lip.

“Yeah?” Copper flicked to my face.

I hesitated. What do I say? Sorry about the past few days? Thanks for not murdering me? Have a nice life? Good luck? None of it seemed quite right. But there was a chance that in a moment, when magic poured out and my eyes drifted closed, they would not open again.

So I had to say something.

I wrung my hem. Glanced around. Sniffled.

“Tell me later.” His voice was rough, but the way he looked at me was not. “When you wake up.”

I managed a watery smile. “N-no more telling me what to do, remember?” I returned my attention to the rustling witchwood. Velvet grazed my hands as pale light appeared beneath my twisting fingertips. “Luridel.”

There was a heavy exhalation of breath from behind me. Then, so soft I barely heard it, a quiet, “Please.”
