Chapter 112

They came in from the north at a hard pace.

Three riders, three horses, spears across the saddles, the horses breathing hard from the road. They dropped down through the same shallow draw the tribute road ran through, the same draw Korr-Va had come down days before at his working pace, and the trees opened onto the cleared ground around Hoshin, and the riders slowed.

What lay ahead of them was not a village.

The gate posts still stood, the two rough timber uprights that had held the woven fence between them, but the fence was gone. It had gone to a low line of black ash along the ground where it had been, and the posts themselves were scorched down one side and pale on the other, the way a thing stands after it has been eaten by fire from one direction only. Beyond the posts the huts were no longer huts. They were just rings. The stone hearths at their centers still held their shape, small circles of blackened rock sitting up out of the ash, and the shapes of the walls could be read on the ground in dark rectangles around the hearths, but the walls themselves were down. The thatch was gone entirely, burned to nothing and blown away in the small wind that had come through in the days since. What remained of the timber uprights had come down in long charred pieces that lay across the ash like the bones of animals lying where they had fallen.

The tribute mat had gone with the rest.

The square in the middle of the village was scorched earth. The dirt had been baked hard by the heat, and in patches the surface had cracked into the small brittle plates that came up when a fire ran long over ground. A wooden bowl lay near the edge of the square with one side burned clean off it. A cooking pot sat where a hut had been, its bottom fused to the flat hearth-stone under it. Nothing else moved.

No smoke rose. The fires had been dead for days.

The three riders drew up at the gate and did not speak.

The largest of them was the first to move. He swung down out of his saddle, both boots landing together and the weight of him hitting the ash with a heavy dull sound that did not carry the way it would have carried on packed dirt. The other two stayed mounted. He walked through the gap in the burnt line of the fence and into the square with his head lowered and his eyes on the ground, and he crossed the open ground slowly, from one side to the other and back again, reading it the way a hunter reads ground.

He stopped near the center of the square.

He stopped over a shape in the ash.

The shape was low and the shape looked human, and the shape had been burned enough that the outline of it was not obvious at first, the arms and the legs folded in against the body by the heat, the plate of the armor blackened to the color of the ash around it and half sunk into it. But the shape was there. The largest Clawborn crouched down beside it. He put one gloved hand out and touched the plate at what had been the shoulder, and he did not brush the ash away, only touched. He tilted his head. He leaned closer. He put his face down toward the burnt place at the throat where a leather cord had burned away leaving scoring barely visible from the char, and where the hilt of a long knife had stood up out of the collar it too had fallen away buried under the ash.

He grunted.

It was a single hard sound low in his chest, and it was not for the body and it was not for the sky. It was for the two men at the gate.

They swung down at once. They crossed the square at a fast walk and reached him without speaking, and when they reached him the largest Clawborn did not stand. He pointed, one single finger extended over the burnt shape at his boots, and the two of them looked down and understood.

The larger of the two reached in and grabbed, what looked like had been the ankles. The other took it by what had been the shoulders. They lifted together. Something in the burnt frame gave slightly under the lift and then held, and they carried him between them back across the square toward the horses, careful in their step, careful with the weight.

The largest Clawborn stood but he did not follow them.

He turned in place at the center of the square and looked out across the burnt ground, once, slowly, all the way around. He looked at the black rings where the huts had been. He looked at the ash where the mat had lain. He looked at the scorched posts of the gate and at the line of the fence gone. He looked at the tree line beyond the cleared ground, at the dark wall of it where the road came out.

He sniffed the air.

The air smelled of old fire and cold and the faint sharp thing that clings to burnt places for a long time after the fires are out, and he breathed it in through his nose in long slow pulls, and each pull moved his shoulders. His lips came back off his teeth. The teeth were long and yellow at the roots.

Behind him the other two had reached the horses.

They set the burnt shape down on the ash beside one of the animals and one of them unlashed a folded wool blanket from his saddle roll and shook it out on the ground, and together they moved the body onto the center of the blanket and drew the corners over it. They lashed the wrapped shape crosswise over the horse's back behind the saddle with the short cord they carried at their belts. The horse tossed its head once at the weight and settled. They tied the second and third lashings without hurrying. When they finished they mounted, one to each of the remaining horses, and they sat and waited.

The largest Clawborn was still at the center of the square.

He stopped and once more, another full slow turn, his faced hardened when a sound came out of the trees.

It was small. It was not a footstep and it was not a call. It was the sound an animal makes when it moves in undergrowth, or the noise a person makes when they are trying not to make any sound at all. It came from the north side of the tree line, above the road they had ridden in on, and it lasted for less than a breath.

His head snapped toward it.

He stood staring at the line of trees. His chest moved, his exhaled harder. The sniffing became audible, short quick pulls in through the nose, and his hands closed at his sides into fists that shook. He bared his teeth and his shoulders rose. His weight shifted from foot to foot, and the two Clawborn on the horses did not look at each other and did not look at him. They kept their eyes on the tree line.

He raised his fists.

The roar climbed up out of his chest and broke against the burnt village in one long sustained sound, deep and full, more chest than throat, the kind of sound that emptied a man and made him have to draw a whole new breath at the end of it before he could stand up straight. It rolled out across the ash, out through the burnt line of the fence and out into the trees, and the trees swallowed it, not send it back.

When it ended, he stood breathing.

Then he turned and stomped across the burnt ground toward his horse. He mounted in one motion. He set his spear across his saddle. He turned the horse's head north, and the other two turned with him, and the three of them rode out of the gate of Hoshin and back up the road they had come in on, at the same hard pace they had come in on, north toward the Clawborn citadel with the wrapped shape lashed to a horse.

The burnt village stood quiet behind them.
