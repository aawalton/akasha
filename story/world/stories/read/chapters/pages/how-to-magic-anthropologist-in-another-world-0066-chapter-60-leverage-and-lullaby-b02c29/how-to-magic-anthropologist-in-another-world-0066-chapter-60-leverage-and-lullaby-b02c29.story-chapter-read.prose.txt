Raik and Vena were still maintaining eye contact, neither of them blinking.They weren’t glaring, not quite. It was something a bit subtler than that.Each of them was trying to read the opponent.

Opponent was the wrong word. Rival, maybe.

“So?” Ja’a said finally, breaking the silence. “In a negotiation between friends, it is best to establish what the bottom line is.”

Her tone was calm, almost casual, but I could see the way her shoulders had squared. She was in her element now, full merchant mode, thinking in terms of leverage, concessions, and outcomes.

It made sense for her to lead the discussion. She had the most experience negotiating, after all.

Still, I remained on guard.

I did not want to impugn anyone’s honor, but I was not naïve. Ja’a would lean toward Raik. He was her childhood friend.

Not that I was any better.

I was clearly on Vena’s side.

If I divide the team into camps, our physical positions explain it all.

Katar and Ja’a stood nearer to Raik.And I stood closer to Vena.In the middle sat Kan and Shingo.

At first glance, one might assume Kan was on Raik’s side. She liked him, maybe even loved him. But she had been hurt in the past by whoever Raik owed allegiance to. Hano’s leadership and the Bloodline nobles.

Shingo was the only true neutral.

“What is the one thing you cannot tolerate?” Ja’a asked, looking between the two of them.

“I don’t want people invading my home,” Vena said immediately. She didn’t bother to soften it with political phrasing.

“That is reasonable,” Ja’a nodded.

Raik was next.

“I don’t want the new portal to fall under High Rock’s sphere of influence,” he said.

His tone was controlled, but there was steel beneath it.

“Good,” Ja’a said. “Now that we have established that, let’s talk about our fears. What do you believe will happen if your counterpart handles the portal knowledge?”

This time, Raik started.

“Currently, there is a balance in the Contested Realm,” he said. “The Bloodline Realm has an alliance with the Soul Realm through Hano and Tree Gate. We are not the best of friends, but we are allies.”

He began pacing slowly as he spoke.

“The Kindred Realm, through Tree Gate, is allied with the dwarves of the northern continent, who control access to the Sealed Realm gate and much of the ancient telepath technology.”

He crossed his arms as he continued.

“The dwarves and Tree Gate are at war. Hano and High Rock are close to open conflict. The Sky Islands, and by extension the Dreaming Realm, remain neutral. Historically, the Mythic Realm has always sided with the Kindred Realm.”

He paused.

“If this portal is discovered and controlled by Mythic authorities, the balance of power would shift toward High Rock.”

That was well thought out.

If the balance broke too far in one direction, it might plunge the Seven Realms into total war.

I felt the weight of that possibility between us.

We looked at Vena.

She nodded softly.

“Everything you said makes sense,” she said. “But allow me to be sincere.”

Her voice changed slightly, colder and harder than her usual soft-spoken cadence.

“I have lived in Hano for almost seven years now. I am going to be blunt.”

Raik stiffened, almost imperceptibly.

“I do not care about the global balance of power,” she said. “Your people, and by that I mean the nobles of Hano, whether they are Soulit or Bloodline, cannot be trusted with access to my home.”

That landed harsher than I expected.

Raik’s jaw tightened.

“Do not misunderstand me,” Vena continued. “There are good people among you. But I can only count them on one hand.”

Her gaze swept across the camp, then fixed briefly on Kan.

“Half of Hano is slums; people barely survive while nobles spend gold on frivolities, balls, and expensive parties, while grain rots in storehouses.”

She shifted her gaze from Kan to Katar.

“You still practice debt slavery under the names of serfdom and indentured service.”

Katar tried not to react, but I saw his shoulders go rigid. Was there something I was missing?

Vena was not finished.

“Things would be worse if not for the Holy Temple’s free healing, orphan care, and soup kitchens. Fifty temple volunteers doing more work than five thousand nobles.”

The anger in her voice was controlled, but unmistakable.

“Do not mistake me, Raik. You are a good man. So is Marina. So is your brother. But I would not trust the fate of my people to General Ta’o or Grand General Okar.”

Kan stood and simply walked to sit behind Vena. There was no speech, but her allegiance was declared all the same.

Raik looked hurt for a fraction of a second.

Then he nodded at Kan in understanding. The leadership of Hano had killed her father, after all. Why would she ever consider giving them more power?

Shingo remained seated in the middle.

We all looked at him.

The mute boy blinked slowly.

He pointed at Raik, then at Vena, and shook his head.

After a brief second, he pointed at me, then at Calr.

Finally, he lifted his hand and pointed toward the Crown of Laurel overhead.

We blinked.

Calr closed his eyes briefly, then nodded.

“You are saying, the right to decide what to do with the knowledge belongs to the ones who discovered it,” he translated. Shingo nodded in confirmation.

Kan chuckled softly. “That’s rudimentary loot distribution rules.”

Because Shingo was not wrong, Vena and Raik might argue all they wanted, but it was Calr and I who had discovered the knowledge. And it was our right, by ancient freelancer tradition, to do with it as we pleased.

For the first time since the argument began, I felt something close to terror.

I was an outsider, and I currently held power that could start a war that would make Earth World Wars seem like child’s play.

I swallowed and looked at Calr, who looked equally conflicted.

“Let’s start by listing our options,” I said, forcing my voice to remain steady.

“We could do nothing,” Calr offered. “This secret remained buried for three thousand years. It could remain buried for three thousand more.”

His tone was careful, almost hopeful, as if saying it plainly might make it possible. As if history could simply be ignored if we all agreed to look away.

“I would agree with you,” I said slowly, “if not for the fact that we know the Old Realm cult had a presence here recently. We might not agree about everything, but we can all agree that the cult would be the worst people to discover the gate.”

Everyone nodded at that, especially Raik.

The image rose unbidden in my mind. The cult was accessing cursed relics from the Mythic Realm, pulling horrors through a doorway we had uncovered by accident. That would be catastrophic.

“A compromise, then,” Calr said, looking at me. “I think both you and I agree that the Veil Holy Kingdom should hold the gate.”

There it was. The thought I had been circling was dragged into the open.

“Sorry, Raik,” I said, giving him a small wince. “From what I have read, the three Holy Kingdoms forbid the hunting of all sentient mythical creatures, including nymphs, djinn, and dryads. That courtesy is not offered by Hano. Morally, I have to stand with the Holy Kingdoms.”

I hesitated, then added more quietly, “Plus, it is the only way this can happen without causing a war.”

Raik frowned. “What do you mean? If the Mythic Realm controls the portal, it would guarantee a war.”

“No,” I said, shaking my head. “You are seeing the Mythic Realm as a monolith, when in fact it is not. The Pantheon is allied with the Kindred. But the Holy Kingdom is a rival of the Pantheon.”

I glanced at Vena before continuing.

“You are more likely to gain an ally in them than an enemy, if you play your cards right.”

Silence settled again, but this time it was heavy with calculation.

Calr stepped in smoothly. He was on the same wavelength as me. “If you make a formal agreement, you could negotiate with the Veil Kingdom to allow the Agame clan a presence in the new city they would build at the gate. In exchange, you help them establish themselves quickly in the Contested Realm and support them before other factions can overwhelm them.”

Raik did not answer immediately. I watched him think, truly considering the option for the first time instead of rejecting it out of instinct.

“Plus,” I added carefully, “if the Agame clan wanted to clear the Dead Zone, could you do it alone?”

Raik’s jaw shifted. “Not on our own. We would need help from at least two other clans. Maybe three.”

“Exactly,” I said. “Vena, how many Paladins could the Holy Kingdoms mobilize?”

She did not hesitate.

“Fifty Paladins. Forty Crusaders. Thirty Inquisitors. Alongside hundreds of knights and clerics. That is the number we mobilize whenever the Pantheon attempts to invade the mainland.”

A chill ran down my spine.

That was a full-fledged army. A Paladin was at least as strong as a freelancer lieutenant, if my estimation was correct. And a single Crusader had hunted rapists under the nose of all Hano’s authority for years without being caught.

“With Holy power being so effective here,” I said quietly, “I doubt they would need half that number.”

“They would not,” Calr agreed. “They would only need to sanctify a road to Ectamel and clear enough land to build a city around the gate.”

His eyes flicked toward the western horizon, toward the heart of the Dead Zone.

“The rest of the Dead Zone could remain,” he continued. “It would serve as a natural barrier. A defensive perimeter that prevents large-scale invasion.”

We all fell silent, imagining it.

The idea was simple, elegant, and brutal.

Turn the scar in the land into a defensive wall.

It would even be perfect for the economy. Hano relied on bones and death cores for fertilizer. Clearing the Dead Zone completely would be more detrimental.

“That is simply brilliant,” Ja’a said.

Calr tried not to react, but he could not quite stop himself from straightening at the praise.

Ja’a elbowed Katar. “And you said not to add him to the team because he would be useless.”

“I did not call him useless,” Katar frowned. “I called him a weakling.”

Calr deflated at that.

We all laughed. The pressure that had been building was finally released.

Raik then turned to Vena and bowed formally.

“Princess Vena Moonbearer,” he said, voice steady but solemn. “I would like to formally request an alliance between the Agame clan and the Veil Holy Kingdom.”

The shift in tone was immediate. This was no longer friends arguing around a campfire. It was official diplomacy.

“It would be my honor,” Vena replied, curtsying with equal formality. “Once we return to Hano, I will introduce you to Prince Marrek the Holy Messenger. We can negotiate the terms with him.”

She hesitated before adding, softer now, “But you must keep this quiet for now. You cannot tell anyone you do not completely trust.”

Raik nodded. The importance of that secrecy was not lost on him.

Then he asked carefully, “Do you believe the Veil Holy Kingdom would truly agree to ally with us?”

Vena met his gaze and nodded.

“If it prevents war with Hano and weakens the Pantheon’s influence in the Contested Realm, yes,” she said. “They would agree.”

“Plus, with that alliance, we can finally have access to Soulbooks,” she added. “That path to power was always blocked to us in the Mythic Realm.”

She spoke like a strategist, not a cleric blinded by faith.

I let out a breath I had not realized I was holding.

Both sides seemed satisfied.

An alliance had been forged in a situation that could have split us apart.

This had started as a random discovery I made, yet it had the potential to cause a ripple that could move armies, forge alliances, and erupt into wars. History in the making, and I did not like the fact that I was an active participant rather than a solemn observer.

Still, I could not shake the thought that history did not care about my intentions. And that my involvement was going to disturb the balance, whether I liked it or not.

When the negotiation was over, we were exhausted. At least I was.

“Can I get the last watch?” I said. “I think I overextended myself with too much teleportation.”

“You can all sleep,” said Katar. “I will be using my Sleepless Night Soulbook anyway.”

I went into the tent I shared with Ja’a and lay on my comfortable chaise. I was dead to the world seconds later. Dreams brushed against me without forming. I felt like I was this close to reaching my sanctuary, but I failed yet again.

Barely five hours later, morning arrived in a pale blue light. The first night was always shorter than the second. The gray fog was thick that morning, as if to compensate for last night’s clear sky. For a quiet minute, the world almost looked like an empty canvas waiting to be filled.

Thanks to Katar shouldering the watch on his own, we all looked rested.

We did not break camp so much as simply extinguish the fire. We left the tents pitched and the bedrolls in place.

We traveled west toward the lake.

The land changed as we walked. The cracked soil softened, darkened with moisture. The air grew cooler, cleaner. The oppressive weight I had felt since entering the Dead Zone eased for a bit, as if the death mana was not as thick here as everywhere else.

Then we crested a low rise.

The lake lay below us, large enough to reach the horizon.

It was… peaceful.

I expected drifting undead to be crowding its shores, or maybe ghosts haunting it. But no.

Instead, the lake was glassy and still, reflecting the sky in deep blues and greens. Reeds swayed gently along the edge. Tangled ribbons of dark kelp-like algae floated near the part of the shallows, vivid with life.

And moving through the water were shapes so large my mind refused to name them at first.

Giant sauropods.

They waded through the lake with slow, deliberate grace, their long necks dipping beneath the surface to graze on the algae before rising again, water cascading down their scales. Their bodies were massive, serene, and utterly unconcerned with the world around them.

“Ja’a, do you sense them?” asked Raik.

“Yes,” she said, eyes filled with reverence.

“How strong are they?” he asked.

“Between 30 and 50 SB,” she answered.

“Do you think they are monsters,” I asked, “or just regular beasts?”

“Definitely beasts,” said Calr. “They would be more aggressive if they were monsters, fighting with each other and jostling for space.”

“How come they didn’t turn, with how thick the Dead Zone is with death mana?” asked Kan.

Calr knelt, stuck his finger in the lake, and gave it a lick.

“It is as I thought. Salty,” he said. “The salt in the lake most likely warded away the death mana somehow.”

Deep down, I was glad they were animals and not monsters. I would not want to fight one of those: Living, breathing, and impossibly ancient creatures from Earth’s past thriving here in a place that should have been dead.

“Do you think we can take one of them in a fight?” said Kan.

“Maybe one,” said Katar. “Not a full herd, though.”

We stood there watching them in silence.

They must have been flourishing here. With no predators left in the Dead Zone, the lake had become a sanctuary. The algae bloomed unchecked, and these creatures had claimed it as their own.

Kan let out a low breath. “I could watch them all day.”

Vena’s expression softened in awe. “They’re beautiful.”

None of the sauropods reacted to us. We might as well have been rocks on the shore.

Something in me loosened.

We moved closer along the shore, carefully at first, then with growing confidence. The water lapped gently in waves, cool and clear. Fish darted near the surface, silver flashes in the shallows.

Suddenly, Ja’a started stripping. She removed the chain mail half of her armor, the metal elastic enchantment making it easy to take off. She remained wearing her spider silk bodysuit and plunged into the water, laughing as she cannonballed.

Two seconds later, Calr followed suit. He double-jumped midair before plunging headfirst into the deeper water.

They emerged and started waving at us to join them.

“Come on, the water is great,” said Calr.

“I don’t sense anything dangerous,” called Ja’a.

Raik hesitated. He clearly wanted to join in, but he also wanted to be a responsible leader and avoid reckless behavior.

“You can go,” said Katar. “I will remain on watch.”

A few seconds later, Kan and Raik were holding hands as they ran into the water.

Vena and I followed suit, while Shingo decided to remain on watch with Katar. I suspected it was because he could not swim, but I did not push him on that.

The silken under-armor worked perfectly as bathing suits, as they let the water through without getting too heavy like cotton would.

Calr started diving deep to check the lake bed, while Vena, Ja’a, and I started a water fight, splashing each other and laughing. I turned to attack Raik and Kan, only to notice that they had started kissing, acting like they were alone in the world.

I hesitated. Ja’a did not. She almost drowned the pair of them with a water attack. When the couple tried to retaliate, she turned invisible, leaping away from the water using flight to hide her silhouette before counterattacking.

I could not help but laugh at her shenanigans. Even Vena was smiling widely.

Calr emerged from a dive holding a clam the size of his head, or maybe an oyster I couldn't really tell. Water streamed down his red hair and shoulders as he waded toward us, triumphant.

“They have pearls inside them. Help me gather them,” he shouted.

Ja’a turned visible again, squealing in delight. “Oh, those would sell for silver. Nice catch.”

With a splash, we all dove down after him.

The lake bed was surprisingly clear. Sand rippled in pale gold patterns beneath the water, broken here and there by clusters of heavy shells half buried in the sediment. They were surprisingly abundant, yet another sign that this place had gone undisturbed for a very long time.

We pried them loose and hauled them to shore, laughing each time someone surfaced with an even larger specimen.

By the time we were done, we had a small pile that could have paid for a team of horses.

We boiled them in their shells after extracting the pearls. The smell of brine and rich meat rose with the steam. The flesh was firm and surprisingly tender. It tasted salty and clean, like the lake itself.

For a place born from death, it provided generously.

After we ate, I lay down in the sandy stretch near the reeds, close enough to the water to hear the gentle rhythm of waves. Vena lay beside me, propped up on her elbows at first, then relaxing fully onto her back.

Our silk under-armor clung to us, heavy with water, but the breeze coming off the lake and the mild warmth of the blue sun began to dry the fabric quickly.

Raik and Kan had retreated to a large boulder a short distance away. Kan sat shoulder to shoulder with him, their fingers laced together. They spoke quietly, foreheads occasionally brushing, lost in their own orbit.

Ja'a was beyond them, counting pearls with a wild, greedy smile.

Katar patrolled the perimeter with disciplined calm, eyes scanning the horizon for any sign of movement. Even here, even now, he did not fully relax.

Shingo followed him at a distance, silent as always.

Calr sat farther down the shoreline, his back turned toward me.

At least, mostly turned.

I caught him glancing over his shoulder more than once. His gaze flickered down before snapping away when he realized I had noticed. The tips of his ears flushed pink.

Now he refused to look in my direction at all.

I chuckled softly.

Sometimes I forgot he was still just a teenager.

Still, I was mildly surprised he was looking at me rather than Vena. She was closer to his age and, objectively speaking, had more impressive assets.

But then again, he had grown up with her in the Holy orphanage. He probably saw her as a sister. Or perhaps he simply had too much respect for her status as a cleric to let his mind wander in that direction.

The thought amused me more than it should have.

The blue sun overhead was gentler than the yellow one. Its warmth seeped into my skin without scorching it. It would probably not take long for our clothes to dry completely.

The sauropods continued their slow, graceful feeding in the distance, long necks dipping and rising in a steady rhythm. Occasionally, one would shift its massive weight, sending large waves across the lake.

A brown feather drifted lazily down from the sky and landed on my nose.

I blinked cross-eyed at it before letting out a small yawn.

That was strange.

I had not noticed many birds flying overhead before, not since we entered the Dead Zone.

Now, a faint silhouette circled high above the lake, looking vaguely human.

I yawned again. My eyelids grew heavier as a second feather landed on my forehead.

This feeling was familiar. Soft and warm, just like the moments when Nina would guide me into sleep with her Dreamer magic so we could enter the dreamscape together.

Just peace.

More feathers were falling from overhead, some of them landing on Vena beside me.

My last coherent thought before sleep took me was the realization that this was probably an attack, but by then it was too late.
