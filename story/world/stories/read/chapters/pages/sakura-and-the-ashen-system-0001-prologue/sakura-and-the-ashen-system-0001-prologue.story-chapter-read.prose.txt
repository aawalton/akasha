Sakura Arai was heading home through Kasumigaseki Station, and that evening she looked no different from the hundreds of other people in the train car: just as tired after a long day, and just as quiet. Her black hair was tied into a braid, and her gaze was lowered somewhere toward the floor. She stood by the door, holding the strap of her bag on her shoulder and trying to ignore the noise around her. The air in the car was warm and heavy with the presence of other bodies. Phones glowed in other people’s hands. Someone nearby was reading a newspaper, the pages rustling softly.

The woman beside her, around fifty, with a cloth bag and a neatly folded umbrella, turned slightly toward her and asked in a quiet voice, “Excuse me, is Kasumigaseki next?”

Sakura blinked as if surfacing from her thoughts and gave a short nod.

“Yes. Next.”

“Thank you,” the woman said, then after a pause gave her a faint smile. “I’m always afraid I’ll miss it.”

Sakura politely inclined her head and said nothing more, but she herself instinctively glanced up at the sign above the door, as if she wanted to make sure once again that everything was going the way it should, that the train would slow down any second now, and the doors would open. Nothing warned of what was coming.

Then the train jerked harder than usual, and at first no one even got scared. Someone only muttered in annoyance, and a man by the opposite wall looked up from his phone and said quietly to no one in particular, “What is it this time?”

The lights flickered once, then a second time, and a heavy jolt ran under the floor, making people sway closer together without meaning to. Sakura lifted her head. The first thing she noticed was the sharp smell. Far too acrid and alarming for an ordinary technical problem. You could immediately smell burning plastic and hot metal, and within a second it started scratching at the throat. Several other people grew alert too. Someone turned around. Someone frowned. Then something gave a dry pop under the far row of seats, and the first stream of gray smoke burst out from a crack near the floor.

People panicked right away, giving in to the worst demon of all: panic. Someone shouted to ask what was happening, and someone else was already lunging for the door.

The train jolted again, only this time much harder, half the ceiling lights went out, and emergency lamps flashed on instead, flooding the car with a harsh red glow. Something cracked again beneath the body of the train, and now Sakura could clearly hear that something below the floor was burning and humming at the same time.

Later, it could have been described simply and dryly: a terrorist attack. But in that moment, it was not knowledge. It was only the feeling that something fast, vicious, and insatiable had flared to life beneath them.

“Open the door!” someone shouted.

“Why are we stopped?!”

“There’s smoke! There’s already smoke!”

People surged toward the exit so suddenly that Sakura was crushed between the pole and other people’s shoulders. The woman with the umbrella beside her let out a quiet gasp and clutched the strap of her bag as if that could help her keep her balance. Sakura heard a phone ringing, and someone was already shouting into it that there was a fire in the car, that they were trapped, that no one was opening the doors, and that person’s voice broke into such raw panic that everyone else started yelling even louder. The smoke moved fast from below, crawling into faces, under clothes, into noses and mouths, and after only a few breaths Sakura felt as though her throat had been scraped raw from the inside.

She covered her mouth with her sleeve and bent forward without meaning to, trying to breathe less. Her eyes stung until the world blurred, but she could still see two men at the far door trying to force the panels apart with their hands. It was hopeless, but desperate. The woman beside Sakura said in a trembling voice, “Miss... please... don’t let me fall.”

Sakura did not even have time to think about what to say. She simply caught the woman by the elbow.

“Hold on,” she said hoarsely. “Hold on to me.”

At that moment, the first open flame burst from under the seats. It shot upward in one short, hungry tongue, immediately caught the plastic panel on the wall, and the air inside the car changed. It was no longer just hot. It was scorching. People screamed so loudly that Sakura’s ears rang for a second.

The crowd lurched toward the next section because someone shouted that the emergency mechanism had been opened there, and that was enough for people to stop seeing each other as people. The whole mass of them surged at once. Elbows, shoulders, other people’s knees, the sharp corners of briefcases all slammed into her body without order or sense. Sakura tried to keep her footing and hold on to the woman beside her. She tried to take at least one step toward where an exit might be, but in the smoke and the crush of bodies, every movement felt wrong, as if she were forcing forward not herself, but some other heavy body.

The car jerked again. Sparks rained down from above. Sakura was still on her feet, but she could already feel how quickly her arms were weakening and how a dull pressure was growing inside her chest. She dragged in air, but got only smoke. It was in every cell of her body.

The woman beside her suddenly coughed so hard she started to sag, and Sakura caught her with both hands, hardly understanding where she was finding the strength. She had never thought of herself as brave or as the kind of person who became a hero in a crisis, but she could not simply let go of someone collapsing right beside her. She pressed the woman against the pole with her shoulder, trying to keep her upright, and braced her own back against the bodies behind her while someone above their heads screamed, “The glass! Break the glass!”

“I can’t! I can’t!”

“Somebody help!”

Sakura lifted her head, and at that moment the car lit up with another burst of fire somewhere outside, beyond the soot-clouded window. Then another impact came from below, as if something under the train had burst open, and the heat became so intense that the skin on her face answered with instant pain. The woman beside her cried out. The crowd lurched back, then forward, then back again, and Sakura lost her footing. Someone’s bag hit her cheek, someone’s hand grabbed her shoulder, and a second later she was thrown sideways straight into a metal pole. Pain flashed white across her temple. She staggered, tried to stay upright, but her knees had already buckled.

She dropped to one knee, still trying to rise, and for the first time truly understood that there was no air left. Every breath now ended in a spasm. Her chest was being crushed as if a concrete slab had been dropped on top of it. Everything before her eyes was turning red and black at the same time. People were still screaming and shoving, still throwing themselves at the doors, but those sounds seemed to start drifting farther away, and in their place all that remained more and more clearly was heat, pain, and that smell that smothered everything else.

Sakura vaguely felt someone kick her and someone else stumble over her. She was still trying to get up, but her fingers would not obey, and her hands were shaking as if they no longer belonged to her. And then, in the middle of that hell, a ridiculous thought flashed through her mind, almost offended in its absurdity: the day had been so ordinary, so completely unremarkable, that it did not deserve an ending like this. She had not managed to do anything important, had not said anything final, had not thought of anything big. She was just scared, in pain, and desperate for air.

The woman she had been holding said nothing anymore. Someone very close by was wheezing. The red light above the door floated before her eyes in a blurred stain. The heat was becoming more poisonous by the second. The world was narrowing, as if it were being pulled into a single point where only that smell remained, that smell alone, so thick and unbearable that a person could drown in it before the smoke ever finished the job.

Then darkness came. Wrapping around her, soothing in its emptiness. Without pain or terror.

But it did not last long, because a moment later Sakura opened her eyes. Through the haze of unconsciousness, she saw low gray clouds above her, heavy with the promise of a storm.
