Daran moved.

Aiven flinched a half-step back before he caught himself. The man on the grass — throat opened, blood still slick on his hands, eyes glassy a heartbeat ago — was pushing himself up onto one elbow. His other hand stayed clamped against his neck.

He could not speak. A wet sound came out of him when he tried, low and broken, and then he gave up trying.

Cyria's tail had gone perfectly still. She then pressed onto one of her bracelets.

"Vane," she said.

She did not raise her voice. The word was a sharp little instruction launched in the general direction of the Vulpine headquarters, and somewhere across the city, presumably, it was already being obeyed.

Aiven was looking at Daran's throat.

The wound was closing.

Not in the way Sylphaine had pieced herself back together in the forest; not with that obscene crackling speed, the flesh threading itself in real time. This was slower. Slower than Sylphaine and Valerion's recovery yet faster than a man's. The torn edges of the throat were drawing toward each other the way wet clay closes when pressed, sealing under the surface even as blood still leaked at the seam.

Aiven's jaw tightened.

Cyria walked over to him, set one boot on his sternum, and pressed him back down to the grass. He did not resist. He could not, with one hand on his throat and the other shaking from the blood loss. She summoned her glowing claws again, the wind-haze coiling at her fingertips, and held them a hand's-breadth above his eye.

"Daran."

Her voice was very even.

"The amulets to the Fold. Give them to me."

Daran's eyes moved up to hers. He did not glare. He did not snarl. He simply weighed her, with the same unhurried patience he had stood in the field with, and Aiven watched him reach a conclusion in the time it took to breathe out. Without his fire, he was bleeding into the grass with a fox-clawed threat above him and the bracelet at his wrist was already glowing faintly with the soft pulse of a magic-sealing script.

He had no choice.

His hand left his throat. It went down to his belt pouch. He fished inside and produced two small amulets shaped like blue teardrops, threaded on dark cord.

He held them out.

Cyria took them without comment."One of them should have belonged to Cyria," Aiven thought.

A rumble of treads sounded across the field.

Aiven looked up. A heavy mana-rover, low-slung and matte black, had crested the rise and was rolling toward them across the churned grass. The side hatch opened before it had finished stopping. Vane stepped down first, and behind him followed a wiry figure with long arms and a tail curled at his back. Aiven had never been introduced to the monkey beastfolk before.

"Boss," Vane rumbled.

"Take him," Cyria said. "Holding cell three."

Vane gave the briefest of nods. He and the monkey beastfolk hauled Daran upright between them. Daran did not fight it, his eyes already turning glassy from the blood, and walked him to the rover. The hatch closed. The treads turned. The vehicle rolled away across the smoking field, taking Daran with it.

Aiven looked at the amulets in Cyria's palm.

"So..." His voice came out lower than he had intended. "We can go to the Fold with these?"

"Yes."

She closed her hand around them. Her golden eyes lifted to his.

"Do you wish to go now," she asked, "or would you prefer to regroup first?"

He let the question land.

The sensible part of him, was already laying out the case. The Vulpine headquarters was a mess. Lyra was on the scout-ship, exhausted and barely back. He himself was running on the dregs of what six days of training had built, and Lucien had Sylphaine and Valerion. Going in now meant walking into a hall with three of them at the worst possible odds.

He should regroup. He should rest. He should bring Lyra and Rysa and whoever else Cyria could spare.

He thought of Virelle. He thought of what Sylphaine's weird magic could do to a mind in the time it would take him to be sensible.

There was no time.

"I need to go now, Cyria."

Cyria's mouth curved.

It was not the smile she used in negotiations, or the smile she used to disarm. It was smaller than those. It was the smile of someone watching a person they had quietly believed in doing the thing she had known they would do.

"Then we will open the portal here."

She held up one of the amulets.

"You still have your teleport talisman? The unused one."

Aiven reached into his coat pocket. The silk wrapping was still there, a small soft weight against his ribs where it had sat through the entire fight with Daran. He drew it out and unfolded it. The talisman lay in his palm, whole and unused.

"Yeah," he said. "Still good?"

"Yes."

He folded it back up. He put it away.

Cyria pressed the amulet against her sternum. The blue teardrop began to pulse, slow at first, then quickening. Around her, the air went pale and the grass beneath her bent away from the ground in a circle two paces across. A portal began to open in the field.

Aiven cleared his throat.

"Cyria."

"Mm?"

"You're sure you're going with me? I—I mean, I appreciate it. Truly. But you don't have to. You've already—" He gestured vaguely at the smoking treeline. At her own city. At the burning headquarters in the haze behind them. "—you have things here."

Cyria looked at him.

She flashed a grin. It was a Cyria grin — slightly fanged, slightly dangerous, fully in control of itself.

"Consider this a thank you," she said, "Besides." Her smile thinned a touch. "If you died in the Fold, Aiven, I would lose the only person currently standing who can stand against Lucien at all. That is bad. Pelka will handle most of the current problems at Vulpine just fine."

Aiven huffed something that was almost a laugh. It did not quite reach his eyes.

"Thanks," he said. "I don't plan to fail here."

He did not say the rest.

Not after I just got Lyra back.

The portal finished opening.

They stepped through together.

The Fold was wrong.

Not in the way it had been wrong the last time he had been here — that suffocating dropped-floor feeling, the air thinning, the slow folding of two layers of reality past each other. This was the same place, but it had been rearranged.

The throne was gone. The polished black floor seemed shorter, the hall ending sooner than it should, as if someone had taken a room he had begun to memorize and quietly clipped pieces off the edges.

What was left was pillars. A pale, sourceless light. And an emptiness that felt deliberate.

"We've been waiting."

The voice came from behind them.

They turned, and Aiven's hand was already up. A mana shield — small, dense, half-formed — bloomed in front of him and Cyria as chains hissed out of the air.

The chains struck the shield. Two of them deflected, ringing. A third skidded sideways and embedded in a pillar. The shield held, then folded inward in his palm, leaving mana dust drifting between his fingers.

Sylphaine and Valerion stood twenty paces back.

Valerion looked vaguely impressed. Sylphaine looked vaguely disappointed.

"I suppose it is true, after all," Valerion said. His voice was the same calm gravel it had always been. "The clerk has finally learned some self-defense."

"I told you we should have struck without a greeting, brother," Sylphaine said, twin-tails swaying. "Greetings are for guests."

"Remember Master's order, Sylphaine." Valerion did not even turn his head. "He is to go elsewhere."

Aiven's stomach dropped.

"What do you mean?" His voice came out sharper than he meant it to. "Where is Virelle?"

Valerion regarded him.

There was no malice in it. He simply lifted one pale hand and pointed, almost lazily, toward a door at the far end of the hall. A door Aiven had not noticed in the rearranged space until now. Tall. Dark wood. Unadorned.

"Calm yourself, human," Valerion said. "She is just beyond that door. In fact, you are to meet her now."

Aiven's heartbeat picked up.

Something in his chest squeezed cold around the relief, before the relief could even land.

They wanted him to go in. They were intending him to go in.

Valerion was not blocking him from her. Valerion was directing him toward her, with the unhurried confidence of a man pointing out an exhibit.

That meant only one thing.

Cyria's voice, low at his elbow, barely a breath:

"It's a trap, Aiven."

Aiven did not look at her.

"I know."

The word came out flat.

"But—"

"Relax." Valerion's calm cut across him. "The elf is indeed there in the room. We are not lying about that. You came for her. There she is."

That was the worst of it.

A small, treacherous spark of gladness rose in Aiven's chest — she is there, she is there, she is alive enough to be there — and as soon as it rose, it confirmed everything Cyria had just said. Of course they would tell him the truth. The truth was the bait. The truth was what made him walk through the door instead of fighting his way back out.

He swallowed.

Cyria spoke again, quieter still. He could feel her tail bristling against his side.

"You sure you can handle it?"

"I have to."

"Oh, sit, fox," Sylphaine called across the hall, her voice bright and sing-song. "Stay. You should be grateful we are not killing you immediately. The new orders were quite specific."

Cyria did not turn her head. Her smile was very small.

"Not that you would be able to."

She did not raise her voice, but it carried.

"Go," she murmured to Aiven, the smile gone. "I'll figure something out here."

Aiven nodded.

He moved.

The walk to the door was, by any honest measurement, perhaps thirty paces.

It felt much longer.

He kept his stride steady. He kept the obsidian arm at his side. He did not run, because running would tell them everything — and his clerk-brain, even now, was watching them as he crossed and filing what it could. Valerion did not move to stop him. Sylphaine did not move at all, except to lean back against a pillar and watch him pass with that small, hungry smile. She made a little wiggling gesture with one finger as he went by, as if waving him off to school.

He did not look at her.

He looked at the door.

Each step closer to it was heavier than the last.

The thing in his chest, the cold spark of gladness, the colder bloom of fear, kept widening as he walked. He could feel his heartbeat in his throat and at his temples. He could feel his palms wanting to sweat. He told himself that whatever was on the other side of the door, he had walked into worse. He had walked into the Sunken Fane. He had walked into the original confrontation with Lucien.

This was not worse.

He almost believed it.

He reached the door. His hand went to the handle.

He stopped, for a heartbeat, with his palm against the cold dark wood. He drew one breath through his nose. He let it out slow.

He opened the door.

The room beyond was long and narrow.

The walls were dark stone, smooth and unfigured, lit by sourceless pale light from somewhere near the ceiling. The floor was bare flagstone for most of its width, but down the centre, running from the doorway to the far end, lay a single long carpet.

It was red. Deep, deliberate red. The kind of red that belonged in a chapel, or a sanctuary, or a place where someone was meant to walk forward toward something they were not supposed to interrupt. It ran perhaps fifteen paces from where he stood to a figure at the far end.

She was standing in the middle of the carpet.

Her back was to him.

Her silver hair fell loose down her back the way it always did, catching the pale light. She did not move.

Aiven stepped into the room.

The door swung shut behind him without anyone touching it. The click of the latch was very small.

The room was quiet.

He could hear his own breathing. He could hear, beneath that, the rapid steady thud of his own heart, the way you hear it in the small hours when there is nothing else to hear.

He took a step forward.

The carpet softened his bootfall to almost nothing. The red ran ahead of him like a tongue.

He took another step.

She still did not turn.

That was the first thing that was wrong. She had a sense for him. She always had. The door closing alone would have whipped her around in a sweep of theatrical coat.

He took another step.

His mouth was dry. His tongue moved through the words before his voice could find them, and he had to swallow before it came out at all.

"Virelle?"

His voice was very small in the long room.

She turned.

It was not a sudden turn. It was not a sharp, theatrical pivot. It was slow — a graceful, unhurried rotation in the air, the coat moving with her in a smooth fall, the silver-lavender hair sliding across one shoulder.

The orb floated at her side.

It was steady. Even. Pulsing slowly the way a sleeping thing pulses.

She faced him.

Her eyes met his.

And she smiled.

It was a beautiful smile. It was warm. It was the smile she had given him across the table in Lowhaven, when she had finally been convinced he could be tolerated. It was the smile she had given him in the cabin, after the wall had come down. It was the smile she had given him in the clearing, before everything had gone wrong.

It was perfect.

For one half-heartbeat, in the quiet of the long red room, Aiven thought everything was going to be all right.

"I've been waiting, Aiven."
