Back in the mountain valley below, Fauna, Tara, and Klax were frantically searching for their lost party member.

When that member appeared out of the wisps of cloud and fog around their camp, they practically dropped to their knees.

“See?” Tara quipped, breathless. “See!? I told ya the girl was fine, Faun.”

Fauna didn’t hide her relief. But she straightened up, putting on her teacher voice.

“We talked about this, Mara. You’re still under our care while on this journey, and I’m not about to lose one of my best students to a dragon’s den.”

Klax and Tara shared a look, feigning shock at Mara’s behavior while the child simply landed and smiled up at her teacher.

“I apologize, Mistress Fauna,” she bowed.

“Don’t,” Fauna replied, flashing a weary but warm smile her student’s way. “I understand the impulse. I was young once, too. As were we all.”

“You been off having some fun, I take it,” Tara winked at her. “Make any new friends?”

Mara spared a look at the mountain peak she’d left behind, expecting to still see the Architect looking after her. Knowing that there was no chance of that at all.

“I’m not sure,” she said.

She was then about to report on her progress – and her findings – when she noted the heavy rucksacks they’d brought with them were tightly packed at the edges of their ruin-base.

“Your eyes don’t deceive you,” Klax smiled. “We decided that tonight it was time to go home.”

Mara noted how Fauna and Tara’s eyes met briefly at those words. Both had clearly shared something tonight – something important.

“So,” Mara said. “Does that mean..?”

“Yeah,” Tara nodded. “We’re done chasing tales. If that big asshole dragon was really here, he’d have probably revealed himself and given us one big lofty speech before just trying to gobble us whole. I’d take the fires of Sanctum over the fires of a dragon’s belly, thanks very much.”

Mara looked to Master Klax, who’s eyes were fixed on Tara’s own belly. He quickly beamed a smile at her before turning away to finish packing.

“We leave before dawn,” he said. “With any luck, we can be on the Southern road to Eastmarch at first light, and enjoy some stew in Reachwind village before heading back to Westerweald proper.”

Mara had to admit that sounded good. The crummy rations they’d been chewing on since they came South didn’t exactly melt in one’s mouth.

“Hold on,” Fauna told her companions as they turned towards their duties.

“Our vote had to be unanimous,” she explained. “Which means that Mara needs to also make a decision.”

Klax and Tara looked at eachother briefly, sharing a nod that said they knew what their last companion’s answer would be, anyway.

Mara saw that Fauna knew, too. This was just a confirmation. She looked up at her teacher and saw trepidation that was no doubt born from her own desires – desires that she could never truly let go of.

She felt like she should say something. Something had to be said to mark the end of their adventure, didn’t it? It had been a year, after all. And now it was ending, ultimately, in failure.

But somehow it didn’t seem like that. Somehow, Mara felt like there really wasn’t much else that needed to be said.

So instead she stepped forward, took Fauna’s paw in hers, and asked her teacher one simply question:

“Are you ready?”

Fauna’s eyes in that moment were for her alone. She bowed her head, bit her lip, trembled slightly at those words.

But the look she gave Mara was not one filled with sadness.

She reached behind her back to open up her pack and produce something from its insides.

Tara and Klax stared on in amazement. Even Mara couldn’t really believe what was happening – not until she felt the rim of the hat fit snugly round her head, and the strange warmth that emanated from its hem.

“It suits you,” Fauna said simply, brushing a stray hair out of her student’s eyes. “I think you should keep it.”

That moment, and that moment alone, was what convinced Mara to keep her meeting with the dragon a secret after all.

They shared a laugh, and came together as a group to big farewell to the Southern mountains, watching the rising sun gently crest the peaks of the largest canyons and throw light onto a new Argwylian day.

Then the three of them turned to walk the path back home.

On the top of the highest mountain in all of Argwyll, the Architect lay.

His eyes felt heavy. Keeping the lids from closing was suddenly the greatest task he’d ever undertaken in his whole long life.

He’d ceased concealing his presence through magical means. Instead, he’d focused on keeping his sputtering coughs at bay, the way he had when he’d been speaking to the little rabbit.

Dawn was approaching, and with it, he knew, the end of his life.

He’d never really given much consideration to what his final thoughts would be, nor had the reality of his own death ever really sank in until he’d felt the compulsion to come back here one year ago – to the only place he’d ever known as home.

He thought he’d been vain enough to remember the days of his kind’s glorious past, when the skies were full of their fire and the humans lived in fear of their wrath.

But instead, he was remembering the last words Ethan Hawke had said to him before they’d made their little deal in the bowels of the earth:

There’s a Hopla child out there called Mara, he’d said. Like me, she has a sense of adventurous wonder about her. Like me, she wants to be the best at what she is. But that’s where our similarities end. Because she’s a child who is already wiser than me, or any of those I’ve faced on this earth. She doesn’t run when shit gets hard. She doesn’t look for a way out. She stands – not for herself, or for her own ego – but because standing is the right thing to do. And even though she could hate those who have twisted this world into what it is, she doesn’t. She has every right to hate. And she chooses the other path.

The Architect didn’t remember how he’d responded. The words themselves seemed dim and hollow in his mind, and that was how he knew his consciousness was fading.

Yet the words of the demon hat came loud and clear again:

It’s not for me that this new world will be built. And it’s not for you. It’s for every child like Mara who deserves a future.

He thought of the little rabbit who had come before him. And he thought of how fate had its strange little ways of surprising even an immortal being like himself.

Let this be my part of our deal, Architect: I will break your chains and open this world to you. Look at it through your own eyes and see what you think. If you truly think it is worth remaining, then let it be. If you’d rather see it die in flame, then let there be a rain of fire. But I am willing to bet on all the Skills I have that if you meet her, and talk with her, you’ll understand why the new Argwyll might just be worth sticking around for.

“Understand, would I?” the Architect whispered to himself. “But the child was right, Archon, I could barely even understand you. And you could barely even understand yourself – who you truly were.”

He felt his eyes close, and yet his mind held on to that thought, and the image of the little rabbit smiling as she left the peak of his mountain.

“…but perhaps there is one way we could both find out.”
