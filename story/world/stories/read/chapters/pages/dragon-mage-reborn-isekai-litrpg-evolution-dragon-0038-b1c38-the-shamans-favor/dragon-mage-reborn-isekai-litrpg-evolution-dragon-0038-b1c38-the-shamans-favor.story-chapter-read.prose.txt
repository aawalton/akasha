What was that all about? Nolan seeing me as something, or someone… experiencing loss—did he have a child? That was the only logical conclusion I could draw from their conversation. It made a lot of sense too, especially with how protective Nolan had been of me lately. Always wanting me to be at his hip, and making sure I didn’t rush into anything by myself. Maybe I reminded him of someone he’d lost.

I felt a hint of guilt for eavesdropping, but I couldn’t deny my curiosity was piqued. There was clearly more to Nolan than what he led on, and so far, he wasn’t open about sharing his history with me at all.

I guess for now I wouldn’t poke him about it. Clearly, that topic put him in a bad mood.

As I made my way back inside, I tried to act natural, padding quietly back to the table where I saw Nolan standing up and Thea sitting down in awkward silence. “Thanks for the food,” he said, his tone icy before he turned around to leave, the gunslinger insisting I follow with his gaze.

Ugh, erm, but I didn’t finish my food?

‘Let’s go,’ he said to me inwardly, and I stared back at him clueless.

Just as I was about to ask him where we were going, Thea said something that made him break his gait, “Since you’re broke, you might as well take a job. I caught a case on my way back, in Obayek village. They have a… civil matter that a shaman by the name of Pozei needs help with. Talk about dark spirits requiring the sacrifice of young, virgin women. Pozei’s daughter, Kauli, has been chosen as this season’s sacrifice. I told him I wasn’t sure if I could take on the assignment, but I’d forward his concerns to someone who may help. It’s urgent.”

Nolan paused for a second and then continued walking for the exit.

“Are you going to take it?” Thea asked as she got off her seat.

Nolan waited a while, reaching the front door with me at his heel before he said, “Yeah.”

I waved Thea goodbye and just like that, we were on our way to an assignment.

The trip to Obayek would mark my first ever guild assignment. Understandably, I was nervous. While eager to help, I wasn’t sure how much use I’d be. Still, it was better than being benched in the café until Nolan came back to raid dungeons.

‘So, what exactly are we dealing with? Dark spirits? Human sacrifice? That sounds like some heavy stuff,’ I said, trotting alongside Nolan.

Nolan sighed, running a hand through his hair. ‘Typical backwater village superstition. Nine times out of ten, there’s no actual ‘dark spirit.’ Just fear, tradition, and usually some corrupt elders using their power to control the population,’ Nolan explained. ‘But sometimes, that tenth time, there’s something real behind it all.’

‘So what if this is that tenth time?’ I asked, nervously. ‘What if there really is a dark spirit demanding virgin sacrifices?’

‘Then we deal with it,’ Nolan said flatly. ‘Either way, Pozei’s daughter doesn’t get sacrificed, and we get paid. Win-win.’

I wasn’t so sure it would be that simple, but I kept my concerns to myself. After all, this was my first job, and I didn’t want to appear doubtful.

‘How far is this Obayek place?’ I asked, trying to keep pace with Nolan’s long strides.

‘Not too far,’ Nolan replied, scanning the horizon. ‘Would’ve been faster with a horse, but the distance is certainly something we could manage.’

So we were hoofing it. We walked for what felt like hours in the heat. The terrain had gradually shifted from sand to rocky paths, with sparse vegetation dotting the landscape. My little dracapod legs were starting to ache, but I refused to complain. I looked over to Nolan, then considered asking for upsies, but I chickened out and sped up my stride instead.

Once we reached the top of the hill overlooking the village, my energy came back to me. Obayek was a small but quaint sight. The village was nestled in a shallow valley, surrounded by a ring of ancient, gnarled trees. Smoke rose from several chimneys, and I could make out thatched roofs and simple wooden structures. A stream cut through the center of the settlement, with a small stone bridge connecting the two halves. It was beautiful.

‘Don’t leave your mouth open for too long. Might catch flies,’ Nolan teased.

‘It’s gorgeous,’ I admired.

‘Obayek isn’t anything spectacular. It’s a very small village compared to most in Nemet.’

‘I don’t care about its size,’ I said, its aura warming my soul. ‘It feels like a dreamscape.’

‘Well, I’d hold off on the sky castle and the gold harps until we see what kind of nightmare is brewing inside,’ Nolan replied grimly. ‘Places like this—isolated, traditional—they’re the perfect breeding ground for the worst kinds of superstitions.’

As we descended into the village, I noticed a smaller scope of beastkin types. Most of them were of the reptilian variety, and they weren’t very inviting either. The peculiar way the beastkin villagers regarded us made me feel unwelcomed. Was it because I was a dragon? Thea was a neko, so technically I was closer to how they looked than she was.

While most beastkin wanted to snatch me up and train me to be their free pass to the skies, these villagers were indifferent. Their stares weren’t exactly hostile, but wary—the kind of looks reserved for outsiders who might upset the delicate balance of their small world. Children peeked from behind their mothers’ skirts before being hurriedly ushered inside.

‘They don’t seem very welcoming,’ I whispered to Nolan.

‘They are a reserved bunch,’ he replied. ‘Besides, the last thing they’d expect to walk through their village is not a human and his... pet.’

I cringed at the word ‘pet’ but now wasn’t the time to argue about semantics.

As we approached, a scaly middle-aged man with a wispy white beard and moss green eyes intercepted us. Strips of white paint decorated his blue face, and feathers and small bones hung from his headdress. He was bulky, with broad shoulders and a height that would make someone like Nolan feel small. He held a spear to his side as he approached us, Nolan suddenly stopping his feet and putting his hands up. This guy had to be the village guard or something….

“Ha-ee!” he commanded, his voice deep and grizzly. “Ock-tabu lun zebewoo!”

Nolan’s face churned. “Egh, I’m not really fluent in your local dialect, but my dragon friend here is.” Nolan looked over his shoulder, expecting me to translate for him.

Luckily for the vagabond, I understood all languages, thanks to my multiverse skill. ‘He said, halt!’ I said inwardly, imitating the gruff in his voice. ‘State your business, trespasser!’

‘You must have been a real riot back on Earth, huh?’

‘That’s what he said!’

‘Tell me how to say we are here to see Pozei.’

‘Okay, well, this is my first time. Heh! I’m a little nervous! Let me see, now. E-hem. Seh-cabba… len-do-rekke, Pozei.’

“All right. Seh-cabba lenno rekke, Pozei,” Nolan said, and instantly, the guard pulled out his spear at us! Two other just as massive guards rushed to his side, both of them armed.

“Aishele, zebewoo!” The middle one chanted.

‘You said it wrong!’ I hissed at Nolan.

‘I said the same shit you said!’ he hissed back. These men were ready to kill us because we’d just said we were here to kill Pozei!

But before Nolan could draw his own weapon to defend himself, the man we were here to see, or kill, emerged from the crowd behind the guards. “Ha-ee!” he shouted at the guards. “Zembow El-ka-ten. They are with me.”

The path of villagers parted as the withered green shaman approached us. His body was hunched with age, his snout longer and more crocodilian than the guards’. Brightly colored beads and feathers decorated his simple cloth robes, and a gnarled wooden staff topped with a strange crystalline stone supported his weight.

“Forgive them,” he said in surprisingly clear common language. “Outsiders are rare in Obayek, especially in these troubled times.”

The guards lowered their weapons reluctantly, still eyeing us with suspicion.

“You must be Pozei,” Nolan said, lowering his hands slowly.

The old reptilian nodded, his cloudy eyes studying us. “And you must be with the raven-haired feline.” His gaze lingered on me with particular interest. “A dracapod. How... unusual.”

I shifted uncomfortably under his scrutiny. Something about the way he looked at me made my scales itch.

“I’m Nolan,” my companion said curtly. “This is my partner, Freya. We heard you have a situation with some dark spirits and—.”

“Perhaps it is best we speak somewhere more, private.” Pozei said, his expression somber. “Please, follow me.”

Hmm, he seemed pretty secretive about why we were here. Could the rest of the village not know why he hired help?

As we followed the old shaman through the village, I noticed more details that had escaped me at first glance. Prayer flags and strange totems hung from doorways, and sigils were etched into the wooden frames of homes. Every few yards stood small stone altars with fresh offerings—fruits, flowers, and what looked like small bones.

‘Nolan,’ I whispered through our mental link, ‘those are definitely sacrificial altars.’

‘I see them.’

Then I saw a group of men who looked like hunters, roasting something green and skinny on a rotisserie stick. Over the fire, the meat roasted, and after closer inspection, I noticed that it was a goblin!

‘Don’t stare,’ Nolan said. ‘Didn’t your mother ever tell you it’s not polite to stare?’

I couldn’t help but notice how casually they were roasting what was definitely a sentient creature. Nolan seemed unfazed. ‘Goblins raid their livestock. It’s common practice in rural areas to eat what tries to eat your food.’

‘But—that's—’ I struggled to articulate my horror.

‘Different worlds, different rules, Freya,’ Nolan said firmly. ‘Focus on why we’re here.’

Pozei led us to a modest hut at the edge of the village. Unlike the other dwellings, this one stood apart, surrounded by a circle of smooth stones painted with symbols that looked foreign to me. The interior was dimly lit by a small fire pit in the center, the smoke escaping through a hole in the thatched roof. Bundles of dried herbs hung from the rafters, filling the air with a pungent, earthy scent.

“Please, sit,” Pozei gestured to woven mats arranged around a low table.

Nolan sat cross-legged, and I settled beside him.

He offered us some tea, but Nolan rejected the offer for the both of us. Pozei then lowered himself onto a mat with a pained grunt, his joints cracking audibly. He placed his staff carefully beside him, the crystal at its top catching the firelight in hypnotic patterns.

“I thank you for coming,” he began, his voice barely above a whisper. “Your associate must have spoken about the seriousness of my plight. My daughter, Kauli, has been chosen as this season’s sacrifice to the Dark One.”

“Who exactly is this ‘Dark One’?” Nolan asked, cutting straight to the point.

Pozei’s eyes darted to the doorway before he leaned closer. “We do not speak his name freely. He is the ancient one who dwells in the cave beyond the trees. For generations, our people have kept him appeased with offerings.”

“And these offerings,” Nolan pressed, “they’ve always been young women?”

The old shaman nodded gravely. “It was not always so. Once, it was merely our finest crops, our strongest livestock. But as seasons passed, his hunger grew. First, it was blood from our hunters. Now...” His voice trailed off, the weight of his words heavy on my shoulders.

“And you’ve actually seen this ‘Dark One’?” Nolan followed.

The old shaman’s eyes widened slightly. “No one sees the Dark One and lives to speak of it. Only the Chosen Ones enter his domain. They do not return.”

“Convenient,” Nolan muttered under his breath. And then he looked back to the shaman and asked, “This new taste in offerings, did it change about the time our gods disappeared?”

“Why, yes. As a matter of fact, it did. The hunger of the Dark One grew more insatiable after the gods went silent. How did you know?”

Nolan exchanged a meaningful glance with me. “Just a hunch,” he said, but I could tell he was piecing something together. “Tell me about the selection process. How is the sacrifice chosen?”

His shoulders slumped. “The village elders cast bones during the full moon ceremony. The patterns reveal the Chosen One.” His voice cracked slightly. “Never in all my years did I imagine my Kauli would be chosen.” His eyes glistened with unshed tears. “She is all I have left since her mother passed.”

I felt horrible for the old shaman. Despite Nolan’s skepticism, Pozei’s grief seemed genuine.

“When is this sacrifice supposed to take place?” The gunslinger asked.

“At sundown, not too long from now,” Pozei replied, his clawed hands trembling slightly as he adjusted his robe. “They will take her to the sacred grove at the edge of the Uchbow forest, where she will be prepared for the journey to the cave.”

“And the elders? They oversee this?” Nolan pressed.

“Elder Kazak leads the ceremony. He is... devoted to the old ways.” Something in Pozei’s tone suggested there was more to this Elder Kazak than mere devotion to tradition.

‘Nolan, ask him where is his daughter now?’ I asked, speaking up for the first time.

Nolan asked for me, and then Pozei said, “She is being prepared at the temple. No one may see her until the ceremony.”

“Well, it’s clear that we need to pay this Dark One a visit before Kauli does,” Nolan said. “I’m going to need to know everything about this cave; its location, any traps or guardians, and if you have an arsenal I could use.” He got up from his seat and looked down at his empty gun holster. “Unfortunately, we were robbed earlier, and my weapons were snatched.”

Pozei’s expression shifted. “No, you must not!” he begged as he struggled to get up on his feet with the help of his staff. “You must not enter the Dark One’s domain uninvited! It would bring terrible wrath upon our village!”

“So you want us to save your daughter, but you don’t want us to confront whatever is demanding your sacrifice?” Nolan asked, his voice hardening.

Pozei wrung his hands, clearly torn. “You do not understand our ways. The Dark One is powerful. Direct confrontation would mean death for us all.” He lowered his voice to a whisper. “But there may be another way….”

I perked up, my ears twitching with interest. ‘We’ll do anything!’ I chimed. ‘If it means saving an innocent girl, I’m all for it.’

“The sacrifice must be virgin and of pure heart,” Pozei continued. “My daughter is 24—much older than our usual sacrifices who range between 14 and 17. But, if someone were to... take her place...” He paused, then unattached the crystal on the top of his staff and cradled it in the palm of his wrinkled hands. “This can be possible with a decoy.”

Nolan scoffed. “I highly doubt I could replicate the mannerisms of a young adult.” Just as he said that, the shaman looked down at me as I wagged my tail excitedly, but when I noticed the expression on his face, my happy panting and tail swaying stopped immediately.

“I was referring to your infant dragon.”
