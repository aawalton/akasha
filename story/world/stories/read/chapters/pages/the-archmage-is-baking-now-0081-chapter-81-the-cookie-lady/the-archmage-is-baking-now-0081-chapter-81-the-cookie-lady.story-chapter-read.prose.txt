The buildings thinned as they climbed the road leading out of Glenfall. Soon they were past the last of the houses, moving through the pine forests that surrounded the town. The road was still well-maintained here, and the horse moved at a steady trot that ate up distance quickly. Jane leaned back in her seat and watched the trees pass by, allowing the driver his silence as he negotiated the last of the tricky roads leading out of town.

The travel went quickly, much faster than Jane had expected. The driver was not a talkative man, which suited her fine. He hummed to the horse occasionally, low tuneless sounds that seemed to calm the animal, and every now and then he would call back to ask if she was comfortable or if she needed anything. She said she was fine and meant it.

She found herself dozing. The motion of the carriage was steady and rhythmic, and she honestly thought she was in control of the effects until she fell sound asleep in earnest. When she woke, the sky was dark and the carriage had stopped. She sat up and blinked, disoriented for a moment. The driver was already climbing down from his seat, stretching his back with a series of audible pops.

They were outside a small building, barely larger than a cottage. A sign hung over the door, but Jane couldn't read it in the dim light. Smoke rose from a chimney, and warm light spilled from the windows, suggesting someone was home and expecting guests.

The driver opened the carriage door and offered her a hand down.

"This is the inn," he said. "Such as it is. It's an expensive place to stay. The woman who runs it grows or raises almost all her own food. She’s the only option anywhere near here, which means she can charge what she likes."

Jane climbed down and stretched her own stiff muscles. Her body was letting her know it wasn't happy about spending so many hours in one awkward seated position.

"I'm glad to pay," she said. "Whatever it costs."

"You already did. It's included in what you gave me for the trip."

The inside of the inn was exactly what the outside had suggested. It was small and simple sporting a single table, a separated kitchen barely visible through the door to a back room, and a staircase leading up to a few rooms for lodgers..

Dinner was stew and bread. Jane ate with enthusiasm, and the driver chuckled like the sight was funny.

"I'm surprised you could sleep as long as you did. Most passengers can't manage more than a few minutes at a time. The road's too bumpy."

"It's been a busy few weeks." Jane tore off a piece of bread and dipped it in the stew. "I think I needed it."

The driver yawned, and Jane got caught up in it despite herself. This was a quiet, quiet place. The night seemed to take some level of sleepy power from that.

"I best get to bed if you want my old body to hold up through tomorrow. We'll get an early start in the morning, and I should have you where you're headed by noon."

Jane followed him up the narrow stairs to the inn's sleeping quarters. There were only two rooms, each one barely large enough for a bed and a small table. The driver pointed her toward one and took the other for himself, closing his door with a quiet click. Jane followed suit, sitting for an hour or so in the quiet dark before she fell asleep.

—

The next morning, Jane dressed and made her way down to the common area. She ate, then allowed the man to load her into the carriage for the final leg of their outgoing trip. An hour or so passed on the move before he talked the next time, using just a few words to explain a sack and wineskin he passed towards the back of the wagon.

"Snacks," he said. "And wine. A bit of wine’s nice on a morning ride, sometimes. Especially when the weather’s so nice."

Jane accepted both and settled back in her seat. She uncorked the wine skin and took a small sip, finding it surprisingly good. It was only lightly alcoholic, which she was thankful for at this altitude.

"Can I pour you some?" she called up to the driver.

He lifted a small flask that had been tucked beside him on the seat. "Already covered. But I appreciate the offer."

If the alcohol affected his driving, Jane couldn't tell. The carriage moved as smoothly as it had the day before. She supposed a man who had been doing this work for as long as he had knew his limits well enough to stay within them.

Time passed quickly. Jane alternated between watching the landscape, snacking on the dried fruit and nuts from the sack, and taking occasional sips of wine. The road climbed again as they traveled, rising back up into mountain territory that felt even higher than Glenfall. The air grew thinner, and Jane found herself breathing a little deeper to compensate.

The trees began to thin out, spreading apart until she could see their destination in front of them.

It wasn't much to look at. A handful of buildings lined either side of a mountain road, with plenty of space between each one. Some looked like houses, others more like places of utility or business. There was no obvious central gathering place, or even any real organization. In most respects, it seemed to just be a loose collection of structures that happened to be near each other.

"We're here," the driver said, pulling the horse to a stop. "Willowbrook. Such as it is. Not a big place, really. You are seeing most of it right now."

Jane climbed out of the carriage and looked around. The air was thin, but absurdly clean-smelling.

"Where do you need to go?" the driver asked. "I can take you closer if you know the address."

Jane didn't. Emily’s letter had given her the name of the town but nothing more specific than that. She had assumed she would figure it out once she arrived.

"I don't really know," she admitted. "Just somewhere in town. I'm looking for a woman who lives here, but I don't know exactly where."

"Might as well start here, then." He gestured at the building they had stopped in front of, a small structure with a covered porch and a wooden box mounted beside the door. "This is where the town's mail comes. If anyone knows where to find a specific person, it'll be whoever handles the letters."

Jane thanked him and gathered her things from the carriage. The driver waited until she was clear, then tipped his hat and clicked his tongue at the horse.

"I’ve got some business nearby, but I'll be back through tomorrow," he said. "Around midday. Just wait on this porch and I'll find you."

"Thank you. For everything."

"Just doing my job." He smiled, the first real smile she had seen from him. "Good luck finding who you're looking for."

The carriage pulled away, and Jane was alone in Willowbrook with nothing but her bag and ta creased letter to guide her Somewhere in this tiny settlement was the woman she was looking for, and it didn’t seem like it would be very hard to find her.

She turned toward the building with the mailbox and went to knock on the door.

The door swung open as soon as Jane touched it, and she realized it didn’t even have a latch outside of a small chain with a hook that could be looped through a hole in the jamb to hold it steady. The building inside was a small space that looked entirely dedicated entirely to the sorting and distribution of letters. The back wall was lined with wooden cubbyholes, each one labeled with small, imprecise handwriting Jane couldn’t make out from the other side of the counter.

Behind the counter itself stood a girl. A very young girl, to Jane’s best estimate somewhere around ten or eleven years old, just tall enough to avoid giving the impression she was peeking over the counter.

Her expression was businesslike, but only in the sense it seemed like she had read how a responsible adult looked in books and was trying copy it. She had a practiced stern expression that Jane steadfastly resisted laughing at. All in all, it was one of the cuter things she had seen in recent memory.

"Welcome to Willowbrook.” The girl curtsied and then planted her hands palm-down on the counter. "Can I help you with something?"

Jane thought about asking why a pre-teen was in charge of the town’s mail, even temporarily. She decided against it. If this small town found her competent enough to watch a counter, she had no business disagreeing with them. She also sensed it would do nothing but hurt the girl’s feelings, and she saw no reason to be mean to her.

"Hello," Jane said, letting the door close behind her. "Thank you for the welcome."

"First time in Willowbrook?" The girl sizing her up, taking in Jane's traveling clothes and the bag slung over her shoulder. "If you need a place to stay, the inn is just down the road, maybe a half-mile. Same building as the restaurant, so you can eat and sleep in one stop. Mrs. Halloran runs it. She's nice, mostly."

"That's good to know." Jane approached the counter. "But that's not what I'm looking for. I'm actually trying to find someone who lives here. A woman named Felicity Cast.”

The girl's expression changed immediately. The friendly act vanish, replaced by a more genuinely childish caution.

"Why are you looking for the cookie lady?"

The cookie lady?

Jane like the nickname, even if it wasn’t what she had expected from someone who came across so scientific on the page. She filed it away to ask about later. For now, she had to find her.

"I'm coming for a visit," Jane said. "She knows I'm coming. A friend of mine wrote to her, and she agreed to meet me."

"Nobody visits that lady." The girl's tone was flat and matter-of-fact. "She's lived here forever and nobody ever comes to see her from outside.”

"Well, I am," Jane said simply. "Visiting, I mean. It's my first time, but she agreed to it."

The girl didn't look convinced.

"Is she lonely?” Jane prompted. “The cookie lady? Because nobody visits her?"

The girl shook her head like such a thought was unthinkable.

“We’d never let the cookie lady get lonely.”

"I didn't think so. I've never met her before, so I’m glad to hear that." Jane tried to think of how to explain her purpose in a way that would make sense to a twelve-year-old gatekeeper. "I just wanted to meet her because of something she wrote. A book."

"The cookie lady writes books?"

"She wrote one. A long time ago." Jane knelt down and opened her bag, digging through the contents until she found what she was looking for. The cookbook was near the bottom, wrapped in cloth to protect it from the jostling of travel. She pulled it out and unwrapped it carefully, then held it up so the girl could see the cover.

"This is the cookbook I learned to bake from. She wrote it." Jane pointed to the author's name printed on the title page. "See? I just want to meet her and thank her for writing it. She already knows I'm coming."

The girl leaned forward to examine the book, her suspicion warring with curiosity. She studied the cover, then the title page, then looked back up at Jane with an expression that had softened slightly.

"She really wrote this?"

"She did."

“And you make cookies?”

“I do. Other things, too, but sometimes cookies.”

"Huh." The girl seemed on the verge of accepting all this, but not of actually spilling the beans on where the woman lived.

"Listen," Jane said, tucking the cookbook back into her bag. "I promise I'm not here to hurt the cookie lady. I just want to meet her. She sent a letter saying she'd be happy to talk to me. Can you tell me where she lives?"

The girl chewed her lower lip, clearly torn. Finally, she seemed to reach a decision.

"Well, I could check," she said.

Before Jane could say anything, the girl was gone out the back door. Jane was alone.

I guess I asked for directions. I’ll just wait to see what I get out of that, I guess.

Jane looked around the empty post office, not sure what to do with herself. She supposed there was a sense in which she looked suspicious now, just standing around the post office unattended. She pulled one of the two chairs against the wall over to a spot where she could see both the door and the counter, then sat down to wait, hands crossed in her lap far away from anyone’s personal correspondence. There was nothing else to do.

I hope she remembers sending the letter. It would be awkward to come all this way only to be turned away at the door.

The door opened a few minutes later, and Jane looked up expecting to see the girl returning with news. Instead, a middle-aged woman stepped inside, pausing when she saw Jane sitting against the wall..

"Where's Cindy?" the woman asked. She didn’t seem worried, luckily. “Did she abandon her post?

"She went to ask the cookie lady something," Jane said. "About whether it's okay if I visit. I think she'll be back soon."

The woman laughed, a warm sound that filled the small space. "That's Cindy for you. Careful around outsiders, that one. Heavens know why. She’s never seen bad from them." She studied Jane with open curiosity. "You're here to see Felicity? I don't think I've ever seen anyone come to Willowbrook specifically for her."

"So I've been told." Jane stood and offered her hand. "I'm Jane. From Glenfall."

"Glenfall? That's a ways away." The woman shook Jane's hand. "I'm Edna. Most people around here just call me Doc, though."

"You're a doctor?"

"Close enough. I handle the medical side of things for the town. In a city, you’d think of me as a very experienced nurse. Out here, I’m the best anyone has.”

"That sounds like important work."

"Someone has to do it.” The woman pulled out the second chair and took a seat. Jane wasn’t surprised. She already had the impression that town moved at a slower pace than other places. There might very well be nothing else to do, at that moment. "So what brings you all the way out here to see our cookie lady? If you don't mind me asking."

Jane explained about the cookbook, about learning to bake from it, about Emily’s research and the letter that had led her here. Edna listened, nodding throughout to let Jane know she was locked in to the conversation.

"I didn't know she wrote a book." Edna said when Jane finished. "A scientific baking book? That's something. She's always been clever, but since she came here she’s just been the cookie lady to us. Always has the oven going making something or other."

"She’s been here a long time?

"For decades. She must be in her eighties now, or older." Edna smiled. "I’m glad to learn a bit about her. She doesn’t talk much about her past.”

The back door burst open again, and Cindy reappeared, looking slightly out of breath. She looked between Jane and Edna, then focused on Jane.

"She says it's fine," Cindy announced. "She says she remembers the letter and she's been expecting you. She also says to tell you that you took your time getting here, and that she's not getting any younger."

Jane bit her tongue and kept the fact that she’d gotten here in almost no time at all to herself. This was actually happening, now. She was minutes from meeting her mentor. The thought of it left her almost dry-mouthed.

"Thank you," Jane said. "For checking. I know you were just being careful."

Cindy moved back behind the counter, resuming her post in the same mock-adult manner as before.

"Her house is the one at the end of the main road. You can't miss it. It's got a garden out front. Lots of berry bushes."

Jane gathered her bag and headed for the door, then paused and looked back at the two of them.

"Thank you. Nice to meet both of you."

"Good luck," Edna said. "And come find me if you need anything while you're in town. I'm usually around."
