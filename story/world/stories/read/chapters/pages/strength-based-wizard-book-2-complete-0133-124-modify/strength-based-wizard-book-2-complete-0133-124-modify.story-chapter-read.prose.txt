Chapter 124

Modify

Years ago…

Joseph was nine the first, and only, time he had ever received after school detention. The room smelled like chalk dust, floor wax, and that strangely comforting smell of the school cafeteria. Outside, the hallways were quiet. Of course they were. Everyone else had left for the day two hours ago. Schools after hours were wrong.

Joseph sat at a desk in the back row with his hands folded in front of him because the principal had told him to “sit quietly and reflect on his choices.”

His right cheek throbbed. The skin was hot and tight, swollen where Vincent’s fist had landed more than once. One eye felt puffy. Tears still stung at the corners, though it was hard to tell if that was because he was upset or because his face had been tenderized like a Salisbury steak. Probably a little bit of both, if he was being honest with himself.

He sniffed hard and wiped his nose with the back of his hand.

A punishment for getting into a fight, he had been told. That was what they called it. A fight. Not “Vincent finally got what he deserved and then sat on Joseph like a brawny little gorilla with anger issues.” Joseph had gotten the piss beat out of him, but because he had “started it,” he was the one sitting in detention.

Life is so unfair, he thought. This wasn’t the first time he had that thought. But this time he meant it. This wasn’t “Mom bought the wrong cereal” unfair or “Liv got to borrow his action figures” unfair. This was the kind of unfairness that sank its teeth in and left marks.

Vincent had been terrorizing him all school year.

“Tits.”

“Lard Boy.”

“Big Joey Boobies.”

Very original names, Joseph had to admit.

Vincent was tall for their grade and already built like he’d been assembled out of leftover middle school parts. He had thick arms, a thick neck, and the confidence of someone who had never been told no by anyone.

He would seek Joseph out. In gym. At lunch. During recess. Sometimes he would purposefully bump into Joseph with a hard shoulder, trying to knock him off balance. Sometimes he just let Joseph do something a ‘fatty’ would do (like eat, or attempt any athletic movement). Each time, he would loudly make a demeaning remark, accompanied by one of the names he’d carefully selected for Joseph. Of course, their classmates would be there. Vincent always needed an audience. They would all laugh.

Joseph had tried ignoring him.

That was the adult advice. Ignore him, and he’ll stop. Fires needed fuel, after all. Except that was a lie adults told because they didn’t want to deal with the part where ignoring someone just made them get more creative.

The day’s disaster had happened during recess. Everyone was playing a big game of kickball.

Vincent had been on first base. Joseph had been up to kick.

He remembered the rubber ball rolling toward him, red and scuffed, wobbling over the cracked blacktop. He remembered swinging his leg and actually connecting well for once. Usually he whiffed entirely, and when he didn’t he’d send a dribbler—a sad little tap that died two feet from home plate.

This time, it was like his right foot had been kissed by a god. The ball sailed out toward the far side of the playground.

For one wonderful second, Joseph thought he might actually make it to first base. It would be a huge accomplishment in his short kickball career.

He ran.

His sneakers slapped against the playground asphalt.

Then he saw Vincent standing there. Grinning, waiting to tag out his favorite victim.

Something inside Joseph snapped in that very moment.

He ran at first base at full speed. But instead of stopping, instead of tagging the base, instead of doing anything a normal kid playing kickball was supposed to do, Joseph planted his left foot and kicked Vincent in the knee with every ounce of force his nine-year-old body could muster.

The sound Vincent made was wonderful for about half a second.

Then it became less wonderful.

The brawny kid did not take it well.

The next thing Joseph knew, he was on the ground. The sky was above him, bright and blue and useless. Vincent was on top of him, heavy as a sack of angry potatoes, hitting him.

Kids screamed and hollered.

A whistle shrieked.

Someone shouted for a teacher.

Joseph remembered trying to cover his face and feeling small and furious and terrified all at the same time.

Now he sat in the classroom with a swollen cheek while the adult world decided what kind of trouble he was in.

Outside the door, voices drifted down the hall.

The principal’s voice came first. Mrs. Wexler.

“Mr. Sullivan, I understand this is upsetting, but Joseph did initiate physical contact.”

Joseph stiffened.

His dad’s voice came next, lower and tighter than usual.

“He kicked a kid who’s been bullying him for weeks.”

“Yes, and we take bullying very seriously.”

Joseph almost laughed, but his face hurt too much.

No, they didn’t.

They took bullying seriously after someone did something about it.

Mrs. Wexler continued, “According to multiple students, it happened during recess. They were playing kickball. Your son charged the other boy, violently kicking him.”

“And… what happened after that?” Dad said.

“We are looking into that. But rest assured, the situation was quickly brought under control before things got too out of hand.”

Looking into it.

Joseph stared at the paper pumpkins the younger kids had decorated the classroom walls with.

“There has to be accountability,” Mrs. Wexler said.

Dad was quiet for a moment.

Then he said, “And the other kid?”

“He has also received consequences. A call was placed into his parents.”

Joseph pressed his lips together.

The classroom door handle clicked.

Joseph sat up straighter and wiped his face fast.

He didn’t want Dad to see him crying.

His father stepped into the classroom without saying anything at first.

He filled the doorway in the way fathers did when you were nine and scared and sitting in a tiny chair in an empty classroom alone for a long two hours. Mrs. Wexler hovered behind him in the hall, lips pinched, but Dad didn’t look back at her.

He walked straight to Joseph. He placed one hand on the boy’s back, warm and broad between Joseph’s shoulder blades.

“Let’s go home, son,” he said.

That was it. Four words.

Joseph stood so fast his chair scraped loudly against the tile. The sound made him flinch. His cheek throbbed again, pulsing in time with his heartbeat. He kept his head down while Dad gathered his backpack from beside the desk.

Mrs. Wexler said something from the doorway about follow-up conversations and behavior expectations and making better choices.

Dad nodded once. Not in agreement. More like he was acknowledging the existence of a stop sign he was planning on rolling through. He handed Joseph his backpack, before leading the two away from the principal, and out of the school.

Outside, the Autumn air was cool. The sun had dropped low, throwing long orange stripes across the parking lot. Dad opened the passenger door for him, and Joseph climbed in, holding his backpack against his stomach like armor.

Neither of them spoke until they were halfway home. The car hummed around them. The radio was off. Dad’s hands rested on the steering wheel at ten and two. Dad always drove like someone might grade him. Finally, he glanced over at Joseph.

“You want to talk about what happened today?”

Joseph tried to say no. But the word got stuck behind his teeth, tangled up with shame and the hot pressure behind his eyes. His throat tightened. His face crumpled before he could stop it. He started crying. It came out of him in ugly little gasps, his shoulders shaking, his hands clenching around the straps of his backpack.

“I’m sorry,” Joseph sobbed. “I’m sorry, Dad.”

Dad’s expression softened, but he kept his eyes on the road.

“There’s no need to apologize for what happened.”

Joseph shook his head hard.

“Yes there is,” he said, voice breaking. “I’m sorry you have a fat and ugly son.”

The silence of the car grew deeper then. Something broke in Dad’s face.

Joseph saw it even through his tears. The way his father’s jaw loosened. The way his eyes flicked over, wounded in a place Joseph didn’t understand yet. Like Joseph had reached out and hit him without meaning to.

Dad put on his turn signal. He pulled the car onto the shoulder of the quiet suburban road and eased it to a stop beneath a row of maple trees. The tires crunched softly over gravel. He put the car in park. Then he turned fully toward Joseph.

“Joey,” he said.

Joseph stared at his backpack.

“Look at me.”

Joseph didn’t want to.

The tears were still coming. His cheek hurt. His whole face felt too big and too hot. His shirt felt too tight on his body, and the seat belt dug into his pudgy form. He wanted to vanish into the seat cushion. He wanted to be anyone else. Any other kid. A fast kid. A skinny kid. A normal kid.

“Joey,” Dad said again, gentler this time. “Please look at me.”

Joseph looked.

Dad’s eyes were wet.

That terrified him more than anything else that had happened all day.

His father reached over and took Joseph’s smaller hand in both of his.

“You listen to me,” Dad said. His voice was soft, but there was iron underneath it. Not anger. Something stronger. Something that made the world lean in. “You are not ugly.”

Joseph’s lip trembled.

“You are not,” Dad said again. “And you are not going to talk about yourself like that. Not ever. Do you understand me?”

Joseph tried to look away, but Dad squeezed his hand.

“No. Look at me.”

He did.

Dad’s thumb brushed gently across Joseph’s knuckles.

“You are my son,” he said. “And you are beautiful.”

Joseph made a broken little sound.

Dad kept going.

“I don’t care what some kid at school says. I don’t care what any kid says. I don’t care what any grown-up says either. They don’t get to decide what you are.”

The words came slow and steady, like Dad was laying bricks.

“You are kind. You are smart. You are funny. You are stubborn as hell, which we’ll work on.”

Joseph gave a wet, involuntary laugh.

Dad smiled, but it hurt.

“And yes,” he said, “you made a bad choice today. Kicking someone in the knee is not how we solve problems.”

Joseph’s face dropped.

“But,” Dad added, “that does not make you bad. And it does not make what Vincent did okay. Or what he said true.”

Joseph sniffed.

Dad leaned closer.

“Sometimes people are cruel because they think it makes them bigger. It doesn’t. It just makes them cruel.”

Outside, leaves rustled over the car. A truck passed on the road, rocking them slightly with wind.

Dad’s voice lowered.

“I wish I could make the world fair for you,” he said. “I do. I wish I could walk into that school and fix everything with one conversation. I wish people always got what they deserved when they deserved it.”

He sighed.

“But life doesn’t work like that.”

Joseph wiped his nose on his sleeve.

Dad pretended not to notice.

“What I can do,” Dad said, “is make sure you know the truth before the world fills your head with garbage.”

He squeezed Joseph’s hand again.

“You are loved. You are beautiful. You matter. And anybody who tells you different is lying.”

Joseph cried harder then, because there was nowhere for the words to go except straight into the broken place inside him.

Dad unbuckled his seat belt and leaned across the center console, awkwardly pulling Joseph into a hug. It wasn’t graceful. The gear shift was in the way. Joseph’s backpack was smashed between them. Dad’s elbow hit the horn, and it gave one pathetic little beep that made both of them jump.

Then Dad laughed.

Joseph laughed too, through tears, because the sound was ridiculous and because Dad was still holding him like nothing else mattered.

After a moment, Dad kissed the top of his head.

“Never talk about my son like that again,” he said.

Joseph pressed his face into Dad’s jacket.

“Okay,” he whispered.

“I mean it.”

“I know. I’m sorry, Dad.”

Then Dad pulled back, wiped Joseph’s tears with a napkin from the glove compartment, and gave him one last serious look.

“We’ll deal with school tomorrow,” he said. “Tonight, we go home. We eat dinner. And maybe we don’t tell your mother about the knee-kicking part.”

Joseph sniffed and nodded.

Dad put the car back in drive.

And as they pulled away from the curb, Joseph leaned against the window, exhausted and bruised and still ashamed—but a little less sad.

I scroll through the list of Spells again. I had considered a couple of options, thinking about anything I could modify in a way that could help my dad. Save my dad.

[Wizard’s Fist] was the first Spell I thought of. My old reliable. My bread and butter. My bread and bludgeon. The spell that has solved more problems for me than communication, planning, or good judgment ever have.

I’ve modified it before, so have an idea of how it could play out this time around. A microscopic spectral hand. Tiny Lefty. A magical fist small enough to enter the body, find the cancer, and destroy it.

For about two seconds, this seems genius. Of course, this is the natural decision!

Then my brain catches up and slaps the idea out of my hand.

That’s so stupid, Joe, I think.

First, that’s basically performing surgery. Except instead of a microscopic scalpel and technology, it’s a Wizard’s Fist. And instead of a highly trained surgeon, it’s me.  Second, I cannot—under any circumstances—trust anything derived from Lefty and Righty to have the delicate touch required to operate inside someone’s body. Let alone someone still living and who I desperately wanted to stay that way.

I imagine it actually playing out. A microscopic spectral fist floating through Dad’s bloodstream like a fucked-up episode of The Magic School Bus, except instead of Ms. Frizzle saying, “Take chances, make mistakes, get messy,” it’s just an autonomous blue punch-goblin with a violent personality and no concept of malpractice.

Class, today we’re learning about the circulatory system!

Lefty cracks his knuckles.

By destroying it.

“Nope,” I say out loud. “Fuck that.”

I mentally scroll further down the list.

[Lesser Summoning: Interplanar Ally].

That one makes me pause too.

Could I modify it to be more selective? Right now, it’s too random. Too dependent on whatever logic the spell uses to reach across planes and grab something that qualifies as “ally” in the broadest, most alarming sense.

Could I refine it?

Could I summon a specific type of ally? Like a healer?

Someone powerful. Someone from a Realm where rare blood cancers are considered annoying instead of terminal. Someone who looks at Dad’s test results and says, Oh, yes, easy. Perhaps a supped up version of Reshma? After all, I had summoned my long-deceased boss before. Or, at least, a facsimile of him.

The hope hurts. And I know it’s too dangerous to place my hope on this summoning spell.

It’s too much to hope that the element of the spell I could change will be it’s randomness. That aspect seems too fundamental to the nature of the Spell.

I don’t feel great about trusting Dad’s life to a cosmic slot machine. Not unless I have to.

I run through the rest of the list.

[Force Blast].

No.

[Force Grab].

No.

[Raining Knuckles].

Absolutely not.

[Slimy Shield].

Maybe defensive, but against what? His own blood? The disease isn’t an incoming attack. It’s already there. It’s inside him. A traitor wearing his cells as a disguise.

Then my eyes settle on the one I keep coming back to.

[Clean].

The spell that removes dirt, grime, sweat, blood, ash, monster guts, lake funk, demon residue, and whatever else may sully my jorts in the line of duty.

It makes so much sense that I’m afraid to select it too quickly.

If [Clean] can remove dirt and filth within range… If it can identify what doesn’t belong and separate it from what does…

Could it be improved?

Not just cleaning my targets. Perhaps purifying even? Could it find cancer cells and clean my father’s blood of their remnants?

My heart starts beating harder.

“Okay,” I whisper.

I select [Clean].

Ping!

You have selected to apply your Modified Spellcasting to [Clean]!

Please select how you would like to use your available Modified Spellcasting point:

I take a breath. The System message window expands, as three options are laid out before me.

I read the first.

OPTION 1: Magic Sapper

Description: A cleansing energy radiates from your body in an aura with a thirty-foot radius. Your aura moves with you, centered on your body. Each hostile creature or being within your aura is “cleansed” of a small portion of their magical strength. Your cleansing aura will expand and grow stronger as you increase in level. At its current strength, your aura will reduce magical potency by 10%. At its current strength, you may maintain your aura for 5 Stamina per second.

If you select this option, you will learn the Spell [Cleanse Magic].

SPELL: [Cleanse Magic]

[Cleanse Magic] (Level 3 Abjuration Spell)

Casting Time: Instant

Stamina Cost: 200

Range: 30-foot radius (scales with Spellcaster’s aura)

Duration: Instant

Description: Hostile spells within the spellcaster’s aura are suppressed. If another person or creature’s spell enters the spellcaster’s aura when active, the spellcaster may target that spell and choose to ‘clean’ it, destroying the magic comprising the spell. This Spell will only be partially successful when targeting spells of equal strength. This Spell will fail when targeting stronger spells. When certain spells are targeted by this Spell, the components of the targeted spell may remain following this spell’s cleansing effect.

I stare at it.

My heart stumbles.

That’s useful. Really useful, actually. A defensive aura. A counterspell effect.

But it’s not what I need. It doesn’t save Dad.

I swallow and force myself to read the second.

OPTION 2: Strength Sapper

Description: A cleansing energy radiates from your body in an aura with a thirty-foot radius. Your aura moves with you, centered on your body. Each hostile creature or being within your aura is “cleansed” of a small portion of their physical strength. Your cleansing aura will expand and grow stronger as you increase in level. At its current strength, your aura will reduce physical potency by 10%. At its current strength, you may maintain your aura for 5 Stamina per second.

If you select this option, you will learn the Spell [Cleanse Strength].

SPELL: [Cleanse Strength]

[Cleanse Strength] (Level 3 Abjuration Spell)

Casting Time: Instant

Stamina Cost: 200

Range: 30-foot radius (scales with Spellcaster’s aura)

Duration: Instant

Description: Hostile physical attacks and skills within the spellcaster’s aura are suppressed. If another person or creature attacks the spellcaster within the spellcaster’s aura when active, the spellcaster may target that creature or person and choose to ‘clean’ them of their power, destroying the strength and energy comprising the attack. This Spell will only be partially successful when targeting skills and attacks of equal strength. This Spell will fail when targeting stronger attacks.

The rooftop seems to tilt beneath me.

Again, useful. An aura that weakens physical attackers.

A way to counter Skills.

But that’s not good enough, I think. It isn’t even close.

My fingers curl against the yoga mat.

“Come on,” I whisper. “Please give me what I want.”

I read the third option.

OPTION 3: Surface Application

Description: You are capable of applying your spellcraft to objects and surfaces and impart lasting effects. When casting [Clean] on any non-living object or surface, such object or surface will be impervious to filth, dirt and foreign substances for a number of minutes equal to your then-current Strength value.

If you select this option, you will learn the Spell [Slippery].

SPELL: [Slippery]

[Slippery] (Level 3 Transmutation Spell)

Casting Time: Instant

Stamina Cost: 100

Range: 60 feet

Duration: 1 minute

Description: Any surface within range is cleansed of all friction, creating a slippery surface.

I blink.

A laugh escapes me. It is not a happy sound. It is a broken little bark of disbelief, the kind of noise a man makes when he digs through a burning building looking for a fire extinguisher and finds a coupon for waxed floors. My hopes are entirely dashed.

“Slippery,” I say.

The word comes out flat.

The window hovers in my HUD, patiently waiting and showing no signs of acknowledging how much it absolutely screwed me.

No [Cleanse Disease], or [Purify]?

My heart crumbles. It goes soft and grainy inside my chest, collapsing under its own weight.

I had known it was a long shot. I had known. But knowing doesn’t help. Knowing doesn’t make this any easier.

I press the heels of my hands into my eyes.

The menu stays open.

The options wait.

Dad’s voice echoes in my head.

You don’t have to fix this today.

I hate that he was right. I hate that I am strong enough to shake monsters apart and still too weak to do anything about this.

I sigh and consider my options the best I can.

They’re good options.

That’s the worst part.

They are genuinely, stupidly good.

On any other day, I would be pacing around the roof, muttering to myself like a madman in my indecision.

[Magic Sapper] gives me an aura that weakens spellcasting. Along with [Cleanse Magic], an actual counterspell option.

[Strength Sapper] does the same for physical power, plus [Cleanse Strength], which would be helpful in fending off attacks from anyone or anything that likes to get up close and personal.

And [Surface Application]…

I keep looking at that one.

Sterile, dirt-proof surfaces are whatever. Useful, probably. Maybe less so since it is explicitly limited to non-living targets.

But [Slippery]?

That could be defensive, creating difficult terrain for my opponents. Make some giant charging asshole lose traction and turn into a physics lesson. Make a battlefield a skating rink. Make choke points impossible to cross without eating floor.

But it could also be offensive.

I picture it immediately. Me launching across frictionless patches like an angry curling stone with fists and a barbell ready to crush skulls. Sliding under attacks by pairing the Spell with [Acrobatics]. It’s absurd.

For a second, I almost choose it.

Then, I exhale through my nose and force myself to be smarter. I need to be smarter.

An aura effect I can maintain at a low Stamina-per-second cost is a game changer.

And the attached Spells are no joke. Especially with [No Pain, No Gain]. If I can boost [Cleanse Magic] or [Cleanse Strength] the way I boost my other spells, then I’m potentially countering some very serious attacks and spells.

The question is which kind of answer I need more. Magic or physical?

I lean back on my hands, looking up at the sky. The clouds are lit orange at the edges.

Physical attacks and Skills are definitely more common.

But I have tools to address those already.

I have my body, which is chock full of Resistances.

Spellcasters are a different beast. Though they have been far less common to encounter in battle, there’s no guarantee that will remain the case. Spellcasters scare me in a way big monsters don’t. A big monster usually tells you what it is by trying to bite you. Spellcasters not so much.

My mind’s focus hovers over [Magic Sapper].

Dad’s face flashes through my mind again.

That old car on the side of the road, years ago. When I was just a kid.

His hand over mine.

You are loved. You are beautiful. You matter.

I close my eyes for one heartbeat.

“I’m sorry,” I whisper.

I select [Magic Sapper].

The System responds immediately.

Ping!

I feel my selection settle into my mind and soul.

Now, only a single minimized notification remains blinking in the corner of my HUD: Phase 2’s opt-in timer.

The question that has been chewing through the back of my skull since I woke up in the hospital.

Opt out, and I live. Probably. Correction: Opt out, and I keep what I have. I stay with Harvest. I take contracts. I build a life where my biggest decisions involve breach response times and sponsorship deals.

I spend time with Dad. As much time as he has.

I become wealthy. Powerful. Safe, or at least safer.

That is not a bad life. That is a good life. A life my father would beg me to choose if I gave him the whole truth. I know that.

Which makes the other option feel even more monstrous.

Opt in, and I gamble everything.

And for what?

A chance.

Not even a guarantee.

A chance to reach the end of the God Game and claim one wish.

Whatever I desire. A wish big enough to cure my father.

I close the window.

The icon retreats again, small and patient as ever.

The sun slips lower over Cleveland. I sit there on the yoga mat, stronger than I’ve ever been and more afraid than I’ve ever felt.

And for the first time all day, I let myself admit the truth.

I’m not trying to decide whether I’m brave enough to opt in.

I’m trying to decide whether I’m selfish enough to opt out.
