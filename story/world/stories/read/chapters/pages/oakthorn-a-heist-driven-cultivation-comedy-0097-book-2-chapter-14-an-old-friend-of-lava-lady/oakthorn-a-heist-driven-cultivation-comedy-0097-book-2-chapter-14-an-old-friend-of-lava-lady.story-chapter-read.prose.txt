They had not lost—not yet.

Cade paced the perimeter of their makeshift camp like a caged predator, the ground beneath his boots whispering with each deliberate step. His mind moved with the precision of a master locksmith, testing each possible solution only to find it wanting.

It wasn’t supposed to be like this.

Gav’s labored breathing punctuated the night air like a countdown—each rasp a reminder that time was their most merciless enemy.

“We’re beyond fucked,” he muttered, the crude assessment hanging between calculation and despair.

Rayka looked up from where she had taken over tending to Gav’s burns with their dwindling salve supply. “That’s your expert analysis? We’re ‘beyond fucked’?” The edge in her voice was sharper than her daggers, and Cade

“Just running the numbers.” Cade’s grin was brittle, and she seemed to sense that. Her expression softened. “Just figuring out the one path amidst the five hundred I’ve considered that doesn’t lead to our excruciating deaths. You know, the usual Tuesday.”

Chubbs sat cross-legged near Jer, her tiny goddess fingers exploring the weathered surface of Bernard’s final mask with fascination. She traced the eye sockets, lips pursed in concentration.

“Touch that filter one more time and I swear I’ll—I’ll—” Elena grumbled, snatching the mask away. “Oh, just go and bother someone else, golden eyes.”

Chubbs responded with a giggle that made nearby flowers burst into improbable colors, their petals unfurling in defiance of the night.

Cade barely registered the exchange.

The equation before him was impossible. Bernard with a day’s head start into the Blightmarshes. Eight crew members and one mask—a lethal arithmetic that ended with transformation into twisted horrors for anyone unprotected within hours of exposure. Arbiters combing the highway with renewed vigilance after their earlier... unpleasantness. Gav sliding toward death with each labored breath. Chubbs—a divine toddler grenade waiting to detonate at the slightest provocation.

Orro materialized beside him, a portion of his usual silent grace having returned, even if his black leather jerkin hid the soot from his recent brush with becoming a piece of coal.

“I scouted ahead, and it’s not good, Cade.” Orro folded his arms over his lean chest. “The Arbiter fortress controls the only viable road. High walls. They’ve got guard rotations of sixteen skilled fighters. They’ll be hunting specifically for us after our performance in the village.”

“And we need more masks,” Nora added from where she knelt beside Evie and Gav. Her massive blade lay across her knees, drinking in the night like it was thirsty for vengeance. “Without them, the marshes are death sentences with extra steps.”

“Getting in, getting masks, getting out, getting to the crown before Bernard—” Cade ticked each impossibility off on his fingers, “—all while dragging a half-dead lycanthrope and a toddler goddess who might accidentally turn us all into butterflies if she hiccups wrong.”

On cue, Chubbs sneezed, unleashing a small explosion of glittering particles that briefly transformed Jer’s hair into writhing tentacles. The acrobat yelped, tumbling backward in an impressive display of reflexes.

“Just temporary!” Chubbs declared with solemn authority as the tentacles reverted to hair. “Probably.”

“‘Probably’ is the least comforting word in the language when it comes from you, tiny terror,” Jer chuckled, patting his head with frantic thoroughness.

The pressure behind Cade’s eyes had nothing to do with exhaustion and everything to do with the crushing weight of keeping his family alive. Eight lives—nine, counting the cosmic toddler—all resting on his ability to conjure a miracle from this disaster.

“I’ll be back,” Cade told his crew. His feet were already carrying him toward the treeline. “Keep Chubbs from rewriting reality while I’m gone. I need to think without something exploding, mutating, or dying for five consecutive minutes.”

Rayka started to rise, concern etching lines around her eyes. “Cade—”

“Let him go,” Orro interrupted, his quiet voice carrying surprising authority. “He thinks better alone.”

Cade didn’t look back as he slipped between ancient trunks, their bark twisted into patterns that reminded him of frozen screams. The forest deepened around him, shadows pooling like spilled ink between gnarled roots. His mind, however, remained crystalline in its clarity.

Option one: Frontal assault on the Arbiter fortress. Success probability: laughably suicidal. They’d be executed before they reached the inner courtyard.

Option two: Infiltration through the gap and into the Blightmarshes, grabbing masks along the way. Better, but with Gav unconscious and Chubbs flexing her burgeoning powers, there were too many variables. Besides, the Arbiters would be watching for thieves after Bernard’s passage.

Option three: Alternative route through the marshes. None existed that wouldn’t leave them transformed into writhing abominations within hours.

Option four: Abandon the chase. Let Bernard claim the crown. Consequence: Certain death when the Lifekeepers and now Arbiters caught up to them, not to mention any hell Astrid might raise when she lost whatever bet she had with her father.

Cade stopped in a small clearing where moonlight painted the ground in fractured silver. His breath hissed through clenched teeth. Destruction magic rose to the surface, coursing just beneath his skin.

“Option five,” he whispered to the indifferent night. “I need an option five, damn it.”

“Option five typically involves dealing with forces one doesn’t fully understand,” said a voice like velvet dragged across broken glass.

Cade’s body reacted before conscious thought, pivoting with silver-ranked grace, one hand closing around his dagger’s hilt. His other hand conjured a sphere of flame above his palm, ready to throw at whatever was approaching. The clearing remained empty except for a peculiar absence—no insects chirping, no leaves rustling, no distant calls of night birds.

The silence had weight. Presence.

Every hair on Cade’s body rose in primal warning. This wasn’t the ordinary danger of a physical threat—this was something fundamental, something that made his newly awakened core pulse in recognition of power far beyond mortal understanding.

He knew this feeling. This was the third time he’d experienced it. And it was getting really, really, old.

“I find it fascinating,” the voice continued from everywhere and nowhere, “how readily mortals rush toward fixing their problems with their feeble strength when all they need to do is ask.”

The temperature plummeted. Frost crystallized along leaf edges despite the warm night. Cade’s breath fogged before him, but his grip remained steady, mind calculating even as instinct screamed danger. He ordered his ball of flame to float a little higher, but even the illumination it offered did not reveal whoever was talking to him so melodramatically.

“If you’re selling something,” Cade replied, injecting practiced nonchalance into his tone, “I should warn you I’ve been on the merchant side of the counter. I’m immune to the hard sell.”

A laugh rippled through the clearing—not a sound of mirth but something ancient and terrible that bent reality around its edges. The shadows beneath an ancient oak tree deepened, then coalesced into a figure that stepped forward with such liquid grace that it made human movement seem crude and wasteful by comparison.

Tall and elegant, draped in a suit cut from fabric that seemed to absorb rather than reflect light. Pale skin with the flawless texture of marble, unmarred by time or experience. Eyes like wells of midnight that contained galaxies within their depths.

The stranger moved with such perfect economy that it almost hurt to witness—like watching the platonic ideal of motion that made all other movements seem like clumsy approximations.

Every instinct in Cade’s body recognized what stood before him, even as his mind struggled to process it.

Not a man. Not a being. A fundamental constant of existence given temporary form.

He knew this being—he’d seen him fly over Elysia while the city burned.

“Cade Stormhollow,” he said, his voice vibrating the air between them. “I’ve been watching your progress with considerable interest.”

Death had arrived. And apparently, he was quite the chatterbox.

“Honored, I’m sure,” Cade managed, “but I wasn’t planning on requiring your professional services just yet.”

Death’s smile was beautiful in the way that a perfectly honed blade is beautiful—all purpose and deadly promise.

“My dear boy,” he said, taking a step that left a perfect circle of withered grass beneath his immaculate shoes, “you’ve been requiring my services since the moment you absorbed that fragment of divinity. The question isn’t if we’ll do business.”

Another step closer. The ground beneath his feet grayed into perfect ash.

“The question is what terms we’ll agree upon when we do.”

Cade had bargained with dangerous people before—crime lords who’d slit throats for showing the wrong expression, mages who could turn a man inside out with a whispered word. He’d learned to read the subtle languages of power: when to push, when to yield, when to deploy charm like a weapon.

But this... this was like trying to navigate a conversation with an avalanche.

“I’m flattered by the divine attention,” Cade said, forcing himself to meet that obsidian gaze without flinching. “Though I usually prefer admirers who don’t murder every living thing in their immediate vicinity while we chat.” He gestured to the expanding circle of desiccated grass spreading outward from where Death stood.

Death glanced down, seeming almost surprised—a disconcertingly human gesture from something so fundamentally not. With a negligent wave of long, elegant fingers, the withering stopped.

“Force of habit,” he said, voice carrying the echo of a trillion final exhales. “One forgets these... minor courtesies after a few millennia.”

“Yeah, I’m always accidentally causing mass extinction events too. Look at how much we have in common!” Cade’s hand hadn’t moved from his dagger, useless as it would be. “What exactly does the literal embodiment of mortality want with a thief having his worst week ever?”

Death moved to a fallen log and sat with impossible grace, crossing one leg over the other like a nobleman at court. The wood grayed beneath him but didn’t crumble.

“Please,” Death gestured to a spot across from him. “I’ve come to offer assistance, not collection. Not yet.”

Cade remained standing, mind racing. Running was pointless. Fighting, suicidal. Information, however—that could be valuable. Mind made up, the young thief dismissed his orb of flame and gave the deity before him a wide smile.

“Forgive my skepticism,” Cade said, leaning against a tree with deliberate casualness, “but I’ve had terrible luck with mentors. The last one tried to sacrifice me because he found a replacement with more expensive shirts. So why would Death himself care about my little predicament? Don’t you have plagues to oversee? Wars to monitor? The general business of ending all things?”

Something flickered across Death’s perfect features—amusement, perhaps, or something older and more terrible.

“Didn’t you know? You and I share a mutual friend,” Death said with a beautiful wave of his hand.

Cade’s mind raced, connecting pieces in his mind in rapid succession. His smile turned calculating. “Oh, you mean lava lady?”

Death’s head tilted just a bit to the side, before he let out a blood curdling laugh.

“Yes. There’s the Son of Ruin I was expecting.” His smile never faded, but it was like the night’s shadows clung to it. “You see, you possess a fragment of Destruction, which makes us almost like relatives.” He let out a slow breath. “But my beloved is... impulsive. Magnificent in her rage, but lacking in subtlety.”

His eyes tracked upward to the stars, expression momentarily distant.

“When she awakens fully within you, she will use your body and mind as her weapon—to be broken and discarded when her purpose is complete.”

The words dropped into the clearing with the finality of a headsman’s axe. Cade’s newly awakened core pulsed in his chest, and a part of him wondered if his magic was aware of their conversation, or if it was merely responding to his own emotional turmoil that he hid beneath his grin.

“And you’d prefer...?” Cade prompted, mind racing through implications, looking for the trap beneath the offered salvation.

Death’s smile returned, sharp enough to cut shadows. “I appreciate craftsmanship, Cade. Precision. The elegant solution rather than the blunt force approach.” He leaned forward slightly, darkness rippling around his form. “My beloved and I have always balanced each other. Where she shatters, I harvest. Where she burns, I collect the ashes.”

“So you’re offering what, exactly? Advanced apprenticeship tips for accidental godhood?” Cade couldn’t keep the skepticism from his voice. “Forgive me if I don’t immediately trust the embodiment of everyone’s ultimate end.”

“Trust would be foolish,” Death agreed with disarming frankness. “I am not ‘good,’ Cade Stormhollow. I am inevitable.” His perfect fingers tapped a rhythm against his knee—one, two, three, four—unnervingly human. “But unlike many who have crossed your path, I keep my bargains with absolute precision.”

“And what do you get out of helping me?” Cade asked, the question that had been circling his mind since this impossible conversation began.

“Balance,” Death replied simply. “My beloved and I have danced across eternity together. Her destruction, my collection. An endless cycle.” His expression shifted into something almost fond. “But her sister, Creation—your little goddess-child—disrupts that balance. As does Life, in his endless, tedious optimism.”

Cade’s eyebrows rose. “So this is a divine family squabble, and I’m what? The new toy everyone’s fighting over?”

“You are a catalyst,” Death corrected with surprising firmness. “A mortal vessel carrying divine potential. Rare and valuable beyond measure.” His gaze intensified. “I would rather see you become something unique—neither purely Destruction’s avatar nor merely a mortal thief—something to reset the balance in our family squabble.”

Cade and Death’s heads turned at the same time, both hearing the rapid approach of someone through the underbrush.

Bunny chose that moment to crash through the underbrush, skidding to a halt at the clearing’s edge. The dragonling’s white scales gleamed in the moonlight, his intelligent eyes darting between Cade and the deity. A low, warning rumble vibrated in his throat.

Death regarded the creature with something approaching amusement. “Your companion has excellent instincts.”

Bunny edged closer to Cade, never taking his eyes off Death. His scales bristled along his spine, a clear sign of his agitation. Cade dropped a reassuring hand to the dragonling’s head, feeling the tense muscles beneath warm scales.

“He doesn’t usually take to strangers,” Cade said, scratching behind Bunny’s crest. “Or, you know, anthropomorphic personifications of universal constants.”

“Dragons recognize fundamental truths,” Death replied. “Even young ones.” He studied Bunny with academic interest. “Especially those touched by cosmic forces themselves.”

Cade’s hand stilled. “What do you mean?”

Death waved dismissively. “A conversation for another time, perhaps.” His eyes refocused on Cade with an unholy intensity. “Your path forward lies neither through direct confrontation with the Arbiters nor through unprotected passage through the marshes.”

“Care to be less cryptic and more helpful?” Cade asked, letting irritation seep into his voice despite the danger. “Maybe draw me a map with a big ‘X’ marks the spot?”

The temperature dropped another ten degrees. Frost crept up the trunk of the tree against which Cade leaned.

“The Arbiters possess a vault beneath their fortress,” Death said, his voice suddenly holding the weight of graves. “Within it, they keep confiscated artifacts—as well as the marsh-masks you seek. Right now, they have twelve spare masks, to be precise.”

Cade’s eyebrows rose. “That’s... suspiciously specific.”

“I see all endings, Cade Stormhollow,” Death replied, rising to his feet in one fluid motion. The thief was annoyed, but unsurprised, to note there was not a single wrinkle across the deity’s fine attire.

“Those endings include those of the foolish men who tried to enter the Blightmarshes ill-prepared.” Something like satisfaction crossed his perfect features. “The vault has a single entrance, guarded by wards that trigger if any living being attempts to pass through them.”

Understanding dawned like a cold sunrise. “But not if a non-living being passes through?”

“Now you begin to think like a proper thief again,” Death’s smile was a terrible thing to behold. “Your little goddess can temporarily suspend the ‘living’ status of a chosen object—or person—for approximately three minutes. Enough time to slip through undetected, if one is quick and clever.”

Cade felt the pieces clicking into place—a plan forming where moments before had been only desperation. Still, wariness kept his expression neutral.

“And why exactly are you helping me?” he asked, voice hardening. “What’s your real angle here? I’ve been betrayed by enough mentors to recognize when someone’s playing the long game.”

“Consider it an investment in potential entertainment,” Death said, adjusting his immaculate cuffs. “My existence is long, Cade Stormhollow. Monotony is my true enemy.”

“Bullshit,” Cade challenged, taking a deliberate step toward the deity. “You’re playing some game with your ‘beloved’ Destruction, using me as your proxy. I’m not interested in being anyone’s puppet again.”

Death’s expression didn’t change, but the air between them seemed to thin, reality stretching like some overtaxed fabric.

“Perceptive,” he conceded with a slow blink that was frustratingly well-timed. “Yes, Destruction and I have our... disagreements on how power should be wielded. She would have you burn the world for the glory of it. I would have you apply your considerable talents with precision.” His voice dropped to a whisper that somehow filled the entire clearing. “The choice, however, remains yours. I cannot compel you—only offer alternatives.”

He stepped closer, and for a brief, terrible moment, Cade glimpsed what lay beneath the humanoid facade—infinite darkness, studded with the fading lights of countless souls, a vastness that extended beyond comprehension.

“Oh,” Death said, his voice suddenly conversational. “The Soulkeeper’s Crown is not what you’ve been told it is.”

The back of Cade’s neck prickled with warning. It felt like this, and not even the hints about the Arbiter fortress, was what he really came here to say.

“What is it, then?”

“A trap,” Death replied simply. “One laid by beings far older than myself. Do not attempt to use it, no matter the temptation.”

“Why tell me this?” Cade asked, suspicion warring with the desperate need for any advantage. “What do you gain?”

“Balance,” Death said again. “And perhaps, in time, an interesting ally.” His perfect features arranged themselves into an expression almost like regret. “Few mortals have ever carried what you now bear, Cade Stormhollow. Fewer still have retained their own will in the process.”

A high-pitched squeal erupted from beyond the trees—Chubbs, her voice unmistakable even at a distance. A flash of brilliant white light pulsed through the forest, casting long, distorted shadows that twisted like tortured souls.

Cade turned instinctively toward the sound, Bunny already springing forward. When he looked back, Death was gone—no footprints, no disturbed vegetation, not even the lingering cold that had permeated the clearing seconds before.

Only the words remained, hanging in the air like a promise or a threat:

“When you need me, simply ask.”

Cade stood frozen for one heartbeat, then two, before cursing viciously and sprinting toward Chubbs’ cry. His mind raced, processing both the immediate danger and Death’s revelations.

The Arbiter vault. Twelve masks. Chubbs’ ability to temporarily suspend “living” status. The Soulkeeper’s Crown as a trap rather than salvation.

Information—valuable beyond measure, yet tainted by its source. Could he trust Death’s guidance? Absolutely not. Could he afford to ignore it? Equally impossible.

As he tore through the underbrush, one certainty crystallized in his mind. Whatever game the gods were playing, he refused to be a pawn in it.

Not again. Not ever.

He would get his crew through this nightmare, protect Chubbs, save Gav, and steal whatever the hell they needed to survive—and if that meant pissing off every deity in the pantheon along the way, so much the better.

Cade burst into their camp clearing just as a second flash of white light erupted from Chubbs’ tiny hands, illuminating his crew’s startled faces with ghastly brilliance.

The toddler goddess sat in the center of a perfect circle of frost, Bernard’s mask clutched against her chest, her golden eyes glowing with power that no mortal frame should contain.

“Cadie!” she squealed with delight, holding up the mask. “I made it better!”

The mask, once simple metal and crystal, now pulsed with otherworldly light, intricate new patterns etched across its surface that hurt the eyes to follow directly.

And beside it were eight copies.

“Well, Death,” Cade whispered under his breath as the first true smile spread across his lips. “I bet you didn’t see this ending, now did you?”
