“Thought you’d never see me again, huh,” said Wu Yan to the pale mayor who said nothing at the sight of the shaman.

Hua was very much enjoying the mayor’s sheer terror. It had functionally required no lightning, only a mortal dressed in robes that spoke of ancient treason and a bitter rebellion put down by her lord father.

He rallied finally. Hua had been hoping for a bit more sweat to break out across his neck. There was naked corruption, yes, but most managed it without breaking so overtly.

“My lord, surely one so esteemed would not trust the words of his hereditary enemies?”

“Got no place hiding will serve you,” Wu Yan said, furious down to the bone, the many rings of her staff jangling.

“Just listen to her. She speaks in tongues.”

That was true if rude. Hua spent so much energy trying to translate anything Wu Yan said.

“Which is why this Lord has brought an impartial observer.”

She gestured once to the Imperials in the crowd. Attention fell upon the scribe whose name she could never remember. After a moment to compose himself, the forgettable scribe rode forward.

“This one is honoured to be Scribe Tao serving the Magistrate of Liaojiangkou. There is much to be discussed on the state of the Western Reaches. This Scribe is eager to understand how the situation could deteriorate so quickly. Now, where is the Magistrate’s lesser counterpart, Justice Deng?”

“Justice Deng is indisposed,” the mayor bit out.

“Is that so? Explain.”

“He fell ill these past few months. He desired that we not send word before he made a decision on a successor. But he fell into a deep sleep before that decision could be made.”

“And you did not think to contact us?”

“He is our superior. One could not countermand his order for silence.”

“How… unfortunate it is that the man Lord Liao was most eager to meet with is unavailable. But no matter, such things happen.”

“Yes, of course they do. Unfortunate business, that.”

Her horse sneezed again. Hua rubbed his neck. “This Lord has need to stable his horse.”

“Of course, we can—”

“He would also speak to a Master Huang. Make it so.”

It was the only name she knew from Coaltown. A relation through Wu Yan’s contact in the city. Best to head off that issue now, see what information they could gain, before anyone could begin hiding their books. If she couldn’t get to Justice Deng immediately, she’d just make it up as she went along.

“I will leave the matter of seeing to this convoy in your esteemed hands… honourable Scribe.”

His eyes twitched, either at the additional responsibility or Hua forgetting his name again. It truly wasn’t her fault a man so forgettable existed.

A dog barked incessantly from in a yard. It had some fat and muscle to it though its ribs showed strangely. Ill, Hua thought. Sores on its motley coat, fleshy leopard-spots, like boils had popped across its flesh. It was a pitiful thing that might survive the winter if it had the scrappy resilience she expected of mongrels. It was chained to the posts at the gate of the manor, as though it refused to enter the home clearly its own.

It yapped with every step they took toward the squat manor, desperate for attention. Hua ignored the yapping for it was of no importance. Even though Qing would have fallen in love with it and pet it and fed it and—no, this has nothing to do with you. She tore her gaze away from the dog before the ghost could play with the dog.

“No animal deserves to be chained up and die like that,” muttered Wu Yan as they followed a soldier who had greeted them at the gates.

Hua gave her a look. “Don’t your people do ritual sacrifice? Why exactly is a dog more special than a goat or crow on principle?” Hua asked, gesturing at her horse. “I’d sooner sacrifice your entire convoy before losing my horse and only because I like him, but not because of some innate belief that some animals are more important than others.”

“Know you got no sense of proper value,” she sniffed disdainfully, looking to the Ranger who accompanied them.

The Ranger shrugged with a Cultivator’s casual indifference. Animals were mortals and mortals were of minor importance.

“Not my business. I don’t get paid to settle disputes over animals.”

Between the medallions and the few hundred silver taels Hua had insisted she receive—practically shoved into her hands much to Xue Meili’s embarrassment and her cousin’s seemingly endless exasperation—there might be an argument to be made that settling disputes between Hua and Wu Yan might be part of her business.

It was a small group that entered the manor of the Master Huang, led by one of the mayor’s pages. The official had hastily agreed to Hua’s suggestion and promised all they wished to know could be found with Master Huang. Hua assumed this an act of betrayal, sacrificing a former ally to gain Hua’s mercy. Shame that it would not work. Like the rest of the city, tarps and complex arrangements of cloth caught the pervasive coal dust that blew across the city.

Except, there wasn’t a speck of dust in this manor.

There were also no mosquitoes in Huang the Lesser’s manor. No insects or bugs. Hua had never entered a home so devoid of insects, never truly perceived the eerie emptiness their absence created.

She could commend a mortal that kept their home clean. I should buy some of his servants for the Clan. This is immaculate. Hua did not know if the entirety of the Liao Clan could have achieved so great a feat in cleanliness.

It was no great estate, no grand manor, but it was treated with dignity.

“Don’t like this place, no, not one bit” whispered the shaman. “Off it is.”

It was sadly hot. Sweat wicking down her spine. Being clean did nothing for the heat. They were greeted by a soldier, oddly, one who led them to an outdoor meeting area. The weather was too hot for it but Hua did not complain. She was here for answers, not games of face.

They were seated at a long table, the sort you acquired when you had been recently elevated in stature. Far too wide and long for practical discussions, with far too much garish ornamentation to be elegant. The sealed meeting room in the Liao compound had a table much the same size as this, but that one was made of simpler, sturdier wood, a solid slab that could take a beating—and had, truly, dinged and scuffed by generations of Cultivators, stained with water marks that might very well have been older than this city. All marks of true use, genuine use, not this lonely thing that did not fit anywhere.

Hua drummed her fingers on the table. Tap, tap, tap, a rolling wave of taps, from her smallest finger to her index. Her thumb joined in only after every third sequence. It was no music or rhythm, simply a matter of alleviating her boredom as they waited for the master of the household.

So they sat in the punitive heat, sweating in their clothes, entertained by the soldier who was also a Cultivator. A weak one with thin Qi. Ranger Xue Meili would break him in half—this, was an objective fact and had nothing to do with the Ranger sitting so close to Hua or the flush heightening her natural tan.

The soldier’s armour was marked by the simple handprint on the breastplate. A good piece of armour. The red print had been sealed in by layers of a lacquer.

“Why the red hand?” her blunt Ranger asked.

The guard was surprised to be spoken to. Surprised but not dismayed. “It’s for this one’s son, honoured daoshi. He found red paint one day and made it his mission to leave prints on everything. Every weapon he could reach. Every piece of armour. This captain’s face when he found my sleeping form. From that day forth, we were known as the Red Hand Mercenaries.”

“Children are a delight even as they are nightmares,” Ranger Xue Meili agreed.

“You have some of your own?”

She shook her head, thankfully. Hua wasn’t sure what she’d do if the Ranger had any children. Not that I care. Qing scoffed in the distance.

“Where we travelled, children were always eager to include us in their games. They always shouted for us to play with them. We did so when we could. They were always my favourite parts of travelling.”

“A wandering daoshi doing a hero’s work. This one is honoured. Were you part of any groups?” She told him. “A Jolly Ranger as this lowly mercenary live and breathe. A true hero. The stories of your group were part of this one’s childhood. One did not know his honour. Forgive his rudeness.” He offered her a martial salute, fist in palm. “One did not know he spoke to a hero.”

Hua was right there. Lord Liao. By definition, she was a greater hero to this province.

“The last. My people are lost. To Kang.”

“So long as you remember, the Rangers will endure,” the soldier promised.

“Thank you.”

He gave Xue Meili a smile a guileless smile.

Xue Meili smiled back.

Sparks danced down Hua’s fingers in time with the fall of her fingers on wood. Bright and sudden, a response so quick she could not suppress it.

Tap, crack.

Crack, tap, pop.

Tap, snap.

Snap, tap, bang.

Hua had never felt quite so betrayed by her own Qi.

The mercenary flinched back. Weiji coughed aggressively. The shaman coughed even more aggressively without bothering to mask her glare.

Hua withdrew her hands and placed them in her lap, beneath the table.

The silence that followed was awkward. Hua kept her head facing forward, not looking at the Ranger’s whose gaze was searing the side of Hua’s face or back at Weiji whose amusement could be felt without looking. Nothing happened. Nothing that needed to be mentioned.

“This lord is curious,” Hua said into the uncomfortable silence, “as to where the city sources its clean water. One cannot say that yellow water seems an appealing prospect.”

If Weiji didn’t stop snickering, Hua was going to rip his heart out. The guard captain pretended nothing out of the ordinary was happening.

“The colour of the lake changes often and is not dangerous; this one assures lord Liao that the water is sweet. But if you are still concerned, most wells and homes are connected to deep aquifers, filtered by layers of earth. Any noble worth something will have pipes feeding directly from the aquifers to their home. Just as Master Huang does,” he added, cautiously, nervously.

“If that is the case, do you not think offering water is the minimum that could be offered,” Hua asked pointedly.

“You must forgive my guard his impoliteness,” came a voice that sent shivers down Hua’s spine.

The master of the manor had arrived and with his arrival, all dignity fled.

Hua was not sure if she was seeing a man or a hallucination. Because no man should carry such a presence. For such a skinny man, immaculately groomed and dressed, he had a particularly vile presence. It explained why the shaman was feeling so leery. Being in his presence made Hua feel dirty in a way that the blood, guts, and shit of bandits could not equal. Such naked greed was the purview of worms, blind and whose only option was to feed and defecate, a constant cycle in then out, their great output merely the shit they left behind.

Warmth wrapped around her hand. Even without looking, she knew the touch of Ranger Xue. Could have known it even without the Ranger being right there. Something must have shown. Some fear or distress the Ranger could see through. Fitting for an archer.

And she’d chosen to reach back to Hua.

Qing needed to stop glaring at Hua.

“Forgive this one’s lateness. One’s son has taken ill, and that concern has seen to my distraction. He hopes the good captain offered no offense too great to apologise.”

He bowed with perfect politeness and his wife, whom Hua only noticed, bowed as well. He spoke at the right cadence and with the correct diction to imply contrition. And yet, Hua found it difficult to believe.

His wife served them. It was a beautiful spread, of that could not be denied. She was no great beauty woman for all her youth. She was unpolished in the acts of service, lacking the smooth grace Hua expected to be served by.

Only his wife served them. Did he not keep servants?

What a strange man. But she could imagine him counting his coins and deciding the rudeness of having no servants was worth the savings.

They did not discuss business immediately though the Master Huang must surely feel a degree of intimidation at so many Cultivators arrayed against him. Or perhaps he took it as his due that even Liao could be made to wait for him. Or perhaps he was simply more concerned about his son and Hua was projecting the irritation of heat on an otherwise decent man.

She asked after his son as it was only polite.

“This one’s son is training in the same arts,” Huang said perfectly pleasant, utterly repugnant. He said ‘son’ as one would say ‘tael’ or ‘tool’. “My pride and joy. I would hope to introduce him to a true Cultivator—he has some modicum of talent.”

Pride that did not verge into arrogance. Good manners and grace, a host we showed interest and answered their questions. He was everything Hua desired in a mortal. Even him making her wait had a reason. Not one she cared for, but a filial obligation to a living son was a worthy reason.

So then why did she want to be anywhere but in this man’s vicinity?

With that pleasantry asked, Hua permitted her cousin and Liu Xin to ask the questions that mattered, most of which she did not know even needed to be asked. They made a decent team, the two of them, if only they didn’t bristle at each other.

“My books?” he asked to Liu Xin’s inquiry. “My books are free for the Lord to peruse. Our taxes are paid on time, always. My Lord may fully avail himself of my records; this Master Huang would never see to avoid cooperating with our protectors.”

“This servant will be certain to examine them thoroughly,” Liu Xin warned unsubtly. “One is highly trained in examining the books of great businesses. One would notice anything missing.”

“Lord Liao is blessed to have such competent servants.”

“Got a question for you,” snarled the shaman who Hua admitted had done a decent job suppressing her irritation.

Had she been a dog, Hua might have rewarded her a snack.

Had she been a dog, Hua would hate dogs.

“Forgive this one, he does not recognise your vestments. You are a priest of some kind, yes?”

“Shaman.”

He nodded, not at all recognising what that meant in any true capacity. “How might one offer assistance?”

“Own the mines, do ya?”

“Indeed, this one has mines counted amongst his holdings.”

“Know a man called Peng?”

His pleasant smile slipped. That thing that was greed and bile showed through. “What has my head miner done that concerns you so greatly? Has that brute caused harm to a priest now?”

Wu Yan slammed her hands on the table and rose. “Got it in me to ask how you’re fucking over an old friend of mine, you sick shit.”

By the time the shaman was done unloading her tirade even Hua was impressed by the number of curses she managed to fit in. Not even the Sealord swore at such a cadence. Devour feculence, she mouthed silently, awed to have such prodigious curses added to her vocabulary.

“You insult me in my household.”

“This Lord wonders if you would offer forgiveness,” Hua said, amused. She could stomach losing some face after learning so many new curses.

Master Huang forced himself to calm, loosening his shoulders as he sat back. “Miners complain of anything. They receive the prescribed market rate as set by the Imperial guidelines, not a tael more but certainly not a tael less. I have never once in my life underpaid another. Such accusations are baseless and speak to the jealousy of my enemies.”

The Shaman was ready with another barrage of vitriol. They were all saved from it by the shouting that cut through the air.

Hua looked back, startled to find another Cultivator bullying his way into Master Huang’s household. A Cultivator as well. Two or three stars if Hua was to guess. That alone should not have been enough to permit the hesitancy the guards had in using their weapons. Did they think Hua would sentence them for breaking the peace? Or had they rightly assessed they wouldn’t survive raising their weapons against a Cultivator?

Master Huang’s face showed disbelief that quickly morphed into a fury so vile Hua could only tighten her grip on the Ranger’s hand. It left her feeling ill, so malevolent was it.

“My Lord Liao, the venerable and Great Master Huang invites you to be wined and dined,” announced the new Cultivator who had three guards clinging to him in a futile attempt to bring him down.

“You dare,” roared the Master Huang.

Hua was completely lost.
