We emerged from the subway station into the pre-dawn darkness of Asakusa, and the first thing that hit me was how quiet everything was.

No shambling zombies here or groans echoing off buildings. The military faction had cleared them all out. All I heard was the sound of some sort of industrial machinery in the distance, a whirring sound that was disturbingly similar to a tank engine idling.

“This way,” Yashas whispered, barely audible even standing right next to him. “There’s a tower three blocks east. Good vantage point.”

We moved through the streets like ghosts. Yashas led the way, his awareness ability painting an invisible map of every undead and living thing within several hundred yards. He’d pause before we got to intersections and raise his hand to stop. We’d wait for skeleton patrols to pass before he motioned me forward.

My [Spacefold] ability came in clutch more than once. When Yashas sensed a patrol coming around a corner too fast for us to hide, I’d grab his shoulder and warp us both onto a rooftop or into a side street. The ability had a limited range, but in an urban environment like this, hiding spots were everywhere, and no amount of patrols would ever compensate for that.

We reached our tower without incident. Another hotel that had probably charged a premium before the Cataclysm owing to the locale. Yashas picked the lock on the service entrance with practiced efficiency, and we climbed the stairs, thus bypassing the skeletons the enemy faction had placed within as lookouts.

The view from the top was grim.

I pulled out a pair of binoculars I’d taken from the Humvee and surveyed the compound while Yashas did the same with his thermal imaging goggles. The Kaminarimon—the famous Thunder Gate of Asakusa—had been converted into the main entrance of a military installation. The massive red gate with its iconic lantern now stood flanked by sandbag emplacements, machine gun nests, and what looked like improvised guard towers with floodlights for nighttime security.

Beyond the gate, the Sensō-ji temple complex had been completely militarized. The beautiful traditional architecture was still there, but now it was surrounded by prefab metal structures, semi-permanent tents, and enough military hardware to make a general weep with joy. Not that Yashas did. If he had, it’d been in terror, because what we were up against was so monumentally imposing.

At least four tanks were positioned at key intersections within the compound, while artillery emplacements sat on what had once been the temple’s main plaza. And everywhere—everywhere—skeleton soldiers patrolled in organized squads. If I hadn’t known better, I’d have said they’d been overrun by zombies.

“Gunships,” Yashas muttered, pointing.

I followed his gesture and spotted two helis circling the perimeter, making low passes every few minutes. Not close enough to spot us in the tower, but definitely close enough to turn us into paste if we were caught in the open.

“They expecting a total war to break out any moment or something?” I muttered. “That’s absurd.”

“Indeed,” Yashas said grimly, pulling off his night-vision/thermal/magnification goggles. “The shield generator will be somewhere within the main temple building. That structure there.” He pointed at the five-story pagoda at the heart of the complex. “But getting there…”

“Is going to be a bitch and a half,” I finished. “Well, we knew that going in. Let’s move.”

We left the tower and began our approach, and I had to admit, this felt weirdly familiar. All those hours playing sneaker games, getting past guards and timing patrols and finding the most efficient route through enemy territory. Except now it was real, the stakes were life and death, and I didn’t have a quicksave button.

This was Metal Gear Solid cranked up to eleven, as Richard would say.

Yashas and I worked well as a team, as we soon discovered. He’d sense the patrol patterns, identify gaps in coverage, and I’d use [Spacefold] to get us past spots that would’ve otherwise resulted in our detection. We moved from cover to cover, using everything from abandoned cars to military wreckage, occasionally ducking into intact buildings, and always staying out of sight of the helicopter gunships overhead.

Along the way, Yashas taught me that not everything was cover. Cover protected you from enemy fire, while concealment just hid you. It seemed many a warrior had died thinking one was the other, and I probably would have too. In most games, even the flimsiest fence blocked bullets…

We were about halfway to the Kaminarimon when things finally went wrong, as I’d always assumed they would. I just couldn’t believe it had taken this long.

A machine gun turret on one of the guard towers suddenly swiveled in our direction, its barrel beginning to swivel up to speed as it tracked our position behind a burned-out delivery truck.

“Move!” Yashas hissed.

I grabbed his shoulder and folded space, teleporting us into a nearby alleyway. Yashas brought out a small mirror, which he snuck out and angled up to see what it was doing.

The turret continued tracking our previous position for a moment before spinning down its barrel, doing a slow sweep pattern as if searching for us.

We pressed ourselves against the alley wall, barely daring to breathe.

Another tenth of a second and it would’ve opened fire, alerting the compound even if it hadn’t hit us.

“How did it detect us?” Yashas whispered. “There were no patrols nearby.”

I thought about it for a moment, watching the turret continue its search pattern through Yashas’ small mirror. “Radar, maybe? Or motion sensors? Their forces probably have IFF transponders to help their defenses determine who’s who. The automated stuff would ignore anything broadcasting the right signal.”

Yashas’s eyes narrowed behind his goggles.

That was going to be a problem. Yashas’ ability could identify living things—and unliving things too, apparently—but not machines. And we had no hope in hell of taking those things on.

“So, I got another idea,” I said, throwing the general my best apologetic grin. “And you’re not going to like it.”

We diverted down another street out of the line of fire of any of the autoturrets, fully aware that we had no idea where one could be hiding. Yashas guided us to a section of the perimeter where his awareness showed a small patrol of just six skeletons making their rounds away from the main gate. Six wasn’t nothing, but importantly, no tentacle commanders were present.

In addition to being annoying to kill on account of their self-destruct feature, they made the skellies way more lethal.

“There,” he said, pointing. “They’re somewhat isolated. But we’ll have to be quick.”

“Can you tell if they’re headed somewhere with fewer witnesses?”

Yashas concentrated for a moment. “They’ll pass through a loading area behind that warehouse in approximately two minutes. No other patrols nearby for at least three minutes after that.”

“Perfect. Let’s get in position.”

We moved quickly, using [Spacefold] to get ahead of the patrol and into the warehouse Yashas had indicated. It was some kind of storage facility, filled with empty crates and industrial shelving. The perfect place for an ambush, though like everything in Japan, far smaller than what I was used to.

The skeletons entered right on schedule, their boots clanking on the concrete floor. They moved in a loose formation with two in front, one at the rear, and the rest in the middle. For buckets of bones and empty skulls, they certainly had discipline.

Yashas caught my eye and held up three fingers, then counted down silently. Three. Two. One.

I [Spacefolded] behind the trailing skeleton, Light of the Fearless already drawn. Its twin abilities activated, and the blade took its skull off in one clean swipe, the bone separating from spine with barely a sound. The body collapsed, but I was already moving, catching it and pulling it away before it could clatter against the ground.

The two lead skeletons started to turn, sensing something was wrong, but Yashas was on them like a grim reaper. One moment he was in the shadows, the next he was on the skeleton, the mace I’d loaned him smashing its spine in two. The creature collapsed in a pile of bones.

The third skeleton had managed to raise its rifle by then, but I was there, [Spacefold] closing the distance in an instant. My sword took it through the spine, severing the connections that kept it animated. It dropped like a puppet with its strings cut.

After that, it was a brawl, but with two of us and three of them, not one that lasted long at all. My flying swords decapitated two, leaving just the one, which Yashas and I took out in a combined attack.

The entire engagement took maybe ten seconds, and not a single bullet had been fired. While we couldn’t be certain they hadn’t alerted their friends, my gut told me they hadn’t. We’d taken them out too quickly.

My heart pounded in my head and adrenaline surged through my veins as I inspected the carnage.

I felt like a goddamn Navy SEAL. Somewhere along the way, I’d actually become a halfway decent fighter, what with all those dungeon fights and Philip’s training. Not just in raw power, which my stats ensured, but in skill.

“Well executed,” Yashas said quietly, already stripping the armor off the nearest skeleton. “Help me with these.”

We worked quickly, removing the tactical body armor and helmets from the skeletons. They wore modern plate carriers with ceramic inserts and load-bearing vests… the works. And somewhere in there was likely an IFF transponder.

I hoped.

We hid the bodies behind a stack of crates, then began donning the skeleton armor. I had to strip off my Grace suit first, which felt like peeling away a second skin. The loss of stats was immediate and uncomfortable. My enhanced agility was gone and I was just Greg. Plain ole’ Greg.

Well, not quite, on account of my rings, but it wasn’t comparable. And despite choosing the set that was closest to my size, my scavenged military armor was just too small.

And no [Spacefold]. That hurt the most. My ace in the hole was gone, and it’d take a minute or longer to strip out of the military armor.

“I feel naked,” I muttered, adjusting the plate carrier. The armor was designed for someone with a much different build—skeletal, literally—and didn’t quite fit right.

“As do I,” Yashas admitted. “Yet this was your idea, as I’m sure I needn’t remind you. Ready?”

I stowed Light of the Fearless in my inventory and slung the Howa Type 20 assault rifle around my neck, sliding the helmet’s visor down to hide my very-not-skeletal face. “As ready as I’ll ever be.”

We left the warehouse and went for the main gate, walking with the confident stride of soldiers who belonged there.

At least, what I hoped passed for confident, because my legs were shaking.

It felt wrong and every instinct screamed at me to find cover and put my Vigor suit on. Yashas was human, and I was only slightly better with the stats from my rings. I felt sluggish, heavy, and very very vulnerable.

But we walked on anyway.

We walked past the machine gun nest that had nearly blown our cover. Past the artillery emplacements and the patrols of skeleton soldiers who barely gave us a second glance, though we kept our distance.

The transponders did their job, marking us as friendlies, and the automated defenses remained silent.

Which was great because if I was wrong, we’d have died a hundred times over.

The Kaminarimon loomed ahead, its massive red pillars and sweeping roof looking even more imposing up close. The enormous lantern hanging in the center swayed slightly and creaked in the breeze.

I’d seen pictures of this gate a thousand times back on Earth, always with people posing in front of it, smiling and taking selfies like the obnoxious tourists they were.

I’d have taken that any day over what it had been turned into.

I felt like we were walking into the mouth of some giant slumbering beast, and I could swear the whistling wind sounded like dragon breath.

A skeleton guard stood on either side of the entrance. They watched us approach but made no move to stop us. One of them even nodded slightly as we passed.

I nodded back, and we walked through.

Just like that. No ID checks or anything. The transponders were working better than I could’ve hoped.

Still, I didn’t dare let out the metaphorical breath I’d been holding. We were surrounded by enemy forces, wearing stolen gear and praying our disguise held. Shit could still go south.

“The main temple is ahead,” Yashas whispered, barely audible over the ambient noise of the compound. “The shield generator will be in the deepest level. We need to find a way down without drawing attention.”

“Right,” I said, trying to keep my voice steady. “So far, so good.”

And not two seconds after he said that, I spotted what looked very much like a checkpoint up ahead. A checkpoint where they were, of course, diligently checking IDs.

“Fuck.”
