“Why?” Veronica asked. She was genuinely puzzled.

The agreement she had outlined was, in every sense, a financial windfall. He would be granted ownership of a trade arrangement with a Mage Tower—an opportunity merchants spent entire careers pursuing. Yet here he sat, nervous but resolute, refusing what most would consider a blessing.

He better have a good reason for this.

“I do not feel my competence is… sufficient for such responsibility,” Galland said carefully. “I fear I would make a costly mistake. One that might harm the Mage Tower, and your own reputation, Miss Veronica.”

Veronica’s eyes narrowed.

“A costly mistake? With your abilities, you lack that much confidence?” she asked.

That didn’t sound like the man who would eventually leave for Annesheim and begin his own merchant house.

He frowned slightly. “You mentioned an intelligence network… gathering information on capable individuals. That is how you found me?”

She nodded.

Technically, the network consisted of herself and Sage.

He shook his head faintly. “Then perhaps your network did not uncover enough.”

Now it was Veronica’s turn to frown. “What do you mean?”

He placed the stack of documents gently back on the table and met her eyes.

“I am currently indebted to High Crates,” he said. “I cannot leave, nor assume responsibility for such an agreement on my own—not until I repay what I owe them.”

“Indebted?” Veronica repeated. “How? And how much?”

She leaned forward slightly. “This contract includes an initial payment,” she continued. “I cannot promise a fortune, but it would be enough for you to manage the initial start-up and operating costs while living modestly.”

His expression tightened, shadowed with shame and regret. Veronica was taken aback by how he looked. It was nothing like what she had heard. Where was the confidence?

“I owe High Crates ninety-four thousand vix.”

Her eyes widened.

“What?” she blurted before she could stop herself. “How could it possibly be that high?”

The compensation she expected to receive once the Mage Tower agreement was finalized would be significant—perhaps thirty or forty thousand vix. It would serve as payment for her initial notes and provide the capital needed to begin operations. But those funds were meant to build something new, not erase an existing debt. Nor was she certain the Ronswick branch could produce such a sum immediately. It would not take long. But “long” was relative.

The Association would need to confirm her identity. Verify she was legitimate and not a fraud. And most importantly, confirm that her work was reproducible and not a single stroke of luck.

The sooner she began operations directly with the Ronswick branch, the more credibility she would gain—and the faster the Association would respond.

“That is why I said your network may not have completed its research,” Galland said quietly.

His gaze dropped to the tabletop.

“A few years ago,” he began, “I was given the opportunity to accompany several senior merchants from High Crates. We were conducting a trade exchange with the Lilybed Company—a large enterprise dealing in exotic plant life. Many of their products are essential to alchemy.”

Lilybed…

She recognized the name.

They controlled vast cultivated lands and specialized in rare herbs and reagents. Their influence stretched across Vitian, supplying Mage Towers and renowned alchemists alike. Few competitors could rival their reach.

He noticed her expression and continued.

“I see you are familiar with them.”

“Yes,” Veronica replied. “I know the nature of their trade. They are indispensable to alchemists.”

He nodded. “During that exchange, the senior merchants were negotiating transport agreements. It was winter, and we were preparing a large shipment. Potions produced by local alchemists were to be sent to Maftem City.”

“Maftem?” she asked.

That was Duke Pelometa’s city.

“Yes,” he said. “It was to be a substantial transaction. Numerous independent alchemists contributed potions as part of their payment arrangements with Lilybed.”

Veronica leaned forward slightly.

“Then what went wrong?” she asked. “What happened?”

He grew solemn, allowing the silence to linger while he gathered his thoughts.

“Another man and I were tasked with selecting the caravan’s route,” he said at last. “Because it was winter, the standard passage was too dangerous—small avalanches, hidden sinkholes buried beneath the snow. We were to determine an alternate path while the senior merchants handled the larger negotiations. It was… supposed to be simple. At least, that is what I believed.”

Veronica remained still, listening without interruption.

He pressed his lips together. “There were several routes available. I chose one that was not the fastest… but not the safest either. Still, it should have been free of hazards. The journey would take two months through snow, but they had ample food and supplies. Everything was accounted for.”

He paused.

“But…”

“But?” Veronica prompted quietly.

“We did not account for bandits.”

Veronica frowned. “Bandits? They attacked the caravan?”

He nodded slowly. “Midway through the journey, the transport was assaulted. I do not know every detail, only that many of the caravan guards and coach drivers were killed. The survivors were forced to turn back.” His hands tightened together. “Because of the route I chose… people lost their lives. And the debt I bear comes from the potions destroyed during the attack.”

He looked up at her, shame plain in his eyes.

“High Crates accepted full responsibility for the incident. They compensated the caravan company and issued formal apologies to Lilybed for the delay and loss. The financial burden… was placed upon me.”

“What about the other man?” Veronica asked. “You said you chose the route together.”

“He… his name was Helm.” Galland’s voice lowered. “He is currently serving time in prison. His name was the one formally recorded on the route document. The legal blame fell upon him. For excessive and dangerous negligence. I was permitted to remain free—under debt.” He exhaled softly. “In that sense… I was fortunate.”

Veronica leaned back, folding her arms.

She had never heard any of this. None of it had reached public knowledge. In the future, Galland had simply appeared in Annesheim and begun building his merchant empire. There had been no tale of failure, no burden of debt—no slow climb from ruin.

“How much of the ninety-four thousand have you repaid?” she asked.

His expression darkened further. “Ninety-four thousand is what remains. The original sum was one hundred and twenty-seven thousand.”

“How many years has it been?”

“Three.”

“What?” The word slipped out before she could temper it.

That made no sense. If three years of work had reduced the debt by only thirty thousand vix, it should take nearly a decade more to repay the remainder. At that rate, he would never reach Annesheim in the timeline she remembered.

Had something changed later?

“It is slow,” Galland continued. “As a junior merchant, I am entrusted with only minor trade dealings. I am not eligible for promotion until the debt is cleared.”

“Is there any way to have it forgiven?” she asked. “Reduced? Renegotiated?”

“I do not know,” he admitted. “The terms were set firmly. The only recourse would be to appeal directly to the heads of High Crates.”

“How likely would that be?” Veronica asked.

“Very unlikely,” he replied. “If you were to advocate on my behalf, they would most likely attempt to seize this contract in exchange. An agreement with the Mage Tower is worth far more than my debt. They would never permit me to depart with such an arrangement unless I represented High Crates in the matter.”

His eyes lifted to meet hers. “They would assign one of the senior merchants to assume the role instead. And judging by your… partiality toward me, that is not an outcome you would accept, is it, Miss Veronica?”

There was a tinge of resignation—bitterness in his voice.

“You do not want High Crates,” he said quietly. “You want me.”

Veronica inclined her head.

“That is the only reason I brought this document to you today. You are the only person I believe capable of carrying this forward,” she said. “A mistake in the past does not define the rest of your life. Mistakes are meant to be learned from, no?”

Silence settled between them.

At last he spoke again, voice much softer.

“This intelligence network of yours… they truly spoke so highly of me?”

“They did.”

He shifted in his seat, visibly uneasy.

For a man burdened by debt and stripped of advancement, there was no logical reason he should be entrusted with something of this magnitude.

And yet Veronica sat across from him, offering it as though it were the most natural decision in the world.

Galland rose and gave a small bow.

“I am sorry to waste your time, Miss Veronica. But until this debt is repaid, I cannot leave High Crates to accept such a contract. I would advise you to seek someone else who can properly manage your arrangement.”

“You won’t renegotiate his debt?” Veronica asked.

She now sat in a different room: A small office. Dark shelves lined the walls, filled with ledgers. Along the opposite wall were award certificates, several of them, all within their own frames.

Galland was sent back to deal with whatever he had been working on before her arrival. Now, it was just her and the other man in the room.

Across from her sat Dain, one of High Crates’ senior merchants. A person of status. His coat was cut from expensive cloth, deep green with gold embroidery along the sleeves. Several rings adorned his fingers as he rested his steepled hands upon the desk.

“I do apologize, Miss Veronica,” Dain said smoothly. “But the error was entirely his. We, as his house, bore the financial consequences of his incompetence.”

His voice was calm, and yet it was filled with control and arrogance. Or perhaps his voice was plain—and Veronica’s irritation was clouding her judgment.

“This trade agreement you possess…” He gestured lightly toward the sealed parchment at her side. “Unless you intend for High Crates to represent you—which we would be delighted to arrange—Galland will remain where he is. He owes us a debt. It would be poor business to release him at the precise moment he becomes valuable.”

Veronica frowned. “Valuable, huh?” she repeated. “How does a single route decision amount to nearly one hundred thousand vix in personal liability?”

Dain sighed, as if recounting a tiresome tale.

“It began during a winter shipment bound for Lilybed—”

“I am aware of the shipment,” Veronica interrupted. “What I wish to understand is how bandits attacking a caravan becomes solely Galland’s fault. And why a junior merchant, three years ago even more inexperienced than he is now, was entrusted with route selection in the first place.”

A faint smile touched Dain’s lips.

“It was his own ambition,” he replied. “Galland insisted on proving himself. He volunteered to accompany the delegation. Naturally, we could not allow him near negotiations. He was assigned a logistical task instead. Two weeks to evaluate alternate winter routes.”

He leaned back. “A simple responsibility.”

His expression hardened. “One he mishandled so severely that we lost nearly one hundred thousand vix. Not only in destroyed goods, but in reputation. We were forced to issue formal apologies to both Lilybed and the Scavenger transport company. Do you have any idea how damaging that is for a trading house?”

Veronica didn't blink. “And so you placed the entire burden upon him.”

Dain regarded her evenly. “Would you not have? It was his decision that led us into such a predicament.”

Veronica leaned forward. “Why is he being punished for this? And the other man. Why is he imprisoned? You had guards along the transport, did you not? It sounds like an honest mistake. If anything, the mercenary company failed to defend the convoy.”

He chuckled softly. “Yes. It does sound simple when you put it that way.”

His fingers unsteepled as he explained.

“That is what would have occurred under normal circumstances. However, the danger level declared for the route was significantly understated.”

“Understated?”

“Our contract required that we declare the minimum and maximum risk for the journey so the Scavengers could deploy an appropriate escort,” he said. “A peaceful road requires only a pair of guards. A monster valley requires squads.”

“Then what was declared?”

“Low to medium risk. Environmental hazards, winter wolves, nothing more. They dispatched five mercenaries. Adequate for the stated threat and value of the goods being transported.”

“And five trained mercenaries could not repel bandits?”

“If they had been ordinary bandits, yes,” he said. “But we later learned the route passed through the heart of a large encampment. Nearly twenty attackers, possibly more.”

He folded his fingers again.

“It took months for word to reach us that the convoy never arrived. We lost goods, lives, and time. Galland was even set on trial for this. Him and Helm—both suspected of purposely misguiding the convoy. Helm tried to claim innocence, while Galland admitted being guilty of negligence. In the end, Helm was imprisoned for showing no remorse or understanding of fault, while Galland was punished with paying back the damages.”

He paused.

“The focal issue here wasn’t the bandits themselves. The issue lay with the declared risk. It was all wrong. Had Galland and Helm conducted more thorough research, the route would never have been chosen. It was the sort of danger a common citizen might have learned of simply by following the news.”

Veronica pressed her lips together. “How did Galland miss reports of a bandit encampment?”

Dain shook his head. “I cannot say. You would have to ask him. Perhaps ambition drove him to move quickly. Perhaps carelessness. If I recall correctly, there was even a recent news article in their shared office, mentioning bandit activity in the region. It appears they overlooked it. A costly oversight.”

Veronica’s expression hardened. That did not match the man she had heard about. He had failed to research the route? Ignored a report sitting in his own office?

“What about the rest of you?” she asked. “How did none of you know?”

“The responsibility was his,” Dain replied. “Senior merchants were occupied with negotiations and transport contracts. Galland and Helm were assigned the research. It was a simple task, yes, but one that required thorough attention. No one else had the time to spare.”

“That still sounds like negligence. Someone should have verified their findings.”

He shook his head. “That’s hindsight, miss. It is easy to criticize after the fact. The truth of the matter is that this is not how it works. We had contractual terms with both Galland and Helm. Should they prove grossly incompetent, they would share the legal and financial consequences. They accepted that risk. We are merely upholding the agreement. Everything has been conducted within the law.”

Veronica fell silent.

He had been a junior merchant then. That he would one day build a trading empire did not mean he had never made mistakes. If the contract truly contained such terms… was there anything she could do? Had Galland truly erred so badly?

She studied Dain for a moment, weighing his words.

“If the debt were resolved,” she said carefully, “would there be any remaining obligation keeping him here?”

Dain leaned forward slightly. “None. Once the debt is settled, he is free to accept your contract. Though, of course that would mean him leaving High Crates. A very life-changing, risky venture, I’d say.”

He watched her, voice firm. “Or, if you prefer, we could dismiss the debt entirely—provided you allow one of our senior merchants to assume the contract you carry. Galland could assist in its management, so long as he represents High Crates.”

Her brows furrowed. “You would trust him to manage it, even after his costly failure three years ago?”

He shrugged. “It has been three years. I expect he has learned his lesson.”

Veronica considered this for a few moments before rising from her chair.

“I apologize, Mr. Dain. But I am only interested in Galland’s personal assistance—not representation through High Crates. I will find a way to settle his debt and release him.”

High Crates was not the one she wanted. Galland was the only one she knew who could survive until the end of the world. A Galland chained down was not the Galland she sought.

His eyes didn’t blink. “I wish you luck procuring nearly one hundred thousand vix, Miss Veronica.” A faint smile curved his lips. One of pleasantries. One of fakes.

“Goodbye, Mr. Dain.”

She offered no parting gesture. She simply turned and left the office.
