Dreams were always a tricky subject. Questionable at the best of times and outright deadly in the worst, they had the tendency to entangle people in a web of illusion. There were only two ways to escape: either be under the protection of a deity or be stronger than the dream.

As I approached the doorway, Pup began to growl.

“You can stay,” I said. He wasn’t going to be much help out of my own dreams, either way.

The dream beast barked.

“Don’t worry. I’ll be back.” They needed me too much to kill me off, especially now.

Given when I had been given the necklace, I might have received a bit more help from my unofficial patron, making a few of my fights easier. If I had done that, I’d be in another deity’s favor, and that was never a good thing. It was bad enough that I owed Hypnos.

Pup barked again.

“Either come along or shut up.”

The creature dissolved into a cloud of mist. Maybe I had been a bit too harsh, but the time for half measures had gone.

Blanking my mind, I stepped forward. The moment I crossed the threshold, the doorway vanished behind me. I was in a dream tunnel once more, and I had no idea where it would take me.

The silver necklace around my throat had also dissolved, transforming into a band of vapor. I passed my fingers through it a few times. The material was light, just like air, yet it refused to move away from me. In different circumstances, I would say that I had been leashed.

The further I continued forward, the more my surroundings gained form. Rocks and trees would appear, creating the impression I was walking through a valley. Entire mountain chains formed, along with the forests that covered them. The sounds of steps echoed within the silence, as a solid path of stones formed beneath my feet.

After a few more minutes, I found myself at the outskirts of a city made entirely of mother-of-pearl and ivory.

Olympus? I wondered.

I had only heard it described in the past. Pact holders had no choice but to be loyal to their patron deities, but they had their vices same as any other participant. Also, the important ones loved to brag.

Despite the beauty, not a single living creature could be seen. Even my Beastmaster card didn’t let me sense any presence. If there had been dream beasts in this section of the dreamscape, they had moved a considerable distance away.

A black silhouette sat in a shining white gazebo. It was impossible to know for certain, but I suspected it to be the goddess who had spoken to me. Was she the same one who had told me how to escape the trials? It was possible, maybe even likely, though not certain.

“A city whiter than light placed on the top beyond the mountains,” I said as I approached. “Is there a reason you recreated Olympus for me, goddess?”

The silhouette didn’t respond. Her golden eyes stared at me for several seconds before she finally gestured for me to join her.

“I hoped that it would put you at ease,” she said. “Although, it seems to have the opposite effect. What happened during the rule-masters’ trial?”

Brief and to the point. A lot of college professors would have loved her.

“Nothing.” I sat opposite her. “We captured a small pest, but let the bigger fish go. Harlequin is pleased, but I doubt he knows who’s behind the mess. So, it can be said that nothing was achieved.”

“Always so cynical,” The silhouette said. “Did you reconsider my offer?”

Still the same tune.

“No,” I replied. There was no point in lying, not about this. “But I have something you might want.”

“Me?” The silhouette sounded amused. “And what’s that?”

“I know what the key is.”

“Fascinating. And what do you think it is?”

I didn’t answer straight away. Provided she was the one who had given me the hint, there was a good chance that she knew the answer. Then again, it was just as possible that she knew the image but not what it signified. Either way, the deities weren’t in the habit of confirming anything.

“The participant I killed was convinced that it was a way out of the Grand Arcana,” I said, focusing on the goddess’ eyes.

Reading the reaction of a deity ranged between unlikely and outright impossible, but the Grand Arcana cards were known to do wonders.

No surprise emanated from the golden eyes. They kept staring at me as if from far away. There was no doubt that deities had nothing in common with humans. Some called them different beings, but they missed the point entirely. They shared our image but remained as foreign to participants as people were to corals. All the conversations and interactions lay in the small sliver of overlap between our goals and theirs.

“That’s what you came to see me about?” the silhouette asked. “To help you find a way out?”

“If they can leave the games, they can harm my family. I’m here to find out whether you could prevent it.”

“Discussing the world you knew is frowned upon,” she said with the warmth of a bureaucrat. “Even those that aren’t in the trials must obey the rules. I can only say that we’ll do what we can to keep an eye on your family. Somehow, I doubt you’ll be satisfied with such an answer.”

“You need me, goddess,” I said. “I’ve no idea why, but you and Hypnos have set your sights on me ever since the first trial. Unlike the other group, you didn’t make any threats. That’s why I’ve come to make a mutually beneficial arrangement.”

Such arrogance normally merited immediate punishment. Since we were in a dream, the usual Arcana restrictions didn’t apply. The goddess might be unable to kill me, but she could flood my sleep with nightmares that would make me beg for death.

A second passed, then another. After the third, I already knew that my proposal was accepted. There would be some dancing around the push, some fake reluctance, but for all intents and purposes the deal was already made.

“The people I’m after were given the key by the deity that interests you,” I continued. “So, hunting them down is no longer enough.”

“You want to do more?” The silhouette leaned toward me.

“I want to find out the reason.” And kill everyone who had set this up. “I was told that I wasn’t targeted by accident. If that’s true, someone somewhere made the decision.”

“You won’t like what you find. What will you do when you find out? Kill a deity?”

“Participants cannot kill deities. Everyone knows that. However, if I agree to assist you, I won’t have to.”

I had no doubt that this was the first time that a participant had offered to work alongside a deity and not for it. The notion was probably completely foreign, yet dream deities had a lot more imagination than most. If anyone was able to appreciate the possibilities, it was them.

“The rules don’t allow for such interaction,” the silhouette said. “Our methods remained limited, even in dreams.”

“I have a solution for that. It might take me some time, but it’s doable. Until then, I’d like to come to an agreement.”

“An agreement?”

“No pact, just an understanding. You have a way of reaching me, and now I have a way of reaching you.” I touched the dream ring around my neck.

The silhouette stood up. As she did, the gazebo’s roof vanished, revealing an open sky.

“Your proposal has merit,” she said. “But I cannot make such an arrangement on vagueness alone. You’re strong and a lot more resourceful than we expected, yet finding a way to freely interact with us and not break the rules… that is difficult to imagine.”

“The Divine Page,” I said. “It allows the mortal that has it to accept tasks from the deities outside of pacts and sponsorship requests.”

I didn’t feel the need to mention that I suspected that the silhouette had let me find the card eight years in my past future. Knowing that encouraged me that she could find a way to do it again.

“An arrangement,” she repeated, no change in her voice or behavior.

“Similar to a pact but without strings attached.”

“It might be considered acceptable, provided you find the card you mentioned. Until then, our assistance will be limited.”

And yet, she didn’t mention anything about the requests. If I knew deities, that was her way of saying that I’d still be getting requests in dreams or through Eleonor; requests that I would be expected to fulfill to their satisfaction without only a vague promise of support. Even that was better than making a pact, though.

“Thank you.” I was just about to leave when the silhouette reached out to me.

A yellowish flash appeared in her hand. Looking closer, I saw that it was a card… and not any card.

“The Drunkard,” I whispered. “The final Chalice card.”

“Consider this a goodwill gesture.”

The silhouette left the card in the air, then pulled back her hand. Since gravity had no power in dreams, my prize remained floating there, waiting for me to pluck it from the air. Probably this was their way of showing me that they held all the cards—literally and figuratively—but it also revealed their desperation. Deities only made deals when they had to. I knew what I was getting out of this, but had no idea what they were and how it was linked with the people who betrayed me.

I grabbed the card and added it to my collection.

One way or another, I was going to find out.
