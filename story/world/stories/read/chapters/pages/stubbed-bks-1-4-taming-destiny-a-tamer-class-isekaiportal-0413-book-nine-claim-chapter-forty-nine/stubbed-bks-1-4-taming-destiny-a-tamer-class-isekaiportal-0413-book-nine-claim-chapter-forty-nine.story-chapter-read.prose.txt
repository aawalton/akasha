The first day is a blur of activity. Heck, the first week isn’t much better. Choosing the spot for our initial settlement takes half a day – I wait until Elarion gets back to me with his group’s findings about which areas they feel would be best for farming. In the meantime, Valence’s scouts and my companions have been updating me with useful information that they’ve discovered about the creatures that live in the local area and their typical habits – planting our settlement on a path that the local denizens use everyday to journey to the river, for example, would be an excellent way to end up having our fields trampled and our buildings flattened.

Finally, though, we take the spot that I suspected we might take to begin with – the tactical advantages of the hill simply outweighed the disadvantages. At least the mages have confirmed that there’s plenty of groundwater to draw on, so our distance from the river is less of an obstacle than it might have been. As for farmland, the most fertile sections are at the foot of the hill, which will require the farmers to commute every day. When we manage to domesticate some creatures, we’ll need to consider how to manage that as they can’t be left at a distance unguarded. Most likely we need to make provision for them within the settlement.

There might be better settlement places around, of course, but this one certainly ticks all of our boxes, and we feel the pressure to choose somewhere and start building.

Once we’ve chosen our starting place, it’s all hands to the plow as we begin transforming it into something that fits our needs.

Unlike settlements on Earth, we don’t put ours on the hill – in a world with powerful flying beasts, that’s asking for trouble. Instead, we only put a watchtower at the crest of the hill, and build the settlement into it. Hollowing out a massive cavern in the hill and reinforcing it so it doesn’t risk dropping on our heads with the first earthquake took the other Earth Mages and me more than a day to accomplish, and two full rituals.

It’s a pity that Kalanthia departed from our company shortly after landing – we could really have done with her help. I’m still not entirely sure why she left – she said she wanted to explore, but from the intensity and urgency that flickered over the Bond, I suspect that that is only half the story. But, holding our Bond or not, our relationship is not one where I could demand she stay simply because it will benefit me. Anyway, if she feels that strongly about it, I certainly won’t stand in her way. Perhaps at some point she’ll trust me enough to explain what was so important to pull her away so soon after landing.

The structural changes to the hill were only the start. First was reinforcing the material. With so many people – and beasts – capable of using earth-magic, the threat of something dropping the whole hill on our heads is real. To avoid that, the combined force of Enchanters and apprentices spent days sinking magical circuits into the rock. They had to work quickly, but also extremely carefully – the spellwork is designed to absorb or deflect any attempts to modify the structure later so it has to be right the first time.

That’s why we created such a cavernous hole in the first place – for the enchantments to work most effectively against beast intrusion, the Enchanters need to make it so that we can’t adjust the size afterwards which means that we had to think ahead to how big the settlement might become rather than making it solely for the present.

Even the wells had to be sunk before the Enchanters got to work because they would otherwise be literal holes in the system. Now, though, their depths glimmer black with water – the enchantments will make sure that nothing other than the water will be coming up from below.

Once the Enchanters have done their job, it’s over to me again. Over the course of two full days, I pour tons and tons of lava out onto the cavern floor. It takes that long partly because I pace myself – sure, I use the mana potions and occasionally the cleansing potion, but I don’t push myself anywhere near to the extent I did when we were escaping the Tier five. The damage I caused myself during that time took almost a month to heal – it was worth it then in exchange for our lives; it’s not worth it now.

It then takes me another day to gently pull the heat out of the lava to solidify it into rock while every Mage with a wind spell or Skill available who isn’t otherwise occupied vents out the toxic fumes.

As for why I decided to layer lava over the enchantments? It’s partly for protection – buried in more than a metre of rock, they will both endure longer and be less accessible to sabotage attempts. It’s also partly for practicality – the enchantments imbuing the original floor of the cavern prevent us from using the rock there to create our homes; the lava rock I lay down has no such limitations.

Importantly from my perspective, it will also allow us to put in a proper system for water supply and sanitation – we’ve sunk five wells in to ensure that no one has to travel very far to access it, but I would really like to institute proper plumbing for everyone. Running water isn’t essential from the start since we have access to the wells, but if we delay putting waste disposal into place, we’ll risk disease taking hold. I’ve learned enough from the healers on the ships to know that concerns such as cholera and dysentery are still an issue in this world, even if the diseases themselves may not be identical.

By the dawn of the ninth day, the cavern is ready for our settlement to begin being built in earnest. I’m currently standing not far from the centre of the cavern, accompanied by five Earth Mages, three Plant Mages who have brought with them some massive trunks of wood, and a non-Classer who Nicholas assigned to me from Azaarde – he studied architecture at the Moriaxar equivalent of a university and has been working in the city planning department for the last ten years.

He consequently has some very firm ideas about how the city should be built, some of which I agree with; some of which I decidedly don’t.

“My lord,” the city planner emphasises firmly. “You cannot have the healers in charge of the water supply! That must be your domain. Let the healers have water, by all means, but we must build the command compound around this well.”

I raise an eyebrow at him.

“Quenir, we’re hardly giving the healers control of the water supply. There are five wells.”

“Yet if you have both healing compound and command compound next door, and it is the healers who have the well, it is them who are in control of it!” Quenir insists with a decisive slash of his hand. “Please, my lord, consider the implications! The message you are sending everyone who notices!”

I wave a hand with a hint of irritation. I wasn’t expecting this to be such a sticking point and we have a good deal more to accomplish today.

“That I value the comfort and ease of those who support the health and well-being of my city?” I ask him sardonically.

“That you consider the healers more important than anyone else – including yourself!”

I eye him. He certainly seems to feel strongly about this matter; his arms wave wildly in the air and his voice has risen in volume. Then I actually think about his words and I suppose I can see his point of view.

It’s not like I don’t consider the healers important – and in some ways they are more important than anyone else, including myself. But declaring that so everyone can see it risks affecting the morale of everyone else. Because right now everyone is important to our success – Warriors, crafters, carers, healers, strategists, mages, farmers….

I don’t know if most people will read as much into the placement of a well as Quenir seems to, but if they do, I admit that it’s not a message I would like to send.

“Fine,” I sigh. “But we make sure that they have water immediately available to them. Eventually, I want every building here to have running water piped directly to them, but I know that’s not feasible right now. However, the healers need it.”

The city planner’s eyes are wide.

“To every house?”

I nod with a small smile tugging at the corner of my mouth.

“And waste disposal.”

His eyes narrow in calculation.

“That would lessen the impact of having the well in your courtyard,” he points out thoughtfully, “though I suppose if the pipes all connect to the well, then you would maintain control over it and be able to cut off water supply in an emergency.”

“Quenir,” I say firmly, putting up a hand to stop him in his tracks. “I accept your point about valuing people equally, but I do not intend to ever try to control them with the threat of cutting off water if they don’t comply.”

He eyes me with an unconvinced look on his face.

“As you say, my lord. I suppose that having multiple wells does make it more difficult to use that as a strategy.”

I sigh quietly but don’t say anything – right now we’re in agreement about the whats. The whys are another question but trying to convince him one way or another would probably use up far too much time.

We continue discussing the design, soon hitting another snag.

“We must ensure that the command building is the most ornate. It must project power,” Quenir asserts firmly.

I gaze at him in exasperation.

“Is that really an appropriate use of our time right now? Can’t we just create something basic and functional and then improve it later?”

Another argument ensues, one that I eventually have to concede. Quenir makes good points about how, as long as we have a design clearly in mind, magic means it won’t take us much more time to create something ornate in comparison to something basic. And that it’s therefore better to consider permanency rather than settling for something temporary.

He also argues eloquently about morale. I would have thought that spending time on making a building ornate when most people don’t even have four walls and a ceiling would poorly affect morale. Quenir disagrees – as do the mages. I decide to bow to their superior knowledge of their own culture.

The enthusiasm some of the mages show at designs that make me recoil in horror convinces me that I’m better off steering the river than trying to stop it in its tracks. At least I’m able to shoot down some of the more outrageous suggestions.

The mages and I get to work first on the healer compound which we make fairly basic – foundations and ground floor walls in stone, and upper walls, ceilings, and floors in wood. We give it three storeys – the top floor holds living quarters for all the healers and their apprentices while the second floor is divided into rooms for long-term patients, guests, or overflow to make space for new healers and apprentices. The ground floor is purely for in-patients and preparation of treatments and includes space for a kitchen.

We’ll need to come back later with the plumbing, but that’s going to take more than a day’s worth of thought. The carpenters will also have to work on the furnishings, but that is less of a priority.

Since Quenir is still busy designing the command structure – pulling designs out of a spatial storage and muttering to himself over them as he annotates with a pencil – we move onto the warehouse. As we’re using the back wall of the cavern, we only need to build three walls and a roof. Since we’re inside a cavern and therefore won’t need to worry about rain, we use a flat roof, as we did on the healer’s compound.

The mages consider it to be a small storage space designed only to hold essentials – at thirty metres long, ten metres wide, and six metres high. Already we’ve been discussing having several of these scattered around the cavern to ensure that supplies are close at hand. We’ll also have a much bigger one near the docks, though we’ll hold off on that one for now – we need to know more about the area before we start making vulnerable structures like that.

Of course, once the Enchanters get in, the ‘small’ storage space won’t be so small – spatial enchantments will be able to easily double what the warehouse can hold. But they’re busy right now.

Finally, Quenir has finished his design – to be fair to him, it’s impressive how quickly he came up with it, even if it seems that he’s just adapted a design from Azaarde. The command centre he’s suggested is more like a small mansion – four storeys high, as long as the warehouse, and designed in a U shape. There are colonnades, arched windows, a couple of turrets, sloped roofs, and even a tower that seems to have little purpose. I also see a faint sketch of gardens.

I eye him dubiously.

“You think it will improve morale for everyone to see that the lord is living in a fancy manor while they’re all still in tents or stuck on the ship?”

“As long as it doesn’t seem likely that it will remain that way, yes, my lord,” Quenir replies firmly. “It gives hope, something to aim for. Something to emulate. If the lord is stuck in an ugly box, then what hope is there for everyone else to live in anything better?”

I frown, but ultimately shrug. I suppose it makes a kind of sense, and I certainly don’t intend on living in luxury while my people suffer in squalor so…perhaps he’s right?

“Fine. Let’s get going,” I sigh.

By the end of the day, we’ve finished those buildings and have started making some rudimentary plans for a sanitation system. Of course, to do that, we also have to have a good idea of what exactly is going to be where. I resolve to bring up the plans with the other lords – Layton and Josiah in particular should be familiar with what kind of things cause no end of headaches in cities. They’ll be planning their own settlements, of course, but that doesn’t mean they won’t be willing to weigh in on this one – until we’ve stabilised our position, they won’t be leaving to take their own territory. Anyway, they might see this as a useful practice run.

Heading back to the ships, I shake my head as I pass through the gap that will soon hold two gates – the smiths are working on some hinges and locks for the gates that will both hold up to a charge of a Tier three, and mean that even the non-Classers can open and close it.

“Impressive work,” I tell the head Plant Mage who’s standing near Sarran, both of them overseeing the work. She has a self-satisfied look on her face but accepts the compliment politely enough.

“Thank you, my lord. It has been a challenge to work with the local flora, but my mages and I feel that we’re making progress on that front.” She pauses for a moment. “It will take more time before we are confident that we can advise the farmers on what to cultivate, however. Some of the plants are rather…reticent to give up their secrets.”

I raise one eyebrow. Before learning how to use magic myself, I’d have found it difficult to believe a plant could be reticent about anything, but I know better now.

“Are the herbalists and Alchemists helping with that?” We’ve brought seeds and starter plants from Moriax, but if we can identify local species that we can eat and cultivate, that would be better. I’m very much aware of the damage that was done to ecosystems on different continents by the colonists on Earth and don’t want to be the cause of another rabbits-in-Australia thing. Then again, I don’t want us to starve either.

“Certainly, my lord,” Sarran replies crisply. Then he nods at the wall. “Though, as you can see, they have had other priorities up until now.”

I nod in acknowledgement – getting sufficient defenses up has been a major priority so the potion-makers and herbalists have been busy treating the wood to make it nonflammable and roughly equivalent to poor steel in durability. It might feel like overkill for a structure that isn’t intended to be permanent, but given how many times our patrols have engaged with a local beast coming to investigate, it really isn’t. Layton has had his work cut out for him in directing the fighters appropriately.

Right now, the Alchemists have finished, but the Enchanters continue to work climbing all over the structure to carve runes and set enchantments to increase its defensive capacity and add a few nasty surprises for anything trying to climb or burst through.

I nod in satisfaction. Perhaps we can start moving people off the ships in the next couple of days – even if they’ll have to sleep in tents until we have houses for them, they’ll be glad to have more space. Though that does rely on us having a sanitation plan in place….

Anyway, it would be good to have a meeting with the other lords for more than just discussing city planning – we’ve been keeping track of what the others are up to, but we could do with going through it all in detail. I think everyone will be too tired tonight, but there’s another option.

Let’s meet up for breakfast at the sixth mark tomorrow, I suggest in the Alliance Bond to a barely withheld chorus of groans.
