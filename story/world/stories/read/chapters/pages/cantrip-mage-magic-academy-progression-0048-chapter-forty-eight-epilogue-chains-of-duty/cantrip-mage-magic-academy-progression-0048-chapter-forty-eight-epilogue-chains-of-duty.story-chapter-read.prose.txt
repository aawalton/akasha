48: Epilogue: Chains of Duty

~Greenarbor Keep, One Week Later~

Seneschal Stoken Dahl was a man bound to his lord by the chains of duty.

Lord Dalton Card’s keep stood a half a mile from Greenarbor. It perched on the cliff that blocked the worst of the waves from the city’s port, and its single tower served double duty as defensive redoubt and protective lighthouse. House Card had always controlled the keep, even when they hadn’t ruled Greenarbor itself. But while it could withstand a siege well enough and its light had protected ships for well over two centuries, the keep was much, much older than that—and its main purpose had always been to control the sprawling dungeon complex below.

Seneschal Dahl was currently standing in front of the chromite-and-iron gates of that very dungeon, accompanied in his note-taking by the sounds of screams and shouted orders.

The culling was complete.

Dahl observed the scene as, for the fourth time in four years, the dungeon’s last pair of functional gates were shut and resealed. Wounded and dying mages littered the cold stone floor deep below the keep; here and there, a fresh-faced healer attended one of the spellswords or battle mages, casting green-glowing healing runes over them before moving on.

The seneschal observed the yearly culling in lieu of Lord Card himself. The ruler of Greenarbor could no longer attend, and no one wanted to risk his death or remaining sanity by forcing him to.

After a moment, Dahl scribbled a final note to himself, nodded curtly, and excused himself as the captain of Lord Card’s personal guard confirmed the gate was locked and magically sealed. The dungeon, like any other in Alemia, had to be culled periodically. Once per year was the fewest cullings the experts recommended—and the most Lord Card would allow it to be opened. So, once per year it was.

The seneschal pushed the battle and bloodshed from his mind. It was done for another year, and it was back to his routine business. Lord Card’s correspondence first, then a verbal report to the man himself. Seneschal Dahl flipped through the letters. Several from other nobles went into a stack—he’d let Lord Card read and respond to those himself—but any that promised words of serious business went into Dahl’s own stack, to be handled personally. Lord Card had no patience for business. Not anymore.

His stack was much larger than Lord Card’s.

And then there was one, from Headmaster Tomlish of Varin’s Academy. It was marked with his own seal and carried a cantrip to prevent unwanted eyes from opening it. The folded parchment was addressed specifically to Lord Card.

That never stopped Seneschal Dahl, though. He had a letter-opener for just such a piece of magic, and in seconds, his eyes perused the headmaster’s letter.

He froze halfway down as a name popped out. It hit him like a physical blow.

“Lemiene? She’s attending Varin’s Academy now?” Dahl muttered to himself. As he read on, his brow furrowed, and by the end of it, his expression of workmanlike disdain for his task had been replaced with worry.

How best to handle this? He couldn’t tell Lord Card, that was for sure. Today was already full of ill news for the old lord, and to hear this would push him over the edge. And keeping the ramifications of the rest of it from falling on Greenarbor or House Card would require a careful, light touch, not that of a broken lord. After minutes spent pondering, he tossed the folded parchment into his own file and stood. It was time to brief Lord Card on the culling and on the other news from across Alemia, as was his duty.

The Lord of Greenarbor preferred it dim, so his throne room was lit only by a single Bright Ball near the entrance. He preferred it quiet, so no attendants or hangers-on filled the wide, echoing space below the lighthouse tower. No tapestries covered its bare walls, and its windows were shuttered, but a cold draft still made it in through the gaps. Lord Card refused the warmth of a fire or heated water; the only comforts in the frigid, empty room were a thin pad on the chromite throne and a single burning stick of incense that wafted a sour, citric scent toward the door.

In the last four years, Lord Card had left his keep only twice—both times to attend the long, agonizing meetings between the most powerful of the Governing Houses. Nothing less than that would root him out of his quarters, and he only attended ‘court’ for an hour or two a day—and only then with Dahl to speak for him. Lord Card was a private, reclusive ruler.

He hadn’t always been, but the last four years had been hard on him.

As Seneschal Dahl approached Lord Card’s throne and looked at the man who’d once been on the precipice of being called a Great Mage, he tried not to stare at the man he’d sworn his allegiance to.

Lord Card had aged rapidly. His skin was sallow, and it hung from his once-chiseled face loosely, like a shawl on bowed shoulders. His eyes seemed almost empty. Cataracts covered them, and he hardly seemed to see Dahl even when he walked close enough to touch the ancient-looking lord. A smell hung in the air around him. The smell made Dahl want nothing more than to leave; it was the stink of death defied only with magic. Here and there, white hairs poked from Lord Card’s liver-spotted head, and a ragged, out-of-control beard tumbled across his robes. His own attunement had betrayed him, and even the other Great Mages could do nothing but delay the inevitable.

No fifty-five-year-old man should be this…decrepit. No time mage should be this…ravaged by the years.

Lord Card had been one of the most powerful men in Alemia. His House had rivalled House Andrese to the south in influence, if not in power. That had been four short years ago, before the Lord of Greenarbor stepped out of the dungeon for the last time, a haunted look on his face. That look had never fully left; it had just been buried by wrinkles. Now, Greenarbor hung on only by the efforts of Seneschal Dahl and a few men like him. Lord Card was…preoccupied…with but one matter.

“Is it finished?” the man on the throne asked.

“Yes, my lord. This year’s culling was successful. Your Guard was able to reach the gate to the second floor before being forced back.”

“Will it hold for another year?” Desperation filled the cracking, whispered words.

“It will.”

“And the survivors?”

Seneshchal Dahl pursed his lips. His brow furrowed, and he looked at his feet. “We found no survivors, my lord.”

The Lord of Greenarbor’s near-white eyes stared at Dahl. A wolf—once powerful and broad, but now as patchy and arthritic as its master—stalked from behind the throne to join in the watching. For a long moment, no one spoke. Then Lord Card nodded. “Ah. Ah, I see…I see…”

The wolf lay down at its master’s feet, and Lord Card drifted into a fugue.

Dahl recognized it the moment it happened. He quickly launched into the rest of his briefing. The other Governing Houses were doing such-and-such. There was the brewing succession war in what had been Duke Kurnix’s lands. Greenarbor’s fishing fleets reported an eight percent decrease in catches from last year. Imports from southern Alemia were up, while exports were down. The head of the Hunters thought there was a new monster in the woods, and he requested Lord Card’s Guard to help hunt it down before it hurt people.

Seneschal Dahl was very careful not to mention the name of the head of the Hunters. Lord Card was so sensitive about that name.

“And there have been fourteen new births in Greenarbor so far this month, according to the Midwives’ Guild,” he finished as Lord Card shook himself back into consciousness.

“Good…good…add it all to the Records,” the old noble said. His voice dropped to nearly a whisper. “The dungeon remains sealed. The rest of it matters not. I leave it all to you. That curse…that curse we found must remain entombed and buried.”

“My lord, if I may…”

“Speak freely, Stoken.”

The seneschal nodded. “Greenarbor suffers for your fixation on the dungeon, and so do you, my lord. Please, leave the keep and see your people again. Perhaps the fresh air will rejuvenate you, and it would certainly do your people good to know you see them.”

“I cannot,” Lord Card wheezed. “If I abandon my post here, watching over that accursed dungeon, Greenarbor would suffer even worse for it. No, not Greenarbor…all of Alemia would suffer. I have seen it. What I fled from…it cannot be allowed to be free.”

Dahl waited, head bowed, as Lord Card drifted once again into a fugue. It came over him quickly; he stared into nothing, eyes not seeing even the vague shadow of his seneschal. Dahl didn’t know what the Lord of Greenarbor experienced in his all-too-frequent fugues, and truth be told, he didn’t care. The Y’aer-cursed things were a nuisance, but they were also useful.

The seneschal slipped out the door before his lord could rouse himself again. As he left, he pointed at a young, pretty maid waiting in the hall. “His grace will need to be taken lunch in his quarters. See to it that he makes it there, and report back to me.”

The maid curtsied. “M’lord,” she said, and she disappeared down the hall to tell the cooks and scullery girls their new instructions.

Stoken Dahl shook his head slowly. Lord Card was sonless, and that was the only reason he hadn’t pressed for the prematurely wizened and withered old fool to step down. As long as he sat on the throne of Greenarbor, Seneschal Dahl could keep doing his job. The moment he died or abdicated, it would be chaos.

And if there was one thing Lord Dalton Card was correct about, it was that the curse within that dungeon could not be allowed to escape.

With Lord Card handled for the day and the kitchens in order, Seneschal Dahl sat at his desk and unfolded the troubling letter from Varin’s Academy’s headmaster.

Duty was a tricky master, but he’d been navigating its mazelike bounds for a lifetime, and as he read and reread its contents, he made up his mind.

To wash his lord’s hands of this mess would require three letters.

The first was addressed back to Headmaster Tomlish. The Great Mage wouldn’t require a response, and it was unlikely that he’d even read it, but even so…honor dictated that Lord Card reply. It was a simple form letter, and it took Dahl less than ten minutes to pen it in flowing Alemic.

The second was also addressed to Varin’s Academy. The annual letter. In the past, it had gone to a house in Greenarbor—to the head of the Hunters’ house, in fact. But this year, it would join Headmaster Tomlish’s in its journey to Alemia’s most prestigious school of magecraft. After all, its intended recipient was a student there, and it would send a second, hidden message to her when it arrived.

Dahl struggled with this letter every year.

It shouldn’t be this hard to write a letter to a commoner girl. There should be a form for it. But none of them fit quite right; how to convey the necessary information, year after year, with the right tone? He worked on it for almost forty-five minutes before setting his scratched-out, ink-pooled draft aside and starting again on a clean final copy. And the entire time he wrote, he thought about his duty. Not just his duty to his lord, or to Greenarbor. But his duty to Miss Eola Lemiene. What he—and his lord—owed her.

When he finally finished, he took a moment to stretch his hand.

Then he began on the third letter.

This one would travel south to Highstone. It was the only way Dahl could see of passing responsibility from his lord’s frail shoulders on to someone else. House Andrese was better equipped to handle the problem of Eola Lemiene than Lord Card was, after all.

He sealed all three envelopes, and on his way to the rookery, he pondered the question that had haunted him for four years: What was the nature of duty when it conflicted with itself? Were his actions those of a coward, or of a simple man doing his duty? Was he to blame for what he was about to do?

And, as always, he came up with the same comforting answer. The Lemiene girl deserved to know that her parents still lived within the dungeon. The monstrosity inside that same dungeon had to remain there. And the Cult of Y’aer must be informed of the potential blasphemy occurring at Varin’s Academy.

All three were true, and Seneschal Stoken Dahl had a duty to uphold. He was bound in its chains.
