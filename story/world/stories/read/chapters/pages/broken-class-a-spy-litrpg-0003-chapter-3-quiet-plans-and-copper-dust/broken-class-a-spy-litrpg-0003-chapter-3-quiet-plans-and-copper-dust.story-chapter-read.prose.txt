Once Arthur stepped out of the mud-brick hut that smelled like dying hope and yesterday’s stew, he did the first thing any trained spy would do: scout for exits. Because nothing said "welcome home" like barbed gates and looming stone walls.

The mine’s perimeter wall stood about twenty feet high. Thick, imposing, and oddly pristine, considering this was a place people volunteered to suffer in. Most slaves looked at the wall with reverence. Arthur looked at it like a rat studies a maze: charting the dead ends, sniffing out escape routes. Scattered along the length were guard posts, sturdy little boxes probably full of crossbows and bad attitudes. He spotted two massive gates, where hulking oxen-like beasts waited patiently in harnesses, each pulling heavy carriages piled high with raw copper. The creatures looked like someone had taken a regular ox and added muscle, malice, and a bad attitude. Clearly just transport animals, but Arthur still filed them under Exit A and Exit B - because if he ever needed to bolt, hiding behind a copper cart wasn’t the worst idea he’d ever had.

After trudging nearly a mile through packed dirt and broken spirits, Arthur got his first look at the infamous Darvaza Mine.

It was... obscene.

An inverted pyramid gouged into the earth like the gods had thrown a tantrum with a cosmic pickaxe. The pit stretched half a mile deep and probably smelled like rust and crushed dreams. The slaves - no need for euphemisms here, looked like ants from this distance. If ants were starved, sunburnt, and one wrong step away from becoming a smear on the rocks.

The place buzzed with motion. Everyone looked half-dead, which meant they were probably considered above average around here. Arthur scanned for others close to his own apparent age, hollow-cheeked boys with thousand-yard stares and backs permanently bent from labor. He mimicked their gait and blank expressions with practiced ease. After all, blending in was his specialty. Spy training had taught him to disappear into a crowd. Unfortunately, this crowd reeked of blood, copper dust, and despair.

They joined a slow-moving line where workers pressed their fingers into a crude ledger and picked up mining tools that looked like they were last maintained during the Bronze Age. Arthur copied the process, even though the handle of his pickaxe had more splinters than wood.

What caught his eye, though, was a man standing off to the side. Arms crossed and an expression carved from stone. This guy wasn’t starving. No, he had the lean, corded muscle of someone who killed before breakfast and did pushups between murders. His armor looked clean. His boots were polished. Hell, the man had posture.

Arthur observed without looking like he was observing. Old habits. This guy was either a Guardian or someone who’d killed one and taken his clothes. Arthur wasn’t sure which was worse.

As he pressed his finger into the ledger, a strange sensation prickled through his skin, as if something was quietly siphoning off a piece of him. Subtle. Faint. But it was there. He mentally filed that under ‘Ask Dad Before It Becomes a Problem’, right below ‘Figure Out What the Hell That Ledger Is Actually Recording’.

They kept walking until the din of hammers and coughing slaves provided some cover. Arthur leaned in just enough and spoke low and flat. “The one near the gate. Clean gear, short sword. Rajak?”

His father glanced over without slowing. “No. That’s Cho. He’s a Guardian, but not Rajak. You’ll know Rajak when you see him.”

Arthur gave a slight nod. Neutral. Forgettable.

“Just follow my lead,” his father said, voice calm and flat. “Do the work. Don’t get noticed.”

Arthur gave a small nod and stepped into the rhythm of the mine like he’d done it a hundred times, only this time with a pickaxe instead of a silenced pistol.

He started slow. Observed.

After less than a minute, he’d already catalogued which nearby workers were competent and which ones probably swung their tools like they were trying to kill a particularly stubborn rat. He focused on the efficient ones. Their stances, their foot placement, the subtle way they transferred weight through their legs and into the pickaxe. He watched how they timed the swing, how the head struck the copper-veined rock at just the right angle to split without bouncing.

It wasn’t perfect, but Arthur mimicked the motions well enough to swing more efficiently than most. Not flawlessly, but better. He estimated a solid twenty percent improvement over where he started, just from paying attention. Not enough to impress anyone, but enough to matter over time. Especially if you were quietly trying to outrun starvation quotas without looking like you were trying.

Thirty minutes passed in silence, save for the rhythmic clink of steel on stone and the occasional grunt from someone overexerting a half-starved body. Arthur kept his eyes down and voice low when he finally asked, “Is there any benefit to mining more?”

His father didn’t look up. “Food’s tied to output. The more we mine, the more we eat.”

Simple economics, if your economy was starvation.

“We’re also behind for this moon,” his father continued. “Because of your injury. And... I’m not what I used to be.”

“How long until the moon turns over?” Arthur asked, still swinging. “And how far behind are we?”

Paul paused, running some numbers in his head. “Ten, maybe twelve days left. Our quota’s four carts per day. We’ve only averaged two.”

Arthur didn’t respond right away. He just kept swinging, thinking. Four carts a day was the target. They were averaging two. Doubling their output would only stop them from falling further behind. But to actually catch up, they’d need to triple it. Quietly. Consistently. Without drawing attention.

The last part was key.

His father misread the silence and added, “Don’t worry about it. If there’s punishment, it’ll fall on me. Just take care of your mother. She’s lucky to have that cleaning job in the waste shed. Better than swinging a pickaxe out here like most women.”

Arthur heard the message between the lines but played dumb. “What kind of punishment?”

Paul didn’t answer immediately. When he did, his voice was lower. “They’ll throw me out into the wilds. That’s what they do with the weak. No food. No protection. Just the wolves and the dark.”

Arthur’s grip on the pick tightened.

“If you hadn’t made it back,” his father added, “your mother was planning to go with me. She didn’t want to be left behind. But now that you’re here... it’ll just be me. Keep her safe when I’m gone, alright?”

Arthur nodded slowly, eyes still on the wall of copper. He’d been raised to suppress emotion. To lie better than he breathed. But despite everything, something in him coiled tight at Paul’s words. A sharp little knot of obligation. Not pity. Not grief.

Something more dangerous.

Resolve.

No, he didn’t care about the quota. But he cared about not watching the closest thing he had to a family get fed to the wilds for failing a spreadsheet.

So he made a decision.

He’d hit that quota. Quietly. Efficiently.

And no one would ever see it coming.
