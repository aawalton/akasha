Beastmancy, or taming, was a serious craft.

It required a specific set of skills—training, nurturing, minding, binding, and the long patience to repeat all of it until the bond held. In proper conditions, under a real beastmancer, the work could take months. A careful taming could stretch close to a year.

Even so, Anne was confident enough to guide the right person through the beginning of it.

With the help of books, of course.

She had to explain that twice before the others even started to believe she meant it. And even then, they did not truly believe in the knowledge itself so much as believe that Anne believed in it. That was fair enough. They had already seen what books could do, but this was still different. Guidance for a hunter was one thing. Advice for a mage or healer was another. Beastmancy, however, seemed like the sort of craft that ought to remain locked behind masters, or expensive apprenticeships—not waiting quietly inside bound paper.

Anne understood their disbelief perfectly.

Beastmancy had become rare over the years, but not in the same way as bookmancy. Bookmancy had faded because the world decided it was obsolete. Beastmancy had suffered something much more predictable.

Greed.

As the number of useful beasts declined while demand for their materials remained high, taming became inconvenient to the people who profited from slaughter. Every beast kept alive was one less source of hide, bone, blood, essence organs, or meat on the market. So the practice became less common, less available, and increasingly reserved for those rich or powerful enough not to care about ordinary scarcity.

From there, the rest had followed naturally.

Only the strongest houses and wealthiest masters kept tamed beasts. Only the finest tamers served such people. Beastmancy became specialized, expensive, prestigious, and increasingly distant from ordinary life. And now, with the Wall’s fall restoring a flood of materials to the frontier, the craft had become more desirable than ever.

Anne knew all of that. She also knew she had spoken of it as if the matter were much simpler than it truly was. And that had not been an accident.

If an opportunity presented itself, why cast a narrow line when a wider net cost nothing? If they accepted her guidance here, their respect for the bookstore would deepen. If the matter succeeded, she might even gain another long-term customer out of it.

Possibly a beastmancer.

So while the others were still staring at her suggestion as if she had offered to hatch a dragon egg in the pantry, Anne remained perfectly calm.

Rick looked from the trapped wolf to Anne, then back again. He clearly did not know how to answer.

He did not want to doubt her, not openly, and certainly not after everything the bookstore had already done for him. But Anne had still placed him in an awkward position. A live beast was not a book. It was a living responsibility. A costly one.

“There is no need to look so nervous,” Anne said kindly. “Just say what you truly think. Do you want to keep it as a familiar or not?”

She almost laughed at how startled he looked.

The distrust stung a little, though not enough to change her conviction. People always doubted the reach of knowledge before they benefited from it.

Rick breathed once, steadied himself, and considered the matter properly. She was right. There was no point flinching away from the question.

A familiar would help him. A grey wolf, especially. It was only a copper-tier beast, but speed and silence suited a hunter well. More than that, it was the sort of beast that could travel the same ground he did and understand the same dangers. If Anne believed it was possible, then there was no sense shrinking back now. He had already wagered too much on the bookstore to become timid only because the opportunity looked too big.

“I do,” he said at last, and this time there was certainty in it.

Anne smiled.

“See? That was easy.”

She walked past them toward the wolf.

“Restrictum!”

With a hand sign and a word of power, she drove the bookstore circle’s energy up through the ground and into the struggling beast. The effect was immediate. The wolf stiffened, shuddered, and dropped into magical restraint so quickly that the trio went tense on instinct. Rick felt a chill run down his spine. He remembered that spell far too well.

Anne did not even glance back at them.

“You can remove the net now,” she said. “Moruh, take it to the backyard.”

Then she turned and walked back toward the bookstore as though binding a snarling wind-essence beast were no more remarkable than straightening chairs after guests.

“Come inside and have some tea, everyone.”

Her voice floated back through the open door as she disappeared through it.

The mixture of warm counselor and ruthless storeowner had already earned the group’s respect. The casual display of power only deepened it. Respect, however, did not entirely rule out discomfort.

Fortunately, Moruh emerged from the doorway and broke the silence before it could become embarrassing.

The familiar did not say anything especially helpful. He simply gave them the sort of look that implied Anne’s competence ought to have been obvious from the beginning. Then he moved toward the restrained wolf.

That was enough to get everyone moving again.

The trio went inside while Rick approached the bound beast and started untangling the rope net. He did so carefully at first, expecting it to lunge or snap at him the moment it found an opening. Instead, the wolf only gave pitiful, frustrated sounds. The restraint spell held it completely. So Rick stopped being cautious and freed the net as fast as he could.

Inside, Anne had already resumed serving tea as if the interruption had merely been a detail in a larger workday.

The meat from the hunt would still have to be sold for coin before it became reading credit. She had already judged that much. She had no need for more meat, and in any case, the more interesting matter was no longer the hunt. It was the live beast.

If she intended to train a beastmancer, then time was of the essence.

“Can you really train a beastmancer?” Iris asked.

The suspicion in her tone was obvious, though less offensive than it might have been from someone else. Anne had already learned that Iris did not control her tongue especially well, particularly when curiosity and doubt intertwined.

Anne took a measured sip of tea before answering.

“The honest answer is maybe,” she said. “More accurately, I can guide a beastmancer with the help of some books. That is not the same thing as guaranteeing success.” She let the distinction settle before continuing. “Resources and guidance are only half the work. The rest depends on suitability and effort. No book can replace those.”

That much they understood.

Rick joined them just as Anne poured his tea.

“I am willing to keep the beast restrained for now,” she said, “but that is only a delay, not a solution. If you want to tame the wolf, then you must find someone to learn beastmancy.”

The phrasing was deliberate.

You must find someone.

Not I will do it. Not Rick should do it. The task belonged to them.

Rick’s eyes brightened anyway, and Anne caught it at once.

She cut it off before it could grow.

“I do not advise you to learn it yourself,” she said, looking generally at the group, though the words were clearly aimed at him. “All of you have already stepped onto a path, and in my opinion, you each have room to progress further on it. Stopping now, or trying to split yourself between paths too early, would be counterproductive.”

Her tone left no room for argument.

Rick understood. Even without being addressed by name, he knew perfectly well who she meant. The disappointment passed through him quickly, but not deeply. He had learned by now to recognize when ambition was useful and when it only became foolish greed.

“And how do we find someone appropriate?” he asked instead.

That earned a flicker of approval from Anne.

He was learning.

“Well,” she said, “there are a few requirements, but nothing impossible. First, the person should not already be too deep into another path. Second, they need a strong soul. Third, they need a strong personality—preferably enthusiastic, but patient enough to endure long repetitive work without falling apart. And lastly, of course, they need to want it.”

Iris frowned.

“That’s... very specific. How are we supposed to know if someone has a strong enough soul?”

Anne had expected that question. The truth was that she was not being excessively demanding. Beastmancy required a kind of attrition between mind, will, and soul that bordered, in some ways, on neuromancy. A weak temperament could be pushed around by a beast instead of learning to influence it. And even with a strong candidate, the work still took patience. Repetition, observation, slow progress, setbacks, and more repetition. A lively spirit helped, and a patient one survived.

Rather than answer immediately, Anne asked them for a moment and went to the back room.

When she returned, she held a small purplish crystal.

“You may use this to estimate someone’s soul strength,” she said. “Anything brighter than this level should be acceptable. Ask the person to hold it and concentrate for about a minute.”

As she spoke, she fed a measured pulse of her own inner power into the crystal, enough to make it glow to the threshold she wanted.

The others leaned in.

Naturally, they all wanted to test it.

An hour later, the trio and Rick left the bookstore with a new task.

The boys carried part of their loot and spoke much more happily than when they had arrived. Hakon’s usual calm had lightened a little. Cyrus already seemed to regard the search for a future beastmancer as an adventure in itself. Rick walked with renewed purpose, mind already turning over possible people, possible problems, and possible outcomes.

Iris, meanwhile, was almost entirely focused on the crystal. She had discovered, to her deep dissatisfaction, that Hakon’s soul shone more brightly than hers. Worse still, her own result was not dramatically ahead of Rick’s or even Cyrus’s. That fact had injured her pride badly enough that she kept trying to coax more light from the crystal through sheer stubbornness, as if offense might strengthen the soul.

This time, Anne did not follow them out to watch their departure.

She remained inside, already at work on a list of books suitable for early beastmancy training.

She could feel it clearly.

Another customer was on the way.
