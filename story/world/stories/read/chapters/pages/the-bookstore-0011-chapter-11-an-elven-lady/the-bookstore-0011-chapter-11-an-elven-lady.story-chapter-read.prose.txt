On an especially bright morning, a fine carriage left Mountainend and made its way toward the forbidden forest.

Its carved frame, worked with silver details in the shape of flowers and trees, looked strangely out of place against the pale, withered landscape and the dust-choked road. The contrast only grew sharper when the three-story building by the roadside came into view. Plain wood, fresh repairs, and a simple sign, nothing about it matched the elegance of the carriage that stopped by its door.

“We arrived, madam,” the driver said respectfully as he moved to open the door.

A slender hand, adorned with a few modest jewels, emerged first to accept his help. Then the owner of that hand stepped down with visible grace. Her skin was flawless and pale, catching the bright morning light without effort. Tall and silver-haired, she paused only briefly to regard the entrance before walking toward it with a restrained smile.

Inside, Anne was in an excellent mood.

The bookstore was running. Not perfectly, of course, but truly running. She had established a route for acquiring more books, secured a promising cartographer for the mountains, gained an eager customer who might turn into future silver, and used her six new pages to create Spring, which was a triumph no sensible person could deny.

Spring, admittedly, was also a headache.

The little helper was learning household chores slowly, exactly as Anne had expected. If things had gone too smoothly, she would have suspected divine mockery. Even so, the progress was real. Spring could already dust and scrub on its own, which left Anne’s mornings freer than before.

Rick had also settled into a useful routine. He came by every afternoon with relentless determination, studying the Syllable so that he could finally begin reading the Beastiarium properly. He could have done some of that at home, of course, but there was something pleasing about seeing him try so hard inside the bookstore itself.

As for Dorgh, he had only returned twice in the last week. After buying a simple copy of Kasar’s Compendium of Mystic Brewing, the dwarf had become consumed with finding proper ingredients and testing what he could. Anne had been sorely tempted when he offered a couple of gold coins for the original codex, but professionalism had prevailed. The book was still a codex, which meant restoration, careful storage, and long-term value. More importantly, she could not bear to part with it without securing something comparable first.

A simple copy for thirty silver had been enough.

The copy was unmagical and practical, which suited Dorgh’s immediate needs. And of course, he was welcome to read the original in the bookstore whenever he wanted. The thought of stable lending income had softened the pain of refusing his gold.

So that morning, with Spring working and no regular customer expected so early, Anne had taken the opportunity to sit behind the counter and focus on research and restoration.

Then she heard someone enter.

“Welcome to the bookstore,” she said calmly.

The greeting came out smooth and practiced despite her surprise. Her training had prepared her well for that much, at least.

She looked up and found a tall elegant woman dressed in sober blue.

At a glance, Anne took in the pointy ears adorned with modest golden earrings and immediately corrected her first assumption. Not a lady. A madam. The woman’s skin was unlined, as elven skin often was, but her bearing was too unique, too mature, too self-contained to mistake for youth. Her eyes were a deep sky blue, and though her beauty was striking, it was not the fragile kind that invited admiration before caution.

No, caution came first.

A noble? Anne wondered, though she kept the thought from showing in her face.

The impression did not fit neatly. The woman’s elegance certainly belonged to nobility, but her clothing did not. The silk robe was finely made and spotless, but too simple for someone flaunting rank. Even the golden headband worked with flowers seemed restrained rather than ornamental. It was the sort of modesty that often hid either very old coin or very real confidence.

Anne already disliked how difficult the woman was to classify.

“I’m the storeowner, Anne,” she said professionally. “May I offer you a cup of tea, madam?”

The visitor studied her for a brief moment before nodding and moving forward.

She seemed pleasantly surprised by Anne’s posture, though she did not make a show of it. As for the respectful form of address, she accepted it without comment. Humans and beastkin often mistook the age of elves, treating mature women as girls and old men as young scholars. Many elves considered that disrespectful. She, however, rarely paid attention to such trivialities.

“Thank you, Ms. Anne,” she said gracefully as she sat at the counter. “I am Ailen of Mountainend.”

Anne doubted that phrasing immediately.

No one dressed, moved, and carried silence like that while truly belonging to a small border town. Still, the introduction was likely intentional. A boundary set politely, rather than a lie. Anne respected it enough not to press.

She waited until Ailen was seated, then served the tea and took her own place.

“Wonderful tea,” Ailen said after the first sip.

The appreciation was clear, but unlike Dorgh or Ceres, she showed no surprise. Anne only thanked her and waited. Facing this kind of person, there was no gain in rushing.

“I am glad you chose our small town to open a bookstore,” Ailen said. “When I heard from my husband, the mayor, I knew I had to come see it for myself and offer my congratulations. I did not think I would ever see such a place again.”

“You have visited a bookstore before?” Anne asked before she could stop herself.

The lapse cost her a flicker of irritation. She corrected her tone immediately, but the surprise had already escaped. She had not expected to find anyone this far into the borderlands who still understood what her craft truly was.

“Yes,” Ailen said, unbothered. “A long time ago. Unfortunately, modern times have mistaken your profession for something antiquated. I never expected to see it again.” She held Anne’s gaze with calm curiosity. “It is Ms. Anne...?”

“Anne Wordkeeper.”

The name still carried pain, but also pride. Anne would not let one erase the other.

“Wordkeeper,” Ailen repeated thoughtfully. “I believe I once met a young Darius Wordkeeper.”

“Darius was my grandfather,” Anne said, and could not help the small warmth that entered her voice.

“At the time, he was an eager bookmancer trying to resist the rise of magitech,” Ailen said with a faint smile. “Seeing you here, I suppose one could say he succeeded in a way.”

“Thank you, madam.”

Anne let the gratitude stand for a moment, then carefully turned the conversation.

“What do you think of books?” she asked. “You seemed familiar with the craft.”

Ailen’s expression softened.

“I love reading,” she said simply. “It is one of the loveliest ways to communicate across time. Sadly, I have not come across any truly new books in decades, so I mostly reread my own small collection.”

That answer pleased Anne deeply.

Then they began speaking of that collection in detail.

Ailen owned only twenty-two books, which was a humble number by Anne’s standards, but not a poorly chosen one. There were biographies, histories, fiction, and a small handful of compendiums. From the way she described them, Anne quickly understood that her strongest interests lay in history and biography, while her favorite craft hobby was jewelry-making.

That last detail proved especially useful.

By the end of the visit, Ailen purchased Ancient Northern Kingdoms and Mastery of Golden Carvings for thirty-two silver. The first recorded the history of the northern continent from a very interesting point of view, while the second was a compendium of golden carving patterns in several styles. Anne, guided by instinct and a growing certainty, insisted on adding Master Inu of Calener as a gift—a biography of an unknown jewelry master from a previous era.

Ailen accepted it with the graceful restraint of someone too well-bred to refuse a thoughtful gift twice.

When the sale was done and the books wrapped, Anne watched the elf leave with the same eager attention she reserved for promising customers.

She had a strong feeling that Ailen would become one of her best.
