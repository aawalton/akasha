With Jeff and Jessie both on their way to the Crone, I spent five minutes debriefing the Portal Tyrant on my fight with the Placid King. It was a quick conversation. Neither of us had the time to spare with unnecessary talking, and unlike Jessie or Jeff, I didn’t feel particularly sentimental about him.

Terrel Young took what I had to say in stride, but I could tell he was nervous. The Placid King’s few moments of partial presence on Earth had put into perspective exactly how dangerous an SS-Plus-Ranked monster was.

When I finished, he stood. “Thank you. I’ll try to rally a few more S-Rankers. Sarah Cullman and Nathan Anders will definitely join up. I doubt I can get the Falcon’s Eye, though, and you’re not getting any help from the Guardians. Not after yesterday. Maybe some of the independents and A-Rankers?”

“I’d rather keep the A-Rankers out of it,” I said. “Independents are fine. Whoever you can get. I need to go, though.” The timer was ticking down. Every minute here weighed on me, and that pressure from my bond with Ellen was rising, too. I shook Terrel’s massive, dark hand and nodded. “Thank you.”

“No problem. Thank you for helping out with the Guardians yesterday. I’ll see you around, Kade Stormblade.”

I shook my head. Having a title, whether it was the Unbroken Storm or the Stormblade, was weird.

Then I retreated to the room I’d claimed. A shimmering, lightning-wreathed portal was already open, and Ellen sat on the floor, meditating. She looked up when I walked in. “I’ve been waiting.”

The tension in her voice was almost a physical thing. She stood, leaning against the wall and flashing her teeth. I shrugged. “Lots of conversations. Jessie, Jeff, Terrel. You know how it goes.”

“Well, Tanat’s waiting. She’s probably got the Lightning Tower back to normal and the Void Garden pretty much cleaned out.” Ellen offered her hand, and I took it.

Then we stepped through into chaos.

The Lightning Tower was not, in fact, back to normal. If anything, it looked worse than it had with the Placid King’s massive tentacles wrapped around it. The Eye of the Storm overhead had all but broken up, and energy from the messy, scattered thunderheads didn’t find any lightning rods out in the field. The walls were in disrepair, and the portal metal frame that should have been channeling electricity up into the storm overhead was twisted and mangled; it creaked in the breeze.

I drew the Storm Blade and looked around. Had we missed a monster Tanat couldn’t handle?

No.

She sat on the throne, eyes closed, seemingly in a trance. As I watched, she twitched, and a massive chunk of brick ripped itself away from one of the lightning rods far out in the garden. It took a moment before I realized what she was doing. Then it clicked.

She was building paths. Brick paths. They weaved through the wreckage of the garden, twisting and turning, creating dead ends and spiraling loops that led to nowhere, only to loop back out. Instead of focusing on the Lightning Tower itself, Tanat was crafting a maze.

When I went to point that out to Ellen, she stopped me. “I see it. I can make it better.”

Then she closed her eyes, too, and began tearing at the very portal world’s substance.

Instead of building simple barriers of shadow in the shape of hedges, Ellen ripped the dirt away around a section of completed pathway, stacking it into the very center of the space between paths. The earthen rampart towered over the paths below, with gigantic trenches leading to dark, shadow-filled pits on either side. At the same time, the wall cast shadows of its own onto the pathways.

After a few minutes, she opened her eyes. “See. When I’m finished, the Lightning Tower’s going to be at the center of a much more dangerous maze. I should have thought about this for the Placid King.”

I shook my head. “It wouldn’t have helped. He’d have used the trenches to move his army. But the God of Thunder’s different. He may have a handful of allies, but they’ll be like Tallas. Shades of his former pupils, but still bound by the same rules as any delver or monster. No aquatic enemies, unless he’s kept Magda around, and no way to get water for them. Lots of fliers, though.”

“We’ll need to do something about that,” Ellen said.

“Yeah.” A few minutes passed as I watched Ellen and Tanat work. Tanat seemed to have figured out what Ellen was up to—or maybe she’d intended it in her design—because her paths were just far enough apart for the wall to stay a more or less uniform height. Ellen finished her first complete section, then coated the wall in pure void, making a black, shining barrier between paths.

But that wasn’t good enough. The God of Thunder would bring flying allies. He flew himself. None of this would stop him. We’d need something different. Something…

“Oh.”

I closed my eyes and sliced off the top couple of feet of the wall, creating a flat surface clear of void and shadow. Then I started moving the first of the lightning rods. It settled onto the wall, and I moved ten feet down and added another. Then another. When I’d finished, the earthen rampart looked like a comb. A very, very spread-toothed comb.

I connected the entire structure to the Lightning Tower through underground conduits. Electricity poured through a moment later, and the storm overhead slowly began to strengthen.

Ellen cleared her throat. She was staring at me, breathing hard from the effort of reshaping an entire world. I looked back, and she smiled again. “We should—“

“You two are driving me insane,” Tanat interrupted.

I jumped, turned in place, and dropped my hand to where a sword would hang from my waist. The black-eyed, too-thin woman sat on the Lightning Tower’s throne, eyes boring into Ellen and me in equal measure. After a moment, she rolled them. “Kade Noelstra, Eleanor Traynor is desperate to bond with you. Even I can feel it. The tension between you two is out of control.”

“I am not!” Ellen said, blushing.

“You are,” Tanat said. “I will admit that the wall idea was well-executed, as are the lightning rods above them. They should make the skies less safe for invaders. However, it is impossible for me to get anything done with your auras behaving the way they are.”

I paused. “You can…feel our auras?”

“Of course I can. Retan is an expert in auras, even more so than swordplay. He taught us all well. And at this moment, your auras are entangled to the point where it is distracting. If you continue this way, I will have no choice but to leave.”

By now, I was red-faced, too. I glanced at Ellen. She looked at me. And, without another word, we headed for the stairs.

“You two have fun,” Tanat called as we left. “And Ellen Traynor? I am incredibly envious of you for your partner.”

“She’s really convinced that our Dual Skill Progression bond is romantic or sexual, isn’t she?” Ellen asked.

We sat, back to back, on the bottom floor of the Lightning Tower. Around us, portal metal shifted slightly as Tanat continued to work on her rebuilding. I shrugged. “It’s an intimate thing, and she’s not human. Her understanding of it seems more nuanced than ours, too. She probably knows more about what we’re actually doing than the guidebooks the GC has.”

“Even so, she…”

I nodded. “She makes you uncomfortable. I can tell. I don’t think it’s malicious.”

“That’s not it. It’s not that it’s malicious. I don’t even think it’s harmful. It’s just…none of our bonds so far have felt like the kind of intimacy she’s talking about. And we’ve…”

“Yeah,” I said. “We’ve slept together. But you’re right, the bonding hasn’t felt like that.”

Ellen shivered. “So, are we doing something wrong?”

“I don’t think so. Let’s get to work here, though. We don’t have a lot of time.”

“This takes no time at all, Kade.”

I laughed. “You’re right. Let’s take our time and enjoy this, then?”

“Shut up.” Ellen’s breathing slowed as she focused on her core. I joined her. My chest rose and fell in time with hers, and I closed my eyes.

I opened them in a brand-new mental space.

The garden—Ellen’s garden—had changed shape. It had been surrounded by tall walls in the past, and those walls had constrained its shape. But now, instead of being bound, it seemed to wrap itself around a central pillar of stone like the threads of a screw, looping around and around as it gradually climbed…my mountain?

No, not exactly my mountain. This spire of stone wouldn’t be climbable by any man ever to live. It was jagged and crumbly, with overhangs worn smooth by time and sharp spikes of black rock. If it weren’t for the ramp circling it, the pillar would be just that—a pillar.

I looked around.

Ellen stood nearby, staring at the spiraling floor below our feet.

She wore no clothes, and it took me a moment to realize that I had nothing on, either. But while the sudden nudity should have been shocking or embarrassing, it felt…correct. Not comfortable, maybe, but not uncomfortable, either. Ellen clearly felt the same way—she started talking the moment she saw me, voice echoing in the empty sky all around us.

“Kade, I think this is more what Tanat meant.”

“You think?” I asked. My voice echoed, too. I slipped a hand into hers. “Come on, let’s climb.”

Climbing the spiral did seem to be the right way forward. High overhead, lightning and thunder poured down to crash against the massive pillar of black stone. We passed cacti dozens of feet tall and palo verde trees that formed arches of green bark over our heads, and a small, shallow brook burbled away beside us as we climbed. The spiral was almost idyllic, especially in comparison to the wreckage of our shared portal world.

But the higher we climbed, the less peaceful it got.

It started with the lightning. My electricity powers had always had a tint of blue-white to them. When Ellen’s shadow entered the mix, they shifted to a green-black color. But by the time Ellen and I were halfway up, it was abundantly clear that the hues were off. These strikes seemed more red-orange, full of anger and hatred. They didn’t match up with the God of Thunder’s lightning, either. His were more like mine—which made sense. I’d learned from him and walked his Path, after all.

I tried to summon the Storm’s Blade.

It didn’t come.

I tried to use my skills and spells.

They wouldn’t cast.

“Well, that sure makes the lack of clothes more ominous,” Ellen joked.

I snorted. She was right, though. Without my skills, spells, or even sword, I had no good way to defend myself against whatever was causing that horrific-looking lightning. “Come on. We’ll have to see what the Law is when we get to the top. It’s got something to do with vulnerability and helplessness, though.”

She paused, her hand pulling on mine as I started to walk. Then she shook her head. “I don’t know that it does. That feels right. It even feels close. But this Law-Learning’s nothing like the previous two. And isn’t C-Rank to B-Rank the first major bottleneck? I don’t know that we should make assumptions about this Law.”

“That’s probably wise.”

“It is, isn’t it?” Ellen said. She nodded, her battle pigtails bobbing against her shoulder blades. And we continued hiking upward.

As we walked, I watched the storm overhead.

It had been a long, long time since I’d considered thunder, lightning, wind, and rain to be my enemies. The last time that had been true had been in the Crone’s forest, when I’d had to tame Stormbreak and get it fully under my control. That was, in fact, exactly what I felt right now. The storm overhead was out of control. It rained lightning down on the pinnacle’s summit, and wind whipped through the palo verde trees.

As we climbed, though, something gradually shifted. It felt like Ellen’s powers at first, but when her wide eyes looked at me, she seemed just as confused and uncertain of herself as I was. But even so…even so, it was recognizably the shadow. Recognizably the void.

The minutes ticked by. I forced myself to remain present. Time didn’t pass in the real world, not during Law-Learning. We were totally safe here, and could take as long as we needed. But it didn’t feel that way. The hungry void’s presence built up around Ellen and me as we walked, still nude, ever upward. Sand crunched softly beneath our feet.

Then, finally, we reached the end of the path. Not the end of the ramp, but the end of the garden.

Beyond this point, there was nothing alive. Cacti and trees alike burned, and the burbling little stream was nothing but a bank of steam as lightning poured into it. The final turn of the ramp seemed to be nothing but a solid sheet of red-orange lightning, and for the first time since the Crone’s forest, I didn’t merely consider the storm my enemy.

It was legitimately frightening.

I waited for a moment. “The First Law of the Hungering Abyss had to do with the Void’s hunger. The second stated that the void removes. But, Ellen, I don’t know what this third Law could possibly be.”

“Neither do I,” she said. She glanced up at the top of the stone spire, then pointed with her free hand. “But the answer’s up there—past the lightning. If we want to find it, we have to keep going.”

She wasn’t afraid? How could she not be afraid of the storm in front of us? I watched more carefully, trying to pick a path through the seemingly chaotic lightning. Control was chaos, after all. But I couldn’t find one.

Then Ellen stepped forward, and I followed her up the ramp. And as we walked, I understood. This wasn’t a test of skill or power. It wasn’t even one of understanding.

It was a test of faith. Faith in the void.

And of faith in each other.

Ellen and I walked out into the storm as one. Lightning poured down onto us from every conceivable angle. And the shadow that had been building the entire time we slowly climbed the screw’s threads erupted out to face it.

Not to face it. To consume it. The void, after all, hungered.

We kept moving. Thunder burst our eardrums. Sound stopped mattering. Our naked bodies shivered in the cold, biting wind. But as we climbed, the void climbed around us, and by the time we reached the summit and collapsed, shivering, into a ball of Ellen and Kade, I understood what the spire and ramp had been trying to teach us.

The void hungered, and it removed.

But it also protected.
