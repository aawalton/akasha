Maiya had no sooner jumped onto the airship’s deck than it took to the sky. She set down the old man she’d been carrying, heaving a sigh of relief as he did. He’d given up on the idea of escaping altogether until Maiya had found him in his home, waiting patiently for the end.

The ship climbed and turned south toward Rani with the few others she had pulled out. Perhaps forty souls. The village had held three hundred that morning, though most had thankfully fled on their own well ahead of the incoming demons. She’d barely arrived in time to save the rest before the invading force arrived.

An hour of effort to buy fifty lives. That counted as a good day now. Among the best she’d had in days, actually.

Yet any joy she felt in that figure was overshadowed by the knowledge of the thousands that had died. Of the thousands more that continued to die every moment despite her efforts.

She hadn’t slept in weeks, and even her prana-enhanced constitution was starting to reach its limits. For days now, she felt half-dead. Going through the motions in a waking daze, slipping up more often than not.

She watched from the air as demons swept through the village like a plague. She knew they would not linger long. There was always another village past the last one, after all, to say nothing of the honeypots they were all eyeing.

Humanity’s fortress cities, for which they were amassing by the thousands.

These days, they tended to loot the village head’s home before proceeding to burn the village, their seemingly insatiable appetite for destruction only bolstered by every win.

She averted her eyes from the carnage and directed the pilot to drop her off at the Kin’jal forward garrison that they’d set up some distance from the village.

Not a single Balarian Warrior had defended it because their attention was required at Jalasa to the south, but it, too, would be under siege soon.

She jumped off the hovering airship, waving goodbye to the pilot, who nodded and commanded the airship to rise a moment later. They’d be bound for Rani. One of the few nations that were still safe, if only on account of being further from the demon lands than most other human nations.

Some soldiers glanced her way before frowning while others just stared off into the distance, eyes blank.

The reactions accurately described the state of the camp.

Those that despised her… and those who’d given up.

Ira was alone inside the command tent when Maiya entered, save for a handful of her personal guards.

She was bent over a map of the realm covered over with markers depicting troop presence, though most of them sat on top of Jalasa, the Kin’jal fortress city the demons were preparing to take.

“You look terrible,” Ira said without glancing up.

“I feel terrible. But I suppose I saved fifty people today, so there’s that,” Maiya replied halfheartedly.

“I heard. Meanwhile, did you hear how many have died today?”

Ira continued when she didn’t answer. “Around five thousand. So far.”

Maiya winced. “Which town?” she asked, the words like sand in her throat.

“Tali, in the north of Hiranya.”

“I hadn’t realized they were moving that direction,” Maiya replied with a concerned frown. “I thought they were focusing south to forge a path to the Pagan Order.”

“As were we,” Ira replied. “Alas, we’ve been proven wrong and to disastrous results. As terrifying as that is, however, I’ve my own issues to worry about.”

Ira moved a marker an inch closer to Jalasa. “My scouts say the demons will be ready to assault Jalasa by mid-morning. I’ve sent four thousand Balarian Warriors to hold it.”

“How long?” Maiya asked, and Ira knew fully well what she meant.

“They should last a day. Perhaps two, if no Samsara are present. Half the city is evacuated, but as usual, there will be a significant portion unable to make it out in time.”

“And then it’s a straight shot to Sonam,” Maiya said, biting her lip. There wasn’t even a single discussion of actually ensuring Jalasa didn’t fall. That was impossible and they both knew it. Now they measured their wins in the amount of time they managed to buy before the inevitable occurred.

“That it is,” Ira muttered. The Imperatrix of the Kin’jal looked as worn out as Maiya felt. “I’d like to think our fortress of a capital will not be taken so easily, but the demons have proven time and time again that walls prove no hindrance to their efforts. Sonam will fall in time, I’m sure.”

“I’ll do everything I can to stop that,” Maiya said.

“Really?” Ira asked, finally straightening, looking her in the eyes. “Like you did at Kanthaghati Pass? Like you’ve done these past weeks? Saving fifty here and there? I’ll be deeply in your debt if you could save a handful of Sawai before Sonam falls.”

Maiya stiffened, realizing where this was going. Ira hadn’t summoned her to a forward command post in the middle of a collapsing front to talk over a town they both knew was lost, after all.

“You know the situation I’m in,” Maiya said curtly. “But go ahead. Say your piece.”

Ira studied her for a long moment. There had been a time the two of them could go weeks without a single argument between them. Back when Maiya had served as her right hand and their biggest woe was the Children of Ash. That time felt very far away now.

“Do you remember what it cost me to take the throne from my father?” Ira asked.

“How could I forget?” Maiya said bitterly.

“You were.” Ira came around the table, stopping so close to Maiya she almost took a step back. “The two of us were nearly boiled alive for our troubles. You remember that part well enough, I think.”

Maiya shuddered. To this day, the memory of the pain and the humiliation had not left her. It was one of the many nightmares that forced her awake at night. Something she shared with Vir.

“Your husband saved us both,” Ira went on, pacing around the tent. “And went on to cripple my father, helping me take a nation that had known nothing but conquest for its entire history. In the years since, and through no shortage of effort, I have turned it toward something else. The arts my father called a weakness flourish more than they ever have, and our trade network has ushered in a new era for the Empire.” She stopped in front of Maiya. “Do you know how much I have toiled to see this dream accomplished?”

“Kin’jal is richer now than it ever was under Andros,” Maiya agreed stiffly. “It would never have gotten here without your effort.”

“Indeed. And do you know how I consolidated my power? How I got my people to love me?” she asked, striding up to Maiya and staring into her eyes. “By never once giving them cause to say that the woman who halted their conquests had made them weak.”

Maiya grimaced, refusing to meet her gaze.

“And then you asked me to hold five thousand Balarian Warriors out of the fight at Kanthaghati,” Ira said. “To have them swear to stand on that Ash-damned battlefield and take no demon life while the wall fell behind us.”

“I did,” Maiya muttered, almost too quietly to be heard.

“I agreed to that plan. I do not pretend otherwise. I trusted you over my own counsel because of how much I owe you. Because I believed in your delusions. That burden is mine to bear. But you need to understand what that decision has cost me, Maiya, because I don’t think you do.”

Maiya understood fully well already. Ira couldn’t have known that she’d been drowning in guilt at what would be little more than a massacre. That she’d received be ridiculed by her people, and that the propaganda against her would be plastered in every street.

“They’re calling me a demon sympathizer now,” Ira said. “The Imperatrix who held her finest warriors in reserve while the most decisive battle of our time was lost. Every detractor that ever wanted me gone—and believe you me, there are many—has their reason now, handed to them on a silver platter. Riyan Savar looks down upon me. Tiyana Matali mocks you in open council… and I sit there in silence, because what could I possibly say? That our Saint was right to stay her hand? That we would lose any hope of humans coexisting with demons if we attacked them? What hope is that, Maiya? Hmm? What hope was ever there of that?”

“So you’ve given, then. On your dream,” Maiya said, but her voice was hollow. She had no leg to stand on, and Ira knew it.

“I’ve given up on a lot of things lately, Maiya,” Ira snapped. “I’ve spent my entire life building the Kin’jal we have today, and one battle at your side was all it took to ruin it.”

Maiya said nothing, knowing fully well her friend was venting her frustrations on the only person she could. Her lack of sleep wasn’t helping either, she was sure.

And yet, Ira’s words cut deep all the same, for every word of it was true.

“My warriors will defend Jalasa tonight. They will fight well, and yet, even if they pull off a miracle and manage to last until tomorrow, their sacrifice will not buy back a single inch of what I have lost at home. This is what your mercy costs, Maiya. Not demon lives or even the untold thousands of innocents who’ve died. Everything I bled for, gone.”

“For what it’s worth, I am sorry,” Maiya said. It was a small and useless thing to say, but it was about all she could do.

“I know you are,” Ira sighed, turning back toward her map. “How I wish mistakes could be undone with an apology. I would be uttering a thousand of them. Yet we both know they change nothing. The dead are still dead, and Kin’jal is in more jeopardy than ever. You know as well as I the only thing that will make a difference.”

Maiya shook her head. “And you know that if I deploy the Asura or use the Vimana, even more will die. Right now the only hope we have is for Vir to come back and force demonkind to stand down. You speak of reputation. What do you think his reputation will be if his wife opens fire on his people? Do you think they’ll be especially likely to heed his words then?

“I cannot open fire so long as there is even a sliver of a chance for peace,” Maiya continued.

“You truly believe he can put an end to the fighting?” Ira asked, showing doubt for the first time.

“I don’t know,” Maiya admitted. “Even if he does return in time, I’m unsure if the situation is recoverable, even for him. He’d put a stop to the Samsara, of course, and perhaps even get the Bairans and the Panav to withdraw. But the Aindri? The Iksana and the Chitran? It’s difficult to say.”

“If he fails, they will fight to the last,” Ira said. “Until they either prevail or are annihilated.”

“And yet, fewer humans and demons would die in the end,” Maiya said, pursing her lips.

“What envious choices we have, don’t we?” Ira asked, barking a harsh laugh. “In a field of equally terrible options, how is a ruler to pick a path, I wonder?”

“By following her heart, I suppose,” Maiya said solemnly, longing for Vir.

“I suppose,” Ira replied with a tight smile. “Good luck, Maiya.”

“Thanks,” she said, replying with a similar expression. “Gods know I’m going to need it.”

They bid their goodbyes and Maiya left, already overdue for her next appointment.

Which was, of course, Jalasa and their hopeless last stand.

All the more reason to be there with them when it happened.

Soldiers of all stripes gave her looks on her way out.

Let them, she thought. They could judge her all they liked. If they knew even a fraction of what she did, they’d sing a different tune. Which was why she didn’t fault them.

She strode right past them to where her airship would soon land.

Maiya had been certain she was choosing the lesser evil, and she still believed that.

From attempting to convince both sides not to fight, to her presence at Kanthaghati Pass, to everything she and the Asuras had done since then, though Maiya’s actions had failed she’d lived the only way she knew.

With her head up, doing the absolute best she could. It was what she would do now.

The airship dropped out of the sky toward her. To another invasion and another paltry handful of lives she might save while thousands more perished.

She boarded the airship. But she was not bound for Jalasa.
