The common room still held a dozen weather-beaten, creased faces, most of them engaged in easy conversation. Teela sat at a table some distance away, playing cards with a small group of men. Her green hat was pulled low and she wore a shark-like grin.

I stood at the bottom landing, watching with a smile as she gesticulated wildly to a chorus of chuckles and clinking tankards.

“You,” the husky voice at my ear made me jump, “Owe me a drink.”

Light pressure closed over my lower back, nudging me towards the bar and sending a thrill of heat along my skin. Several moments later Renner and I sat at a small table in the tavern’s most secluded corner.

Shadows cast half his features in gloom as he sipped a tankard of dark, earth-scented ale, watching me over the rim. Finally he declared, “Lighthold’s your best bet.”

“What?” I closed both hands around my own drink. Creamy foam swelled with the smells of rye and oak.

“For help. There’s libraries there. Big ones, full of information about Fae and magic and all that salt. It’s packed with scholars, too. Some of them spend their whole lives there, studying that sort of thing.”

“Lighthold?” I blinked. “You want me to go to Lighthold?”

He scowled. “If you’re looking for help, that’s where we’ll find it. We can bring the shard to whatever Thalessians are in Sedgewick tomorrow. But we’re not marching up and announcing you’re bound to a Fae.”

“You… you keep saying we.” I watched his expression carefully. Arched a brow, hardly believing what either of us was saying. “You want to go to Lighthold, now? What do you get out of it?”

He regarded me for a moment. Then, evenly, “Not much, but I won’t have to lose sleep thinking about how you’ve frozen to death on the side of the road.”

I rolled my eyes. “You know I can make fire, right? I’m not going to freeze to death.”

“Fine. Murdered by bandits, then, because you and your farmboy were too busy trying to see the good in them to fight back.”

“Well, maybe there’d be fewer bandits in the world if more people bothered to try and see the good in them.” He started to open his mouth, but I leaned forwards with a little growl. “And would you stop with the farmboy thing? Royce is a good person. He’s brave and kind, and he’s always thinking of others. Also, his mom has a garden. Not a farm. And he’s not mine.”

He paused, tankard halfway to his lips. “Does he know that?”

“Yes,” I bit out, massaging my temples. “Just… look, this has all been hard for him. I know it’s difficult, but… please, try to get along with him.”

“He and I don’t need to get along.”

“No, but it would be nice.”

“I’m not nice.”

“Well, he is!” I snapped. I winced almost immediately afterwards, realizing the unfairness of that statement, and amended quietly, “Well… he usually is. I know there have been a few moments… I’m sorry he hasn’t been the most cordial. He’s… having a difficult time. Trust me, he’s usually very friendly.”

I half-expected another snide comment at that, but all I got was silence. I glanced over just in time to see a distinctly pleased expression flicker across his shadowed features.

Right. Men. At least he’s not… er, leering.

My chest swelled in a long, weary breath. “Renner, Lighthold is… it’s a good idea, but it would take weeks to get there. I know you don’t like it, but asking the Order for help has been my plan since the beginning. Ever since I found out I was… you know. I need to bring them the shard, but it’s more than that. I want to tell them everything. Even if they can’t help me, stars, even if they really think I deserve to die, I want to at least try.” I propped my chin in one hand, staring forlornly into my untouched drink. “And saints’ know I’m tired of lying.”

He went still. My skin prickled beneath the weight of a heavy gaze.

Then, too quickly for me to do anything but squeak, one hand snapped out. An iron grip caught me by the forearm and he tugged my glove free.

“That so?” He stared pointedly at five fresh red cuts. They stood out starkly against my skin.

Of course he noticed. Of course he did. “I didn’t lie. I said it wasn’t worth mentioning.”

The silence was deafening.

“Slip just… it was an accident.” I pulled away, tracing my marred skin gingerly. “I think she actually felt terrible about it.”

His eyes narrowed.

“That’s the truth. It was an accident.”

“Fine. But you looked scared out of your mind earlier, so what else happened?”

I lowered my lashes. Flattened my palms against the wood grain. Listened to the echo of Horace’s cruel, mocking words. “Just… someone tried to frighten me. That’s all.”

The weight of Renner’s gaze felt like it would bore right through me. I studiously ignored him, tugging my glove back on and sliding both hands into my hair in a familiar, soothing motion. That’s all it was. He wanted to scare me. Make me squirm.

“Seems like it worked.”

That made me wince. “I’m going to the Thalessians tomorrow. I need help, and if there’s a chance of getting it now…”

I didn’t look up, but I could just feel the air around him grow frosty.

“Help. Right. You know, the Order teaches new recruits all about the sanctity of life. Defending every mortal soul, shielding people from the dark, that sort of thing. Paladins have their grand, heroic reputation for a reason, ladyship. They’re unmatched in combat, and they take their oaths seriously. Most of them are men and women who’d fight to their last breath for a stranger. Wouldn’t think twice about it.”

I eyed him cautiously, wondering where this line of conversation was headed. “But…?”

“But all that righteous thread starts fraying when someone’s bound to a Fae. You asked what happened to the pacted up in Lighthold.

I shivered at his tone. Noted the little sneer at one corner of his mouth. “And you said they died.”

“They did,” he said curtly. “But not in Lighthold. Not most of them. We routed the caves. Two dozen pacted, with a handful of runekeepers and city folk being held captive. It was a bloodbath, and when the smoke cleared we took prisoners. That’s what we’re supposed to do with pacted.” His voice lowered to an ugly, vicious pitch. “Get them bound, make sure they can’t fight back, keep them alive, and bring them to the temple in Pinnacle.”

“Why?”

He was quiet for a while. Then he took a long drink, set his tanked down with a thud, and leaned forwards. “Consecration.”

My brows knit together. “You said that word before. When I asked you what would happen to me. You said it’s a… a test, of sorts.”

His lip curled. “Yeah. Funny thing is, most members don’t actually know what it means. We all know the word, but it’s just another ritual in a whole damn line of rituals. Only the actual paladins, seasoned warriors who’ve received Thalessa’s blessing, participate.”

“And… you?”

He rubbed his jaw, glaring at the table. “I saw something I wasn’t supposed to. I’m jumping ahead, though. You asked about runekeepers, too. Well, we rounded everyone up and headed back to Pinnacle. Anyone who’d claimed to have divine magic got brought along.”

“What were their runes like?” I leaned forwards, hands tight around my cup. “Did you see any of their magic?”

“Honestly? It was… sad.” He shook his head, grimacing at the memory. “They just had little things. Sparks to light campfires, frost to cool a cup of water, the ability to mend clothes… minor magic.”

“Oh, but that isn’t sad!” I cried. “It’s… it’s not as impressive as some things, perhaps, but even magic like that is-”

“That wasn’t why it was sad.” He frowned at me, looking speculative. “It was the way they’d do it. It was all older folks who swore they’d been using runes for decades. But they’d… salt, they’d almost break down and weep about it, sometimes. Either they’d use a rune and just stand there, waiting, and nothing would happen, or it’d be something small. A handful of sparks, and then the runekeeper would insist it should’ve been more. They’d talk about their magic like it was dying. ”

I shivered. My knuckles grew white as I contemplated such a feeling in horror, with no idea how to respond.

“There’s a reason,” he said slowly, searching my face, “I was so sure you were lying. Back when we met. I’ve seen runekeepers. More than that story, and it’s all the same. You’re a dying breed.”

“That’s what he said,” I whispered, gnawing my lower lip. “He called us a… ‘a nearly extinct breed.’ He said we’d been losing our magic for twenty years.”

My companion snorted. “I don’t suppose he mentioned why?”

I spread my hands. “No. I mean, of course we both know something pretty significant from about twenty years ago. The hero, the Nightmare, the sword, all of it… but I can’t imagine what any of that would have to do with magic.”

He took another sip, brow furrowed in thought, and shrugged. “Well. Whatever the reason, they were like that the whole trek. Damned miserable bunch. When we got back to Pinnacle the runekeepers and pacted were taken for consecration, and the rest of us were given leave. Told to go relax, enjoy a much-earned rest after all the mess. Most of us did.”

I searched his eyes. Saw the storm growing behind them. “But not you.”

“Not me.” He inclined his head. “I was curious.”

My pulse thrummed. Questions hovered on the tip of my tongue. What did you see? What happened to them? What will happen to me?

Do I want to know?

“They were put in-”

“No.” I stood so abruptly the table jostled, nearly tipping my drink. “Renner, I… I don’t want to hear this.”

His voice was knifelike. “Well, it seems like you need to.”

“Why?” My lips thinned. “So you can talk me out of asking for help? You said it yourself, I didn’t do anything wrong! And once I explain it all-”

“They won’t listen.”

“You don’t know that!”

“I do. They-”

I shook my head. “No. I… look, I’m sorry, I know you disagree, but I…” my gaze cut around, ensuring no one was nearby as my voice began to tremble. “I can’t live on the run, hiding everything from everyone! I can’t. I’m scared, I need help, and the Order is my best chance.”

Wood scraped as he stood, chair sliding back, and leveled a dark look at me. “It’s not any chance.”

“Well… I can’t afford to believe that. You can have my drink. Goodnight.” I spun away, hurrying to the stairs before he could convince me otherwise. I took the steps two at a time and practically burst through my bedroom door. I stood trembling for a moment, then pulled my boots and gloves off.

They’ll hear me out. They will. He’s just being cynical and… they won’t just kill someone who walks up and surrenders! I’m not dangerous, I haven’t hurt-

“Brin.” My name was laced in that sharp, dangerous edge.

I groaned. “I’m going to bed.”

“You need to listen.”

“No! Don’t you get it?” I rounded on him. “I don’t want you to try and change my mind! I want to wake up tomorrow and hope for something good, Renner!”

He didn’t relent. Just stood there, shaking his head. “And I’m trying to tell you-”

“Goodnight.” I turned away. “Please close the door behind you.”

After a long, uneasy silence, the door clicked shut. My breath gusted out in surprised, but pleased, victory.

It was short-lived. Rough leather closed over my wrists.

“Fine.” He gave a sharp tug, pulling both arms behind my back and earning a startled squeak. “Don’t listen. I’ll show you.”

“R-Renner?” Heat that somehow managed to be confused, indignant, and a little bit excited all at once flooded my belly. “What are you-”

“Giving you a little taste,” his growl sent a shiver down my spine, “Of consecration.”

“Let go.” I twisted, but remained trapped. “I don’t want-”

“It starts like this.” His grip tightened, drawing my limbs together between us. “Hands behind your back, and a gag in your mouth. Kinda like the other night. You remember how that felt, right?”

My mouth went dry. I tried to step away and was yanked back so hard I nearly toppled, half-falling against him with a yelp. “Renner-!”

“It won’t be quite the same, though.” His voice dropped to a vicious pitch. Stubble scraped against my cheek. “No one at the inn tore your clothes off. That’ll change.”

I stiffened. Ice trickled through my veins. “W-what?”

He waited. Hands iron-tight around my wrists. Letting the threat settle like a frost on my skin. “You heard me.”

“S-stop it.” I twisted, harder, to no avail. “Let go. You’re… you’re scaring me.”

“Good. You should be scared.” He drew me close, chest hard against my shoulders. “Let’s see… Naked. Shackled. Helpless. And then it’s time for the ritual. Time to see if their damned goddess wants you. They’ll drag you out into the cold,” he shifted forwards, pushing me along until we reached the bed. Both hands dropped to my hips and shoved, sending me to the mattress with a gasp. “And throw you to the ground.”

I seized the pillow, turned, and swung with all my might. “Saints, you are a prick sometimes, you know that?”

He yanked my meager weapon away and tossed it behind me. “Uh-uh, ladyship. No fighting back. You won’t have the chance.”

“Stop.”

“No. They won’t.” He loomed over me. Jaw tight, lips curled. A ruthless look I’d seen before, and it chilled me to the bone. “Now, if you were just a runekeeper, that’d be it. Bloodless, painless, and they’d free you after a few heartbeats. But you’re not just a runekeeper, so that’s not what’s gonna happen.”

Blood roared through my ears. I couldn’t seem to tear my gaze away.

“You’ll be cut open. Not once, not twice. A hundred times. Every bone in your body’s going to be snapped in half. It’s an ugly way to die. You’ll feel every second.” He searched my face, letting the words sink in, then added with a snarl, “And you know what the worst part is? They’ll watch. A dozen men and women who could end it at any moment, give you the mercy of a quick death, and they’ll just stand there. They’ll enjoy it.”

I inched back, refusing to believe any of this. Everything he claimed was too awful, too cruel to possibly be true. It didn’t make sense. Execution was a brutal enough form of justice, but what Renner was describing was… inhuman. Monstrous.

“That can’t… why would they… why?”

“Thalessa’s will,” he spat the phrase like a curse. “That’s the answer I got, anyways. It’s not torment, it’s holy. Damned divine. What a joke. Heroes of the salted realm.” A bitter laugh cracked the air. The disgust written across his face was as fierce as it was baffling.

“Why are you so upset about it? You’ve made it perfectly clear how you feel about people like me. Salt, you want to see us all dead!”

“I think a mad dog should be put down,” he snapped. “Quick and simple. I don’t think someone should be dragged halfway across the realms and tormented.”

The statement hung between us. Renner winced, looking as if his own words had stung him. We spent a long moment in silence. The very walls seemed to hold their breath.

“Well,” I finally said. Curled my knees up, offering a hollow smile. “It… it must have been a really important favor you needed, for you to have… made an exception.” My throat thickened. “Or… or did you just really hate me that much?”

Another flinch. All the hard edges of his countenance, so callous a moment ago, softened with a heavy sigh. “Look, for what it’s worth… I changed my mind.”

“Obviously.” I sniffled. Then, for good measure, I grabbed the pillow and gave him a half-hearted whack.

He snorted. “Earlier than you’d think.”

“That doesn’t make me feel better,” I grumbled, though it wasn’t completely true.

“I’m not trying to make you feel better, I’m trying to keep you from doing something stupid.”

“And here I thought you were done telling me what to do.”

Renner crossed his arms and, with a skyward glance and the air of someone who was nearing the end of their rope, “I’m telling you what not to do. We can bring them the shard tomorrow. That’s what your mom said, right? Deliver it to the Order. We’ll tell them about Fellbrook, describe the Fae, and that’s it. No one needs to mention your deal. Or your magic, for that matter.”

My gaze fell. “Or my magic. One more thing to hide, huh?”

He didn’t answer.

“Magic was always… I don’t know. Good. Even when I was little, when it was new and frightening, it was still this… incredible, amazing part of my life. Like… like some special piece of me that my mom always…” I swallowed, blinking back the tide of emotion. “I never imagined I’d have to hide it from people.”

“Well, maybe one day you won’t. But tomorrow you need to.”

“I’ll… I’ll think about it.”

The very air around Renner seemed to relax. As if he’d been holding a breath this entire time, and had just finally let it out. He looked far from happy, but my answer was apparently enough to satisfy him for the night. He regarded me for a long moment, then gave a short, sharp nod, turned, and left.

I sat on the bed for a while. Thought. Twisted my hands, gazed out the open window, tried to calm my racing pulse. Wondered how much of Renner’s little demonstration had been cruel exaggeration and how much had been… true.

He said they’re heroes. That most of them would die for someone without a second thought. People like that don’t just… torture prisoners. They don’t enjoy watching someone suffer. They can’t. It doesn’t make a shred of sense.

All the same, my heart wouldn’t stop pounding.

I spent some time looking at my runebook and, when the lines began to swim and the familiar nausea swelled, turned instead to my mother’s letter. I unfolded the parchment reverently and curled under the quilt. Read the familiar words over and over, as I had on countless nights before.

When my eyelids grew heavy I set the letter down near my pillow, then nuzzled my cheek against soft cloth. Drank in a midnight-blue skyline and the smell of crisp pine.

My last, begrudging thought as the room grew dark was that, under very different circumstances, Renner tossing me onto a bed might have been a singularly thrilling experience.
