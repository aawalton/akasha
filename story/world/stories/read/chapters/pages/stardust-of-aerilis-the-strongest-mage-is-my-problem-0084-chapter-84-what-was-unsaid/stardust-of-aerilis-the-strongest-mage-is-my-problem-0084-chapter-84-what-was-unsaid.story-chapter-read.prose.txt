Aiven opened his eyes.

The ceiling above him was high and unfamiliar. Pale wood panelling, set with recessed mana-strips that pulsed a warm, natural light. There were no windows. There did not need to be. The light did the work of windows on its own.

He knew, after a moment, where he was.

The vast room. The lush green tapestries on the walls. The faint scent of cedar and something tropical underneath it, citrus or perhaps a flower he could not name. The four-poster bed beneath him, piled with linens so soft his cheek had barely registered the pillow.

A Vulpine guest chamber. The same one he had stayed in before the crossing to the Sunken Fane.

He pushed himself up onto his elbows.

His ribs complained. His back complained louder. The bed was so deep beneath him that the movement felt like climbing out of something rather than rising from it. He looked down at himself.

He was bandaged.

White linen wrapped his torso from collarbone to hip, with a thicker pad strapped over his left side where the lightning had grazed him. A second strip ran across his back. His left forearm had a long bandage along the outer edge. His hair smelled faintly burnt.

He had, for one wild private second on first waking, allowed himself the thought that none of it had happened. That the red carpet had been a dream. That the Virelle he had spoken to in that long quiet room had been a construct his exhausted mind had built out of fear, the way grief sometimes built whole conversations in the hour before dawn.

The bandages took that away.

The relief, brief and small, dissolved. What replaced it was heavier than what had come before.

He could not, at this moment, find the energy to wonder why he was alive. Some part of his clerk-brain wanted to walk back through the barrage and tally the impacts and the angles and ask the obvious question: if she had meant to kill me, why did I wake up? But the rest of him refused to engage with it. The question was too sharp. He set it down.

He swung his legs over the side of the bed. The mattress gave deeply beneath him as he shifted, and the wooden floor when his feet found it was cool and smooth. He pushed himself up.

His knees folded. He slid off the edge and went down with a soft thump against the boards. The bandages around his ribs pulled at the burn underneath them. He did not have the breath for a curse.

He could hear footsteps in the corridor.

The door slid open.

"Aiven!"

Lyra. She was carrying a small wooden tray balanced against one hip; a covered bowl, a cup, and a folded napkin. The moment she saw him she set the tray on a side table without looking and dropped to one knee beside him.

"Easy. Easy. Don't try to stand yet, you've barely been awake an hour total, you're—" Her hands moved quickly. She slid one arm under his shoulder. "Up. With me. There. Slowly."

She got him back onto the bed. The linens swallowed him gently. She pulled a light cover up across his lap. She crouched in front of him and looked into his face for a long, searching moment, then exhaled and sat back on her heels.

"You scared me," she said, quietly.

Aiven swallowed. "What happened?"

His voice came out rough. It barely qualified as a voice.

Lyra glanced at the tray, then back at him.

"Cyria called for help. From the clearing in the middle of Fangreach. Vane arranged for a retrieval. Cyria was on her feet. You weren't. They got you to a rover, brought you straight back to the headquarters. The upper floors are still being aired out, but this wing is fine. The healers have been with you off and on for the last six hours."

Aiven processed it.

He had not been killed. He had not been taken. He had been put back.

His mind, against his will, returned to the room. The red carpet. The white circles overhead. The fireball blooming in her palm at point-blank range, and the smile — the worst smile he had ever seen on her face — and the soft, considered word.

Die.

The word, even remembered, pressed against his chest like something with a weight.

He looked down at the cover.

Lyra was watching him.

"Aiven," she said, very gently. "Is there anything I can do?"

He shook his head.

He tried, in his throat, to find a kinder version of what came out. He could not.

"I just need some time, Lyra."

She nodded.

She did not protest. She did not press. She rose, smoothing the front of her tunic, and her eyes did not leave him.

"My room is two doors down the hall," she said. "If you need anything. Anything at all. Water. A different bandage. Just — knock on the wall, if you can't make it to the door. I'll hear."

She paused at the doorway.

She wanted to say something. He could see it in the way her shoulders set, the way her hand lingered on the frame. There was something she had been holding since the moment he had come back without Virelle, and she had been holding it the whole half-day he had been asleep, and she had been holding it now as she had bandaged him and brought the tray and crouched in front of him.

I'm sorry.

She had thought it more than once already, he could see. The thought had a shape behind her eyes. If you hadn't come for me, she would still be here. He had not framed it like that to himself yet, and Lyra, who knew him better than almost anyone, knew that her saying it now would not ease anything. It would only put a new weight on his shoulders that he did not have the strength to carry.

She let it be.

She left, and the door closed behind her, and the recessed mana-strips pulsed warm in the high ceiling, and the room was very quiet.

Aiven sat on the edge of the bed.

The linen was soft under his palm. The tapestries on the walls held their colours in the warm light. There had been a second small clatter of mana-rod and prismatic orb in this room the last time he had stayed in it, a low theatrical voice making cutting observations about the bedding being almost adequate.

There was no second sound now.

His fist closed slowly in the sheet.

The fabric balled in his hand. His knuckles ached against the linen. He held it like that for a long time, head bowed, eyes on nothing, while the warm light held and the great vast room held him.

In the Fold's throne room, the throne stood at the head of the chamber, and Lucien was seated on it with the patience of someone who had nowhere else he needed to be. His robe fell in clean folds around him. His hands lay loose on the rests.

At the base of the dais, two figures.

Sylphaine had draped herself sideways against a pillar, twin-tails over one shoulder, picking at the edge of a fingernail. Valerion stood beside her with his arms folded, faintly disinterested, the way he had stood in nearly every villain-side scene he had ever appeared in.

"Where is the Starweave?" Lucien asked.

He did not raise his voice.

Sylphaine glanced up, lazily.

"In her chambers, Master." A small shrug. "She has not come out since the deal. Not for anything."

"No matter," Lucien said.

He sat back.

"It is time to move to the next phase. The Anchor is in our hands. I trust Lyra will do as she has been told."

He smiled.

It was a small smile, private, almost contented. The smile of a man who had set a long mechanism into motion many years ago and was, finally, watching its last gears begin to engage.

Sylphaine pushed off the pillar.

"Master." Her tone had the careful brightness she used when she was about to disagree with him and hoped to disguise it as curiosity. "Forgive me. I do not understand. They were here. The clerk was here. The fox was here. We had them in the hall, surrounded, outnumbered, the elf already in our hands."

She spread her fingers.

"We could have killed them both right there. I do not see why we permitted the elf to dictate terms. Why allow her to play whatever dumb drama she had in mind?"

Lucien did not look at her.

His gaze remained, idly, on the high arches of the ceiling.

"Sylphaine. If you can accomplish what the Starweave can accomplish, then by all means. We shall do it your way. Kill the clerk. Kill the fox. We waste no further time."

He let the offer sit a moment.

"Can you?"

Sylphaine opened her mouth.

She closed it.

A small, bright flush rose up the back of her neck and stained the tips of her ears. She glanced sideways at Valerion, who did not look at her, which was somehow worse than if he had. Her twin-tails twitched once. She returned her gaze to the floor.

"No, Master."

"No." Lucien's tone did not change. He did not gloat. He had only ever stated facts. "Then you understand why the terms were the terms."

Sylphaine inclined her head.

She did not speak again.

The chamber they had given her was lavish.

It was three rooms larger than any chamber Virelle had ever cared about. Marble underfoot, veined in pale grey, polished to a mirror finish. A bed wide enough to sleep four, hung with sheer ivory drapes from a carved canopy. A vanity with a mirror taller than she was. A pair of high arched windows that did not, in fact, open onto anywhere — the view beyond them was the calm pearl-grey sky of the Fold, eternal, indifferent.

The room was bright.

It felt very dark.

Virelle sat on the edge of the bed.

She was not floating. She had not floated since the door had closed behind her after the deal. Her boots rested flat on the marble. Her hands lay folded in her lap. Her back was straight. Her gaze was on the floor.

The orb hovered at the bedside, low and quiet, its pulse slowed to something almost imperceptible.

With Sylphaine's blood-magic, she had remembered most of it now.

Her origin, older than this current shape, older than the elf-form, older than the name Virelle. Her purpose. The fields she had once cleared with a thought. The cities. The work, as Lucien had called it. Meaningful. Glorious. The grand cold structure of what she had been made for and what she had done.

She had remembered the nature of what she was, beneath the costume.

And in remembering all of it, she had arrived, with terrible clarity, at the fact she could no longer set aside.

She could not be with Aiven.

Not because of what Lucien had said. Lucien's arguments had been the polish. The thing beneath them was simpler and worse. She knew, now, what she actually was. She knew the shape of her, the use of her, the things her existence cost when it was let off the leash. He could love her. She could let him, yet they cannot be truly together. The longer she stays with him, the worse Aiven would suffer.

That was the math.

That was why the deal had been the deal it had been.

Then Virelle heard a knock at the door.

"It is me," Lucien said from the other side.

She did not answer.

The door opened anyway.

She did not move. He did not enter the room more than a pace or two; she registered that, distantly. He stopped just inside the doorway, hands folded loosely in front of him, and surveyed her with the patient cataloguing of a tutor checking on a student during quiet hours.

"We will require your assistance for the next phase, Starweave."

His voice was the same as always. Soft. Pleasant. Almost academic.

Virelle did not lift her head.

"Fine," she said. The word came out flat, drained. "I will do it. See that you uphold your end of the arrangement."

"I will."

He let a small pause sit between them.

"I keep my word, Starweave. When I make a deal, I honor it. You will find I have always done so."

She did not respond.

Lucien took a single slow step further into the room. Not close. Halfway between the door and the bed. He stopped there.

"I must say." His voice held the faintest trace of warmth, the kind that was worst when it touched the calm of him. "Your performance with him earlier was a fine piece of work."

She did not look up.

"I do, however, hope," he went on, lighter now, almost musing, "that some morning, somewhere in our long collaboration ahead, you will discover you actually mean some of what you said today. About me, I mean. About the work. About the glorious purpose."

"If you are here to make noises with your mouth," Virelle said, her voice low and even, "then you can leave. The deal is the deal. I shall fulfill my part of it. You may go."

Lucien's smile did not move.

He stood there a moment longer, contemplating her — the bent head, the folded hands, the boots flat on the marble.

"Very well," he said.

He turned, unhurried.

At the door, he paused.

He did not turn back. He spoke to the threshold.

"Do remember, Starweave. Whatever you tell yourself about today, about him, about the deal, at the end of all of it, you and I will still be here, and he will not."

He stepped through the doorway.

"Rest, if you can."

The door closed behind him with a soft, expensive click.

Virelle sat very still.

The orb beside her pulsed once, faintly. The pale grey light through the arched windows had not changed. It would not change. There was no morning here.

She stayed on the bed.

After a long while, she lifted her head. She looked at the polished marble between her boots. She looked at the canopy above her. She looked at her own folded hands.

Her own words came back to her.

A mere human.

Filthy hands.

I shall need centuries to wash the taste of that affection out of my mouth.

She had said them in her own voice. In her own cadence. With her own face. They had been so well-shaped that even she, hearing herself, had almost believed them. Aiven had believed them. She had watched him believe them. She had watched the moment his eyes had stopped looking for her in her face, and the silence that had bloomed in him afterward, and the way his shoulders had folded inward as he had risen from the carpet for the last time.

She had done that.

The first tear surprised her.

She lifted one hand slowly and touched the side of her face, and her fingertip came away damp, and she stared at the drop on it as if she had not seen one before.

A second tear followed the first.

She tried, briefly, to hold them in. She thought she knew how to.

It did not work.

Something behind her ribs that she had been clamping shut since the moment the thread had gone in finally gave, and she folded forward over her knees, and her face went into her hands, and she wept.

She wept in silence for a long time. Carefully. Quietly. The way a person weeps when she does not want the walls to hear.

The orb floated low beside her, dim and pulsing, as the only witness.
