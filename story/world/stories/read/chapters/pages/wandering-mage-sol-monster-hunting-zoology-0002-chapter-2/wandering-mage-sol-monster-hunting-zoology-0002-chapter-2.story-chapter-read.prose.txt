Falidorn was a massive walled city, set below a waterfall that echoed down the Afuriba mountains, with the highest tier of architecture being the palace above the walls, the library, and the noble estates just below.

Aldmern led her through the gate at the western wall—unknown to him was a secret passage further down along the cliff face, which Tressa would use to slip in and out of the city when she didn’t want to answer any questions.

“Lady Tressa.” One of the guards saluted at the gate. “Welcome back.”

She gave a polite smile and a nod, though she was examining their postures for anything telling. Aldmern simply saluted in return.

She was sure she was just being paranoid that her secret sketchbooks might have been found, but it wasn’t as if low-ranking guards would be let in on a noble daughter being suspected of such crimes. She wouldn’t know what this was about until she made it home, she supposed.

They made their way up the city streets to the higher residential area and finally, the Windshade family manor.

As they entered, Tressa’s mother stood waiting in the entryway—a beautiful High Elvish woman with sharp, elegant features and a poised demeanor that seemed etched into her bones. Her silver-blonde hair was woven into an intricate braid, and her deep violet robes bore their house’s emblem of the eagle clutching a scroll.

“My lady, I have retrieved Tressa as requested,” Aldmern said with a deep bow.

“Thank you, Aldmern. I appreciate your haste in retrieving my daughter,” her mother replied coolly, then turned her assessing gaze on Tressa, her eyes sweeping over her dirt-smeared legs, hair tied back in a ponytail like a common Wood Elf, and her rumpled clothing.

“So, what does Father want?” Tressa asked.

“Why does Father wish to see me,” her mother corrected smoothly. “And the Elders have come to speak with you. But first, come with me—you should get cleaned up.” She then turned to one of the maids standing nearby at attention. “Please inform my lord husband that Tressa has arrived, and send word to the elders she will be with them within the hour.”

As she turned away, Tressa shot a look at Aldmern and muttered, “Be glad you weren’t born a noble.”

He smirked and whispered back, “Oh, but surely it has its advantages.”

She wrinkled her nose at him, then followed her mother. In truth, her mother wasn’t so rigid when they were alone. But the moment others were present, she transformed—strict, polished, and insistent that Tressa behave like a proper lady as well.

But more importantly, all of this seemed normal. If the Elders had come here to accuse her of crimes, surely there would be more tension in the air. Quite the opposite, really. Her mother looked unexpectedly… pleased?

When they made it to her bedroom, not a single thing was out of place. She quickly glanced to the floor around her bookcase, where a trap door into a crawl space was further concealed by magic and hid her most compromising research notes. It could only be accessed by moving the bookcase, which she would have to use magic to lighten first, and surely, if that had been done, they wouldn’t go through the trouble of putting everything back as it was. Hell, she would have been ambushed right at the front doors, dirty knees or no.

Tressa let out a relieved sigh, realizing her fear was nothing but paranoia after all. But the question still remained—why were the elders here to see her?

Her mother opened the wardrobe and searched through the rows of carefully kept dresses before selecting a light green one with long, flowing sleeves and silver embroidery stitched along the collar and hem. It was elegant, soft, and perfectly suited for a noble daughter—which meant Tressa hated it immediately.

“Must I dress so formally just to speak with them?” Tressa muttered.

“Of course you do. Your father has worked hard to maintain his place in the High Council, and as his heir, you reflect upon him. You should show respect for what he’s built for you.”

Tressa wanted to grumble and complain, “It’s not like I asked for that…” In truth, she did not want to be his heir and hoped they would have another child to save her of the burden.

Her mother’s expression softened. “I know it’s hard for you. You know, you take after your uncle—you always have.”

“Uncle Caelen?”

“Yes. Caelen was never meant for courtly life either.”

“Father never speaks of him.”

“No, he wouldn’t,” her mother said quietly. “They fought, in the end. Caelen refused to take up his seat on the high council. He said the laws they upheld were written by the blood that once burned our people’s libraries.”

Tressa looked up sharply. “He said that? Of King Faldin? Out loud?”

“Oh yes,” her mother said with a wry smile. “At a public banquet. In front of three elders and not to mention two of the king's personal guard.”

Tressa let out a breath of disbelief. “So what happened?”

“Caelen was stripped of his titles, though I doubt it mattered much to him, as he willingly left the city the next morning. No one’s seen him since.”

“So where did he go? A human city?” Tressa asked excitedly.

“No, no, I doubt it. He didn’t much like humans. Not after the wars.”

“Hm…”

Her mother smiled. “I suspect he’d gone to Legaro and found the lower of our kin. Those that wouldn’t be touched by Faldin’s rule.”

“King Faldin isn’t anything like his father, though. He even created the high council and rescinded many of his powers to them to make up for what his father had done.” Tressa had met him several times and felt that, for a king, he was quite kind and caring.

“Be that as it may, he is the Mad King’s son, and Caelen could never quite accept that.” She chuckled softly. “But in any case, I bring it up because I’m sure you would have gotten along quite well with your uncle. Now then, hurry and get washed up, and if you don’t start trouble, I’ll share more stories of him another time.”

Tressa narrowed her eyes. “Oh, I see. Luring me in with an incentive.”

“It’s called diplomacy, Tressa,” her mother said, cracking a smile. “A skill I suggest you learn. Especially if you insist on bending the rules. Now then…” She opened the door to leave and called in a servant. “She is all yours, Maikia.”

“Of course, my lady.” The maid entered and bowed deeply.

Tressa sucked in a groan and resigned herself to the horrors that now awaited her at the hands of her mother's personal maid.

Maikia closed the door behind her mother, then folded her arms and looked Tressa up and down in a very disapproving way.

“So…” Tressa said.

“My Lady. I cannot believe this. Your hair looks like an abandoned bird’s nest, you’ve ruined your clothes, and tracked in enough dirt to begin a garden, I suspect!”

Tressa looked down at herself. “It’s not quite that bad.”

The maid raised an eyebrow at Tressa, then let out a low, menacing chuckle. “Well, what is done is done. But sometimes, my lady, I fear you are far too independent for your own good.”

“I don’t see that as such a bad thing.”

“No, I imagine you wouldn’t.” Maikia stepped behind her and began pulling her mud-stained outer tunic. “Arms up.”

Tressa sighed but obeyed as the maid stripped away the ruined clothes, then marched her nude to the bathroom while Tressa kept an arm over her bare chest. Maikia scrubbed her down so ruthlessly that Tressa felt she was trying to remove her skin, then finally allowed her to submerge in the bath while she washed her hair.

Once finished, she was dried off and dressed in the soft green dress. Maikia adjusted the lace at the back and tied it off, fitting the dress snugly to Tressa’s body.

“There. You look lovely now, my lady. That dress suits you beautifully. It brings out your emerald eyes—why, they almost seem to glow.”

Tressa turned and stared in the large mirror above her boudoir when a soft knock came at the door.

“Enter!” Maikia called, and the door opened.

A younger elf stepped inside, her chestnut hair tied back in a loose braid, and she panted softly as if she were winded from hurrying here.

“H-Hey, Tressa!” she called.

“Lady Tressa,” Maikia corrected.

“Maikia!” The maid jerked upright.

“Yes, and say it properly. Lady Tressa.”

“Lady Tressa?” the younger maid squeaked out.

“Lemia!” Tressa said with excitement as it was her favorite handmaid who had entered.

“I’m here to do your makeup Tressa—Lady Tressa!” Lemia said, and as soon as Tressa was seated, she got to work.

When she finished with the last of the blush, she stood back and said, “Beautiful, my lady!”

Soon after, her mother re-entered, this time with a critical eye, looking Tressa up and down. Her hands clasped before her in that familiar, elegant posture. “Thank you, girls; she looks far more presentable now.”

“Of course, my lady,” Maikia said with a deep bow. Next to her, Leimia startled, then quickly bowed as well.

Tressa messed with the sleeves, and her lips curled into her teeth. She hated lacy dresses.

“Tressa?” her mother asked, snapping her out of it.

“Yes, Mother?”

“Do you have anything to say to them?”

“Thank you for dressing me and getting me ready to see the elders. I’m sure your efforts have saved House Windshade from utter shame and ruin,” Tressa said with an exaggerated bow that had Lemia stifling a chuckle while her mother and Maikia raised brows nearly in unison.

But when her mother caught her eyes, she gave a look that silently said, “I’m warning you to behave yourself, Tressa.”

“Now let us go,” her mother said after Lemia checked Tressa’s hair one last time and gave a quick touch-up to her face. “Your father is already waiting downstairs.”

Tressa carefully watched her step, holding up the dress with one hand as they went downstairs, and ruminating on how traversing tangled undergrowth in normal clothes was easier than this.

Her father was waiting in the receiving room, standing near a tall window that overlooked the city, dressed in a way Tressa always thought looked more like a military commander than some polished lord, and to be fair, that’s exactly what he once was.

Dark violet tunic stiff at the shoulders and tight to his throat, silver clasps on his sleeves, polished boots, the Windshade family emblem pinned to his breast, silvery blond hair pulled back, and a sword at his hip.

She felt his assessing gaze the moment he laid eyes on her.

“Tressa. Stand straight. Let me look at you.”

She winced, then did as he asked. He seemed even more rigid than normal at the moment.

“She looks lovely, doesn’t she?” her mother said, stepping alongside her.

Her father’s expression softened slightly. “Yes. Yes she does.”

Tressa dipped in a shallow curtsy. “Thank you, father.”

He gave a curt nod, and she couldn’t help but feel him continuing to assess her. “The elders have been waiting. When we arrive, you will greet them politely and apologize for the delay.”

Tressa wanted to point out that it’s not like she had missed an appointment and therefore couldn’t be blamed for not being available when they randomly showed up, but she held herself back.

“So what is this about anyway?” Tressa asked as her father began leading the way.

“They didn’t say. But be sure to mind your manners. Show them respect and do not interrupt.”

Tressa sighed. “Of course, father.” He was acting like he already expected her to walk in and start flipping tables and yanking pictures off the walls or something.

Her mother cleared her throat. “I have my suspicions, but I think it's better they tell it themselves.”

Tressa once again noticed the lightness in her tone. Whatever it was, it was certainly far from Tressa’s fears of it being about her sketchbooks, though there were still a number of things her parents might be pleased about that Tressa would absolutely despise. Especially if it had anything to do with the temple.

As the three left the manor, a couple of guards filed in behind them, though they kept their distance. Tressa felt it was a formality, more for show, because Falidorn was already an isolated city and it was unlikely that some assassins might be here for the heads of the Windshade family.

They made their way down to the library, which Tressa was quite familiar with, but if they were here to see the elders, they would be walking through the library and to the connected temple, which Tressa had never been further than the front room of before.

The library’s great doors stood open, and Tressa stepped inside between her parents.

The familiar scent of old parchment, ink, and polished wood immediately eased some of her tension. It was one of the few places within Falidorn where she could spend an entire day without feeling trapped, and while much of their records had been burned by the Mad King, she could still often find something to help with her own research projects.

They crossed the main floor, passing the desk where books, scrolls and stone tablets were checked and cataloged. Tressa knew most of the librarians by name, though none dared greet her with more than a respectful nod while her father was present.

At the far end of the library stood a pair of tall white doors, a soft glow of mana surrounded them that Tressa knew her parents couldn’t see. She was abnormally keen to mana herself, but always wondered if it was a spell on the doors or something else, as she had never seen far beyond them except just inside the threshold during public ceremonies.

As they walked in, Tressa couldn't help but look over her shoulders and just stare at the doors, watching as they closed shut with that odd golden glow. Once they were shut Tressa felt her anxiety return. If this was all for the sake of confronting her about something she now had no escape. Not that running would have ever been a true option anyway.

“Tressa, eyes forward,” her father scolded.

“Right, sorry, Father,” she answered.

“What were you looking at?” her mother asked more conversationally.

“The doors. They are embedded with magic. I was just wondering if they were enchanted after being crafted, or if they were carved from a tree which already had magic properties…” she pondered, staring at the doors again.

A deep, old voice echoed towards them. “Hahaha such a bright young elf.” Tressa jolted upright and turned to see one of the oldest elves she had ever seen.

He was tall and had slight smile lines wrinkling his face. His ears were pointed much like hers, and he stood straight and elegant. His hair was slicked back and adorned with a laurel crown made of silver or some type of metal. He had very attractive features for his age. He still had a strong jaw and a pointed nose. His teeth were straight and so white that Tressa figured they would probably reflect the light of the sun.

“Welcome,” he said. “I am High Elder Ornel. It was a shame I missed you earlier, young Tressa.”

“Oh, my apologies for my absence,” Tressa said, bowing to him. “Had I known of your visit, I’d have taken care to make myself available.”

“Not at all, I did arrive unannounced after all. Thank you for accommodating me on such short notice.”

My point exactly; Tressa would have liked to throw in her father’s face. But of course, she held herself back.

“Oh, that’s alright. I mean, um, of course, High Elder,” she quickly corrected herself. She couldn’t help but feel that this whole meeting was overhyped. He did not seem as formal as her parents insisted she be. Tressa glanced back at the door.

“Haha! Well, you look as though you have something you wish to ask.”

“Oh, well… What's the door made from?” She blurted. “I think it’s made from Tamerdin oak.”

His smile faltered, and he blinked several times. Then laughed a full, hard laugh. “You’re almost right. It's ash Tamerdi oak.”

Tressa clicked her teeth. “Darn.”

“You were close. How could you tell?”

Tressa looked surprised. “Um, the color for one. Tamerdin Oak has a hard, grey bark, but once removed, it's white in color. It can be used to imbue with magic or as a catalyst. Some of our mage staffs are made from it.”

He clapped his hands. “Well done. But yes, the doors are indeed enchanted. But to keep that magic going, you need to have—”

“A catalyst. So I'm assuming some form of crystal or a gem. Maybe even a lacryma,” she said, and covered her mouth, realizing she had just cut off the elder midsentence.

She glanced over to her father, whose posture stiffened further, and he opened his mouth, seemingly unsure whether to apologize for her or chastise her until—

“Hahaha!” Ornel laughed. “Well done, well done. You seem well-studied.”

“There are some around the forests, but they’ve lost their properties,” Tressa said. “I read a little more about them in the library because I was curious.”

Next to her, her father still seemed momentarily stunned at his daughter interrupting a high elder. But the elder seemed to be enjoying himself, so he relaxed ever so slightly.

“Ah, I’m so sorry, Lucyia, Altex. How rude of me,” he said, addressing her parents. “I truly am happy to see you well.”

“Thank you, Elder,” her mother said with a bow.

“Of course,” her father bowed as well. “And I apologize for the earlier delay.”

“Please, none of that,” he said, waving his hand. “As I said, it was I who had shown up unannounced. Even so, it is getting late in the day, I’m afraid. Tressa, would you please follow me? It is time.” He gently placed a hand on the small of Tressa’s back as he invited her forward, then said to her parents, “We won’t be too long. I promise to return her shortly.”

Her mother bowed and her father said, “Of course, Elder. Take all the time you require.”

“Time for what exactly?” Tressa asked Ornel once they had left.

Ornel gave her a warm, close-lipped smile. “You will understand shortly.”

That vague answer only heightened her anxiety more. He seemed nice, but maybe this was all about questioning her in private, in a secluded area. She supposed interrogating a high councilman’s daughter would be politically sensitive. She was unfamiliar with this man, so she couldn’t reliably gauge his body language to tell if he was hiding something.

But another part of her insisted she was just being paranoid at this point. There was no evidence anyone had been investigating her in such a way whatsoever. Surely if her arrest was on the agenda, she wouldn’t have spent the afternoon going through this whole song and dance of getting dressed up.

“I’ve never been this far into the temple before,” Tressa said, breaking the silence aside from their echoing steps.

“Few ever are, prior to their trials,” Ornel responded. “The secret of the trials is carefully guarded. We cannot allow other races to learn of the treasure the goddess left behind. It is a sacred gift granted to us by Goddess Emera herself.”

“Is that why I’m here? But my trials are not for five years.”

“Normally you wouldn’t be called so soon,” he said, a wry smile playing on his lips. “But it is at the discretion of the dragon when an elf is called here.”

Tressa blinked, piecing apart those short words in her mind. Getting dragged into an interrogation room was at least something she could get her head around, but what was he saying?

“My apologies, Elder, but… Dragon?”

His smile only deepened as they turned a corner, where the shelves of books gave way to an open hall lined with high columns.

“Long ago, our ancestors built a statue to honor the goddess who created us. Or rather, one of the goddesses. Tressa, what do you know of the story of our creation?”

Tressa cleared her throat. “Well, there is Analeah the First, the ruler of all gods. Everything starts with her, as she set out to create a world more beautiful than any that came before, and in doing so, inspired the other creator gods to follow her lead. Among them was our own creator god—Goddess Emera.”

Ornel nodded in approval, and in such a way it invited her to continue. Tressa had heard the story many times and did her best to recite it as it had been told to her.

“Emera looked upon the races Analeah had created and wished to add her own song to the chorus. And so she gave life to us—the elves. We were different than our kin of other worlds, more special. Here, we are her chosen.”

“Very good, Tressa,” Ornel said, and took over for her. “And as a show of gratitude, our ancestors in the Age of Myth erected a statue in her honor. Not only to Emera, but also to her blessed beast, the serpent dragon Glormect. A creature without wings, yet one that soared through the skies of many different worlds before ours had even been created.”

They stepped into a large chamber, and Tressa stopped in her tracks. A massive set of doors just as beautiful as the front doors stood over them, but far more imposing. Tressa saw that same glow of mana, but this one was different. It was not just blue and gold—that was the majority of it—but there were many other colors as well. And it was so dense, the colors so vivid. Tressa was always keen to perceive mana more clearly than most, but not even she had ever seen it so vividly before.

“I now understand the dragon’s interest in you,” Ornel said, and Tressa flinched.

“Elder?”

“You see the mana, don’t you?”

Tressa swallowed. She had kept her ability to see mana so well secret. It was something her mother had insisted she keep to herself since she was little. Especially before the Mad King’s death, but even still afterwards. It was better that people not know.

Ornel gave her a knowing smile and then waved his hand. The doors began to slide open, and as they parted, the mana came spilling out in heavy waves that rolled across the floor and lingered at Tressa’s feet like fog. “Come along, Tressa,” he said, and led her through the doors.

In the center of the room was a massive statue that towered before her. It was at least thirty feet tall and carved from white and grey stone. It depicted Goddess Emera, draped in a flowing tunic, a band circling her left arm, her long hair captured mid-sway. One hand rested at her side, where the coiling body of a serpent-like dragon wound around her legs, up her torso, and across her shoulder. Her other hand caressed its scaled neck. The lower part of the dragon spiraled down to the base of the statue, where its claws gripped the edge of the stone, just shy of a pool of still water.

Tressa’s mouth fell open. She had often caught glimpses of mana lingering around the library, but now she saw clearly where it was coming from. Once she focused on it, soft waves of blue and gold radiated from the statue, drowning out the other colors as the blue and gold mana was far more potent.

“What do you see now?” Ornel asked.

“Blue and gold,” Tressa answered as she watched the mana pouring directly from the statue. “It’s so potent, it’s drowning out all of the other colors of mana.”

Ornel let out a low, approving hum. “So You can see it. And your assumption is reasonable, but it would not be drowning out the other lesser forms of mana. Quite the opposite. It is creating them, or, more aptly put, they are being born from it.”

Tressa’s eyes drifted along the colors swirling together, and the blue and gold especially moved almost as if they were living things.

Ornel watched her, watched how her eyes followed the mana, and he whispered, “Fascinating. Tressa, please place your hand upon the nose of the dragon.”

“Huh?”

“Go on. Step forward, child.”

She nodded, stepped toward the dragon, and carefully reached her hand up toward its nose. The craftsmanship of the statue was truly amazing. It looked so lifelike, she could hardly fathom the skill and patience required to carve it. But she didn’t understand why it was emitting such incredible mana.

Her hand was shaking a little, but finally she set it down on the dragon’s nose as Ornel had told her.

For a moment, nothing happened.

Then, the colors suddenly exploded outward like a heavy breath, and the dragon moved.

Tressa jumped backward, yanking her hand away as the dragon lifted its head. Its body cracked and groaned, curling around the body of Emera. Then finally, as it adjusted itself to rest its snout over Emera’s shoulder, it relaxed its position, and its curling tail stopped, still wound around her torso and legs.

Then, its eyes shot open, one blue and one gold. It stared down at Tressa as it took her in, and, to her further astonishment, it spoke. Its mouth opened as a deep gravelly voice emerged.

“Tressa Windshade. Child. What do you see?”
