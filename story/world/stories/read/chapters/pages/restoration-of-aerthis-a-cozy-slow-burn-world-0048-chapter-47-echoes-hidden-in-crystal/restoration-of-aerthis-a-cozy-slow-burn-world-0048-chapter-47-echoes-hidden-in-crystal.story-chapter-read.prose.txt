They settled a fair way away from the bunker – far enough that the white dust felt like a distant memory rather than something breathing down their necks.

The broken hallway opened into what used to be a courtyard: half-collapsed walls, bent columns leaning like tired giants, and scattered beams that once held a ceiling now long gone.

The partially open sky above them bled twilight through drifting clouds.

Starpuff floated down, landed atop Elynara’s head, wiggled about and simply… sat down.

Or whatever counted as sitting for a small fuzzy ball of light. Its faint weight warmed her scalp, its glow dimmed to a gentle green.

Aru sat across from her, legs folded neatly, the pile of recovered devices arranged between them in perfect rows. The crystals were grouped by size and shape; the machine fragments by function – at least whatever she suspected the function might have been.

Aru took a slow breath, the motion new to her, and strictly unnecessary – as the girls have already checked – yet one she felt unwell without.

“…There is something I should explain before we begin.”

Elynara nodded, staff laid across her lap, hands resting atop it.

Aru picked up a small, silver fragment. Tiny, flat, delicate, and covered in script designs. All long faded or chipped away at by time.

“This was once part of a personal artefact,” she said. “A tool my people used to access information stored within data crystals and the larger Magiweb.”

Elynara leaned closer.

The thing was no larger than the tip of her pinky finger.

Thin as a fingernail, and incredibly intricate. One of its edges was cracked clean through.

“It’s tiny,” she murmured. “And fragile.”

“Yes.” Aru turned it between her fingers, careful not to press. “These rarely survived even a decade without proper care, needing replacements every so often. And after centuries…”

She shook her head lightly.

“Even this one is too damaged, despite looking nearly pristine. Its core channels are cut through, and the damage made the rest… misaligned.”

Elynara swallowed, eyeing the crystalline threads inside the fragment. Mana circuitry, of sort? Not something she knew enough about yet to say for sure.

“Could you have used them before? You know… when you were still…”

She gestured vaguely at Aru’s new, living form.

Aru nodded gently.

“Automatons like my former self could interface with crystals directly. We did not need these tools. But now…”

Her hand pressed faintly to her chest, where a soft pulse of living mana glowed beneath the plating.

“…I cannot seem to have the ability to do so directly anymore. At least not yet.”

Elynara’s gaze softened, and Starpuff peeked up from the nest it made of her hair, watching Aru curiously.

“And the other devices we found…?”

“They are meant to bridge that gap.” Aru looked at the small machine beside her – something barely the size of a book, full of crystals and thin wires, yet only a few buttons. “If I can repair one, it may allow me to copy data again. Or transfer what remains onto the blank crystals you brought.”

Elynara hummed thoughtfully, glancing at the tiny artefact fragment again.

“…These were implanted, you said?”

Aru nodded.

“Into the body. Every Elthusian of age had one of those implanted, and there were specialized professions and procedures just for that.”

While Aru was talking, Elynara slowly looked around the courtyard, taking in everything around her.

The half-buried walls, still whole and in great shape, despite the ages. The dust-filled shadows hiding rubble all around. The wind slipping through broken stone, the only thing moving anything in this dead space.

And the quiet silence of a place that had waited far too long for someone to return.

“…Well,” she said softly, a small, sad smile tugging at her lips, “I don’t think any such specialists are still around today.”

Aru’s eyes dimmed in agreement, joining Elynara in quiet observation.

The two watched how the pale twilight softened the jagged silhouettes, turning steel bones into soft shadows. How moss clung to cracks in the ground where stubborn life insisted on surviving. A single glowbug drifted in the distance, then vanished behind a broken archway.

All in all… very little remained of the old world here. There were no crowds, no Elthusians either. What remained was merely their most sturdy work. The architecture and this broken city.

Just the echoes that once mighty civilization had left behind.

Aru placed the fragment aside and folded her hands on her knees.

“There is no telling what we will find inside these crystals,” she said quietly. “What they recorded. What they chose to preserve. What memories remain.”

She looked at the hundreds of shards spread before them, their surfaces reflecting bits of starlight.

“But among all of this… something is bound to be useful.”

Elynara nodded.

Her heart tightened, beating a little faster at the very weight of possibility.

The knowledge they might uncover. Truth they’re yet to discover.

And maybe… finally begin to understand what happened here.

She reached forward and picked up one of the blank crystals, turning it in her palm. Its surface caught the starlight and shimmered faintly, as if eager to be filled.

“Then,” she said softly, “let’s start waking the past up.”

It was both more exciting than Elynara expected… and far less at the same time.

Exciting because – after she supplied mana into the device’s side slot at Aru’s direction, and Aru then carefully arranged two crystals into their respective grooves – the relic worked.

Light flared into existence.

Elynara didn’t know what to expect, but if she were told to expect a light show, she would have still expected it to be more of a faint glow or dull outline after centuries of no use.

Yet what greeted her instead was a clean, sharp stream of mana-bright lines that burst upward into the air above the device, unfolding into a perfect little floating window, it’s clarity rivaling the world around it.

Her eyes gleamed in wonder at what, her oldest memories supplies must have been a hologram.

And it was glorious, like a piece of memory made solid.

Elynara leaned forward so fast she nearly bumped her chin on her staff.

“It’s so clear,” she whispered. “So lifelike.”

Aru nodded, equally transfixed for a heartbeat.

“These displays were quite common, even if only really used by children too young for their own interfaces.”

Elynara choked.

“By children?!”

Aru blinked, uncertain why that was surprising, opened her mouth, shrugged, and instead clicked one of the buttons again.

And before Elynara could comment further, the hologram shifted to display a list of options. Aru swiping at the one in the middle.

The soft blue light sharpened into a rotating diagram of a boxy machine with far too many levers. Then the device emitted a bored-sounding voice speaking in perfect monotone:

“–and always ensure that the emergency lock is disengaged before attempting to recalibrate the second-phase mana intake converter–”

Both girls stared.

Elynara’s excitement froze mid-breath at the nigh-incomprehensible nonsense spewing forth. All delivered with the level of excitement that, despite only going on for mere seconds, was already making her sleepy.

The two listened for a while longer, before Elynara reached the only conclusion she could.

“…Is this a safety guide?” she asked flatly.

Aru touched the side control.

The hologram flicked to a new recording.

“–if you observe any irregular vibrations, please notify your immediate supervisor. Do not attempt to fix internal mana conduits by hand. Failure to comply may result in injury or disciplinary action–”

Another diagram appeared. Some sort of rotating ‘mana pressurizer’ with blinking warning signs.

Elynara slumped.

Aru tried another file.

“–standard procedure for shutting down a dual-core magitech mill–”

Another.

“–proper handling methods for storage of volatile containment mixtures–”

And another.

“–a reminder that personal protective equipment is mandatory at all–”

Elynara groaned and placed her face in both hands. Starpuff sagged dramatically into her hair, losing the will to sparkle.

The worst part wasn’t even the monotone voice constantly droning on. Rather, it was the fact that, despite all the fancy sounding words, the machinery and diagrams shown were… superficial. The names meant little to Elynara, the precautions silly centuries later. Whatever was actually there could not even be used to infer how anything might have really worked.

It was all just… dry precautions.

“…Why,” Elynara muttered, “would anyone bring this with them to a bunker meant for saving their lives?”

Aru considered this for half a second.

“A hurried worker, perhaps. Someone who forgot it in their uniform pocket when evacuation began.”

“That… actually makes it even worse.”

Aru hummed.

“Indeed.”

Still, she efficiently copied the contents onto a blank crystal, set the original aside, and placed it into what would soon become their low-value pile.

It made a very sad little clink against the metal slab.

Elynara exhaled and flicked a lock of hair out of her face.

“…Right. Let’s hope the others are better.”

Aru nodded solemnly.

One might even say determinedly.

Starpuff perked up, hopeful once more – mostly because it seemed to realize that if this continued, it might die of boredom before any sand ever touched it. It chirped encouragingly at the next crystal, threatening it into having something interesting inside.

Elynara couldn’t help but smile.

“Okay,” she murmured. “Let’s try again.”

Aru reached for the next data crystal.

The following hours became something far stranger than Elynara expected.

A curious mix of shock, excitement, mild horror, rising fascination, and times where the creeping boredom made her eye twitch in slow intervals.

The first crystal after the safety guide one lit up beautifully, turning into a cascade of blue geometries unfolding in mid-air like a blooming flower. The mana inside the device pulsed rhythmically, humming with a life of its own. Elynara leaned closer every time a new hologram unfolded, each one crisp enough that she half-expected the people inside to step out and greet them.

But the content… Oh, the content.

“…Is that a recipe?” Elynara asked, staring at a recorded pair of hands mixing something in a bowl. The voice narrating the process sounded cheerful enough, and the food actually looked edible compared to what she had been eating lately.

“That’s a recipe.”

Aru nodded, adjusting the data transfer settings with careful taps.

“It appears so.”

“And this one is about… hair care?”

Aru paused the projection.

“Yes.”

“And this–”

“A shopping list.”

“And this one–”

“Instructions for repairing a household heater.”

Elynara dragged a hand down her face, groaning softly. Starpuff mimicked her groan with shiny abandon, except its version sounded like a confused flute note.

The next crystal activated with a soft click, projecting a small child on the screen waving enthusiastically at the recorder.

“Hello everyone! Today we’re going to learn about–”

Aru switched to the next file.

The image turned into a rather baffling diagram of a device shaped like two circles fused together, with glowing lines running through it. The bored voice from earlier returned:

“–when operating the dual-turbine mana recycler, always ensure that containment gate B is locked–”

Elynara stared at the diagram, then at Aru, then at the diagram again.

“…Do they just… record everything?”

“Yes,” Aru replied calmly. “It was common. Very convenient.”

“Convenient,” Elynara repeated in disbelief. “They made a video explaining how not to lose your fingers to a mana-powered recycler and the solution was… ‘don’t put your fingers inside’?”

“That was step three.”

Starpuff flopped dramatically across Elynara’s lap, its glow dimming to tragic levels.

Yet not all crystals were quite so… disappointing.

Some were beautiful.

They held stories written in long flowing holographic texts, poetic yet humming with certainty. Some had music – soft, melodic, haunting in its age. A few had personal journals where voices spoke in excited tones about new projects, upcoming celebrations, and small joys.

One crystal held an entire pile of doodles – children’s drawings filled with stars, spirits, and moon goddesses.

Elynara had to wipe her eyes at that.

But there were still so many crystals to look through. Hundreds of them. And each one packed with its own little world.

Yet she knew they were only what remained. A mere fraction of what the world once was, of what it once held.

And still more than enough to be oh so very captivating.

Blueprints of machines neither of them recognized – spirals, cylinders, cages made of shimmering circuit lines. There were tools whose purpose she couldn’t even guess at. Tutorials for things she didn’t understand. Even short… clipped… bursts of facts that seemed to have no context at all.

“Top five myths about mana pressure you absolutely must stop believing–”

Aru promptly switched to the next file.

Even she seemed bewildered.

Elynara brightened most at the educational material.

Entire clusters of crystals that held recorded classes – both for children and adults – complete with diagrams, examples, and teachers trying to keep order in rooms full of excitable kids.

“…They had so many books,” she whispered, watching a holographic librarian guiding someone through shelves taller than the bunker entrance. “So many lessons… And all of them on those crystals?”

Aru’s voice softened.

“They valued knowledge greatly.”

It made the silence of the ruins feel heavier. But practical concerns eventually intruded.

Elynara’s satchel crystals began to fill – one by one, their soft glows deepening as more information was transferred into them. And when only a few remained, yet there were still so many to copy over, she felt a little panicky.

“Oh no. We’re almost out of space.”

Aru blinked, then smiled at her.

“Don’t worry. We can simply overwrite one of these.”

Elynara pointed immediately at their least cherished crystal.

“That one. Absolutely that one. If I hear about ‘proper containment of volatile mana mixtures’ one more time…”

Aru picked up the safety guide crystal with careful fingers, the gesture almost ceremonial despite the crystal’s tragic contents.

Starpuff poked it, probably to make sure it knew its days were numbered.

The tiny stack of “low-value” crystals grew slowly – safety guides, maintenance tutorials, snack reviews, repetitive announcements, and what Elynara suspected might be a running joke involving a glowing toaster that she absolutely did not understand.

They watched, skimmed, organized, sorted, copied.

And still… the pile of untouched crystals remained dauntingly large, each one a treasure vault filled with far more than one might expect.

It was becoming clear that they had stumbled onto something enormous, and the girls stopped trying to look through all of the content they had to offer quickly. Skimming through more and more with each one. Sometimes only watching more because the data transfer took time.

Going through everything they had to offer… It would not be a project for a day, or a week. It was a veritable library that would follow them for months.

Maybe even years.

Elynara didn’t mind. Not really.

Everything here was a piece of the world she wanted to understand.

A piece of Aru’s people.

A piece of what once was.

She just hoped the next crystal didn’t start with:

“If you hear an unusual rattling sound coming from your recycler–”

Starpuff groaned again, the little spirit getting really good at reading her thoughts. Or it was just bored and matched her mood instead.

Elynara patted it sympathetically, eyes drifting to the next shining shard waiting to be unlocked.

“…All right,” she said, drawing a steady breath. “Let’s see what the next one holds.”

Aru nodded, her hands as precise as ever.

And as their work continued, the hours blurred together into something strange. Not unpleasant, not exactly overwhelming, but layered. Much like wading through warm water only to hit cold pockets without warning.

Elynara wasn’t sure whether to smile or cry or wince or look away.

Because the crystals didn’t just hold diagrams and guides and dusty instructions on how not to lose hands to machinery.

They held people.

And that part was the hardest.

Simply because they were so… normal.

Painfully, achingly normal.

Families cooking together in tiny kitchens. Parents recording their children’s school projects. People laughing as they filmed each other tripping over pets, or burning a meal, or showing off a new gadget.

Quiet nights spent reading.

A man singing terribly off-key while folding laundry. A girl yelling at her brother for eating her snacks again.

Small things. Little pieces of life.

Pieces that had survived while all the people who made them… had not.

Elynara’s throat tightened every time a new personal clip lit up in the air.

Every excited voice felt like someone calling out from across centuries. Every smile a warm touch she could never return.

And every sad confession made her chest ache.

Aru sat across from her, watching each hologram with a still, quiet expression. But Elynara could tell the girl felt it too – the soft tremor in her hands, the faint dimming of her inner glow during certain recordings, and brightening at others.

The diagrams and notes were easy to handle. Elynara even liked those – machines, techniques, scribbled lessons, teaching materials. Those were useful. Grounded. They didn’t demand anything from her heart.

But the personal recordings…

Those took pieces of her she didn’t realize she had left to give.

Especially when the tone of the recordings began to shift.

The early clips were hopeful, bustling, bright. But the later ones, made in the last days, carried a tension that even Elynara, with no understanding of their world, recognized instantly.

Arguments. Debates. Long rants left half-finished.

Tired faces, strained smiles.

Some talked about “unchecked advancement.”

About warnings ignored. About safety concerns dismissed.

Others – far too many – responded with unshaking faith:

“Dismoria would never lead us wrong.”

“Progress is everything.”

“Stopping now would dishonor the Goddess!”

“If someone gets hurt, perhaps their conviction simply wasn’t strong enough.”

Elynara felt her fingers curl slowly around her staff.

Aru froze when one recording of a pompous-looking man with gleaming badges proclaimed with conviction:

“Those who die simply do not believe strongly enough in the path of advancement. Their souls merely ascend faster! It is a blessing, not a tragedy.”

Elynara felt something inside her twist sharply.

“No,” she whispered before she realized she had spoken aloud. “No, that’s–”

Her words died as she looked around them.

At the ruins, cold and broken.

At the bunker entrance that had sealed itself so perfectly that not a single living soul ever walked out.

At the dust that had eaten away people, machines, everything.

At the silence of a civilization that once believed it was unstoppable.

Aru didn’t blink, didn’t breathe, but she trembled – just slightly, just enough for Elynara to see.

The next clip flickered to life.

A woman crying quietly as she held a crystal to her chest, whispering that she was afraid. That the power consumption reports didn’t match. That the Source was drawing in too much power, fluctuating too much out of the safety range – though no one listened.

The hologram cut out abruptly.

Elynara swallowed hard.

Patterns were forming.

Patterns she didn’t want to see.

Patterns that made her stomach feel like a stone sinking into cold water.

She looked again toward the bunker doors that had never opened again after sealing. Thought of the dust within, the bones, and the emptiness.

Then back to the hologram where the pompous man continued shouting about destiny and sacrifice.

Her grip tightened until her knuckles whitened.

“I…” she whispered, the words barely coming out. “…I don’t like this at all.”

Aru lowered her gaze. Her voice came soft, quiet as settling ash.

“…Neither do I.”

And the crystals continued to glow between them, offering more truths neither girl had been prepared to find.

It was only morbid curiosity… and something quieter, something that lived deeper than curiosity, that kept them going.

A need to understand, to bear witness, and to push through the ache tightening in their chests.

They worked in silence now more often than not.

Where earlier the clips brought laughter, confusion, irritation, even sparks of joy, now the two girls sifted through each crystal with a solemnity that turned the quiet courtyard into something sacred.

There were manuals – hundreds of them. So many manuals that Elynara felt her fingers twitch every time a new index page flickered open.

And the books too.

Real lessons. Actual guidance written by people who had once lived and breathed magic and machinery in a world that seemed vibrant and loud and overwhelmingly full.

Pieces of her old excitement flickered alive. She would learn so much from them. Finally know where to start, what to study, how to build up her foundations instead of flailing in the dark.

But each new glimmer of hope brushed against the growing ache.

The heaviness of what they were uncovering. The undercurrent she could no longer ignore.

It took hours before they reached the second-to-last crystal in the pile.

Neither knew why Aru reached for that one near the end and not before. Neither knew why Elynara leaned forward more than she had for the others.

But the moment the hologram unfolded, both girls stilled – Aru’s hands poised mid-air, Elynara’s breath caught just short of a sigh.

A man appeared.

His eyes were tired, but his smile was… warm. Vibrant and caring. He reminded her of Father Alaric almost immediately.

An ordinary father in ordinary clothes, holding up a crystal at just the wrong angle so his face filled half the frame. His voice trembled with joyful laughter.

“Come on, sweetheart – try again. Come on, you can do it–”

The recording shifted.

A tiny girl, barely steady on her feet, waddled toward him with arms outstretched.

Her steps were uneven. Her face shone with pure, bubbling pride.

The father nearly burst with joy.

Elynara covered her mouth, heart twisting painfully.

The next clip showed him coaxing her into eating her meals.

“Here comes the flying dragon! Say aaah!”

The girl squealed. Aru blinked as if the scene pulled something deep inside her too.

Neither of them were able to peel their gaze away.

And then there were the drawings.

The girl held them up to the crystal one by one, each one more incomprehensible than the last. A splatter of colors. A giant sun. A house with a crooked door.

What might have been the two of them eating something called “pizza.”

“Is that us eating pizza in our house?” her father cried, delighted. “I’m so proud, my little strawberry!”

The little girl giggled, cheeks flushed.

“Whee!”

“Oh, you like swings? Let’s go again, my little strawberry!”

More clips.

A lifetime’s worth of tiny joys compressed into minutes.

The last wholesome recording ended with the father whispering into the lens:

“Shh… my little strawberry is sleeping. I’ll leave this little present here for her. Tomorrow we’ll talk about fairy gifts and spirit guidance…”

The image faded.

Elynara didn’t know why they didn’t switch crystals then.

They should have.

But neither she nor Aru moved.

The father and daughter had pulled them in. A thread wrapped around their ribs with tenderness that had no right to hurt as much as it did.

Aru slowly tapped the next file.

The street appeared – clean, bright, vibrant. Caretaker automatons tended gardens decorated with glowing flowers. The father and daughter walked side by side, each holding a cone of ice cream.

The girl bounced with every step.

“I love you Daddy! You’re the best Daddy ever!”

“Oh, I love you too, my little strawberry. I love you too.”

Elynara felt warmth behind her eyes again.

“You know Daddy, when I–”

The world shook.

Not violently at first, but enough that the crystal recording shuddered.

A pressure wave rolled across the street, bending light, making the air ripple with something Elynara could only describe as wrong.

Panels along the buildings cracked.

A long jagged line fissured across a walkway in the background. Gardens shook violently; glowing petals scattered like frightened birds.

The father wrapped himself around his daughter instantly, shielding her.

“Daddy, it hurts!”

“Shh, shh – it's okay. You’re alright. You’re alright.”

A caretaker automaton sprinted toward them.

The clip cut.

Aru’s fingers shook above the last file.

Elynara reached out, then stopped.

Her whole body trembled.

“…We should–”

Aru pressed the button.

The bunker.

Crowded. Echoing.

Dim.

Caretakers rushed between families, passing blankets, holding lanterns, keeping children close.

People huddled together. Some crying. Some praying.

Some staring at the walls with soft, numb disbelief.

The little girl sat wrapped in a too-large blanket, sipping something warm from a cup. Her hands shook.

Her father’s smile was thin. It didn’t reach his eyes this time.

“Daddy, I’m scared. Can we go home now?”

“Soon, my little strawberry. Soon.”

A beat of silence passed between them.

“Daddy… I’m cold.”

The father pulled her into his arms, tucking the blanket tighter around her tiny frame. White dust drifted through the air in soft pale clouds – barely visible, barely settling.

Elynara felt her heart stop.

“Oh, come here,” the father whispered, his voice cracking. “Don’t worry, my little strawberry. Daddy will protect you.”

The recording ended.

Silence fell like a living thing.

Elynara didn’t breathe. Couldn’t.

Her eyes blurred, tears spilling hot and fast down her cheeks. She made no sound – only the soft, shaking breaths of someone whose heart had cracked open in a way she didn’t expect.

Aru’s voice came faintly. Quiet.

Barely more than a breath.

“…I found this crystal between two bodies clinging to each other.”

Elynara pressed both hands to her face.

The tears didn’t stop. They weren’t meant to.

Starpuff crawled into her lap, small glow dimmed to a single trembling spark.

And for a long moment, none of them spoke.

Because there was nothing left to say.

Only the weight of a lost world… and the quiet ache of the love it left behind.
