Entering the Abyss

The warm sunlight, streaming through the window, fell on the bed in the somewhat cramped room.

The old housecar was now suspended in that silence that only comes after shared desire: a silence from slow breaths, the sweet fatigue clinging to the skin, and from the mingled scent of their bodies, warm and grounding.

Lizbeth and Mai lay tangled between wrinkled sheets—an entire jungle of pale fabrics carrying their imprint. Outside, the world seemed to breathe with them: leaves swaying, birds waking at the wrong hour, and the distant murmur of a river fusing with the sighing of wind through the plants.

The interior carried Lizbeth’s beautiful chaos—paintings hung without order, photographs taped with worn corners, small plants growing in recycled cans. Three plush toys—two potato soldiers and an orca—rested on a shelf beneath the low ceiling. A pendant shaped like a compass lay on a small nightstand beside a stack of books.

Their clothes were scattered across the floor. All of it made the place feel more alive than many cities they had known.

Mai’s lips rested on Lizbeth’s chest, kissing her softly while Lizbeth caressed her hair, flushed, listening to that steady rhythm that calmed her so deeply. Neither of them remembered when the first time had been—whether weeks or whole seasons ago—but the sensation remained unchanged: a warmth, intimate and certain. As if this way of fitting together had existed long before they met. They couldn't say for sure how long, but it had been a long time since the day they met.

“How long are you going to keep that up?”

Mai pulled back a little and licked her lips. “I’m helping you.”

Lizbeth adjusted herself, and Mai sat over her.

“My turn,” Lizbeth said, lowering her mouth to Mai’s breasts.

“I still can’t believe you’re using this place,” Mai murmured, her voice ragged from sleep and the affection Lizbeth was giving her as she stroked the siren’s blond hair.

Her hand traced a slow circle along Lizbeth’s side, as if drawing her to make sure she hadn’t vanished.

“I’m not abandoning it,” Lizbeth replied, half-laughing, half-sighing. “If it leaks, I’ll fix it. If the door jams, I’ll fix that too. And if the bed sinks in the middle… it’s still my home. Some of the best moments of my life happened here.”

It’s home.

A simple phrase, yet Mai felt it slip into her chest like a warm thread tightening in silence.

The light crept further across the old wooden floor. It reached the bed. Kissed their shoulders.

Mai lifted her head to look at her—at the woman she had once seen only from afar, in blurry photographs and names written in fading ink.

And now she could touch her face, her body, with a fingertip—slowly, like someone touching a sleeping star.

Lizbeth stopped sucking and narrowed her eyes at her, letting Mai explore. The two kissed, exchanging the taste of each other.

It was one of the moments when Lizbeth’s face lost all hardness, all the emotional scars the war and years of missions had left behind. Mai adored that instant. She kept it, mentally, the way one guards a relic. Lizbeth was now one of Nevermore's best agents, but what Mai liked most was seeing her carefree, slightly mischievous, smiling attitude.

“You’re thinking too much again,” Lizbeth whispered.

“I always think too much,” Mai murmured against her skin, leaning into her. They rolled together, ending up facing each other again with sunlight washing over them.

They stayed like that.

The sun rose a little more. The breeze outside was soft, carrying with it the humid scent of the jungle. Somewhere far away, an herbivorous dinosaur released a deep sound that vibrated like an echo through the open window.

The sound lulled them, slowly, gently.

Mai closed her eyes.

Lizbeth too.

The bed creaked, but it didn’t matter.

The world was small, warm, perfectly bearable as long as they remained like this.

They felt themselves sinking into the mattress—that delicious sensation of falling without danger, drifting toward a shared dream.

The air thickened.

The light grew more golden, softer, more distant.

Mai thought of saying something—I love you, or maybe don’t let go—but she didn’t make it in time.

Sleep took them with the same gentleness with which the jungle breathed around them.

And then, without warning, everything changed.

The sunlight vanished all at once, as if someone had slammed the sky shut.

The trailer shuddered.

The air turned cold—violently cold.

The smell of damp earth grew sharp, almost painful.

A thunderclap split the silence. Then another. As if someone were hammering the clouds with a colossal mallet.

Mai opened her eyes a little, dazed—but immediately fell back into sleep. Lizbeth stirred as well, uneasy, breath sudden and tense.

A hurricane wind struck the housecar walls, making the frames, photos, and plants rattle.

Rain fell as if the sky were collapsing in one solid blow.

The bed kept sinking—but it was no longer a dream. It was something else.

Something that pulled. Something that called.

Something that had been waiting in a place that did not belong to that morning or that stolen calm.

And as the world outside erupted into storm, both women remained suspended in the instant just before waking…

The door of the housecar opened with a tired creak, swallowed almost instantly by the roar of the storm. A wind heavy with leaves and mud slipped in first, and then the wolf entered: her dark fur and red-tipped ears dripping, weighted by the rain, her blue eyes glowing with something almost electric.

She shook her body once, as if trying to rid herself not only of the water, but of the bad omen clinging to her back.

Above her, the small blue bird arrived—its feathers bristling from the wind, its eyes like tiny fragments of sky trapped in a trembling body. It fluttered twice, shook off the droplets, then settled atop the wolf as if the storm meant nothing, as if the only thing that mattered was inside that housecar where an impossible silence reigned.

Together, the two animals approached the bed.

The floor groaned under the wolf’s paws, and the soft whistle of the bird floated like a thin thread through the heavy air.

When they reached the edge of the mattress, the wolf whimpered quietly—a sound half-question, half-fear.

From up there, the impossible was clear:

Mai and Lizbeth were sinking.

Their bodies descended into the bed as though the mattress were a deep, silent lake swallowing them inch by inch. The sheets curved downward, taut, following the shape of that soft, impossible abyss.

Their chests rose and fell with slow, deep breaths—too deep.

Too distant.

The bird tilted its head to the side, watching them with that absolute attention only winged creatures possess.

It let out a brief chirp almost a question.

Then it hopped onto the bed, sinking further and further into it as if the mattress had no bottom. Mai and Lizbeth looked as though they were at the bottom of a well, yet still asleep.

The bird glided toward the center, toward the dark depression opening between the sleeping bodies. It sang once more—a blue note, light, sharp as a flicker—and then leapt without fear, disappearing into the soft abyss that was devouring the two women.

The wolf stepped back, her heart hammering beneath her ribs.

She looked at the dark hollow in the bed.

Watched as the fabric stretched and stretched and trembled. The familiar landscape of the housecar was starting to fade, little by little.

She whimpered, almost childlike, and moved closer again. Her claws shook as she placed them on the edge of the mattress, which bent under her weight as if it were no longer cloth or foam, but a living surface.

A thunderclap burst outside, illuminating the scene for a heartbeat: the trembling wolf, the sinking bed, the world reduced to that single point.

The wolf blinked, swallowed and stared at the place where the bird had vanished.

The howl that escaped her throat was one of unease.

She crouched, braced herself and leapt.

She sank.

The mattress swallowed her without resistance.

There was a single heartbeat. Then nothing.

Just Darkness.

A darkness so deep it had no sound, no texture.

Only an endless ocean, still and impenetrable.

The wolf kicked at the absent air. The bird drifted forward, floating as though swimming through shadow. Together they descended, or ascended, or simply slid toward a point without direction.

And then—a tremor.

A breath.

The darkness cracked like glass.

Suddenly they were there, suspended in a place that was neither sky nor earth, facing an impossible structure: an enormous pyramid of black stone, so tall its peak vanished into a purple haze.

Each of its sides exhaled a faint glow, like an ancient breath. It was as if the scene were coming into focus and the entire setting were just taking shape before the newcomers' eyes.

Like a sleeping eye.

The wolf and the bird froze, tiny before the geometric mountain.

The storm was gone, the world as they knew it, too.

This was the threshold of something new.

They looked at each other, and in that distortion of space they soon found a point of reference. They began to descend toward the grassy ground far from the pyramid.

When they reached the ground, they looked around.

In the distance, there was a storm, but on the opposite side stood a hill, long stairs and, at the top, the pyramid and the clouds that made the moon flicker.

The she-wolf looked at the bird, and then they both gazed at the landscape before them. A long flight of stairs ascended the mountain toward that pyramid.

“I hope this works,” said the she-wolf.

The bird perched on the animal’s head and began pecking at it angrily, then uttered just one word.

“Shh.”

“Yes, I know. These were their memories, and now we’re in the most dangerous part.”

The she-wolf began walking toward the stairs while the bird led the way.

In the sky above the pyramid, a blue electrical glow appeared.

It lasted only a second. But the roar and tremor that followed told them that something had happened at the base of that pyramid.
