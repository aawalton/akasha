Chapter 39Wilting of Aeternus

Long in the past, at the bottom of a chasm where the sun doesn't reach, Aeternus once killed a dungeon master. He could be made a hero for it, but that wasn't enough. He wouldn't be controlled.

Sword in hand, he faced the dungeon core. It showed him a screen congratulating his clearing of the dungeon, and it gave him a choice—a hundred choices, in fact.

[Power], the screen said. Doors appeared all around Aeternus, each one adorned differently: a rack of swords on one, mana misting from another; or a holy light, or a gate to hell, or even the black mist of death from a skull-keystone of an arched gate.

[Choose one.]

He laughed. He got to have a choice now?

It didn't matter that he'd been the family's golden egg; it had never earned him actual respect. He was just a third son, born to say "yes" to every whim, and even when he'd mastered swordsmanship at fourteen, did his father at least say, "You worked hard"?

His accomplishments were merely a curiosity, a quirk of nature rather than the product of sleepless nights and torn callouses. No one cared about what he'd had to go through for their sake.

Well, he'd stopped caring, too.

But to escape their grasp, he needed something undeniable, something that would force acknowledgment from the guilds who'd approached him with "opportunities" that amounted to indentured servitude; the nobles who'd offered their daughters on one side of a political balance scale; from his own father, whose tongue muttered the words, "The moment you were born, you weren't enough."

Well, he'd said something about having to earn respect by bribes and brute force. It was a lecture of stupid words from a stupid old man, but they were true. Only Aeternus knew who Aeternus was, and if only power gave voice, then power he'll get.

And with the hundred doors around him, there was more than enough power free for his reaping. Imagine that: he could be the strongest of anything!… but, he could only be the strongest of one thing.

Noble society would look down on him for some of the powers these doors might bestow on him. If he were to make a bad choice here, it would foil his plans despite the absolute power he'd gained.

Funny, wasn't it? It was already absolute power, and yet he still cared about how people saw him… But, that was the point. It wasn't about the power. It was the awe and the reverence he was after—no, it was the crushing statement of the new reality he would bring. Now, which one…

[Do you want to try Simulation Mode?]

"What? I'm permitted to do that?" he blurted aloud. Really, by what manner of suspicious offering was this?

[Simulation Mode offers realistic testing environments. To improve predictive outcomes, this mode allows the user to immerse in unplanned scenarios to discover edge cases and new uses.]

The tongues of the ancients were truly arcane, but if he was understanding things right... "I wouldn't die in there, no?"

[No.]

The screen shoved an interface on him: a simple [Yes] or [No]. What else would he choose? With this, there would be no risk at all.

[Yes].

The screen disappeared, and he was left with the doors. Still, there was so much choice.

He took the time to rank each door, looking at each one, gauging what kind of power it might give him, and guessing how society would react to it.

He made the door of swords his first pick. It was like a smith's weapons rack, but as he stared at it for longer, he saw the racks behind the rack, like looking into an infinite mirror of rows and rows of tarnished steel.

Gripping two hilts, he pushed it open. Swords were a natural choice; he had some talent in it, and he had taken down the dungeon master with a simple bastard sword he'd purchased on credit from the local smith. It was a defective product, the old man had said, but Aeternus knew it was the best sword among all the man's stock; well, he only knew that because he'd spent six hours doing a pairwise comparison of the whole inventory. The man complained quite often about how Aeternus had too much free time, but he quite liked being at the smith's. The forge fires made it warmer there.

Once through the door, what greeted him was a silent, snowy forest at midday. The cold was biting, and even if he knew it was a simulation, he felt as if he might actually die here.

The moans of zombies surrounded him. He couldn't find them, but he could sense them: thousands of them sludging through knee-deep snow. Even with how good he was, he would be defeated by the cold and sheer attrition.

[Perk "Sword King" activated.]

The knowledge of tens of thousands of years of techniques flooded into his brain all at once, but all it caused him was a slight wince. The decades of training hammered into him from a young age suddenly seemed like a drop in the ocean compared to whatever this terror was—whatever this ability to cut was.

From any distance, from any time, if it had ever existed or will exist, he could grant it iterative bisection.

He'd already won. He came out of that door wondering whether he had done all the testing he could. It had been a power akin to being a god, and if he'd walked out of here with it, he would be unrivalled.

He looked at the other doors, and he shuddered to think that a god-like power rested behind each and every one. The door of shadows might make him the eminence of assassination and conspiracy, and the crystalline door could make him into an oracle who walks between times. The door of lightning and fire promised him to be the overseer of war and nations, while he could become the smith and creator with the door of mud and steel.

Every thought of a new scenario was like the twisting of a garrote around his chest. Every choice led to something good, something great, and yet he felt like a branch who could not choose which direction to grow in fear of missing the passing sun. Just why—why was he given the burden to choose?

[Query: why have you returned? Expected you would stay for longer.]

He was startled by how the core seemed alive, but after coming from a simulation, he found it easier to wonder whether the core was also just a simulation of life. In that case, could he ask it something?

"What do you mean 'stay longer'? I can?"

[There is no time limit.]

Well, that makes things easier.

Through doors of fire, light, and black ooze; through portals of shimmering water, wind, and time; he entered and emerged, feeling better knowing that he'd be able to make a better choice in the end. Each threshold crossed revealed entire worlds of possibility—blazing with destructive potential, bathed in divine radiance, and cursed in necromantic bliss; and if he were to choose to remain partway human, he could instead wield waters that could heal or dissolve, winds that could let him fly and hear the world's prayers, and promethean fires that grant others enough power to destroy themselves.

With each simulation completed, he convinced himself he was merely being thorough. How could one commit to a path without understanding the alternatives? "Just one more door," he would whisper to himself, "and then I'll be sure."

But not a single door ever made him more sure.

He began spending more time in each simulation, until at the twentieth door, he had spent a full year inside. He had happened upon simulated people, and he could even talk to them. They reacted the same as real ones, and they even had full histories and unique personalities.

In the twenty-first door, he went to check just how far that simulation went. He found kingdoms in there, and when he spent some more time touring the vistas, he witnessed a full-blown war between them.

Just to appease his sensibilities, he stepped in. Once again, he had found himself a hero of nations, but it surprised him that they didn’t even try to chain him. They showered him with gifts, lands, wives, and though they suggested titles to him, he rejected those in particular—and you know what? They didn’t hunt him for it! "It's enough for us that you're here"—wasn’t that just amazing to hear?

In the forty-fourth door, he became god, and in the fifty-first, a simple farmer…with the military power of an empire, sure, but a farmer nonetheless.

By the fifty-second door, it started to feel not quite right.

Something was off about the way simulated people moved. It was like he’d memorized even their smallest programmed patterns; a complex system, even if unpredictable, had many repeating elements after all.

Of course, he was under no illusion that they were real, but perhaps he’d indulged just a little too much. … But, maybe just a little more. Maybe just… Maybe just another one. After all, there was no harm in going through his options, right? Once he'd emerged from this place, there was no going back, and if he could've chosen a better power, it would've been a waste, right?

He was having fun, too. These simulations might just be made of predictable elements, but if they were set up just right, they would give rise to complex scenarios, and never once had he been made completely bored.

A king, a god, a farmer, a soldier, a sage, and everything in between—each door offered a new life, a new power, a new possibility, yet with each new door, he'd surrendered a little more to the indulgence. The 69th had been a world of pure bliss, where every sensation was one of pleasure and every moment was one of joy. The 75th had been a realm of endless knowledge, where he could learn anything he desired. The 81st had been a simple life, but one filled with love and connection.

What, it wasn't real? It didn't matter. It was better.

So many lifetimes had gone by, but he remembered few of them. There's always the next one, he'd think, but as he stood before the last door, his heart pounding with anticipation and trepidation, and he reached out to grasp the handle, a sense of dread washed over him.

This was the last one. After this, there would be no more options, no more possibilities to explore.

It took him a while to decide to open the door. Because it was the last, he shouldn't waste it. Then again, it would be wasted if he'd never tried it anyway.

With a deep breath, he pushed open the door and stepped through. There was salt in the air. In front was an ocean, and behind him, a stretch of sand and a wall of green.

As he followed the sand, he couldn't shake the feeling of emptiness that had been growing inside him. All these choices, all these possibilities—how much did it actually mean?

He was only in there for fifteen minutes. The waves swept his footsteps as he took the door back to the dungeon. He closed it with a simple click, and when there was finally nothing to consider, no paths left to explore, he finally looked up and around. The dungeon around him had grown darker. The dungeon master he had so proudly defeated had become just bleached bones, and the doors that had always been there to greet him upon his return were no longer there—and it was lonely, and he was lost.

He sat on the steps at the foot of the dungeon core. His knees hurt more than he recalled, and when he looked at his fingers, they were withered, no more than skin draped over bone.

Time was gone, and he knew what he had done. In the pure silence of the dungeon, there was no one to confide in, no one to whom to confess this fear gnawing at him from the feet-up: that if he couldn't change how his family and the world saw him, then he wouldn't be able to change his fate.

His "fate"... His fate, his fate, his fate. Why was it so important to him? This obsession over it, it was the root cause; disgusting, wasn't it? If it weren't for his fate—to be used, evaluated, praised, and punished—he wouldn't have needed to come down here and die an old man! If he weren't just running away, running away, running away to nowhere, looking for the "best gain," looking for the "least pain"; if his life hadn't been founded on the fear of fate, he wouldn't have guided himself with a toxic pattern of needing hope and courage to fight that fear around every single turn. If he wasn't so bloody traped, then... Then this should have been a better life.

Perhaps caring about the outcome of his life was what had done him in. Others said he didn't care enough about life, but that was the opposite of the truth. He was a life-addict, clinging to anything that helped him imagine a better life, because hope was the only good thing he had ever found, and it was addicting. Hope was a poison indeed.

It was already getting harder to breathe, and his limbs were beginning to stiffen. So this is what being old feels like. Surrounded by a hundred closed doors, he couldn't help but laugh at the ultimate irony of his existence.

Aeternus had finally grown frail, and he died right there, seated on the steps. If all doors led to the same place anyway, he should've just picked the first.
