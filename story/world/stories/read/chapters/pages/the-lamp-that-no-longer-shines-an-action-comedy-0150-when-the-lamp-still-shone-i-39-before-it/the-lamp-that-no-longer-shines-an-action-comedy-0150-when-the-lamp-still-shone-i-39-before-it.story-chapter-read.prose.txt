[Time]: Year 1921 · The Day of the Conversion Ritual[Location]: The Palace of Rex Mundi · The Proving Grounds

The culmination of the ritual did not arrive with light. It arrived as sound.

A low, subsonic reverberation moved through the marrow of the bone: a deep frequency the body registered as wrongness long before the eye could find its source. Then the sky responded.

Above the Milan'thirskaya estate, the heavens reorganized themselves with the speed and violence of something deeply offended. Cloud cover was dragged, caught in a spiraling vortex that tore the stratosphere open and bled a necrotic, suffocating purple across the afternoon.

At the absolute center of the maelstrom, the Nether Spring forced itself through: a breach opened by sheer, uncompromising mathematical violence. Inside the breach, deep-frequency lightning rolled with the sluggish, terrifying density of liquid voltage.

Then, she descended.

Her wings beat, and thunder answered from a cloudless sky. Her descent into the afternoon extinguished the sun.

She passed through the Nether Spring as if casually stepping off the far shore of eternity. To look at the dragon was to short-circuit the optic nerve and strike something older.

Her scales were a deep, abyssal indigo, almost black: the exact, fleeting color of the sky in the final microscopic second before twilight surrenders entirely to night.

Running through every scale were jagged veins of violet lightning, resembling the crazed glaze of antique porcelain, pulsing as if barely sealing in some catastrophic, world-ending pressure.

But it was the wings. The membranes were impossibly transparent: as the necrotic light of the Nether Spring struck her from behind, looking through them revealed a compressed void. A darkness that preceded light.

Her scale made the Palace of Rex Mundi look like a child's porcelain dollhouse, her shadow swallowing the mountains.

The sensation of looking at her was identical to the promised rapture of the end of the world: a primal, paralyzing terror inextricably braided with a weeping, rapturous joy. It was too much for a nervous system. The body processed it as pure voltage.

On the observation terrace, the Milan'thirskaya family stood paralyzed.

Ash's facial muscles had simply ceased to function. Every conceivable emotion: terror, awe, jealousy, relief, struck her processing core simultaneously. The intersecting signals canceled each other out exactly, leaving behind a blank, hollowed canvas. She stared at the sky.

Beside her, small Liandra had pressed herself into Ash's side. She was shivering violently, her small fingers white-knuckled in Ash's coat. Her pupils were blown to the edge of the iris: too terrified to look away, too mesmerized to close her eyes.

Tasia and Alucard stood shoulder to shoulder at the railing, both of their heads tilted upward into the howling displaced air.

Alucard's hand had found Tasia's in the blind, animal instinct of the apocalypse. The grip was so agonizingly tight that her manicured nails had broken the skin of Tasia's palm, tracing thin crescent moons of blood.

Tasia did not flinch. She did not feel the pain. Her eyes were locked on the sky and there was nothing else in the world.

Behind them stood the architects of this moment.

In Francesca's eyes: the burning, avid focus of a researcher watching an impossible hypothesis walk through the door.

Beneath that was the heavy, measuring awe of the Kings of the World, recognizing a sovereign that had just eclipsed them. And beneath even that, something infinitely more complicated: a strange, misplaced warmth. A phantom maternal pride, soft and swelling, the particular satisfaction of watching a child finally come magnificently into their own.

But Ovelia was not their child.

Their actual child was standing inches away, forgotten in the shadow of the dragon. Angie's gaze had settled, quietly and without ceremony, on Ash.

She did not look away from Ash.

The bleeding vortex began to knit itself shut.

The colossal dragon banked in three sweeping, imperious circles above the estate, each wingbeat summoning thunder from a sky that had no storm left to give, before the monumental architecture began to fold inward.

The light compressed, shedding mass, shrinking rapidly, until a pair of boots landed lightly on the proving grounds' flagstones.

Ovelia stood in the center of the cratered stone.

Her human silhouette had returned, but the conversion had left its permanent marks. Horns of abyssal indigo curved back from her temples, threaded with the same violet lightning that ran through her scales.

Pressed flat against her back were the translucent wing membranes, the void within them compressed to near-stillness. And trailing behind her, entirely unimpressed by the concept of cooperation, was the tail.

The tail dragged across the paving stones, and the flagstones it touched simply went dark: not scorched, not shattered, but quietly extinguished, as if the concept of their continued existence had been cancelled without notice.

She frowned at it.On the terrace, the paralysis broke.

Tasia drew a slow, stuttering breath. She hasn't changed at all, she thought. Something bloomed in her chest, warm and disarming. Watching this cosmic disaster lose a battle with her own world-ending appendages was, inexplicably, so endearing it actually ached.

Ash finally blinked."You can retract the tail," Ash said. Her voice was flat and dry.

The tail froze mid-swing. Ovelia's head whipped around. She fixed Ash with a glare of profoundly unearned irritation, and lifted her chin until the new horns caught the light.

"I succeeded," Ovelia declared. The two words carried the weight of a conquered universe.Ash looked at her. "Oh."

Francesca stepped forward, her heels clicking against the cracked stone. Her eyes moved over the horns and wings and the sheer density of mana pooling at Ovelia's feet with the avid, burning precision of a researcher cataloguing an impossible result.

"Congratulations, Ovelia," Francesca said. "Have you given any thought to what you intend to do next?"

Tasia leaned forward against the balcony railing, her heart hammering.Ovelia brushed a speck of dust from her sleeve. "I am going traveling."

The entire estate went dead silent.Tasia's mind went completely still. She replayed the sentence twice, confirmed it had not changed, and still could not produce a coherent response.

Alucard pointed a trembling finger at the first Apocalypse Dragon Witch in recorded history. "To where?!"

Ovelia shot her a look of withering impatience, the specific expression of a cat being required to explain its personal schedule.

"I suppose I will know when I arrive," Ovelia said, with absolute, infuriating self-righteousness.

She left. And the absence stretched from months into years.

The only empirical proof that Ovelia still retained a passing familiarity with basic social protocol was the erratic, sourceless arrival of letters: appearing on desks, on windowsills, once directly in Tasia's hands in the middle of a corridor, bearing no postmark, no return address, and no discernible delivery mechanism.

They came from wherever Ovelia was, which remained a location she saw no reason to specify.

Because she found separate correspondence too exhausting, she never wrote individual letters.

A single, sprawling stream-of-consciousness scroll would arrive every few months, addressed to the Palace of Rex Mundi in general, reading like a running lecture delivered at speed: thousands of words cataloguing tectonic shifts and fallen civilizations and complaints about local rainfall, all crammed onto one page because picking up the pen twice was an administrative redundancy she refused to endorse.

But the gifts that accompanied the omnibus scrolls told a different story entirely.

They were meticulously, obsessively separated. The packaging was a masterclass in logistical care: an abyssal grimoire sealed in triple-layered, lead-lined [Abjuration] glass so its spiritual radiation would not compromise the delicate silk fan in the adjacent compartment.

Volatile alchemical reagents suspended in temporal-stasis gels. A polished scrying mirror wrapped in silver-backed velvet, carefully isolated from the cursed scrolls that would otherwise infect it.

It was a strange, jarring thing to register: the apex of the universe is in an unmapped jungle somewhere, actively worrying about whether our souvenirs will cross-contaminate in transit. Like a feral cat vanishing into the wilderness for a year, only to drop an immaculate, exceedingly rare dead bird on the center of your pillow.

The family sent replies. Consistently, persistently, diplomatically. They extended the invitation to return, careful not to make it sound like a demand. They waited.

And finally, inexplicably, the suggestion was accepted.

[Time]: Year 1936 · Fifteen Years Later[Location]: The Palace of Rex Mundi · Dining Hall

Tasia was twenty-five years old.She was a witch come into her own now, no longer the quiet child on the terrace.

It was during a standard family dinner.The wind chimes in the entryway sang their chaotic, ringing welcome.

The heavy doors opened, and she walked in.Tasia looked up from her plate.

The dragon was gone.

In her place walked a Cat Witch: a pair of large, velvet-furred feline ears perched atop her dark hair, and trailing behind her, swaying with a lazy, independent buoyancy, a single tail of impossible, voluminous fluff.

It was so massive, so profoundly cloud-like, it looked far more like the tail of a mythic fox than any cat.

And yet, looking at her, it made perfect, unassailable sense. This unapologetic, feline arrogance fit her soul in a way the cold armor of scales never had.

Ovelia walked to the table, pulled out an empty chair, sat down with the casual ease of someone who had never left the room, and arranged her nine tails around her with unhurried entitlement.

Across the table, Ash's hand gave a single, microscopic twitch.

Liandra, now sixteen years old, sat frozen. Her eyes were fixed on the woman across the porcelain plates.

"Ovelia," Liandra said, testing the weight of the syllables.

Ovelia let out a low, musical laugh. The dark eyes literally sparkled, pinpricks of actual starlight manifesting in the irises. She leaned forward, resting her chin in her hand, her voice dropping to a teasing, velvet purr.

"Ah, Liandra. I used to hold you when you were nothing but a squalling infant. Have you forgotten me already?"

A violent, catastrophic red flooded Liandra's face. She dropped her gaze to her lap, her fingers twisting in her napkin, before forcing her chin back up with the stubborn dignity of someone who would rather suffer than appear flustered.

"I remember you perfectly," Liandra said.Two seats down, Alucard shifted in her chair, the friction of her coat loud in the quiet room.

Ovelia caught the movement. The corners of her mouth curved into a lazy, deliberate smile.

She turned, bringing the full, unhurried weight of her attention to bear on Alucard, her tone settling into something of deliberate, manufactured innocence.

"Did you enjoy the gifts I sent you?" Ovelia asked.

Alucard's face ignited. She crossed her arms, radiating a chaotic mixture of intense shyness and defensive indignation, and addressed a candelabra with great intensity.

"Who even remembers what you sent," Alucard muttered.

Then, Ovelia turned.

She looked directly at Tasia. The teasing warmth evaporated from her expression, replaced by the sharp, focused attention of a peer: a gear-shift, sudden and absolute.

"Your theoretical frameworks on spatial folding technology," Ovelia said, her voice settling into genuine, assessing weight. "They are highly intriguing."

Tasia stopped breathing. She set her silver fork on the porcelain plate with a soft clink."The anchoring parameters are—" Tasia began.

"Indeed!" Francesca's voice sliced cleanly across the table.

The matriarch leaned forward, eyes bright with the immediate, consuming fire of a senior academic seizing upon a worthy debate. "The localized gravity inversion in the third chapter—if you apply that drag coefficient to a multi-dimensional fold, the mana degradation alone—"

Ovelia's attention pivoted to the matriarch. The two of them instantly spiraled into a dense, high-speed exchange of theoretical arcane physics, generating a conversational density that left no oxygen in the room for anyone else.

Beside Francesca, Angie sat quietly.

She read the microscopic tremor of raw, suppressed excitement in Francesca's usually impenetrable posture, and the unbridgeable gap it created across the table, and wordlessly reached out to refill Ovelia's teacup.

Tasia sat in silence.

She left the fork where it was and watched Ovelia speak, watched the nine fluffy tails sway behind the chair, watched the starlight catch in those dark eyes. The conversation moved around her like a river around a stone.

The dinner concluded. The chairs scraped back.On Tasia's side of the table, her plate had grown cold, untouched.

The heavy oak door of her bedroom clicked shut.Tasia walked to her writing desk.

Sitting on the polished wood, exactly where it had sat for seventeen years, was the sealed crystal case.

Inside the vacuum-sealed glass, the dark rose rested.

A single blood droplet hung motionless in the air above the petals, caught in the perpetual, arrested act of falling, frozen in the precise instant before it would have touched the bloom. Not a fraction of a millimeter of descent. Not a single degree of warmth had penetrated the stasis.

Down the hall, the wind chimes sang softly as someone opened a window. The gravity had returned.

Tasia stood in the dark, looking at the suspended drop, and for a very long time, she could not name what she was doing.
