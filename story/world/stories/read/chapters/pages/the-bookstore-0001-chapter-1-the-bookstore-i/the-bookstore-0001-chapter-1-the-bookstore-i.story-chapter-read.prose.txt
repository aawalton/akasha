“You call that a door?”

Anne said it with open scorn, choosing not to point out the more obvious flaws yet. If she wanted the price to fall to something she could actually pay, it would not do to attack everything at once. Better to sound unimpressed and let the old dwarf work for it.

She stood with the composed bearing typical of the northeast, tall and straight in a modest black overall that suited her serious expression. At her neck hung the only thing that truly looked out of place—a blue cat talisman, polished enough to catch the light even in this neglected stretch of road.

The town asset manager only smiled.

“The location may look remote,” Dorgh said, already fishing a rusted key from his pocket, “but it is actually good. This is the only road to the forbidden forest. Any adventurer going that way has to pass here. For business, that is no small advantage.”

He spoke with practiced ease, his tone calm, almost reassuring, but his eyes never left her. He had the look of a man used to measuring buyers while pretending not to. Anne disliked that kind of competence in a seller. It made things expensive.

The place was already better than the report had suggested.

From outside, she had expected some decaying shed, perhaps a broad warehouse with rotting beams and a leaking roof. Instead, the building was plain but solid, much larger than she would have liked, with at least three levels and enough breadth to swallow her remaining savings whole. Even neglected, it had the shape of something built by a rich man. That certainly was a problem.

Still, she was not ready to lose.

Dorgh wrestled the key into the lock. Metal scraped. Wood groaned. The hinges answered with a shrill, miserable squeak.

Anne took the opening without hesitation.

“Well, I still prefer a small store in town,” she said, making her voice flat with disinterest. “It is not as though travelers heading for the forest would stop here for studying or research.”

While he fought the warped door and its swollen frame, she was already running through possible versions of the conversation ahead. If he says this, I answer that. If he lowers it to fifteen, I act offended. If he speaks of future value, I press current isolation.

The door finally gave way, dragging open with the stubbornness of something long abandoned.

Anne cut off her thoughts and stepped inside.

Only then did she properly understand the building.

The front room was a simple lounge with a counter and an open passage leading to an area with scattered tables. No mystery there. It had been built as an inn, and not a small one. Behind the counter stood two secured back doors that likely led to restricted rooms or storage areas, while a staircase between the counter and the table space climbed upward toward the guest floors.

The air inside smelled of dust, old wood, and the faint trapped odor of a place that had once held food, drink, and far too many strangers.

Anne began immediately.

“This stain will need work. That beam should be checked. These planks are warped. There is dust everywhere. Were those curtains always this worn?”

She did not even try to be subtle about it. If she found a flaw, she named it. If she found something merely inconvenient, she named that too.

Dorgh, sensing the danger, hurried her toward the larger room.

“Although the tables count as furniture and are not included as part of the building,” he said, adopting the grand tone of an official explaining a favor, “they have been left as a gift to the new owner. As you can see, they are practically untouched. Only a bit of dust. This is the largest room, spacious enough for quite a few guests, and it has a separate entrance in case you ever want to use it independently. There is also a large room behind the counter where the kitchen used to be.”

He crossed the room as he spoke, trying to carry the pace of the tour with him.

Unfortunately for him, Anne did not mind being left behind.

She stayed where she was and looked properly.

The room had possibilities. That could turn expensive.

Even under neglect, the space was broad enough to host a serious business. The tables, though dusty, were sturdy. The walls were plain, but plain could be repaired. The room behind the counter was likely large enough for storage, perhaps even work. And the separate entrance... that could become useful.

Very useful.

Which meant she had to dislike it judiciously.

Dorgh turned back, saw her still inspecting the main room, and sighed inwardly. Rushing her would not work. He changed tactics.

“Miss Anne, what do you think about serving food here?” he asked, sounding almost casually curious. “You said you intended to open a magic store, did you not?”

That touched a sore point.

“Perhaps tea,” Anne said. “Maybe some light bakery. No meals.” She let out a faint, annoyed breath. “And... actually, I am opening a bookstore.”

The dwarf blinked.

“Oh. A store for reselling magic scrolls.” He tried to sound respectful, and almost succeeded. “That is very... specific.”

The mocking look he failed to hide was enough.

By the Gods, did these people truly think books meant scroll bundles and magitech scraps?

“I would never help those corrupt magitech empires earn a single coin,” Anne said, her pride rising before she could fully restrain it. “No, sir. I am opening a real bookstore.”

That landed.

Dorgh’s brows pulled together. Surprise first, then confusion, then something like recollection.

“Pardon the question, miss, but... are you a traditionalist?”

The word scraped.

He did not seem insulting on purpose, but Anne disliked it anyway. Traditionalist sounded like a relic displayed for history lessons. Something quaint. Something already defeated.

“You could say that,” she replied. “I come from an ancestral line of archivists, mostly known as bookmancers.”

His expression went blank in the way people’s expressions often did when they had never heard the term before and did not want to admit it too quickly.

“I am not surprised by your confusion,” Anne continued. “Even I only know of a few others, and I may well be the last one still practicing it in this country. I will spare you the long history. Once, we were respected—more than mere scholars, in some places. Then magitech rose, and suddenly people like me became backward crafters.”

She had meant to say it more lightly. Instead, the last words came out carrying the old bitterness beneath them.

Her grandfather’s lessons. Her years of preparation. The books gathered one by one at absurd cost. The patient effort of building a foundation for a craft the world had already chosen to forget.

Dorgh surprised her by looking interested rather than sympathetic.

“Is that so?” he said. “Then perhaps Mountainend is exactly where such an enterprise could take root. A large, affordable building like this could be a great advantage.”

There it was again.

Anne almost winced at herself for letting the conversation shift. Dorgh was good. Better than she had hoped, which meant more dangerous than she liked.

“I do not think so,” she said, schooling her tone back into disappointment. “The town looks pleasant enough, but this place is too isolated, and the size is a burden. How am I supposed to manage it alone? And I cannot imagine enough customers passing through to let me hire help.”

That, at least, was close enough to the truth to sound convincing.

Mountainend’s situation was pitiable. Ten years ago, when the Impassable Wall began showing signs of change, the borderlands had drawn the attention of the entire continent. The mayor and local merchants had gambled hard—roads, renovations, investments, hopes piled upon hopes. Mountainend had been close enough to dream of becoming important.

Instead, Riverend, two hundred kilometers south, devoured the future meant for them.

Some adventurers had come here at first, but most had not stayed. While the rest had drifted away after the first taste of easier profit elsewhere.

Dreams, Anne reflected, were expensive things to build roads upon.

“Well,” Dorgh said, waving lightly, “let us not rush to conclusions. Look here—the oven has barely been used. And this door leads back toward the entrance, while the other gives access to the restricted areas. Let me show you the stables before we go to the second floor.”

They continued through the building.

He pointed out facilities with the determination of a man trying to sell value through momentum. Anne countered by finding every flaw she could name without sounding absurd. Broken corners, worn boards, bad placement, wasted space, drafts, dust, likely repair costs. The exchange went on long enough that the rhythm itself became part of the negotiation.

By the time they entered the former staff room area on the first floor, Dorgh’s confidence had developed a small crack.

Anne noticed it immediately.

He hesitated before one sealed door.

“Oh,” he said, too casually, “there is also a rather wonderful natural event that might add some distinction to your business. This room was originally intended as a staff chamber, but an unexpected spatial rift appeared within it. Nothing dangerous, I assure you, so long as it is left untouched. And surely a place this size can spare one room, yes? Let me show you.”

He opened the door with wary care, presenting trouble as novelty.

Inside, the room was dominated by a tear of colorless shifting energy hanging in the air like a wound that had forgotten to bleed.

Even prepared for it, Anne felt a pull deep inside her chest.

A stable rift.

She had spent too much money, crossed too much distance, and gambled too much hope for this moment to let a single flicker of excitement show on her face.

So she frowned instead.

“Marvelous is certainly one word for it,” she said. “Troublesome would be another. It is one more thing I would have to spend money on if curious customers decide to get themselves killed. You say it is harmless untouched, but if someone plays with it and gets injured, would I be held accountable?”

Dorgh looked relieved that fear had not immediately ended the visit, but still tense enough to know he had not escaped the problem.

The tour continued for more than an hour.

He worked to control her impressions. She worked to damage them. He found clever answers to most objections. She forced new ones. For every practical flaw she raised, he found two possible uses. For every virtue he presented, she answered with cost, work, or risk.

Yet the central issue never changed.

Any business here depended on Mountainend becoming something it was not.

At last, Anne angled herself toward the path.

“Let us finish this,” she said. “It is already past noon, and we still need to return to town if I am to see the other options.”

That finally shook him.

For the first time, Dorgh looked genuinely nervous. He dabbed his forehead with a pocket kerchief before answering.

“The place is somewhat far from town, yes, but consider what this amount of space offers. Even if you do not need it now, you might later expand, or rent rooms, or open another service. And... ahem... although the building was originally constructed by a wealthy merchant, it now belongs to the town. Given that your business could be considered an investment in Mountainend’s future, we may be able to discuss installments.”

Built by the wealthy. Investment. Future.

He emphasized each word like bait on separate hooks.

Anne lowered her eyes, let her shoulders soften, and spoke with contained sincerity.

“Mr. Dorgh, you seem like a decent man, so I will be honest. This is my last chance to save my ancestral craft. I have already spent too much on attempts that led nowhere. I only have a few gold coins left. If I take installments for a business this uncertain, I will already be failing before I even begin. I hoped Mountainend might be my opportunity, but...” She let the words falter. “At this point, I can only go to Riverend and try to survive the year with a simple stall. I am sorry to have taken your whole morning and forced the town to spend on a coach. Here. It is not much, but please accept it as thanks.”

She took a silver coin from her pouch.

Not the full one, of course. The deflated one. The one that looked tragically near empty.

And held it out with embarrassed reluctance.

It was not entirely an act. If this failed, she truly would be standing at the edge of ruin. But there were times when a truth became more useful if properly worded.

Dorgh stared at the coin, then at her face.

“Miss,” he said at last, “how could I accept that without doing my work properly? This place is worth a great deal. It is no exaggeration to say the original owner spent at least thirty gold on construction alone. Unfortunately, his timing was poor. It may still take a few years before this town becomes the bright point it was meant to be. But one day it will. Mountainend will become the pearl of the province.”

He seemed to be reassuring himself as much as her.

Then he inhaled, hesitated, and made his decision.

“Perhaps you may help that day arrive sooner. So, as a servant of the town, and in the hope that your business helps Mountainend grow, I can offer a better price.” He grimaced slightly, as though each word cost him commission. “Twelve gold coins. That is already half the evaluation value. I truly cannot go any lower.”

Inside, Anne’s pulse jumped.

Outside, she only let her mouth tighten with pained temptation.

Twelve.

Still too high to accept immediately.

But close enough to make the next step possible.

And now the real bargaining could begin.
