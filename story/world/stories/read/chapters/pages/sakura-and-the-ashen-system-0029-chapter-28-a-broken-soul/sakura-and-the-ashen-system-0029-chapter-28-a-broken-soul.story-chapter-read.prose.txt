Faerd no longer remembered how long the chains had held his body in that cold dungeon. He did not remember when exactly he had ended up there or what had come before it. Sometimes it began to seem to him that no life had ever existed beyond the pain that had become his only reality. And the moment he tried to look deeper, to remember who he was and what was hidden in his past, a monstrous, searing pain pierced him at once, as if lava were pouring through his veins and slowly burning him alive from the inside.

The torture here was rarely the same. Sometimes they simply left him hanging in chains so long that his hands went numb, his shoulders cramped, and his own body began to feel foreign to him. In moments like that, he did not feel sharp pain right away. The real agony was in how it built slowly, spreading through his joints, digging into his back and neck until it became the only thing he could think about at all.

Sometimes people in white came to him. They spoke to him calmly, like he was a sick man who needed help, not a prisoner they were breaking day after day. One held metal instruments, while another wrote something down in a thick book. The third asked questions Faerd did not know the answer to.

“Who are you?”

“I don’t know...” Faerd answered hoarsely.

“Who do you belong to?”

“I don’t know.”

“What do you remember?”

“I remember... the sky.”

He tried to answer honestly, exactly the way they wanted him to. But it almost always ended the same way. The moment any memory from the past came too close, the pain flared inside him again, deeper than a knife.

One day they pushed something thin and cold under his skin, and after that he shook so violently that the chains rang. The world blurred, and the voices around him stretched and turned thick, as if they were speaking from underwater. Then he saw pale hair and a woman’s hands. A child standing in a doorway. Something warm and full of sunlight, something that his mind tied tightly to the word “home.”

Those memories came alive inside him so vividly that he wanted to seize them, but they sank back into his subconscious too quickly, as if they had never been there at all.

That was exactly what they had been waiting for.

“Good,” someone said calmly. “He needs to forget everything. He needs to remember only this place and recognize only our orders.”

Faerd had no idea how many times he lost consciousness and then was drenched with water to drag him awake and start again. It was always torture, like being pulled out from under an ice-cold waterfall that beat him with its own current. Faerd could not count how many times he fell into that waterfall, but he remembered his exhaustion very well. An exhaustion so deep it felt older than the world itself.

But there was one thing they had never managed to take from him. A dream. It came back again and again, refusing to go out even under layers of terrible pain. It seemed to Faerd that he saw that dream every single time he blacked out.

And it always began with summer sunlight. The light fell across the grass in soft patches, slid over a wooden fence and an old well by the road. In that dream, there was always a farm. It was so simple and ordinary that it felt impossible to connect it to his real life. There was the smell of fresh grass, the sound of wind, the rustling of leaves, and the ordinary sounds of a farm, cows and chickens and all the rest.

Faerd could not see himself from the outside, but he felt that in that dream he was a different man. It did not carry the prisoner’s familiar heaviness. There was no constant expectation of pain. There were no chains he had come to hate with all his heart. There, he could breathe freely.

And she was there. A woman stepped out of the house onto the porch, holding the door with one hand while carrying a child in the other. It seemed to him that nothing more beautiful existed anywhere in the world, and looking at her, Faerd truly wanted to smile.

He remembered her name: Maev.

In her arms was a little girl. Very small, with fine pale hair and round cheeks. She babbled something and reached her fingers toward the light.

In that dream, Faerd always walked toward them, or rather, ran. The girl saw him and reached toward him too. Faerd wanted to hold them both, but he never had time. The dream always broke at that exact moment.

And then the pain came back again.

At first, Faerd did not understand what they wanted to do to him. They took him out of his cell and into another room. It was no less cold, but it no longer looked like a damp, moldy dungeon. It smelled of metal, powders, something burned, and something too clean. The walls were covered with formulas, rune-circle diagrams, and calculations whose meaning Faerd did not understand. Long tables held tools, crystals, thin needles, and pieces of obsidian, all of them worked so carefully they looked almost perfect.

On the floor, a black circle spread out in many layers, one laid over another in complex patterns. Later, from the conversations of the people in white, Faerd learned that the outer ring of that circle was for containment, the inner one for stabilization, and between them were thin linking runes meant to keep his body from tearing apart during the first minutes of the implantation.

“He has a high pain threshold,” one of the scholars said. “And strong residual magical conductivity. He’s the only one who survived the earlier stages.”

“Then there’s a chance.”

The word “chance” made Faerd sick, because those people spoke about him not as a man, but as some senseless vessel or a stone they could do anything they wanted with.

They laid him inside the circle and strapped him down. Then they smeared a cold, viscous substance over his chest, neck, and arms. It smelled of iron and herbs. The needles came next, and there were dozens of them. In the moment, it almost seemed to him there might be a hundred, but he dismissed the thought. Through those needles they pushed a solution into his body, thick with obsidian dust and some alchemical conductor. The solution was icy only for the first instant. Then it was as if a volcano woke up inside him.

Faerd screamed.

The heat spread through his vessels, his nerves, his bones. It was like neither poison nor ordinary magic. It truly felt like lava pouring through him from the inside. It was a foreign element that did not recognize flesh, did not respect the boundaries of the body, and wanted either to bend it to itself or split it apart from within.

The outer runes flared. The circle beneath him began to hum, and the air turned heavy and charged. One of the mages started monitoring his readings: temperature, pulse, breathing, and the rest. Another watched the veins rising under his skin. At some point, Faerd suddenly understood why they were watching them. Because they were darkening. Their color was not naturally blue or even red. They were turning black, as if liquid obsidian were beginning to flow through them instead of blood.

“He’s holding,” someone said.

The resonance came almost immediately after those words. Faerd felt something inside him respond to the obsidian. It was true bodily coercion pushed to such an extreme that the body finally chose not to resist, but to adapt, just so the pain would stop being endless. Faerd could feel something alien already taking root inside him. It was like someone had sewn a sixth finger onto him and then forced his body to accept it and use it just like the others. Only this hurt beyond reason.

Only this hurt beyond reason.

And in the exact moment when Faerd thought his body would finally give out and collapse from the inside, a pale system window flashed before his eyes. It appeared so suddenly and so cleanly that, against the pain, it almost seemed unreal.

OBSIDIAN INTEGRATION INITIATED.EXTERNAL INTERVENTION CONFIRMED.HOST RESISTANCE: CRITICAL.FORCED ADAPTATION IN PROGRESS.

VESSEL COMPATIBILITY: SUFFICIENT.MENTAL INTEGRITY: UNSTABLE.PAIN RESPONSE: EXCEEDED SAFE LIMITS.

OBSIDIAN CHANNELS FORMED.SKILL PATH PARTIALLY OPENED.

Faerd could barely think now. The half-understood words trembled before his eyes, broke apart into pale lines, and gathered again into strict, merciless rows. The System did not explain what they were doing to him. It only recorded the result of the experiment in words he did not understand.

Then one more line appeared:

NEW ACTIVE SKILL ACQUIRED:— OBSIDIAN CONSTRUCT

Faerd choked and arched against the straps so hard that one of the holding circles flashed brighter. Something inside him had opened.

The first thing they made him create was a small, nearly useless shard.

They ordered Faerd to stretch out his hand and direct his will into the dark stain on the table. He truly did not understand how to do that until one of the overseers dug fingers into the back of his neck and hissed that obsidian was no longer an external material, but an extension of his magical channel. Then Faerd tried several times, and the air in front of his palm cracked, and his first black spike crawled out of empty space.

The people in white were pleased, but of course it did not end there. If anything, the worst part was only beginning. The people in white set up endless tests for him, and not on simple targets, but on other mages.

They brought them into the next chamber one by one. Every single one of them was filthy, exhausted, and looked not much better than Faerd himself. Some looked like they were about to die where they stood. They did not always explain why those mages had been brought in. Sometimes they told them it was only to test their potential. Other times they threw them into the room without a word.

Faerd was always ordered to attack them. At first with shards he made from his own mana, and later with daggers. Obsidian daggers were more difficult. They required a more stable matrix: a dense core, a sharp edge, and only a short retention of form in the air. Faerd learned quickly, because every failure was punished, while his successes were simply ignored.

After that they forced Faerd to create spears. Spears required more mass and more control. They were harder to hold together, but they punched through defenses with almost no loss. In that form, obsidian behaved strangely: not just like a projectile, but like a spell with its own inertia, drawn toward a magical source. The people in white called it “vector aggression of the material,” though Faerd did not fully understand what that meant.

And finally, spheres. The most destructive and unstable thing in his arsenal. To make an obsidian sphere, it was not enough just to gather mass. It had to be held under compression and kept from bursting into a swarm of fragments too early. A charge like that took in far too much energy at once and always hit with excessive destructive force.

The people in white were more than satisfied with that. They recorded everything.

How many seconds the formation took. How quickly the caster was drained. How many targets were struck in one discharge. How much flesh a dagger tore through. How deep a spear entered. At what distance a sphere turned a wall into rubble.

Faerd remembered the faces of the people they tested his magic on more poorly than he remembered his own pain. But he remembered the feeling. Every time obsidian burst out of him, something inside him felt emptier. As if with every blade, every spear, every sphere, something else was being pulled out of him too, something that had nothing to do with magic.

They did not want merely a strong mage.

They wanted a perfectly obedient obsidian bearer, a living weapon, intelligent enough to understand an order, and broken enough to want nothing else.
