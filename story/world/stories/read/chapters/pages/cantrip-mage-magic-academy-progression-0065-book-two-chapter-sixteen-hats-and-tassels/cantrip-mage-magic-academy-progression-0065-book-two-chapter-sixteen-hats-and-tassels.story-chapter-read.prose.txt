~Colin Tremory~

Colin Tremory understood the test well enough to be nervous.

Five spells in succession. He’d picked his well. First, a Mana ritual. Soul Sponge. It’d slowly trickle Mana into his soul from the aggregate around him. The professors would seal him away from the library’s vast reserves, forcing him to rely on a normal amount of ambient Mana, and Instructor Tarik had pointed out the ritual not because it was commonly used, but because it almost never was—especially not for the exam. If he ever stopped casting to recover, he’d fail the exam.

For most students, Soul Sponge would be far too slow to help, and it was too expensive for them to bother. But for Colin? He’d done the math. With six bars of Mana and careful sketching—the only kind he could do—the ritual would restore enough Mana to allow him to slide in under the five-spell line.

From there, he’d picked the four cheapest First Order spells he could find, regardless of functionality. Simple was effective in this case.

The badge and the hat awaited him. So did the ritual Instructor Tarik had promised if he could just pass the trial. He’d even get the right to work in the hospital without another student looking over his shoulder all the time, and be able to enroll in Instructor Clearance’s advanced medical magic courses. But more importantly than any of that, Colin had been working toward this for a long time.

His parents had spent a lot of money to get him into Varin’s Academy. He needed to pay off the debt he owed them, and he would. Siege magic. Healing and support rituals. He’d be a great asset to a caravan when he graduated. This was just one step on that path.

“Are you ready, Mr. Tremory?” Professor d’Leonide asked. The big man’s voice was surprisingly soft, even in class, and it took Colin a moment to parse what he’d said.

He nodded.

He needed to succeed, not only for himself, his parents, or the professor who’d helped him come up with his strategy, but for his friends. For Eola.

Instructor Tarik had taken him to his office, cast a Second Order ritual over the whole room, and quickly laid out exactly what was at stake. The political winds in Alemia were shifting, he’d said, and that was going to be a problem for any student with a ‘broken’ attunement. Something was wrong with magic. It would only get worse. There was every chance that the solutions Eola and the others had discovered would draw the eyes of the Cult of Y’aer sooner rather than later.

But the ritual Tarik had cast would hide all of it from prying eyes. And Tarik would teach it to Colin—just as soon as Colin proved he could learn it.

So, Colin walked on legs of jelly down to the gigantic ritual circle in front of the empty Great Lecture Hall, stood in front of the three professors, and began casting.

Atta stared balefully at Eola as she worked through yet another intersection in the Emerald Cursive section of Varin’s Notebook.

The principles of magic don’t function. Not the way the Cult of Y’aer says they do. They’re convinced of the Soul, and of Mana, but beyond that? They don’t care to [evaluate/understand/rationalize/salmon] any further.  Even the Great Mage, Mistress Frelanne, doesn’t care to [same words, possible different meaning] the [rational/inexplicable/bluegill/unknown].

“Why are all of your translations coming out so…fishy?” she asked.

Eola shrugged. “I have no idea. This language is ridiculous in every possible way. And no, it’s not because it’s fishy. It’s because these intersectional lines could change the meaning of everything Varin just wrote there, so the fish options might be right. It wouldn’t be the first time I thought I had a breakthrough, only to discover he was really writing about his evening meal. I’ll never understand how the elves use this language.”

“You might surprise yourself, silly girl,” Atta said, and Eola went back to her translation. If she could just follow these last few lines through a few dozen more intersections, she could start circling the ‘correct’ words and stop balling up copious amounts of parchment to throw in the rubbish bin.

So, over a meal of [trout/redfruit/rational/evaluation], I—

“Oh, for Y’aer’s sake!”

And so the translation went. Compared to the Old Alemic sections—which mostly just sounded archaic—her translation of Varin’s Emerald Cursive was proving to be a miserable process. It wasn’t just that Emerald Cursive was the worst language in all of Alemia. It wasn’t even that Varin was exceptionally vague about whether or not it was dinner or the secrets of the universe he was writing about.

Varin, as a young man, might’ve been interested in secret codes, cryptology, and languages—but he hadn’t been very good at them. The grammatical errors alone were enough to drive Eola to tears.

In a way, it was a blessing when someone started shouting outside.

Eola shrugged on her cloak—after all, Patrice had threatened them all with either celebratory or consolation drinks in Varin’s Town, pocketed her wand and spellbook, and opened the door even as Atta sprinted for the ladder, fur poofed out and eyes wide, then transformed into a figurine in her pocket. She poked her head around the door, then broke into a wide smile.

Patrice had Colin’s hands in hers, and they were both jumping up and down. He’d screwed his eyes shut while she made a wildly piercing screeching noise that was interrupted slightly every time she hit the floor. Colin’s hat—a pointed one that added almost a foot to his height and was wrapped in blue and white cloth reminiscent of the banners in the Great Lecture Hall—almost hit the ceiling with every bounce. And on his chest, hanging from his badge, was a single silver tassel.

Patrice let go of his hands and pulled him into a massive bear hug, still bouncing up and down. Eola simply waited. It was better to let the redhead work it out of her system, anyway, and Colin…

Colin was a Journeymage now.

“Congratulations,” Roth said. His scowl had lifted almost entirely; he looked almost handsome when he wasn’t angry. “So, the ritual? Did he give you the ritual?”

“Yes, he did. It’s—“

“Not now, boys!” Patrice said. She let go of Colin and whirled to face Roth, robes spinning like skirts. “Eola’s ready. Get yourself dressed for the outdoors. We’re going to Varin’s Town—and not to the Chubby Saber. Not this time!”

Colin collapsed onto the couch. “Patrice, we still have classes, and I like the Chubby Saber. Ursula’s cooking is—“

“The best in town. But we’re not looking for cooking. And no. Today, none of us have classes.” Patrice’s grin split her face. “Your homework from Maestro Professor-Instructor Patrice Clerk is to celebrate.”

“Fine. But you’re buying,” Roth said as he disappeared into his room, the ghost of a smile showing in the second before he vanished.

Eola sat down beside Colin, close enough that her knee hit his. Then she reached over and pinched the tassel between her fingers, looking at it closely. After a few moments, she let go, laughing softly.

“What’s so funny?” Colin asked.

“Nothing. Only, it’s Old Alemic and Forsooth Script symbols. They’ve been twisted and bent, but they’re still there. It says ‘Journeymage’ on it in both languages. Actually, in Old Alemic, it says ‘Magus of the Path,’ and in Forsooth Script, it says ‘Footwizard.’ But Journeymage would be the closest translation in modern Alemic.”

He smiled. Exhaustion welled in his eyes, but even as they sat there, with Eola focused on the tassel and its linguistic history, he seemed to revitalize. Eventually, she caught his eye. “Um, congratulations, Colin.”

“Thanks. It’s a big step. But now I can start learning this Second Order ritual, and it’ll—“

“You two are talking business on a celebratory day,” Patrice said. “Stop it! Roth’s ready. Let’s go.”

And so they went.

As the three apprentices and the Journeymage stomped through the snow that had started clogging Varin’s Town’s streets, Eola was on high alert. She’d forgotten her sword—or more accurately, left it behind intentionally—but Headmaster Tomlish’s warning about fights between the academy’s students and visiting mages rang in her mind. Worse, Patrice led them not to one of the many steep-roofed, fire-lit taverns and inns that sprouted from Varin’s Town like weeds, but down a narrow alley and to a nondescript door. She knocked twice, then three more times, then twice again, and a small viewing port opened.

“We’re here to see the man about a flight of stairs,” Patrice said to the wrinkled, serious-looking set of eyes on the other side.

The port screeched closed, and the sound of locks unlatching filled the air.

“What is this?” Colin asked. He still wore his Journeymage hat and badge decoration, and he reached up to check it like he wasn’t sure it was still there.

“This,” Patrice said dramatically, “is a speakeasy. They serve real liquor here, not watered-down beer and overpriced wine—and even better, Bertrand the Dove-Voiced is here for two weeks. He’s a vocal-attuned bard.”

“Really?” Roth asked, eyes lighting up a little.

“Yes. Vocal and aural attunements are uncommon, but he’s found a way to make them work with Ordered Magic. And no, it’s nothing like what you do.”

The door opened, and Patrice led them down a narrow, rickety flight of stairs and into the depths of a basement. The smell of coal filled the air, along with something sharper and more cloying. The doorman stared at them the whole time they descended the rickety stairs and all the way until they arrived at a door. Then Patrice repeated the knock; it opened, and the sound hit them.

Bertrand the Dove-Voiced was a shockingly small man. If Eola hadn’t known better, she’d have sworn he had some gnome blood—but no. Gnomes and humans weren’t compatible. He was simply small, with long brown hair and an outfit designed from the puffed-up sleeves to the long, colorful ribbons hanging from his waist to catch the eye.

His magic was fascinating, though. Not a single spell used his voice. In that regard, her angry suitemate was already leaps and bounds ahead of the Truemage on the small corner stage. But unlike Roth, he’d managed to figure out how to use his attunement with Ordered Magic, and that combination produced some truly haunting sounds.

Not that Eola paid him any real attention. She sat next to Colin, her foot hitting his occasionally when they shifted and fidgeted. Patrice paid even less attention to the bard; she seemed to have taken on the role of hostess for their little impromptu party, right down to paying for as many drinks and as much food as the table wanted.

Roth, however, hadn’t broken eye contact with the bard since they’d walked in and found their table in the far corner. His first drink sat untouched as he tried to take in everything all at once.

“So, anyway,” Colin said a little too loudly, “the Journeymage exam’s pretty simple. Like everyone says, five spells, back-to-back. Then the proctors con…conf…talk to each other and decide if your method and technique were worthy of the rank. Tarik said that they never reject anyone from Journeymage. Truemage, though? Well, uh, they want to know your technique is good before they let you…um, ‘have free access to spells of that magnitude.’”

Eola took a sip of something surprisingly fruity that Patrice had set in front of her. “Right. I’m not worried about Truemage, though. How many bars do you have?”

“Only six, so I had to be clever. See, there are rules to the trial. You can’t stop casting, and they cut you off from the library dungeon’s Mana flow, so you only have ‘normal’ ambient Mana to work with. But Instructor Tarik showed me a ritual that works for me. See, I can’t cast fast.”

“You really can’t,” Patrice said.

“But I can cast slow. Soul Sponge filled two whole bars during the exam, I cast so slowly. And I never stopped. Professor d’Leonide almost exploded with rage, but even he had to admit that I passed by the letter of the rules. He might advocate setting a time limit on future exams, but I doubt it. It’s not just Varin’s Academy he’d have to change—it’d be all of Alemia. And changing how Ordered Magic works in all of Alemia…that’d be a tough task.”

Eola’s shoulder bumped his as he reached for his drink. “Sorry, Colin.”

“You’re fine. It’s tight in here.”

It was. The speakeasy, as Patrice called it, was small enough that only seven tables could be crammed into the room, with just enough space for Bertrand to sing on the tiny stage and for a bar along one wall. A fireplace on the far wall threw a shocking amount of warmth into the basement, and the sheer number of bodies packed in made it downright hot. It also forced Eola a lot closer to Colin than she’d intended to be. Not that she minded.

“Right. Um, Colin, we need to talk,” she said.

“Oh? What about?”

“About, um…” she trailed off as Patrice’s eyes locked on her, sparkling with mirth. “About the Inner Vision spell. I cast it on Patrice, and—“

“Celebrating, Eola, not talking school!” the redhead half-shouted.

“—and her soul doesn’t make sense to me. I was hoping you’d be willing to cast it on both of us, and maybe I could look at yours too. Something’s off, but I don’t have a good baseline of what things should look like.”

Patrice glared at her. Eola ignored her as Colin screwed his eyes shut and nodded. “I can. I’d be happy to. But if you really want a baseline, you might want to find someone else. Someone who’s got a very normal attunement—one that’s expected and has an attunement modifier, spells to cast, and so on. My partner couldn’t even get Inner Vision to work on me last term.”

“Huh.” Eola closed her mouth and thought about that as she took another drink.

And so the afternoon went, turning to evening as Bertrand sang and Patrice kept a steady but sustainable flow of fruity, spicy, and sour drinks flowing to the table. At first, Eola didn’t want to know how much she was spending on drinks and food. Later, she didn’t even stop to think about it.

Somehow, she ended up with Colin’s new hat perched lopsidedly on her head, half-covering her eyes. Roth had started singing along with Bertrand as the bard worked his way into more songs he knew, and Patrice occasionally joined them, along with several other patrons of the secret, basement bar. And, as so often happens, the rest of the day disappeared. By the time the four of them stumbled out into the evening and sobered up instantly from the bitter cold, the night was pitch black.

It took Eola three tries to summon a Bright Ball, which she used to guide them back to Varin’s Town’s main streets. From there, they staggered back to the dorm building, then through the suite door.

Eola’s eyes locked on the couch.

“Absolutely not,” she murmured.

“What?” Colin asked.

Then she wrapped her arms around his thin back, pulled him close, and kissed him. Not a quick peck to the forehead like she’d given him after the Library Exam. And much better than the awkward kiss he’d planted on her days ago. No, this was a real kiss, lips lingering and waiting.

The room went silent. Eola’s heart pounded in her chest. What if he didn’t respond like she knew he would?

He kissed her back, his arms wrapping around her, too. It was a beautiful moment. Absolutely everything Eola had been imagining. She pulled away. “Congratulations on Journeymage, Co—“

“That’s how you do it, Eola!” Patrice said, laughing and hooting.

“For Y’aer’s sake, that took four weeks too long,” Roth added.

Patrice shook her head. “Five. Maybe six.”

Eola blushed. So did Colin. Then she grabbed his hand—still wearing his hat on her head—and dragged him into her room, shut the door, and locked it behind her. Now that that was taken care of, the two of them had a lot to talk about—and she didn’t need Patrice and Roth’s comments and teasing while they had that conversation.
