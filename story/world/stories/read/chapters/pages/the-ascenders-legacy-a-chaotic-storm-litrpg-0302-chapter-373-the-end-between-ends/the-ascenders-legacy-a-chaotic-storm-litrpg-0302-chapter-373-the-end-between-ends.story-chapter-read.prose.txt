Aodhán had always known death would come quickly for him.

There would be no slow fading out, no ragged breaths, no lingering bleed-out. He had cheated death too many times for such luxuries.

His end had been instant. Swift. Painless.

One moment he stood in a world of color, and the next he floated in a vast oblivion of darkness.

There was no sight. No sound. Nothing.

He had simply reached the end.

He turned—or perhaps his consciousness turned—searching the vast oblivion for that light everyone always talked about. That distant glow said to greet the dead. That beacon believed to guide souls onward into the next cycle.

He found nothing.

Wouldn't he even get that much? Was he doomed to spend eternity in this darkness—awake, aware, yet... dead?

He turned again, searching the void around him, yet no release was in sight.

That was when he began to panic, his consciousness thrashing through the endless black as he became certain this was the realm mortals called hell.

If the purpose was torment, then it was succeeding magnificently.

His panic escalated, fear and confusion overwhelming his entire being until suddenly, the darkness around him warped and a room appeared before him—a room so familiar he could have described it while comatose.

And then a voice. “What have you gotten yourself into now, Little Seed?”

Relief blooming within him, Aodhán turned to see Az'marthon Ranok standing at the entrance of the room, a cup of steaming tea in hand.

It was such a relief to see someone else—even if they'd been plucked from his own mind—that he didn't immediately notice the changes in Az'marthon's appearance.

When he did, however, his expression sobered.

Az'marthon had changed drastically. Gone was his aura of power, and even his presence felt feeble, wispy, as though a stray current could blow him away. But it was his eyes that had changed the most.

Gone were his runic irises, and in their place were a pupilless swirl of white-gold.

The implication was obvious.

“You’re losing power,” Aodhán stated.

Az’marthon shrugged. “Every time I show up to keep you company, the power embedded in me is drained.” he smiled then. “Our meetings are a finite resource. You should cherish them.”

He walked into the room, his smile still in place. "What has it been? Two, three months since we last spoke?"

Aodhán looked down in grief. It seemed he was losing everyone today. But then again, he had lost himself too, so perhaps they were losing him.

"I don't know," he sniffed. "Two, maybe."

Az'marthon nodded. "Okay. How can I help you this time?"

Aodhán stared at the man, a thousand questions tumbling through his mind. But of them all, one was most urgent.

Inhaling deeply, he asked. “Am I dead?”

Az’marthon smiled, almost as though he’d expected the question.

“Yes and no,” he replied, his smile widening. And then, after a moment of confused silence, he gestured toward the stool by the bed.

"Why don't you sit? It seems there is much to talk about."

***

Meanwhile, as inconsequential as Aodhán's reversal of time had seemed, its echoes rippled violently across the battlefield—past fleeing Mythics, across blood-soaked hills and shattered skies, all the way to the previous battlefield where a certain double inheritor still raced desperately against time itself.

Formerly cultivating the Path of Relentless Velocity, Seamus had prided himself on his speed.

He had been the fastest of the Specials—faster even than Nymera herself, if one ignored the gulf in tier between them.

But that speed had failed him when it mattered most.

It had failed to bridge the gap between him and time itself.

In the past six days, trapped within that sliver of time, Seamus's path had slowly begun to shift. Each near-escape forced a change in his perception of the storm as speed. Each glitch of reality broke his mind a little further, shattering the walls that should have stopped his imagination from running too wild and his consciousness from straying too far.

Seamus himself cared nothing for the madness eroding his mind. He was too focused on gaining ground, on breaking through to see his family again, to see his friends, to live in the present once more.

And as that madness eroded the barriers within him—chaotic and wild—it brought with it unimaginable ideas, twisting his perception in impossible ways until his path became something else entirely: a path born from the realization that to close the gap, he would have to steal seconds from existence itself.

A path tainted by the genius of madness.

The Path of Stolen Seconds.

It was a broken path, barely useful for anything beyond theft, but Seamus had been ecstatic, stealing milliseconds from falling rain, from raging fires, from his own future stride, even from tears in reality itself. It was healing in a strange and violent way. With every second he pulled from a disaster—halting it mid-motion or reversing it outright—reality mended around the wound, frozen in stasis as causality itself was drained away.

Two days had passed since then, and in that time Seamus had stolen enough seconds to drag himself an entire minute and twenty seconds forward.

He was now only thirty seconds behind the present.

And he would have gladly continued his theft for another day when suddenly the ripple of Aodhán's time reversal reached him—a ripple drenched utterly in the concepts of storm and time.

A ripple born from the bloodline of an Ascendant.

It was packed with insights, and Seamus's broken mind soaked them up like a sponge. He saw what Aodhán had done—not through sight, but through understanding. The storm did not only move forward. Time did not only advance. Both could be bent by force, redirected by authority, compelled to yield.

Seamus laughed, wild-eyed and breathless, as revelation struck him.

He had been stealing seconds like a beggar snatching coins from the road. Milliseconds from rain. Moments from flame. Instants from collapsing stone. Useful thefts. Clever thefts.

Small thefts.

Why steal from scraps when the future itself existed ahead of him in endless abundance? Why claw seconds of motion from falling droplets when tomorrow held oceans of them?

The thought hit him so hard his path trembled, then cracked, then began to change once more in real time.

The stolen seconds gathered around him, no longer jagged fragments but smooth streams of possibility. Concepts aligned. Madness sharpened into genius, and what had once been theft became credit. What had once been scavenging became claim.

Seamus reached forward—not with his hands, but with the immense pool of willpower swirling within him—and touched the unseen horizon of moments yet to come.

Reality screamed.

Seconds tore free from the future in shining strands, racing backward into him. His body surged with impossible momentum. His wounds reversed before they could finish forming. His exhaustion vanished beneath energy that had not yet been born.

And as the old path shattered completely, a new one rose from its ruin.

One formed from the merger of three different paths.

The Path of Borrowed Future.

Seamus threw back his head and roared with laughter as thirty seconds became twenty.

Twenty became ten.

Ten became three.

And then—

Time collapsed as the gap between him and the present splintered like rotten wood beneath a hammer.

One step carried him across thirty seconds.

The next carried him through a battlefield he had not yet reached.

The third carried him through the fabric of space itself, tearing through cloud, war, and distance alike as he chased the source of the ripple like a hound on a blood-scent.

He found it kneeling.

Aodhán—wounded, broken, half-dead beneath the shadow of eight enemy Calamities.

One of them raised a hand, radiance swelling with murderous intent.

Had Seamus been in his right mind, he might have realized that standing against eight Calamities was not a wise decision.

But he was far beyond wisdom.

Without hesitation, he stepped between Aodhán and the gathered monsters, maddened fury blazing in his eyes as he raised both hands in defiance.

"Get away from my friend."

***

Meanwhile, back in the vision, Aodhán frowned. “I don’t understand. What do you mean by yes and no? Am I both dead and not?”

Az’marthon shrugged. “I don’t know. There are a lot of technicalities involved, but I’m certain it has something to do with your manipulation of time.”

“Time,” Aodhán’s frown deepened. “How did I even do that? That’s not a power I should have been able to control at all. It’s not even an aspect—” A thought rose in his mind and he glanced up at Az’marthon in shock. “I must have inherited it from my mother.”

Az paused, his teacup stilling halfway to his lips. “You know about your mother?”

Aodhán’s frown returned. “I do. I also know about the Collective. I have learned many things since we last spoke.” His gaze sharpened. “The question is why I didn’t learn them from you.”

Az stared at him for a moment before slowly placing his cup down. “I wasn’t at liberty to share them. Your mother insisted I wipe your memory to protect you.”

“And you did,” Aodhán said accusingly, feeling a twinge of pain twist in his gut.

Az opened his mouth to speak then sighed. “I did.”

Silence reigned for a moment as Aodhán tried to decipher how he felt about that. He failed to settle on a single emotion, though after a while he asked, “What was her name? My mother.”

Az smiled then, happiness blooming across his face at the memory.

“Irina Aurelius.” he shook his head, his smile still wide. “Oh, she was a beautiful woman.” He glanced at Aodhán. “She was a noble too. I’m sure her family still exists to this day.”

“Her family?”

Aodhán froze.

“Yes,” Az’s expression turned sheepish.

“I guess they’re your family now too. Whenever you return to Lutia, you should find them.”

Aodhán pressed a hand to his head as a headache began to form. “Why? Why did she leave?”

Az’s gaze sobered and he sighed. “I never quite figured that out. She had a good life in Lutia; she just never wanted any of it. Right from her awakening, she’d detested her abilities—not the affinity, but the fact that she’d had one at all. She wanted to be mundane.”

He shook his head. “I’ve never seen anything like it, and I still don’t understand it.”

A faint smile returned to his face as he looked at Aodhán. “She wanted a cottage in the middle of nowhere, with flowers, a family, a husband, and peace. She wanted to live like a peasant, yet she was too strong—too exceptional to have it.” His smile turned distant, almost wistful. “Oh, how excellent she was.”

Aodhán stared at him for a moment. “You loved her.”

Az scoffed “I did more than love her. I cherished her. I ran from the Collective to be with her... to see her, to see the life she’d built for herself.” His expression dimmed and he shook his head. “But I inadvertently led the Collective to her. To your father. To you.”

“Is she dead?” Aodhán blurted out, unable to hold the question back any longer.

Az paused once again, contemplating his answer. A moment later, he sighed.

“I don’t know. I have no knowledge of anything after my creation. All I know is what the real Az’marthon knew when he created me.”

He watched Aodhán’s face fall, then added, very hesitantly, “But I suspect she is. I suspect we both are. We are immortals, after all, and only a few hundred years have passed. After leaving you my legacy, I turned myself in—”

“To be with her,” Aodhán finished.

There was no anger in his tone. No hatred. But the resentment was undeniable.

Az met his gaze for a moment, then shamefully shifted his eyes to the wall behind Aodhán.

“It was never my intention to destroy your family,” he began slowly, his voice soft. “I would have returned to the Collective regardless, but I was happier to do so because I knew she would be there.” He glanced back at Aodhán, a sad smile touching his lips. “Maybe I am indeed the villain in your story.”

Aodhán held the man's gaze, his vision blurring slightly as pain twisted in his gut. “Why? Why didn’t you tell me?”

“Because that was what your mother wanted,” he replied without hesitation. “To give you a life of peace and tranquility for as long as she could. A normal life away from war and bloodshed. And I wanted to delay any overly ambitious thoughts for as long as I could.”

“I don’t have any ambitious thoughts,” Aodhán defended with a scowl.

But he was lying, and it was obvious from his gaze.

Az’s expression softened.

“I know you well enough to know how you think, Aodhán. You see everything as an obstacle to overcome, a problem to solve. But not this. The Collective is not evil. It is a force of good against eternal darkness—”

“They destroyed my family.”

The words cut through the room with brutal simplicity.

Az opened his mouth to speak, perhaps to defend the Collective, but then he sighed. “It seems you do have ambitions after all.”

Aodhán looked away, thoughts of his mother racing through his mind. He had tried not to think about it ever since he found out three days ago, but apparently his subconscious had been content to do the thinking and planning for him.

Exhaling deeply, he forced his mind back to the problem at hand. “How do I get out of this weird alive-unalive state?”

Az frowned thoughtfully. “I wish I had an answer for you, but I don’t. I suspect perhaps you were not the only one messing with time, and so you’re in a sort of limbo until time regains—” he paused, then smiled. “Ah! It seems I was right.”

“What?”

“There’s a very enthusiastic fellow trying to save you.”

Aodhán glanced up as cracks began to spread across the vision. Then he looked back at Az’marthon. “I...”

His jaw firmed.

“I still have a few secrets to uncover, don’t I?”

Az stared at him for a moment, then nodded. “You do, but I’m sure you’ll figure it all out on your own.”

The cracks widened. The room began to dim.

“I will still see you again, right?” Aodhán asked, feeling a storm of emotions begin to well up within him.

“You will.” Az smiled kindly. “I’m not out of power yet.”

Aodhán nodded. “That’s good. That’s very good.”

The room faded as a blinding brightness took over, but just before the vision collapsed completely, Az spoke.

“For the sake of your ambitions, Aodhán, focus on gaining power, on growing stronger, on honing your skills until they are razor-sharp. Everything you need to know will come to you in time. You, however, need to be strong enough to hear it.”

He smiled. “And go read a book on the descendants of Ascendants and their inheritances. You might learn a thing or two about—”

The vision collapsed completely.

The blinding brightness engulfed him, and in the next instant, Aodhán found himself standing once more in chaotic space, utterly exhausted and spent as the eight Calamities sneered down at him.

It was like reliving his death all over again.

Whoever had tried to save him had done an awful job.

But then a voice rang out—a voice Aodhán had never thought he would hear again—and despite his exhaustion, he jerked his gaze upward to see Seamus standing above him, eyes wild with fury as he shouted,

“Get away from my friend!”

Aodhán couldn’t believe it.

“Seamus,” he croaked, wanting to be sure this wasn’t just another dream. Or worse, a hallucination.

Seamus must have heard him because he moved to stand beside him, arms still spread toward the Calamities. “I said get away.”

The Calamities glanced at one another in surprise.

Then they sneered, pooling their powers together into an attack that would completely eradicate both of them in an instant.

Aodhán winced as his eyes dimmed with exhaustion, darkness creeping into the edges of his vision. He wanted to scream at Seamus to run. To get away before it was too late.

But he had neither the energy nor the breath.

His body slumped. His consciousness faded.

But just before darkness swallowed him once more, he heard a new voice enter the fray, light and resonance humming with their arrival.

“I advise you to rethink this.”
