Feeling like a rat in front of a cat, Niala sat motionless, staring back wide-eyed at the primordial construct. She didn't know if it was aware or if it could do anything to her, but she didn't feel exactly safe.

Beside her, soul-Annabelle sighed. “Don't worry. I don't think it's angry or anything. I'm not even sure it's self-aware. It's just reacting to something you said, probably that last word.”

Niala's ears twitched as she looked at the little girl from the corner of her eye. “What do I do?”

Annebelle waved a hand. “Nothing. It'll go back to doing its own thing soon. I could draw its attention? I know a few other words that seem to annoy it.”

“Like what?”

“Hmmm, like Sculptor.”

Proving her right, the concentric grinding wheels reoriented to “glance” at Annabelle, though it soon resumed its focus on Niala.

The catkin winced. “I think my word annoyed it more than yours.”

The little girl tilted her head. “Weird. I wonder why? What's that thing, anyway?”

“It's... something that a dead, or maybe sleeping, god created to bring it back to life. It's been using people to collect the god's essence from the aetheric realm. It's... not a good thing.” Niala explained.

“It really does sound bad. Are you and David taking care of it?”

“We're trying to. It's a lot more powerful than we thought it was. It talked about how the Narrator kept it from meddling with souls too much, so I thought going over my memory of my encounter with it might help, but...”

Annabelle smiled wryly. “But you got pulled here instead. Did you get anything useful, at least?”

Niala finally decided that standing still wasn't doing anything, and turned her head to look at the girl. “Just more questions. Like: is the real Annabelle aware of you?”

“I don't think so. Whenever she goes to sleep, I get a flash of her memories, and none of them ever had me, or that, in them,” she said, pointing at the Narrator with her chin. “But I can do something nifty, though!” She revealed with a grin. “I can be a mini-narrator!”

Niala blinked. “You can what?”

Annabelle puffed out her chest. “A mini-narrator! I can pull some lesser souls and send them along to the real me!”

“How does that even work?! Lesser souls? Like animals?”

“I don't know! And yep, animals, insects, plants. Little things that aren't too smart. The first time, I managed to get a bunch of frogs. I like frogs!”

Niala slowly nodded. “That's... amazing. And the frogs just appeared?”

“Yep! Fully formed and all! You can ask the real me about it. I also sent some caterpillars, a big patch of moss, and I even managed to pull a whole squirrel, though that one was hard! Probably the limit that I can do!”

Both girls flinched as a third voice cut in, tiny and raspy. “And he would appreciate if you didn't pull on your unfortunate link to the wellspring anymore than that.”

As one, they turned to look at the source: a little luminescent ball, standing on spindly legs, and two small arms that were just long enough to cross in front of its spherical body. As it spoke, its light pulsed. “I was told he's letting you have that little bit of fun because he felt bad for not realizing your soul wasn't available for recycling.”

Niala's ears twitched. “...Hi. I'm Niala. Who are you?”

The ball turned her way. “Oh, Hi, Niala. I don't have a name. I don't think I'll be alive long enough to warrant one. By the way, you and David's experiments with aetheric life are fun. He says you're more than welcome to keep coming up with these things, as long as you don't start meddling with the souls themselves.”

“I... ok? I'm sorry, but what are you?”

The ball scratched the side of its head. “I'm not sure. I mostly only know a bunch of stuff from what you call the Narrator. I think it made me to speak with you.”

Niala looked up at the grinding wheels, which were still “pointed” in her direction. She returned her attention to the ball. “I'm... sorry to hear that. Is there any way to give you a longer, more meaningful life?”

The ball laughed. “Hahaha! Oh, you're exactly how he remembers writing you! I can tell he's very happy that life hasn't changed you too much! You were a bit of an experiment, I think! But don't worry! I'm happy with my life. I was made to be. You can't tell right now, but I'm having the greatest time! It's like my body was made to relish having this conversation! Nothing else matters!” The ball said, flapping its little arms.

She felt as if she should be having an existential crisis. Maybe she would, later, once she had time to process everything. Shaking the thoughts out of her head, she looked at the ball. “Ok, well, if you're sure... Did you have something to tell us?”

It slapped a fist into its palm. “Right! Yes, I did! You need to know about the thing you called the Orchestrator. It's not alone; Jerivil made three more, every one of them a blemish on existence. Think of it like something embroidered on the back of a tapestry; it's hidden from his view, but he can tell it's pulling the threads. Minor imperfections, but things he can't take care of because he can't see them, just their effect.”

Niala furrowed her brow. “Who's Jerivil? Is that the god's name? And he made more Orchestrators? So the chamber we found under Montaroyal...”

The ball raises its arms in a shrug. “I don't know all the details. If you think it's the same installation, then it must be one of its siblings. They're not all Orchestrators, though. The Sculptor is another one; it was the first to wake up. The Orchestrator was the second.”

Annabelle spoke up. “What's the third and fourth?”

“He doesn't know. He only becomes aware of them when he hears their name. Like I said, invisible. And yes, Jerivil is the 'god' who made them. He was a real piece of work. The Narrator is both proud and incensed at him, in equal measure. Like all the other gods, for that matter.” The ball said, nodding at its own words.

Niala tilted her head. “Why is that? The gods, I mean? Didn't he make... or write, them?”

The ball shook its head/body. “No, no. He never wrote any gods. Those souls used what they have been given to break a few rules. But that's fine. He likes people who go beyond the margins, push the limits, that kind of thing. In fact, it's what he likes the most. The only rule that he won't allow to be broken is creating souls, or meddling with their writing.”

“Isn't that kind of what David and I did, though? With the mana constructs, and the mana cores?” She asked.

“Ha! No, no! What you guys did was create a new form of receptacle, and he filled them with an appropriate soul. I can assure you, you would know if you were meddling with soul stuff directly. He says to go ahead and keep trying new things; he's very interested in what you and your friends will come up with.”

Niala let those words sink in. The... maybe not the creator of life, but certainly the entity that was involved with animating it, was giving her carte blanche? To create new life?

She was glad she was sitting, because she felt he mind swim a little. Next to her, Annabelle had her eyes riveted to the little talkative ball. “Hey. Can you tell me what I am? You said the Narrator was sorry about what he did to me?”

The spherical being angled to face her. “Ah, sorry, sorry. The narrator isn't certain, to be honest. Your soul envelope never ruptured, so your essence wasn't released. It's like you surmised; little bits of you are floating around in the wellspring, and they get reused, and that's why you get flashes of memories from other times and people. He's trying to piece you back together. In time, he says he'll manage it, and you'll be whole again, but that might take a long while.”

“Then, what's going to happen to me when the real Annabelle dies?”

The ball grabbed his elbow and put a hand where its mouth would be. “Hmm, he's not sure. Because bits of you are stuck in other people, even when you die, your envelope won't pop. It's a bit of an all-or-nothing. If every soul that has a bit of you were to be dead all at once, then yeah, you'd get recycled. In the meantime, you'll probably be stuck here.”

Seeing the little girl's despairing face, the ball shot its hands out. “But don't worry! When the time comes, he'll figure something out! It's hard for him to understand characters; that's why I'm here, but he is at least aware that you process time linearly, and that you get depressed when you have nothing to do for too long.” It said with a reassuring tone.

Annabelle nodded absentmindedly, only to look up at Niala when the catkin put a hand on her shoulder and gently squeezed. “I'll come visit you, now that I know how to do that.”

The little girl blinked, then smiled, and threw herself against Niala's side in a hug. “You're the best, Niala! It gets really boring out here!”

Patting the girl's head, the catkin looked back at the ball. “So, what else about Jerivil and his creation? Do we need to destroy them?”

The ball slumped. “Unfortunately, yes. They're carefully created receptacles that mimic having a soul. It's allowing them to affect the story, but because they're not part of it, they don't carry the self-correcting process that souled life does. The story isn't close to unravelling, but it's a kind of rot that can't be allowed to remain.”

Niala narrowed her eyes. “Why is it unfortunate?”

The ball threw its hands up. “Because! Even if it's an artificial soul-mimic, they're still alive, in a way. They were brought into the story without being given the sacred chance to live out their life. The Narrator doesn't like that.”

“But... wait. What about golems? Aren't they similar?” Niala asked.

“Ah, not exactly. Here's a little secret about golems; they do have a soul. They're built by people or things that have souls, so they are part of the story. And I know what you're going to say; Jerivil has a soul, so his creations should be the same, but no. That's where his deviousness comes into play; he identified the space inside his creation that should have a soul, and filled it up, so there was no space left for the Narrator to give them one.”

She rubbed her temple. The little ball was dropping one bomb after another. “Alright, so... the Orchestrator must be destroyed because it's... insulated from some sort of self-correcting system, so its actions are damaging the story, but the Narrator wishes it weren't so.”

“That sums it up, yes.”

Niala looked down, letting her thoughts tumble around. Things began falling into place, and then a line formed. She raised her head up and stared down the Narrator's envoy with newfound determination. “What if we gave the Orchestrator a new body? One that could receive a true soul?”

The ball somehow conveyed that it was furrowing its brows. “What are you suggesting?”

Ears twitching, Niala smiled. “First, I'll need everything you know about the Orchestrator.”

David watched his wife pull the unusual-looking memory-book out of the shelf, open it with reverence, stare at it for a second, and then gasp and pull back as if she'd been holding her breath for an hour.

He was catching her before he could finish the thought. “Kitten! What's wrong?!”

She blinked, looked at the book in her hand, and then up at David's worried face. “I'm alright. It's alright. This is...” she closed the tome and held it up. “This is more than it looks. Long story, I'll tell you later. For now, I'm going to need your help.”

He nodded. “Anything. What do I need to do?”

With a silent thanks, she stood on her own two feet and slid the book back into its shelf before turning to look at her husband. “We're going to free a god's child.”

David blinked, looked from Niala to the book, then back to her.

“What?”
