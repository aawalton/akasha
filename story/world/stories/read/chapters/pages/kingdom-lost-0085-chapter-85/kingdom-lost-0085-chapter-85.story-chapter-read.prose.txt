CHAPTER 85

The mine was the best place Zelgra had found so far and not for comfort's sake, it was familiar. The ceiling was low enough that she had to angle her shoulders on the way in and the air tasted of wet stone and something sharper underneath it, a mineral bite that she recognised before she had taken three steps into the main gallery. But it was dark and it was cool and the work made sense in a way that nothing above ground had since she arrived. Down here the language was simple. Strike. Listen. Clear. Assess. Rinse and repeat. She understood that language better than she understood the world right now. She had been swinging a pick for two hours before Landryn said anything beyond the necessary.

He was working the adjacent wall, chipping carefully at a seam he had been following since before she came down. He worked the way she imagined he did everything, methodically, without wasted motion, pausing regularly to examine what he had exposed before deciding where to strike next. She had watched engineers before. Most of them talked too much. Landryn did not talk at all and she found that she respected it without being conscious of it.

"You know minerals," he said.

"I sold them for twelve years," Zelgra said.

Landryn nodded and went back to his seam. Zelgra paused for a moment waiting for the engineering chatter that always followed, the questions and observations and opinions that most men in his profession couldn't seem to help. Nothing came. She went back to work.

That was the extent of it for a while. The sounds of the mine filled the space between them. Pick on stone with the soft exhale of effort. The occasional crack when something gave way cleanly. Somewhere deeper in the gallery two of Riley's soldiers were working a secondary cut, their sounds arriving as faint echoes, regular and distant.

Thoughts of the hospital crept into Zelgra's mind.

She had not meant to think about it. She had come down here specifically to avoid thinking about it and the mine had been doing its job until now. But the rhythm of the work had loosened something and the image came back anyway. The beds in their two rows. The lanterns burning low. The soldiers carried in one after another with the same careful hands that she had watched throw timber around like it weighed nothing. She had not expected that to affect her the way it did. She had grown up around hard men doing hard work. She was not someone who frightened easily or felt things in public if she could help it. Being a Droll required a thicker skin than most.

But she had stood in that doorway and gone completely still and she did not entirely understand why.

She struck the wall harder than she intended and a chunk of stone the size of her fist broke free and dropped. She crouched to examine it out of habit and then stayed crouched longer than the examination required.

Landryn's pick had stopped.

She did not look up. "I keep seeing it," she said. "The hospital."

A pause. "Yes," Landryn said.

That was all. She did not need more than that and he seemed to understand it. She set the stone aside and straightened up and found her place on the wall again.

She had built things her whole life, not with her hands the way Landryn did, but with transactions and relationships and carefully maintained trust. Twelve years of knowing which veins ran deep and which ones played out early. Twelve years of understanding what a material was worth not just at market rate but to the specific person standing across the table from her. She had always known, in a general way, that the ore she sold became things. Walls. Weapons. Tools. She had simply never been present for what happened to the people who used them.

Last night she had been very present.

She worked in silence for another stretch. The sharper smell in the air had been growing steadily since she moved deeper into this section of the gallery and she paused now to breathe it in properly, turning her head slowly, locating the source. She brought her torch closer to the wall and crouched.

The seam was narrow, running diagonally across a darker band of rock, and the colour was wrong for iron or copper. Yellow. Not the bright yellow of fool's gold but a duller, more powdery shade, the kind that came off on your fingers when you touched it. She touched it. It came off on her fingers.

She held her hand up to the torch.

"Landryn."

He came over and crouched beside her without being asked, his own torch held low, examining the seam with the same careful attention he gave everything.

"Brimstone," Zelgra said.

She ran a finger along the seam, tracing its line up the wall and down toward the floor. "It goes deep. This is not a surface deposit."

"Riley has standing orders for it," Landryn said.

Zelgra looked at him. "Why?"

"She was processing it and it caused quite a light show when she burned it," he said.

Zelgra looked back at the seam. She had sold brimstone before, small quantities, mostly to tanners and metalworkers, more of a special order than a common request. She filed the question away in the same place she filed most things about Riley, in a growing collection of observations that was beginning to suggest the gap between what she had assumed about this warden and what was actually true was wider than she had first thought.

They marked the seam and moved on.

The chalkstone announced itself differently. No smell, no colour. Just a change in the texture of the wall under her pick, a softness that did not belong in the surrounding rock, a give that made her slow down and look properly before she struck again. She brought her torch close.

Bright off-white. Almost luminous against the darker stone around it. She pressed her thumb against its surface, and it left a clear impression, the material yielding like something between chalk and soft cheese. She broke a piece off with her fingers without needing the pick. It crumbled slightly at the edges and left a fine white dust across her palm.

She held it up and blew across it gently. The dust drifted in the torchlight.

"Chalk stone," she said. "Good chalk stone."

Landryn had come over again. He took the piece she offered and turned it in his hands, testing the weight, pressing his thumb into the surface.

"It's soft," he said.

"That's the point." Zelgra turned back to the hole her pick had opened in the wall and crouched in front of it. She worked the edges back carefully, clearing loose material, letting the torchlight reach further in. The vein started narrow at the face but as she dug deeper the white widened, spreading into the rock like something that had been waiting a long time for someone to find it.

"Landryn. Come and look at this."

She stepped aside. He moved in and held his torch up to the opening, leaning forward to get the light into the hole. The chalkstone caught it where the deposit opened up behind the face, bright white against the dark surrounding rock, spreading wider the further back it went until the shadows swallowed it completely.

"How far do you think this goes?" he asked.

Zelgra smiled. "Unclear. Very deep I hope."

She stood and brushed the dust from her hands. "You burn this hot enough and it becomes quick chalk," she said. "Mix it with water and sand and you have mortar that sets harder than the stone around it. The great castles, the ones that have stood for centuries, their walls were built with it." She pressed her palm flat against the deposit. "This is what keeps stone standing."

Landryn was quiet for a moment. She could see him running calculations, the way engineers always did, area measurements and load bearing and material quantities, the invisible arithmetic of someone who thought in structures and space.

"We have been piling the loose material just outside the entry," he said. "I didn't know what it was. I had the soldiers set it aside rather than discard it."

Zelgra's smile got a little brighter.

"How much do we need?"

Zelgra looked at the deposit. She thought about the walls she had seen above ground, the ones already standing and the ones still going up. She thought about what a settlement needed to become something that lasted. "More than this," she said. "But this is a start."

They stood together in the torchlight for a moment, the mine quiet around them, the distant sounds of the soldiers in the secondary gallery arriving as faint, steady echoes.

"Riley will want to know about this when she returns," Zelgra said.

Landryn gave a nod. The moment was a welcome distraction from the weight they had carried hours before, even if it would be short lived.

Neither of them said anything about when that would be or what she would be carrying when she got here. Neither of them said anything about Valrik either. But the hospital was still there between them the way it had been since they came down, present without being spoken, the way certain things were in the hours after something that could not be undone.

Zelgra's shoulders rolled forward as her smile faded. She turned back to the wall and raised her pick.
