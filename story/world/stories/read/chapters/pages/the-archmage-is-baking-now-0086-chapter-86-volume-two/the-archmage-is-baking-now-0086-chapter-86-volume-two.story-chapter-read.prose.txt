Jane finally sat up. She swung her legs over the edge of the bed and sat there for a moment, letting the blood flow back into her legs. Then, with no excuses left, she got up, got ready for her day, and headed out.

When she made her way downstairs, she found Dayle waiting for her in the common room. He was sitting at one of the tables with a cup of tea in front of him, and he looked up as she descended the stairs.

"Morning," he said. "How are you feeling?"

"I don't know yet." Jane took a seat across from him. "Ask me again in a few hours."

Dayle nodded. He stood and disappeared into the kitchen, returning a moment later with a plate of eggs and toast and a cup of tea. He set them in front of Jane.

"Eat," he said. "It’s not good to be hungry in times like these."

Jane obeyed. She figured that at the very least it couldn’t hurt.

"Edna came by earlier," Dayle said. "She wanted me to tell you that everything is being taken care of. The arrangements, I mean. You don't need to worry about any of it."

"Thank you." Jane took a sip of her tea. "That's kind of her."

"She also said to come find her before you leave. She has something for you."

She finished her tea and stood. "Thank you for the breakfast. And for everything else."

"Don't mention it." Dayle started gathering the dishes. "I was glad to have a guest."

Jane stepped outside and walked slowly down the main road. She found Edna's house without too much trouble, guided by a sign that read Doc in neat painted letters. Jane knocked on the door and waited. A moment later, Edna appeared, looking almost as tired as Jane felt.

"Jane. Come in." She stepped aside to let Jane enter. "I'm glad you came. I was hoping to see you before you left."

"How are you feeling?" Edna asked, gesturing for Jane to sit.

"Better than I expected." Jane took a seat on the chair Edna had indicated, across from the doctor and just to the side of her front window.

"Good. I was hoping that would be the case. I wanted to talk to you about a few things, if you're up for it."

"Of course."

"First, the arrangements. The town is handling everything. She'll be buried in the cemetery on the hill, next to the other people who've died here over the years. There's going to be a small ceremony tomorrow morning."

Jane considered this. Part of her wanted to stay, but she realized very quickly the ceremony wasn’t for her, so much. It was for the town, and they’d come together as a town for it. Her being there would put another element into the mix that might get in the way of that. She had already had her chance to say goodbye in a way none of them had. That would be enough.

"I don't think I will.”

Edna nodded. She seemed to have expected that.

"That's fine. If you ever find your way back here, there will always be someone to show you to her grave. Now, just wait a moment. I need to get something." Edna stood and crossed to her desk, opening a drawer and pulling out a small bundle wrapped in cloth. She brought it back and set it in Jane's lap. "I found this this morning, going through her things. Most times, I wouldn’t have known what to do with it. Today, you’ve saved me a lot of thinking."

Jane unwrapped the cloth carefully. Inside was a large bound journal. The cover was plain, smooth leather, without any indication of what was inside. She opened it to the first page and immediately knew what she was holding.

Volume Two.

"She wrote more.”

"Of course she did. The woman never stopped.”

Jane turned the pages carefully, scanning the contents. There were recipes, but also observations, experiments, and theories. It was obviously unorganized and unedited, which made it all the more important to Jane.

"I can't accept this," Jane said. "It's too valuable."

"She wanted you to have it. I’m sure. She even wrote it for you, Jane, before you ever met her. I guarantee you if she was here she’d chain that book to your hands if she had to. It’s for you."

Jane could deny a lot of things, but she couldn’t deny the truth. She pulled the book towards her chest, hugging it to herself like an infant.

"Thank you," she said. "I'll take care of it."

"I know you will. Now, if that’s done, I’m afraid there’s a lot to get through this morning. I think I saw your driver getting ready at the stables. That’s just down the way.

Jane stood and hugged the woman. If she did ever come back, she knew she had a friend here.

"One more thing," Edna said as Jane moved toward the door. "Know that everyone in town is thankful you came. You did a service for that woman. We all know it.”

"I'm glad I came too," she said. "More than I can say."

Jane found the driver at the stables, just as Edna had said. He had already hitched his horse to the carriage and was running a brush along her flank. The horse looked well-rested and eager to move, tossing her head as Jane approached.

"Good morning," the driver said, looking up from his work. "I hope you slept all right."

"Well enough." Jane stopped a few feet from the carriage. "Thank you for waiting. And for the note. It meant a lot."

"It was nothing." He set down the brush and wiped his hands on his pants. "I meant what I wrote. Take as much time as you need."

"I think I'm ready to go, actually." Jane glanced back toward the main road. "There's a funeral this morning. I don't want to be the in the way of it."

The driver left that statement without a response. It seemed to make enough sense to him that he didn’t feel it needed one.

"Did you have a chance to eat this morning?"

"I did. The innkeeper made me breakfast."

"Good. I ate as well, so we're both set." He moved to check the harness one more time, running his fingers along the leather straps to make sure everything was secure. "I'm ready to leave whenever you are, but I don't want to hurry you. If there's anything else, we can wait for it."

Jane thought about it. She had said goodbye to Edna. She had the journal tucked safely in her bag. The woman she came to see was gone, and there was nothing Jane could do to change that.

"No," she said. "I think it's time."

She took one last look at the town, to better remember it after she left. It was the same. Nothing looked to have changed, even though so much had.

A group of people was walking up the road toward the cemetery. Jane counted maybe two dozen of them, which was probably all of the town's population. Cindy was among them, walking between a man and a woman who Jane assumed were her parents.

She spotted Jane standing by the road and waved. Jane waved back but didn't approach. She knew better than to think she could help Cindy more than her parents could. The procession continued up the road, and Jane watched them go until they rounded a bend and disappeared from sight in the trees.

"Ready?" the driver asked.

"Ready."

She climbed into the carriage and situated her things, tucking her bag into the corner where it wouldn't shift during the ride. The driver climbed to his seat and gathered the reins. He made a clicking sound with his tongue, and the horse started forward, her hooves clopping vigorously. The wagon rolled along after it, and in just a few minutes the town was behind them.

They didn't talk much. The driver seemed to understand that Jane wasn't in the mood for conversation. She spent a good portion of the day dozing, slowly sloughing off the exhaustion of her vigil. When she wasn't sleeping, she leafed through the journal. She tried to read some of it, but soon came to the conclusion that she wasn’t retaining anything. The words would hit her eyes, but they wouldn’t stay in her brain longer than a moment or two.

Eventually, she gave up. Forcing it wasn't going to help. There would be better days to tackle what was in there, days when she could cook with the book open on her countertop and learn every lesson inside of it. She wrapped the journal back in its cloth and tucked it into her bag, then leaned back against the seat and watched the landscape roll past.

They stayed at the same inn that night, the little building in the middle of nowhere that the driver had brought her to on the way up. Jane ate and slept, redressing in the cleanest of the clothes she had packed when she got out of bed, looking forward to her clean, more comfortable clothes she had left at home. The driver had the horse hitched and ready by the time Jane came downstairs, and they were on the road before the sun had fully risen.

By noontime, they were overlooking Glenfall.

The carriage rolled to a stop at the same spot where Jane had first climbed aboard, near the edge of town where the warehouses and stables. The driver climbed down and came around to Jane, offering his hand to help her step out.

"Thank you," Jane said, gathering her bag and slinging it over her shoulder. "Not just the ride. I know you didn’t have to wait."

"Just doing my job."

"And you did it well. Here’s some thanks for that."

Jane reached into her coin purse and pressed several coins into his hand before he could protest

The driver looked at the coins in his palm, then back at Jane. For a moment she thought he might try to refuse, which she would hate. She’d have to argue with him then, and it would take up time she didn’t want to spend in awkward negotiation. He closed his fingers around the payment, eventually, and let the matter lie.

"If you ever need a ride again, you know where to find me." He tucked the coins into his pocket. "Or if I'm not available, ask for my son. I'd trust him to take care of you."

"I'll remember that."

"Safe travels, Miss Jane." He tipped his hat to her. "And I'm sorry for your loss. From what you say, she seemed like she was a good woman."

"She was."

Jane turned and began walking into town. It felt not exactly strange to be home after everything that had happened. She looked for the differences in the town, but found the same cobblestones and buildings she would have always expected. Eventually, she came to terms with the idea that it was just her that had changed.

No use delaying life, though. Let’s get ready for tomorrow.

Her first stop was the market. She needed to restock a few things, and more importantly, she needed to let her suppliers know she was back and would need her regular deliveries to be resumed. Jane arrived at her little market district soon, and wove through the crowds as she approached the only supplier she truly needed to talk to.

The salt merchant's stall was near the far end of the market. Claudia was a sturdy woman in her fifties who sold all things non-perishable, making her a vital stop for Jane. She looked up as Jane approached and clapped her hands together just once.

"Jane! You're back." She set down the scoop she had been using to measure some unidentified powder onto a scale. "I was starting to wonder if you'd run off for good."

"Just a short trip." Jane pulled out her list and handed it over. "I need to restock. The usual amounts, if you have them."

Claudia scanned the list and nodded. "Should be no problem. I can have everything delivered to your shop by the end of the day."

"That would be perfect. Thank you."

Claudia began measuring and packaging. As she worked, she glanced up at Jane.

"You've been gone from your shop more often lately," she said. "First the showcase, then this trip. It seems like a busy time."

"I suppose so. It’s not by choice. Just life, I suppose"

"I hope it's not another dragon." Claudia winked as she said it, but there was a hint of concern beneath the more apparent humor. "We've had enough excitement for one year, I think."

"No, probably not." Jane managed a small smile. "Just personal business this time. Nothing dangerous."

"Well, that's a relief. Still, you might want to keep the shop open if only to let folks know everything's all right. You know how people talk."

Jane did know. She had lived in Glenfall long enough now to understand how information flowed through it. Her shop had been open without fail for months and months. Now that routine had been disrupted, first by the king's arrival and the showcases that followed, then by her trip to meet her mentor.

She could see how that might make people nervous, given her history. When the archmage's shop was open, things were calm. When it was closed, anything could happen.

It wasn't an unreasonable way to think about it, really. Not that she planned to stop taking trips, but perhaps she could find ways to keep folk’s panic in check when she did. She almost laughed as she imagined the most obvious ways she’d try to do that.

Just gone to see some family, the sign would say. Don’t worry, the town won’t explode.

"I'll keep that in mind," Jane said. "Thank you, Claudia."

"Any time, dear." Claudia waved her off. "Now go get some rest. You look tired."

Jane completed her remaining errands quickly, placing orders at a few other stalls and arranging for delivery. The walk home afterwards felt longer than usual, as she moved as quickly as she could towards her own space. She simply couldn’t get home fast enough.

Her bakery came into view at last, and Jane was finally home. She unpacked, tossed her dirty things in a pile for later laundering, and climbed into an overdue bath.

She heated the water with a bit of magic, then worked through a quick application of soap and a rinse before climbing out. Today was not a day for a long soak, for whatever reason. She got clean, then shimmied into more comfortable clothes before sitting down on her bed and assessing her emotions.

She would feel better tomorrow, she hoped. If she was lucky, simply being in her own home would be enough to yank her out of her new sadness and put her right back into the normal cheerful wonderful that was her life in Glenfall. She didn’t intend to ignore what had happened, of course, but dealing with it chunk by chunk was a much more appealing idea than just spending her days in out-and-out mourning for a friend.

She leaned into what little bits of routine she could get out of the evening, cleaning her nails and getting herself presentable for the next day’s business. Jane had just finished combing out her hair when she heard a knock at the door.

She wasn't expecting anyone, and it was getting late for casual visitors. Most of her friends knew she had been away, too. The knock came again, more insistent this time. Jane hustled down the stairs to the front door as quickly as she could. The knock sounded a third time just as she reached the door, unlatched it and pulled it open.

Allen stood on her doorstep, holding a covered pot and grinning like a loon. Bella was beside him, carrying a basket of her own.

"You're back," Allen said. "We brought food."
