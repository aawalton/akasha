The main road was a bit longer of a walk than Cindy had made it seem. Jane understood why the little girl had been out of breath, now. The buildings that had been thin and sparse from her drop-off point started to get even thinner almost immediately, but none of the houses was even close to what she was looking for. She kept walking. After what felt like at least a mile, she finally spotted something that matched Cindy's description.

The garden was indeed bigger than the house was, partially because it was so huge. Berry bushes dominated the area near the fence, enclosing row after row of vegetables. It was an orderly affair, put together by someone whose mind calculated the best use of space in a nearly optimal way.

But it wasn’t just that the garden was huge. Behind the garden, almost as an afterthought, sat a tiny house. Calling it a cottage would have been generous. The building was a few small rooms, one over the other, with a single chimney rising from the smallest roof Jane could imagine. A raised porch ran across the front, almost doubling the amount of ground the house took up all by itself.

Jane understood now why the garden seemed so much bigger than the house. Part of it was the garden's respectable size, but part of it was also that the house was genuinely small. This was a cottage built for one, a place a single person might come to retire and no more than that.

She approached the gate. It was latched but not locked, and she lifted the hook and stepped through. A stone path led from the gate to the front door between the beds of vegetables. Jane followed it, careful not to step off the path onto the growing things.

The front door was a plain slab of wood, and as soon as she arrived Jane raised her hand to knock on it.

"You must be Jane."

The voice came from behind her, somewhere off to the left. Jane turned and saw a figure kneeling among the tomato plants. The woman rose slowly and turned to face her, revealing Jane’s teacher at last.

She was old. Not just elderly, but genuinely, impossibly old. Jane had met aged people before, but the woman was in a different league. Her hair was white and thin, pulled back in a simple knot. Her hands, still holding a small trowel, were gnarled and spotted..

Doc had said she was in her eighties. Looking at her now, Jane knew that estimate had been polite. This woman was ninety if she was a day, possibly older. She moved slowly and deliberately, like every step was its own individual job. Jane had once heard that aging was a matter of moving from must move to can move, and watching every will-power fueled footfall drove in exactly what that meant.

Jane’s aunt had told her not to put this visit off, and the wisdom of that advice suddenly felt much more real. If she had delayed, if she had put this trip off for another few months or another year, she might have missed her chance entirely.

"Yes," Jane said. "I'm Jane. You're Felicity Cast?"

"One and the same." Felicity set down her trowel on a small cart. "I have to say, I was shocked to get your friend's letter. There weren't more than a few dozen copies of that book printed. I'm surprised anyone even read it, let alone got any use out of it."

"It taught me to bake."

Felicity smiled, setting off a chain reaction of wrinkles all over her face.

"Your friend Emily said the same thing in her letter. I'll admit, I don't quite believe it. The kind of education it would take to understand that book is rare. I wrote it for myself, really, as a way of organizing what I knew. I never expected anyone else to make sense of it."

Jane understood the doubt. She knew just a few people who might have made heads or tales of some of the tougher aspects of that book, and only then because her training and work had introduced her to more experts than an average person could dream of meeting.

"They sometimes call me Lady Jane," she said. "I'm an archmage."

Felicity went still for a moment, then laughed.

"Yes, that would do it, as far as training goes. An archmage who bakes. That's not something you hear every day."

"I'm a baker who does magic," Jane corrected. "The baking matters more, these days."

"Even better." Felicity gestured toward the house. "Come inside. I've been working in that garden all morning, and my knees could use a rest. We can talk properly over tea."

Jane followed her up the path and onto the porch. The woman opened the front door and stepped inside, and Jane got her first look at the interior.

It was unmistakably the house of a baker.

The kitchen dominated the space, taking up nearly half of the ground floor. A large oven sat against the back wall, its iron doors blackened with use. Her shelves were full of jars of ingredients, each one labeled clearly and organized like-with-like throughout. The whole room was organized to a particular person’s taste, keeping everything where she could get it with a single reach.

What remained of the living space was minimal. A dining table sat near the window, large enough to accommodate two people. The single overstuffed leather chair filling the remaining corner had seen better days, and was cracked and worn in a way that spoke to how heavily it must have been used over the years. That was it. There were hardly decorations besides that, and absolutely no other furniture. If there hadn’t have been a second floor, Jane would have thought the woman slept standing.

"Sit," Felicity said, gesturing at the table. "I'll put the kettle on."

Jane set her bag down by the door and took a seat, watching as the woman moved around her kitchen. Despite her age, she seemed completely at home there. She was weak, almost too weak to bring her jar of tea down from the shelf or to lift her own kettle to the stovetop.

"This place is amazing," Jane said. "It's like a professional bakery, but smaller."

"I like to think it’s professional, in the way I run it. Of course, I don’t sell anything" The woman retrieved a tin from one of the shelves and pried off the lid, revealing a stack of cookies inside. "I supply the town with their baked goods, and they pay me in trade. Between that and my retirement funds, I get by just fine."

"Cindy called you the cookie lady."

"Did she?" Felicity smiled, the expression deepening the wrinkles around her eyes. "Most people do, these days. I suppose that's fair. I do make a lot of cookies."

She brought the tin to the table and set it between them, then returned to check on the kettle. Jane took a cookie. It looked right in all senses, perfectly brown and moist. She wasn’t sure if she should take a bite, yet, considering her host had yet to sit down.

"Go ahead," Felicity said without turning around. "That's what they're for."

Jane took one and bit into it. The recipe was familiar to her, but these were improved. The woman had apparently not stopped experimenting in the intervening decades since she had penned her introduction to the art of the oven. They were better than what Jane made in more ways than one.

"These are incredible."

"I started baking when I was younger than you. Kept at it ever since. I’d be ashamed if they weren’t, by now."

"Sixty or seventy years, then." Jane tried to imagine doing anything for that long. Young as she was, she had no frame of reference that could make it make sense to her. "That's a lot of practice."

"Practice and process. That’s all that book really was, you know. A way to get a person to the point where they could understand the basics, and then move on to their own baking. It wasn’t much, really.”

"It's more than that. It's organized. Scientific. I don’t think I could have learned any other way. You have no idea how lost I would have been without it."

Felicity poured tea into both cups, the liquid steaming in the cool air of the kitchen. She settled into the chair across from Jane with a sigh of relief, her old bones clearly grateful for the rest.

"I was a metallurgist, once. You know what that is?”

Jane did. This woman was once the mind behind a foundry or forge, and she didn’t guess it was a small one. A lot of pieces fell into place at once at that realization. The knowledge of heat made sense now. The precise understanding of ingredients did to, now that she understood it came from an intellect that had once controlled the precise makeup of alloys.

“I do. That’s heady work.”

“It was. But it was as impersonal as you’d imagine. I wanted to learn to make things that people enjoyed, instead of just needed. So I started to expand my ability to bake. I had always liked it, but I don’t suppose I took it seriously until I was well into my thirties. I started seriously then, trying to quantify what made various things work or fail.”

"And you figured it out."

"Some of it." She picked up her tea and took a careful sip. "Even now, some people who have never heard of science make me look like an infant with the things they can do with an oven. I mean that, too. There are real masters who understand more than I do, even if they could never explain it."

"I was lucky," Jane said. "My training as a mage taught me how to think about systems. When I read your book, it made sense to me. You should have seen me flailing around with normal baking books. They made no sense at all. I just didn’t have the mind for it, I guess."

"An archmage who bakes." The woman shook her head. "I’m still trying to wrap my head around it. What made you start? Baking, I mean. It doesn’t make any more sense than me moving from metallurgy, really.”

"I had been trained my whole life for one thing, and then I decided I didn't want to do it anymore. Baking was supposed to be my first attempt at being normal, I think. I like bakeries, but I don’t think I ever expected it to work out for me. But then I actually started doing it, and I found out I loved it."

They chatted for a little while, long enough for Jane to give the woman a bit more of her own background while learning more about how the author of her favorite book had arrived at this town. It wasn’t as if Felicity had fled metallurgy, really. She had worked a long career in it until years spent in close proximity to burning coal and super-heated, dry air had taken their toll on her. She had never made a living as a baker, and was still not dependent on it. It was just a lifelong love affair with cakes, cookies and loaves, something that had obviously made her life richer.

“Well, we should probably get started, then.” Felicity set down her teacup and stood. “Before we lose the light.”

“Started? On what?”

“On baking, dear. If you’ve come out all this way, I certainly want to see what my book taught you. I’d imagine you can teach me some things as well.” Felicity crossed to her oven, where she cranked it open with some difficulty. “First, show me what you learned about fuel. How would you heat my oven?”

Jane looked over the few fuel sources the woman had, made some educated guesses about their properties, then made a few back-of-the-envelope calculations. Using a simple loaf of bread as an example was the way the cookbook had handled it, so Jane did as well, explaining how much fuel she’d use and how long she’d let it burn before putting the dough into the oven.

“That’s close enough, I think.” Felicity nodded. “But consider that my oven isn’t set into the wall. It sits outside it. That actually means less fuel, believe it not, because there’s less conduction into the mounting. Now…”

They spent a full half-hour just talking about firewood. The woman’s insights were just as sharp in person as they had been in the book. More so, really. Jane was daunted by it all at first, but then fell into a sort of learning trance as she gained more and more minute understandings of the way heat and her baking interacted.

It was a beginning. Finally, once Jane and Felicity agreed absolutely on the right amount of wood, Jane loaded the oven.

It was time to get to real baking.
