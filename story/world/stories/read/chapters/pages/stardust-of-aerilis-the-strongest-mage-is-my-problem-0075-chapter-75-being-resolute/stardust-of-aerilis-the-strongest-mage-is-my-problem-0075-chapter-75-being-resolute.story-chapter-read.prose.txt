Somewhere inside the Fold, in a small chamber that did not have a window because the Fold did not have an outside, six torches burned in iron sconces along a circular stone wall.

The light they gave off was not warm.

It was the dim, sallow orange of a flame that had been burning too long without ventilation, the kind that turned the air faintly acrid and the shadows long. Where the torchlight reached the floor, it lit a circle of polished black stone. Beyond the circle, the chamber faded into a dark that did not bother to define its own edges.

In the middle of the circle, two chains hung from the ceiling.

Each chain ended in a wide iron cuff. Each cuff was clasped around a wrist.

Lyra Skysong hung between them.

Her feet were on the floor. Bare. The polished stone was cold against her arches in a way she had stopped noticing some time ago. Her arms were raised above her head, drawn taut enough that her shoulders had been pulled into a long, aching line, but not so taut that her toes left the ground.

She was wearing what had once been a pale linen dress.

It was not pale anymore. The hem had been torn into uneven ribbons. The collar had been rent along one shoulder. Across the cloth, in several places, thin red lines crossed and re-crossed each other in patterns that looked almost intentional, almost like a script. The script was the language of the thin chains Valerion used. It said the same thing every day, in the same handwriting.

Her hair had fallen forward over her face.

Through it, her eyes were open.

They were not looking at anything.

A line of dried blood had crusted at the corner of her lower lip. A fresher trickle, only an hour or two old, traced down her jaw and disappeared into the collar of her ruined dress. Her breathing was deep and slow, the kind of breathing a person settles into when shallow breathing has become too painful to maintain.

She did not move when the door opened.

Two sets of footsteps came down the hall.

One was measured. The other was lighter, faster, the click of a heel deliberately tapped against stone for the pleasure of being heard. Lyra recognized the cadence of both before the figures stepped into the torchlight.

Valerion came first.

He was dressed as he always was. Pale skin, dark coat with the high collar. His red eyes caught the orange light and did not warm with it. He came to a polite stop just outside the circle of torchlight, his hands clasped behind his back, the way a man might stop before stepping onto a guest’s lawn.

Sylphaine came behind him.

She did not stop. She drifted into the circle and around it, twin white tails swinging, the heels of her shoes clicking out a small private rhythm. She was smiling. She was almost always smiling.

Lyra did not lift her head.

Valerion drew in a breath.

“Master has sent me,” he said. His voice was pleasant. It always was. It was the pleasantness of a man reading the terms of a lease aloud. “He has authorized me to inform you that the terms of your continued service are open to renegotiation.”

Lyra did not respond.

“He would,” Valerion continued, undisturbed, “be willing to permit you to serve him again. Properly. Without the current arrangement.” He gestured, lightly, at the chains. “If you are willing to do something for him.”

There was a long pause.

Lyra lifted her head, slowly, just enough that her eyes could meet his through the curtain of her own hair. Her voice, when it came, was not steady. But it was clear.

“You need me to locate the Loom-Breaker?”

Valerion shook his head once.

“No,” he said. “Daran has secured the Fate Anchor. The Loom-Breaker is, at this point, no longer required.”

Lyra absorbed that.

“Then Master wants me to re-seal him.”

It was not a question. It was the next option on the list. The list was short. She had been running it through her own head in the long, empty stretches between Valerion’s visits, the way she sometimes ran through old recipes from her parents’ shop, or the names of the constellations her father had taught her. The list was a way of keeping her mind from going where her body was. “You want me to put the seal back on him. Tight. Tighter than before.”

Sylphaine, mid-stride, let out a small high laugh.

“Oh,” she said. “Oh, that. Please. As if Master would need that.”

She tossed one of her twin tails over her shoulder.

“No matter how strong that former clerk is, he is no match for our Master. The mere suggestion is, ah, what is the word. Insulting.”

She turned in a small pirouette of pleasure at her own delivery.

Valerion ignored her. “That is also not the request.”

Lyra closed her eyes briefly.

It did make sense, when she thought about it. As practical as Lucien was, as cold as he was in the matter of risk-management, he was also vain.

Asking her to re-seal Aiven would mean Lucien thought Aiven was dangerous enough to require muffling.

Lucien did not enjoy thinking that. Lucien did not enjoy being seen to think that.

If a problem could not be solved without admitting fear of it, Lucien would simply solve a different problem and rename it.

So this was the different problem.

“Then what?” Lyra said. Her voice was rasping now. The chamber was so dry. “What does Master want?”

Valerion stepped, just once, across the line of the circle.

He came to a stop in front of her. His hand rose to the side of her face, and for a moment Lyra thought he was going to grip her jaw. He did not. He simply leaned in, the way a man leans in to share a confidence, and spoke softly against the shell of her ear.

His breath was cool and smelled faintly of nothing.

He spoke for perhaps ten seconds.

Lyra’s eyes went wide.

All of the careful, dissociated stillness she had been wearing for the last few days dropped from her face. It was as if a sheet had been pulled off a portrait. The hollow, empty eyes filled, suddenly, with horror.

“No.”

Her voice cracked.

“I cannot do that. I just…can’t.”

Valerion did not move from where he stood.

“If you are certain,” he said, in the same pleasant voice, “then your arrangement with Master is at an end.”

Lyra opened her mouth.

“You would lose,” Valerion went on, “everything. The terms you signed. The pieces you preserved. The promises you collected. All of it would be released, at Master’s discretion, into nothing.”

From the edge of the room, Sylphaine’s voice slid in.

“Oh, why must you whisper.”

She had drifted closer. Her eyes were narrowed in petulant curiosity, her lower lip pushed out into a small, theatrical pout.

“What could possibly be so secret that I cannot hear it. We are all friends here. Surely.”

Valerion turned his head with the precise economy of a man who had been interrupted exactly this way many times before.

“Master’s instruction,” he said. “He specifically told me you were not to know. You are, he said, too much of a loudmouth, Sylphaine. And I do agree with that.”

Sylphaine’s pout deepened.

“He said that?”

“He said that.”

She made a small, wounded noise. Then, with a great deal of theatrical sulking, she turned on her heel and phased through the chamber wall in a sweep of crimson coattails, leaving only the faint smell of iron behind her.

Valerion did not watch her go.

He turned back to Lyra.

“You had better agree, Lyra.”

His voice was still pleasant.

“If you do not, Master will send me here again. And again. And again. And the daily sessions will continue. You know what they look like. We have, by now, found a rhythm together, you and I. Though, I must say, I am enjoying our sessions so far.”

He let that hang in the air.

“And,” he added, almost as an afterthought, “if you have not given Master your decision before he next visits the boy. The deal is off. Permanently. Hearthport will be gone. Truly gone. Every soul. Every stone. Every name.”

He let that hang too.

“Including the ones you have been hoping for.”

Lyra’s hands, hanging in their cuffs, curled into fists.

She did not speak.

Valerion waited a polite ten seconds for a reply.

When none came, he gave a small nod, the way a tutor might at the end of a lesson that had not gone particularly well but was not, in any single respect, salvageable today either.

“Well,” he said. “Since you have not given me a decision today.”

He raised one pale hand.

Above his open palm, a single faint sigil traced itself in the air, drawn in a thin violet line. Below it, six lengths of fine, silvery chain unfolded out of nothing, each no thicker than a sewing thread, each ending in a small barbed hook. The chains drifted in the still air the way underwater grass drifts in a current.

“It is time for today’s session.”

Lyra closed her eyes.

She drew in a long, deliberate breath, the way she always did. She found the place inside her head that she had practiced finding, the small quiet corner that was not in this room. She put the rest of herself in there as carefully as she could. She left her body where it was.

The first chain struck.

It cut through the tattered cloth of her dress and across the skin of her ribs, and the line of fire it drew along her body was, for a moment, the entire world.

She winced, but did not scream.

In the quiet corner inside her head, where the rest of her had gone, Lyra thought of a morning on a delivery dock.

She thought of an awkward, anxious clerk in a too-large coat, frowning down at a manifest. She thought of the way he had not been able to meet her eyes when she had told him to chase his dream. She thought of the small, defeated half-smile he had given her, and the way her own heart had broken a little, watching him fold the dream back up and tuck it away again.

She had told him, that day, that he was worth more than a clerk’s desk.

She thought of the look on his face yesterday morning, in the clearing on the islet, when she had walked into the crater and stood beside Lucien.

She held that look in the quiet corner inside her head.

She used it as a wall, and she put her back against it, and she waited for the session to be over.

Six days passed.

Six days of bolts and pillars, of lightning aimed at the open sky and not, mercifully, at any further livestock. Six days of Virelle drifting closer and closer to the woman she had been before the clearing, the woman with the bombastic opinions about chairs and the elaborate threats about chandeliers, until by the fourth morning she had been near enough to her old self that Aiven had caught himself, twice, laughing without thinking about it.

The islet had not survived the experience neatly.

There were thirteen earthen pillars of varying heights distributed across its grassy back, most of them now slumped or weathered into something that looked, from a distance, like a clumsy graveyard. There was a long blackened streak across the cliff edge where Aiven had failed to control a particularly enthusiastic lightning arc.

By the end of those six days, he could do things he had not believed possible a week before.

Mana bolts had become reflexive. He could fire eight in succession without his stance wavering, and each one struck where he aimed. The white-blue arc of his lightning could now be directed in a clean line for thirty paces, or split, with effort, into two smaller forks. He could raise earth into walls, pillars, angled shields, and crude spear-poles, and he could do it with one knee instead of two, and with one hand instead of both, and the lag between the request and the response had shrunk to a heartbeat.

He could lift small objects without touching them. Stones, mostly. The principle, Virelle had explained, was a simple gravity manipulation. You did not push the object. You convinced the world that the object weighed slightly less than it did, in a direction of your choosing, and then the object fell upward, or sideways, or however you had asked. Larger objects took more convincing. By the fifth day, he could throw a stone the size of his head across the islet without touching it. By the sixth, he could throw a stone the size of his torso, although less accurately.

He had tried, several times, to combine the branches. Summoning a spear of earth from the ground beneath an imagined opponent and then hurling it forward with a gravity-twist. The combination, when it worked, was vicious. The spear left the ground at the speed of a falling tower and struck the practice target with a sound that was less a thunk than a brief, percussive end-of-discussion.

He had tried, as a separate experiment, to form spears of pure mana rather than earth. The way Virelle did. He had assumed it would be easier than earthwork.

It was not easier.

The mana refused to hold the shape. Beams worked. Bolts worked. A spear, apparently, required a specific kind of attention he had not yet developed, a way of asking the mana to remain a particular thing instead of agreeing to whichever thing he happened to be thinking about. Virelle had observed his fourth failed attempt, sniffed, and announced that the spear could be deferred until after the world stopped trying to end. He had agreed.

And he had learned to fly.

After a fashion.

His flight, as Virelle reviewed it on the fourth evening over dinner, could most charitably be described as ambitious. He could lift himself off the ground. He could remain off the ground for short periods. He could move himself, in a roughly chosen direction, at a roughly chosen speed. What he could not do, yet, was do any of these things gracefully. He wobbled. He lost altitude unexpectedly. He drifted. He had, twice, attempted a small mid-air banking turn and ended up, instead, with a small mid-air falling.

On both occasions, Virelle had been waiting beneath him with the resigned exasperation of a woman who had assumed this exact scenario and resented being correct.

On the second of those occasions, she had simply lifted her hand without looking up from her book, caught him in a soft cushion of lavender mana, and lowered him to the grass with the words, “You are not yet ready for banking, Master. Please return to straight lines.”

He had returned to straight lines.

Mana shields had been added, in the last two days, because not all battlefields, Virelle had said dryly, would be polite enough to provide him with obedient earth.

The shields were small. They were translucent. They did not last long. But they would, if interposed at the right angle, turn the kind of strike Lucien might casually throw into the kind of strike Aiven might survive.

Probably.

On the fifth day, the Cinder-Fox came.

Cyria stepped off.

She was, as ever, exactly herself. Her amber hair was pulled into a loose tail at her shoulder, the single fang at the corner of her smile catching the light. Her tail moved in a slow, unconscious sway as she walked down the ramp. She was carrying, of all things, a wicker basket.

“Aiven,” she called pleasantly. “I have come bearing edible diplomacy.”

Virelle eyed the basket suspiciously.

“Is that.”

“Tortoise buns, yes.”

“Oh, for-, given the scale of your company, why are you so obsessed with tortoise buns? Aren’t there more delectable choices?”

“I brought eight.”

“Give me one.”

Aiven did not say anything.

They moved to one of the Cinder-Fox’s upper meeting rooms.

It was a room built for fifteen and currently hosting three, with a long oak table and a wall of brass-rimmed portholes that looked out across the cloudbank toward the distant smudge of Fangreach. The space felt larger than the conversation that was about to happen inside it. Pelka had been deputized to bring tea, and brought it, and excused herself with several small bows and the expression of a woman who was relieved to leave a room.

Virelle floated into a chair, eating her third tortoise bun with the visible disdain of a person who was nevertheless going to finish it.

Cyria sipped from a porcelain cup.

She let a small silence settle into the room before she spoke. That was a Cyria habit Aiven had learned to recognize. She used silences the way other negotiators used opening offers. They softened the room. They let her measure who was going to speak first.

It was, this time, her.

“Are you resolute, Aiven?”

She set her cup down.

“In refusing him.”

Aiven met her golden eyes.

“Yes.”

“Despite the fact that he has Lyra.”

“Especially because he has Lyra.”

Cyria nodded slowly. Her tail flicked once and stilled.

“Good,” she said. “I suspected as much. But I needed to hear it from you directly. Diplomatic hygiene.”

She lifted her cup again.

“I know less about Lyra than you probably hope I do. I want to say that first. I will tell you what I know. It is not very much.”

Aiven leaned forward slightly.

“Tell me anyway.”

“Lucien brought her into the circle several weeks ago. Suddenly. Without warning. He announced her as another human anomaly, the way he later announced you. He did not explain where he found her or how he had identified her, which is consistent with how he announces all of his finds. He prefers to keep his sources private.”

She paused.

“Her expertise is in seal-based magic. Specifically the kind of seal that locks or regulates large mana sources. With her help, Lucien has acquired several high-tier artifacts that had been sealed inside ruins or behind warded chambers for centuries. The kinds of seals that had stood up against every previous attempt to crack them. She broke them, one after another. As long as it is not unsealing a dungeon core or something of similar scale, she can do it. He calls her his keysmith, on the rare occasions he is feeling sentimental.”

She gave a small, dry smile.

“Anyways, she has become Lucien's favorite. This annoys Sylphaine to a degree I have, at times, found genuinely funny.”

Aiven was frowning.

“That does not make sense,” he said slowly. “I grew up with her. She had... she had average mana. She struggled with the same lift-a-feather exercise everyone struggled with. She was not a seal-mage. She was not a mage at all, not really. She liked adventure stories and she wanted to see the world.”

He pressed the heel of his palm against his temple.

Cyria gave him a small, careful look.

“I do not know how that happened,” she said. “I noticed the same incongruity. I asked around it once, lightly, the way one asks around a thing one is not really supposed to ask about. The conversation moved away from me very quickly. I let it move. I was not in a position to push.”

She paused.

“I can tell you one other thing, though. Whenever we had our scheduled meetings in the Fold, Lyra was always present. And she was always flat. Her reports were brief. Her tone was the tone of a person reading aloud from a document she had not written. She never volunteered. She never met anyone’s eye unless the meeting required it. She handled herself like a woman who was waiting for the meeting to be over.”

She set the cup down again.

“It was very clear to me, Aiven, that Lucien was forcing her to serve him. Through something. I do not know what. But the leash was visible to anyone who cared to look.”

“You do not know what the leash is.”

It was not quite a question.

Cyria shook her head. “I do not.”

She paused.

“To be fair, Lucien stopped giving me anything close to key information some months ago. He did not tell me why. He simply began compartmentalizing. The instructions became narrower. The briefings became shorter. The strategic context disappeared.”

She gave a thin smile.

“In retrospect, I should have read that change as a sign that he had begun to suspect me. It was, of course, the entirely correct thing for him to do. I did plan to double-cross him. He simply got there first, in his own quiet way.”

She turned her cup slowly in her hand. The amber liquid inside caught the porthole light.

“My original arrangement with him was straightforward. A few years ago, he approached Vulpine. He saw an opportunity in our market position, our analyst network, our infrastructure across Aerilis. He offered an arrangement. He would lend Vulpine the use of certain high-tier artifacts, the kind that would let us pinpoint valuable resources long before our competitors could even find the rumor of them. In exchange, Vulpine would, on occasion, perform certain services.”

She set the cup down.

“It was a good deal. The terms were favorable. The risk was contained. The threats, of course, were present. Lucien made it clear that disobedience would not be tolerated. But there was no reason to disobey. The deal was profitable. We were happy. Vulpine grew. I told myself I had outplayed the situation.”

She gave a small, mirthless laugh.

“And then, he told me the rest.”

Her tail had gone still.

“He told me about the rotting world below. He told me about the dungeon cores. He told me about his plan to reshape the world, and what reshape meant, and how many beastfolk and humans would be considered acceptable rounding error. He told me, in that same patient teacher’s voice he uses, what the floor of the new world looked like, and who was scheduled to be standing on it.”

She looked at the table.

“It is ironic. I had planned to play him and it turned out that I had been the one being played, all along. I had been winning a game whose rules he had written. I had been losing since the day I signed the original contract.”

She looked back up. Her smile was small and clean.

“I just did not know it.”

Aiven did not say anything for a moment.

Cyria did not seem to require him to. She had said what she had come to say. She was not asking for sympathy. She was simply, for the first time, reading the balance sheet aloud in front of someone who happened to be in the room.

Beside him, Virelle had finished her fourth tortoise bun. She was looking at Cyria with an expression Aiven could not quite parse. It was not contempt. It was not pity. It was something closer to recognition, the way one tactician recognizes another’s losing position on a board.

Cyria let the moment breathe, then gave a small, brisk smile.

“Forgive me. I have spent your precious time on a background story neither of you really needed to hear today. I am, in my defense, not in the habit of having anyone to tell it to.”

She inclined her head.

“What I came here to say, properly, is this. I am grateful that you are not joining him, Aiven. Genuinely. And I intend to honor my part of our original arrangement. I owe you the rest of what I know about Virelle.”

Virelle’s shoulders shifted, very slightly.

Cyria caught the motion and softened her tone.

“Though I suspect now is not the moment. Tomorrow is the confrontation. There is no point in handing you a heavy story tonight that you will then carry into a fight. I will save it for after.”

Virelle was looking down at her teacup.

Aiven, after a moment, nodded.

“After,” he agreed.

Cyria set her cup down with a small, definitive click.

“Aiven. Are you certain you will make it through tomorrow alive?”

It was a flat question, asked the way she would ask whether a shipment had cleared customs.

Virelle’s head came up before Aiven could answer.

“Are you,” she said sharply, “doubting my Master’s abilities, fox?”

Cyria did not flinch.

“Over these past six days, my Master has learned things that take ordinary mages months. He has flown. He has called lightning out of an empty sky. He has reshaped the ground of this islet to such a degree that the islet now requires a small apology. You will not find a comparable rate of progression anywhere in the public records of the Guild. He is—”

“Virelle, I know.”

Cyria’s voice was gentle.

“I do not doubt his progress. His progress has been remarkable. I have read Pelka’s reports and I have, frankly, had to reread them twice each. But.”

She turned her cup once, slowly, in her fingers.

“Someone who has just learned to walk, even in record time, even more quickly than anyone in living memory, is still not yet a match for someone who has known how to fly for a very long time. That is the situation in front of us. Lucien is not on the same scale of mage your Master is on. He is a different scale entirely.”

Virelle drew breath to retort.

Aiven put his hand on the table.

It was a small motion, palm down on the polished wood, no force behind it. But Virelle stopped, because she had learned to read his small motions, and she settled back into her chair with the unmistakable look of a woman who was reserving the rest of her argument for a more receptive audience.

Aiven looked at Cyria.

“You are right,” he said.

Cyria’s ears tipped forward fractionally.

“The goal is not to beat him,” Aiven said. “I know that. I am not going into tomorrow under the impression that I will defeat Lucien at the end of one week of training. Anyone who told me that would be lying to me, and I would know it.”

He drew in a breath.

“The goal is to get Lyra back.”

Cyria nodded slowly.

“I thought as much.”

Aiven went on.

“She is under some kind of compulsion. Lucien can inflict pain on her remotely, at will. I saw him do it twice in the same minute on the islet last week. The first time was to drag her out of the meeting room and the second time was to bring her to her knees in front of me, just to remind me that he could.”

He swallowed.

“I asked Virelle about it. Spells of that kind. Whether they can be broken.”

He looked at her, briefly.

Virelle inclined her head, the smallest of nods. “They can. Most can. Some require very specific countermeasures. But yes, in general, they can.”

“Right,” Aiven said. “So. There are artifacts that help with that, presumably.”

Cyria gave him a long look.

“There are,” she said. “Vulpine has access to several. I will see that the appropriate ones are aboard tomorrow.”

“Good.”

Aiven let out a slow breath.

“So the plan is simple. I am not fighting Lucien tomorrow. Not really. I am buying time. I am going to try to get Lyra free of him long enough to break the compulsion. I have allies. I have her. I have you, if you stay in.”

He paused.

“Once she is no longer a hostage, the calculus changes. I do not have to win the fight tomorrow. I just have to survive it, and take her out of it. Whatever comes after that, we will handle when we get there. I think, with enough hands and enough time, there is a way to deal with Lucien eventually. But not tomorrow. Tomorrow is just the rescue.”

Cyria looked at him for a long moment.

Then she smiled. Slowly. The fang caught the porthole light.

“Aiven,” she said. “You are beginning to think like the protagonist of a story.”

Aiven gave her a small, tired half-smile back.

“Maybe I am,” he said.

He picked up his tea.

“The world has given me second chances, Cyria. More than once. I am not going to fumble this one. Not tomorrow.”

He took a sip.
