If I wanted to make sure Hinlaw couldn’t catch us flat-footed, I needed to know what he was planning. Sure, there was something about the orcs. But I needed more than just ‘maybe the orcs are coming.’ I needed to know when they would attack, at the very least.

After dinner and our usual evening training (where I made sure to save plenty of Presence—twelve points, enough for two full uses of my Art) I snooped around the keep.

At first, I was tempted to use my illusion pendants to transform myself into Sir Ricdine, but that would arouse more suspicion. At least this way, I would have some excuses if I got caught. I could be heading to the library, or looking for the Drillmaster.

I crept through the hallways inside the keep. It was large, but it couldn’t be that large, and there had to be something I could do to find Hinlaw.

A few thegns stayed in the guest rooms within the keep. It would’ve once been an Ealdorman’s residence, so there were plenty of rooms designed for sleeping and housing important staff. But I knew Hinlaw and his family would be in here—it was just a matter of finding exactly where.

As I walked through the halls, I passed a few other thegns from the tournament. I didn’t recognize many of them, except Sir Peofrith. I almost gave him a nod, but that would indicate that I recognized him, and that wouldn’t be true.

Whenever I passed them, I stepped to the side and averted my gaze, not drawing attention to myself. None of them stopped or said anything to me.

Next, I extended my hearing. It was hard to define what that actually meant in practice, but my ears had grown far more sensitive over the past few days of enhancement. My resonance nodes worked in conjunction with the System to improve my Perception. If I focused, I could pick out noises from the nearby rooms.

Voices jumbled together, and most of them were too soft to hear their specific words. But I needed to know who was here. The tone of voice was far more important—and I’d heard both Hinlaw and Hinfic’s voices. With my Focus as high as it was, I wouldn’t easily forget those voices.

Finally, I picked up Hinfic’s voice muttering something. It was a room in the corner of the third floor, isolated and well defended. But the hallways were still too busy to just stop outside his room and listen. Someone would be suspicious.

Taking note of the room’s location, I left the keep, then circled around its exterior. By now, there were only shreds of twilight left in the sky, and it was dark enough that my standard trainee garb—a tunic and pants—would blend into the shadows.

I climbed the outside of the keep, my fingers clinging to the stones effortlessly. I hadn’t even really considered that I might be able to climb much better now than I had before. Before, I hadn’t exactly been the best climber, but with brute force, it was far easier. I had enough grip strength to press my fingers onto the cracks in the stones and haul myself up. If I needed to, I could hold on with just one hand.

When I reached the third level of the keep, I shifted around until I was right below the window of Hinlaw and Hinfic’s room.

For a moment, I pulled my head up, daring to peer inside. My dark hair would blend in with the night, and I only let a slice of my skin shine through.

It was an average room. The best were larger than I thought they might be, and certainly more comfortable looking. It reminded me of a mid-budget hotel room on Earth. Hinfic sat on the edge of his bed, picking grime from his fingernails, while his father paced.

For a few minutes, they spoke about completely inconsequential things. Matters back home in their domain, or about Hinfic’s lands.

After a short while, Hinfic complained, “In what world was Ceonwin going to fall for that letter, father?”

“It was worth a try,” the older man replied.

“Not particularly. Now he’ll just be on edge.”

“Don’t lecture me, boy. Know your place.”

“Fine, father.” Hinfic flopped back on his bed. “If the assassination failed, though…how many more failures are you going to accept?”

“We have our backup. I think it’s about time we used it.”

“Sure, father. Trust a Black Captain.” Hinfic rolled his eyes.

“Othmuk. He’ll come. Another word of doubt from you, boy, and I’ll give you a beating you won’t forget. I’m still the master of this house.”

Hinfic said nothing.

“He’ll be here with his men, and he’ll serve us,” Hinlaw said. “I have the Warlord’s word. I’ll send out the command, and Othmuk will come. It may not be ideal—and I wouldn’t put my faith in a Black Captain unless I had to—but if nothing else will end Ceonwin, then what other choice is there?”

“And Othmuk will be enough?”

“We won’t stand in his way. Lord Ceonwin and a bunch of Silvers will be no match for him. They’ll be busy with their joust, riding the last course—and you’ll be there to distract them. The orcs will come, Ceonwin will die, and at the same time, the sapper training program will crumble.”

“The Drillmaster is a Gold as well,” Hinfic muttered.

A crack echoed through the room. Hinlaw snapped his fingers, and an invisible palm Hinfic on the side of head. “Don’t question my judgement. The Drillmaster hasn’t attended any jousts, and I have arranged a meeting for him on the day of. He won’t be present.”

“Yes, father.”

I was pushing it, and I’d heard enough. Before I could get myself caught or in any worse trouble, I descended down the side of the building and returned to the barracks for the night.

Whatever they were doing with the orcs, it would happen during the last joust. And that tied my hands, too—the final three matches were all planned for the same day. I’d ride against Hinfic, Ceonwin would ride against his foe. Then, it would be the final tilt, and the fate of the domain would be up to the victor.

Or, apparently, a disastrous orc attack would sweep over the castle, killing Ceonwin and destroying the training program. I had to warn someone.

The next morning, instead of trying to convince Lord Ceonwin, I found the Drillmaster in his office. I said, “Master, I have troubling news.”

“Levi?” The Drillmaster tilted his head. “Come in. What is it? I don’t have much time to spare, so be quick and concise.”

“I have reason to believe that there will be an orc attack during the final joust. Have you heard of a Black Captain named Othmuk?”

“Once or twice,” the Drillmaster said. “He’s a powerful Gold-tier, and he’s not to be underestimated. As the orc Path-walkers go, he’s more akin to a druid than anything else, and he can transform into a powerful drake. He’ll be leading the attack?”

“Potentially. Do you know if he’s in the area?”

“I haven’t seen any reports of it, but our intelligence could be faulty,” the Drillmaster said. “How do you know this?”

There’s a traitor here who has made a deal with them, and plans on using them to interrupt the tournament.” I crossed my arms. “Even if I say who it is, there isn’t anything you can do about it without incurring wrath from on high. Especially when there’s not enough proof.”

“You’re getting savvier,” the Drillmaster said. “If you believe it, I will trust you. Too many commanders are too prideful, but what will it hurt to post a few scouts. Last we knew, Othmuk had set up a bastion in the ruins of Cyneirin—that’s across the Thelea, a few weeks or so northeast of here. An ancient fallen bastion of ours.”

“Anything special about it?”

“Highly defensible from all sides, and there’s a Labyrinth below it to retreat into. A frontal assault won’t work without overwhelming numbers.”

“Is there anything we can do?”

“I’ll position scouts between here and there as a tripwire, and I’ll call on the nearby battalions. The 294th should be able to send riders to assist. We don’t need to get caught flat-footed.”

“Thank you,” I said.

“Now, I believe you have training, and you have a Labyrinth run of your own coming up. There’s no sense in letting yourself fall behind.”

Near the end of the week, I met with the druids again in the evening, bringing the maegen-infused rations I’d promised them in exchange for the potions, wrapped in a felt cloth. I met them in the same location, at the same time.

For a few seconds, they did nothing except stare at me. One blew a smoke ring from his pipe, and I couldn’t help but glare at him. Did they not realize the danger they were in?

But no one else knew what was coming. It was like we were standing at the edge of a cliff, about to jump hundreds of feet into a deep ocean, and yet I was the only one who could see it.

After a few more seconds, I cleared my throat, and the druids glanced at me. One of them jumped to his feet and said, “Ah, yes. Sergeant Levi. Sorry. Let me go get the potions.”

He raced off to the druids’ barracks, then returned a moment later carrying a heavy chest. He unlatched it, revealing three potions with swirling gray liquid in them. I’d have said they were dirty bathwater had they not been glowing—and at the same time, radiating a pressure that made the back of my mind ache.

“How strong are they?” I asked.

“Pure,” the druid said. “They’re just Pure quality. Two steps below Heavenly quality, but they should work for a Dupe’s purposes.”

I passed him the pouch of rations. He unfolded it, checked them over, then nodded. “A pleasure doing business with you, Sergeant.”

I nodded, remembering not to shake his hand, then took the chest of potions.

With that, I had all I needed to reforge my body. Now, I just needed my squad. I wasn’t waiting any longer, though. It was time to get started.
