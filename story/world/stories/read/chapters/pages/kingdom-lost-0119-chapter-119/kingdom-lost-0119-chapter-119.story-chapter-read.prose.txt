Chapter 119

The morning came in cold and clear, the smoke from the cookfires going straight up into a sky that had not decided yet about the day. Riley crossed the yard with the whip coiled at her hip and her mind already at the river. She had thought about this walk since before she opened her eyes, and now the walk was taking care of itself.

The sentry above the gate straightened as she came. He turned and the gate began to swing before her boots reached it, and she went through it without breaking stride and gave the guards on the wall the small nod they were waiting for.

The path down to the bank was harden from the many that traveled in since she had first arrived here. She could hear the pit before she could see it, the low steady breath of a well-run fire, the crack and scrape of shovels in charcoal, the flat wet slap of clay being turned on a table. Then she came around the last stand of alder and the whole yard opened in front of her.

It was a operation of its own now, it would continue whether Nami was there or not.

Two of the tables held finished green pots waiting on their racks, and two more held Hoshin working fresh clay under their hands, one in the fast, tight motion of small pots and the other in the longer sweep of the larger ones. The drying racks were large, and the shelves were full. Along the near edge of the pit, four women raked charcoal from a cooled corner and shovelled it into a cart, and the cart had a driver at the head end who was already easing the horse into position for its run back. Behind the pit, a new stack of fired bricks had grown taller than a man since the last time she had counted them, and a foundation trench dug clean, the outline of a real kiln.

She stopped at the edge of the yard and let herself look at it for a moment before anyone had noticed her.

She had told Nami the pots would be key, and Nami had heard her.

Nami saw her from the forming tables and set the pot she was working on down on the board, wiped her hands on the front of her apron and walked towards her. Nami’s hair was tied back and there was a streak of grey clay along the line of her jaw where she had touched her face without meaning to. The apron was heavy with dust.

"Warden."

"Nami."

"You're early."

"I couldn't wait." Riley smiled, the small smile she had for Nami. "Show me what you have."

Nami turned and led her along the edge of the yard.

"We finished the small kiln in the night," she said. "The bricks held through two test firings and the seams did not open. It is not the big one, that will still be some weeks. But it will hold a proper temperature for a good amount of time, and we can control it in a way the open pit will not let us. We were going to start firing the next batch of pots in it this afternoon."

"No."

Nami stopped and turned to her.

"Not pots. Not yet." Riley looked at the small kiln. "I want it hotter than you have ever run anything. As hot as it will go and hold there. And I want you to load something else in it first."

"What?"

Riley reached into the pouch at her hip and drew out a piece of the stone. She held it flat on her palm so Nami could see it. Pale grey, chalky along the broken edge, softer than the granite Landryn brought up from the mine. Along the flat face of it was the faint curled print of a shell that had lived a long time ago and had left the shape of itself in the rock.

"Limestone," Riley said. "Landryn cut this out yesterday. There is more of it than we know what to do with."

Nami took the piece and turned it in her fingers. She scratched the flat with a thumbnail. A pale mark came up in the line where her nail had passed.

"It is soft."

Riley took the piece back and set it into Nami's palm.

"Break it down into pieces about the size of a fist. Load the kiln with as much of it as it will hold. Get the fire in it as hot as you can, and hold it there for a full day and a full night. Watch the colour. It goes in pale grey. When it comes out I want it pale, almost white. Chalky white. If any of it still has the grey through it, it goes back in."

Nami nodded slowly. She was already thinking about the fuel.

"When the colour is right, pull the fire and let the kiln cool for several hours. The workers who go in to take it out, they wrap their hands. Cloth over the mouth and nose, cloth over the eyes as much as they can and still see. They do not touch it bare. They do not breathe over it. And most importantly no not let it get wet."

Nami looked up at her from the stone.

"It burns them."

"It burns anything that is wet, people are mostly wet." Riley let that sit for a moment. "Straight from the kiln into sealed clay pots. Small ones. Thick lids, tight. Sealed the moment they are packed. Then to the guarded storehouse, not the open one. I want each pot painted with a red band so no one opens them. Red for this, only red."

"Red," Nami said.

"Only red."

"I understand." Nami closed her hand around the piece of limestone. "When do you want us to start."

"Today. Landryn is bringing the first load down before midday."

Nami nodded.

Nami turned her head and called a name across the yard, and one of the older women at the forming table set down her tool and started walking toward them. Nami said something short to her about the small kiln and the fire and the fuel, and the older woman nodded twice and went back toward the pit without asking a question. Nami turned back to Riley.

"There is more you came to see."

"There is. The small pots. The thin ones."

"Come."

Nami led her along the drying racks to the far end. On the second tier there were pots Riley had not seen before, rows of them, no bigger than a fist, the walls so thin that the light from the yard came through them where the sun caught the shelves. Nami reached in and drew one out and turned it in her fingers so Riley could see the walls of it.

"Thin as we can make them and still have them hold their shape through the fire," Nami said. "Any thinner and they crack in the drying, or they come apart on the way out of the kiln."

She held it out and Riley took it. It was almost weightless. She could feel the give of the wall between her thumb and forefinger, a small flex that was there before the break would be.

"May I."

"Please."

Riley crossed to the edge of the drying lean-to where the ground was open. She bent and scooped a handful of loose dirt from the ground and let it fall into the pot, half full, and then a little more. She weighed the pot on her palm for a moment. The workers along the racks had stopped what they were doing, because she was clearly about to do something they had not seen anyone do with one of their pots.

She turned to the nearest tree and threw the pot at it.

The pot went straight and it hit the trunk about a hand's width off centre. It went to pieces the moment it touched the wood, a small dry crack of a sound, and the dirt came out of it in a low grey cloud that hung for a moment in the still cold air and then began to drift.

Riley smiled.

"Perfect."

Nami had come up beside her while she threw, and she was watching the tree, and Riley heard the small pull of breath at her shoulder that was Nami not letting herself grin.

"Make several dozen more," Riley said. "As soon as you can."

"Already done."

Riley turned to look at her.

"Already done?"

"You said thin, and you said many. I did not know what for. But you said many, so I ran the line for two nights on nothing but the thin ones. I have a hundred and twenty finished, and eighty more on the racks, and the next firing will pull the total to two-fifty by tomorrow."

Riley laughed, once, short. Then the smile stayed.

"Great. Keep making them. I'll have this batch collected today."

"What will go in them?"

"Something important."

Nami nodded, and then she turned her head and gestured to another rack a little further along the lean-to.

"There is more. The pots you asked Tama to make wicks for. She gave me the first bunch of wicks yesterday, and I have been weaving them into the lids. I have a batch ready for you to see."

Riley followed her to the rack. Nami reached in and lifted one down and set it on the table between them, and then a second, and lined them up so Riley could see each one at once.

They were beautifully made.

The lids sat flush against the shoulders of the pots with no gap Riley could see. Through the centre of each lid Tama's wick came out. Riley bent and looked at the underside of one of the lids. The wick ran down into the body of the pot in a coil, so that once the wick was lit the flame would have a length of it to travel before it reached the heart of the mixture. Time. Range. The number Riley had asked Tama to find.

"We have both, like you asked." Nami said. "Thin walled, for the ones that break on impact. The wick is short in these. They will not sit long once thrown." She moved her hand to the second. "Thicker walled, for the ones that need to hold together while they burn. The wick runs longer, and the seal is heavier. The mixture inside stays where it is until it is ready."

Riley looked at the pots on the table. She looked at Nami. She was smiling before she had decided to.

"Great. Let's pack them. I'll bring you the mixtures myself, one for each. I want each kind painted a different colour so no one on a wall picks up the wrong pot in the middle of a fight. The thin ones one colour, the thick ones another. I'll tell you which is which when I bring the mixtures."

Nami nodded.

Riley reached across and put her hand on Nami's shoulder. Nami went a little still under it, the way she had gone still the first time Riley had crossed to the edge of the table in the tower and told her the line was hers.

"Nami. This is excellent work."

Nami looked down at the pots on the table, then up at Riley, and the smile she had been holding since the beech tree finally came the rest of the way through.

"No, really." Riley met her eyes. "This is better than I hoped."

The two of them stood there for a moment with the line running around them, the shovels in the charcoal, the wet slap of clay on wood, the low breath of the pit, and the small kiln low and pale behind it waiting to be given the first thing that was not a pot.
