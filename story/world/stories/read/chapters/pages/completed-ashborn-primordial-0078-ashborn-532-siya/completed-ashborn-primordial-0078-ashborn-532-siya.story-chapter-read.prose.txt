For a moment that seemed to stretch on forever, neither Vir nor Maiya said a word.

Siya stood at the rooftop entrance with crossed arms, watching them with dark, judging eyes that bore far more intelligence than a child her age ought to have.

Vir didn’t really know what he’d expected. He’d spent weeks in the mindscape preparing to transfer her soul and he’d thought of this moment so many times that he’d lost count.

Now that she was standing here, however—the last survivor of a civilization that hadn’t existed in an Age and someone who had lost everything she had ever known and loved—he was at a loss for what to say.

Deciding anything was better than this silence, Vir went down on one knee so they were at the same height and smiled, despite being more anxious than he’d ever been in his life.

“Welcome back, Siya. You have been asleep for a very, very long time,” he said in the most soothing voice he could muster. “If it’s not too much trouble, would you mind altering your speech to match ours? I have a great deal to explain. But everything is alright. You are safe now. Do you remember anything?”

Siya studied him for a moment before shaking her head. “Only dreams. Terrible dreams,” she said without any accent at all. “For speech to have shifted this much… and my affliction… how? How long?”

Vir glanced at Maiya, who nodded back.

“Let’s find somewhere comfortable first,” he said. “I promise, I’ll tell you everything.”

Vir had worried he’d have to coax Siya to follow a pair of strangers she’d just met, but she did so without any fuss or protest, likely realizing the gravity of the situation.

Obediently, she followed them into the lift that took them to the bedchambers Janak had assigned them.

Vir had to remind himself he was not dealing with any ordinary child, but rather a being from the Age of Gods. He suspected her development would not be anything like that of a normal child’s, to say nothing of the difficulties she faced in dealing with her mortal illness.

A cruel fate for one so young. Vir had to wonder whether she had ever had the luxury of acting her age.

But that was all in the past now. He’d made a promise to give her a better life.

Vir stopped mid-step, forgetting precisely whom he’d made the promise to, before suddenly remembering.

Janak, of course. Her father. Who else could it possibly have been?

Ridiculous that he’d forgotten, even for a moment.

Maiya walked close beside him, holding his hand in hers, while Siya followed a few steps behind, seemingly scanning every inch of the tower with her inquisitive eyes.

“What of the time effects?” Maiya asked, keeping her voice low enough not to be overheard by the girl. “With the prana gone, Mahādi’s time dilation will have disappeared as well. The people of this new, unified realm will be in a state of panic. They’ll be desperate for guidance.”

“I know,” Vir said with a sigh. “I’m afraid they’ll just have to wait just a little longer for that. For now, I want to get her settled first. The Asura stationed near Mahādi should be close to here. We’ll link up with them soon and…”

Once again, Vir felt as though he was missing something. Had he really been about to suggest they use Ash Gates to travel the realms? But that was impossible, now that Janak—who'd facilitated those Gates in the first place—was gone.

He shook off the sensation. “And take skyships to where we need to go,” he finished.

Maiya nodded, falling silent.

The bedchamber was still as they’d left it, which felt strange given all that had changed since they last sat in it. Since Vir had prepared to meet his doom.

Vir gestured for Siya to take a seat at the dining table before he and Maiya did the same, sitting across from her.

The girl obliged, her spine so straight it would’ve impressed the Head Handmaiden. All while watching him with those serious eyes of hers.

“I wish to know everything. Not just the abbreviated version,” Siya said, staring at Vir.

No, not at me, Vir realized. At the prana core.

The girl’s eyes were fixated on it, and why shouldn’t they be? She, like everyone else of her race, was born with Prana Vision, or likely something even better. The roiling infinity of prana within Vir must have appeared like a bottomless black pit to her eyes.

Vir looked at her for a long moment, debating how to approach this. She was young, certainly. But her intelligence was undeniable, and the directness of her request left no room for treating her like a child, no matter how much he wished it. She was of the race of the gods. Treating her as anything less would be a mistake.

“Alright,” he said. “From the beginning, then.”

He started with her father. With the illness he fought so hard to cure, and the machine Janak built to search the timelines for a cure.

He watched her face as he described it. For someone so young, she had quite the mastery over her expressions. Often, her only outward reactions were a slight narrowing of her eyes or the balling of her fists under the table. Something Vir only saw because of his own Prana Vision.

She froze upon learning that Janak had found timelines where she had lived without the disease at all, but none where he had found an actual cure.

“I suppose he couldn’t accept that,” she said quietly.

“That he certainly didn’t.”

Vir then told her about the Fateweavers. What her father had seen when his machine breached their domain, and what they had done in response. He described the Fall as gently as he could, letting her absorb the fact that her entire civilization had been destroyed in an instant as a direct consequence of her father’s research.

She frowned at that, staring daggers into the table, but said nothing.

The most difficult part for Vir was telling her what she had become, and so he chose his words carefully. Not to patronize her, but because the facts were genuinely so difficult that even he struggled with them.

Which was why Vir was both relieved and terrified when Siya said it for them.

“I was outside this tower, wasn’t I?” she said. “Circling… for an eternity. That was no dream, was it?”

Vir shook his head.

“And… my father was inside all along.”

“Yes.”

“I wonder if some part of me longed to be with him. If that was why I trapped him so.”

“What was done to you was done by our mutual enemy,” Vir said flatly. “They manipulated you to ensure this happened.”

“Vir is right,” Maiya added. “You bear no blame in this, Siya. None at all.”

To her credit, she didn’t cry, despite looking on the verge of tears. She just nodded and stared at the table.

As tempted as they were to console her, Vir and Maiya said nothing, letting her take all the time she needed to sort through her feelings.

Eventually, she lifted her gaze back to them.

“Continue,” she said. “I wish to know the rest.”

And so he did. He told her about her father’s greatest creation and all that he had accomplished in his final moments. The shard of Fate he crystallized and bound to his soul, resulting in a blind spot for their enemy, as well as the chain of reincarnations that created the being called the Akh Nara, culminating in Vir.

He explained what he was, what he could do, and why the Fateweavers couldn’t predict or control him.

“In a sense, you are him,” she said. “The seventh incarnation of my father.”

“You’re not wrong,” Vir replied. “Though your father’s dream of reincarnating with his memories was foiled by our enemy, his soul does live on within me.”

She looked at him with a blank expression he couldn’t fully read, and for a moment, Vir wondered if he’d said something wrong. If he’d said too much too soon. She was a child, after all.

His concerns were proven unfounded, however.

“He chose this path,” Siya said. “Rather than leave the world a failure, he chose this. For me… so that I could live again in a new body. Even if it took millennia.”

Vir could only nod. The girl had once again surprised him with her maturity, and his chest tightened at the thought.

“Just like him to do such a thing,” she muttered, and a lone tear streaked down her cheek. “The fool…”

Vir waited a moment, then summarized the rest. The machine below, what it had been built to do, the arts required to operate it, and the cost of the process.

Hearing of Janak’s copy’s sacrifice didn’t seem to move her in the slightest. Vir supposed she must have already guessed his fate. She likely didn’t consider him her father at all, despite how much he’d felt that way.

Vir thought that was a little sad, and he hoped to one day convey the lengths Janak’s copy had gone to ensure she lived.

He went on to describe the three realms collapsing into one, the new world outside Mahādi, and the sun she hadn’t seen in thousands of years crossing a sky no longer choked by Ash.

He was midway through explaining the soul transfer process when it hit him without warning.

A pressure so fierce it paralyzed him. A pain he knew all too well.

Vir collapsed over the table, his breath ragged as he clutched his chest, the core within him heaving against the prison of his soul.

He only vaguely registered Maiya’s panicked cry and the sound of Siya’s chair falling.

When he looked up, her small hand was on his chest, and her face was screwed up in an expression of intense concentration.

He went still, searching her eyes.

“You’re dying,” she whispered, her gaze locked on something deep within him.

“Is it… that bad?” Vir managed through gritted teeth.

“I do not know how you are able to stand. The pain must be unbearable.”

“What’s going on, Siya?” Maiya asked, full of worry. “What’s wrong?”

“He contains a near-infinite amount of prana within him,” Siya said half in awe and half in terror. “The result of... a failed process. I fear the instability will only grow worse with time. No soul was ever meant to bear such a burden.”

Vir opened his Heart Chakra, straightening up as the pressure eased and the damage to his soul began to mend.

“I know it’s a long shot, but is there any way you might help with this, Siya?” he asked, guilty to be asking such a thing of someone so young, much less someone who'd been through as much trauma as she had.

Siya frowned, withdrawing her hand hesitantly.

“I am unsure,” she said. “With the correct tools, perhaps… but I am no expert. All my knowledge comes from what was given to me. I have never operated on anyone before.”

Vir nodded. There would be time to revisit this later.

“Come,” he said, returning to his seat. “I’ve yet to tell you everything.”

“You’re sure?” Maiya asked.

He nodded. “It comes and goes. I feel fine now, I promise.”

Maiya didn’t looked convinced, but neither did she refuse. She knew as well as he did that there wasn’t much anyone could do to help Vir with this particular issue.

And so he continued the tale, leading up to the present and the state of the world, as best as he understood it.

It was only when he reached the end, when he told her that her father’s copy was gone and his soul had been burned as fuel for the machine that the tears ran down her face.

Even then, her expression remained entirely composed. She didn't sob or crumble. She just sat with her hands folded neatly on the table as the tears fell, her eyes on his.

“My father died long ago. But you are his reincarnation,” she said at last. “And so, in a sense, he isn’t gone. Is he?”

Maiya, who’d been quiet through Vir’s narration, rose from her chair and pulled Siya into a deep hug.

Siya went stiff for a moment, but no matter how composed she tried to be, she was still a child.

A child who had been through far, far too much.

In the warmth of Maiya’s arms, she finally broke.

Her wail filled the room, but Maiya only pulled her tighter, her own tears falling as she wrapped her arms around the girl’s small shoulders.

Vir added his own arms to the mix, hugging both of them together.

“I cannot claim to be your father, even if his soul lives on within me. But if you’ll have us, it would be the greatest honor and privilege of our lives to care for you as our own.”

Even if he hadn’t made that promise to Janak, he would have said the same words.

His only response was a broken, wordless sob, but it was enough.

The three of them stayed that way for a long time, entirely alone in a tower of a city that had been dead for five thousand years.

A city that, against all odds, felt the warmth of the sun once again.
