[Time]: Summer, 1981[Location]: Dragon Era Park / The Umbral Cathedral

The Dragon Era Park occupied an enormous sky-island above the Listening Sea, and the cloud-highway delivered it in stages: open ocean first, then the park's silhouette bleeding into frame in increments, with distant dragons visible long before the platform arrived.

Irene had booked a luxury, dual-recliner touring bus that offered a full circuit of the park's grounds, pausing at any stop of interest.

Their voices, just above a murmur, barely disturbed the air between them. Outside the panoramic windows, the impossible, majestic wilderness of the reserve rolled past: scale so grand it seemed designed by something that had never worried about expense.

The rhythmic, low rocking had a deeply therapeutic quality, like stepping out onto a sunlit balcony and stretching your arms wide after a month in a windowless archive.

"This swaying," Irene murmured, resting her chin in her hand. "It's remarkably comforting."

She looked across at Ovelia, and a soft, helpless laugh bubbled past her lips. The Grand Witch's long cat ears were swaying left and right, in perfect synchronization with the chassis. Paired with her inherently lazy, sun-drenched feline expression, the visual concentration of cuteness was frankly criminal.

"You feel comfortable? Good," Ovelia said.

She took a sip of iced coffee. Paused. Then her cheeks puffed out, her head gave a violent full-body shake, and she exhaled a sharp breath against the taste, an entirely genuine, unguarded feline shudder.

It should be illegal, Irene thought, lifting her own cup to hide her smile.

The bus rolled to a stop at the first scenic overlook, and they had barely stepped onto the sun-drenched platform when—

"Excuse me! We are so terribly sorry to impose, but would the two of you be willing to allow us to take your photograph?"

It came from two Witches bearing heavy professional cameras, their accents marking them clearly as scholars from the Eastern Archipelago. It was obvious, from the comfortable choreography of their movements, that they were a couple.

Irene assessed them in a microsecond. Their eyes held only polite, artistic admiration, missing entirely the usual reaction: the instant, delighted stampede of an ordinary crowd realizing the cat behind every lucky-charm statuette in the Inner Sea had just wandered within arm's reach. A faint, frictionless hum of mana at the edge of her senses confirmed why: Ovelia was running a cognitive interference field, quietly enough that to the rest of the park, the Witch of Eternal Slumber currently registered as nothing more than an exceptionally pretty girl in a hiking vest.

Still, she hesitated. Her eyes moved sideways to Ovelia.

Ovelia turned her head away, expression tightening fractionally. "...Fine."

The photographers spoke in soft, melodic tones. "One, two, three... Cheese."

The flash detonated, briefly consuming the afternoon sun in a harsh burst of concentrated white.

Irene felt the immediate hostile shift in the air: the quick, agitated thwack of Ovelia's tail against the air, the flare of biological aversion against being captured on film. Without a word, she reached out, her gloved fingers brushing gently against Ovelia's hand. Ovelia looked down. Irene opened her palm.

"A reward."

It was a grapefruit-orange jelly confection, forged by crushing an entire, excessively juicy grapefruit and six tangerines into a single, highly concentrated block of sweetness.

"The fault is mine, of course," Irene murmured, dropping her voice into a register she had built for exactly this. "I know what lenses do to you. I ought to have sent them away the moment they asked, and instead I..."

She let the sentence die where Ovelia could watch it die, then looked up through her lashes.

"I wanted proof. Something physical, that you were here with me today, and not another dream you take away tomorrow."

She had assembled the rest of it on the walk over. That last part had arrived on its own.

Ovelia stared at the candy. The lashing of her tail hitched mid-stroke, and her ears went back. "...I'm not that petty."

"Oh? I find that difficult to believe."

"Hmph. Foolish girl."

Ovelia snatched the candy from Irene's palm, inspected it briefly against the light with the focused seriousness of a jeweler evaluating a stone, and bit down.

"Ladies! The composition is absolutely stunning!"

One of the photographers jogged over, holding up the camera's display screen. In the frame, both of them were throwing spectacularly uncool, archaic peace signs. Ovelia stared directly into the lens with the unblinking, territorial intensity of a cat confronting a vacuum cleaner.

And yet. The professional technique was undeniable. Masterful composition, vibrant color grading, precision grain reduction: the image's texture transcended its awkward posing. Ovelia and Irene appeared to be softly, inexplicably glowing against the deep afternoon blue of the sky. The photographer had also framed the background with perfect instinct, capturing the silhouettes of two massive dragons wrestling in mid-air, scale so enormous it looked cosmological.

"The result is..." Ovelia's tail gave a slow, measured swish. "...acceptable."

Irene's face broke into a brilliant, sun-drenched smile. "You think so too? Wonderful. I can show this to Allison and the others later."

"Show Allison?" Ovelia repeated, ears swiveling forward.

"Yes. And Kara, and—"

"Kara and the others!"

It was as if Irene had unknowingly struck a volatile switch in Ovelia's behavioral matrix, something igniting in the depths of her black eyes. Before Irene could fully process the shift, the Big Cat was already marching toward the observation deck railing, waving an imperious arm at the photographers.

"Take another! Ensure the entire dragon cavern is in the background!"

The two photographers, animated by excellent professional instincts, abandoned their private discussion and scrambled to follow, cameras raised and tracking.

"Take several!" Ovelia shouted into the wind, her eyes narrowing into smug, glittering crescents. "Cat requires optimal framing to show off to those perpetually slumbering cats when we return! Meow-ow!"

Irene stayed where she was and watched her go. What came up in her was the impulse of a hand closing over a songbird: to gather this thing up and hold it somewhere against her chest that nothing in the world could reach into, and the perfect understanding that if she held it as tightly as she wanted to, she would crush it.

I would burn anything in this world to keep you exactly like this.

She closed the distance and reached up to smooth the collar of the hiking vest and tuck back a stray lock of raven hair. "There's no need to run. Look, you've disheveled your clothes."

Click. Click. Click.

Irene turned her head at the sound, and the flash caught her again.

"Perfect!" the photographer called out, reviewing the reel. "The unscripted expression of deep, intimate affection!"

The rest of the afternoon passed in a golden blur.

They leaned against the observation railings and watched the drakes dive through canyons at full speed. Guidebooks, ecological reports, the aerial circus. Whatever the draconic hospitality industry considered a signature experience, they partook in it.

As the sun dipped below the cloud layer, painting the sky in bruised violet and gold, Irene stopped at a souvenir pavilion to select gifts for the Aspects.

"What do you think of this plush dragon-head helmet for Shaar?" she asked, holding up a remarkably, aggressively silly oversized cloth hat.

Ovelia leaned against the counter, let out a long, luxurious, bone-deep yawn, and regarded the hat with the calm certainty of a woman who knew her household. "She will absolutely adore it," she purred. "Meow-ow."

But Shaar was only the opening move. Irene went through the pavilions the way she had once provisioned an army, and by sunset there was something for every one of them: an arcade cabinet six months ahead of the public market for Daena, sweets by the crate for Avenka, something in a jar for Kara, warm milk and cracked crab for Vila, and for Aerthain the shiniest object on the sky-island. Allison's was the most expensive item in the shop: a standing membership at a tea salon kept by an electromagnetic Greater Lantern Cat, twenty-six thousand varieties on the menu, leaves not included.

Then there was Solvane.

In the park's curiosity pavilion, tucked behind the taxidermy hall, a row of display coffins waited for the kind of clientele who found dragon bones more interesting than dragons. Irene stopped in front of one scaled to a child's frame and looked into the open dark of it.

Had it been her in the ground, with Ovelia left behind in the cold, she would have wanted the cat in the box beside her, and she would have demanded it. So she knew, down to the marrow, what the dead Irene was waiting for, and what the widow in the grave-cloth wanted to climb into.

Irene's lip curled. If you wanted to keep her, you should have had the decency not to die. Whatever that failure had dropped belonged to her now.

She pressed the back of her hand into the lining: thin satin over hard oak.

"Rip the base out," she told the clerk. "Fit it with goose down and heated dragon-fleece. Put air-vents along the lower seam, and replace the lid latches with an interior release."

The clerk blinked. "My lady... that would be a bed."

"Yes," Irene said. "It would."

She had a small marble headstone cut to go with it, and when the mason held her chisel over the stone and asked for the dates, Irene stayed her wrist with the tip of her cane.

"Leave it blank," she said. "She is only sleeping."

"Cat can state with absolute certainty," Ovelia said, "that this will be the finest gift she has ever received."

The amber lights of the Umbral Cathedral burned low and quiet when Ovelia returned, and her fatigue had evaporated somewhere between the doorway and the central lounge. She stood in the center of the room, arms crossed, radiating an aura of insufferable, triumphant satisfaction, and spread her set of photographs across the coffee table for the assembled Aspects to witness.

The room was already a landscape of Irene's meticulous provisioning. Shaar was aggressively adjusting the oversized plush dragon-head helmet. Aerthain had mounted her saccharine-gem sculpture of the one hundred and sixty-seven Lantern Cats on a velvet pedestal and was still correcting its angle by fractions. Solvane sat perfectly still beside her coffin, gauze-wrapped fingers tracing the blank edge of the headstone. Kara quietly catalogued her jarred false-god specimen in a round, soft hand that closed every letter into a full circle.

Avenka, cheeks stuffed with wholesale mung bean cake, leaned over the table and paused at the first frame of the set, the two of them shoulder to shoulder at the railing with their fingers up in those archaic peace signs.

"Cat wants to go play with Irene at the park too," Avenka complained around a mouthful. "Why does only Big Cat get to go?"

Behind the arcade cabinet, the blips stopped.

Daena did not turn around. "It's already buried," she said, barely above a whisper, her voice sanded down by a familiar, rehearsed exhaustion. "This afternoon. In the mist."

The playful, triumphant energy in the room went out like a bulb pulled from a socket.

Shaar's head came up. "No," she said. "I refuse it. Scrub the Red Light District, fine, I grant you that one. This stays."

"If we leave a single trace of it standing," Allison replied, her voice dropping into icy practicality, "then every piece of evidence I have spent the past year burying becomes entirely pointless."

"Then do it where she can see you." The dragon helmet was still on Shaar's head, and she had stopped remembering it was there. "Bury it in front of her and let her hate you to your face. She counts every pebble on her road, and she will find this one missing some year from now, with nobody in the room to argue with. Empty her hands and she will find something to put in them. I have seen what she reaches for."

Aerthain turned away from the pedestal, and the room took a charge. "And what of her legitimacy?" she said. "A sovereign stands on a foundation. You have spent a year taking hers apart and calling it housekeeping.

"The heaviest bond this civilization recognises is the one you already hold with her. Heavier than marriage. Heavier than blood. A sole disciple is the heir, the keeper of the thought, the will that walks on after the master sets it down, and every house on this continent has honoured that since there were houses to honour anything. Say it aloud once, in front of witnesses, and the age hands her the whole of it on the day you stand aside. The succession stays peaceful. She arrives whole, and she remains so."

From beside her coffin, Solvane stopped tracing the blank face of the headstone. Her gauze-wrapped fingers rose to the knot at the back of her head, worked it loose, and let the white cloth fall into her lap.

One eye was Ovelia's black. The other was gold, the same gold as the eyes in every photograph on the table, and she turned it on Aerthain.

"She does not need that kind of legitimacy," Solvane said.

The violet along Aerthain's horn flickered once and went out. The gold eye was laughing at her, idly, the way a playwright watches a stagehand wander on in the wrong act and start arguing with her leading lady, while the black one beside it looked at nothing at all. The King of the World held the gold for a moment, and then looked away first.

Solvane bound the cloth back over her eyes.

Ovelia kept her eyes on the photographs. "Business as usual, then," she said. Her voice carried no warmth and no inflection. "Vila. Go and purge the records from those two photographers."

The vibrant girl in the hiking vest, the one who had run ahead to demand better photographs, who had eaten a grapefruit candy in the afternoon sun, who had yawned on the bus with the exhausted, contented weight of an afternoon well spent, had left the room several sentences ago. What sat there now was the Head of the Grand Witches.

Vila, having just finished the last of her honey-sweetened milk, stood from her chair in absolute, solemn silence and walked into the shadows, disappearing to erase the only external evidence that the afternoon had ever existed.

At the coffee table, Kara sat perfectly still. She reached out, her fingers resting against the glossy surface of a single photograph, the candid one caught by professional instinct, of Irene adjusting Ovelia's collar in the wind. Both of them radiant and alive and entirely, devastatingly normal.

Kara lowered her eyes, traced the frozen moment with her fingertips, and said nothing.
