Instructor Clearance had covered the Srilla in exhausting detail.

The monster’s general appearance—a tricky thing to describe, due to their ever-changing nature. Instead of shapes or colors, she’d focused on the uniformly magical aspects of their description. The way their scales, skin, or fur constantly shifted in tone and hue. The uncountable numbers of limbs due to changes in their bodies. Their size, which was just as dynamic as everything else about them. No monster changed as much as a Srilla did, or anywhere nearly as rapidly.

In fact, that change was their only true weakness. Instructor Clearance had made that abundantly clear in her lectures; the best way to kill a Srilla was to lock it in one unchanging form. Without the constant outlet of evolution and transformation, it would stagnate—and to a Srilla, stagnation was death.

Very few First Order spells could affect an entire thirty-foot-long magical monster, though.

So, instead, she’d recommended deterrents. Catrine’s scent magic, or other unpleasant spells, could keep one at bay until it decided to hunt something else—or until the students unlucky enough to run into one managed to escape from the third floor and retreat behind the gates. Trapping one on a floating section of library when the stairs moved could also work, but only until the Srilla transformed enough to fly. Once that happened, it would be right on its target again; Srillas were singular in nothing but their focus.

Unfortunately for Eola and her friends, none of them had a viable deterrent in their repertoires.

Instead, they’d opted to exploit a different weakness of the Srilla.

The six of them jammed tightly together, tension dripping from the students as they crept into the  Srilla’s lair. The room was perfectly round, with a distinctive, tell-tale guard rope around its entire edge save for the three stairwell arches that divided it into thirds. The velvet rope was redundant, though; every inch of the room’s edge was lined with shelves, and a second row on the inside turned the entire thing into a narrow, round hallway. Eola pointed. Her mouth moved, but words didn’t come out, and she shook her head, then tapped Roth on the shoulder and pointed again.

It was dark. Colin cast a Bright Ball, then tossed it up again. Eola tensed. After a moment, she relaxed. The Srilla hadn’t noticed it yet. She ran her fingers along the spines lining the outer shelves as Colin did the same on the inner ones. And, with every second, Roth moved forward, brass flute playing soundlessly.

Then Patrice tapped Eola’s shoulder hard and fast. She didn’t stop until Eola looked behind them.

The Srilla was coming around.

A passage loomed in front of them, leading to the dark center of the floating room. She ducked inside, careful to keep Colin’s hand on her shoulder. The others followed. Roth breathed heavily and silently, then kept playing.

Eola’s eyes were locked on the passage between the shelves. They were putting all of their eggs in a single basket; if Roth’s spell failed, they had nothing to handle a Srilla of this size. Atta hopped up onto her shoulder, and she jumped from the sudden claw-pricks on her neck. The other familiars watched, too, Roth’s white songbird flapping its wings nervously.

And the monster slid by. Or at least, it started to. Then it stopped. Colin gasped as the Bright Ball he’d tossed slowly drained away, its light fading like a dying star. Roth dropped a hand to his bag and started groping for a different instrument—a wooden flute with a reed for a mouthpiece. Patrice grabbed it and handed it to him, and for a moment, the countertonal ward fell apart.

The Srilla stiffened and turned.

It stopped.

Then, as Eola watched, its snakelike head rotated away, and it continued its patrol around its territory. Tension bled from her shoulders, and she tapped Roth on the shoulder. He glared at her, and she gave him a quick thumbs-up, then kept searching the shelves.

In the end, it took two more Srilla laps for one of them to find Recorded Musings of Apostle Antoinnene, Volume Seven. Patrice pumped her fist triumphantly as she pulled it free from its spot and dropped it into Colin’s satchel—much to his silent, wordless annoyance.

Then it was just a nerve-wracking exit to the nearest occupied stairwell. That took almost ten minutes, and by the end of it, Roth looked physically exhausted from playing. As the stairs pivoted toward a new floating room, he opened his satchel and slotted the wooden flute into its leather-bound place. Then he blew out his cheeks. “Y’aer curse it, you three are slow! Speed it up next time.”

“We did our best, Roth. I didn’t see you searching,” Patrice shot back.

“I was busy saving our lives!”

“If I might make a suggestion,” Instructor Tarik said. That got an immediate glare from Tagg, but the professor continued as if he wasn’t even there. “You should figure out who’s to blame for that escapade, and who the heroes are, when you’re not in the middle of the Library Exam.”

“Fair ‘nough,” Tagg muttered. “That’s good advice, and I won’t call it interference.”

Patrice nodded, and after a second, so did Roth.

“The part I don’t understand is why you can’t carry the book,” Colin said.

Patrice laughed quietly as she headed for the next floating room. “Because, Colin, I don’t have a bag.”

“Ah.”

“You’re all lucky that book wasn’t a shrieker,” Roth said. “You were all making enough noise I could barely hold the spell together.”

“We get it,” Eola said quietly. He’d been complaining for the last five minutes as they worked their way across the library, purifying his Mana the whole time. Not that he was wrong, to be honest—and since he had single-handedly taken care of the Srilla, she wanted to be more patient with him. But at the same time, enough was enough.

They’d just entered a long, single-aisled section of the library. The room had to be a good three hundred feet long, with alcoves every twenty or thirty feet along the right side; every alcove was lit from above by a Bright Ball, and every one of them featured an oak reading desk looking out into the abyss. The second book, A Briefe Historie of the Alemic Tongue, should be here somewhere.

And, even better, there was nowhere for a monster to hide, and the section was beautifully weeded. It’d be safe.

Probably.

Hopefully.

“Spread out?” Colin asked.

“Not too far. Stay within spell range. We’ll be safer that way,” Eola said. She’d thought the room the book monster had ambushed her in was perfectly safe, too, after all.

So, as she explored the shelves and skimmed past titles like History of the Lost Divine, The Dark Heart of Auserse, and Lines of Control: Notes on the Old Gods Theory, Eola kept looking over her shoulder. Colin had practically glued himself to Patrice’s hip; they stayed within a shelf or two of each other, leapfrogging like it was the second floor—not that they’d gone far from the entrance. Roth, meanwhile, was working his way toward her, but it’d be a minute at his current pace.

She was, for a moment, alone.

“Atta, how are we doing?” she whispered.

“Fine,” the cat said curtly.

“Fine?” Eola raised an eyebrow, then dropped to a knee. Her eyes scanned a shelf while her fingers scratched her familiar’s neck. Atta didn’t even purr, but she didn’t try to scratch Eola’s fingers, either.

Something felt…off…with her.

Atta always had a snide comment.

“Is anyone else’s familiar acting strange?” she asked after a moment.

“Yes. Nera won’t stop running between my legs,” Colin said from the other side of the floating room. “He’s been doing it ever since we left our dorm.”

“Move,” Patrice said. “He’s on edge because of your familiar. Can you get Attic to stop harassing him, please? He’s going to trip me up.”

“Got it!” Roth called from his shelf. “Let’s get out of here before—“

But whatever Roth was going to say cut off as a spiderlike monster twice his height strode gracefully over one of the alcoves’ railings. Its long, spindly legs punched through the oak desk’s surface as it silently slid between him and Eola; two scythelike blades hung from arms held close to the thing’s mantis-like head, and a spinarrete trailed behind it. Thick white hair covered its abdomen, while its head and upper legs were blood red. Everything else was covered in black chitin.

A Dalyn.

A second spider—the Davek half of the pair—climbed over the shelves and landed between Eola and both Patrice and Colin. This one’s body was covered in the same black chitin, though thin stripes of white hairs broke up the armored casing, and its shorter, more powerful legs and atrophied mantis blades were the same red color. So was a long, whip-thin stinger that trailed behind it.

Eola’s heart dropped even as her smallsword flew to First Guard, then up from there to High. The four of them had, somehow, gotten separated—and in that moment, the Dalyn-Davek pair had attacked.

One glance at Instructor Tarik and Librarian Tagg made it abundantly clear that neither considered this an emergency. Neither so much as made a move to intervene.

The apprentices were on their own.

Dalyn/Davek pairs were some of the most dangerous monsters Instructor Clearance had taught them about—and for good reason. Even as Eola reacted, the Dalyn strode toward Roth, and the Davek rushed Colin. They’d split the group into three. The spiders were exceptional at ambushes, at identifying both threats and weaknesses, and at acting as one with their psychically-bonded partner.

Her eyes flicked to Colin, on the wrong side of the room.

“Cover Roth!” Patrice yelled. Her hammer spun in a wide arc, meeting one of the Davek’s mantis blades. Something cracked, and Patrice went flying; the Davek had to outweigh her, armor and all, by at least three or four times.

She was right, though.

“Keep Colin safe!” she screamed back. Her sword flashed, and she started casting with her off-hand as she sprinted after the Dalyn. Her wand drew the spell-chain for Swiftblade, marks filling in quickly, and she poured Mana into it.

Roth hit the ground hard. Eola thought she saw blood, but she couldn’t be sure; the Dalyn was moving too fast, and its body was the same dark red color. She lunged. The smallsword caught in the spider’s back leg, right at a joint. The impact jostled Eola.

But not as badly as the Dalyn’s reaction. It shrieked and whirled while she was halfway through Peacebonded, jarring her wrist and elbow. The spider’s voice cut off, and a clicking, chittering sound filled the air. It sent shivers from the base of Eola’s skull to her tailbone, and sweat suddenly covered her palms.

She kept the spell-chain going through sheer force of will, parried a lightning-fast mantis blade, and kept drawing with her wand hand. The Dalyn was fast—faster than even Ferrin had been with her Second Order spell—and aggressive. Eola knew exactly what it was doing.

The only good news was that it wasn’t trying to kill her. Wounding her or driving her off so it could take Roth would be good enough.

Stilled finished, then Hasty. Eola counterattacked desperately as the two scythes slashed down on her and the Dalyn’s six legs punched shallow craters in the tile floor.

Behind her, something crashed into a shelf. Wood shattered, but she couldn’t afford to glance backward. All she could do was hope Patrice was handling her half of the fighting.

Personal finished. The four modifying marks linked to the Swiftblade rune. Eola uncorked her Mana pool and filled them, one after another. Four bars of Mana—maybe five—gone just like that.

But this time, when the two scythes flashed in, Eola didn’t just parry them. She saw a window. It wasn’t much. A quarter second where the left mantis arm was vulnerable. If she could—

She started sketching, parried another blow, and activated the Safe Shield as the Dalyn pressed its attack. Her whole body was already moving of its own accord when the mantis-spider’s attack shattered the Safe Shield. It had hardly slowed the scythes down, and for a moment, Eola screamed. Had she miscalculated?

No.

A massive wave of heat splashed against her back, thighs, and neck. She ignored it. Her smallsword flashed up as she pushed off the ground. She flicked the blade at the apex of her jump.

The Dalyn shrieked again and skittered away. One of its scythe arms stayed behind.

Eola reset to High Guard. Dalyns could, with time, regenerate lost limbs. That made them tenacious; this one was still in the fight, and they were smart. It would almost certainly keep up the attack—or worse, go for Roth.

She broke into a sprint, sword still held high. The Dalyn’s legs pushed it forward. Then, as Eola lunged at its legs, it stopped. Her attack caught nothing but air, and her feet slid on the ichor-coated tile floor even as she recovered. She’d left herself open. The scythe should be coming down on her back.

But no. The Dalyn shrieked a third time, then started up its chittering. Eola finished recovering to High Guard and eyed it warily.

It took one step back, then another. As it did, Eola pressed forward. Her smallsword probed the monstrous mantis-spider’s defenses, looking for a weakness. Three steps. Four. Five.

Then it reached the edge of the room. Its legs stepped gracefully over the railing, and a thick strand of silk squirted from its spinarette. The wet white rope stuck to the side of the room, and the Dalyn dropped away.

Eola watched it swing under the room—no doubt to its horrid nest—then turned to help Patrice with the…

Very incinerated, very broken corpse of the Davek.

She suddenly felt exhausted as the spike of adrenaline and magic faded from her body. “Good job,” she muttered at the beaming, ichor-covered girl. One quick glance at Colin was enough to tell he was fine. But Roth…Roth had been her responsibility.

He sat on the floor, curled over something and sobbing. It took Eola a moment to figure out what it was.

His satchel had probably saved his life. But that had come with a cost.

The Dalyn’s mantis blade had shorn off a corner of it during its initial attack, and only luck had stopped that from being Roth’s arm. As Eola stared at the destroyed wooden flute in his arms and the look of pure anguish on his face, though, she decided that now wasn’t the time to tell him that.

~Roth Gerr~

Roth felt empty.

He wasn’t injured, and he should still have two bars of Mana left. Maintaining his distraction ward over the others had been tough—they didn’t cooperate under the best conditions—but not that tough. He was already purifying, though. Or at least, he had been.

No, it was the flute.

He’d always been interested in music, ever since he could remember. Clapping, humming, singing nonsense words. Then drumming on barrels and boxes. Music made his heart beat in a way nothing else had as a child. Singing with his mum, or listening to the rare bard who got lost and ended up in the far north of the Spine Mountains, or even thinking about new songs to try out—those were where Roth found joy in the always-freezing, always-painful life he’d been born into.

The village was a harsh place, but song gave it a little warmth, and the alder flute his closest friend, Nathien, had carved him was…had been…the warmest thing in his life. On hunts, they’d huddled together in their tent to keep the freezing wind at bay, and in the village, they were all but inseparable. And Nathien, while he might not have had a musical bone in his body, had always appreciated Roth’s voice and songs. The flute had been…

It had been a promise that they’d have each other’s backs.

The flute was the only one Roth’s father hadn’t broken, but only because Roth had hidden it away. To Goethe, music was a waste of time. There was only survival against the Spine Mountains. Monsters. Wind. Snow so deep it buried the village and forced folks to dig out for hours to find fresh air. No son of his was going to be a Y’aer-cursed bard. Roth would be a hunter, a knife fighter—a man of the village.

Then House Andrese had closed in on the village, and things had gotten worse.

Much, much worse.

Eventually, Roth had…left. The leaving hadn’t been quick, and it hadn’t been happy or painless, either, but he’d done it—just like he’d told Eola. He’d taken his flute, his spellbook, and enough food to get to Varin’s Academy. Magic would be his path forward. Magic, and music. If he hadn’t been hurting so much, he’d have snorted. Music and magic, the love of his life and the thing he’d hoped would keep him alive—and help him avenge Nathien. They’d been one and the same the whole time. Nathien had understood, even if his own father hadn’t bothered. His friend had understood him.

And now, the flute was gone. It was the last good memory he had of his home, and it was gone.

As they pushed through the library, Roth kept thinking about the shattered, split flute in his bag. He had others, but none of them meant what that one had. It hurt, but worse, it felt empty.

It wasn’t just his Mana. He should still have two bars.

But every step rattled the flute’s fragments against each other, and every time they clacked together, his heart broke a little bit more.
