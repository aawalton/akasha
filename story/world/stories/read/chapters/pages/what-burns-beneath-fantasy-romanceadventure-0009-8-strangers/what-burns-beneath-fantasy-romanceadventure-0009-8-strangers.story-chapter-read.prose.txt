I spent quite a while just staring at the door.

It seemed to be made of witchwood; in fact, sections of the whole building seemed to be built out of it. I’d heard that places of rest were often built, at least in part, from beautiful silver branches, but I’d never seen such a structure before. The thought of stepping inside made me feel uneasy. I’d gotten used to the bracelet and its silent beauty, but the idea of being entirely surrounded by unliving gray trees was… unsettling.

I could hear laughter coming from inside. There were a few windows dotted along the gray siding, but my attempts to peer in were obstructed by dusky shadows and smears of mud along the glass.

Laughter means people. Obviously.

Strangers.

For the first time in my life I was going to step into a place devoid of familiar, friendly faces.

I took a breath, then tentatively elbowed the door open. The laughter grew louder, and I was hit full in the face by the overwhelming smells of roasting meat, herbs, and smoke. There were wooden tables spaced throughout a large common area, a bar lined with wooden bowls and plates, and a lit fireplace over on one side. Off to the other side were several doors; they were all closed and had numbers painted on them. I assumed they led to bedrooms.

I stepped inside, flexing my fingers in the warm air. The fireplace gave a comforting crackle. I limped towards it, savoring the flush of heat, while my eyes darted to the patrons.

The laughter had come from a table of dirt-spattered, well-tanned older men who were sitting with tankards and rolling stone dice. One barked out an oath in a tone that made me jump, then took a long drink from his cup. He slammed it back down, belched, and passed the dice to the next man. I watched them with a mixture of nervousness and fascination.

Strangers. People I hadn’t known my entire life. Were they friendly? Safe? They were certainly human, so they couldn’t be worse than the intruder from last night. They didn't seem to notice me, just continued to drink and play their game, so I turned my attention to the three other people in the room.

The first sat quietly in a shadowed corner. They were very slim and wearing a broad brown hat, the brim of which was tipped down low. Beneath it I glimpsed a pert, pointy nose and thin red lips.

My gaze drifted along to the next patron; a man sitting alone and glaring into the contents of a tankard. He had a sharp nose and short, tousled hair the color of chestnuts. His jaw was dark with stubble, as though he hadn’t shaved in several days. I couldn’t help but think that he was quite handsome, albeit in a sort of scruffy way. He didn’t seem to be much older than me, though the dour expression made it hard to be certain.

His eyes slid up to meet mine, and I quickly turned my attention to the crackling flames. When I dared to look back, his head was cocked and a half-smile twisted one corner of his mouth.

I swallowed, glancing towards the bar, behind which the last individual was standing; the owner of the establishment, presumably. He was short and heavyset, with deep lines creasing the sides of his eyes. He had a shiny bald head and a thick, charcoal beard.

I set my things down on a chair, inhaling the scent of meat and herbs. They’re people. Just people. It’s silly to be so nervous. I squared my shoulders and headed towards the innkeeper, legs aching with every step.

The man tipped his head in greeting. “Evening, m’lady. Didn’t expect to have more traffic tonight, but you’re surely welcome.” His voice was gruff, but not unfriendly.

I managed a tiny smile. He’s just a person. He even seems nice. “Thank you. I’m really glad I found this place. I’ve been walking all night and day. I was starting to think I’d have to sleep on the side of the road!”

He looked me up and down. “All night and day? Where’d you come from, miss?”

I leaned my forearms against the bar top. It was a swirled brown palette of stains and smudges. “Fellbrook.”

Bushy black eyebrows shot up.

I held out my bandaged hands. “There was an attack last night. Fae.”

Behind us, the group of men brayed raucously. A tankard slammed down.

The sound made me jump, but turning showed that they were absorbed in their game and paying me no mind. The solitary man was a different story. He was watching me closely, both forearms braced against the table.

“Fae in Fellbrook…” the innkeeper muttered, reclaiming my attention. “You sure? Not to cast doubt, but that place is s’posed to be salted near untouchable.”

“That’s what we thought, too. Listen, is there a Watch outpost nearby? The town’s alright, but people should be warned.”

The innkeeper gave me an odd look. “Warned? Miss, no offense, but nobody north of your town needs to be warned. Not lately.”

“Oh. ‘Not lately.’ Have… have things been very dangerous lately?” I felt heat spread up my neck and added, feeling almost embarrassed, “I’m sorry, we don’t… the town’s very isolated. We don’t get a lot of news about… er, everywhere else.”

“I’m sure. And aye, there’ve been a few more grim tales than usual, the past few months. You should head back to town at first light. The roads are no place for a young lady alone.”

My shoulders sagged. “I’d really rather not be alone. You have no idea, actually.” That earned another odd look. “Ah, sorry. I just meant… I’m sure I’ll be alright, but thank you for your concern. I would love a room, if you have one available.”

“Of course, miss. You can sleep in the common area for free, but I’ve got beds and a hot meal if you can pay.”

“That would be lovely. Please.” I’d had the good sense earlier to transfer some of my new fortune into a small pouch, rather than affix the entire calfskin bag to my girdle. I began to fumble with the little coinpurse. My cloth-wrapped hands burned, slow and clumsy. “How much will it cost?”

“Ten coppers.”

I frowned into the pouch. Bright, shiny gold; no copper or silver to be seen. A well-dressed runaway claiming to be from quiet, simple Fellbrook, carrying a veritable fortune in gold… would he think I’d stolen it?

Well. It’s not as though I can do anything about it. And I shouldn’t complain; having too much money is a silly thing to feel odd about.

I reached into the coinpurse just as a husky voice said, "Put her on my tab."

I caught a whiff of leather and oak and turned, quite certain of who would be standing behind me.

Sure enough, the stranger and his uncanny half-smile stood just a breath away. He wasn't particularly tall and I only had to lift my eyes a bit to meet his. They glinted in the firelight, hued like two copper coins.

Ironic, since that’s exactly what I don’t have. I shouldn’t let him spend his hard-earned money, though. Especially not when I’m carrying around a fortune.

I offered a smile. “Thank you, that's very kind. I can pay, though.”

"I don’t mind, sweet thing.” He slid beside me and half-turned, propping an elbow against the bar. Lean muscle shifted beneath his sleeve. “I gotta say, I didn't expect anything as pretty as you to come wandering out of this salted backwoods."

Heat crept up my neck. "I... I... wait, thing?"

A sound from the corner drew my attention. The slim figure seemed to be choking down a laugh.

The stranger chuckled. The sound held a rusty tinge, as if he didn't do it often, and it held an unpleasant edge. I noticed with a shiver that he had several sheathed daggers and a sword all affixed to a dark leather belt.

"My mistake. Lady, clearly." He leaned forwards, brushing one knuckle against the silver embroidery at my collar.

I flinched, stepping back.

“So.” His gaze slid down. “You’re travelling alone?”

"I… yes, but I don't... that is, I’m not… um, interested.” I cleared my throat. “I'll pay for my own things, thank you."

He frowned, but said nothing as I fumbled into my small bag and placed one too-bright coin onto the bar. Then I practically fled to the fireplace, collapsing into a chair.

I didn’t like this place, I decided with an air of finality. I didn’t like the men shouting nearby, I didn’t like the stranger with his unfriendly smile, and I hated the dead gray walls surrounding me.

I’ll eat, then lock myself into one of those rooms and get some rest. Ashes, I’m so tired. I need to sleep. I’ll feel better after I sleep. And then in the morning I can ask for clearer directions to the next town up. And maybe they’ll have a Watch, or at least people who will be more helpful. Not everyone can be like-

"Story of your life, huh?"

I jumped. The slim figure had approached. Up close and better able to see beneath the broad hat, I could make out her features much better.

She was tall and stick-thin, with high cheekbones and a face that was all angles. Her eyes, bright despite the hat's shadows, were slanted, set wide apart, and a startling shade of crimson. Beneath the hat I could see short, choppy red hair. She looked quite young, maybe a year or two younger than me. She grinned, revealing crooked teeth.

"I'm... sorry?" Salt, I sound dim tonight. I could blame it on my exhaustion after last night’s madness and then having walked all day, but mostly I just felt very out of my depth in this strange building full of... well, strangers.

She swung one lanky arm towards the bar. Her red sleeve was faded and bore several small rips. "Oh, you know! Pretty girl walks into a tavern, and suddenly all the men start drooling and acting like animals." She patted me on the back, giving an exaggerated wink. "That's why us girls have gotta stick together." Her cheeks dimpled as she settled into the chair beside me.

I leaned forwards in cautious optimism. “Would you believe that this is my first… um, ‘tavern’ experience?”

She cocked her head. "What, they don't have one in… Fellbrook, was it? I heard you talking about what happened.”

“Yes. Fellbrook. And we do have a tavern, but… I suppose it’s different? In a little town like that, everyone knows each other.”

“Right, makes sense.” Her head bobbed up and down. She crossed long, slender legs and leaned back in her chair, eyeing me curiously.

I glanced down, noticing holes and patched areas in her baggy brown trousers. I bit my lip. Those, and the ripped shirt, couldn't be comfortable to travel in, especially with the weather growing colder. Maybe I could offer her some of my spare clothes.

"I'm Teela, by the way."

“It’s very nice to meet you, Teela. I’m Brin.”

The sound of sloshing liquid, followed by the jingle of coins, drew my gaze towards the bar. The unfriendly stranger had brought his tankard back up for a refill.

“Yeah, you too! So Fellbrook was attacked?”

“Last night. It was terrible. There was a Fae man, and he had these shadowy monsters with him. Like dogs, but big and vicious. One came right into my bedroom, even though the door was shut!”

She gave a low whistle. “Wow. I haven’t seen a Fae before. Did anyone get hurt?”

“Just my friend. Royce. But he…” My hands clenched. The surge of hot pain made me tremble, but I managed to keep my voice steady. “He’s alright.”

“Well, that’s a grace. Hey, looks like your food’s ready. I’ll get it!”

She hopped up and pirouetted towards the bar. Her shirt was tightly tucked in the back and bulged a bit, almost like she had a coil of rope underneath it. On the way up she tripped a bit, caught herself on one of the game-player's chairs, chirped an apology, then continued towards the food. A moment later she returned, a steaming plate balanced in one slender hand.

I accepted my meal with a smile. "Thank-”

A large, sweaty hand clapped onto her shoulder. The man whose chair Teela had bumped into loomed behind her. “Salt-blasted lowlife.”

Teela tried to pull away, but his grip held firm. "Um, excuse me," her voice was shrill, "But I really don't know what-!"

He yanked her to him, thrusting out his other hand. "Give it back, scum.” Then, over his shoulder, "Check your belts; this one's a pickpocket." He shook Teela, hard, and she paled. "Give it back!"

I stood. "Let her go! You can't just accuse people of-"

Teela twisted, giving a little whimper. She managed to slide free and the man lunged forward, and then several things happened at once.

The first was that he tripped over Teela’s chair and fell straight into her. His weight sent both of them crashing to the floor. Her hat fell off, and my jaw dropped.

There were two sharp, flame-colored horns, similar in shape to those of a small goat, poking out from the crown of her head.

The man shot to his feet, a leather coinpurse held triumphantly in one hand. “Salted thief... and tainted, no less.” He spat at her feet. "Should've guessed. Filthy half-breed."

Tainted? I stared at the young woman. Half-breed?

I’d heard of her kind, but we’d never had any in town. Fae-touched; the children born of a human and Fae coupling. Durst said they weren’t common; most monsters killed their victims, in one horrible way or another, after whatever torments they saw fit to dole out. But every now and then some poor human woman would return home and bear a child marked by wicked, violent parentage. I’d never heard anyone speak of such unfortunate people with derision or anger, merely pity.

I shook off my immediate shock and helped her up. “Are you alright?"

Teela bolted for the tavern door.

The man lunged forward and grabbed her again, and this time his friends came forward. Their faces were twisted into cruel, flushed leers.

“Let me go!" she shrieked, trying to pull free. The bulge from under the back of her shirt shifted abruptly, revealing a sharply pointed, flesh-colored tail as long and thick as my arm. It lashed frantically from side to side. "You got your stupid purse back- let's call it even!”

“Even?" The man’s jowls were shaking. He shot a look towards the innkeeper, who was watching the scene with his massive arms folded. “You serve her kind here?"

To my shock, the man shook his head. "I surely don't. But if you're looking to shed blood, take it outside."

“Hear that, mongrel?" He dragged the struggling, white-faced young woman towards the door. "Even... think I'll carve a pair of salted horns, maybe then we'll be even.”

“Let her go!” I rushed after the pair, but one of the other men blocked my path. "S-stop this! She's a thief, but you can't... this isn't right!"

The door slammed shut. The figure before me looked down in vague disbelief.

I spread my hands, entreating him. "Surely there's... I don't know, a guardsman or a Watch or something nearby! We can turn her into the proper authorities and-"

He laughed. It was a harsh, ugly sound. "Proper authorities? Cute. There's only one brand of justice for tainted. Stay here, or you'll get the same as her." He turned and lumbered out the door.

There was a horrible sound from outside, like flesh hitting against flesh, and then a man's voice loudly swearing.

I whirled towards the innkeeper. "Sir, we have to stop them!”

He fixed me with a grim, stern look. "You seem like a sweet girl. Turn your eye."

My jaw dropped. Were these people mad? Surely this couldn't be what the world was like outside of Fellbrook; drunk, hateful men dispensing violent justice as they pleased.

Upon seeing my expression, his face softened. "There's no place here for half-breeds preying on hardworking folk. Her kind are a disease."

The finality of his statement made any further attempts to ask for his help die on my tongue. I turned to the only other person in the room; the man who'd returned to drinking alone. He had pointedly ignored the chaos.

"We have to do something," I pleaded, hurrying to his table.

“Sorry, ladyship." He eyed the door, lips curled. There wasn't any trace of apology in his tone. “She’s a thief. I’m not spilling blood for a criminal.”

“She’s a thief, but she doesn’t deserve… They’re going to hurt her!” He offered no response and, emboldened by sheer desperation, I slammed a hand onto the wood. Silverware rattled. “They might kill her!”

He took a long drink. Then raised his mug, as if in a toast, towards the entrance. “Tell you what; leave the door cracked behind you. If one of you starts dying, holler and I’ll step out.”

A feminine cry sounded from outside.

I spun and, shaking with frustration and disbelief, ran to the door.
