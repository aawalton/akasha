The night before the assault, the entirety of the Salaqa Region breathed like a great beast that knew dawn would come with teeth. Hundreds of carts rattled on the stone lanes across the city, quartermasters counted coins in the dark, and men muttered prayers into their sleeves while women tied charms to bayonets and pretended they were just knots. Nerves sharpened every footstep as everyone prepared for the coming battle that’d make or break the Attini Empire—but Zora alone sat in the last garden that still remembered it was a garden.

This particular garden behind the Salaqa Manor was the only one that hadn’t been swallowed by barracks, stables, tents, or war tables. This particular garden kept a little pond and walls of willows, and it kept a bench and a cobbled path that remembered soft shoes. Midnight dew wore the smell of mint and earth, and he inhaled the cool, basking in the crisp air that he may not get to feel again for a little while.

For him, it was a cool, surprisingly gentle night. The first in a long while.

He’d had three months to slay and consume more bug meat in the eastern end of the empire than he’d ever consumed in the first two years of his long march, and he put most of his points into strength, toughness, and perception. After all, if he wanted to cast ‘earth wall’ and make it larger, he had to believe he had the physical strength to rip out the earth wall from the ground itself, so strength was, by far, his most important attribute. Toughness was so he could cast stronger ‘barriers’ and ‘blocks’, while perception was useful because he was blind, but… well, speed was just for dodging and general agility. He wasn’t a melee fighter, so he didn’t see much use in raising its level.

A great defense is a strong offense, or so the Nocturna say.

Apart from raising his attribute levels, he’d also unlocked two more branch mutations for ‘Basic Digestion’ and ‘Basic Wings’. Since he’d been hunting bugs in the supremely dense colossal fungi forests of the east for the past three months, there were many days when he didn’t want to eat bug meat, but also didn’t have much choice of food around him. ‘Lignin Digestion’ allowed him to get better tastes and nutrients out of fungi bark, and ‘Resonant Wings’ allowed him to beat his wings even faster than he already could, adding an extra layer of scare for the bugs whenever he approached them.

And if they feared him, they were weaker.

… How long would it take me to beat Decima at my current level, I wonder?

As he flexed his hand, a branch cracked, and soft footsteps crossed the gravel behind him. Kita wore ordinary noble sleepwear, loose with silk fabric yet elegant in form, but unless she went to sleep carrying two sawtooth blades, he presumed she wasn’t here to try out a different bed.

Zora smiled faintly.

“You’re up late,” he said. “Or up early, if you prefer the monk’s clock.”

“I’m neither,” she said, voice level. She took her place at the far opposite end of the garden, as if drawing a line with her feet. “I want to fight.”

He turned his head. The pond beside them made a soft circle when a leaf fell into it, and he imagined her reflected there: hair tied back and shoulders set, the kind of stress that a young lady shouldn’t be bearing before bed. “We fought three months ago in the academy already,” he said, “and if you don’t remember, I already lost that fight.”

“That was you restrained.”

She ripped her sawtooth blades out of their sheaths.

“With everything you have,” she said sternly. “I want to fight again.”

He weighed her tone. There was no performance in it—shed come to scrape the bottom of her own courage and see if it squeaked.

So he rose slowly and took his staff, feeling the smooth black chitin creaking into his palm.

“All right.” He sighed. “But if I accidentally redecorate the garden, I want it on record that it was your choice.”

“I don’t mind.”

“Of course you don’t,” he said, grinning. “Hearts that mean to be brave never mind the bill.”

He thumped the staff’s butt once in the grass, feeling the earth wake under it. The willows answered with a rustle. The pond drew a deeper breath. Across the way, Kita set her feet and reared her sawtooth blades back.

Half the garden lay in injured patterns. A few willows leaned against the brick wall. A bed of herbs had been pushed aside by the rude elbows of earth-spikes. The pond had claimed many fallen branches, and mounds of dirt sat where they shouldn't be, which annoyed Zora more than he liked to admit.

“... Fix the garden, please.”

His voice rippled out, and the willow trees hovered before floating over to where they once rooted. The mounds of dirt reburied the trees. Fallen branches lifted out of the pond and settled in neat piles beside the water. The wind repaired the garden that he'd half-destroyed himself, but all things considered, the destruction could've been much worse.

Kita lay flat on her back in the grass, chest heaving as if the air itself was too heavy. Her sawtooth blades still rested in her hands—she hadn’t dropped them, which Zora respected—but sweat laced her temple, and she was definitely out for the count.

She'd lost.

“It's not really fair,” he said, glancing at her as he took his seat on the bench again.

“I know.”

“In a wide arena, you must first close the distance while I rip up the earth and barrage you with spells from afar. If you want a fair battle, you can choose a smaller ring where I have less time to wear you down.”

Kita rolled onto her side and sat up slowly, shaking her head. “There is no such thing as a fair fight.”

And as she picked herself up so she could walk over, plopping herself down on the bench beside him, he gave her a small, wry smile.

“Still annoyed?” he asked.

“... Strangely, no,” she said quietly. “The girl you met when we first crossed paths would have been furious. The sole heiress of the Salaqa Household losing in a battle is not something she could've accepted… but after what happened in the academy, I've come to see that the Swarm do not care for our pride and honour.”

“Oh?”

“There are outliers. The Walking Legends. The men who can best the Swarm by themselves,” she said, “but I am an heiress to an empire made of millions, and the empire was never meant to stand on one pair of legs. We are meant to be many.”

She looked out across the garden, where the willows had regained a measure of elegance. “This civil war, and the way the Outer Regions have come together despite a decade of humiliation at the hands of the Divine Capital… I was childish to have put so much pride in my ability to beat you one-on-one. If I fall, someone else will pick up my blade. If they fall, someone else will pick up theirs. That is the true essence of the Attini Empire.”

“So what you mean to say…”

“Is that if I can clone myself a hundred times, I can probably tire you out and beat you eventually,” she grumbled at last, sulking a little. “It's not fair you're strong in long range and close range.”

Zora huffed a laugh and leaned back. “Enki is an outlier as well. Cloning himself, opening wormholes in the air… at least my spells are constrained by what I can imagine myself doing. I can never imagine opening a wormhole.”

“Where did he come from, anyways?”

“I have no idea.” He glanced around and took note of the boy in the distance, who was sitting cross-legged on the roof of the manor, staring up at the moon. “But I suppose it doesn't matter either way as long as he hates the Swarm as much as I do.”

The night flowed around them. In some distant courtyard, a patrolling soldier tried to sing low and stopped after two bars when the other soldiers around him started laughing.

Laughter was good. They may not get much of it after tomorrow.

“... What else did you write?” Kita asked after a while.

Zora tilted his head, trying to recall.

“A handful of children’s books,” he said slowly. “Little Things with Good Bones. The Wind and the Stone. A Sparrow Counts the Moon, and The Boy Who Traded His Name for a Ladder. Most of them found bookstores in a few small towns, but only a few got popular enough to be widely circulated in the large cities.”

“I can't believe I recognize two of them,” she muttered. “The covers were garish, weren’t they?”

“That wasn’t me,” he said, amused. “The Fabre Household did all of the printing and the selling. I wrote them purely because I was bored, but the adults in the household sent them out like seeds to spread the Fabre name even wider. It helps us get more jobs, you see ”

Kita started fidgeting at that, the movement small but unmistakable—fingers worrying the edge of her sleeve, eyes fixed somewhere that wasn’t him. Of course he noticed. For someone so composed in command, the shift was almost endearing.

Then, as if catching herself in the act, she exhaled sharply and drew a folded sheet of fine paper from her sleeve.

“What’s this?” he asked.

She hesitated, then held it out at him.

“An… autograph,” she said plainly, though her voice carried a touch of embarrassment. “You’re the writer of The Earthen Princess. It’s been a dream of mine to meet the original author one day, so… uh, can I—”

“No.”

She looked so crestfallen he immediately laughed.

“Just kidding.”

“... Screw you.”

He took the sheet, and she handed him a quill. The tip scratched faintly as he wrote, but as he curled his name across the page, a sudden spark of memory crossed his mind.

“Ah,” he said. “Speaking of names and signatures, I just remembered something.”

“What is it?” she asked, a small smile lifting as he handed the autograph back.

“A piece of reading,” he said—then corrected himself with a wry tilt of his head. “Listening, rather. I had Sora tell me a bit about the Attini Empire’s so-called ‘divine bloodline’.”

“And what about the divine bloodline?”

“By tradition, when a Divine Empress dies, Her Four Households choose the closest and oldest living female descendant of the First Divine Empress to take the throne,” he began. “Since the First Divine Empress was the strongest and most powerful of all fifty-six Regional Lords when the Swarm first arrived, the fifty-six regions unanimously decided that she alone should lead the newly formed ‘Attini Empire’, while the four households bordering her southernmost region swore to serve her directly as Her Four Households. Those five regions were thus combined into one large region now named the Divine Capital, the first and final bastion against the Swarm in the south.”

Kita nodded once. “Yes, that is the history as we were taught. What are you—”

“And to honor the First Divine Empress, who was so charismatic, intelligent, and powerful that she rallied all fifty-six regions into one empire like a divine being, the lax fixed the custom: all future lords of the empire must be female, and they must be a descendent of the First Divine Empress. That has been the empire’s tradition for sixty-three years, and we both know you people love your traditions,” he said. “But I was curious, so I had Sora do a bit more reading for me in some very old records—ones only the Spinneret Society keeps—and I noticed something… strange.”

“Strange how?”

He settled his staff against his knee and hummed. “When the First Divine Empress perished about fifty or so years ago, the Second Divine Empress that was chosen wasn’t the oldest female descendent of the First Divine Empress. She was the second-oldest. The second daughter of the First Divine Empress.”

Kita blinked. “The second daughter?”

“Yes. Because the first daughter was willful. She was aggressive. She liked to fight her own battles against the Swarm rather than let courtiers script them. She read and collected childish storybooks instead of ‘proper’ literature, and—several years before the First Divine Empress died—when an advantageous marriage to a powerful Noble-Blood household was arranged for her, she refused. She thought the man was weak, and so she did not like him… and when pressure from Her Four Households became force, she left the Divine Temple entirely.”

Kita’s silence had edges.

“In short,” he finished, “she was a massive problem child for politics and public image. So, when it became time to choose the Second Divine Empress, Her Four Households decided—quietly—to scrub away all records of the first daughter’s existence and that the second daughter should be crowned. Even though, by bloodright, the first daughter should have been the Second Divine Empress instead. Since then, it has been the second daughter’s descendants who have become the next four Divine Empresses, including the one we shall be meeting tomorrow.”

The willow leaves made a patient sound above them, and somewhere in the manor, a latch touched wood and settled. Zora heard the tiny give of old iron and knew the door had been rehung last month, but he let the sound pass before speaking again.

“You’re wondering what I’m trying to say,” he said.

“... Yes.” Kita exhaled, narrowing her eyes. “What are you trying to say?”

“That I simply wished to know what became of the first daughter who left the Divine Temple,” he said simply. “So I had Sora look into her history a little further.”

He felt Kita lean a hair closer. “And? What did you find out?”

“The first daughter eventually married into a household of her choosing, to a man she did love and respect for his strength. She had a family with him. Of course, she abandoned her real identity and her bloodline, but they didn’t seem to matter to her at all, because it wouldn’t have taken Sora more than a few hours to find her if she went around claiming she was the daughter of the First Divine Empress.”

Kita brimmed with curiosity. “And? Where is she now? Is she still alive?”

“Of course not. She died over forty years ago alongside her husband when she fought to defend her town from a Swarm infestation,” he said plainly. “But her descendants still live on, and…”

He stopped there and let the words remain what they were.

Suddenly, he didn’t quite feel like saying the rest of it.

Kita waited. Then, with a crease of mock-injury, “You’re leaving me on a terrible, terrible cliff.”

“... I guess it’s not relevant to us at all, is it?” he said, shaking his head with a sly grin. “If all goes well tomorrow, the Sixth Divine Empress will remain the Divine Empress, and hopefully your father—and the rest of the empire—will do a better job of keeping her from being manipulated by bugs again. The Attini Empire is built on traditions, and the one tradition that mustn’t change is the Divine Empress. There must always be a Divine Empress on the throne, for she alone is the symbol that represents the blood of the southern earth.”

She kept pestering him, poking him to tell her the rest of the story—like who were the first daughter’s descendants, and where they were now—but he still didn’t feel like it, so he only stood and stretched his arms over his head until his shoulders clicked softly.

“Another story for another day,” he concluded. “For now, how about one more round of sparring before we kill the Magicicada Witch tomorrow and end this nonsense for good?” He tapped his staff against the ground, half an invitation, half a tease. “I’ll let you stand a bit closer this time. Might help your chances at winning.”

The heiress went quiet at that.

Then her brows knit, and a small scowl tugged her mouth down.

“Don’t look down on me, Thousand Tongue.”
