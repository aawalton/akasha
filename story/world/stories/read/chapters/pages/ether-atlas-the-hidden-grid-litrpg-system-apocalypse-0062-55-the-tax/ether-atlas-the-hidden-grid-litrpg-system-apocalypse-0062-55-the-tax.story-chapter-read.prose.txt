The boy couldn’t have been older than fifteen.

He was walking beside his father through the market clearing, left arm in a sling improvised from a bedsheet, the fabric spotted brown where blood had dried. His right hand held a cloth pouch. Three cores inside, from the shape of it. Arthur watched them from the watchtower shade where he was testing his grip on the newly bound hammer, getting used to the forward-biased weight.

Two of the Baron’s fighters intercepted them at the eastern edge of the market. The taller fighter held out his hand. The father said something in Maltese. The fighter shook his head and said two words Arthur didn’t need translated.

Temple tax.

The father opened the pouch. There were three cores inside. The fighter took two. Left one.

The boy’s face didn’t change, as if he had expected this. The father hadn’t. He spoke again, faster, one hand gesturing toward his son’s sling. The fighter looked at the arm then at the cores and shrugged.

Arthur’s legs carried him forward. The decision arrived somewhere around stride three.

He crossed the clearing in eight strides. He hadn’t raised the hammer. He hadn’t needed to. The fighter’s eyes went to the Ironhold first, then to the Ether luminescence along the hammer’s head, then to Arthur’s frame.

“Give them back,” Arthur said.

The fighter faced him. He was in his mid-thirties, thick through the chest, and his right hand was wrong. The knuckles were swollen and ridged, the skin stretched over bone that had been rewritten. Core absorption.

“Temple tax,” the fighter said. “You want to live in the zone, you want to fight in the zone, you pay.”

“The boy’s arm is in a sling. He earned those cores. Give them back.”

The fighter’s mutated hand closed around the cores. His body dropped into a fighting stance he probably didn’t know he was taking.

Donna had appeared behind the father and son. She’d come from the watchtower on one crutch, the axe held low in her free hand, her weight balanced on her good leg. She flanked, on one leg.

“My friend,” the fighter said to Arthur. “This is not your business.”

“Archie,” Arthur said, without looking away. “His level.”

“Seven. Mutation consistent with mid-grade goat-derived core absorption. Aggression markers elevated.”

Steve arrived at the edge of the clearing. He raised the focus rod, light building at its edge. The threat was the display, not the discharge.

The standoff held for three seconds.

A woman pushed through the gathering crowd. She was in her late thirties, professional posture even in civilian clothing. One of the Baron’s lieutenants. She assessed the scene in a glance. The father, the boy, the cores, the fighter’s stance, the engineer with the bound hammer and the geometric intelligence floating behind his shoulder.

“Mela,” she said to the fighter, and then they exchanged rapid Maltese. The fighter’s jaw worked, but he listened. The lieutenant took one core from his hand and held it out to the father.

“A gesture of goodwill,” she said in English. Her accent was softer than the fighter’s, the consonants rounder. She smiled. Professional. “A misunderstanding.”

The father took the core. He looked at it, and said, “Grazzi.” Thank you. For the theft of a third of what was his.

The lieutenant steered the fighter away. Arthur watched the father put the single core in the pouch beside the one he’d been allowed to keep. Two of three. Arthur’s grip on the hammer tightened.

As Arthur turned, there were shrieks from the southern perimeter. And the eastern wall. And the western ridge behind the watchtower. All at once.

The first lizard came over the southern wall at a dead sprint. It was the length of Arthur’s arm, built low and wide, four legs splayed for grip on the vertical stone. Maltese wall lizard stretched to nightmare proportions. The head was flattened and widened, the jaw hinged for a gape that showed rows of teeth like broken glass.

Behind it came more. They poured over the walls on three sides, adhesive pads gripping the limestone, bodies flat against the rock. Arthur counted eight. Ten. Stopped counting at twelve.

The market erupted.

Arthur set his feet and struck the shield with his hammer.

The ring carried across the clearing and eight of the lizards locked on the sound, turning mid-stride on the wall. Eight. That left seven unaccounted for. Seven creatures in a market full of civilians.

He set his feet and braced. The first lizard launched from the wall in a horizontal leap that covered four metres. Arthur caught it on the Ironhold. Sharp and fast, nothing like the guardian’s geological weight. The creature bounced off the shield, and the hammer caught it before it landed. Chitin cracked and it dropped.

The second and third came together. Arthur deflected one with the shield edge and stamped on the other. His new strength baseline made the stamp something the creature’s ribcage couldn’t survive. The deflected one scrambled upright, and he brought the hammer down on it before it could leap again.

Steve’s concussive blast hit two on the southern wall simultaneously. At this range, against creatures that weighed less than a dog, the force was terminal. They fell and didn’t move.

A sixth came at Arthur’s shield arm from below. He caught it on the hammer’s backswing and felt the chitin give.

A seventh launched at his face. He got the shield up. The impact rang through his arm and the creature fell, stunned. He finished it with the hammer.

Seven kills. The shield was slick with something that smelled awful.

The screaming hadn’t stopped.

Across the clearing, Azzopardi’s soldiers were fighting the remaining eight in a chaos that had no formation and no choreography. The creatures were among the civilians now, running between legs, launching from the ground, from the walls, from the slab where the woman had been trading meat for cores. The slab was overturned. The woman was on the ground.

A soldier hacked a lizard off a man’s arm with a machete. Another pinned one to the ground with a sharpened stake. Two more soldiers cornered a third against the watchtower base and killed it between them.

Four lizards broke and ran, carrying pieces of the market with them. One had something in its jaws that Arthur’s brain refused to process for a full second before it resolved. A human forearm, severed below the elbow, the fingers still curled. Another dragged something heavier across the limestone, leaving a red trail that caught the afternoon sun.

Donna threw.

The axe turned once and buried itself in the skull of a lizard lunging at a woman who’d fallen near the eastern wall, pinning the creature to the stone a hand’s width from the woman’s face.

“Someone get my axe!” Donna shouted.

A soldier wrenched it free and sprinted it back to her. Donna caught it, sighted, and threw again. The second throw crossed thirty metres of clearing and hit a lizard that was climbing back over the western wall with meat in its teeth. It dropped both the meat and itself.

But four had already gone. Over the walls and into the scrubland, fast enough that pursuit was pointless.

“Lacertidae mutation,” Archie said. “Adhesive lamellae on the limbs. Gecko-convergent, not standard lizard scaling. This island’s fauna is developing three-dimensional hunting adaptations within two weeks of saturation.” His geometry rotated toward the walls where the survivors were clinging to each other. “This is ahead of schedule.”

The clearing went quiet except for the sounds that come after violence. Breathing. Groaning. Crying. Shouting.

Arthur’s hands were shaking as he set the hammer down and looked across the clearing and wished he hadn’t.

Two people were on the ground and not moving. A man near the overturned slab, throat torn, eyes open. A woman near the eastern wall who’d gone down in the first seconds and hadn’t got up. A soldier was kneeling beside her, pressing cloth to a wound that the cloth couldn’t fix.

Two more were alive and screaming. A man clutching the stump where his forearm had been, blood pulsing between his fingers in rhythmic jets. A teenage girl, sitting against the watchtower base and staring at her left leg, which ended below the knee in a mess of torn muscle and shattered bone. The lizard had taken the lower limb and run.

The notifications arrived with their usual indifference.

Arthur crossed the clearing to the Baron’s lieutenant, who was standing at the edge of the carnage with her four fighters. They had their weapons drawn but had not moved to help. They were making a statement.

“Get the healer,” Arthur said.

The lieutenant looked at the two people bleeding out on the ground. The man with the stump. The girl with the leg.

“I’m sorry, my friend. The Doctor has a full schedule today.” She said it with the same rehearsed courtesy she’d used for “a gesture of goodwill.” “And these two, even if she were here, the Doctor cannot regrow what is missing. They will not survive the hour.”

“They will survive the hour if someone stops the bleeding. I’ve seen her work. She can close an arterial wound in minutes.”

“The Doctor’s schedule is not mine to change.”

Arthur looked at her, at the fighters who’d arrived after the fighting was done.

“You were close enough to hear the shriek,” he said. “You arrived after they were dead.”

The lieutenant’s smile didn’t change. “We arrived when it was safe to assess the situation, my friend. The Baron’s personnel are not first responders. We are an organised force. Organised forces assess before committing.”

Arthur turned away from her. Steve was kneeling beside the man with the stump, using his belt as a tourniquet, his blistered hands fumbling with the buckle. Azzopardi’s soldiers were working on the girl. Her screaming had stopped, which was worse than the screaming.

The woman near the eastern wall was dead. The soldier kneeling beside her had placed the cloth over her face. The man by the slab had been dead since the first seconds.

Arthur pressed his hands flat against the watchtower wall until the shaking stopped. The stone was warm. The sun was still shining. Two people had bled out on limestone older than the pyramids, and the Baron’s lieutenant was walking back down the southern track with her fighters, unhurried, their weapons still clean.

“Their staging position was within audible range,” Archie said. His geometry was dim, the facets turning slowly. “The delay between the first contact and their arrival was ninety-four seconds. That is not a response time. That is a policy.”

Sarah heard about it that evening. Two dead. Two more might not make it through the night. The military camp’s only medical knowledge was a soldier who’d done a first-aid course, and he’d used the word “tourniquet” like it was a prayer.

She sat in her room with the door bolted from the outside. She put her hands in her lap and looked at them. She could have closed the arterial bleed in four minutes. She could have stabilised the girl’s leg in ten. She could have saved both of them if someone had come to get her, and nobody had come to get her because the Baron’s lieutenant had decided the dying weren’t worth the Doctor’s time.

Her hands were shaking on her knees. She couldn’t tell if it was exhaustion or rage or the grief of a doctor who knows exactly what she could have done and wasn’t allowed to do it.

She thought about the man with the shield. The one who’d put himself between the Baron’s fighters and a civilian family. The one who’d held the creatures on himself while the market came apart.

Someone had told her he’d asked for her. Demanded her. And been told no.

THE GIFT

Grazzi. The father said grazzi for a third of his own property. I have been awake three hours trying to write around it and I cannot.

Marcel Mauss published "Essai sur le don" in 1925, trying to make sense of Polynesian gift-exchange systems, the kula ring in the Trobriand Islands, the potlatch ceremonies of the Pacific Northwest. His conclusion was that the gift is never free. Every gift creates an obligation. The recipient owes a counter-gift, and if the counter-gift cannot match the original, the asymmetry persists as social hierarchy. Mauss thought he was describing pre-state economies. He was describing the operating system.

The lieutenant did not return property today. She created a debt. The father walked out of that market understanding, at a level he probably could not articulate, that he is now in her column on a ledger he never agreed to. The next time the Baron's people come for cores, he will not argue. The lieutenant's gesture of goodwill will be the reason. She was kind once. She might be kind again. Kindness is rationed. The cost of arguing is losing access to a kindness he is already counting on.

The same protocol runs everywhere I look. The Inca mit'a took labour from a household and returned a portion as ceremonial food at festival. The Roman patron-client relationship took political loyalty from a freedman and returned bread, legal protection, a place at the funeral. American sharecropping took the harvest and returned a settled tab at the company store. King Leopold's concession system in the Congo took rubber by the tonne and returned brass tokens stamped with the Crown's insignia. Every one of those systems looks, from inside, like generosity meeting need. From outside, every one is the same line of code running on different hardware.

If the protocol is this consistent across this much time and this many cultures, it is not a cultural artefact. It is load-bearing infrastructure of the species. Baked in before agriculture. Expressed identically in every social configuration that develops a power asymmetry. The Baron is not running a strategy. He is running the default.

✎ The lieutenant smiled when she handed back the core. The father will tell his son that the Maltese say grazzi even to people who steal from them, because if you stop saying it the next visit costs more.
