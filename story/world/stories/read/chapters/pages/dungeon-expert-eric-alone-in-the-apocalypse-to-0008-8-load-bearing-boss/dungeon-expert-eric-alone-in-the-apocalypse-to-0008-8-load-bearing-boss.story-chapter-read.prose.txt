Eric rushed out of the forest, charging directly at the tent. His socked feet slapped against the hard-packed earth, and his snakeskin tunic bounced against his thighs. He charged silently, his jaw set in determination, his eyes on the snakeman directly between him and the tent—no; on a point six inches behind it. That was where he’d swing to. Just like his father had taught him: swing through. Not at the ball; through it.

The snakeman hissed and crouched, lowering and bracing its spear, ready for Eric’s charge. Seconds before he reached the monster, Eric slammed his foot down and dodged to the left, then unleashed a massive swing. The monster’s head and spear both were knocked aside, and it slumped to the ground.

He’d always been a lefty. It meant he rarely hit a ball from the rec league pitchers who weren’t used to pitching to left-handed players, but he’d swung at them anyways just for fun. He hadn’t been particularly good at baseball, for some reason. It was a real mystery. Also, outfield was so boring, so he mostly ended up picking daisies and building houses from plucked grass. His dad’s dreams of him becoming an ace baseball player were dashed before he hit grade school.

But it did mean that the monster’s right-handed spear grip was easier for him to knock aside, and as the next snakeman closed in, he wound up once more, planting his feet and squaring his stance. “Home run,” he whispered to himself, and swung for the fences. Blood splattered against the tent.

A hiss from behind and the skitter of claws on dirt was his only warning. Eric jumped to the side, and a spear thrust inches from his chest, slashing the underside of his arm as it passed. He clamped his arm down and twisted his body. The snakeman resisted, but it couldn’t fight Eric’s superior bodymass. He wrenched the spear out of its hands, then released it. It clattered to the ground. Spinning on the spot, he found the snakeman too close to smash with the bat. He yanked the knife out of his belt and slashed the snakeman’s throat. It gargled on its own blood, clawing at its neck as it dropped to its knees.

Two guards remained. One dropped its spear and ran, leaving Eric to his rampage. The other let out a fierce cry and charged. Eric dropped the knife in favor of the bat, and one more snake head met wood.

For a second, he just stood there, panting. Bodies laid all around him. He bent and picked up his knife as they began to smoke, both their bodies and their blood washed away by the System’s garbage retrieval system, or whatever it was that destroyed them after combat. Once more, he had no option to pick up a spear as the weapons dematerialized along with the monsters.

There were still a few snakemen in the camp, but none of them could see him from where he stood in front of the tent, and most of them were too busy dealing with the chaos he’d created, anyways. He patted his pockets. Still a few candies there, ready to go. He’d equipped his weapons, and his shoddy disguise-slash-armor was still holding strong. Everything was in order. He gritted his teeth and strode into the tent before he could double-guess himself. Defeating the boss was a clear condition. It was time to get the job done.

He lifted the flap, letting it fall behind him. The interior of the tent hung in perpetual twilight, only a small section of the tent illuminated by daylight for a split second while he stepped inside. He stepped softly into the space, looking around. It was a massive space, as big as a big top, but completely empty on the inside. Just a huge hollow space with nothing inside it. The tent draped overhead and a hard dirt floor let up tiny clouds of dust as he walked deeper inside. In the center of the tent, a huge pole held its lofts high, and smaller supporting poles held it up at the sides. But aside from the supports, it was empty.

Eric frowned, looking around. Was it really empty? Why would they guard an empty tent? He focused on his Mana Sight, but still saw nothing of note. The mana in the tent was almost perfectly distributed, nothing but a wall of pale gold. Usually there were currents or tides in the mana, but here, it was completely still. Was it still because he was indoors? Or was something else going on?

I’m going to bet on ‘something else,’ Eric thought, gripping his bat tight. He stepped further into the tent, grateful that his socked feet made his steps almost silent. There had to be something in here. The fact that he couldn’t see it didn’t make it any less real. It’s like the snakes that I couldn’t see in Mana Sight, just an advanced version of that. He swallowed, extending his bat out in front of him. If the snakes were invisible to his mana senses, then the boss might be just plain invisible as well as invisible to his Mana Sight. One step at a time, he went deeper into the room, bat stretched out ahead of him so he wouldn’t smack into a hypothetical invisible boss face-first.

On the other side of the tent, something stirred. Scales scraped against dirt. Eric fell still, listening with all his might, then saw it; or rather, he didn’t see it. Dust rose off the floor, clouding out on either side of what must have been an enormous, generally round body. There was a dry brush of scales-on-scales and a soft hiss, and the roof of the tent bulged slightly where a body pressed into it.

Eric swallowed and took a step back. This thing was big. Big big. I might have bitten off more than I can chew.

No. Eric pushed his doubts back. Joe had believed in him. Joe had thought he could kill it. Joe didn’t want to send him to his death… not until he’d made Joe millions, or whatever, anyways.

That’s right. Believe in Joe’s greed. He wouldn’t have sent me here unless I could beat it.

He drew his Magical Wand and backed up, finger primed to press the wand’s on switch. Ditching the bat into his belt—what could it do against a snake that monstrously huge, anyways—he drew his dagger instead. Better to believe in the 10% poison chance than hope +1 made a difference against something that immense. I can do this. I can defeat it. I have to believe that, or the battle is already over.

“Here goes nothing,” Eric muttered to himself.

The second he spoke, the tent fabric shifted. He couldn’t see the snake’s head, but he felt its gaze on him nonetheless. The tent fabric went loose again, no longer pushed up from below, and wind rushed at him. He threw himself to the side. Scales clipped his ankles, and a beat later, dust flew as something scraped into the ground at the entrance to the tent where he’d been moments ago.

The tent flap flew, blown by the force of the snake’s impact. Sunlight beamed through the flap, and for a split second, Eric caught a glimpse of the snake’s snout and deadly, slitted eyes as it gazed directly at him. For a split second, both of them just stood there, frozen. Then he turned and ran, and it chased after him.

Wait—I can see it? Eric glanced over his shoulder, but the snake was invisible again. He could spot it only by the dust thrown up by its massive body as it moved… and by a small black coil of the massive snake’s body by the door, illuminated where the sun fell on its scales.

His eyes widened. He looked up at the top of the tent. They weren’t just keeping the snake in here as some sort of housing. The tent itself was part of the snake’s arena! Without the tent, the snake would be laid bare in the sun… or draped by the tent, and either way he’d be able to see it.

One massive, central pole held up the tent. Eric spun and charged toward it, hearing the rush of scales on hardpacked earth as the snake gave chase. He had a plan. He didn’t know if it was a good one, but it was better than not having a plan.

In a few short paces, the pole stood before him. It was wider than he’d realized, like a full-sized tree had been stripped of branches and tarred with pitch. Eric reached into his pocket. He tossed a grandma candy in his mouth and clenched it between his teeth, not yet sucking it for the antidote inside. Lifting the Magical Wand, he flicked the switch.

LEDs shone, making the tinsel inside sparkle. Twinkle Twinkle Little Star started playing from the tinny speakers. Putting his back to the pole, he pointed the wand at the snake and snapped his mana into place, preparing to fire a shot.

The snake hissed. The dust stopped flying as it came to a halt. It reared up, the tent bulging overhead, and then lunged, throwing itself forward with all its strength.

Eric grabbed the pole and spun himself around it, putting it between him and the snake. The snake slammed into the pole, and wood cracked. Splinters flew, scattering across the floor and bouncing off Eric, but the pole held.

The snake retreated with a hiss. Eric took off again, and once again, the snake gave chase. In his Mana Sight, the mana began to swirl around it, as if responding to its foul mood. It wasn’t much, but it gave him just enough information on where it was about to lunge to get out of the way before it crashed down. He raced to the edge of the tent, directly for one of the supporting poles on the side wall. The snake followed, letting its coils pull around the central pole. The wood groaned, and the crack at its base burst with more splinters.

Eric spun around, lifting his hands to taunt the snake, only to find it already cocked and ready to strike, pushing up the tent overhead. And then the tent went slack.

Oh shit! He kicked off the ground and full-on dove to the side. The snake bashed into the support behind him and through the fabric out into the sun. Guy lines sang, creaking as they took on more of the tension of the tent. Sun fell on the snake, and the snake’s sleek black head appeared for a moment before it whirled around and surged under the tent once more. Dust flew as it chased after Eric.

He led it around the central post. Its coils piled up against the central pole, and the pole cracked loudly. The snake charged again and again, chasing after him, and he led it toward external post after external post. It didn’t break all of them, but it knocked out enough that the tent started to sag. With fewer of the exterior poles, more weight fell on the central pole and the guy lines. The guy lines snapped, while the central pole creaked and began to list, sagging under the entire tent.

Eric ran, rushing toward the edge of the tent, then slid under the now-slack tent wall and out into the sun. He whipped around and lifted his Magical Wand, pointing it at the central post. Snapping his mana circuits into place, he lifted the wand. Tinny music swelled in volume, light surged around the cheap plastic star, and a blast of star-shaped light erupted from its tip, smashing into the top of the pole. The pole creaked, swaying back and forth, then, with an ear-splitting snap, it gave way. The entire pole crashed down on the snake, taking the tent with it. The pole struck the snake, and it hissed in pain, rounding on the pole. The tent seethed as the snake struck the offending pole repeatedly.

Eric pointed the wand again, this time clearly able to see the entire snake. He hadn’t wanted to fire his shots until he could see the entire snake and be sure of its location, but now he could. It had cost him one of his shots, but the falling post had also done damage to the snake. That was a trade he was willing to make.

“Here goes nothing,” he murmured, and pointed the still-active wand at the snake. He pointed it at center-mass, then thought better of that and leveled it at the snake’s head instead. Snakes were hardy creatures. He’d once watched one survive getting run over by four consecutive cars. If he didn’t take out the head, he couldn’t guarantee killing it.

The snake struck again and again, its head darting back and forth. Eric held his breath, watching it in silence. It would get tired of striking the pole. He just had to wait.

At last, the snake withdrew from the pole. It settled down in the darkness and went still. Now! For the second time today, he snapped his mana circuits into place, and the star-shaped projectile hurtled toward the snake. The snake, wrapped in the tent, couldn’t see it coming. The star smashed into its head, burning a hole through the tent and cracking the snake’s skull. The snake hissed angrily and thrashed in pain, twisting and knotting in the tent. It stiffened, then slowed, thrashing once or twice more before it sunk down into death.

Eric lowered the wand. Belatedly, he shut it off. He didn’t want to waste the batteries. His whole body felt spent and exhausted, like he’d just done a long workout, but he still had enough in the tank to run back to the entrance. Not much more, but enough. If the snake wasn’t dead after all that, he’d have no choice but to abandon ship, and to heck with Joe’s finish it tonight. His life came first. He had to retreat while he still had enough energy to get out. If he spent every last scrap of energy on this battle, he’d only have sealed his own death.

The giant snake vanished. Black smoke swirled out from under the tent. Loot boss? the System prompted.

“Sure.”

An enormous snakeskin roll entered his inventory along with a savage curved blade shaped almost like a snake’s fang. He called that one out to get a closer look. It was pitch black and matte, like it was absorbing all the light around it, save for its silver hilt and black-leather grip. A snake twisted around the hilt and bit the blade, the little snake’s fangs straddling the blade.

Blackfang Sabre

Conducts mana like a wand, staff, or other implement. Also it looks really cool. Look how cool it looks. It’s like, black and stuff. Super cool.

“Great. A wand that I can stab myself with,” Eric commented. But at the same time, he couldn’t help but grin. It was really cool. The blade looked awesome, and being able to defend himself with a sword while he cast spells sounded neat as hell. The problem was, of course, that as Joe had already pointed out, the System wouldn’t make him a swordsmaster overnight, and on top of that, he wasn’t even a sword-wielding class. The Blackfang Sabre was nothing more than an oversized butter knife for him to fall on when he wasn’t paying attention.

Ah, well. I can still use it, maybe. I’ll practice when I’m back at the station. He sent it back to his inventory.

First to defeat a boss: Ultrarare reward granted

For a second, Eric just stared at the long list of rewards, including the rare and ultrarare rewards. He raised his brows. Wow. I’m about to get equipped up. Two levels, too! That was insane compared to taking a year plus to gain ten levels in the Tutorial. Is the Tutorial that much a baby mode? Or am I just underlevel and catching up? I’m supposed to be level ten when I fight my first dungeon, after all. When I think of it like that, only two levels from defeating an entire dungeon feels cheap… but then, Joe did say that they’ll be weaker now, before they have time to mature, whatever that means.

Then he read the line at the bottom and paused. Did he want to teleport out? He wiggled his socked feet against the dirt, thinking. On one hand, his boots were still out there in the forest. On the other hand, he was spent. If he encountered another snakeman right now, he wasn’t confident he’d win that battle. Was it worth risking his life for a pair of boots? Especially when there was a non-zero chance he’d get some boots or shoes in the loot anyways?

He looked down, thinking. I can always come back later when I’m fresher. I think the obvious choice is to abandon them for now, reconsider based on the loot I acquire from the clear, then come back when I’m fresh. Pushing himself further now was foolish. When he was tired, he’d make mistakes. Not just strategic mistakes, but bodily mistakes. He’d put his foot in a hole because he wasn’t paying attention and twist his ankle. Run into a branch because he wasn’t looking up and give himself a concussion. Better to retreat, recoup, equip up, then come back out ready to tango.

Fundamentally, it was the same as beating a dungeon in a video game, then levelling up, putting his new equipment on and tearing through the enemies that had challenged him moments ago, except he wasn’t a tireless avatar on a screen. He was a real person who got tired and made real mistakes. If he kept pressing on without resting, he’d inevitably end up dead.

“Yes,” he said aloud.

Light sprang up around him. A strange sensation fell over him, almost like he was lying back in a pool—as if something else was taking his weight—and then everything went black for a beat. When the world faded back in, he was at the front of the dungeon. For a second, that weightlessness clung to him, but only for one second. The next, his weight slammed back home, and he dropped. Instinctively, he panicked, throwing his arms out and spreading his stance, only to hit the ground a split second later.

“Oh,” he muttered to himself.

And then the dungeon collapsed.
