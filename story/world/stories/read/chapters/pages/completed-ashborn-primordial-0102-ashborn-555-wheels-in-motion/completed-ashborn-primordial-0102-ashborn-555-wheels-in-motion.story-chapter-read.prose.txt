“...and I can have a thousand Asura at Kaia by nightfall. Two thousand by morning! We have several airships ready to deploy from Sonam, and—”

“Maiya.”

Maiya had been about to ignore Riyan’s interruption, but stopped herself upon seeing the look on his face.

“Sorry,” she said. “Guess I was rambling a bit.”

“Perhaps,” Riyan replied across the communications projection display in the Sarvaak’s command center. A Saunak improvement over the standard communications orb, it was useful for holding meetings such as these.

The projection’s large size also allowed for far more detail, and Maiya couldn’t help but notice that he looked considerably older than he had just a week ago. She somehow doubted she looked any better.

“Maiya, I have an army,” he said.

“I’m aware, of course,” Maiya replied frustratedly. “But you can never have enough troops, yes?”

“While I would not ordinarily disagree, our foe is not a country fielding an army of their own. There isn’t a single troop sieging Kartara’s gates. A thousand of your Asura will not help. What I need is to know where they are going to strike next.”

Maiya scowled at that. That answer was the one thing she didn’t have.

“I’m hearing of copycat attacks in Rani, Matali… even Kin’jal, of all places,” Riyan continued. “With incidents in every major human town and city, no amount of troops is enough. You know this as well as anyone. Tell me, what has the Pagan Order said?”

“They’re denying any involvement,” Maiya replied with a sigh. “The Tribunal is claiming this is an act of rogue extremists, and that they are tracking them down with extreme prejudice.”

“And? Do you believe them?”

“They’ve certainly mobilized enough of their assets to make me wonder...”

“You are being duped. Their hatred of us is no secret. With the Akh Nara out of the picture, they saw an opportunity, and they took it.”

“It certainly looks that way,” Maiya admitted. “But even if true, it hardly matters now. The reports from Falguna confirm both Aindri Warriors and Iksana ghaels in the strike force. Same for Hiranya. Even if they might’ve been the instigator, the Pagan Order is clearly coordinating with the Aindri and the Iksana, and both clans also claim that these attacks came without their sanction. Whoever is behind this, I won’t know with any certainty for weeks, perhaps much longer. I don’t have enough evidence to go after anyone right now.”

Riyan's face was the very definition of disgust. “So we may be at war with two demon clans and an organization that has spent generations preparing for this exact war.”

“You know very well that I’ll do my Ash-damnest to ensure it doesn’t come to that,” Maiya replied. “I’ll have a coordinated realm-wide response within the week.”

Riyan snorted. “Let us pray you’ll have more than just that.”

The projection went dark.

Hema, who’d written down the meeting’s transcript for the official record, cleared her throat.

“My Lady. New reports are in.”

“Let’s hear it,” Maiya replied, already bracing for bad news.

“I’m afraid demonic sentiment across the human territories has continued to plummet. Sawai lords in several countries are demanding restrictions on demonic residents, and there have been dozens of reports of violent incidents, though thankfully all have been diffused thus far.”

Maiya didn’t need Clarity to know where this was headed. Humans were beginning to suspect every demon they saw, and demons were never the type to take affronts to their honor lying down.

The peace she and Vir had slogged for years to build was being undone down in a matter of days.

“Which is the whole point,” she muttered.

“My Lady?”

“Of the strikes. They were never just about Matali or Sai. Whether it’s the Tribunal or rogue elements, large swaths of the Pagan Order have always wanted to have their revenge against humans. I mean, they’d even planned an all-out war for decades before Vir came along and convinced them otherwise. Except they don’t even need a full war now. As Riyan said, someone must’ve realized that and capitalized on the opportunity, now that Vir is indisposed.”

“Pardon my conjecture, but it does seem like the Tribunal must be behind this in some capacity,” Hema said with a frown.

“I agree, but it honestly doesn’t matter anymore. The realms are connected now. Whoever ordered the strikes has their pick of several demonic clans to disappear into. Even if I prove the Tribunal sanctioned it, I’d have to fight through their army to get to them.”

“And risk turning all of the Order—moderates included—against you,” Hema concluded.

“Yep,” Maiya replied. “Which I will absolutely risk, given the stakes, but that will all take time. Even if we held the Tribunal accountable, it’s not like the attacks will suddenly stop now that we’re seeing imitation attacks from demons who have nothing to do with the original attackers.”

Feeling a headache come on, Maiya massaged her temples. “And if it is rogue agents, I have to find their leadership before I can act, which takes months I don’t have. Either way, there’s no easy solution here. Ekat’Ma is good, but this is an incredibly difficult operation, especially considering all the orders we could’ve intercepted have already been given. We’d have to find records, and I somehow doubt they just left them lying around.”

The doors to the Sarvaak’s command center opened, revealing Cirayus’s four-armed form, still in the armor he’d worn at Camar Gadin. Ira followed in her court dress, a grave expression on her face. Maiya knew the look. Ira had come prepared to argue.

As for the location, the Asuran command ship had proven far more versatile than any stationary base of operations. To the point where Maiya had chosen it over even the Vimana they’d commandeered, which was once again trundling its way back to the human realm. As a show of force, if nothing else.

“Lay it true, lass,” Cirayus said without preamble. “How bad is it?”

“Well, the short version is that we need to act, but I’m completely at a loss as to how.”

“Deploy the Asura,” Cirayus said immediately.

“And hasten the war that looms at our doorstep?” Ira said, shaking her head.

“Do you have a better plan, then?” Cirayus asked, turning to the Imperatrix. “When people see the Asura defending them, they will see the truth.”

“Will they?” Ira shot back. “Seems to me all they will see is demons invading their cities. With the blessing of their rulers, no less. That sounds to me like fuel for our foes to ignite.”

“Only initially, lass,” Cirayus replied. “The strikes have succeeded because the human territories have no defense against such an assault. With our Iksana spies, we can at least learn where they’ll strike before they do. Once they see our forces saving human lives, they will be happy to have us.”

“There are not enough Iksana with Sight to cover so much land, Cirayus,” Ira replied. “Even if there were, your plan would require Asura posted at every intersection in every city. That creates a different problem entirely.”

“And what problem might that be?” Cirayus asked.

“Loathe as I am to admit it, there are many towns across the human nations where demons cannot walk down the streets without being followed by children with stones, or worse,” Ira replied. “You are proposing we put Asuras in such places. The Asura will not have a single opportunity to prove themselves. They will be attacked on sight out of fear alone. I know how well trained they are, yet everyone has limits, and it only takes one of their strength to misstep. The result will be a massacre.”

“Then we station them in cities where they will be better received,” Cirayus shrugged. “At least initially. Once the bigots see just how safe those cities become, they will change their tune fast enough, I think.”

Maiya listened to their debate in silence. Neither side was giving bad advice, but their perspectives colored their opinions. Cirayus fundamentally believed in demonkind and moreover, his prodigious strength. Which was a pitfall most demons shared. Their natural strength blindied them from understanding the woes of the weak.

“We’ll deploy the Asura,” Maiya said, cutting their debate short. “All of them. Every Warrior we have available. I want them stationed in every major human city and town within forty-eight hours.”

Ira's eyes closed for a moment as she steeled herself to patiently reiterate why this would be a terrible idea.

“But not alone,” Maiya continued, stopping Ira’s rebuke before it ever left her lips. “I want Altani and Kin'jal Balarian Guard mobilized wherever the Asura are. Side by side in a joint deployment. The Asura might be far more powerful than both forces combined, but the average human doesn’t know that. They’ll see Balarian Guard and Altani mejai outnumber them five to one, and think the force favors humans. That will allay the panic, and moreover, it’ll show a united front of demons and humans, working hand in hand.”

“That… could work,” Ira admitted. “A few well-timed speeches on our behalf extolling the virtues of interracial cooperation and it could very well work.”

“Agreed,” Cirayus replied. “Though I still feel you worry too much about demons’ perception, more bodies to fight back against these insurgents would be helpful.”

“Even still,” Maiya said. “This is nothing but a stopgap, as Riyan so astutely called out. We need to know who’s directing these people and instigating events. The majority of outbreaks might not be caused by the perpetrators behind Sai and Matali, but I’m betting they still have a hand in this. We need to find out who they are and where they’re based out of.”

“Easier said than done,” Ira replied. “Surely you remember how long it took you to infiltrate the Children of Ash? I spent years putting operatives in places sensitive enough to feed that sort of information.”

“I know. And I know we had no choice back then, but I can’t tell you how much I regret scaling back our intelligence network now.”

“Ash Tears were the bigger threat,” Ira replied softly. “We all made sacrifices we wish we rather hadn’t. But that is neither here nor there.”

“Indeed,” Maiya replied. “We’ll begin seeding operatives into the Pagan Order, the Aindri, and the Iksana. Most of our human operatives are still around, and Ekat’Ma can help with infiltrating the demonic clans.”

“Still,” Cirayus replied, stroking his beard. “As you both were quick to point out, it will take time before we see usable intelligence. By then, this conflict may well have resolved itself. One way or another.”

“I know,” Maiya replied, fearing very much it would be the ‘other’ way. “But what else can we do?”

Cirayus placed a comforting hand on Maiya’s shoulders. “Stay true, lass. The Akh Nara will return before the worst comes to pass. You must believe in him.”

“You know I do,” Maiya replied.

Frankly, he was her only real hope right now. Vir descending from the sky to wipe out the rebels with a hundred immortal warriors would do more to turn the tide of battle than any fiery speech she could give or army she could muster.

Still, he’d entrusted her with the realm, and she wasn’t about to rely on him to stop it all from going to Ash.

After talking through some of the details—which was mostly Maiya delegating mobilization to Ira and Cirayus—the two left to execute the plan, leaving Maiya alone with her aides and her thoughts.

Not for long, as it were.

The communications crystal on her table lit up soon after, and a glance at the label under the crystal showed it came from Matali. The impoverished kingdom only had one orb at their capital, which meant it would be King Erhan calling. No doubt asking her about the soldiers she’d promised to help quell the unrest in his land.

Right after they finally got a break from the Ash, too, Maiya thought. Their country seemed cursed with misfortune.

And yet, when the projection above the crystal resolved into a face, Maiya froze, for it was not Erhan’s face that appeared. Nor one she’d ever thought she’d see again.

Tiyana of Matali stared back at her, her hair longer than Maiya remembered, grime visible on her armor. She was standing in what appeared to be a war tent.

“Maiya,” Tia said curtly. “May we speak?”

“Princess Tiyana…” Maiya replied, caught off guard. “I… was not expecting you.”

Tiyana barked a short, harsh laugh. “That’s an understatement if I ever heard one. Don’t worry, I know how busy you are. I won’t take up your time. I’m calling you to tell you that a Pagan Order force at Falguna came to capture me. They failed, and though we captured some, they committed suicide before we could question them.”

For the first time in a very long while, Maiya found herself speechless. Both at Tiyana’s appearance and the bombshells she’d just dropped.

“That means…”

“Yeah,” Tiyana replied. “Trained operatives, though with the Order, it wouldn’t surprise me at all if their elites were extremists operating on their own.”

“You’re… surprisingly reasonable about this,” Maiya said softly. “I take it you had a hand in sparing the ones who attacked you?”

Princess Tiyana shrugged. “Seemed pointless to kill someone without knowing the full picture first. Especially when they were so much weaker than me.”

Weaker? Maiya thought. How in all the realms is that possible?

Princess Tiyana disappeared off the face of the map years ago and was presumed dead. Was she just boasting?

“Anyway, my brother intends to use me as Matali's spear to enforce order in the country. I’ve told him that while I will do my duty to my people, it would be a waste of my potential if I limited my scope to a kingdom. I wish to help the realm, and that means coordinating with our de facto Saint. Still don’t know how you pulled that one off,” she muttered under her breath, though Maiya heard it nonetheless. “So if you’re willing to let bygones be bygones, I am offering you my blade.”

Maiya took a moment to digest everything she’d just said.

At the very least, one thing was abundantly clear. Wherever Tia had been had changed her. The woman on the other end of the projection was not the selfish, spoiled princess Maiy remembered.

The brusqueness was there, to be sure. Maybe even greater now… but it was different. Supported by the sort of confidence that only came with experience. Vast amounts of experience, at that.

The Tia she remembered had been seventeen and angry enough to fly into a blind rage at the mere sight of demons. This Tiyana seemed older than the years between them should have made her.

“An enticing proposition,” Maiya replied. “Especially if you’re half as strong as you claim to be. But the last time we met, you hated demons with every fiber of your being.”

Tia’s lips went taut. “I did. I don’t deny it.”

“What changed?”

“Ten years in the Ash will do that to you. Something, I’m told, you know well.”

Maiya went still. “Ten years?”

“At least. As you know, it is rather difficult to keep track in there with the time effects.”

Time effects? Maiya thought. That she knew about it at all meant she’d ventured somewhat deep, where the flow of time shifted and slowed relative to the other realms.

Maiya’s mind reeled. That should have been impossible. Even for her, it had taken years to acclimatize.

It was only possible thanks to Vir’s Asura program that guaranteed her access to the Ash’s periphery, and that had only been possible thanks to Janak creating the Gates for them remotely from his tower.

“Er, Maiya? Or, I suppose I should call you Saint Maiya? That’s your title these days, isn’t it?”

“Sorry,” Maiya replied, frowning. “I was a little distracted. Humans cannot survive in the Ash. Which logically means you had some other means to pull that off… and I can only think of one thing.”

“Well,” Tiyana huffed. “You’re about as infuriatingly sharp as ever. But you’re right. Vason, Haymi, and I all had Artifacts that kept us safe from the prana poisoning… not that that meant a whole lot when literally everything in that wasteland wants to kill you.”

Tia smiled slightly. Considering Maiya had never once seen her smile in front of her, that might’ve been the biggest change yet.

“The one the demons brought across when Vir was a baby,” Maiya said. “I admit, I’m impressed. For a whole slew of reasons, actually. Not only did you somehow find three Artifacts that had been presumed lost, you actually decided to venture there without a means to return. That was… incredibly brave.”

“Let’s be honest,” Tia snorted. “It was the dumbest, most reckless decision I have ever made. But I, uh… wasn’t in a good place back then. Don’t really want to talk about it. Anyway, I survived.”

“Clearly,” Maiya said. “For over ten years, which I assume was due to the time dilation effects. Which means you made it fairly deep. And I know more than most what that means.”

“Good,” Tia replied. “Saves me the trouble of proving myself. Seems that’s all anyone’s interested in these days. The Balarian proctors say I’m officially rank 3,400 now, for all that means. It turns out the Balar scale breaks down when the numbers get that high.”

Maiya chuckled. “Yes, my husband discovered that some time ago.”

Ever since Camar Gadin, she’d been preparing to fight this war with the resources she had on hand. Not insignificant, if one wished to wage war… but woefully inadequate for the delicate task of creating harmony between humans and demons.

But this? This changed everything, and Tiyana’s powers were the least of what made her valuable.

Tiyana was a Matali Princess. A human princess, and moreover, a well-known demon hater who’d had a change of heart.

Most importantly, she wasn’t saddled with the label of ‘demon lover’ as Maiya was.

Humans respected her for her power, yes, and her influence. But she knew full well that any personal attempt to bring humans and demons together after what just transpired would explode as badly as a new pranik activating a Fire orb for her first time.

“I have some conditions,” Maiya said. “This will not be a partnership. You will report to me as your superior officer. That said, you will operate with autonomy. Gods know I don’t have the time to manage anyone right now. But I assign your missions, and that’s not up for debate. That also means you needn’t worry about your brother or anyone else, and I certainly won’t have you taking pointless tests. You will move without any council’s approval, carrying my direct authority. I must warn you, however, that your role will be mostly political. I’ve thousands of Asuras who can match or exceed your personal power. What I need is a spokesperson, and you may very well be perfect for that role.”

Maiya had expected an argument, but instead, she just got two words.

“I accept,” Tia replied, smiling. “In fact, I was hoping you’d say that. I’ve had my fill of fighting for a hundred lifetimes.”

Maiya laughed. “I don’t doubt it. I’ll fetch an airship for you. Come to Sonam posthaste. We need to meet in person, and soon.”

“I’ll be there,” Tia replied, and the projection winked out.

Maiya knew better than to celebrate. Especially in a world where their enemy affected the flow of events themselves.

And yet, for the first time since she had watched Vir fly off to fight that Vimana, she felt the slightest flame of hope once again.

Fate, perhaps, may not be as all-controlling as she’d feared. At least not in this universe. Not yet.

She could only pray her luck held.
