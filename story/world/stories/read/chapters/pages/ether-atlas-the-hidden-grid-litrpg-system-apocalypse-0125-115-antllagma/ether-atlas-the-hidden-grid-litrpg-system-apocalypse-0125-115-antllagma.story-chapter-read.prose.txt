Chapter 115

A wall keeps as carefully as it bars. — Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

Arthur counted the sentries before they were close enough to be stopped by one. Two on the door, a third on a folding chair in the shade of the portico and three walking the length of the building's south face. Six men for a building that could have been held by two. Either the Council was worried, or it wanted to look strong.

Petros walked half a step ahead, as he had since they left his mother's house forty minutes ago, steering them through streets that Arthur was now beginning to recognise.

The road from the Vassilou house ran uphill through two blocks of shuttered shops and then opened onto what had once been a municipal square, paved in pale stone with a dry fountain at its centre and a row of orange trees along the eastern edge.

The building at the north end was three storeys of mid-century concrete and steel-framed glass, the kind of structure that survived earthquakes because it was too ugly to notice. Arthur read it because buildings were his thing: the ground-floor windows had been bricked halfway up with salvaged block; the entrance had been narrowed to a single reinforced door. Chest-high sandbag rows were placed in strategic positions. The mortar joints on the window infill were uneven and still chalky. Recent work, and not a mason's.

“Here we are,” Petros said, and straightened his collar as though the gesture would make a difference. “Ela, let me do the talking.”

The guard on the folding chair stood when they were ten metres out. He was young, armed with a hatchet on his belt, and he looked at the party's weapons before he looked at their faces. Donna's axe was on her shoulder, and his own hammer hung from a loop at his hip beside the shield, and they looked less like petitioners and more like the kind of trouble the sandbags were for.

Petros spoke to the young guard in Greek in a relaxed and unhurried manner, his hands moving. The guard answered in a tone that was polite but firm, and Petros turned back with a shrug.

“He says Antoniou is in a meeting. We wait.”

“Siga siga,” Petros said, seeing Arthur's jaw tighten. “This is how it has always worked, ne. You wait. Then you wait more. Then a man says come, and you are grateful, like he did you a favour by making you stand in the sun.”

“How long is the meeting,” Donna said.

“Who knows. A meeting is as long as the important men inside want to feel important.” Petros shrugged, one shoulder against the warm concrete, watching the guards with the interest of a man who knew half the coast and expected to know the other half by lunch. “Ela, leave it to me.”

“You sit. In Greece, when a man says wait, you wait. If you stand, you look angry. If you look angry, you wait longer.”

Arthur sat on the low wall beside Petros and watched Donna take a position against the fountain's dry basin where she could see the door and both corners of the square; he did not comment on it because she would have done the same thing if they were visiting a church.

Steve settled on the wall beside Petros and pulled out his pencil and the battered notebook that had survived three countries and looked ready to survive a fourth. Sarah stood near the guards, and after a moment she said something in Greek that made the younger one's posture soften.

He was talking to the guards inside two minutes, and inside five he had one of them laughing.

Petros extracted more useful information from the guards than Arthur could have managed with a week and a translator. He did it without effort, one story about a fishing trip, a question about someone's brother. Petros complained about the requisitions, and the guards complained back, and complaints turned out to be a language everyone in the queue spoke fluently. And all the while Sarah kept translating the useful bits.

“They took his cousin's boat winch,” Sarah said quietly. “And gave him a paper. Everyone now has a paper for a useful thing that they used to have.”

“The whole city, they have made it sea,” Petros said, turning back to include them, his hands going up. “You understand? The Council, with their carts and their papers. Sea.”

Steve opened his mouth, then decided to take the easy route and looked at Sarah.

“He means they've made a mess of everything,” Sarah said. “A complete shambles.”

“Sea,” Petros agreed, satisfied.

Then Arthur heard a word he recognised. Lykoi. Wolves.

The meeting inside, it turned out, was about the container yard attack and had been going on for over three hours.

“They are worried,” Petros said, leaning back against the wall. “The wolves, you understand. He says there have been messages from Megara, from Eleusis. Same thing there as well. Getting worse.”

“Messages how?” Donna asked.

“Runners. Boats, when the sea allows.” Petros made a rocking gesture with his hand. “Antoniou has been talking to the other settlements for weeks. They all say the same: more creatures, closer to the walls, bolder at night.”

• •

A clerk came out after almost an hour, a thin man with ink on the side of his hand, and said the Civil council leader would see them, and then held up a flat palm.

“No weapons inside,” he said. “You leave them here. They will be safe.”

“Nobody is touching my axe,” Donna said. She had not raised her voice. She had become very still. A person who had decided how the next several seconds would go and was giving everyone the chance to arrive at the same conclusion peacefully.

The younger door guard stepped forward, and Arthur watched Donna's weight settle onto the balls of her feet, and then the guard spoke to Sarah.

“You are the doctor,” he said, in careful English. “From the container yard. In the market.”

“I was there, yes,” Sarah said.

“There was a boy. His leg.” The guard touched his own thigh. “He is my cousin. I'm Thanasis,” he added.

“He is walking now,” Thanasis said with a smile. “His mother even wanted me to find out your names so she can light a candle.”

He looked at the axe on Donna's back, then firmed his eyes and said, “Nothing will happen to the weapons. But nobody goes in armed. It does not matter who. This is the rule.”

“Give it to him,” Sarah said, touching Donna's arm. “I think we know his family,” she added in a whisper.

Petros put the final nail in it, cheerfully. “Make sure the gear is safe, Thanasi,” he said, wagging a finger, “or I come down to your house myself, and I talk to your father. And your father, he still owes me for the thing with the outboard, so it will be a long talk.”

Thanasis laughed, and that was that. Arthur unslung the maul and set the Breakwater shield against the wall beside it. Donna handed over the Ravener and her prybar, and Thanasis placed himself between the weapons and the square. They did not seem to understand the reason for a focus rod and no one asked Steve for it and he did not volunteer.

“Weeks off the books,” Donna said as they walked towards the corridor. “No Council contact. No name on any register. And now we walk in unarmed and introduce ourselves.”

• •

The room at the end of the corridor had been either a small conference room or a large manager's office. A long table ran down the middle, and it was covered in paper. Requisition lists. A hand-drawn map of the coast weighted at one corner with a flat stone. Tallies of timber and iron and men, ruled in columns, some lines crossed through and rewritten. Antoniou was the man the carts reported to.

He rose when they came in, which Arthur noted, because men who make you wait at the door for an hour do not usually then get to their feet. He was somewhere in his fifties, broad and softening. A man who had once been strong and now did his fighting at a table, with a grey moustache and reading glasses pushed up into his hair. He came round the table with his hand out and shook each of theirs.

“Sit, sit,” Antoniou said, waving at the chairs, and to the clerk, without turning, “Coffee. Six.” He lowered himself back into his own seat and folded his hands on top of a requisition list. “Petros Vassilou I know. Petros Vassilou everybody knows, God help us. The rest of you, I know by story only, and the stories have grown legs.” He smiled to take the edge off it. “The container yard. Andreadis speaks well of you, and Andreadis speaks well of almost no one. So. You did the coast a service, and I am grateful, and gratitude is a thing I try to pay while I can still afford it. What do you need?”

“A boat,” Arthur said. “Across the gulf. We have a captain willing to take us, but he won't sail the far water without the Council's word.”

“Spyros,” Antoniou asked, looking at Petros.

“Spyros,” he confirmed.

“A careful man. And a careful man keeps his berth.” Antoniou nodded slowly. “Across the gulf where. There is not much across the gulf now that a man needs to reach so badly he brings four fighters and Petros Vassilou to ask me nicely.”

“Aegina,” Arthur said. The party had agreed that lying about the destination would get them into trouble with Spyros.

Antoniou waited for more, and when nothing else followed, he spread one hand, palm up. “And on Aegina is what? I ask because I sign the paper, and my name on the paper is a permission. A man likes to know what he is allowing.”

Arthur felt Donna tense beside him, a shift of weight that would have been invisible to anyone who had not spent years learning how she carried discomfort. The real answer was a triangle drawn in Steve's ledger, and a temple that might be a node. And of course he would rather not have to say any of that.

“Family,” Petros said, stepping into the short silence. “We have family on Aegina. My mother's cousin and her people, in a village on the far side, and since the sky changed, nothing. No word. You understand?” He let that personal stake sit for a moment. “I could not go alone. One man in a boat, for what, to drown politely? But these four, they are not nothing in a fight, you have heard the stories yourself. With them I can go and come back breathing.”

Antoniou looked at them for a long moment as if trying to decide how much of the story he believed, and then let it go. “Kala,” he said. “Family. There is no arguing with family.” The coffee arrived, and he waited while the clerk set the small cups round. “And unfortunately my answer is no.”

• •

“Understand me,” Antoniou went on, before anyone could speak. “I have six sound boats left on this whole coast that can sail without an engine and eleven men who can sail them. And now every week the sea takes one and the wolves take another, and I do not get them back.”

“These boats feed the city and if you do not come back, I have five boats and ten men and the city has less food.” Antoniou's voice carried no anger and no apology. He turned the little cup a quarter-turn on its saucer without lifting it. “I am sorry for your aunt, Petro. I bury people I am sorry for every week. I will not add five to the list to make myself feel kind.”

“The world already takes five a week from you without asking,” Petros said, leaning in. “Wolves in the streets. Things who walk on two legs one night and four the next, inside Athens, re. Everything is dangerous now.”

“Petro…”

“I am only saying the danger is already at your door. You did not order it and you cannot send it back.”

And there, in those last sentences, Arthur found his quid pro quo.

“The attacks,” Arthur said. “How often now.”

Antoniou's hand tightened on the edge of the table. “Often enough.”

“You know about the container yard,” Arthur continued, his voice level. “You know what we did there. How long have the attacks been escalating?”

“Weeks,” Antoniou said with a steadying breath. “The settlements to the north and west report the same. Megara has lost people. Eleusis has pulled everyone inside their perimeter. We have reinforced the harbour line and the hill, and it has not been enough.”

“And how do you plan to handle it, long term?”

“How does anyone handle it? We lose people, we bury them, we build the wall higher. We fortify the Acropolis.” Antoniou's hand made a small tired gesture at the map, at the tallies, at the whole labour of stone and timber going up the hill. “We hold what we can hold and we grieve what we cannot.”

“We can handle it for you,” Arthur said. “We know where they are coming from.”

“The creatures are coming from a location south-east of here,” Donna joined in, catching on to what Arthur was trying to do. “A hill with a ruined church on it. Kastella.”

“And how do you know this?”

“We saw it a few weeks ago. Killed six. Saw the dungeon but did not go in,” Donna said.

“Dungeon?”

“Think of it as a wound that won't close,” Steve said, joining the conversation. “There are places now, since the sky changed, that gather Ether and concentrate it, and past a certain point they start creating things or changing things that were inside.”

Steve pointed at the map on the desk, placing his finger on Kastella. “If the interior is cleared, the source stops producing, at least for a time. It's the difference between treating symptoms and treating the disease. You can reinforce your harbour line every night, and every night there will be more of them, because the thing that makes them is still running.”

“And you know this how?” Antoniou said, but he had not looked away from the map.

“Because we have done it before,” Arthur said, but did not elaborate further. “If we clear Kastella and your attacks fall off, that's a win for you, in front of the whole coast. The Council closed the wound. In exchange, you get us our boat.”

For a moment nobody spoke. Petros watched a deal he had not seen coming assemble itself in front of him. Antoniou sat with his hands folded on the requisition list and ran the scenarios that men in his chair always ran, weighing a public victory against the potential loss of a boat and a captain.

“I have heard about the container yard,” Antoniou said, moving a few papers about. “I have heard you gave Andreadis a core freely, and that you killed five wolves and something larger in less than a minute. I have heard a great deal about you. Now you say you will help the city and let the Council take credit.”

“Say whatever keeps your people calm,” Donna said. “We don't need the credit. We need the boat.”

Antoniou laughed, a short, surprised bark, and for the first time it reached his eyes. “Someone who does not want the credit. Po po po. I have not met one of those in this building in thirty years.”

“And a notebook,” Steve said sheepishly.

“Re?” Antoniou said, with a puzzled look on his face.

“I see you have a lot of papers. My current ledger is getting full, so if you can spare one…” Steve left the sentence unfinished.

“I will have the paper done by tomorrow. The crossing to Aegina, pending clearance of the Kastella site,” he said, pulling the reading glasses down from his hair. “And a new empty ledger.”

• •

They came back out to find Thanasis still standing protectively over their gear.

“You see,” he said to Petros. “Safe. No talk with my father.”

“Good boy,” Petros said, clapping him on the shoulder.

Arthur slung the weight of the shield back onto his shoulder, and only then did he realise how much he had disliked being without it in that room. Donna checked the Ravener over once, an edge-to-heel glance that was half habit and half principle.

“Stavros should have the gear in another day or so,” Arthur said as they made their way to the Vassilou house. “We collect the weapons and then check out what is happening in Kastella.”

FIELD NOTE — The Work-Order

I have spent my career reading the monster-slayer as a story a culture tells about itself. Perseus, Bellerophon, Herakles and his ten years of odd jobs, Theseus clearing the road to Athens one bandit at a time. I taught them as aspiration and as propaganda, the polis dreaming itself brave, and I once marked an undergraduate down for arguing the dragons had ever been anything but metaphor.

I would like that mark back.

Because I sat in a requisition office today and watched a tired man with a map and a column of dead names do exactly what a Mycenaean wanax must have done, which is look at the thing killing his people faster than he could bury them, understand that he could not spare the men to end it, and hire it out. The hero was never the point of the story. The hero was the line item the city could not afford to keep on staff. Nobody composes an epic about the man who mends the aqueduct, because the aqueduct is permanent and the mending is dull. They compose it about the man who closes the wound, because the wound is mortal and the man is passing through and will, gods willing, be gone by winter with their gratitude and their cores and none of their bread.

Theseus cleared the Isthmus road so the merchants could reach the Saronic ports. I always knew that. I filed it under trade history and never once let it stand next to the myth, because the myth was sacred and the drainage was profane, and I had built a whole discipline on keeping the two in separate drawers. Could every dragon on every old map be a maintenance backlog that somebody, eventually, paid a passing stranger to clear?

✎ The hero is not the city's dream of itself. He is the contractor it calls when the killing outpaces the burying, and every epic is a work-order the polis could not bring itself to file in plain words.
