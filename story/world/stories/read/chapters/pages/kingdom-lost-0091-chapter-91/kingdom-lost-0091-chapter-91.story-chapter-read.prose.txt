Chapter 91

Riley broke the wax seal with her thumbnail and unrolled the scroll across the table. The handwriting was the first thing she noticed. Small, even, every letter sitting at the same angle, every line spaced the same distance from the line above it. This was not the hand of someone who had taught themselves to write by candlelight. Whoever had drafted this had been put through a desk and a tutor and probably a slap on the knuckles when the loops did not match. House Corvessa kept educated clerks. That alone told her something about the operation she was looking at.

It was not addressed to anyone. There was no greeting, no salutation, no recipient name. The only mark of identification on the entire document was the Corvessa sigil stamped in ink at the bottom, the same shape as the wax seal she had just broken. A ledger entry dressed up in the form of a letter, intended for someone who already knew what they were reading and did not need to be told.

She started at the top.

The first half of the scroll was a delivery record. Dates ran down the left margin in a neat column, going back through the previous three months. Beside each date, a short list of commodities, then quantities, then a destination code Riley did not recognize but could guess at from context. Ore by the hundredweight. Timber by the wagonload. Grain by the sack. Two entries listed gold. Not coin. Raw.

She did the arithmetic in her head and felt her stomach tighten. The numbers were too large for what one compound, even a busy one. The raiders had been doing more than hitting wagons on the road between Rivermark and Stonehaven. There were location codes scrawled in the margins beside several of the entries, four of them recurring, each in a different hand from the main script. Field notes, added by whoever had compiled the ledger from incoming reports. Four collection points, at minimum. The geography those four points implied stretched well past anything she had imagined the raiders to be working. This was not a gang. This was a regional operation with a payroll and a structure, and the compound she had burned was one node in a possible network.

She read the top half twice to be sure she was not making the numbers up, and the second time she was less sure she was making them up than the first.

The bottom half of the scroll was where the document changed.

The handwriting stayed the same but the tone did not. A short paragraph, six or seven lines, written in a colder register than the ledger above it. No commodities, no quantities, it was a directional note. The next handoff to a Corvessa agent was imminent. The exact day was not given but the language made it clear, days, not weeks. The raiders were expected to deliver on schedule. They had not delivered. The agent would be dispatched to inquire.

Riley read that paragraph twice as well.

Then she came to the last two lines.

She put the tip of her index finger on the page and read them with her finger tracking under each word, the way she used to read instruction manuals when she was small and wanted to make sure she had not misunderstood a single thing.

The new warden. Tower north of the divide. Assess, observe, report. Do not engage.

She lifted her finger off the page and sat back in the chair.

She looked at the ceiling. She looked at the beams of the tower roof above her, the dust motes turning slow in the column of afternoon light coming through the high window, and she did not see any of it. Her mind was somewhere else.

Corvessa had known about her. Not in the last few days, not since the raid on the compound, not since any of the recent escalations she could think of. Long enough to have a standing instruction in writing. Long enough that someone had taken the time to put the order at the bottom of a routine ledger, where it would travel with the regular paperwork of the operation and reach all the people who needed to know without ever being highlighted as something special. Assess, observe, report. Do not engage. That was not the language of an army about to march on her gates. That was the language of a house gathering information for use later.

The scroll had been in the raiders' basement.

Which meant the raiders had read it, or had been told what was in it. Which meant the disfigured leader, when he had stood in the longhouse with his blade against Valrik's throat and looked across the room at her, had known exactly who she was. Not just the woman whose soldiers had broken his gate. The new warden. The one his employer had been watching for months. He had used Valrik as a shield because Valrik was the closest body to hand. He had been looking at her the whole time.

Riley pulled in a long breath through her nose and let it back out.

This had never been only about ore. She had been on someone's map before she had even known there was a map. Whatever Corvessa was building toward, she was already a piece of it, and the piece they had assigned to her was passive observation, which meant they wanted her in place. Whatever they were going to do with her, they wanted her exactly where she was, exactly the way she was, until the moment they decided otherwise.

She thought about the agent who was being dispatched right now to a compound that was no longer there. Whoever that agent was, whatever rank he carried, he was going to ride up to a burned-out hole in the ground with no answers and no explanation, and he was going to ride back to whoever had sent him with that fact and nothing else. There was a window between the moment that report was made and the moment Corvessa decided what to do about it. Riley's shoulders tightened and her brow furrowed.

She rolled the scroll closed and tied it with the cord that had held the wax. She set it on the table beside the chest and pushed her chair back from the table and stood up.

The chest could wait. The history book could wait. This was the new clock now.

She walked to the window and looked down into the yard and watched her own settlement working below her, the figures of soldiers crossing between buildings, the smoke rising off the forge. She stood there with her mind empty, just existing in the moment. Her mind was processing on a deep level.

Then she turned and went down the stairs.

The yard was busier than usual when Riley reached the ground floor. She was crossing the open space between the tower and the inner wall when she heard the call from the gatehouse, and her head came up.

Hoshin.

The gates were already swinging open by the time she got within sight of them. She slowed down. This was her people doing their jobs and she trusted them. She stopped at the edge of the yard and watched them come in.

The group was bigger than the first one. She could see that even at distance. The first arrivals were already through the gate, an older woman in a brown shawl with two children clinging to her skirts, a man behind them carrying a bundle that was almost as wide as he was, more families this time. People who had stayed behind when the first group had left and had now made their own decision. They came through the gate in a loose, tired column, and the sound of them, the children mostly. The murmur of relief from the adults with small sharp questions about food and water.

Thorne walked through the gate ahead of the group. He raised one hand to the soldier at the inner wall, a gesture Riley had seen him use before, all clear, keep them moving. Soldiers stepped forward and began directing the new arrivals toward the bunkhouses. A child started crying somewhere in the middle of the group and was hushed. The press of bodies eased forward into the open space and began to spread.

Riley was about to step out and meet Thorne when she saw him pause.

He had been turning his head along the line of arrivals, counting, a habit she had watched him do every time soldiers came back from anywhere. His eyes moved along the group, and then they stopped.

There was a straggler at the rear. He was wearing the same rough-woven tunic the rest of them wore, and his hood was up. That was not unusual on the road. A few of the older Hoshin men had come in with their hoods up.

What was not the same was the way he was walking.

The Hoshin moved like people who had been walking for hours longer than they wanted to. This man's posture was not tired enough. His shoulders were level. His feet were placed cleanly. His head was up and pointed at the gate, not at the people around him. He was looking at the watchtowers as he came through.

Riley felt the small live hum of the whip on her hip wake up.

Thorne did not look at her. He turned his head back to face forward and said,

"We are almost there. Keep moving. There is water inside."

His tone was the same as it always was, the calm carrying voice he used when soldiers were tired. Then he turned to the gate soldier and gave a small gesture with two fingers, low at his hip, that Riley had never seen before but understood the meaning of immediately.

The soldier said something to another nearest the gate.

Riley stayed where she was.

The Hoshin group flowed through the gate. Soldiers moved among them, lifting bundles, pointing the way to the bunkhouses, taking the smallest children from the arms of mothers who had carried them too long. A woman Riley did not yet know by name stopped and bent over with her hands on her knees and breathed for a long moment before another woman put a hand on her back and walked her forward.

The man at the rear came through the gate with the last of them and stepped to one side.

He did not try to keep up the act. He pushed the hood back off his head and stood quite still in the space he had chosen, hands open at his sides where they could be seen, and waited. He had timed his approach for the noise and now that he was through the gate he was not pretending anymore. His face was tired but his eyes were not. He was looking for someone, and he was prepared to wait until that someone came to him.

Thorne turned around fully and pointed at him.

"Take him."

The two soldiers at the gate moved at the same time. One on each of the man's arms. They did not strike him. They did not need to. He let them take him, lifted his hands a little to make it easier, and stood between them with the patience of a man who had been expecting this exact moment to go this exact way.

Some of the Hoshin near the back of the group heard the commotion and gasped, the soldiers among them whispered to them and lead them away.

Thorne had already moved in. Riley started walking towards the scene. Thorne saw her coming and stepped to the side to clear her line of sight to the man between the two soldiers.

She recognized him.

It took her half a step longer than it should have because the setting was so wrong. She had only ever seen him behind a small folding table covered in tea tins, in a market street in Rivermark, with his sleeves rolled up to his elbows and a cloth over one shoulder. The man in front of her now had no table and no tins and his sleeves were not rolled.

Tarin.

She stopped in front of him. The two soldiers held him without strain, and he made no move to test their grip. He looked at her and waited until she had stopped walking before he spoke, and then he spoke in a voice low enough that only the people inside the small circle around him would hear.

"Riley. I bring news."
