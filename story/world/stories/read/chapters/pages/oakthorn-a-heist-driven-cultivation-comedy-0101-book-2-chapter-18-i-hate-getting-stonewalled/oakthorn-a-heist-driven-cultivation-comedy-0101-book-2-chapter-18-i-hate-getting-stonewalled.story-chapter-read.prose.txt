The cathedral loomed against the storm-ravaged sky like the skeleton of some ancient behemoth, half-devoured by the ravenous marsh.

Jagged spires that once defied gravity now bent at impossible angles, their weathered stone blackened by countless lightning strikes. Massive archways sagged beneath the weight of centuries, yet somehow retained their impossible elegance—architecture that existed nowhere else in the known world.

“That,” Jer announced, voice pitched high with mock reverence, “is the creepiest building I’ve ever wanted to break into.”

“Oh please,” Elena scoffed, water streaming from her mask as she squinted through the deluge. “You said the same thing about that abandoned manor in Darkreach.”

“Yeah, and I was right then too! But at least that place had the decency to put skulls on the gate. This one’s just... waiting.”

“Waiting for what?” Rayka asked, wringing water from her sleeve.

Jer gestured vaguely at the looming structure. “To digest us? I don’t know!”

“If you’re done with the architectural critique,” Orro cut in, eyes scanning the perimeter, “perhaps we could focus on not dying in this increasingly targeted storm?”

He wasn’t wrong. The tempest, which had been steadily worsening since they entered the marsh, now seemed deliberately concentrated around them. Lightning struck with increasing precision, each bolt landing closer than the last. The wind no longer merely howled—it screamed, a sound almost like words just beyond comprehension.

“Progenitor architecture,” Nora murmured, eyes fixed on the cathedral. “I’ve only seen fragments in forbidden texts.”

“I thought Progenitors were bedtime stories,” Gav said, shielding Chubbs from the driving rain. “Boogeymen the gods invented to scare little clerics.”

“Most myths begin as history,” Nora replied without looking away from the cathedral. “And end as warnings.”

“Riveting philosophical discussion,” Jer shouted over a deafening thunderclap, “but can we maybe have it somewhere dry? Preferably before we’re charred to a crisp?”

The sky directly above their position darkened to an impossible black, clouds spiraling into a vortex that seemed to stretch upward into infinity. Lightning no longer flashed in random patterns but circled the perimeter of the spiral, creating a cage of crackling energy.

“Move!” Cade shouted, pointing toward the cathedral’s massive doors, barely visible through the sheets of driving rain. “NOW!”

They broke into desperate motion. Marsh water splashed with each frantic step, mud suddenly deepening as if actively trying to impede their progress. Wind battered them from constantly shifting directions, threatening to topple them into sinkholes or acid pools.

A bolt of lightning struck so close that Cade felt his hair stand on end, the thunderclap immediate and deafening. The shockwave knocked Elena sideways into a puddle that immediately began to bubble around her boots.

“Shit!” she shrieked, scrambling away as the liquid ate through the leather.

Jer hauled his sister upright while Rayka steadied them both. Their movements were practiced, instinctive—the silent choreography of people who’d faced death together too many times to count.

“This isn’t random!” Cade shouted, struggling to be heard above the storm’s fury. “She’s targeting us specifically!”

Another lightning strike, even closer. The ground where it hit crystallized instantly, marsh mud transformed to glassy obsidian.

“You think?” Gav bellowed, his voice distorted as partial transformation triggered involuntarily, eyes flashing amber. “What gave it away? The lightning cage or the death vortex?”

Above them, the spiral tightened impossibly, energy visibly concentrating at its center—a blindingly bright singularity that pulsed with malevolent intent.

Cade knew what was coming. Destruction had tired of playing with them, of wearing them down with near-misses and threats. This was the killing stroke—divine wrath concentrated into a single, annihilating blast.

There was no outrunning it. No shelter that would withstand it. No defense that mere mortals could mount.

Unless...

“GET TO THE CATHEDRAL!” he roared, shoving Chubbs from his arms into Rayka’s. “GO! I’ll try to hold her off!”

“Are you insane?” Rayka screamed, even as she secured the toddler goddess against her chest. “You can’t fight a—”

“GO!”

Silver energy flared around Cade’s hands as he faced the storm, pulling destruction magic from his core to counter a goddess’s wrath. He threw his power upward in a desperate surge, silver light slicing through rain and wind to collide with the vortex’s underside.

For one heartbeat, it held—two—then collapsed as divine fury overwhelmed mortal magic. The vortex pulsed, energy building toward critical mass.

“CADE!” Nora shouted from somewhere behind him. “YOU CAN’T MATCH HER POWER!”

“Watch me,” he snarled, though no one could hear him through the din.

He tried again, channeling everything he had into a dome of silver energy that engulfed their group. Sweat or blood—he couldn’t tell which—ran down his face despite the driving rain. His arms trembled with the effort of maintaining the barrier against Destruction’s concentrated assault.

The shield lasted three seconds before shattering like spun glass.

“IT’S NOT WORKING!” Rayka’s desperate cry barely penetrated the storm’s roar.

Frustration and helplessness crystallized in Cade’s chest, sharp enough to draw blood. They’d endured so much already—hunted by Lifekeepers, falsely blamed for Elysia’s destruction, attacked by Arbiters, made pawns in games played by gods and criminals alike. And now they would die here, in this gods-forsaken marsh, because he wasn’t strong enough to protect them.

The thought ignited something—not in his mind, but deeper. At his very core, where silver destruction magic had made its home, something else stirred. Something darker, more primal. Something that resonated not with the storm but with the patterns that governed reality itself.

“Enough,” he whispered, the word lost to the howling wind.

Then, louder: “ENOUGH!”

He reached inward, not for the familiar silver energy but for the foreign presence he’d sensed lurking beneath it. His fingers splayed as if physically grasping for power buried deep within himself, beyond conscious thought, beyond trained technique.

For one terrible moment, nothing happened.

Then—

Silver erupted from his palms, different from before—brighter, more concentrated. But threading through it, so briefly he might have imagined it, was a flash of red. Not gold, not silver-gold, but crimson as fresh blood. It vanished almost instantly, subsumed into the silver torrent, but the magic that emerged was transformed—wilder, more potent, yet paradoxically more controlled.

The energy shot skyward, colliding with the vortex’s underbelly. This time, the storm recoiled. Lightning lashed down in retaliation, striking Cade’s shield repeatedly. Each impact sent pain lancing through his body, but the barrier held.

Not just held—it expanded, silver light forming a dome that encompassed his entire crew.

“RUN!” he shouted through gritted teeth, every muscle straining against the divine assault. “I DON’T KNOW HOW LONG I CAN HOLD THIS!”

They needed no further urging. With Bunny leading, they raced toward the cathedral, slipping and stumbling through treacherous terrain made deadly by the storm’s fury.

Cade shuffled backward, maintaining focus on the shield while moving toward safety. Each step felt leaden, the unfamiliar magic draining him faster than he’d anticipated. Black spots danced at the edges of his vision.

The vortex followed their movement precisely, remaining centered above them despite their retreat. Lightning intensified, no longer merely striking the shield but actively seeking weak points, probing like sentient fingers for a way through.

Twenty yards from safety, his vision began to blur. The silver energy around his hands flickered dangerously, its pattern somehow different—more intricate, with underlying structures he’d never noticed before.

Ten yards. His legs threatened to buckle, muscles burning with fatigue. The shield contracted, barely encompassing their group now.

Five yards. Bunny reached the cathedral doors first, squeezing through the narrow opening. The others followed quickly, Orro pausing at the threshold to look back.

The assassin’s eyes widened at whatever he saw. “CADE! NOW!”

Through rapidly tunneling vision, Cade glanced upward. The vortex had contracted to its smallest point yet, energy compressed into a spear of pure destruction. This wasn’t just another strike—this was Destruction’s final, focused wrath.

With his last reserves of strength, Cade abandoned defense for speed. He dropped the shield and sprinted the final distance, diving through the doorway as divine fury descended.

The world exploded into blinding light and deafening sound, the ground where he’d stood a heartbeat earlier vaporized into nothingness. The shockwave slammed him fully inside, throwing him across ancient flagstones to crash against a broken pew.

Then darkness claimed him.

“—not natural progression—”

“—should be dead already—”

“—stop saying that around him!—”

Voices penetrated the fog enveloping Cade’s consciousness, fragments of worried conversation floating above him like debris on troubled water.

He forced his eyes open, blinking against the pain that immediately lanced through his skull. Seven concerned faces swam into focus, plus Bunny’s snout inches from his nose.

“Welcome back,” Rayka said softly, relief evident in her voice.

“Was I gone?” Cade croaked, throat raw.

“Only physically,” Jer quipped, though worry tightened the corners of his eyes. “Your spirit of terrible decision-making remained present throughout.”

“Facing off against a goddess,” Elena marveled, shaking her head. “New levels of recklessness, even for you.”

“It worked, didn’t it?” Cade attempted to sit up, muscles protesting with each movement.

“Barely,” Orro said, offering a waterskin. “Drink.”

Cade obeyed, the liquid cooling his parched throat. As awareness returned fully, he took stock of their surroundings.

They were inside the cathedral now, sheltered from the storm that continued to rage beyond massive stone walls. Lightning flashed through broken windows and collapsed sections of ceiling, illuminating a space that defied easy comprehension.

The interior stretched impossibly far, dimensions that seemed to exceed what he’d observed from outside. Columns carved with intricate symbols rose to dizzying heights, supporting arched ceilings that vanished into shadow. Remnants of pews created obstacle courses across a floor inlaid with metallic patterns that resembled star charts.

But what truly captured his attention were his own hands. The familiar silver energy still pulsed faintly around his fingers—but different now. More intense, more structured, with a complexity that hadn’t existed before.

“Something’s changed,” he murmured, flexing his fingers experimentally.

“Yeah, you could say that,” Gav said, arms crossed over his chest. “You’ve gone Late Stage.”

The words took a moment to penetrate Cade’s exhaustion-fogged mind. When they did, cold realization followed.

“That’s not possible,” he said, echoing what he knew they all must be thinking. “Middle to Late in what, a week?”

“Six days,” Nora corrected, her expression grim. “It shouldn’t happen that fast.”

“Maybe I’m just naturally talented?” Cade attempted.

“No one’s that talented,” Nora replied flatly. “The body has natural limits. Progression requires adaptation—cellular restructuring, neural pathways that have to develop gradually.”

“She means you should be bleeding from your eyeballs right now,” Jer translated helpfully. “Possibly melting from the inside out.”

“Thank you, Jer,” Elena muttered. “Very comforting.”

“What? It’s true! When Uncle Tobias tried to force-progress, his teeth fell out and his skin turned green!”

“That was because he drank swamp water, you idiot,” Elena countered.

“Regardless,” Gav interrupted, cutting off the twins’ bickering, “accelerated progression like this should be causing massive physiological trauma. Yet you seem...”

“Fine,” Cade finished, though the word felt hollow. How would he know if something was fundamentally wrong? “Maybe I’m special?”

“Or maybe there’s more to your magic than we understand,” Rayka suggested, her gaze too perceptive for comfort.

Bunny chirped, butting his head against Cade’s knee in what might have been agreement or simple affection. The dragonling seemed utterly unfazed by recent events, as if divine confrontations and impossible magical progressions were perfectly ordinary occurrences.

“We can dissect my potentially fatal magical acceleration later,” Cade said, struggling to his feet. “Right now, we need to find what we came for before Bernard gets away with it.”

“If he hasn’t already,” Orro noted, gesturing toward a distant corner where fresh marks disturbed centuries of dust.

Cade followed the assassin’s gaze, noticing what he’d initially missed in his disoriented state. Someone had been methodically examining the cathedral’s artifacts and inscriptions—not randomly, but in a pattern that suggested specific knowledge.

“Look,” Rayka said, crouching to examine a boot print preserved in mud tracked inside. “Arbiter issue. Honor Corps standard.”

“Why would Arbiters be investigating a place they’re supposedly quarantining?” Elena asked, frowning.

“The same reason anyone breaks rules,” Jer answered. “They think the prize is worth the risk.”

“These markings,” Nora murmured, running fingers over symbols carved into a nearby column. “Progenitor script. Most of it i’s untranslatable.”

“But someone’s trying,” Cade observed, indicating where certain symbols had been carefully cleared of grime while others remained untouched. “Selectively, too. They’re looking for specific information.”

“Maybe they already found it,” Gav suggested. “Along with Bernard.”

The mention of their adversary rekindled Cade’s sense of urgency. Whatever their theological discoveries, they still had a practical mission—retrieve the Soulkeeper’s Crown before Bernard could use it.

Chubbs, who had been uncharacteristically quiet since their entry, suddenly pulled away from Evie’s watchful grasp. The toddler goddess toddled determinedly toward the cathedral’s central altar, her tiny form dwarfed by the massive stone structure.

“Chubbs! Wait!” Evie called, hurrying after her.

The child ignored her, moving with purpose that belied her apparent age. When she reached the altar—a massive slab of unmarred white stone—she placed both hands on its surface and turned to look at them expectantly.

“Shiny crown hiding,” she announced with childish certainty. “Under big rock.”

Bunny, apparently sharing Chubbs’ confidence, scampered up to join her at the altar. The dragonling sniffed industriously along its base, pawing at a specific section where the flooring pattern converged beneath the stone slab.

“Well, that’s convenient,” Jer remarked. “The resident goddess and dragon agree. Under the altar it is.”

They gathered around the massive stone, examining what had captured the attention of the childof child goddess and dragonling. Behind it, partially concealed by centuries of debris, stone steps descended into darkness—the entrance to what could only be the crypt.

Gav cleared away some of the fallen rubble, revealing more of the passageway. He bent lower, frowning as his enhanced senses detected something the others couldn’t yet see.

“Wait,” he said, voice tight with disappointment. “There’s a problem.”

As he moved more debris aside, the issue became apparent to everyone. The stairway wasn’t merely cluttered with collapsed stonework—it was completely sealed by a wall of fused rock and crystal. The barrier wasn’t natural—the stone had been melted and reformed with deliberate magical precision, spires and layers created specifically to prevent passage.

“Someone didn’t want visitors,” Elena muttered, testing the barrier with the tip of her dagger. The blade scraped uselessly against the magically reinforced material.

“Bernard,” Cade growled, frustration rising anew.

“Of course it’s Bernard,” Jer threw his hands up dramatically. “It’s always Bernard! Bernard this, Bernard that. Does the man never take a holiday?”

“Apparently not from ruining our lives,” Elena snapped, kicking the barrier. She yelped as her boot connected with the unyielding surface. “Ow! Dammit!”

“So what now?” Rayka asked, the question hanging heavy in the air. “We came all this way for nothing?”

“We could try to break it down,” Gav suggested without conviction. “Though if Bernard created it...”

“We’d need a siege engine,” Nora finished grimly. “Or earth magic. Which none of us have.”

“Maybe we could go around?” Evie ventured. “Find another entrance?”

“In a building this size? We’d be searching for days,” Orro countered. “And I doubt Bernard left any other routes unblocked.”

Their voices rose, frustration and disappointment feeding on each other as they debated increasingly improbable solutions. The argument intensified, echoing through the cavernous space, each suggestion more desperate than the last.

Cade barely heard them. Something was building inside him—not just anger but resolve, crystallizing with dangerous intensity. He’d endured too much to be stopped by a wall. Hunted by Lifekeepers who blamed him for crimes he didn’t commit. Attacked by Arbiters for the crime of existing. Made a pawn by the Clover Clan, by Death, by ancient gods who couldn’t be bothered to clean up their own messes.

Even Destruction, his supposed patroness, couldn’t ease her global tantrum long enough to prevent them from being obliterated.

He was tired. Tired of running. Tired of reacting. Tired of being the one who bent while the rest of the world remained rigid and unyielding.

“—could try to dig under it—” Elena was saying.

“—with what? Our winning personalities?—” Jer countered.

“—if we had earth magic—” Gav began.

“—but we don’t—” Rayka cut in.

Their voices faded to background noise as Cade rose slowly to his feet. Silently, he moved toward the barrier, ignoring Rayka’s questioning glance. When he reached the fused stone wall, he placed both palms flat against its surface. The material felt cold beneath his touch—unnaturally so, as if actively resisting intrusion.

“Cade?” Nora asked cautiously. “What are you doing?”

He didn’t answer.

Instead, he closed his eyes, focusing on the dual sensations within his core—the familiar silver destruction magic and something deeper. Something older. Something that had flashed crimson during the storm.

The crew fell silent, watching as silver energy began to swirl around Cade’s hands. It pulsed in rhythm with his heartbeat, growing brighter with each passing second.

“I don’t think that’s going to—” Orro began.

“BURN,” Cade commanded.

The single word reverberated through the cathedral with impossible force. For a split second—so brief most of them missed it—a thread of crimson intertwined with the silver around his hands. Then the energies merged, and the barrier began to glow.

Not just glow—transform.

Stone liquefied beneath Cade’s touch, becoming molten without crumbling. The material flowed like water, responding to his will with impossible precision. He shaped it deliberately, forcing the molten rock to spiral outward in a perfect arch large enough for them to walk through upright.

Heat radiated in waves, but strangely, Cade felt nothing but cool determination. The effort drained him, but nowhere near the exhaustion he’d experienced fighting the storm. This felt... right. Natural. As if he’d finally found a door that matched his key.

When he stepped back, a tunnel carved through the barrier stood before them, its interior surfaces smooth as glass, still glowing dull red from the intense heat.

Silence fell, broken only by the distant rumble of thunder and Bunny’s appreciative chirp.

“Well,” Jer finally said, voice pitched higher than normal, “that’s one way to make an entrance.”

“How did you do that?” Nora asked, the question simple but loaded with implications none of them were prepared to address.

“I asked nicely,” Cade replied, examining his unmarked hands with a calmness he didn’t entirely feel. The silver energy still pulsed around his fingers, but there was no sign of the crimson thread that had briefly manifested.

“That wasn’t destruction magic,” Gav stated flatly. “That was something else entirely.”

“Creation maybe?” Elena ventured. “You made something new.”

“From something that already existed,” Rayka countered. “That’s transformation, not creation.”

“Whatever it was,” Cade interrupted before the debate could continue, “it worked. We have a way forward now.”

“That we do,” Orro agreed, already drawing his blades in preparation. “Though I suggest we proceed with caution. Bernard clearly wanted to prevent anyone from following him.”

“Not to mention all the fun things that might be included in the spooky-crypt package we received when we agreed to this job,” Cade said, stepping toward the still-cooling tunnel.

“Aren’t we going to talk about what just happened?” Nora pressed, concern evident in her voice.

“Later,” Cade promised, though the word rang hollow even to his own ears. “After we find the crown.”

Bunny approached the tunnel first, sniffing cautiously before chirping his approval. The dragonling seemed utterly unfazed by Cade’s display of power, as if this development were perfectly natural.

“I’ll lead,” Cade decided, already moving forward. “Orro, watch our tail. Gav, Nora, Rayka next. Evie, the twins and Chubbs stay here in the center where it’s safer. ”

For once, no one argued with the assignments. Whatever waited below clearly warranted their most experienced fighters.

“Be careful,” Rayka urged, genuine worry creasing her brow. “And maybe try not to melt anything else for a while?”

“No promises,” Cade replied with a confidence he didn’t entirely feel.

He stepped into the tunnel, surprised to find the interior cool enough to touch despite its recent transformation from solid to liquid and back again. The passage extended perhaps twenty feet before opening into absolute darkness—not merely absence of light, but a darkness that seemed to possess weight and substance.

As Cade emerged from the tunnel into the vast underground chamber beyond, his eyes struggled to adjust, perceiving shapes and dimensions that seemed to shift when directly observed. The others joined him one by one, their combined magical energies pushing back against the strange darkness, revealing glimpses of their surroundings.

The crypt—if that term could possibly encompass what they’d discovered—extended farther than their light could penetrate, its ceiling lost in shadows despite being underground. Columns carved from single pieces of crystal supported unseen arches. The floor, visible in patches beneath centuries of dust, consisted of metallic tiles arranged in spiraling patterns that mirrored the cosmos itself.

And at the center of it all stood a simple stone sarcophagus, its lid partially pushed aside.

“We’re too late,” Rayka whispered, the words falling like stones into still water.

The true challenge had only just begun.
