Chapter 73

"The discipline taught me that the most interesting question is the one the evidence does not yet let you answer. Today I walked through a corridor that had been hidden for a hundred and ten years, and I am no closer to a question the evidence will let me ask."  - The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr, Tarxien Temples

Arthur came off the last step and the chamber opened before them.

He had expected another working space carved into Maltese limestone, scaled like the rooms above. What he stepped into was older than the Hypogeum and built using a different expertise.

Though the room still raised awe, they had seen its like before. Humans, given time, will adjust to anything.

“Portal room,” Steve whispered. “Our Malta path is done.”

The walls were the same dense grey-blue stone he had seen before, beneath a London church. The dome above was carved as a single catenary curve, joined to the floor in a seam he could not locate by eye. The air was warmer than the staircase. It was also denser, and cleaner, and the Ether moved through his Nadi-Matrix in a nourishing wave.

Sarah stopped beside him. “That feels different,” she said quietly.

“This is your first time seeing a portal chamber,” Arthur said. “The Ether in these is denser and cleaner than anywhere else.”

“You guys came to Malta through one of these?”

“Yes. From Marseille and London before that.”

Donna made a circuit of the room and came back to the group.

“Two doors,” she said. “Same as the others. Standard issue.”

Steve was already at the chamber’s edge with the pencil out.

The platform at the centre sat raised by half a metre, its surface the same near-black material they had stood on beneath the Temple Church and at Cosquer. It caught the soft blue light from the wall channels and bent it sideways. From the front it was solid. From the corner of his eye, it had a depth the room did not contain.

Concentric rings were cut into the platform’s face, each ring carrying its own family of symbols, and a single white mark held the centre.

“Hello again,” Steve said, writing down the symbols.

“It’s the same room,” Arthur said. “Built by the same hand.”

“Built to the same specification,” Archie said from the centre of the chamber. “The construction crews change. The drawings do not.”

Steve looked at the passage leading away from the chamber.

The second opening was set into the wall on the chamber’s western arc. It was taller and wider than the staircase they had come down, with a flat machined floor that ran into darkness.

* * *

Sarah came up beside Arthur and put one hand on his forearm.

“We have to go back.”

Arthur turned his head to look at her properly.

“To the camp. And then to the Baron’s. We told him we will train whoever we can.”

“Sarah.” Donna walked over and stood square with her. She chose her words carefully. “I’m not telling you no. But I will tell you what the picture looks like. He’s a smuggler. Smugglers run on ledgers. He let you go because the maths that day said you cost him more than you were worth in a fight. The maths on the day we walk back might be different. He will look at us and count what he can recover.”

“He needs the training more than he needs me,” Sarah said. “There are four nurses in Mosta. Two midwives in Marsaxlokk. A man in Cospicua who ran a hospital pharmacy for thirty years. They have hands and they have instincts. I can give them three days and turn them into people who keep his fighters alive after I’m gone. He gets a healing corps. The island gets people who heal it after we leave.”

“And if he decides three days is not enough.”

“Then I leave anyway. That is the part you can hold me to.”

Donna held her eyes for a beat.

“Arthur.”

“You’re both right.” He kept his voice level. “She is not wrong about the healers, especially with Steve mixed in. You are not wrong about the maths the Baron will run. We said we would, so we will. We go in on our clock. We leave on our clock.”

* * *

The cargo passage ran straight and level for what Steve later estimated at four hundred and ten metres. The walls were the same dense grey-blue stone as the chamber. The ceiling held a uniform four metres. Polished channel cuts ran along the floor at intervals, smooth in the bottoms where runners had moved across them for a long time.

“Is it morbid that I am relieved nobody is hunting us?” Sarah said.

“Depends on whether that makes you happy or sad,” Steve said from behind her.

The passage rose at the last forty metres in a long, shallow grade, and terminated at a stone-cut door, its sides finished with the same machine precision as the portal chamber.

Arthur stood next to it with his shield ready and gave it a shove. The door swung outward on pivots with remarkably little effort. Then they were standing in the light after three days.

“That door would be what, ten tonnes?” Steve said, coming out. “It’s remarkable, how easily it swung open.”

“The Pantheon’s bronze door is about seventeen and you can still push it with one hand. Same technology,” Arthur said.

They had come out in a chamber half-collapsed by time. Stone megaliths leaned at the angles time had set for them. Carved spiral slabs had broken inward across centuries and lay where they had fallen. A statue base sat in the centre of the room. The statue was missing from the knees up, and the lower half of it wore a pleated skirt that the wind had been polishing for five thousand years. The feet were remarkably well carved.

“Oh,” Steve said, looking around the chamber.

“What.”

“This is Tarxien.”

He turned a slow full circle, taking in the broken slabs and the colossal half-statue and the carved friezes on the surviving walls. Sarah stepped past him into the light, blinking hard against it.

“This is Tarxien,” Steve said again. His voice had dropped into the register it used when he was paying attention with everything he had. “The Magna Mater. The animal friezes and those decorated slabs. We came up inside the southern chamber of the Tarxien complex. It is four hundred metres north of the Hypogeum. It was excavated in nineteen-fifteen and everyone has been calling it an offering temple for a hundred and ten years.”

“And is it not an offering temple?” Sarah asked.

“Well, not in a way the community meant. The Hypogeum was the testing chamber. Tarxien sits at the surface end of the same route. It could be where they prepared the test subjects. It could be where they held them before the descent. It could be where the families waited. It could be where the rites for the ones who never came back were done.” He looked around with a helpless expression. The researcher in him was struggling with too much new information.

“I don’t have enough to choose between those, and anyone who tells you otherwise is lying. What I can tell you is that Tarxien served the Hypogeum, and that fact has been sitting under our discipline’s nose since nineteen-fifteen.”

He looked back at the passage mouth. “The entirety of human history will have to be rewritten. It would take hundreds of researchers and tens of thousands of man-hours.”

“Steve,” Donna said, her axe coming up.

“I know I’m cascading, but—”

“Steve. Ridge.”

Five shapes crested the slope to the north and started moving towards them. Compact bodies, low to the ground, thickened around the skull and the lower legs in heavy bone plating that caught the sun. They came down the slope at speed.

Arthur was off the temple stones in three strides, shield up, hammer free.

* * *

“Five,” Donna said. “Sarah, behind me.”

“Level eleven to twelve,” Archie said, geometry rotating once. “Cranial plating dense. Tibial plating secondary, partial. Pack coordination is functional.”

The first caprid reached Arthur at full charge and he took the impact on the Ironhold and slid two metres backward across the loose limestone.

The second one was a lunge behind the first. Arthur braced and activated Counterforce Plant for the first time.

The blue lattice on his arms brightened just as the caprid hit the shield. His body did not move. The impact went into him and gathered in his arms. He could feel it sitting there, ready to be spent.

The caprid bounced off, found its feet, and turned for the next pass.

Arthur swung the hammer and let the stored force go into the strike.

The hammer came down at the seam behind the cranial plate where the neck joint sat undefended. The impact landed harder than anything he had managed so far. The lattice flared at the contact point and the head disappeared in a shower of gore.

“Holy shit,” Steve said.

Arthur gave a grin and moved up to the first caprid, leaving the other three for Steve and Donna.

Donna triggered Threshold Drive for two seconds. To an observer, it would seem she was moving in stop-starts, but her next start was always much further than her last stop. The axe came down twice on the third one and it collapsed. As she reversed her axe and swung upward at the fourth, a heat lance passed her by and impacted the side of its neck. The creature folded.

Steve raised his rod and channelled Convergence on the last creature. It was coming at him and then it stopped, unsure of what was happening to it. Heat and cold on the same creature at the same time. A second later Donna’s axe found its neck.

“Hey! I was trying to understand the interaction,” Steve said.

Arthur had finished the one on him and joined the other three, picking out bone fragments from his vest.

“The new technique is amazing. It is also messy,” he said.

“I just levelled,” Sarah said, blinking her eyes.

“The Level 15 technique is available as well, but I’m not integrating it standing in the open. Later.”

Sarah drew the small knife from her kit and went around digging into the caprids, looking for cores.

“I can’t tell you how happy I am that you do the corpse diving now,” Donna said.

“Two from five,” she said, standing and wiping her hands on a piece of cloth Donna had handed her without asking. “Three were on the way to it.”

“You are getting good at this,” Donna said.

* * *

They walked back across the open ground toward the road and Tarxien fell behind them.

“The whole southern half of Malta could be a single integrated facility,” Steve said. “We have been touring it as a chain of unrelated heritage sites. I can think of at least four papers I can write.”

“And who will publish it?” Sarah asked with a smile.

“Whoever figures out the printing press again, I suppose. That doesn’t mean the paper doesn’t merit writing.”

“Steve.”

“Yes.”

“Eat a fig.”

He took the fig Sarah held out and ate it without breaking stride.

Sarah dropped back beside Arthur.

“How did the Counterforce Plant feel,” she asked.

He thought about it as he walked. “Like the force was somewhere else for half a second. The hit landed but I didn’t feel it push against me. The weight went into my frame and sat there.”

“Do you feel any internal damage?”

“None I can feel. I think the technique stored the force without routing it through the joints.”

“I’ll check anyway when we stop.”

“Wouldn’t expect anything else.”

She smiled and squeezed his elbow.

Donna, walking alongside, cracked a small smile of her own.

* * *

They reached the camp just as the sun was going down.

A man in legionnaire grey was walking the perimeter with another man in dark canvas Donna did not recognise. The camp seemed to have grown in the days they had been away.

Captain Azzopardi was walking out to meet them before they reached the line, his hand raised in greeting.

“Mela. You are back, and you are walking on your own legs, which is more than I had hoped. Welcome, my friends. Your tent has been kept for you. The boy will take you to it. Eat. Sleep. Rest. We will talk in the morning.”

He shook their hands and walked away, towards his own tent.

“Did Azzopardi seem weird to you guys?” Steve asked in a whisper as they walked towards the tent allocated to them.

“Yes,” all three said in unison.

“Could be something to do with the Baron. He did seem overly friendly. Maybe he wants to keep us on his side,” Arthur said.

“Or maybe it is something else entirely and we should keep our eyes and ears open,” Donna responded.

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

Tarxien Temples, Malta

THE LONG SILENCE

The discipline taught me that the most interesting question is the one the evidence does not yet let you answer. Today I walked through a corridor that had been hidden for a hundred and ten years, and I am no closer to a question the evidence will let me ask.

The corridor was not concealed. The corridor was excavated. Themistocles Zammit and his team pulled the limestone fill out of the Tarxien southern chamber in 1915 and worked the complex for the next four years. They published meticulously. The site has had continuous attention from the international community since the interwar period. We have surveyed Tarxien with ground-penetrating radar, magnetometry, resistivity, lidar. The passage was visible the entire time. We called it a ritual deposition chamber and stopped asking.

Which is the better problem, because it tells you something about how a framework chooses what its members are allowed to see. We were looking for a temple. We found temple architecture. Everything outside the category went into the footnotes as anomaly. The colossal half-statue. The animal friezes. The decorated slabs. The cargo passage. A century of archaeologists inherited the interpretation and reinforced it, because to question the offering-temple framework would have required questioning the entire Maltese megalithic literature, which would have required questioning the model of how Neolithic societies organised themselves. The cost of seeing was too high. So nobody saw.

I would like to leave it there. The discipline failed because frameworks are sticky. Kuhn wrote a book about it in 1962. I have cited him on six occasions in my own work, and I have always preferred his account to the alternative.

The alternative is that somebody made sure the framework stuck.
