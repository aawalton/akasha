The new inn had solved the wine problem in the same way a larger cup solved drowning.

It had helped.

Alric was willing to admit that much, if only because the old inn had reached the point where every decision began with moving three barrels out of the way and ended with putting them somewhere worse. Barrels had been tucked into rooms, stacked in passages, and arranged in corners with a confidence that disappeared the moment anyone needed to open a door.

The new inn, by comparison, had space. Too much space, which was worse in a different way.

He had spent most of the morning using his item box to clear out furniture from rooms that had once belonged to guests with lives, complaints, and luggage. Beds vanished. Small tables vanished. Chairs vanished in groups, as though embarrassed to be seen going one at a time. It was not dignified. It was, however, effective.

By the time he returned to the still house site, his coat smelled faintly of old linen, dust, and wine casks. He brushed at one sleeve as he walked, accomplished nothing, and gave up.

The still house had changed while he was away.

That was becoming a habit with buildings. One left them unattended for a few bells and returned to find a wall missing, a roof opened, or a dwarf standing in the remains with an expression that suggested he was having a fantastic day.

This time, two sections of wall had been taken down entirely. The old internal divisions were gone, leaving the shape of the place exposed and strangely honest. Beams had been propped where they needed to be propped. Broken stone had been dragged into piles. Dust lay over everything, but not evenly. The floor had been swept in long rough paths where people had been moving through, and the air had that dry, gritty feel that made every breath memorable.

Stromni stood near the centre with a measuring cord in one hand and chalk in the other.

He looked up as Alric approached.

“Ye took yer time.”

“I was moving furniture.”

“Aye. Serious work, that.”

“I moved beds.”

Stromni gave him a look. “Did they fight back?”

“One of them, a little.”

“Then ye should have brought me.”

Alric stepped over a loose stone and entered the open shell of the building. The space looked larger without its walls. Less useful, somehow, but more willing.

Stromni followed his gaze. “Right then. Show me this offence ye’ve been smiling about.”

Alric ignored him and took the chalk.

That was probably a mistake. He knew it the moment Stromni’s eyebrows shifted. Not much. Just enough to show that the dwarf had noticed ownership of the chalk passing from hand to hand with more confidence than usual.

Alric crouched near the far side of the building, the side opposite the river, and drew a broad rectangle on the stone.

“Grain starts here.”

Stromni looked down. “Aye?”

“Germination along this side. Kilning beside it. Crushing here, close enough that sacks don’t need to cross the whole building before they become useful.”

“Mm.”

“Dry work stays together. Away from the still rooms. Away from the main drainage. Away from anything that might decide to become fire.”

Stromni grunted, which was not disagreement. Alric had learned to recognise the difference. Disagreement had more beard in it.

He drew three shorter marks along one side of the building.

“Boilers here. Three copper boilers like the breweries.”

Stromni’s eyes narrowed.

Alric glanced at him. “The same should do, I’ll explain as we go.” Stromni merely grunted.

Alric marked the positions anyway. “They sit higher. Not too high, but high enough to pour into troughs.”

Stromni stopped looking at the chalk and began looking at the walls.

That was better. That meant he had moved from complaint to calculation.

“How high?”

Alric pointed along the line. “Enough to carry the hot mash down to the centre without lifting.”

“Centre?”

Alric walked there and drew two circles. Large ones. Stromni did not react at first. He simply looked at them. Then he looked at Alric.

Alric had just marked out the space where the washbacks would go, each would be roughly fifteen thousand litres. Liquids would run from the boilers into the washback where it would cool and then ferment.

Dust shifted somewhere behind them as a worker dragged stone across the floor. The sound arrived too loudly into the gap between words.

“Each,” Stromni said “How many?”

Alric tried not to smile as he held up two fingers.

Stromni stepped closer to the first chalk circle, then to the second, measuring with his eyes and apparently not liking what his eyes had to say. He walked the edge of one slowly, boots scuffing through dust.

“That ain’t a washback, lad. That’s a pond.”

“It works.”

“It had better.”

“Each one fills the big still five times.”

Stromni stopped walking. There it was.

Not the alarm exactly. The pause before alarm, when a number had found the right door in someone’s mind and entered without wiping its boots.

“Five times? And there are two?”

“Yes.”

“And the boilers feed these?”

Alric drew lines from the boiler side toward the washbacks. “Through troughs.”

“Hot?”

“Yes.”

“Full.”

“Not all at once.”

Stromni turned his head slowly.

Alric sighed. “Fine. Sometimes all at once, if someone is an idiot. Which is why there will be overflow points. The washbacks will have holes in the side that goes into a trough and runs out to the river.”

Stromni looked down at the chalk lines again.

Alric drew them in before the dwarf could ask. Short cuts from the main troughs. Drain marks. A side channel that would catch a bad pour before it reached the middle of the floor.

“The troughs from the boilers run down to the washbacks. The washbacks sit here, in the centre, because everything needs to gather around them without everything needing to carry to them.”

“Everything needs to fall to them,” Stromni said nodding slowly.

Alric moved to the other side of the washbacks and drew two more lines, longer this time, running toward the river end of the building.

“Then the washbacks run into their own troughs.”

Stromni followed. Alric continued walking as he drew, chalk scraping over the stone in a pale line.

“To the still.”

The far end of the building was closer to the river. Even with the outer doors closed, the smell found its way in through gaps and broken mortar. Damp. Mud. Old reeds. City water carrying things no one wanted to name too carefully.

Alric had not liked the river when he first viewed the inn. He liked it more here. Here, it was useful.

“The still goes here,” he said.

Stromni stood beside him and looked back across the building.

From this end, the chalk made more sense. Grain at the far side. Boilers along one wall. Washbacks in the centre. Troughs carrying the work onward. The whole building leaned in the mind before a single stone had been raised.

Stromni rubbed at his beard.

“Opposite end from the boilers. Long controlled run,” Stromni said nodding.

Alric marked a thick line where the first new internal wall would go. “This wall makes the big still room.”

He marked another beside it. “Collection room next to it.”

Stromni crouched without being asked, peering at the lines.

“Why a wall between?”

“Heat, access, control. Also, people are stupid.”

“Aye, but that’s usually handled with shouting.”

“Shouting doesn’t stop vapour.”

“Maybe you don’t shout at it right?”

Alric ignored that too and tapped the line between the two rooms. “The still room is where the dangerous part sits. The collection room is where people handle what it gives us. They shouldn’t be the same room.”

Stromni nodded slowly. “Window?”

Alric smiled properly this time. Stromni saw it and immediately looked offended.

“A window,” Alric said, drawing it into the wall. “Here.”

“Why are ye happy about a window?”

“Because it means someone can watch the still without standing beside it. Also the washback troughs will run through the window into the still room.”

Stromni stared at him.

“The room will be hot,” Alric continued. “And dangerous. If we need to check the run, they can look through the window first. Then only go in when it’s needed.”

“And if they need to get out?”

Alric moved toward the river-facing wall and marked three channels.

“Drainage. Also we will have a door here, metal though. Nothing that can burn in the still room.”

Stromni did not speak.

Alric added another line, deeper, with a second pass of chalk.

“If the still room spills, it runs this way. If something catches fire, the channels take it out. If the still has a bad day, it goes toward the river.”

“The river.”

“Yes.”

“Ye are designing where the fire goes.”

Stromni glanced at the river wall. Then at the chalk. Then back through the building.

His expression had changed.

Alric knew that look too. It was not concern. It was concern finding structure and becoming something worse. Interest.

“Show me the fall again,” Stromni said.

Alric walked him back to the boilers.

They went slowly this time. Stromni stopped at each point, asking for heights. Not exact numbers yet, but enough to trap the shape in his head. Boiler platform. First trough. Washback lip. Overflow. Second trough. Still feed. Drainage into the river-side channels.

Each answer seemed to make Stromni less comfortable.

“So lad, you make mash that side, then pour it inta the troughs to the washback, which has a hole and drain for overflow, that then runs ta the still.” Stromni paused for a long moment looking at the design, “It’s rude.”

“To who?”

“To work.”

Alric glanced at him.

Stromni stood in the centre of the building now, hands on hips, looking at the washback marks as though they had disappointed several ancestors.

“Ye’ve made a still house that doesn’t need stillmen.” Stromni lowered his hand. “Aye. That’s the problem.”

Alric waited.

The dwarf did not speak immediately. He turned instead, slowly, taking in the whole building once more. Workers were still moving stone near the entrance, but they had begun doing it more quietly, which meant they were listening while pretending not to.

Stromni noticed and did not care.

“Every guild in this city would hate this,” he said.

Alric looked at the chalk lines. “Probably.”

“Not probably. Would. Still hands most of all. Ye’ve taken half the things a man learns over twenty years and put them in the floor.”

Alric opened his mouth, then closed it.

Stromni jabbed a finger at the washbacks. “A man learns how full is too full. Ye put a hole there, and now the hole knows.”

“That is the point of the hole.”

“Aye. I ken the point of the hole. That’s why it’s offensive.”

Alric could not help it. He smiled.

Stromni saw that too.

“Don’t look pleased with yerself.”

“It reduces lifting. It reduces burns. It reduces ruined batches. It keeps people from having to carry hot liquid across a floor because someone thought tradition needed more blisters.”

“Tradition does enjoy blisters,” Stromni admitted.

“Experienced workers should be checking quality. Watching the run. Fixing problems. Teaching people. Not spending the day hauling weight because the building is too stupid to help them.”

Stromni’s mouth twitched.

Alric pointed back along the fall of the trough. “Visible skill is often just visible risk.”

The twitch disappeared. For a few breaths, Stromni looked genuinely annoyed. Not at Alric, precisely.

“That,” Stromni said at last, “is the sort of thing a man says before a guild throws stones through his windows.”

“I have shutters. Should we brace them?” Alric asked with a grin, it got one from Stromni too.

“Dwarf holds would hate it too.”

Alric looked at him. “The holds?”

“Aye.” Stromni lifted his chin, looking over the gutted shell as though judging it on behalf of a distant mountain. “Every hold under stone has some old master who thinks the shape of a workshop should prove the worth of the hands inside it. Tools where they belong. Fire where it can be read. Benches at the right height because someone’s grandfather cut them that way and his grandfather was never wrong unless dead men are allowed ta be.”

“That sounds like a lot.”

“It is. Mostly shouting.” Stromni folded his arms. “But this? This is worse. Ye’ve hidden the craft in the building. A fool can stand in the right place and seem less foolish because the wall did half his thinking first.”

“That is also the point of the wall.”

“I ken the point of the wall.”

“You keep saying that.”

“Because the point keeps insulting me.”

Alric nodded toward the chalk. “And?”

Stromni looked at him.

“And what?”

“You said every guild and dwarf hold would hate it.”

“Aye.”

“And?”

Stromni stared for a moment longer.

Then he grinned.

It arrived slowly, forcing its way through the offence until the offence had to make room.

“And they’d be right,” he said.

Alric laughed.

Stromni took the chalk from him.

“Still an abomination.”

“I know.”

“No, ye don’t. Ye think ye know, but ye’re foreign in all the ways that matter and some that don’t. This will make proper men furious for reasons they’ll struggle ta explain without sounding frightened.”

“That is usually how things improve.”

“Do not say that ta a room full of dwarves.”

“I wasn’t planning to.”

“Good. They’d hit ye with something educational.”

Stromni crouched beside the trough line running from the washbacks to the still. He studied it, then thickened the chalk mark.

“This fall is too soft.”

“It needs control.”

“It needs ta move. Control comes here.” He tapped a point near the still feed. “Gate there. Removable, not fixed. If it clogs, ye dinnae want some poor bastard taking apart half the trough.”

Alric crouched beside him.

“That works.”

“Aye, because I said it.” Stromni shifted and marked a small square. “Inspection hatch here.”

“For a trough?”

“For the thing that will clog because grain is spiteful.”

“Fair.”

“And this overflow from the first washback.” Stromni leaned back, squinting. “Too close ta the working path.”

Alric looked at it again.

He hated that Stromni was right.

Stromni noticed.

“Enjoying yerself less now?”

“A little.”

“Good.”

He drew a new line, sharper, angled away from where workers would stand. “Here. It spills behind the stone lip and into the waste channel. If some fool misses the level, he gets shame, not boiling wash in his boots.”

The building changed under the chalk.

Not physically, yet, but the lines began to settle. Some were rubbed out with a boot and drawn again. Others were thickened. A few were circled in a way that meant they would become arguments later.

Stromni insulted most of it. He improved all of it.

At the boiler side, he demanded heavier supports and a wider working platform because, as he put it, men carrying tools near hot copper were already trying to die and should not be helped. At the washbacks, he added space around the base, not for beauty, but because anything holding fifteen thousand litres deserved room for people to panic professionally.

At the still room wall, he stopped for longer.

“Thicker and stone.”

“I was going to use stone.”

“Thicker stone.” Stomni thickened the line before moving to the collection room.

Stromni nodded, satisfied despite himself. “So ye left a little craft in the corner. Kind of ye.”

“I was trying to make whisky, not enemies.”

“Then ye should have chosen a different profession.”

They stood in the collection room lines for a moment, looking through the chalk window toward where the still would be.

They tried to imagine it. The grain coming in at the far end. The wet, warm smell of germination. Kilns working. Crushed malt carried to where the boilers waited. Copper catching light along one side of the building. Hot liquor pouring into troughs. The first run downward. The washbacks filled in the centre, huge and quiet and patient. Fermentation turning volume into consequence.

Then the second run. Toward the still. Toward heat. Toward the river, if the river was needed.

Stromni stood beside him, unusually silent.

Alric glanced down and realised his own hand was still dusty white from chalk.

“You’re thinking something,” he said, “Is it expensive?”

“Very.”

Alric closed his eyes briefly.

“Also,” Stromni added, “this place doesnae trust its own workers.”

Alric opened his eyes. Stromni was looking across the chalk lines, not at him.

“It trusts them to be tired,” Alric said.

The dwarf’s gaze flicked to him.

“And new,” Alric continued. “And distracted. And rushed. And wrong sometimes. It trusts them to be people.”

Stromni’s beard shifted as his jaw worked slightly.

“That sounds prettier than it is.”

A worker passed near the entrance, carrying a broken length of timber. He slowed just enough to hear whether either of them would say something else, then apparently decided wisdom lay elsewhere and kept moving.

Alric crouched again at the main drain line and redrew the final section toward the river.

“Failure has to go somewhere.”

“Aye.”

“At this scale, pretending otherwise is just choosing where it goes without admitting it.”

Stromni took the chalk from him again and deepened the channel line.

“Ye keep saying things that would get ye thrown out of guild meetings.”

“I don’t attend guild meetings.”

“That may be why ye’re still alive.”

Alric stood and stepped back. The building looked different now.

Chalk lines crossed dust and broken stone. Some were smudged where boots had dragged through them. Others stopped at missing walls or vanished beneath rubble. The two large washback circles dominated the centre, absurdly large even as drawings. The boiler marks along the side looked almost modest by comparison, which was ridiculous, considering what they represented.

The still room sat at the river end like a warning. Beside it, the collection room looked small and careful. The drains knew where they were going.

Stromni walked to the far end, near the grain section, and looked back. Alric joined him.

From there, the whole process lay in front of them.

The dwarf looked down at the chalk, then across at the boiler side, then along the fall toward the still. He seemed offended all over again. Properly this time. Deeply.

Then he nodded. “Every guild in the city will spit blood if they understand it.”

“Then we won’t explain it that way.”

“They’ll understand eventually.”

“Probably.”

“Every hold would call it an insult ta craft.”

“You said.”

“It is.” Stromni crouched beside the line of the first trough and rubbed out a short section with his thumb. “Also, this needs a steeper fall.”

Alric looked at him. Stromni drew the line again.

“An abomination should at least drain properly,” he said.

The chalk scraped over stone.

Outside, somewhere beyond the broken wall, the river moved on with the dull patience of something willing to accept whatever the city sent it.

Alric watched the new line settle into the dust. The still house was not built yet.

It had no walls, no copper, no washbacks, no window into the dangerous room, no collection vessels waiting in neat rows. It had no smell of malt, no heat, no men swearing at gates that stuck at the worst possible time.

But the work had found its shape.

Stromni stood, brushed chalk from his fingers, and looked back across the building with open disgust and private admiration.

“Aye,” he said. “I love it already.”
