The meeting hall looked different at night. The evening light streaming through the windows gave the warehouse a sort of dreamlike illumination Jane was almost too tired to appreciate. The rough tables had been pushed together into one long surface, which bakers were already loading with their products.

Jane had packed her goods carefully. Each offering was wrapped in clean cloth and nestled in its own basket to guard against jostling on the walk over. The burnt-top bread was still slightly warm, fresh from the oven and timed to be at its best right around now. The keln was wrapped in layers to keep it from drying out. The buns were arranged in a neat row on a pan, each one identical to the others in a way that filled her with quiet pride.

She found an empty spot near the middle of the long table and began unpacking. Other bakers were doing the same around her, each one claiming a section of table and arranging their work with varying degrees of care. Jane spread out her cloths and set up her food simply, reminding herself that the presentation didn’t have to be fancy. In the end, it was quality that counted.

Hopefully.

A stack of paper and a few pens sat at the end of the table for labeling purposes. Jane wrote her name on a small slip and set it in front of her display, then stepped back to look at the full spread.

It looks good.

Shaking her head, she corrected herself.

It IS good. Maybe not better than most of the others, but I know it’s really, really good.

In this moment, Jane decided, that was all that mattered.

She found an empty chair against the wall and sat down, grateful to be off her feet. The last few days had been exhausting, and she was running on less sleep than she would have liked. It was a relief to know she had done all she could do.

The hall was filling up with bakers, along with a few scattered family members. The noise level rose with each new arrival. Jane let her attention drift, too tired to engage with the crowd. Conversations washed over her in fragments, peppered with bits of gossip and speculation that she only half-heard.

"He was originally supposed to arrive in another few days, but I suppose the new rumor is it could be any time now."

"It still doesn’t doesn't make sense, though. Why Glenfall? There's nothing here that isn't also somewhere else, closer to the capital."

Jane closed her eyes and let the words blur together. The king's visit was all anyone could talk about. She supposed that made sense. It wasn't every day that royalty came to a mountain town, even one as great as Glenfall. Still, she almost wished everyone would talk about something else for a few minutes, so she could rest her mind as well as her feet.

"I heard it's about the prince, actually. The king's been taking a hand in his romances, they say."

Jane's eyes opened. Two women were standing a few feet away, speaking just loud enough for her to hear.

"Taking a hand how?" the second woman asked.

"I don't know all the details. But my sister's husband travels, and he said he heard something like that. That the king is looking for a wife for his son."

"A wife? Here?"

"Not just here. Other places too, probably. But here is one of the stops, if any of it’s true at all."

Odd, Jane mused. Who would the king even know in Glenfall?

The town wasn't exactly overflowing with noble families or wealthy merchants. Most of the eligible young women she knew of were working folk. Glenfall seemed like a strange place to look for a royal bride.

Very, very odd…

She shook off the thought before it could develop further. It was probably just rumor anyway. She had heard tons of similar things in the capital, and they rarely panned out to anything approximating truth. The king could be visiting for a hundred different reasons, most of which had nothing to do with marriage or princes. People would talk either way.

Eric, the unofficial leader of Glenfall’s bakers, stepped to the front of the room and raised his hands for quiet. The crowd settled gradually, conversations trailing off into expectant silence.

"Thank you all for coming," Eric said. "I know it's been a busy few days, and I appreciate everyone's hard work. We have a fine selection of goods here tonight. I'm proud of what this town's bakers can produce."

He gestured toward the long table, where the assembled offerings made an impressive spread. Jane counted over a dozen different displays, packed with everything from simple loaves to elaborate pastries.

"The judging will be informal, as we discussed. Everyone take your time. Sample what you like. When you're ready, write down the names of three bakers whose work impressed you most. We'll count the votes and announce the winners afterward."

The crowd began to move, flowing towards the table in a mass that quickly broke into smaller groups. Jane stayed in her chair for a moment longer, watching the chaos, then forced herself to stand and join the fray. She was here to compete. That meant tasting other people's work, as well as having her own tasted.

The next hour passed in a blur of flavors and faces. Jane sampled bread after bread, pastry after pastry, making mental notes about what she liked and what she didn't. Some of the offerings were clearly better than others.

Jane found herself gravitating toward the work of a few bakers in particular. An older woman had made a honey cake that Jane very much liked. A man she vaguely recognized from the market had produced a savory pie with a crust so flaky, she was jealous of it.

Her own display attracted its share of attention. Everyone nodded upon tasting her keln, a few bakers even smiling in satisfaction. Several people stopped to examine her burnt-top bread more closely. When she happened to be nearby, some of them even asked questions about the technique.

"How do you get the sear so even?" Lynn, the baker who had come to fetch Jane for the initial bakers’ meeting, ran her finger along the loaf’s crisped surface and sighed. "I've tried this before, but it always comes out patchy."

"Practice, mostly,” Jane replied. “And you have to know when to quit. It’s easy to overdo it."

Jane didn't mention the magic. It felt like cheating somehow, even though it wasn't really. The magic just helped her sense when to pull back the heat, and gave her a more simple way to apply it. The skill was still hers.

The buns drew more complicated reactions. Jane watched people's faces as they bit into them, trying to read their expressions. Some looked surprised, which could mean anything. Others looked as if they weren't quite sure what to make of what they were eating. A few nodded in what she hoped was approval.

Nobody said anything negative, at least not to her face. But nobody said anything particularly positive, either, and Jane found herself wishing she had stuck with something more traditional.

The buns were good. She knew they were good. But they were also different in a way that might not play well with this particular crowd.

Too late now, she told herself firmly. What's done is done.

As the hour wound down, people began drifting towards the bucket that had been set up near the front of the room. Slips of paper and pencils were available. Jane watched as baker after baker wrote down their votes and dropped them in.

When it was her turn, she thought carefully before writing. The honey cake woman was an easy choice. The savory pie man was another. For her third vote, she hesitated, then wrote down the name of a younger baker whose simple wheat bread had been surprisingly excellent in a way that revealed mastery of fundamentals. She understood that pursuit herself, and decided to reward him for it.

She folded her paper and dropped it in the bucket, then stepped back and waited.

The counting took longer than she expected. Eric sat at a small table near the front, unfolding each slip and making marks on a larger sheet. The crowd grew restless as the minutes passed, conversations starting up again in the absence of anything else to keep them entertained.

Finally, Eric stood. The room instantly went quiet.

"We have our three winners," he announced. "These bakers will represent us at the showcase. I hope you will all join me in congratulating them."

He looked down at his paper.

"First, Marta Denning."

The honey cake woman. Jane felt a surge of satisfaction that her judgment had been validated. Marta stepped forward to accept congratulations from those around her, looking pleased but not surprised.

"Second, Thomas Albrecht."

Jane didn't recognize the name, but she recognized the face when a man near the back raised his hand in acknowledgment. She had seen him at the market before, though she didn't think they had ever spoken. His display had been near the end of the table. Jane realized with some embarrassment that she had never actually tasted his work.

“And third…”

The older baker paused, and Jane felt her heart rate spike for no reason she could identify. There were at least fifteen other bakers who might have won that third spot. The odds of it being her were —

"Lady Jane."

Just her first name, no family name attached, but with an annoying “Lady” she had neither earned nor asked for. She supposed everyone knew who she was by now. She was Jane, the baker-archmage who had moved in almost a year ago and never quite stopped being new.

Still, the third spot was hers. She had won.

Oh, yes!

The first thought to hit her was a surge of triumph and validation that made her want to pump her fist in the air.

Then the second thought followed immediately.

Oh, no…

A cold wave of terror washed over her at the realization of what this actually meant. She would be baking for the king. For the queen. For the prince, whoever he was and whatever he was doing here. Her work would be judged by people who had eaten the finest food in the kingdom, and whose standards she couldn't even imagine.

People were congratulating her. Hands on her shoulders, voices in her ears, faces smiling at her from every direction. Jane heard herself saying “thank you” and felt herself smiling back, but she wasn't entirely present for any of it.

What have I gotten myself into?

The crowd dispersed quickly after that. The noise of chatter faded as Glenfall’s bakers collected their remaining goods and headed home for the night. Jane packed up her own display mechanically, wrapping the leftover bread and buns in their cloths and placing them back in the baskets. Her hands knew what to do, even when her mind was elsewhere.

Marta found her near the door. Jane was somewhat comforted to see that the honey cake woman looked just as overwhelmed as she felt.

"Congratulations," Marta said. "Your burnt-top bread was excellent. I've never seen anyone get the sear that consistent."

"Thank you. Your honey cake was incredible." Jane meant it. "I've never tasted anything like it."

"Family recipe. Four generations now." Marta smiled. "I have to admit, I'm terrified. Baking for the king? I never imagined something like that was possible."

Jane nodded fervently. "Neither did I. But I suppose we'll figure it out together. All three of us."

“I suppose we will. It’s nice that we’re not in there alone.” Still smiling, Marta turned toward the door. "Good night, Jane. Get some rest. We have a lot of work ahead of us."

Jane watched her go, then stepped out into the cool night air herself. The walk home was quiet, the streets mostly empty at this hour. She let her feet carry her while her mind churned through everything that had happened.

She had won against bakers who had been doing this for decades. Who had shops that were older than she was. Who knew things about flour and yeast and ovens that she was only beginning to understand. Now she would be representing them, and her entire profession, to the most important people in the kingdom.

Suddenly, Jane laughed at herself. The short, sharp sound echoed off the empty buildings around her.

It was ridiculous, all of it. A year ago, she hadn't even known how to bake bread. Now she was competing with masters and winning spots at royal showcases. If someone had told her a year ago that all this would happen, she would have called them insane.

But it had happened.

She reached her shop and let herself in, setting the baskets on her worktable and lighting a candle. This was her place. Her work, her friends, her life. Whatever had happened at the showcase, whatever the king would think of her food, she had this.

Nothing could take that away.

Jane climbed the stairs to her bedroom and fell into bed without bothering to change out of her clothes. Sleep came quickly, deep and unbroken by any dreams.
