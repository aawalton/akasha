Dark clouds crested the horizon, peeking over the jagged summits of the mountainous region bordering the Qin Empire. Streaks of orange and crimson slashed across the sky, painting a scene so breathtaking it might make one forget, if only for a moment, the weight of looming war.

A man in a clean blue robe, his pencil-thin mustache immaculately groomed, regarded the view with a distant air. Though he held an ornate fan in his left hand, he waved it with idle disinterest—his mind clearly occupied elsewhere.

Perched atop a chestnut gelding, his pristine appearance stood in stark contrast to the dirt-smeared, battle-worn soldiers surrounding him. Though they lingered nearby, none dared approach. His presence created a quiet rift, one not bridged easily.

One soldier did. Clad in armor and carrying the stiffness of nervous obligation, he stepped forward tentatively, his apprehension nearly visible in the dimming light.

“G-General, your tent has been set up,” he announced, cupping his fist in salute.

The man on the horse—General Hayden Hart—glanced at him and offered a slight nod. His intelligent eyes, usually sharp, appeared distant today. A rare thing.

“Start the cookfires,” Hayden said calmly. “And have the crown prince meet me in my tent.”

Without waiting for a reply, he returned his gaze to the fiery sky. The soldier bowed again and scurried off, clearly relieved to escape unscathed.

Only a handful of horsemen remained.

With fluid grace, Hayden dismounted, absently patting his gelding’s neck. Another soldier appeared promptly and took the reins without a word, leading the horse toward camp.

“General Hart, shall we bring your meal to your tent?” one of the nearby officers asked.

“No,” Hayden replied curtly. Then he turned and walked away, leaving the soldiers exchanging uncertain glances behind him.

Either unaware or indifferent to their looks, he moved through the encampment stretched out at the foot of the mountain. Canvas and hide tents clustered around boulders, pitched on uneven patches of dust and gravel.

As he walked along the central path, soldiers straightened at once, saluting in silence. No one dared speak. Their unease buzzed in the air like the hum before a storm.

But Hayden seemed unaffected, his expression unreadable.

Near the camp’s center stood a tent markedly larger than the others—sturdy, spacious, and adorned in subtle signs of rank. Wooden planks and thick ropes held it firmly in place. Two guards flanked the entrance, spears in hand.

Upon seeing him, they tensed and saluted.

“General, the crown prince is awaiting you inside,” one of them said respectfully.

“Leave us,” Hayden ordered. “Ensure no one approaches.”

The guards hesitated. A flicker of doubt passed between them.

With a sudden snap, Hayden closed his fan with a sharp flick of the wrist.

The sound cracked the air like a whip, making the guard flinch.

Without another word, the two men retreated, leaving the general alone.

Hayden didn’t spare them a glance. He entered the tent.

Inside, the space was simple but refined. A low bed sat in one corner, a square wooden table at the center flanked by three stools. Waiting beside it was a young man in ornate armor—more regal than military. He rose to his feet at once, posture respectful.

Despite the dirt smudging his face, the young man’s features remained striking. Tousled brown hair framed his forehead, and stubble clung to a square, chiseled jawline—faint signs of nobility not yet worn down by the rigors of war.

“General Hart, you wished to see me?” Prince Leo asked, his voice tight with tension.

“Sit,” Hayden ordered flatly, leaving no room for refusal.

Though visibly dissatisfied, Leo obeyed without protest. On the table between them lay an unfurled map, two goblets, and a pitcher filled with what appeared to be water.

Hayden reached forward, poured himself a drink, and downed it in a single, unceremonious gulp. Wiping his mouth with the back of his hand, he turned his eyes toward the prince, gaze sharp and unreadable. It wasn’t the way one man looked at another. It was colder—analytical. Like a merchant inspecting livestock for defects.

“Who was it that suggested you retrieve our troops from Qionglin?” Hayden asked abruptly.

Leo blinked, caught off guard. “It was my father who ordered me on this campaign, General. You know this.”

“That is not what I asked, Prince Leo.”

The impatience in Hayden’s tone made the crown prince wince. He straightened quickly, gathering his composure.

“It was Duke Tinzel, the Minister of Intelligence,” Leo replied. “He believed it was the crown prince’s duty to—”

Hayden raised his fan, silencing him with a flick.

His free hand moved to stroke his chin as he muttered quietly, lost in thought.

“And your brother? Was it also the Duke who suggested he be sent to Xiandong?” Hayden asked, eyes now focused on the table.

Leo paused. “No. His participation wasn’t discussed in court. I believe my father arranged it afterward.”

He was about to inquire why this mattered, but once again Hayden’s fan lifted, cutting him off before the words could leave his mouth. More muttering followed. The general’s eccentricity left Leo visibly uncomfortable, but he said nothing, arms folded tightly across his chest as he waited.

Then came the rain.

It began as a soft patter against the canvas above, but quickly grew into a torrential downpour. The roar of water smothered all other sounds from the camp, enclosing the two men in a bubble of isolated tension.

“General,” Leo said at last, the irritation seeping into his voice, “if there’s something you need from me, you can just ask directly.”

The words seemed to rouse Hayden from his musings. He raised his head slowly, and the intensity in his eyes returned—razor sharp and unyielding.

“Then I will no longer stand on ceremony,” Hayden said coolly.

The sudden shift in tone, paired with the piercing stare, made Leo recoil slightly. Whatever remnants of courtly decorum remained began to wither under the general’s scrutiny.

“You, Prince Leo, have cemented yourself as the true and rightful heir to the Marxx Kingdom,” Hayden continued. “The nobility sing your praises. The people of the capital adore you. You are spoken of as a just man, and you carry a reputation for sound leadership—both in matters of war and governance.”

“You received the title of Crown Prince at the age of fifteen and began attending court alongside the king—all while enrolled at the Royal Academy. Not only did you excel in strategy and tactics, but even your swordsmanship was praised by the Boyd and Hail families.”

Hayden rose from his stool, clasping both hands behind his back as he fixed his gaze on the prince. The storm outside intensified, and he raised his voice to be heard over the relentless pounding of rain on canvas.

“Of course you would be the obvious choice to inherit the throne,” he said, beginning to pace the room with deliberate steps. “Even if there were other—more viable—candidates, surely none could rival your standing in the eyes of the people.”

He paused for a moment, retrieved his fan, and began waving it slowly, as if to cool the heat of his words.

“But for all your strengths—for all your accolades and achievements—there lies a fatal flaw.”

Snap.

The fan snapped shut with a sharp crack that cut through the sound of the rain. Prince Leo frowned, leaning forward on the table, his expression guarded.

Though clearly displeased by the claim, he responded with caution.

“Please, enlighten me, General,” he said, his voice low but firm.

There was something in Leo’s eyes—an ember of pride, or perhaps defiance. Hayden recognized it instantly, and it brought a wide grin to his face. He let out a short, strange laugh.

“Yes, that’s it,” Hayden said, pointing the folded fan toward him. “You carry the bearing of a king, Leo. A king who would fight tooth and nail to see his ideals made reality. A man who would rather break under pressure than bend to another’s will.”

A flicker of confusion crossed Leo’s face, but Hayden only shook his head, a note of disappointment in his voice.

“Since you’re slow on the uptake, allow me to be blunt. You, Prince Leo, cannot be controlled.”

“Controlled!?” Leo stood at once, his polished decorum shattering in an instant of raw emotion. “What nonsense are you spouting? When I ascend the throne, no one would dare attempt such a thing!”

The tent fell silent, save for the violent drumming of the rain.

The two men stared each other down, one calm, the other seething.

Hayden gave a slow shake of his head, lips curled in something like pity.

“Open your eyes, Leo,” he said softly. “Haven’t you noticed the change in your father? Can’t you see how he’s been led by the nose?”

His voice grew louder with every word, sharper—like a blade carving through illusion.

“Valand City. Xiu Fortress. Clayton City. The entire northern reach of the kingdom is lost. And this war with the Qin Empire? It was never about honor or retaliation. It was a trap—one Lhair used to divide our forces and break us from within.”

His voice dripped with venom. “And your father let it happen.”

Hayden stepped forward, each footfall deliberate as he closed the distance between them. He now stood only a breath away. Despite being taller, Leo shrank slightly under the weight of the words—though the fire in his eyes remained.

Anger and shame warred across his features.

Hayden clicked his tongue and turned on his heel, resuming his slow, agitated pacing.

“There are traitors in the court,” he muttered. “Burrowed deep, like worms in rotten wood. I don’t know who they are, or how long they’ve been feeding—but I’m certain of it.”

“Though I may not know who is responsible,” Hayden said, his voice low but firm, “I know exactly what their goal is. Since they can’t control you, they’ll prevent your ascension to the throne.”

He paused, expression tightening.

“Of course, the easiest way to do that… is to kill—”

A cry rang out—sharp and muffled by the storm outside.

Hayden’s words died in his throat.

His head snapped around, eyes widening as he took in the sight before him.

Blood sprayed in a vivid arc.

Leo clutched his throat, eyes wide with panic. A garbled sound escaped his lips—half-choke, half-scream—as crimson streamed between his fingers. His body trembled, spasmed… then collapsed to the ground with a dull thud. His shocked expression froze in place, lifeless.

Where his tall form had once stood, a figure now emerged from behind—cloaked in dark robes, dagger gleaming in the tent’s flickering lantern light.

The assassin was short, her form lithe and unmistakably feminine beneath her fitted garb.

Hayden’s hand slid subtly into his sleeve, his instincts kicking in even as his expression shifted—from surprise to chilling calm.

“So…” he said, voice almost casual, “you must be the assassins sent from Lhair?”

The woman didn’t reply.

She knelt beside Leo’s corpse, casually wiping the blood from her blade onto the fallen prince’s tunic, showing no regard for dignity or legacy. Once finished, she sheathed the dagger into a holster strapped beneath her robes.

Then, in an unexpected gesture, she reached for her hood and pulled it back.

The flickering light revealed a strikingly beautiful face. Her brown hair was drawn into a precise, tight bun—flawless. Olive-toned skin, cold brown eyes, and a calmness that didn’t match the brutality of her actions.

Hayden’s brows furrowed in recognition—followed by a flicker of disbelief. He muttered to himself, the words unintelligible, as his fingers left the hidden weapon and returned to his fan, which now waved erratically.

“Descendant of the Blood General,” the woman said, her voice soft, yet eerily clear over the drumming rain. “You’ve failed your mission.”

His fan stilled.

Hayden’s eyes narrowed sharply, cutting through the gloom like a blade.

“Why are you here, Victoria?” he asked coldly. “Shouldn’t you be with Luke?”

For the first time, the assassin faltered.

A flash of surprise flickered in her eyes—but it vanished as quickly as it came. A smirk curled her lips, amusement dancing behind her gaze.

“Who do you think sent me?” she replied.

Hayden’s expression darkened.

“Impossible…” he murmured, shaking his head.

Victoria laughed. It was smooth, velvety—and filled with mockery. She stepped forward, her movements graceful and unhurried. With each step, a black mist leaked from beneath her feet, spreading across the floor—unseen, unnoticed.

“Hayden Hart, you poor man,” she said, voice laced with condescension. “Just another pawn. A blunt instrument, charging forward without knowing the true shape of the game.”

She closed the distance between them, stopping just inches from him. Her hand rested gently on his shoulder, almost affectionate.

Pity softened her expression.

“Return to Ralis,” she whispered. “Perhaps Luke still has a place for you—after all, you let me kill the prince without even raising a finger.”

Hayden’s eyes flashed wide.

He moved without hesitation.

Three silver needles shot from his hand, striking Victoria cleanly in the neck. She staggered backward, hand flying to the wounds.

But then—pain.

A sharp, jarring sensation exploded through Hayden’s arm. He looked down.

His forearm twisted at a grotesque angle, bone pressing beneath the skin. Clearly broken.

“What…?”

He raised his gaze—and flinched.

Victoria now stood several paces away. The silver needles still protruded from her neck, but something was wrong. From each puncture, thin wisps of black mist curled upward into the air, like smoke rising from an incense burner.

Not blood. Not poison. Mist.

Something dark. Something unnatural.

A low growl rumbled from her throat, animalistic and filled with venom.

But then, a shriek tore from her lips—a blood-curdling scream that echoed like a banshee's wail, reverberating through the tent and beyond.

Hayden didn’t hesitate.

He turned on his heel and fled into the storm.

“Assassin!” he bellowed, voice raw. “Assassin in the tent!”

Rain battered him the moment he emerged, soaking him to the bone in seconds. It poured from the heavens like stones crashing down the mountainside.

The camp exploded into motion.

Guards stumbled from their posts, gripping weapons with shaking hands. The two who he had dismissed earlier now scrambled to respond, wide-eyed and soaked.

Hayden clutched his broken arm tightly, breathing hard. He watched as dozens of soldiers stormed the tent behind him, lanterns flickering in the wet darkness.

His thoughts swirled.

The black mist… Victoria’s words…

He whispered through clenched teeth.

“Luke Drakon…”
