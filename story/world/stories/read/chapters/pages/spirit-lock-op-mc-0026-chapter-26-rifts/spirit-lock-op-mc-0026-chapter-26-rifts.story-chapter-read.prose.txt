Jora scoffed. “Oh, so you’re that type of teacher…”

“Rifts, Mr. Hedra. Usually near the end when someone has the rift key, as investigations can't happen and all the evidence completely disappears.”

“Let’s get something straight. I’m not a stern teacher—I’m not a teacher at all. I’m an A-rank rifter from a guild that’s more influential than most galactic chains. The only reason that I’m teaching this class is because I feel it's vital information, and rifters don’t care enough about it. Well, you’re going to care. I’m going to make sure of it.”

Carian turned on a widescreen in the room, showcasing dimensional portals opening up throughout the universe. Next came video clips of massive riftwalkers—beasts that lived in rifts—destroying human cities.

“Every year, dimensional portals open up. And every year human rifters are required to eliminate them. Can someone tell me why we’re needed in an age of robots and advanced weaponry?”

Emmi put up her hand.

Carian pointed at her. “Yes?”

“Rifts have electromagnetic dampening fields that prevent electricity, and a gas that prevents combustion. That prevents our weaponry from working.”

It also has a high-pressure combustion inhibitor known as Kineton Gas that becomes extremely dense at high pressure, eliminating the ability for pistons to fire in conventional engines. That gas also prohibits the intense combustion of conventional bullets and missiles. These rifts were clearly constructed to prevent us from using conventional technology. Who can give me an educated guess as to why someone would do that?”

No one answered. Carian’s eyes glided across the room and stopped at Hayden, whom she regarded intensely. “Mr. Reskco?”

The class fell silent, waiting for his answer.

He answered smoothly. “The rifts seek out suitable planets to house the beasts and biota inside. That seems to indicate that ancient civilizations sought a way to spread their life to a suitable universe before theirs died. If we shut them down, the architects will have failed at their goal.”

Carian studied him closely. “That’s the leading theory, yes.” She then turned to the others. “Here’s a more serious question. When rifts break, they send ten to thirty square miles of rock and plant life hurling to our planet. Why doesn’t the impact cause a world-ending calamity? It’d only take a six-mile asteroid to destroy the average planet.”

“The bottom has magic that stops it,” a student answered.

“Is there an answer that doesn’t involve magic?” Carian asked dryly. “This was an entrance exam question.”

“There’s an ancient artifact that converts cora crystals into gravitational repulsion to eliminate the kinetic energy,” Emmi said, glancing at Hayden. “That said, it might as well be magic. It’s been two hundred years and we still haven’t figured out how it works.”

Jora rolled his eyes. “By removing the key from the rift. Once you find the crystal, you jump out of the rift with it. Once it leaves the wormhole, it closes.”

“Correct. Do you know what a rift key is?”

Jora looked away. Carian looked at Hayden.

He contemplated in silence for a short yet significant amount of time before saying, “No. Do you?”

Hayden’s question was nearly accusatory. Jora grinned, waiting for the backlash.

Carian wasn’t mad. She simply grinned and said, “No. I don’t.” She looked away.

An electric buzz spread through the room when Hayden won the stare-off—or at least tied with her.

“Mr. Reskco clearly knows a thing or two about rifting. I want to know if you do as well. As the students began their assignment, Jora turned to Hayden. Jora turned just in time to see Hayden pass a physical sheet of paper to Emmi. Emmi opened it, blushed, and suppressed a giggle as she glanced at Jora. That expression made Jora’s neck veins bulge. He wanted to beat Hayden on the spot—rid him of this place before he leaked what happened to the school, but he couldn’t do so easily. There were cameras everywhere around Junolyx. That said, he had to move. Judging by Hayden’s arrogance and sudden rise in popularity, the dry, smile-flashing dead boy in front of him would spill the truth at any second.

Jora needed to stop him, and communicate in a way that couldn’t be admissible in court later. In the digital age, messages were permanent, traceable pieces of information, and he didn’t have Hayden’s contact information, let alone a means to contact him from an untraceable line. He also couldn’t talk when people recorded everything. It was a serious problem.

Then it hit him: the idiot gave him the solution! Writing.

It was physical evidence, but it was anonymous who wrote it, right? There was no digital signature, and as long as no one saw him write it, it couldn’t be tied to him. It could be anyone, right?

That’s why Hayden was doing it. He was probably sharing secrets at that moment, but didn’t want to have his ass sued into oblivion. It was a good idea.

The only problem was—Jora didn’t have any paper.

Physical writing was an art practiced by elites, and usually only for political signatures. It wasn’t something people carried around.

Or maybe…

Jora looked in his bag and saw a birthday letter from his sister, a silly tradition where she could practice her calligraphy. Thank you, sis… he thought. He pulled out an ink pen with that letter, ripped off a blank section, and wrote, “If you tell anyone, you’re dead.”

He then folded the paper and hissed to the man next to him. He lifted the paper slip to show the man, and then pointed at Hayden.

Confused, the student took it, waited until Carian wasn’t looking, and then passed it to Hayden.

Jora’s heart thudded when he realized that Hayden was looking at him from the corner of his eye.

Did he watch me write it? Jora wondered. Wait. It doesn't matter. I need him to see it. Still… Why does it feel like I messed up?

Jora wanted to take it back, but Hayden already had the note and was unfolding it.
