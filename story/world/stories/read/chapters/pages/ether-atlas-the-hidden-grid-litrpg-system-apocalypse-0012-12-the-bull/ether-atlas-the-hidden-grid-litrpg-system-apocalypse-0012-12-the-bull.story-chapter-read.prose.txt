Chapter 12

"Should the laminar flow turn to chaotic Ether, the Watcher must not engage. The creatures are not the invasion. They are the rot that precedes it." - Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

The Bloomberg lobby was not empty. Its marble atrium held about forty people in a rough horseshoe around the reception desk. Someone had dragged three potted ficus trees across the revolving door as a barricade, and it was probably the same genius who had placed a rope barrier behind the plants.

Arthur's eyes catalogued the failure points automatically. One of the three pots in the ficus barricade had a spiderweb crack down one side. The reception desk was heavier but nobody had positioned it and the glass walls left the sightlines wide open.

He would have liked to set it up properly but the pull behind his eyes had sharpened into something with a direction and a clock. Two hundred metres, straight down.

A security guard stood up hurriedly when he saw them and took in the shield, the axe, the billhook, and the blood on Steve's jacket.

"What's happening out there?"

"Nothing good and getting worse. Stay inside and don't open the doors." Donna said.

"Are you... are you with the response?"

“We are the response,” Steve muttered, and caught himself. “Sorry. I mean, nobody’s coming, it’s down to us tonight.”

The guard absorbed that with a puzzled look on his face but didn’t argue.

A woman near the desk pushed forward. "Safety in numbers. If you are heading somewhere..."

"We're heading down," Arthur said.

"Down?"

“I’ll have to inform my supervisor. Best you wait up here with the others till then, yeah?” The guard said.

“Inform whoever you need to, just know that downstairs might not be safe.” Arthur said as he moved past the desk and the bank of dead lifts, towards the grey service door marked STAIRS B.

Steve slowed at the desk. "Attribute points," he said, sideways.

"Forty strangers. Glass walls and we don’t know how many security guards are in here. If it gives you a seizure, that would happen in front of a crowd. The answer is no." Donna said, without breaking stride.

"I know. I just had to check."

"Points for asking."

The stairwell beneath the Bloomberg lobby smelled of wet concrete and old loam.

Each flight took them further from the corporate world above and closer to the frequency that had been building behind Arthur's eyes since Cannon Street.

Donna led. As she always led when the disposition of a space was unknown, her chem-light held low and forward, the acid-green glow sliding across walls that were changing as they descended.

Modern poured concrete gave way to Victorian brick, and then to something rougher. Massive blocks of ragstone set in mortar that were crumbling at the edges, scarred by moisture and the slow patience of two thousand years.

On the second landing, Steve tried again.

"Walls," he whispered. "Door behind us. Defensible line of sight back up the stairs. This technically meets all your criteria."

"We're descending into a probable encounter. Would you be happy tumbling down the stairs, if the allocation does something to your body?" Donna said without turning. "Allocation happens after we see what is down there, not before."

"You keep adding clauses."

"I'll stop when you stop finding loopholes."

Arthur heard the exchange and kept walking. Steve was right that Donna was building out the rule in real time. He did not mind. On a site, the supervisor who tightened the policy after every near-miss was the supervisor whose crew went home with all ten fingers.

"We're below the Roman level," Steve said from behind Arthur, his voice hushed with reverence. "The Bloomberg excavation only went so deep. This corridor isn't on any site plan I've seen. This is new."

After another three flights the corridor opened.

Donna's chem-light arced through the darkness and clattered across ancient stone, skidding to a stop near a low rectangular altar, the green glow spread outward in a slow wash, revealing the underground space in pieces.

Raised stone benches ran the length of both side walls, flanking a narrow central aisle. The vaulted ceiling was low enough that Arthur could have touched it if he raised his arm. And at the far end, carved directly into the bedrock wall was a relief that stopped Steve in his tracks.

Mithras, his cape billowing, had his short sword driven into the neck of a massive bull. Around the central figure, creatures were arranged in an arc: a dog, a serpent, a scorpion, a raven, each carved with a precision that made the two-thousand-year age of the stone feel like a lie.

"That's not a restoration, that's original. Look at the surface patina, the tool marks. This hasn't been touched since it was carved." Steve whispered.

"Hidden beneath a finance building. London buries everything." Donna said.

Steve moved towards the relief, his hands hovering centimetres from the surface. "This is the Tauroctony I was talking about. Every Mithraeum from London to Syria follows the same architectural blueprint. Almost the same dimensions, same orientation and the same constellations."

"As if someone gave them instructions," Arthur said.

"Exactly as if someone gave them instructions." Steve said, looking back at him.

Donna moved closer to Steve, her gaze still at the shadows. "Can we admire the ancient mystery cult after we've cleared the room?"

And just as she finished, something moved behind the altar.

Arthur's hand found the shield before his conscious mind registered the sound. It was a low scrape of a weight on stone. Heavy, rhythmic and from the sound of it, four-legged.

The creature stepped out from behind the relief like something that had been carved from the same bedrock and then given a heartbeat by mistake.

It had been a bull once, in the same way the foxes had been foxes. The ancestral shape was still legible beneath layers of mutation that made the biology obscene. Bone plates jutted through hide the colour of wet slate, each one ridged and overlapping like tectonic shelves.

The shoulders were massive, thick with layered muscle and chitinous growths that caught the green light and threw it back. Two horns curved forward from the skull, black as iron, tapering to points that Arthur's brain automatically classified as concentrated-force penetrators.

And beneath the hide, visible through the gaps between bone plates, faint blue lines of Ether pulsed in a slow, steady rhythm. The thing was running on the same energy that hummed beneath their skin.

It exhaled a hot, metallic gust that shimmered in the chem-light and carried a smell like heated stone.

"That's got to be a guardian or something similar," Steve whispered from behind Arthur's shoulder in a thin voice. "The bull from the carving. It's a guardian."

Donna's weight was low, her axe cocked and angled towards the creature's flank. "Arthur. Plan."

Arthur read the animal the way he would read a building. Mass: enormous. Centre of gravity: forward and low, built for linear force. The horns were the primary weapon, a charge from this thing would be like being hit by a car with spears welded to the bonnet. The bone plating was heaviest across the skull and shoulders and thinnest along the ribs, where the plates separated to allow the torso to flex.

The chamber was narrow and straight. There were stone benches on both sides with no room to dodge laterally. The central aisle was a kill corridor twelve metres long with the altar at one end and the stair door at the other.

The creature lowered its head.

"I hold the centre," Arthur said, raising the shield. "Steve, stay at range. Watch how it moves. Donna -"

The bull charged before he could finish.

The acceleration was horrifying. Something that size should not have been able to move that fast from a standing start. Arthur had half a second to read the geometry of the upcoming collision. A head-on brace would fold the bin lid like wet cardboard and drive both horns straight through his chest and a dive sideways would put Steve in the path of the charge. The podia flanking the aisle were too close on either side to clear.

You don't stand square to a force you cannot absorb. You turn it. Arthur angled the shield obliquely and pivoted his hips away from the line of charge.

The bull hit the shield at an angle. The skull glanced off the steel at forty-odd degrees and the creature's momentum carried the whole mass of it along the shield face rather than through it. One horn scraped the length of the buckling metal with a shriek that vibrated through his molars. The other passed wide of his shoulder, close enough that the heat of the creature brushed his jaw.

He paid for the redirection with his shoulder rather than his life. The force transferred up through his braced arm and the joint popped with a wet crack that sent a bolt of white hot pain from his collarbone to his fingertips. The shield buckled along the impact line. His boots slid sideways, and the raised edge of the left podium struck the back of his thighs. His torso folded back over the podium and he sprawled half-seated on the stone, shield still on his arm, the shock of the landing knocking a grunt out of him.

But he held on.

He held on because the angle had done its work. The mass had gone past him instead of through him, and the podium had folded him backward instead of letting the slide continue. The shield, even crumpling, had deflected the blow rather than absorbing it. Engineering and Geometry. The angle of deflection mattered more than the force of resistance.

The bull pulled back for a second strike and Arthur found that he couldn't lift his left arm. The shoulder was all wrong, dislocated or partially torn, and the joint was screaming at him.

"The ribs!" Steve shouted from the back of the chamber. He'd been watching the Guardian. "The plating separates when it breathes, there's a gap below the shoulder on the left side. Five centimetres, maybe less."

The construct's geometry brightened briefly.

Donna heard the assessment and moved. Years of close-protection drills had wired her body to respond before the conscious decision finished forming.

She came in low on the creature's left flank, timing her approach to the bull's inhalation. Just as the torso expanded and the bone plates shifted apart, her axe found the gap. It was a short chop rather than a full arc, and she drove the blade edge-first into the soft tissue between the third and fourth plate with the precision of a lockpick finding the last tumbler.

The bull bellowed. The sound wasn't like any animal biology could conceive. It was geological, a grinding shriek that resonated through the stone benches and shook dust from the vault above. The creature swung its massive head towards Donna, its horns slashing, but she'd pulled back, resetting two metres away with her weight centred and the axe dripping.

Arthur forced himself upright. His left arm hung useless at his side, but the crowbar was in his right hand and the bull's attention was now split. A wounded flank, a threat on the left and an obstacle ahead. The Guardian had expected to kill its prey with the first charge and was now recalculating.

Steve jabbed the billhook forward from range, hooking the curved blade behind the bull's leg and pulling, the force was not enough to injure but it did enough to unbalance. The creature stumbled sideways, and for one second, the gap in its armour opened again as the ribs expanded.

Donna rushed in and hit the same spot again, shoving in her axe deeper this time. The axe blade disappeared to the haft inside the guardian and there was another bellow. The bull's front legs buckled and it went down on its front knees, the massive weight cracking the ancient stone beneath it.

Arthur stepped in with the crowbar raised. One arm. One weapon. He brought the crowbar down between the horns with everything he had left, a single vertical strike aimed at the point where the skull plates met. It was the structural weak point. The keystone.

The crack that sounded was deep and final.

The bull collapsed forward, its weight hitting the temple floor with an impact that Arthur felt through the stone and into his knees. The horns gouged parallel tracks in the ancient flagstones before the head came to rest.

Then all was silent.

***

Arthur slumped down on the nearest stone bench, his legs having made the decision for him. His left shoulder was still in the socket but barely. Something had torn and the joint screamed every time he moved it. His right hand wouldn't release the crowbar.

Blue text assembled in his vision.

THREAT NEUTRALISED

ETHER ABSORBED: +40

CURRENT LEVEL: 2

INTEGRATION: 55 / 119

Donna was on one knee, breathing through her teeth. The guardian’s convulsion after the axe swing had turned the axe and wrenched something in her lower back. A shallow gash ran along her forearm where one of the bull's horn-tips had caught her during the second strike.

Steve leaned against a stone bench at the back of the chamber, the billhook planted on the floor like a walking stick. His fox bite had reopened during the fight and fresh blood was seeping through the compression bandage and dripping from his elbow.

Arthur could hear Steve’s blood dripping on the stone. And above them, through the stairwell and the concrete and the Bloomberg lobby and the dead city, it was still Tuesday. He should have been clearing up after the D&D session right now, putting the minis back in the case, then walking to the Tube. The distance between that life and this one was hours and infinite.

"Right.” Steve said finally. His voice was shaking. “Right. That answers the question of whether these temples have security.”

Donna let out a breath that might have been a laugh if her back wasn't spasming. "Next time, I want advance warning from our floating consultant."

The geometry materialised near the altar, its lines tight and compressed, rotating in the same closed pattern it held when it had nothing meaningful to share. "A guardian presence was predictable from available contextual data. Your approach was not adjusted accordingly."

"Available to whom?" Donna said.

The construct's geometry contracted. It was a brief tightening that was gone before Arthur could be certain he'd seen it. It did not respond.

Arthur's shoulder was starting to feel warm. The absorbed Ether trying to heal the mess inside. It was slow going, the Ether insufficient to restore the joint, but the sharpest frequencies of pain were dulling.

Behind them, thin blue light began to bleed through the cracks of the Tauroctony relief.

Steve saw it and turned towards the altar, his injuries forgotten. A pattern was forming and Steve's brain didn't give him a choice about those.

The carved figures were illuminating in sequence: the bull, then the dog, then the serpent, then the scorpion, each one flaring with blue light along its engraved lines until the entire relief was glowing like a circuit board waking from a long sleep.

Floating blue lines assembled in the air above the altar, forming luminous words:

Where the Bull-Slayer bleeds the constellations of old,

The arc of the Zodiac begins to unfold.

Seek the Line where the turning spheres divide,

On the Hill of the Stars where the First Signals hide.

Steve stared at the glowing text, his lips moving silently as he reread the lines. "That's another riddle," he whispered. "A navigational riddle. The Zodiac arc, the Line where spheres divide, Arthur, I think that's-"

"It can wait thirty seconds," Donna interrupted. She was looking at the bull's carcass, specifically at the wound her axe had opened in its flank. "There's a light inside this thing."

Arthur looked at the dead creature and saw what she was pointing at. Deep in the creature's chest cavity, visible through the torn hide and the gap between the shattered ribs, a faint blue glow was pulsing in the same cadence as the Ether lines that had run beneath its skin.

Donna didn't wait for discussion. She reversed her utility blade, cut through the fossilised tissue with a few efficient strokes, and reached in wrist-deep. When she pulled her hand free it was slick with dark fluid and something in her grip caught the chem-light and threw it back.

It was a crystal. Irregular and roughly the size of a walnut. The blue light inside it pulsed and gave out a warmth Arthur could feel from two metres away. The energy within the crystal was dense and concentrated and it felt like the same energy that was rewriting their biology one notification at a time.

Donna held it at arm's length. Her expression was the one she wore when handling an unknown device extracted from a hostile source, fascination held at professional distance.

Steve looked between the glowing riddle and the glowing crystal and back again, and Arthur could practically hear his brain trying to process both simultaneously.

"We need to talk about that riddle," Steve said.

"We need to talk about what's in my hand," Donna said.

Arthur looked at the dead guardian, the glowing relief, and the crystal pulsing against Donna's skin. His shoulder was a ruin. Steve's bite had reopened. Donna's back was probably hurting. Then there was a riddle pointing somewhere they couldn't see yet. And a crystal doing something none of them understood. And the clock, somewhere above the violet sky, was still counting down.

"One thing at a time," he said.
