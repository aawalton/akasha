Chapter 23

“The Templars believe they guard a holy secret. They are not wrong. They are simply wrong about which secret it is.”- Codex of the Long Watch, Field Observation, 12th Century, Jerusalem

The Temple Church’s pull gripped Arthur’s mind while the other nodes were directional hums. He could tell by the intensity that this was going to be more significant than anything else so far.

From two streets away they could see the church’s round nave rising above the rooftops of Inner Temple. Pale Portland stone stood under the violet sky, the conical roof intact.

“Temple Church,” Steve said, the Templar history wedged under his arm. “Consecrated 1185. This was the Knights Templar’s English headquarters. The round nave is modelled on the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem.”

“Relevant details, Steve,” Donna said.

“They were the first widespread international banking network. Deposit gold in London, withdraw it in Jerusalem. For two hundred years they ran a logistics network spanning Europe to the Holy Land, and nobody has ever fully explained how they did it with medieval transport. Sure, there are theories but nothing I would publish with hundred percent certainty.”

“Is that what you call relevant?”

Steve looked up from the book, his eyes shining. “Unless they had infrastructure nobody else knew about, of course.”

As they reached the gate of Inner Temple, Arthur could see cracked flagstones and toppled benches. The round nave rose ahead of them like a stone drum and light pulsed in the seams between the stonework in a faint, rhythmic blue-violet glow.

Donna stopped and did her customary micro-sweep. “The air’s different in here.”

Arthur felt it too. Ether density here was unlike anything they had felt before. It was like the other nodes but heavier. It wasn’t the warped and chaotic density of the Printworks; this felt almost filtered.

“Sanctum-adjacent density. Caution is advisable.” Archie stated.

“That’s Archie for ‘worse inside,’” Steve said.

“It is going to get worse inside,” Archie confirmed.

Steve paused at the doorway, fingers brushing a now familiar three-line symbol on the archivolt. “Seeder marks. The convergence symbol. Same mark as the Deptford wall, same as Göbekli Tepe. Eight hundred years and nobody noticed because it’s buried in Norman carving.”

“Steve. Inside.”

He followed but touched the marks one more time as he passed.

Inside, six clustered Purbeck marble columns rose around the centre of the Round, supporting the gallery above. Knightly effigies lay across the floor, most in two groups and one apart by the south wall.

William Marshal lay among the southern group, one hand resting on the hilt of his sword. Steve moved closer to the effigy.

“You know, Marshal wasn’t a Templar during the life everyone remembers. Not formally. When he was in the Holy Land, he privately committed himself to the Order but only joined it officially on his deathbed.”

Arthur looked down at the stone figure. “He kept it secret?”

“Some records say he promised to join the order before he passed away. He even brought back two lengths of silk to be fashioned into a Templar mantle for his funeral. When he knew the end had come, he summoned the Master of the Temple to his deathbed.”

Steve crouched beside Marshal’s sword hand.

“So, he committed himself to the Templars but did not actually join them for more than thirty years,” Donna said. “Why?”

“The ordinary answer? Full vows meant poverty, chastity and obedience. Marshal had a wife, children, estates. He could not remain Earl of Pembroke and submit himself completely to the Order.”

“And you doubt have a less ordinary answer?”

“Freedom of movement. Independence gave him access to kings, courts and places an obedient brother of the Temple could not easily reach. A man formally inside the Order had rules. Earl of Pembroke could go almost anywhere.”

“You think he was working for them?” Arthur asked.

“I would have vehemently said no a few days ago,” Steve said, straightening up. “His near-contemporary biography records the rest of his life in extraordinary detail, but the two years he spent in the Holy Land are almost blank. He left as a secular knight then returned with burial silks and a commitment to the Templars that he carried silently for the next three decades.”

Arthur looked down at the stone face.

“Then spent those decades beside kings. Only showing his commitment to the world on his deathbed.”

“Yes, but it proves absolutely nothing.”

“No,” Donna said. “But it is interesting.”

***

A quick smash dealt with the museum glass and Arthur knelt and placed his palm flat against Marshal’s effigy. A faint sensation passed between his palm and the stone. Almost like a handshake. The stone posing a wordless question and his Nadi-Matrix responding in a wordless answer. A deep mechanical click ran through the building, as though a lock the size of the church had just disengaged. The floor vibrated for a few seconds and then everything was quiet again.

"What the bloody hell was that?" Donna said, raising her axe.

Steve was turning in a slow circle, scanning the space. Then he dropped to his knees. "The other effigies. Arthur, the swords are glowing."

The eight stone knights apart from Marshal lay still in their rows, centuries of marble patience behind museum glass. But the swords were now glowing. Faint blue light ran along the edges of each carved blade, and symbols appeared in the steel-grey stone that hadn't been there before.

As Arthur looked around, he noticed that the swords weren’t the only thing that had changed. Around the perimeter of the nave, eight narrow recesses had opened in the circular wall. These were vertical slots shaped like a sword; each one was maybe three feet tall and wide enough to accept a blade. Each slot was framed with its own set of Seeder symbols and was glowing the same faint blue.

"It's a key system, then," Steve said. He was shuffling on his knees reading the symbols on the nearest effigy's sword through the glass. "Each sword has a symbol set. And each wall slot has a symbol set. We match them."

"Through the glass cases?" Donna arched her eyebrow.

She then reversed the axe and drove the blunt end into the nearest case, fracturing the glass into heavy, squared pieces. This was museum-grade safety glass, designed to collapse into blocks rather than shards.

“Worth it. I’ve wanted to do that since Arthur broke the first one.” she said smiling.

Seven sharp impacts later, the Seven cases were reduced to rubble.

Steve pulled the first sword from its effigy and almost dropped it. "Christ. Fifty kilos, easy." He wrestled with it for several moments, then dropped it back.

“That’s six Strength for you, Steve,” Donna said from the doorway.

"The symbols on the swords describe something," he said, ignoring the jibe and rotating the blade to read. "And the symbols on the wall slots describe something else. Different vocabulary sets. The mapping isn't obvious. It's not word-to-word."

He noted the symbols from the sword into his notebook and hurried to the nearest wall slot for comparison. The slot's symbols were different in form and structure from the blade's. He tried the next slot but could not match that as well.

"It's not linguistic," Steve said, sounding frustrated. "The blade symbols are from one notation system, and the slot symbols are from another. I can probably translate each set individually but I'm confident they won't correspond."

While Steve was comparing symbols Arthur had pulled the second sword free. It was heavier than he'd expected. Dense, well carved marble. The blade was wide with a pronounced fuller down the centre. The cross-section was almost flat with a deep channel. For a second he was surprised how easily he was holding the thing up and then he reminded himself that he was at seventeen STR, he was almost superhuman.

The wall slot Steve was standing beside had a shallow recess. He looked at the sword in his hands. This blade was wide with a deep fuller.

He moved to a different slot, further along the wall. This one was wider and he held the blade up against the gap. The profile was close but not exact.

The third slot was wider than the second but this time it was too wide.

The fourth looked promising. As he held the blade, aligning the cross-section with the opening he could see the fuller matched the raised ridge inside the slot. The blade width matched the gap and the thickness matched the depth.

"Steve. Stop reading the symbols."

"What?"

"This isn’t a language puzzle. Don’t look at the symbols, look at the swords." Arthur held up his blade, turning it so Steve could see the cross-section.

"Wider fuller and a flat profile. Now look at this slot." He pointed at the recess in the wall. "Wide channel. Shallow depth.”

Arthur aligned the sword with the recess and pushed it in; it fit perfectly. The sword locked into the wall and the symbols on both the blade and the socket pulsed once and then went steady as energy hummed behind the stone. A thin blue line traced itself from the socket to the centre of the nave.

“The sword fits this slot because the blade was carved to match the socket. It's a key cut to fit a lock."

"The recesses predate the surrounding masonry by approximately thirty-three thousand years," Archie observed. "The Templars incorporated them into the nave wall during construction. Their stonemasons carved decorative borders around each one without recognising that the slots were functional." Now that they have figured out the puzzle, he seemed to be able to comment on it.

“Are you two now going to start arguing about who solved it, or get on with solving it?”

“What do you argue? I solved it,” Arthur said and pulled another sword free; the fact that he was breaking stone free from carved effigies with his hands was not lost on him.

The one was different from the first two. It had a narrower blade and a diamond-shaped cross-section rather than the flat profile with the deep fullers of the previous ones. He walked the perimeter, checking each slot. Some were obviously wrong, either too wide or too shallow. Two were close though. He held the blade against the first one. It was close but not quite; the diamond edges didn't sit flush against the socket walls.

The second fit and he pressed the blade home.

"That's two," Donna said from the stairs. "Six to go. The building's been humming louder since you started."

The next four swords were straightforward. Arthur's hands had learned what to look for. The cross-sections were different enough to sort quickly.

A lenticular profile with a swollen belly that only fitted one slot. A slender triangular section that slotted into a narrow vertical recess near the entrance. A distinctive cross guard with a very wide blade. Each one locked with the same mechanical click and pulse of blue light. And each time, a thin line of energy ran from the wall socket towards the centre of the nave.

The seventh and eighth swords tested him. His arms had started to shake by now, carrying around fifty kilo swords and testing them against the slots at various angles was getting too much even for him. And these last two were nearly identical.

He carried the first sword to one of the two remaining slots. Held the cross-section against the opening and pushed gently; the blade entered the socket and caught. The fuller's curve didn't match the ridge inside; he had to struggle to withdraw it.

The second blade slid into the same socket and locked with a click that resonated through the wall. The last one was straightforward as expected, and Arthur took a second to marvel at the engineering. A mechanical construction, thirty thousand years old, was still in working condition.

Just as the blue ether line from the last sword reached the nave, all eight sockets pulsed simultaneously. Ether lines drew themselves on the walls and floor, turning the entire central nave into what looked like a circuit diagram with William Marshal, at its centre.

“Eight swords in the walls and one protector in the middle holding the circuit together.” Steve said quietly standing over Marshal's effigy watching the lines converge.

The convergence point beneath Marshal blazed white and then covered him, wrapping the protector in a blue shroud.

Steve stood in the glow, his face lit blue from below.

“The puzzles are changing,” he said. “Every riddle until now was knowledge and pattern recognition. This was structural analysis.”

He looked at Arthur. “The path isn’t testing one skill. It's a testing range.”

Donna interrupted him as her axe came up. “Discuss the curriculum later. The dead knight is moving.”

Arthur was absorbed in studying the nave architecture and looked back sharply. “Oh, sod off. You can’t be serious.”

William Marshal’s effigy pulled itself up, eight feet of medieval armour rendered in pale grey rock. The sword carved at his side came free into his hand, its edge glowing faint blue.

Donna’s Reflex Acceleration triggered before she consciously decided to use it. The sword was rising in a wide lateral arc that would cover a third of the nave.

“Wide arc left!” she shouted. Then the acceleration cut and headache arrived. But it was worth it.

Arthur had moved in front with his shield up. He triggered structural reinforcement, as the sword hit the shield.

The physical force was manageable. The Bullguard distributed the force and Arthur’s locked frame held. But behind the sword came something else. A wave of concentrated Ether that passed through the shield, then through his Reinforcement and detonated against his internal structure.

Arthur flew backward and hit a marble column. He couldn’t breathe. He could feel his ribcage was pressing against something it shouldn’t, and every attempt at inhalation sent a white spike of pain through his left side.

“The sword carries an Ether pulse,” Archie stated. “Your shield cannot block it. The cycle is approximately seven seconds.”

“Seven seconds!” Steve shouted from the gallery. “Two second build-up, five diminished. Move between the pulses!”

Donna had flanked the effigy while Arthur was flying back, and the Conduit-Edge Axe struck the guardian’s knee joint. It cut two inches deep into stone, chips spraying. The guardian pivoted faster than eight feet of rock had any right to.

The sword came down in a vertical strike, and she rolled clear by inches. It hit marble and the shockwave rippled outward, a visible shimmering blue ring of compressed air that caught Donna mid-roll and threw her sideways into the gallery railing. Her temple hit stone and she tasted blood.

Steve grabbed her arm and hauled her upright. “It’s learning our positions! Stay mobile!”

Donna wiped blood from her eye. Her hand was shaking and she couldn’t tell if it was the head impact or adrenaline and it didn’t matter because the guardian was turning towards Steve.

She triggered Reflex Acceleration again and the world slowed. The guardian’s pivot, the sword rising, the angle of the incoming strike were all mapped in a breath. She shoved Steve clear, rolled in the opposite direction, and the sword split the air between them.

As she tumbled, the acceleration cut. The headache this time hit like a railway spike through her right eye. And this time, along with the headache, she also felt something else: a hollowness. She knew instinctively that the tank that powered the bursts was empty. Whatever came next, she was fighting it with her stats alone.

Arthur forced himself upright. Every shallow sip of air was an effort of will. There was no way he could tank another hit like that. His entire combat doctrine was useless. Plant, absorb, hold the line. None of it worked when the force passed through the thing it hit.

But while he was getting back up on his feet, his mind was screaming at him. This was an engineering problem. His subconscious had studied the problem, and his active brain was just catching up.

A shockwave isn’t a load. It’s energy in transit. You don’t resist energy in transit. You redirect it. It’s the same principle that keeps a lightning rod from exploding. You give the current a path to ground.

A few feet away the guardian swung at a column near Steve. The shockwave blasted outward as Steve threw himself flat and got covered in marble dust.

Arthur set his jaw and ran as his ribs screamed at him.

He reached the guardian in the dead window between pulses and slammed the Bullguard Shield against its torso while triggering Structural Reinforcement.

The technique responded but it was sluggish this time. The Ether that had flooded his frame during the first Reinforcement now came in a trickle. His body had a finite amount to give, and the first activation had taken most of it. What remained was thin and weak, but it would have to be enough for what he was going to try.

He planted his feet and locked downward driving the Ether through his legs and into the Purbeck marble floor, He stopped trying to be a wall and let the force run through him, down through his legs and into the floor.

The guardian’s sword came down on the shield, the sword and the wave creating a boom within a second of each other. The Ether pulse hit but this time found a path down through his arms, his spine, his legs, into the floor. The marble cracked beneath him, but Arthur didn’t move.

The guardian pressed harder, the sword grinding against the shield and a second pulse started to build, faster than the five-second estimate. The energy slammed through Arthur’s frame and the floor cracked wider, fracture lines radiating outward, and his ribs cried again, his vision going grey at the edges.

He held on only barely, but he couldn’t move, couldn’t strike, couldn’t do anything but channel the force downward and hold the guardian’s attention. His body was telling him this was unsustainable.

“DONNA!”

She came in bloody. The cut above her temple was streaming blood into her left eye and she compensated by closing it. Fighting one-eyed, the axe aimed at the neck joint where the helm met the gorget. A structural weak point.

The first strike bit but didn’t sever. Stone shrieked against enhanced steel. It was the binding at work; a blade should have been useless against stone. Even then, the damage caused by the axe was much lower than what it would do to flesh and bone.

The guardian’s off-hand came around, a backfist of solid rock and caught Donna across the shoulder, spinning her. But this time she rolled with the strike, absorbing the kinetic force into her momentum. She rolled twice and came up with her axe at the ready.

Arthur’s grounding was failing. His body felt liquid inside, but he knew he needed to give Donna more time. He deactivated Structural Reinforcement and hit the guardian in the face with his shield and followed that with his hammer opposite to where Donna had struck earlier.

“Again!”

The guardian raised his sword for an overhand chop. This was it; his Structural reinforcement was tapped out and in his condition he wouldn't be able to tank another hit. But he had given his friends time. He raised his shield in defiance.

Steve’s Reacher hit the guardian at the neck from the left and he moved back immediately. The strike was less than useless, a bladed weapon combined with abysmal strength against stone. But it distracted the guardian a second longer before it dismissed Steve as a threat and that second saved them all.

Donna’s next strike wasn’t clean. It wasn’t precise. It was a two-handed overhead blow driven by the weight of every fight since the alley, driven by the fear and fury of seeing her friends in mortal danger, driven by the injustice of the last few days.

The Conduit-Edge Axe’s Strike Acceleration drove the blade into the damaged neck of the guardian and through it.

The body stood for one second and then collapsed in rubble.

Arthur sat down hard on cracked marble and breathed; his ribs were screaming in pain.

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

London

THE HUMAN GRID

The Knights Templar did not invent banking. That sentence appears in bad documentaries and worse books.

What they built was more interesting.

The New Temple accepted deposits, guarded royal treasure, issued and honoured bills of exchange, loaned money and settled debts on behalf of people who might be hundreds of miles away. A traveller could place value into the Order’s custody in one country and recover it from another Templar house without carrying the original gold between them.

The gold did not need to make the journey. An authenticated claim to the gold did.

One order. Many houses. A common set of rules. Trusted intermediaries confirming that something deposited at one point could be recognised at another.

It was essentially a network of nodes.

Their architecture travelled the same way. The Round Church here in London reproduced the form of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. Other round churches appeared across Europe. They were not identical buildings, but repetitions of an authorised shape, each connecting its local congregation to a distant sacred centre.

Beneath the London copy was a system thirty-three thousand years older than the church.

We treat ancient sites as passive. People choose a hill, cave or spring, then successive cultures inherit its importance. Archaeologists call that continuity of sacred landscape. We rarely ask whether certain places might exert pressure of their own, just enough to make one location feel appropriate for worship, protection, storage or authority.

✎ The Templars may never have discovered the Grid. They may have spent two centuries rebuilding its logic above ground with horses, parchment and vows.
