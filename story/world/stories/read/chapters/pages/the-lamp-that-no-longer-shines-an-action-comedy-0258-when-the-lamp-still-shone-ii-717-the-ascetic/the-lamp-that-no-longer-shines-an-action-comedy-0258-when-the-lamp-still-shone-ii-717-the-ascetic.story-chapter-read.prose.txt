[Time]: 1980[Location]: The Umbral Cathedral

For a woman of Irene Habsburg's administrative rigor, the developing flaw in her schedule was both undeniable and, in her private estimation, entirely justified.

Whenever the grinding internal restructuring of the Habsburg dynasty grew too heavy, whenever a canceled meeting with an entrenched family elder freed an hour that the day had no right to waste, Irene did not return to her meticulously curated estate. She stepped into the nearest shadow and walked the Umbral Cathedral's silent corridors without a second thought. She knew every passage now, every invisible staircase, every spatial fold that Ovelia hadn't bothered to tell her about and that she'd mapped on her own anyway, because she was going to know everything about this place eventually.

It was extraordinarily peaceful here. Largely because the Cathedral's primary residents spent the majority of their time unconscious.

Lantern Cats drifted through the grand halls like slow, luminous debris. Asleep mid-float, they bumped softly into pillars and silently ricocheted off vaulted ceilings: a colony of sleepwalkers navigating purely by buoyancy, spherical forms trailing faint constellations of warm light through the perpetual Cathedral dusk.

Irene paused: a very specific anomaly was drifting toward her. The Eternal Slumber Lantern Cat: Ovelia's signature feline form, and an entity that committed several crimes against the natural order simultaneously. The fur was the deep, absolute black of the space between stars, dusted with microscopic specks that pulsed softly with every exhale like a compressed galaxy. The tail was noticeably larger than a standard Lantern Cat's, thick and improbably fluffy, curling behind the spherical body in a comet trail of impossible softness. Two enormous cat ears stood perfectly upright. And cascading down from the creature's round, dignified face were a pair of spectacular, drooping whiskers, long enough to qualify as the facial hair of a meditating sage who had been at it for several centuries.

Irene stood very still as the floating sovereign drifted close on an invisible current, exhaling in slow, self-satisfied snores. Her hand moved before her brain had filed a motion: with deliberate, careful gentleness, she stroked one of those philosopher's whiskers.

The sleeping sage's eyes snapped open. The universe briefly suspended its commitment to Euclidean geometry as a perfectly spherical entity somehow managed to violently arch its back: fur spiking in every direction, form doubling in volume inside a single second.

"Meow-ow!" the cat roared, a sound that blended wounded majesty with pure, incandescent outrage. How dare you!

Using the kinetic momentum of its own geometrically impossible back-arch, Ovelia kicked off the empty air and dove directly into the shadow pooling on the floor, vanishing with a sharp, indignant ripple of darkness.

Irene stood alone in the grand hall. She lowered her hand slowly, rubbing her thumb against her fingertips to preserve the precise silken texture in meticulous long-term storage, and smiled.

When the Cathedral's shadows condensed in the uppermost sanctum and produced one extremely offended Grand Witch, the Aspects were already mid-game.

Ovelia shed her feline form and stood at the edge of the table, watching. The game proceeded without acknowledging her, and after a round or two her tail began to sway. She pulled out a chair and sat down. Several rounds later, a set of cards hit the polished wood.

"Cat wins!"

Shaar leaned back in her chair until it balanced on its two hind legs, one black-booted foot propped against the table's edge with cheerful structural disregard for furniture welfare. Ovelia's most rebellious Aspect had skin the shade of fresh milk: a jarring, deliberate contrast to the untamed black curls that cascaded from atop a pair of massive, wolf-like cat ears. The ambient mana bleeding passively off her frame darkened the air around her chair to the shade of a local power outage.

"Big cat has to wear a swimsuit for the entire day," Shaar announced, gesturing at Ovelia with gloating majesty.

Ovelia straightened her spine and crossed her arms. "Fine," she said, with the complete, unstudied indifference of a cat who has already won and is simply waiting for the room to catch up. "Cat will wear one."

Allison paused in the middle of stacking her losing hand. She looked at Ovelia with a perfectly flat expression.

"You are omitting the variable," Allison said, "where Irene will return this evening and see you."

Ovelia's tail curled up behind her. "That cat," she said, gesturing at Shaar, "did not specify the structural parameters of the swimwear. Cat only needs to ensure everything is properly covered. If Cat wraps myself tightly from neck to toe, what exactly is the difference from formal attire?"

Shaar let the front legs of her chair hit the floor. She rested her chin on her knuckles and let out a low chuckle, the sound of someone who had placed a very long-odds bet and was already quietly confident about the morning line. She did not say a word.

By evening Irene was back, navigating the Cathedral's corridors on autopilot, ascending through the spiraling shadow passages to the uppermost sanctum. She knocked twice and pushed the door open.

The room looked exactly as expected: Aspects in human form draped across the furniture with their books, two more drifting above the bookshelves in Lantern Cat form like oversized, self-illuminating throw pillows. Her attention reached the sofa and stopped.

Ovelia was sitting there in a swimsuit.

It was a vintage design: conservative in construction, structured like a one-piece tube top, with a small ruffled skirt hem circling the hips and waist that carried the wholesome, bashful modesty of a much older era. The material was a blue-black satin that absorbed the ambient light. Nothing was technically exposed, and nothing needed to be: beneath the satin, Ovelia had layered a full-body stocking in pure, matte black velvet.

The velvet encased her from collarbone to ankle with the frictionless, total precision of a second skin: every curve committed to and outlined, every line of her waist and her legs present in the fabric's quiet insistence. The contrast between the dark satin and the light-devouring velvet was not what a reasonable person would call revealing. It was the beauty of strict prohibition: the thing hidden so completely it demanded to be seen. Hidden yet outlined. Conservative yet ruinous. Every aesthetic decision Ovelia had ever made about herself, accidentally distilled into swimwear.

Ovelia had sensed her the moment her boots touched the hallway stone. She pushed off the sofa with one light toe and floated toward the doorway, carrying the blue-black satin with languid grace.

Every composure reserve Irene had ever built converted itself, in one motion, into something with teeth.

She had gone weeks without setting foot in a pleasure house, and the woman responsible had wrapped herself from collarbone to ankle in velvet and satin, tied a ruffle round it, and floated to the door like a dish carrying itself to the table.

Irene crossed the room, the cane tapping a slow cadence off the parquet until the gold ferrule came to rest just short of those bare insteps, caught the edge of the ruffled skirt, and lifted it a fraction of an inch.

"Do not take another step, Master," Irene said, low and pleasant. Her gaze went down the blue-black satin to the place where it met the velvet, travelled the rest of the way to the bare feet, and came back up. "Stand this close, dressed like that, and I cannot guarantee what I am going to do to you."

Ovelia paused mid-float and looked down at her, and her tail curved into a smug and triumphant crescent.

"Perverted girl," Ovelia pronounced, with immense, satisfied dignity.

She drew herself up, shoulders back and chest out, and the blue-black satin took every bit of the shape.

"When Cat served as an instructor at the Academy," she said, basking, "a sweeping majority of students voted Cat the faculty member with the finest figure. It is on record. You may praise Cat more."

Irene's hand tightened on the head of her cane until the leather creaked. Her eyes had not left Ovelia since the doorway, and they went up and down her, taking everything that was offered and assessing, with some care, the tensile strength of the satin.

"They undersold you," she said.

Ovelia leaned down the last inch and touched the cool tip of her nose to Irene's cheek, brief and absent-minded, the greeting a cat gives somebody who lives in the same house. The strike had landed dead center at full power, and the target had filed it under grooming.

The historical figure data having been received and acknowledged, Ovelia lost interest. She hummed something tuneless, drifted toward the bookshelves, pulled down a heavy tome, and began explaining to Allison the rite she had finished that morning, the blue-black satin swaying as she went.

Irene stayed where she had been left, and her eyes went past the swaying hem to the rest of the room.

Shaar had tipped her chair back onto two legs and was grinning straight at her, enormously pleased with herself. Ah, Irene thought. So that is whose idea this was.

Her gaze kept travelling: Allison on the sofa by the shelves, Kara curled up with a book, Avenka sprawled across the rug, Daena and Vila asleep above the bookcases in Lantern Cat form. The household ran to nine, and seven of them were in this room. The cat had once come through a wall to take an evening of nine away from her, and two short was a discount Irene could live with. If I lock the doors, she wondered, how many of them could I get through before morning? That the least of them could tie her in a knot with one tail did not strike her, just then, as a relevant consideration.

Shaar went on grinning as the count went past her chair. When it came to noticing danger, she and Ovelia shared a single brain cell, and it had the evening off.

Allison looked up from her page, met Irene's eyes, and raised her book in front of her face, which left the rest of her exactly where it had been.

Irene pressed her lips together and crossed to the armchair by way of the shelves, which took her behind the sofa, close enough for her sleeve to trail across Allison's shoulder. A shiver went down Allison from the tips of her ears to the end of her tail. A foot away, Ovelia went on explaining the rite to the back of a book.

Shaar's chair came down onto all four legs. The grin had gone, and the skin of her scalp had drawn so tight under the curls that both ears stood straight up.

Irene sat, crossed one leg over the other, rested her chin on her knuckles, and looked at Ovelia.

Halfway through a sentence, Ovelia stopped. The fur at the base of her tail prickled. She turned from the shelves and found Irene in the armchair with no book anywhere near her, and her ears went flat; she drew the tome closer against the satin at her chest, and her tail wound itself round her calves.

"Have you nothing better to do?" Ovelia asked. "Patents to file? A family to usurp?"

Irene smiled at her, in an extraordinarily good mood. "I am not going anywhere."
