After leaving the notary office, Veronica lingered nearby, several blocks away. She had watched the armed guard—the supposed member of the Scavengers—enter shortly after she left. A moment later, he peeked through the doorway, scanning the street before hanging a small wooden sign outside.

Fortunately, Veronica could sharpen her senses, stretching her vision across the distance. From where she stood, he had no reason to suspect he was being watched.

Well, that’s suspicious.

Closing the building right after she came in to ask questions?

Veronica hummed to herself and began walking away.

There was nothing more to gain there. She’d gotten the answers they were willing to give.

But there was still one more place worth visiting.

“I’m sorry, but that’s all I can tell you,” said the middle-aged clerk.

A ledger lay open on the desk before him. The entry he had shown her contained little more than a date, a route designation, and a short note: cargo lost during bandit attack. No names. No cargo manifest. No escort records.

The information was so sparse that Veronica had learned almost nothing.

“Not even the names of the people who died?” she asked. “I’m worried someone I know might have been part of that caravan.”

The man pursed his lips. “I understand the concern,” he said politely, “but that information is restricted to Scavenger personnel. I’m afraid I cannot share it.”

“What about the cargo?” Veronica asked lightly. “I heard it belonged to an alchemist group heading toward Lilybed.”

“That documentation is confidential as well.”

His tone remained calm, but his responses came a little too quickly.

“Do incidents like that happen often?” she asked.

“Bandit attacks?” the clerk replied, tilting his head.

“Yes.”

“Not frequently,” he said. “But the valley routes can be dangerous. That particular shipment was… unfortunate. For everyone involved.”

“Were any traces of the caravan recovered?”

The man’s mouth twitched as if he had nearly answered. His eyes flickered briefly before he corrected himself.

“I… well, yes. That’s how we confirmed the attack. Everything had already been taken. That is where we found the bodies.”

“What about the bandits?” Veronica asked. “Were you able to track any of them down? Any clues left behind?”

Before the clerk could respond, heavy gear rattled across the floor above. A man descended the stairs into the lobby. Several people moved about the building, carrying documents and equipment, but this man stood out immediately.

“Good afternoon, miss,” he said as he stepped between her and the clerk. “I’m afraid information regarding past contracts is restricted to senior members of the guild. Our clerk here would not be able to answer such questions.”

Veronica narrowed her eyes.

The man was tall and broad, his posture stiff with forced courtesy.

“Unless you have business with us—either to hire the Scavengers or enter a contract—I will have to ask you to leave,” he said.

Silence hung between them as they studied one another. This man stood his ground, refusing to budge.

Perhaps she overextended her stay here, too.

At last, Veronica stepped back. “Alright,” she said. “If that’s company policy, I wouldn’t want to cause trouble.”

She gave a small nod and turned toward the door.

Without glancing back, she walked out of the Scavengers’ headquarters.

After a brief visit back to her inn, Veronica walked through the city’s merchant square: an area where unaffiliated merchants all took up stands, buying and selling wares. There were still a few items she needed to sell, which she had taken off of Lorence’s body—the mysterious man who enslaved Gwen. This included the defensive cloak he wore, another ring he carried, and a type of ceremonial dagger.

While she could have gone to a merchant house, it probably wasn’t the best time.

In truth, she would have preferred to sell the items through Galland, though that option carried its own risks. She did not want to draw further attention from High Crates while she was still investigating the circumstances of his servitude. If the merchant house became suspicious, they might restrict him from meeting with her at all, or worse, begin trashing records. She felt safe investigating anything but High Crates for the time being.

For now, she wanted a little time alone to think.

There was also the matter of meeting Gwen the following day. She could afford to slow things down until then.

Her visit to the notary office and the Scavengers’ headquarters had made one thing clear. Acting openly and asking questions on her own was not necessarily the wisest approach. The clerk at the office had seemed slightly… irked by her questioning. Perhaps even unnerved. Meanwhile, the man at the Scavengers’ base had been obviously nervous, not to mention the senior member who had come down and told her to leave.

There was definitely something there.

Helm had claimed that the notary office was deeply connected to the Scavengers Transport Company. If he truly had been the one who brought about their downfall in the future, then Veronica could not afford to dismiss his suspicions.

The problem was how to expose it.

She could attempt to break in and steal the relevant documents. Another option would be to hire someone to infiltrate the office on her behalf. Either method might uncover evidence.

Yet in the future, that alone had not been enough.

A recorded confession. Spoken words that admitted guilt, captured by a hidden recording artifact.

That was what had ultimately brought the Scavengers down.

She needed something similar. More importantly, she needed someone who was deeply involved in the scheme. Someone she could pressure into revealing the truth.

Perhaps Dain. However, it was hard to tell if he was actually involved and not simply a senior merchant who understood the details of Galland’s case.

According to Sage, there were piles of documents that had been revealed and brought forth during the trial against Scavengers in the future. Sage even had some stored within its memory, though they were few and far between.

However, from the ones that were available, there were many implicating, criminalizing details.

The difficulty lay in tracing their origin. She could write the information down herself, but such claims would hold little credibility. Instead, she needed to locate the original documents and take them directly. Her only advantage was that she already knew what she was looking for.

In the worst case, she could involve the viscount. That, however, would damage their relationship if she continued relying on him. Asking for his help with her identification was already a high demand.

Another possibility was releasing additional theoretical research to the Mage Tower. That approach would bring even harsher consequences. If she revealed too much too quickly, she would lose the advantage gained by slowly feeding knowledge into history.

She needed to control the pace.

Veronica continued through the merchant square until she found a stall that was not already occupied with customers. It was not a simple cloth laid across the ground with items scattered on top, nor was it one of the poorly constructed wooden stands nearby. This one was larger and carefully arranged, with decorative carvings along the frame.

She stepped up to the counter.

“Welcome!” the merchant greeted her enthusiastically.

The man appeared to be in his early thirties and stood slightly shorter than average. A neatly trimmed beard connected to a well-kept mustache, both shaped with obvious care. His short brown hair matched the tidy brown vest he wore over a clean shirt.

“Name’s Wiliker,” the man said. “You looking to buy or sell today, miss?”

“I’m looking to sell,” Veronica replied.

The instant the words left her mouth, she could have sworn his eyes dulled, if only slightly.

“Great. You’ve come to the right stall,” he said anyway, grin easy and practiced. “What’re you selling?”

Veronica reached into the small satchel she had purchased earlier and set the first item on the counter: a neatly folded dark cloak. On the surface, it could have passed for an ordinary garment from a clothier. Wiliker, however, recognized its worth at once.

“Oh, ho,” he murmured, leaning closer. “Magical items. Why didn’t you say so sooner?”

This was the cloak Lorence had worn, the one capable of turning aside even her own third-tier spells.

“It can form barriers,” Veronica said. “Similar to those created by Tier-3 mages.”

Wiliker reached toward it, then paused halfway, glancing up at her. “Mind if I…?”

“Go ahead.”

He lifted the cloak with careful hands, turning it over and inspecting each side. He rubbed the lining between his fingers, stretched the fabric gently, then held it up toward the light filtering in from the street.

After a moment, he set it down and bent behind the counter.

He rummaged through a few cloth-wrapped bundles before retrieving a palm-sized pane of glass mounted in a circular brass frame. Faint etchings ringed the edge, and the sunlight glanced off its surface as he raised it.

“Simple appraisal lens,” he said, angling it toward the cloak. “Let’s see here. Hmm…”

Veronica waited while he examined the garment again, moving the lens in slow, deliberate passes as though reading invisible script woven into the fibers.

When he finally set it down and fell quiet, she spoke.

“So? Is it worth anything?”

Wiliker lowered the lens and straightened.

“Oh, it’s worth something,” he said. “That thing’s probably worth twenty thousand vix.”

Veronica lifted a brow. “That much?”

He scoffed. “Miss, as you said, it can block Tier-3 offensive spells. Not indefinitely, though. Maybe a dozen casts, give or take, before it needs mana again. But it’s even stitched with mana-absorbing lacing. Leave it in the sun or some mana-rich place long enough and it’ll recharge on its own over time.”

He folded his arms.

“Not that the self-charging matters much if you can afford the cloak in the first place,” he added. “At that point you can just buy a few minor mana crystals and recharge it whenever you like.”

Wiliker’s eyes rose to her face. “Did you buy this?”

Veronica shook her head. “No. It was… passed down to me.”

“Hmm.” He studied her for a moment. “Must’ve had a rich family, then. That recharge feature alone nearly doubles its worth.”

Veronica nodded once. “Is your offer twenty thousand, then?”

A short laugh escaped him. “Hah. No. I can’t afford it. Hell, most people in this square can’t. You’d be better off taking something like that to a merchant house.”

Veronica sighed upon hearing those words.

Maybe it was too much to expect from unaffiliated merchants.

Wiliker pursed his lips. “What about the other items? I can appraise those too. Might not be as pricey.”

She nodded and reached back into her satchel.

“These two I’m less certain about,” Veronica said. She placed a ring on the counter, then set down a dagger still tucked in its sheath. “The ring may have an enchantment, but I haven’t been able to determine what. And the dagger looks more ceremonial than functional.”

Wiliker nodded, rubbing his hands together as his interest returned.

“Well,” he said, “let’s find out.”

He lifted the appraisal lens again and examined the ring first, rotating it carefully between his fingers.

“I see…”

Veronica watched him quietly.

“Well?” she asked once enough time had passed.

Wiliker finally looked up, tilting his head slightly as if reassessing her.

“Hmm. Well, it’s a lot cheaper than the cloak, that’s for sure,” he said. “I can actually afford this one.”

“What does it do?”

He lifted the ring a little higher, letting the light catch along its edge.

“I’m not sure what you’d call it exactly,” he said. “A connecting ring key. Maybe a communication key ring.”

Veronica’s brow rose. “What?”

Wiliker stroked his chin with his free hand, thinking as he spoke.

“You know communication crystals, right? This ring is basically the key to a secured one. A locked crystal won’t respond unless you have the paired anchor. You imbue this with mana while you’re near the crystal, and that’s what activates the connection. Pretty archaic, but good if you need a secure channel.”

A communication crystal… Maybe Lorence was talking to someone. A buyer or handler, maybe people from an organization he was a part of.

“Is there any way to track where it’s connected to?” Veronica asked.

Wiliker lowered the ring and set it back on the counter. He shrugged.

“Don’t know,” he admitted. “I’m not that magic-savvy. I can appraise their functions. I don’t engineer them.”

“Is it possible?” she pressed. “Or would someone be able to do it?”

“You could try the Mage Tower,” he said. “Arrogant bunch, but their engineers are competent. If anyone can inspect it properly, it’s them.”

“A magic engineer…” Veronica murmured under her breath, her thoughts already shifting.

Wiliker cocked his head. “So, not selling it?”

She closed her fingers around the ring and drew it back into her palm. “Probably not. But if I did, what’s the offer?”

“Five hundred vix,” he said. “It’s nearly useless without the paired crystal, but the materials can be repurposed for a fee.”

Veronica hummed, unimpressed. “No. I’ll keep it. I’ll see what the Mage Tower can tell me.”

Wiliker shrugged, unbothered, almost expecting the answer. “Suit yourself.”

He raised the appraisal lens again and shifted his attention to the remaining item.

“Dagger’s turn,” he said, reaching for the sheathed weapon.

He slid it closer, turning it so the hilt faced him. Even without drawing the blade, he studied the craftsmanship: the ornate pommel, the decorative inlay, the fine stitching along the sheath.

Then he glanced up at Veronica.

He angled the lens over the sheath first, then drew it along the hilt. The faint etchings around the brass frame caught the light as he adjusted his grip, squinting in concentration.

A few seconds passed.

“…Huh.”

Veronica’s focus sharpened. “What?”

Wiliker did not answer right away. He shifted the dagger a fraction, then ran the lens over it again, slower this time. His thumb traced the base of the guard, as though confirming what his eyes refused to take for granted.

“Well,” he said at last, “I know what it is.”

“And?” Veronica asked. “What is it?”

“Like you suspected, it’s ceremonial,” Wiliker replied. “Likely a piece from a fallen noble line. The craftsmanship is excellent. The etching alone is the work of a professional. Whoever commissioned it had money.”

“And the price?”

“It’s more decoration than weapon,” he said. “I can’t identify the family, so I can’t attach a name premium to it. But as a baseline? Five thousand vix.”

Veronica blinked once. “Cheaper than the cloak.”

He shrugged. “I know what the cloak is worth because I can judge the enchantment. With this, lineage matters. Without the name, it might just be something you hang on a wall among other pretty blades.”

“What about combat?” she asked. “Is it useful in a fight?”

“Sure,” Wiliker said. “It wasn’t designed for battle, but it’ll still outperform any cheap steel you’d pull off a blacksmith’s rack.”

She gave a quick nod. “Then I’ll keep it for now.”

Wiliker set the dagger down and looked up.

“One hundred vix for the appraisals, then,” he said. “Since you’re not selling or buying.”

Veronica paid it without hesitation.

“Thank ye, miss,” he said. “And good luck with those items.”

She gave a small nod and left the stall.

Even if the cloak was worth twenty thousand vix, it was not enough to erase Galland’s debt, not even when combined with the Mage Tower’s payment.

“Since Gwen follows the Path of Blooming…” she murmured. “Then maybe…”

If the cloak could block Tier-3 spells, it could serve her well. At least until Gwen reached the third tier herself. The only question was whether she would be willing to wear something taken from her former captor.

Maybe it wasn’t a big deal, but she couldn’t be sure. Still, she couldn’t dismiss the feeling outright.

Veronica walked past the merchant stands and lifted her gaze to the sky.

“Plenty of day left.”

She turned back toward her inn.

Perhaps it was time to form her third set of mana rings.
