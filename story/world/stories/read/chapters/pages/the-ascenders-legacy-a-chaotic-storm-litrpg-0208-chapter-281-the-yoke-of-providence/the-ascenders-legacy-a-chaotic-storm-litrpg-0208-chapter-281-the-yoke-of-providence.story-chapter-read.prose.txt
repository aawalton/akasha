While the Elders and Devoteds waited impatiently within the amphitheater, murmuring and wondering where the Fated had gone, Jethro sat on the floor of the watchroom, Sárán's diary in his hands and a glare on his face.

The scent of death and decay hung thickly in the air, but Jethro barely noticed, his senses having dulled to the vile odor over the years.

Exhaling deeply, he glared at the silent watchers, so close to their graves now. They looked so weak—bones peeking out from voluminous robes—and yet, they emanated an undeniable power. The faintest wisps of their combined aura were enough to vibrate the entire room to its core.

Their bodies and souls might be dying, but their cores were just as powerful and potent as ever—or at least that was how it seemed to Jethro in that moment.

A grimace appeared on his face in sympathy, and his glare softened subtly.

These dying calamities were the ones who'd groomed him ever since he inherited Sárán's legacy. They had placed him on a pedestal and given him purpose.

He, a common blacksmith's apprentice, was now a man of glorious fate and ambition.

The watchers had given him that. They had taught him all that he knew about Sárán—his mission, his prophecies—but most especially, his power. His pure, overwhelming power had drawn men and women alike from every corner of Lutia to him like moths to a flame.

Not once had Jethro doubted the authenticity of those teachings—so blinded by the prospect of overwhelming power.

Until today.

No, that was a lie.

He may not have doubted, but a part of him—a very tiny part—had questioned the growing weight he'd felt in his spirit after harvesting his first dozen seals. That weight had slowly grown into pain with each new seal he added, until he'd finally linked his spirit to that of his first pet, Jian, the wind transmigrant.

The relief had been glorious, a true breath of fresh air—but as he grew in power, harvesting more skills and seals, the weight had returned stronger, agonizing, as if his spirit was being stretched thin.

The watchers had given him his second pet after that, even though he'd never complained or voiced his concerns—afraid that they might throw him out as a failure. And once more, the pain subsided.

But like a cancer, it returned, growing more agonizing with each innate skill or seal he harvested and grafted into his spirit. He had tried to figure it out on his own, deluding himself into thinking that the pain was a natural consequence of his affinity—a phase he would soon overcome.

And for a while, he had learned to believe it—to believe that he was fine and that nothing was wrong.

Until Aodhán Brystion had come with his poisonous words and vile accusations—with golden eyes that had seemed to see through him without restraint—pointing out all his fears and laying them bare before the watchers to see.

Jethro had kicked against the transmigrant's words in desperate panic—terming them lies meant to sow discord and mistrust. The watchers had believed him and had acted against Aodhán, but Jethro had been completely unable to rest ever since then.

All the fears he had been nursing, all the flaws he'd been hiding—they had all bubbled up to the surface, and that part of him that silently questioned became louder, with one question being the loudest.

How had Sárán Béithir really gained his power?

His patron wasn't an inheritor or transmigrant, meaning he as an inheritor should have been stronger—more powerful—yet he had barely scratched the surface of what Sárán had been capable of at his tier.

At the fiftieth tier, Sárán had already begun prophesying and leading armies, yet here he was, barely able to hold a hundred seals in his spirit without ripping himself apart.

He wasn't just struggling. At this rate, he was failing, and failing woefully.

He had studied Sárán's diary in search of answers time and time again, but each time, he came up empty. There was nothing in the diary that spoke of how Sárán had gained his oppressive strength. Nothing aside from the linking technique he had passed on—the yoke of providence.

But as powerful as the yoke was—despite the help he was receiving from the spirits of his pets—he still wasn't strong enough.

It didn't make any sense to him.

There was something the watchers weren't telling him, and after all this time, Jethro was no longer scared of voicing it. They couldn't cast him out anymore. What time did they have to begin searching for a new fated?

Jethro's glare returned, and he looked back up at the rasping corpses of the watchers. It was high time he asked for help.

Shutting the diary with a loud snap, he pushed himself to his feet, drawing the attention of the sleeping watchers. They glared at him from their high thrones, annoyed at the interruption of their sleep, and after a moment, the Time Watcher rasped, time essence puffing out of his mouth like a chimney.

"What do you want, Jethro? Shouldn't you be watching over the harvest tournament?"

Jethro bowed. "I ran into some complications, Time Watcher. I need your aid." An awkward heartbeat later, he added. "I need your wisdom."

"And what are these bothersome complications?" The Fate Watcher rasped, her robes swishing in annoyance. "Is this about the storm transmigrant?"

"No," Jethro replied quickly. "I have no problem with the storm transmigrant at all. What I've come for is much graver."

"Spit it out then." The Time Watcher snapped.

"It is about my spirit." Jethro frowned nervously and pushed forward. "For a long time now, I have felt a growing strain in my spirit with each skill and seal I harvest. With Jian and Kira, I have been able to manage the pain. But with the harvest tournament and the constant harvesting of seals, I find myself reaching a limit."

The word "limit" was like a shock to all three watchers, and almost simultaneously, their gazes widened. They sat up in their thrones, and the Karmic Watcher rasped. "That's not possible. What do you mean, limit?"

"Exact... Exactly what it means, sir." Jethro stuttered as the latent auras of the Watchers suddenly burst out of them, shaking the entire tower to its core. Fear raced down his spine, and suddenly, the watchers didn't seem so feeble anymore. In fact, they looked alive enough to live another hundred years.

Pushing down his fear, Jethro continued. "If I do not find a way to strengthen my spirit soon, I will inevitably reach my limit, bringing to an abrupt halt the mission of Sárán Béithir and the purpose of the Order!"

"Quiet, boy!" The Karmic Watcher snapped, karmic essence roiling around him like a brewing storm. "Do not speak such evil words. The pain you experience is normal."

"Wh... what?" Jethro asked in shock. "I... there was no instance of my patron experiencing this pain. It was never mentioned."

"Not everything will be mentioned, boy," the Time Watcher said to him reproachfully. "Why do you think Sárán created the technique of the yoke of providence? To alleviate the strain upon his spirit."

Jethro's frown deepened. "But I have yoked two transmigrants, and yet the pain hasn't been alleviated. Rather, it has gotten to the point where I can hardly breathe from the weight pressing down on my chest. If I don't get the storm awakened as a third link, then I will surely reach my limit."

The word "limit" for a third time in a row was apparently too much for the watchers, and their auras slammed down on him with the force of many mountains. Jethro collapsed like a sack of potatoes, but his head never touched the ground.

He was the Fated after all. Even the watchers were hesitant to hurt him.

"Mention that word once more, and you will bear your first scar, Jethro." The Fate Watcher spoke on behalf of the others, her voice low yet powerful.

Jethro nodded. "I will never mention it again. But the fact remains—I need a better, more lasting solution than linking more transmigrants."

The watchers exchanged glances, their ancient gazes weighted, and then the Time Watcher spoke. "The time has come then. We thought your case might be different because you never complained of any pain. We thought it might be a result of your status as an inheritor, but apparently, that is not the case. You should have come to us sooner."

Jethro rose back to his feet, eyes darting from one watcher to the next in confusion. "What is it I need to know?"

The Time Watcher laughed. "Sárán suffered the same pain you experience, but at an even greater level, because, as you know, he wasn't an inheritor. His spirit wasn't as strong as yours. That was why he created the yoke of providence. But the linking and sharing of burdens are not the only purposes of that technique."

"What other purpose does it serve?"

"The same purpose as everything Sárán built his power and legacy on." The Karmic Watcher smiled. "Absorption. Have you never wondered why you've never seen an image of Sárán with his transmigrants?"

"To protect them?" Jethro replied, still confused.

It was a matter he had given much thought. The transmigrants were a weakness. If any of them died in battle, the burden of his spirit they were carrying would fall back on him. And if they both died, then he wouldn't be far behind. They were his Achilles heel. That was why he'd gotten so angry when Aodhán had threatened them with death.

But it seemed he was wrong. Very wrong, because the watchers laughed.

"It wasn't to protect them," the Fate Watcher rasped after her laughter died down. "In fact, Sárán loved bringing them to battle. They were usually the first line of defense."

Jethro's breath hitched. "They would die."

"And he would absorb the strength of their spirits—becoming stronger with their deaths."

"What?!" Jethro's eyes widened, and then he remembered Aodhán's exact words, the anger on his face, when he'd asked. "And what happens to them when they die in battle still bonded to you?"

He hadn't understood it then, but it hadn't been a threat. It was anger, and he had missed it.

A smile appeared on his face as the realization dawned.

He could strengthen his spirit with the death of his pets. With one death, he could gain the relief of linking half a dozen transmigrants.

He could become more powerful.

His smile stretched into a cold grin, and he bowed to the watchers. "Thank you for your wisdom, watchers. I pray to Sárán that you live another thousand years."

"Do not curse us, child!" The Karmic Watcher scowled down at him. "Now begone from our presence, and let us rest."

Jethro bowed once more, and without another word, he stepped out of the watchroom. Isobá bowed the moment he appeared in the hallway, her fiery hair rising into the air above her. "You seem happier, Fated."

"I am happier." He smiled as he began walking toward his chambers. "Come with me, Isobá. I need you to kill someone for me."

Isobá grinned, not bothering to ask who it was. It didn't matter. She would burn the world to fulfill the Fated's wish.

While they walked, Jethro contemplated which pet he would like to put down and eventually decided on Jian, the wind transmigrant. She was weaker than Kira, and he loved the death awakened more.

They soon reached his private chambers, and Isobá pushed the door open to reveal both of his pets sleeping peacefully on his bed. Oh, how lovely they looked.

Jethro stared at both of them quietly, his smile wavering for a moment before hardening into a thin line of grim determination. He didn't need to speak. He simply pointed, and Isobá moved, an expression of glee on her face.

Jethro smiled. It must be exhilarating to kill a transmigrant.

Like death itself, Isobá silently walked to the bed and raised a hand above Jian's chest. Jethro felt an instinctive moment of panic, but he pushed it aside.

He had ascended above his feeble beliefs. He was entering a new realm now.

A fiery sword manifested in Isobá's palm, the essence painting Jian's face orange. Heat engulfed. Her eyes snapped open. Widened—

The blade plunged straight into her heart and twisted. She screamed.

But Jethro could barely hear her cry as an ocean of spirit strength collapsed into his soul—vast and unyielding like a tidal wave of wind essence. It struck him with such force he staggered, nearly collapsing to his knees as his body convulsed under the weight. Then, slowly, it merged with his soul—thread by thread, bone by bone—until the unbearable strain within him seemed to melt away.

His chest heaved as if it might burst, but the pain gave way to awe as wind sang freely through his lungs for the first time in a long while. The ache that had gnawed at him for months—the splitting strain of a spirit stretched too thin—shrank to a faint throb, almost laughable in its weakness, and for the first time in a long time, Jethro felt as though he could carry everything alone. He didn't need anyone else.

Greedily, his gaze slid to Kira who had jerked off the bed in fright, eyes wide and filled with terror. "No, no..." she cried, but Jethro wasn't listening. Greed yawned within him like a chasm—sharp and hungry. Kira was stronger than Jian. Her spirit would make him soar.

But then she began to cry, glistening tears falling down her face, and the yawning greed in his chest was immediately smothered. Forcing his lips into a soft smile, Jethro spoke, his tone cajoling. "Don't cry, Kira. I would never do to you what I did to Jian. You know I like you more."

But Kira didn't seem to be hearing him, so overwhelmed by fright and grief. Jethro hated seeing her like that. His expression hardened, and this time when he spoke, it was a command.

"Wipe your tears, Kira. Never cry. And stand tall—you are a transmigrant, not a servant."

Against her will, Kira's tears dried up immediately, and her expression morphed into serene calmness. Her body shook with sobs, and her eyes itched with unshed tears, but no tears fell. She stood tall, the very image of poise and prestige.

It was a beautiful sight.

Jethro smiled and glanced back at Isobá. "Why don't we go back to the amphitheater? I feel so much better already."

Isobá bowed in response. Her sword vanished and she walked to open the door for the Fated. Jethro glanced at Kira as he left. "Stay and dispose of the body. I don't care how you do it. Just get rid of it."

Kira stood frozen long after the door shut, her body unable to tremble, her eyes dry, completely unable to express the overwhelming grief she was feeling at the loss at a dear friend. Tears pooled in her eyes, yet none fell. She couldn’t cry, despite the yawning ache in her belly.

Her closest friend and partner in captive had just died and she couldn’t even mourn.

Her body was no longer her own. It hadn’t been since the day she’d been foolish enough to join up with cultists in defiance of Unoros.

She had paid for that defiance with captivity—a yoke so tight she couldn’t even breath if commanded not to. It would have been preferable to die.

But it had only gotten worse. Now, she had no partner. No one to share her burdens and pain with. Now she was alone.

Now she was—

The weight of the Fated’s command loosened slightly in that moment, and Kira collapsed immediately, her hands trembling as she reached across the bed to touch Jian.

A single tear fell as her fingers brushed against Jian’s already cooling skin. Her lips quivered. Her throat clenched, desperate to release so much more than a single tear—but the command weighed on her like iron chains, choking the sound before it could ever leave her lips.

Her whole body shook. Her grief had no outlet, no escape. It roared inside her, a dam begging to be set free.

“Jian…” Her lips shaped the name, silent, without voice. Just the shape of it burned.

The ache in her chest shifted, twisting into something darker—something sharp. She could not mourn. She could not scream. She could not even weep. But she could act.

The fated has asked her to dispose of the body. He didn’t care how.

That gave her choices. Her eyes widened, and her mind began racing with ideas.

She could accelerate the decay of the body to pure death essence to enhance herself and her bloodline. She could even invert its vitality into necrotic energy to sharpen the power of her body cultivation.

There was so much she could do with the corpse, but none would provide her more potential benefits than reanimation.

Even in the order, reanimation of corpses was a crime. But the Fated had commanded her to dispose of Jian’s body however she chose. He didn’t care how. And Kira had every intention to exploit it.

She pressed her palms flat against Jian’s breastbone, essence brimming around her, wild and unfocused. Reanimating a corpse was as easy as breathing. The simplest act of necromancy. A skeleton, a shambling husk, a puppet to obey. She had raised hundreds before her captivity.

But Kira didn’t just want to reanimate this corpse as a skeleton. She didn’t want a puppet.She didn’t want a hollow servant.

She wanted revenge. She wanted a chance to pay the Fated back in his own coin.

And in that moment, a memory from her home world surfaced—a memory of fear, of whispered tales told to rebellious children on windy nights,of a creature born from despair and vengeance.

It was a nightmare made flesh, and just imagining Jian turned into such a horror made her shudder—but it would get the job done. And in that moment, with grief boiling within her like molten pitch, vengeance was all Kira cared about.

She glanced at Jian’s face, her breath hitching as she tried to rationalize what she was about to do. Jian would want this. She would want revenge on the Fated for all his crimes—for the pain, the humiliation, and the unyielding yoke.

She had always said that if she ever died, she wanted her death to mean something.

Kira’s hands trembled. “It will,” she whispered to the cold corpse. “I’ll make sure it does even if it kills me and damns your soul. I’ll make sure our existence means something.”

And so, Kira did the unthinkable.

She bared her grief like an open wound and hurled it into Jian’s body—every shred of agony, rage, and yearning she had ever buried. She poured in not just essence, but memory, will, and hatred. She poured in her refusal to break.

It was no necromantic ritual. It was desperation, raw and naked.

Power surged within her spirit, pouring out of her core in a wave of death and decay. Her seals dimmed—one, two, three—every shred of quintessence she possessed poured into her spirit, mixing with the river of death energy surging out of her core.

Heat engulfed her like an inferno, burning from within, yet Kira didn’t stop. Willpower poured out of her core next, an entire river of it, merging seamlessly with the river of death essence and quintessence in her spirit until her very spirit felt like it was unraveling.

Her body shook, blood vessels straining under the weight of power no one in the early advanced tier should have been able to bear. The air around her thickened, suffocating, every breath laced with the stench of rot and ozone.

Kira pressed harder, palms grinding against Jian’s chest as if sheer force might tether the soul she was calling back. Her vision blurred; stars danced at the edges of her sight. But still she gave more.

The chamber groaned. Shadows twisted across the walls, lengthening like grasping fingers. They engulfed her and the body beneath her, cold as ice, yet burning.

Kira screamed without a voice, her throat locked by her invisible bindings. Her eyes reddened and itched with tears she couldn’t release, but her spirit bled all the same. She reached deeper, clawing at the depth of her reserves.

And then—she poured it all into the corpse.

Jian’s body arched, back snapping upward as though seized by lightning. A hollow gasp tore from her throat, a breath drawn not from lungs but from the wave of death howling invisibly through the chamber.

The wound on her chest sealed, death rushing to replace the damaged heart with something darker. Skin knitted in warped whorls of grey-veined black. The torn edges of breastbones reformed into jagged ridges, as if her body had been reforged by hands that cared nothing for symmetry. Veins turned umbral, crawling like rivers of ink beneath her skin.

And her body… her body had been transformed. Jagged bone jutted from her form in places, wind threading through the gaps like smoke, giving her an ephemeral, half-solid silhouette.

Her lips darkened to bruised violet, and when her eyelids fluttered open, the ringed green and silver of Jian’s pupils had vanished—replaced by a swirling maelstrom of gray and black, hollow yet alive, or perhaps alive because of their hollowness.

Her chest rose and fell, steady and deliberate. Her hollow gaze locked onto Kira. And then she rasped—a hollow, whispering sound, carried not just through the air but through the very currents of Kira’s spirit.

“K…Kira…”

The name was torn, stretched, and threaded with wind and death, a voice that was hers yet not entirely hers. It clawed at Kira’s chest, tugging at the raw edges of her grief.

And then, as if timed to that first word, a sharp ping echoed in Kira’s mind.

Congratulations! You have created a creature never before seen in Abyssos.

Name Assigned: Skalk—a myth made flesh—an object of folklore from another world, given form through death and wind. [View Skalk status screen.]

NB: You have full control over the Skalk for the duration of its seventy-two-hour lifespan. Command the Skalk to register your voice and status as its master.

Kira glanced back at Jian—who was now something called a Skalk—and gave her a watery smile. “Hi, Jian.”

“Hi, Kira,” The Skalk responded in the same hollow tone. “I am filled with so much rage. So much anger. I need to channel it. before it consumes me.”

Kira nodded. “Then go. Go to the order and kill as many cultists as you can find. Make sure you’re never caught, and make sure to sow despair. The cultists have taken your life. It is time to take their peace.”

The Skalk glanced at her, and then it smiled, a cold, lifeless smile that sent a shudder down Kira's spine. Its form shivered, shards of bone and wind scattering like smoke, and in the next heartbeat, she was gone—vanishing into the air as easily as the wind itself, leaving only the chill of her presence behind.
