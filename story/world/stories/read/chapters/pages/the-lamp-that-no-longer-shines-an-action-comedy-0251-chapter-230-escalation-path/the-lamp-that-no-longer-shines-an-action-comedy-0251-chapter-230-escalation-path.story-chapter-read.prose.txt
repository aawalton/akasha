[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 112 — 3:10 PM[Location]: Ailuronia · The Palace of Rex Mundi · The Arch-Witch Boxes

Adeline had stopped listening somewhere around the second date.

By ninety-three she had already arrived at the place the Tarnished Crow was walking her toward, and the rest of the delivery was a woman enjoying her own footwork. Twelve years, four seats, every departure with a reason that survived inspection, and every square vacated had gone, cleanly and without contest, to the same buyer.

No seat on that spiral turns without the First Seat's assent. Somebody had been laying paving stones down the side of a mountain, one at a time, from the summit.

Adeline leaned on the rail and looked out over the empty chamber, and let the last of it come through the crystal.

Emilia Lucas walked out of the Habsburg residence with three hours of session still to sit, and went home.

Adeline turned her head. Four tiers along the same curve, the Lucas box had been shut up for the day.

She believed every syllable of it. Evangeline Wellington told the truth, for the same reason a good architect uses sound steel, and she had held a great deal back, and shaped the gap to shape the rest.

The members were sound, and you can hang a woman off sound steel. But a player prices the messenger before the message.

In ordinary hands the package was a lever against Irene Habsburg. Show any ambitious witch that ten years of vacancies had filled in a single direction, and that the power of a continent now sat in one pair of hands, and she reaches for the obvious move without being taught it. Go and take it. The parlors of the Inner Sea would have expected exactly that of her: one seat short in 1999 and still watching the spiral from a box, with Paddy alongside her, five years into the bottom rung and out of patience years ago.

Evangeline Wellington knew exactly whose hounds they were, and she had dialed anyway.

"Your sender did not come here for Irene," Adeline said.

The line was enjoying itself enormously. "I have already told you. I am only the messenger."

Adeline turned that over, and the shape of it kept improving. A blade had been aimed at the First Seat and put into the hands of a Wellington, and the Wellington had carried it with her devotion in perfect working order.

A Wellington kneels to one house.

Adeline's pulse came up under her collar. The witches who had stood near enough to watch Ovelia work had been making the same joke for decades: that there was more than one of her in there.

"Which of her Aspects hired you," she said, "to bleed the main body?"

Evangeline let the pause run exactly as long as the hit deserved.

"Are you finished with the appraisal?" she asked, lazily. "It is time to settle the bill."

Adeline looked across at the spiral. "Anyone who tries to use me in this is my own kind until it concludes. Name your price. I will pay you more than you ask, and you would do well to think very hard about why."

"Teach me the method," Evangeline said. "How to leave a cage, walk back into it, and have every witness swear the door never opened."

Adeline closed her eyes.

Opening a locked door and leaving it locked behind you are two separate disciplines. Evangeline was shopping for the second, which said everything about how far she had already got with the first, and there was exactly one witch on the continent who sold it.

She is buying passage through a door Irene built herself.

Adeline let out a short, cold sneer. "Deal."

The connection clicked shut.

The Palace of Rex Mundi had been raised to make an individual feel like a rounding error, and the replica had been raised to do it slightly better than the original.

The chamber was a colossal ring, an ancient senate scaled up until it stopped being architecture and started being weather. At the center, anchoring everything, stood the Pillar of Greatness: a single shaft of transparent crystal running from the floor to somewhere the eye declined to follow. The ten sat in open balconies wound up the outside of it in a descending helix, Ovelia holding the sky at the apex and Heidi Lucent at the tenth, almost on the marble.

Adeline's box was where her rank put it, on the tier facing the spiral, and the view of the whole board came with it.

A shadow came across her table, followed by the sound of somebody making herself extremely comfortable.

Paddy dropped into the chair beside her, stretched her legs out across two-thirds of the available floor, and put her hand into Adeline's bowl without breaking stride.

"I am so completely done with that garbage seat," the Ninth Seat announced, crunching a candied lotus seed. "My neck is going to come off."

"If I had gone up when I was meant to," Adeline said, coming back from somewhere with ice still on her, "Marlena would have been swept out of this building years ago. If you loathe it that much, give me the chair."

Paddy rolled her eyes to the ceiling. "Like the seating chart is mine to hand out." She took another fistful. "And Marianne has the actual nerve to talk to Marlena across Irene. And Marlena is sitting in the single best angle in this room for it and never once uses it. Every time I finally crank my head back far enough to catch a strip of Irene's coat, Marlena and Sonia shift and close the lane. I want to take a bat to both of their knees."

Everything conversational went out of Adeline's face, and what came up underneath dropped the temperature in the box by ten degrees.

"Yes," she said quietly. "The Fifth Seat's position is truly terrible."

Paddy's hand stopped halfway back to the bowl.

She turned her head. The thing looking out through her face had put four witches in the ground to reach a chair it did not enjoy sitting in. "What do you know?"

Adeline looked across the gulf at the empty seat one turn below the Fourth. "You would not want Irene to end up like us. Would you?"

Ovelia was permanent. Adeline knew it the way she knew which direction was down. So twelve years of clearing her own board to raise one woman up it had been building something other than a successor.

No Plumed Dragon Witch had ever sat on the Council before Paddy. They had come this far the only way open to them: by taking the work nobody else would put a name to, and putting theirs on it. The more the Council handed down, the more there was to swallow. Adeline had swallowed hers. Paddy swallowed one most seasons and grinned through it.

Famia Schüder had been used since before the war, and there was a vow behind her, and a house, and every witch in this chamber knew whose she was. Eleven years, and nobody in this chamber could have told you whose the Fifth Seat was.

A glove goes on. A glove comes off. It is never the hand.

You raised her to burn.

Paddy's expression went somewhere that had no name.

She looked down into her lap. Her fingers took a lollipop out of her pocket and unwrapped it with enormous care, one fold of paper at a time, and when she raised her head again the bright, open, entirely trustworthy grin was back on her face and seated perfectly.

"Business as usual, then," she said around the stick. "Whatever we do, Irene doesn't hear about it."

Adeline nodded once. "The Tarnished Crow means to sell this more than once. The market for it is small."

"Her not being dead," Paddy sighed, and something cold went across her eyes, "is the worst news I have had all season. That contract is not going to hold her much longer."

"I have agreed to teach her how to leave a room and be standing in it the whole time," Adeline said, flat. "The day she uses it, I will know."

The great chime went through the crystal, and the recess ended.

Paddy stood, brushed the crumbs off her skirt, and stepped out onto the bridge back toward the pillar. She settled into the Ninth Seat and looked up.

Irene Habsburg had come back from the recess. Standing at her shoulder, bent down with her mouth almost against the Fifth Seat's ear, was her secretary: closer than the Sixth, closer than the Fourth, closer than anybody on this continent was permitted to get.

Grand Witches had secretaries. Paddy knew precisely what her own secretary did all day, which meant she knew precisely what that one did all day.

I should have taken the civil service exam.

It was far too late. She had ruined it years ago by becoming a Grand Witch.

Up in the boxes, on a considerably better sightline than the Ninth Seat had ever been granted, Adeline watched the same secretary lean in.

She crossed her arms.

I should have taken the civil service exam.

[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 112 — 3:45 PM[Location]: Ailuronia · The Palace of Rex Mundi · The Fourth Seat

Marianne Horton sat at the Fourth with the new issue of Sophistry open across her knee, at the back of the primary paper, on the acknowledgments.

Blackwell had demanded they come out. Blackwell had been right, in the narrow sense in which Blackwell was usually right, and the girl had left them in.

Marianne had reviewed that submission herself and written four pages. They were, she felt, among the finest four pages she had produced in a decade: impeccably courteous, exhaustively qualified, and committed at no point to any position a person could carry out of the room. It had not occurred to her that telling the child what to do was one of the available moves.

Somebody had done it in four sentences.

Marianne let her eyes rest on the page a moment longer, then lowered the journal and looked one turn down. The Fifth Seat was immaculate. She was taking a junior member's economic resolution apart with three unhurried questions, and she was radiant.

Fine was where Irene Habsburg lived. Fine was the floor. Radiance was an increase, and an increase is a thing being carried, and Marianne had watched her hold that particular grade of brightness perhaps four times in eleven years.

Something had happened today. Marianne had no idea what. She catalogued it the way she catalogued everything, and went on down the spiral.

Near the bottom, the Ninth Seat was beaming around a lollipop with the sunlit good faith of somebody you would hand a purse to and then never think about again.

Marianne knew what that face cost to produce. She had seen the machinery behind it, once, years ago, and had never mentioned it to anyone.

Something had moved down there too. She let her gaze travel across the lower curve, gathered the room, and took her eyes away.

Not yet.

She looked up instead, past the Third, past the Second, all the way to the top of the crystal.

Even from the Fourth Seat, the apex was an ocean off.

The whole climb had brought her within speaking distance of exactly one chair, and it was the one below her own.

Ovelia was speaking, which happened rarely enough that the chamber had gone still for it.

The item on the floor was the Sylvan Fairies: populous, agreeable, soft-eared, and pleasant enough to have made them welcome in a great many houses. Several of their best had been taken up over the years.

Then a genius had been born among them, good enough to have been made a witch herself, and she had spent that on building her own people a closed and self-sufficient production cycle.

Delighted with themselves, the Sylvans had come to the Council and asked to be enfeoffed. Their own territory. Their own governance.

The witches, who gave away pocket dimensions as party favors, had gone cold.

They had been granted one plane. A dead desert.

And the Sylvans, missing every layer of it with perfect optimism, were at this moment preparing to move their entire species onto it.

Every witch in the chamber had run this weeks ago and arrived in the same place. Two errors, Marianne thought, turning a page she was not reading. The first was wanting to stand alone. The second is worse. By packing for a wasteland they have announced that they would rather have the ash than have us, and for a species that has lived this close to our throat, that sentence is terminal.

At the top of the pillar, Ovelia put her cheek against her knuckles and offered the chamber a solution.

During the migration, she said, it was entirely possible that her tail might brush a meteor summoning array. And it would be quite the coincidence if that desert plane came apart into stardust while the Sylvan people were standing on it.

"It bears on nothing larger," the First Seat said. "And the talk grates."

The proposal went to the floor. Procedurally it was the least interesting thing that had happened all afternoon.

Marianne looked down into her text.

A Mercurial Lover.

She turned a few degrees in her chair, leaned toward the turn below, and offered the Fifth Seat a small remark about the temperature of the hall: courteous, precisely weighted, and carrying nothing at all.

Irene looked up, golden eyes catching the light, and gave her back something warm and flawless.

Marianne faced the floor again.

An Erastes of the abyss.

[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 112 — 7:20 PM[Location]: The Umbral Cathedral

The session had adjourned. Ovelia came in through the inner doors and let the dark close behind her, and went the length of the hall to the sofa without hurrying.

Allison had one end of it, with her feet drawn up and a book open against her knee, and she found her place with a finger before she looked up.

"Evangeline has lit the fuse," she said. "We are going to be busy."

Ovelia closed her eyes and let out a long, unhurried sigh. "So little patience."

"Shall we intervene?"

"Let her," Ovelia said. "It is not a bad thing."

She sank back into the cushions and let her head fall against them, and raised one hand, and turned a small bamboo slip over and over between two fingers.

"Hathaway von Ludwig," she murmured to the vaulting.

Then she flicked her wrist, and Allison took the slip out of the air without appearing to look.

Ovelia opened her eyes.

"It is time," she said. "Bring her home."
