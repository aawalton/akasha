The morning sounded loud.

Boots thudded behind the room’s walls. Doors clacked open and shut. Someone somewhere was arguing with a clucker. The smell of baked grain drifted in soft but insistent whiffs.

Elynara pulled the blanket over her head. It was a good plan. Foolproof. Until a puff of pure mischief body-slammed her cheek like a bouncing mana comet.

“Starpuuuff,” she groaned.

The spirit gave a delighted trill and flopped against her neck, warm and smug.

A noise from the other side of the room suggested Mina was also alive, though if her half-muttered complaints were anything to go by, she was not thrilled about it. She didn’t speak up, but a grumbled shuffle under the covers followed by Pufflet’s soft sneeze filled in the blanks.

Elynara pushed herself upright with a sigh. Her arms didn’t protest, but her back voiced an opinion. One she pointedly ignored.

Rubbing sleep from her eyes, she pulled her staff from where it leaned beside the bed and dropped into a seat again.

No sense rushing. The day would wait a few more minutes.

Stretching lightly, just to get all the morning jitters out of her system, Elynara shifted around until she found the most comfy seating position she could, before turning to her morning training.

The cycles flowed easily, though she didn’t push. The hum settled across her chest like warm tea, each breath drawing clean through her limbs. Cycle after cycle, she meditated the sleepiness away. Her plan wasn’t to do much today, instead taking longer breaths in and out, just feeling her muscles relax.

It was only a while later that she checked on her new max.

Two more points gained, good enough for the morning’s start.

Starpuff circled once above her head like a lazy moon, then zipped toward the window and flopped into the curtain with all the force of a particularly heavy feather.

It jolted her old memories, surprisingly. It was something utterly silly too. About how much more would a pound of feathers weigh versus a pound of metal?

She wondered how much would a pound of Starpuffs be.

The spirit in question chasing the sleepy Pufflet around now.

Suppressing a giggle at its antics, Elynara stood up, brushing hair out of her face with one hand as she slung her cloak around her shoulders with the other. Her bag clipped closed with a light snap. Boots on. Satchel checked. Staff tapped once against the floor and balanced against her palm like it belonged there.

“Alright. Let’s go.”

Mina grunted into her pillow.

Downstairs did not wait for her arrival.

The moment her foot hit the bottom stair, Rina came out of the kitchen holding an apron like a drawn weapon.

“Up at last! You have hands, so come help!”

Elynara caught it on reflex.

Before she could raise a word of protest, Finnie barreled past with a fistful of greens and a question that started with “Can spirits eat each other?” and ended with “What if you froze one first?”

“I don’t know,” Elynara said, slipping the apron over her head. “But no freezing spirits. Probably.”

“Aww. Not even as a prank?”

“Especially not then.”

Finnie made a disappointed sound and wandered off muttering about how a dessert made from spirits would taste.

Elynara shuddered at the notion. Her own curiosity paled in comparison to that of a child motivated by food.

Mina arrived moments later, eyes half-shut, robe uneven, and Pufflet clinging to the edge of her satchel like it had barely survived the stairs. Her hair stuck to one side of her face in tangled defiance of combs and good intentions. She looked like she had just lost a duel with her blanket.

“You look radiant,” Elynara said sweetly.

Mina gave her a thumbs-up without lifting her hand. Pufflet sneezed and tucked itself deeper into the crook of her arm.

Breakfast was fast and slightly chaotic. Theren appeared long enough to steal a roll, grunt out a good morning, and ruffle Elynara’s hair, to her clear displeasure.

A hug with Rina, and the wagging tails of both of them later and the man was already heading out into the fields.

Mina slid into a chair beside her and leaned over the table like gravity had claimed her entirely.

“I didn’t know mornings on farms started so early.”

“You get used to it.”

“I don’t want to.”

They ate in silence for a bit. Rina passed around mugs of tea that could double as paint remover. Someone brought in a basket of eggs that got confiscated the moment Rinnie tried to juggle them.

“Right,” Rina said, stepping back from the table with flour still stuck to her sleeve. “You’ve got fields to wander and spirits to pester. Aren will take you there. Don’t let him wander off.”

“I’m right here,” Aren called from the front door.

Rina didn’t look over.

“And you’re lucky you still are.”

She handed Elynara a fresh bread roll, one still warm from the oven. No further explanation followed.

Outside, the light had settled into a soft gold, still shy of noon. Clouds shifted lazily overhead. Damp earth clung faintly to the edges of the path. Aren leaned against the gate with a slouch that suggested his soul had gone on ahead without him.

“You’re late,” he said, voice dry.

“We’re on time. You’re just early.”

He stared flatly.

“I live here.”

Mina coughed into her sleeve to hide a laugh. Pufflet peeked from the travel pouch she had reluctantly adopted, its ears twitching like it was judging the atmosphere.

Elynara adjusted her grip on her staff and stepped forward.

“Let’s go.”

The road pulled gently ahead, soft and packed from the rain, edged in stubborn grass and small stones that clicked faintly under boot.

Starpuff zipped out ahead in loose loops, occasionally dipping low enough to stir leaves or buzz a passing branch. Aren led with an easy pace, not fast but steady, glancing back just once to make sure they were following.

Mina fell into step beside her without speaking, eyes tracking the treeline as they passed the orchard bend.

Elynara surprise-hugged the little girl before letting the rhythm of the walk settle in her bones.

The quiet didn’t need filling. The world moved around them with patient calm. A finch darted from the underbrush, wings flashing. A pair of crickets startled from a puddle. Every so often, the wind carried the faint rustle of grain from the higher fields.

By the time the shrines came into view, the light had shifted again, not quite afternoon but no longer morning. The pond shimmered just slightly under the clouded sun, water calm save for a pair of darting bugs skating its surface. The shrine stood clean and still near the edge, its stones recently swept, the small wooden charm left from their last visit still tucked near the base.

Elynara stepped forward and let her bag fall lightly to the grass. Her hand found her staff’s grip without thinking. She knelt, drew in mana, and offered it forward with a quiet breath.

The spirits came slowly.

One small, quick. The other larger, and more deliberate. They passed through the air in soft arcs, the kind that only caught at the edge of sight. To her, they were more than that now; defined not just by shape but also rhythm. A pattern of motion, a texture of presence. She let them come, let them take what they needed.

Behind her, Mina crouched with the second charged piece of wood.

“They’re curious,” Elynara said. “Try on your own now.”

Mina hesitated, then extended the offering. In a slow, careful movement.

The spirits took it. Happy to munch on the offered mana.

Elynara smiled. Of course they did, they were Starpuff’s kin, and that little troublemaker was always greedy for more!

Mina didn’t speak. Her hand didn’t waver either. She kept her eyes fixed where the spirits moved, even if she couldn’t see them. The mana in the piece dimmed slowly. When it finished, the spirits dipped once in what felt like thanks, then drifted back toward the shrine’s base.

“They liked it,” Elynara said.

Mina blinked.

“I can’t feel a thing.”

“You don’t have to.”

“Still weird.”

Elynara smiled faintly and took the wood back to recharge it once more, before giving it back to Mina.

They packed up with quiet care. Aren hadn’t moved from where he leaned against the path marker, chewing a blade of grass like it owed him money.

He nodded at them and turned down the trail again.

One shrine checked. Others to go.

The day was still young.

PoV Celestia

Another leyline collapsed.

With no cry, flame, storm, or final surge. Just a tremor through the lattice, thin and taut, then nothing. Mana had pulled too far from itself, thinned beyond breath, and slipped quietly out of reach.

No longer even able to support its own flow.

Celestia felt it pass, her heart clenching at the loss.

It wasn’t unexpected, not the first of its kind, nor even a hundredth. Made familiar through pain and repetition, flitting through her senses with familiar shape. An absence left in place of motion.

She moved at once. Her lack of hesitation borne out of having to make the same choice over and over again.

There was just no better way.

The threads nearby flared in response. She wrapped them tight, looped the breach in woven magic, and closed it off before the decay could spread. Her power flared once, burned sharp through the veil, then dimmed again to a whisper. One more seal. One more sliver of herself spent to buy time that would not last.

Time which had not lasted for centuries.

Her hands lowered from the weave, light curling back through her fingers as the lattice held. Fragile. Temporary, but still intact.

She surveyed the flow, and seeing it normalize did not linger there.

Her gaze returned to the world below.

It moved slowly in the hours she could not count, though it always moved. Cities sagged against riverbanks. Forests dulled where spirit-light once bloomed. Shrines emptied and her fellow gods slumbered.

And yet… the girl remained.

Elynara.

She observed her doings whenever the ephemeral whispers of time allowed.

Watching how, with no prophecy, title, or even a clear path forward, the once lost soul traveled and grew. Just the quiet strength of stubbornness wrapped in awkward grace guiding her. The many times she faltered. Yet every time Elynara would just brush dirt from her knees, or laugh about a miscast flame. And then just kept trying.

Kept on going.

A fine conviction to have, and one Celestia was glad to see in a girl so young.

Though… the age of her soul could likely rival her own.

Celestia watched her feed a spirit that chirped in sparks and spun like a child dancing. She watched the girl give away her time without asking for more. She watched the words fall from her lips with the kind of care people reserved for things they wanted to understand, not command.

An admirable trait, and one she had much hopes for.

In the lattice, the gods had long ceased to speak. Most threads curled inward now, fractured or silenced. The last few choosing slumber over slow withering.

They called her foolish, for hoping.

…Yet left their own hopes in her hands all the same. Made the Goddess of Creation their last Guardian. The last to still believe. And so they believed in her.

Her gaze turned back towards the latest of her little hopes.

Saw how the girl smiled.

She had found something in the morning, some small joy tucked between fieldwork and mud. Celestia could not hear the words, only the rhythm of her breath and the warmth behind her movement. Elynara spun her staff in a careless arc, corrected herself mid-turn, and nearly dropped it when a puff of light divebombed her shoulder.

Celestia’s smile was faint. But it reached her eyes.

Hope was not built in declarations.

It was coaxed.

Taught.

Earned.

Tested and tried. Over and over again.

And this girl - this small, bright thread - carried it without knowing. With no grand speech and no golden crown. Just an apprentice cloak and a heart that hadn’t quit.

Her gaze lingered.

Longer than she should have let it.

The lattice tugged again.

Another fault line stretched beneath an old mountain. The mana inside it was weak. Dull. She had maybe hours before it broke.

She turned her hand outward, fingers tightening into light.

“Please,” she whispered to the fading stars, “let her bloom before I am gone.”

Then the wind shifted.

She looked away.

And the weave followed.

The next shrine sat low beneath a ridge, half-wrapped in tall grass and wild bean vines. Elynara stepped lightly through the field, brushing the blades aside with her free hand, the staff tucked close against her shoulder. Sun filtered through the patchwork leaves overhead. The air smelled like warm stone and late bloom.

It didn’t feel forgotten here. Just quiet.

Mina came up behind her, careful with each step. She held the shaped wood like it might crack if handled wrong. Her grip had improved though, much steadier now. Not tight with nerves like yesterday.

“The roots run deeper,” Elynara said, crouching beside the shrine. “Look here.” She brushed the grass aside and pointed near the edge of the stone base. Thin shoots of green wound through the cracks, but none of them clawed or split. They followed the shape, not fought it.

Mina knelt beside her and looked closer.

“Is that because of the mana?”

“That’s a part of it.”

She reached out with a thread of her own, shaped the pull just enough to call. The spirit responded slower than the others had. It drifted from under the stone’s edge in a slow coil of shimmer, barely more than a heat-haze ripple. She kept her hand steady.

Starpuff trilled lightly overhead. The sound came with a faint, pleased vibration like a string plucked just right. Elynara ignored it.

She nudged her hand slightly forward and let the mana thread settle near the shrine’s edge. The ripple drifted closer. It didn’t touch, not fully, but the edge of its glow curved toward her palm in a slow, gentle arc.

Then it flickered.

The second one came smaller, rounder, bouncing behind it like a leaf caught in lazy wind. The way it dipped reminded her of the children at the church when they played tag and didn’t want to admit they were tired. This one felt newer.

Mina watched with quiet focus. Her posture was better this time. Balanced. Ready.

“Your turn.”

Elynara said softly, pulling her hand back.

Mina hesitated, then offered the charged wood. Her breath caught halfway through the motion, but she didn’t stop. The offering settled flat across her palm. The spirit drifted closer.

It pulsed once, though it went undetected by Mina.

Elynara took it as a good sign nonetheless.

“They’re curious.”

She leaned her weight back, letting the scene play without interruption.

The glow from the spirit dimmed slightly as it touched the mana in the wood. The draw was slower this time, like they were pacing themselves.

Which was new.

“They’re not feeding all at once,” Elynara said, watching closely. “Usually they rush. This one’s… sampling?”

Mina’s fingers curled slightly under the offering, but she held position.

“Is that good?”

She shrugged at that.

“I think so? Every spirit is just a little different.”

Starpuff had settled on a fencepost now, its green glow dimmer than before but streaked with yellow across the sides like pollen clinging to wings. It pulsed softly in rhythm with the spirit’s flickers.

“You’re syncing with them,” Elynara said under her breath.

Starpuff responded by sneezing sparks and tumbling backward off the post.

She stared at it in disbelief for a good moment, to Mina’s obvious amusement.

The second spirit circled once more, then floated back beneath the stones. The glow dimmed behind the cracks. The air settled.

Elynara knelt one last time to press her fingers against the shrine base, pouring some of her mana in. Just to mark she was there and leave a snack for the spirits.

When she rose, the leaves around her boots had perked slightly.

“Alright. Next one’s by the bend near the old scarecrow. Then we loop south.”

Mina fell in beside her.

“Do you know where they all are?”

“Not yet.” She tapped her satchel. “That’s what making the map is for.”

Aren led the way this time, his tail flicking lazily behind.

The path curved wide between furrows of corn and faded orchard lines. The ground felt firmer beneath their steps now. Mana wasn’t thick here, but it wasn’t as thin as in town either.

Elynara smiled.

It would do.

A few steps more, and the barn came into view, slouched beside a wide field with a wagon sunk half an inch into the mud out front.

Another clap landed square between her shoulder blades. This one nearly drove her forward into a watering trough.

Mina made a sound very close to a snort.

Elynara shot her a look.

Mina didn’t stop smiling. If anything, she looked delighted.

“Come on, I’ll show you the spots. Fence line’s safe, but there’s been some weird scratching near the coop. Might be nothing. Might be the boars again.”

Elynara exhaled slowly.

“Anything with tusks?”

Mina brightened.

“He’s doing the patting thing again.”

Too late.

Mina blinked.

“Great,” Elynara muttered. “Add goats to the threat list.”

Mina matched her pace easily. Pufflet hopped down to explore with mild suspicion and immediate distraction. Starpuff bobbed overhead again, clearly recovered.

“Think he’s always that loud?” Mina asked.

“I think he was holding back.”

They passed the first shrine within a minute. The stone was pale with age, moss curling at the corners, but she could see a little glowy puff peeking from behind.

“One is still here,” she murmured.

Mina watched quietly, the charged wood already in her hand without needing a prompt.

This one would be simple. The others - maybe less so.

The next shrine went much the same, another spirit floating around it, quickly emerging at seeing their mana offerings.

Elynara smiled happily at that. It was nice when things were simple.

As the farmer said, the last place was located rather far away, and they took a cart to get there.

It was little more than a sloped stack of stone where a ridge met a runoff ditch. Elynara crouched beside it, brushing dry moss from the edge with the heel of her hand.

But there was no spirit in sight. She tried searching with her mana sense, but nothing came either.

It was a dud.

When they returned and she told the news to the farmer, he scratched his beard and looked away, boots shifting in the patchy grass.

“Never figured we would lose any of ‘em. Always thought they would be here longer than I would.”

She straightened slowly, the strain in her knees barely worth mentioning.

“Some are. Two is better than none.”

The man gave her a nod and stepped back without another word. His second clap to the shoulder came lighter this time, more like reassurance than praise.

Elynara gave a small nod in return and watched him walk off toward the barn, his shoulders a little more slumped than before.

Starpuff made an undignified noise and dove nose-first into the weeds beside her, chasing after Pufflet’s tail. Mina’s companion snorted once in frustration and ran behind a fern.

They slowly made their way towards the edge of the farm, until reaching a clearing.

Across it, Mina knelt by the fence line, hands buried in a patch of young clover. Pufflet spun three distracted circles around her boot before flopping dramatically in the dirt. She didn’t laugh, but her shoulders relaxed enough to show she wanted to.

Elynara watched her for a beat longer.

The strip of charged wood still hung from the strap at her side, brushed lightly by her fingers as she stood. And she reacted to it, slightly. Not deliberately, but it showed in the way she moved.

She was getting closer.

The way her eyes tracked movement just past the edge of sight. The way her breath caught when a flicker passed through her peripheral vision.

The rhythm was starting to make sense to her.

It might not happen today, but soon.

“Alright. Time for you to head back.”

Mina stood without question, brushing off her sleeves.

Mina paused just long enough to glance back, then nodded and walked off with Pufflet trailing close behind.

She didn’t look scared anymore. Just focused.

Time to sweep the boundary in search of the errant beasts.

Just to see if anything was hiding.

Always took a little while to get used to.

Elynara didn’t wait for a charge. A single cast low across the ground caught their limbs before they could lunge, the resulting explosion taking care of the rest.

Her opponents felled, she moved on.

Near the orchard line, she stepped over a half-fallen rail and noticed a Bristleboar mid-nap.

Unlucky for it, she wasn’t a forgiving girl.

Soon after leaving a - now a little more smoky - clearing, a new threat emerged.

It was strange, and wrong. And came in fast. Its spiky body with rocky skin promising great pain if it hit.

She didn’t wait.

Her explosion probably hit a little harder than necessary. The body of the lunging monster cracked, sizzled, and dropped into the earth like stone dropped in water.

Almost completely gone, save for some soot and burned bits.

Mana throbbed at her fingertips as she steadied her stance. She held her breath, scanning the place. The forest around her stayed still.

No follow-ups. Nothing else looked to be nearby.

She rolled her shoulders back and let the spellwork drain out clean, before resuming her patrol.

The rest of it passed uneventfully, Elynara noting the herbs she was sent out to gather before, but decided weren’t worth taking.

Light filtered down through the canopy again, warmer this time.

Afternoon arrived, maybe even a little later.

She turned toward the fading trail and headed home.

The farmer met her near the barn, arms folded, boots scuffed with dust. His brow lifted in question, but he didn’t ask. Elynara gave the report in quick, clipped lines. One Bristleboar, two Bonefangs, and something sharp-eyed and fast with too many limbs and spikes. All dealt with.

His eyes narrowed at the last one.

“Didn’t see tracks for that, dunno what it would even be.”

Elynara shrugged, saying she took care of it regardless.

That earned a grunt. No questions after that.

Elynara looped a borrowed rope through the legs of the second Bonefang and dragged it behind her with stubborn determination and several quiet insults. “Still counts as strength training,” she muttered, adjusting the weight.

By the time they finished, her arms tingled with effort and the small of her back had collected a full patch of sweat. She leaned on her staff, satisfied.

Still breathing, and no pulled muscles!

“You’ve got good instincts,” he said. “No fuss. Clean work. I’ll send word to a few of the others. You’ll want to talk to Malen. Grove on the far edge of the village. Might’ve had a shrine there once.”

Elynara straightened.

He nodded.

“Yep, been there longer than anyone. Nobody builds like that anymore. Folks still bring things there. It’s a bit of a gathering place.”

She thanked him, adjusted her pack, and set off down the trail again, boots light now that the weight was gone.

By the time Rina’s barn came into view, the light had shifted soft and low, streaking orange over the fields. The house windows glowed faintly, and someone had lit the kitchen lamp. She followed the clucking.

And then paused.

The side barn had been opened, and a tide of chickens poured from it with single-minded purpose. In the middle of it all, Mina stood rigid, feed scoop held like a weapon. Feathers stuck to her sleeve. One particularly bold clucker was halfway up her leg.

Elynara stared.

Mina looked up, saw her, and narrowed her eyes like betrayal had a name.

Elynara tried very hard not to laugh.

“I didn’t do this to you,” she said.

Mina’s expression remained unconvinced.

“Probably.”

Starpuff darted overhead and trilled in what could only be described as smug endorsement. Pufflet waddled by dragging a leaf stuck to his tail and looking vaguely concussed.

Elynara rolled up her sleeves and stepped into the mess. Feed got dumped. Feathers got scattered. The cluckers were relentless but not coordinated, which gave her enough room to crouch beside Mina and start working through the troughs without getting trampled.

Mina didn’t speak, but her posture eased.

By the time the last of the feed was poured, the chickens had finally calmed enough to stop lunging. Mina stood up and dusted herself off like she had aged a year in thirty minutes.

“I hate them,” she said without heat.

“They do have opinions.”

“Very loud ones at that.”

Elynara gave her a small smile and followed her out of the barn, just as Finnie sprinted by yelling that chores were over and dinner was ready. With no follow up, gone the next second.

Dinner waited like it always did here—simple, hot, and served in large quantities.

Rina ladled thick stew into bowls while Theren quietly enjoyed the meal, and listening in as his family complained about puffed grain. Mina sat a bit slower than usual, limbs tired, but her plate was full within minutes.

They ate without rush. The warmth settled in low. Elynara finished her stew, traded plates, and nearly fell asleep before dessert arrived.

Sweetroot slices hit the table with perfect timing. Starpuff tried to steal one, but got slapped by Mina on accident, as she just so happened to reach for the same one in that exact moment. The spirit spun upward, looking theatrically offended, but completely unharmed.

Elynara barely spared a glance at its theatrics, too busy eating herself.

She leaned back in her chair and let the noise roll around her like a blanket. Familiar. Loud. Alive.

Later, in the quiet of the guest room, she pulled out her notebook and marked the confirmed sites. She left a small margin note by the weird spiky creature entry. She didn’t know its name, so she left it at just a question mark.

Her handwriting sloped. Her vision blurred slightly. She didn’t fight it.

Mina had already collapsed onto her side of the bed, one hand tangled in Pufflet’s fur. The animal let out a sigh that sounded suspiciously like a snore. Starpuff landed with a flop across both of their legs, still humming faintly from stolen sugar.

Elynara set the notebook aside and tucked the blanket around her knees.

No dreams followed.

Just the soft hush of a house at peace, the rustle of night air through the cracked window, and the gentle weight of a long day, well spent.
