Chapter 121

Now Streaming: From the Depths of Lake Erie

Opting out of the System’s God Game had worked wonders for Scott McMillen’s digestion. He had learned that peace was not a destination. Peace was a kitchen nook in Sterling Heights, Michigan, a chipped ceramic bowl full of crunchy neon sugar-bombs, and the confidence that nobody was going to kick his door in and demand he fight a lake monster before noon.

The tablet sat propped up against a napkin holder shaped like a rooster. The rooster had been a gift from his Aunt Patty, who believed every kitchen needed “a little whimsy.” That’s what was so funny about the days before the System’s arrival. Back then, small kitchen tchotchkes were more than enough whimsy. Now, System Users paraded around the streets with magic at their fingertips.

On the screen, Stephanie Wolf’s latest special loaded beneath a dramatic title card:

FROM THE DEPTHS OF LAKE ERIE

Scott took a spoonful of cereal and chewed thoughtfully.

Stephanie Wolf’s productions always sounded like the apocalypse had hired a composer and given him a cocaine budget. Deep, rumbling bass underneath a frenetic wall of sound. The title card text faded, revealing a crumbling concrete pier stretching out into the dark, churning waters of Lake Erie. The latest special from Wolf Productions promised heroic action with dramatic flair. Scott had considered becoming a heroic figure, using his new-found powers to defeat bloodthirsty monsters. Just like the protagonists from some of his favorite Shonen anime. Of course, that had been before he learned even with access to the System he wasn’t the leap-into-battle heroic type. Nope. Once a nerd, always a nerd. Now, he was just a nerd with access to magic.

He’d been living a far more relaxed life since opting out of the Game. Some people probably called that cowardice. But Scott never claimed to be brave. He was offered the chance to keep his ability to cast magic, and never have to worry about a Quest notification again. The deal had seemed too good to be true. His powers were all derived from dissecting how magic worked, and finding new ways to apply and enhance spellcasting. He didn’t need levels for that, and so happily took the deal.

Now, he was free to focus on his craft. Spellforms. Arrays. Mana channels. The blessed miracle of watching someone finally understand why they kept blowing the backs off their own spell circles.

McMillen’s Spellslinger School.

The name still made him smile.

Currently, the “school” consisted of Scott, his kitchen table, a moderately expensive webcam, and online private lessons with System Users who ranged from brilliant to “should not be trusted with a spark cantrip near curtains.”

I hope that guy’s house didn’t burn down, Scott thought while munching on another spoonful of sugary crunch, thankful for the System’s natural regeneration as he could sense it fight off the tooth decay.

And one day—hopefully one day soon—he’d have a storefront and a real school for teaching others how to use their magic. A big sign out front, somewhere in a nice suburban strip mall. Little casting rooms in back. Maybe even reinforced walls that could withstand high-level spells... Definitely reinforced walls, now that he thought about it.

He took another bite of cereal, which was starting to get a little soggy from the 1% milk. Not 2%. He was watching his numbers. The System did a lot, but it apparently did not prevent a man from staying a bit softer around the midsection. It prevented tooth decay, but couldn’t auto-grant abs. Life was so unfair sometimes…!

On screen, the special opened with a montage. Sweeping aerial shots of Lake Erie. AEA boats cutting through black water, pulling large, flat platforms towards a growing whirlpool in the center of the lake. Dramatic clips from the mass outbreak in Cleveland the night before—the one that had swept across the nation so hard even Scott had heard about it, and he did his best to avoid national news like it was cursed.

Why drown in anxiety over things completely out of his control? That was his official policy. If the world was ending, someone would text him. Probably his mother, or his cousin Mark. Then, he’d panic. Until then, he had his morning routine of sugar, coffee, and something to watch and disassociate to.

The montage kept going. Footage of flying imps. A crater in Public Square. A giant glowing fist descending from the sky. Stephanie Wolf’s voiceover, all velvet and doom, explaining how Cleveland had survived one catastrophe only to face another mere hours later from the waters of Lake Erie.

Then, there was a segment focusing on the high stakes. Two of Pegasus Guild’s Captains had given their lives during the previous outbreak. Massive losses and sacrifices from the U.S.’s top Guild.

They weren’t even meant to be there for that outbreak, he thought. Pegasus Guild had sent them in to deal with the impending outbreak in Lake Erie, and they just happened to be there. They were there and helped because it was the right thing to do. Reshma Murmu and Matthew Bruck. Two heroes.

Scott sighed through his nose.

“The exposition is always too long,” he muttered to the rooster napkin holder.

The rooster offered no rebuttal.

He scraped his spoon along the bottom of the bowl, gathering a few last survivors from the milk. On screen, the tribute to Pegasus Guild burned away into live-style footage of an AEA platform offshore, the lake rolling dark beneath a bruised sky. Municipal Guild members stood ready, interspersed among the AEA agents clad in black suits.

Then, the camera cut to a familiar figure.

Scott froze with the spoon halfway to his mouth.

A man stood on the platform wearing a cape, boots, and the kind of three-inch inseam jean shorts that looked like their barely fit over his bulging quad muscles. A pointed, wide-brimmed hat sat on his head. The man was shirtless, sporting a physique that would make any professional athlete jealous. A bright-colored fanny pack hung lazily around his waist. In his hand he had a staff… Well, wait… Was that a barbell?

Scott blinked.

“No way,” he whispered.

A graphic caption introduced this man by name.

Joseph Sullivan.

Scott pulled out his phone and checked his contacts, just to be sure.

Yup, he thought.

There was no denying it now.

The guy from the spellcaster convention.

The Muscle Mage with the barbell staff. The guy who’d pulled out an Augmentation Array like it was some common spell glyph and asked if they could put [Fly] into a staff… A barbell staff.

Scott leaned closer to the tablet, cereal forgotten.

Scott smiled despite himself.

“Well,” he said, picking up his spoon again, “this ought to be fun to watch.”

The shot cut away from the assembled Guild and AEA forces—boats, floating platforms, spellcasters in formation, armored tanks ready to absorb aggro, agents in tactical gear to support them, all arranged in a careful semicircle around the black wound churning in Lake Erie.

Then it pushed forward. Half a mile closer to the whirlpool than anyone else. There, standing alone aboard one of the AEA floating platforms, was Joseph.

The platform was small. A square of gray metal surrounded by railings and rune-marked stabilizers that glowed faintly beneath the slap of lake spray. It bobbed on the dark water like a lunch tray in a bathtub, which felt like an objectively bad place to stand when the bathtub contained an active Gate breach and something called the ‘Trench King’ according to the special’s blurb.

Joseph stood in the middle of it, a solitary figure draped in blue in the gray landscape, cape snapping behind him in the wind. He stood straight, boots planted wide and barbell staff resting across one shoulder like it weighed nothing. The wizard hat sat low on his head, brim wide enough to throw his eyes into shadow.

The camera zoomed in on Joseph’s expression. His mouth was twisted into a frown.

Gone was the confused, goofy, almost apologetic expression Scott remembered from the convention in Detroit, when Joseph had stood in front of him holding a giant barbell staff and asking, with complete sincerity, whether enchanting it to [Fly] was possible.

That guy had been ridiculous. This guy was all business.

Scott lowered his spoon into the bowl.

“Is that really the same dude?” he murmured.

The rooster napkin holder remained silent, but Scott felt it judging him for talking to kitchen decor again.

On screen, Stephanie Wolf’s voice cut in, hushed and theatrical.

“Joseph Sullivan of the Harvest Guild stands alone on the forward platform, positioned as the first line of engagement between the breach and the mainland.”

Scott let out a humorless laugh.

“First line of engagement,” he said. “That’s a fancy way of saying bait.”

The platform carrying Joseph moved forward, pushed by hidden AEA propulsion. It glided toward the whirlpool while the rest of the task force held back, ready to provide backup from what was probably considered a safe tactical distance.

The whirlpool itself was enormous. A dark spiral in the water, wide as a city block, rotating with slow, hungry force. Whitecaps foamed along its outer rim. The center was black and impossibly deep, as if Lake Erie had grown an eye and that eye was staring down into somewhere else.

Scott’s stomach tightened. He hated Gates. He had only ever stepped foot into two: his Initiation Gate and his Bronze Gate.  He hated everything about them. Especially the monsters that lived within them.

Joseph’s platform crossed into the outer turbulence. Water slapped against the metal sides. Spray exploded upward. The stabilizers flashed brighter, fighting the pull.

Joseph didn’t move.

The camera zoomed even tighter, catching the lake mist beading on his shoulders. His expression didn’t change. The shadow under his hat swallowed his eyes completely, giving him a palpable aura of the silent hero type from classic Westerns.

The water near the whirlpool bulged.

Scott leaned forward.

“Oh, that’s not good.”

The first monster surfaced with a wet, violent lurch.

It was humanoid in size and shape, but only in the way a nightmare might be humanoid if it had skimmed a biology textbook and gotten bored halfway through. It had long arms with too many joints, slick gray-green skin, and a face that looked like a fish had been forced to experience rage. Gills fluttered along its neck. Spines ran down its back. Its mouth opened wide, revealing teeth like broken glass. It stepped onto the choppy surface of the lake as though the surface of the water hid a glass platform.

Then another emerged.

And another.

Then more.

They came from the depths in a ragged swarm, breaking the surface around the whirlpool and clambering over one another through the froth. About thirty of them, each one roughly human-sized, each one distorted by sea-creature wrongness. Crab claws. Eel tails. Lamprey mouths. Barnacled shoulders. Eyes too round, too black, too empty.

The camera tracked them with perfect clarity as System-identification boxes flickered across the footage too quickly for Scott to read. He wasn’t sure if that was actually the System generating messages, or helpful editing by Wolf’s production team to help fill in System Users and Non-Users alike.

Joseph still didn’t move.

Scott realized his hand was gripping the spoon too tightly.

The monsters spread out, forming a crescent between Joseph’s platform and the whirlpool. Their bodies rose and fell with the chop, heads bobbing above the water, webbed hands scraping at the surface.

One of them shrieked. The sound came through the tablet speakers distorted and sharp, a wet metal scrape that made Scott’s teeth ache.

Joseph’s frown deepened.

Scott recognized that expression now.

It wasn’t fear. It was annoyance.

Like the man had been hoping the lake would get to the point faster.

Then, the whirlpool changed.

The entire spiral slowed for one impossibly long second.

The water at its center rose. A dome of black water swelled upward, stretching higher and higher, until gravity seemed to remember its job all at once and sheeted away from the shape beneath.

A head emerged. It was massive. A shark’s head, but twisted into something almost human by the cruel imagination of a drunk god. Black eyes set deep beneath a heavy brow. A mouth wide enough to bite a van in half. Rows of serrated teeth glistened as water poured from its jaw in rivers.

Then shoulders surfaced, followed by a torso. It was at least the height of five tall men, maybe more. Gigantic and muscular, with gray-blue skin stretched over slabs of meat that looked carved from shipwrecks. Its arms were enormous, long enough to drag through the water at its sides. Fins and jagged ridges ran along its spine. Its chest was broad, scarred, and armored in places by natural plates like sharkskin had decided to become steel. The humanoid sea monsters shrank around it by comparison, suddenly looking less like an army and more like refuse.

A dramatic caption bled onto the screen, introducing the gigantic shark-man like it was the start of a boss fight in a video game. Scott knew from watching enough of these specials that it was a generally used trope of the genre. People expected to know who all the players were, and unfortunately the boss monsters didn’t have the benefit of montages for their backstories.

NEREUS, THE TRENCH KING.

On screen, the camera held the shot.

Joseph on his little platform.

The monsters between.

The colossal shark-man rising from the depths beyond them, water pouring off him in curtains.

The thing opened its mouth.

Even through the tablet, even through Stephanie Wolf’s production filters and whatever audio correction her golems were using, the sound that came out was enormous. A roar that seemed to contain pressure and depth and hunger. The roar of something that had never known walls, never known mercy, and had decided the surface world looked chewable.

Joseph Sullivan didn’t step back.

The platform rocked beneath him. Spray hit his chest. His cape snapped sideways in the wind.

He lifted the Full Metal Staff from his shoulder.

Slowly, like a man picking up a tool. A tool he knew precisely how to use.

Scott felt a chill run along his arms.

Nereus opened his mouth, and the sound that came out was part voice, part garbage disposal choking on a whale. Scott’s tablet speakers crackled. The shark-man’s lips moved around wet, mangled consonants. Somehow, impossibly, the words were English.

Scott couldn’t tell if Stephanie Wolf’s production team was translating and using a voice actor, or if his System access was quietly doing the heavy lifting inside his skull. There were subtitles crawling along the bottom of the screen, so it could have been either.

Nereus spread his enormous arms, water pouring from the ridges along his forearms like storm drains.

“I AM NEREUS, TRENCH KING OF THE BLACK PRESSURE, LORD OF THE SUNLESS PALACE, DEVOURER OF—”

Scott squinted.

The subtitles kept going.

“—THE ABYSSAL FLOCK, BREAKER OF REEFS, SCOURGE OF THE DROWNED EMPIRES. I HAVE COME TO CLAIM THIS SURFACE WORLD IN THE NAME OF—”

“Oh my god,” Scott muttered. “He’s monologuing.” If all the media he consumed taught him anything, it was monologuing was never a good idea.

On screen, the gigantic shark-man continued, booming over the lake while the thirty sea-mutants hissed and gnashed around him like backup dancers from an aquarium-themed metal video.

He talked about conquest. About the weakness of the air-breathing world. About cities being dragged beneath the waves. About the surface learning the meaning of pressure and hunger and darkness. He laughed, a horrible booming sound that sent ripples across the water.

Then he pointed one clawed hand at Joseph.

“AND THIS IS WHAT THEY SEND BEFORE ME?” Nereus roared. “A PUNY LITTLE LAND-WALKER? A SOFT-FLESHED JESTER WITH A STICK?”

Scott looked at Joseph on the platform.

The Muscle Mage didn’t move.

Nereus kept going.

“I WILL BITE YOUR WORLD IN HALF. I WILL—”

Joseph dropped his staff.

Not dramatically, or with a flourish. He just let it fall. The Full Metal Staff hit the platform with a heavy metallic clank, and Joseph casually stepped onto it like a man stepping onto an escalator at the mall.

Scott sat up straighter.

Joseph did not wait for the giant shark-man to finish his speech.

The staff launched forward, muscled wizard man atop it. One instant Joseph was standing on the floating platform, shadowed eyes fixed on the Trench King.

The next, he was gone.

The camera jerked to follow him, and for a split second the feed blurred into blue-white streaks and lake spray.

Joseph blinked out of existence. He reappeared directly in front of Nereus’ face, bypassing the entire half-circle of sea monsters like they were traffic cones.

His body twisted upward. His fist came with him.

The uppercut landed under the shark-man’s jaw with a sound that made Scott’s spoon jump in the cereal bowl.

It wasn’t a punch noise. It was a structural failure noise.

“Ooo…!” Scott exclaimed.

Nereus’ head snapped back so hard that his whole colossal body followed. The Trench King shot upward out of the lake, arms pinwheeling, water exploding off him in sheets. For one absurd, perfect moment, the giant shark-man was airborne, lifted by a single punch from a man wearing jorts.

Scott’s mouth fell open.

On screen, Joseph hovered on the flying staff beneath him.

Then, the wizard flexed. He hit a full bodybuilding pose in midair, chest out, one arm curled, the other braced, muscles swelling beneath steam and lake mist. It was ridiculous.

Blue light gathered around him. The dense, violent light of compressed magic.

Joseph’s frown did not change, not even a flicker of emotion.

He released the spell.

A beam of blue energy screamed upward through the air, cutting through the gray sky. As it closed on Nereus, the end of the beam shaped itself into a massive fist.

Scott recognized enough spellcraft to understand exactly how unfair that was.

“That’s a projectile morphology shift,” he said, because his brain had decided to hide behind terminology instead of confronting the fact that Joseph Sullivan had just dragon-punched a kaiju shark with a fist-shaped Kamehameha blast.

The blue fist slammed into Nereus.

The Trench King exploded in a flash of light.

A huge blast of blue-white radiance cracked open across the sky like fireworks on the Fourth of July, clearing the clouds in a wide circle. Sunlight poured down through the hole.

The lake flashed silver.

The tablet feed went white for half a second, then corrected.

When the image returned, Nereus was gone.

Scott sat there, frozen.

The remaining sea monsters seemed equally confused, which was fair. Their king had spent five minutes introducing himself and approximately four seconds being converted into inspirational weather.

Then, they remembered what they had come for and in unison they shrieked and surged toward Joseph’s platform.

They didn’t make it far.

The lake around them erupted.

More figures burst from the water—humanoid creatures with cephalopod heads, slick bodies, curling tentacles, and long spears of bone and coral. They rose from beneath the waves in disciplined lines, slamming into Nereus’ forces with brutal precision.

Gateborne allies, Scott realized. The ones Stephanie Wolf had mentioned during the montage while Scott had only been half-listening. It had all been exposition, after all. Filler.

The cephalopod-headed warriors crashed into the shark-man’s army, and the whole patch of lake became violence. Tentacles wrapped around eel-necks. Coral spears punched through barnacled armor. Clawed limbs thrashed, and black blood and green blood and something that looked unfortunately like soup sprayed across the water.

Joseph didn’t stay to watch.

He turned his staff toward shore and flew away.

Just flew away. Like his job was done and he had a lunch reservation.

As he went, he looked back over his shoulder.

Then, he flexed again.

Scott made a strangled noise.

Blue spectral fists appeared above the battlefield.

Dozens of them.

They fell upon the shark-man’s army. One after another, fists rained from the sky, hammering into the sea monsters with thunderous impacts that turned water into geysers. The battlefield vanished beneath explosions of spray and blue magic. Each strike knocked Nereus’ forces sideways, broke formations, flattened monstrosities against the lake surface, and opened clean paths for the cephalopod warriors to finish the rout.

It was not a battle.

It was a scheduled demolition.

The camera pulled back, giving the audience the grand view: Joseph shrinking in the distance as he flew toward shore, cape trailing behind him, while behind him an invading army collapsed under magical artillery and angry squid people.

Stephanie Wolf’s voice came in breathless and reverent.

“What we have just witnessed may be one of the fastest neutralizations of a classified Level One Gate Threat since the arrival of the System.”

Scott stared at the tablet.

The whole thing had been over so fast his cereal was still crunchy.

He slowly lowered the spoon back into the bowl.

For several seconds, he said nothing.

Then he leaned back in his kitchen nook, blinking at the screen, and whispered to the rooster napkin holder, “I helped make that staff fly.”

The rooster, wisely, did not respond.
