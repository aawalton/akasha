‘I remember when we lived in a simpler time. Before the monsters and the demons.’

—Liao Weiji, Deputy to Lord Liao.

The morning came and with it, a flurry of activity in the Liao Clan. There wasn’t one servant who could claim to be free and unbothered. She saw silver-haired relatives helping to move things, organise servants, and get transport organised. So important was this great exodus that even the main branch of the clan had been put to work.

It would be staggered, their leaving and not everyone would leave—such would be madness; the Liao Clan must always have a presence by the Liao River.

Grandmother having agreed to wrangle the Elders meant Hua didn’t have to deal with their last-minute politicking. Hopefully, the most cantankerous of them would be sent to the middle of nowhere, burdened with a baggage train that plodded along at no speed whatsoever. Hopefully, if it took them months to reach the outlying territories. Hua knew she’d be back in three weeks at most. That would give her a period to rule over the clan unopposed. Lay down the foundation of her rule and break the entrenched position of the Shen Generation.

That was the plan. It was a nice, simple plan. Her favourite kind of plan.

There was just one thing she’d forgotten in all the chaos.

“Hua,” her sister said, stomping up to where she knelt in meditation in the temple. “You’re leaving us again.”

Song was in a particularly petulant mood. Arms crossed, fists balled. If she had any Qi, she’d surely be sparking.

“I am. This is important.”

“This is about that Weilong nonsense, isn’t it.”

“In a way it is,” Hua said gently. “There’s chaos everywhere. So much has gone so very wrong and the mortals no longer behave as they should. Everything bad you’ve heard about mortals, they’re proving true. But we still need them to till the fields and work the mines. All the things I don’t even know exist, that we need mortals to do, and they won’t do it while they think we no longer watch over them. They have forgotten the face of their lord. I am the Lord Liao now and I need to project our power and consolidate our territory. As it has always been, Liao must stand sentinel over the Amber Sea. I wish Brother was here, then I’d be able to stay. But he isn’t, and so I must go.”

“Don’t blame him for this. You’re the one choosing to leave. Brother didn’t make you do anything! He’s never been able to make you do anything. You never stay. You always leave.”

Hua felt her brows reaching her hairline. Meiling never defended their brother, not even on a good day when he smuggled in snacks they weren’t supposed to eat. If it wasn’t for the blood they shared, Hua doubted they would ever be in the same household at the same time.

I seem to fail everyone I love, she thought, never looking at the apparition in the corner. Was I cursed from the moment I was born to never be enough?

“But I always come back.”

“The last time felt didn’t. You almost didn’t. I know you say that lightning bolt wasn’t anything serious, but I saw what they did. Don’t lie to me and say it didn’t nearly kill you.”

“You’re too smart for your own good. But look, these strange eyes of mine are proof that I survived. Proof that I got stronger.”

“You’re just saying that. Stay, please. Don’t leave us again.”

“Power and authority tend to come along with this pesky thing called duty. I do this for you, for Grandmother, for every cousin who can’t fight and all the ones who will. Everything I do is to make sure you’re always safe on this hill. So yes, I have to go for a while, but I’ll be back soon.”

“What about father? He’ll be vulnerable,” Meiling said, ruthlessly bargaining with their father’s life.

“You two are strong enough to protect him. I know you are.”

“Be serious, Hua. We’re not even real Cultivators. Not like you.”

“You will be, I know it. And you won’t be alone. Elder Qiang is remaining. You like her, I know you do. But don’t reveal the full extent of what’s happened to father. And make sure you don’t cause too much trouble for her. She’s enough of an ally that I need you to behave for her.”

“I don’t care about your politicking,” Meiling snapped, crossing her arms. Her green eyes were wide, wobbly with furious tears. “You just got back. You can’t leave.”

Hua reached out and cupped her sister’s face. She still had a thick layer of baby fat despite the rigorous training they went through. It softened her features, enough so that with her eyes glistening with angry tears, Hua almost caved.

Almost.

“I’m sorry.”

“I hate you.”

“You’re allowed to. Now, where’s Song? I hope to speak to her before I leave.”

“She’s too upset with you to see you.”

“Tell her not to cry so much. She’s a big girl now.”

Meiling bit down the first thing she was about to say. She worked her jaw, worked her thoughts. “I will tell her that when she isn’t trying to punch through a boulder. Because I don’t want her to punch through my ribcage for calling her a crybaby.”

Song was hiding just outside the temple door, around the corner and behind the entrance pillars. Thinking Hua couldn’t tell she was there. Or maybe, knowing her deception pass muster but doing it anyway. Hua would respect that choice.

“Can you pass on a message for me, then?”

“No.”

“Please.”

“Fine,” Meiling bit out.

“Tell her that I do this for you so that you will always be safe on this hill you call home. Tell her... that I love you more than lightning over the mountain and the first sunrise of summer. That even if you're angry and hateful and scared, I will always return for you. And that I love you more than anything you could ever do wrong.”

“Do you really mean that?”

None of the adults in her life had ever chosen the twins, so it was up to her to raise them. Hua didn’t have the slightest idea what she was doing. She never had. But she’d seen what not to do and tried to be different from the clan members who hurt their children. She couldn’t be like the fathers who used their large hands to wail on their children or the mothers who watched in silence. Would never accept a life where she made them flinch away when they moved too quick.

It still felt like she failed each time her siblings doubted her. They deserved more, deserved better, and she did not know how to give that to them. All she knew was blood and lightning.

She drew her sister in for a tight hug and just held her for a time. “Always. Every moment of every day. If everything else in this world turns out to be false, that will stay true.”

“I really hate you.”

“Tell me all about how much you hate me when I get back, alright. Write me poems about it. Drawn me a few paintings. Make a caricature of me.” She smiled at the thought. “And then show them all to me when I return.”

When she let go, Meiling was sniffling and only the patter of small footsteps spoke of Song’s fleeing presence.

“Promise?”

“I promise.”

***

With her siblings gone, Hua could continue the work she’d been interrupted from. The task of sealing the clan compound fell to its Lord. The formations were always there, a tangle of webs residing in the back of her mind. Tied to her through blood and Qi and the five elements. She reached for them, through them, and became them. Her body distanced itself from the world and she saw more of the hill than should be possible.

Instinct guided her. No, instinct was wrong. Hua could see the echoes of those who had come before. Everyone who had borne the title of Lord before her. They guided her along the boundaries that were their clan territory, down streams and along ridges, ascending the heights of the mountain and buried deep underground. She felt the animals, heard the plodding crunch of deer exploring the mountain, the song of birds, and felt just how overburdened the anchors were.

That shouldn’t be happening, she thought, realising each of the five anchors was saturated with Qi. A vessel filled to the brim with water. There was just too much Qi for the wards to absorb properly, and much of it was overflowing across the hills.

It was beyond Hua’s ability to fix the chaos heaven had unleashed upon the world, at least right now. Later, she promised. Later, later, later, the never-ending tasks that kept piling on.

She twisted the wards in… it felt counterclockwise to Hua, but also forward and back at the same time. It meant lock and the wards reacted to her actions instantly.

The five anchors snapped into place. The energy they fed, diffuse and loose was pulled taught. The wards slammed down. Her intent directed them to gather strongest around the temple. They settled thick around the small temple. Powerful enough they might hold back an elder.

Good. The temple would be protected; the twins would have somewhere safe to retreat. It wasn’t like all the elders were leaving, just the ones Hua took the most issue with. Elder Qiang had gotten used to taking care of the twins when Hua was gone and she would remain. Just a few weeks. They had been apart longer. They would endure and Hua would return to see them whole and hale, ready to curse her out for being gone so long.

Hua ignored the prayer room containing their revered Scripture and made her way to the closed cultivation chamber. It was lit by the glowing formations carved into the ground and stone walls. They were made to keep Qi stable and stop it from leaking out into the environment.

Inside, she saw her bedridden father swaddled in many blankets at the centre of the formation. It was the only thing keeping his Qi from fleeing his body. Hua approached, letting her boots thud against the cold stone

That was the best that could be said of the Radiant Lightning Body. A legend withered, brought low by his hubris—it was not hubris, he was meant to reach the realm beyond. Hua could still see the echoes of his strength in the broadness of his shoulders and the thick barrier around his skin from his Qi. Grandmother had done something to slow down the movement of his Qi, keeping his dantian stable, but so long as it could not move then he could not fully heal. Only the passive effects like the aura all Cultivators had to protect them remained. The power in that barrier reminded Hua of being thrown into a thick wall and breaking her collarbone.

“Father, I am here,” she said softly, kneeling beside his bed. She took one of his painfully thin hands in hers and held it. Even now, it felt like one hand could engulf both of hers. “And I hope for your wisdom. Will you help me in my time of need? Can you hear me, father?”

He blinked many times. There was a lucidity to his gaze that wasn’t there prior. Hua felt hope bloom in her chest. Maybe this time—

“Jiang, my son, I see you.”

And only him. Hua swallowed whole her bitterness. Whatever else was between them, whatever difficulty lay in their relationship, she would not hold this against her father. Not for a moment. He deserved better.

And if it was a son he desired, an heir to carry his name and legacy, then Hua would become that. To honour him and all he had done for her, for the clan, she would be worthy of him.

“Father, this son goes to secure our territory. The mortals are acting foolishly and so I must make them see sense. Some have grown bold and think to command us. I will bring them to heel,” she promised. “Forgive me for I will be away for a time.”

His weak fingers tightened around her hand. “You are a good son.”

“I will make you proud, Father. I promise I will be worthy of you.”

She pressed a kiss to his shaky hands. Squeezed them gently, taking solace in the memory of the great man he had been. That was who she would remember each day she was away. The hero, the legend, the greatest of them.

“I love you, Father.”

That done, she had one last goal to deal with.

She really hoped Weiji wasn’t still angry with her. It would make her next request very awkward.
