The carriage wheels groaned through soft earth and puddled roots, each turn stirring the scent of damp moss and new growth.

Two weeks by carriage across the Lysorian spring passed by quickly, with a few days still ahead of us before we'd arrive back in Elyndor.

I’d long since learned that the climate in Lysoria and the northern reaches of Cael, where Sealrite stood, rarely shifted more than a few degrees. Still, it felt strange to see no melting snow or lingering storms in these final days of winter.

With wyverns, we could have returned alongside the Lieutenant and Carla in about a week's time. But I had grounded us from the start, refusing the quick route and ordering the long way through the hills. Not for secrecy or speed, but because the knights needed training.

The trip through the Lysorian wilderness and the occasional town on the way to Silverwater City had been too easy, in my estimation. Somehow, the beasts and monsters that should have attacked the small group of a half dozen carriages had tacitly avoided us, providing me with little to no opportunity to gauge their abilities.

Strictly speaking, both Dralos and Benedict had assured me these knights were the best Elyndor could offer, aside from those already sworn to other noble houses.

But what I’d seen on the way to Silverwater—and in the city itself—told a different story. The Alistar Territory had clearly enjoyed peace for too long. These knights were not used to real hardship. They seemed overly reliant on me, likely a result of Duke Alistar’s constant protection.

I doubted some had even seen true battle before the Hydra attack. And even then, that had been one beast, large as it was. Very few among them gave off the aura of veterans, despite their reputation. Dralos and Benedict were right to send them with me. I’d hesitated at the thought of training fledgling warriors, but the success I’d had obtaining the Cultivation Guide had put me in a generous mood. Before any real lessons, however, they needed to feel the weight of soaked armor. They needed to march until their legs collapsed, to rise at dawn for sword drills before breakfast.

The humidity of spring did little to make that any easier.

Only slightly more beautiful.

Budding trees stood silent as we passed beneath their dripping canopies, the morning rain still clinging to the leaves while the sun crept up behind the horizon. Wildflowers bloomed beside rotting stumps. The world seemed to be shaking off the soft death of winter.

Every incline was a test. Every river crossing a lesson. The knights had to carry the carriages together, dragging them through mud, over stone, and above rivers. The hopeful, peaceful expressions they’d started with had long vanished. What remained was sheer, grinding determination.

They had no choice but to endure, and I offered them no alternative.

The things we’d seen in Silverwater and the harshness of this journey were finally beginning to shape them into real knights. Still, they lacked experience. Desperately.

They lacked the understanding of fear and terror, of being in a position where you had to save yourself. The fight with the Hydra had included dozens of very powerful individuals, including me. What they needed was to save themselves in moments of true desperation.

To see that they could.

Dralos rode ahead with the scouts, his dark cloak caked with mud at the hem. Josephine sulked at the rear beside the supply wagons, muttering curses at the pollen in the air. I sat atop the lead cart, legs crossed, arms draped across my knees.

The breeze carried the scent of wet bark and flowering thistle. It did nothing to wash out the taste in my mouth. That lingering sense of being watched. Of being hunted.

No. That wasn’t right.

The message in the Silverwater mansion’s dungeon hadn’t felt threatening. It hadn't even been hostile. It had felt... like longing. Like a letter from a lover who had been waiting too long.

That left an even worse taste. I had no lost lovers, no romances. That kind of emotion had been beaten from my soul long ago.

So then, who the fuck is leaving messages like that for me?

I tapped a finger lightly against my chin thoughtfully.

The words were still etched into my memory like they had been carved into my soul. They didn’t belong to the Demon Progenitor—I was sure of that. It had already seen me in the Misty Viels. There was no reason for it to later search for me.

No, this was something else entirely.

The storage ring that held the cursed chains now hung suspended between two stone slats in the rear wagon. It was wrapped in thick linen and etched runes, yet still radiated unease. Like a sealed eye, waiting to open.

And what in all the Gods’ names are those chains made of? I’ve never seen anything like them.

They didn’t just absorb mana and energy. They seemed to twist it, to weaponize it, and turn it into something that burned through my Gold-realm body like it was nothing but dry paper.

None of the knights rode inside the carriages. Those not flanking in a loose perimeter were up front, dragging the carriages in place of any horses or mules.

Ahead, the rolling green hills gave way to a thick wall of trees. A narrow path barely the width of a carriage cut through them. We had gone around the forest on our way to Elyndor as I’d been in somewhat of a rush to get on with it.

Now, I had some time.

The knights around me stirred, their nerves and anxiety palpable in the humid air. As the carriage wheels creaked across the crumbling stone road and into the forest, a heavy hush settled over the group. I saw their faces pale. Fingers tightened around weapon hilts. Skittish eyes darted through the dim, tangled branches.

“My lady,” called the knight captain. “Should we not go around?” His name, I’d been reminded a few days ago, was Marcus.

Like the rest of the Alistar knights, the captain had golden eyes—but his were a muted shade, closer to bronze than the eerie, brilliant gold I’d seen in others. He had a solid, battle-worn build, his scars speaking of true experience rather than just discipline. Yet his face was unexpectedly soft, a strange contrast to the rest of his hardened frame. He stood about half a foot taller than me, golden armor fitted snugly over what were undoubtedly well-trained muscles.

“Continue forward, Captain,” I said dismissively, voice calm. “I trust my knights are strong enough to handle the mid-tier beasts in this forest.”

My words were only partly true. Most of the beasts in Pike Forest were low or mid-tier, but I could sense a few high-tier ones scattered throughout. That, and I didn't truly expect the knights to defeat every threat in the forest. That wasn’t the point.

This was a lesson. A test.

The captain, Marcus, nodded in recognition of my command. “Understood, duchess,” he said, before turning to bark orders at his knights.

The deeper we moved into the forest, the darker it grew. Soon, several knights summoned floating orbs of flame to illuminate the path ahead of the knights still pulling the carriages. Shadows pressed in close, curling at the edges of the firelight.

It was time to see what two dozen of Elyndor’s best knights could do when surrounded.

For a few minutes, the forest beasts lingered in the shadows, silent but not idle as they moved to encircle us. I could hear the soft shuffling of feet and the quiet rustling of foliage as unknown creatures moved cautiously through the darkness. I felt their calculating gazes on us, weighing and judging if the risk of battle was worth the taste of blood.

The knights, under Marcus' commands, responded by creating a thin circle formation around the carriages best their numbers allowed.

Then a knight stumbled. A gnarled root caught his foot, forcing him down to one knee in an attempt to break his fall. His focus broke, and the sphere of fire hovering above his shoulder winked out. Darkness rushed in like water filling a vacuum, completely consuming the knight and those around him in mere moments.

The forest struck.

Beasts roared as one, their cries merging with the startled shouts of the knights stationed in the now darkened quadrant.

Steel clanged against bone and armored hide, breaking the quiet calm of spring. The first scream came a heartbeat before a knight had the mind to ignite new spheres in place of the fallen knights', finally casting flickering light over the scene, too quickly soaked in blood. It had taken too long for one of the knights to reignite a sphere. The sweet scent of blossoms had swiftly been choked by the iron and ash of darkness.

Some of the knights threw me questioning glances. Others shot me looks of alarm or confusion.

I ignored them all.

No matter the looks they gave me—confusion, fear, silent pleas—I stayed perched atop the carriage. Leaning back, legs dangling above the driver’s bench, I watched with a neutral, unmoving expression.

Watched them struggle to survive.

Watched as the realization that no one was coming to save them crept into their eyes.

Their expressions slowly shifted even as one of the knights took a powerful blow from the club of a towering troll at the head of the forest's army. Alarm, confusion, and panic all hardened into something sharper. Grit. Will.

The troll opened its giant maw and roared, spittle cascading from it like a grotesque waterfall. It glared at the knights coming to aid their comrade, batting away stray magic and arrows with its hardened hide. Despite its great size, it was swift, using its green coloring to blend in and out of the crowd of trees.

Training took over. Movements previously sluggish with nerves and fear snapped into rhythm. Though they took some damage, the knights began to stabilize, pressing forward to reclaim lost ground as Marcus barked orders with the force of a warhorn.

He was everywhere at once, shouting formations, dragging knights out of the fray, and cutting down beasts with unflinching precision. His sword carried a thin blade with surprising strength, finding little trouble in cutting through thick hide that others seemed to struggle to pierce.

He’s good, I thought. Even without his commands, he was keeping the line from breaking through sheer skill alone. His core strength was somewhat lacking, but his technique? Razor sharp. Every strike, every step, calculated and clean.

And he did it all while watching the battlefield and giving split-second commands that slowly continued to swing the tempo of the fight back in their favor.

Could I keep up with him in a duel of pure technique? I wondered.

I wasn’t sure.

I’d have to spar him after this. Find out where he trained. Who molded that control into him. Even if Marcus's core wasn't where it needed to be, he was nearing qualifications to be a true knight of mine. If he was this strong, what about his master?

I observed how most of the knights relied more on heart energy than mana. Only a few mage-knights lingered near the center, casting support spells from a safe distance.

Most of their affinities were predictably earth and fire, with only a scattering of water and air. Two had wood affinities and fought closest to Marcus, drawing strength from the forest around them. They took point with Marcus, using the environment's connection with their wood-affinity to control the monster swarm and delay the approach of new beasts.

I kept watching, hoping for something rare. Something unexpected.

Nothing yet.

A moment later, I felt a shift in the natural mana before I saw it.

A presence, a flicker of malice, cut through the chaos.

My gaze snapped to Caleb, one of the knights who’d carried Raphael. He was locked in combat, shield raised against a pair of snarling wolves.

He'd completely missed the third.

All three of the wolf-creatures had fur that rippled like charcoal black smoke laced with streaks of silverlight that shimmered like shifting constellations. Their movements were silent, predatory, too fluid for creatures of mere flesh. Their eyes glowed with the same cold, luminescent blue. There was no warmth or empathy in them, only hunger.

The third, unseen wolf stalked through the trees like a phantom, limbs and claws the color of deepest shadow, slipping in and out of existence as if it moved between this world and something darker. Heart energy clung to the third wolf, manipulating the darkness and shadows with purpose.

Where is that heart energy coming from, I wondered. It felt distinctly human. Not monster.

The shadow wolf closed in on Caleb without a sound and lunged from the darkness, silent as mist. One of the other wolves had its fangs clutched tightly to the knight's shield, while the other lunged at his legs.

Even if he could react to the two in front of him, he wouldn't be able to answer the third wolf.

The hidden wolf’s claws were inches from Caleb’s stomach when my heart core flared to life. A thin veil of Authority surged forth. It was subtle, nearly invisible, and would be too faint for the others to sense. But it was enough to twist the angle of the wolf's strike by a fraction.

The claws still found flesh, raking deep across his side. The claws were shifted just enough to miss the gut. Miss the kill.

Caleb screamed, his pain contorting as a river of thick red spilled freely from just above his hip. Still, he kept his shield up, just long enough for another knight in golden armor to come to his aid, swinging a massive claymore to cut the head of the wolf, keeping Caleb's shield stationary.

I pulled my Authority back and watched, again uncaring .

Caleb had made a mistake.

And mistakes always demanded a price. His price today was blood, so that next time it wouldn't be death.
