Chapter 117

"Warden," Nikola said.

Riley turned and looked at him.

"There is an option in the forge to fabricate a catapult. It sits at the bottom of the siege list. I ran the list last week when I was assembling projections."

"I know."

"The requirements are steep but not out of reach. If we time it against the mine's next intake we could begin the build within the tenday."

"I know that too."

Nikola waited.

She turned so her back was to the silo and she could look at both of them at once.

"I don't want the system's catapult," she said. "I want one you build. The catapult the interface fabricates fires a projectile the system chooses. The projectile is fixed. It is a stone. You cannot swap it. You cannot alter what comes out of the arm and you cannot alter how it comes out. I need a machine that fires what I want it to fire. And I have an idea about how to make it more precise than the stock version would give us."

Nikola's head inclined a fraction. Landryn had shifted his weight and watched her with a puzzled look on his face.

"You've sketched it," Landryn said.

"I've sketched it. You'll have it before I leave for the depot. Use it as a base. If you find better on your own, tell me and we'll change the base."

"Alright."

He rubbed a hand along his jaw. There was dust in the crease of his knuckles from earlier and he had not lost it. He glanced at Nikola once and then back at her.

"Warden," he said.

"Go on."

"A catapult is a siege weapon."

"It is."

"Are we attacking with this."

"No."

Neither of them said anything for a beat. Landryn's face had begun the shape of a question and had stopped short of finishing it. Nikola's face had not moved at all, but she could tell from the small change in the angle of his shoulders that he was working through the same shape internally and coming out the same side Landryn had.

"Defending then," Landryn asked.

"Defending."

Both men stood waiting for an explanation that didn’t seem to be coming.

Finally, Landryn responded. "Alright, Warden."

She left them to it.

Landryn had already started saying something to Nikola about the north side of the cavern and about where a fourth silo might go with adequate placement. Nikola was answering in his usual short sentences. The sound of it followed her up the shaft for a stretch and then thinned.

The mine went up in a long shallow rise. The lamps ran ahead of her at Landryn's spacing. She could feel the temperature climb as she moved toward the mouth. The cool wet smell of the saltpetre wall thinned out of her nose and the older, drier smell of the yard and the outside began to come down toward her.

She reached the mouth.

The light stopped her for a moment. The sun was still up. She had lost the sense of it inside.

She stepped out into the yard and nearly put her shoulder into Zelgra.

Zelgra was carrying the front half of a wolf across both arms. Thorne was three steps behind her with the back half slung over one shoulder and the neck of a second animal knotted around his fist. Both of them were black with blood to the elbows and beyond. The wolves were bigger than she had been ready for. Ridge-backed animals with heavy jaws and grey fur gone dark and matted where it had soaked through. The smell came off them in a wave.

Zelgra saw her and grinned.

"Warden," she said. "The spoils of the hunt."

She let the front half of the wolf go. It dropped in the dust at Riley's feet with a wet weight. A puff of dry dust came up around it and settled again.

"That's a lot of wolf," Riley said.

"Two wolves ready for the tonight feast.”

Thorne came up beside her and set his own load down more carefully, though the second wolf's head still hit the ground with a thud. He straightened. His hair had come partly out of the tie he wore it in and there was blood along the line of his jaw. He wiped his hand along his trouser leg and left a wide stain that added to the others.

"It went well," he said. Then, after the smallest pause, "It was strangely eventful."

Riley looked at him.

He did not look back at her the way he would have if he had meant nothing by it.

Zelgra was still grinning.

"How eventful," Riley said.

Thorne considered her a moment. He glanced at Zelgra, who was still smiling, and then back.

"Well," he said, "we should have these wolves processed before we lose the light. The pelts are worth more than the meat and neither will keep out here."

Riley narrowed her eyes at him.

He did not add anything to it.

"Right," she said.

She was not going to press him where Zelgra could hear it, and Thorne knew she wouldn’t.

"When you're done with those," she said, "I need you to scout the depot. The prisoners gave us a line of country I want you to ride. Report back with what you see.”

"Understood."

Zelgra shouldered her half of the wolf again with a small grunt walked off. Thorne bent for his. He paused before he lifted and look Riley in the face.

"It went very well, Warden," he said.

He picked the wolf up and followed Zelgra across the yard.

Riley stood for a moment and let dust settled where the wolf had laid on the ground. There was a wet mark in the ground the color of old rust.

Strangely eventful.

She filed it away and started for the workshop.

The clay yard sat on the south bank of the river outside the walls, where the good clay ran in a band along the water. Nami had picked her ground so the wind off the river came down the length of the yard and took the smoke from the firing pit with it, and the smoke did not gather where the wheels were working.

Nami had a length of clay on the wheel and both her hands wet to the wrist. Her hair was tied up off her neck. Two of the older Hoshin women were working the second wheel across from her. A boy was cutting the wire on trimmed forms and setting them out on the drying board. The firing pit was low and steady in the middle of the yard with the racks of small pots along its edge.

Mali was at the stacking end.

She was small enough that the cooled pots came up to her belly when she was stacking them on the low shelf, and she had made a game of it. She would take one pot in each hand and carry the two of them together the four steps from the cooling rack to the shelf and place them down side by side. If she managed it without either one clinking against the other, she would do it again the same way. If one clinked, she would slow down for the next trip.

Two Hoshin men came by to take a full tray of the fired pots for the next step at the powder shed. One of them ruffled her hair with a hand that smelled of clay and rosemary. Then the men left singing an old Hoshin song to pass the time, leaving her to continue her game.

She lifted the next pot from the cooling rack with both hands. It was one of the smaller ones. Round belly, narrow neck, the kind Nami had been shaping in dozens for the last three days. It was still warm on the outside from the earlier firing though the inside had gone cool.

She turned toward the shelf and grabbed one pots. She started to place it on her flattened hand when something caught her attention. A sound in the distance, maybe it was an animal or nothing at all but the pot slipped from her hand and struct a stone on the ground, leaving it in two pieces.

Not shards, it was two halves. The pot laid on the ground and Mali stood very still.

She looked up along the path where the Hoshin men had gone. They were nowhere. She looked back toward the wheel. Nami's back was to her and Nami was working the clay with both hands and did not turn.

Mali crouched down.

She sat back on her heels and then she sat all the way down on the ground with her legs folded under her. She took one half of the pot in each hand. The edges were sharp along the break. She turned them and fit them together the way they had been. They matched, like perfect puzzle pieces.

She held them like that a moment. Both hands cupped, one half against the other.

She closed her eyes.

The wheel made its low round sound and one of the older women said something to the other in a voice too soft to carry. The fire pit hissed once as a drop of resin came out of a piece of firewood. Then yard went quiet.

The wind lifted. Not much. A little. It came around the shoulder of the ridge the way it always did and slid down over the fold in the ground, and the leaves in the low trees along the west edge of the yard began to move against one another. Softly at first. Then a little more. A dry rustling sound that went through the yard from the west side to the east side and did not stop.

From up the path Nami's voice came.

"Mali."

Not loudly, just projected. Nami called when she was working and did not want to lift her voice more than she had to, the way a mother calls her young child as not to startler them.

"Mali."

Closer now. Nami had come off the wheel.

The leaves went on rustling. Mali's hands were closed around the two halves of the pot.

"Mali, where have you gotten to."

The wind eased. The leaves settled back down against each other and went quiet the way leaves do when the wind has passed.

Mali opened her eyes.

The two halves were one. The pot lay in her cupped hands the way it had been before she had picked it up off the cooling rack, round belly and narrow neck, and the seam was solid again. She turned it slowly in her fingers. The wall of it was smooth. The place where the stone had broken the pot was smooth.

The pot tipped gently against the heel of her hand.

She set it down on the rack.

Nami came around the edge of the drying board with a wet cloth in one hand and clay to the elbows.

"There you are."

Mali looked up.

"I called you twice."

Mali reached down and picked up the pot. She stood, and she held it out.

Nami looked at it a moment, and then she took it. She turned it in her hands the way a potter turns a pot, checking the neck, checking the belly, checking the base. She nodded.

"Good stacking, love. That's the last of the small ones from the morning. Come and help me at the wheel.”

Mali smiled.

She followed her mother across the yard.
