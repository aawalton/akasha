Devastating news of Matrazere reached the advancing armies of the allied forces, as reported by distant observers and scouts only. Their worst fears were realized: an undead transformation had taken place, ensnaring vast hordes of people, and greatly adding to troop numbers, as the majority of ‘support personnel’ had become undead along with the soldiers. Camp aides, servants, townsfolk… none were spared.

Almost none. Some didn’t seem to take and were captured or killed. Plenty kept inside the fortress also avoided the dreadful fate, of course.

Something different happened with women affected by the transformation. Why it was different wasn’t clear, but instead of becoming walking corpses with quickly graying skin, they quickly turned white, their eyes glowed red, and they began floating around ominously, perhaps in some sort of pantomime of a stereotypical witch. Each had long, sharp talons grown out from their fingers, pronounced canines, and a stretched mouth, the latter indicating powerful biting capability.

Some sort of ancient peculiarity. This spellcrafted curse has been waiting around for a long time.

When Ash was consulted, she called them banshees. That sounded really bad, but Ash claimed the power of their death wails only came over long periods of time. At this stage, it was ‘merely’ terror-inducing and rattling. An awful debuff, but not lethal. Otherwise, they were fast, magic-resistant flyers who’d try to get in melee, ultimately similar to gargoyles. Unlike gargoyles, one had to hit their brain. They’d literally float around as heads even if decapitated.

It had the strong practical bonus of massively reinforcing the Dominion’s flying forces. There would be a meat wall in the air as well as on the ground. The ground forces did not quite appear mindless, either. The troops… moved less, held in eerie calm, but they seemed to retain discipline. Weapon-use. Did not talk nor seem to need it. Obviously, they wouldn’t need food, drink, or sleep, either.

Perfect soldiers, essentially.

Samantha mentally contacted Palth immediately with the news. He didn’t seem surprised. “It was doomed to be,” he said, “when I failed to stop it myself. They became paranoid of leaks, unwilling to wait or risk interference, impossible though it might’ve been with the power leveraged through the soil, blood, and bone of that place. Without the presumed element of surprise, they had every reason to hasten this travesty. All that is left is to put them to rest and punish the masters who abused them.”

“We will, Palth,” Samantha said with cold determination. “I promise you. You will see the ultimate absolution possible when I break the unbroken and trample its dust with spite.”

After a long pause, he replied, “I think Palth must die, now. I held to certain hopes… to rally those left, against… Well. It doesn’t matter now. I take your naming in full, now, Your Majesty. I am Odysseus. May it be a name made into a curse, to my former masters.”

“As you wish.”

Samantha sent out word to all leaders that they’d be facing undead, and to prepare accordingly. All novelties, ideas, and suggestions for advantage with this new dynamic were welcome.

This got a quick response from a unique group: The Elven Conflux. This was a fairly new sub-organization bundling a great variety of elves together — something they didn’t normally particularly like, but the realities of a massive war camp on the move had gradually convinced them they preferred the ‘tweaking’ of complete autonomy. Groups were naturally gravitating toward each other, some intermingling as cousins never encountered, or not in aeons, at least, separated by distance and customs of supreme isolation.

More specifically, ‘Aodhan’ requested an audience to offer ‘assistance.’

Samantha arranged for it in the command tent when the armies were camped. The tent was a copy of fifty others in camp, all with the same exact external protections, and no leader arrived directly through flaps, but via mirror-walking in less-assuming locations. As such, when a command meeting was in session, there was no convenient, obvious target to bomb. The enemy might hit a tent full of lesser-ranked officers, perhaps, as the tents were rarely empty, but it was essentially never easy to locate her most valuable generals.

Samantha, Redberry, Zadkiel, Dax, Bast, Canmore, Azure, Linnore, Eupraxia, Michael, Aramis, the Cat Sith Pythia and Merlin, and a few others waited in the tent, as they’d been advised to give the elves a very honorable reception for anything in which they requested an audience. The word ‘assistance’ didn’t translate very well from Elven, which was commonly understated, with possibly undetectable sarcasm. An elf could say ‘an unfortunate scenario ensued’ with solemn seriousness and actually mean genocide. This was something big.

Aodhan and six other elves arrived, impeccably and gorgeously dressed as always for ‘true elves,’ as Samantha secretly thought of them. Most elves were a kind of ‘new generation’ derived from Redberry. Though she’d long forgotten the exact details, they were newer fae-kin, with smaller percentages of the older blood. These elves before her were ancients themselves, likely with ten times or more forgotten epic accomplishments than remembered. The antler-headed Aodhan Fleeting Moss was an unusual bridge-maker, being a Redberry Druidic Priest and then a Pantheon Priest. Most elves of his stature didn’t have anything in particular to do with Redberry.

The elves were also followed by an unusual sight: a walking skeleton, bare, without weapons or clothing.

Everyone stood. There was surprisingly little ceremony. The elves bowed, and the group bowed back to them, then took their seats. Aodhan stepped forward, smiling easily as he swept a hand. “Quite the reception you’ve made for us, despite the humble nature of the march. You have our thanks, noble deities, lieges, and generals of great repute.” His eyes flashed over to Azure. “A pleasure to see you again, as well, Azure.”

Hmm, was that an Elven dig?

Azure scrunched her face briefly, clearly amused at the friendly jab. “I’m reserving judgment in saying the same, Aodhan.”

“We thank you for coming,” Samantha said, “though I withhold my thanks to the skeleton.”

A few of the elves smiled at that. Aodhan chuckled and raised an eyebrow, asking, “Aren’t you worried you insult a solemn ancestor?”

“No,” Samantha said confidently, glancing at the skeleton. “I may not be an expert on elves, but I doubt your kind would do that, nor would you be likely to bring them unadorned, without honor, if you might. Aside from that? It didn’t even bow. Must be a human skeleton — it lacks instinctual humility.”

That got a couple of chuckles.

Aodhan dipped his head to her. “Shrewd, correct, and entertaining, as expected, Your Majesty. It’s here merely for demonstration. We’d like to offer the assistance of our specialists against the undead. Extending to times long forgotten has been a tradition, waning but surviving, to prepare for the perpetually expected… hmm.” He frowned and squinted. “There’s no way to put it without sounding ridiculous, is there? We prepared for an undead apocalypse that constantly threatens us.”

Samantha sighed and nodded. “Trust me, Aodhan, regardless of how it sounds, I am soberly aware of that threat.”

Zadkiel shrugged and grinned. “I’ve seen enough ridiculous things with my own eyes in the world that still came close to ending me. I’ll belittle no paranoia, reckless as I choose to be.”

Eupraxia nodded. “Those of us formerly of Traesh well understand the danger, too, with a source so close. Our own preparations had to err less specialized, however.”

“Indeed,” Aodhan said. “I feel relieved to hear your understanding. Very well. To combat this potentiality, and in consideration of the vulnerability of deities to our dread enemy, we sought more arcane specialization as proof compared to the fickleness of the spiritual world. The undead are the antithesis of spirit. An inversion of sorts. A special energy is required for their vulnerability, especially when there is nothing inherently divine in it. The means and details of invention are lost, and true ingenuity is dead, but the results and tradition still exist. We call this energy the Sacred Radiance, for it lends the needed seed of divinity through ancestral ire toward the unnatural.”

Without prompting, an elf lady elegantly extended a bejeweled hand out toward the skeleton, and a sun-like ray flashed from her palm briefly, streaking across the distance and enveloping the skeleton’s arm. The arm disintegrated into dust, and the elf lady dropped her hand and bowed once.

“Leiande is our foremost specialist of this tradition,” Aodhan continued. “She and her order offer you their unique services, and lay down their lives in understanding the priority threat they are likely to become when utilized in the days ahead. The Conflux as a whole asks you to please preserve them if possible, noble leaders, so this tradition does not die with them.”

Aodhan bowed very low and then stepped back amongst the others.

Leiande stepped forward and bowed, then looked up to meet the eyes of the seated leaders in a sweep, wearing a faint smile. “Aodhan and the Conflux consensus choose to exaggerate slightly. We do not fear death, and we know the importance ahead has come to dwarf the fears that once drove us. Fears that drove me, certainly, though that distant time is now like the fog of Limbo itself, nearly blank in the glare of the Sage’s scouring.

“I must confess to stand before you heretofore unminted by this war, reclused, pessimistic, and apathetic to the plights and purposes for which it came to be. I must confess myself a foolish, one-time fierce hater of the gods, for the failures of those days and the losses forever crushing my heart. It is only for the magnitude and intensity of the success of these armies that I was convinced, now, in the eleventh hour, having denied call after call.”

Her face showed a grieved apology as she gazed at them, hands clasped together in front of her, and her eyes welling with tears. Though there must be a little show to it, her words came with intensifying passion. “Forgive me. On the bandwagon, now, I jump, shamed for my delay! It is clear that, despite my prejudices, it was deity that made a path, a march, a charge toward salvation. How much sooner could it have started, if we were not stubborn for allies? How much less suffering would there be if our fear did not rule us? So little we accomplished, when the gods, aligned anew, leading as only they can, smashed the enemy to tatters and now threaten their greatest fortress! Shame on us all!”

This hung over the meeting with a solemn silence enforced by the aura of her and her passionate words. The other elves were stone-faced.

Leiande bowed her head, closed her eyes, and swallowed. Finally, she took a deep breath. “I deserve no answer; let action show our contrition. Please allow the remainder of us, who hold the title of [Sacred Radiancer], to serve in utmost effectiveness, regardless of the danger.” She pulled forth an object of four thin gold chains, hanging from an ornate handle, suspending a golden sphere. It was flagrantly magical. The metal twisted and opened itself, revealing a bright, warm radiance that filled the tent.

As soon as it touched the skeleton, it disintegrated into nothing. It extended and crowded the borders of the command tent, as if it wanted to expand much further beyond.

Holding the artifact up high, Leiande declared, “We shall make a path, too, Your Majesties. Through our ancient enemies, now reduced to mere corrupted meat… and presaging in their final remains what will become of their treacherous leaders’ precious tower.”
