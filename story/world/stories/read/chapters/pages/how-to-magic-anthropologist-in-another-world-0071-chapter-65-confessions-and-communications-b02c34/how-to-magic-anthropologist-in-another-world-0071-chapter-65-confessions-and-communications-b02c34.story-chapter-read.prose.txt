The team sat around the small fire inside the sanctified camp as night finally settled over the dead zone.

The light of the flames flickered across tired faces. Beyond the circle of sanctified ground, the darkness of the dead zone pressed in, quiet but never truly peaceful, as the undead gathered on the horizon in their never-ending march toward Ectamal.

Raik sat with his arms resting on his knees, staring directly at Calr.

Calr sat across the fire from him, shoulders slightly hunched, eyes fixed on the ground.

Kan noticed the tension first. She opened her mouth as if about to say something, then thought better of it and closed it again.

The silence stretched long enough to become uncomfortable.

I decided to break it.

“First things first. Let’s start by catching up.”

Everyone looked at me.

“I was sun-drying myself next to Vena when I got put to sleep with dreamer magic. By the time I noticed the feathers, I was already falling asleep. When I woke up, you three were already awake.”

I gestured toward Kan, Raik, and Calr.

Raik’s eyes flicked briefly toward Calr again.

“Somehow Calr is immune to dreamer magic,” he said. Suspicion crept into his voice.

Ah, shit. Only I knew about Calr’s secret.

I needed to back him up.

“That is good, isn’t it?” I said, looking straight at Raik. “Otherwise, we all would have died in our sleep.”

Raik rubbed his jaw and nodded slowly.

“I know,” he said. “You are right. I just do not like secrets on my team, and he was hiding something big.”

“Well, some secrets are not yours to know,” I replied. “Especially if they have nothing to do with you.”

Raik opened his mouth to argue.

“It is okay, Alice,” Calr said quietly.

His voice sounded distant for a moment. His eyes unfocused slightly, fogging over as if he were staring somewhere far away.

Then they returned to normal.

“I think it is fine if I share with the team.”

“Are you sure?” I asked immediately. “You do not have to do it.”

Calr looked around the circle of firelight. His gaze lingered briefly on each of us before settling again on the flames.

“You have to offer trust to make friends,” he said. “And right now, I am in need of friends.”

He paused.

“One of the enemies already knows my secret. Since you know as well, a secret shared with three people is no longer a secret.”

He let out a long sigh.

“I am a descendant of the ancient telepaths.”

The fire cracked softly.

No one else spoke as he continued.

“Through some kind of ritual magic, Saint Sarah the Wise created my bloodline. She brought my kind back from extinction.”

Raik blinked twice.

Then he facepalmed.

“That is far too big a secret to be sharing so casually,” he groaned. “I am sorry I even asked. You should have brushed me off.”

“What is the big deal?” Katar said with a shrug. “The telepaths were not that strong to begin with.”

Ja’a turned toward him slowly.

“Are you serious, you glorified pair of scissors?”

Katar frowned.

“What?”

Ja’a pointed at Calr.

“Feed him to a strong enough monster, and you might get a Dravac. The SoulScribes would also want to breed him for his mind.”

Vena looked horrified.

“The pantheon of mythic gods might try to sacrifice him,” she added quietly, “to revive the Silent One, god of travel.”

Katar blinked.

“Oh.”

Kan leaned forward, resting her elbows on her knees as she studied Calr more closely.

“Why are you telling us all this?” she asked.

“Because I trust you,” Calr replied simply.

Kan blinked in surprise.

He shifted slightly and then looked directly at Raik.

“You are the one I am worried about.”

Raik raised an eyebrow.

Calr sighed.

“Bloodline nobles do not have the best reputation when it comes to my kind.”

He hesitated before continuing.

“Isn’t the soul of one of my ancestors powering the Satori Blade held by General Kiddo?”

Raik grimaced.

“You know about that?”

Calr nodded slowly. Raik rubbed his temples.

“I would love it if people judged me more by my actions rather than my peers’,” he sighed.

“As for the Satori Blade,” Raik added slowly, “it indeed contains the soul of an ancient teleporter.”

Vena frowned slightly at the talk of soul magic, but she did not interrupt.

“It is not some nefarious thing,” Raik continued. “From what I know, the Satori patriarch was a descendant of the Silent One. He willingly gave his soul to create the blade.”

Calr’s brow furrowed slightly.

“The bloodline became diluted over time,” Raik continued. “They started losing the ability to teleport and to create portals. So they sealed the souls of their elders who were born with the gift into the blade.”

He shrugged.

“At least that is what the Agame clan histories say.”

“We are getting off topic,” Vena said gently.

Everyone looked back toward Calr.

“Yes, I am kin to the telepaths, but calling me a telepath is actually a misnomer,” he said. “I do not have any telepathic abilities.”

That made everyone lean forward slightly.

“I call what I have Analyse.”

He tapped the side of his head.

“I have perfect memory and the ability to recall and cross-reference everything I know in a fraction of a second.”

Ja’a let out a low whistle.

“That means I do not need to be told most things,” Calr continued. “Give me a few hints, and I can usually reach the correct conclusion.”

Kan stared at him.

“That is terrifying.”

Vena tilted her head slightly.

“And you only told Alice about your secret. Why?” she asked slowly, being his childhood friend, she must have felt slightly betrayed that I knew before her.

“He did not tell me,” I said immediately.

I leaned back slightly and crossed my arms with a smug grin.

“I figured it out.”

Vena stared at me.

“You did what?”

I shrugged.

“It turns out I am pretty good at unearthing secrets.”

Across the fire, Shingo tilted his massive head slightly, clearly following the conversation even if he could not contribute.

Calr groaned quietly and covered his face with one hand.

“I hide my power so well, only for her to figure me out because of this.” He pointed at his blood-red hair. “Apparently, my ancestor had this shade of hair.”

“How did this power help you resist the dreamer magic?” asked Raik.

Calr blushed slightly for some reason.

“Well… the shadows were wrong.”

“What do you mean?” Ja’a asked, clearly confused.

Calr shifted a little under everyone’s attention.

“In the dream, I was talking with Alice,” he said slowly, choosing his words carefully. “But her shadow was wrong. Wrong for that time of day, I mean.”

Kan frowned.

“You noticed a shadow?”

Calr nodded.

“It was pointing the wrong way slightly.”

Ja’a blinked.

“And just like that, you knew you were dreaming?”

“No,” Calr said, shaking his head. “I knew it was not my dream.”

The group stared at him.

“It was someone else’s dream,” he continued. “I do not dream of wrong shadows.”

He sounded oddly offended at the idea.

Ja’a stared at him for a moment before turning toward me.

“You see the problem with you smart people? Half the time, you make no sense when you are talking.”

I tried not to laugh.

It actually made sense when you thought about it.

The dream we were having might have been shaped by our imagination, but the space we were dreaming in belonged to the dreamer, like a game engine running a simulation.

The dreamer’s mind controlled the rules of physics.

And apparently, that dreamer had made a mistake when calculating the direction of shadows.

I kept that thought to myself.

“That is not that strange,” Calr said. “The shadow angle was wrong by a few degrees. It was as if we were farther north.”

Raik leaned forward a little.

“So you noticed something impossible,” he said slowly, “and that told you the dream did not belong to you.”

Calr nodded.

Vena tilted her head slightly.

“Is that something your analytical ability does automatically?” she asked. “Or did you have to concentrate?”

Calr thought about it for a moment.

“It is difficult to explain,” he said. “My mind constantly checks things against what I know. When something does not match, it becomes… uncomfortable.”

“Uncomfortable?” Vena repeated.

“Yes.”

He tapped his temple again.

“Imagine remembering exactly where every object in a room should be. Then suddenly, a chair moves without anyone touching it.”

Ja’a nodded slowly.

“Your brain would scream that something is wrong.”

“Exactly.”

Raik rubbed his chin thoughtfully.

“So the dreamer made a mistake.”

“Yes,” Calr said. “A small one, but enough.”

Katar leaned back on his hands.

“That still does not explain why you woke up before the rest of us.” The swordsman was probably salty that he missed the fight.

Calr hesitated.

“Once I realized it was not my dream, I stopped following it.”

Kan raised an eyebrow.

“You can just do that?”

Calr shrugged slightly.

“I stopped interacting with it and bit my tongue hard enough to wake up.”

Ja’a let out a low whistle.

“That is actually impressive.”

Vena nodded slowly.

I was impressed as well. I had to do a lot more to wake myself up.

“So what happened next?” I asked.

Calr shifted slightly, looking uncomfortable under everyone’s attention.

“Well… I tried to sneak toward Raik to wake him up,” he said. “But their soul seer noticed me and started shooting at me.”

Calr scratched the back of his head.

“I had to deflect one of her arrows toward Raik to wake him up,” he admitted sheepishly.

Raik snorted quietly, rubbing a spot on his chest as if remembering the moment.

“That explains the bruise.”

Katar leaned back with a grin.

“Why is their soul seer more capable than ours?” he asked mockingly.

Ja’a immediately sat up straighter.

“It is not my fault she outranged me,” she said defensively.

Katar waved a dismissive hand.

“I was talking about her being a combatant and you being a civilian.”

Ja’a frowned.

“Well, why would I be a combatant when I have you for that?”

“That is the first smart thing you said all day,” Katar replied.

Raik sighed and raised a hand.

“That is enough.”

Both of them stopped talking.

Raik turned back toward Calr.

“After that, something strange happened,” he said slowly. “Between you and the other red-haired girl.”

Calr nodded.

“Yes. The other girl was a telepath.”

Kan leaned forward.

“A telepath like you?”

Calr shook his head.

“Yes, but a true telepath. The kind that can read minds.”

That made everyone shift slightly.

“Apparently, she followed us here looking for Alice,” Calr added.

“For me?” I asked, surprised.

Calr nodded.

“She tried spying on you earlier in Ectamel,” he explained. “When she entered your mind, she heard you thinking to yourself, ‘Oh, this girl has red hair. I wonder if she is a telepath.’”

Everyone slowly turned toward me.

“Oh,” I said.

I rubbed the back of my neck.

“Sorry about that.”

Ja’a burst out laughing.

“So you basically announced her existence inside your own head?”

“How would I know when someone is listening?” I protested.

Calr gave a small shrug.

“I think it worked out for the best in the end,” he said.

Raik raised an eyebrow.

“How so?”

Calr hesitated for a moment before continuing.

“Well… once our eyes met, we formed a two-node hive mind.”

Kan blinked.

“A what?”

“A hive mind,” Calr repeated. “Minds connected together. That is how my ancestry used to operate.”

He tapped his temple again.

“We started viewing each other’s memories.”

Ja’a slowly leaned back.

“That sounds deeply uncomfortable.”

“It was,” Calr said.

His voice grew quieter.

“I could see everything. Her childhood, her training, and the way Donas raised them.”

He paused.

“The things he did to them.”

The fire crackled softly in the silence that followed.

Calr stared into the flames.

“The environment they grew up in was… horrifying,” he said. “Children were forced to compete for food. There were rewards, punishments, and constant manipulation.”

He swallowed.

“I tried to break the hold Donas had on her mind. The grooming.”

He looked at me when he said grooming. He was using the word I had taught him earlier.

“You managed to do that inside the hive mind?” I asked.

“Not completely,” Calr said. “But I showed her things. I showed her how Donas’ kindness was staged, how he created suffering just so he could pretend to save them.”

Kan shook her head slowly.

“That is twisted.”

Vena looked ready to go to war.

I felt a knot forming in my stomach.

For the first time that day, I felt something close to sympathy for our enemies.

If you grow up in a place like that, what kind of person are you supposed to become?

Raik broke the silence.

“That is why you did not shoot them with your crossbow?” he asked.

Calr nodded.

He stared at the ground.

“I had memories of growing up with them,” he said quietly. “Their hunger, their fear, and their childhood.”

He rubbed his eyes.

“I could not do it.”

He looked up again.

“Sorry.”

Kan leaned back and crossed her arms.

“You tried to save her mind instead of shooting them,” she said.

Calr looked uncertain.

“Yes.”

She shrugged.

“That sounds like something an idiot hero would do.”

Calr looked embarrassed.

Then she smirked.

“Which means it sounds exactly like something someone from this team would do.”

Ja’a laughed.

“Anyone but Katar, that is.”

“You are goddamn right. Anyone who ambushes me deserves no empathy from me,” grumbled Katar.

Shingo gave Calr a few pats on the shoulder. He looked approving of his friend’s choice to try to help the girl.

Raik exhaled slowly and rubbed his forehead.

“Next time,” he said, “try to save the mind-healing session until after the battle. You have no idea how many arrows Kan deflected that were about to hit you.”

Calr nodded quickly. He then turned toward Kan and bowed.

“Thank you.”

Kan waved a hand dismissively.

“There is no need for that. We are a team after all.”

She deflected the praise as smoothly as she had deflected the arrows.

“So that is everything on Calr’s end,” I said. “What about you two?”

I looked toward Raik and Kan.

Raik leaned back slightly, stretching his shoulders as if remembering the fight.

“Well, it started as a normal fight,” he said. “And we were winning. At least, we were winning until the girl with the moon-colored hair stole my power.”

“How did she do that?” I asked.

“It was strange,” Raik said. “She somehow stole my name.”

Kan nodded.

“She claimed to be Raik Agame, daughter of Taka,” Kan explained, “and said that he was the one without a name.”

“That sounds like name magic,” I said slowly.

I rubbed my chin thoughtfully.

“Aren’t I glad I decided to use a fake name?”

Everyone turned toward me.

The silence that followed was almost impressive.

“Your name is not Alice?” Ja’a asked, looking betrayed.

Everyone stared at me as if I had just announced I was secretly a dragon.

“No, my name is Alice,” I said quickly. “But my family name is not Hecate.”

I shrugged.

“I made that part up.”

Kan frowned.

“You invented a fake family name just in case name magic existed?”

“Yes.”

The group stared at me again.

“I am sorry,” Raik said slowly. “Is name magic common where you come from? Because I had never heard of it until today.”

“Some Fay magic in the mythic realm may have strange interactions with names,” Vena said with a small shrug. “But you usually have to give it willingly, not have it taken through other means.”

“Well,” I said, “since today seems to be the day when everyone reveals secrets, I might as well start revealing a few of mine.”

I looked around the group.

“It is not like I can keep this secret much longer anyway.”

I started counting on my fingers.

“Vena already knows. Calr probably figured it out with his Analyse ability. Nina has seen my dreams. And the Temple of the Holy clearly suspects that I know far more things than someone in my position should.”

I took a breath.

“I am not from the Seven Realms.”

The words settled over the group like falling ash.

Ja’a blinked slowly.

“What do you mean?”

Kan frowned in confusion.

“There are more realms than the Seven?”

“Apparently,” Raik muttered.

I nodded.

“I was teleported here from another world,” I explained. “A world where magic is almost dead.”

“Dead?” Kan repeated.

“Not completely gone,” I said. “But in my world, magic mostly survives in myths and legends.”

I leaned back slightly.

“In my world, we do not have growth spells to help crops grow, and we do not have decay magic to fertilize the soil. We do not have kindred strength to move heavy loads either.”

I gestured around us.

“So instead, we learned to harness other sources of power.”

“Like what?” Ja’a asked.

“Fire and lightning, mostly,” I said.

They all stared at me.

“Yes, actual lightning,” I added.

“We use it to power machines. Machines that move without horses. Machines that can carry hundreds of people at once. Machines that can cross entire continents in hours.”

Kan’s eyes widened slightly.

Raik looked skeptical but thoughtful.

“And information,” I continued.

“We also learned how to store information using lightning and metal. Devices that can hold more knowledge than an entire library.”

“That sounds suspiciously like a dreamer artifact,” Ja’a said.

“In a way,” I admitted.

I reached into my pocket and pulled out my phone.

The small black rectangle immediately attracted everyone’s attention.

Kan leaned closer.

I tapped the screen.

A moment later, music filled the quiet night air.

Soft piano notes drifted through the campfire light.

Beethoven’s Moonlight Sonata.

The group froze.

Kan slowly turned her head toward the sound.

Ja’a leaned forward with wide eyes.

Calr stared at the phone with intense curiosity.

Raik’s expression shifted from suspicion to fascination.

Even Shingo tilted his head slightly.

Vena grinned in recognition, already familiar with the song.

The music continued to flow through the clearing.

Soft, melancholic, and almost haunting. It fit perfectly with the dead zone atmosphere.

“In my world,” I said quietly, “this was written by a man who lived more than two hundred years ago.”

No one spoke for a moment.

The only sound was the piano echoing through the night.

Finally, Ja’a broke the silence.

“So,” she said slowly, “you are telling us that you come from another world.”

“Yes.”

“A world without magic.”

“Mostly.”

“But with artifacts that store knowledge.”

“Yes.”

“And tiny music boxes that summon invisible pianists.”

I smiled faintly.

“That is one way to describe it.”

Kan leaned back slowly.

“I am not sure which part of this story is the most unbelievable.”

Raik rubbed his chin thoughtfully.

“I am more concerned about the implication,” he said. “If there is an eighth realm, there might also be a ninth.”

Ja’a pointed at me.

“To be fair, that does explain a lot about her.”

I sighed.

“Yes,” I said. “I get that reaction a lot.”

“So are there any other ground-breaking revelations?” Raik asked.

Vena shrugged lightly.

“Sure. Why not?”

She straightened a little, as if deciding there was no point in hiding it anymore.

“I was sent here to Hano on a mission,” she said. “My orders are to rise high in the city’s hierarchy.”

Ja’a raised an eyebrow.

“That sounds suspiciously ambitious.”

Vena ignored the comment.

“Since the freelancers effectively control the city’s military power, my goal is to rise through their ranks,” she continued. “Eventually, I am supposed to become a commander and protect the mythic population of Hano from the city’s elite.”

She glanced at me briefly before looking back at the others.

“It is not as ground-breaking as Alice’s story,” she admitted. “But it is the reason I traveled across realms to come here.”

Kan tilted her head thoughtfully.

“Does this have something to do with Knight Franz’s execution?” she asked.

Vena nodded.

Kan folded her arms as she remembered the story.

“He was executed during Hano’s last rebellion for the murder of several nobles,” she said. “He did not resist his execution.”

“Yes,” Vena said quietly. “But he also did not lose his class.”

The group went silent for a moment.

Calr frowned slightly.

“That means the Holy judged his actions as honorable,” he said.

“Exactly,” Vena replied. “Even though he killed nobles.”

Raik grimaced.

“It is really hard to rise past captain if you are neither a soullit nor a bloodline,” he said. “The leadership does not like promoting outsiders.”

Vena smiled slightly.

“That is because Hano has never had a paladin before.”

She looked toward the distant lights of the city.

“Once I ascend, that will change.”

There was a quiet determination in her voice.

“I will show everyone the true strength of the Holy.”

Ja’a whistled softly.

“That is quite the career plan.”

Calr leaned back and crossed his arms.

“You know, becoming a paladin is not exactly easy,” he said. “Most clerics rarely reach that level.”

Vena’s expression did not change.

“I know.”

“And if you fail?” Kan asked.

Vena shrugged.

“Then I will still have helped people along the way.”

“Are there any other revelations before we go to bed?” Raik asked.

I hesitated for a moment, then raised my hand slightly.

“Actually… yes,” I said. “What is the deal with Katar? You two seem to have a strange bond.”

Raik blinked.

“You do not know?” Ja’a said, surprised. “Katar is Raik’s slave.”

“He is not my slave,” Raik said immediately.

Katar shrugged.

“I am technically his slave.”

I blinked at that revelation.

Apparently, everyone on the team already knew that except me.

In hindsight, I suppose it made sense. I had missed a few hours when I teleported away to the Sunless Reach, and then again when I jumped back to Hano. Important conversations had probably happened while I was away.

“What do you mean, slave?” I asked carefully.

Katar leaned back, resting his weight on his hands.

“My father borrowed a lot of money,” he explained. “And he put the family up as collateral.”

I felt my stomach tighten.

“That sounds like a terrible contract.”

“It was,” Katar said calmly. “Then he lost the money on bad investments.”

He paused for a moment.

“After that, he killed himself like a coward.”

The words were delivered without much emotion, but the silence that followed grew heavier.

“So the debt passed to the family,” Calr said quietly. “To be paid as indentured servants.”

Katar nodded.

Raik spoke next.

“I knew him when we were kids,” he said. “When I learned what happened, I managed to buy his debt before he was sent away.”

“Sent away where?” I asked.

“Who knows,” Ja’a said flatly.

I did not like the sound of that.

“I bought his contract,” Raik continued.

“And I bought his mother and his little brother,” Ja’a added.

“His mother grows the best tea in the Seven Realms,” she said. “So she now manages the tea gardens on my father’s estate.”

“That sounds less like slavery and more like employment,” I said cautiously.

“Technically, it is still a debt bond,” Ja’a explained. “Until the debt is repaid.”

Raik shrugged.

“I offered to write off the debt anyway,” he said. “But Katar refused.”

Katar stretched his arms behind his head.

“Being a certified Agame servant allows me more freedom than being a nobody,” he said.

“Much better. I almost signed up as his treasurer,” Ja’a said with a laugh.

Raik’s expression darkened slightly.

“There was one person we could not save,” he said quietly.

Katar’s expression shifted just a little.

“My older sister,” he said.

Raik looked down at the fire.

“We were just kids at the time,” he said. “By the time Ja’a and I gathered enough money to buy the rest of the contracts, she had already been transferred.”

“Transferred where?” I asked.

“No one knows,” Katar said.

The casual tone did not quite hide the tension in his voice.

Kan frowned slightly.

“There are more uses for teenage girls than for seven-year-old boys or mothers still nursing a baby,” she said.

The fire crackled between us.

For the first time since the conversation started, Katar looked slightly uncomfortable.

“The trail went cold after Hano,” he said.

“She cannot be in Hano,” Calr said. “Any brothel that dared to use a girl like that would get a visit from the Crusader.”

No one spoke for a moment.

The mood around the campfire grew noticeably heavier.

Even Ja’a did not try to make a joke.

I stared into the flames, trying to process everything.

First, Calr had formed a hive mind with a telepath.

Then I revealed I was from another world.

Then Vena admitted she had crossed realms on a secret mission to become a commander.

And now I had learned that one of my teammates was technically a slave because his father had gambled away his entire family.

I slowly shook my head.

“This team has the strangest collection of life stories I have ever heard.”

Ja’a finally broke the silence.

“And we did not even get to Shingo yet,” she joked. “Any secrets, big guy?”

Shingo nodded solemnly.

He pointed at himself, then pointed at Ja’a. Then he pointed at her bag of holding and mimed pulling something out of it and eating it. After that, he pointed at Ja’a again and made the universal sign for silence by placing one finger across his lips.

“You caught Ja’a sneaking candy from her bag of holding, and she has been bribing you to keep it secret,” Calr translated.

Shingo nodded sharply.

Katar stood up, showing more emotion than when discussing his life story.

“I knew you were cheating on your diet,” he said. “You did not lose nearly as much weight as you should have despite my harsh training.”

We all laughed, and the mood finally shifted back to something lighter.

Raik shook his head as he stood up.

“That is enough revelations for one night,” he said. “If we keep talking, someone else will reveal that they are secretly an immortal.”

Everyone slowly began settling down for the night.

I offered to take the first watch. As the fire burned low, I lay back and stared at the stars above me.

Another world, secret missions, debt slavery, telepaths, and cultists. I had only been here a few months, and somehow, this was already starting to feel normal.
