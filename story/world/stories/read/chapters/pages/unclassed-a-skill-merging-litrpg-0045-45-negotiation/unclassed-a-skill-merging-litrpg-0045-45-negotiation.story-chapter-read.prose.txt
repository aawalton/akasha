I’d thought it’d be easier and less suspicious to enter Vulrak’s camp alone, or perhaps with one other person with me, but apparently, I was wrong.

Everyone besides Marcois—who had volunteered to tally the day’s haul—accompanied me to Vulrak’s camp. This was expected by the guards and didn’t look amiss.

Simply put, it was stranger to enter another group’s camp alone than it was in large numbers, just in case the group attempted to capture and ransom you, or do something else nefarious. If you went in not looking worried at all, it’d be assumed that you were either expected to be there or happened to know in advance that you were safe.

Us travelling as near-enough a full group gave the impression that we didn’t want to be here. That we were wary of Vulrak’s group and were choosing to enter the lion’s den simply because we needed healing so our work wouldn’t be impacted.

The two guards watching the outside of the camp still stared at us as we passed, but neither said anything. They seemed more tired than they did interested in our comings and goings.

I wondered how many groups regularly visited for healing or other services. I’d be sure to ask.

Once we were inside Vulrak’s camp, a younger girl, maybe fourteen, waved to us from atop a wooden fortification.

“Hiya! Who are you looking for?”

Toar answered. It took me a moment to realise that this was the same girl I’d walked beside on the day we’d first arrived here, the one who’d asked questions and gawked at everything she saw.

“Okay! I’ll take you to him!” She slid down a ladder and bounded the last few feet, landing before us. She pointed her finger.

“Mercury’s set up on this end of the camp. Apparently, it’s more damp over here. I thought that would’ve been a bad thing, but he said that water mana can be really useful in helping to power healing spells, so he wants to be close to it.” She heaved a breath. “Really fascinating stuff.”

Wow. She hadn’t changed one bit. I mean, her previously bright blonde hair was a shade or two darker now, untouched by the sun in many long months, but besides that, this place didn’t seem to have broken her.

I wondered if she was naive or just lucky. Maybe both.

Mercury seemed surprised to see me. Apparently, he’d also been under the impression that I was dead. The girl—her name was Cynthia—was told to resume watch and left, but two older boys and one girl watched us as we approached Mercury’s tent. Only I was allowed to go inside, and the rest would have to wait for me.

One of the boys asked if Mercury would need any ‘assistance’ with me, which was a thinly veiled way of asking if he needed a guard, but Mercury said he was fine, which I was glad for. I wanted privacy for this.

The two of us walked inside. I was taken aback by the smell.

It reminded me of the markets in Melusia. The strong scent of herbs, medicine, and incense carried through the air, like a bazaar in full swing. The only thing that was missing was the people, the shouting. Baskets of various ingredients were piled up along one wall of the room, and on a nearby table was a series of glassware—some of it looked similar to things I’d seen—and taken from—the labs underground.

“So… you return?” Mercury asked.

The question was leading, but I simply nodded.

“I had you pegged for dead, honestly.” Mercury bit his lip. “I find it hard to believe that you disappeared to the underground and came back with only… two broken fingers?”

He grabbed my hand and held them close, inspecting them. He squeezed one, causing me to flinch.

“Broken today,” he added.

I yanked my hand away. “Yeah. That’s what happened.”

“You don’t trust me with the full story?” Mercury blinked twice, as if he were offended. He pushed his long hair over one shoulder and strode further into the room.

“Well, I’m not an idiot,” he deadpanned. “ You must’ve had something keeping you alive and healthy while you were gone—my guess is potions.” He stretched, as if he were tired. “And now, the day that you return?” He let the question hang for a moment. “You come to me with an injury you need fixing. So, did you run out of potions? Or are you here for another reason?”

“Well, you figured that out quickly.”

“There wasn’t much to figure out. So, what exactly were you hoping to discuss?”

“It’s about Vulrak.”

Mercury squinted. “What about him?”

“Have you made any progress with figuring out his collar?” I asked.

Mercury’s voice lowered, deeper than I’d ever heard it. “We might have.”

“What if I could help to remove it?”

Mercury tilted his head. “With your god-touched skill, you mean?”

I reached for a knife in my [Hoard]; it was instinct.

“My what?”

Mercury didn’t move. He was a powerful-looking guy. Seventeen or eighteen, his build more befitting a fighter despite the robes he wore, and I had no clue what he could do with magic. If things devolved and we came to blows in such a tight space, I’d have to use everything to stand a chance.

Rationality flooded my brain. I couldn’t start a fight here. We were heavily outnumbered in Vulrak’s camp, and Marcois wasn’t even here. Still, I felt vulnerable. Mercury was clearly suspicious of me. Did he know about [Hoard]?

“I analyse and process information on every miner in the rift,” Mercury stated. “When you came to me for healing, you gave me your severed finger. Examining it for a time taught me much about you.

“Incredible mana capacity for your Tier. A host of strangely-acquired skills. A skill I couldn’t possibly analyse, even when I tried. And a finger so perfectly preserved that freezing it wouldn’t have been sufficient.”

He held up a finger for each as he rattled them off. He glared at me.

“You’re really something,” he finally concluded. “My examination is infallible up to Supreme, so either you had a Legendary skill at Tier 0, or you are in fact god-touched.”

I didn’t answer. I wasn’t about to divulge what I had. Not unless my whole group’s lives were threatened.

“I initially assumed your skill was only to do with preservation, but now you’re talking about disabling Vulrak’s collar…”

He stared at me. “Can you create perfect ice?”

I only stared back.

“No.” He shook his head. “You’d have taken over this mine the moment you walked in. It doesn’t match your bloodline, either…”

A few seconds, and Mercury’s eyes widened. He snapped his fingers.

“You… possess some kind of perfect inventory skill. Don’t you?”

Again, I only stared, but I felt my heartrate quicken.

“It’d make sense. You could have scores of potions on you at all times. Mountains of food. An arsenal of weapons. Plenty to survive the hostile environment of the underground, no?”

I raised my hands.

“No clue what you’re talking about.”

Mercury grinned. “I’ve never met a god-touched before. You’re rarer than epic classes. I wonder what you’re capable of doing.”

The smile left him. “I doubt you’re physically capable of much. You’re likely stronger than when I last examined you, but even still, your other skills weren’t outrageous…”

He pointed to my hand. “And you also break easily. I can’t be certain what you’re capable of, but I doubt that you could take on our entire group alone.”

I definitely couldn’t. But I’d let him continue wondering.

“That said…”

He leaned into me.

“If you attempt to do anything sinister to Vulrak, I will try my hardest to kill you.”

I’d never heard him so serious before. He’d been light and whimsical in our previous meetings, even aloof. A little matter-of-fact, maybe. To think he’d threaten my life so openly.

“Can I assume you’re open to considering my offer?” I asked him.

“You’re a stern negotiator, aren’t you?” Mercury snided. He opened his arms. “I assume you aren’t expecting to do such a thing for free, assuming you even can.”

“I can.”

I was almost certain of that. As long as the collar wasn’t technically a part of Vulrak, it shouldn’t be an issue.

“In that case, what is it you want?”

“That’s a discussion I’ll have with Vulrak,” I told him bluntly.

Mercury snickered.

“I suppose there’s been no reason for it to occur to you—how do I put this?” He bit his lip. “Every decision that Vulrak makes goes through me first. I am his… advisor and confidant.”

I raised an eyebrow.

“You’re saying you’re the actual leader?”

“Vulrak leads, I tell him how.”

I blinked. “Why? If you’re the brain behind everything, why aren’t you in charge? Because he’s stronger?”

“They’re his ideals,” Mercury explained. “Looking after the people down here. Making a difference.” He flashed me a smile. “I’ll be honest, I didn’t give a shit about any of it. I just wanted to ply my trade in peace. But he’s persistent. And he’s convincing.” He sighed.

I’d never imagined using my skills to let someone else lead before. For a cause that someone strongly believed in. Or… for someone I strongly believed in?

I tilted my head.

“Do you care about helping people now?”

“I think I’ve gotten less selfish over the years,” Mercury admitted. “A little. Mainly, I just wouldn’t want to look like an ass in front of him.”

They were clearly close. He cared about Vulrak. I didn’t know if that made my proposition easier or harder.

“I want an alliance,” I told him.

“What kind of alliance?”

I chose my words carefully. I knew that a ‘no’ here was going to be extremely damaging to our efforts.

“Vulrak was willing to fight this place,” I said. “He was willing to die over it.”

“Yeah, because he’s a damned idiot.”

“You fought to protect him, didn’t you?” I looked him dead on. “I even heard they selected you to die, and you were willing to face it.”

“There was no other way out of that situation,” Mercury defended. “I’m not typically so self-sacrificing.”

“What if you could fight again and win?”

Mercury’s eyes shifted at that. Their brown pools turned shiny.

“You wish for Vulrak to lead some kind of rebellion.”

“An ambush,” I clarified. “One that’ll take time and planning, but that we stand a real chance of winning.”

“They won’t spare him twice,” Mercury said. “Not after something so brazen. I don’t want him to die.”

“What’s the alternative?” I raised my voice. “Vulrak told me he’s a slave. Owned by the Association. What happens after he leaves here?”

“He goes to an adult facility,” Mercury replied, his voice low. “I’ve been planning to go with him. To set up shop there. I’m about halfway to being able to buy out his contract.”

“I see. How long have you been working to save that money?”

“Two and a half years. Closer to three now.”

“And you think you’re going to finish in another two or three years?”

“That’s the plan.”

An edge entered my tone.

“Because I can tell you now, there’s zero guarantee that you make the same money in an adult facility, or anywhere close.”

Now it was Mercury’s turn to be silent. He already knew this.

“You’re group leaders here,” I stated matter-of-factly. “Of the biggest group in the rift, no less. You’ve got the most lucrative spots and over a dozen people kicking up to you. Plus endless security for all your wares.”

I shrugged. “What happens when you end up somewhere Vulrak’s not so impressive. Where a Tier 3’s middle of the pack, or even worse, a small fry?” I smirked. “And who’s to say they even honour that buyout? What if they decide he’s worth more and raise it? What if they keep you running on a wheel your whole damn life, struggling and failing to find a way out?”

Mercury tutted and turned away. “I don’t want to listen to this anymore.”

“Just listen to my plan. Hear me out once, and if you don’t like it, I’ll remove Vulrak’s collar for free.”

He turned to me, his eyes severe. “Why would you—”

“Because this is happening with or without you,” I told him. “He’s in pain, isn’t he? I heard about what it does to him. Wearing that thing must be terrible. You’re not gonna let me leave without removing it.”

I leaned into him. “And when I do, I’m telling him my plan. And he’ll accept. And if he does that without your guidance, and if you don’t assist us, aren’t we all twice as likely to die? You’re the smart one, after all.”

I shifted my head. “Or, I dunno. Maybe you just let me leave. If you’re willing to do that. I don’t think you are.”

Mercury growled. For a moment, I thought he was going to attack me. His hand glowed blue.

“Let me see your fingers.”

I gave him my injured hand. In the span of thirty seconds, my fingers had popped back into place.

I almost screamed.

It hurt much more than a healing potion’s mending did, more than breaking them. I almost wondered if that had been intentional on Mercury’s part.

“Fine. Tell me your plan.”

***

We argued terms for over thirty minutes. Truthfully, after discovering Mercury was the decision-maker in Vulrak’s group, I wanted his involvement in this plan more than I did anything else. Going around him and dealing with Vulrak directly was a bluff I was sure he’d cave to, and I was glad he did.

Mercury had a lot of insights concerning the guards. He knew most of their tiers, had a general idea of their shift patterns, and crucially, he’d seen a guard die.

Mercury had been in the mines for four and a half years. Over four years ago, in his first few months on the job, Mercury saw a Tier 2 guard get crushed by a cave-in during an inspection. He estimated that the rocks had weighed a few hundred pounds at the very least, as he and the other miners nearby had struggled to clear the debris from the body while someone else called for help, but once they’d unearthed him, they’d found he’d suffocated.

Mercury made sure to highlight that he believed it was the lack of airflow that had killed the man, rather than the cave-in itself. His body hadn’t been disfigured beside one snapped leg and an array of bruises.

That was important. Knowing that even a Tier 2 guard could withstand more than twice their body weight falling on top of them and come out of it only injured was a testament to their durability. Mercury did also mention that the guard in question had a very strong build and was thick like a wrestler. There was a fair chance he’d had some kind of skill that contributed to his durability.

Still, that they could die from suffocation fairly easily. Apparently, it’d taken less than ten minutes to unearth him. In that respect, your average guard likely wasn’t superhuman.

Mercury said Tier 3’s are a different story, and he knows as much from Vulrak. He didn’t give me too many details, but apparently, Vulrak had been through and survived a similar cave-in with only minor breaks in the past year. Moreover, he didn’t have to be dug out. He remained conscious and punched through half of the fallen rocks himself.

“Would you say that Vulrak has many vulnerabilities?” I asked, using him as a substitute in my mind for a Tier 3 guard with a warrior class.

Mercury didn’t look excessively comfortable with the question, but answered.

“He’s durable, but piercing strikes in weak areas, blood loss that compounds over time, and some magic attacks are likely the best ways someone weaker could challenge him,” Mercury said.

“Which magic attacks?”

“That’s not relevant.”

“It is if we’re going to fight similar people.”

“Vulrak’s class is Warlord,” Mercury explained through a tut. “He’s not going to be like most other fighters you encounter. From what I know, a vast majority of the guards here aren’t especially magically capable. People who are tend to use it often, and I’ve been asked to heal guards on more than one occasion.”

“That’s how you know more about them.”

“Partially, but I can only get so much from dead skin.” Mercury clicked his tongue. “Anyway. While I imagine just based on what I’ve seen that most of the guards fit standard warrior classes, warriors do often train in at least some magical defense. It’s usually rudimentary and based in elemental magic, though some acquire basic barriers.”

That sounded tough. “How do you know all of this?”

“I came from a house filled with books,” Mercury explained. “Books worth more than I make in a month here.”

That confused me. Why would he be here if he came from such wealth?

“I see the question on your face. It’s simple, really. My bloodline is that of the shark, and my family adheres strongly to the principles set by our founding ancestors. We are educated and cared for until the day of our selection, and then we leave. We’re allowed to take nothing but a weapon and the clothes on our backs, and are forbidden to return until we have reached Tier 3 at minimum.”

“That’s… horrible.”

“It wasn’t as bad for a lot of my siblings. The education in my first thirteen years was sublime. I learned more than many must in their entire lifetimes. I devoted much of my time to the study of magic and medicine. I hoped for a Healer class.”

“Did you get one?”

“I didn’t. I received a common class named Medic. I received a barely functioning healing spell which could scarcely handle scrapes and a small improvement to my handling of medicines.”

“That was the best you were offered?” I asked, shocked.

“In a field I desired to pursue, yes. The other two options were uncommon, but I wasn’t interested.”

Wow. He was similar to me, in a way.

I mean, he’d had far more opportunities in his early childhood, despite the brutal send-off, but the only difference between us is that his selection had given him something he actually was interested in, even if it was at a lower rarity than he’d have liked.

“If you hadn’t been offered Medic, would you have rejected your choices?”

Mercury scoffed. “No, I’m not mad like you.”

My nose twitched.

We returned to discussion on guards. Apparently, mindful warriors typically trained resistance in elemental magics, though some didn’t bother. Tier 3’s were more likely to have some innate magical resistances, and some might even possess minor regeneration or stamina-boosting abilities.

When it came to Tier 3’s, Mercury was certain that at least four guards among the eighteen in the mines were Tier 3, and there were five he was unsure about. Among those was the guard captain, Maxwell, who he assumed was at least Tier 3.

Made sense that he wouldn’t be Tier 2 as the captain. The implication that he could be higher was scary, but expected.

“What are Tier 4’s like?”

“Powerful. Most have trained an ability to epic rank at that point. Minor supernatural feats tend to be within their reach, and they’re often more physically impressive than Tier 3’s, but not always.”

Basically checked out with what Toar had said.

“If you had to kill one, how would you go about it?”

“Poison,” Mercury answered in a heartbeat. “I could probably concoct something potent enough, but finding an effective delivery system could be an issue. Inside food or drink would be easiest, but good luck handling a guard’s food.”

“Do they all eat from the same mess hall?” I pondered.

“It’s heavily guarded. There’s zero chance of you getting in there. Give up on that thought.”

We spoke about delivery methods for a little while. Arrowheads seemed to be the most viable, but would require someone actually be able to hit a Tier 3 or 4 with an arrow and pierce the skin. Even then, there was the worry of regeneration or poison resistance skills to consider. Still, it was an idea.

“Your trapped dead drop idea is flawed, by the way,” Mercury told me.

“Flawed how?”

“What happens if the first group we tell where the weapons are hidden goes and tells the guards?”

“They get the trap sprung on them.”

“And then what?”

I considered it for a moment. “If they’re not dead, we ambush them.”

“So you plan to split these guards up, send them to multiple different locations chasing one cache of weapons, and then have teams set up at each location to spring the traps and finish them off?”

“...something like that.”

“What happens if literally any of them run into each other? Or have some other way to communicate, perhaps with a spell?”

I hadn’t even considered a communication spell. My eyes hit the floor.

“You’re right. We’d be fucked if any of them spoke to one another.”

“And that’s even assuming that no one notices the first guards to go missing. Or the amount of miners that are conveniently absent from their posts that day during check-ins.”

“You’re saying it’s really obviously a trap.”

“Even if the first iteration works, the rest likely won’t. You might kill one or two with a plan like this, assuming you use the right trap and get lucky enough. After that, they’ll know they’ve been hit. Worst case, they send for reinforcements.”

I decided to sit in one of the chairs in Mercury’s tent. He had a little while ago, and I was only realising now that I’d been on my feet literally all day, besides a brief reprieve at camp.

I huffed a sigh. “Then… what?”

Mercury hummed.

“I think we need something that’ll hit more than a couple of them at once. Like your idea to poison their food supply, but more feasible.”

“You’ve come around to this rebellion thing pretty easily.”

“Like you said, this seems to be happening with or without me.” Mercury peered at me. “Considering all of our necks are likely to be on the line regardless, I’d like to ensure you don’t muck it all up.”

I wasn’t offended by that. I thought I’d react more strongly to having a flaw pointed out with my plan, but in truth, I appreciated the growth opportunity. I wanted to get this right. If listening to smart people helped me to refine my ideas, then I gladly would.

“Well, do you have any suggestions for what that big thing would be?”

“I’ve been thinking about it,” Mercury said. He sipped on a cup of tea. “Nothing concrete yet. The most salient thing to me has been that we’ll only have one chance to strike before they know they’ve been hit. The checks they carry out aren’t simultaneous, so we can’t hit too many that way. We need to gather them.”

“What gathers guards to one place?” I asked. “They all came for the Overseer’s demonstration. What if we killed one and they tried to round us up again, but this time we were armed and ready?”

“Too risky.”

I agreed. They’d be ready to fight at that point. We couldn’t conceal a bunch of guns, either. There was no guarantee they’d even try to repeat the demonstration. From what I’d heard from Toar, they’d executed multiple people over the last two weeks with no ceremony whatsoever.

I considered orchestrating a monster attack for a moment, but it was infeasible. I only had a handful of control stones, and even if I could pilot multiple monsters at once, I’d seen how reluctant guards were to deal with them. They were here to police us, not protect, and considering I’d managed to survive every monster I encountered in the underground, even if I’d struggled, I doubted it’d be easy to find ones that posed a real threat to the staff.

What made them gather? What was important? What did they care about?

Obviously, they cared about keeping us in line. They cared about money. They cared about if we tried to steal from them. They cared about…

I searched back through everything I’d learned in the previous months I’d worked here. Honestly, I’d never had much in the way of a formal introduction. The tour had been brief. We’d seen some of the work areas, we’d been given very little instructions, besides the rules. In fact, the only thing we’d been told to do was…

The drills. The magically powered drills that operated in the central caverns and beyond, mindlessly pumping away. I’d seen a few of them scattered around—they were hard to miss, rising twenty feet into the air. I remembered staring at one on my first day here and wondering how exactly it was powered. The thought had soon gone to the wayside as questions of survival began to take the forefront.

But now, I was returning to that thought. To my previous curiosity. Mainly because the man that had mentioned them to us, Maxwell, had specifically told us not to touch or go near them.

But that wasn’t all he’d said…

These tools are worth more than your lives. Meddle with them, and a cave-in will be the least of your worries. If you ever see one malfunctioning, you report it to a staff member immediately. Understood?

Yeah. I understood.

“Here’s a question,” I asked Mercury, a smile playing on my lips.

“Just how explosive are the tools down here?”

Mercury’s brow shifted three times in the span of five seconds as he processed my question. The B Grade Resonance Stone in my [Hoard] glinted as I examined it.

“But… how would we…”

“Let’s just say I’m full of surprises.”

***

The rest of the conversation consisted of bartering on costs. We knew we’d need to commission someone to make arrows for us, an expense I’d cover, and it’d cost Mercury about 600 gold of supplies to generate enough poison to coat knives and arrowheads for everyone, a cost he’d eat from his own stores.

Apparently, coating bullets was pointless. Mercury explained to me that the heat and velocity of a gunshot burned away poison, so I shelved that idea pretty quickly.

I didn’t explain exactly what the Resonance Crystal I had was, I just told him I’d found explosive material in the underground. That said, if he could create anything combustible that would amplify an explosion, or help to spread flames, that’d be brilliant. Mercury said he’d attempt to make some highly flamable alcohol for just that purpose, but wasn’t sure how he’d fare with his current ingredients.

I gave him some of the glassware I’d found in the Drassian lab as payment, stuff I had duplicates of. He seemed fascinated by all of it, and already I assumed he had a better handle on what it was all for than me. Maybe I’d ask him for some tuition, when this was all over.

Then there was the matter of training. I had one copy of the gun manual I’d picked up, but it was written in Drassian. I resolved to give tutorials to the group leaders once everyone who agreed was on board, and then they could teach their own subordinates.

The hard sell of rebellion was discussed. Me and Mercury both agreed that even coming from Vulrak’s camp, leading the conversation with ‘we’re planning to take this place down’ was the most likely to result in premature snitching.

Instead, we’d be contacting each group individually and telling them that they’d been selected to merge with Vulrak’s group, and that they could tell no one. Once we were sure we could trust an individual group, we’d let them in on the full plan. For now, this was a power play to consolidate Vulrak’s strength and each group we invited was the one lucky enough to be selected.

As for my role in this, I was the resident gun expert. Vulrak had commissioned me to show his new members how to operate ‘rift tech’, nothing more.

As for my continued comings and goings? I’d just incurred a ‘debt’ by having Mercury heal me while I couldn’t afford to pay. My regular comings and goings amounted to interest payments and nothing else. I’d turn up holding a little bag of gold each time, looking upset to be there. Simple and easy.

“How are we splitting the spoils?” I asked.

“You mean what’s inside the rift once we’ve killed them all?” Mercury asked. He tapped his chin. “In theory, there’s so much here that we could take one percent each and be wealthy, if not rich. But we’d have to carry it, and going back into the world with cartloads of gems and minerals is going to attract attention.” He rubbed his eyes. “We don’t even know if this alliance will hold. Once the guards are dead, some groups might try to take the valuables from others. It could be a whole new conflict.”

That was very true. I tried to consider it.

“We should gather everything valuable once the fighting’s over and divvy it up. Our groups should be in charge of distribution.”

“So we can take more?” he asked with a look like he knew me.

“So we can give stuff out without a riot,” I corrected. “However, we’re taking on the most financial burden here. Those guns are expensive. Same with the knives, plus whoever we have to pay. We should get first pick of the loot.”

Mercury nodded. “We should. We’ll keep forty percent of the valuables between our groups. That’s almost two percent per person. The remaining sixty can be divided amongst the sixty or so others. Assuming everyone lives.”

That was a dark implication. I suppose a part of me hadn’t been calculating on the basis some of us would die. But I knew it was likely. That being said…

“How’s that forty percent being split?” I asked, impulse rising in my brain. “Twenty to your group, twenty to mine? And what are we doing regarding weight distribution?”

“I assume we arrange things so everyone’s payment is as light as possible compared to its value. As for the split…” Mercury’s eyes narrowed. “Twelve to you, twenty-eight to us. There’s seven of you. There’s fifteen of us.”

“I’m removing Vulrak’s collar,” I argued. “That’s worth more than twelve percent.”

“Twelve. We’re a crucial part of this. We’re taking on risk.”

“I want twenty percent,” I told him. “Twenty, or I walk away from this.”

“Excuse me?” Mercury flicked his bangs, glaring at me. “You offered to help Vulrak for free not even an hour ago!”

“That was then. This is now.”

Mercury’s tone turned whimsical. He laughed.

“You’re so funny. You realise you and your group are standing in the middle of our camp, outnumbered three to one, do you not?”

I laughed back. “And you realise I’m a god-touched mage that survived the underground for months on my own, don’t you?”

I took a sip of the tepid tea he’d made me twenty minutes ago. I leaned back in my chair.

“If you want to go that route, you should stop worrying about whether I’ll save Vulrak, and start wondering whether you can beat me without him.”

Mercury stared, mouth agape as I pushed the front chair legs back with one of my own, balancing precariously as I cupped my drink.

“You’re…”

What? Insane? Wicked? Terrible?

“Fifteen percent.”

No. I was very sane.

Mercury wasn’t willing to blow up this deal over percentages. He cared about Vulrak. He wanted any outcome that resulted in his freedom. After everything he’d told me, that was the absolute truth.

“Twenty.”

I wasn’t willing to blow this up either. If he was actually serious about retaliating, I’d fold immediately, but he wasn’t, so I wouldn’t. I could see it in his face. In the way he dithered. The way his eyes darted around.

I’d had this conversation before. I was in control.

“I mean it,” Mercury said. “Don’t push me.”

“No you don’t.”

I yawned.

“Twenty.”

Mercury bowed his head. He sighed, long and hard.

“You’re being completely unreasonable.”

I smirked at that.

“You’re right. How about twenty-five?”

His face flushed.

“You expect me to lose money over this?”

“You said it yourself,” I reminded him. “One percent of what’s in this place would probably set someone up for life.”

“Assuming they could carry it.”

“Why are you haggling for things you can’t carry?”

I took another sip. His face burned red.

“Twenty-two.”

I gave him a long look. I cracked a smile.

“Alright. Done. Take me to him. Bring my group with me.”

We shook on it. Mercury’s hand was hot and clammy. I only noticed because mine wasn’t.

I was ice cold.

Truthfully, I didn’t care about that last 2 percent. Mercury was right, we wouldn’t be able to carry everything.
