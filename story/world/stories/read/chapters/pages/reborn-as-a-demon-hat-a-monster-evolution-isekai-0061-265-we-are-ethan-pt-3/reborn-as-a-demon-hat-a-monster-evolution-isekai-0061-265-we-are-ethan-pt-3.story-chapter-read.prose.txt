Passing through Artorious’ Memory Sphere was like being sucked through a dense mist.

Ethan moved, though not of his own accord. All he was truly aware of was the feeling of motion. He was bound for Lamphrey’s Sphere nestled somewhere deep within his mind. If his assessment of the Tialax Oneiromancer was correct, she’d probably been waiting for him all this time, ever since she’d given up her life for their shared vision.

Ethan felt his hat-body contort to the flow of energy through his own mindscape, traveling down a path that showed him nothing but a blank void that an occasional Sphere would pass through before ascending to the clouded sky and pop – vanishing from his memory.

Yet he still didn’t feel that he was forgetting the things he held dear to him. His friends. His home. His link between this world and the world he’d been born into.

Two such spheres emerged and seemed to be following him side by side. One of them displayed a grand stage on its translucent surface. The other – a verdant field of strawberry flowers.

And within each of them stood two solitary figures, both smiling at him from either side of his path.

“The two of you came to see me off, I see,” he said, nodding to the fashionably clad Kaedmon on his grand wooden stage and the ragged, though much more luminous looking Jun’Ei. “If you’re looking for someone to record your last words, I’m afraid I’m on a bit of a tight schedule.”

Both of them seemed too amused to care about his remark. Kaedmon saluted Jun from across Ethan and smiled at her, teeth glinting as though only a few hours before they hadn’t been full of his own blood.

“It was a grand old game, truly, Jun,” he said. “But in the end, neither of us could win!”

Jun’Ei shook her head, not quite sharing her counterpart’s level of enthusiasm.

“We both felt this coming a long time ago, Kaedmon,” she said – voice buttery smooth and calm as stone. “Had you heeded my warnings earlier, much loss could have been prevented.”

“There is no preventing loss, my dear,” the God of men replied. “This loss and that loss, perhaps. But not loss. Not suffering. You know as well as I do that suffering is chieftest of all mortal concerns. It is a thread in the fabric of life that not even we could cut.”

“And yet it is they that shall inherit the earth,” the ancient Lycae replied. “Not an old, jaded artist like you stuck in the past, or a ragged Lycae who looks only to the future. The present is now. And it is theirs. And I think that may be the way things ought to be.”

Kaedmon’s smile was almost infectious. “The way things ought to be,” he repeated.

Ethan rolled his eye at the two of them.

“If you’re both quite finished..?”

“We meant to tell you that you have accomplished what not even we could, Ethan,” Jun’Ei said. “And – if it means anything at all – we would ask not that you forgive us, but that you understand, now, why we did what we did.”

“I understand that even Gods can fall prey to the most basic of all desires,” Ethan replied. “The desire to keep on going, even if you know it’s pointless.”

“Life is never pointless, dear Demon Hat,” Kaedmon said with a childlike twirl. “You’ve shown us that. This world was far more interesting with you in it. I’ll grant you this, Jun: you really did choose a good one, in the end.”

“That may be the first compliment you have ever given me,” the Lycae prophet sighed.

“And the last!”

Ethan coughed – somehow.

“Anyway…”

Both Gods took his message. Keadmon leaned back, happy as a clown, and took a bow – making his final farewell to life a performance on his own little stage – before his Sphere floated away into the ether and melted away. Jun’Ei, for her part, flashed Ethan one last smile before she turned and walked towards a rather bulky Lycae who was waiting for her nearby. He’d been out picking her some flowers. And he looked familiar.

As her Memory Sphere ascended slowly to join Kaedmon’s, Ethan wondered why they’d even decided to show their faces at this moment. Maybe even Gods wanted to have some companionship at the end of their lives just like the mortals who’s lives had been theirs and theirs alone for untold centuries.

He couldn’t help but think that their smiles at the end were genuine. That the climax of their lives had given them, too, a sense of freedom.

But such concerns were soon to vanish with the rest of his faculties if he didn’t manage to find Sys. And for that, he needed Lamphrey.

So he focused his thoughts on moving forward – toward the blinding light that was fanning out from the far distance.

And when he finally pushed through it, his vision dimmed to a world of darkness.

He was in a massive cave system, hemmed in on all sides by natural walls covered with moss and intricate etchings that displayed Tialaxes dancing with ancient dragons. A single fire burned nearby that threw light into the rocky expanse all around, and the faint sounds of rushing water could be heard from somewhere to the north.

Ethan locked eyes on the six Tialax who were huddled round the fire – each of them engaged in some kind of ritualistic display, judging by the quick, jerky contortions of their limbs and the frenzied little shrieks they were making. Approaching closer, he could see that they were, in fact, blind. And even through his fading memory, he recognized them.

The sisters of the Umbral Order…

He looked past them to the one who was sitting closest to the fire, then. A Tialax shrouded by a veil and dressed in tatty rags that looked like they’d never once seen water.

And he knew who that Tialax was without even needing to meet her beady dark eyes.

She threw up a hand when she felt Ethan’s eye on her from across the flickering flames of her little campfire.

“Sisters,” she said. “A great warrior has come.”

Ethan scoffed as the Umbral Sisters each bowed low in the dirt.

“Oh, rise, rise!” he said. “I’ve had enough of performances for one day.”

Lamphrey’s smile was strange. Everything about this memory was. He’d expected to find her riding some ancient dragon, or counselling Karfangg in the open air of Argwyll rather than down in some dismal tunnel. But the smile she flashed when she told her Sisters to take their leave was oddly serene. It was like this was the only place she’d ever wanted to be.

“A small memory,” she said, more to the fire than to Ethan who flopped down beside her, basking in its artificial warmth. “Truly, we do not know which times will be most important to us until they have passed.”

“This is back in Argwyll’s first age,” Ethan said as he looked around, admiring the colorful etchings on the moist cave walls. “Before…everything.”

“Before everything,” Lamphrey agreed. “It is fitting that this should be my final place of rest. For there is no true journey without a return.”

The Tialax seemed less labored than she ever had been in life, Ethan noted. Her breathing was clear, her voice less raspy. She seemed, for the first time since he’d known her, to be genuinely relaxed, preoccupied only with the present, and not the distant future.

“You have done well,” she said suddenly. “You have done everything you wished to do. Followed the signs. Found the Enemy. And now you stand on the precipice of your final victory, knowing what it is you must sacrifice.”

“Why couldn’t you tell me?” Ethan had to ask. “About Sys. About Keadmon, and about Jun?”

“If I told you, the Parasite would know,” she replied. “I ventured to plant as many seeds as I could before my end. He eventually came to realized that I, like Jun’Ei, knew his secret. And so he acted.”

She gazed deeply into the flames, as she and probably all her kind were doing in these subterranean passages below the world, each of them navigating a pathway through the darkness they knew would come, each of them trying to see any hope of light returning.

“If I may be allowed one regret,” she said after a few minutes, “it is that I did not tell you more. But for we who gaze into the shards of time, a single twist can shatter the whole edifice.”

“You were afraid that pushing me too much might lead to a different outcome,” Ethan said. “So you laid breadcrumbs, planted seeds – like you said – hoping I’d come to the right conclusion about how to ultimately fix up this broken world.”

Ethan felt her move beside him. When he then looked up, he saw that she was staring at him, eyes wide and smile infectious.

“You came to your own conclusion,” she said. “I could not have predicted the decisions you would make for Argwyll. I could not see how exactly you would defeat the Parasite. So, I watched through your eyes. And these things I found…entertaining.”

Ethan couldn’t believe what he was hearing. Right now, she looked almost as giddy as a child that had tasted candy for the first time.

“Hah! You sound just like old Arty. He enjoyed himself, too, watching the show from his comfy Memory Sphere. For different reasons, of course.”

She closed her eyes and shook her head at the mention of the Lightborn. Ethan suddenly had a little scenario playing out in his head – episodes of the Lightborn visiting Lamphrey and vice-versa during all this time they were in his head. Maybe swapping some perspectives and actually talking to each other, inviting Rachneros over when they needed a mediator for their debates…

“You sacrificed everything for Argwyll, didn’t you?” he asked her – the question being one that he’d been holding for a long, long time. “In the end, you just wanted to make sure this world kept on going, didn’t you?”

Lamphrey’s lip curled.

“Perhaps that makes me as weak as the Gods who wished only for preservation,” she said quietly. “Because I find that, here, at the end, I wish for just a little more time…I wish that I could do more than I did…”

“Weak is the last thing you are,” Ethan stated. “You might be cryptic, creepy, and just a little bit annoying, but not weak. And as for what you did…fuck sake, Lamphrey. You did it all. While I was running around collecting Cores, focused on power, focused on the next level, the next Skill, the next big milestone – you were the one who always brought me back to what was really important. Lamphrey, without you we wouldn’t have stood a chance. So – thank you. For everything.”

He looked to the Tialax and saw her incline her head slightly, gently, as though she was trying to hide her face.

He never took her for a bashful girl.

“I’m proud that I could finally make you crack a smile, by the way,” he said. “In fact, when they do write my legacy, I’m thinking that just might be at the peak of my list of achievements.”

She leaned back, focus on the flames’ embers again.

“You are thinking of the end,” she said. “Your end. And his.”

Ethan’s humor drained from him. He nodded.

And then he felt the same tremors that he’d felt in old Arty’s Memory Sphere before it had been deleted – a seismic eruption that started in the sky and slowly melted down to consume the fragmented world below.

Lamphrey’s Memory Sphere was dying.

He knew that she could feel it, too. And though she gave a little shudder beside her fire, she did not seem afraid.

She closed her eyes and waved both her hands over the flames, and from their center a small red sphere grew – and continued to grow before them like a balloon soon to be presented to a carnival-going toddler.

“He waits for you,” she told him. “At the very first Sphere – at the Core. The place where none other may venture but him and you. He is at the first memory from which all have been spawned. A memory connected to a past that only you can see. To finally see your journey completed, you must go to him, Ethan.”

“I know.”

“And you know what you must do?”

He gave her a thready, stitched-together smirk.

“Try looking into my future,” he said. “Maybe you already know the answer.”

She stared at him wide-eyed, a small chuckle emanating from her withered throat.

“I…cannot,” she said.

Ethan laughed with her as the cave walls began to crumble and crack, and the fire that lay between them was washed away by a river of all-consuming white light.

“Then I guess we’ll find out.”
