The Tutorial Shop greets me with six neatly arranged icons, each labeled in a rare and deeply appreciated act of basic usability.

Which is good.

I was mildly concerned I would be forced to navigate something resembling a department store the size of a small nation, organized by whatever abstract logic the System finds amusing—like a library sorted purely by vibes—but it appears someone, somewhere, made a responsible decision for once.

Altogether, we have Weapons, Armor, Accessories, Consumables, Utility, and Miscellaneous.

Clean. Efficient.

Acceptable.

Naturally, I select Weapons first.

Armor is unnecessary if I simply avoid being hit, which is, as always, the plan. Consumables are for hoarding in a carefully curated pile of “I might need this later,” where they will never be used.

And Utility sounds suspiciously like “things that are useful but not immediately fun,” which places it firmly below “sharp objects that solve problems.”

My personal moral framework continues to strongly favor excessive violence paired with tasteful dramatic flair, so a new weapon is clearly the most responsible choice.

…Assuming any of them can survive my flames, of course.

The interface shifts, replacing the menu with rows upon rows of weaponry, arranged with what can only be described as enthusiastic disregard for any form of organization.

Common iron daggers sit next to elaborate legendary spears. Warhammers share space with rapiers. Something that looks like it was stolen from a decorative wall display is positioned directly beside an item that could probably level a building.

Highly questionable curation.

I scroll for a bit, mostly out of curiosity.

Nothing above Legendary.

Tragic.

Pricing is… informative. Common weapons sit comfortably between fifty and one hundred fifty points, scaling steadily upward with rarity. Legendaries begin at around five hundred thousand, with some of the more ambitious designs pushing toward a full million.

Which means I could, technically, purchase multiple.

A comforting thought.

I continue scanning the list until I find the only sword of note so far, “Sword of the Waning Moon,” priced at a respectable eight hundred thousand.

A katana.

I pause, briefly consider it, and then scroll past without hesitation.

No.

Objectively inefficient design. Overcommitted to aesthetics. Poor structural philosophy. That is a hill I will not only die on, but defend with considerable enthusiasm.

I refuse to become an anime protagonist.

Which means it is time to use the filter.

I tap the option in the corner and sort by most expensive to least expensive, because I am, at present, obscenely wealthy, and if there is ever a time to indulge in questionable financial decisions, it is now.

The list refreshes.

Legendaries flood the screen. Axes, spears, hammers, each more elaborate than the last.

I ignore all of them.

Because one entry does not belong.

It sits at the very top, not just by placement, but by presence. Its cost alone eclipses the second most expensive option by over double, which is already an encouraging sign of excessive quality.

Naturally, I focus on it.

The weapon is long—just about my height—with the proportions of an extended western longsword: slightly broader than an ordinary blade, yet narrow and light enough to remain elegant despite its reach. Straight and double-edged, it tapers to a keen point, its surface a deep, lightless black that refuses to reflect anything around it.

Not matte or glossy.

Just… absent.

Like light reaches it, evaluates the situation, and decides it would rather be somewhere else.

The guard is minimal to the point of near nonexistence, little more than a subtle crosspiece, leaving the weapon visually uninterrupted from tip to pommel. The grip is extended for two hands, wrapped in smooth black material and set with small, dark gems that catch just enough light to remind me they exist.

I study it for a moment longer than strictly necessary.

Then arrive at a perfectly reasonable conclusion.

This is it.

Still, standards must be maintained.

I inspect it, even though I have already decided.

It can wait.

This cannot.

The motion is instinctive.

Effortless.

Like reaching for something that has always been mine.

Astra Nocturne appears.

No flash. No dramatic burst of light. It is simply there, resting perfectly in my grip as if it had never been anywhere else.

I take a moment to study it properly.

Up close, the blade is… wrong.

Not in a flawed way. In a deliberate one.

The black is deeper than it should be, but not empty. If I look closely, faint flickers of light move beneath the surface, tiny, distant stars appearing and vanishing in a slow, endless cycle.

It reminds me of my own eyes.

…Almost.

There is a difference. A subtle one.

Mine feel deeper.

Which is a mildly concerning observation that I will absolutely not be unpacking right now.

Aesthetically?

Ten out of ten.

Obviously.

Naturally, I test it.

The nearest object is my makeshift stove, a relatively flat rock that has been subjected to repeated, highly professional cooking sessions.

I raise the blade and bring it down in a clean vertical cut.

There is no resistance.

None.

The stone parts as if it was never there, the blade passing through with the same ease as air. No friction. No pushback. No satisfying grind of material being overcome.

It simply—

Separates.

A knife through butter would imply that butter existed. This is more like the concept of stone politely stepping out of the way.

The cut continues.

And then—

My starlight supports split apart.

That gets my attention.

There is resistance this time, but minimal. Far less than what should be required to damage something I personally made, something that has consistently demonstrated a deeply stubborn refusal to break.

I pull the blade back before I accidentally perform an unscheduled structural reorganization of my floor.

Then I blink and nod once.

Alright.

Very important test incoming.

I angle the blade slightly, which is mildly awkward given its length, then pull a small lock of my hair forward.

This is, objectively, a terrible idea.

I proceed anyway.

The blade moves.

The hair parts cleanly, a small section remaining in my hand.

I stare at it. Then at the blade.

Conclusion:

My hair is not, in fact, invincible.

Highly disappointing. Also mildly painful.

I ignore both of those facts immediately.

Instead, my tail swishes in appreciation.

Eleven out of ten.

Easily.

I am very much looking forward to my next moderately competent opponent.

As for the hair—

Pride will fix it.

Probably.

A thought occurs to me.

Curious.

Slightly experimental.

I inspect the strands still in my hand.

To my mild surprise, the System actually responds.

A rare and valuable trait.

One moment it exists, the next it does not, leaving behind only the cleanly bisected rock, the equally offended starlight supports, and a few strands of sparkly white hair that I choose to pretend never existed.

A weapon I do not need to carry. A weapon I cannot lose. A weapon that answers when called.

My tail swishes in approval.

As it should.

I reopen the System and return to the Tutorial Shop, now significantly lighter in funds but still operating at what I would describe as a comfortably excessive level of wealth.

Just below a million and a half points remain.

Enough to be dangerous.

Unfortunately, this means I should probably stop making immediate, emotionally correct purchasing decisions and instead adopt a more measured, strategic approach.

A tragic development.

I will need to review the remaining categories properly—Armor, Accessories, Utility, Consumables, and Miscellaneous—before committing to anything else, like a responsible individual making well-informed decisions.

I pause.

Then nod.

Yes.

This is clearly the optimal approach.

I will proceed with the careful, methodical discipline of an overly cautious raccoon that has somehow acquired access to a credit card and intends to use it responsibly.

…

That specific analogy feels slightly questionable, but I stand by it.

First up: armor.

I apply the most expensive filter immediately this time, because I have learned from my previous success and will be repeating that success with confidence.

The results are… disappointing.

A sea of heavy plate mail greets me first, all bulky silhouettes and aggressively unnecessary weight. Next comes a selection of medium armors that lean heavily into “grizzled mercenary who definitely has a tragic backstory,” followed by a number of outfits that appear to have been designed exclusively for stealthy rooftop lurking.

It’s as if whoever designed these decided that the easiest way to make something look complex was to simply… keep adding straps until the problem went away.

A bold strategy. A classic one.

Eight out of ten.

These are not armor.

Not really.

My ears perk slightly.

Extremely convenient. Massively practical. And, most importantly, it allows me to maintain proper aesthetics outside of combat without sacrificing performance.

A rare instance of both form and function aligning perfectly.

Good design decision.

I approve.

With renewed interest, I scroll more carefully this time, filtering out the aggressively over-armored, the unnecessarily edgy, and anything that looks like it would clash with my entirely reasonable sense of style.

Eventually—

I find it.
