Forests are like people—each one unique, yet all shaped by the same underlying structure. Just as it's rare to find a man born without four limbs, a head, and a torso, trees tend to follow a familiar form. Beyond that, though, no feature is quite alike. Straight or bent, lush or ghastly, crowded or sparse, forests are a unique bunch.

The one stretching before Kalsin was on the thicker side, with knee-high undergrowth covering the ground like a blanket. Ash woodlands, if he had to guess, but he wasn't an expert on the topic. He was, however, sure he was at the right place judging from the faint roar of rushing water reaching his ears.

"You've gotten lucky," Cinder said, one leg bouncing to an unheard rhythm.

Kalsin, who'd just finished telling his sister about the ambush, ignored the remark. "Short story short, we managed to flee united with our heretic friends and followed the fracture. Figured we could use a watch party, so we split up." He glanced back toward the empty boat dragged to the shore of the river, then back at Cinder. He drew up a brow. "But you probably know that part, considering you appeared not a minute after they left. How long have you been sulking here decorating the flowerbed, your Clennliness?"

Cinder muttered something too low for Kalsin to hear. Sap, drying off in the sun nearby, flattened his ears in response. A trustworthy signal that Cinder wasn't quite singing his praises.

Out loud, she said, "Threatening to murder the people we mean to ask about the cure, walking into an ambush during Blindlight, and to top it all off, that being an ambush you could've prevented by letting Jack kill the collector in the first place? That's an avalanche of disasters, Kalsin. Even for you."

Kalsin stood, stretching. He clicked his tongue for Sap to follow and started walking toward the tall mass of trees. "Yes to the first two. The last one, nope."

"Do elaborate," Cinder quipped in a sarcastic tone as she matched pace with Kalsin. "Because from here, it looks like a dead collector is a collector unable to pepper your pretty backside with arrows."

"Right now, they have nothing on us." Kalsin explained. "In the eyes of public law, we have done nothing wrong, or at least nothing the law wants to draw attention to. So long as our only crime is heresy, they'll keep things wrapped nice and tight with the least amount of people involved possible. We're but a handful of ideologues, a tiny fire they aim to keep under control without bringing about an audience. Lest those susceptible to rebellious ideas catch flame."

Kalsin took Cinder's silence as understanding, though still not appeased. "Now, if we killed the collector right in the middle of the region's capital, during celebration time?" He posed the question. "Then you're looking at a group of murderers with the blood of a high-standing overworlder on their hands. Identifiers of our scars would decorate every single notice board across Grenstel, if not the entire Republic."

"We're already past murder," Cinder said. "From what you've told me, several of the collector’s men died during the second ambush."

"Men with no names, no titles and no importance," Kalsin said with a nasty taste in his mouth. All this was speculation, of course. And killing Clenn never came easy to him. "Pawns they can afford to lose. I saw none use manifestation—all underworld hirelings, I'd bet. Nails. The lowest of the Clenn. They would gladly throw more our way until we're either dead or captured."

"How much longer can we weather the tide before we break?" Cinder asked quietly. "Another ambush? A dozen? They know about us, Kalsin, and like you said, the collector won't stop until he wins. I only see one solution for that. A permanent one."

Kalsin wanted to deny it, but he knew his sister was right. This was not a problem that would go away just by ignoring it. He remembered Quin, the torture, the casual manner in which he talked about dissecting them and the way he justified his actions. Which brought Kalsin to the pinnacle of his worries; did he want to deal with a fanatic reaching from under the shady side of the law, or face trial by daylight's justice? Evade Quin for as long as he could, or get him off the board and let the Civil House come after them in full force? Two bad options. And not making a decision would be a decision in itself.

Hence, his plan to get the killing done, and damn the consequences.

Leaves shushed, gently brushing their feet with sunlight streaming through the treetops high above as they delved through the forest. It wasn't long until the sound of rushing water overtook any and all attempts of birdsong, with moisture steadily gathering in the air, carrying a fresh and earthy scent.

"Did you bring it?" Kalsin asked, stepping over a thick knot of roots.

Cinder raised a sack seemingly out of nowhere with her one good hand, dangling it under Kalsin's nose. "A little decomposed, but eh, who cares. It's a sacrificial goat head, for gods’ sake."

Kalsin swatted it away, deciding he was better off without asking questions.

Soon they reached a river, too wide and not-at-all depthless to be mistaken for a fracture, then followed it until the forest opened up into a wide glade. The river disappeared at the edge of a twenty feet drop, where it formed a small lake below with a flat slab perched right in the center like a miniature island. Bathed in spraying water and sunlight, the rock gleamed wet, almost unnaturally bright.

"Well, that's an obvious spot if I've ever seen one," Cinder said.

Getting down proved to be fairly easy as the ground wasn't too steep around the waterfall, with vines to hold onto as they slid down. They didn't worry about Sap having to find his own way, and thankfully he trotted off to take a wide circle around instead of diving off the edge in water-loving bahten fashion.

"So what now?" Kalsin asked, standing at the lakeside as the misty water seeped through his clothes. Though the rumble wasn't deafening, they still had to raise their voices to hear one another.

"Now we see if we can squeeze some actual, old fashioned monster-hunting between all the excitement you've managed to stir up," Cinder said. She leaned against a tree, folding her arms in a way that hid her stump. "Today's special menu is a fossegrim. Shall I give you the rundown?"

Kalsin nodded. This part of their hunting routine was always the same. Cinder, being the knowledgeable one who spent all her waking hours studying the mythos, would give an outline of the dangers ahead. But even on rare occasions when Kalsin knew everything about the mythos they were about to face, he always made a point to stop and listen to his sister's report. Though Cinder didn't show it, Kalsin knew how much she loathed having to stay on the sidelines. She couldn't fight, and so she was determined to be useful in other ways. It was the most a one-armed person could do, and Kalsin would never take that away from her.

"Remember that pale womanoid creature we encountered at the borders of Araz two years back?" Cinder asked. "Idyllic little river? A copse of purple trees we lit on fire?"

"Hard to forget," Kalsin said, ignoring Cinder's flippant way of saying female mythos. "The diwata, I believe you called it." He could still hear the terrible screams as she burned alive.

"We're looking at a similar case. A seemingly friendly bugger who's, oddly enough, obsessed with music. Sources say he's a master of singing and instruments that vary between retellings. Lutes, bells, harps, flutes, and the likes. Some even say he holds the power of storytelling, able to paint such a vivid picture the images materialize in your mind as if you're in the midst of tales and legends."

"I'm guessing you reckon it's mostly bullshit."

Cinder half smirked. "When is it not? Anyway. The one constant is his behavior; he always tries to teach the human he encounters. Believe it or not, it's actually a running joke among jealous bards, claiming the skill of their rivals come from some supernatural blessing of a fossegrim instead of hard earned work. Truly fascinating shit."

"So where does that put me?" Kalsin asked, adjusting his sword sling.

"I'm afraid you'll have to forgo weapons." Cinder said, stepping behind him, her fingers running across him as she fiddled with the buckle. Kalsin allowed being disarmed without complaint.

If Cinder said he couldn't bring a sword, then he couldn't bring a sword. Simple as that.

Cinder explained further as she confiscated the knife in his boot as well. "He won't answer the summons feeling threatened, and if he can somehow sense dead goats being sacrificed from afar, I doubt sharp steel on your person is going to pass by his attention." She leaned close, her breath warm on his ears, hand making a tour one last time to make sure he was disarmed.

Stripped of his blades, Cinder gave his shoulder a playful shove. "There's a good lad. So, what are you planning in that dense head of yours?"

Leaving his weapons behind wasn't a hard decision for Kalsin to make, considering he hadn't been planning to outright fight the mythos. He was in a rather refreshing situation where, for a change, other people could do that job for him.

"I've got an enemy at my back and an enemy at my front. The former is unaware of my newly formed friendships, and the latter should assume I'm a friend. If an enemy of mine is also the enemy of my friends, and vice versa, then my currently known enemy and the friend who's really an enemy should assume they're enemies themselves."

"Okay, so in that equation, who is exactly what and who the what the fuck are you talking about?"

Kalsin patted Cinder back in return. "My line of thinking exactly."

"No need to be patronizing, you know," Cinder muttered. "I'm taking first dibs on his victims' riches, if there's any." She tossed the goat-head sack to Kalsin, who caught it against his chest. "Now, be a dear and swim onto that rock so you can begin."

Following his sister's instructions, Kalsin was soon standing on the wet slab in the middle of the lake, soaked from head to toe as he untied the mouth of the sack. He stole one last glance at Cinder's back disappearing in the forest. Carefully, ignoring the decaying stench, he gripped the goat's horn and turned it facing northward, holding it out over the roiling waters. For a moment, he doubted if anything would happen, but he then shook off the uncertainty and let go. The goat head plunged, disappearing under the waves.

Not a second after, a tall shadow fell on him.

He wasn't alone on the rock anymore.
