Ten minutes later, Craft had split the handle in half, and now he was carving out hollows on both ends to receive the limbs. Lei-rei and Dane were watching with anticipation, with Lei-rei eating cookies while slapping Dane’s hand away from the tray. Otto was going around collecting puzzle piece-shaped metal plates from the ground, the earlier ‘lava’ pools having cooled down.

Frankly, Craft was scared as shit about this design. Once it was done, on either end of the handle, two bow limbs each would be sticking out, held together only by lashing that would be wrapped around the handle. If the lashing were to come loose, the bow would deploy itself into a fancy quad-nunchuck.

His hands stopped moving. “Ah.”

“What’s wrong?” Lei-rei asked.

“I just realized I don’t have anything to lash this together with.” He sighed. He turned towards Dane. “You don’t happen to have some twine or something?”

“Y’know, I usually do, but — sorry, pal,” Dane said with a shrug.

“Usually,” Craft parroted. His head hung down. “Usually, huh.”

“How about this?” Lei-rei said. Craft looked up and found her pulling out some kind of wire saw from a pouch. … No, it was too thin to be a wire saw.

Craft realized what it was. “Is that a” —

“I haven’t killed anyone with it, no,” Lei-rei pre-empted.

It was a wire garrote. Craft received it sheepishly. He wasn’t surprised she had one, but the bits of dough clinging along the wire begged a different question.

He looked at Lei-rei and asked her by pointing at the crumbs. She raised an eyebrow, relaxing as she understood his question.

“Don’t worry, it’s a spare.”

“That’s not what I” — Craft sighed — “A spare? Do you just have these on you?”

“It turns out to be the perfect weapon for silently taking out unsupecting rolls of dough — multiples of them all at once, in fact.”

He could imagine it: Lei-rei, her cold eyes, and her cheek stained by a spot of cookie dough, as she whispered goodnight to collaborators of the Brownie Republic, massacring them with silver threads shimmering velvet and chocolate under a star-lit night (the star being mounted on a huge five-tier cake).

Well, wire’s strong, I guess. “I mean, sure, I’m taking it, but it’s too thin. I’m worried it might dig into the wood.”

“Ah, I’ve got some leather scraps. How ’bout that?” Dane chimed in.

Craft smiled. “That’ll be great, thanks.”

“Wait.” Lei-rei grabbed Dane’s arm just as he turned. “Will you be alright?”

“I’ll be fine, I’ll be fine!”

Dane jogged back to his carriage, leaving Craft wondering what that was about. He looked at Lei-rei. “What do you mean” —

There was the sound of things and boxes crashing inside Dane’s carriage. “I’m okay!” the man shouted before the two could say anything. He hopped down and jogged back to them, his skin dotted by glowing blue spots, but strips of leather in his hands regardless.

Lei-rei sighed. “Dane, find a Tetrist.”

He shook his head and waved his hand. “Bah! I gotta learn how to do it myself either way, right?”

Lei-rei dismissively chuckled. “Years of experience doing a poor job doesn’t count as experience.”

While Dane and Lei-rei bickered over the earlier’s work ethic, Craft got to work. He took one split of the handle and saddled a short and long limb into the hollows of each end, keeping the arrangement from falling apart with a jig of well-placed rocks. He then saddled the second split over everything from the opposite side, wrapping the splits together with leather, then the wire over the leather to really clamp it down. He used to use the same method to clamp hoses over pipes, though officially he’d have use a plier-like tool. For now, he improvised with the back of his knife and one of Lei-rei’s spare tactical knives to do the job.

It had to be a tactical knife, as those things were conveniently filled with holes to thread the wire through. Why were tactical things always full of holes? Anyway, it helped him execute a wire clamp.

It didn’t take more than a few minutes to clamp the handle in four places. Craft was proud of the rhythmic parallel arrangement of the wire around the handle. Very neat and shiny.

“That looks tight,” Lei-rei said. “I thought the wood would’ve exploded.”

“Glass can survive the bottom of the ocean, after all,” he replied.

He had to string the smaller secondary bow to the main bow next, but that was the easy part. … Wait, no — “I don’t have bowstring material.” Why was he like this? He sighed in the throes of frustration.

That got Dane and Lei-rei to stop their verbal sparring, looking towards Craft, one with regret and the other with worry.

“Sorry, buddy, I ain’t got any kind of string,” Dane said, but paused. “What kinda string you need, anyway?”

Craft shrugged. “Strong, light, not much stretch — but anything up from cotton, really. This is just a one-off build anyway.”

Some stretchiness might actually be better for shock absorption when working with fragile woods.

“Oh, sounds like you’d wanna take a look at spider silk sometime. There’s a place called Monstera — has an oversupply of the stuff.”

He thanked him and kept that in mind. Still, it meant he didn’t have anything to build with anymore. This was probably it for him and this project.

“I see you’re the type who only gets the things you need only when you need it,” Lei-rei said.

“Yeah, well, can’t help it.” He stood up and patted himself down. “I’m surprised you’d talk about yourself, though.”

“Touché.” She handed the cookie tray to Dane. He was happy for a moment, but there were no more cookies. Dane was sad. Lei-rei reached behind her head. “Well, you did your best. That’s what counts.”

The moment she took off her band and let her hair fall, Craft came upon an idea that would jeopardize their friendship.

Before year zero of Earth’s common calendar, frequent were the stories of women’s hair being used to weave the throwing arms of siege machines. Now, in Year 10,000 of Amatoria’s decimal calendar, Craft found himself on the verge of becoming part of the first such story ever told in this world.

By the Rules of Amatoria, Lei-rei would undoubtedly accept the request — but at what cost? He was no such animal to be driven purely by a craftsman’s instinct.

Lei-rei looked at him and tilted her head. “Craft? What is it? You look like you’re about to confess to your first crush.”

“Er, no” — he scratched his head — “how do I put this…”

“You’re even saying the same things.” She narrowed her eyes. “…Craft, I hazard to wonder about this, but” — her eyes nearly disappeared into an event horizon — “do you like me?”

Dane whistled. “Wow. Lei-rei. Never thought I’d see the day.”

“I have another garrote here. Would you like to experience being dough for a split second? You won’t feel a thing,” Lei-rei said without so much as turning towards him.

Dane raised his hands in surrender. “Whoa-kay, we’re all civilized folk here!” He looked around in a panic — and pointed at Otto. “Oh, would’ya look at that! Man’s havin’ trouble with his Hobby — guess I’ve got no choice but to help him out, huh!”

He ran away. Lei-rei huffed — the “yeah, that’s right, run away foo’” kind of huff.

Craft shook his head fervently. “That’s not it, that’s not it.” That would even be less awkward to talk about! “It’s just, er” — he halfheartedly pointed at Lei-rei’s hair — “that.”

She glanced behind her towards the uncertain direction he was pointing, and when she looked at him with growing confusion, that forced him to point at her hair a little more directly. She started pointing at things over her shoulder, but he shook his head. They found themselves playing artillery and observer as Craft kept doing all these hand signals to adjust Lei-rei’s point of aim — soon, a third dimension had to be added, as she could never have guessed that the target was literally a part of her.

With her arm raised from all the pointing, she realized the target may have been on her…arm. “This? It’s the Nightshade Damage Detection bracelet” —

“No — what?”

“What?”

Silence came between them.

“If she takes damage, this glows” —

“No, yeah — but why? ”

She craned her neck. “Because she lives alone? If she had agreed, I would have settled with a perimeter alarm system, but this was what we agreed on.”

Craft couldn’t quite digest the fact that Nightshade had preferred a personal monitor over a house alarm. What’s privacy between best friends, am I right?

Lei-rei huffed. “Craft, just use words,” she said with the patient disappointment of a first-grade teacher.

He hesitated. “Your hair.”

A pause lingered between them.

She finally pinched a lock of hair, and Craft nodded. “This?” she said.

“That, yes.”

“My hair?”

“Yes.”

“What about it?”

He falteringly flipped up his hand. “Well — bowstring material.”

Silence and pause met up between them and, after a good first meeting, agreed to go out on a date next time.

“Sorry?”

“Bowstring material.”

She thought about it…and shook her head. “I understand the words separately, and the phrase as a unit, but not the context as a whole.”

Craft thought life would be easier if Otto could punch him and let him respawn in the temple. Maybe then he could avoid this conversation for ten minutes. … Then again, that also meant extending his suffering by ten minutes. Funny how that worked. He bowed his head. “Hair is valid bowstring material.”

“Oh.” A moment passed…and she blushed. Indeed, there were some societies where donating hair to the local siege engineer was a normal thing to do, and by now, it was something she had heard about — along with other things like a lover giving away a lock of hair to the other going to war, preferably outside their farmstead while awaiting imperial army officials were watching.

…But what’s all that embarrassment in front of the abyss of archery! With unerring precision, she swiped the dagger from her hip and placed the blade against the roots of her hair — but she felt oddly frozen, and only then did she realize that Craft was oddly close.

He had stepped in and locked her arms, keeping her dagger from meeting her hair by just a scarce few inches.

“Y-you don’t have to,” Craft clarified. “It was just a weird flash of inspiration.”

“B-but” — she flipped her dagger around, allowing her to come at her hair from a different angle in a hammer grip, but even that, Craft foiled with a simple tug of her arm — “the bow! I’ve never shot one like that before!”

“I can finish it tomorrow!”

“I wanna shoot it now! Now!”

Rule Two had been invoked. Craft fell to a knee, though he still clung to Lei-rei’s arm. “Ha,” she said with the tiniest, smuggest grin, juggling her dagger to her free hand and soundlessly cutting away a lock of her hair. She crouched down with him, taking his hand and opening his palm, coiling her hair into it. “Don’t worry. It’ll grow back overnight,” she said.

That didn’t help him not feel weird about this whole thing. He looked at her. Her blush hadn’t gone away. “For the court’s record, I made every reasonable effort not to go down this route,” he said.

Lei-rei shook her head. “Strange thoughts don’t define your intentions, and I know your intentions very well.”

He raised an eyebrow. “And how could you tell whether I had a strange intention?”

She chuckled. “I’m the least romantic person in the world. Regardless of your intent, I’d deflect it without even knowing.”

He laughed. “That just makes the two of us.”

A sense of comradeship somehow displaced the weird atmosphere. “Hey, can I have your hand for a bit?” Craft said.

She extended it thinking it was going to be a handshake. He proceeded to use it as a tensioning post to weave her hair into string. Comradeship gave way to lowkey betrayal, but she didn’t complain. It was all, after all, for the sake of both their Hobbies.
