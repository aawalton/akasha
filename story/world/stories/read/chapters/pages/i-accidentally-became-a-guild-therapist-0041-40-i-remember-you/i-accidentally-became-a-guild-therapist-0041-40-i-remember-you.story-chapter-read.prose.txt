ARC III: THE WORLD REMEMBERS

SYNOPSIS: Day 18. The wildcard voting ends. Nyx investigates the unregistered account. The account sends a second message. And then Alondra walks into the planning room and says the thing she has been carrying for years— not to Livia . To Miles.

Livia read the numbers at 08:00 and felt nothing in particular about them. The gap was the gap. Yuki had done what she said she would do and done it well. That was not a problem. That was a person being who they were.

What she felt something about was the session request sitting in her portal marked unread since last night.

I remember you.

Account created four minutes before the message. No server record. Outside registered zones.

The system said: do not delete it.

I did not delete it.

I also did not reply.

I don’t know yet what replying means.

She went to find Nyx.

He was already in the corridor outside the planning room when she opened the door, which meant he had been waiting without announcing it, which was Nyx’s version of urgency.

His Voidmoth was with him. Its wings were slightly spread — the alert configuration, not the at-rest one.

“You saw it,” Livia said.

“I monitor the portal,” Nyx said. Not apologetic. Just factual. “For security. Since the LiviaFan69 incident.”

She had forgotten about the monitoring. She had probably not thought carefully enough about what monitoring meant in the hands of someone who had hacked real-world systems before the age of twenty.

“What did you find?” she asked.

He handed her his HUD projection. A clean layout — account trace, transmission path, origin node.

She read it.

“This is outside the server,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Completely outside. Not a registered zone, not a hidden zone, not a deprecated zone. Actually outside.”

“Yes.”

“That’s not possible.”

“I know.” He paused. “I traced the transmission path backward. It didn’t come from inside the server architecture. It came through a gap in the outer boundary — a very small gap, very specific, the kind that looks like a maintenance port but isn’t.” He looked at the projection. “Somebody on the other side of the server’s boundary knew exactly where to push.”

The other side of the server’s boundary.

The real world.

Someone in the real world sent me a session request.

Three words: I remember you.

I remember you from where.

From before the server.

From before Aethelgard.

From the world I was in when the motorcycle—

She stopped that thought.

Not because it was wrong.

Because it was too large to hold before she had more.

“Can you trace who sent it?” she asked.

Nyx was quiet for a moment. The Voidmoth’s wings adjusted.

“I found a partial ID,” he said. “Just a fragment. The transmission was designed to not be traceable. But…” he hesitated, which was unusual. Nyx did not hesitate about information. He hesitated about what information meant. “The fragment matches a pattern I’ve seen before. In real-world systems. Corporate. Tech sector.”

“Tech sector,” Livia said.

“The kind of company that builds things like Aethelgard,” Nyx said.

Silence.

[System Note: Transmission trace: confirmed external origin. Partial ID: redacted. The system notes: the system has also traced this transmission. The system notes: the system has more of the ID than Nyx does. The system notes: the system is choosing what to share and what to hold. The system notes: not yet. The system notes: the person who sent this message knows things about Livia that only someone in the real world could know. The system notes: the system notes: the system is not ready to say this out loud. The system notes: but the system knows. The system notes: the system has known for some time.]

“They might send another one,” Nyx said.

“I know.”

“If they do, I can get more of the ID.”

“I know.” She looked at the portal. The request was still there, unread marker blinking. “Don’t reply to it. Don’t trace it further yet. Just… watch it.”

Nyx nodded. He understood watching without acting. It was his primary skill set.

He turned to go. Then stopped.

“Livia.”

“Yeah.”

“Whoever sent it knew your name,” he said. “Your actual name. Not Empath Eternal. Not Buffer. Not NPC. Your name. Livia Marcelline Quinn.” He paused. “That’s not a name you get from the subreddit.”

He left.

The Voidmoth followed, wings folding back to rest.

Livia stood in the doorway of the planning room for a moment.

My actual name.

From outside the server.

From the real world.

Someone there knows I’m here.

Someone there remembers me.

She went inside and sat down.

The lamp was on. The armchair was neutral. The notepad was open.

She wrote one line at the top of a fresh page:

Who remembers me?

She did not answer it. She left it there, open, the way you left a question when you knew the answer was coming and needed to make sure you were ready.

mars—

[System Note: Second transmission received. External origin confirmed. Partial identifier: mars—. Transmission duration: 9 seconds before automatic closure. Then a third time.

marshmallow_thesis_2024.

Her hands were very still on the notepad.

She was aware, distantly, of Lumen drifting in the guild garden outside the window. Of the event leaderboard updating somewhere. Of the guild hall being its usual evening self.

She was aware of all of it and none of it touched her right now.

People in the real world miss me.

I didn’t know that.

I think I had decided, somewhere in the past nine months, that I was only real here.

That the world I came from was a place I had failed and left behind, deliberately or not.

She sat with that for a moment.

Nine months. She had been here nine months and had not noticed herself making that decision. Deciding the real world was over. That she had no one left to miss her or to miss.

It was a small grief to hold, quiet and familiar, like furniture in a room you had stopped noticing.

But someone there knows my name.

Someone there is finding gaps in server boundaries to say: we miss you. Please keep going.

Please keep going.

I’m already going.

I named my jellyfish Lumen.

I’m staying.

I’m… okay.

I need to find a way to say that back.

I don’t know if I can.

But I need to find a way.

She wrote it in the notepad, under the question she had left open this morning:

Who remembers me?

Someone named mars—. Someone who also said: please wake up. Someone who found a gap in a server boundary nine seconds wide to say: we miss you.

I don’t know their full name yet.

But I’m going to.

She was still in the planning room at 20:00 when she heard it.

Not loud. Not dramatic.

Just: two sets of footsteps in the east corridor, stopping.

Livia looked at the door. It was half-open — the planning room default. She could see the corridor from where she sat.

Miles. And Alondra.

At the midpoint of the corridor, the same place they always stopped.

But this time Alondra was not managing the encounter. She was not recalibrating in real time. She was standing still with the posture of someone who had decided something before they arrived and was now executing the decision.

Livia did not move.

She did not announce herself.

She was close enough to hear, and she chose to hear, and she chose this deliberately.

Alondra said: “Miles.”

He stopped. “Alondra.”

A beat. Then:

“There’s something I need to tell you,” Alondra said. “It’s about the operation six years ago. The one where you lost the Contract.”

Silence.

Miles was very still. The specific stillness she had learned to read: not neutral, not composed. The stillness of someone who had just heard something they did not expect and whose control was deciding, in real time, how to hold it.

“You knew,” he said. His voice was flat. Not accusatory. The flat of someone who was verifying a fact.

“No,” Alondra said. “I didn’t know what he was going to do. I told him where you’d be. I thought I was…” She stopped. Started again with the precise, difficult language Livia recognized from session three. “I believed something about him that wasn’t true. And I told him something I shouldn’t have. And I’ve been carrying that for all this time.”

Another silence. Longer.

Livia’s pen was still on the page. She was not writing. She was not breathing loudly. She was just present, in the half-open doorway of the planning room, witness to the thing that had been inevitable since Alondra had walked into session one with a binder full of frameworks she had built to avoid saying exactly this.

Miles said: “Who?”

“Caelric,” Alondra said.

One word. His name. And then Alondra waited.

Miles did not move for a long time. The Emberwing on his shoulder was very still, fire dimmed to almost nothing, the way fire dimmed when it was waiting for a decision.

Then he said, very quietly: “Did you know what he was?”

“No,” she said. “I thought he was…” the word cost her, “…someone who saw me.”

Miles looked at her for a long moment.

Then he looked away. Down the corridor. At nothing.

“Thank you for telling me,” he said.

Alondra’s breath came out slowly. Not relief exactly. The release of something very heavy being set down.

“I’m sorry,” she said. “I know that—”

“I know,” Miles said. “I know you are.” He was still looking away. “I need…” he stopped. “I need a moment.”

“Of course,” Alondra said.

She walked away down the corridor. Her footsteps were even. She did not look back.

Miles stood at the midpoint of the east corridor, alone, for a long time.

The Emberwing’s fire came back slowly. Not the full flame — just the ember. The lowest setting. Something waiting to decide what it was going to be.

Livia watched him from the half-open door.

She did not go to him.

This was not a session. He had not asked. He needed the moment he had said he needed, and her job right now was to give it to him and be available if he turned around.

He did not turn around.

After a while, he walked away. The opposite direction from Alondra. Toward the main hall, toward the exit, toward wherever he went when he needed to be alone.

Livia sat in the planning room.

The lamp was on.

The armchair was neutral.
