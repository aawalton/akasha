Chapter 103

Korr-Va rode at the front of his small entourage, and the southern road ran out before him under a sky that could not decide whether to clear or to close again.

The trees along the path stood half-bare. Wind had stripped most of the upper leaves and left the lower ones curled and brown on the branches, and the ground beside the trail was soft from rain that had come and gone in the night. His horse moved at the steady working pace he had set hours before, the breath of the animal coming in small white clouds, the leather of the saddle creaking under him with each long stride. Behind him, his two Clawborn kept the same distance they always kept. One length back. Spears across the saddles. The sound of three sets of hooves on damp ground.

It was the last tribute run of the season.

Hoshin was the third village on his circuit. The first two had paid. The grain had been low at the second, and he had taken a man's smallest finger for it, and the rest had come faster after that, the way it always came faster after. He had ridden out of the second village with the wagons full and the people standing very still in their square and the smell of the burned hut still on his sleeve, and that should have settled him for the rest of the day.

It had not.

The news rode in his head and would not be put down.

He had heard it at the second village from a Clawborn courier passing south, a young man with a fresh wound at his shoulder who had not stopped to dismount and had spoken from the saddle in the common tongue, low, fast, before he rode on. The northern force had taken losses. Not a defeat. A bleeding. Whole companies thinned in a single engagement at a place the courier had not named. The Empress was furious. The citadel was tightening. The shortfall would not be one shortfall now, but two, and the second one would have to be made up from the tribute lines before the snow closed the passes.

Korr-Va had taken the news without changing his face. He had nodded once at the courier and watched him ride south, and then he had mounted up and turned his own horses toward Hoshin, and the news had stayed.

It sat behind his eyes like a hot stone.

He thought of the Clawborn dead in the north. He thought of the names he did not know, men he had drunk with in the lower garrisons, men who had cut their claw marks the same year he had cut his. He thought of the empire's enemies, whoever they were, sitting on a hill somewhere counting Clawborn bodies, and he felt the burn rise from the back of his throat into his face and stay there. His hands tightened on the reins until the leather creaked. His jaw locked. The horse felt the change in his weight and broke its rhythm for a single stride before he set it back to the working pace with a small motion of his knee.

The Hoshin would pay.

Not for what they had done. They had not done anything. They would pay because the empire needed paying, and they were on his road today, and the burn in his face needed somewhere to go. The thought steadied him. He breathed out slow through his nose. The horse settled. The Clawborn behind him did not speak.

The trees opened and path bent down through a shallow draw and out into the cleared ground around the village, and the gate of Hoshin stood ahead, the rough timber posts and the woven fence between them, and the square inside it visible in pieces between the huts. He saw the shapes of villagers along the edges of the square. He saw the tribute mat of woven reeds laid out in the middle of the open ground the way it had been laid out on every visit for as long as he had been coming to this village.

He did not see the tribute; the mat was empty.

The mat was clean and dry and freshly swept, and there was nothing on it. No baskets of ore. No bundles of cut wood. No sacks of grain. The mat sat in the middle of the square the way a table is set before a meal that has not been brought.

Korr-Va did not slow.

He rode through the open gate at the same working pace. The Clawborn came in behind him. The villagers along the edges did not look up. Their heads were down. Their hands hung at their sides. He saw Tama standing at her usual place near the mat, and she did not look up either, and the burn in his face climbed higher and stayed.

He rode to within four paces of her and stopped.

The silence in the square was the silence of a held breath.

He dismounted. He did not step down the way he stepped down on the visits he was bored at. He swung his leg back over the saddle and dropped, both boots together, and the weight of him hit the packed dirt of the square with a heavy crunching sound that carried across the open ground and into the silence behind it. The crunch was the only sound. No villager moved. No horse stamped. The wind that had been moving through the trees outside had died at the gate as if it too were waiting.

He walked the four paces to Tama.

She was looking at the dirt in front of his boots. Her hands were folded in front of her at her waist. Her face was lowered, and from where he stood, he could see only the top of her head and the line of her shoulders and the fold of her hands, and he could feel the steadiness in her, the planted weight in her stance, and the burn behind his eyes went past hot and into white.

"WHERE IS IT!"

The shout left him before he had chosen the words. It tore out of his chest and broke against the silence of the square and there was no echo to bring it back, because the village swallowed sound the way villages did, but the air after it was somehow louder than the air before.

Tama did not raise her head.

"There is no tribute," she said.

Her voice was quiet. It was not the quiet of fear. It was the quiet of a thing decided.

Korr-Va stepped back from her.

The world tilted at the edges. He felt his feet leave the ground in small jumps, his arms going up, his shoulders rolling, the body moving before the mind could catch it, the way it had moved at the second village when the boy had said the wrong word, the way it moved when the rage came up out of him and needed to be somewhere. He bounced on the balls of his feet. His arms swung.

"WHAT!"

The word came out of him three times before he heard himself.

The Clawborn had not dismounted. They sat their horses at the gate and watched him without expression, the way they watched him when he became this thing he became, and Tama still did not look up, and the villagers along the edges still had not moved.

He stopped jumping. He stood and breathed and let the air come back into his chest in long heaves, and his eyes went over the village in slow sweeps. The huts. The faces along the edges. The lowered heads. He counted them without meaning to. He weighed them.

"Then I take some of you as the tribute!"

He started his walk.

He moved counter-clockwise around the inner ring at a pace that was not a pace, more of a prowl, his shoulders rolling, his head tipped forward. He stopped in front of the first cluster. He looked into the faces of the people there one by one. They did not lift their eyes. He bent close to a young man and he snorted at him, a short hard breath through the nose, the way a hunting dog snorted at a thing it had found, and the young man did not flinch.

He moved on.

He did it at the second cluster. He did it at the third. He prowled and he leaned and he snorted, and at each cluster the villagers held their ground and did not look up, and somewhere in the back of his head, under the burn and behind the hot stone of the northern news, a small clear part of him registered that this was wrong, that on every other visit there had been weeping by the third cluster, that on every other visit the first child had been pushed forward by its mother by now, that this was not the silence of fear, this was a different silence.

He pushed the thought down.

He kept walking.

He made it three quarters of the way around the ring.

"No."

The word came from behind him.

He stopped.

It was Tama's voice. She had not moved. He could feel her at the middle of the square behind him, in the same place she had been when he had walked away from her, and her head was still down, he was sure of it without looking, and she had said the word the way a person closes a door.

He turned.

He did it fast. He stomped his feet around in a half circle and brought himself to face her, and his boots hit the packed dirt with sounds that were meant to be heard, and he crossed the ground between them at a heavy aggressive walk that ate the distance in five strides.

"WHAT NO!"

Tama did not move.

"No," she said. "You take no one."

He looked at her. He looked past her at his two Clawborn at the gate. The Clawborn were watching now, sitting forward in their saddles, the dullness gone out of their faces. He looked back at Tama, and the laugh came out of him because nothing else would, a hard short laugh, and after a beat he heard the laughter come from the gate as well, his two men joining him because that was what they did when he laughed, but it was a different laugh from theirs, theirs was the laugh of men watching a play they did not understand.

His own laugh was the laugh of a man who had not yet decided which part of her to cut first.

"Clawborn take," he said. "You give."

"No," Tama said. "No more."

He opened his hands. He turned in a small slow circle in front of her with his hands open, looking at the villagers along the edges, looking at his Clawborn, looking at the empty mat behind her, as if to ask the world to confirm what he was hearing. The world did not answer.

He stepped back to her.

"You no learn," he said, "or you forget. I teach again?"

He shaped the common tongue carefully. He did not always bother. He bothered now because he wanted her to hear each word.

Tama lifted her head.

She looked up into his eyes for the first time since he had ridden through the gate. Her eyes were brown and calm and they were not the eyes of the woman he had pushed aside on the last visit. They were not the eyes of the woman whose tribute pile he had walked past. They were the eyes of someone he had not met before.

"No," she said.

He stepped forward and put his forehead against hers and pushed.

"Yes."

He held it for the count of two. He felt the small steady pressure of her forehead pushing back against his. He stepped away.

He laughed.

It was a big laugh this time, a chest laugh, the kind he used in the lower garrisons when a man told a joke about a southerner, and he turned his back on her and walked it back to his horse with his shoulders rolling. He reached up to the saddle and pulled the long knife out of its sheath, the blade the length of his forearm, the edge dark with oil, and he turned with it in his hand and walked back across the square at the same loose laughing pace.

"You learn. I teach you."

Tama looked up into his eyes.

"No," she said. "You learn now."

His head tilted.

The laugh died in his chest. His eyes narrowed. Something in the angle of her shoulders, in the lift of her chin, in the steadiness of her hands at her waist, was wrong in a way he could not name. He tipped his head to the side like a confused dog trying to process what he was seeing.

The silence shattered with the crack of a whip.
