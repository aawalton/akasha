I fought to keep my breathing even as I stared up at the dull white thing above me. The full-body scanner was too cramped. It felt like there was barely enough space in there for me, like I could somehow get jammed in there and never be able to escape. That wasn’t possible, though. The machine was open at the end. I knew it was, even if I couldn’t lift my head to check.

A long clunk came from directly in front of me, and my body jolted, but I refused to let myself react any more than that. I should have been used to the unexpected noises that it made by then. I’d been in the machine many times before, but it still made me jump every single year.

There was a quiet whirring noise, and I shut my eyes instinctively, knowing what was about to happen. Even with my eyes closed, the tube was so bright it burned. I longed to lift my hands and attempt to block out the light, but I forced myself to stay still. If I moved, it would mess with the results, and I’d just be stuck in the machine for even longer.

A series of loud, mechanical bangs came from around me, and the urge to open my eyes or flinch away from them became even stronger. My body was frozen in place, though. No matter how fast my mind began to race, my body would not move.

After what felt like an eternity, the light grew dim again, and the noises stopped. I waited anxiously, not wanting to get my hopes up in case the doctor needed to run more tests or get some more images. That happened sometimes. It wasn’t always a bad sign, I tried to tell myself. Maybe I just didn’t hold my breath for long enough or something.

I was starting to get worried. My anxiety had just begun to grow again when a new sound filled my ears. My heart leapt as I heard the telltale buzz of the machine powering down.

“Alright, Daffodil,” the doctor’s voice came from near my feet. “All done!”

The bed below me jerked slightly before sliding smoothly out of the machine. I blinked, my eyes struggling to adjust to how much darker the rest of the examination room was in comparison to the machine.

“Was everything okay?” I couldn’t help but ask.

The doctor’s smile widened slightly as she held a hand out to help me sit up.

“Yes, Daf. Everything’s fine,” she told me. “Do you want to get dressed again before we discuss your results?”

My mind turned blank. My thoughts stuttered to a halt before beginning to race, and I stared up at Doctor Tulip in shock.

“My results?” I repeated. “It’s time?”

“It is.”

“But…” I started before trailing off as my voice deserted me. A strange combination of terror and excitement, stronger than I’d ever felt before, warred within me. “Do I not have any more tests to do?”

I didn’t want to do them, not really. Part of me was hoping for it, though. I still didn’t really remember much about the world, but I did know that I’d been working towards that exact moment for so long, and I wanted one last chance to prove myself before it was too late. Nothing I did after that would matter. It wouldn’t impact my results.

The doctor chuckled softly, and an amused yet kind noise came from the medical assistance robot, as if it were trying to laugh as well.

“No,” Doctor Tulip said. “That was the last of your medical testing, and you finished all of the psychological testing last week. There’s nothing more we need from you. Unless…”

I stared at the doctor, trying to work out where she was going with that, but I couldn’t.

“Unless?” I prompted.

“Is there a specific reason you want to do any of the examinations again?” she asked, her tone perfectly even.

It was a trick. She’d phrased it to sound like a normal question, but there was a correct answer, and I knew it.

“No,” I replied, trying not to sound too rushed as I climbed off the bed of the machine. “Nothing more than a normal uncertainty that comes from the unknown.”

I had no clue where that came from. It felt strange and alien in my mouth, as if they weren’t truly my own, but they had the intended effect. The doctor’s expression seemed to soften slightly, and she nodded.

“That’s normal, Daf. Everyone gets a little nervous sometimes, especially when facing a situation like this, but everything will be fine,” she promised me. “Why don’t you get changed, and then we’ll go over everything?”

The urge to refuse rose within me, almost reaching my lips before I was able to claw it back. I just wanted to hear whatever the doctor was going to tell me, but I forced myself to nod, glancing down at the thin dress I was wearing. It looked strangely similar to a hospital gown that I’d seen on television in real life, and I knew I couldn’t stay in it.

That would be the wrong decision. It was fine to be a little anxious and impatient, but I had to show that I had control over it. I needed to have mastery over my emotions. It was fine to experience them. Everyone did, after all. They were a good and necessary part of the human experience, but I couldn’t allow them to rule my life.

“That sounds good,” I replied.

“Great.”

The doctor turned away from me, moving back towards the giant screen on her desk, and I fought the desire to follow her. She didn’t even glance back at me, though. Even once she’d sat down in her chair again and began typing on the keyboard, she didn’t look at me.

I longed to see what was written on the screen. I wanted to read whatever notes she was hurrying to make, to know whether I’d passed her tests or not, but I had to do as she said. I needed to get dressed, and then I’d be told everything.

Well, not everything, but I’d have some answers, and that would be enough. It had to be. I had no other choices, and there was nothing I could do to find out everything. Maybe that would happen in time, but there was no guarantee, I told myself, not quite understanding why.

The medical assistance robot drifted closer to me, snapping me out of my thoughts, and I hurried to untie the strings that kept my medical gown closed. I’d just been doing nothing and staring at the doctor for too long, I realised as I pulled my arms free of the thin material and slipped it off my body. I had to follow her instructions quicker.

“Thank you,” the medical assistance robot said as I bundled the gown up and placed it on the tray it was holding.

“Thank you,” I muttered, unable to stop myself.

Some of the older generations didn’t always like it when we thanked the robots. They said there was no need to do it, but that was becoming less of a thing. I couldn’t recall the specifics, but I was sure I’d been told off for it as a child.

Maybe it was an old teacher, I thought as I stepped into my underwear again. Someone had definitely told me it was pointless to thank them, that they were just doing what they were programmed to do, and it was like thanking a door for opening, but that was stupid. It felt stupid, and I didn’t really care whether the medical assistance robot had been programmed to help me or not. It just didn’t feel right to ignore it.

The fabric of my jumpsuit felt strange beneath my fingers as I began to pull it on. It was unlike anything I’d ever felt before in real life. Even though I wore underclothes, just like everyone else, the inner lining was almost impossibly soft. I couldn’t help but run my fingertips over it, wanting to burn the texture into my brain.

My eyes flicked towards the unblinking medical assistance robot, and a faint fear bubbled in the pit of my stomach as I slid the zip on my jumpsuit up as quickly as I could. I was acting weird. I knew I was. I wore that jumpsuit every single day. I had ever since I’d grown out of my last jumpsuit, and that one had been lined with the same material. They all were. I must have felt it hundreds of times. Thousands, even.

“All dressed?” Doctor Tulip called.

I glanced over at her, my anxiety rising. She wasn’t even looking at me, though. Her gaze was still fixed on the screen.

“Yes,” I replied, shoving my feet into my boots and buckling them up.

“Great,” the doctor said, finally turning towards me. “Come sit down.”

My heart pounded in my throat, and I had to swallow as fear gripped me. I felt frozen to the spot, utterly unable to move, and suddenly, I didn’t want to. I didn’t want to hear whatever the doctor was about to tell me. I wanted to turn back time, to be younger again. It had happened too fast. I’d grown up too quickly, and I wasn’t ready.

But I had to be. There was nothing I could do to slow it down or prevent the doctor from telling me whatever she was about to tell me. It was my sixteen birthday, and that meant it was too late. All I could do was listen to what she had to say.

My body moved automatically. I could barely feel it as I crossed the room slowly and sunk into the padded chair across from her. It felt as though I wasn’t actually in my body, as if I were floating above it or only half in the world. My mind felt hazy and blurry, and my ears were ringing.

I couldn’t breathe. My chest was too tight, I realised distantly. It felt as though the air had been sucked out of the room, like there wasn’t enough in there for both Doctor Tulip and me. Only one of us could survive, and of course, it had to be her.

Maybe that was what was happening, my brain suggested. Perhaps the tests had discovered that there was something wrong with me, or maybe they’d just realised I wasn’t worth the resources to keep alive. I didn’t contribute to the running of the ship and never would, so they might as well just put me out of my misery before I became even more of a drain on the people who lived there.

It did make sense, after all. I couldn’t disagree with that, no matter how much I wanted to. The ship was small. There was only a limited amount of space and resources. Everything had to be carefully planned to ensure that people had enough to survive, and the colony continued onwards, so if I was nothing more than a burden, there was no need to keep me alive.

Perhaps it had already happened. Maybe the medical assistance robot injected something into me during the blood test, and I didn’t even notice. I had been too distracted. Doctor Tulip had talked more than usual. She’d explained everything that they were testing for and how the tests were run, and it had been fascinating, but perhaps she’d only been talking to distract me whilst the robot killed me.

Surely, that would be a waste, though. Each life on the ship was so carefully chosen. Each person was so thoughtfully raised. Something must have gone horribly wrong for them to decide that I did not deserve to be alive.

“Would you like a drink?” Doctor Tulip asked, her voice sounding strangely distant to my ears.

“No, thank you,” I forced myself to say.

I did need a drink, though. My throat was so dry that it was hard to even swallow, but I couldn’t bear the thought of drinking anything. I was pretty sure that if I did, I’d be sick. The cup of water I’d had in the waiting room already seemed to be sloshing around in my stomach, and I had to press my lips together tightly to keep it from reappearing.

“Okay, great,” the doctor said. “As I’m sure you’re probably aware, before we discuss your results, I need to give the usual spiel about our purpose.”

Her tone was light, playful almost, and it took me a moment to realise she was waiting for me to respond. She hadn’t asked a question, though. There was nothing really for me to say or object to. It was part of the process, and I knew that, no matter how much I wished she’d just hurry up and explain whether I was dying or had a life ahead of me.

“Of course,” I replied, unable to think of anything else.

Doctor Tulip smiled brightly.

“Fantastic! Many years ago, our ancestors did not live amongst the stars. Instead, they lived upon a planet that provided them with everything they could possibly need. They had fresh food that grew on the trees, animals that wandered the land, and clean water that sprung forth from the rocks,” she started. “But the humans living upon that planet learnt a valuable lesson that cost the lives of many; corruption shall destroy all.”

Her words sent a shiver through me, and I had to bite my lip to hold back the questions that threatened to escape. I could feel myself getting sucked into the doctor’s story. It seemed as though she’d told it hundreds of times throughout her life, and I was almost certain I’d heard it before too, but I could still feel myself getting sucked in. I couldn’t stop myself from leaning forward in my clear, eager to hear more.

“Greed and ignorance tore through the land, and soon, the planet began to die,” the doctor continued. “Many died along with it, and it became clear that Earth could no longer sustain life. We needed to turn elsewhere, to find a new world where we could start over with the knowledge gifted to us from the Earth’s sacrifice.”

She paused for a moment, and something stirred within me. I needed to say something. There was a correct response to her words that I’d been taught before.

“We shall not make the same mistakes again,” I promised, my voice shaking slightly from the nerves.

“We shall not,” Doctor Tulip agreed, her tone sorrowful. “Twelve other ships had left before ours, and none was successful. Alpha suffered a severe electrical fault just four months into their journey, and there were no survivors.”

“We honour their sacrifices,” I hurried to say as the doctor paused to take a breath.

The smile that pulled at her lips told me I’d made the right decision by choosing to speak once more. I wasn’t sure what prompted me to do it. Just like before, the words spilled from my lips before I could stop them.

“We do. May their memories live on in the hearts of all the lives their sacrifice helped preserve,” Doctor Tulip said before continuing her speech. “Beta managed to make it further before failure, and Gamma reached the edge of our old solar system before a brutal solar flare destroyed the shields, causing irreparable damage. Once more, there were no survivors.”

She fell silent, bowing her head in mourning, and I hurried to join her, but my mind was racing. The intrigue, the fascination, that I felt about the world and what had happened to it was almost overpowering. It had managed to block out some of the terror I’d felt before, and I hadn’t even noticed how much easier it was to breathe. My attention was too fixated on Doctor Tulip, and I just wanted her to continue talking.

My head remained bowed as I watched her out of the corner of my eye. I longed to hear more, but for some reason, I knew that I had to wait until I saw her move first. It would be disrespectful not to, and I couldn’t have that.

Finally, after what felt like far too long, she raised her chin and looked at me once more. I felt the urge to say something burning within me again, but no words came to mind. I couldn’t think of anything appropriately respectful to say, so instead, I forced myself to remain silent until Doctor Tulip began to speak once more.

“Delta was the first success, as far as we are aware. Two years into their journey, our ancestors lost contact with them, but we believe they are still out there. Perhaps when we find a new haven, they shall already be there,” she said, an almost wistful edge entering her voice.

“May the stars lead us both there.”

I wasn’t sure what that meant, but Doctor Tulip’s smile grew a little wider.

“And the distant suns illuminate our passage,” she responded, taking a breath before continuing. “After that first successful mission, several more took to the stars before ours. We received reports of three more unsuccessful flights, but five reached the edge of our solar system, and we believe that they are still out there, searching for a new home for us all.”

I couldn’t stop myself from nodding eagerly. Throughout my childhood, we’d been told stories of the six successful ships that left Earth before ours, and I’d always wondered what happened to them. Had the people on board truly survived? Or had they simply made it further than the first few before falling victim to the cruelness of space?

That was possible. I knew it was, but I liked to believe they were still out there. Some of the other kids said it was unlikely. They told me the others were dead, but that didn’t stop me from dreaming about the day we finally found a new planet that was just like Earth. I spent countless nights imagining the moment when we landed and finally left the ship I’d lived upon for my whole life, only to be welcomed by them.

The thought almost brought a tear to my eyes.

“However, that hope must be balanced with the possibility that they did not survive,” Doctor Tulip said, causing my heart to sink. “We must also prepare for that eventuality.”

No one had ever said that before. They’d always told us they were still out there, and I didn’t know what to say. I didn’t know how to react to that. The others were still alive. They had to be, didn’t they?

Or was that just a lie they told the kids to stop them from feeling so alone?

“How?” I heard myself ask. “How do we prepare for that?”

I may have been mistaken, but it seemed as though pride flashed through Doctor Tulip’s eyes. My head was spinning too much for me to pay any attention to that, though.

“By looking after ourselves and ensuring our own survival,” she told me. “Even if the other ships did survive and we are reunited, it may be several years before that happens. Generations could pass before we find a planet to call our own, and we need to make sure we survive until then.”

My mind wanted to reject her words. I wanted to believe that she was wrong, that it would not take more than weeks, maybe even a few years, before we found the haven we had all dreamt of, but I knew it may not be that easy. Our ancestors had left Earth too long ago. They had been searching for so long, and it was hard to ignore the possibility that nowhere would be safe for us.

“Of course,” I muttered.

“And by following the rules set forth by the governor and those who came before us,” Doctor Tulip continued. “They foresaw this possibility. They anticipated that we may be alone for many years to come, that the survival of the entire human race may fall on our shoulders, and that is a burden we all must bear.”

I nodded, but fear had begun to build within me again. I’d never really considered that. In either world. In my reality, it didn’t really matter. The planet was dying, but it was slower. It was unlikely that I’d need to do anything in my lifetime to ensure the continuation of humanity, but on the ship, it was different.

Maybe I’d always known. In a way, I’d always understood my responsibilities. That had never really been hidden from us, and perhaps that should have helped, but I still found myself at a loss for words as the true reality of that pressure, of that burden, revealed itself to me.

“But of course, none shall shoulder that weight alone,” Doctor Tulip told me. “We all carry it together, and that makes it easier to bear.”

“Of course,” I said, aware that I was repeating myself, but I couldn’t think of anything else to say.

“Great!” Doctor Tulip said, her voice suddenly becoming bright. “Are you ready to receive your results?”
