The quill is mightier than the sword. Lira’s father used to say that often, so it was ironic, in a cruel way, that he would die by the sword.

They took his head in the public square and left his only daughter to the mercy of a city that had none.

Stories like his were not rare in Halthar. Gareth Velora was a good man, a careful man, ground down by a world that rewarded neither goodness nor care.

Lira Velora still remembered him as something greater than the world deserved.

She remembered the small things the most. The way he turned pages with his ring finger instead of his index, careful not to smear ink across the margins. The way he whispered translations under his breath, testing the words before committing them to ink. To him, language was an art form that needed tender care, with the patience of a stone carver working on mosaics.

He had brought her into his world as early as one could, placing a quill in her hand as soon as she could hold one. Letters had been her toys, and knowledge her inheritance.

She had loved it all with a child’s devotion, never imagining that it would become the thing that destroyed them.

The workshop smelled of old paper, candlewax, and the faint metallic tang of ink. Shelves lined the walls, sagging under the weight of manuscripts and scrolls, some newly commissioned, others forgotten works. Light from a window fell across the central desk, where father and daughter worked side by side.

Lira’s hand moved with care across a sheet of vellum, her quill tracing precise loops and elegant flourishes. Next to her, Gareth worked on a larger manuscript, its pages dense with unfamiliar script.

“Steady your hand, Lira,” he said without looking up. “If you rush, the ink will blot.”

“I’m not rushing,” she replied, her voice tight with focus.

She finished the final line and lifted the quill. The closing words curved across the page in careful calligraphy, the last stroke tapering into a clean, deliberate finish.

Her father let out a quiet chuckle. “You have a gift, my girl. One day, you will surpass even me.”

Lira allowed herself a small smile. Praise from her father was rare and always welcome.

The moment did not last.

A knock at the door cut through the room, sharp and deliberate.

Gareth’s expression changed at once. The softness vanished, replaced by something harder, more guarded. He did not need to raise his voice.

“Lira, fetch the ledger.”

It was not a request.

They had practiced this before. The ledger was no ledger at all, its hollowed pages designed to swallow anything that should not be seen. Loose sheets, half-finished translations, forbidden commissions, all of it disappeared within its worn cover.

That day, it held something worse.

A lexicon of a dead language, filled with meanings that were not meant to be understood.

Lira moved quickly, her heartbeat rising as she gathered the scattered pages and slipped them inside. She then hid the ledger in a false drawer in the kitchen. By the time she returned, her father had opened the door.

The man who stood there did not belong in their workshop.

His clothes were too fine, his posture too rigid, his face sharp, and his eyes colder still.

Lord Kenth Kelroth.

“Master Velora,” he said, his voice smooth and measured. “How goes the translation?”

Gareth inclined his head. “It is nearly complete, my lord. However, I must caution you again. The nature of this text...”

Kelroth raised a hand, silencing him.

“This is not your concern,” he said. “Your concern is to finish what you were paid to do.”

He stepped closer, his shadow stretching across the desk.

“Quickly, and quietly.”

Lira tightened her grip on the doorframe as she watched from the corner. Her father’s jaw set, but he nodded.

“I will be back tomorrow,” said the lord as he left.

The door closed, and the room felt smaller for it.

That night, Gareth did not sleep.

He worked as though time itself had turned against him, his hands trembling with fatigue and his eyes red from strain.

Lira tried to help, but there was little she could do. She sharpened quills, replaced candles, and watched as her father pushed himself further than she had ever seen.

Morning came too soon.

Lord Kelroth returned.

This time, her father was ready for him. Lira watched as the noble took both the ancient manuscript and her father’s translated copy.

His face turned with glee, as if he had finally found what he had been looking for all his life. He dropped a coin purse on the counter and left.

Gareth sighed in relief as the lord left their workshop, grateful to be rid of him and his forbidden manuscript.

Sadly, that relief was premature because barely five minutes after the lord left, the door broke open as guards flooded the workshop.

“By order of the crown, Gareth Velora, you are under arrest for the possession and dissemination of banned texts.”

Steel and authority filled every corner as shelves were overturned, papers scattered, and the careful order of their lives reduced to chaos in moments.

Gareth did not resist. He stood at the center of it all, already surrounded.

They forced him to his knees.

Lira could not move at first, fear rooting her in place, barely breathing.

Then her mind found something to hold onto.

The ledger.

With the manuscript gone, the ledger was the only thing linking her father to the banned text. If they did not find it, there would be no proof.

Her father had spent the night in his room. The ledger would still be there, hidden among his things.

She moved away. In the bedroom, she found the ledger on her father’s desk, where anyone could see it. She pulled it close, hiding it beneath her robe, and ran. She slipped through the back. Her steps were silent, and her heart was pounding so loudly she was certain it would give her away.

She ran without looking back, clinging to a single thought as if it were the truth.

Without the ledger, they would have nothing. Without proof, her father would be released.

That was the law.

By the next day, she understood how wrong she had been.

The square was full.

As people gathered for another public execution, the crowd watched with the detached interest of those who knew it would never be them, and Lira stood among them, small and unseen, the ledger pressed tightly against her chest.

Her father was brought forward in chains.

He did not look for her.

That was the last kindness he could offer.

Lord Kenth Kelroth himself read the sentence.

The outcome had already been decided long before any of it began.

The blade fell.

At age 12, Lira Velora learned the truth her father never had.

The quill is not mightier than the sword.

And the sword is not the true power either.

The true king is coin.

Coin buys the quill.Coin hires the sword.Coin buries the truth.

And when someone like Lord Kelroth decides a man must die, then he dies.

Truth does not matter.Proof does not matter.Only payment does.
