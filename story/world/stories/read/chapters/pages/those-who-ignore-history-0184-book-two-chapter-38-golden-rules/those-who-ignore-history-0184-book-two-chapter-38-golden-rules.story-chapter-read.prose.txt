I had been inside my soul before. The paper sun burned above a world of glass and shimmer, folding upon itself like a page in a great cosmic book. Around it drifted the crystal forests, the silver asteroids, the fragile fragments of memory orbiting in perfect, luminous silence. It had always felt like standing inside a dream made of paper and light—fragile, eternal, and alive.

But this wasn’t that.

The paper sun was there, still hanging like a torn halo in the sky, yet its warmth was gone. Its glow had thinned into something hollow, a flickering phantom of radiance. The air felt wrong too—dry and heavy, as if the space itself had been drained of color.

“Where… am I?”

The question rang out, but it wasn’t alone. It was my voice, multiplied, distorted, echoed through a thousand throats. Every syllable overlapped with versions of myself I remembered from the trials. The mirror world. The dream that had eaten me alive.

“Who’s there?!”

The chorus answered again, not with reply but with repetition, a storm of overlapping tones that spun and warped until I couldn’t tell which of them was truly mine.

Then, the dark changed.

It didn’t open like a door or shatter like glass—it simply wasn’t dark anymore. I blinked, and I was seated. A chair beneath me. A long surface before me. The air smelled faintly of metal, dust, and something acrid, like aether that had gone sour. The world around me was made of lines and symmetry: walls too smooth, lights too white, shadows that didn’t know where to fall.

It reminded me of the temples of Marr, but stripped of all art or warmth. There were no sigils, no icons, no scent of incense or oil. Only polished planes and humming light.

A table stretched out before me—black and perfect, like obsidian polished to a mirror. Nineteen chairs lined it, and each was already filled.

They were all men. Or the memory of men.

Faceless things in fine attire: coats of deep gray, gold cords around their throats, and skin as pale and smooth as sculpted porcelain. Their features refused to take shape, and yet I felt watched. Each tilt of their heads, each still fold of their hands carried an unspoken familiarity.

The silence was so complete that I thought I might hear my own thoughts bleed into theirs. Then, as one, they turned to me.

“Welcome back, Alex.”

The voices overlapped, nineteen threads woven into a single sound. It wasn’t cruel. It wasn’t kind. It was patient—measured, practiced, like an administrator greeting an old colleague who had been gone too long.

“Been awhile.”

My pulse quickened. My chair felt smaller than before. I couldn’t shake the feeling that I was in the presence of something that understood me in ways I didn’t.

“What is this place?” I asked.

“To answer, we would have to go back,” one of them said, though his voice was indistinguishable from the rest. “To the day you were advised to seek the Archimedes Principle.”

Another leaned forward. “Or to the day your father outwitted us in a game of riddles.”

Their heads shifted slightly, like a forest of trees bending in a single wind.

“But to answer you directly,” said the first voice again, “this is what remains of the world we came from.”

The lights above flickered, and suddenly I saw it—the reflection of another world superimposed upon the sterile space. Towers of black glass clawed at the sky. Streets pulsed with streaks of gold and violet. The world was alive with light and noise, a hive of motion and purpose, yet suffocating in its order.

It reminded me of that other realm I’d glimpsed once—the neon domain of the Viraloid, where machines ruled men and men called it progress.

“This world,” they said together, “was built by those who believed that power was best kept in ledgers. That empires could be managed with numbers, not swords.”

I stared at the long table, at the golden cords cinched neatly around their necks. They looked too much like nooses to ignore. “You were part of it,” I said quietly.

“All of us were,” they replied. “We were architects. Executives. Planners. Builders of systems that kept the world alive, or at least convinced it was living. When our time ended, fragments of us were bound within a Skillcube. The one you consumed.”

“Gluttony of the Golden Hydra,” I whispered.

At once, every faceless head turned toward me. “Yes,” they said in unison. “That was our name, before it became yours.”

My throat tightened. The memory of that first absorption still lingered: the pain, the hunger, the endless mouths whispering through my mind. It had taken everything I had to survive that integration, to keep myself from being consumed. And yet here they were—what remained of those countless voices, organized, waiting, civilized.

“You were supposed to be gone,” I said.

A ripple of laughter spread down the table—dry, humorless.

“Gone?” said one of them. “No, Alexander. When you take something into yourself, it doesn’t disappear. It adapts. It hides in forms your mind can understand. What you see now is simply the shape your soul gave us.”

I looked around again. The sharp lines, the sterile light, the hum of unseen devices. I didn’t understand what it was, but my soul apparently did. That frightened me more than anything.

“What do you want?” I asked.

The one closest to me leaned forward. “We want what you want. Ascension. Continuity. Survival. You came here seeking the roots of your bloodline, to change what you are. That means facing what you’ve already consumed.”

Another voice—softer, steadier—spoke from farther down the line. “Each time you absorb a fragment, it leaves a residue. You call it memory, or instinct, or dream. But it is more than that. It is identity. You have taken ours, Alexander. Yet you never truly listened to what we were saying.”

Their words pressed against me, heavy and slow. I remembered the storm of voices within the cube, all of them shouting, begging, demanding. I had thought them incoherent. Perhaps they weren’t.

“You’re saying I ignored you?”

“We are saying,” they replied as one, “that you feared what listening would make you become.”

For a long moment, no one spoke. The only sound was the faint hum in the walls—the heartbeat of whatever system governed this strange, reflective place.

Finally, I asked, “Then why bring me here?”

The voices answered together, a chorus that filled the vast, soundless room. “To remind you that evolution is not consumption. You do not grow stronger by swallowing without understanding. The Hydra devoured itself because it mistook hunger for purpose.”

The words hung in the still air, their echo rippling like waves across a black sea. For a moment, I thought that was all they would say—but another voice spoke, different in tone, older somehow.

“You neglect to use our talent,” it said. “Yes, we consume, but we learned—consumption without understanding means nothing. You are afraid, little heir. Afraid of devouring artifacts, grimoires, Skillcubes, scrolls, anything. Everything except the trivial. You nibble at coins and crumbs of magic because you fear that one true meal would be too much. You fear that if you swallow power, it will swallow you in turn.”

The others shifted slightly, like a ripple through a hive-mind. One faceless man tilted his head. Another tapped his finger once against the black table, the sound impossibly loud in the dead air.

A third voice spoke then, quieter, steadier. “Alex. We do want what you want. But we also understand what we are. We are not your rivals. We are not parasites. We are a remnant of a time long gone. Our body has long since turned to dust, our scales scattered across forgotten seas. Our soul—what remains of it—sleeps inside that cube you claimed. We have no need for your body. We have no desire to take what is yours.”

The faceless figure closest to me leaned forward. “Do not be afraid to use our gift. We are yours, as much as you are ours.”

Then, in perfect unison, they spoke again, nineteen voices folding together into a single sound. “So we’ll return the question you asked us. Why are you here? And don’t lie. Especially here. Especially now.”

Their tone left no room for deceit. The weight of their combined will pressed against me, crushing the air from my lungs. There was no pretending in this place. No distance between thought and speech.

I let out a long breath that shuddered on its way out. “I’m weak,” I said at last. “I’m pathetic. I’m a rabbit.”

A few of the figures tilted their heads, as if listening closer.

“I’m surrounded by people who were born stronger, trained better, bred for power. My siblings—my mother—everyone I’ve ever known has been more than me. Even the things I’ve killed seem to live on stronger than I do.”

I swallowed hard. “Each of you… you’re draconic in nature, right? Or—no, not quite. You’re a dragon. A gestalt, a single entity split nineteen ways. You devoured gods and worlds, became a myth, and died for it. You were everything I’m not.”

Silence again. The kind that felt like it watched.

“I’ve gained new power,” I continued. “Barbra’s methods, Gin’s lessons, the Hydra’s blood—but I’m still scared. Scared of the old power I already have. Scared of the new one waiting inside me. Every time I reach for it, I feel that hunger. That endless, gnawing pit that wants and wants and never stops.”

I clenched my fists, looking down at the gleaming black table. My reflection was faint—blurred by the strange light of this place.

“I want to evolve,” I said. “I want to be feared. I don’t want to be the rabbit that runs and hides and apologizes for being alive. I want to be the thing that the predators look at and hesitate.”

I paused, struggling to find the right word. Something more than strength. Something more than survival.

“I want to be…” My voice faltered, then steadied. “I want to be a Hydra.”

For a heartbeat, nothing moved. The silence stretched so long that I began to think I had said something wrong—something they would reject. But then, faintly, the air trembled with a low, resonant sound. Laughter.

It wasn’t mocking. Not exactly. It was the laughter of something ancient remembering what it once was.

“Good,” one of them said.

“Very good,” said another.

“You speak at last as one of us,” said a third.

The voices rose in overlapping cadence, a choir of thought.

“Do you think we were born gods? We were not. We were creatures of appetite, cast into the dark. We devoured stars not because we needed them, but because we feared the void that came after hunger.”

“We learned, too late, that hunger without comprehension is nothing but decay.”

“You fear your appetite, Alexander. That is not weakness. That is awareness. But fear must serve you, not bind you.”

The light above dimmed, the false sun flickering like paper catching fire. Shadows deepened beneath the table, stretching upward, climbing the faceless figures like ink bleeding through cloth.

One by one, their forms began to shift. The suits and ties melted away. Their heads split open into crowns of horns. Their torsos stretched, scales emerging where flesh had been smooth. Wings—vast and broken—unfurled into the room, each pair larger than the space should have allowed.

When they spoke again, their voices were no longer human. They were thunder.

“You call yourself rabbit, but we remember when we were worms,” the hydra-voices said. “We remember the first thing we consumed. Our own tail. It is in the act of devouring what we fear most that we transcend it.”

The table cracked down the center, and a golden light poured from the fissure.

“Do you understand, little heir?”

I tried to speak, but the air seared my throat.

“You fear to consume because you think it will erase you. But every power is an echo of something older, and every echo reshapes the one who listens. You are not meant to remain pure. You are meant to remain whole.”

The word whole reverberated through my bones.

I looked around at them—at the nineteen half-draconic, half-human remnants of something far older than myself—and for the first time, I didn’t see gods. I saw fragments. Shattered reflections of something that once understood its own hunger.

And maybe, I thought, that was what they wanted me to see.

I straightened slowly, the trembling in my hands subsiding. “Then teach me,” I said. “Teach me to understand it.”

The hydra-heads smiled, or perhaps the shadows simply bent that way.

“Not teach,” they said. “Remember.”

The golden light beneath the table flared, and the faceless figures dissolved into it, their shapes melting into a single vast form that loomed behind me—wings outstretched, eyes burning like suns.

Then the world split open.

I felt the paper sun shatter. The crystal forest burned to dust. The void roared, and for the briefest instant, I thought I heard their final whisper, overlapping as they sank into me once more:

“Become what you already are.”
