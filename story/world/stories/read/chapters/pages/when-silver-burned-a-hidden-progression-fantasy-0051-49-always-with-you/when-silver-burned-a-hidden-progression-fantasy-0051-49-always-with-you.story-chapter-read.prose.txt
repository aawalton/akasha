The night had carried them farther than Kalsin’s legs cared to measure. Eastward first, five miles or more, before the company bent northward like a line of hunted deer. The wall lay to their side now, far enough away for the curvature of the earth to hide it from their gaze, and the Republic with it. Ahead stretched only the red tundras—damp, heaving plains under a bruised sky.

They marched without pause. Only when the winds had sagged into a soft breeze and the first light of dawn crowned the horizon did the company slow, and then finally, eventually, stop.

The halt was a quiet affair, more from uncertainty than from exhaustion. Fals was the first to kneel and begin tugging canvas out of her pack, muttering at Littleguy until he shuffled over to help. Quiescent was already off by himself, carrying his sleeping bag under his arm and, apparently, not at all bothered to sleep out in the wet flatlands.

Two tents went up—standard issue, compact and dependable little soldiering packages. Pike made a point of hammering down every stake twice, joking about Veiled scouts and how they liked to crawl inside a man’s bed if he let even the tiniest crack open in his shelter. Shoreline had nothing to add, only a set of steady hands as she held a pole upright, eyes squinting up at the overcast purple clouds. She caught a piece of jerky when Cyanfish tossed it over. It was more chewy than dry, from the looks of it.

Kalsin led Sap and Hollow over to a tiny hairline fracture just barely visible across a dry patch of land. They drank, their serpentine tongues just barely able to scoop liquid out of the crack. After making sure they didn’t mind the taste of red grass, he left them to graze.

Most of the company had trickled inside the tents by then.

Kalsin did not join them. He stayed out in the murky dawn, watching the poles with their clothes strung up to dry, the fabric snapping in the breeze like makeshift flags. His body was still chugging along, and he didn’t think sleep would come for him if he decided to lay down.

It really was beautiful out here.

He wasn’t sure when Jack had joined him. He became aware of her arm slung across his hips at some point, holding him gently and watching the tundras with him.

“It’s not what either of us expected. Hm?”

Jack extended a hand, half framing the sunrise between two fingers, evaluating. She then made a so-so gesture. Been there, done that.

“I remember back in Gulling, right, I remember how you were beating the ever loving hell out of the adults in the training ring. The only reason I had the guts to vault over that fence was because I figured you’d go easy on someone closer to your age.”

Jack huffed with amusement. She indicated a height that barely reached her waist.

“I know. Don’t get me wrong, you were damn impressive. But more than anything, it spoke to your character that you proceeded to beat up a younger, scrawnier kid. Made me think to myself; ‘that's the morally bankrupt kinda gal I want protecting my back’.”

Kalsin jumped when she pinched his butt. He looked up to see her grinning down at him with a wolfish smile, as if eyeing a sheep too adorable to eat.

The damn girl was getting too bold for her own good.

And he—he felt his chest flutter with joy as he realized he could finally look at her without one little imperfection souring the whole picture. Her hair was a mess. Muck colored one side of her cheek. She was stunning in the morning light.

Wolves had keen senses, Kalsin read somewhere. They could smell blood in the air from a good distance away. The beasts could learn from his bodyguard.

Her lips descended on his like the first bite of a predator trying to catch a skittish antelope before it could hop away. Kalsin felt himself unable to react at first, thinking to himself with the absurd hilarity of it—did she just surge-kiss him?

Then the kiss turned softer, more questing and tentative, prodding for a response. He gave it. Fucking hell, did he respond the way he wanted to for a very long time now.

He slid one hand on her hips, the other curling around her neck to pull her further down into him. She let out a pleased little breath in response, but she somehow forced the pace to remain slow. It wasn't a contest like everything was with Ringtail. It was more tender. More—intimate.

Kalsin loved her for it.

His fingers trailed up her spine, feeling each knot of muscle that soldiering life had carved into her. She tilted her head, trailing down to the corner of his mouth. When she finally broke away, it was only to let her teeth graze his bottom lip before pulling back just enough to see his face.

Her eyes were half-lidded, amused. She tapped his chest with two fingers. Breathe, boy.

Kalsin sucked in air like he’d just surfaced from deepdiving a fracture. “Gods, Jack.”

She huffed silent laughter, gently straightening the front of his jacket. Her hands lingered there a moment more before she shifted a step back, tilting her chin toward the tents. Sleep. She was telling him he ought to try.

“Not yet,” Kalsin said. He looked out over the sea of grass. He could see her standing there in the distance.

Jack dug into his hair with mock sternness, tossing his noggin to the side.

“I will, Jack. Just an hour more, I swear. You know how I get when there’s something on my mind.”

She understood, he thought. The way her expression turned wistful at first, then just a little bit sad. She put two fingers to her lips, then a hand over her heart, watching him.

I’m sorry, Kalsin. Sorry that I never told you.

Kalsin felt emptiness try to claw its way up his throat. He ignored it, pulling Jack close one more time in a firm embrace. “It’s okay. That’s the point of moving forward, right? There comes a point where you just can’t afford to ignore shit anymore.” He backed away then, hands held up with a faint smile. “I’ll deal with my problems the way I should have done years ago. I dunno what’ll come of it… but make sure to leave some room in your bedroll for me, eh?”

His bodyguard’s tall form slowly retreated toward their grazing bahten. Just them and the open sky. She’d probably be snoozing by the time he returned.

His own path led the opposite way, quiet steps carrying him toward the direction of the rising sun. He found a knuckled rise of land protruding above a hairline fracture. He sat, heels digging into the soft mud, and listened to the faint birdsong carried on the slow morning breeze.

“Földfutó,” Cinder said.

Kalsin looked at his sister sitting next to him, legs crossed and her arm resting on her lap.

“Those little birds the Veiled flung at us during their assault, with the screw-like beaks?” she mused, listening to the uneven chirps. “Földfutó, is what they’re called. They have these weird spirals lining the inside of their mouths that scramble the sounds.”

“Where’d you even hear that?”

“Eh. You know, here and there. I’ve had a lot of time in libraries while you were off playing find-the-cure.”

Cinder picked a blade of the red grass and twirled it between her fingers. The stalk bled color into her skin, leaving faint pink streaks on her thumb. “They use the spirals to signal their mate coordinates, letting them know where the ground is soft enough to dig a nest. The recipient dives, then, if they’re interested in the lady bird. The deeper he burrows, the better their prospects to make a family. It’s kinda romantic.”

“So you’re telling me our glorious army got harried by weaponized romantic songbirds.”

Her mouth tugged at the corner, a smile not quite forming. “I guess so. Maybe that’s what the little critters thought as they burrowed into righteous Republic soil behind our backs. ‘To claim this land for the missus.’ And who knows. Maybe some of them did, afterwards.”

Kalsin leaned back, craning his neck to see where the murky clouds broke and the sky began. No such luck. It was going to be one of those damp autumn days. A good time to be cooped up inside somewhere warm and dry.

Downwind at the camp, he caught sight of Wardoll emerging from one of the tents. She was barefoot, her pale feet sinking into the wet earth as she padded toward the drying clothes. She wore nothing but her tunic, sleeves rolled halfway up her arms as she chose a pair of socks and, after deeming them dry enough, ducked back inside.

“I like them,” Cinder said.

“Who?”

“Your mates. This little regiment that you ended up dragging along with yourself.” She sounded dreamy. “You could have done a lot worse when it comes to folk watching your back. It’s better this way, I think.”

“Hm.”

“Better than running off with just Jack and a pair of scaly horses, I mean.”

Kalsin muttered, still watching the tundras, “You don’t have to pretend for my sake, Cinder.”

“I’m not pretending.”

“They treat you like shit.”

Cinder remained silent.

“Hatred… hatred or spite or resentment, those I can understand to a degree. The collective debt Clenn have racked up against our kind is high enough, and no one expects centuries of abuse to heal in a fortnight. I get that,” Kalsin said slowly. “But I hoped, you know. I hoped that once they learned the truth—that a way back finally existed—they’d abandon this twisted mirror outlook of theirs. Their scars could be wiped clean, their surges restored, their paths to advancement open again. I thought that just knowing this fact, just seeing the possibility, would be enough for them to treat the Clenn stranger among them like she damn well existed!” His voice cracked into a near shout by the end.

Kalsin closed his eyes for a minute, feeling the damp earth beneath him. He was an idiot; a small part of him still clinged to that young, idealistic ember buried deep inside him. A tiny fragment of the child that life had beaten down again and again survived, despite every blow hammering home the reality that goodness in the human collective did not naturally occur.

His mind knew that. He’d been convincing himself of this fact for many, many years now. But maybe his heart was not yet callous enough to accept it. And that’s why it hurt.

“It’s not their fault.”

Kalsin opened his eyes. He looked at his sister with a stony expression.

“It’s not, Kal.” She fidgeted in place. She was not meeting his eyes. “How would they know at first glance I was Clenn? Most of ‘em are from Újtavak, probably. From Határ and Grenstel. For folk around those regions, displaying scars is not part of their culture. Even if they…” She faltered for a moment. “What I’m trying to say is; they would have no way of identifying me as Clenn at first glance. I’d be just a girl to them. Seriously. Think about it longer than just five seconds.”

Kalsin threw his hands up. “Then why, hm? Why’s it so hard for people to simply tell you ‘good day’.”

Cinder kept staring at the ground.

“You know why.”

Kalsin opened his mouth. No. No, I don’t, he wanted to say. But he couldn’t ignore the little details tugging insistently at the back of his mind. Somewhere in his brain, there was a box filled with a dusty collection of oddities he had just carelessly tossed things inside.

Inconsistencies. Things that didn’t quite make sense. Like the fine print at the bottom of a contract, there were… specifics that somehow a traumatized mind had filed off in his subconscious. ‘Do not touch!’, the label read.

Better to stay ignorant. Because ignorant was still functional.

Kalsin slowly began to circle the box. “You’re scaring me, Cinder,” he murmured.

“I know,” he heard her voice, sad. “I know, and I hate this. I hoped that we could have gone without this conversation forever and ever. It would be wonderful if I could simply let you close your eyes and believe everything was still the way it had always been.”

He felt her hand grip his leg, and he saw her lean in, pleading. “And it will! You hear me, Kal? This changes nothing between us. I’m here, and I’m not going anywhere. All you need to do is accept that… all that’s left is for you to realize that…” her voice cut off, broken.

Kalsin stood with the lid open above the box. Just a crack, a tiny opening to see through.

She stopped speaking as if she knew exactly what he was looking at.

The box was full of things about her. Starting at the top with the latest memories he had been through, dumped in here for disposal.

"Madness," Pike muttered. His eyes scoured the storm, looking ahead and only finding rain, rain, and more rain. The man did not seem to be at ease following the commands of a lunatic. But stranger things have happened tonight, right? Maybe the company figured it was time for a leap of faith.

Next image.

She slowly reached up and touched a cold finger against the scar between his eyes. His first scar, the one he’d taken so so long ago. “Oh, Kalsin. I’m so, so sorry. Other people can’t— they could never… Because I’m…”

Another one. Jack’s words.

There are some things that I’m purposefully not saying here. It’s not the time and not the place for that, I don’t think—it probably has to do something with your mysterious talent. You’ve already been through so much, and I don’t want to add another bucketload of trouble to all that. Later. There will be time for that later.

From farther back, sneaking out from camp during those early soldiering days.

Cinder snapped off a nearby branch. "Well, it's not like I asked her. She still, you know. Treats me as if I..." She fiddled with the leaves, tearing off one or another. Kalsin could tell something was nagging at her. Cinder butchered another set of leaves. She sneaked a glance at Kalsin, an intense and thoughtful look. As if holding back something important. As if wondering if he was ready for it.

Deeper in the box, then.

"What are you two, anyway?" Kalsin asked, sitting around a campfire with Jack, Áron, and Erazza. "The collector referred to you as siblings. Around the darkfinder, you claimed you were a couple. I don't mean to intrude, but setting the record straight would be appreciated."

Kalsin swore he could hear Cinder’s mirthful snort coming from somewhere inside the forest.

More. Grab them faster.

"Did you bring it?" Kalsin asked, stepping over a thick knot of roots.

Cinder raised a sack seemingly out of nowhere with her one good hand, dangling it under Kalsin's nose. "A little decomposed, but eh, who cares. It's a sacrificial goat head, for gods’ sake."

They hurt. Every single one of them.

"Heretics primed to flee the city under Blindlight via darkfinder, hive. Intersect at O;NW;two-fifty-two. Optics advised."

Cinder was at his shoulder in an instant, eyeing the note. She let out a snort. "Just our luck. The message we're looking for ends up being the coded one."

…Jack stared at Kalsin with a strange look on her face as Cinder rambled on.

The box was deep. So very deep.

Before Kalsin could speak, Cinder said, "I heard."

Kalsin made a noncommittal hmph in reply. Jack shifted uneasily. He didn't need to look at his bodyguard; she always wore the same expression around Cinder. He knew it by heart. Her brow crinkled, her lips hung slightly downturned. She never gave a definitive answer why she treated his sister the way she did.

Like she wasn’t even there.

It went on and on, memories piling up on top of each other. Stretching back years, lining all of his adulthood with small things and details that were easy to miss. A strange glance here when he listened to her ramble, an offhand comment there when he ordered for three in a place rough enough to not care about Scarrd-Clenn propriety.

But more glaring were the lack of things. The absence of words said. He rummaged through the box, tossing and clawing at pictures to find a single moment where anyone else—anyone had acknowledged her. A greeting. A smile. An exchange that indicated a presence from anyone other than him.

He couldn’t find one.

These scenes, they were little things. Fragments you didn’t know you were missing from the whole picture. It was easy to think they were inconsequential. Unless you knew exactly what you were looking for.

But if she’s not here—Kalsin screamed in his mind, tearing deeper and deeper into the box—then where is she?

Because she was there, at some point. She did exist. She had a family; a mom and dad and people who loved her very much. Friends, and a life.

What happened to my sister?

He felt the bottom of the box before he reached it. It was a dusty, forgotten thing. Scabbed several times over, like someone had been tearing it up and letting it heal wrong again and again. The wood was faded, and the walls that rose at the sides contained everything else in a sturdy little frame.

The box itself was the memory. To seal away the hurt and compartmentalize.

And Kalsin remembered.

Relief squeezed his chest when his neighborhood came into sight. He barely noticed the last threads of his shoe give way, a hole tearing open at his toes. He was home. He pushed himself to keep going, but it took effort to keep himself together for just another minute. Kalsin brushed his nose, sniffing. His hand came away with flakes of dried blood.

The door to their house was locked. Unsure if his voice still worked, he knocked instead. He waited, shifting from foot to foot. Once again he was at the edge of collapsing, the wait worse than the walk, but somehow he managed to stay upright. He knocked again, louder this time. It sent a sharp pain along his knuckles.

The door was suddenly wrenched open, his father standing in the entrance with a stormy expression, but his shout died on his lips as he looked down at Kalsin. His eyes were red and bloodshot as if he hadn't slept a minute. Kalsin shivered under the gaze that was filled with horror and disbelief. Faint crying came from deeper inside.

Terrin was blocking the doorway, but Kalsin could see a bit inside. There was a bundle of pelts laid out in the entrance hall. Mera sat on the lowest stair, clutching something to herself with a blonde mop, rocking back and forth. The body that connected to the head, laying on the pelts, dragged with her movements like a ragdoll.

His mother cried on.

…

Back in the alley, Kalsin’s body was locked in a seizure, sprawled in the dirt after he had touched her scar. The thief was looking down at him in the dark, speaking words of disdain that he couldn't hear. She spit on him. She took his coin pouch from the ground, the knife along with it, and disappeared into the night.

Just before she turned the corner, he could have sworn that the ugly scar between her eyes, the one she had just forced him to touch—it was no longer there.

…

It took him some time to realize Anise had a hand on his shoulder, shaking. "Kalsin? Kalsin, can you hear me?"

He looked into her eyes. They were damp, but she wasn't crying. He nodded, not trusting his voice. When he started turning back to his sister's still form, Anise stopped him, holding his head in place. "Here, now. Everything's alright. Kalsin, it's okay." She smiled tightly. "Cinder is going to be okay."

Kalsin looked from one eye to another, sometimes lost in the wrinkles in-between. Searching for assurance in those old, knowing eyes. "She is?" he asked.

"She's fine, love. Trust me. She will be fine. Your sister just needs some sleep."

Kalsin stared down at his sister, laying still on the cold cellar floor. Her eyes were open, staring up blindly at the ceiling. The severed stump of her arm never bled—but the puddles beneath the barrels were thicker than wine.

"She's fine, love. Trust me. She will be fine.”

“Trust me. She will be fine.”

“She will be fine.”

“Cinder will be fine.”

“She’s alive.”

“She has to be.”

“Go, now. You've done well. Go home, I'll take care of her. Get some fresh air, clear your head, will you?”

”Enough excitement for one day.”

”Enough pain for one day.”

”Enough terror for one day.”

The box was empty.

Kalsin opened his eyes.

Under the dawning tundras, the thing wearing his dead sister’s face smiled back at him.
