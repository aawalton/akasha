“Is everything ready?” Viktor asked.

[Yes, Master. I have made all the preparations you asked of me.]

“How is the situation at the cathedral?”

[Sebekton and the others have managed to fend off the first assault, but the titan has just birthed a fresh batch of fire golems. They are coming again.]

That was more or less what he had feared. So it was confirmed, then. This battle of attrition, one he had hoped would grind the enemy down before it ever reached this place, was not a fight he was going to win. And that meant there wasn’t any room left for hesitation. He had no choice but to go through with this.

He turned to Galatea. “Let’s go.”

She said nothing in response, only nodding once, before drifting forward to fall in beside him as he made his way toward the door.

Her face had lost its usual glow. Figuratively, and literally. Well, he supposed he couldn’t really blame her for that, given what he was asking her to do.

“I know that I’m putting you at serious risk in doing this—”

“Don’t talk like it’s someone else’s problem, Master. While it’s true that I’m the one who will speak the word, I have no doubt you will be the one to bear the worst of whatever comes after. And, as I’ve already told you many times, we have absolutely no idea what will happen.”

That much was true. Invoking a god was a dangerous and thoroughly unpredictable business. The only point of reference he had to go on was the story Galatea once told him, about the Prophet who had recklessly spoken his own god’s name aloud and, as a result, ended up permanently redecorating the room he was in.

But it was not as though Viktor had a choice here. Waiting for Xander to run dry of whatever divine power fueled that oversized body of his was simply not an option. Sebekton’s line of defense would be overrun long before such a thing ever came to pass. Hence, he needed a way to bring down an avatar of a god. And as it turned out, the only method he could think of was, rather inconveniently, to invoke another.

At the very least, he still had “Rekindled Ember” to fall back on. He was not walking into this entirely defenseless against whatever catastrophe he was about to personally invite into his dungeon. Probably.

Stepping through the threshold, he found Kazyk waiting for him outside.

“Master...” the gremlin boss said, his voice betraying a hesitation that was very uncharacteristic of him.

“Kazyk,” Viktor said, allowing himself a chuckle at his subordinate’s unexpected unease. “Is everything ready?”

“Yes, Master. But... are you sure you want to go through with this?”

Now even Kazyk was concerned about safety. I really am about to do something insanely stupid, aren’t I?

“I am,” Viktor said. “Celeste and Galatea already tried their best to talk me out of it, by the way. So don’t bother.”

“Very well, then.” Kazyk let out a deep exhale. “Follow me, Master.”

The gremlin led him and Galatea to where a massive slab of meat lay waiting, its slimy, glistening skin crawling with suckers that pulsed as though the thing were somehow still alive despite having been severed from whatever it once belonged to. Galatea grimaced the moment her eyes fell upon it.

That was a section carved from the colossal tentacle the Acolytes of the Deep had summoned, something they called the Hand of God, a fragment of the Great One’s own body brought into this world.

Yes, this was an essential part of the plan. The true name of a god was merely a trigger, after all, so it required divine residue close at hand to actually take effect. And what could serve that purpose better than a piece of a god’s own living flesh?

At Kazyk’s prompt, the nearby gnolls hauled the massive piece of meat up, dragged it over, and began strapping it to him. Several ropes went round and round in tight, overlapping coils until they were sure the tentacle had been firmly fastened to his body.

He intended to use the thing as a divine bomb of sorts, but that required it to reach the titan more or less intact. Simply lobbing it at that walking mountain of fire would not do; the meat would be thoroughly cooked well before it ever closed the distance. Thus, for his plan to have any hope of succeeding, the tentacle needed to survive the trip, which meant someone had to go with it to protect it from harm. And, of course, the somebody in question was going to be him. Now, if that were the way of it, there was little sense in flying separately from the piece. Better to strap it to himself and be done with it.

Of course, having a mass of that size lashed to his body was not going to do wonders for his flight. In normal circumstances, the wind gems would have sufficed, but today, he needed far finer control than that.

Fortunately, he happened to have exactly the right tool for the job.

Once the piece of meat had been strapped firmly to his body, once every last knot had been checked and rechecked to make certain nothing would come loose, the gnolls lifted him up bodily from the ground. He could feel their rough hands gripping the tentacle meat bound tightly against him, the slimy mass pressing cold and wet against his back through the fabric. Now and then a sucker twitched, and he couldn’t quite help the involuntary flinch that followed.

Without further ceremony, his minions carried him away. The stone floor stretched out wide and flat around him as they went, and high above, the trebuchet came into view. A towering arm. A massive counterweight. A long sling that waited in patient, motionless silence.

Viktor chuckled. When he had first decided to have the thing built, it had never once crossed his mind that he would ever use it like this.

Galatea drifted along at his right, Kazyk walked at his left, and both of them wore the particular expression of people who were desperately racking their brains for some argument, any argument, that might convince him to abandon this reckless plan of his, and failed to find any.

The gnolls brought him to the cradle. Up close like this, the pouch looked considerably bigger than it had from a distance. Well, of course it did. It was large enough to hold a boulder, after all, so naturally there would be more than enough room to accommodate a man bound to a god’s meat.

They lowered him carefully into it. Despite their effort, the tentacle still let out a wet, unpleasant squelch as it settled.

“Everything set?” Viktor asked.

“Yes, Master.” Kazyk nodded. “Just push the lever, and you’ll be on your way.”

With that, everyone quickly retreated to a safe distance from the siege weapon. Well, everyone except Galatea. Being a projection, she could neither obstruct the machine nor be struck by it. And so, she simply hovered above him, gazing down in silence at the man lying in the cradle of the trebuchet.

“Any last advice?” he asked, grinning. “I’m about to go through the same thing you’ve endured in that charming little drowned temple. So a few words of wisdom from my predecessor would be much appreciated.”

She didn’t grin back. “Don’t do this.”

“Too bad then,” he said with a shrug.

He raised a hand, palm to the lever, and let loose a compressed blast of wind.

Iron groaned, chains rattled, and the counterweight dropped.

The world lurched as the arm swung upward with the enthusiasm of something utterly unfamiliar with the concept of “gentle.” An invisible force pressed him deep into the leather cradle, flattening his chest and squeezing the breath from his lungs. Wind roared in his ears until it drowned out every other sound in existence, and for one long, dizzying moment, all sense of direction abandoned him. Up, down, sideways, none of it meant anything. There was only violence, with him in the middle of it.

Then, he was airborne.

His stomach churned, twisting sharply enough that he had to fight down a rising urge to vomit right there in midair.

This was, beyond question, not recommended. Without the slightest shadow of a doubt, not the right way to use a trebuchet. Small wonder that even Kazyk, hardly famous for his restraint, had never thought to attempt this.

Oh, well. Whatever.

It would have been great if Celeste could simply have teleported him and his grisly cargo directly at the titan and been done with it. But teleportation came with that unwelcome stasis effect. There would be a few seconds after arrival during which he would be little more than a sitting target, and appearing close to a god’s avatar in such a state would have been suicide. Alternatively, he could be teleported to somewhere farther off, but then he would have to fly the last stretch of the journey entirely on his own, which was also the part that mattered the most, and that defeated the entire point of teleportation.

So here he was. Whatever else could be said about the decision, it was made now. The arrow, quite literally, had left the bow, and there was nothing to be gained by dwelling on it. All that remained was to see where it landed.

The trebuchet was a Reliquary. It granted the one who triggered it—him, of course, who pushed the lever—the ability to steer the projectile it launched—also him, who lay in the cradle with the meat—toward any point his own eyes could fix upon.

And he did exactly that, guiding his own body up through the yawning gap in the ceiling, no different from how he guided the boulder just earlier, albeit now using his own eyes instead of the vision given by Celeste.

He rose higher and higher, until at last he broke through into the Domain of Light on the fourth floor, where Sebekton and his troops were locked in combat against a tide of fire golems that showed no sign of ending. They wouldn’t hold much longer. He needed to bring an end to this. Fast.

Through yet another opening set into that blindingly bright ceiling, he passed into another tunnel that carried him onward into the Domain of Water.

Sky above. Sea below.

And, in the distance, wreathed in flame that scorched the very air around it, stood the burning silhouette of the titan.
