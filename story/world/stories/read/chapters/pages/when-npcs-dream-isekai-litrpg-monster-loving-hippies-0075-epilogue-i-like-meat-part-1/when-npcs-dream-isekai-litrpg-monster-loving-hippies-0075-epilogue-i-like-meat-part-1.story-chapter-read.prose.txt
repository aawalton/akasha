A long time ago, in a far-away place, Hitler and his Nazis enslaved the Jewish people—my father’s old tribe. They deprived them of meat and forced them to build giant pyramids. But the Jewish people were loved by the gods. To save them, the gods cursed the Nazis, turning their water sources to blood and killing all their cattle. Defeated by hunger, the Nazis were forced to let the Jewish people go.

The gods told them about a promised continent, a faraway place that lay beyond a huge desert, where they could live a peaceful and quiet life.

The gods instructed them to build a giant airship and to take male and female pair of every animal species with them, in order to breed them and eat their meat. They flew across the desert, and when they ran out of food, the gods made it rain meat upon them.

Eventually, the Jewish reached the Promised Continent. They lived happily and peacefully, with bellies full of meat and no more wars.

This is the story of my ancestors. My story is similar. But it is also different.

For a start, we ran away before the Nazi Bamboos could enslave us. That made us smarter, I think, but also more cowardly. I’m not sure if that is good or bad. Also, we didn’t bring monsters to breed on our airships, and that definitely made us stupid.

The gods talked to us, too, and told us about a promised land where we could live peacefully. But in that land, there were no monsters to hunt. In that land, there wasn’t any meat.

And I liked meat. I really, really did.

It has been more than two years since we came here, and sometimes it was very hard.

Even though I was very small back then, I remember the scary airship ride clearly. Our airships broke all the time, and we almost died. The grownups made pitiful attempts to look calm, while obviously almost shitting their pants in panic. Yeah, I was scared then, but I tried to be brave. Or if I couldn't be brave, I tried to look brave. That is what my second mom used to say I always needed to do, so I listened.

After we arrived, we had nothing really. No homes, almost no food, no meat, and little water. But for some reason, they were all very happy.

Then we built. We have a lot of mages and super-strength people, so it was fast. In days, they dropped hundreds of trees and made many, many houses. But building was the easy part. The hard part was food.

In the first days, all we did was walk in the forests, picking berries, fruit, and vegetables. I remember crying a lot at that time. Berries wasn’t food in my book. And I wanted meat. When I forced myself to eat disgusting fruit, it didn’t satisfy my hunger, no matter how much I ate. I was always hungry.

We eventually discovered the Snail-Shrimp in the sea and the Many-Moths in the forest. These were disgusting animals, but when cooked and eaten in large amounts, I could at least stop being hungry for a short time.

When I could finally stop being hungry, I decided to stop crying, too. My second mother used to say that crying was for the dying. I didn’t want to die, so I listened.

At the beginning, the grownups talked about farming and fish ponds all the time, acting like it was this amazing thing that would make us live like kings. It only made me angrier. Things grown on farms weren’t meat, and fish were just low-level meat.

But after months, the grownups actually did it. They dug large ponds, and all the fish they brought laid many eggs. The eggs hatched, the fish grew, and finally, we could eat fish.

I won’t forget taking my first bite of a cooked fish. I was so happy my eyes got wet. It was the thing that most resembled real meat I’d had in a long time. I also had to admit that the bread they made from the farming stuff was a non-disgusting non-meat, and was actually fulfilling. It made your belly feel heavy and good.

Only at that time, when we had a steady supply of something close enough to food, did I start thinking of this new place as home.

After we got the fish and farming going, things started to feel more relaxed. The grownups stopped talking about survival stuff and started to talk about how to have fun.

They were all busy and excited about making alcohol—a poison water worse than sea water that made you dumb and sick. Grownups were silly like that.

They also built a large theatre in the Order of the Phoenix village, which I liked. They put on large plays with many costumes, decorations, and plots from the old Naked Nula world. The play I liked the most was The Princess Bride. It was so exciting and fun! I went to watch it every single time, always shouting along with the actors during my favorite part:

“My name is Inigo Montoya! You killed my father!! Prepare to die!!!”

But when things got more fun and relaxing, Mother didn’t like it.

She always talked about building "The Machines." I didn’t know what they were, but she said the machines would give us power greater than knives or magic—power enough to cross the desert back to our home continent and defeat the Bamboos and the Agents.

Mother always urged people to work on mining and crafting, the stuff needed to build the machines. She said that while the forest gives wood, the earth hides metal, and it is ours to take, shape, and build what the spirits alone will not.

Mother was our leader, but not all the people wanted to work hard on mining and crafting. A lot of them just liked our life here, now that it had become more fun.

But Mother didn’t budge. She argued with people, and she could be very scary. She even argued with Felix and the other tribe leaders a lot. And she wasn’t alone—Father and Hyun-woo said that the Bamboo could come here, too. That it was only a matter of time.

Eventually, they all agreed to work hard on machines, but also compromised to have some fun time, too. With that, Mother agreed.

I like fun, especially the theater, but I want to return, too. My second mother and Grandpa are still back there. I miss them a lot, and I worry. Even if I know they are both really strong.

Besides, there was a lot of meat back there. And that alone was enough to convince me it was worth fighting for.

That’s right. That was another difference between the Jewish story and us. Unlike the Jewish people, we were going to go back, take revenge, and kill our enemies.

We were Inigo Montoya.

Today, I was bored.

I went to the fish pond and saw Arilli, the birdfolk worker. He was sleeping instead of actually working. I sighed. I liked it when there was a challenge to stealing a fish—using some smart method to distract Arilli long enough for me to hunt. This way, it wasn’t much fun. Even the fish were fat, lazy, and easy to skewer with my knife.

Boring.

I went to a secluded spot, a hiding place nestled between the roots of two great trees where the bushes grew thick. I made a pile of some dried twigs I found and traced the simple fire magic circle Mother taught me. In a moment, the pile of twigs was ablaze. My prize fish, skewered on a sharp twig above the fire, began to drip fat into the flames. The smell of cooked fat and charred skin filled the air, and I drooled happily.

I ate the cooked fish, and it was tasty. But now, I was bored again.

I went back to my village and walked around, watching all the working people, looking for something to do.

“Hi Miriam,” said Lakshmi, waving to me from atop a roof. “How are you doing?”

“Fine,” I said.

“Hey, Miriam!” Camila walked past me, carrying a huge log on her shoulder. “Want to come along and help us build the new textile factory?”

“Nah, I’m good,” I said.

“Hey, Miriam.” That was Father. He was sitting on a porch with Yichen; they were writing stuff on parchments.

I kept walking, pretending I didn’t hear.

“Miriam, come over here.” He repeated it in a firmer voice, so I reluctantly walked over.

“Miriam, you…” He sniffed at me, his nose twitching. “Miriam, were you stealing fish again?”

“No.”

Father squinted his eyes at me. “Miriam, it’s time for you to practice reading,” he said. “Come here and read the nice children’s book Hyun-woo made just for you.”

“I don’t like that book,” I said. “It's about weird things. That atom mole-cool stuff.”

“Yes, but it has nice drawings, too.” He showed me the stupidest drawing I ever saw.

“Isn’t three years old too young to start reading?” asked Yichen.

“If she can steal fish, she can learn to read,” Father said wisely.

But I was already scanning the crowd, frantically looking for a way out. I spotted Naeem carrying a wooden wheel thing.

“I want to read, Father,” I said, putting on my most honest face, “but Naeem was supposed to take me to the forest and teach me… forest stuff.”

“Are you sure, Miriam?” He squeezed those eyes of his again. “He looks busy to me.”

“Not at all!” I said, and ran along before waiting for an answer.

“Miriam!” he called, but I was already gone. If it had been Mother, I wouldn’t have bothered trying to argue—Mother was a lot scarier than Father.

I ran up to Naeem. “Forest trip,” I said. I really wished my lie would become true. A forest trip with Naeem and Samar was one of my favorite non-food-related activities; unlike reading, they actually taught useful things.

“Sorry,” Naeem shrugged. “I must get this wheel to the Order’s carpentry. We are building carriages.”

“Oh. That’s too bad,” I said.

“Some other time,” he said, and continued on his way.

So now I was safe from my father’s pestering, but I was bored again.

“There she is!” I heard a young kid's voice.

I looked and saw Advik walking with Rollito, the bug-boy. It seemed they were looking for me. They looked tired from a long walk.

“We walked all the way,” Advik huffed and puffed, clutching his knees. “I’m tired.”

“I’m not,” said Rollito. “It’s not that far. Anyway, we are here.”

“Here for…?” I asked.

“The adventure,” he said. “A huge, awesome adventure today! That was what you said yesterday.”

“Awesome adventure!” repeated Advik.

Oh. I remembered. It was when I had just caught a fish and didn’t want to share it. I had tried to brush them away and, for some reason, promised them an adventure tomorrow. And that was today.

I didn’t mind, actually. I was bored, after all.

“Well, yes. It's an awesome adventure,” I said. “But it's dangerous, too. The weak will not survive.”

“I’m very strong,” said Rollito, tapping on his carapace. “My skin is hard as iron!”

“I’m strong too!” squeaked Advik, even though he was definitely not. At four and a half years old, he was older than me, but non-goblin Naked Nulas grew really slowly. At four and a half, he was basically a big baby.

“Great,” I said.

“So what is the awesome adventure?” asked Rollito, all wide-eyed, his antennae moving excitedly.

That’s right… I needed to actually come up with something.

“Well, I’m glad you asked,” I said. “That is… well… ah… hunting…”

“Hunting…?” asked Advik.

“Monster hunting!” I said. “In the deep forest!”

“But there are no monsters in this continent!” Rollito said. “All the grownups said so.”

“No, no,” I explained. “The grownups just didn’t find any monsters. When they go to the forest, they look for stupid berries or collect bee honey. If we look for it properly, I bet we find some.”

“And then what?” asked Advik.

“We kill them, of course,” I said. “Then we skin them—slice the belly from neck to tail, peel the hide back so we don’t ruin the leather—and then we eat the meat, of course.”

“That sounds weird,” Advik said.

“Real meat…” said Rollito dreamily. “It’s been so long…”

“I don’t remember eating meat ever,” Advik said. “I don’t remember much from the old place.”

“It’s the best thing in the world,” I assured them. “There is nothing like it! Nothing!”

“And I bet we will be really important,” said Rollito, “to be the first to find monsters.”

“Yes… that too,” I said. “Come on, let’s go.”

“Shouldn’t we invite Juliette to join us?” asked Rollito.

“Fine,” I said. “Let’s go to his house.”

We went to the only house in the village with two stories, a weird roof, and a lot of decoration. I took a small pebble and threw it at the wooden window. After a short moment, the window opened and we saw Juliette peek out.

“Come out!” shouted Rollito. “We are going to… ah, a cool place.”

“I’m grounded,” Juliette said sadly. “Mother won’t let me leave the house…”

Not this again. His mother sucks. “Come down now!” I shouted at him. “Or I’ll call you a coward forever!”

He looked like he was debating it, weighing the fear of his mother against the fear of my mockery. He looked at my deadly serious face and started to climb out of the window.

He tried to hold the window rim and jump onto the first-story roof, but his hand slipped. He flailed for a second, feathers puffing out in panic, and then—BOOM. He fell off the roof and landed right on his ass.

“Cumkettle-snortpiss!” he cursed. “That really hurt!”

“Come on,” I said. “We need to go before…”

“Hey, Miriam!” It was Father’s voice. I shut my eyes. He was such a nudnik!

“Yes, Father?”

“Didn’t you say you were going to the forest with Naeem?” he asked, walking up to us.

“We already came back,” I answered.

“Miriam, you are lying all the time!” he scolded me. “Lying is bad! If you keep it up, no one will ever believe you.”

“Sorry, Father,” I said. “I will never lie again.”

He looked at me and sighed. “Where are you all going?” he asked.

They all looked at me.

“Not past the river,” I said.

“Not… past… the river?” he repeated slowly. “Listen, come here, Miriam, for a moment.”

He walked a few steps aside, and I went with him.

“Look, Miriam,” he said in a low voice. “I know you, ah… like your freedom. Stealing fish, going on forest trips, training in fire magic alone. That’s okay—well, not really, but I know you are a capable girl, and I trust you.”

I nodded.

“But it is a different story when you are going on an adventure with your friends,” he said, his tone serious. “You are amazing, Miriam. I bet you can give an armadillo-bear a good fight. But the other kids are a different story. While Rollito is a tough bug-boy, Advik is a Naked Nula, and at four years old, they are practically babies. And Juliette, well… he is clumsy, and Duchess will kill you if he gets injured. So remember, you are something of a leader to them. They are your responsibility.”

“I understand, Father,” I said. “I will keep them safe.”

And I meant it, too. It was like he said: I was the strong and smart leader, and they were weak and dumb, so only I could keep them safe. That was why it was my job.

“The forest here is not very dangerous,” he continued. “There aren’t monsters or animals, after all, but there are the Sleep-Gas Roses and the Poison Ivy Vines. As well as many thorn plants, pits in the ground, and sharp rocks.”

“I understand, Father,” I said. “I will be very careful, and make sure Juliette and Advik don’t get a scratch.”

Father kissed me on the forehead. “Good girl.”

This time I would keep my promise, I decided. I would be an awesome leader. And if we did find monsters, I would protect my band and slay the monsters before they could even blink.

We walked the road to the Order’s village, which passed along the river. Around the middle point, there was a wooden bridge that crossed the water and led to a small path into the forest.

That was the normal part of the forest, where we often went for walks or to find berries. But this continent has a huge mountain in the center that can be seen from everywhere. At the foot of the mountain, a large, dark forest could be seen—one much thicker than this one. This was where I wanted to go. If there were monsters, they would be there.

So I led my small band that way. There was no path here, only thickets and dense trees. As a leader should, I made sure to pick the easiest path, kept Advik and Juliette close to me, and always helped them through the hard parts. I hold back branches so they wouldn’t snap back in their faces, and push aside thorny branches so they wouldn’t get scratched.

“We are very far,” noted Advik. “Are we lost yet?”

“I’m half-goblin,” I explained. “I can’t get lost.”

And this was true. I always knew exactly where I was and remembered forest parts with great clarity. I truly didn’t think I was capable of getting lost.

“I’m tired,” Advik complained, dragging his feet. “When do we get to the monsters?”

“It’s not far now.”

“I’m tired too, chuu!” complained Juliette. “And my feathers are all getting tangled in the bushes.”

“We are really close,” I said, starting to get angry, too.

“I’m hungry, too,” whined Advik. “I want to go back home.”

I stopped and looked at them.

“This isn’t a game,” I told them in a serious voice. “We are on an important mission to find monsters and feed our tribe meat. Our people depend on us! Are you warriors or cowards?”

“Warrior,” Rollito and Juliette said.

“…Warrior, sniff,” Advik murmured.

“Good,” I said, looking each one in the eyes.

Then we continued, and for another half hour, I heard no complaints. We finally got to more or less where I thought about going. We were close to the mountain now and the forest was at its thickest.

We entered a small clearing. It was interesting—a lot of new plants, colorful flowers, berries, mushrooms, fruit trees, and long columns of fat caterpillars crawling dutifully in a line. They looked very serious. I wondered if they tasted good… No! Stop thinking like a bug eater! We came here to find meat!

“It’s a cool place,” said Rollito. “Many pretty flowers.”

“And food!” exclaimed Advik, picking up a handful of berries.

“STOP!” I said to him, and walked over to check what he had picked. “The red goods… this fruit is okay… blue ones are disgusting but edible, suit yourself. Oh, this pink one will kill you on the spot. Don’t eat it!”

“Ok!” said Advik, throwing away the pink ones and getting right into munching the others.

Yes. I was a good leader like that.

“But… where are the monsters?” asked Juliette.

They all looked at me. And I really didn’t know what to say.

“Maybe…” I tried, “they ran away, scared of us.”

“Oh,” said Juliette, looking disappointed.

“But maybe we will still find them,” I said. “Patience is the sharpest arrow in a hunter's quiver,” as my Grandpa used to say.

“Well, we can rest while we wait,” said Juliette, laying back. “Maybe sleep a little.”

“Let’s eat berries and rest a little,” agreed Rollito. “Then if no monster shows up, we go back.”

“Yes, we should go back soon,” I agreed. “If we stay too long, our parents are gonna kill us.” I looked at Juliette. “Especially you.”

“Chuu! I forgot about that!” he said.

I was hungry too, but I despised berries and fruits. I would wait until we got back, then I would see if Mother and Father had something nice ready, or just steal another fish.

Now I just lay on the grass bed and looked at the column of busy caterpillars. When I looked closely, each carried a tiny red something, or a tiny triangle leaf. What were they gonna do with that? Build something fancy? Do you even taste good? Probably not. You aren't monsters.

I, too, felt sleepy. It was safe to sleep here, after all. No monsters at all.

My eyes were getting heavy… I thought about where I should go next time to search for monsters. But was there any point to it? Did I truly believe we would find monsters today? No… It was stupid to believe the silly story I told the others. The grownups didn’t just look for berries near the village; they made a full survey of a large part of the continent in the first month when we arrived. Of course they would have found monsters if there were any.

I was hungry now. We should go back. We should…

Suddenly—a RUSTLE.

Faint, far away, but unmistakably the sound of something stirring in the bushes. And it wasn’t the wind. I knew the difference well. This was a sound I knew, one that I hadn’t heard in more than two years.

My great goblin ears perked up. I jumped immediately and went a few steps forward, listening carefully.

Again. Rustle.

I also knew it wasn’t people; they produce a different sound, heavier and clumsier.

“Wait here,” I told the others, and hurried in the direction of the noise. I was ecstatic, going full hunter mode, all my senses sharpened to the limit. I lowered my body, stepping softly, heel-to-toe, making myself as light as a ghost.

It wasn’t far. I saw the bush that was stirring. I hid now, quiet, barely breathing. My hand clenched tightly around my knife.

Then I saw it come out of the bushes.

A creature I never saw before. The closest thing it resembled was maybe the Zebra-Deer, but it was much smaller than that huge beast. It had four sturdy legs and no tail, with scales like a fish and a large, spiral horn on its forehead. Also, it had a weird, long purple tongue with spikes that sprawled out of its mouth in a very creepy way. My hunter sense told me I should be careful of the tongue.

It was a monster.

And it shouldn’t be here. No matter what I told the others, I didn’t think there were monsters in this continent. But here it was.

Meat.

My meat.

I looked at it, slowly, oh so slowly. I sneaked, quietly, with all the stealth I could muster.

Then—

“AAAAA!”

A scream tore through the air.

Shit! My band! What happened? I needed to go back to them now.

I gave one last look to the creature and hurried back to where my friends were. And there they were—another three of the creatures. They surrounded my friends and walked slowly toward them, spiral-horns forward, sharp teeth, and pointy purple tongues lolling out. Juliette, Rollito, and Advik walked slowly backward until they touched each other, making small whimpering sounds, each in their unique Elian species way.

These creatures were carnivores. They were moments away from eating my friends. I knew I had to act now.

I walked quickly but quietly to the closest monster, sneaking behind its back. The other two noticed me and gave me a look, but my target was too focused on his own prey.

I gave a short run, closed the gap, and jumped on the monster's back, sticking my knife in its throat—the viciously sharp knife my second mother gave me. It went right through the fish scales, and splashes of purple blood shot out. The creature jumped and threw me off its back. I flew and landed hard on the ground, but immediately jumped to my feet.

The two other monsters didn’t move. They didn’t run away, but they didn’t advance either. It was clear they were trying to assess the danger. I needed to show them that they needed to fear me.

I moved toward them and shouted a goblin war cry. It didn’t sound like my goblin mother’s tribe cry, as my voice was small and high, but it was scary in its own way, as I gave the cry my all.

Rollito understood what I was doing. He pulled a small knife himself and shouted, too.

The two monsters didn’t budge. We were little, so they weren’t sure if we were actually a danger to them.

But then—THUD!

The first monster I stabbed fell to the ground, kicked a little, and then stopped moving.

That was proof enough for the other two that I was dangerous, and they ran along.

I shouted another cry, this one a goblin triumph cry. Rollito joined me. But Juliette and Advik just sat on the grass and started crying.

We won. I killed a fearful monster. I also acted as a good leader and protected my band. I felt very happy.

Now for the prize.

I went to the dead creature and looked at it closely. Oh… why did it have to have purple blood? We would need to check it with the poison detector magic device when cooked. Then we could eat it. But I hoped it tasted good.

I decided to cut off one of the large legs to take home; I couldn’t carry more. When they saw it in the village, they would immediately come to take the rest and maybe hunt more.

I started working, cutting at the leg, and then—

Puff!

The creature vanished before my eyes!

I was stunned, but… yes, Father talked about it. Monsters vanish when Players kill them. Now that I thought about it, they did say when we got here that if we found monsters, NPCs like Mother would have to do the kill.

I was so dumb sometimes.

But then I felt a weird sensation. Like something was filling up inside me. Oh, Father talked about leveling up. Is that it? I guess it is.

So what now? Oh, I didn’t remember.

“I think I leveled up,” I told Rollito.

“Really? That’s cool,” he said. “Now choose a skill to spend it on?”

“How do I do that?”

“Just do what you want to level up,” he explained.

That was simple enough. I made a few moves with the knife. I felt some pulling from the weird energy that filled me, flowing through the knife movement. I continued doing them, and suddenly, I was doing them better. Faster. Stronger. I had a skill in knives now. I was happy.

“We should go back now,” said Rollito.

“Yes, please!” cried Juliette.

“I want home!” cried Advik. “I’m scared!”

“Sure, let’s go,” I said.

But just then, I heard a rustling sound again.

“Quiet!” I whispered. “Don’t move!”

They all stood like statues.

I listened well, concentrating. This was definitely a stirring in the bushes. But it had a different rhythm to it. These were steps. Like people steps. Not the sound of a beast.

“I think the grownups are here,” I said. “Probably came to look for us.”

“Oh good!” squeaked Juliette. “Wait… I hope Mother is not with them!”

“I hope it is Mother!” cried Advik.

“Let’s shout to them,” said Rollito, “so they know we are here.”

“Wait!” I said, as the rustling sound got closer. Something in my gut told me it was wrong. It was the sound. It was very similar to a Naked Nula walking… but… not quite that.

“Hide,” I instructed. “Be quiet, not a word!”

I hurried them toward a large thicket of bushes. I pushed Advik and Juliette deep into the foliage, then pulled Rollito down with me behind a fern. We lay flat against the earth, covered by leaves.

We waited. I looked at Advik; he was scared the most. His eyes were wet with tears, but he held his crying and kept his mouth shut. It was hard for him; he wasn’t like me. I guess he was also brave in a way.

Not long after, we saw them.

People, yes. But not our people.

These were Red Lizard people, wearing clothes and talking.

These were Players.
