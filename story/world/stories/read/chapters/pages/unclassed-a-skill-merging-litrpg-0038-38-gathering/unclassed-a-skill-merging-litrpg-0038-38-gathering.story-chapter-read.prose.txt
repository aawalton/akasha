I wound up being less guarded with the Drassian interspecies ambassador than I thought I’d be.

Apparently, that wasn’t Alai’s official title. She was usually called an ‘assistant mayor’, but was the most senior government official in the area to have taken a course in alien languages sufficient enough to speak with me.

That said, her Tellos common was a little broken. She looked absurdly relieved when I switched to Drassian.

“You speak our language?” the young woman proclaimed in surprise.

At least, I thought she was young. Alai had short white hair, pale green skin, a slender frame, tiny horns, and stood about two inches taller than me. No Drassian I’d seen yet was wrinkled, and their skin was almost always completely unblemished. I imagined she could be in her twenties.

“I spent some time in your facility reading your books and talking with your robots,” I half-lied, figuring it was a decent cover. “I have an ability that makes learning languages easier for me.”

She looked very surprised at that. Her eyes were wide and sparkling like garnets.

“We’ve heard a little about ‘abilities’ from our previous reconnaissance with aliens, though we’ve not met many and our knowledge is fairly limited…” she let the thought trail. “Do you possess a system?” she asked. “And a… core, I think the word is?”

“I do,” I answered honestly.

“In Drassia, we only learned of the existence of mana in the last fifty years. Our attempts to harness it, so far, have been rather slowgoing.”

She sat at the foot of my bed as she spoke, which was large enough that it didn’t feel uncomfortable. She’d remained standing at first, but I realised after a time that she was looking a little pale speaking to me and offered her to sit.

“We believe we have ‘cores’ as well, though they seem to primarily be a more dormant organ in our bodies,” she told me. “There’s been a lot of theorisation about what causes that mutation to develop in a creature, as well as the environment that might foster the ability to use mana, or gain… what do you call them? Abilities?”

“Or skills,” I clarified. “Either works.”

“Fascinating…” She blinked a couple of times. “And it’s with one of these… abilities that you were able to disappear from the view of the scientists that rushed to receive you?”

I remained silent at that. I knew what she was talking about.

“You disappeared from our sensors entirely, for hours. When you reappeared, you were unconscious. No one knew what to make of it.”

“It’s… a defensive skill,” I lied. “I wasn’t sure if I’d be in danger coming here.”

She smiled at that. “Well, rest assured, you’re in no danger. Frankly, most Drassians are likely more scared of you. We’ve never had a… warlock walking around Bartan before.”

A warlock? That’s what they think I am? Maybe the translation is bad.

“Is Bartan the name of this city?”

She nodded.

“And you’re going to let me walk around?” I asked, the question slightly more pointed than I’d intended.

“We’re planning to,” she answered. “From what we can tell, your body is in good condition following your treatment. You could have ran off if you wanted. With that in mind, plus your role in saving one of our citizens, and the information you gave me about the dangers around the excavation site, as well as the fate of the group sent there…

She bowed her head slightly. “We believe you have more than earned free rein in our city, as well as a reward for your hard work.”

“Are there any conditions to that?” I asked, keeping my tone level.

“We’d ask you not to go into the wilderness,” Alai replied. “The wildlife on Drassia can be quite dangerous, and the storms deadly, especially without Bartan’s lightning rods to protect you.”

“Anything else?” I pressed.

“We aren’t going to force you to answer questions if you don’t want to,” Alai assured me. “You’re not deemed as a security risk. As far as we’re concerned, while we’re curious about your origin, and your culture, we respect your right to privacy and understand if you do not wish to discuss your planet.”

That was… surprisingly diplomatic. The Drassian people, at least from the impression I was currently getting, seemed pleasant.

Only…

“Do you know why the researchers in the mine started fighting in the first place?” I asked Alai.

She sighed at that, looking down at the floor.

“Drassia has never had one standing political party,” Alai explained. “That’s worked for us. There’ve been scarcely any wars on our planet for centuries, and while each city in Drassia is self-governing, we’ve formed strong bonds and treaties with other surrounding settlements. In Bartan, we’ve established a free market and trade network that allows people social mobility while also prioritising the poorest.

“That said,” she continued, “the poverty rate—those who cannot afford to take qaurtecly vacations, those who cannot take more than a season off work per year—is still almost thirty percent.”

I didn’t understand everything she was saying, but I blinked at that. “Wait… that’s poverty? Not being able to take a season off of work per year?”

“Yes,” she said, blinking a few more times, her eyes misty. “It’s very sad. Still, we’ve endeavoured to provide free healthcare, homes, and tuition to all of our citizens. For a long time, the people in Drassia were happy with this.

“But then we made contact with other worlds, and we learned of their central governments,” Alai said, her tone turning bitter. “Some people on our planet have gotten ideas since. Some seem to think that the ideal way for us to advance our society is to place one group in charge and allow them to control the laws, finances, and decisions of the entire planet.”

I tried to compute it. Who wasn’t happy with the current situation the Drassians had? It sounded perfect. How greedy would a person have to be to want more than that?

“So that’s what a unionist is?” I asked.

“Yes,” Alai nodded. “Most unionist groups are headed by large businesses, though we don’t always know which. Their plans are often covert. It’s clear they wish to destabilise the structure of Drassian society and then swoop in and ‘save’ everyone,” her tone was acidic, “just to claim the planet for themselves. It’s sickening.”

She wiped her eyes, then looked at me.

“I-I’m sorry, I get a little carried away. The point is, it’s likely that at least some of the researchers sent to that excavation site were unionists attempting to hide or weaponise their discoveries. More interested in advancing their political interests than helping the planet.”

I nodded. I never would’ve guessed any of this on my own.

It wasn’t often I felt clueless. But the complexities of something like a political schism were pretty difficult for me to grasp, even as Alai explained it to me. It was so far away from anything I’d ever had to experience before. The closest I’d seen were gang disputes, and perhaps group dynamics in the mines.

It sounded as if it were an ongoing issue, too. Maya had mentioned something about a unionist in her dream, so they’d been around then, and they were likely still around now. I wasn’t sure if there was anything I could do or say about it to help.

“Enough about all of that,” Alai said with a smile. “Let’s discuss your reward, as well as your next steps.”

“I was actually hoping to see Maya,” I told her. “I wanted to have a conversation with her.”

“She’s asked about you as well,” Alai said. “She’s recovering from surgery, right now, but you should be okay to visit her in a day or two. Would that be agreeable?”

I said yes. I was fine as long as I got to see her. I wasn’t sure how long I wanted to stay here, nor how long I was permitted to, but a couple of days didn’t—

“As for your reward, we can start with this.”

She handed me a round, silvery disc. I stared at it.

“Wh-what is…”

“It’s a payment fob, linked to the local government account.” She smiled, a glint of mischief in her eye. “Usually, these are loaded with ten thousand sarcs a day for civil servant use, but yours has a couple of extra benefits.

“For one, your account is programmed to give you a 96% discount at all restaurants and entertainment venues in Baytan. That’s the lowest I could program the discount without the system crashing…” She flicked her bangs. “However, it’ll still be enough that you can essentially dine anywhere and still have money spare.”

She raised a finger. “There are a couple of limitations, however. For one, you cannot purchase weaponry, or anything else that’s considered to be inherently dangerous.”

I raised an eyebrow at her. “I thought you said that I’m not a security risk?”

“You aren’t, but card-carrying citizens of Drassia—which you have all rights to become—still need permits to buy items that are considered dangerous. The paperwork can’t be skipped through.”

She winked at me. “Rest assured, if you desire to leave here with a weapon, we can peruse the local armory before your departure. That’s assuming you wish to leave.”

I hung on that for a second. She was saying I didn’t have to leave here. That I could become a citizen if I wanted.

I ignored the impulse. “Anything else I should avoid?”

“Further to the previous limitation, you cannot purchase property in Bartan without citizenship, nor engage in financial markets. You also cannot purchase alcohol, or any other adult experiences, without identification.”

I scoffed at that. “Why not?”

“While we aren’t experts on human physiology, Adam, we believe that you are still in your adolescence.”

“I can buy what I want back home,” I countered, feeling like the statement was an accusation.

“We respect that you may have cultural differences,” Alai told me, her eyes soft. “But laws like this are engrained in our society. They can’t really be circumvented. That being said, we understand that the place you came from may have been different, and we’re committed to the prospect of giving you a happy, healthy childhood. Of proper schooling and care.”

I squinted at her. “I’m not a child,” I said to the assistant mayor in harsh, guttural Drassian. “I’ve provided for myself since I was six.”

The look in her eyes disgusted me. It was pity.

“I’m sure you’ve done an excellent job providing for yourself,” Alai said. I could tell she was holding more back, but she kept it in.

She gingerly stood, as if she were worried I’d get angrier if she moved too quickly.

“A nurse will come and remove your tube soon. Then you’ll be free to explore. A guide will be waiting outside your room to show you around, if you’re up to it.”

“Thanks,” I breathed, managing to sound more cordial than I felt.

“I’ll come and see you again when Maya’s recovered. I’m sure she’ll be happy to see you.

“It was lovely meeting you, Adam.”

With that, she inclined her head once more and left, leaving me to stew in emotions.

I eventually said ‘fuck it’ and stood up. I’d thought about all of it for a little while, but thinking had just made me angrier. Who was she or anyone on her planet to classify me? They didn’t know a single thing I’d done to get here.

I calmed a little once the drip was removed from my arm. I flexed it as I considered my new powers.

I still had plenty of stuff to test, but I could also feel my stomach growling.

She’d said I could leave if I wanted. There was no obligation to stay here.

And I’d just been handed a payment fob with ten thousand sarcs loaded on it… how much was that anyway?

How much could I spend here? How much could I store to take home?

I wasn’t locked into anything. I might as well enjoy the hospitality.

As I left the room, a guy on my left waved at me.

Blue skinned, long horns, cropped hair, six and a half feet. I recognised him as the attendant that had come with the nurse to check on me earlier. He wore a skintight, black shirt, trousers combo that revealed his insane muscle definition. As I turned to him, he stiffened, his face serious.

“Uh… greets, human. I here… to… show you… things.”

“It’s greetings,” I replied in Drassian, almost smirking at the horrible attempt at common. “I speak Drassian, don’t worry.”

“Oh, thank god,” the man responded, immediately relaxing his posture, looking exhausted. “I’d been rehearsing that line all day, and I was really worried I’d get it wrong.”

I laughed at that. “You’re fine. Languages don’t come naturally to me either.”

He squinted at me. “But you speak perfect Drassian.”

“Uh…” I’d kinda just blurted that, thinking about my struggles to pick up a bit of orcish years ago. “I had a lot of time to practice.”

He brightened. “Got it! Well, anyways, it’s my job to show you around.” He patted himself on the chest. “I don’t usually do this escort stuff, and I’m not actually from Bartan, but I think I know a couple places you’ll like. If you wanna follow me, that is.”

I considered it for a moment.

“I wouldn’t mind a guide. What’s your name?”

“I’m Daryn,” the brick of a man said, “nice to meet ya.”

“I’m Adam.” He started walking, and I followed his lead until we reached an elevator.

“You uh… know how these work?”

I resisted the urge to grab one of the handrails. “Yeah.”

We descended, less scary this time than the last, and walked through clean white corridors until we cleared the reception area. It wasn’t lost on me that a lot of eyes turned my way. Some looked interested, others concerned. A child tugged on her mother’s arm and pointed at me.

“You’ll get used to that,” Daryn said as he walked. “It’s not as obvious here, but back where I’m from, I’m a bit of a giant.”

“It’s obvious here,” I told him with a side-eye.

“Most of ‘em don’t mean any harm, they’ve just never seen a human before. If you’re anything like me, you’ll get used to it.”

I didn’t reply. I wasn’t used to attracting attention, and I wasn’t sure I was super comfortable with it, either, but with a massive dude like Daryn beside me, I was hardly worried about being assaulted, so I chose to put it out of my mind.

“So…” I started as we walked out of the vehicle stable and over toward a set of pavement. “Where did you want to take me first?”

Daryn turned to me with a grin. “Well… have you ever heard of a theme park, Adam?”

“Theme… park?”

“They’re great. Trust me. This one time, I went on the Roller Blade so many times I barfed cotton candy. Best reason to visit Bartan. One million percent.”

“Barfed?” I asked him, wondering if this was a language barrier thing.

“Threw up.”

“And… you were happy about that?”

“You’ll understand when we get there. Just trust me.”

Toar and his group had laid low for the last week or so. He’d told his group everything, and with Finn and Jackal corroborating his tale, his group had allowed him back in without too much fuss and agreed to keep their secret.

Of course, Toar hadn’t mentioned the plans he and his cousin had shared, nor the details of the conflict between him and Adam, but he’d deal with those issues later. For now, what was important was getting his story straight with everyone, and while Toar hadn’t been welcomed back in the group as a leader, he at least felt accepted enough that he wasn’t worried about anyone ratting on him.

That said, he realised there wasn’t a lot of love for him in the group right now. There was a good chance that if snitching on him didn’t implicate Finn and Jackal, one of them would’ve done it in the last three days.

The story was pretty simple. Toar was recovering in the medical bay from his documented injuries when the place had been ransacked. When he’d woken up, Mansol was dead, and he didn’t know who the culprits were. His Tier advancement was documented and considered to be a primary reason that he’d made a sharp recovery, and the staff investigating had seemed fairly certain that Toar couldn’t have killed Mansol alone, especially in his condition.

That was all they heard about it for two days. Then, a day later, a woman named Tari came by to ask him more questions. A day later, she was back, asking the same questions. Then, nothing for days. Toar had begun to wonder if the matter had simply been marked as unresolvable.

He’d briefly spoken with Ghrinda, the new leader of Selsor’s group, to see if she’d heard anything, and from the sounds of things, her group had also been asked the same vague questions.

That made it sound as if they had no solid leads. Toar imagined if they were questioning everyone like this, it wouldn’t be long until they gave up.

All he had to do was keep his head down, not do anything else even adjacent to rule-breaking, and go under the radar for the next few months.

His contract was up for renewal in half a year. Toar had made enough money from his time in the mines that he could likely leave and buy property. Nothing remotely extravagant but at least enough that he wouldn’t be completely reliant on his family. He had a rare class. He doubted he’d struggle to find work after all of this.

The idea of leaving his family was still scary. He knew he’d likely struggle without their resources to do more than just get by, and that the kind of work his class was likely to get him was dangerous. Fitting in somewhere new would be a struggle as well. But he didn’t like this place. He was ready to leave all of it behind.

One day recently, when he’d been working on an ore deposit with Finn, the younger boy took a break. He sat on a large rock, pulling his boots off and massaging his own feet. Toar continued to mine until he came across a stubborn cluster. When he asked Finn for help, Finn told him to wait.

“We don’t have all day,” Toar barked. “Get to it.”

“Don’t tell me what to do,” Finn replied, fishing pebbles out of his boot. “Do it alone for all I care. I’m tired.”

“What did you say to me?”

“I said don’t tell me what to do,” Finn repeated, louder this time. “Did you forget you’re not our leader anymore? I don’t have to follow your instructions.”

Toar dropped his pick. He turned to face Finn.

“What, so you’re done sucking up to me now? I don’t have to deal with your fakery anymore?”

“I was never fake,” Finn replied, his voice calm. “I thought you were a good guy, deep down. Looking out for the rest of us. But you’re not.”

Toar didn’t know what to say to that. He felt his fists clench, but they didn’t have a target.

“I saved you,” he protested, though his voice sounded weak. “I saved your life.”

“That doctor was your cousin,” Finn stated, like it was a piece of evidence. “He said he wanted to hurt us to educate you.” Finn dropped his boot; he crossed his arms. “I’m guessing he got you this position. That you killed Fallos because he told you to. That you started fucking with Adam because he told you to. That the last place Adam was seen was with your cousin because you wanted him dead.

“It seemed nice when you were protecting us from a bigger asshole,” Finn continued. “When you were pushing us to work hard so we could make our quota. But what the fuck did a little thirteen year old kid ever do to you? Huh?”

Toar felt the pressure against his chest. Finn had gotten up and shoved him. He’d barely noticed the motion.

“I used to be scared of you,” Finn said. “I used to like you, in spite of that.” He shoved at Toar again, pushing him back a step.

“Now I just think you’re a coward.”

Finn spat on the floor and left. It’d been the first time he’d said more than a few words to Toar since they’d returned to camp.

He’d quickly given up arguing. He couldn’t deny anything that Finn had said. Maybe Jackal and the others hadn’t given it as much thought, or maybe they didn’t care as much, but Finn had apparently cared for him. Thought he was something more than a piece of shit.

And now he didn’t. Finn had learned the truth, and everything was worse.

The others didn’t treat him quite so harshly. Maisie even sat with him and brushed his mane one evening after he’d killed himself working into the night, scared to come back and face the rest of them.

“I can sing you something,” she said to him as she brushed. “Like I used to when you’d get nightmares.”

“Not tonight,” Toar grumbled. He grit his teeth as she pulled another knot from his curls.

“Thank you, though.”

The brush paused. “You’re welcome?” She giggled. “Sorry, I’m not used to you talking so politely.”

Was Toar thanking someone really so strange?

“What are you planning to do when you leave this place?” he asked her after a few quiet moments.

“We’ve talked about this before,” Maisie said. “I’m gonna help my mom pay off some of her debts and then try to find whatever work I can with my improved healing skills. Probably join an army if they’ll have me. Whatever puts food in my mouth.”

They had talked about it before. Over a year ago, between cuddles. Things had been different then. It’d been easier to confide in Maisie. She’d liked him more.

She hadn’t seen how… me I was.

“Do you think it’s selfish if I leave?”

He’d expected a harsher brush stroke in response, a nasty snag and a stab of pain. Toar didn’t receive one.

“I think all of us have to look out for ourselves,” Maisie said. “If leaving makes more sense to you, then you should.” She sighed. “I’ll be forced out within a year. I doubt the Association would keep me on as an adult, but even if they did, I’d look for other work. I want to help people, but…” Her brush paused. “It’s bleak. My songs don’t really do anything. We’re just marching to the same tune over and over. Killing monsters and hitting rocks.

“And then, someone dies. And someone else shows up to take over.”

“And all we can really do is hope we’re the ones who live,” Toar finished.

“If I’m going to live through that, I deserve to do it for myself,” Maisie said. “My siblings will be older. They can pick up the slack for me. I deserve a chance at happiness.”

She did. Maisie had joined the mines at fourteen, three years ago, her mother drowning in debt with too many children and an absentee father. She’d thought that she’d be able to make a lot of money and come out of this place wealthy, at least enough so that she could buy a bigger house for her and her family and enroll in a school to learn more magic.

Then she’d ended up in Toar’s group. He, as leader, had made enough money that he could likely do all of the things Maisie wanted to, or at least most of them, and he’d also benefitted from arriving with no debt to his name.

Maisie, however, would be lucky if she could clear half of her mother’s accumulated debts and still have a hundred gold remaining. It wasn’t just the structure of her contract—the interest on her family’s debt was staggering. The only reason they held off on collecting was because her earnings were funnelled directly toward the repayments. She’d leave here with a bag of gold small enough to fit in her pocket, her reward for years of labour.

And the part that made Toar angry was the knowledge that she wouldn’t quit there. Maisie wouldn’t admit it to herself, but she was the most caring person Toar had met. He heavily doubted she’d offload the responsibilities of the family to her siblings, even if they could take over, and imagined if she couldn’t find the right job, she’d run herself into the ground for another decade if it meant looking after her mother. Even if it was thankless. Even if she had more kids.

Toar got up and left without a word the moment Maisie was finished. He felt guilty just to be around her.

To think he was considering leaving. What did he have to go back to? Nothing, no one.

His group might not have wanted him, but they were his. They were the people he’d risked his life for. Not for his benefit or personal glory. But because he’d wanted to. And now, he wanted to run away because it was too difficult? Because people didn’t treat him like a king anymore?

Fuck that. He’d earn his position. Never mind the glory that came with it. The task was all that mattered.

Toar had a group to lead.

It was on the evening of the tenth day after Mansol’s death that the camp was encroached upon for a third time.

Ceri had been grilling sausages. The meat was lamb, though no one else knew it—Toar had a very discerning taste—and with a little seasoning, they tasted spectacular.

Toar had been considering whether there might be a market for Ceri’s cooking with the other groups, the third in a short list of money-making schemes he’d been thinking of since his conversation with Maisie, when the dinner was interrupted.

Toar’s thoughts of how to ensure his group left the dungeon with the payment they deserved were cut short by the march of steel boots and a loud call-to-attention.

Maisie shot awake. The six of them stiffened—the whole group in attendence—as the leader of the procession of armed adults, a well-groomed soldier with short hair and polished armour, spoke to the group.

“Workers, we order you to come with us to the central cavern immediately. An announcement is to take place.”

Toar felt fear in his chest—maybe it was nothing, the last announcement they’d all been gathered for was nine months ago and had been about a cave-in. This could be something similar.

“What kind of announcement?” Jackal asked, his mouth still full of sausage.

“I’m not answering questions,” the guard speaking said. “Come with us, immediately.”

“Kinda rude to barge in on us while we’re eating,” Jackal continued.

Toar couldn’t tell if he was trying to act less suspicious or just being his usual self. Either way, he didn’t seem to give a shit about any of this, and was convincing enough that Toar almost believed it.

“Maxwell, please deal with that one.”

What happened fast happened too quickly for Toar to describe. The scruffy-looking guard with wavy hair, standing beside the speaker, rushed Jackal, clearing a twenty foot space in two seconds and punching him right in the gut. He’d gone from standing stationary to standing over him in less than three. Jackal gasped, spitting his half-chewed food onto the floor as he clutched his belly.

“That was a warning,” the guard said. “Next person who objects will receive a punishment.”

Toar watched as Jackal hurled up a portion of his food before being hoisted onto Maxwell’s shoulder.

That wasn’t a punishment?

The six of them fell into lockstep, walking behind the procession of guards and toward the central cavern.

Toar found it difficult to believe that this wasn’t related to Mansol’s death considering what had just happened. He’d seen guards slap around workers before, but never on orders, and announcements had never involved armed escorts before now.

He kept silently praying that he was mistaken. That this wasn’t anything to do with what he’d done and that he and his group would be fine after this, that they could go back to their work and he could go back to his planning, mining, and searching for traces of Adam.

His outlook on the reality of that hope began to dim as he saw the sight waiting for them.

Every miner within the rift seemed to have been gathered in this central area, a lot of them looking tired, some as if they’d just been roused from sleep, some in the simple grey cloth of their bedclothes.

There were low murmurs all around. There was clearly a fear in the procession, likely reinforced by the score of armed guards that stood stationed all around the hundred or so teenagers, feeling as if they were blocking every exit.

But Toar still couldn’t see the source of the panic. As he drew closer to the circle of antsy miners, he noticed something out of the ordinary.

A stage of some kind seemed to have been constructed at the far end of the open space, large, wooden, and imposing, with stairs and a large platform, though see-through from underneath, wooden slats rising up and over the top of the dias and reinforcing it at the ceiling.

On it stood a well-dressed man in red, white, and yellow silks, wearing a gleaming golden necklace, looking down on the children below with a blank expression.

Beside him there stood a man garbed entirely in black. He tussled with a length of sturdy rope.

Toar’s breath caught in his throat. This wasn’t a stage.

He’d seen a structure like this before, in his hometown. He’d seen it used when someone broke the island’s laws, when someone angered his lord family.

This was a gallow.

They meant to kill us.
