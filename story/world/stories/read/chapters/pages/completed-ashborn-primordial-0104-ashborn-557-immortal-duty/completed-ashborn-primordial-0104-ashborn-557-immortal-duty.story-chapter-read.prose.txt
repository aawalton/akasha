The communications orb in Roshan’s hand went dark as Maiya finished speaking.

Yet another miracle made possible only by the Akh Nara. Who had enabled demons like Saunak to construct marvels like these.

The one who had given all so that they might live.

The Akh Nara’s wife had given him the report as she always had these past weeks, and Roshan was honored to receive such attention from one as incredible as she.

The news she delivered was neither unexpected nor favorable. Demonkind was moving toward open war. Not among the clans, as they always had, but against humanity.

It seemed the human kingdoms were fortifying their borders and their cities, while the clans were busy mustering a combined army the likes of which the realm had never seen. Not even during the Chitran-led campaign to vanquish the Garga.

The Akh Nara’s wife had spent every hour of the past days trying to slow it, though it seemed it had not been enough.

“I’ll do everything in my power to ensure the worst does not come to pass,” she told him before ending the communication. “But I’d be lying if I said I believed it could still be stopped. I’m sorry, Roshan. I know what this means for you.”

He listened to her words and nodded along, though not once did he correct her. To tell that, in fact, this outcome was not the tragedy that she believed it to be.

Nor was he alone amongst the Samsara in that sentiment.

Roshan glanced at the Akh Nara, who still sat where Roshan had set him down weeks ago.

The spitting image of a deity, he sat with his legs crossed, his eyes open and unfocused. Fixed upon some reality a thousand years past. Or possibly into the future?

Roshan would never know, for the Akh Nara did not eat or sleep or talk to anyone.

He simply sat there, fighting a war the realm would never know. Saving their lives again and again. One painful second at a time.

As always, Siya sat beside him, patiently accompanying him.

How things change, he mused. Not long ago, he’d envied the Akh Nara. Who wouldn’t?

As a Raja’s son with a budding name of his own, Roshan had been inundated with stories of the young demon. He’d aspired to be like the Akh Nara, not least of all because of their similar age.

To be raised to godhood, beloved by every clan. The most powerful being to walk the realm since the Age of Gods.

But now? Now he was not so sure.

Roshan thought that no one should bear such a heavy burden. Not any demon. Perhaps not even a god.

The camp that that sprung up around the two of them in the weeks since Camar Gadin boasted tents in ordered rows, and aid in every conceivable form had flooded in from demons the realm over.

Food the Samsara did not need and that Siya had barely touched. She accepted regardless because refusing them would have gone against the teachings of the Akh Nara.

Of all the refugees, a hundred seven had organized themselves into an elite fighting force the likes of which the realm had never seen.

A number of them had even been Asuras before they’d died. Before they’d become something… more.

Roshan was convinced of that now. As strange as their new existence was, it was a thought that united all Samsara.

The knowledge, deep down, that they had been revived for a cosmic purpose the likes of which no mortal could hope to fathom.

Roshan had built them as a guardian force, primarily. A symbolic gesture, and nothing more. As much as Maiya and Goddess Siya claimed that Vir was helpless in his current state, Roshan doubted it.

The Destroyer of Vimanas and Uniter of Realms would not succumb to a mere blade.

It proved an easier task than normal.

That was the strange part.

Like their Lord, they could have stood motionless for weeks without complaint.

Still, they maintained the habits of a living camp anyway.

To forego those meant giving up the last vestiges of who they were. The lives they had lived and the comforts they had known.

More than once, Roshan had wondered what was to become of them.

For days now, several had been itching to fight. The Asuras, at first… then others.

Their voices had grown steadily louder with every report Maiya brought Roshan.

And why not? Nothing short of severing the tether that bound them to their god. Each of them knew this as surely as they remembered their deaths.

What better soldiers could there be than Warriors who could not be killed? What better boost to morale than an immortal commander?

What were they doing in a field while their clans prepared to bleed for their cause?

Roshan had held the Samsara back. He’d told them his duty—as well as theirs—was to the Akh Nara. That they would remain at his side until commanded otherwise.

And yet, each passing day had him less and less sure of that answer.

Indecision helped no one, however, and so Roshan made his decision.

He crossed the camp to where Siya sat. Blasphemy though such an action might be, as their leader, it was Roshan’s burden to bear.

The little goddess looked up as he approached. Though she bore a child’s body, Roshan was convinced he would never even begin to comprehend the workings of her divine mind.

“Roshan? Is something the matter?”

“Goddess Siya… May I request a moment of your time?”

She rose without a word, stepping a few paces from the Akh Nara. Far enough that their voices would not distract him, though Roshan was not entirely certain any distance would be far enough to avoid his ears.

“Every demon clan in the realm is mobilizing,” Roshan said in a low voice. “Maiya believes the war between humans and demons can no longer be stopped.”

“I know. She told me when we last spoke but Vir saw this reality well before that. Back when he reunited the realms.”

“He… saw this?” Roshan gulped. “Has the Akh Nara seen the outcome as well?”

Siya shook her head. “You misunderstand. He saw this as one possibility. I do not believe its outcome was written.”

“Of course, my goddess,” Roshan replied, his head bowed, regretting asking the question.

He had come to ask her but one thing, and yet, he found himself broaching another topic first. The easier one.

“If we are to remain here through the war, I am of the mind that we should build properly. Permanent structures and a real supply line, not the offerings from my father that we have been subsisting on. If the Akh Nara is to recover here, the camp should be built to last. I can have the Warriors begin within the day.”

Siya went quiet for a long moment, frowning in thought. “We will not be here long enough for that,” she said at length.

Roshan frowned. “I do not understand. The Akh Nara has not moved in weeks…”

“He has spent this time learning,” Siya said, glancing back at the Akh Nara. “Learning and hopefully… suffice it to say the rupture is nearly stable, for now.”

“That is… good news, then,” Roshan said, daring to feel a flicker of hope. A flicker that was doused almost before it had lighted.

“Only in the way that the encroaching Ash didn’t immediately consume your realms, I’m afraid,” Siya replied. “Nothing I know can close it, nor can Vir. We have tried everything. None of it has been enough, and sooner or later, Vir will slip up. He has not slept once since this began, you know?”

Though Roshan was quite sure the Akh Nara had long ago moved beyond such mundane concerns, he dared not voice his question. “There is truly no hope?” Roshan asked instead.

“There is always hope, dim though it might be,” Siya replied. “Janak left copious research behind. Workshops and laboratories, scattered across the realm, from before the Fall and from his time after. If there is an answer to what is happening to Vir, it may very well reside in one of them. As soon as he can control the rupture well enough to travel without endangering the realm, we are going to find them.”

Roshan took a moment to absorb this new information. And more importantly, its ramifications.

That this field had been a waystation and nothing more, and he had been standing guard over it as though it were the center of the world. Had even been concocting grand plans of fortifying it into a flourishing city.

“When?” he asked, his voice coming out hoarse.

“He is close now,” Siya replied. “Days, perhaps. He has spent every hour learning to suppress the rupture well enough to move. I feel he is nearly there.”

“Then the camp…”

“Does not come with us,” Siya completed. Her voice was gentle, but it left no room for argument. “Vir wishes for no one to follow. When we go, we go alone. The Warriors stay. Stay or go where you please, it matters not to us.”

And just like that, the conundrum that had shackled Roshan to this place was gone.

That the duty he had built his newfound identity around was nothing more than a misguided illusion.

There was, it seemed, no reason to suppress their desires.

“Then I shall take them to war,” Roshan said, the maelstrom that had roiled in his gut all this time finally calm.

Siya gave him a conflicted look.

“The ones who wish to fight, I mean,” Roshan clarified, misinterpreting her look. “There are many among us. They have been asking for days. I stopped them because I believed our place was at the Akh Nara’s side, but if he has asked us not to follow, then it seems clear to me that our place is not here. He does not need us. Demonkind, however, does. The Chitran are dead and my own father is mobilizing. There is a war coming while we sit in a field doing nothing.”

Was this, in fact, not the best possible path for them? To champion great feats in the name of the Akh Nara. To do something for demonkind that could never have been done before?

“Though I've not known him long, I know that Vir would not approve of this.”

“Are these... his words, my Goddess?” Roshan asked, a conflicting tangle of emotions running through him.

“Vir does not know of any of this, and I dare not disturb him,” Siya said, looking unsure for a moment. “But would someone who has fought so hard and so long for peace wish his own immortals to go to war?”

“I fully understand, Goddess Siya,” Roshan replied, bowing even further. “However, are we to simply stand by as the humans attack us? They precipitated this war, not us. To refuse to end this would be a sign of weakness. It goes against the blood in every demon in the land.”

“You understand what you would become, yes? On a battlefield, I mean. Warriors who rise from killing blows. Humans will not see you as soldiers. They will see… something else entirely.”

“I am aware,” Roshan replied, “and I fully wish them to see it.”

Roshan did not know humans. Had not met a single one aside from the Akh Nara’s wife… and though she seemed nearly as divine as his lord himself, she seemed like an exception to the rule.

As far as he was aware, humans were far weaker than even the average demon, and average the Samsara were not.

If humans bore any similarity to demonkind, they would call them an abomination. They would call them an army of the dead. They would say the Akh Nara had committed the most heinous sacrilege to vanquish the living.

And, he thought, they would not be wrong.

Let them say it. The Samsara would strike terror into every human soul. Would accomplish the impossible and vanquish the enemy. Falling and rising. Falling and rising. Until the last human in the realm surrendered.

They would be the Akh Nara’s hands and his feet. They would execute his will across an entire realm, and they would answer the wrong done to the Chitran. To Matiman, and to every demon who had died to human hands in these past weeks.

“Some of them will not want to leave, you know?” Siya said. “They will not all follow you.”

“I shall not force any of them,” Roshan replied immediately. “Those who wish to fight will come with me. Those who wish to stay or return to the Baira may. I encourage it, in fact.” Roshan looked at her. “But what of you? I don’t dare to presume, lady goddess, but will you be alright? With the Akh Nara as… preoccupied as he is?”

Something crossed Siya’s face that he could not read, but he thought he caught a flicker of a smile.

“I have been alone longer than you could possibly imagine,” she said with some amusement. “I am simply glad to be with family who loves me once again.”

To that, Roshan had nothing to say.

He looked at the Akh Nara one last time. The demon who had saved him from death. Who had breathed new life into him when by all rights he should have perished.

The deity to crown all deities.

Bowing deeply, one final time, Roshan turned and went to gather his warriors for war. And he went with pride. For before he marshalled the Samsara, he had a stop to make first.

It was high time he met with his father.

Raja Sagun’Ra perched high atop one of the many posts that filled his chamber, itself nestled within the Origin suspended high above the ground of the Grand Cavern that comprised Jalak Kallol. The underground stronghold of the Iksana. Never invaded, and mostly unvisited and unknown to those not of the clan.

From the bare rock, his distant predecessors had carved out this space, leaving an interconnected tangle of soaring rock buttresses, all to support the suspended structure he called his home, as had countless Rajas before him.

Were an outsider to enter this sacred area, they would find nothing but a maze of rock pillars that soared almost to the ceiling, with scarcely enough space to walk between them. Sagun’Ra felt most would find it less a home and more a demented prison, dark as it was.

Yet to a clan that saw without eyes and perceived prana in a way the others could never dream, to a Raja who moved through the shadows as if it were an extension of his own body, no space was more comfortable. A sentiment shared by any of his clan who gained the privilege of visiting this holy sanctum.

His guest today was, however, not among them.

“I must admit,” Raja Jagath Aindri said, looking unsettled as he perched atop the rock column next to Sagun’Ra’s own. “I had not expect an audience with you here, of all places.”

“Great honor,” Sagun’Ra replied.

“Indeed,” Jagath said, and he knew the Raja was lying. But Sagun’Ra had not invited this foreign Raja here to set him at ease.

No, after Jagath’s incessant requests to learn of any progress with the investigation into Raja Matiman Chitra’s death—a task which Maiya had personally tasked him with—Sagun’Ra found it fitting that he join him here, in his inner sanctum, to receive the news.

“Your team has concluded their investigation then?” Jagath asked, to which Sagun’Ra did not reply. He’d never known why the others sought to ask meaningless questions. As if he would have invited the Raja for any other reason.

“N’Ardra,” Sagun’Ra rasped. Jagath frowned, opening his mouth to ask who he was referring to, when a third figure rose from the shadows atop a nearby pillar.

“I bring my report, Raja,” N’Ardra said, kneeling and bowing her head as was customary.

“Begin,” Sagun’Ra replied.

“It is this one’s opinion that the information be shared in confidence, Raja.”

“The other Rajas will learn the truth soon enough,” Jagath said dismissively. “Your Raja summoned me here expressly to hear this news. I think there should be no problem here?”

N’Ardra looked toward Sagun’Ra, who nodded slightly.

“Sabotage and misdirection,” N’Ardra reported, wasting no time getting to the point. It was one reason why Sagun’Ra preferred her. “We’ve reason to believe the perpetrators were demons, my Raja.”

Sagun’Ra’s eyes widened.

“Preposterous!” Jagath roared. “Everyone saw what happened that day!”

“Eyewitness accounts were unanimous,” N’Ardra agreed.

“Then—”

“There was another,” the Iksana spy said, cutting off the Raja. An action Sagun’Ra approved. Jagath, like most others, spoke much and said little. “An old Iksana in the crowd. Bearing Sight.”

“Oh?” Sagun’Ra asked. “Who?”

“Jai’sal,” N’Ardra replied.

This time, Sagun’Ra did visibly react. “Him? Why…”

N’Ardra shook her head. “Our former Raja is as inscrutable as ever. Even learning of his presence there was a task, let alone finding him after.”

The Iksana Raja glanced at his counterpart, who’d gone suddenly silent.

“Jai’sal speaks no lies,” Sagun’Ra said at length. “What did he see?”

“Demons of no clan, my Raja. Acting in concert. Based on his words, I strongly suspect these were operatives of the Pagan Order. This concludes my investigation.”

“Well found. You are dismissed.”

N’Ardra bowed her head and disappeared into the shadows without another word spoken.

Silence reigned for a time after, and it was Sagun’Ra who broke it.

“You knew,” he said.

“Only after the fact,” Jagath replied. “I would never have condoned such a thing.”

“And yet, you do now.”

Jagath’s lips twitched. “That I do.”

“It was why you wished to be here.”

“I do not deny it,” Jagath replied, straining to find the Iksana Raja’s eyes in the gloom.

“Why?”

“To present you with an offer,” Jagath replied. “One that you cannot refuse.”

“A promise was made,” Sagun’Ra said. “To Maiya, to investigate. No lies will be told.”

“Nor am I asking you to,” Jagath said, shaking his head. “Your oath was to investigate. That is now done. Simply delay your findings for a time.”

“In return…”

“I give you Mahādi,” Jagath said with a smile. “Yours, to rule as you see fit. A new bastion for the Iksana.”

“The City of the Gods,” Sagun’Ra replied.

“A sacred place for all demonkind, yes, but none more so than the Iksana.”

“Not yours to give. The Asuras. The Akh Nara.”

“The Asuras merely occupy it as a temporary measure. When war ignites between humans and demonkind—and I assure you there will be war—the Asuras will be spread thin. As for the Akh Nara, well, if he does return, what will he do? Deport your clan and risk the ire of demonkind? All while a war is on? I think not. I think he will leave you be, for fear of opening old wounds. The last thing the Akh Nara wants is a civil war.”

He considered the Raja’s words, but he did not linger on them long. Little risk in this plan of Jagath’s, and much reward. Should the Raja fail, the Iksana would reveal his treachery. Should he succeed, however…

Sagun’Ra’s eyes glistened. The Iksana’s most sacred place was a most worthy reward indeed.
