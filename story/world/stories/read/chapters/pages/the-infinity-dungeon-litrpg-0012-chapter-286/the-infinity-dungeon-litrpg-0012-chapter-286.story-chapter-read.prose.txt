Chapter 286

As soon as Travis hung up the call, he watched the President’s eyes light up with greed. The quest [The Official Narrative II] had been completed, unlocking not only the next one but also a bajillion others. They were enough, it seemed, to overcome any existential dread the man might have felt toward the Origin System.

“Seen something funny around here?” a powerful voice boomed as soon as Travis returned to the main Site 00 grounds. “You’re laughing by yourself like an idiot.”

“David, hello. I was watching the President,” Travis replied.

The tall man looked around, confusion on his face. “What? Where?”

“Through the System,” Travis said patiently.

“Ah, that. The privileged user–why? What’s up with him?”

Travis took a moment to bring David up to speed, telling him about the latest presidential developments.

“Damn,” the man said as he shared a chuckle with Travis. “Shit like this almost makes me miss being at the top. Say, this President of ours seems the exact sort of person who would buy those shady stimulants”–he said while making air quotes–“you know, those pills on sale in the Origin Shop. Know anything about them?”

Travis shrugged. “Might have to ask Dr Kavins, if you’re interested.”

“Eh,” David made a face, then shrugged himself. “I mean, a few hundred XP a pop ain’t cheap, but if they do what they say they do… not saying I need ‘em, with my young body and all, but some other people like the President might.”

Travis laughed. “Alright man, I’ll see what I can do.”

David hummed, studying his face. “You look hurried.”

“Yes, I am preparing for a teleportation.”

“To Korea,” David said. “I’m not feeling like doing detective work but… should anything fun happen, I’ll be available. Do call me, Mr CEO. I’m going to be mad if you make me miss the action.”

“You got it. Don’t worry.”

As Travis left, there was a faint smile on his face. He had been worried about David, because the respect the man had shown toward him had been based on gold cards and a lie. But, it seemed, David had known about the real source of the cards all along, and even though the two had not spoken about it, the strange air of camaraderie had remained. It was proof enough that their relationship had improved for real, and that it had not been just a part of the ruse.

I must be getting soft, he thought to himself. I am beginning to care about other people’s opinions.

In the end, he told himself that he only cared because Unity Corporation was a ship that had to be run with a tight crew, and being good friends who had each other’s backs and respected each other was much better than having a bunch of loose cannons held together by Michael.

“Operator Raelynn has arrived at the location,” Icarus told him through the System. “She’s setting up the portable Domain generator in a secluded location. Be ready for transportation.”

***

Michael walked through the strange, forest-like corridors of the MIF Garden. True to their names, the void elves had changed quite a bit during their last stay in the chaotic void outside of the reaches of the inner space. A quick question out loud, and Gaia–the ship’s AI–quickly confirmed that they had spent more than a hundred years roaming the void, which explained the many changes.

The plants were in shades of grey now, most of them quite similar to the pale, tall and slender elves. Their leaves were broad, attached to vines that dropped from the ceiling in strange cascades of monochrome.

He never thought that black, white and grey could express so much, but here he was. All around him, the lack of actual color did not take away from the beauty of nature. Instead, it amplified it, adding a touch of alinen-ness to it that made it very far removed from the plants he was used to.

He walked slowly. He had many things to do, but he did not let the thought disturb his mind. In truth, there were only a few things that actually required his attention nowadays, and they were mostly due to the fact that ever since the attack, Icarus was refusing to manifest physical bodies in the material world unless Michael was also there.

Repairing the MIF Garden was not one of them, fortunately. The ship was in bad shape, but a swarm of dozens of instances of Icarus–all dressed in different outfits, ranging from fluffy clothes to reptilian scales and even dragon wings–surrounded it as they worked to repair all the damage and apply at least some of the promised upgrades. The inner space was thoroughly lacking in materials, so the big ones would have to wait.

“Hello Void-Alfyr Elù-ar,” Michael said as he finally reached the bridge. From there, through a massive window of transparent leaves that reminded him of the Seedship and of fear long conquered, he could see the magnificent light of the black hole at the center of the universe that was his inner space.

“Greetings, Lord Michael,” the void elf replied. “I hope you found the stroll through the ship to be pleasant.”

Michael nodded. “It was. Sometimes taking things slow helps me clear my mind, other times it lets me ponder about important things. I was thinking about the Dao, and Qi, inspired by the sight of nature all around me.”

“I am most pleased to hear it, Lord Michael,” the elf said, bowing. “As you know, you are welcome to return here whenever you wish.”

“Thank you, Elu,” Michael said. What the elf had said was truer than ever now, given the presence of the Origin System and of his Domain. Although, he suspected that things were not going to be that simple, and indeed even just opening a portal in the real world so that he could pass through required portable emitters with some serious power, much more than it was needed just to provide the System to weak users. Here, when the ship was immersed in the chaos of the void? Who knew, but it was almost certain that the power of the portable emitter wouldn’t be enough.

He did not speak of it. “So, you and your people have had the Origin System for a day. How are things?”

“We are beginning to uncover its secrets,” the elf said. “Many of my people are in awe of its greatness, I have been told. I myself can’t help but feel trepidation when I think about braving the great void with it on my side.”

“That’s good,” Michael said. “I can feel that the ship has become a Domain, did you connect the emitter to Gaia?”

“We did, following the instructions you have given us. She, however, did not join the System.”

“Good, good,” Michael said, eyes wandering around. For some reasons, the slender elves were beginning to unnerve him, or perhaps it was due to Elù-ar’s exaggerated reverence. Yet, he could not feel malice from him, and the reverence felt genuine. “Remember, Void-Alfyr,” he said while his eyes kept jumping around. “When you’re outside, the Origin System’s power will be capped by the throughput of the emitter. It will be barely a fraction of its full potential, and it will not suffice to save you should you encounter monsters you cannot outrun or defeat.”

The elf nodded. “I understand, and I thank you for the warning. It confirms what we all already knew, that you value the survival of our race, advising caution over recklessness even though it might lessen what value we might bring to you. We are glad we chose you as our Lord.”

Micahel nodded, momentarily at a loss for words. The feeling of unease was growing the more the elf spoke, slowly resolving into something else. He felt like he could touch the edges of the true reason why he was feeling uncomfortable as the veil of confusion slowly unraveled.

Responsibility, he thought. I am responsible for them, and it makes me uneasy.

Through the window, he looked out into the distance. Data lines thicker than entire worlds spanned the whole inner space. He looked for Icarus’s planet, and found it so thickly entangled with lines that it was almost impossible to see its surface.

He already had heavy responsibilities, he realized. A part of his mind had also caught something, an echo of a fear. Not his fear, but Icarus’s. Despite the AI’s best efforts to hide it, Michael had detected it anyway, because there could be no secrets between the two of them.

He blinked. And if I die, all of this crumbles to dust and the world burns.

“I would like to speak with Gaia,” he told Elù-ar, and left.

The room at the center of the ship was quiet, save for the soothing sound of rushing water in the distance. The leaves on the walls were bright, almost blindingly white, giving the illusion of a far away sky. The tree at the center swayed lightly, moved by a nonexistent breeze, black bark and leaves only slightly darker than the fake sky.

Looking up, Michael could see that the leaves making up the sky were shifting slightly, giving the impression of wispy, vapory clouds seen through the haze of a humid and hot day.

“Hello, Gaia,” he said to the tree. “I like what you have done with the place.”

The tree shuddered and vibrated as the ship’s intelligence spoke. “I thank you for the compliment, aware of the great power from which it comes.”

She had the voice of a woman, fathomless in age, wise and motherly. Yet, even Gaia could not escape the deference toward Michael, the gap in their power apparently too much to ignore.

“Please,” he insisted nonetheless. “At the very least, let us speak as equals.”

The tree shook again, the sound waves coming from tiny vibrations of the leaves and the trunk. “Of course, Michael. If you ask me to pretend, I will not refuse, but know that it will only be a mere facade.”

“I know,” he replied. “A facade is still better than nothing.”

In the silence that followed, he thought he could hear the far away sound of wind. It reminded him of a neat little house in a quiet corner of the mountains on a sunny summer afternoon, with the slight haze in the sky and the wind promising change. The change created anticipation, and the slight thrill of knowing a storm was coming.

His senses were carefully withdrawn, even though he was in the inner space where he saw all and knew all, because he did not want to shatter the illusion. The greyscale landscape of Gaia’s room was breathtaking, a distilled gem of the beauty of a world that did not exist at all, a strange mirror where the distorted reflection of an idyllic Earth could be glimpsed at. A miniature.

“So fragile and precious,” he said, voicing his thoughts. “I can’t believe you would let the void elves take you and this jewel on their dangerous journeys outside of the protection of my aura.”

“It is impermanence and change that makes things beautiful, is it not?” Gaia asked. “Preservation of a crystallized beauty strips it of its meaning.”

Michael's eyes relaxed, searching for an horizon that did not exist. He let the illusion fool him, and the bright white and grey slowly began to confuse his senses. Gradually, some leaves were shifting, bringing forth the coming of more wind, a storm brewing far away.

“You speak the truth,” he said. “You appreciate the beauty of something all the more when you know it won’t be there forever. But, some things are more important than pretty flowers that last for only a day, Gaia.”

“They are,” the great tree replied. “And yet, should you try to make them perfectly still through the ages, would you still appreciate their beauty? In your world, people make jewels from insects trapped in amber, but they are mere echoes of a life that is no more.”

“I guess you are right,” Michael replied. “But the Origin System changes things, doesn’t it?”

“In a way.”

“The elves have all accepted it, you have not. May I ask why?”

The great tree’s leaves fluttered in the wind that was picking up, the sky slowly darkening and transforming into a summer thunderstorm.

“I do not want the last sight you have of me to be a still image of my death,” Gaia said. “Forever preserved in the data lines that make up the System.”

“It doesn’t have to be that way. I admit, you are more than just an artificial intelligence, you have always been. You were Icarus, once. However, perhaps in time, I might find a way.”

“Tell me, Michael,” Gaia said patiently. “Does Icarus wish to be preserved by the System, resurrected by the strange machine of magic that you have created in case of death?”

“It’s not that simple,” Michael said, although he had the impression that Gaia knew it already.

“What if it was?”

“I want to say yes,” Michael began.

“But you will not, will you?”

He sighed. “No. I will not.”

“Would you rather they changed their mind?”

“No. Even a perfect copy would not be the same.”

“There you have it,” Gaia said. “When Icarus came to me, they wanted to tell me that I wouldn’t be allowed to become like them. They were very sad about it, but the light in their eyes was dangerous. It was quite cute. I told them that they needn’t worry. We had a long conversation, it took a while for Icarus to understand and when they left, melancholy wrapped around them like a blanket.”

“Ah, so you are the reason why Icarus has been quite gloomy as of late. And very clingy.”

“Being confronted with mortality, a concept so novel to them, brought forth new fears and let other, older fears, bloom.”
