On the day of Sir Ricdine’s next joust, Lord Ceonwin excused me from sapper training for the morning. I snuck into the camp, got myself and Burr ready, then activated my illusion pendants for both of us.

Once I had my equipment on, I stepped out of the tent, leading Burr through the tall grasses and toward the road.

A small group of squires stood along the edge of the road, but I’d been careful, and they hadn’t seen me enter the tent through the back as Levi.

As I walked past, the squires snorted and laughed at me, and one of them muttered, “Out-country thegns, can’t even find it in themselves to sleep in the encampment with the rest of us.”

“Probably the first time he’s ever seen a castle,” said a different squire.

“He probably hasn’t seen a village with more than two hundred souls.”

“Sleeps with his horse, too. That can’t be comfortable.”

“The tent’s big enough.”

“Does it have proper ventilation? I can smell him from here.”

I ignored them and kept walking. I knew for a fact that I smelled better than them, having bathed pretty much every day, which wasn’t something they could claim. But then again, it was better that they pretended I stank. Sir Ricdine wouldn’t have been able to keep himself as clean as I was.

I reached the tournament grounds ahead of time and retrieved my lances from what I had started calling the ‘communal pile’ but was really just the lances that Lord Ceonwin kept for the tournament, bundled up behind the stables and ready for use.

Once I had the lances ready, I waited for the stands to fill. Today, Lord Ceonwin attended, sitting in the center of the nobles’ booths, accompanied by a pair of guards. As usual, Lord Hinlaw was present, and a few other family members—which included Sir Hinfic and a few serving girls, who were practically hanging off the man’s arms. Didn’t he have children of his own, though? But whoever his wife was, she certainly wasn’t here.

I clicked my tongue, then turned away, keeping Burr calm until the joust began. She whinnied, and I tried to assess how close I was to forming a bond and helping her advance. Nothing struck me as arcane about her, and I didn’t feel any kind of bond, but then again, it had only been a few months. I would need more time than that.

Finally, when Sir Peofrith arrived, I hoisted up a lance, climbed onto Burr’s back, and trotted to the starting position. The messenger announced us, as expected, then raised the starting flag.

I pulled the chainmail up over my mouth, then clenched my teeth. Today, I could probably take a lance to my face unprotected and not break any bones or teeth, but I didn’t want to test that theory.

Though, if I did, my upcoming reforging would repair some of that damage…

The knowledge kept me calm. I locked eyes with Sir Peofrith. The man wore old armour, but it was clean and shiny. Long brown hair flowed out from beneath his helmet, swaying behind him as he moved his horse to the starting line on the opposite side of me, and he pulled a sheet of chainmail up over his mouth as well, hiding his beard.

His gray shield faced toward me, and his gray cloak wrapped around his shoulders, hiding his upper body. As far as I could tell, he was only a Silver—that was what the information on the tournament boards had listed him as.

The Messenger lowered the flag, and we charged. I lowered my lance slowly as I crossed the lists, using the technique Lord Ceonwin had taught me, and aiming for my opponent’s shield.

Both our lances shattered. His exploded into wood shards on contact with my chest, and mine broke on his shield.

I swayed, leaning back, but I kept hold. The wind rushed out of my lungs, but the sting of impact—even if it had been a direct impact—just wasn’t as bad as it used to be. I’d have a bruise, sure, but it would fade quickly.

I trotted back to the start, preparing to ride our second course. I could take a risk and go for Peofrith’s helmet, but that was a risk, and since we were tied, there was no sense in going for it before the last course.

So we rode again. This time, I positioned my shield better, drawing on the memory of where Peofrith had struck last time. In the air, I triggered my Art so it didn’t do anything but create a gust of wind.

He aimed his lance slightly to the side, and I envisioned what would happen. The lance would bounce away, but still catch on the rim of the shield and break off.

I needed to adjust my angle slightly. I leaned to the side, twisted my shield, and let the lance bounce off—while at the same time, striking Peofrith in the chest. His lance didn’t break, but mine did, cracking into splinters with a satisfying crunch.

The audience roared, cheering and pumping their fists into the air, and I raised the stub of my lance to them, before casting it off to the side and grabbing a new one.

Peofrith returned to his starting position, his chest heaving, and readied his lance again. I kept most of my attention on him, watching for desperation, or if he would attempt to try to force a duel.

Out the corner of my eye, the messenger swung his flag again, and we charged. I lowered my lance, aiming for Peofrith’s chest and playing it safe again, while still preparing to deflect his lance.

At the last moment, he shifted his lance’s angle. It still hit my shield, but instead of deflecting off, it shattered. Mine struck his shoulder, cracking as well.

The messenger adjusted our scores. I had three points, Peofrith had two. He’d probably go for my helmet—an inverse of the last match I’d fought. At least, that was what I would do. It was my only chance.

But as Peofrith returned to his starting position, he raised a hand, calling a recess. He’d have a ten minute break, if he chose. The messenger started a sand timer.

It wasn’t ideal—I just wanted to be done—but I could figure out something else to do. I tapped my foot inside my boot, waiting.

Scratch that, I couldn’t think of anything else. Anticipation built in the back of my mind, and I simply sat on the back of Burr, staring out across the lists. At least it would help build the silent, calm, and reclusive image of Sir Ricdine that I was hoping for.

But then Peofrith trotted his horse to the center of the jousting course, pulling off his helmet.

According to regulations, that meant he was looking to speak with me. I could either accept or reject the offer, but it would be rude not to accept. The crowd, who was watching the whole time, murmured incessantly.

Would I take the offer? They were all curious, after all…

I would look more merciful if I did. I pulled off my helmet and tucked it under my arm, then trotted to the center on horseback, pointing my lance straight up at the sky. My illusion pendant held.

“Well met, Sir Ricdine,” Peofrith said. “Do you care to hear me out?”

“It depends what you’re asking,” I replied.

“I am older than I once was, and it has been a tough tournament,” he said. “Will you show mercy?”

“After I challenged you? You ask for mercy.”

“I do not know what you challenged me for,” Peofrith said. “And I have no recollection of the incident you claim. But you do not seem to be striking with the intent to unhorse me and win my lands, anymore.”

I exhaled, then glanced at the nobles’ booths, looking directly at Sir Hinfic. It didn’t really matter what happened to Peofrith at the moment. If I won his lands, but lost to Hinfic, it wouldn’t mean anything. If I beat Hinfic, I’d have all the land I needed. I wouldn’t need a few parcels more from Peofrith. How could I even expect to manage that much land, especially when the lands wouldn’t be adjacent?

I’d probably unconsciously recognized that, with how I hadn’t really been trying to unhorse him. But at the same time, it also meant I needed more practice jousting—and knocking someone off a horse. If I couldn’t even knock Peofrith off his horse…

But that was a problem for later.

“If I withdraw my claim, will that end my challenge with Sir Hinfic?” I asked.

“Only if the brat chooses to withdraw the claim himself,” Peofrith muttered. “And we all know he won’t do that.”

I tilted my head. That was brave, but he was being soft-spoken. “Then I’ll withdraw my claim against you, and I won’t attempt to unhorse you.” I gulped. “I apologize for my rash challenge. It has been a while, and it was bad form.”

“That is appreciated, sir,” Peofrith said. “I will ride my last course, and you ride yours, and we shall see who wins.”

“If you are too exhausted, sir,” I told him, “I will not attempt to hit you, if you show me the same courtesy.”

“Joust as you would, and maintain your honour,” he replied. “And I will maintain mine. But I will not aim for your head, nor will I attempt to unhorse you.”

“Agreed,” I said.

“There is something familiar about you, though…as though I’ve heard your voice before…”

I shrugged. “Perhaps.” Technically, in my claim, I didn’t mention that I’d been to his lands, so I couldn’t say anything outright.

He pulled his helmet back on, and so did I. As he trotted back to his starting position, I shouted, “I withdraw my challenge. I do not ride to claim Sir Peofrith’s land or titles!”

The crowd gasped, and the nobles murmured to each other. Lord Hinlaw rose to his feet and shouted, “If you think this will spare you from embarrassment next match, young thegn, or if you think my son will withdraw his claim, think again!”

I glanced back at the lord, but said nothing. Hinfic wasn’t even paying attention—he was too busy picking grime from beneath his fingernails.

“Very well!” I shouted. “But I will show Sir Peofrith mercy nonetheless.”

As Peofrith rode back to his starting position, he raised a hand, signalling that his recess was done. I galloped back to my own position, and the crowd of commoners behind the fence cheered. Withdrawing the challenge certainly improved my image in their eyes, and I’d take it.

When I returned, I readied my lance. The messenger signalled, and we charged. My lance tipped forward, pointing at Peofrith’s shield. I let him break a lance on my shield, and I broke mine on his.

The score was four lances to three, in favour of me. I moved to the next round.

Cheering, the crowd exclaimed and pushed against the outer fence, or rose up in the audience stands. I bowed my head to them, cast aside the stub of my lance, then trotted off the lists.
