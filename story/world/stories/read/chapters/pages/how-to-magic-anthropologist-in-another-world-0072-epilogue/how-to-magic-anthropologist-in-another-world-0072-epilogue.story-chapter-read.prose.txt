Calr

Calr woke up for his watch shift. Alice had taken first watch, Raik had taken second, and he had volunteered for third.

For a few seconds, he lay still, listening. The camp was quiet rather than silent. There was the faint crackle of the fire, the occasional shift of fabric from the tents, and the distant, irregular sounds of the undead moving far beyond the sanctified perimeter.

He pushed himself up and stepped out of the tent he shared with Shingo.

Cold air brushed against his skin. The fire had burned lower, its glow softer, casting long shadows across the camp.

He immediately scanned the area for discrepancies.

Raik was sitting by the fire, his hand hovering just above the flames, his posture was rigid and his focus absolute. Other than that, nothing seemed out of place: the sanctified boundary still held, and there was no movement where none should be there.

Calr’s eyes drifted toward the tent Alice shared with Ja’a.

The flap had not moved.

That meant Alice had not returned after her shift ended. She had likely teleported to the Sunless Reach again. That seemed to be her habit when she had free time, disappearing somewhere beyond their reach to train or experiment.

He decided to walk toward Raik.

As he approached, the intensity on the Agame’s face became more apparent, his brows were slightly furrowed, and his gaze was locked onto his own hand as if he were trying to bend reality through sheer will.

“What are you doing?” Calr asked.

“I am trying to make cold instead of heat,” Raik replied without looking up.

Calr paused.

That was not something he had expected.

He closed his eyes for a brief moment and ran through his memory of the battlefield again, activating Analyse.

He had been out of it at the time, but now he was able to reconstruct the sequence.

Kan’s chains had chattered, and there had been a faint trace of frost at the edges.

“Did NoName use cold with your powers?” Calr asked.

“She did,” Raik nodded once.

Calr frowned. “That is weird.”

“Tell me about it,” Raik replied, finally glancing at him. “Do you have any advice for me?”

Calr considered the question seriously for a moment, then allowed a small, almost amused smile to slip through.

“Let me access all your clan archives, and I might be of use to you,” he said, half joking and half completely serious.

The amount of knowledge stored within an ancient clan like the Agames was staggering to think about.

To his surprise, Raik did not dismiss the idea outright.

Instead, he actually considered it.

“I will see what I can do,” he said after a moment.

Calr blinked.

That was more than he had expected.

Raik stood and stretched slightly, as if preparing to end his shift. Calr hesitated, then spoke before he could walk away.

“Before you go, there is something I wanted to tell you in private.”

Raik stopped.

“What is it?” he asked, a slight frown forming.

Calr took a breath.

“Donas has your father’s book.”

The reaction was immediate.

Raik stiffened, and heat burst out from him in a sudden wave. The air itself seemed to tighten. The fire flared brighter in response, as if resonating with his aura.

Calr instinctively stepped back, putting distance between himself and the surge of power.

“Explain,” Raik said.

His voice was controlled, but the tension beneath it was unmistakable.

“I saw it in one of Kitame’s memories,” Calr said. “Donas has it. He claimed he was the first to arrive after your father’s final stand.”

His voice wavered slightly despite himself.

Raik forced his aura back under control, though his fists remained clenched.

“Describe the book... please”

“It was leather-bound,” Calr said. “The cover had a heptagram. Each point was a different color. Red at the top, then orange, yellow, green, cyan, blue, and purple. Inside, there was text written in multiple languages.”

Raik exhaled sharply.

“So the bastard does have it,” he hissed. “I thought it was lost with my father.”

Calr studied him.

“What is so important about it?”

Raik hesitated.

For a moment, it seemed like he might answer.

Then both of them felt a subtle disturbance, a familiar ripple in the air, or rather in the space their auras occupied.

Raik straightened.

“We will talk about it later.”

Alice appeared a second later.

There was no flash of light or dramatic entrance. One moment the space was empty, and the next she was there, as if she had always been standing in that exact spot.

Her expression was anything but calm.

She was frowning deeply.

“What is it?” Raik asked immediately, his earlier tension gone, replaced with clean composure. “Did something go wrong?”

“I tried to teleport next to Katar’s sister,” Alice said. “It did not work.”

Raik blinked.

“You can do that?”

“Yes,” Alice replied. “My power is wish-based. It should have worked. Instead, I lost all my mana and did not teleport.”

Calr felt a slight chill.

That was not how most teleporters’ abilities behaved. He wondered if she would open up and explain in detail how she did it. His ancestors had teleporters too, after all.

“Does that mean she is dead?” he finally asked.

“I do not think so,” Alice said, shaking her head. “If she were dead, the wish would be impossible. It would fail without costing mana... I think. I have tried wishing for impossible things before.”

Raik crossed his arms.

“Then what do you think happened?”

Alice hesitated for a fraction of a second.

“She is probably out of bounds.”

“What is your range?” Calr asked.

“It used to be about a week of travel,” Alice said. “But I have grown stronger since then, so it should be more now.”

Raik nodded slowly.

“From the Sunless Reach, right? That removes a lot of possibilities, but not enough.”

Alice let out a long breath.

“I guess I need to grow my soul some more,” she muttered. “I really need to push my kindred side next.”

She grimaced.

“I hate cardio training.”

Calr almost smiled at that.

Raik gave a short nod.

“We will figure it out later. For now, get some rest.”

Alice nodded and turned toward her tent.

Raik left shortly after, his earlier composure still in place, though Calr could tell that the conversation about the book was far from finished.

The camp grew quiet again.

Calr remained by the fire, taking his place for the watch. The flames crackled softly in front of him. Beyond the sanctified ground, something moved in the distance. Something big. Ectamel was probably having an orange-flag kind of night.

He leaned back slightly, letting his gaze drift across the camp.

Even so, his thoughts drifted toward Kitame.

Was she safe traveling at night in the dead zone ?

Kitame

After hours of running southward, the four girls finally stopped.

Obora was panting hard. She had stopped flying an hour ago, and the strain was catching up to her. It was already dark, and Suu had kept guiding them away from stronger undead while they ran.

“I had him. I had him. If not for that Karda girl, I would have beaten him,” shouted NoName.

“Sorry, I was not able to keep pinning her down,” Suu replied.

“No, everything is my fault,” Obora said between heavy breaths. “They kept escaping my dream trap for some reason.”

“Did we blow our cover for nothing, or did you get anything useful from that girl?” NoName asked.

They all turned to look at Kitame.

The red-haired girl hesitated. She considered telling them everything, but Calr’s parting words echoed in her mind.

“Make sure you do not reveal my existence to anyone,” he had said. “Otherwise, Donas might decide that incest is the most efficient way to build his army of teleporters… cousin.”

Kitame swallowed.

Damn him! Damn that boy for making her mistrust her sisters, for making her mistrust her father.

“I am not sure,” she said slowly. “Her mind was… weird.”

“Weird how?” Suu asked.

“Weird, like when Obora spends too much time in the great dream,” Kitame explained.

Obora frowned slightly but did not interrupt.

“I saw a world full of magical marvels,” Kitame continued. “Buildings reaching toward the heavens, strange artifacts attached to walls and carried in everyone’s pockets, and carriages that moved without horses.”

“So she is a dreamer,” Obora said with a nod. “That explains why she was able to awaken.”

“Dreamer or not, she is important,” Kitame said. “She had a lot of obscure knowledge, I think.”

“Didn’t the report we bought say she was mythic?” Suu asked.

“The report could be wrong,” NoName said with a shrug. “It is not like she was a known entity before the Pikar Steppe incident.”

“Anything else?” NoName asked, looking at Kitame again. “Did you figure out how she realized you are a telepath?”

“She did not,” Kitame said with a sigh. “She just knew that telepaths had red hair like mine.”

“Red hair, like that boy too,” Suu frowned.

NoName’s eyes widened.

“Wait, is that boy a telepath too? Is that why you had that weird interaction?”

“No,” Kitame said quickly, a hint of panic slipping into her voice. “He is not.”

She looked around, unease creeping into her expression.

“What did he do to you?” Obora asked, her concern replacing the earlier frustration in her voice.

“I think he had some kind of psychic affinity,” Kitame said, forcing the lie out. “He kept showing me terrible memories.”

“Memories of what?” NoName asked, frowning.

“Terrible things,” Kitame said, shuddering. “I kept reliving the worst moments of my childhood.”

The group fell into silence after that.

They resumed walking through the dark, fighting off a few weak undead and carefully avoiding stronger ones. Suu continued to guide them, her senses keeping them just out of danger.

During that time, NoName kept talking.

She complained loudly about the Agame boy, calling him a coward and insisting that she would have won in a proper one-on-one fight.

No one challenged her.

After a while, the number of undead began to increase. The distant shapes in the darkness multiplied, and their movements grew more frequent.

Then, just as suddenly, they vanished.

The number dropped to zero as they reached a valley.

“We are here,” Suu said.

At the edge of an open field stood a single zombie.

Its eyes glowed with a faint red light. Apple blossoms grew from the side of its neck, their pale petals stark against the decaying flesh.

Suu stepped forward.

“Lady Kiri, in the name of your alliance with the Old Realm cult, we ask for asylum.”

The zombie did not respond with words. Instead, it reached down and pulled open a hidden trapdoor camouflaged beneath the grass. Beneath it lay a narrow staircase descending into darkness.

The girls exchanged brief glances before stepping inside.

The zombie closed the trapdoor behind them, plunging them into complete darkness.

For a moment, no one spoke.

Then Obora fumbled with her pouch and pulled out a lightstone. A soft glow filled the tunnel, revealing rough stone walls stretching downward.

They began to descend.

The tunnel was long and sloped steadily downward. Their footsteps echoed faintly as they walked.

After nearly an hour of moving in a straight line, the passage finally opened.

They stepped into a wide cavern.

Unlike the tunnel, this space was lit.

A dim, unnatural light filled the area, casting long shadows across the uneven ground. Across the walls, corpses similar to the one guarding the entrance stood shoulder to shoulder, their backs pressed against the stone, all of them had similar blossoms.

At the center of the cavern, a woman worked over a corpse.

She was stitching limbs together using a sharp wooden needle and thick fibrous stems. Her movements were steady and precise, as if she had done this countless times before.

The woman looked pale, almost corpse-like, yet undeniably beautiful. Her silver hair fell loosely around her shoulders. Tree roots grew from her heart, branching outward into thin, vein-like lines that spread across her body.

The scent of apples hung heavily in the air.

NoName stepped forward and bowed. The others quickly followed.

“Sorry for bothering you,” she said. “We needed a place to rest and recover before we were overwhelmed by the undead.”

The woman waved a hand in a dismissive gesture, not even looking up from her work.

Suu’s breath hitched.

She had noticed something hanging nearby.

A torso.

Half-burned and charred, missing its head and limbs, suspended by chains from the cavern ceiling.

“Is that… Todor?” she asked, her voice trembling slightly.

“Yep,” the necromancer replied casually. “I agreed to build him a stronger body in exchange for his help.”

She pulled the thread tight, securing another limb in place.

“With his help, I can finally move forward with my goal.”

She smiled faintly.

“Claiming the powers of Merumom, Dravac of death, the Lord of Bones.”

She paused, her fingers brushing over the corpse as if admiring her work.

“Once this body is done, we will finally be able to venture into the center of the dead zone.”

“I will no longer be weaker than my sister.”
