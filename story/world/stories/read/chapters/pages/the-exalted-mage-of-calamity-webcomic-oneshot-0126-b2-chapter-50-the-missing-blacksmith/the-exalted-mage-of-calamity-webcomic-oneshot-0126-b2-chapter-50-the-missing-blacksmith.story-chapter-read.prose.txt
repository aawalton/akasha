“Why… did things have to be this way…”

The sound of a broom sweeping across the flat stone floor echoed through the smithy. The anvils were gone, the forge taken, all the weapons and armor missing from their decorative stands. There was nothing left inside except the cold air that had settled in with the absence of the blazing furnace.

Despite the emptiness, the gloominess—Theo kept sweeping. It was the only thing he could think to do. The heavy dust from those people’s boots still covered the floor. Though he had managed to sweep up most of it, deep bootprints remained tracked into every corner of the forge. They had been thorough in taking everything. Everyone.

He had never realized how strange emptiness could feel. Even on days when Master Orban wasn’t working, there was always a lingering warmth in the smithy. A sleeping heat buried beneath the coals he had looked at each day. The burning, smoky scent of ash and metal.

The signs of this happening had been… vivid. Each time he looked at his master, he saw that deep, deep feeling of being lost. That at any time, things would just end.

…That feeling had come true. The memories of the past several years. All gone overnight.

He kept reading the words over and over in his head, all in Orban’s voice.

Theo,

I sold the smithy. Those rats dressed in fancy suits’ll come by to claim the place over the next few days. Don’t cause any trouble, you hear? The remaining vix is yours. Use it to find a better master. One that won’t disappoint. And don’t search for me. Don’t waste your life on a failure of a blacksmith that can’t even keep his own forge.

—Orban.

That… was it. Just a few sentences on a piece of paper slid under his door. The strange thing about the note was that Theo couldn’t imagine his master ever saying any of those words to his face. He was more prideful than that. More headstrong. He would never utter anything so shameful and defeated.

But at the same time, Theo knew it was from him. No one else had handwriting that terrible.

After reading the letter, he went straight to the smithy, only to find it completely ransacked. Everything had been taken. It seemed the commerce groups had been anticipating this for a long time. They hadn’t even waited half a day before cleaning the place out.

And so Theo swept. Thinking. Thinking, that maybe… just maybe, his master would change his mind and come back.

It had been a few hours since he arrived at the smithy. With every passing minute, that hope only grew dimmer.

The front door creaked open a short while later, causing Theo to perk up. But then he remembered that his master would never use the front entrance. The front was reserved for clients; Theo and Orban always entered through the back.

Mrs. Bellory stepped through the doorway. She was a middle-aged woman who owned the shop next door, selling dyed cloth and small materials used for sewing, such as buttons and other tools. She had known Master Orban longer than Theo had been his apprentice.

She approached the counter slowly, holding a wrapped bundle in both hands. “Theo?” she called softly.

“I’m here,” Theo said, trying to sound composed.

Mrs. Bellory’s gaze moved across the smithy. From the cleared shelves to the empty stands and unlit hearth, it was obvious what had happened. Her expression tightened with sympathy.

Theo looked at her and clenched his fists around the broom. That expression only made everything feel more real. He averted his gaze and went back to sweeping.

“I brought you something to eat,” she said, setting the bundle on the counter. “Fresh-baked rolls and an omelette. I figured you hadn’t eaten yet.”

“Thank you, Mrs. Bellory. But I’m not hungry right now.”

Her expression remained soft. “No one is hungry when they’re sad, Theo. But still, you need to eat.” She didn’t say it like a lecture, but as simple advice from someone who cared.

Theo didn’t answer and continued sweeping.

“You’ll dig a hole through the floor if you keep sweeping like that, dear.”

“Then I’ll fix it.”

She sighed. “Now you’re sounding like him.”

Theo stopped sweeping, his lips thinning. Hearing that hurt more than it should have. Master Orban hadn’t even seen him off. Not even a goodbye. Just a note slid under his door, telling him he was leaving.

That… hurt the most.

“I’ll leave the food here,” Mrs. Bellory said before turning toward the door.

Before she could grab the handle, Theo called out, “Did you see him leave?”

She stayed quiet for a moment, resting her hand on the doorknob. Then she answered. “…Yes. He left this morning. Around six. He had a horse and looked like he was heading toward the north gate.”

Theo looked up at her, then just as quickly lowered his gaze to the broom. “I see. Thank you.”

Mrs. Bellory nodded, though Theo couldn’t see it. She opened the door and left.

Theo kept sweeping, drowning in his thoughts. A few moments later, he heard Mrs. Bellory’s voice outside. She was… speaking to someone. A group of people.

“Yes, unfortunately. But his apprentice is still inside, if you’d like to speak with him.”

It was Mrs. Bellory’s voice. But… who was she talking to?

The smithy had been closed for days. They had no orders left to fill; Master Orban had already completed or refunded every outstanding commission. If it wasn’t a client, then…

The Commerce Groups?

Theo’s fingers clenched around the broomstick until his knuckles blanched. Hadn’t they taken enough already? First his master, then the forge, and now they were coming back again? They had the gall to return after taking everything?!

A few moments later, the front door opened.

Theo, alone in the darkness of the forge, barely caught a glimpse of who had entered.

It didn’t matter.

“Get out!” he yelled, hurling the broom as hard as he could at the approaching group.

Before the handle could strike the person in front, another figure flashed forward from behind them. In one swift movement, a glint of steel flashed between them. The crack of splintering wood echoed through the empty forge before both halves of the broom clattered to the floor.

The girl who had cut the broom flourished her daggers, twirling them in her hands before sliding them back into the straps along her thighs. She stared at him sharply. She was beautiful, with scarlet-red hair, dressed in all black.

Behind her, another woman stepped forward—the one who had opened the door. She was young, maybe in her late teens or early twenties, with slightly tanned skin. Even in the darkness, there was… a flicker of recognition in Theo’s mind. He had seen this woman somewhere before. Very recently, in fact. But where…?

“Well, thanks for the warm welcome, Mr. Apprentice,” the woman said. She flicked her fingers, and a mote of light appeared, illuminating them all. “My name is Veronica. Is the blacksmith here available for a chat?”

Orban had been riding for hours.

The horse beneath him was old, borrowed from a stable owned by an old friend in the city. They didn’t care whether the horse returned. They only hoped Orban would treat her well on what might be her last journey. He intended to. After all, she was his only means of travel now. Just him and the horse—a grayish coat, uneven gait, ears twitching at every noise as though everything irritated her.

A fitting creature.

He sat low in the saddle, a dark cloak wrapped around his body. A metal shield was strapped across his back, while his smithing hammer hung at his waist. It wasn’t designed as a weapon, but damn him if he was going to use anything else. Nothing suited him more.

The horse sneezed.

Orban snorted. “Dun’go getting sick now, girl. We got a long ways to go.”

It had been hours since he left. Judging by the position of the sun, it was probably a little past noon. He stared down the road ahead.

Boy’s probably read the note by now.

Leaving wasn’t what he wanted. Not at all. If he had won that damned starmetal at the auction, maybe he could have found a buyer looking for a forged weapon. With material that rare, he could have earned enough to leave Maftem with Theo and start a new forge somewhere beyond the reach of those greedy bastards.

But no. Some damn noble vixen had stolen it right from under his nose. She didn’t even seem affiliated with the other merchants. Not that it mattered. One of them would have outbid him eventually anyway.

After everything he had done to keep the smithy alive, it still hadn’t been enough.

Few smiths were capable of forging starmetal. If he had acquired the ore himself, he could have charged a premium for whatever weapon he made from it. But if the woman who bought it came to him with the material already in hand, he could hardly justify demanding a life-changing sum for the work alone. At that point, she could simply find one of the few other smiths in Vitian capable of refining it for half the price.

His hands tightened around the reins. “Maybe it’s better this way,” he muttered.

The words didn’t sound convincing. Not at all. But the small reprieve of hearing him say it—that was enough for him.

As he continued riding, Orban noticed that the horse’s ears twitched. Orban frowned and glanced slightly to the side, not quite looking behind him.

At first, he thought it was only the wind shifting the leaves on the trees. But then he heard it again. And again. A faint rhythm matching his horse’s pace.

There was nothing behind him on the road. No riders.

No—they were in the trees.

He didn’t slow. Instead, he guided the horse around the next bend. The road opened up into a wider path near a shallow creak. It wasn’t ideal ground, especially with the running water, but it was good enough. Orban hopped down and tested the soil with one foot. Hard-packed earth, and probably old gravel beneath it.

He could work with it.

“You go’head, girl,” Orban said, patting the horse’s saddle. “Go. And don’t come back.”

The horse neighed, then ran ahead along the creek, deeper down the road.

Orban turned to face the direction he had come from.

Moments later, three figures dropped from the trees.

Humans, all of them. At least at a glance. They wore plain traveling cloaks, but kept their faces exposed despite their hoods. Either they didn’t care if he saw them because they were that confident—or they didn’t expect him to live long enough for it to matter.

On the left stood a woman with blonde hair, a large nose, and a crooked smile. The middle figure was a bald man with a black beard. He was large, though not particularly fat or muscular. Just… large. The man on the right was more ordinary, carrying himself with a quieter, more reserved posture.

He was definitely a mage.

Orban exhaled through his nose. “Who are the three of ya? Why’ve ya been on my trail for the past hour?”

The larger man in the center stepped forward. “Leaving so soon, Master Orban?”

Orban stared at him flatly. “Road’s free for travel. Don’t recall needin’ to tell nobody ‘fore I leave.”

“True. But we’d like a word before you go, if that’s possible.”

“A talkin’, huh?” Orban narrowed his eyes. “Shoulda come by in the mornin’ while I was still in the city.”

The man chuckled. “Who wakes up at six, old man?”

Orban’s hand drifted toward the handle of his hammer. “Never said I woke up at six.”

The woman on the left clicked her tongue. “Way to go.”

The center man rolled his eyes at her, then looked back at Orban. “You probably already know why we’re here. So? You wanna fight and lose a leg, or come quietly? Smiths don’t need their legs to work, after all.”

“You’re makin’ a big mistake, boy.” Orban gripped his hammer and shrugged off his cloak, quickly pulling the metal shield from his back. “I can tell yer all from Twistwell. I may be old, but I ain’t no pushover.”

“How do you know we’re from Twistwell and not Caroline or Trailing Star?” the man asked, raising a brow.

Orban chuckled.

The ground around him began to rumble. Earth erupted beside him in a long, square pillar before twisting and compressing in less than a second, forming the shape of a massive hand.

More specifically—a fist.

“’Cause those two got brains in charge that know better than to pick a direct fight with me.” Orban raised his hammer, eyes hardening. “Now, I don’t care who any of the three of ya are.”

The stone fist clenched beside him. “Yer all being buried today.”
