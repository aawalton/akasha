I kicked through the bodies of the fallen Dupes and militiamen, making sure we’d taken all their armour and valuables. After one last check, I tossed Anchor my siphon and said, “Harvest them. Make it quick.”

While he worked, I turned to the rest of the survivors—those who had surrendered. There had to be about thirty of them. They watched Anchor siphon their fallen brethren and gather their presences, eyes wide. One militiaman puked.

“It would’ve been easier if they’d surrendered,” I said, pacing in front of the gathered men. “You all had the right instincts.”

They were all sitting with their legs crossed, arms behind their backs, as we had commanded them to. I stopped pacing and knelt in front of them, bringing myself closer to their height. “I’m sorry about them. I know some of them were likely your friends. I know you had fought alongside them.”

No one said anything.

“Right now, I see two reasons you gave up. One, you didn’t want to die. Two, you didn’t have something worth dying for.”

I’d practiced speaking to surrendered convoy guards before. This wasn’t my first time delivering this same speech. It was like when we would have a class of beginning skiers at the hill, and I had to give them all the same introductory speech. I got pretty good at it.

“Well, if you’re in the first camp, you have two options: follow the Warlord, and let the orcs overrun you for his ambitions, or join me, help me win, and survive. If you want to live, joining me is the only way.” I stabbed my spear into the ground and put my hands on my knees. “Now, if you don’t believe me, you’re free to leave. Spreading rumours does nothing but help me.”

They were still silent. Now, however, their eyes turned to me. A few mouths slipped open. They’d felt [Will to Power] activate, and that was far more important than any speech I could make.

“However,” I continued, “if you’re in the second group, I can offer you something better to fight for. You were bought for ten sceats apiece—don’t bother arguing, I know the rates they pay militiamen. As for the Dupes, well, you get even less for your monthly wage. I can offer you something to die for that’s better than money.” I motioned toward the rest of the sappers, who were gathering around behind us. “They fight for me because they still believe we can have a future. You’re men of Gate too—you could share in that future. And if not for you, then for your brothers and children.”

I let the silence hang in the air for a few seconds, before finally adding, “You have ten minutes to decide while we pack up the wagons. If you agree, you’ll come with us to Hyrdford. There, you’ll join the Gate’s shadow army. We’ll feed you, clothe you, house you, and yes, when the war is over, you’ll be more than compensated for your efforts. Most importantly, you’ll still have a home.”

“You talk a lot about home for someone who doesn’t come from here, soulstealer,” one of the captured Dupes complained.

I nodded, rising to my feet. “You’re right. But I’m never going back to my own home. Some cosmic twist of fate, or higher power, or just luck dictated that I was going to get a second chance. No one else here is going to get as lucky as I did. It’s the least I can do.”

At that, I turned away, then walked to the end of the column, where the sergeant of the other squad stood—Martol. I had no idea how he’d come into the name, because it sounded like a relatively normal Gate name, but I wasn’t going to complain.

“What have we got?” I asked.

“This was a shipment meant for Steelshore, sure enough,” Martol replied. He, like most of the other sappers of our generation who had graduated our program, wore full Imperial Steel armour. His scale mail glinted in the sunlight, and his orange cloak fluttered in the faint breeze. “Mostly short swords and battle axes. The last wagon has plenty of standard shields and armour, though, which we could always use more of.”

“What about the elixirs?”

“Basic maegen elixirs,” he replied. “Likely all meant for the new Ealdorman.”

I sighed. “No Presence, then?”

“Nothing we can use, my lord.”

“Not entirely true. We can trade these with the Dun Altir order. Maybe they’ll foot more of our bills.”

Over the summer, we’d greatly increased our potion base production. Digging wells was an excellent physical conditioning activity for our army, and the Dun Altir order bought up everything we could produce.

At the moment, I suspected they were acting as a middleman. They were buying up everything we could provide and selling it to other orders who needed it.

Well, that was fine by me—I didn’t want to worry about distribution right now. Just as long as we got our payment in the end. We were using the funds to pay our troops, as well as to buy better equipment, food, and Presences for them. Best of all, to acquire all the nearby mercenary companies, so no one could use them against us.

Martol gave me a nod, then turned to the rest of his squad and commanded, “Gather the horses. We’re taking the goods back to Hyrdford.” He dropped his voice to a whisper. “Sir…about the ones who don’t come with us…”

“The usual,” I replied softly. “You know what to do about them.”

For all our talk about keeping them alive and letting them go free, I couldn’t actually have anyone spreading rumours about my return. The last thing I needed was for the Warlord to hear about my return and go looking for me. I had to be more careful.

My biggest advantage was that all my enemies thought I was dead. I couldn’t give that up so soon.

~ ~ ~

My squad rode ahead of Martol’s, and I rode ahead of my squad. Martol could handle the convoy, redirecting it and bringing it back to Hyrdford, and my squad needed to get back to Hyrdford even faster. That way, we could continue growing our campaign against the local Ealdorman.

If I got there sooner, then all the better. We’d cleaned up the lands west of Hyrdford over the past month, and there weren’t many threats that could bother us anymore.

Burr galloped at the speed of a regular horse, but did it without stopping, and without showing any sign of stress. It allowed me to travel much faster than the typical travel times noted on maps.

Still, it had been a month since I’d last been to Hyrdford. The latest convoy hadn’t been the only one we’d raided. We’d helped a couple squads of sappers clear out a cultist nest, and we’d captured plenty of other convoys intended to help bolster Steelshore’s defenses.

It wasn’t meant to keep orcs out. Chances were, they were just trying to maintain an escape route for the Warlord’s allies when the orcs finally destroyed the southern part of Gate.

The city had changed while I’d been gone. Watchtowers perched on the valley slopes, and wells dotted the land, with new wellmaster houses to guard each one of them. Most of the farmhouses had gotten upgrades, and the ranchers had much larger barns. Certainly too large for the amount of cattle and sheep they had.

Well, those barns weren’t just holding cattle and sheep.

As Burr galloped past a barn on the way in, I spotted a group of regular Dupes helping repair the thatched roof, and plenty more turncoat militiamen who were helping tend to the inside and lending the local farmers a hand.

After all, we needed some place to store our army that wouldn’t be instantly obvious to any of the Warlord’s men who passed through—and to the new thegn and portreeve of Hyrdford, who we’d (so far) kept in the dark.

The city itself had outgrown the old palisade walls that we had built, and so Lord Ceonwin had ordered the construction of a new stone curtain wall around its outside. It wasn’t entirely complete, but many of the workers were undercover sappers who wore broad hats to hide their identities, and they worked much faster than mortal men.

As I approached, I pulled up the hood of my tattered brown cloak and slowed Burr to a trot so I didn’t attract any suspicion. There was a time and place to reveal to the townsfolk that I was still alive, but now wasn’t it.

I rode Burr through a gap in the wall on the western side, keeping my head down. The guards knew to always let me in, even if I wasn’t wearing any armour to identify myself with. It was all in Burr’s saddlebags.

We rode to the center of the town, crossed over the river, then aimed for the great hall. I had plenty to talk about with Lord Ceonwin.

The grounds around the hall, which had previously been barren, were now filled with tents. Local mercenary companies housed their forces here, along with training grounds for the local militia and a few of the sappers.

After the incident at Steelshore, we didn’t get to keep all the sappers. The rest of the class had finished their training—a few more weeks working with the boats, then a swearing-in ceremony. In the following weeks, they deployed across the nation, taking on their assignments as if nothing had changed.

Of course, we still received a few squads in the south. For example, Martol was assigned to guard Steelshore. A few others, like the remains of Habit’s squad, and Yesman’s squad, had chosen to stay in Hyrdford. Because of their high placement in the training rankings, they’d been allowed to take placements where they chose.

When I arrived, I handed Burr’s reins to a mortal militia guard. The guard took Burr to the stable without question, but he still bowed his head and whispered, “Welcome home, my lord.”

I nodded back, then set off toward the hall, marching up the front steps. Instead of stepping inside directly, I walked around to the corner of the hall’s front terrace and pulled a string hidden among the thatching at the edge of the hall’s awning. It ran all the way through the dark edges of the hall, then to a back room where Lord Ceonwin usually stayed. A tiny bell chimed—which my enhanced senses allowed me to pick up on, no matter how far away it was.

If the local thegn or the portreeve were present, I would sneak in the back entrance. Or, like today, if they weren’t present, Lord Ceonwin would greet me at the front door.

He pulled it open, then bowed, greeting me as an equal. I bowed back.

“Lord Levi,” he said softly. “We have many things to speak about.”

“Bad news?”

“For once, bad and good. Come inside.”
