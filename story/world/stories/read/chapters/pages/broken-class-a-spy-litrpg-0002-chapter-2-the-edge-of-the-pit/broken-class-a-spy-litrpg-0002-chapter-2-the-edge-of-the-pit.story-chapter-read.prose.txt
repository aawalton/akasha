Arthur woke to the growl of his stomach and the ache of a body still on the edge of collapse.

The sleep had helped. His thoughts were clearer now, more focused. But the hope that yesterday was just a nightmare faded quickly.

This was real.

He was still in that crumbling mud hut. Still trapped in someone else's body.

And not just anyone's. This one was a wreck - weak, underfed, and barely able to crawl across a dirt floor without feeling like he'd just run a marathon.

In his old life, Arthur's body had been a precision-crafted machine. All honed muscle and lightning-fast reflexes. He used to scale buildings like a cat on caffeine and shrug off injuries that would leave most men laid up in a hospital bed. He used to leap walls, not wince at standing up.

Now? Just getting upright felt like an epic quest with a tragic backstory.

It was infuriating. Like being shoved into the skin of a scarecrow and told to win a war. But hey, you play the hand you're dealt. Even if the hand is skeletal - skin stretched tight over bones, every joint jutting out like a warning sign. The kind of hand you'd expect on someone who hadn't seen a full meal in months and had to ration hope like water.

He shifted slowly, careful not to wake the thin man lying nearby. The man's chest rose and fell in slow, shallow breaths. Pale skin. Hollow cheeks. Sunken eyes. He looked like the kind of person life had been chewing on for years.

Arthur guessed this was Paul. His father, apparently.

The room was dim, lit only by the early grey of dawn leaking through the cracked doorway. There was nothing here. No furniture. No warmth. Barely even a blanket between them. He took it in with the cold eye of a trained observer: if poverty could be weaponized, this family had been on the receiving end of it for a long time.

In the corner, a small fire crackled faintly. His mother, Mary was hunched beside it, stirring something in a dented pot. It didn’t smell appetizing. It barely smelled like food. But after three days unconscious, his body didn’t care.

Arthur pushed himself upright. His arms trembled with the effort, muscles screaming in protest. He dragged himself across the dirt floor until he was close enough to speak.

“Mom… can I have something to eat?”

She jumped, startled by the sudden voice behind her. For a moment she just stared at him. Relief, exhaustion, and worry all tangled together on her face. Then she nodded quickly and turned back to the pot.

Without a word, she poured something thick and beige into a wooden bowl and handed it to him. He took it with quiet gratitude. It was bland, lukewarm, and barely more than flavored sludge.

But it tasted like survival.

He finished it in minutes. She hadn’t served much. Probably half of what she’d prepared for the whole family. The guilt settled in quietly. He didn’t ask for more.

Arthur had never known what it felt like to have a mother. In his old life, he was an orphan who had grown up in the sterile halls of a government facility. No parents. No softness. Just steel, schedules, and surveillance. The people who raised him trained him to kill, lie, and disappear. Never to feel.

And yet, here she was. Starving beside him. Still offering the last of what she had. He didn’t say anything. He didn’t need to. Some debts you pay without words.

Behind him, Paul stirred awake. His voice was raspy, his body barely holding itself together, but the warmth in his expression was unmistakable.

“You’re up,” he said with a tired smile. “Good. You scared us, son. Your mother said you lost your memory. Any better this morning?”

Arthur kept his face neutral. Calm, confused, and a little ashamed. Just like training.

“No… still blank.”

Paul nodded slowly. “It’s alright. Whether you remember or not, you’re still our son. That doesn’t change.”

There was a long pause. Then his father looked down, guilt tightening his features. “We’ve already lost your siblings,” he said softly. “If we’d lost you too… I don’t think we could’ve kept going.”

Arthur didn’t know how to respond to that. But he didn’t have to. Paul kept talking. “I’m sorry you got hurt because of me. I should’ve been stronger. I was too slow. I couldn't keep the cart moving fast enough, and Rajak noticed. He was already in a foul mood. Then you looked up, just trying to see who was coming. He caught your eyes and decided you were being disrespectful. He didn’t need a reason, but that gave him one. You took the punishment meant for me.”

Arthur sat in silence for a moment, then asked, “Who is Rajak?”

Paul’s face hardened. “He’s what they call a Guardian. One of the enhanced. They’re stronger, faster, tougher than any of us. Guardians are supposed to protect the mines from the monsters that sometimes make it over the walls. It doesn’t happen often, but when it does, they’re the only reason any of us survive.”

Arthur frowned. “Then why is Rajak like this? If he's here to keep us safe, then why is his idea of punishment harsh enough to kill me?”

Paul let out a slow breath. “He wasn’t just a Guardian. He was someone high up. Important. And whatever he did, whatever got him sent here, it stripped all that away. Now every day, he’s reminded of how far he’s fallen. And we’re the ones he takes it out on. Because we’re weaker and we can’t fight back.”

Paul’s voice dropped lower. “The other Guardians avoid him. They don’t speak to him unless they have to. Don’t linger when they pass through. Like they know something’s off. Like they’re afraid he might snap again.”

Arthur hesitated. “Then why stay here? Why not leave the mine?”

Paul gave a tired shake of his head. “Where would we go? Out there, beyond the walls, it’s worse. Monsters roam the wilds. Whole caravans vanish without a trace. The cities won’t take us - not without coin, skill, or a name that matters. And we’ve got none of that.”

He looked at Arthur, voice low but steady.“Here, we have food, shelter, and just enough protection to survive. It’s not freedom. It’s not fair. But it’s what we’ve got.

Arthur processed that. Be enslaved, or be eaten. Those were the options. According to everyone here, there was no in-between.

But Arthur had never been good at following the rules. And he wasn’t about to start now. He needed more information. About the mine. About the Guardians. About the monsters outside.

And if this world really was some kind of game… he wasn’t going to play by the rules. He was going to break them.
