In the battle's aftermath at River Erithas, the legion encamped just beyond the slaughter fields to recover its strength. Auxiliaries ventured to salvage the battlefield and harry the Ürkün, who fled to their unfortified camps. On the fourth day, the cavalry returned, spears bloodied, with five hundred prisoners in tow, and the legion formed up to depart. Arrayed across the rolling fields, the legionnaires faced their superiors’ inspection, then embarked as a massive column heading northeast towards the city of Nerithon, where awaited the Fifth Legion. From their position in the lowlands, all that was visible of the city were smokestacks rising towards a clear sky, but upon the correct hour of sunset, one could spot the smudge of marble pillars from its chief hilltop temple. There awaited all their fates, and the heretic magus who repelled the Fifth Legion some two winters past, and now reigned over the city.

In spite of the looming peril, their daily tasks remained much the same: awake to trumpets, eat breakfast, pack down the camp, fall in line, march for six hours, arrive at the new camp, set up and eat dinner… but here, Skippii’s routine changed. Instead of relaxing in the twilight hours, or attending to chores, his companeight picked up his slack, and Skippii departed with Cliae to the camp’s outskirts for training.

As he passed through the legion’s camp, he caught the stares of the legionnaires in his cohort and felt their whispers at his back. “Is that him?” “The Ürkün’s bane?” “The Quakelord’s Champion?” “The Imperator’s Flame?”

“I’ll explain to them tomorrow,” he said to Cliae as they passed from the camp’s perimeter into the surrounding forest.

“I don’t think the rumours are negative,” Cliae said. “They know you’re on their side.”

“I should introduce myself anyway,” Skippii said. “They’ve got a right to know who’s in the tonnage with them.”

“But not tonight?” Cliae asked.

Skippii sighed. “Tonight I want to train again. We have to make use of what time we’ve got. And I feel like I’m almost there with Seismic Quake.”

“Yes, legio,” Cliae grinned.

Passing beyond the camps of auxiliaries who surrounded the legion’s perimeter, he followed a stream into a forested gorge. Cliae took up a perch upon a boulder, wax tablet in hand, and Skippii stripped down to his loincloth, not to damage his possessions while training. Cliae and he had devised four distinct evocations in his ordinatio. Blazing Fist and Blazing Armour came to him easiest. With a breath, he drew power from the earth and lit the halo at his core. That golden ring glowed like metal steeped in a furnace; it was not visible to Cliae, but shone solidly in his mind’s eye, residing in the centre of his chest.

The air was brisk; the waters icy. His bare chest streaked with sweat, and mist rose from the reddish hue of his skin. With a thought, he danced the flames over his flesh, and extinguished them at will.

“Excellent,” Cliae said. “Great control.”

“Yeah,” Skippii said. “I don’t feel like I’m getting stronger, but…”

“You will be,” Cliae said. “You just won’t notice that. Besides, strength isn’t what’s important now. Control is key.”

“You’re right,” he said, taking a swig from his waterskin. “Right then, Firetail Lance.”

Using his kukri, he fashioned the branches of trees into staves, and held one aloft like a javelin. Empowering it with his flames, he launched it at the rocks of the streambed, where it exploded in a shower of embers. By the time his bundle was spent, the rocks were grazed with soot, and the branches above them were crinkled and charred.

“Magnificent,” Cliae said. “But how could it be improved?”

“I don’t know,” Skippii said. “There’s only so much power I can put into a spear before it explodes in my hands. There’s a sweat-spot that makes it fly, but I don’t see a way of going beyond that.”

“Let’s move on to something else then,” Cliae said. “Focus on your core. That’s the key to developing your ordinatio. It’s unique to you.”

“My edge,” he said.

A memory of Aetheria’s vehement accusation flashed in his mind: “The Gods did not suffer equals when the pantheon was created. Why should we?”

“Too right, we’re not equals,” he muttered. Drawing his magia from that source deep beneath the earth, he looked inward and felt his halo brighten–a ring of golden energy which formed a shell around the power which he could maintain. At its centre was a red spot–an ugly graze. It had grown larger since he evoked the power of a Seismic Quake on the riverbank. Breathing deeply, he drew magia up through his feet to flow in his chest; the halo shone–he could feel the fires simmer beneath his flesh, awaiting his command. But the red spot at its centre remained distant and dim.

“I don’t know how to reach it,” he said. “This new power.”

“How did you do it on the riverbank?” Cliae asked.

“Then, I went too far. I fell into it.” He pressed his fingers into his sternum, trying to demonstrate the feeling. “Like I was crumpling up. Burning up.” He shuddered.

“Some, who beseech the Gods, experience such things. Magia consuming them. My father once tutored an apostle of Chrysaetos. He always preached caution. The Golden Eagle’s champions suffer the shortest life-span, but burn the brightest.”

“And… How does it work for them? How do pantheon magus control their magia?”

“In the words of my father,” Cliae cleared their throat. “The key to a strong invocation is twofold: The magus must have a devout and unwavering connection to their acheron, and the application of their invocation must align with that deity’s convictions. For instance, it wouldn’t be a good idea to call upon Erymenes to unmake a golden breastplate for it to be minted into coins. That would anger the great Fabricator; however, it might please Junorix the Imperial Star. The invocation may work, but a magus’ connection to their acheron would fray. They are always working on borrowed magia–earned favour.

“You, however…” Cliae looked him deep in the eye. “You have the liberty to decide.”

“Still, if I lose control, the evocation doesn’t flitter out. It carries on and burns me up with it.”

Cliae nodded. “In that, you are not wholly unique. Apostles of the Sun suffer the same fate. Their acheron—Chrysaetos—demands every morsel of their devotion to wield his magia; it takes the most trained magus to resist that flood of celestial energy that pours into each of their invocations. Their struggle is in not abandoning their mortal instincts to survive. Many give too much to the Sun. That’s how they die.”

“I don’t know if that’s the same for me,” Skippii said. “It doesn’t feel like devotion or persuasion, it just felt like falling. Like being torn apart.” He laughed shortly. “I do want to live. I haven’t abandoned that.”

“All the more reason to master your core more swiftly, then.”

In the quiet that followed, a woodpecker chiselled a tree above them, and songbirds gathered, serenading the orange setting sun. Skippii took a breath to calm his nerves. He had sworn to the Imperator to command his power, but how could he control that which he feared?

“So, falling,” Cliae said, adopting a straightforward tone. “And… if you were to fall from a ledge, what would you do?”

“Grab on.”

The slave’s eyes searched the canopy for answers. “Is there something to grab on to? To ground yourself? A ledge? Maybe me? I could hold you up.”

“Maybe there is,” he mused. In recent training, he had transformed the halo at his core from an ethereal glow to a solid ring. And in solidifying it, he had created a shell of sorts around his core–allowing the magia which he drew from the earth to remain stable inside his body. It had taken a great strain of will, but he had not felt himself in danger, as he had been on the riverbank. He expected something similar was required to elevate this pervasive, deeper power.

“I’ve got an idea,” he said.

Drawing a breath and shutting his eyes, the light of his core shone in his mind’s eye as heat pulsed in the centre of his chest. He focussed on the halo, empowering it. After just a few breaths, it glowed white-hot. He kept his focus on its fires burning beneath his flesh and felt them awaken upon his skin. Though his attention remained on its shell, a distant vestige of his mind probed inwards, venturing deeper and further–like a single finger outstretched. All the while, he breathed, concentrating on the immediacy of his flesh. His surroundings. The heat. His heartbeat.

Suddenly, the probing finger stung as it touched the depths. Drawn to it, his gut wrenched as he felt he was standing atop a high cliff, leaning over the edge. Tensing his abdomen, he breathed slowly, backing away from the ledge, grasping with his other hand at the halo like the root of a tree.

Torn between two extremes, he held on tight. Shaking with exertion, he brought the depths up to meet him–submitting the very earth itself. Slowly, the distance between the inner and outer extremities of his core shrank as the two combined into one.

“Come on,” he said through gritted teeth. The red spot brightened and grew as magia flowed into it. He saw that it was not an ugly graze, but a diminished gem, and he was hauling it into the firelight to where again it could gleam.

His grasp loosened, and he felt himself being pulled in towards that impossible power; a ferocious heat deep at the earth’s centre, drawing him into its obliterating maw.

With a jolt, Skippii let go. The earth shook, and the canopy rained twigs and leaves down on their heads. But the tremor was minor compared to the one he had evoked upon the riverbed. However, if he had not jolted away—if he had not broken the connection just now…. would the same have happened? Would he have fallen to his death?

Cliae watched him mesmerised, stylus and wax tablet at the ready. Their bruised eye had nearly healed, and twitched with anticipation.

“I almost had it,” he said, then shut his eyes again. Indeed, the red gem was there at the centre of his halo. But it diminished again. “That was a bit loud, though. Think our guest will mind?”

Opening his eyes, he cast them towards a dark silhouette on the hillside above. There, the arcanus, Clarivoxa Kylinissa, stalked him like a shadow.

“She’s there again?” Cliae said, glancing around.

“Up the hill,” Skippii said. “Just her, I think.”

“It can’t be helped,” Cliae said. “You have the Imperator’s leave to train. She is likely just here to observe.”

“And report what she sees to the Coven,” Skippii said.

“We don’t know that for sure,” Cliae said.

“Oh yeah? Who do you think she answers to?” Skippii said.

“I believe all arcanus answer to the Legion,” Cliae said, setting aside their wax tablet to gaze up the hillside. “Though, it must be difficult for her.”

Skippii huffed. “For her? How so?”

“Well, she is an acolyte of Kylin, the same Goddess whom the Coven worship. She might have had ambitions to join their ranks one day. Or, she might see them as a divine authority, whereas the Legion is more a mortal authority. When she testified at the Imperator’s counsel, she was forced to make a decision between the two.”

Skippii scowled, gazing up at the dark figure watching over them. He had feared her for a time, but now he was not so sure.

“She did speak honestly at the Imperator’s counsel,” Skippii said. “But that doesn’t make her my ally. I bet, if she had even the slightest suspicion or lapse in judgement, and deemed me a heretic for whatever reason, the Coven would hear about it first, and I’d wake up with daggers at my throat, and chains.”

“All we can do is keep training,” Cliae said tentatively.

“I don’t know why the Coven don’t just follow the commands of the legion,” he continued. “Make them a contingent just like the cavalry and the archers. Fit them in. Things would work better that way.”

“Ahh, but the pantheonos wouldn’t like it. They would declare that the legions were placing themselves above the authority of the Gods.”

“But the legions answer to the senate. They are the hand of the Imperium. The will of the people. If the Gods wanted something doing, then why don’t they just do it themselves?”

“The Coven would argue that they perform the will of the pantheon, which supersedes the senate.”

“Alright,” Skippii said exasperatedly. “Whose side are you on?”

“I… No, I meant-”

“I’m just kidding,” Skippii smiled. “You’re right. I’m just unlucky enough to be stuck in the middle of it all.”

“At least the Imperator trusts you.”

Skippii sighed, adding to the evaporating mist. “He’s gambling with me. High risk, high reward. If I can prove I’m not a heretic or liability, then he thinks I’ll make a good weapon.”

“What do you think?” Cliae asked.

He shrugged. “I think I’m glad they didn’t toss me to the Coven. I think I better get control over this thing before he changes his mind.”

“No,” Cliae persisted. “What do you think about being used as his weapon?”

The question seemed so plain that it caused Skippii to stumble. “Well, as a legionnaire, I’m a spear. I am his weapon, that’s all I’ve ever wanted to be. With this power, I could be more. I don’t…” he shook his head. “What more is there than that?”

“I don’t know,” Cliae said. “I was just wondering.”

***

For three days, the legion marched nearer to Nerithon, and each night, Skippii and Cliae ventured out to hone his magia. Practice turned from wielding his evocations to the task of empowering his core in an attempt to bring the dull red gem at its centre to bear. Each night, he grounded himself, and grasped the solid core of his halo, reaching into its depths. But the weight of power dragged him down. Each night, he severed the connection, and the earth trembled in defeat. What would happen if he didn’t let go, and he fell? Would it be the same as on the riverbank, where his life-force itself was piled onto the evocation’s pyre, seething to burn his soul?

“You’ll get it next time,” Cliae consoled as they returned to their companeight’s tent in the early hours of the morning.

“I’ll have to,” he said.

On the fourth day, as they ventured from camp, he grabbed Cliae by the wrist and ran from the path, attempting to slip the arcanus’ watch. But before long, he heard her footfalls in the forest from behind, ever keeping her distance.

“She must be able to smell us,” Skippii said.

“Cast it from your mind,” Cliae said.

“Yeah,” he grumbled. “This might be our last night. I don’t know how much time I’m going to be able to find away from duties once we reach the city.”

“Can’t you request from the Imperator that you be given more time to-”

“I won’t,” he interrupted. “I’m a legionnaire first. I’ve sworn oaths to my company. I won’t disregard them.”

“Okay then,” Cliae said. “Are you ready to plumb the depths?”

Skippii looked around the dusk hillside. In the distance was a quiet farmstead, orange glow emanating from its windows. Above them, the arcanus had sunk into the shadows, but he could still sense her presence, like a fox on the prowl.

“Okay,” Skippii said. “I’ll do it. I’ll go as far as I can. At least, if I lose control here, I won’t take out half the cohort with me.”

“That’s the spirit,” Cliae smiled nervously, taking an involuntary step backwards.

“But get ready to carry me back to the legion if I pass out,” he said.

“The arcanus will help me, I’m sure,” Cliae said with a smile.

Skippii grinned and shook his head, then steadied his nerves with a breath. With an inhale, he drew in magia from the source deep underground. Holding his breath, he felt the heat swim inside him, igniting his core. He grasped the solid rim of the halo in his mind, then cautiously reached into its centre, to where an unwrought gem was buried. A stream of magia washed over him, ever thickening as he delved. His sense of direction wavered—his perception of his body fled. Vertigo overcame him, and he fell headfirst into the power. But rather than run, he embraced it, gritting his teeth, reaching down, stretching far beyond the limitations of his flesh.

His heart pounded. Heat filled his body, and he wobbled dizzily. The sensation of magia mingled with his blood—his life-force—until the two were the same. The golden halo diminished in his mind as his grasp faltered, and the distinction between him and the earth failed. An ocean of power appeared beneath him, gaping its jaws to swallow. But Skippii did not jolt. He reached out towards it. It had to be tonight. It had to be now.

Something solid brushed his fingertips—the crest of an unearthed gem.

Like the formidable hilt of a blade, he grasped it.

“That’s it.” He gritted his teeth, drawing it from the depths. The ground beneath him hummed–a low vibration that sang inside his body–resonating in his core. Its earthen tomb crumbled. The gem shone brighter as it rose from the depths until it encompassed his whole mind.

Finally, he landed, and the world opened up beneath him. With a breath, magia gushed into his lungs and rushed into the gem at its centre. It brightened at his touch, growing larger in his mind, a ruby pupil to the golden halo’s iris.

Skippii breathed, withdrawing from the earth, returning to the sensations of his flesh. His head fizzled with energy, dizzying and confusing his thoughts.

“I think I’ve got it,” he panted, careful not to move a muscle.

“Now? Really?” Cliae said. “Contain it. Don’t release it all at once.”

“I’m standing on it.” He grinned. “The power… It’s everywhere. It’s mine.”

“Control it,” Cliae pressed.

With an exhale, his magia rushed between hidden fissures and tunnels underground—a network of arteries and muscles and limbs. A thin mist rose from the forest’s damp undergrowth. The earth rumbled with power–thrumming at his fingertips. He had only to command it, and the earth would split.

But he clenched his will, and with an exhale, spread the power out beneath him. Looking inwards, he saw the ruby at his centre gleam. Tensing his abdomen, he pressed on. The ground shook beneath him, awaiting his command to explode. But he contained the impulse. The strain on his body worsened. Clenching his jaw, he spread the magia almost beyond his grasp. Beneath him, the rock turned to coals. Dizzy and dehydrated, he held on. Just one more second… One more metre of expansion…

With a gasp, Skippii recoiled his magia. His ears popped as the ground shuddered beneath him. But as his vision stabilised, and he swayed, he saw that the ground did not crack asunder. Only a fraction of his power had escaped his clutch. The rest he had dispelled with control, or else contained now in the glowing ruby of his core.

The mists cleared in the brisk twilight wind. Skippii knelt and drank thirstily from the stream as Cliae returned from a safe distance.

“It’s gone?” Cliae said. “The power, you controlled it?”

“All but that little shudder,” he said.

“And your core?”

“Changed.” A grin spread over Skippii’s face. “The gem is bright now. And I can feel its connection with the earth. It’s unlike the fires of my halo. This is something else.”

“So,” Cliae said. “You do have the power of Seismorix.”

“Or he has mine,” Skippii said lightly.

“Tell me, were there voices? A sense of communion, or the presence of something within the earth?”

Skippii shook his head. “No. Not since I awakened this magia. That voice… I barely remember it. Arise, it said. But that was all. No direction. No moral imperative. No guidance, but I’d trade that for being a pantheon slave, and a zealot like the Coven. If this power is mine, I can do with it what I want.”

“Whatever the Imperator wants.”

“Yeah, well. That goes without saying.”

Cliae smiled softly, and Skippii suddenly realised how hard he would have found it all if the slave hadn’t been there for him.

“Let me summon it once more” he said. “I won’t spread as far. I’ll try and contain it.”

Cliae retreated up the streamside, and Skippii worked his essence into the ground. With guiding breaths, he kneaded his magia into the earth, drawing it back and sending it forth in ripples. By keeping a steady rhythm, he built the energy up, not needing to exert himself with brute force.

Back and forth, the ripples formed small waves as the earth shook with power. But it did not tear. However, it took tremendous strain to control. Skippii panted, pouring with sweat, and finally was forced to relent. The waves of power subsided to a slow, hot slush beneath his feet.

Cliae clapped, clearing the silent night air. “That’s better than last time. You made it to one-hundred and thirty count.”

“You were counting?”

“I was.”

“Sure you didn’t just count quicker this time?”

Cliae smiled and shook their head. “I have a system.”

“Ohh,” Skippii said. “You have a system? Very fancy. I should be paying you for all of this, really. All the knowledge you possess. You could be a professional.”

Cliae grimaced–a sort of cynical smile.

“You’ve got a bad deal,” Skippii said.

“You’re telling me,” they said, picking at the grey cloth of their toga.

“Well, with all the clothes and armour I’ve burned, I’m in as much debt as you. If it keeps going this way, I’ll be fighting the whole campaign for free.”

“Or fighting for a warm meal and tent above your head,” Cliae said. “Like me.”

“Hm. Guess I should be paying you as well for your services.”

Cliae smiled; their lips twitched, hesitant to form a rebuke. “What do you think the second wax tablet is for? I’m calculating your exuberant bill.”

Skippii chuckled, and his belly rumbled, and he didn’t care who overheard it.
