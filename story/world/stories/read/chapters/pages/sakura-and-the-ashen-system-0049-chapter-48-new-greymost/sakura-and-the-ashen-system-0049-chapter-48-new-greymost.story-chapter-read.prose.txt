Kelvin sat at a narrow desk in the lower office of the city administration and copied out the third report about incorrectly applied runes on shackles. One of the junior lawkeepers had once again mixed up the order of the closing lines, and because of that the suppressing circuit had not held for longer than an hour. They had managed to catch the mage being led through the city in those shackles before he escaped, but Kelvin was still forced to describe the mistake in such detail, as if the fate of Greymost depended on his words.

In the administration, they constantly pretended that they only needed runic mages as convenient pairs of hands: put on shackles, remove shackles, check a seal, or draw up a legal magical scroll. Sometimes he had to explain to guards why they should not poke an unclosed circle with a finger. Kelvin was used to it. He was not a mage by birth, but he knew his work perfectly and was professionally skilled at reading and composing runes.

So when two men from the city guard came for him, Kelvin first decided that someone had mixed up the signs on shackles again.

"Harn wants you upstairs," one of the guards said. "Urgently."

At that moment, Kelvin thought that if someone had placed a suppressing rune over a holding rune again, he was going to bite someone today. But aloud, for once, he did not say that as he got up.

Upstairs, it turned out everything was much worse than Kelvin had managed to imagine.

In the old quarter, near the warehouses and the former dyehouse, they had found an empty house after an old neighbor complained about it. She had talked about suspicious figures in cloaks, candles in the windows, and the smell of burned herbs. The guards went inside and saw runes on the floor.

Kelvin was sent there with three investigators and six guards. Among them, he was the only runic mage. The group knew how to catch runaway mages, but they understood almost nothing about runes and scrolls.

The house stood on a narrow street, squeezed between an empty warehouse and a junk dealer’s shop. Kelvin stopped at the threshold. A circle had been drawn on the floor of the main room. The lines ran across the boards, climbed the walls, split into short branches by the windows, and returned to the center again. Candle stubs stood in the corners. On the windowsill lay a cracked cup with a dried gray coating inside. By the wall, someone had left three empty reagent vials.

"Well?" the senior investigator asked impatiently. "It’s mages, right?"

Kelvin crouched at the edge of the circle, took out a thin bone stylus, and carefully ran it along one of the lines. The dust on the floor trembled. A weak residual tension still held inside the rune.

"Yes," he said. "Magic was used here."

The guards behind him exchanged glances. Kelvin stood and examined the wall, where short signs looked like preparation for a holding circuit, but too large. If someone had wanted to hide the work, he would have used thin lines and done it in a lower corner, behind furniture, under a rug. Here, everything had been placed right in front of the first person who walked in.

"Something wrong?" the investigator asked.

"Too much of everything," Kelvin answered.

"Mages draw runes. We found runes. What else do you need?"

Kelvin said nothing. Explaining the difference between a working circle and a staged circle to guards was like explaining tax collection to a dog.

He found the scroll behind an overturned bench. It was a combat scroll, but a weak one, elemental in type. It could be used once, as long as one was not afraid of burns on the fingers. The scroll was not valuable.

"Were they preparing an attack?" the investigator asked when Kelvin showed him the find.

"Possibly."

"Possibly?"

Kelvin looked at the circle again.

"Or they wanted us to decide they were preparing an attack."

The investigator frowned.

"Why?"

Kelvin did not answer right away. He did not know yet himself. But the house did not look like a secret hideout. It looked like a message. As if someone wanted the administration to find all of this and get nervous. It was suspicious, and Kelvin could not understand what about all this disturbed him most.

In the report, all of it still appeared differently.

Kelvin wrote about the demonstrative nature of the circle, the too-obvious traces, and the strange carelessness of the abandoned scroll, but the senior investigator only snorted when he read his conclusions. To the administration, everything looked simpler: mages had gathered in the city, kept a combat scroll, and were clearly preparing something.

So by evening, the working version sounded like this: "Underground mages are preparing an attack."

Kelvin did not argue. In truth, he was not in a position to argue with people of higher rank. But he still felt that something here was wrong. He just could not understand what exactly.

Three days later, a guard was crippled. Kelvin learned about it from a clerk who ran into the archive tower with huge eyes. The clerk said a night patrol near the warehouses had been attacked, and that several mages in cloaks had done it. The guard was alive, but his leg had been broken so badly that the healer swore even in front of the assistants.

Kelvin should have waited for an order, but instead, he took his cloak and went to the infirmary.

The pale, unfortunate guard lay on a narrow cot. One leg was fixed between wooden splints, and a burn showed on his neck.

"I already told the interrogator everything," he rasped when he saw Kelvin.

"I am not an interrogator. I read the runes in the house."

The guard blinked.

"Where they gathered?"

"Possibly."

That was enough to make the guard talk. There had been three or four of them. Smoke had come from every side. One had sent lightning across the stone so close that sparks scattered over his face. The second had activated a scroll. And the third had stood by the wall. Or maybe it was the same one, but the guard was not sure. Everything had happened too fast.

"Why didn’t they kill you?" Kelvin asked.

The guard clenched his teeth.

"How should I know?"

"They could have."

"They could have," he said angrily. "But one of them said... before leaving. Said mages would soon rise from their knees."

Kelvin fell silent. He understood that everything he had seen in the house where the underground mages had met was continuing. The new move was just as demonstrative. Leave a witness alive with a message to frighten the others. As if someone was trying to scare the city.

The next morning, Kelvin saw a new message from the mysterious mages operating in the city. He was walking to the administration in the early gray hour, when the shops were still closed. At the turn toward the market arch, he stopped.

On the wall of the guardhouse, dark, sweeping letters had been written:

MAGES WILL NO LONGER KNEEL.

The blood was already drying at the edges. It was real, and only later did Kelvin learn that it was not human blood, but pig’s blood. That changed nothing, because by noon half the city was standing in front of the wall. People anxiously studied the inscription, whispered, prayed, and clearly panicked.

Kelvin spent the whole day writing reports, answering questions, and rereading his own notes again. Before him were three parts of one picture: the secret meeting in the house, the crippled guard, and the bloody inscription on the wall.

By evening, he was assigned to duty in the archive tower, in the upper hall where confiscated scrolls, discarded diagrams, and shackle blanks were stored. Formally, he was not alone: guards sat on the lower floors, a watch stood at the entrance, and an old clerk dozed in the neighboring wing. But at the top of the tower, Kelvin remained alone.

The raven arrived after midnight and struck its beak against the shutter with such confidence, as if it had spent its whole life carrying letters to the city administration. Kelvin opened the window, the bird left a bundle on the sill, and flew away.

Kelvin read the letter without even stepping away from the window:

You saw our message in the house and understood more than the others.

But no one listened to you, did they? It is convenient for them when you do their dirty work and explain what they themselves do not understand. But they will still make decisions without you.

You are a mage too, yet you work under their boot. They tolerate you only as long as you are useful to them.

Do you want to be free?

Soon, everything will change. This city will no longer be the same. It will be ruled by people with magic.

You can refuse and remain part of the old system that will be buried alive. Or you can make the right choice and become part of the new Greymost.

If you are ready to prove that you are not just their leashed dog, bring the confiscated combat scrolls from the archive. As many as you can.

Leave them in the green house by the old dyehouse, under the third floorboard by the back wall.

After that, I will meet with you.

Kelvin reread the letter several times. His heart was beating fast, but there was less fear than anger. Leashed dog. Did they really think that would work? That he would take offense, cry over his fate, and run off to steal scrolls for people who broke guards’ legs?

On the other hand, the letter was a chance. If he simply took it downstairs, his superiors would take the case away. Kelvin would be praised for his vigilance, ordered to keep quiet, and someone else would be put in place to wait for the next raven. But if he pretended he had taken the bait, he could lead them to a meeting.

Kelvin looked at the cabinet with the discarded scrolls. He knew the weakest ones were stored separately. There were many unstable ones among them, almost empty, more dangerous to the user than to the target. They were kept for training checks and mistake analysis. Losing two such scrolls would be a violation, but not a catastrophe.

The runic mage stood before the cabinet for a long time, then took one fire scroll with a cracked seal and one smoke scroll, discarded because of an overly sharp release. He wrote down the numbers on a scrap of paper and hid it under the lining of his sleeve.

The green house by the old dyehouse smelled of damp and rotten boards. Kelvin found the third floorboard by the back wall, lifted it with a knife, and placed the scrolls inside. In that moment, he felt like both a criminal and the smartest man in the city.

He tried to see who would come to retrieve the scrolls, but he failed. Clearly, the person he was dealing with was smarter than Kelvin had thought. And he could not keep watch for days, since duty called him back.

The second note came to Kelvin the very next night.

You have taken the first step. Now come yourself tomorrow, before dawn. The old dyehouse. Lower entrance from the river side.

If you bring the guard, I will know.

Kelvin did not keep playing alone after that. He went to Commander Harn and placed both letters and the numbers of the discarded scrolls in front of him. Harn first turned pale, then red, then spent ten minutes explaining to Kelvin what kind of idiot he had been for agreeing to cooperate with criminal mages. But Kelvin did not lose his composure, knowing how to explain everything properly to Harn.

"I gave them a small prize," he said when Harn finally fell silent. "Two discarded scrolls. But now we have a meeting place and a person who thinks I am ready to join his side."

"And if they had taken the scrolls and vanished?"

"Then we would have lost two discarded scrolls. But they want to talk. They need me."

Harn looked at him as if he were still choosing between a promotion and shackles.

In the end, he chose an ambush.

The old dyehouse looked dead before dawn. It was crooked, old, and cold. The guards hid in neighboring buildings: two with crossbows took the roof of the warehouse. Harn had a runic net, one of those that temporarily suppressed active magic, and Kelvin had shackles hidden on him.

He stood by the lower entrance alone, as required.

The man in the gray cloak appeared almost silently. He was taller than Kelvin had expected, thin, pale, with white hair and the face of a man who had not slept in a long time. There was a sense of sickness and something foreign about him. Kelvin did not feel magic the way true mages did, but he still understood: the person before him was no ordinary man.

He looked more closely, the way he had done hundreds of times while checking detained mages, and the world obediently assembled dry lines before his eyes:

Gilven Neld

Race: Human

"So there are still people in the administration who understand which way the wind is blowing," the stranger said.

Kelvin forced himself not to look at the roofs.

"I brought what you asked for. Those scrolls..."

"I know."

Those two words sent a chill down his back.

"What do you want from Greymost?" Kelvin asked quietly.

The stranger stepped closer.

"Greymost will open the road soon."

"Where?"

"You know that yourself. Mages will take what is theirs. After the first city, the others will understand there is nothing left to fear."

A dim lantern hung by the lower entrance from the river side. It looked ordinary, like someone had simply forgotten to take it down from the dyehouse wall. In truth, the lantern played an important role in Harn’s plan: the light was weak, but strong enough for the guards on the warehouse roof to see the signal.

Kelvin stood so the lantern fell on the edge of his cloak. A small polished plate had been sewn to the inner side of the hem. Nothing remarkable, just a piece of metal that could easily be taken for a clasp or decoration.

The conversation with the suspicious white-haired mage was enough. Kelvin slightly turned the edge of his cloak with his fingers. The light fell on the plate and reflected in several flashes.

Then the silhouettes on the roofs tensed. A moment later, footsteps struck from all sides.

"Stop!" Harn roared. "Hands where I can see them!"

The stranger did not run. He only turned his head, as if he had known beforehand where the ambush was. Then his hand slid to his sleeve, and a scroll flared with dry white fire. Smoke struck the faces of the nearest guards. In the same instant, lightning lashed the dyehouse wall, showering the yard with stone chips.

Someone shouted. A crossbow bolt struck the ground at the stranger’s feet. A second passed through the edge of his cloak. The criminal mage knocked one guard down with his shoulder and threw another back with a short discharge to the chest, but he did not finish anyone off.

The runic net unfolded from above. The stranger raised his hand, as if he were about to tear it apart, but suddenly swayed. His face turned gray. The net fell over his shoulders, wound around his arms, and the runes bit into the fabric.

"Shackles!" Harn shouted.

Kelvin ran up himself. The stranger raised his eyes to him.

"Now you’ll have to find out how many of us are left," he said quietly.

Kelvin snapped the shackles onto the criminal’s wrists. The suppressing circuit closed without error. Kelvin himself had worked on the runic pattern on these shackles, so he could be certain they would not fail.

Only then did he allow himself to breathe.

By morning, all of Greymost knew that one of the main mages of the underground had been caught by the old dyehouse. By noon, Harn summoned Kelvin to the administration. He no longer scolded the young runic mage. This time, he looked at him for a long while, then placed a new badge on the table.

"Starting tomorrow, you’ll be my right hand in runic matters," Harn said. "Don’t make me regret it."

Kelvin took the badge. He should have felt joy, and he did. It was pleasant, almost childish. But along with it, somewhere deep inside sat the captured mage’s words:

Now you’ll have to find out how many of us are left.

Kelvin closed his fingers around the badge and told himself it was only the threat of a defeated man. A very convincing one, but still only a threat.
