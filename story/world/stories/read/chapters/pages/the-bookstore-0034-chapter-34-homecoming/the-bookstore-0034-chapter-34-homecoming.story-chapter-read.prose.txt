The group of five moved through the dark without speaking unless necessary.

Under ordinary circumstances, common sense would have told them to make camp, hide the light, and wait for dawn before continuing. But common sense had very little value when danger was behind them and might still be gaining. So they pressed onward through the moonless night with only a weak torch to guide them, each step measured between urgency and the fear of breaking an ankle in the dark.

Escaping Riverend had gone far more smoothly than the golden trio had expected. Dorothy had spent less than an hour calling on old favors and contacts before getting them through one of the smaller gates. That part had seemed too easy, but what came after corrected the balance.

Roughly three hours into the journey toward the crossroads, they heard a carriage racing behind them. The sound reached them before the light did—wheels hammering the road, horses driven hard, the sort of speed that belonged either to officials or to furious people. Dorothy did not need to say what everyone else was already thinking. If not the dead elf’s relatives, then men hired by them. Either way, being found by that carriage would end badly.

So they ran into the wild. They had enough warning to gain a lead, and once the pursuers were forced off the road, their advantage shrank. A carriage could outrun people. It could not chase unseen targets through brush, uneven ground, and darkness.

Eventually the sounds of pursuit faded. Yet, no one trusted that fully.

Not long after, when they judged the risk of stumbling blind to be greater than the risk of showing a little light, Iris used a fire cantrip to set a makeshift torch burning. The glow was poor, but it was enough to keep them moving. Being caught by pursuers was dreadful, but facing beasts in the dark, when the beasts could see better than they could, was even worse.

They could not return to the road. So they shadowed it instead.

Fortunately, none of them carried much. Dorothy had brought only what she could not bear to leave behind, and the trio had already spent most of Anne’s coins on supplies. They stayed close enough to the road not to lose direction, but not so close that dawn would expose them to the first rider passing by.

The night passed that way—hard, stretched, and full of the sort of tension that made time feel both sluggish and treacherously quick. When the sun finally rose, they lost the cover of darkness but kept moving. Somehow, by the grace of luck, exhaustion, or simple obscurity, the day passed without incident.

Only then did they stop. They rested in a patch of rough ground Dorothy guessed was perhaps half a day from the crossroads, though no one trusted guesses very much by then. When they finally reached the landmark unchallenged, they left the wild, regained the Mountainend road, and breathed a little more easily.

Two days later, the town finally came into view.

Anne watched Rick finish his last notes on Evading. The hunter bent over the page with growing confidence, no longer looking as though writing itself were a hostile craft he had to outwit by stubbornness alone. He was preparing to return to the forest and continue searching for easier paths through the mountains. Even without measuring the improvement properly, Anne could see it. His arrival times had changed. So had the way he carried fatigue. He moved better now—faster, smoother, with less waste in the body.

The training book had been worth it. He still needed his new weapon and armor before undertaking anything truly ambitious, but the running and evasion work had already paid for itself. With those skills, the Mapping Book, and a little caution, he might finally find a passage worth more than local convenience.

Rick finished the final line, looked up, and gave her a small signal. Anne answered with a nod.

He closed the book, gathered his things, and left.

The bookstore quieted again.

Anne returned to the volume in front of her, though part of her mind remained on the road. By her estimate, the golden trio should be back soon, perhaps even that day. She was not worried for them exactly, but she had learned that the borderlands always found new ways to complicate ordinary tasks.

The book she had chosen for the afternoon inspection was one of the better preserved volumes Ceres had brought some time ago. Anne had given it only a preliminary assessment earlier because more urgent work kept pushing it aside. Now she could finally examine it properly.

It was either a work of fiction or a historical record concerning the southern duchy of the old Oryn Kingdom before the Clows’ arrival.

The distinction was important. Anne had always been especially careful with history. The line between embellished memory and historical truth was often thinner than people liked to admit, and many books crossed it without any guilt. In such cases, a bookmancer’s duty was not merely to store text, but to understand what kind of text she was storing.

There were two ways to settle the matter. If the author was reliable, that simplified everything. Anne could verify the name and reputation, note the conditions, and shelve the volume accordingly. If not, the text itself had to be checked against stronger records.

Unfortunately, the writer had not been a bookmancer. The name attached to the work belonged, supposedly, to an amateur archivist in service to a local lord. A century earlier, that would not have been especially strange. Many rulers once employed archivists to preserve the major events of their regions. The practice had withered badly over the last century, which Anne found both unsurprising and offensive.

So she took notes. Dates, locations, claims, mentioned houses, and page numbers for later comparison. Most of it would likely remain unverified, which meant the book would probably end in the semi-fiction section. Even so, semi-fiction could still prove useful in future research.

She was in the middle of noting one questionable sequence when the sound of visitors interrupted her.

“Miss Anne, we’re back.”

Iris’s voice carried into the room before Anne looked up. The golden trio entered first, followed by two women Anne had never seen before. She noticed the tired state of all of them immediately. Dust, road-weariness, the stiffness of people who had slept badly or not at all, and beneath it something more strained. Cyrus looked especially ashamed, which made Anne’s first assumption simple enough: they had failed at least part of the commission.

But the newcomers painted an entire different picture. One was a dwarven woman with a long bright red braid and steady, assessing gaze. The other was a younger half-elf girl whose distress stood out so strongly that Anne had to resist the urge to go to her immediately.

Interesting.

Anne stood and moved into welcome before questions.

“Come in, all of you,” she said warmly. “Let’s sit and speak properly over tea. And once you’ve all breathed again, perhaps you can introduce those lovely ladies.”

Before reaching the bookstore, the group had agreed to keep the dead elf out of their mouths. That much was already hanging over the room before anyone sat. The plan, such as it was, had become to describe the dwarf and half-elf as women who had decided to leave Riverend because money had run dry and prospects had worsened. It was not entirely a lie, which made it useful.

Still, Anne could tell at a glance that the situation went far beyond ordinary hardship. She served the tea first.

Only after the first few sips did the group begin to loosen. The trio relaxed under the effect of the soul tea and the simple relief of being indoors again. Alanis seemed to be holding herself together by force and little else. Dorothy, on the other hand, became deeply occupied with the tea itself.

That did not stop her from studying Anne. The dwarf had expected something else. Anne could see it in the way her eyes moved across the room, the arrangement of the tables, the books, the teaware, the soft hospitality that did not resemble any magic emporium Dorothy had likely imagined. She drank, observed, and judged with the contained caution of a woman who had survived too much by underestimating no one.

Anne liked her immediately. More importantly, Anne recognized the intelligence in the way Dorothy took stock of the situation. The woman noticed the bookstore owner’s own assessment just as quickly. She understood that Anne was probing without looking as if she were probing. She also understood why the tea came before the questions.

Professional, Anne thought. And tired.

Anne answered Dorothy’s quiet evaluation with nothing more than sincere smiles and another careful refill.

Then she addressed the problem she could solve without forcing anyone into explanations before they were ready.

“Ma’am,” she said to Dorothy, “would you like to freshen yourself while we finish the tea? The roads in this region are never kind, and you look as though you have had a rather harsher time of them than most.”

Dorothy nearly choked on the tea. The offer had struck exactly where Anne intended. The dwarf glanced briefly toward the others, perhaps wondering if this was only a polite way to separate her from the conversation so the younger ones could speak freely.

It was not only that. Dorothy badly needed it.

“Miss Anne, I would not wish to trouble you,” Dorothy said, recovering her voice with admirable speed.

“It is no trouble,” Anne said. “And I insist.”

She smiled more lightly then, deliberately easing the pressure.

“Later, you may repay me by giving me some hairdressing advice. I admire your braid.”

That earned the exact reaction she wanted—a shy smile and the first real softening in the dwarf’s posture.

Anne rose, and Dorothy, after only a little hesitation, followed. The two of them passed through the hall and toward the quieter part of the building. Anne led her to the room between her own bedroom and the sealed room containing the rift. It had become a useful spare room over time—simple, but clean enough, with a table and some chairs kept in reasonable order thanks to Spring’s diligence.

Anne left Dorothy there only long enough to fetch what was needed from her own room.

She returned with a bucket of clean water, cloths, and a small assortment of practical grooming tools. Dorothy tried to refuse some of it on principle, but Anne ignored that completely and instructed her to make use of the cloth as best she could to improvise something fresher before the water cooled.

Then she stepped out and closed the door gently behind her. Whatever story the dwarf and half-elf carried into the bookstore, it was larger than coin alone. Anne knew that already. But even without understanding it yet, she would not ignore another woman who so clearly needed clean water, privacy, and a little dignity restored before she could speak properly.

So Anne returned to the main room. The others looked up when she entered, each in their own way. Tired, uneasy, and expectant. The half-elf girl looked very near tears again.

Anne resumed her seat and arranged her expression into something steady and kind.

Though she knew almost nothing yet of Dorothy’s life, she could already feel something taking shape. Not a passing customer, but something more.
