Trevor and Adabelle went on ride after ride, never staying in one place for long. Her enthusiasm was insatiable, trying everything she could both big and small. He enjoyed every moment of her unbridled happiness.

They only started to slow down in the evening when their stomachs protested. Their tickets had come with unlimited meals and they had used it for water and snacks, but it was time for something substantial. Finding a map was easy since there was one at every intersection, and they decided on a restaurant.

Getting there meant they would have to ride the Lime Line, but Trevor was fine by that. Trains were great, even if this was the slower, open-air type. It was a relaxing ride that gave them time to wind down before they ate.

The newlyweds, a little worn on the fruit theme, chose a place called Barbecue City which seemed promising enough. When they arrived, they weren’t disappointed. Their plates were loaded up with ribs, pulled pork, vegetables, and rice. They had skipped lunch entirely in their excitement to get to the next ride and they were ready to correct that mistake.

Sitting down in the outside eating area, Trevor smiled at his wife. “I’m pretty sure I know how you’re feeling, but I want to hear it from you. How’s Lemon World treating you, Adabelle?”

“Very well,” the demonkin replied. She sat across from him, resting her leg against his. “I think it’s starting to lose its luster, but not so quickly that I’m not enjoying myself. This is still a very novel experience.”

“Yeah, that’ll happen,” he said with a sage nod. “So many little rushes in a row can dull that out for you a bit. I’m glad it hasn’t gotten rid of your enthusiasm entirely.”

“Absolutely not. I’m still ready to go,” Adabelle laughed as she focused on her plate. “I do have to admit, I wasn’t expecting Aletander to be so… different.”

“What with all the tech and stuff?” Trevor asked.

She nodded. “I’ve heard a lot about Aletander when we were back home. It’s a little hard not to considering the events of the past, even if it’s been peaceful since then. I just can’t help but wonder how they turned into, well, all of this. It’s miraculous, isn’t it?”

“In a way, yeah, but I don’t think I would call it a miracle.”

“You don’t?”

“It’s cool and I’m glad that we’re not fighting them anymore, but this is just kind of how technology works,” Trevor answered with a shrug. “It has a tendency to snowball, getting better and better over time. Like, I don’t remember the exact numbers, but it was only something like sixty or seventy years between Earth’s first flight to landing on the moon.”

“Hold on, I thought that whole moon landing debacle was a propagandist hoax?” Adabelle asked, scrunching up her nose in thought before she began eating.

The courier offered her a thin smile. “Wayne?” he asked in return, and his wife sighed. That was all the confirmation he needed. “Yeah, that’s definitely him messing with you. Wayne’s a smart enough guy to know better than that. It was a conspiracy theory that people subscribed to in my day, though I’m not sure if it was still a popular one.”

“I can see what you mean when you say technology has a tendency to snowball,” she mused, skewering a carrot with her fork. “Seventy years sounds like a long time, but it really isn’t in the grand scheme of things. People live longer than that thanks to the advancements we’ve made in alchemy and magic.”

“The same is probably true here, too, thanks to medicine,” Trevor said with a nod. “Having someone like Sammie the Gamer show up was the real miracle. Being able to piggyback off of her work and build things like motors, even if they’re not as good as the stuff she made, would be a huge boon to any civilization. And that was just off of her Power, we don’t even know if she was a proper engineer back in her world.”

“That is very insightful,” Adabelle praised with a smile as he took a bite of saucy ribs. Some of it lingered on his cheek, and she gestured that there was something on his face. He was quick to wipe it off with a napkin. “I know the reason why Lucent is so advanced is because of Jackson, so magical research must work the same way. Starts off slow, then picks up.”

“Doesn’t seem as fast, though,” he remarked as he looked around. “Then again, magic is harder to wrangle than technology, isn’t it?”

“I reckon it is harder to wrangle, yes,” she replied teasingly, adopting the cowboy’s accent for a moment. “You might be spending too much time with Wayne.”

“Ha ha,” he said sarcastically, rolling his eyes before poking his fork in her direction. “I’m just trying to say that you need wizards to fix up magic. Or dragons. Or Summoned. It’s not something everyone can do.”

“No, but those who do work on it do so fairly tirelessly.”

“Yeah, and I hear you, but the skill floor is much higher than it is for technological advancement. I have to assume that’s true, anyway.”

Adabelle tilted her head. “I don’t think I’ve heard that term before.”

“Sorry, it’s not every day I get to dip into my fighting game jargon,” he said with a smile. “Not that they owned the term, that’s just where I heard it first.”

“Why, Trevor, I thought you weren’t a fighter,” she teased.

The courier laughed. “I’m not, that’s why I pretended to play one on TV.” He shook his head. “A skill floor is basically the lowest level you can operate at to be proficient at something. Alternatively, there’s a skill ceiling, but I have to imagine magic’s is higher because you’re literally altering the fabric of reality.”

“Score one for magic, then.”

“Oh? Are magic and technology competing?” Trevor asked, raising an eyebrow.

“Maybe. Go ahead and keep explaining,” Adabelle told him, placing an elbow on the table, and her cheek in her hand as she watched her husband.

“Alright. So the skill floor in magic is a lot higher. Like I said, you have to be a wizard or Summoned if you’re not lucky enough to be born a dragon. Of the two, the latter grants you vast power but you don’t always have the knowledge needed to fully dive into it. For instance, how much magic did you know before you came to this world?”

“Not much,” she admitted before taking a drink. “I knew the basics from Reddigar because he liked to talk about his passions, but I didn’t know enough to cast any spells.”

“I assume that’s normal where you come from,” Trevor said, and the demonkin nodded. “Right, and that level of knowledge would be normal for people summoned from magical lands. It’s not all that different for technology, though. I’ll tell you that I wouldn’t be able to, say, engineer a bridge. Not a good one that would last, anyway. The difference, though, is that the skill floor is lower.”

“Thus, access to proficiency is easier,” Adabelle stated.

“Exactly. Wizardry requires years and years of study. You have to have the right aptitude, you have to be smart enough, and you have to find someone who wants to train an apprentice if you’re not lucky to get into magic school, like that one you mentioned to Matilsa. Then there’s all the secrecy, too.”

“There has to be plenty of secrets in the world of engineering, since that’s what you brought up before,” she interjected.

Trevor nodded. “I’m sure there is, but the difference is that you can reverse-engineer a bridge. If I spent some time studying the ones in Tosa, which are fairly simple, then I could eventually build a replica. That’s a lot harder with magic like, say, the enchantment on your sword.”

“That is a good point,” Adabelle conceded. “It would be harder to come up with a ritual to impart an enchantment with only the finished product because you’ll be missing out on a lot of the components that disappear once it’s finished.”

“And that can happen in science, too, like chemistry. But it’s more mundane. Technology is accessible to those who are curious but can’t practice magic. I mean, I learned how to fix a washing machine by finding a video on the internet that had specific enough steps for me to do it myself. Same with my car. They might have been mundane things, but that’s the strength. Anyone could do it with the right tools.”

“You’re thinking about it from the viewpoint of someone already from a technologically advanced world,” the demonkin said. “We don’t have that kind of access in this one.”

“Fair point,” he agreed, nodding. “I’m not saying anyone can build a computer or anything—clearly they can’t because we haven’t seen one—but they’ll get there someday. There were more mechanics in that garage yesterday and today than wizards I have met, even in Lucent. That’s why I say the skill floor is lower. Dissemination of information, ease of access, and less time learning the secrets of the universe in order to do something like chunk out a piece of Floatstone.”

“Which is why you say all of this isn’t a miracle.”

“Yeah,” Trevor confirmed, shrugging before pointing his fork at her again. She watched the utensil carefully. “The march of progress is inevitable. Lucent and our continent are more magic focused because we got someone like Jackson, but imagine if we had summoned Sammie the Gamer instead. This could have easily been Eskretet right now if they were switched. The only miraculous thing is that someone wanted the Power of Innovation. And that it was actually shared instead of shoved into a dark cupboard somewhere.”

“I believe you’ve made your point, though I’m not sure I agree,” Adabelle said. She brought her fork up to meet his and flicked it with a flourish. It shot upwards out of his grip. Trevor stood up to catch it before it could fall, and she giggled.

“Sheesh, don’t even need a sword, do you?” he asked after he caught the utensil and sat back down.

“You challenged me by pointing first, which I graciously ignored the first time,” she replied innocently as she ate a carrot off of her fork. Trevor looked down. There had been one left on his plate, and she had stolen it while he was distracted. “To the winner goes the spoils.”

“You’re so smug.”

“You love it.”

“I love you,” he said with a chuckle. “And because you can be smug, I suppose I can love that, too.”

“Good,” she replied, smiling. “Though, the reason why I don’t agree was because my idea of a miracle can be different from yours. You must have thought magic was miraculous when you first arrived, no?”

“That’s… fair, yes,” Trevor conceded.

“It’s the same with me and technology. Your world couldn’t have made an Anderson Bag, but neither this one or mine could have created something like Lemon World without help. It’s a matter of perspective.”

“Yeah, there’s no arguing with that logic,” he agreed as he looked back down at his plate. “For the record, I think that Jackson did a fine job with what he had considering only, like, two percent of the population learns to use magic and far less become wizards powerful enough to make their own towers. It’s been seven hundred years since he got here, but Lucent already has so much stuff that I didn’t feel like I was missing out on much of anything important when I came here.”

Adabelle glanced at the people eating around them. Some were giving them curious glances, which she had gotten used to since humans like her husband were so rare here, and paid them no mind as she leaned in.

“Getting the Quest Master attuned to our world was a long project, according to his notes, so he spent even less time than you think he did helping accelerate the magical advancements that made Lucent what it is today. He really only started when he founded Tosa.”

“That was three hundred years ago, right?” Trevor asked, thinking back to his first meeting with the elf.

“Correct,” Adabelle confirmed. “And in that time we’ve gotten things like magical appliances and plumbing, items that act like your cell phones such as the Two-Way Blackglass, and a plethora of new rituals for things like climate control. Much of which has spread throughout the continent, at least for the high class and governments who could afford to install them inside public property.”

“Yeah, that kind of convenience is pretty nice everywhere,” the courier agreed. “Still no amusement parks, though.”

“We had something similar, once, but they couldn’t figure out how to keep the magical attractions from interfering with one another. It didn’t last very long, and I don’t think it even opened to the public.”

“Then I guess it’s a good thing you’re going to talk to King Eskretet then, huh?” he said with a smile. “Can’t wait to get some engineers back home who can replicate some of this.”

“Putting the cart in front of the wracor, honey, but I suppose it’s good to dream,” Adabelle replied, reaching out to pat his hand. “For now, though, eat up. I want to check out the Dragon’s Drop before it gets dark. The view from so high up ought to be great.”

“You got it,” he said, nodding. “I want to see it, too. We’ll be almost done with our circuit after that, so what will you want to do next?”

“Whatever we can again, of course,” she answered as though it were obvious. “I’m still down for being bad. If this place is open all night, then let’s go all night. Get our fill while we can.”

“Alright, lady. Let’s go all night,” he agreed easily.
