Ten years later…

“How is she?” Rex jumped up from his chair as the priest quietly exited the room.

“I’m sorry, my boy. She’s got the bluelung. Nothing to be done about it at this stage. I’ve done all I can for her,” the priest said, placing a hand on Rex’s shoulder. “The rest is up to the gods.”

“That’s it? You’re just giving up?”

“I know it’s hard. But I’m afraid so. It’s just how it is. The coldrock gets everyone eventually. That’s life in the mines. The Goddess Effyana tests us every day on Dorog. And we suffer what we must. It’s all part of her plan, you see—”

“Plan? This is her plan,” Rex gestured toward the partially opened door. “She’s been in pain for months. She can barely breathe. What kind of plan is that?”

“I know,” the priest sighed. “Us mortals cannot hope to understand the machinations of gods. As for the…”

The priest shied away awkwardly as he stood there expectantly.

“Yeah, I know. Nothing comes free,” Rex said, handing the man the pouch of coppers he had promised.

“Effyana go with you, Rex,” the priest said, waving about a charm-covered staff and then leaving.

Rex fell back on the chair and ran his fingers through his hair. His mother was all he had. Even after her leg had been mutilated, she never stopped working the mines. Never stopped putting food on the table, no matter how hard it got.

And I can't even save her.

He got up and peeked through the door. She was there, breathing heavily.

“Is that you, Rex?”

“Yeah,” Rex said, stepping through the door.

His mother didn’t say anything more, but a thin smile creased her face. Even if they weren’t talking, he knew his mother just enjoyed his presence, and so he sat beside her.

“My neck,” Rex groaned and rubbed at his eyes. He’d fallen asleep in the chair and was feeling several aches. “Ma? You awake?”

Sleeping in that chair did not do me any good.

He cracked his neck and stretched, trying to loosen the stiffness and aches out of his body.

“I think I’m going to have to stop…” Rex trailed off as he looked over at his mother. She was unusually pale, and her lips were blue.

“Ma? Ma! MA!”

He grabbed her shoulders and stared at her for a moment, then shook. “Ma! WAKE UP!”

The following days after his mother’s passing were not easy on Rex. But after two days of sitting about, he forced himself back into the mines. Not because he wanted to, but with his mother gone, someone had to pay the bills.

“So that’s why you’re back so soon? To keep that shitty little hovel of yours?” Old Greg said, wiping his brow and taking a sip from his canteen between whacking at the coldrock with his pickaxe.

“Hey, yours isn’t any better,” Rex said and slammed his pickaxe into the coldrock veins that snaked through the rock.

“But what for?

“What do you mean, what for? I dunno. A place to live? What, you think I want to sleep on the street?”

“Ha! You know what, I was wrong. You are one dumb kid, Rex!”

“Me? You’re one to talk. I still remember when you mistook that molerat as your—”

“Hey! That was between you and me. And come off it. I’m sick of you moping around. Whatever happened to the Rex who was going to learn magic like that sexy elf with long legs, and the bountiful…” Greg trailed off, licking his lips.

“You are so gross. And nothing. That was just a dreaming boy who didn’t understand the world. I’m a man now, or close enough to it. I understand how things actually work now.”

“Oh, whatever. Was it just your ma, or did you lose your balls, too?”

“Oh, come on! Give me a break, Greg!”

“No, I won’t give you a break, dumbass. You wanna get bluelung like your ma? Look. I can already feel the coughs coming up in meself. Rex,” Greg turned to him and grabbed his shoulders.

“That smell,” Rex winced and turned from Greg’s mouth. “You’ve got grog in that canteen, don’t you?”

“Don’t worry about what’s in Old Greg’s canteen. This is important! I remember those spellcircles you used to draw when you were little. Damn good they were. You’re a smart kid. You’ve got a future. A real one. Not like the rest of us fools in here. Don’t be a fucking pussy and waste it in the mines!”

“You don’t know what you’re talking about, Greg. What good are spellcircles without mana? I’m not like Lavrian. I don’t have what it takes.”

“Hog shit!” Greg slapped him across the face. “Cry all you want; it ain’t gonna bring her back. You think she’d want you to have the same future?”

Rex touched his cheek. It felt hot and sore. It was… nice. It was the first time feeling something other than numbness since his mother had passed. It was like it reminded him of what it felt like to be alive.

“Did that knock some sense into you, dumbass?”

“I… I think it actually did.”

“Good! I’m sick to death of watching you all… all… Just hanging out. All nothing like. It pisses me off.”

“Sorry. I didn’t mean to.”

“It’s fine. Want a drink?”

“No. No. I’m good.”

“Alright. Suit yourself. More for Old Greg.”

That evening, Rex couldn’t stop thinking about what Greg said. He was obviously drunk and probably didn’t mean half of it. But that hardly mattered. He was right. What was he doing following in his mother’s footsteps? The only reason he was still stuck on Dorog was his mother in the first place, and… not being Classed.

That was a big problem.

Without a Class, he couldn’t use the Gates, and without the Gates, he couldn’t leave Dorog even if he wanted to.

If only I had actual magic…

Magic wasn’t the only means of being recognized by the Shrine and granted a Class. But it was one of the easier options, assuming you had talent. But more importantly, it was what he had been dreaming of since he was a child—since seeing the elven mage.

And Greg was also correct about his spellcircles. They were pretty good. He had always had a knack for drawing them. But without mana, that didn’t mean a whole lot. The Shrine wouldn’t recognize spellcasting without casting a spell, and that required mana.

Unless…

As he considered alternative options, he was a little annoyed that he had given all of their savings to the priest. But a pang of hope lit up within him nonetheless. There were other ways to get coin, and his eyes started scanning the room with sudden bursts of determination.

“That might just work!”

He jumped up, grabbed a sack, and started throwing things in it.

“That! And that! And that will definitely sell! So will that. And that. And that. And that!” Rex said, stuffing anything he spotted of value into the sack as he ransacked his own home.

He mightn’t have been able to use mana naturally; he’d tried enough times already. But there were other means of awakening one’s mana. Poor people like himself rarely did it. But most richer folk bought mana crystals for their children.

To miners, mana crystals didn’t come cheaply. But to the rich upper-crust, it was a small investment to be sure whether or not your children had magical potential. The chances of it working weren’t high, but they weren’t zero, either.

“Yeah, that’s it. A mana crystal. There’s still a chance that I can become a mage!”

He could barely sleep that night, and when the moment morning came, he sprang out of bed, grabbed the sack, and shot through the door, heading straight for the market.

However, the moment he stepped outside, he almost fell over somebody’s foot. A few dozen people from his neighborhood were marching through the streets, celebrating, throwing flowers, and waving banners.

“What’s all this about?”

“Hey, Rex! Come join us! We’re marching in celebration of Lavrian’s Shrine attempt! Tomorrow he’ll face Effyana, and take the guardian on, and become the first Arcane-Classed man from around here in over a century!”

Rex spotted Lavrian. The tall, handsome boy his age was being held up in celebration by several others as they cheered, as the prettiest girls from the neighbourhood watched on and giggled.

“I’d love to. Seriously. But you know, I’m super busy. What with everything…”

“Oh! Sorry, my boy,” the man faceplamed. “I almost forgot about your mother,” the man lowered his head. “If you need anything—”

“Hey, Bert! Over here!” Another man shouted and wandered over on wobbly legs. He wrapped a joyous arm around him when he got there and pressed a bottle to his lips. “DRINK UP! It’s party time! To young Lavrian!”

“Don’t mind if I do!” Bert said and gulped the bottle, seeming to have already forgotten about Rex.

Well, that was quick, Rex thought as the celebrations marched on, passing his hovel as they cheered Lavrian’s name and drank liberally. He didn’t really blame them, though. There wasn’t a lot to celebrate around the coldrock mines.

“Watchya bringing me all that junk for?” Damo, the merchant said, shifting through all of Rex’s worldly belongings.

“I want to sell it all. How much?”

“All of it?” Damo raised a brow. “The coldrock finally drove you mad, did it?”

“No. I just need coin.”

“Do you?”

“Yes,” Rex replied, pointing to a glowing red crystal. “I want that.”

“A mana crystal? Wait, are you going to…”

Rex nodded and crossed his arms.

“Alright, well. Can’t exactly blame you for wanting to get off this ugly rock. This junk isn’t quite enough, though. But you’ve been a good customer over the years. I suppose I could make the trade… You know, as a favor.”

“That’s one hundred percent bullshit, Damo,” Rex said. “I’m not a sucker.”

“Look, if you don't want–”

“Fine, just give me the crystal, Damo. I'm not in the mood to barter."

“Lucky you,” Damo said, scooping everything into a chest and handing him the crystal. “Good doing business with you.”

Crystal in hand, Rex perched atop a small cliff overlooking the harsh landscape of Dorog and was reminded just how much he hated it.

“What’s that?” said Ike, a local kid who had apparently snuck up on him and was currently knuckle deep in his nose. Ike scooped out a disturbingly large booger, flicked it away, and then pointed at the crystal.

“Ah, what is it with people today? Can’t I get a moment of peace?” Rex said, and the boy stared back absently. “Alright, fine. It’s a mana crystal. And it’s what’s going to make me a mage.”

“Oh, neat. Can I watch?”

“I suppose so. Just try to keep it down. I’ll need to focus for this.”

“Uh-huh.”

The kid was actually pretty good at being quiet. It was the staring that put Rex off. But he had already agreed to let him watch, so he tried to ignore it.

“Alright, let’s get this magic going,” Rex said, rubbing his hands together. “Time to escape this shithole.”

He glanced down at the harsh mining landscape, then up to the sky where Elysia hung tantalizingly close. All it took was a look at the bluish, green world to imagine frolicking elves and wizards. Magic everywhere.

Technically, Dorog was Elysia’s moon. Although it might as well have been a distant realm. Without the ability to use the Gates, there was no means of travel between them.

Rex squeezed the crystal in his grasp and remembered his mother’s face as he lay dying.

“So, are you gonna start or…?”

“Yes! I’m getting to it. And remember what I said about being quiet?”

Ike nodded.

“Good. Now, stand back and watch the magic.”

He took a deep breath, cleared his mind, and his hands were steady. He began drawing a circle and crossing lines through it. It was a binding circle. A basic spell for amateur arcane users trying to learn how to control mana. Its purpose was simple. The circle would draw out and hold one’s mana in place, allowing it to then parse it into another spell.

This is it. I hope it wasn’t just the booze talking, Greg, Rex thought as he tried to feel mana and connect with the spell circle.

He closed his eyes and tried to imagine the spell coming to life. Power pouring from his fingertips and weaving into magical energy. But it was taking longer than expected.

“Is something meant to be happening?”

“What happened to quiet?”

Ike shot him a thumbs up, and he turned back to the spellcircle, looking for imperfections. But everything looked right, just as the tomes instructed. Of course, it wasn’t his first time failing to cast a spell, but he hadn’t had a mana crystal on him those other times.

His grasp around the crystal tightened. It was now or never. Rex took a deep breath. It was time to make mag—

“Wait, are you eating a carrot now?”

“Er, what carrot?” Ike said, slowly removing the carrot from his mouth and shoving it back into his pocket.

“My goodness. Goddess, give me patience to deal with this!”

Rex tried to shake the nervous energy free and concentrate. The spell was taking too long. He had read many tomes about magic, Arcane Classes, and spellcircles. They were basic, cheap tomes. Scrap shared with them by the local lords, but he trusted what he had read about basic spellcircles. And the one he was using was supposed to draw mana almost immediately.

About to give up, he looked down at the crystal in his hand, and it was glowing. Suddenly, a glimmer of hope sparked within. It was working. The crystal was glowing, and that meant he had a chance!

This is it! Mana! Real life mana!

Excitedly, Rex slammed his hands down against the circle, and—

—and… nothing.

Absolutely nothing. Just the cacophony of miners, oxen, and clinking metal below. And crunching carrot.

“Sooo… that was it?”

Rex slumped. “Nothing? Really? Not even a tiny sliver of mana? Please. I’m good for it. For Shrine’s sake! C’mon, Goddess Effyana. I’ll sacrifice a goat or…” he said, turning to Ike. “Or maybe a kid…”

“Kids? Oh, I know! Let’s sacrifice Johan!” Ike jumped on his toes excitedly. “He’s definitely a virgin!”

“You're both definitely virgins! Oh, just shh, would you? I need to focus!”

But when he looked down at the crystal again, it was already starting to flake away, disintegrating before his eyes.

“Oh, so I did manage to activate it… That’s good to know, I suppose.”

Rex slumped. It was a bitter pill to swallow, but it was better to have tried and failed than to have never tried at all.

It would have been nice if I hadn’t sold everything I own, though.

“Tsk, tsk, tsk. So, this is where you’ve been, is it? Hiding over here with Ike? I was kind of hoping you’d join the celebrations.”

Rex turned. It was Lavrian. The boy was the same age as he was, but taller and more handsome. Not to mention his spellcasting ability, or the fact that he was looked up to like a hero in their neighborhood.

“Hi,” Ike said. “You come to watch too?”

Lavrian ignored Ike, his eyes trained on Rex’s spellcircle. “You’re not seriously still trying to use spellcircles, are you? Why waste your time? C’mon, Rex, if you were going to do it, you’d have done it by now. Let’s be realistic, shall we? There’s more to life than magic.”

“That’s easy for you to say.”

“I also agree with Lavrian,” Ike said.

“Shut up, Ike!”

“Ha, I suppose you’re right,” Lavrian shook his head playfully. “But that doesn’t make me wrong. Remember when we used to play as kids? You’ve got guts, Rex. Maybe even talent. You’ve just got to aim a little lower. Not everyone gets to be a spellcaster. If you really want to challenge the Shrine and get off Dorog, there’s still a whole world of Archetypes and Classes out there to discover. Don’t be stubborn. You’ll get stuck here just like Greg or your mother.”

It was all true. Rex didn’t have to learn Arcane magic. Compared to the masses who had given up hope and resigned themselves to mining the entirety of their short lives, any Classed person was basically a celebrity on Dorog. But Arcane Classes were the A-listers. They shone above the rest, and no matter how hard he tried not to, he still remembered the elven mage.

Lavrian sighed when Rex didn’t reply, and he took a step up to the circle. Without even touching it, the circle lit up with an ethereal, blue glow.

“See that, Rex? He made it work! First go, like it was nothing. Just routine and effortless magic.”

“Exactly, that's how it's supposed to work,” Lavrian said. “There’s nothing wrong with the spellcircle. You just don’t have talent for magic. Come to my celebrations. Relax, drink some ale. The adults will be too drunk to care. Maybe even trick a girl into kissing you. And when you wake up in the morning, go practice something more—I don’t know, Rex-like. You know what I mean? And maybe one day, you can even join my party.”

“You should take him up on that. That’s not a bad offer,” Ike said, biting his carrot.

Lavrian stepped up to Rex’s side, patted him on the shoulder, and smiled. “I know your mother just passed, but I’d like to see you at the party later. I’ll be challenging the Shrine tomorrow, and it’d be nice to have everyone there. And I’m sure others would appreciate it too. It’s hard to get the entire borough together in one place these days. Remember Rex, sometimes it’s important to do things for others.”

Then, the smug boy turned and walked away.

Rex gritted his teeth and nodded. But in truth, he kinda wanted to smack him. Lavrian had a real knack for sounding like a pompous douche. But smacking Lavrian wouldn’t change the reality of his failure.

He glanced down at his trembling fists. The mention of his mother had made him angrier than he even realized. And the moment he cooled down, he just felt bad again.

“Soo…”

“What are you looking at, Ike? I mean… I’m sorry. It’s not your fault.”

Rex slumped. Maybe his dreams were stupid, but Greg was right, even if he had been hammered. What point was there in staying on Dorog just to mine coldrock until he got sick, as his mother had?

“You know, you don’t have to use magic to get a Class, right?”

“I know that, Ike.”

“Soo…?

Rex raised his head. He hadn’t wanted to listen to Lavrian, but they had a point. He didn’t need magic. All he needed to do was show off a skill to be recognized by the Shrine. Of course, there was also the matter of defeating the guardian, but he could figure that out later.

“Hang on, you might actually have a point. But what skills would I use?”

He couldn’t do just anything and expect the Shrine to care. The more mundane a skill was, the better you had to be at it for the Shrine to acknowledge you.

“What about your bow?”

“Huh? My bow?”

Rex was pretty good with a bow. Food was expensive on Dorog, and he regularly hunted waste gerbils with both bow and traps to add a little extra protein to his diet. But he wasn’t sure he wanted the kinds of Classes that would be offered if he used those kinds of skills. He certainly wouldn’t be like the elven mage or even the warrior who had charged the demons.

“I don’t know if I want to use my bow. Is it dumb that I don’t want a Veil Class? Or a Strider Class for that matter. I want to be a hero. Not some sneaky rogue, or wandering vagabond…”

“Y’know, you could use multiple skills.”

Rex raised a brow.

Ike bit his carrot, loudly crunching it as he spoke. “Yeah. Like, if you use multiple skills when challenging the Shrine, it takes all of them into consideration when choosing your Class, right?”

“Right,” Rex nodded along to Ike’s logic. “So, if I got myself a sword…”

“Yep, you might get a Vigor Class. Might even become a knight. Even if you can’t use the thing. Kind of stupid though. Why'd you want a Class you can't use properly? I suppose you could learn how to use a sword properly… But still. Not to mention every extra skill you use makes the guardian stronger–”

“Shhh. Unnecessary details, Ike. What’s important is that I get off the world. And with a heroic Class, preferably,” Rex rose proudly, buffing up his chest.

It wasn’t his first choice, but becoming a brave warrior who held the vanguard and charged the enemy lines was probably his second.

And waiting around to master the sword enough to impress the Shrine would take him years. But if he used multiple skills to push himself over the line… well, then he could fast-track his way off Dorog.

Rex bounced to his feet and started rushing down the cliff edge.

“Welp, he’s definitely gonna die,” Ike shook his head and bit his carrot.

There was a problem. Rex didn’t have a sword. And he had just sold everything he owned.

“OH! Dammit! Why do I have to be so impulsive!”

The coin he had spent on the crystal would have bought him a decent enough sword. And even if he saved every copper possible, he’d have to spend months, maybe years, in the mines trying to save up again.

He went through the little hovel, pulling it apart in the hopes that he had missed something here or there. In the end, he barely managed to pull together a half dozen, bent old coppers.

“Eww, what’s that?” he murmured as he picked the coins clean. “This is nowhere near enough to buy a sword… Then again, Damo is a mate, isn’t he? Maybe I can borrow one?”

Neither he nor Damo was likely to call each other friends, but that was an inconvenient fact Rex decided not to think about. He had hope now; he just needed a damn sword.

After all, as long as he had a sword to swing around, there was a chance he could get lucky and get gifted a knightly Vigor Class by the Shrine. And if not, it wasn’t so bad. He’d get a Veil Class, or something, assuming he survived.

“As long as I get off this rock.”

He turned to leave for the market square, but stopped by the door. He looked over at his bag of traps for gerbil hunting, hanging from the coat rack.

“Dammit, fine!”

He sighed and grabbed the bag of traps and loose odds and ends. It was insurance just in case his skills weren’t enough for the Shrine. Because if there was one thing Rex knew, it was that he was either getting off this world or going to die trying.

Because there was no way he was going to go out as his mother had. He would pick a quick death at the guardian’s hands any day of the week over lying in bed in pain, growing weaker and weaker as his mother had.

At least he had a choice this way.

It was his opportunity to either succeed or fail.
