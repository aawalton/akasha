Lira’s first morning in Steward Harwin’s offices began with the smell of ink.

The dry, dusty scent of the old library was swapped with something sharper and fresher.Ink mixed with parchment, ink mixed with people, and ink mixed with the faint tang of metal coins.It clung to the back of her throat as she climbed the narrow spiral stair built into the inner wall.

The bookkeeping tower rose like a thin spine from the castle’s inner ring. Wide windows let in pale morning light, lighting up a large open room with strips of long tables serving as rows of desks, where ten men and a couple of women worked restlessly on bound ledgers. Voices murmured constantly. Quills scratched in uneven rhythm. Pages turned. Somewhere above, on the upper floor, Lira could hear someone arguing over numbers in a strained whisper.

This was the beating heart of the kingdom's economy, and every person here was handling the lifeblood of civilisation: money.

Lira paused at the threshold only long enough to take it in, then stepped inside.

“Who are you?”

The voice snapped like a twig underfoot.

Cedric stood near the central table, a stack of ledgers braced against his hip. He was thin, sharp-featured, his eyes already tired despite the early hour. Ink stained his fingers, his cuffs, even the edge of his jaw where he must have brushed himself without noticing.

“I am Lira. I was sent here to help,” Lira said quietly.

He glanced toward a window, then grunted. “Who are they sending me now?”

He shifted the ledgers onto the table with a dull thump. “You are one of Celene’s girls.”

“Yes.”

“Then you can carry that tray.” He jerked his chin toward a side door. “Tea duty. The clerks start whining if they go an hour without it.”

Lira did not move.

“I can write,” she said.

Cedric snorted. “So half of the maids say that, thinking that working here is easier than scrubbing floors.”

“I can copy cleanly,” she continued, steady. “And I can keep columns straight. I can also do math, but I doubt you would trust me with that right away.”

He looked at her then, properly this time. Then he glanced at the overworked scribes and bookkeepers around him.

“How can I tell if you are telling the truth?”

Lira held his gaze. “Then test me.”

A pause.

Around them, the room did not stop; quills scratched, someone coughed, a chair scraped. A never-ending orchestra of work.

Cedric exhaled through his nose, irritated. “Fine, but if you are wasting my time, I will dock your pay for the week.”

He dragged a ledger toward her and flipped it open. “Copy this page. Exactly. If I see a single number misplaced, you go back to carrying tea.”

He shoved a blank sheet and a quill toward her. The ink pot followed with a sharp tap.

“Sit.”

Lira did.

Situated at the edge of the room near the exit door, the bench was hard and the desk low. The seat was meant for waiting claimants, not work.

The parchment beneath her fingers was smoother, less brittle, and more expensive. The ink was darker and softer, not the ash-and-water mix used by common people.

She dipped the quill and started working.

The first stroke mattered. It always did.

She matched the angle of the original hand, the spacing, the pressure. Numbers aligned in quiet columns. Lines stayed straight without guide marks. She did not rush, but she did not hesitate either.

Around her, the tower breathed, work flowed, and time passed in the midst of bureaucracy and routine.

When she finished, she set the quill down and joined Cedric near his desk.

He blinked as he took the paper. “That was fast,” he said dismissively at first, then his eyes sharpened.

He compared the two sheets, his gaze moving quickly at first, then slower. His brow furrowed. He checked a column, then another, running his finger down the margins.

“Hm.”

He flipped the page. Checked again.

Another pause.

“This is almost perfect,” he said at last.

“Almost?” Lira asked.

“You changed a word in the third line.”

“That was a clerical error I corrected.” Lira frowned.

He glanced at her, something sharper now beneath the irritation. “You should have asked me before correcting it.”

Lira said nothing.

“You were right. It was a mistake, but you do not get to make decisions until you are trusted more.”

Cedric clicked his tongue. “Fine. You copy. Do not speak unless spoken to. Do not touch anything that is not placed in front of you. Do not make decisions before consulting me, and if you slow me down with stupid questions, you are back to tea.”

“Yes.”

He hesitated, then added, almost grudgingly, “You sit there.”

He pointed to a seat near the center, where other scribes were huddled, closer to the real work.

Harwin did not appear that morning. At least Lira didn’t see him.

Yet his presence was everywhere.

Coins changed hands quietly. A clerk found a coin on top of his finished stack of papers, placed there when everyone's eyes were away. A maid checked her apron pocket only to find two coins there after she served a tray of pastries. Even Cedric, at one point, found a coin left beside his elbow and pocketed it without comment. It seemed to be the natural state of affairs in this tower; no one questioned where those coins came from nor when they got there.

Lira did not know if she kept missing seeing Harwin or if he was using some kind of magic to reward his workers.

One thing was sure to her, however, this tower ran not only on ink and numbers… but coins as well... apparently.

Lira copied.

Lists of expenses. Candle deliveries. Broken hinges. Feed for horses. Repairs to gates and roofs. The work was dull on the surface, but beneath it lay a structure of patterns, and flow.

Money moved and goods followed.

By midday, her hand had settled into the rhythm. By afternoon, she no longer needed to think about the shape of numbers. Her mind was free to focus on another task: listening.

Voices carried across the room, whispers caught in the constant motion.

“…twenty bushels short…”

“…no, you counted the winter tithe twice…”

“…Lord Berthwyn delayed again…”

Names, places, and fragments surfaced and vanished in the noise.

She stored them.

By the time the bells marked the end of the workday, Lira set down her quill with steady hands.

Ink stained her fingers. The smell of it clung to her clothes. The numbers still lingered behind her eyes.

Around her, the tower did not truly stop. It only slowed.

Cedric was already moving on to the next task. Clerks gathered their sheets ready to leave. A maid slipped in with a tray and found a coin waiting for her near the table.

Lira remained seated for a moment longer.

She looked at the ledgers stacked before her, at the endless columns of figures, at the quiet flow of wealth that passed through this place unseen by most.

Then she rose.

As she stepped away from the desk and into the thinning crowd, Lira knew one thing with certainty.

This place held power.

If her only objective in life was to feed information to Pallan, this was the place to be.

Her mind drifted back to the library, to the vast knowledge hidden between its shelves, to the magic she could learn.

No. She had tasted true power when she lit that candle with nothing but words and intent.

She could not give that up. Not for this.
