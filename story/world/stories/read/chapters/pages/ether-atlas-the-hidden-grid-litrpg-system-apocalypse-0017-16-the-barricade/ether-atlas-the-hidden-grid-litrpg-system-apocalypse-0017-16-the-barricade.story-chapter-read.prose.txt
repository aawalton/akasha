Chapter 16

"The human brain is an extraordinary filter. I watched a man today try to pay for a coffee while the barista was convulsing with blue light under her skin. He didn't scream or run. He complained about the contactless reader and asked for his receipt.” - The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr, London, Day 1

Donna approached the hardware store with her weight low, weapon visible but unthreatening, eyes reading the space faster than her feet covered it. Arthur followed three paces behind, shield forward, crowbar strapped across his pack. Steve hung back near the corner, billhook planted like a walking stick, watching the street.

A face appeared above the makeshift barricade blocking the door. It was a young man in his early twenties, his hands white-knuckled around the grip of a cricket bat, a Gray-Nicolls Astro. The lad had good taste.

"We're not hostile. Three people. Just passing through." Donna said, her voice pitched to carry.

"How do I know that?" the young man said, his arms trembling slightly.

"Because if we wanted in, we'd already be in." She let the statement sit. "Your barricade has a weakness on the left side. We can help you fix it. Then we're gone."

A woman appeared behind him. She studied Donna over the barrier, then her eyes moved to Arthur. The shield, the dried blood on his sleeve, and the sling on his shoulder.

"You've been fighting," she said.

"Not much else to do around here," Arthur replied, his voice calm and steady.

"Ravi, let them through."

Inside, the hardware shop smelled of the stale exhalation of too many frightened people in too small a space. Arthur scanned the aisle, counting six and getting nods. There was Priya, the shop manager who had called from the barricade, her nephew Ravi, a young couple huddled together against the back display wall, and two construction workmen in paint-spattered trousers who had ducked into the first open doorway they could find when the world broke.

Arthur walked straight to the barricade at the front entrance. As he reached overhead to inspect the shutter rail, his left shoulder caught, firing a jolt of pain from the joint he had been ignoring since the fight in the Mithraeum. He winced, adjusted his stance, and worked around the injury.

"You've built too tall," he told Ravi. "Height without mass is a curtain, not a barrier. Fill the gaps at the bottom. Bags of cement, sand, gravel; you're in a hardware shop, use what you've got. Mass at the base and a low centre of gravity."

He found an L-bracket and four coach bolts in aisle three and reinforced the shutter's cracked bolt mount in ten minutes. It was the kind of repair he'd have charged four hundred quid for in a survey report. Tonight it was free, and it might keep six people alive.

Ravi looked at the cricket bat in his hand. "What are you?"

"Structural engineer." Arthur said, wiping his palm onto his trousers.

"Right," Ravi muttered, blinking at the bolts. "Right."

“And get another weapon. For one thing, it would be a shame if you broke that astro.” Arthur said, motioning at the cricket bat. “For another, a bat is weighted at the bottom; it's great for stroking a one-sixty gram ball through the covers, not so great as a blunt weapon. The handle will come loose the first time you hit anything with proper mass.”

“I was on my way to practice, when….you know.”

“Yeah.”

***

The nurse was staring at the faint lattice on Steve's neck. She'd been off-shift when the sky changed and her partner had found her on a street of collapsed bodies.

"We saw the blue lines, under the skin. Do you know what they are?" she said quietly, gesturing toward Steve.

"It's called the Nadi—" Steve began, then froze, catching himself a second too late.

Priya's head turned sharply. "Nadi?" She stepped closer, her eyes narrowing as she looked between Steve and her nephew. "That's Sanskrit. The channels for the energy pathways in the body; it's from Ayurvedic medicine. My grandmother used to talk about them."

Her eyes narrowed and she lifted her arms. "Why are you using a Sanskrit word for whatever this is?"

Steve looked genuinely caught out, shifting his weight awkwardly on his feet. "That's what... well, whatever this is ….” Steve said, gesturing at the world outside. “...calls it, The Nadi Matrix. I didn't name it."

"Calls it?" Priya's eyebrow rose.

"Long and complicated story and probably irrelevant for this conversation." Steve said, waving a hand dismissively.

"My grandmother used to tell stories about how her teacher could draw the nadis from memory. Every channel and every junction, on a chart the size of a door. And he never once cut open a body. You cannot find a nadi with a knife, she used to say.”

Steve gave them what information he could without giving away the larger pattern behind it. Cross-cultural references to bodily energy channels: Chinese meridians, Tibetan lung, Greek pneuma. He explained that the underlying concept predated any single human civilisation. The glowing lines were not a disease, nor were they harmful and every person who had survived the initial transition possessed them. It was a biological adaptation to the new reality.

The nurse leaned forward, wanting more details, but Steve held his tongue. Too much information often overwhelmed people who weren't privy to the underlying framework. He'd seen it in fieldwork: show a community their site is older than they believed, and half of them would simply shut down.

“You lot need to find a better place to hole up.” Arthur said, hoisting his shield. “We ran into a pack of mutated dogs on the way here. Can’t be certain they won’t expand their hunting radius. Get yourselves inside a building. A stairwell’s a damn sight easier to defend than a shopfront.”

“Sodding mutated animals. So it’s true, then? What the fuck is going on?” Carl, one of the construction workers, asked.

“Nothing good.”

Priya walked them back to the shutters. She added a handful of cable ties and a tube of superglue to their bag. "For repairs. You look like you'll need them."

“What about them dogs? You didn’t kill the pack, then?” Gary, Carl's mate, asked.

“There were eight of them, mate. We legged it.” Arthur ducked outside.

***

Arthur waited until they were half a block away before speaking. "They couldn't see you, just like Emma’s restaurant." He said to Archie. "Six people in that shop, and none of them reacted to a floating geometric shape in their stockroom."

"Perceptual filtering is active. Visibility is restricted to the candidate and designated party members."

"So, you are invisible, to everyone except us?" Donna asked.

Archie had only just begun detailing the optical refraction parameters of his interface, when Donna said, "An invisible floating cube, of course you are."

"I am not a cube," Archie corrected. "My geometric configuration is significantly more complex than a standard six-sided—"

"Invisible floating cube," Donna repeated, cutting him off without breaking stride. She remained quiet for the next block, her axe held ready as they navigated around an overturned delivery van.

"They are not going to last too long, if they don’t get out of that shop." She said softly.

"But that barricade will hold tonight. And tonight is the only problem they can solve."

Greenwich was close. Arthur could feel the pull getting stronger and sharper with each step they took.

***

They found a block of flats fifty metres from the park wall, with roof access and a heavy door securing the bottom of the stairwell. Donna cleared each landing as they climbed, pausing to listen at every door. Four flights up, they still hadn’t heard a sound.

The stairs opened into a squat brick structure that capped the building, an old water-tank room. It had one steel door and blockwork walls thick enough to stop weather and, Arthur hoped, considerably worse. A single window sat in the south wall, its glass opaque with grime but for a wiped arc someone had cleared long before the sky changed.

Donna walked it once, end to end, and gave a satisfied nod. "This is better," she said. "Four walls, one door, and an open terrace ahead. This will do.”

Arthur stepped to the window, using his sleeve to wipe away the remaining grime. Beyond it the observatory sat on its hill to the southeast. He propped the Bullguard against the wall and lowered himself to the floor, and the moment he was still, every bit of fatigue he had been outrunning arrived at once. Everything momentum had been masking, suddenly present and accounted for.

“I’m knackered.”

“This is as good a place to rest and eat as we’re likely to find,” Donna said, then glanced at Steve and corrected herself. “Eat, sleep and distribute.”

She unzipped her pack and distributed rations. Water, protein bars, and a tin of tuna opened with the blade of her multitool. They ate in the silence of people who needed fuel before they had the energy to do anything else.

Steve was the first to speak.

"Status," he said and set his empty water bottle down. His eyes stayed unfocused for a long moment then turned frustrated.

“Archie, nothing happened.”

“What are you trying to accomplish?” Archie asked.

“I want to see, you know, my character sheet, to allocate the attribute points.”

“Then focus on your Matrix. You do not need to shout a command. Apart from being useless, it makes you look deranged.”

“You are getting awfully familiar, awfully quickly.” Steve said, his eyes drifting out of focus again. Whatever he saw captured him completely. “Go on. Have a look at your status window. Focus inward with the specific intention of seeing your parameters. Best we sort this out now, before we meet another pack of mutts.”

Arthur closed his eyes and reached for the internal awareness Archie had described. And there it was. Faint translucent text, hanging in the dark behind his eyelids.

ARTHUR Ashworth

Level 2

Integration: 55 / 119

STR: 10  DEX: 7  CON: 9  ACU: 6  RES: 10

Unallocated Points: 3

“Five attributes. Strength, Dexterity, Constitution, and two I don’t recognise. ACU and RES.” Arthur said.

Steve's gaze drifted as he processed the terms. "Strength, Dexterity, and Constitution are self-explanatory. Standard D&D physical baseline. But there’s no Intelligence or Wisdom.”

His fingers tapped the guidebook. “ACU. If the system maps to biology… Acuity? Processing speed? That would track with Ether manipulation.”

He looked at his own stats. ACU 12 was the highest number in his column. “And RES is definitely not Resistance, that’s usually a defensive stat. Resonance, maybe? Ether perception?”

“Archie, is he right?” Arthur asked, looking up.

"Acuity governs Ether processing throughput," Archie confirmed. "Resonance governs Ether perception and environmental sensitivity. The candidate's companion has successfully identified both functional parameters from observable data."

Steve's face broke into an insufferable grin.

Steve read his numbers out loud without hesitation and with zero instinct for information security. STR 6, DEX 7, CON 7, ACU 12, RES 10.

Donna recited hers next. STR 8, DEX 12, CON 8, ACU 6, RES 8.

Steve pulled out his notebook and jotted all three stat blocks down in the margin, his pencil pausing over the three distinct columns.

"Look at these figures," Steve said, pointing at the page. "Not a single number matches across our profiles except for RES for Arthur and I."

"Why would they match?" Donna said.

"Because I assumed the system started everyone from the same template, a uniform baseline. Ten across the board or something like that, and then levels moved the numbers around." He tapped the page. "It didn't. There is no template. I think these numbers are what our bodies actually measured at when the system turned on. My Strength is six because I have the upper body of a man who lifts artefacts, not weights."

"And my Dexterity is twelve because I've been training reaction speed for fourteen years," Donna said, her voice carrying a faint note of validation. The satisfaction of seeing a number confirm what she'd known.

"I thought the base for a normal human was ten. The standard off-the-peg version." Arthur said.

"Ten is the threshold for an awakened human frame. The system measures your bodies as they are." Archie said, rather ominously.

***

"Three points each. The question is where they go."

Arthur had been turning the decision over since Bermondsey. "My function is to absorb impact and hold the line. Strength and Constitution. Two points into Strength, one into Constitution."

"Dexterity. Two points. The gap between my movement speed and those beasts is a gap I will eventually die in. The third point goes into Constitution. Speed's useless if I bleed out from one hit." Donna said without hesitation.

Steve was staring at his own numbers. "My Strength is six, which is a liability. But putting points into Strength is throwing good money after bad. What I can be is the reason the front line knows where to stand." He grinned. "Acuity. Two points. Plus, you know, magic."

"There's no magic, Steve."

"There is going to be magic, Donna. And I intend to be ready for it. Third point in Resonance. Better detection, better environmental awareness."

"Allocation triggers biological restructuring over approximately thirty seconds to two minutes per point. The process is not dangerous. It is merely unpleasant." Archie informed them.

"Merely," Donna said.

Arthur closed his eyes and focused on strength and constitution. One point at a time. The physical restructuring hit his system like a high energy electrical current. Every muscle fibre pulled taut and released at a slightly higher tension. His bones ached and his jaw locked as involuntary micro-spasms rippled through his back.

Archie had said thirty seconds for each point. It felt far longer.

When the sensation finally faded, his hands looked identical to how they had a minute prior. But when he made a fist, he could feel the difference, a sense of denseness that wasn't there before.

"That," Arthur said, taking a slow, steadying breath, "was bloody unpleasant."

Steve was pale from watching Arthur. "The things I do for bandwidth," he muttered.

Donna allocated hers with her teeth clenched and her eyes open, staring at a fixed point on the parapet. She didn't make a sound during any of the three restructurings. Arthur saw the veins stand out on her forearms during the second and the slight tremor in her hands when the third finished.

Steve's Acuity allocations were the worst. He locked up during the first, his body going rigid, eyes rolling back. The second was comparatively easier. The Resonance point was different; he described it as the feeling of putting on glasses for the first time. The same world, seen suddenly in higher resolution.

Arthur: STR 12, DEX 7, CON 10, ACU 6, RES 10

Donna: STR 8, DEX 14, CON 9, ACU 6, RES 8

Steve: STR 6, DEX 7, CON 7, ACU 14, RES 11

"Current party composition remains heavily biased toward physical engagement," Archie pronounced from the centre of the room. "Ranged threat response capacity is absent. The Acuity investment by Steve has been noted but will not produce combat applications until a higher threshold is reached."

Steve looked up from his notebook. "Did Archie just review our party build?"

"He reviewed your build," Donna corrected smoothly, "and designated it incomplete."

***

"Get some sleep," Donna said. She'd set herself down beside the door, angled so the wiped arc of window and the stairwell approach were both in view. "I'll take first watch. Arthur at two. Steve at four. We move at dawn."

Steve curled up against the far wall, billhook within arm's reach, guidebook open on his chest, and within minutes, his breathing had changed.

Arthur should have slept too. Instead he sat down beside Donna by the window and watched the city, a scatter of unlit blocks below in the violet dark.

"The people in the shop," he said.

"Don't."

"They reminded me of the pub. Before the sky changed. People sitting around a table, not knowing what was coming."

Donna was quiet for long enough that Arthur thought she wasn't going to respond.

"Emma's still in that restaurant." Her voice was controlled. “I told her to barricade. I told her I'd come back."

Arthur didn't say we'll find her. He didn't say she'll be okay. Those were empty platitudes that offered temporary comfort while functioning as lies.

"When we're done with Greenwich, we go back for her." He said.

"That is a promise you cannot guarantee, Arthur."

"No. But that is the heading I am setting."

Donna was quiet again. Then she shifted slightly, her shoulder touching his for a moment before she resettled. A shared weight between two people. It wasn’t much, but it was enough.

"Get some sleep, Arthur."

He closed his eyes. In the quiet of his mind, the frequency hummed steadily, and atop the hill to the southeast, the observatory waited.

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

THE OLDER READING

The numbers are not assigned. They are measured. That much is clear, and it should have been the end of it. Unfortunately it is not.

Donna's Dexterity came up as twelve. Twelve is fourteen years of reaction training rendered as an integer. For the system to have produced that number on Day Zero, it had to already hold the fourteen years somewhere.

So the measuring might be older than the waking. The Matrix was not switched on and aimed at us like a camera. It was switched to visible when the sky changed. How much older I cannot say. A day. A decade. A life. Long enough to have measured Donna’s life.

I keep reaching the same place by different roads. Marks scratched into stone for eleven thousand years. A system that speaks fluent Dungeons and Dragons because someone spent fifty years teaching us the grammar. Now attributes logged before the log was ever made legible. This is not a thing that arrived suddenly. It is a thing that was always running and has only now stopped hiding.

✎ Day Zero was not when the measuring began. It was when the results were published. The sky did not change us. It stopped pretending it had not already read us to the last digit.
