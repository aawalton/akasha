That earned a small smile from Duvas.

“I knew you would ask,” he said. “So I worked that out as well. Since the paper and medicine sales are being kept hidden, we only have to pay tax on the remaining revenue. Without those off-the-books sales, our monthly declared revenue is only 615 gold, compared to around 500 gold last year. Still, the tax is going to be high because the rate is now at 25%, so for the latest month that comes to around 154 gold.”

He turned another page.

“We also had zero revenue through the winter months because the northern road was blocked, so that helps. Somewhat. I can only estimate the full tax demand for now, but if revenue stays near the current level, I think the total tribute to the Count by the end of autumn will be around 1,400 gold. If we start selling more coal to Ulriga the way we plan, and since that cannot really be hidden, then the tax demand will rise closer to 1,800 gold for the current harvest year." He looked back up. “So assuming the count does not get greedy and raise the rate again, that is what we need to save by autumn.”

Kivamus clenched his fists. “That is insane... I really, really want to tell the count to fuck off! That's a hell of a lot of gold, and you are right in calling it a tribute. That's exactly what it is. We only have to pay this just because they are stronger than us... They do absolutely nothing for us in return!”

Duvas gave him a tired look. “As powerful as Tiranat has become compared to last autumn, we are still nothing compared to Cinran, who can call upon 300 to 400 well-trained guards if the Count asks for help against us from his other barons, as well as the dozens of knights they employ. That doesn't even include what your brother might send the Count as help from Fort Aragosa if needed. So we don’t have a choice but to keep paying. At least the wheat harvest should arrive a month or so before then. That should lower our expenses for a few months and will help us to save more to pay the tax. Uh... the tribute.”

“It won't do that much, but yeah,” Kivamus grimaced, “it still helps."

He thought for a moment, then nodded. “It is still a huge sum, but we have around 5 months before the tax collector arrives at the end of autumn. Assuming we use the remaining 900 gold of savings for this, then we only need to gather another 900 gold to pay that official tribute to those bloodsuckers.”

He did some calculations in his mind.

“Say it takes us 2 months to become profitable. Then we just need to save an extra 225 gold a month after that. Hmm... That is more than doable. If we raise medicine sales by 50% like we plan to, that alone should give us around 300 gold of extra profit each month. Anything from soap or paper would be on top of that, as well as whatever we can earn by increasing coal production further above the 90 wagon loads we estimate in two months.”

His mouth tightened into a grim little smile. “So by my reckoning, we should be able to pay the tax in full and still keep a little in reserve. Not much, but something."

Then another thought occurred to him. “That said,” he went on, “won’t Ustaimo start asking questions once he sees how many people we are paying?”

Duvas shook his head. “Even as the count’s official tax collector, he only has the right to inspect our revenue ledger. If he is suspicious, he can ask to see receipts to confirm the income, not that visiting traders always provide those in this remote region. But how a baron chooses to spend his money is his own concern, as long as he keeps paying the tax. That means we do not have to show Ustaimo the expense ledger. A baron may be paying his workers properly, or he may have made all of them slaves and might not be providing them anything more than the minimum amount of food and keeping the rest for himself. It's his own choice. Either way, our expenses are not going to be in the open.”

He allowed himself a thin smile. “Of course, Ustaimo is not a fool. One look at the village and all the changes compared to the last year, and he will probably guess what is happening. But I think we can persuade him to look the other way.”

Kivamus nodded. “You’re right. Ustaimo will surely notice the larger population in the autumn even if nothing else gives it away. But it’s not as if he will have the time to do a census of the village by himself. He will just have to trust our figures. And if needed, we can provide him some incentive to ignore what we need him to ignore. That brat, Sir Tuilas, is going to cause trouble again, especially if he notices something is amiss, but we'll deal with him when the time comes.”

"Alright, so let's leave that for later." Duvas lowered the ledger a little. “That leaves one last matter,” he said. “It's only a small thing in the larger picture—as you like to say—but still something we should settle now. Do you also want to pay the bonus now to those who earned medals?"

Kivamus nodded at once. “That is the most important thing,” he said. “Ridono gave his life for the village. Nesser was burned badly. Others were seriously injured too. And we cannot ignore Syrene’s work in discovering the process for the medicine, or that servant who suggested using the wild inedible root in the paper pulp. Those are exactly the kinds of things we need to reward. That is what keeps people working harder, and keeps our village safe, while also growing our revenue from new discoveries. So how much will it be?”

Duvas looked down at his ledger.

“We had decided that those who had gotten injured and were receiving the Baron’s Medal of Sacrifice would get the equivalent of 1 month of pay; those receiving the Medal of Courage would get 3 months of pay for extraordinary bravery; and the dependents of those who lost their life in the service of the village are were receiving the Medal of Valor would get 1 year of pay as bonus. We never settled on an amount for the Medal of Science, since only Syrene and that servant have received it so far.”

“Then let’s say 3 months of pay for that as well,” Kivamus said. “How much does that come to?”

Duvas glanced up. “You do realize the amount changes depending on what they were already earning, don't you?”

Kivamus frowned. “Wait, what? That doesn't seem right. That would mean people get different rewards for the same injury or discovery just because one of them earned more beforehand. And a junior guard would get less bonus for risking his life than a senior guard who took the same risk. That is not a good idea. It will feel like discrimination.”

He held out a hand. “Give me the estimates first.”

Duvas checked the figures again. “The Medal of Sacrifice would come to around 3.3 gold for the new guards. The Medal of Courage is around 10 gold each. The Medal of Valor is 34 gold for Ridono. For the Medal of Science, Syrene’s bonus would come to 22 gold, while the servant's bonus would only be around 8 gold.”

Kivamus thought for a few moments. “Then we make the amounts fixed,” he said at last. “No matter what they were earning before.”

Duvas straightened slightly, waiting for his order.

“Let's make it 3 gold for the Medal of Sacrifice, 10 gold for the Medal of Courage, and 30 gold for the Medal of Valor,” Kivamus said. “As for the Medal of Science, Syrene and that servant are on opposite ends of the wage scale. So we take the middle. Make it 15 gold. That will serve as a good reward at any wage level.”

Duvas wrote quickly in the ledger, then checked the total.

“In that case,” he said, “the full amount for all medals comes to 96 gold.”

Kivamus winced. “That is a lot. But these are some of the most valuable people in the village. We'll do it.”

He looked toward the doorway, thinking ahead. “Let's present the bonus when you begin paying wages in coins for the first time. Let me know when the arrangements are ready. I will personally hand out the rewards to the medal recipients, and then you can distribute the regular wages after that.”

Then he remembered something and gave a short smile. “By the way, Syrene already said she doesn’t really need the money from her wage right now. Earlier she was even talking about refusing any coin to help the village. She will almost certainly say the same about the reward.”

Duvas gave him a questioning look.

“So I suggested something else,” Kivamus said. “I told her she could deposit the amount back into the manor's treasury, and we would pay her a small interest on it. Then she could withdraw it whenever she needed it.”

Duvas stared at him for a moment. “Is this about that bank idea you mentioned in the past?”

“Yeah,” Kivamus said. “I’m sure many of the guards who receive these rewards will do the same once they hear about it. Most of their needs are already provided for, and those who don't have any family to take care of won't even know what to do with it other than to spend it in the alehouse. Everyone wants something put away for a bad day. If they can earn a little extra just by keeping it with us and doing nothing for it, most of them will take that option. They won't even need to worry about someone else stealing their coin either, since our strong box is the safest place in the village.”

He leaned back slightly in his chair. “So in a way, much of this bonus money will come back to us anyway. We can use it to help pay taxes and cover other expenses in the coming months, while they still earn the interest they deserve. It will just need you to do a little more accounting work, and the withdrawals will be rare enough that it wouldn't cause us any problems. I think some of them might want to take out their money to start paying the rent and live in a proper room once the tenement block is ready, but again, it will come back to us. Anyway, once word spreads, some of the servants and maids might start doing the same thing with part of their normal wages too.”

Duvas listened without interrupting.

“We’ll keep this limited to those living at the manor for now,” Kivamus said. “Later, when our finances are steadier, we can expand it to the whole village as a proper bank.”

Duvas gave a slow nod. “Alright. Then I will start a new banking ledger for anyone who deposits money with us.”
