Chapter 34

“I watched a man I have known for twenty years choose to stand up inside a cage that was breaking him. I don’t know what I was supposed to learn from it. I know what I learned.” - The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr, Marseille

As Arthur dropped, the cage turned opaque. Dense blue Ether, solid as stone, and Donna screamed again because she couldn’t see him.

“The evaluation is complete.” Archie’s geometry was brighter than it had ever been, the rotation fast and uneven. “He will be functional.”

“Functional?” Donna turned on Archie with a look that should have cracked him. “He was breaking apart in there. Both his knees, his arm, his eyes. What kind of evaluation does that?”

“The kind that determines whether the candidate will hold when the load exceeds his specifications,” Archie said. “He held.”

Inside the opaque cage, Ether flooded Arthur’s body. It entered through his skin and his lungs and the channels of his Nadi Matrix and it renewed. Ligaments reconnected. Cartilage rebuilt. The bones in his elbow realigned and fused. His eardrums sealed. The burst vessels in his eyes closed and his vision came back in stages, red to pink to something approaching normal.

The process took perhaps five minutes of real time. Arthur would not have been able to confirm this, because inside the cage the duration had stretched into something he could not measure.

Then the cage dropped and Donna found Arthur on the crypt floor, panting. His eyes were open and blank, staring at the vault ceiling. Donna was beside him in a heartbeat, her hands on his shoulders, and Steve was on his other side, and both of them were looking at him with expressions that said they’d just watched someone die and come back.

“I’m all right,” Arthur said. His voice sounded strange in ears that had been deaf. He gingerly tested his elbows and knees and sat up. Everything seemed to be moving as intended.

“You are structurally intact,” Archie confirmed. “The evaluation’s restorative protocol has returned your musculoskeletal system to pre-evaluation baseline.”

Donna sat back on her heels. The operational flatness was gone and what was underneath it was visible for a little while before she put the wall back up. She looked at Archie. “If you ever let him walk into something like that again without warning us, I will find out what part of your geometry is load-bearing and I will remove it.”

“I am uncertain which component of that threat is meant to be persuasive,” Archie said. “My geometry has no load-bearing components. Your concern is noted regardless.”

Steve let out a breath that sounded like it had been held for the entire evaluation. “Arthur. Mate.” He put a hand on Arthur’s shoulder. “I want it noted for the record that my official position, going forward, is that we do not touch anything in a crypt without Archie saying the word ‘safe’ first. Twice. In writing.”

Arthur tried to laugh and it came out as a cough.

His brain wanted to catalogue his renewal. Understand it. But that would have to wait. The floor was speaking.

***

The map covered the full width of the crypt’s exposed floor.

Lines of light connected points across what Arthur recognised as coastline. The Mediterranean. He could trace the shape of it, southern Europe curving down to North Africa, the boot of Italy, the scattered islands. Connecting everything was a web of luminous threads meeting at fixed points. Dozens of them. Scores. Each intersection pulsing with its own light, some bright, some faint, some dark entirely.

One cluster, their cluster, Marseille, pulsed strong and steady. Three points close together. Saint-Victor, the brightest. And two dimmer nodes nearby.

Beyond Marseille, the web spread in every direction in a complex network of overlapping and diverging threads. This was infrastructure. A system designed to cover an entire sea.

Steve made a sound of pure awe.

“The Grid,” he whispered. “The actual Grid. The regional network.” He was on his knees, hands hovering above the glowing lines. “There must be, how many nodes? Fifty? A hundred? Across the entire Mediterranean basin. Every major archaeological site, every ancient monument, every place historians have ever argued about. They’re all connected.”

Archie’s geometry brightened at the edges. “You are the first candidate on this planet to see this map. Three hundred and fourteen generations of Curators have maintained it without being permitted to look at what they were maintaining.” His rotation slowed. “This is the first time it has been lit.”

Arthur stopped trying to count the nodes after fifty. Whatever they were doing in this crypt was one small piece of something that spanned continents.

“We’re a footnote,” Donna said quietly. She’d come to stand beside Steve, the axe finally lowered. “Three people in one crypt and this thing covers the entire Mediterranean.”

“We’re the three people who just turned the lights on,” Steve said. “That’s not a footnote. That’s the beginning.”

***

The riddle appeared once the last map line had stabilized.

New letters formed in the tiles around the Saint-Victor node. Latin this time, not the Koine Greek of the transit chamber. The words wrote themselves into the stone one by one, Ether light tracing each character.

Steve read them aloud, his Latin steady and precise.

Leave the martyr’s stone for the limestone height.

Seek the Gilded Mother in her crown of light.

Touch the brass-bound star where the sea-winds blow,

To wake the high beacon for the world below.

He sat back and was quiet for a moment. Undoubtedly pulling references. Then the engine engaged.

“The martyr’s stone. That’s here. Saint-Victor. Leave this node and go to a limestone height.”

“How many limestone heights does Marseille have?” Arthur asked.

“The city’s built on limestone. Hills everywhere.” Steve frowned. “But the limestone height, the defining one, is probably Notre-Dame de la Garde. Highest point in the city. You can see it from anywhere.”

“Probably,” Donna said. The word carried weight. They’d learned in London what ‘probably’ cost when it was wrong.

“Gilded Mother could be the golden Madonna on top of the basilica,” Steve continued, chewing the words. “It is the most famous landmark. But it could also refer to any number of Madonna statues across southern France. I won’t commit without seeing the site.”

“And the brass-bound star?” Arthur asked.

“Something brass at the location. A fixture, a marking, an object. I’m not sure.” He paused. “And ‘where the sea-winds blow’ is either a direction or a feature related to wind. Marseille has the Mistral. I don’t know how that connects to brass yet.” He looked at the riddle text still glowing in the tiles. “I’m at about sixty percent. I need to get up that hill and see what’s there before I can finish this.”

Arthur nodded. Sixty percent and honest about it was better than a hundred percent and wrong.

***

Steve noticed the mosaic while Arthur tested his rebuilt knees against a slow walk across the chamber.

He’d been studying the construction above the map surface, running his fingers above the tile work and comparing craftsmanship to photographs in the Goldsmiths survey, when he stopped.

“This is Curator work,” he said, the certainty already in his voice before he’d built the case for it. “The mosaic is Hellenistic on the surface. Alexandrian school, beautiful technique. But the knowledge inside it, the Grid map, the node positions, the navigational logic: that’s not something a second-century mosaicist figured out on his own.”

He stopped and stared for a second before continuing “Someone told him what to build. Someone who understood the entire network. The same people who told the cathedral architects where to put the windows and the Templars where to build their round churches.”

He looked at Arthur. “The Curators. Everywhere we go, London, Marseille, there’s a layer of human construction guided by someone who understood the Grid. Thousands of years of it. An organisation older than writing, older than cities.”

He turned to Archie. The floating geometry hovered at the edge of the mosaic’s glow, its rotation steady and slow.

“Archie. The Curators. Who are they? How does a secret order survive for thousands of years?”

Archie’s geometry went still. The locked-partition silence that meant the answer existed but the authorisation to share it didn’t.

“Authorisation insufficient.”

Steve stared at him. “We’re walking through their infrastructure, solving their puzzles, following their trail, and we can’t know who they are?”

“The candidate’s current progression tier does not permit disclosure of Curator organisational architecture.”

“Organisational architecture.” Steve repeated it slowly. “A thirty-thousand-year-old secret society has org charts.”

“Add it to the list, Steve.” Donna was already at the entrance, the axe in her hand and her eyes on the stairs. “The list of things Archie won’t tell us is longer than the list of things he will. We keep moving.”

She was right. But Arthur also knew Steve wouldn’t let this drop for long. The temptation to peek behind the curtain was too much. The promise of something vast visible through a gap of a partially excavated wall.

Arthur stood. “We know enough,” he said. “We know the next riddle. We know the Curators built the road we’re walking on, even if we don’t know their names. That’s enough for today.”

Steve didn’t argue. But he stood at the mosaic’s edge for a long moment, looking down at the web of light still glowing in the tiles. The network would fade when they left. The knowledge of it wouldn’t.

He picked up his Reacher and followed Arthur toward the stairs, unaware that an old acquaintance waited along the path of Notre-Dame.

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

Marseille

THE REGIONAL NETWORK

The anchor at the base of the Black Madonna is a crux dissimulata. Third century, Roman persecution period. Early Christians used the shape as a disguised cross before the real one was safe to display. Fourteen centuries of pilgrims have lit candles at the feet of the Lady in Black without knowing what was carved into the stone beneath her. None of them knew. That is the part I keep coming back to. The Curators did.

They built the Christian layer over a Roman martyr’s tomb over a Hellenistic necropolis over whatever the Seeders laid down first. Five civilisations stacked on the same point. Each one of them certain they were the first to find something sacred here. Each one of them a cover story for the people underneath. The mosaic is Alexandrian school but the knowledge inside it is not. Someone with a map of the entire Mediterranean basin told a second-century mosaicist where to put every tile. The same someone told the Templars where to build round naves. The same someone told medieval cathedral architects where to seat the rose windows. The human hands are different every time. The geometry is the same. Archie went quiet when I asked who they are. Not his ordinary quiet. The kind of quiet that has a door behind it.

✎ All the nodes are not lit. The map showed them to us because we turned them on. I wonder if someone else - somewhere - is also meant to be doing this.
