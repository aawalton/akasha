Eola stared at the sheet of parchment on her desk, then back at her familiar. Then she lunged for the cat, but Atta, despite her sleepy appearance, was faster. She leaped to the desk, arched her back, and hissed.

“This is…what do I even do, Atta!? This is like destroying a holy relic!”

“You relax, sleep it off, find something to eat—that’s what I’d do.” Atta hissed one more time for good measure, then curled up against the wooden wall. “Once you’ve gotten some rest, you’ll realize that the diary in question was from before Varin became someone important. If it wasn’t, it’d be on the first floor, behind crystal glass and chromite locks, being admired by every traveling sightseer who stopped in Varin’s Town for the night.”

“That doesn’t matter. It was the Great Mage’s, and I destroyed it.” Eola sat back down, head in hands and fingers woven in her black hair, and peered at the cat-slimed parchment.

“Technically, you didn’t.”

“What do you mean?”

“I mean,” Atta said, “that the monster did, not you.”

Eola growled in annoyance.

“But I do see your point. You feel responsible for destroying a two-and-a-half-thousand-year-old book belonging to someone most people agree is responsible for saving the world—the second-most important figure in history, maybe, behind Y’aer. Looks like there’s only one thing to do, then. You need to put it back together.”

The words on the page blurred, and Eola closed her eyes against tears of frustration. “That’s not realistic.”

“It doesn’t matter if it’s not realistic, Eola. You were somewhere you shouldn’t have been. You weren’t skilled or strong enough to be there without help. Now, you feel like it’s your fault the diary was destroyed. So, whether it’s realistic right now or not, you have a mission. Go back in there, defeat the feral book, and restore Varin’s apprenticehood diary. Now, finish reading that thing. Tomorrow, we’re getting to work.

“And for what it’s worth, I believe you can do it.”

Eola’s eyes snapped open, and she lowered her hands. Her eyes returned to the first line of the diary.

This being the diary of me, Varin, apprentice to Archmage Frelanne, I record my thoughts for my future self here.

I have been apprenticed to Frelanne for two months, and she’s encouraged me to begin documenting my runes and marks in this book. Frelan’s a great spellcrafter. Her runes and marks are leagues better than what I can hope to achieve. However, she’s a harsh mistress to learn from. I don’t have the strength of soul to do what she’s asking of me, so most of our training time is focused on strengthening it. Honestly, I doubt anyone has the strength of soul to keep up with her. She’s been training for two hundred years or something.

One thing, though. I’ve noticed she always, always uses the same mark when she casts. I’ve been spying watching when she casts, and I think I’ve got the mark itself figured out. I just wish I knew what it did.

Eola turned the page over, hoping against hope that the young apprentice Varin had recorded the modifying mark for her. Then she pumped a fist in the air. “Yes!”

“What?” Colin asked from the door, eyebrows furrowed in confusion.

“Nothing!” Eola said. She covered the yellowed, faded page with her hand. It wasn’t all there—time had faded the sketch to almost nothing—but enough was. “I was just working on some homework from Instructor Tarik’s independent study.”

“Alright. Good luck with that. I’m heading to bed, but if you need anything, just bang on my door. I’ll be there.”

“What?” Eola asked, echoing Colin’s confused look.

He fidgeted awkwardly and looked at the floor. “I mean, because you’re still recovering. Instructor Clearance asked me to keep an eye on you, since we’re suite mates and she’s teaching me a little about rituals on the side.”

“Oh. Thank you, Colin.”

“Of course, Eola. Night.”

“Night,” Eola said, eyes flicking back to the page.

The symbol was in Forsooth Script. From what she could see, it was also disgustingly simple—a single word, written in a single loop. But that simplicity was only surface level; Eola couldn’t figure out what it did any more than Varin had, apparently, been able to. If she had more of his notes, they’d probably explain it, but as it was…

She glanced up, yawning. Atta’s golden eyes were locked on the page. She watched her familiar for almost fifteen seconds before the cat tore her gaze away from the mark and shook her head. “That’s what I was afraid of. Not enough to go on. We’ll just have to put this aside until we’re ready for the library’s third floor. Luckily, time is on our side. Get some rest. You’re back to your normal schedule tomorrow.”

Eola shook her head slowly and pulled the first piece of parchment from the large stack Colin had haphazardly propped on the side of her desk. It was from Mana Studies, asking for a full page, pen-written, on formal applications for Mana lines.

“Y’aer save me,” Eola said, reaching for her pen and getting to work. The sooner the homework was done, the sooner she could focus on her own goals.

The mountain of homework turned out to be more than a one-night project, and it was a couple of days before Eola found the time to meet with Maestro Yarrowbloom. The elf’s class had changed for the better while she’d been out, and their combat drills were fast-paced, brutal affairs with training swords and a limited spell pool to choose from—but no restrictions on how those spells were used. Eola stood on one end of the practice pitch, breathing hard. On the far side, Catrine pulled off her practice helmet. The two girls had been going at it for almost three minutes, with little to show for it but a few bruises each.

Eola’s helmet was still on. She scowled under the mesh basket; Catrine had gotten a lot better at swordplay. Either that, or—more likely—Eola had gotten out of shape after a week in bed. She unbuckled her own helmet and wiped sweat off her brow.

To her surprise, the noble girl was smiling—and also to her surprise, Catrine looked genuinely happy. Eola’s scowl deepened. “What?”

“Oh, I’m just thinking about what happens when Maestro Yarrowface unbars all the spells, toddler,” Catrine said. Eola winced, but something seemed different about Catrine’s tone. “It’ll be a lot of fun to push my Journeymage soul to the limit. You’ll be my sparring partner for that, won’t you?”

Catrine’s purple-red owl sat on a nearby chair’s back, staring with wide eyes. Atta didn’t make an appearance in Magical Dueling, and Eola felt a little tingle on the back of her neck as the bird familiar’s eyes bored into her. She nodded. “If you want. I’m pretty good with the sword.”

“I’m sure you are, but House Andrese’s attunement’s a real show-stopper. Now that I think about it, you don’t have what it takes, toddler. I’ll stick with Garreth.”

One by one, the other students pulled off their padded gear and filed out until it was just Catrine, Eola, and the professor. Eola glared at Catrine, who glared back. After almost two minutes, Catrine broke. “Maestro, normal office hours, right?”

“Correct, Miss Andrese.”

“Great. Thanks. I’ll be around.” The noble girl left, and Eola breathed a sigh of relief.

It was short-lived.

“Miss Lemiene, if you have questions, they’re best suited for my office—and they’d better not be about Steel Snare or any other ritual magic. You’re far too talented at the dance for that drivel.” Maestro Yarrowbloom started following Catrine out the door.

“Wait, sir,” Eola said. “I was hoping—“

“For private lessons?” the elf asked. “No.”

“No. I’ve got a question about the Taron-Li. What do you do when—“

The professor sighed and sat in the same chair Catrine’s owl had been perched on. “Instructor Clearance sent a letter out to all of your instructors. She told us that if you asked, the answer was no.”

“What do you do when enemies can’t be beaten with a smallsword, though?”

“This is second-year stuff, for Spellsword Tactics students, Miss Lemiene. You’re still wearing your gear, though. That’s a good sign. Get that helmet on,” Maestro Yarrowbloom grinned suddenly—and ferally. Eola second-guessed herself, but a practice sword appeared in his hand almost as if by magic; he held it out like a piece of chalk. “You’re going to need it.”

Eola tried not to wince as she pulled the practice helmet back over her sweat-matted hair. She dropped into First Guard.

“Wrong. You’re dead.” Maestro Yarrowbloom’s sword—no, his entire body—was a shadowy mass of smoke. The wooden blade passed across Eola’s neck as she tried—and failed—to block it. “You’re trying to fight something that you can’t hurt with a sword. It stands to reason you can’t block its attacks, either.”

“Not well, at least,” Eola said.

“The Taron-Li doesn’t have a solution to enemies like me. There’s not a move in the dance that beats incorporeal enemies or ones with no anatomy to hurt. But your spell selection can matter. Let me see your sword.”

Before Eola could react, the swordmaster plucked the training blade from her hand and held in in front of him. “Get your spellbook and pay attention. You’re going to use Bright Ball, but you’ll apply a new modifying mark to it. This is Imbued. It’s used to apply spells to objects. Bright Ball’s the least dangerous option for you to practice with, so we’ll start there.” The elf’s delicate fingers moved across the blade, tracing the mark directly onto the wood. It faded into nothing. Then he repeated the action, this time with the Bright Ball rune added first and the modifying mark adjusting it.

Eola took the practice sword from him. Then she dropped into First Guard again.

“Still wrong. You’re dead.” The shadow sword lashed out across her throat again before she could react, and Maestro Yarrowbloom laughed kindly. “Try starting in Fourth Guard instead. The different Guards are useful against different enemies, and I’m attacking the same way every time.”

They sparred for all of three more minutes—and Eola lost every time—but by the end of it, she’d picked up a few new ideas. None of them would beat Eddarn in a fight, to say nothing of the feral book, but they were starting points.

Then, Maestro Yarrowbloom tucked his practice sword away. “Get changed. I’ve got another class in five minutes, and you need to be gone when they come. Otherwise, I’ll have to explain to a whole gaggle of first-years why you get special lessons and they don’t, and Y’aer knows I don’t have that kind of time. And don’t try imbuing outside of practice with Bright Ball until next year. We’ll use it more in Spellsword Tactics, but it can be dangerous for you and everyone around you.”

Eola’s experience with the third floor—coupled with Tarik’s warning about it—were enough to take this one seriously. She could learn from her mistakes.

Especially the painful ones.

As soon as office hours started, Eola bundled up and stepped out into a small grove of yew.

Instructor Clearance could have met her anywhere, yes—the hamadryad’s saplings and trees were all over the campus, leaves long shed against the long northern winter. But she insisted on holding office hours in her home grove, near her heart tree. That meant extra stockings, a second wool shirt, and gloves under the thick mittens Eola had finally broken out as the midterm exams and Y’aer’s Day approached.

She didn’t expect the Introduction to Monsters professor to be quite as combative as Maestro Yarrowbloom, after all, and she’d prefer to be warm.

So, she stood in the center of the grove, waiting, and after a minute or two, the dryad stepped out of a tree. She wore nothing, as usual, but her caryatid wooden body didn’t feel obscene. If anything, Eola was jealous that Instructor Clearance didn’t seem to feel the biting wind.

“Miss Lemiene, what brings you here this evening? Most of my students know that attending office hours is a mistake, and they get their questions in after class.”

Eola took a deep breath, then let it out. It hung mid-air and crystallized against her scarf. “I’m here to trade information.”

“A trade?” A wooden eyebrow raised, and Instructor Clearance stepped closer, feet leaving root marks in the snow.

“Yes. I want to tell you everything that happened on the third floor. In return, I want to know what you can tell me about feral books.”

“Agreed. One question, though—do you intend to return to the third floor?”

Eola shook her head. “Not for a long time. I’ve got a lot to learn before I’m ready for that.”

“And, I’d hope, a few friends to bring with you next time.” Instructor Clearance seemed to relax. “Now, feral tomes. I covered them during the second floor lessons. However, you’re not looking for solutions like weeding to keep them from forming or fire to destroy them, are you?”

“No.” Eola explained, in brief, everything that had happened to her on the library’s third floor, up to the point where she arrived in the octagonal reading room. She was only interrupted once.

“You ran into the Scholar's Cult? Unlucky girl,” the professor said. “They’ll have your description now. If you have to go back to the third floor anytime soon, be careful—in fact, be careful on the second floor, too. The librarians usually keep the third floor’s gate warded so nothing gets by, but those cultists know more than they let on, and it’s not unheard of for them to, however briefly, get through.”

“Right.” Eola launched back into her explanation, detailing her retreat up the stairs and the mythologies and histories. Then she stopped for a breath. “I took a look at a journal, but before I could get a good look, something attacked.”

“A feral tome?” Instructor Clearance asked.

“I think so. It was taller than me—taller than you, too—and made from paper. It had the same kind of whirlwind shape as the one Colin and I fought on the second floor.”

“That seems like a feral tome with a wind attunement.”

“Right. But it used an empty cover as a head, and it had folded pages into two massive sets of claws. It used those to attack and to fly.”

“Hmmm.” Instructor Clearance went quiet for a moment. “It could be an older tome. They grow into more defined shapes as they—“

“And it had no Mana vines and a glowing purple core,” Eola finished.

The hamadryad raised a bark eyebrow. “You’re certain? Did you make out any runes in the core?”

“No. At least, I don’t think so.” Eola closed her eyes and thought back to the monster. “I was a little busy trying to fight it, then trying to run away, though. I do know it didn’t follow me when I fell through the barriers, though. I was hoping that was my spell, but…All I cast was Bright Ball, so it was probably the barriers.”

“Most likely.” Instructor Clearance wasn’t paying much attention anymore, and Eola balled a fist inside its mitten. It was so frustrating to be here for information and have the professor lose focus. “That description doesn’t match any of the feral books I’ve heard about, from any of the first three floors. They almost always maintain Mana vine connections to the shelves around them, and the core is suspicious as well.”

“What do you mean?” Eola asked.

“Whatever it is you found, it’s most likely not a feral book.” Instructor Clearance turned and started walking back to her tree. “Thank you, Miss Lemiene. You were very helpful, and I’d love to stay and discuss the Scholar's Cult more, since that’s something I can help you with. However, what you’re talking about is something I haven’t seen in my bestiaries, and I’ll need to talk to the librarians about what they think. Good luck with your studies, and stay out of the third floor.”

Before Eola could protest, the hamadryad disappeared into a yew, leaving her alone in the twilit grove. She rubbed her hands together for a moment, trying to decide what to do next. Then she kicked at the snow. “That was a waste of time.”

Part of her wanted to return to the library as soon as she could. She needed more information—and more modifying marks—if she wanted to get good enough at magic to explore the third floor and recover Varin’s apprenticehood journal. That journal held secrets no one had seen in Y’aer knew how long, and they might help her save her parents.

In the end, though, she retreated to her room.

The midterm exams were approaching, and Eola needed to pass them if she wanted to stay at Varin’s Academy long enough to see the journal finished.
