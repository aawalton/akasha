Combat training passed efficiently. My mind was getting far better at adapting to new routines, and of turning monotonous tasks into a blur.

Mornings were group sparring practice. In the afternoons, Moonhand summoned us individually to check how our combat skills were progressing. He would beat us up, give tips, beat us up again, then congratulate us for surviving with a ‘Hm.’

On the third week, however, the Drillmaster marched down the center of the mess hall one morning. He hadn’t made an announcement in weeks, so it had to be important. When he reached the plinth at the end of the mess hall, he said, “Greetings, all.” His voice had an unusually somber tone.

I glanced at my squad. They all shrugged. Then, I glanced at Yesman’s squad, who sat beside us on the benches. A few of them shrugged as well.

“It is with great displeasure that I tell you this: Squirrel has died.”

I narrowed my eyes, then searched around the hall for Habit’s squad.

Sure enough, there were only three Dupes left in his squad. Habit looked down at his breakfast plate, pushing around the infused food lazily with his knife. Lark, another Dupe in the squad, shook his head. I looked away.

“We found him in the fields yesterday evening, having over-exposed himself to Archerbane venom. He was continually attempting to push his attributes even higher than he could, and he consumed nearly thrice the lethal dose for someone with as much Vitality as he had.

“I am here to remind you to exercise caution. You are not invincible, but you are immensely valuable to our nation. To lose you at this stage would be an immense waste of resources.”

I inhaled sharply. Squirrel, just…gone? Why had he done that? Was he trying to push his mental stats even farther?

I swung my legs over the bench, then inched closer to Yesman’s squad. “Why would he do that? Was it…it wasn’t suicide, was it?”

“Unlikely,” Yesman said. “I heard him talking the other day, something about how he wanted to try to catch up, that he felt like he was holding his squad back. He wanted to follow your example, he said, and push himself even harder and faster.”

My stomach plummeted. “I didn’t say anything to him—”

“I know you didn’t,” Yesman replied. “No one blames you, Levi.”

I stared down at the ground. “Maybe…but I should’ve noticed. I shouldn’t have beat him so quickly in sparring, or—”

“You couldn’t have known, dustbrain,” Sage said. “It is not your responsibility to make sure that everyone else is advancing properly…”

Owl had been assigned to assist a group of Dupes from the 107th Battalion. They were spread thin through multiple forts—or burhs, as the locals called them—watching the front lines, and responding quickly to orc raids as they tried to cross the Thelea River.

Tonight, he patrolled the ramparts of his current fort, two storeys off the ground, watching the empty fields.

Almost every night, there had been an attack. Some sort of tiny raiding party. Maybe the orcs were hoping to get lucky, or maybe they were hoping to lull the defences into submission.

Or maybe they were just trying to tire out the defenders.

Owl patrolled the rampart, holding a spear in one hand and a torch in the other. Every few seconds, he glanced to the southern horizon, where a different fort stood. Its guards waved torches now and again, signalling that they were still active.

As he paced, he yawned, and his eyes glazed over. He hadn’t had a proper sleep in weeks, ever since he’d been deployed out here. He’d killed more orcs than he could count, and no matter how strong he was, he could still get tired.

It was like Vaelheall Week all over again.

When he reached the farthest east point of the fort, he stopped, leaning against the wall’s wooden merlon, and he stared blankly toward the Bane-lands. The air was too hazy to see it or the river properly, but it was close by.

In the silence, his mind drifted. How were his recruits doing? They had to be exhausted. Their combat trainers would be pushing them harder than ever. What about Levi? Was he still as headstrong as ever? What about Yesman or Habit? What about Squirrel? It took an impressive amount of will to survive Vaelheall week and still see yourself as your own man, especially for a Dupe.

Gaining consciousness at eighteen. Forced to sleep fifty to a room, yelled at for months on end in Homecamp. Broken down early. But most Dupes’ souls survived that. When they were deployed, they found brotherhood.

Still, they deployed somewhere they didn’t recognize. A shield and spear thrust into their hands. It is your duty. It is what your country demands.

They gave you life. Return the favour.

Just as the Eyes of Welkinmere birthed the world, the fleshknitters and the Warlord birthed you. You owe them everything.

And to them, you are just a number.

When the Dupes deployed, they didn’t know any of the non-Dupes they were protecting, and it wasn’t easy to make friends. Sure, there was the promise of freedom and land, a light at the end of the tunnel if you survived.

It didn’t make it any less chaotic. There was always a din of swords clanging or the palisade walls being repaired. Hatchets striking wood, or fires crackling.

Orcs are coming. Any day, they will be here.

You must be ready. You must sacrifice.

Owl stared off into the distance. He thought those days were behind him, but apparently not. Every day, their numbers dwindled. The night always devoured one or two of them.

It was natural for the Dupes to hate their creators at first. That left them soon enough during their training, until most of them were at least indifferent to their plight, or understanding of it.

After all, they had souls. They could understand why they existed, even if they didn’t like it.

When it came to sapper training, it would take tremendous will to pull through and still believe you belonged to yourself. Even many years after Owl’s own Vaelheall week, he still knew his place in the world. Besides, the alternative was far worse. The alternative was the destruction of the world of men.

He blew a long breath out into the frosty air, watching it turn to steam. As he watched, though, a rumble passed through the floor. Something thumped in the distance.

Owl thrust his torch into a snowdrift that had wisped up against the merlons, putting it out with a hiss of steam. It made it difficult to see, with the light gathering close to his head. His enhanced Perception was strong enough to not require torchlight.

He gazed as far as he could, looking into the misty fog that swarmed near the horizon. The ridge ahead of them trembled. Distant humanoid forms surged over the hill like ants. He couldn’t count all of them, and he didn’t want to.

They were heading straight toward the fort.

Pulling off his gloves, he put his fingers in his mouth and whistled as loud as he could, creating a shrill sound to wake everyone within the fort. Then he retrieved the horn at his hip and sounded it, sending a blast of noise across the land. It was loud enough that the other forts would hear it.

In seconds, the fort buzzed with activity. Dupes ran up to the ramparts, their gear clattering. They stuck with their sergeants as they took positions on the walls.

“Loose arrows at your discretion,” Owl ordered. “Wait for your sergeants’ calls. Lieutenants, you have command on the ground.”

He pulled his gloves on, then pulled his shield off his back. A single use of his Perception Skill illuminated the ridge, allowing him to see the nearest orcs clearly and sense their weight. The ridge lit up with golden light. There were hundreds of them out tonight.

The forts were meant to handle it, though.

“They’re within range of the trebuchet!” Owl called. “Fire!”

Dupes held a torch to the side of the catapult payload, lighting the oiled outside of the ceramic ball with flame. With a leathery groan, the counterweight dropped, and the fort’s only trebuchet fired, launching its payload over the walls.

The ball slammed into the grass and shattered on impact, slathering orcs in burning oil. They screeched and clawed at themselves.

In the distance, the other forts launched their payloads as well, sending flaming flares streaking through the night.

Owl turned to a regular lieutenant and said, “Send a man to Kelcott. Evacuate the town.”

“Where will they go?” the lieutenant asked.

“Anywhere. We will buy them time. Go—”

Before Owl could finish, a pressure weighed down from in front of him. He rushed back to the front of the ramparts, squinting into the glow of the torchlight and the burning streaks the catapult payloads left in the fields.

He spotted an ogre immediately, and as soon as he had his chance, he lobbed his spear at it. It impaled the ogre’s eye, sending it tumbling back.

The pressure still remained.

“Owl!” a voice called from the ground. The fort’s back gate swung open, and a mounted rider—Lieutenant Case, from the Sappers as well—rode in. An arrow’s stub stuck out of his leg, and something had clawed him across his chest. “Hangmen. They’re coming.”

“Are you certain?” Owl drew his short sword from his sheath and gave it a whirl. As they spoke, bowstrings twanged, and arrows whistled through the night. Orcs screeched. Behind them, bells tolled, warning the villagers to run away.

“They slew Effort, Chain, and Fancy,” Case shouted back.

“Where are the Greencloaks? Askan was supposed to be here!”

“He was taking care of a different threat!”

Owl used his Perception Skill again, sending a wave of arcane presence rolling over the fields. In a flash, he picked up a heavy weight. Something heavier than Gold.

It surged toward him just as the Perception Skill was wearing off. Before he recognized what happened, an alien pressure and warmth burst in his gut. Darkness blasted in front of his vision, but he never saw what caused it—only a faint tingle in the air and a ringing in his ears.

He couldn’t see the wound below his armour. His body was a punctured water flask. He slumped forward, falling face-first into the ground. Dupes on the wall screamed and shouted, swinging their weapons at nothing.

The animalistic part of his mind, the non-Dupe soul, knew something was horribly wrong. He couldn’t explain it.

The last thing he remembered was someone dragging him away from the wall.
