Chapter 48

“The island temples are the roof. Do not confuse the roof with the building.” - Codex of the Long Watch, Field Observation, 4th Century BCE, Malta

The transit compression was as bad as Marseille.

Arthur’s body folded through a space that shouldn’t have fit a hand, let alone a man carrying a shield and forty kilograms of scavenged equipment. His lungs flattened. His ribs tried to occupy the same volume as his spine. Then the pressure released and he was standing on stone in the dark, ears ringing, the taste of copper flooding his mouth.

He planted his feet and waited for his balance to return. That was the lesson from the first transit: your legs arrive before your inner ear does. Lock the knees, widen the stance, and wait for the room to decide which direction is down.

Donna was already upright beside him, hand on the axe, eyes sweeping the chamber walls before her breathing had even settled. Steve was on one knee, breathing hard, focus rod clutched against his chest like a talisman.

“Everyone intact?” Arthur said.

“Define intact,” Steve managed.

“Upright and bleeding from zero wounds.”

“Technically.” Steve stood and took in the chamber. The proportions matched every transit space they’d seen, circular and domed and Seeder notation banding the walls. The platform beneath them glowed white at its centre, the rings starting to dim.

Arthur glanced at Archie.

Archie’s geometry had dimmed. The facets that normally held a steady blue-white luminescence were muted, the rotational drift slowed to a crawl. It was the same state that had lasted twelve hours after London.

“Again,” Donna said.

“Again.”

The riddle manifested in Arthur’s peripheral vision before they’d stepped off the platform, blue-white text fed through the Nadi Matrix. He read it aloud.

Seek the Soft-Stone Mother where the trilithons rise,

Where the barren rock sits in the sea and the cliff-hawks cry.

Sight the first light through the window of bone,

And press the Root-palm to the pitted-heart stone.

Steve’s lips moved, repeating the lines silently. His hand drifted toward his pack where the Goldsmiths books sat.

“Not here,” Donna said. “We don’t know what’s outside. Riddle later. Exit first.”

Steve looked like a man being asked to leave a restaurant before dessert. But he followed after scribbling down the riddle.

Two passages led from the chamber. The one angled upward was sealed with no visible mechanism. The other curved away into darkness and rose gradually. The surface exit.

Arthur led, shield forward. The passage was Seeder-standard, grey-blue stone with machine-precise angles, wide enough for two abreast. The air grew warmer as they climbed. It was also drier and carried a hint of sweetness.

Light came down through the passage ahead, warm against his face. The violet was a shade paler than they were used to.

Arthur squeezed through the fissure, and the Mediterranean hit him, though this was much hotter than Marseille.

The light blinded him after the passage. White limestone cliffs dropped away to a cove where turquoise water lapped against rock. The violet sky sat differently here than it had over London or Marseille. The sun burned through it with a clarity that two weeks of northern European autumn hadn’t prepared him for, and the heat on his face was immediate and startling. The air tasted of salt and rosemary and hot stone.

He was standing on a ledge partway up a cliff face. Below sat a fishing cove with stone jetties and a slipway, the hulls of small boats pulled above the tide line. Some of them looked recently used. Above them was a limestone plateau edged with dry scrub and yellow grass that moved in a breeze he couldn’t feel from here. The sea stretched south to the horizon, and sitting in it, some five kilometres out was a small island. It was striking yet bare and treeless, nothing but pale rock against the blue.

The Ether density was higher than anything Arthur had felt on the surface in Marseille. The pressure behind his eyes that accompanied proximity to high-saturation zones was making him aware that something on the plateau above was generating it.

Steve came through the fissure behind him and stopped breathing.

He stood very still for perhaps ten seconds, looking at the island. Then at the cliffs. Then at the plateau above. Then at the island again. His brain pulling records and pattern matching them.

“Steve,” Donna said, emerging behind him.

"The barren rock," he said in a speculative voice. "Sitting in the sea. Comino, maybe."

"What's Comino?"

Steve frowned. Looked again at the distance, the bare profile, the absence of any vegetation. "No. Too small. Too far south. Comino sits north of the main island, and it's got scrub all over it. This is bare."

His eyes refocused. "Filfla. That's Filfla. Uninhabited island off Malta's southern coast. Nothing on it but rock and seabirds. I've seen it in -" He caught himself trying to pinpoint the source in his head. “There was a BBC documentary on ancient temples and megalithic engineering. How Neolithic people moved stones they shouldn’t have been able to move. I watched it twice because the structural analysis was extraordinary and once more because I thought the presenter was wrong about the ramp angles.” He pulled the Megalithic Temple Cultures book from his pack and started flipping pages with excitement.

“We’re on Malta,” Donna said.

“We might be on Malta.”

“Steve. The island is right there. You just identified it.”

“I identified one geographical feature from a BBC documentary. That’s not—” He found the page. A photograph of golden limestone blocks arranged in a trilithon entrance. His eyes moved between the book and the plateau above them. “Trilithon. Three-stone entrance. Two uprights, one lintel. That’s the riddle’s first line: ‘where the trilithons rise.’”

“Yeah, okay, we are on Malta,” he said, looking up from the book.

Arthur looked up as well. From this angle, the plateau rim was a clean line against the violet sky. He couldn’t see any structures.

“What’s the soft-stone mother?” he asked.

Steve turned pages. “Globigerina limestone. The whole island is built on it. Soft, golden, and weathers easily. And ‘mother,’ the temples here are famous for their figurines. Obese female forms. Fertility icons. They’ve been found across every Maltese temple site, carved from the same limestone.” He looked at the cliff face they were standing on. Reached out and pressed his thumb into the rock. It gave slightly. “Soft stone. We’re standing on it.”

“So the first two lines point here,” Donna said. “What about three and four?”

“‘Sight the first light through the window of bone.’” Steve frowned. “Window of bone could be an architectural feature. Some of the temples have porthole doorways cut through single megaliths. The limestone weathers to a bone colour over centuries. But ‘first light’ is a solar alignment. There are numerous such references for many other sites as well. Sunlight entering through a carved aperture at solstice or equinox. Several Maltese temples have documented astronomical orientations.” He shook his head. “I’d need to see the temple. There’s nothing in the book about a ‘window of bone’ by that name or description.”

“I’m assuming that they are not referring to a CT scan bone window,” Arthur said with a slight smile. “What about the pitted-heart stone?”

“No idea. Could be a weathering pattern. Globigerina pits when it erodes; the surface develops holes, like pumice. A pitted altar stone, maybe. But I’m guessing.” Steve closed the book. “I can solve the first half from geography and a stolen textbook. The second half needs the monument.”

“There should be temples up there,” Steve said. He nodded toward the plateau.

“How far?” Donna asked.

“Twenty-minute climb. Maybe less,” Arthur replied. He had been reading the goat tracks since they’d emerged, switchbacks cut into soft rock with the gradient manageable if steep.

Donna was watching the plateau rim. “Steve. These temples. How old are they?”

“Older than the pyramids. Older than Stonehenge. Built between 3600 and 2500 BCE by people who had no metal tools, no wheel, and no writing system. The oldest free-standing stone temples on Earth.”

Nobody spoke as the implications settled. The sea lapped against the rocks below and somewhere on the cliff, a bird Arthur didn’t recognise called once and fell silent.

“And do you archeological nerds know who built them?” Donna said.

“First, I take the ‘nerd’ as a compliment,” Steve said, straightening his pack.

“And secondly, well…no,” he continued hurriedly.

“The builders appeared around 3800 BCE. What followed was thirteen hundred years of construction and then they vanished. No war, no disaster evidence, no mass graves. Just gone.” Steve looked at the plateau above. “Until now, nobody had a credible theory for why.”

And then between one breath and the next, Archie’s facets snapped to full luminescence, the rotational drift accelerating to operational speed. This was much faster than Marseille. Twelve minutes, not twelve hours.

“Sector authorisation recalibration complete,” Archie said. “Operational parameters updated. Continental guidance mode active.”

“The substrate beneath your current position is upper globigerina limestone. Miocene formation. The structures on the plateau above are consistent with Seeder-influenced construction methodologies adapted to local materials and workforce capability,” Archie continued.

Steve stared at him.

Donna looked at Arthur. “Did he just volunteer a geology lecture?”

“It was not a lecture,” Archie said. “It was a substrate composition assessment. Lectures imply pedagogical intent. I have no interest in your education.”

“You’ve never offered that kind of detail without being asked,” Arthur said.

Archie’s geometry rotated once. “Operational parameters have been updated. My assessment scope now includes regional geological and archaeological context relevant to the candidate’s navigational requirements.”

“So you can tell us more now,” Steve said.

“I can tell you what is relevant to your current operational phase. The boundary of relevance has expanded. This should not be confused with generosity.”

“Wouldn’t dream of it,” Arthur said.

“Don’t encourage him,” Donna said. But the corner of her mouth moved.

Steve laughed. The sound carried across the empty cove and bounced off the limestone cliffs, and for a moment the violet sky over Malta felt less alien than the one over London had.

Donna had moved past all of them. She was ten metres up the first goat track, scanning the plateau rim when she stopped.

“Arthur.”

He followed her sightline to figures on the ridge, six or eight of them, spread in formation, spaced and deliberate, covering angles. Sunlight caught something metallic on some of them. Either they hadn’t seen the party, or they had and were choosing not to react.

“Trained,” Donna said. “Patrol formation. Sector coverage.” She watched for three seconds. “They’re holding a perimeter.”

The figures moved along the ridge and disappeared behind the plateau’s edge. The limestone shimmered. Arthur’s Resonance pulled upward, indifferent to armed strangers.

“Single file,” he said. “Donna on point. Steve, between us.”

They started climbing.
