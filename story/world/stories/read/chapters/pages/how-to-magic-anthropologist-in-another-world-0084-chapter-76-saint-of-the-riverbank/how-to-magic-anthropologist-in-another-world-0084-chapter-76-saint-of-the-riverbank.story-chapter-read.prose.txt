We traveled southeast and camped out for the six-hour short night before continuing our journey the following morning.

The roads grew progressively worse the farther we moved away from the Hano-Ectamel Road. What started as packed dirt eventually degraded into a rough trail carved through the wilderness by repeated wagon use.

It made sense when you thought about it. Roads connecting settlements directly to Hano were maintained because commerce with a city of five million people was always profitable. As a hyper-consumer city-state like Hano, where wealthy nobles and loot-flush freelancers had enough money to spend on everything, it always made sense to ship your goods there instead of to poorer neighboring settlements, and the Merchant Guild as an institution had learned that lesson long ago.

“The roads are maintained by the Merchant Guild as part of their taxes to Hano,” Ja’a explained as the road beneath us became increasingly bumpy, especially while we passed through the redwood forest, the same forest where I located Luna and the rest of the cult kidnapping victims a few weeks ago.

“That redwood lumber camp we just passed has a packed road directly leading to the Hano-Ectamel artery,” Ja’a added. “Same thing for the Stridwing ranching commune south of here, while the road between them looks like this.”

I guessed that made sense. Even Ectamel, supposedly the second-largest city nearby, barely held a population of one hundred thousand. Compared to Hano, it was less a rival metropolis and more a large satellite town orbiting an economic superpower. Their main exports, bones for fertilizer and death cores, were funneled straight toward Hano to feed an always-hungry population.

As a result, roads leading toward important production regions received constant maintenance, while roads linking smaller settlements to each other were largely neglected.

Honestly, the entire regional infrastructure map looked less like a spiderweb network and more like a dandelion.

So we kept riding southeast as the jagged mountains of the Kurco Range grew closer. This time, as the long night approached, Raik decided we should stop in the next town to rest.

Calling it a town was a misnomer. Compared to Hano and Ectamel, it was comparatively tiny. The place was not a village either. It probably housed around seven thousand people, making it larger than many medieval towns back on Earth.

But here lay the dissonance for me. On Earth, back when towns similar to this existed, even Constantinople, Baghdad, or Chang’an could never have dreamed of reaching Hano’s population.

While this place looked backward and medieval compared to modern standards, it also showed signs of convergent evolution. Most places were not feudal in nature but instead centered around some kind of industry.

Take this settlement as an example. It sat beside a broad muddy riverbank rich in clay deposits, and nearly everything about the town reflected that.

Kilns dominated entire streets while smoke rose constantly from brick-firing towers, giving the air a dry, earthy smell mixed with ash and wet soil. Stacks of clay bricks sat drying in courtyards beside rows of pots, bowls, water jars, and ceramic roof tiles waiting to be shipped elsewhere, probably to Hano. There were a few farms to sustain the population, but agriculture clearly was not the focus. Hano could feed itself using growth Soulbooks and Ectamel fertilizer, but selling clay, either raw or as finished products, was far more profitable.

Workers moved through the streets with permanently stained hands and dust-coated clothing. Even many of the homes incorporated clay bricks into their construction, giving the town an unusual reddish-brown appearance beneath the evening lantern light.

We eventually found an inn near the center of town. It was a large red brick building, and unlike many other places we had visited, it doubled as a stable, tavern, restaurant, and social gathering hall where locals met traveling visitors.

The common room was crowded and lively. Merchants, laborers, and travelers packed the wooden tables while servers moved between them carrying trays of stew, bread, and mugs of weak alcohol.

We sat down, and Raik ordered food and drinks for all of us, making sure Shingo received a double serving of stew. In places like this, you did not get a menu. You either ate what was served or went somewhere else, and in a town this size, somewhere else usually meant the river, where you tried catching your own dinner.

Still, I had no complaints once the food arrived. It was decently spiced, fireroot being a good crop to grow at this altitude.

I had barely begun eating when my attention was dragged toward a commotion.

Near the far side of the room, a young man enthusiastically retold a story to an increasingly captivated audience while a pretty young woman clung tightly to his arm.

I narrowed my eyes. They looked rather familiar.

“And here I was, finally a married man, about to begin the most sacred duty,” the man declared dramatically while spreading his arms wide. “The burden of reproduction!”

Playful cheers followed those words. The wife blushed heavily and smacked her husband on the side, though even she could not fully hide the grin spreading across her face.

“So, as good holy people, we were both cleansing our bodies in the river, as the Lady advised,” he continued dramatically. “Always cleanse your body before and after union, she says.”

“Could you imagine my rotten luck?” He shook his head sadly. “There I was, armed only with my manhood when the mossbear arrived.”

Some people gasped while others laughed at the crude joke.

“Can you believe my brave husband stood between the beast and me without hesitation?” the wife finally spoke, her voice soft and breathy.

“Yeah, well, not that I could have beaten it, mind you. I was mostly hoping it would choke on me long enough for my lovely wife to run away.”

Ja’a looked at the spectacle, then slowly turned toward my expression.

She leaned slightly closer.

“Do you know them?”

I slowly lowered my face into my hands.

Oh no, no, no. It is the nude couple I saved at the river. I really hope they do not notice me.

“When the beast came creeping out of the reeds,” the man continued, “the only thought in my mind was: please do not let me die a virgin when I am this close to heaven.”

He gestured vaguely toward his wife as laughter erupted across the room, and his blushing bride smacked his shoulder again.

“The beast growled at us,” then the man lowered his voice dramatically, “and just when death itself stood before us... she appeared.”

Oh no.

I sank deeper into my chair, nearly sliding beneath the table.

A few villagers leaned closer.

“Who?” one asked.

The young man pointed upward.

“A woman descended from heaven.”

“She appeared without sound beside the riverbank, holding a pitchfork crackling with lightning.”

The wife nodded rapidly in excited confirmation.

“She moved faster than my eyes could follow,” the man continued. “One moment the beast stood there, the next... BAM!”

He slammed his hands together loudly for emphasis, startling a nearby child.

“The monster spasmed like it had been smote by the Lady herself!”

“And then the mysterious woman who slew the beast in a single strike...” the man continued dramatically, “vanished before we could even thank her.”

“She vanished like a spirit or a fay,” His wife nodded rapidly.

“It is the saint,” one of the older villagers whispered. “There have been rumors coming from Hano that a new one is rising.”

“No, it is not,” grumbled another. “The saint is a healer curing the green fever. This one is a fighter.”

“The saint is a fighter, you idiot,” said the first villager. “She helped Lady Kerissa kill those damn cultists a few weeks ago, close to here in the redwood forest.”

The rest of my team slowly looked toward me while I sank even deeper into my seat.

Calr was visibly trying not to laugh.

“Your legend is growing,” said Vena.

I buried my face in my hands.

Meanwhile, the man continued speaking with increasing confidence.

“After the woman saved us and vanished without a word, I was left standing there beside my wife and a three-hundred-kilogram dead bear.”

He started rummaging through a large backpack before pulling out an extremely intact green bear pelt.

“And that is the story of how, on my nuptial night, my lovely wife helped me skin a bear.”

The watching crowd cheered at the sight of the pelt. The skinned head still attached to the thick fur gave it a strangely fearsome appearance.

“It was not the type of exertion I was hoping for, but I will take it.”

“And did I mention,” the man added proudly, “that my wife and I were completely naked during this entire event?”

The room exploded into laughter. And his wife smacked him across the shoulder for the third time.

Even I started laughing at that point despite myself.

“I need to leave before either of them recognizes me,” I whispered into Raik’s ear.

He nodded, so I quietly ducked out of the room, summoned my spear, and with one small wish, I vanished.

Back in the Sunless Reach, I considered what to do next.

I should probably visit Nina. I had promised the girl I would dream with her again, and I still needed to show her the workout equipment ideas I had promised Yon.

Honestly, knowing the girl, she was probably already bored of glasswork and wanted something new to work on.

Before I could formulate the wish, however, the image of the newlyweds resurfaced in my mind.

What if there really was somebody nearby who needed help?

Would it really be alright to go have fun with Nina while people needed me?

I knew I was spiraling a little. Rationally, I understood that I could not save everyone. Even if I dedicated every second of every day to helping people, death would still exist, whether through disease, accidents, or monsters.

Still...

If my power could bring me somewhere useful, then checking one more time did not seem unreasonable.

Just once more.

I looked upward toward the endless sky of the Sunless Reach, watching for a shooting star. I barely waited a minute before one showed up. The familiar sensation of star mana surged through my body.

“I wish to safely be somewhere I can be useful,” I called softly.

Reality folded around me, and a moment later, I found myself standing inside a forest.

Tall dark trees surrounded me on all sides, their branches thick enough to blot out most of the remaining sunlight of the already dark Waterday dusk. Damp moss covered the ground while cool night air carried the scent of pine sap and wet earth.

For a few seconds, I stood completely still. Using my perception skill, I checked for monsters or humans. There was nothing at ground level, but then the sound of crying led me to focus upward.

A child sat perched several meters up inside a tree, clutching the trunk so tightly his knuckles had turned white. Tears streaked down his dirt-covered cheeks while he tried and failed to muffle panicked sobs.

The moment he noticed me, he flinched violently.

“Please do not eat me!” he blurted out immediately.

I blinked.

“Why would I do that?” I asked.

The boy stared at me with wide, terrified eyes. Well, he had probably just seen me appear from thin air.

He could not have been older than eight or nine. His clothes were rough homespun fabric stained with mud and pine needles, and one of his shoes was missing entirely.

“You are not a monster?” he asked cautiously.

“Do I look like a monster to you?” I stuck out my tongue and started dancing erratically while acting like a monster. “Boo. Look at me, I am a monster.”

The boy could not help but chuckle between sniffles.

“So what are you doing up there?” I asked.

“I got lost,” he whimpered. “...And then I heard growling, so I climbed the tree.”

Honestly, that was actually a pretty good instinct for a child. Come to think of it, the boy had slight feline features, mostly around the eyes.

“Do you know where your village is?”

The boy shook his head miserably.

“I was gathering mushrooms with big sister, but then I got lost.”

I glanced around carefully while extending my lightning senses as far as I could. Nothing bigger than a sparrow triggered my Soulbook perception powers.

Which honestly reassured me more than anything else. If there had been some ongoing tragedy nearby, I suspected my wish would have dragged me there instead. Meaning the child probably was the reason I had been brought here.

And if the worst problem in the area was a scared, lost kid hiding in a tree, then maybe nobody nearby was currently dying in some preventable way.

“Alright,” I said while smiling. “I am going to need you to trust me.”

The boy looked uncertain.

“Are you a ghost?”

“No.”

“A fairy?”

“No.”

“A saint?”

I physically winced.

“Now, why would you ask me that? You are kindred, are you not? Leave the saint talk to the Holy people.”

He squinted suspiciously.

“So you are a saint.”

“No, I am not!”

He somehow seemed satisfied enough to trust me and started expertly climbing down. I stepped closer and extended both arms upward to help him, but the boy was a natural-born climber.

Yet the moment his feet touched the dirt, he immediately latched onto my sleeves.

The poor kid was terrified.

“What is your name?” I asked as we started walking.

“Teren.”

“I am Alice.”

He stared at the white travel clothes I wore on top of my chain armor.

“You really look like a fairy, Miss Saint.”

I sighed deeply.

“I told you I am not a saint. Who even taught you those words? This is the Contested Realm, not the Mythic.”

“My friend Nessa is Holy. Her dad was talking about a saint helping a kindred person become a cleric. I figured I could become a knight too,” said the child excitedly. “I have seen Edmund once. He had shiny armor. I want shiny armor too.”

I blinked at the deluge of information. He seemed surprisingly informed about Hano’s freelancers despite living outside the city, but I probably should not have been surprised. Elite freelancers were basically this world’s celebrities after all.

Finding the boy’s route was not too difficult. The ground had enough muddy spots that every few meters, I was able to locate a footprint. I was no tracker, but even I managed it.

So we walked, and the boy chatted nonstop about his sister, his Holy friend, and about that one time the Twenty Men Army visited their village, hunted three boars, and fed everyone in the village with them.

Along the way, I started sparking small arcs of lightning between the prongs of my bident spear under the pretext of providing light, though honestly, it was mostly to entertain the child, whom I concluded was babbling out of nervousness.

Eventually, after perhaps twenty minutes of walking, we spotted lantern light flickering through the trees ahead.

“Teren!”

A girl’s panicked scream echoed through the forest.

The boy immediately lit up.

“Big sister!”

He broke into a sprint toward the lights.

A moment later, he collided with an older teenage girl hard enough to nearly knock both of them over. She grabbed him desperately while alternating between hugging him and scolding him through tears.

To my surprise, the girl was a cat girl. Like, she fit perfectly into the anime cat girl stereotype, cat ears and all. I had seen a cheetah arena fighter before, but she only had the long tail and patches of fur, not actual cat ears.

“You idiot! We have been looking everywhere for you!” the older sister chastised.

“Sorry,” he mumbled into her clothes.

I raised my spear and started tapping into my star mana, hoping to disappear before I attracted too much attention.

As I was about to vanish, I heard the kid tell his sister,

“I was saved by the saint.”

A second later, I stood beneath the familiar stars of the Sunless Reach.

“That little shit. I told him I was not a saint,” I shouted into the calmness of the night, startling a celestial owl that had been perched nearby. “Arg.”

I let out a long, slow breath.

Then I collapsed into my chaise lounge and began recharging my star mana in peace.

I appeared next to Nina’s workshop door.

Given the late hour, I decided not to teleport directly on top of her just in case she was sleeping or bathing.

Though that worry quickly proved unfounded.

Warm light leaked through the curtained window beside the door, and I could already hear faint movement from inside.

I knocked lightly.

A few seconds later, the door swung open, revealing the pigeon-winged girl wearing her customary pastel sarong dress. Her hair looked slightly messy, and she held a broom in one hand.

The moment she saw me, her entire face lit up.

“Alice!”

Before I could properly greet her, Nina lunged forward and wrapped me in a hug hard enough to nearly knock me backward. Her soft, feathered wings folded around me instinctively, warm and faintly dusty with down feathers.

“You are back,” she chirped happily. “I missed you.”

“I missed you, too,” I admitted while awkwardly trying not to get smothered by her wings.

She finally released me and immediately started dragging me inside by the hand.

As I stepped through the doorway, I noticed the floor was still wet from cleaning, and muddy footprints immediately appeared beneath my boots.

I stopped mid-step.

Nina blinked back at me curiously.

“What is wrong?”

“You just cleaned this.”

“It is fine,” she said dismissively.

This part of the workshop normally stayed open to customers, meaning shoes were technically acceptable here. Still, I did not want to make an extra mess while somebody was cleaning.

So I crouched down and removed my boots.

Then, while Nina watched, I reached into my bag of holding and extracted a pair of soft slippers made of thick leather before slipping them on instead.

The pigeon-winged girl tilted her head.

“You carry indoor slippers in your magic bag?”

“I am a civilized woman,” I said defensively.

I reached into the bag again and pulled out a spare broom.

Nina blinked.

“You also carry a broom?”

“I put most of the stuff I had in my dorm room in here, especially since I barely used the place at this point.”

Nina chuckled at this.

I started helping her scrub the floor. Nina insisted I rest while she “finished quickly,” but I refused to accept no for an answer. Eventually, she gave up and simply started cleaning beside me while we chatted.

The workshop smelled faintly of clay, hot glass, soap, metal, and sawdust. Half-finished projects covered nearly every available surface. Glass tubes, copper fittings, molds, notebooks, and experimental lenses cluttered the nearby tables in organized chaos.

A cooling furnace glowed dim orange from deeper inside the workshop while magenta moonlight filtered through the windows overhead.

“You have been busy,” I observed.

Nina puffed up proudly.

“Very busy.”

She immediately launched into a talk about her apprentices.

Apparently, the first few days had been difficult. The help Je’e provided was having trouble handling the delicate mix of glasswork and metalwork.

“But they are improving,” she finally said while mopping the floor to a shine. “They mostly needed to stop treating metal like glass.”

She continued rambling enthusiastically while we worked.

Apparently, the first batch of spyglasses had already been completed and shipped out. And thanks to Tan Je’e’s recommendation, several influential military figures had already received a free sample.

“Commander Marina got one,” Nina explained proudly. “And Commander Kitchi as well. Also, we sent one to General Kiddo and another to General Ta’o.”

That actually made me pause.

“Oh wow. You are moving up in the world.”

“I know!” she chirped. “It is all thanks to you, my muse.”

Her wings fluttered excitedly enough to stir a few loose papers.

“Tan Je’e said if the generals like them, the Freelancer Guild may finance a larger workshop.”

That honestly sounded huge.

“I will let the apprentices handle the bulk work when they are ready,” she smiled. “You know me. I am more about chasing the next invention.”

On my side, I told her about our trip through the undead zone, the sanctified camp, the cult attack, and how I escaped a dreamer attack by finding my sanctuary.

Nina listened with complete fascination as I told her about the mine we cleared and how the spawner created monsters on the spot.

“I wonder if we could create something like that, but docile. Something that keeps churning out spyglasses nonstop or something similar,” I said.

“Maybe. It might be possible, though you would probably need Soul magic for that,” she said.

Eventually, we finished cleaning, and Nina flopped down beside me near the workshop counter while stretching her wings lazily.

“I am glad you are back,” she admitted more quietly this time.

Something softer entered her expression.

“Dreaming alone was becoming boring.”

I smiled faintly.

“Yeah,” I admitted. “I really missed that, too. Luckily for us, I will be back in Hano in a few days, and we can resume our sleepovers.”

Nina brightened immediately.

“What about today? Are you staying the night?” she asked hopefully.

“Yes, but...” I combed my hair back with my hand. “I need a bath first. Is there a bathhouse nearby?”

“No need. You can bathe here. I have a tub.”

She stood up, locked the door, closed the curtains, then dragged a wooden tub out from a nearby closet. Finally, using a water pump, she started filling it up.

I stepped next to her.

“Do you have a way to heat it up?” I asked.

She nodded, reached into the closet again, and pulled out some sort of contraption. It looked like a metal plate with a red monster core embedded at the center.

She dropped it into the water, where it floated on top. The core started glowing while emanating heat.

A few minutes later, as the water began to steam, she reached in with prongs and carried the device out before placing it back into a metal stand.

I ended up helping her wash her extensive wings as we bathed. The girl looked completely relaxed while I carefully combed beneath her wings, removing loose down feathers.

As we both soaked quietly in the warm water, I slowly opened up about my emotional turmoil. The dead mercenaries, the feeling that I could be doing more with my teleportation power, and even my growing discomfort over the saint rumors spreading around me.

Nina did not say much.

Instead, she simply moved closer and wrapped one warm, wet wing around me while holding my hand.

I slowly relaxed into her grasp.

Nina always made me feel calmer. Honestly, Nina’s workshop felt more like home than my own guild room at this point.

Maybe it was her Dreamer gift that made people sleep more soundly, or maybe she was simply good at making others feel safe. Either way, the tension slowly drained from my body while we rested together in comfortable silence.
