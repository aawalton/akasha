A new voice interrupted their conversation. Moswen stepped out of the crowd, offering a hand. The silver-haired warlock took the purple-skinned magia’s arm, giving it a shake.

“Magister Moswen,” Sylvester greeted.

“Lord Potami would like to speak with you,” Moswen said. “Follow me, if you don’t mind.”

“Of course.”

With the magia leading the way, Gavin, Elroy, and Sylvester entered a small parlor. Lord Potami was a tall and broad man even among warrior-class demons. If he stood upright, the crown of his head would breach the eight-foot barrier. Most notable were Drugi Potami’s nose and ears. Both were long, with the great demon’s nose particularly uncanny and off-putting.

Seated nearby was another guest, a salaman with a protruding face and pale skin covered in dark scales. A high demon of the fourth consolidation, Sylvester noted. The lizard man studied Sylvester in turn with his slit, yellow eyes.

“■■■,” Moswen chanted.

Magic buzzed, sealing the room as the magia pulled the door closed behind them. Moswen’s spell worked in conjunction with the wards set in the floor, walls, and ceiling. Sylvester cast his crimson gaze across the room, verifying the spells and enchantments met his standard.

“Viscount Potami,” the gentleman greeted, doffing his hat while offering a bow. “It has been many years.”

“So it has,” Drugi confirmed. The great demon gestured to the salaman beside him. “This is Hiền Dinh of Firestorm. He’ll be accompanying us for this meeting. As for your companions, Gavin Shacklebreaker and Elroy Marteau need no introduction.”

“I prefer Elroy of Est Sombre,” the werewolf rumbled, offering a bow. “Black Hammer is too crude a name and conveys a cruelty I reject. And forgive my manners if they’re unrefined. I am not accustomed to high-class persons like yourself.”

“We all serve the flame,” Hiền Dinh hissed, his forked tongue sliding past his teeth.

“Sir Dinh has it right,” Lord Potami confirmed. “Take a seat and we can begin.”

“I don’t mind if I do,” Sylvester said, claiming a cushioned armchair. He set his cane and his metal briefcase down beside him, hanging his hat on his cane’s handle. “Is Firestorm just here to listen, or will they be offering direct assistance?”

“Firestorm will not face Lord Azazel,” Hiền Dinh replied. “But we are willing to fight Heaven, if your plan is reasonable, effective, and, most importantly, violent.”

“The dungeon core is lost,” Sylvester said, leaning back in his chair. “We won’t fight for it. From now on, our goal is to snatch back the kidnapped members of the Bluelight.”

“Oh, good,” Gavin said. “My wee little self couldn’t do much against an archdemon.”

“Don’t be too excited,” Sylvester followed. “Because one week ago, the Obsidian Tower sent the kidnapped demons to Heaven's embassy in Aidonopol. Which means, if we want them, we’re going to have to break them out.” His gaze shifted to the viscount and the salaman. “Assuming that’s possible.”

“And here I thought we were going to do something reasonable for once,” Gavin snarked.

“Look on the bright side, you’ll be earning your name ‘Shacklebreaker’,” the warlock teased.

“Heaven’s embassy must be heavily defended,” Elroy murmured, stroking his chin.

“It’s unassailable,” Hiền Dinh hissed. “Firestorm tried to take it a century ago. The building in Aidonopol is just a front. The real embassy is in the diakenon. The structure is guarded by a large-scale force barrier. In addition to their security force, they have 8 lightning cannons, 2 calamity cannons, and 5 wings of their Iber Hatz. We brought ten ships backed by an archdemon last time we attacked. We lost nine of them.”

Iber Hatz was Heaven’s premier anti-personnel weapon. Each wing was an arrangement of pipes: two long, three medium, and five short. Bolts were loaded into the pipes and fired like bullets from a gun. The longer pipes had more powerful projectiles with greater range. The strongest hits compared to a heavy ballista while the lighter strikes hit as hard as a second consolidation archer.

But that didn’t capture why the Iber Hatz was so effective. The weapon system had a high rate of fire. So much so that each wing could compare to 10 or 20 ballistas.

“Any force approaching from the diakenon would be ether deprived,” Sylvester noted. “The embassy has a massive advantage. We’d need thirty ships to crack it with force.”

The diakenon had thin ether while the embassy was plugged into the plane. Over the course of an extended battle, that would give the fortress a ten-to-one advantage on firepower. A conventional army had little hope of victory.

“If you sent so many ships, Demon King Zagan would catch wind of the attack and intervene,” Lord Potami inserted. “By treaty, Hell is required to defend the embassy from outside attack.”

“But not from the inside,” Sylvester noted.

“Even if Zagan was willing, Lady Charmeine would never allow it. The embassy has too many secrets,” Drugi agreed. “But the internal security force Heaven provided isn’t any weaker than its external protections. The embassy has five hundred employees, all of whom are second consolidation or higher. One hundred of those men and women are part of the guard. Then there are the five Transcendents to consider, along with an army of phantasms.”

Hiền Dinh was correct. This wasn’t an embassy. It was a fortress.

“The best way to crack a castle is from the inside,” Gavin pointed out. “Is there a way to slip in through the backdoor? Cause a little sabotage?”

Moswen shook his head. “The embassy uses a series of enforced law wards which suppress all forms of infiltration. We know they can open gates out, because they do so to secretly move members of the Inquisition. But without knowing the concept behind their laws, it’s too great a risk to insert an agent.”

The element law was a combination of space and causality. With the help of that magic, a mage could apply large-scale conceptual effects. Law, however, had a fault. It could suppress or amplify, but it could never entirely stop. If Sylvester used edict to declare: ‘disguises won’t work’, the only result would be that the guards would be twice as likely to see through deception.

But there was a solution to every problem. An enforced law ward achieved its effect by letting a second spell piggyback on the first. A law like: ‘flying is forbidden here’, could be tied to chains of earth. Then, when someone tried to fly, they’d be immediately struck by the curse. The combination of effects would be enough to stop even a Transcendent.

Or, if the ward designer wanted to be nasty, they could drop a lightning bolt on the violator’s head instead.

But while law wards were difficult to circumvent, they had a fatal flaw. Laws were built on ideas, and ideas could be reduced to words. And words could be manipulated. A mage could counter law with law, taking on the role of a lawyer. For instance, a ward that said: ‘flying is forbidden’ could be circumvented if Sylvester magically declared: ‘I’m not flying, I’m falling in my preferred direction.’

Which, incidentally, was exactly how flux shift worked.

“An idea just struck me,” Sylvester said, a smile spreading on his lips. “I think we have a way to insert a friendly agent. And all it will require is a small donation by an old ‘friend’ of ours in Iacchus.”

Gavin laughed. “Ah, Sylvester, you have a vile mind.”

Sylvester smirked. “I’m just fixing a regret. However, there are a few things to consider. First, I have to know, can Firestorm or the Clockwork Men temporarily disappear an angel? Preferably someone who could ‘visit’ important security sites in the embassy.”

“I think I know your plan now,” Drugi said with a trumpeting laugh. “A third of the staff lives in Aidonopol. There’s definitely someone among their number who meets our requirements. Sir Dinh, I will leave it to Firestorm for the capture. Their disappearance must be so short that there is no suspicion.”

“Our agents can handle that,” Hiền Dinh accepted. “But we will not commit to an attack on the embassy without a clear path to victory.”

“But if we have a clear path, will you speak with your upper echelons?” Sylvester asked. “Even if we open the gate, we’ll need an archdemon and a lot of support to pull this off.”

The salaman’s forked tongue slithered. “Firestorm has long hoped to see an embassy burn. To light one on fire during the Exposition would be a special delight.”

“Even if we plant a spy, we need a way to get the information out,” Moswen noted. “And the mechanism must not trigger the wards or be revealed during a security check.”

“I’ll discuss possibilities with you later,” Sylvester replied.

“Gavin and I will work with the gangs and the local members of the Bluelight,” Elroy added. “I fear, having lost the dungeon core, Heaven may seek results.”

“If they do, our men will be there to take advantage,” Hiền Dinh said.

“Let the Bluelight members who are willing know that we can move them somewhere safe,” Sylvester added. “And be careful. I don’t want to add to the rescues.”

“Don’t worry, I’m quite good at hiding my face,” Gavin joked.

“I will use my lieutenants as proxies,” Elroy added. “I have become familiar with this style of warfare, though I do not favor it.”

“Do you require help snatching the item?” the purple-skinned magia interrupted.

“No. I’d rather do it myself,” Sylvester answered, shaking his head. He turned to the great demon. “Lord Potami, may I have a word in private?”

The viscount waved a hand, indicating everyone else should leave. When the door was secure, Sylvester picked up his metal briefcase, then set it on a table. Before he could pop the locks, Lord Potami stood, then fell to a knee, his head bowed deeply.

“Forgive me, Your Eminence, for not being able to greet you properly,” Drugi Potami solemnly declared.

Sylvester let out a sigh and allowed his visage dissolve.

“You may rise.”

As the karnabo returned to his feet, Sylvester’s face melted. Flesh and bones shifted, his cheeks becoming rounder and his eyes wider. Crimson irises softened into fractal pink, filling with ancient starlight. The handsome warlock was now the petite and adorable Sylvia Swallows.

“To serve the Apostle of the Thirteenth Piece is my greatest honor,” Drugi said, returning to his seat. “The Clockwork Men believe in the golden dream. We long to become instruments of the Great Work, like those who brought the Heavenly Will into this world. When I saw the shift of fate, I nearly cried tears of joy. To think, this old man would have the glory of ushering in a new era at your side, Cloud Princess Swallows.”

“So that title reached you as well,” Sylvia groaned. “I’ll have you know, ‘may you live in interesting times’ is considered a curse.”

“Creation always brings destruction,” Lord Potami said firmly. “But the destruction brought by progress is not to be feared. It is to be embraced. I have learned much of the Bluelight, and I see in it hope that the golden dream can be restored.” The viscount paused. “I forget how young you are, Lady Swallows. Are you familiar with the golden dream?”

“I am,” she answered. “It is the belief that if the people strive for it, the netherworld can become forever better.”

“It is more than a belief. It is a faith,” Drugi declared. “It is the guiding light in our hearts which leads us to plant the seeds of a better tomorrow. Heaven was once ablaze with this light, but now they cling to their false utopia. It is on us to light that fire anew. To see it burn in Heaven, Hell, and the Fey Federation. To dream of greater worlds and use our hands to create them.”

“You don’t hate Heaven then?” Sylvia questioned.

“I do not. The angels in Heaven are our brothers. Once, they led the netherworld. Now they are lost in the dark. It is our duty to guide them back into the light,” Lord Potami answered. “But do not mistake them for our allies. They fear His Great Work and will seek to destroy it. So we must fight them with all we have.”

Sylvia gave a nod. Then she popped open the locks of her briefcase. From within, she drew out a large crystal. The giant jewel thumped in her hand, red spindles flowing through the solid object like blood.

“This is a dungeon core,” she explained. “Dungeons act as supporting agents to the Bluelight. I need to be sure this one will be put somewhere safe. Neither Azazel nor Heaven can be allowed to take it.”

“The best solution is to hide it in a sympathizer’s fief. That way, it will be guarded by military strength and politics,” Drugi stated. “But it cannot be me. I was too bold in my youth. Too many know my name and my persuasion. Whether against Zagan or Azazel, I will not be able to protect it.”

Though Lord Potami was a fifth consolidation great demon, his political power was limited. Most demons of his strength held the title of count or marquis. Drugi, however, was forever restricted to the status of viscount in large part because he was a known member of the Clockwork Men.

“I’ll be counting on you then.” Sylvia put the core on the table before locking her briefcase. Then she settled back into her chair. “Tell me, how do the Clockwork Men view the Bluelight?”

“With caution,” Lord Potami answered. “Throughout the centuries, His pieces have offered many Great Works. The Thirteenth Piece moved fate more than any other, but we debate among ourselves whether the Thirteenth Piece offers chaos, utopia, or merely an opening for the Tenth’s gambit. I, however, see victory in the Bluelight and in no other.”

“I see,” she said. “Then have any of you accepted the Bluelight for yourselves?”

“No. The Clockwork Men work by proxy. To do otherwise would risk dissolving the political power of our order,” the viscount said. “For that same reason, I cannot be seen in Heaven’s embassy. But fear not! For I happen to own a titan body in grave need of a test!”

“I’ll look forward to watching your titan smash through a horde of angels then,” Sylvia laughed. Then her expression turned serious. “Lord Potami, may I ask a bit of advice?”

“I live to serve, Your Eminence,” Drugi replied, his left arm crossing his chest in a salute.

“I find myself on the cusp of Transcendence,” she admitted. “But I lack inspiration and hope to learn from a man who has already crossed the second grand mutation.”

Lord Potami went quiet. Sylvia felt a chill crawl over her. The viscount’s demeanor had changed. Silently, he studied her, his piercing, yellow-green eyes like those of a basilisk.

“Once again, Your Eminence exceeds all expectations,” Drugi said, sucking in a breath. “Then you are fourth consolidation in truth. Is this the power of the Bluelight?”

Sylvia, by netherworld reckoning, was only fourteen years old. Even if Lord Potami thought her ten years older, her talent was a thing of legend. It was natural, under those conditions, to assume Sylvester’s strength was a front. There were ways, after all, to hide one’s power or to falsely display a higher cultivation.

“Talent is just one of the benefits the Bluelight offers,” she said simply.

“I know now why the netherworld shuddered when we shook hands on the Utrecht.” The great demon sighed. “With a world tree in hand, the rise of the Bluelight is all but assured. No one is prepared for what is coming. When you are safe and secure, I must talk with my fellows of the Clockwork Men. We have gravely underestimated the Bluelight.”

“Not everyone is as talented as me,” Sylvia reminded.

“They do not have to be,” Drugi replied. “Will others reach Transcendence in their first life?”

“Yes.”

“How many?”

“Thousands,” she answered. Then she sucked in a breath. “Perhaps tens of thousands after transmigration. Only a handful will reach the fourth consolidation in their first life. Most, however, have the potential to reach it in their second.”

“Then war will come in less than a century,” Lord Potami concluded. “Thank you for sharing this secret with me, Your Eminence. It is a grand trust. Many will need to prepare for what is coming. And, I believe, we will need many more cores like this one.”

Drugi tapped the dungeon core sitting on the table.

“But we digress. Allow me to answer your earlier question,” the viscount continued. “Transcendence is not Awakening. It is a conquest, not a conversation. You enter your soul to declare who you will be. Forget your past. Forget your mortal concerns. Instead, look upon your failures and regrets, then trample them beneath your feet. In Transcendence, there is one rule above all others. Do. Not. Hesitate!”

Drugi Potami’s final declaration was a roar, a brass instrument sounding over the battlefield. Sylvia shook with the emotion.

She closed her eyes.

Finally, she grasped what she’d been missing. Transcendence is a conquest, not a conversation. Sylvia had been trying to get in touch with herself. To understand her ideal. But that wasn’t how Transcendence worked. She wasn’t finding her ideal. She was deciding it.

Awakening was the conscious mind coming to a compromise with the unconscious self. This time, it was the role of her superego to go ape on her id.

“The more I doubt, the greater my struggle,” she realized. “Yet, I cannot help but fear it. What happens if I choose the wrong ideal?”

“There is no right or wrong in Transcendence, Lady Swallows,” Lord Potami said, brooking no argument. “There is only what comes after. Set aside your fears and doubts, then fix your eyes forward without looking back. Change is not to be feared, for change makes us greater.”

“I suppose I will just have to do it then,” she decided. The silver-haired girl picked up her metal briefcase. “Thank you for your advice, Drugi. And I will be relying on you in the days that come.”

“It was my pleasure, Your Eminence.”

Embassies and Diplomacy

After the third great war, Ragnarok, the netherworld sank into the Silent Age. During this period, all three major powers established rules of diplomatic conduct in hopes of avoiding a fourth great war. This included definitions of diplomatic immunity, as well as the establishment of embassies.

As Hell has 9 major planes, it was decided that each power would be entitled to 9 embassies. As a result, each of the major Hells has an embassy for Heaven and the Fey Federation, while only 9 of the 33 Heavens sport an embassy from Hell.

Ambassadors to the various planes are almost always Transcendents, with the foremost ambassador for any individual embassy being sixth consolidation. This is to ensure the diplomat has sufficient social status and weight without introducing undue risk or an implicit threat. Supporting ambassadors are usually fifth consolidation, creating clarity within the embassy of its power structure.

Unlike Earth, netherworld embassies provide little support to the citizens visiting distant planes. This is because, outside of Heaven, the major powers have a very weak definition of citizen. Titles like demon or fey are granted simply by living in the host nation for an extended period of time. Heaven, however, does have a formal system of citizenship and keeps the majority of its major planes closed to foreigners.

A secondary function of the embassies is spying. Spying is both formal and informal, with the formal spying tolerated as a mechanism to ensure the major powers are complying with their treaties and obligations. It is in Hell’s interest, for instance, that the Inquisition pokes around enough to leave Heaven confident that Hell’s government isn’t directly supporting entities like Firestorm.

Informal spies, ‘NOCs’, are illegal. They are also incredibly common. Heaven is considered the expert when it comes to spy games, mostly because Hell and the Fey Federation have relatively chaotic societies. This makes it far easier to insert agents or flip locals. Heaven, by contrast, binds its entire population to the karmically enforced Heavenly Law which heavily undermines human intelligence efforts.
