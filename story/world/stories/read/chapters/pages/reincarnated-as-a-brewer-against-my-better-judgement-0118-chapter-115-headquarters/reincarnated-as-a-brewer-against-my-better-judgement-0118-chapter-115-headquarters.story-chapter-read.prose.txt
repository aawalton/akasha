The main avenue had not improved while Alric was being measured.

It remained too busy, too clean in places that wanted coin, too loud in places that wanted attention, and too full of people who seemed to know exactly where they were going. Alric followed Seren through it with the uncomfortable sense that everyone else had agreed on a pattern of movement he had not been told about.

He was also still thinking about trousers. Not by choice.

Seren walked half a step ahead, carrying the tailor’s notes, and the expression of someone who had put a crisis into order and found the order wanting.

They turned off the main avenue before the merchant district ended. The side street was quieter, but not poor. The buildings here had good doors, clean stone fronts, and shutters painted often enough that rain had not yet been allowed to comment. Wagons could pass two abreast if both drivers were polite, sober, and not carrying furniture. Several shops had narrow awnings. A shopkeeper stood beside a doorway, watching a boy sweep dust from one place to another with the grave determination of youth given a pointless task.

Seren stopped outside a tall building set back just enough from the street to make the space in front of it feel deliberate.

Alric stopped beside her. For a moment, he said nothing. The building was an inn.

Not the kind of inn a man found by accident while tired, hungry, and prepared to accept questionable bedding. This was the sort of inn that expected carriages to arrive and expected those carriages to contain people who would complain about the mattress while paying for the privilege.

It stood wide across the front, two full floors, stone below and timber above, with a broad entrance and shuttered windows set in even rows. The sign bracket still hung out from the wall, though the sign itself had been removed, leaving only two rusted iron loops.

Alric looked at Seren. Seren looked at the building.

“It’s huge,” he said.

Seren turned her head slightly. “You have already filled one inn.”

“That wasn’t the original idea.”

“No,” she said. “But it was very thorough.”

Alric looked back at the building. It was too large. That was clear immediately. There was no need to go inside and prove it with walls.

A man stepped out from the recessed doorway. Alric’s mood worsened at once.

The estate agent smiled at him with the smooth, prepared warmth of a man who had seen objection before and considered it a temporary stage of purchase.

“Master Alric,” he said, bowing neatly. “Mistress Seren.”

Alric stared at him. The man did not visibly weaken.

The estate agent’s smile adjusted only slightly. “I am pleased to see you again.”

“I’m sure you are.” Alric said with a flat expression.

Seren gave Alric a look, not sharp enough to count as a warning, but sharp enough to suggest she had one in reserve. Alric ignored it.

The agent turned toward the building with professional relief. “Mistress Seren asked for something large, close to the main avenue, suitable for both residence and business, with private quarters, yard access, and room for staff.”

The agent paused with admirable judgement, then continued. “This property became available recently. It is in sound condition, though not fully furnished. The previous owner retired from innkeeping after his sons chose trade instead.”

“Wise sons,” Alric muttered.

The agent smiled as if this were agreement. “Shall we?”

Alric did not move. Seren did.

That left him standing in the street looking stubborn beside a building large enough to make stubbornness feel childish. After a moment, he followed.

The entrance hall opened into a common room large enough to hold a respectable crowd, or a less respectable crowd with good spacing. Sunlight came in through the front shutters and lay across the floorboards in pale strips. The room was empty now, which somehow made it worse. Empty rooms had a way of offering suggestions.

A long bar ran along one side, polished in places where years of hands had done what cloth had not. Behind it, shelves stood bare. A serving hatch opened toward the kitchen. Two hearths sat at opposite ends of the room, both cold, both large enough to heat the space.

This room did not yet contain barrels. That made it vulnerable.

The agent moved ahead, gesturing with one hand. “The front section is arranged for public business. Common room here. Kitchen through that door. Bar storage behind. Dry goods room beyond the kitchen. There is a small office near the rear stair.”

Alric heard common room, kitchen, bar, storage, office.

The kitchen was better than he wanted it to be. Too large to dismiss. Wide hearth, ovens, preparation tables, scullery, a rear passage for deliveries. It needed cleaning and proper stocking, but it was not ruined. It had been built by someone who understood that feeding people was mostly a matter of movement, heat, and having somewhere to put hot things, preferably before they burned your fingers.

The agent led them past the rear of the common room and through a broad inner doorway. The building opened.

Alric stopped properly this time.

A courtyard sat at the centre of the inn, square and open to the sky. The afternoon light fell cleanly into it, touching stone paving, two benches, and three trees planted in square beds along the far side. They were not large trees, but they had survived long enough to develop the stubborn confidence of things no one had been able to move.

Covered walkways ran around all four sides. Above them, a second level mirrored the first, with wooden railings and doors set at even intervals. Guest rooms faced inward. All of them. Door after door, each one opening toward the courtyard rather than the street.

The air felt different here. Quieter, despite the city beyond the walls. The square held sound gently. A cart in the street passed outside and became a softened rumble. Somewhere above, a loose shutter tapped once and settled.

Alric stepped forward. His first thought was that it was too much. His second was that it had flow. He hated the second thought. The third and final thought was that it was beautiful.

The agent’s voice took on the pleased tone of a man revealing the expensive part. “The guest wings run around the courtyard. The covered walkways keep the rooms accessible in poor weather. There are four rooms per wing on the ground floor, and the same above.”

Alric nodded, not really listening, he was entranced by it. This was the sort of home he would never have afforded in his previous life. A voice in the back of his mind reminded it was far too big but he ignored it. He began counting before he could stop himself. Four along the left. Four along the back. Four along the right. Two floors.

The front wing had its own arrangement because of the common room below and the owner’s apartment above, but the doors continued around with very little mercy.

“Twenty-four,” he said.

The agent brightened. “Yes. Twenty-four guest rooms. Although, guest rooms wouldn’t be the words I’d use. Each has a small lounge and separate quarters.”

Alric looked at Seren.

Seren was studying the upper walkway, not him. That irritated him more than if she had been smug.

“Twenty-four, what? Apartments?” he repeated.

“I, don’t know that word?” the agent said.

Alric looked back at the doors.

Twenty-four rooms. It's a little absurd. This is like owning an apartment block, but wow, it's beautiful.

He had no need for twenty-four rooms. One man did not need twenty-four rooms unless something had gone badly wrong in a way involving heirs, cousins, or a belief in indoor hunting.

Then the word atelier arrived in his mind, unwelcome and carrying its own chair.

He could put Margaret’s girls somewhere safe if needed, if he went ahead with it. He could house visiting craftsmen. He could keep staff on site. He could host suppliers without them sleeping over barrels. He could keep one room for ledgers, one for maps, one for private meetings, one for things that should not be left where Moreen could see them. He could move finished goods. He could separate people from production. He could stop using beds as temporary warehouse shelves.

Twenty-four rooms remained too many. It was only that too many no longer meant useless. It took a moment before Alric realised he was performing mental gymnastics to justify the purchase.

I don't need to do that. I just like it. That's enough.

Seren looked at him then. He glanced away but he was sure she saw his approval.

“It will do for size,” he said.

The agent smiled. Seren gave a small nod and turned away with a smile.

They began the tour of the guest rooms.

The first door opened with a slight complaint from the hinges. Inside was what Alric really would have thought of as a small apartment. An open lounge area with four connected rooms. One room could be described as a master suite, the others smaller for servants and staff.

The next suite was much the same, though one bed was missing, and the wardrobe had a lean to it that suggested firewood in its near future. The third had three beds. The fourth had none, only bare floorboards and hooks along one wall.

He looked down at the courtyard again. The covered walkway meant barrels could be moved in bad weather.

He closed his eyes briefly.

They went up to the second floor. The staircase was broad and set near the front corner, with a turn halfway up. It did not creak in the way old stairs sometimes did when preparing to become stories told after funerals. The upper walkway gave a clear view down into the courtyard. From there, the inn looked even larger, doors opening onto doors, space arranged around the hollow centre.

The agent talked about respectable travellers, merchant families, visiting officials.

Alric looked at doorways and thought about locks. A gust of wind moved through the courtyard and stirred dust along the rail.

He put a hand on the wood. It was solid. It felt good. The rear of the property held the stables.

Alric had intended to dislike them on principle, but the stables made that difficult. There were stalls for several horses, a tack room, a loft, and a yard large enough to turn a wagon without needing prayer. Beyond it, a carriage shed stood along the wall, with space for two carriages if the drivers had not been raised by wolves.

There was also a well. Alric glanced at it and glanced toward the river. He looked down the well, unable to see the bottom. The well mechanism made up for that by having a lot of rope.

The agent gestured. “The carriage access is through the rear lane. Deliveries need not come through the main entrance. There is a small hallway connecting the stables to the courtyard that can be barred from the inside.”

Alric looked at the gate. A wagon could come in. Be unloaded. Leave without blocking the street.

Goods could move from the yard to the passage, from there into storage, or through to the courtyard if needed.

They returned inside and went up the front stair to the owner’s apartment.

The apartment occupied most of the upper floor above the common room. It was not grand in the noble sense. It had no carved ceiling, no painted panels, no corridor designed purely to make visitors feel small. But it was large, private, and arranged for a family that had once expected life to continue in several directions.

There was a sitting room overlooking the street. A bedroom large enough for a proper bed and several pieces of furniture. A smaller room beside it. Storage cupboards. A steaming room for cleaning.

Then came the smaller rooms. One. Two. Three.

Narrow, bright rooms with low shelves, scuffed floors, and marks on one doorframe where someone had measured height over years.

Alric stopped in the third doorway.

The room was empty except for a small bed pushed against the wall and a faded cloth curtain hanging beside the window. The curtain had little stitched birds along the bottom edge. Someone had cared enough to make them unevenly.

He was suddenly aware that this had been a home.

Not merely a business with beds. A home. People had grown here, eaten here, run through these corridors, been shouted at for running through these corridors, left marks in doorframes and forgotten toys under beds.

Now he was standing there wondering whether the floor could take storage shelves. The thought made him feel mildly monstrous.

Behind him, Seren stopped. She did not say anything at first.

The agent, mercifully, was in the sitting room explaining something about window shutters to no one in particular.

Alric looked away from the curtain that was fluttering by the shutter. Someone had thought to stitch small birds into the bottom. “There are a lot of small rooms.” he said.

“There are.” Seren said nodding.

“Too many.”

“For you, yes.” After a moment she said, “They could be used for records. Cloth. Spare bedding. Things that should not be in the common room.”

“Things?”

“Things,” she said.

Alric narrowed his eyes. “You mean barrels.”

“I did not say barrels.”

“You were thinking barrels.” Alric said as he began laughing.

“I was thinking you should not put barrels in children’s rooms.” Seren turned away, putting a hand over her mouth, but her chest moved.

“You also said records. You are just as bad.” Alric said in a mock accusing tone.

Seren looked at the faded curtain. “I guess we are then,” she said with a smile.

The cellar entrance was near the kitchen at the front, down a stone stair with a wooden door at the bottom. The air cooled as they descended. The smell changed from dust and old timber to earth, stone, and the faint stale trace of metal.

The cellar was large. Not merely useful large. Dangerously large.

It stretched beneath the courtyard in long vaulted sections, pillars placed at intervals to support the weight above. Shelving lined some walls. Hooks hung from beams. A row of smaller doors opened off the main space, each leading to narrow rooms that had once held stores, casks, tools, or staff belongings.

Alric walked in slowly.

The temperature was steady. Cool enough for storage. Dry enough that the walls did not glisten. The floor was stone. The ceiling was low in places but not badly so.

The current inn cellar had surrendered after eight barrels. This one would require a campaign. Alric suspected it would lose anyway.

Seren’s shoes clicked softly on the stone behind him.

The agent lifted a lantern from a hook and lit it, making the far end of the cellar appear in pieces. “The staff quarters are through here. Not luxurious, of course, but practical. The old owner kept kitchen staff and stable boys below, with senior staff in rooms above when occupancy allowed.”

Alric turned. “Staff quarters?”

The agent opened one of the side doors.

Inside was a narrow room with two bed frames, a small chest, and a shelf fixed into the wall. There was a vent set high near the ceiling. Another room beyond it had space for three. A third was empty but had hooks, a washstand, and a narrow slit of light from somewhere above.

“I’m not putting staff in the cellar.”

The agent’s face developed the cautious look of a man trying to find out whether he had just lost a sale by describing standard practice.

Seren stepped closer, looking into the room herself. “They are dry.”

“We aren’t running this as an inn, we’ll put people in proper rooms.” Alric said glancing at her.

She stepped into the room, checked the vent, then the wall, then the floor. “These are better than some rooms I had in the past you know.”

“I’m not putting someone through that, you know that too.”

“No,” Seren said, but this time there was no bite in it. “But it is what many people expect. If you give them proper beds, heat, food, pay, and days where no one screams at them over burnt porridge, they will be like the brewery staff and almost revere you.”

Alric looked around the small room again. He still disliked the idea of someone living in a cellar. He disliked that she was probably right more.

“How many staff rooms?” he asked.

The agent recovered quickly. “Six small rooms, two larger. Some can be converted back to stores if preferred.”

“They will be converted,” Alric said.

Seren turned her head slightly to hide the smile. Alric walked back into the main cellar space.

The large space opened around him again. It was too useful to ignore. There was room here for wine. Spirit later. Beer, if needed. Secure storage. Separate areas. A place for finished goods, away from the still house, away from the growing impression that every building he touched eventually developed a drinking problem.

He stopped near one of the pillars and rested a hand against the stone. Cool. Dry. Solid.

Of course it is. Of course the absurd inn has such a good cellar.

Somewhere above them, wood shifted as the building settled. It was a small sound, ordinary and harmless, but it moved through the stone.

They finished the tour in the storage rooms behind the kitchen. One had shelves. One had hooks. One had a drain in the floor, which made Alric stop for long enough that Seren noticed and the agent began describing the previous owner’s cleaning arrangements with unnecessary enthusiasm.

There was a small office near the rear stair, dusty but usable. Alric stood inside it and immediately envisioned Vale sitting at the desk with ledgers stacked high enough to be considered a defensive structure.

He left the office quickly.

Back in the courtyard, the light had shifted. The upper railings cast narrow shadows across the paving. The trees moved faintly in the breeze, their leaves making a dry whisper above the stone benches.

The agent stood near the doorway to the common room, hands folded, waiting in the manner of a man who knew silence could sometimes earn more than speech.

He had wanted a house. A place to live. Somewhere that was not full of barrels, royal summons, charcoal drawings, and dwarves claiming ownership of buildings by beginning demolition.

A place that could hold staff, rooms, goods, horses, ledgers, kitchens, arguments, mistakes, and whatever new absurd category his life invented next.

It was not what he would have asked for. Unfortunately, it was closer to what he needed.

“We’ll take it,” he said.

Today has been a great day.
