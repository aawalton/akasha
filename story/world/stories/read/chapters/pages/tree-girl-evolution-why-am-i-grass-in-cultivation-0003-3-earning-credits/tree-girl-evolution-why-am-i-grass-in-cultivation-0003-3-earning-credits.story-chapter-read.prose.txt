The world hadn’t changed much. Still blurry. Still too green. Still… unfairly huge. The skyscraper-grasses around me stood tall, five times my height at least. Imagine being reborn as the smallest kid in kindergarten—except everyone else was photosynthesizing colossi.

…Although. Maybe I had grown? Yesterday my stalk ached just standing upright, today it felt only mildly stiff. A whisper of height gained, perhaps half a centimeter at best. Great. At this rate I’d reach dandelion height by the time the next dynasty fell.

It’s like the universe sat down and brainstormed the least dignified ways to sustain me and picked the one that sounded like a fetish. Still, the water was cool and clean, sliding down into every fiber of me. Along with it came that mineral tang—the earthy broth of soil.

That left my poor leaf.

Ah, my one proud blade—my green sword—had not fared well. Yesterday’s stomp had snapped it in half. Now that I could focus without writhing in pain, I finally looked closely: half of it lay curled like a corpse at my feet. The living part that remained was short, stubby, and covered in a gel-like liquid slicked across the wound. When I tried to flex, a sharp ache answered, squirting more of the stuff out like an unfunny ketchup bottle. Yeah—this leaf was out of commission.

So, Roots it was.

Tempting. But digging down felt… wasteful. Because look at what I had literally right beside me: my own broken half-leaf, gift-wrapped and waiting. And a neighbor’s limp body, already yellowing. All-you-can-eat buffet one inch away, and I’m supposed to dig down into mud like a fool? Please. Resources lay above me.

So I cheated.

Instead of drilling deeper, I forced my roots upward. Slow, grating, painful. The soil protested with every shove. Bending was worse. Imagine trying to steer a broom handle that doesn’t want to bend but insists on cracking instead. My roots creaked and strained, but millimeter by millimeter, I angled them toward the surface.

I named him Pebble-Biter. Because, well, he looked like a pebble… until he opened those jaws.

Beetles don’t eat grass, right? I tried to be polite—don’t laugh—wriggling my half-working leaf like a hand raised in greeting. Hello, fellow tiny survivor! My silent wave earned nothing but a slow, suspicious gleam from his beady eyes. He paused, his jaws twitching in my direction as if weighing whether grass could be friend, food, or background.

A chill ran through my roots. I didn’t like it. Not one bit.

And when he turned toward my neighbor’s corpse—the one I’d already half-claimed as my personal buffet—a different feeling surged up. Not fear. Not disgust. But anger.

That’s mine.

I needed a plan. He was small—barely the size of my snapped leaf—but the way his jaws carved through tissue made my imaginative stomach churn.

Roots, then. My only weapons.

I began to shift them, slow and careful, turning from delicate straws into crude spears. It was agony; bending underground was like forcing stiff wires through a wall of sand. Still, one by one, I pushed half my roots off their normal path, redirecting them toward Pebble-Biter’s scuttling legs.

Meanwhile, he didn’t stop eating. Half my neighbor disappeared in less time than it had taken me to grow in the first place. I couldn’t fathom how such a tiny body stored so much hunger.
