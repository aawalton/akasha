The sun hung high in the late morning sky, but its light did nothing to warm the hollow shell that had once been the Silverwater mansion.

We crested the paved hill in silence. No horns. No announcements. Only the crunch of our boots through soot and cinders as we stepped into the mansion’s outer courtyard.

What remained of the estate looked less like ruins and more like a cauterized wound. The once-grand facade had melted into itself, scorched with deep, unnatural burns. Stone and glass fused into warped black streaks, twisted as if recoiling from the flames that had devoured them. Ash drifted lazily in the air, disturbed by our presence despite the fire having died weeks ago.

It was clear this destruction hadn’t come from any ordinary blaze.

My gaze lingered on the bones of the entry archway, its keystone drooping like melted wax. This was undoubtedly the mark of a progenitor’s Flames of Nothingness—a power I had hoped never to witness again. These flames didn’t flicker in wind or snuff under water. They consumed everything—living or not—with a hunger so absolute there was no collateral damage. Only obliteration. As if punishing the living for existing… and the inanimate for daring to be still.

“By the Gods,” the priestess murmured behind me, folding her arms over her chest in the shape of an X. “What in all the worlds is this creature you hunt, Duchess?”

I didn’t answer.

Unlike the random chaos left behind in the city, the mansion still pulsed with lingering ancient power.

As we pressed deeper into the estate, the temperature quickly climbed. The cold gave way to sweating heat. I ignored the jagged warnings from the ground beneath us, each footstep echoing too long and too loud. The mansion had been hollowed out like a carcass, ribbed with collapsed arches and scorched rafters. A fountain in the courtyard was frozen mid-collapse despite the heat, its water crystallized into black glass, as though the laws of nature had been rewritten.

My eyes drifted to the space where the heavy entrance doors had once loomed. Now, only a shadowed threshold remained, wide, gaping, and hesitant to let light pass.

I had only vague memories of the place, nothing but vague memories of hallways and faceless maids for the six months I'd spent in the estate after inhabiting Lilliana's body. But as I looked at the broken remains of the Silverwater Estate, fragments of Lilliana's memories surged to the forefront of my mind.

Bruises and blood on marble stairs. Broken bones in the finely trimmed courtyard. Beatings masked as lessons. The baron’s cold, calculating smile as maids locked her in cramped, dark closets to keep her "out of the way."

My jaw clenched under the weight of those memories and the raw emotion that accompanied them.

Good, she seemed to say. I’m glad it’s gone.

“It was definitely here,” I said flatly.

“Your Grace, is it possible the beast is still here?” Dame Carla asked. I hadn’t gotten a good look at her the previous night, so I took a moment now: a few inches taller than me, with a lithe, medic’s frame and deep-set blue eyes. Brunette.

“No,” I said, shaking my head. “It allowed itself to be imprisoned. I don’t know why, but the Silverwaters were fools to believe their little barony could contain a creature of such power.” I glanced at the priestess. “When it accomplished whatever it set out to do, it freed itself."

Tension thickened despite my words, the further we trekked into the estate's courtyard, sweat beginning to swamp us with each step. The knights pressed forward, a half-dozen suddenly jogging ahead into a new formation, while those behind moved to circle me protectively.

“I apologize, Your Grace,” said their captain. I searched my memories for his name, but came up only with the letter M. “I know you prefer to lead, and that you outmatch any of us individually, but let us do this. If something lies within that even you can’t handle, we’ll hold it off. Long enough for you to escape.”

I nearly told him that anything capable of threatening me would tear through them like wet parchment, but a glance from Dralos stayed my tongue. This was, after all, partially a training exercise for the knights.

Even so, their loyalty unsettled me. These weren’t the type of knights I had experience with. Back when I’d been Queen, those under my command had treated me like one of their own, if of a higher rank. Protecting me had never been part of the arrangement.

I had always led the charge. And those who followed had trusted my strength to spear through enemy lines.

After arriving in Graedon, I'd dealt with the Silverwater knights. Other than the Silverwater knight captain and Dame Shael, the rest had treated me even worse than back in Ordite.

A disposable tool, I thought bitterly.

“Fine,” I said. “But don’t get in my way. I don’t sense anything inside, but if I engage something, fall back. Understood? I don't need a bunch of knights dying for fodder."

The captain saluted. “Understood, Your Grace.”

I wasn’t convinced he understood at all, judging by the pride glinting in his eyes. But I let it go.

“What are you hoping to find?” Dralos asked as he slipped between two knights and fell in step beside me. We stepped through the shattered doorway into what was left of the main foyer.

I swept my hand, igniting my heart core, to conjure more of the silver spheres. They hovered in the air, casting pale moonlight across the destruction.

“I want to know what made them think they could imprison a creature like that,” I said, recalling the time Lilliana had been locked in the dungeon for speaking up about one of Brian’s beatings. I directed the knights toward the stairwell that descended into the estate’s dungeons. “While the Baroness and her children were many things, blind fools wasn’t one of them.”

The entrance to the dungeon stairwell came quickly, half-buried beneath rubble and scorched beams. Two knights strained to pry the warped cellar door from its stone frame. The iron hinges squealed like a wounded animal. A rush of stale air spilled out, dry and sour with dust, decay… and something older.

I descended first, ignoring the knights’ calls behind me. I ducked under a half-melted arch where stone had fused with wood, heart core casting more silver orbs ahead into the black.

“The burying feels deliberate,” Josephine said at my shoulder. “Like at the Sanctum.”

“I’d imagine so.” My boot crunched over rubble as we descended. “Wouldn’t you destroy the place you were imprisoned in?”

“If it’s as strong as you say, would it even care?”

I snorted. “Power doesn’t make you immune to pain. Whatever its reason for allowing capture, it was still captured. And likely tortured.”

“How could they torture something they couldn’t hurt?” Dralos asked.

“That’s what we’re here to find out.”

Dungeons, in my experience, all shared the same shape: a long descent beneath the earth, far enough that screams wouldn’t reach polite company. The air was always moist and clinging. The silence, heavy. At the bottom, a cramped foyer opened into a corridor lined with stone cells—barely more than pits with doors. If you were lucky, you got a bucket.

This one had six cells. Three on each side. The bars were thick, carved with crude containment runes. When I brushed one, it surged forward—hungering for energy—only to recoil, somehow severed from its source of power and unable to absorb my own. Many bars had melted, twisted into slag. Others had been utterly vaporized by the black fire.

Only two cells retained their bolted-down beds. The others held nothing but ash and scorch marks.

At the far end loomed a steel door, bent in half and hanging from a single hinge. Hundreds of tiny runes in gold and silver were etched into its face.

The precious metals had liquified and run down the steel face like tears.

I stepped closer, then channeled a pulse of heart energy and ripped the door free. It clanged to the ground, revealing the final room.

Calling it a cell was generous. It was a containment chamber. A cage for a beast to be tortured and studied.

Chains hung broken from the walls, fused into the floor where the anchors had shattered. Black fire had scorched a spiral through the space, its residue still crackling faintly in the air. But there was no sign of life. No decay. No blood.

“A message?” I muttered, kneeling beside one of the flame marks, fingers tracing the burn. The air buzzed around my skin.

Dralos knelt beside me. “Could this have actually held it?”

“Not a chance,” I said, standing. “You saw how easily I tore off the door. I’m not even close to its power.”

“Maybe the chains were anti-energy. Anti-magic,” Josephine offered.

I shrugged. Aside from scorch marks and ruined chains, the room held no clues. No tools. No remains. Just emptiness.

Frustrated, I planted my hands on my hips and threw my head back with a sigh.

That’s when I saw it.

A perfect circle of black ash streaked the ceiling and, within it, a message. Not in Lysorian. Not in any tongue known to Graedon. But I recognized it instantly.

The language of the Aedronir.

Where Lilliana’s memories guided my understanding of this world's common tongue, I read the current words plainly. They burned into my mind with perfect clarity:

Lilith, where are you? I’m coming to find you and bring you home. If you see this, give me a sign.
