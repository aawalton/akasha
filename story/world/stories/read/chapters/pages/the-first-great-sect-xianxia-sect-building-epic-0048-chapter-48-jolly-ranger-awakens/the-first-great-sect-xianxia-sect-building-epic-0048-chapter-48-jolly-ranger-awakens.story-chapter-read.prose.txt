If not for her cousin grabbing her by the wrist and stopping her, Hua may very well have walked out of her tent with her breasts exposed for all the world to see. It would constitute a small world, yes, amounting to no more than a quarter of her camp, but for someone who could not name most of the people who served her, having them know the specific curve of her breasts and colour of her aureoles seemed an intimacy too great.

Hua gave her cousin a muffled thanks and easily accepted his huffed amusement, throwing on robes haphazardly. So long as she was covered, that was enough.

The healing tent was buzzing with activity. Hua made her way through easily, only having to shove aside one mortal—by their scantily clad nature, one of the soldiers or servants was fucking her which was truly tasteless; these refugees were barely better than savages—that Weiji saved from landing in the mud. On any other day, Hua would have made a production of her annoyance but today, she had reason to be in a forgiving mood.

Young Ren allowed her into the medical tent holding the Jolly Ranger.

Past his shoulder, she was Wu Yan, unfortunately also present in the room, standing over the not-so-enthused Jolly Ranger. Swaddled in blankets, the Ranger looked a small woman. The shock of what she had been through detracted somewhat from her natural looks. Vitality and clear skin were the greatest signifiers of her beauty. Had she not been injured, Hua would be very surprised to find her unmarried.

What care have I that she should be unmarried? Hua thought, staring at the bruises that might have detracted from the Ranger’s beauty were she a fickle person.

“How is she?” Hua asked calmly, simply.

Young Ren gave her the same unimpressed look he did whenever she hid an injury. “She will likely make a full recovery barring the more… permanent injuries.”

“So, I can see her, yes?”

He did it with a great sigh. He wasn’t much older than her and he’d practised his healing skills on her. His stitches were always crooked. The healing tent was kept in far neater condition than his stitches could ever dream to be.

The healer looked past Hua, to where her cousin was looming. Whatever she saw made the healer sigh. “Yes, go right ahead.”

As if his disapproval would stop her. Hua came to sit beside the Ranger, taking up a stool. She ignored the shaman and offered the Ranger a confident nod and a gentle smile.

“This lord greets you. I am lord Liao Weilong, heir to the Radiant Lightning Body, and you are within a camp manned by my soldiers and servants. You are safe now. Be at ease.”

“Liao,” she rasped, eyes bright with fever. Her Cultivation hadn’t been shattered. That was the only reason she was as lucid as she was. “Why did you not stand sentinel? Why did you let us all die?”

Hua swallowed, throat suddenly tight. “This lord is here now and he has vowed to cleanse this land of bandits. He will not rest until their heads are mounted on spikes.”

“Heard her vow and it was a true vowing she made,” the shaman needlessly said, stealing the Ranger’s limited attention. Hua shot her a glare.

The Ranger’s lips curled in a violent smile. Then, she coughed a wet, bloody cough. Hua drew a napkin and wiped the Ranger’s face clean. Her cheekbones were truly beautiful. She resisted the urge to trace the gentle slop of her cheek.

“Tell me where they are so that I can avenge you.” Hua let a hint of her lightning Qi seep past her flesh, enough so that the Ranger could hear the truth in her words. “Tell me, and I will make a ruin of them.”

“South,” the woman rasped. Hua gestured and water was brought. The Ranger drank with assistance. “Camp. They have a camp there. We tracked them to it. Hunted them all the way where three streams met.”

The Ranger reached out, southward, towards her duty even with missing fingers. What honour, Hua thought. Hua reached back and held her bandaged hand gently, ignoring the shaman’s gasp. Her first and middle fingers were cut off, which made Hua suspect her to be an archer, and Kang’s viciousness particularly vile. Hua would make him suffer for doing this.

“You accepted a hundred silver taels to hunt down this Kang.”

“Yes. Contract. That’s the promise. Never failed… until now. Damn it, Tong is gone. They’re all gone now.”

“Your bravery is to be commended,” she said gently, squeezing her hand, startled when tears beaded at the corners of the Ranger’s dark eyes. “I promise, it is worthy, what you did. But what makes you so different? No Cultivator would accept so little money.”

“The weight of your dharma is greater than the weight of gold. We live and die by our deeds.”

“Mostly dying,” Weiji muttered in the back.

Hua hadn’t realised she had an audience and shot a dark look at him. Could he not see that the Jolly Ranger required kindness? That such a delicate chin had been bruised and eyes blue as summer’s sky were streaked red, puffy with tears, was a travesty, and Weiji lacked any ability to handle something delicate. Oh, but beneath the tears was strength and fury. Her determination enduring in the bleakness of her grief, beautiful in its harshness.

Hua found her easy to admire.

“Tell me how the battle went. Any information will make it easier to avenge your comrades.”

“We went in as we always do. Announced ourselves and demanded Kang face us in honourable combat.”

You were blessed with looks and cultivation, but not smarts, Hua thought, doing everything to keep her expression clear. Honour was not often smart. To live and die by a code was worthy, even if Hua did not believe in that same code.

She could just imagine her, dark hair a veil, thick Qi a mantle, bowstring pulled taut, an arrow to be loosed and take a life. A warrior facing a threat worthy of her honour.

“Then he ordered his men. They fired their arrows at us and then charged us with their numbers.”

“A group of mortals charged a group of Cultivators?” It wasn’t impossible a thought. Numbers could overwhelm a Cultivator. But against a group, that was a degree of bravery that bordered on suicide. “How many of their own Cultivators did they have?”

“I do not know.”

“How can you not know? You were in the battle.”

“I was hit with an arrow first,” the Ranger explained, her words frigid water that cooled Hua to the bone.

“The arrow that hit you in your arm? That arrow? Took you out of the fight?”

“They struck me when I was down. A dozen of them. Before I could do anything, they were upon me.”

“Right, so you were immediately attacked by what sounds like an army. Why didn’t you run off when you realised you were outnumbered?” Hua asked the plain-faced woman, wanting information so she could go kill some people, and maybe have a good time in the process of fulfilling her promise to the Shaman.

“Couldn’t. Not part of the contract. Fair fight, always.”

She blinked, wondering why she was holding onto a foolish cripple. Hua let go of her hand. You were cursed to be a fool with no battle instinct. There were many things Hua could ignore but combat incompetence was pushing it.

“Ah, that is… truly a plan of all time. I hope you forgive this lord for not following it. So somewhere due south is where all your friends died, yes?”

The Ranger choked. The Shaman choked. Someone behind her choked and she looked back to find both Weiji and Liu Xin sharing a look. Dismay and shock. It was perhaps the first time they had ever agreed on something and likely the last. Hua did not like it. When they got in here alongside the healer and Captain Yao all the villagers peeking in through the tent flaps, Hua did not know. She’d probably just been distracted by the Ranger’s story.

“Liao Lord, the Ranger is needing a rest,” Shaman Yan said sharply, glaring at Hua. “Maybe let her sleep.”

“Yes,” she agreed, rising. They walked out, though Hua paused once to look at the woman who might have been interesting. She felt a moment of shame seeing the crumpled expression of the Ranger. Such a shame such beauty was wasted.

“This shaman would hope that the Lord Liao is honourable.

And then she turned on her heel and left.

“Men,” she muttered so low only a Cultivator could hear it, “all the same.”

Hua was bewildered. Shamans were strange creatures indeed.

“My Lord, going alone is unwise,” said the perpetually aggrieved Captain Yao who had only become increasingly disillusioned with his new lord. Thankfully, he had learned there was no hope in arguing with Hua’s whims. “If you must go, take someone with you.”

Weiji, her equally aggrieved deputy, shook his head. Captain Yao nodded with grim resignation and did as he always should have, which was to accommodate her whims.

“As you say, my lord. Should you not return, this captain’s head will be sent to his family for dereliction of duty.”

“Unless this Kang is in the Foundation Establishment, he will be no threat. And if he were, he would have created a true fiefdom, not a camp of roving bandits. My deputy and I are the strongest Cultivators in the west.”

“This captain hopes his lord wears armour and dark clothes.”

Instead of the colours of the sky on a clear day in spring, he did not need to say. Hua changed quickly into a darker set of clothes. She did not have armour, had foolishly considered it unnecessary. She bound her chest, tied her sleeves around her wrists and pants cuffs around her ankles, and joined her cousin outside where he gave her a conical hat to hide their distinct hair that reflected any light source that fell upon it.

With final words of caution, the two slipped into the darkness, running at a sprint as fast as any galloping horse.

South. They would head west through the night, alone and vulnerable. There would be no better chance to ambush her. She was glad to have Weiji with her instead of someone like his grandfather. That would have been a guaranteed betrayal were Hua foolish enough to walk with her back to the elder. But Weiji’s presence was an old comfort.

Hua felt herself slipping into a trance as they dashed through the dark. Lulled by rustling leaves, whistling wind, the pitter-patter of small critters; letting the darkness of the forest envelop her as a lover’s embrace.

She remembered a time in her youth running through a forest like this; tall cypress, wide pine, sky darkened by clouds draped over ancient constellations. Years younger, stars dimmer, running from expectations that bowed her head as surely as the jade pendant she now wore about her neck. Lost in the deep woods, running into the dark hills. Lightning struck, radiance revealing the dark places of the forest. Showing her a way forward between the bone-shuddering rumble of thunder. The enormity of her foolishness was revealed by a home only minutes away, just past a hill, distance inflated by childish sensibility.
