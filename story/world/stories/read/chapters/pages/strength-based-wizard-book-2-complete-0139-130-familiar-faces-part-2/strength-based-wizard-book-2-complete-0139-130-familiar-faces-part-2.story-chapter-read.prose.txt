Chapter 130

Familiar Faces, Part 2

Yup, that’s definitely Sarah—my former fiancé—standing right in front of my apartment building. For half a second, I just stare at her. Not because I can’t believe my eyes. That ship has sailed. That ship sailed, hit an iceberg, got resurrected by System magic, and was then eaten by a shark king before breakfast. I just had a casual sidewalk conversation with Sloth. Compared to that, seeing my ex-fiancée outside my apartment building is almost mundane.

Sarah is exactly as stunning as I remember. She’s tall, with the perfect posture of someone who’d been trained to look flawless during both dance recitals and while riding on horseback. Her long blonde hair is effortlessly pulled away from her face, revealing sharp cheekbones and calm, brilliant blue eyes. I can tell channeling the System’s power has given her eyes an almost frosted sheen.

“Hey, Joe,” she says. Her voice is softer than I had expected. Most of my most salient memories of her were of times of her complaining, about me or the life we had in New York. There was no whining, or anger or embarrassment here. “It’s been a while.”

“Yeah,” I say. “Hey.”

Her eyes move over me once. I notice because I used to notice everything she did. Especially before it all blew up. The small shifts like the quirk at the corner of her mouth like a mask slipping for the briefest of moments. The little assessments she’d make when we’d see each other after a long day.

The weirdest part of this encounter is what I feel. Or don’t feel. I had expected the old pain to rise up like bile in my throat. The betrayal. The humiliation. The memory of her calling off the engagement months before the wedding and leaving me with a life that felt like it had been unplugged. I had even expected longing, maybe. Anger, most likely. That terrible ache of wanting someone who decided you were not enough, and the guilt and shame for having those feelings at all.

There’s nothing like that though. In fact, there’s almost nothing. Sure, she’s still the last person I want to see. But the past we shared together all feels so… Small now. Insignificant. I feel annoyed that I even need to confront the source of all that pain I had to deal with. Mostly, I feel tired. And suspicious. It’s not my [Perception] or [Aura Sense], but something is tickling the back of my mind, screaming that this doesn’t pass the sniff test.

“What are you doing here?” I ask.

Sarah’s mouth tightens by a fraction. “Pegasus Guild business,” she says.

Of course, I think. What else would bring the Princess of Greenwich all the way to the humble shores of Lake Erie? I appreciate the candor. Straight to the chase.

“Is there somewhere private we can speak?” she asks.

My gaze flicks toward the glass doors of my building. My apartment is upstairs. And so is Jelly Boy. Jelly Boy, who knows all about Sarah. I told him too much during a three-hour grief spiral one night after he convinced me to share my entire backstory. I may have described Sarah as “the woman who detonated my old life.” I may have said she broke my heart. I may have been dramatic. Jelly Boy absorbed and dissolved a throw pillow in solidarity, and vibrated with angry pulses that indicated she and the slime would be blood enemies.

So, no, I am not bringing Sarah Zorbas into my apartment. Because there is a nonzero chance my slime roommate sees her, interprets her presence as a hostile incursion, and magically nukes my ex-fiancée before she can say “Pegasus Guild.”  And then I have to explain to Jerome why a Pegasus Guild’s Captain got dissolved in my living room by the blue slime I share a living space with. No thank you.

I withdraw the Full Metal Staff from my Inventory. The barbell appears in my grasp in a brief flash of pixelated light. I instantly drop it. It stops several inches above the sidewalk, hovering.

Sarah looks down at it, eyebrow raised.

“Yeah,” I say. “Sure. Hop on.”

Her eyes lift back to mine.

“Hop on?”

“Unless you have a flight or levitation Skill.”

“I don’t,” she says.

“Then yeah. Hop on.”

A man walking past slows slightly, sees the floating barbell, sees me, sees Sarah, then decides with the survival instincts of a city pigeon that he has somewhere else to be. And he’s suddenly very late.

I step onto the staff first. It dips under my weight, then steadies. The hum rises through the soles of my sneakers, familiar and strong. I’ve gotten good at this. Standard flight doesn’t require my cape’s increased balance capabilities anymore, though active combat is a different story entirely. I can balance on the staff through turns, acceleration, gusts, even abrupt stops. But I’ve never flown with anyone other than Jelly Boy. Jelly Boy is a blob. Sarah is not a blob. She has real weight, and a slip and pull by her could send us both falling off. So I decide to equip my cape, anyway. It materializes around my shoulders with a soft snap, settling like it was always there. Balance flows through me, subtle and immediate, as if the world itself just widened the black iron bar of my staff.

That earns the faintest hint of a smile. She steps onto the staff behind me. Her balance is good. She doesn’t wobble. Exactly what I should expect from someone who spent years of training in ballet, gymnastics, and horseback riding. She reaches forward and grabs hold of my cape. Not me. The cape. The move is subtle, but deliberate. It sends a clear message: she’s not here to send any wrong messages. How considerate, I think.

I look back over my shoulder. “Careful. It’s indestructible, so I don’t want you falling off and taking my head with you.”

Sarah gives me a flat look. “I won’t fall.”

I face forward, chuckling. “Well don’t jump, either. And hold on.”

The staff rises. The sidewalk drops away beneath us. Someone near the entrance to my building gasps. I wave an apology, as I’m not technically in a permitted flying objects zone. Wind catches my hair and snaps the cape behind me. Sarah’s grip tightens. Not panic-tight, but she definitely feels the acceleration. We glide upward along the face of the building, windows flashing past, our reflections appearing and disappearing. I angle us around the side of the building, then up toward the private rooftop.

I slow the staff over the roof. Sarah lets go of the cape and leaps off first. She lands lightly on the rooftop surface, knees bending just slightly. I step off after her. The staff hovers for another heartbeat, then I deposit it back into Inventory. The cape follows, folding out of existence around my shoulders.

Sarah looks around, taking in the private space and the view of Cleveland’s lakefront in the distance. Sarah stands near the edge of the roof, hair moving in the wind, and turns back to face me. I keep a few feet between us.

“All right,” I say. “What does Pegasus want to talk about? I’ve already provided my report of what happened during the Joint Guild Exercise to the Pegasus agents who came to Harvest HQ.”

Sarah meets my eyes. The air between us tightens.

“Er… Was that rude?” I clear my throat. “Let me re-start. Want anything to drink?” I step towards a minifridge I keep on the roof, stocked with waters and N.A. seltzers.

“No. Thank you.” Sarah looks at me across the rooftop, wind pulling loose strands of pale hair across her face. For a second, neither of us moves. The private rooftop suddenly feels too small. Sarah folds her hands in front of her, leaning against the rooftop’s edge.

“Silver sent me,” she says.

“Yeah, I would assume so if you’re hear on Guild business. He is your boss.”

“No, Joe. He sent me personally. He wants me to recruit you to Pegasus,” Sarah continues. “And to join one of our parties for Stage Two of the Game.”

I open my mouth. The rejection is already loaded. It is not polite. It begins with “absolutely” and ends with a word Jerome would underline in red if this was an official Harvest Guild response. Sarah raises one hand before I can fire it.

“I’m here to complete the task he gave me,” she says. “But I’m not here to actually recruit you. Only passing along the message. I’m well aware that the prospect of you joining Pegasus is close to zero.”

I stop.

The wind moves between us.

“What?” is all I can say.

“I have no intention of trying to convince you to join Pegasus,” she says. “And I’m not going to deceive you. Not about this.”

I stare at her.

My brain, which has already had to process running into Sloth in yoga pants today, makes a sad little grinding noise. Sarah may have broken my brain entirely. I can feel the metaphorical smoke coming from my ears.

Sarah looks away, face twisted in thought, before turning back to meet my eyes again.

“I’ve been in Cleveland for a couple days,” she says. “Trying to decide how to approach this.”

“You flew here days ago to not recruit me?” It means Silver must have given the order as soon as news of the demon attack broke to national news outlets.

“I flew here because Silver ordered me to. I stayed because I wasn’t ready to see you.”

That hits me harder than I could have anticipated, like someone ripping open a drawer I thought I’d nailed shut.

Sarah takes a breath. “I’m sorry, Joe.”

My body reacts before my emotions do. My shoulders tense. My grip tightens around the cold brew I am somehow still holding. Ice rattles inside the plastic cup. I place it onto the ledge of the rooftop.

“I know that doesn’t fix anything,” she says. “I know it doesn’t undo what I did. But I wanted to say it to your face.”

I say nothing. Because if I speak too quickly, I might say something mean. Or worse, something honest.

Sarah’s eyes lower for a moment, then lift again. “I never loved you the way I should have,” she says. “The way you deserved. The way I could never.”

There it is. A sentence that walks up, takes off its shoes, and stomps directly onto the old bruise.

“I cared about you,” she continues. “I liked being with you. For most of the time we were together, I really did. But I didn’t love you. Not really. And I should have ended things long before we were engaged.”

My head is spinning. I open my mouth but nothing comes out.

“I was shitty to you,” she says.

The word sounds strange coming from her. Did I have a stroke? I think. It’s the only explanation for what I’m hearing.

Sarah goes on. “My family got in my head. The talk about me being too good for you. About what I deserved. What kind of man I should marry. What kind of family I should marry into. I let it justify everything I already wanted to do… that I already did...”

She gives a small, humorless laugh. I remember her mother’s smile. I hate that I remember it so clearly. That polished, aristocratic little curve of the mouth when she looked at me across dinner tables. Like I was a stain on a tablecloth someone important might notice. Her father asking questions about my career in the tone people use to discuss used cars with flood damage. Cousins who would snigger behind my back when they thought I couldn’t notice them. Or who didn’t care if I noticed, anyway.  I remember Sarah not defending me. I remember making excuses for her. She’s under pressure. Her family is intense. This is just how they are. It’ll get better after the wedding.

Jesus. What an idiot.

Pain rises. Old, rotten pain. I remember the aftermath. Not one moment. A blur of little heartbreaks. Our relationship cracking before it shattered in one miraculous moment. The spiraling thoughts, screaming in my head. Then the silence.

My apartment in New York feeling too quiet. Her things gone. Her side of the closet empty. My phone face down because every vibration made my heart climb into my throat. Work becoming impossible. Sleep becoming a faint memory, and then becoming a fugue state that got me through the day. Food tasting like cardboard. Looking in the mirror and seeing a husk in a dress shirt.

A husk with a good job.

Then losing the job.

Then Dad showing up to my apartment with his car ready to load my life into the backseat and drive back to Cleveland.

And finally returning home, to cram the remnants of my life into my parent’s basement. Of my parents being so patient with me the entire time. Their love pulling me out one inch at a time. Mom making food I didn’t want and sitting with me until I ate anyway. Dad standing in the garage with me, pretending we were fixing a shelf while he found ways to say I was still worth something without making it sound like therapy.

The Zorbas family thought they were so much better than what my family could offer their daughter.

They weren’t. Not even close.

My family is amazing. My family would do anything for me. And I’ll do anything for them. The pain blossoming in my heart shifts. It shrinks and changes. Like something inside me finally exhales and realizes it doesn’t have to hold the shape of that wound anymore.

Sarah’s voice softens. “I don’t expect you to forgive me. But I wanted to say this. Face-to-face.”

The wind moves across the rooftop. I take a breath. Then another.

“It’s fine,” I say.

Sarah’s brows knit together.

I huff a laugh. “No. That’s not right. It’s not fine. You destroyed me.”

She flinches.

Good, I think. Not because I want to hurt her. Okay, maybe a little. But mostly because it’s true, and the truth deserves to land.

“You destroyed me,” I repeat. “For a while, anyway. I was a shell after you left. A shitty, unemployed shell with enough emotional baggage to qualify as a Spirit Airlines passenger.”

Her mouth twitches despite herself.

“But sometimes,” I say, staring past her at the skyline, “it takes being broken down and starting from square one to really get better. As shitty as it was, I think I needed it to happen.”

The words coming out of my mouth surprise me. I’m not that guy anymore, I realize. The one waiting for Sarah Zorbas and her family to validate him. The one trying to marry into a life that never fit. The one who lost himself long before she started twisting the knife.

“I wasn’t a good partner either,” I admit.

Sarah looks up sharply. I keep going before I lose the thread.

“I was obsessed with work. With money. With proving something. I wanted the Manhattan apartment, the title, the bonus, the wedding, the image. I lost myself before you started acting like I wasn’t worth keeping.”

“That doesn’t excuse what I did,” she says.

“No,” I say. “It doesn’t.”

I meet her eyes. They’re a little wet. Was she holding back tears? I don’t think I’ve ever seen Sarah cry in all the years I knew her.

“I don’t think what you did was right,” I say. “But I can move on.”

She stands very still.

“That’s… not what I expected you to say,” she says.

“Yeah. Me too.”

“Thank you.”

I shrug because anything else feels too big for the moment.

Sarah looks away, out over the edge of the building. Then, she glances down toward the street below. “I think I can just jump down and be fine,” she says.

“You’re really still in the Game?” I ask.

She turns back. “Yeah.”

“Why?” The question comes out sharper than I intend. “You have everything anyone in the world could want. Money. Power. Influence. Silver backing you. Family connections.” I gesture around vaguely. “What’s in it for you?”

Sarah’s expression closes. I see the practiced armor slide into place for a breath, before she tears it back down.

“If I had a wish,” she says slowly, “I could be free.”

“Free? From what?”

She looks down at the city street again. “The shadow of everyone in my life who controls me.”

“I… don’t think I understand,” I admit.

“I have everything at my fingertips,” she says. “But it’s all given to me. Every door opened by someone else. Every opportunity tied to a name, a family friend or business partner, the Guild, a bargain with Silver. Even my power doesn’t feel truly earned, with the resources Silver makes available to power-level.”

She laughs softly. There’s no humor in it.

“I know how that sounds,” she adds

“Privileged? Dramatic? Rich girl problems?”

It should annoy me. It does annoy me. But under that annoyance, there’s a pang of pity. Because a cage is still a cage, even if the bars are gold and the food is better than anything in my fridge. And because for all Sarah’s damage, for all her ambition and cold edges, she looks tired in a way I recognize.

“Yeah…” she sighs.

“I’m not joining Pegasus,” I say.

“I know.”

“I’m not joining your party.”

“I know.”

“And I don’t trust you or your Guild.”

Sarah’s eyes flick to mine.

“Good,” she says.

That is not comforting.

We stand in silence for a moment.

Then she steps toward the edge of the roof, placing one foot on the elevated ledge.

“Sarah,” I say.

She pauses.

I probably should let her go.

“Reshma… Was she the best of Pegasus?”

“The strongest?”

“No…”

Sarah’s eyes glisten with understanding. “Yes, she was the best of us. Certainly the Captains. She really cared.”

I appreciate the honesty in her answer.

“You should know something,” I say. “This next phase isn’t just Earth Participants.”

Her face stills, she takes her foot off the ledge.  “What?”

“There are parties from other Realms,” I say. “Nine parties. Three other Realms, all of which have had a Game of their own. Veterans from prior God Games. They can steal Earth’s Wish.”

Sarah’s eyes widen. Enough to tell me this is all news to her. That Pegasus didn’t know. Or Silver didn’t tell her.

“Where did you hear that?” she asks.

“Someone who would know.”

“Who?”

I shake my head. “No.”

Her jaw tightens, but she doesn’t push.

She looks out over the edge of the roof, processing. I can almost see the calculations forming behind her eyes.  Then she asks, “Have you been contacted by a God yet?”

“No,” I say. “Have you?”

She nods. “Most of our parties have received several offers for Blessings. When the Gods… When they reach out to you, it could be subtle.”

“Subtle?”

“The Gods cannot directly interface with the Game or its Participants,” she says. “Not in the way people assume. They can influence the Game around the edges, mostly through Participants. Some present themselves through visions, symbols, messengers, controlled circumstances. But direct interference is limited. Just keep your eye out.”

“Thanks… I will.”

Sarah’s eyes narrow slightly, but she continues. “The Blessings they can bestow are powerful. If you get offered one, don’t wait too long before accepting. They’ll be a huge advantage in this next Stage of the Game.”

Of course they are. They were also one of the objectives of this next phase of the Game.

“So, you have one? You’ve accepted a Blessing?” I ask.

She nods. “My Party all have the Blessing of the Silent One.”

“The Silent One?”

She shrugs. “He presented himself to many of us as the Golden Father. Not sure how the Gods choose their names.”

Sarah steps backward onto the ledge. The wind lifts her hair. For a split second, she looks like the Sarah from old photographs. Beautiful and untouchable, like a piece of expensive jewelry behind a pane of glass. Then she casually steps off the edge of my rooftop.

“Jesus—”

I rush to the ledge.

She falls fast. At the last second, blue-silver light flickers around her feet. She lands on the sidewalk with a dancer’s grace. Pedestrians nearby jump back. She glances over her shoulder and up at me. For one brief second, we look at each other, eyes locking in a final moment of recognition and appreciation.

Then she turns and strolls away.

I stay at the ledge long after she disappears down the street and around a corner.
