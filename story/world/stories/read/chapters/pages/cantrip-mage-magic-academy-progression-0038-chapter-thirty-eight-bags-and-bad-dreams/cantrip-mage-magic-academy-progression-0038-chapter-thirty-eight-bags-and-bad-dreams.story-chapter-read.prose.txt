“What I don’t understand,” Atta yowled softly from her perch on top of Eola’s sword-belt, “is why you need all this stuff right now. You should be continuing your studies. That’s what will get you through tomorrow’s test safely, not a few pounds of silvered steel.”

“That’s because you’re a silly girl, Atta. Now move,” Eola muttered.

The cat stared balefully at her, no doubt about to say something, but before she could, Eola tugged the belt, and the smallsword’s scabbard clattered across the wooden loft. This time, Atta’s yowl was much louder and much more cat-like.

“Everything, um, going alright in there?” Colin asked from the shut door.

“This Y’aer-cursed cat’s being a pain. As usual.”

Atta yowled a third time, voice dripping with indignation. Eola ignored her and gathered the last of her mother’s spell-sword gear in her arms, then joined the others in the common room.

Right now, it looked less like an academy dorm space and more like a small baron’s armory had belched all over it.

Patrice had her thin-tipped, long-hafted hammer propped against the couch, her plate—freshly un-dented courtesy of the Varin’s Town smithy—sitting upright on the cushion. She’d also covered the study table with a mix of trinkets and talismans, all of them ensorcelled. Eola couldn’t help but stare, and Patrice shrugged. “None of these are at the same level as what Catrine had for your duel. The Clerks aren’t rich enough to give their collateral line or marriage stock that kind of firepower.”

“Ah,” Eola said. “You’re sharing, right?”

“Obviously. We agreed to share everything.”

“We’ve got a good mix of defensive amulets and stuff, though,” Colin said. He’d spread his gear across the floor—Eola and Patrice had adjusted the straps on his padded coat and scale mail, but they could only do so much for the fit without a full rebuild, and none of them could afford that. He also had a leather satchel crammed full of herbs, spellbooks, and all sorts of tools. It looked like it’d be a nightmare to find anything between the leather-sheathed knives, the coil of tangled rope, and two small wooden branches that Eola was pretty sure were supposed to be torches.

“Did you pack enough?” Patrice asked, raising an eyebrow.

“Can never be too careful, Patrice, and they won’t let us come out for more stuff. If we aren’t carrying it, we won’t have it.”

“Better you than me,” Roth said.

“Pot, meet kettle,” Patrice said, rounding on the sullen, dark-haired boy’s pile of gear. He also had a satchel. An assortment of wood and metal instruments poked out of its top, but when Eola opened it—earning a glare but no comments—each of the six was carefully packed into its own, leather-divided section of the bag. She recognized a fiddle, three different flutes, and a pair of linked drums.

Roth also had his armor—leather with rivets, custom-built but unadorned and simple—and his hatchet. That was locked in a leather case that covered its blade, and, to Eola’s disappointment, it wasn’t ensorcelled.

“Okay, this is what we’ve got,” Patrice said after everyone and their familiars were done building their piles. “We’ve got everything Colin could think of—“

“Rude.”

“—and an entire orchestra in a bag—”

“Y’aer take you, Patrice.”

“—so, let’s get all of my goodies divided up.”

When Patrice was done handing out her trinkets, they’d gone through Colin’s bag and reorganized it, and Eola had closed the door on Atta to stop her from stalking and roughhousing with Nera,  Eola had a new necklace that, according to Patrice, could simulate a Safe Shield, but needed to be recharged with Mana between uses. It wasn’t exactly the best of the ensorcelled items Patrice had handed out, but it was more than Eola had ever had before.

The third floor was dangerous enough that even Roth accepted his item—a gaudy ruby pin that he fiddled with until Patrice fastened it to his cloak like a broach—without too much protest. In an ideal world, Eola would have gotten his; it created a temporary illusory duplicate of him, although the First Order spell inside only projected it standing there. And, according to Catrine, it had come with enough Mana for eight uses, but her older brother had used six of them already, and it wasn’t rechargeable.

Colin and Patrice both got warding charms that would protect them from a single ranged attack, with the ability to recharge them through the same ritual Eola’s required.

Then, and only then, did Eola manage to slip out of the common room-turned-armory and into her own bedroom. She’d have just enough time to go over her spells and—hopefully—crack the modifying mark.

Atta glared at Eola from her perch on the desk, and when she tried to sit down and get to work, the blue cat batted her hand. She pulled back, but not before getting a pair of bloody scratches across her fingers. “What was that for?” she asked crossly.

“For interrupting my hunt,” Atta said primly.

“You do know all you familiars are coming—you know what, never mind. I’ll work on my bed, since you’re being a pain.” Eola pulled her parchment pile off her desk and curled up on the small section of the bed that wasn’t covered in notes.

“Oh, I very much know we’re coming, but when we step through that gate, we’ll all be allies. I’ll have to put up with the ferret—“

“Weasel.”

“—but I won’t have to like it. If you’d let me out, I’d show them what I really think of him now.”

“And that’s why you’re staying in here all night—without chicken,” Eola said absently. Her nose was already buried in her spellbook as she reviewed her spells.

In addition to Call and Calm, Chromite Cloth, the assorted non-combat spells she’d learned, and both Ward Watch and Lighting Wing, Eola had learned three new spells.

All of them had combat potential, and only one was First Order.

Sort of.

The first was Spark Shot. She’d used it before, usually as a tool for weeding books before they went feral. But Eola had added Hasty, Reaching, and Stilled to the spell. It still wasn’t a gout of flame like Colin’s ritual, but that wasn’t the intent. Instead of a small flame, Eola got a thin, whiplike column of blueish fire that extended to six feet before fading quickly. She’d experimented with adding a fourth mark and replicating the battle mage spell Flame Pulse, but it raised the Mana required to fill it by far too much, so her Spark Snap was good enough.

She’d also refined Bright Ball, adding Hasty and Echoing to create a spell that, while relatively inexpensive, could be used as a disruptor in battle, and was only a half-second slower than an unmodified Bright Ball. Instead of forming a solid ball of soft light, the Hasty, Echoing version pulsed rapidly between harsh light and nothing for three seconds before burning out. Colin had helped her test it, and he’d had a headache for hours afterward.

Her final spell had been why she was so curious about the spell Ferrin had used in their duel. It was also the only First Order spell she’d added to her repertoire.

Eola had reversed the rune for Swiftblade.

As far as First Order spells went, Swiftblade was on the weaker side. It was also one of the general, attunement-flexible spells she’d gotten from Instructor Tarik. It was actually a poorly-named spell in Eola’s mind. Instead of making the sword cut faster, it sped up the sword-wielder’s reflexes. It didn’t increase her speed or power to compensate, and it required the Peacebonded, Hasty, Personal, and Stilled modifying marks, meaning she couldn’t Hold it for when she needed it. Worse, it was expensive. She estimated that of the eleven bars she could fill, it consumed four or five.

Eola hoped to avoid casting it unless she had to. It was only a part of her kit to help her keep up with Eddarn or another Scholar’s Cultist—or the handful of monsters she hoped they wouldn’t run into. The spell Jeanette Ferrin had used was significantly better, but Eola couldn’t cast Second Order equivalents, so that didn’t matter.

She was almost as ready as she could be.

All that remained was solving the modifying mark and figuring out a place to apply it.

But the mark was incomplete, and in its current state, it simply wasn’t functional. Eola spent almost two hours tinkering with it—far from the thirty minutes she’d hoped it’d take—before a pounding on her door interrupted her. “Eola, it’s getting late. Get some sleep. We’ve got our work cut out for us tomorrow, and if you’re not pulling your weight because you stayed up past your bedtime, this won’t work.”

Eola thought about protesting, but Roth was right. She had to be at her best, and if her best didn’t include the incomplete modifying mark, then so be it. “Fine. Leave the door alone. See you in the morning.”

“Bright and early. That means you, too, Patrice,” Roth said.

“I’ll be up.” Patrice yawned loudly from across the common room, and Eola cleared her bed, changed into a sleeping shift, and curled up under the blankets.

She opened her eyes to a biting wind and the taste of salt in her mouth.

The sky overhead was gray and overcast, and she held the hand of a short—but tall to her—woman, with brilliant blonde hair that shone even in the washed-out morning sun. Their toes sank into the sand as they walked the beach, listening to the gulls overhead and the crashing waves on the nearby cliffs.

She wore nothing but a little girl’s dress, soaked through from playing in the waves, while her mother was dressed similarly, with one exception. Her smallsword hung from her waist, and her free hand kept straying to the hilt.

This was a dream.

It had to be.

Eola had never been to the beach with her mother. She was…not afraid of the open water, but uncomfortable. And she’d been busy more often than not. Lord Card had enemies. Most nobles did, especially members of the Governing Houses. But somehow, Lord Card seemed to have more than usual, and Sienne Lemiene was always investigating, bodyguarding, or attending to the ruler of Greenarbor’s needs.

Sienne had been a wonderful mother. She’d supported Eola in her darkest moments, right after her attunement came in, and she’d done her best to teach her everything she needed to know growing up. Eola loved her with all her heart, and she’d give anything to have her back from the dungeon below Lord Card’s castle. But she’d been the serious parent; she hadn’t had time to be more than that.

So, when she’d gone to the ocean near Greenarbor, it had always been with her dad. Toma had been born in Greenarbor, and he knew the coastlines like his own hand.

So, this couldn’t be real.

More importantly, even if it was real, Eola did not need this right now. She had her own mess to take care of.

Eola tried to push the dream out of her mind, but as she did, her mother’s grip tightened on her hand. She winced, but the woman’s hand didn’t loosen, and Eola found herself dragged off the beach and up to the base of a towering cliff, her body moving on its own. Her mother’s bare feet barely seemed to press against the pine needles and jagged rocks as she climbed a narrow pathway, but Eola found herself whimpering with every step.

They stopped at a thick wooden gate, barely taller than Eola but wider than even her father lying down with his hands over his head. Strips of iron were bolted to it both horizontally and vertically, and no handles or latches could be seen. “Sweet, pay attention,” Sienne said, and Eola’s eyes locked on her mother’s before she even realized she’d done it.

“Magic may always be beyond you, but your father and I both believe in you. Even if you never fill a single spell beyond Child’s Magic, we’ll both be proud of you.”

The dark-haired form of her father materialized, seemingly out of nowhere, and reached down to ruffle her hair. No, not only that. Toma Lemiene pulled a strand of seaweed off Eola’s head and laughed—then rested his palm on her head. “That’s true. There’s nothing that could change that. Nothing at all.”

“If you do follow your heart, Eola, and that path remains sealed despite your best efforts, take up the sword,” Sienne said. “The life of a spell-sword isn’t as glamorous as I’ve told you, but it requires little Ordered Magic. You’ll be a fantastic spell-sword, no matter what happens.”

“Know that your mother and I love you very much,” her father said.

“Goodbye,” her mother said.

Then, before she could protest, the gate opened, revealing nothing but the darkest black Eola had ever seen, and her parents stepped inside. She tried desperately to force her bare feet into a sprint, but her body refused to move.

They vanished into the blackness, and the gate slammed closed as tears ran down Eola’s face. She looked upward, her body tearing her eyes forcibly from the sealed wooden gateway.

Dark stone and mortar loomed high overhead, and a single tower stabbed toward the sky. A flicker of light flashed at its peak, and Eola knew exactly where she was.

The base of Lord Card’s castle.

Right in front of the dungeon.

Eola woke up to the taste of blood and a pair of yellow eyes inches from hers. A paw pressed against her lips, and she bit back a scream.

“Sorry. You were thrashing around and muttering something about ‘Mommy.’ It was quite embarrassing,” Atta said. She pulled her paw away, leaving a thin wet line across Eola’s lip. “You’re better than this.”

“I was dreaming about my parents, but it was all wrong. I never went to the beach with my mother,” Eola said. She ignored her bloody lip and the cat’s biting tone, closing her eyes and focusing on the dream. “Then we walked up the cliffside and to a gate into…into…”

She couldn’t bring herself to finish. She could barely bring herself to breathe.

“Into the dungeon again.” Atta did it for her. It wasn’t a question.

Eola nodded anyway. “Yes. But that’s not how it went. I couldn’t have been more than eight or nine, but they knew I couldn’t do real magic. And they didn’t go into…into the dungeon from the outside. There is no outside entrance—the only way inside is supposed to be through Lord Card’s wine cellar or something. They didn’t go alone, either. They had—“

“Yes, you’ve said this before. A half-dozen other spell-swords,” Atta interrupted. “Eola Lemiene, you had another nightmare, and you know it. I’d tell you to go to sleep, but…”

Eola stared at her familiar’s outstretched paw, and at the spring sunlight already creeping in through the unshuttered window. She nodded, breathing deeply, and disentangled herself from her sheets.

There would be no more sleep. The Library Exam started in less than three hours.

~Colin Tremory~

Eola wasn’t the only one up.

Colin hadn’t slept well either. Truth be told, the combat track and the Library Exam both sounded like a good way to get himself into trouble. He was a pretty decent ritualist, but compared to a proper siege mage, he didn’t have what it took. He was too slow, too precise. That would help him later, but right now…right now, he was the weak point of the team.

Even Patrice was proving to be more effective than him with that Y’aer-cursedly long hammer of hers.

So, instead of sleeping, he’d spent the night staring at the satchel he’d be carrying into the library. His mother had always insisted he be ready for whatever. It was just good business acumen, according to her. He’d been going over it mentally for the forty-sixth time when he’d heard something from Eola’s room. Squeaks, or soft moans, or something.

For a while, Colin ignored it.

He even ignored the screaming.

But as the sixth bell rang and the nightmare cut off suddenly, he decided to knock on Eola’s door. “Eola, you okay in there?”

Silence. Then Eola’s voice, fragile and raw. “I had a nightmare, that’s all. Nothing to be worried about.”

Colin took a breath. She’d been opening up more—coming out of her shell, eating more frequently, and all of that. She still made him a little nervous, though. It was the intensity. That never stopped, and he still had very little idea what was driving her so hard. Everything they’d done together, all the snowball fights and dinners in Varin’s Town, had to fight that underlying intensity. She could hide it from the others, but Colin’s mother had taught him how to see through people, and even she couldn’t hide it from him.

“Would talking about it help?”

More silence.

“I’ll be on the couch. It’s too late to sleep, and even if I had time, I wouldn’t be able to,” Colin said after a minute. “Patrice never wakes up early, and you should have time before Roth’s out and about.”

He started walking away from the door. Eola whispered something from behind it, then cleared her throat. “Give me a few moments.”

“Of course.”

When she came out, dressed in her robes and carrying her sheathed sword and sword-belt, he was waiting. She sat next to him; the couch shifted slightly from her weight. Then she coughed once. “Don’t tell the others any of this.”

“Not a word,” Colin promised.

So she told him. Told him about her parents, and about the dungeon below Lord Card’s castle. About her years after, living with her uncle and convinced that they had to be alive, and how her love of magic was at war with her need to harness it as soon as she could. About the sleepless nights and starving days in the library, trying to find an answer. It went on and on, and by the end of it, Eola Lemiene had changed in Colin’s eyes. She’d gone from a slightly terrifying, anxiety-inspiring thing to…

Eola stopped gradually, trailing off. Her familiar’s yellow eyes locked on Colin from her bedroom door. He ignored them. “You’ve been carrying all that for three years?”

Her shoulders shook. She’d shrunken as she’d talked—she’d never been the biggest girl, but she looked so fragile…so exhausted. Before he even realized he was doing it, Colin had wrapped her in a hug. He’d wanted to do it for a long time, but he’d always imagined it under very different circumstances.

Less wet, sobbing ones.
