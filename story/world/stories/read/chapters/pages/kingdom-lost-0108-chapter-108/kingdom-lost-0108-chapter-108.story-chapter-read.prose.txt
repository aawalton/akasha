Chapter 108

The chest had not moved since the last time she had stood here looking at it. The iron bands sat undisturbed, and the small ward lock at the front of the box was the same small ward lock that had given her a quarter turn and then refused her every approach afterward. Riley let her hand rest on the lid for a moment, fingers spread, the wood cool under her palm. She drew a long slow breath, grabbed the knife and tried again.

She had tried this lock before. She remembered the exact feeling of the turn, the small clean click that was not quite a click, the way the cylinder had given a fraction and then locked itself against the next motion the way a door locks against a wrong key. She had pushed harder for a few seconds and then she had not pushed any further, because the box and lock were old and her dinner knife was the wrong tool for any of it. She had set the knife on the table and walked away.

That had been days ago. Days that had filled themselves with other things. The Hoshin coming through the gate. The two Clawborn led across the yard in irons. The whip riding at her hip now even when she sat. The scroll with the broken seal still where she had left it.

She wondered what was inside.

She did not know whether the chest was a prize. She did know that things from the raiders' loot had turned out to be more than they looked. The whip. The scroll. The book that lay six inches from her hand right now, leather-bound and creased at the spine.

The book, that was the other thing.

She glanced sideways at it. It was the size of a ledger. The cover was dark, dyed brown so long ago that the dye had gone almost black at the corners, and the leather had taken the shine of many readers' hands. It had been on the table for too long not to have been read by now.

She let her eyes go from the book to the chest and back to the book and over to the window. She had a hundred small things she had not done today and a half dozen large things she should have been tending to.

“If I could only clone myself,” she thought. “I delegate and there still isn't enough time.”

She leaned across the table and picked up the knife.

It was where she had left it, the same dinner knife she used before, the blade dull and the handle worn smooth where she had gripped it. She brought it down to the lock and bent over the box and set the tip into the lock. She felt the small inner shape of the mechanism through the steel. She turned the blade against the resistance with a slow even pressure, the kind of pressure her father had taught her was the only pressure that ever worked on small mechanical things, and the lock gave her exactly what it had given her last time. A quarter turn. A small clean almost-click. Then nothing.

She held the pressure for a count of three. Then she eased off and tried again. Same place with the same stop. She tried a different angle on the knife, blade tilted higher, then blade tilted lower. She tried easing the cylinder back the other way to see whether the wards were sprung by reverse motion. Nothing. She tried lifting the lid against the lock with her free hand while she turned the blade, to see whether the cylinder gave under load. Again, nothing.

She worked at it for a little longer, then she set the knife down on the table.

She did not slam it, she placed it. The blade made a small dry sound on the wood. She straightened and rolled her shoulders back and looked at the chest and at the knife, then at the place along the lid where she had been pressing the heel of her hand without realising it. She had been at this long enough that her wrist was sore. She let her hand drop to her side.

The chest was going to wait one more day. Again.

She sat down.

She pulled the leather-bound book toward her across the table with two fingers under the spine and turned it so the cover faced her and opened it.

The first page was blank. The second held the title in a careful hand, with a line of attribution under it that water had smudged at some point. She did not stop to read it. She turned through the early pages, getting the shape of it. Half the book had text. Some pages had drawings, thin pen sketches, the kind you make to remember something you can't keep. A third of the way in, the entries got shorter. Halfway through, the text stopped. The rest was blank, more empty pages than the writer had ever used. A book begun and not finished.

She flipped back toward the middle and stopped on a page and her eyes hardened.

It was a drawing of a Clawborn.

She knew it at once. The hunched shoulders, the heavy brow, the long forward jaw, the small forward-tilted ears. The proportions were exaggerated, but the features were the features she had seen across the yard.

The text under the drawing was in the common tongue. She could read it.

She read it slowly at first, then more easily. The page described a conflict between the Clawborn and the House of Corvessa. The two had quarrelled over mining. Both had significant mining interests. Corvessa held the south. The Clawborn dominated the north. This led to a regional war that ended not in conquest but in a truce. A line had been drawn. The north remained Clawborn and the south remained Corvessa. She had heard parts of this from Garrin and Zelgra.

The page turned, and the next part she had not heard.

The truce had not divided the trade evenly. The south held the shipping ports. The south held the road. The road ran from the southern interior down through the lower kingdoms to the coast. The road Riley herself had travelled with Garron. She felt that recognition in her chest before she had finished the sentence. She had walked the road. She had ridden it.

The Clawborn had no road to port. No port at all. They had the mines, the men to work them, and the mountains to guard their borders, but no way to move what they dug out except long overland hauls to whatever neighbor would still trade with them. After the truce, the writer said, fewer would. The Clawborn fell into isolation. The south built instead. It cut new roads, raised its towers and markets, and traded along the coast, while the north turned inward and kept mining a wealth it could no longer sell.

There was more and she continued.

The development in the south had been rapid, the writer said. Roads cut, garrisons built, the markets in the lower kingdoms growing fat on the through-trade. The development in the north had not. Their kingdom had suffered. The writer did not say poor. He said suffered. The Clawborn had the mining output but lacked distribution.

She had been about to turn the page when the knock came.

Two knocks, even, the way Thorne knocked.

"Come," she called.

Thorne entered.

"We have interrogated the prisoners," he said. "There seems to be more than just the Hoshin under their heel."

"Yes." Riley closed the book over her thumb to keep her place. "I assumed there would be. I bet there are more than a few. You have the details of the run? How they transport all these materials?"

"Yes. As one could have guessed, they stockpile it. Rather than running it back and forth to the kingdom directly."

"Right." She set the book down. "We will hit them where it hurts. You and Valrik work out the details. This will have to be timed correctly."

"At once." Thorne gave a half nod. He turned and started down the stairs.

"No one has access to the prisoners," Riley called after him.

"Not a soul, Warden," Thorne answered back, from the stairs.

Riley sat for a long moment with her hand on the closed book. The chest sat on the table a foot from her elbow, the small ward lock still the same small ward lock that had given her a quarter turn and nothing more.

She looked at it.

She reached over and picked up the knife.

She tried the lock again.
