Over the next few days, Lira settled into a steady rhythm between cleaning and reading, dividing her time carefully so she would not overexert herself again.

The library, once overwhelming, had become something more manageable, and the shelves that had once been nothing more than dusty obstacles were now more organized. At least a quarter of them were; it was still a work in progress.

More importantly, she began to understand something far more significant. The way magic was normally learned was far more restrictive than she had first assumed.

The first realization came slowly, built from fragments across multiple letters she found in a box beneath the spiral staircase leading to the upper floors.

Mages did not truly learn magic the way she did.

They relied on magical primers, structured books designed not just to teach magic but to imprint it into their minds. The words alone were not enough; each primer carried a guiding effect, a subtle magical influence that forced the reader into the trance required to internalize a spell. Without it, most people could not grasp the concepts deeply enough to make them real.

The second method was even more direct. Masters passed spells down to apprentices using specialized magic that forced the experience into the student’s mind, bypassing the need for discovery entirely. The whole process was efficient, controlled, and completely opposite to what Lira had been doing.

That led to her second realization: creating new spells was not something ordinary mages even attempted. It was described as the domain of grand wizards, individuals who had spent decades, sometimes lifetimes, studying the language of magic itself. The texts spoke of it with a certain reverence, as if the act bordered on the divine. It required not only understanding individual concepts, but also how those concepts interacted, overlapped, and transformed when combined. Most mages never reached that level. They learned what already existed and refined it to their individual use.

Which meant her father’s lexicon was something mages would kill for. Did he know the nature of what he was creating, or was he just thinking he was decoding some dead language with dubious usefulness?

Lira sat with the ledger open in her lap one evening, her fingers resting lightly on the worn pages. A lexicon of magical concepts, incomplete but functional, something that allowed her to bypass the need for primers entirely. It was crude compared to the structured teachings described in the letters, but it gave her something far more valuable.

Freedom.

She was not bound to what already existed. She could experiment, test, and build in ways that normal mages simply could not.

Her third realization came from a different line of reading. Among the more theoretical texts, she found references to a school of magic called divination. The idea caught her attention immediately. Magic that did not act directly on the world, but instead gathered information, interpreted it, or revealed what was hidden. It was not as flashy as fire or wind, but it aligned with something she understood far more deeply.

Knowledge, perception, and understanding.

Lira doubted she could create a flight spell without experiencing flight on her own first, which was frankly impossible.

But divination felt closer to her path than anything elemental ever had.

With that in mind, she returned to something more practical and finished reading the rest of the water primer. Now that she understood how it worked, she figured there was no real downside to learning the spells within it.

The process was easier now. She allowed herself to follow the structure, to let the guided trance take hold just enough to understand the spell and imprint it.

The result was a simple but useful ability. By touching water and casting the spell, she could absorb it directly into her body, bypassing the need to drink.

The sensation was strange at first, a cool seep through her skin that spread inward, but it worked exactly as described. It hydrated her just as effectively as drinking would have. It even worked with dirty water, absorbing the pure water while leaving the filth behind.

But more importantly, it gave her another reference point for spell structure.

“Yorad. Maha. Nagla.”

Body. Water. Absorption.

Lira repeated the words quietly, letting their meaning settle into place. The structure was simple, but it revealed something important. Absorption was a defined concept, something that could be applied beyond water. Her thoughts shifted almost immediately to her earlier idea.

Reading faster. Absorbing knowledge from books.

Not by moving her eyes more quickly, or by manipulating time or something equally absurd. No, she could simply absorb the knowledge the same way she absorbed water, changing the whole process of reading entirely.

She sat down with a blank sheet of parchment and began assembling the components in her mind.

Pika for mind.Linasara for books.Cognar for knowledge.Coopar for containment.Nagla for absorption.

Each word defined a part of the intended outcome. The goal was clear: convert the knowledge contained within a book into understanding and absorb it directly into her mind.

“Pika. Linasara. Cognar. Coopar. Nagla.”

She said it softly, testing the flow of the five magical words.

Until now, every spell she had encountered followed a certain pattern. The number of words was important. The candle spell was simple and took three words. The warmth spell was more complex, thus five. There was a structure to it, a balance she did not fully understand yet, but she could feel it was right. Longer spells carried more defined outcomes and more controlled effects.

So she chose five words.

But as she sat there, preparing to attempt it, another memory surfaced. The first time she had learned a five-word spell without enough preparation, the backlash had been immediate. The headache had nearly dropped her where she stood. She now understood that she had forced her mind to accept a structure it was not ready to hold.

This spell would be worse.

It was not just a complex spell; it interacted with the mind in ways she had not fully explored. If she attempted it now, she would likely overwhelm herself again, perhaps worse than before.

Lira exhaled slowly and set the quill down.

Rushing this would be a mistake.

If she wanted to build something stable, she needed a foundation.

Her gaze drifted back to the governess books.

Three cantrip spells for each affinity were the recommended number before learning a tier-one spell. That was what she needed. Three separate pieces, each tied to the mind, each reinforcing the others. Something to bridge the gap between what she understood and what she was trying to create.

Only then would she attempt the reading spell.
