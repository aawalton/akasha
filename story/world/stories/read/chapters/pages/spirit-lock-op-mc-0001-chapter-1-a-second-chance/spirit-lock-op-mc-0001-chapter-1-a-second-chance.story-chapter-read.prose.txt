Hayden walked across the Violet River, water parting with every step. From a distance, it appeared a simple crossing, but anyone up close could see the river bed cratering under his feet. He thought back to what a god had said from across the river when Hayden and over a hundred other demigods showed up to cross it.

This river separates the land of the mortals from the realm of the gods. If you think crossing it will be easy, you’re a fool. The gravitational pressure increases with every step, and if the water touches you, your body will wither to dust. Therefore, to cross, your spirits must work together to part the river, as you bear the weight and pressure alone.

All had died but Hayden. He remembered the way their muscles dissolved, leaving only loose skin stretched over bones, before being swallowed by the river. He used that image as fuel to push on.

Suddenly, the deep water parted, exposing a young boy sitting on the shore with a rueful smile.

“It’s pointless, mister,” the child said. “The tenth step is stronger than the entire path you’ve taken so far. You won't survive it in that body.”

He had heard that many times before. Before he took the test, an old lover tried to stop him.

Hayden, she had said. You need ten spirits to cross the river, and you only have nine; you need a divine constitution to bear the pressure, yet your body remains mortal. You’ve become a demigod despite these limitations, but this is a trial to godhood. If you disregard the rules, you will die.

Hayden heard the same doubt when he vied to become an Immortal—and then again when he strove to become a demigod. Now, he challenged the river with demigods who had ten spirits and divine physiques, and he was the only one left standing.

So he didn’t respond to the child. Instead, he took the tenth step and gritted his teeth when the weight of a planet crashed onto his shoulders. The riverbed cratered, and the water swayed, spilling drops that fell dangerously close to his skin. Yet to the boy’s amazement, Hayden remained standing and lifted his foot for another step. The boy’s face was incredulous, but sure enough, Hayden successfully completed the step—and bore the increased weight yet again.

The boy suddenly ran away. Fifteen minutes later, as Hayden had finished his sixth step—leaving him just eight feet from shore—the boy returned with a man with long gray hair and a leathery face.

“Can’t you do something, grandpa?”

The old man frowned. “I cannot. This trial must be walked alone.”

“But he deserves it!”

“I know… Life knows no end to cruelty.”

Hayden resented their pity. He screamed and took another step. The pressure doubled again, and his bones fractured. His core was screaming. The water was spilling over. He couldn’t move.

Hayden had reached a point where his skill and indomitable will couldn’t overcome physical realities. If he had a divine physique and a tenth spirit, he could take the trial with ease. Yet he didn't—and nothing he could do in the next fifty thousand years would change that. This was his final trial—and hard work and talent truly weren’t enough.

He had failed, but instead of giving up, he merged with his spirits and took one final step to honor their legacy. Though mute and simplistic, the spirits conveyed their will to win, so he declared they would have one last triumph—a cold, defiant act in the face of insurmountable odds.

To the old god’s surprise, Hayden successfully took the step—reaching just four feet from shore—before the water crashed in on him.

Suddenly, the water stopped, suspended in time. The atmosphere warped and turned ambient, leaving glittering water droplets hanging like stars around Hayden.

“Young man,” the old man said. “Allow me to ask you something.”

“What… is it?” Hayden asked in his thoughts. In the frozen state of time, he couldn’t move, but he couldn’t feel the pressure or pain either. It allowed him to communicate clearly.

“Would you risk your soul for a second chance?”

“What do you mean?”

The man lifted his hand, and a golden butterfly flew out from his palm, leaving hypnotic afterimages in its wake. “I can grant you a second chance by anchoring your soul onto this shore, and then sending you back in time. But if you fail to collect your soul before dying, you will lose it forever.”

“If those are the only terms, I accept.”

“Don’t be too hasty. One cannot do this without a cost. If you accept, the laws of causality will subtly interfere with the universe in an attempt to stop you. Of course, that won't eliminate your natural fortune, or prevent you from accomplishing your goals. But it will make your journey substantially more difficult, especially as you reach major inflection points.”

“Just to clarify, you’re saying that causality won’t make me unlucky or deprive me of things I would have gotten otherwise?”

“It will not. Causality intervenes through rare inciting incidents—events that bring people together at certain times, circumstances, and misperceptions. It doesn’t directly affect you or those around you.”

Hayden internally grinned. “Then I accept.”

“Are you sure? It will be difficult.”

“Then let it be difficult. If causality wants to stop me… let it try.”

The old man laughed and released the butterfly spirit. It entered Hayden’s forehead, causing him to experience a profound sense of vertigo as the butterfly stole his soul. The spirit then left his head and landed on a small flower, melting into it. Hayden could feel it bind him to Riaka—the land of the gods.

Somehow, in a twisted string of fate, Hayden made it over the river. That made him grin in triumph.

The old man touched the flower and smiled strangely. “Interesting… Listen, young man. I’ll sacrifice much to tell you this: a great power exists within your first spatial dimension.” The man aged considerably just by saying that—proving that there were costs to meddling with time. “That’s all I can say. You have one thousand years to return. I’ll be waiting.”

The god snapped his fingers, and the river crashed over Hayden, swallowing him whole.

Hayden felt immeasurably weak when he awoke. His bones felt like brittle glass, and he feared that a single fall would shatter them. It was a logical feeling, given that he had just broken all of his bones in the Violet River.

Or did he?

He recalled the conversation with the old man and shook himself awake, peeking at the sunlight drifting through the window.

He felt a mixture of déjà vu and nostalgia when he scanned his surroundings.

Why does this look so familiar? he wondered. Is this my past life?

The god said he would send Hayden back in time—but he didn’t say to when or where. The obvious time would be before he maimed his core. If that were the case, it would be five thousand years in the past. It made sense he wouldn’t remember anything.

Hayden was surprised to find that he could walk, given how weak he felt. He also saw a poster of a woman in armor on his wall, and he could read the words, “Keep Rising,” on it.

It seems this body remembers how to read and function.

“Let's try this out…” he said aloud. “Jalino maka tiana…”

It seems I can speak in both languages. That’s a relief.

Hayden searched the area for clothing and found an orange and white uniform folded neatly on his desk. It bore a pickaxe-leaf insignia on its breast. The words below it read: “Galma Remedial.”

He had a hazy flashback of using a flower catalyst in a meadow. Students were wearing orange and white uniforms around him.

It seems I can at least remember the place that maimed me…

Hayden found a sink to drink and wash his face. He drank with pleasure, but froze the moment he touched his skin. He shot up and stared at himself in the mirror.

Hayden neither had the time nor the poeticism to describe the wretched creature before him. His face was diseased, covered with thick red bumps tipped in white, and his bone structure seemed crafted by a god who took sadistic pleasure in imperfection. Hayden refused to believe that the… thing before him was himself as a younger man.

I need a cleansing elixir, he thought. Immediately.

A sudden knock broke him out of his trance.

“Hayden! The Burning is about to begin!”

The woman’s words struck him like lightning. He stared into the mirror to process his situation.

He sent me back to the day it happened, he thought. I truly have a second chance.

“Hayden!” the woman screamed. “I swear to God. If we're late, I'm going to feed you to Rosen.”

Hayden’s confused expression transformed into a callous mask. He didn't like being threatened.

“Give me a moment,” he said chillingly. “I'm changing.”

“Okay…” she answered hesitantly. “Just hurry up.”

Hayden changed into his uniform and opened the door. The woman who was waiting took a step back when she saw him.

“Hey, what happened to you?”

Her reaction didn't surprise him. One could tell someone's emotions, maturity, and temperament at a glance—and he held absolute confidence in himself. That seemed to concern her, a sign she was used to him being docile. She was perhaps even an antagonist—but that didn't matter.

The woman he would soon know as Lina Iska was little more than a child—nineteen at most—and Hayden was infinitely more patient with children than with adults. That's why he didn't scold or warn her off.

That said, he didn't let his guard down. Lina’s features were unnaturally perfect. In the Digital Universe, that signaled that wealthy parents had genetically engineered her. And there was no one more dangerous than immature children with powerful relatives. Especially those who were unique—and Lina Iska was truly special.

Hayden knew that the moment he saw cora flow into her core. An augmentor… he thought. How rare.

Lina turned her body nervously. “What are you looking at?”

“Nothing,” he replied.

“You’re acting weird. You know what? Never mind. We need to go!”

Lina strode through and out of the wooden building, leading them into a forest.

Hayden studied his surroundings in amazement.

This universe is surreal… Hayden thought.

He had lived in Elysian—an alternate universe—so long that he forgot about his original one.

A massive spaceship sat atop a mountain, surrounded by smaller shuttles. Motion sensor lights flickered as they walked, and everyone they passed wore bracelets on their wrists. He would soon learn that these bracelets connected to neural implants that allowed people to access the internet, record POV videos, or make telepathic calls. All of these functions were managed through a chip inside the brain; the bracelets merely lit up in different colors to show what the user was doing. The system prevented people from thinking the user was having a stroke when they stared into dead space.

Hayden glanced at Lina's bracelet. It was lit red, but he didn't know what that meant, so he turned his attention back to his surroundings.

The forest didn't reveal much, but the sky did. There were three daytime moons, which confirmed he was on Heline, the planet that would house the largest grand rift in the history of the Digital Universe. It was the rift the old man mentioned—the rift with immense power—the power Hayden needed to become a god. Given that today was the burning ceremony, he only had two months to prepare for it.

Three men suddenly barred Hayden's path, breaking him from his musings.
