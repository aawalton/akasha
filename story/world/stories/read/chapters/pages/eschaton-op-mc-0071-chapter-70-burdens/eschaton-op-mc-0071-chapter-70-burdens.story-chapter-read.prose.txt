“Morgan, I’m back!” I call out as I step into the gallery. She knows I’m here though. Given she taught me about Aura control, I’m sure she detected me miles out. I place the hot tub sized tank containing the huge water lily down just inside the door.

She turns around and floats over to me before I let her drag me to the floating illusionary Hydroponic Arcology design.

“Where are your apprentices?” I ask, looking around. She waves a hand dismissively.

“Oh, they went to bed. Very tired, long hours, anyway, take a look at this!” She points at a blown up section of the design. It looks like a tightly packed series of slabs in long rows. I can see that they’re coated with green, with strips of light crystals in-between.

“What’s that?”

“It’s a hydroponic block! Each one has five platters and can feed thirty people year round, and it takes up less square footage than a master bedroom. We made some small improvements to the hydroponic platter, managed to squeeze some more output from it.”

She runs a hand through her hair..

“I can’t take credit for all the work, Juarez, one of my apprentices, did a lot of heavy lifting here. He worked for an experimental hydroponics company before the Apotheosis.”

“The gap between those hydroponic walls is just two feet. That’s too small for someone to work.” I point out.

“Too small for a human to work,” she corrects. “We’re planning to use gardener golems and mundane automation methods. Hashimoto is already hard at work on a few prototypes.”

“Is thirty people per block enough?”

“No,” she says, shaking her head sadly. “We’ll need three to four times that at least. But we’re making good progress.”

“I’ve only been gone for a few days, Morgan. This is impressive work.”

She shrugs. “This is the easy part. All of this is really just relying on stuff we already know, there’s not really a ton of magic involved. This magi-tech stuff—Opuses are different, I’ll have you know—is outside my wheelhouse, really.”

“In that case, how is the study on the Hearts of Growth coming along?”

She grimaces at that.

“Slowly. I need to be very careful with them. It’s impossible to know if we’ll be able to find new ones, and if they’re as dangerous as you say they are, growing new ones is out of the question.

“I’m going as fast as I can, but I’m trying to exhaust all other non-destructive research options before I crack one open.”

“If absolutely necessary, we could probably attempt to grow one on the compound,” I offer. “Between the two of us, I’m sure we could put down a Carnivine easily.”

Morgan snorts.

“I saw the footage of you fighting the full-fledged one. Watching it kill itself via fan blade failure was pretty funny. I suppose you’re right, we could attempt to grow additional ones, but there’s no guarantee they’d be magical in the same way. Or that they’d actually grow. I don’t even know if they’re seeds.

“Anyway, enough about that, it’s still very early on. Did you bring anything to help remedy that?”

“I brought a lot of things, mostly plants, like that one,” I say, pointing at the tank with the water lily. “I brought it alive so you could maybe replant it?”

Morgan carefully peeks inside the tank before giving me a flat look.

“Mach?”

“Yes?”

“This is a water lily. A massive one. The size of a car.”

“Yes.”

“They grow on lakes and ponds.”

“Yes, the Fracture had a lake,” I confirm.

“Mach, we don’t have a lake here. Where am I going to plant it?”

“Uh.”

She rolls her eyes.

“Well, we’ve got plenty of space over on the west side of the compound. I guess we’ll go build a lake.”

“Just like that?” I ask.

“Just like that. Come on, I’m awesome, you’re awesome, how hard could a lake possibly be? It’s just a hole with water in it.”

“Two of the strongest individuals in the world, defeated by a lake!” Morgan cries.

“And look who said it wouldn’t be hard?” I tell her dryly.

“I’m an artist, not a damned hydrologist or whatever,” she grumbles.

Our combined attempt at making a lake has been met with a resounding failure. Though, our plan wasn’t really all that complex or well thought out, to be honest. We dug a bowl in the ground, a hundred feet across and thirty feet deep, and filled it with water. Which was promptly drunk by the dry soil. So we filled it again. Which was also drunk by the soil.

After a few more attempts we were left with a very, very muddy pit instead of a lake.

“There might be more than just filling a hole with water to this lake business,” I admit.

“So get on the internet and google how to make a lake!” Morgan says with false anger and a shaking fist.

I hesitate for an instant, and Morgan catches it.

“What’s wrong?” She asks.

“I…”

How do I tell her the sheer agony that looking at the internet brings me? Every time I hook into the telecommunications network, all I can think about is the death and destruction sweeping the planet. Atrocity after atrocity after tragedy after nightmare filling my head.

How do I tell her that it makes me sick to a stomach I no longer have? That I feel light headed yet never waver. How I want to scream yet stay silent.

How do I tell her I’m weak?

“I’ve been asked not to hijack telecomm satellites?” I lie. It hurts to do that, too.

“What’s wrong? You don’t need to lie to me.”

“I… just can’t do that anymore. Imagine doomscrolling at the speed of a datacenter, all the time. Every horrible thing that’s ever happened beamed directly into my head. It’s a waking nightmare.”

“Oh, God,” Morgan says, horrified. “How long have you been doing that?”

“Since New York.”

“Christ.” She hugs me, and I jolt. “You don’t have to do that. You can stop.”

“But what if I miss something? What if I miss a rupture? That’s how a lot of ruptures and Fractures were sighted in the early days. Posts on social media were critical in saving so many lives.”

“Mach, if it’s killing you, you can stop. We can find another way.”

“I just need to toughen up about it. I can do it, I can.”

“Mach, no, you can’t. I can feel it in your damned Aura, you can’t hide that from me. It’s killing you. It’s probably the only thing that can!”

“I’m fine,” I say reflexively, and pull away from her.

“STOP SAYING THAT!” Morgan screams at me. “YOU’RE NOT ALRIGHT! WHO THE FUCK WOULD BE AFTER LOOKING AT EVERY TERRIBLE THING EVER FUCKIN’ DONE?!”

“I have to do this,” I say weakly. “If I don’t, someone might die that I could save. There’s already so many people I can’t. It’s not actually killing me. I can keep going. I have to keep going.”

“Mach, no one is asking you to set yourself on fire to warm others!”

“I’m fireproof.”

She hits me in the chest, and I stumble backwards.

“This isn’t fucking funny! You do so, so, so much for so many people! You don’t have to give up your soul, too!” Morgan screams the words at me, tears in her eyes.

“How can I hold anything back if it might mean the difference between life and death?” I whisper. “How could I slow down, even a little bit, if it might save a life? If I hold back and someone dies, that’s my fault. I want to be a good person.”

“Mach, do you think I’m a bad person?” Morgan asks.

“What? No!”

“I don’t give every bit of myself up. I don’t crawl through internet gore videos to hunt for the needle in the haystack of people I could help. I don’t run myself into the ground in a month-long running-battle spanning the continent. I’m not setting myself on fire to warm someone else. By your definition, that makes me a bad person! Am I a bad person?”

“No,” I whisper.

“Then treat yourself how you would treat others, please. You are so kind, and so gentle with everyone you meet, always trying to take on their burdens. You push yourself beyond your limits. You run your soul over broken glass to maybe save one person. Would you ask anyone else to do that?”

“No, absolutely not,” I say instantly. “Besides, who else could do this?”

“The data harvesting and processing algorithms the NSA uses?” Morgan says. “How else do you think they know where to send you? You think they have an army of people just scrolling every goddamn social media site ever?

“Look, Mach, you don’t need to do this. Let others take a burden from your shoulders for once. You’ve said that you were never going to save the world on your own. That it requires everyone else to do their part.”

She takes a breath.

“SO FUCKING LET THEM!”

I slump into the dirt, splattering mud everywhere. Morgan sits next to me, getting mud on her coat, though it quickly slides off. She leans on my arm.

“Mach, out of all the things in this world, this is what will break you. And you’re right, the world needs you to be fine. But lying to yourself and everyone around you is the exact wrong thing to do. They need you to be actually fine. Not whatever you think fine is.

“Besides, we both know we didn’t save Washington alone. That we didn’t save New York alone. That we didn’t save Grace in Amiens alone. And I know you didn’t find that water lily alone. You’ve already taken the steps to allow others to help. Just take one more.”

“But I can’t ask anyone else to do this because it hurts,” I plead. I’m not sure why. “How can I ask someone else to do something I could do?”

“Because you aren’t. You can’t do this. I asked you to google how to build a lake and you flinched.

“We need you, Mach,” she says softly. “We need you here, with us. Not a ground-down husk of a person. Please. Do this for me?”

“Fine, okay,” I whisper. “Morgan?”

“Hm?”

“I’m glad you’re my friend.”

“I’m glad you’re my friend, too, Mach.”

We sit there in the mud for what feels like hours. I don’t really know what to do, what to think, how to feel. I should do everything I can to save a life, right? No matter the cost to myself. How could I be a good person if I don’t give it my all. How could I call myself worthy of the title of ‘hero’ if I don’t go the extra distance?

But Morgan is a good person. I firmly believe she’s a hero, too. But the standard I hold myself to would claim her no more a hero than Captains Illya and Tiller. And I refuse to deny Morgan the title of ‘Hero’.

So maybe I don’t have to destroy myself. Maybe I don’t need to commit emotional suicide.

Wouldn’t that be nice?

“What about the flower?” I finally ask.

“I’ve got a friend in California who could probably help us out.”

“Right,” I say exhaustedly. “But where in California?”

“San Francisco! He works at the California Academy of Sciences, it’s awesome. You’ll love it. It’ll be a good time.” Her good cheer is only partially restored.

We stand up, and the mud sloughs off us both. She picks up the tank with the water lily with a thought and raw magic. They both float off towards her gallery.

“Come on, I gotta make a call.”

I dutifully follow her towards her gallery. After we get back, she gently sets the tank down, and leans back, still floating like she always does. Lounging on the air, she calls her friend at the California Academy. It rings only once before whoever is on the other end picks up, and Morgan puts them on speaker phone.

“Hey, Benny! Thanks for picking up so quick. So, me and Mach here have a tiny favor to ask.”

“How come you only ever call when you need something?” Benny asks dryly.

“Because you’re very good at helping?” Morgan says glibly.

“*Well, how important is this favor? I was on my way to another meeting about—well, it doesn’t matter. But it’s important.”

“I’ve got a sample of a living flower from a Cat-2 Fracture that needs cultivating, or at the very least, kept alive. Oh, and a magical fish,” Morgan says, noticing the fish I captured. We put it in the same tank as the water lily.

“…Ah. I see. That is important. And why are you calling me instead of just planting it in a flower pot?”

She looks at the car sized tank we have the water lily stored in.

“Because its roots are about thirty feet long and the entire thing is the size of a smart car.”

“Surely there’s somewhere in Denver you can have it planted.”

“Benny, you’ve got a literal friggin’ jungle in a jar. No one knows bio-domes and self contained climates better than you guys. Tell me who’s better than you and I’ll go to them.”

“Fine,” Benny responds after a brief pause. “This is pretty important, I don’t know of anything that large being successfully recovered alive from a Fracture. We’ve got some small scale stuff but nothing like that. How long has it been uprooted for?”

“About six hours,” I tell him. “But we put it in a tank of water as soon as we could. Hot tub sized.”

“Right, we can work with that. I’ll set a team up to figure out where to put it. Something that large is probably gonna go in the biodome, but who knows. When can you get here?”

“We could be there in five minutes,” Morgan says. “But I assume that’s too early.”

“Uh, yeah, just a little. Show up in an hour. I’ll either have something for you then, or I’ll use you to kick some asses into gear. Gotta go. See you later, Morgan.”

Benny hangs up, and Morgan slides her phone into a pocket.

“Who’s Benny?” I finally. “He sounded important.”

“That’s Doctor Benjamin Lam, double Ph.D. holder in both Ecological Conservation and Complex Adaptive Systems Science. He’s the Executive Director of the California Academy of Sciences and has been for the last ten years.”

She gives me a catlike grin. “We go way back.”
