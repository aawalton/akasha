Chapter 128

Six wagons on the road headed toward the Clawborn depot, two drivers on the bench of each, six soldiers on horseback strung along the column, and above them the sky had been the colour of iron since first light.

The rain smelt of cold earth, horse and the sap of the trees along the western verge. It came down steady, not hard, not soft, but relentless. It struck the wagon covers with a low continuous drumming, and struck the packed dirt of the road with a hiss that carried further than the drumming did.

The road ran over the small hills, up and down in long slow rolls that put a driver on the box against the sky for a moment at the top of each rise and then dropped him back into the wet green of the next dip. On the western side, tall trees crowded the verge, their trunks close enough together that a man could not have walked between them without turning sideways. On the eastern side, the tree cover was sparser. Small bunches of the same tall species at irregular intervals along the shoulder, standing in twos and threes and fives, with open ground between them where nothing but low grass grew. The dense line to the west, the broken line to the east.

The wagons made the sound wagons made in rain. The wheels were big, iron-shod, and each one turned up a slow wet crescent of mud with every revolution and dropped it back on the road behind. The mud was thickest where the wheels dug in deepest, and the riders who rode at the rear of the column were spending the morning falling steadily further back to keep clear of the airborne mud. The horses' breath came out in white curls and the horses' hooves came down heavy on the wet road with a sound between a slap and a thud. The Clawborn in the saddles grunted low when the mud came high enough to strike them, and narrowed their eyes when the rain hit them in the face.

Riding through weather was not new but a group this size was. Six wagons already slowed a column to the pace of the slowest wheel. The rain cut the convoy's speed in half of that.

All six soldiers were in full armour, the plates darkened with wet, the buckles wound tight against the movement of the horses. Their weapons were unslung and set across the saddles ready to come to hand. That was not the way transports rode. Transports rode with weapons stowed and a lighter escort. This escort was double the usual and armed for engagement.

The complement said what the orders had said: the empress was pressing the depot for its stores. The need was clear enough. The reason behind that need was not.

***

Riley had climbed to the top of one of the burnt towers of the Clawborn depot to get a better lay of the land, something the HUD map couldn't give.

The tower was three storeys of scorched stone with the roof gone and the top floor open to the sky. She had come up the inside stair a step at a time because two of the treads near the top were cracked, and she had stopped at the top with a hand on the parapet and looked out at the yard below. The rain had settled into the same continuous soft-hard weight it had held since before dawn. It ran off the parapet in a thin steady sheet and off the front of her hood in drops that fell between her boots.

Her single wagon was in the middle of the yard with two of her people at the traces and more loading it from the depot's inner stores. The load was slow because everything they carried was slick with rain, and slower still because of the yard mud.

Below the wagon, strung out along the track that ran south from the yard toward the treeline, her first bearers had already gone. Some were mounted, riding double where they had had to, with the heaviest of the depot's stores strapped behind them. The rest were on foot, walking with sacks on their shoulders and her dead wrapped and tied to what horses they could spare. The plan was to drop the largest part in the treeline near Hoshin, where the villagers would keep it dry under the eaves of the old grain shed, and then to carry the lighter part on to the tower and the community. The plan had assumed dry ground. The plan had not been consulted about the rain.

She watched the bearers move off into the wet green. The column strung longer than she wanted it, thinner than she wanted it, slower than she wanted it, and she stood at the parapet and did the maths in her head and did not like the answer.

Then she thought, if this weather has us moving at half pace, it has everyone else moving at half pace too. And if there was a Clawborn wagon out there coming to the depot then the same rain and the same mud would slow them too.

Riley paused on that thought for a beat longer than she needed to. It calmed her. Then she let it go and opened the HUD map, mentally moving on.

The notification icon had been flashing for a long time. She had let the messages pile up like an overfilled email inbox. The idea of going through all of them overwhelmed her so she had been ignoring it. But today she decided to open them because she was curious.

Riley's eyes widened when the list appeared. There was something different. The regular messages populated, but there was something new: a notification in a different colour. Purple.

The sender was Nikola, not the system.

She opened it:

❖ You summoned most of the soldiers. Do you want the newly trained ones sent as well?

She stared at it for a beat.

She thought about it a second longer. It made sense. The tower had rolled over to level ten in the last week. She had not opened the change notes. She had not had time. Nikola apparently had.

She thought her reply into the box the way she had thought at menus a hundred times before.

❖ Depot is ours. Resources moving in stages, treeline first, tower second. Give me a minute on the new troops.

❖ Acknowledged.

She almost laughed.

Then the next question came up on its own. Did the messaging work with anyone?

Down in the yard, one of her people had stopped beside a Clawborn body near the wall and was bending to lift it. She watched him for a moment. He was a regular soldier, face she knew. She pulled the marker over him on the HUD.

She selected him.

It did not show her a message box. No frame. No line. No cursor she could think into.

She tried again. Same result.

She thought about that. She scrolled the map back toward her tower.

The tower's icon appeared, and around it were the icons of the soldiers. Blue markers, all of them. She had looked at the markers a hundred times before. She did not immediately see what was different.

Right next to the tower, close enough that at a smaller zoom the two icons overlapped, sat one marker in the same new colour as Nikola's box. Purple. Something she had not seen before.

She selected it.

The panel opened. A small menu unrolled beneath the icon. She read the first line and stopped reading, because the second line said message.

She was not sure how one pressed a thing that lived inside her head. She willed the option instead, and the message box opened the way it had opened for Nikola's line, framed in the same purple.

There was no keyboard, so she thought at the box.

"Do I just will it," she said, softly.

She thought:

❖ Nikola stop being lazy and get to work.

The words appeared inside the box, one at a time, in the order she had thought them. She smirked. She thought: delete.

The words vanished.

She stood with the rain running off her hood and did the small mental accounting of what she had just learned. Purple was different. Purple was who she could talk to. Blue was who she could not. Nikola was purple. A soldier picking up a body in the yard was blue. Purple was for the ones at the tower who were something more than soldiers. Blue was for the ones underneath them. That felt right in a way she could not account for, and she did not push it further, because there was work to do and the wagon in the yard was almost loaded.

She thought her real reply into Nikola's message box:

❖ Send the new troops. Meet the returning column at the rally point near Hoshin.

She waited.

❖ Acknowledged.

She let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

She stood at the parapet with the rain on her hood and looked out at the treeline. Somewhere further south, the rest of her people were carrying resources through the wet toward the Hoshin rally point, and somewhere behind them a fresh column of soldiers was going to leave the tower to meet them, because she had thought a sentence at a box in her head and Nikola had thought a reply back.

Then the second thought arrived.

If she had this, did others have it too?

The Clawborn on the roads she could not see, possible wagons somewhere out in the same rain she was standing in, the empress in her warm room far into the north. If the empress could think a sentence at a box in her head, then the empress had known things Riley had not known she knew. And it went further than the empress.

The Kingdom to the south had a king. The king had a spy. The spy was Tarin.

Tarin had sat in her tower's meeting room with a cup in his hand and a small polite smile on his face and had spoken about tea. He had walked the outer yard. He had looked at the walls. He had counted, without appearing to count, the doors and the guards at the doors and the shape of the roofs and whatever else a man of his kind counted when he was not appearing to count. If he had had a purple icon of his own in whatever HUD he opened when no one was watching, he might have been reporting all of it, in real time, to another.

She stood on the burnt stone at the top of the tower she had captured and thought about the tower she owned, and the man who had drunk her tea inside it, and the four hundred leagues of ground between her and every place she could not check now.

The rain kept coming and Riley stood in it.
