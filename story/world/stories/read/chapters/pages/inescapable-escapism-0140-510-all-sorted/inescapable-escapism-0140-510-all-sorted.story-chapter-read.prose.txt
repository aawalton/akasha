I opened my mouth to reply to Rowan, a lie forming on my lips, but no sound came. I’d intended to tell her that I was fine, that I was barely even anxious at all, but the words died before they could escape. Shock slammed into me, and I stared at the giant window on the far side of the waiting area as my brain stuttered to a halt, unable to process what I was seeing.

Vaguely, I was aware of movement around me. One of the guardians spoke again, but their voice was nothing more than an indistinct buzz to my ears. I knew that I should have responded. They were probably waiting for me to respond, but I couldn’t think of anything to say. My thoughts were too consumed by the sight before me.

We were not on a cruise ship, I realised slowly as my eyes darted back and forth, trying to take it all in. It was a ship of sorts, I guess, but it was not a cruise ship, and I was not cursed to spend my life sailing the oceans in search of the mythical scrap of land where we could be safe. In fact, I’d probably never see the ocean again.

Not that I’d ever actually seen it before in that world.

“It’s beautiful, isn’t it?” Cal said softly, a strange tenderness in his voice.

I tore my eyes away from the window for just a moment to glance at him, but he wasn’t even looking at me. His gaze was fixed on the vast expanse of space outside our ship.

“Yeah,” I just about managed to get out.

“I mean, I’ve seen the stars hundreds of times, if not more,” Cal continued. “And yet, they never fail to take my breath away.”

My eyes left his face again, returning to the smattering of lights that twinkled in the distance. I didn’t have a response to him. How could I? There was nothing I could say that wouldn’t seem weird, and I knew it.

I should have been used to being in space. In that world, I’d been born there. I’d spent my whole childhood, my entire life, on board that ship, and I’d never know anything else. Countless nights had been spent sneaking into the observation platform, pointing out the stars and planets as we drifted past them until we eventually fell asleep.

The sight should not have been so incredible to me, and yet it was. I’d never seen anything like it before. Not in real life, anyway. There was another world, ages ago. I was pretty sure I’d been in space there, but it didn’t really count. I didn’t actually go outside ever, and I wasn’t sure if I ever looked out. Surely, I would have remembered it if I had.

“Do you want to sit down, Daf?” Rowan asked gently. “You can keep standing if that’s more comfortable, though.”

I felt my cheeks flush as I blinked quickly, looking away from the thick glass to glance at her. She was seated already, but Cal wasn’t. He was still standing next to me, looking down at me with a kind smile that reminded me of my dad. He wore the same expression when he looked at me sometimes too.

“Oh, yeah,” I said quickly, looking away. “Sorry.”

“No need!” Rowan hurried to say. “I can’t fault you for loving them.”

“It is probably your fault, after all,” Cal added.

There was a grin on his lips, but Rowan’s expression became playfully indignant.

“What did I do?” she demanded.

He sighed heavily and shook his head.

“I mean, all those stories about the constellations you used to read them when they were kids? I have no doubt they had an effect.”

Rowan laughed, and I felt myself join in, but my attention had returned to the window.

I did remember the stories. Some of them, anyway. Every night before bed, she used to read to us. Still did, sometimes. The tales about the people who’d been cast into the sky and immortalised in the constellations, blessed to watch over the planet forever, had been my favourite when I was younger.

That had changed as I grew up, though. I stopped believing that it was a blessing; that was the problem. After a while, all I could think about whenever Rowan read us the stories was how sad the constellations must have become when they saw what humans had done.

My gaze roamed the stars as Rowan and Cal continued to talk, but I barely even heard what they were saying. I was too distracted by the excitement fluttering in my stomach. I was in space. I was living amongst the stars.

I couldn’t quite wrap my head around it. It felt so foreign and strange, and it was, in a way. It was a completely different life, a completely different world, but a lot of it was weirdly familiar, I realised as I scanned the few memories that had revealed themselves to me.

Most days were spent the same way they were on Earth. I woke up, went to school and came home. Of course, I didn’t go back to my house. I went back to the youth dorms where I lived, but that was close enough. I had my own room there, and the guardians looked after me and the other kids.

That was where the similarities ended, though. Most days, I worked for a few hours in the evening. I couldn’t quite recall what I did, though. There were flashes of different things, different places, and that made me think it must have varied. I probably didn’t do the same job every single day.

And there were the safety drills. I didn’t do those in my real life either. Once a week, we ran a drill. They were always at different times, but they tended to be on a Tuesday. Sometimes, it would be an airlock breach or an impact, and we’d have to rush to safety whilst the engineers resealed the holes, but my favourites were the landing drills.

I liked to think about what we’d do when we finally found a planet to settle on. It would happen soon. In my lifetime, at least. That was what the governor always said. Maybe not in his, but we would walk amongst the surface again.

A shiver of excitement washed over me at the thought of being able to stand barefoot in the dirt and look up to see the sky rather than the metal ceiling and bright lights of the ship. Instead, I’d see the sun. Or maybe suns. Some solar systems had more than one, like the one we were heading towards. Maybe we’d settle there.

I’d never really thought about that before. In my real life, I’d never stopped to think how incredible it was that I could go outside, that it was safe for me to just walk out into the world without needing any protective equipment. I couldn’t do that in the new world. Perhaps I’d never be able to.

My head snapped around as the gentle sound of wheels on the polished flooring reached my ears, and my heart leapt into my throat, but it was just the medical assistance robot returning with a jug of water for Rowan.

She jumped up, hurrying to take the tray from her.

“Thank you!”

The robot’s smile seemed to stretch even wider.

“You’re welcome,” it said before turning towards Cal and me. “I’m aware you both said you didn’t want anything, but I brought some spare cups just in case either of you change your mind.”

“Thank you. That was very kind,” Cal told it.

The robot’s eyes seemed to settle on me.

“Daffodil, it might be a good idea to drink some water in order to make your blood test easier. A hydrated vein is easier to tap, after all!”

I had to suppress the urge to shudder at its words. Did I want my veins to be easier to tap? Probably, but there was something about the way the robot phrased it that made me want to run away.

“Thank you,” I muttered, accepting the cup from Rowan.

The hard plastic was cool in my hands, and I forced myself to take a sip of the water. Surprise washed over me, and I drank a little more. It tasted of absolutely nothing. Water normally had a taste. It was usually faint, but I could always taste something. The water on the ship had no taste, and that felt strange.

“Might be worth trying to drink the whole thing,” Cal told me softly as the robot turned and rolled away again. “You didn’t drink much at breakfast, did you?”

I scanned my memories, trying to work out whether I did. I assumed he was correct, but I wanted to double-check, just in case. It was hard to recall anything, though. I could just about remember a bustling room, and I was almost certain there had been a full cup of water next to my barely touched breakfast.

Focusing on that memory, I tried to pull it closer, watching as it gradually became clearer. He was right, obviously. I’d had a sip of water, but it had turned my stomach. That wasn’t what interested me in the memory, though. It was the food.

Something about it just looked… off. It was too picturesque. Everything about it was a little too perfect, and it seemed almost as if it had been designed rather than cooked. The sausage patties were perfectly circular. The scrambled eggs were light and fluffy. Each curd was exactly the same size, and even the grilled tomatoes were perfect. They had sagged slightly when they’d been cooked, but somehow, they’d retained their symmetry.

It was kind of unnerving to see, even in my memory.

“No,” I said, feeling Cal’s eyes still on my face. “Wasn’t thirsty.”

A soft smile appeared on his lips as I took another sip, and my anxiety began to creep back in.

“That’s completely understandable, but try to drink as much as you can now, okay?”

“Okay.”

“I’m sure you’ll be fine,” Rowan told me. “The doctor’s never had any problems with your blood tests before, have they?”

“No.”

I didn’t think they had, anyway. I couldn’t really remember, but I was pretty sure they’d all been fine. The doctor was good, and it wouldn’t be the end of the world if they couldn’t get any blood from me anyway. They’d just give me a blanket and make me drink a bunch of water, and it would be fine, I told myself.

That didn’t really help, though. I couldn’t stop myself from bringing the cup to my lips and draining it as nerves vibrated through my body. Despite my reassurances, I was still terrified. It felt like I was about to do something scary, but I wasn’t sure why.

I couldn’t even recall why I had a doctor’s appointment, but it felt weird that I had one on my birthday. The governor had mentioned that it was an annual thing, though, so maybe that was normal. Perhaps it was just a regular check-up that everyone had on their birthday?

But then, he did say it was a big one, and that worried me. It was my sixteenth birthday. One of the guardians had said that, but why would that matter? Surely, it should have been a day for celebrating, not for medical appointments.

That would come later, I recalled. Regardless of the outcome of my appointment, there would be a celebration later. The guardians would do their best to ensure that it was a happy event, that the atmosphere was not overshadowed by whatever the doctor told me, just like they always did.

It didn’t always work, though. Sometimes, regardless of how hard Cal and Rowan tried to make sure the birthday person was happy, they were not. It was hard when they’d just been given news they didn’t want to hear.

Like with Vio. I had no clue who he was, but I knew he’d not been happy with the outcome of his medical appointment. His glowering face was burned into my memories, but I couldn’t remember why he’d been so angry.

No, not angry. He was sad. Heartbroken.

Everyone deserved to be happy and fulfilled, and he was. Eventually. It took time, but I was pretty sure I’d seen him since his celebration, and he looked happier. He learned to be thankful for his assignment. It had just taken time.

But that just made me more nervous. It wasn’t what I wanted. I’d been so scared about what the doctor would tell me for so long, and I didn’t want it to be something that I had to come to terms with or adjust to. I didn’t want to have to learn to accept it. I just wanted everything to be… easy.

The governor did say I’d be happy with my results, though. I played the conversation back in my mind, trying to remember exactly how he’d phrased it. That had to mean something, didn’t it? Surely, he wouldn’t tell me something like that unless he meant it. It would be cruel to give someone false hope.

Unless he just said that to everyone he ran into on the morning of their sixteenth birthday. Maybe he was just trying to reassure me and stop me from panicking in case that somehow skewed the results.

Could that even happen? Surely, there was nothing I could do to change the outcome. It had to be too late for that, didn’t it? The decision must have already been made. They must have already decided exactly how I would spend the rest of my life, and there was no way I could screw it up—

A door whirred quietly in the distance, but to me, the sound was deafening. I stared at the corner before me, barely able to breathe as I waited for the doctor to appear. I could hear her footsteps. They marched steadily closer, and with each step, my heartbeat seemed to speed up.

“Good morning, and happy birthday, Daffodil!” Doctor Tulip called as soon as she spotted us.

“Good morning,” I replied, my tone tight. “Thank you.”

“I see you’ve already had some water. Good! Are you ready to come in for your appointment now?”

The urge to say no gripped me. It was a rhetorical question, though. I knew I had to agree, no matter how much I longed to refuse. The doctor had other patients to see after me. She had things to do, and I didn’t want to cause her to fall behind schedule.

“Yes.”

“Fantastic!” she said cheerfully. “Do you want either of your guardians to join you today?”

I glanced across at them, unable to reply for a moment. I longed to say yes. I wanted them to come in and sit beside me, holding my hand throughout the entire appointment like they used to do when I was much younger.

But I was too old for that. I was sixteen now. I had to be an adult, to face it alone. It was childish, immature, to cower behind them.

“No, but thank you,” I said as I rose from the seat on shaky legs.

“Here,” Cal said, holding out his hand to me. “I’ll wash up your cup whilst you’re in there.”

“Thanks,” I replied, passing it to him.

“That’s alright! Good luck in there.”

“You’ll do great,” Rowan added.

I couldn’t reply to them. A thick lump of anxiety and fear had lodged itself in my throat, making it impossible for any words to escape. Instead, all I could do was look up at the doctor. Her green eyes were warm as she met my gaze, and the skin around them crinkled slightly as her smile grew.

“Shall we?” she asked, gesturing towards the examination room.

There was no way I could speak. The ability had abandoned me, but I nodded, silently following the woman as she walked away. My heart pounded in my ears as we made our way down the spotless corridor, and my head spun. My thoughts were fleeting, frantic and impossible to grasp, but the moment she stopped outside one of the doors, they stopped.

I held my breath as the door opened again, and the faint scent of disinfectant stung my nostrils as we stepped inside. I couldn’t help but look around, scanning the room as subtly as possible, trying to work out if it looked any different to how I remembered it looking.

Not that I could really recall too much. My memories from that world hadn’t become solid enough yet, but there were bits. Enough to tell me that the room was almost identical. A medical assistance robot, just like the one outside, stood next to Doctor Tulip’s desk as it always did, and there were two chairs, one in front of the desk and the other to the side.

What would have happened if I had said I wanted one of my guardians to accompany me? Would they have had to stand the entire time? Or would the medical assistance robot have disappeared outside to fetch them a seat? I wasn’t sure, but I kind of wanted to know. I pushed that thought aside as my gaze continued to roam the room, taking in the weighing scales near me before landing on the machine in the back corner.

My heart seemed to stop for a moment. I’d seen it before. I knew I had, but the full-body scanner still scared me. It was always there, and part of me was used to the huge white tube, but the rest of me was terrified. I didn’t want to set foot inside it, but I knew I’d have to. It was part of the examination.

“Do you want to just hop up onto the scales so I can check your weight before we get started?” the doctor asked as the medical assistance robot moved towards us.

I glanced at the scales, fear fluttering in the pit of my stomach. It would be fine, and I knew that, but I was still scared. There was no way I could refuse her, though. And I had no real need to. The doctor had to check my weight. It was a necessary part of the examination, and if I refused, we wouldn’t be able to continue.

“Sure,” I said, trying not to sound as nervous as I felt.

Stepping onto the wide metal platform, I held my breath. I wasn’t sure why I was doing it, but I was too scared to breathe. It felt like doing so would throw off the results or something.

“Hm.”

My head whipped around to face the doctor, who was peering at the screen on the robot.

“Is everything okay?” I asked, my voice coming out more high-pitched than expected.

Her expression made me nervous. There was a furrow between her eyebrows, and she’d cocked her head to the side slightly.

“Yes,” she muttered before looking at me, her voice returning to its normal volume. “You’ve lost some weight again.”

“Oh.”

“It’s not too bad,” she was quick to reassure me. “Not too far outside your normal fluctuations, but we’re going to have to monitor it a little closer for a bit just to make sure.”

“Okay,” I replied, unsure how to feel about that.

“Have you been eating alright recently?” the doctor asked. “Making sure to finish all of your meals?”

I wracked my brain, trying to find an answer.

“Umm… mostly,” I said as honestly as I could. “I’ve not really had an appetite for the last few days.”

Doctor Tulip nodded.

“That’s understandable. It happens to quite a few people in the run up to their appointment, no matter how hard we try to reassure you that there’s nothing to worry about. For now, I’ll up your intake allocation. Just for the next week or so, and we’ll see if that helps bring it back to normal. How does that sound?”

I assumed by that she meant my food. I’d never heard anyone refer to meals as ‘intake allocation’, but it made sense. Kind of.

“Okay,” I replied, unsure of what else to say.

“Great! If you find that your appetite doesn’t return, come back and speak to me, and we’ll look into it more. Okay?”

“Sure.”

The doctor’s smile widened.

“Fantastic. Alright, jump down and come stand next to Meddy so she can check your height,” she told me, patting the medical assistance robot fondly.

I did as she instructed, hopping off the scales and moving back towards the robot. I wasn’t sure where exactly she wanted me to stand. There were no markings or a camera that I could see that indicated how it would measure me, but I let my feet move instinctively, bringing me to the robot’s left side.

“Okay, now stand up big and tall,” the doctor instructed. “Straight back, and look straight ahead.”

In both worlds, I felt my spine straighten as I waited to be measured. I’d never really considered my height in the other worlds. I’d just assumed that it would be the same, but I hadn’t thought to check. I wasn’t sure if the doctor would tell me, though. She hadn’t said how much I’d weighed, and the medical assistance robot’s screen was tilted away from me, making it impossible to read.

Would she tell me if I asked? The question pulled at me, tempting me to open my mouth and ask the doctor, but I couldn’t quite bring myself to do it. I wasn’t sure if it was normal in that world or not, and I didn’t want to risk slipping up, just in case.

“Fantastic,” Doctor Tulip said, looking at me again. “Well, that’s all sorted.”

I forced myself to smile, fighting to keep the disappointment from my face as I realised she wasn’t going to tell me. Maybe I could ask later, I told myself.

“Great.”

“Next up, blood pressure and a few samples. Have you been remembering to take your supplements every morning?”
