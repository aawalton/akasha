The stray cat pawed at the wheel of the propped up cart, seeing something only a stray cat could see. Kalsin couldn't make out much from where he was sitting, through the window glass smeared by spots of dried rain, but still he stretched his neck to see the fluffy tail sway back and forth in excitement. It paused, stood still for a breath, then lurched together with the rest of the cat's body in an almost surge-like snap.

"Your corner posts are crooked," said Nyugat.

Kalsin glanced down at his drawing. He shifted the paper so Nyugat, still gawking over his shoulder, would have to circle around the desk to keep it in his sight. "They're not."

"But they are."

"Not from where I'm sitting."

There was silence from Nyugat—knowing him, he made sure if the professor had still not entered the classroom—then a scrape of wood signaled a chair being dragged over. Kalsin reluctantly shifted his legs as the older boy crowded in next to him, planted his elbows on the narrow table, making it slant slightly under the weight.

A stick of coal started rolling toward the edge. Kalsin grabbed it before it could fall. He scowled down at his dirty hand, wanting to reprimand Nyugat and tell him to stuff it, but without the confidence to turn daydream into reality, the words never made it past his tongue.

"Look. See the railings, how it keeps at the horizon from both sides?" Nyugat said, looking out the window toward the fence Kalsin had captured. He held his pen at arm's length, one eye closed to gauge distance, moving his thumb up and down until he caught the correct proportions. "Uhum. Just as I thought. Watch the part where the corner post blocks out the fence, right at the bend. The one with the chipped tip. It's angled way too steeply."

Kalsin looked. He frowned yet again. He held out his arm, using the same technique they've all been taught, his bare coal painting his thumb even more black. Nyugat was right, the angles were skewed just a touch. Enough to be noticed if the professor checked.

Even as Kalsin murmured his thanks, he noticed a commotion had driven the cat from the cart as he nimbly scurried up the length of the fence and fled, clawing and jumping down along the road. Did he catch his prey? For all Kalsin knew, his retreating tail was a declaration of victory, a proud flag whipped by the wind this way and that. Kalsin’s hand twitched with the sudden impulse to immortalize the fluffy creature within his drawing, and he scowled deeper when he remembered he was supposed to draw things stationary, not those in motion.

"You're welcome," Nyugat said.

Kalsin flinched, having forgotten the boy next to him, now wearing a hostile expression with his nose in the air.

"I said thank you!" Kalsin said, defensive. "And I had it right for the most part, anyway. It's good enough."

"Ha! I want to hear you say that to Professor Stalble. And he will say the same thing I do, which is, good enough is not accurate enough."

A hundred different responses flitted through Kalsin's head. You're right and I'm sorry. Shut up you idiot. I don't care because my drawing is prettier than yours. Can we be friends? You want accurate, I'll give you an accurate close-up of my fists.

Kalsin didn't know which one to say, if any. So he said nothing. Nyugat dragged his chair back to his table with a louder scrape than before, though it could've been just his imagination. The ever-present background hum of a class without supervision continued. Kalsin breathed a sigh of relief. He wiped his hand in his trousers and found them slick with sweat.

The door to the classroom burst open and bounced off the wall with a twin of deafening cracks. A woman Kalsin could only describe as maniacal, half marched, half stumbled inside, her once powdered eyes desperately searching as runny smears glistened along the wrinkles of her face. The frills of her dress were brown and dirty, as if she'd trampled them in the mud.

"Reni!" the woman wheezed, clutching at the arm of a girl who was staring with wide eyes and cheeks red with embarrassment. "Reni, we have to go! Quick, please, listen to me, come."

"Mom? Wha—"

"I'll explain later, but you have to come now. Okay? Now!" She tugged harder on her daughter’s arm, nearly wrenching her from her seat. Her mother's desperation must've been contagious as Reni seemed to likewise forget about the spectacle they were causing. She still resisted being pulled from her seat, but real fear discolored her voice as she stuttered to ask what had happened.

"That is enough, Mrs. Vezry!"

The booming command came from Professor Dusking, the head of the school in the position of Tanár, and a man Kalsin had seen smiling more often than not. He wasn't smiling now. Three adults crowded in the classroom behind him, all employees at the school, and all having the same disheveled look of people who'd just sprinted across the entire length of a city.

"I have a right to see my daughter, just as surely as I have the right to take her home whenever I wish."

"You can refer to all your rights when you calm yourself. In the office. We can look at paperwork. We can sort this out. If you will."

Professor Dusking's tone was measured, reasonable, but Kalsin saw past his words and found a man tired and distraught, a man who had already arrived at a conclusion and was preparing for the backlash. He slowly stepped toward Mrs. Vezry, who still held a white knuckled grip on Reni's arm. "You're hurting her," he said softly.

Mrs. Vezry let go of her daughter. She gasped at the sight of the red lines she'd left on her skin, and fell to her knees, cupping her daughter’s face, sorries and intimate endearments pouring out of her that should never be witnessed by an audience. Children in the classroom averted their eyes, shifted, uncomfortable. Kalsin did not turn away. He watched, his attention a string in resonance, beyond curious.

The mother's apologies had a sour quality to them. Airy and spacious, and not weighed down by guilt one bit. Like she was trying to soothe a wrong that was not her doing, something she was not responsible for, but the source stemmed from her own person anyway. Words stumbled, broke halfway out and collided with fresh strings in a hopeless rush of someone trying to cram everything inside a bag before it had to be tied, sealed, shipped. ‘One more thing before I forget, and this, this is important as well, and here, one last thing just in case.’

The daughter received it with confused tears and a head shaking in denial. Eyes that rolled for a way out from a dream more than they pierced toward the truth to understand. ‘Everything was well a moment ago. How do I go back? Please, just let me go back.’

Behind them, the professors and staff talked in hushed voices, peering at the distraught pair from time to time, but not making a move otherwise. They didn't seem happy, and Kalsin didn't think they would be after what happened. They were waiting for something. A question whispered, an answer muttered from the corner of a mouth, an eye glancing to the entrance, then to the mother, and then back to their colleagues.

It wasn't long until Kalsin's suspicion proved right. He noticed a new figure standing outside the room, barely on the edge of his vision. He stretched his neck and caught a flash of a translucent white robe and a large expanse of bare skin underneath, before Professor Dusking stepped into the doorway and blocked his line of sight. "There's children inside, for the sake of the Gods!" they heard him snap. A voice answered. The professor said, "Do it outside the premises, then. I won't have a Fény in front of my students."

Kalsin listened with his head toward the window to hide his burning cheeks. He'd never seen a Fény before, and now he understood why adults always herded children away whenever their services had to be called.

He remembered Cinder telling him all about the Fény; a handful of devout men and women, picked from among the priests of Tűzmother, who filled a vital role in the Republic. Whenever an argument arose whether a person was Clenn or Scarrd, they called a Fény to perform a private inspection and verify the claims. They wore nothing but a see-through dress so the truthfulness about their own body could not be drawn into question. Though here in Araz, where a public display of scars was the law, they were rarely seen.

Mrs. Vezry let out a shriek when a pair of freshly arrived city guards restrained her. By now, everyone understood the procedure—the students, the staff, the few onlookers who had gathered outside. The mother did as well. Surely she knew better than anyone, because she begged, spat, clawed at them throughout every inch they pulled her from her daughter. A chair fell over when she broke free for a moment, a desk toppled with an explosion of dust when a guard tackled her into it. She did not let up for a second, but they dragged her outside just the same.

That day, a husband drowned in a fracture. A wife broke with grief and turned Scarrd. A daughter became an orphan, losing one parent to the afterlife and the other to the cruel realities of human nature.

And a boy with odd, silver eyes—he learned.
