Eric cracked his knuckles as he stepped out into the sun. The heat beat down, dense and humid. Birds sang nearby, and in his Mana Sight, the clearing was empty, devoid of threats save the horned rabbit. The tiny creature continued to chew peacefully on some leaves. By now, it was used to Eric, and didn’t even flee at his presence. Instead, it sat boldly in the sun and stared directly at him, as if daring him to try something.

Eric looked at it. He snorted. “Haven’t you noticed what I’ve done to everyone else around here? Joe was right. Ignorance really is bliss.”

The rabbit wiggled its nose, swallowed the leaf, then cocked back its head and scratched just behind the horn with its back leg.

“Yeah, yeah, I get it. You’re living the good life.” Leaving it behind, as always, Eric headed back toward the snakeman dungeon.

He had a full loadout: the full-size bat on one hip and the dagger on the other, with the wand stuffed in the back. Fortunately, he’d been wearing a belt with his khakis; it went a long way toward holding up all the weapons, even if it looked ridiculous over his truck stop outfit: black shirt with a yellow tire mark, yellow shorts, and a shiny, expensive leather belt strapped over the slightly-too-large shirt like it was the world’s ugliest medieval tunic.

No, wait—I recognize this look. This is how I dressed as a kid when I was pretending to be a fantasy prince and kill imaginary dragons. He sighed, putting his head in his hands. He was going to get popular. Become a famous player. Get all the cool gear, and look awesome, instead of like a five-year-old playing pretend. Also hopefully get real armor at some point, instead of walking around in a cotton t-shirt and jersey shorts.

Back at it. He quickly retraced his route back to the dungeon. His Mana Sight had gotten clearer at some point, and now it was easy to see the voids of mana where the snakes were. Either that, or the mana emanating from the dungeon was thicker. One way or another, he had no problem spotting and promptly dispatching the snakes on his way back. The cockroaches didn’t even qualify as a problem anymore… as long as they didn’t swarm. He still hadn’t figured out what triggered that, or if it was a rare or one-time kind of situation, but he hadn’t seen it since the first day. For all he knew, the cockroaches were starving from just spawning in, and that was what had triggered the feeding frenzy.

Whatever the case, he was glad to see no more of that. One cockroach swarm was enough for his entire life.

He slowed as he approached the dungeon. Whether it “respawned” or not, the central camp would have surely refreshed its outer guards. If they were intelligent and not automatic, then they likely would have also reinforced the guards. No… why am I thinking like this? Rather than automatic, the assumption should be that they’re intelligent. He grimaced. This whole scenario was so game-like that he was having a hard time separating reality and game. He had to keep swapping his expectations from reality to game and back again, and it made tracking what was going on just that much harder.

I’ll adjust eventually. It was hard because he wasn’t used to it. Once it became normal. he’d have a better grasp over what to expect and not expect.

Eric passed through the entrance of the dungeon at a crouch, gripping his bat of +1 damage in both hands. He swung wide around the entrance stones, peering behind them. One snake man, standing behind the stone the same as before, gripping the same spear as before.

Suspicious. Either this dungeon was respawning like a true game, or something was up. Why wouldn’t they reinforce? It had been two weeks, but on the other hand, it had only been two weeks. Maybe he was wrong. Maybe he was overthinking it. Maybe the snakemen were relatively simple creatures, who didn’t have the concept of reinforcement. But it struck him as odd that they’d only set one snakeman at the front of their home.

He paused, sweeping his Mana Sight over the front gate. A gap of mana hung over the stones, mingling with the tree branches. He narrowed his eyes, leaning slightly left and right. A net full of black snakes hung over the entry rocks, ready to drop its deadly load on whatever walked through the stones next. Eric leaned back the other way, tracing a tripwire back down to the entryway of the stones, where a thin wire glinted across the opening. Ah.

Eric approached the stone from the stone-side, keeping the stone between himself and the snakeman. He peeked around the corner. The snakeman stood there, bored, not really paying that much attention to the opening. It sat back on its thick tail and fiddled with its spear, tightening the twine fixing the blade in place. He retreated around the corner, took a deep breath, then crouched and reached out with his bat. Slowly, he poked the wooden weapon around the corner. It slid over dirt, further and further. Still no resistance. Sweat dripped down his face, and he took a slow breath, then pushed it a little further.

At last: resistance. The string jittered up the wood without catching, sliding along the smooth surface. He pulled the bat back and adjusted it, catching the string with the butt of the batt instead of the side, then pushed hard. Ropes creaked, and heavy, ropy bodies struck the ground with angry hisses a moment later. Eric backed up to give them time to clear, only for the first snakeman to come running out of the rocks, angry snakes draped over its shoulders and biting its head. It hissed in annoyance and frustration and clawed at the snakes on it. Eric stared at it. It stared at Eric. For a split second, they both froze.

The snakes and snakemen generally didn’t bother one another. Eric had triggered the trap with the intention of clearing it out and maybe distracting the snakemen. He hadn’t expected the snakes to attack the snakemen, but he didn’t mind that they had. He snapped out of his shock a second before the snakeman did and slammed his bat home. The snakeman stumbled back into another snakeman, which was also fleeing the snakes. The second snakeman shoved its dead compatriot away, giving Eric the perfect opening to swing again. Another skull cracked, and the second snakeman hit the floor.

He’d barely finished his second swing before a third one charged him, and a fourth, and a fifth, all of them fleeing snakes or being bitten by snakes. Eric just kept swinging. Foreswing, backswing, hammering back and forth until the snakemen stopped coming out of the stones. The snakes slithered by his feet. His heart raced and he stood on his tiptoes in fear. The snakes streamed past, ignoring him in preference for escaping into the forest.

A snakeman rushed him. He stepped back and felt something rope-like under his foot. A snake hissed and reared, striking his calf. At the same time, the snakeman thrust its spear at his heart. Eric struck upward with the bat, and wood clattered against wood as he clumsily knocked the spear aside. It jittered up over his collarbone, cutting a gash through his shoulder muscles. The snakeman snarled at him and kept closing in, rearing back its head to bite, too.

There was no time to get the bat back into position. He yanked the knife out of his belt and dropped the bat to catch the snakeman’s head instead, jamming the knife into the base of its long neck. It hissed and kept fighting, trying to strike through his grab. Eric shouted and jabbed it over and over again, until blood spurted out of its tough hide and it went limp in his hands. He dropped it to the ground and panted, finally lifting his foot off the snake underneath him. The snake jerked its tail free and slithered away, hissing.

No more snakemen came at him. The snakes all slithered away. Eric stumbled back and retrieved one of his grandma candies, cracking it open with his teeth to get to the poison-curing goo inside faster. He activated Regeneration, closing his wounds, not that there were many. The bites and the slash were all shallow or small, and healed quickly. He took a deep breath, then looked around him. All the monsters he’d killed were rapidly dissolving into loot, black smoke wafting off into the air.

Time to clean up.

He got more snakeskin, a candy, a packet of pretzels, and a wafer bar. The wafer bar was new, so he examined it.

Wafer Bar (Chocolate)

If you need a break, break this bar!

Uh… what? He tried examining it closer, but it didn’t give up any more than the description already had. Eric shrugged, making a mental note to ask Joe about it later. He didn’t know that the man would tell him, but he didn’t have a better option. Either that or just break it and see what happened, which was always an option.

I still have that tent that creates a temporary safe zone. It might be a smaller version of that, maybe?

He had to clear the dungeon today, according to Joe. He didn’t have time to sit and stare at it. He put it in his inventory, took a deep breath, and set off.

The three-way intersection was exactly as he’d left it. He encountered no more snakemen between the entrance and the intersection, and quickly dispatched the snakes he ran into. The intersection was unguarded. He quickly scouted out the left path and found the cave empty and unguarded. Retreating back to the intersection, he took the right path instead. This time, as he approached the turn in the path, he slowed down, softening his steps as he approached. At the very last second before the turn, he went still and held his breath to listen.

Nothing. Birdsong. The rush of the wind. Then, mixed into the creak of a tree: a hissing voice and the scrape of claws on stone.

Makes sense. Of course the overlook would be manned. These guys were essentially the lookouts for the village. They could go without the cave and retreat back to a position of power, but these guards were more essential to the safety of the army below.

Eric remained where he crouched, frozen still, barely breathing. One scrape. Two. A low voice and a higher voice. The snakemen grumbled at one another; he couldn’t understand the language, but he could follow the conversation enough to tell that there were only two of them. Two guards, the same as before? They didn’t reinforce the lookout?

It was a narrow shelf. There’d barely been room for him and the two snakemen before. They might just not have enough room to reinforce it.

But there are other ways to reinforce an overlook. Have someone watch the watchers, for example. Eric edged toward the corner, barely peeking around it. Two snakemen stood there, just as he’d thought. He got a quick survey of their gear, then backed around the corner.

Two snakemen. Armed with one spear apiece, and likely knives. No bows. No ranged weapons save the single spear.

I can probably dodge two spears. He’d always been proud of his footwork in basketball. His shooting wasn’t great, and three-pointers were a dream, but he could maneuver and dodge with the best of them. How far could they throw, anyways, with their stumpy limbs? Probably further than he thought, but not much further.

Retreating a little distance from the overlook, he lifted his eyes, gazing outward instead at the walls of the ravine. It took him a moment, but he found it: a second cleft. He squinted, narrowing his spreadsheet-dulled eyes to see a little further, and saw them at last: two black-scaled bodies, clutching spears and gazing across at his cleft.

For a second, Eric’s heart caught, but then he realized: if they’d seen him already, they would have raised the alarm. He was still hidden behind the trees. Only the cleft itself was cut back to have a clear view of the village below. He searched a little longer, but only found one more group of overlooks on the very far side of the encampment. Encampment, he repeated to himself, reinforcing the idea in his mind. That wasn’t a village. It was an encampment, a deliberate, aggressive emplacement of individuals. He’d find no families down there, just battle-ready adults.

He leaned left and right to get a better view of the encampment through the trees. In his memories, there were dozens of snakemen, but his memory had exaggerated the threat; only about twenty wandered below. Twenty was still a lot, but it was far fewer than “dozens.”

Okay. Twenty snakemen below. Six lookouts. If I take out one of the lookouts, the other two raise the alarm. He thought for a moment, running through his options.

One: take out the lookouts. Two snakemen down with little threat to him. Downside: the other lookouts would raise the alarm, and the entire encampment would come rushing his way. His only ranged weapon almost knocked him out after two shots. He could stay firmly on his feet after two shots now, after his weeks of training, but three shots was still outright impossible; it was just that he could still fight manually after the first two shots. Still, he wanted those shots for the boss. It felt like a waste to use them on the lookouts. Plus, they were loud and flashy, and the second he launched one, the lookouts—or even just the encampment—would raise the alarm anyways.

Ignoring the Magical Wand and only considering his melee options, he had a nice natural funnel what with the narrow path, But on the other hand, the narrow path also meant that he was cutting off his own escape route. There was one way in for the snakemen, and one way out for him: through them. The path dead-ended at a cliff behind him, and as far as he knew, the snakemen hadn’t survived the fall. He didn’t like his odds, especially not in hostile, snake-infested territory.

Two: leave the lookouts. Take on the camp. Upside: he didn’t immediately back himself into a corner. Downside: the lookouts would raise the alarm the second they noticed something suspicious. His window for any sort of guerrilla warfare was extremely narrow, and although twenty-on-one looked inevitable, he still didn’t like the idea of procing it immediately. On the other hand, he could always turn tail and hoof it if things went sideways, which appealed to him much more than the idea of locking himself into a dead end. The lookouts didn’t seem to have ranged weapons, which was awesome, but six spears was a lot more to dodge than two. In the heat of battle against twenty snakemen, he was pretty sure he wouldn’t even notice the spears until it was too late. He could always figure out how far they could throw their spears and stay outside of that range, but that required information he didn’t have yet. He’d learn it too late to act on it.

Three:

Eric thought for a long time, but nothing came to him. He was sure there was a third option, but it wasn’t coming to him right now. He grimaced, trying to make the best of an impossible choice. I can’t back myself into a corner. I think I have to leave the lookouts. It felt like a bad idea, but putting his back to a cliff also seemed like a bad idea.

If only I could funnel them into a chokepoint. Taking them on one or two at a time was far superior to taking on twenty at once. If he had a chokepoint, then he could… he could…

Wait a minute. I have a chokepoint. Eric looked over his shoulder, back at the entryway. Sure, it was a bit of a hike, but these guys were kind of dumb. All the ones he’d seen had immediately rushed him with intent to kill. If he went down there, got their attention, and then ran for the entryway with the three stones, not only would he have a natural chokepoint, but he’d also have an out if things went real sideways.

No, I won’t. I can’t leave the dungeon in combat. He twisted his lips. That was right: he’d found that out last time. He was still putting his back to a wall, but he wasn’t completely out of options. He could probably scramble over the boulders for high ground, or…

It was a good idea. It just needed refinement. He ran it over a few more times, making a few amendments, then took a deep breath. Okay. Here goes nothing.

He lunged out of the path and ran straight for the lookouts. The first one startled, barely able to react before he was on top of it; its skull shattered under his bat, and he kicked the body off the cliff. The second one jolted off the stone it’d been using for a seat and scrambled for its spear. Before it could arm itself, Eric closed the gap and took its head with a gruesome crack. It toppled backward and dropped off the cliff directly.

Across the way, one of the lookouts startled. They pointed, jabbing their finger at Eric’s cliff. Their buddy fished around on its chest for a whistle, but Eric was already sprinting back down the path the way he’d come. The second the cliff turned into more of a slope, he hopped off the path and skidded down the hill instead. The incline was steeper than he’d realized, and he slipped along, out of control, falling more than sliding. He grabbed at a bunch of undergrowth and yanked it up by the roots rather than slow himself, then careened into a bunch of saplings, bouncing off directly at a huge oak tree. Grimacing, Eric extended his legs and braced for impact. He hit the tree feet-first, and for a second, his feet snagged in a crag in the tree for just a moment. His upper body kept falling, while his feet stayed put, stuck in the tree.

Eric shouted in shock and yanked his feet free, saving himself a broken ankle, but it was too late; he’d already whipped around, no longer falling feet first but instead, head-first. Dust spurted up in his face, getting in his eyes and lungs. He coughed, blinking desperately. Tears welled up, running down his dusty face, and cleared his eyes just in time to see the bush he was hurtling toward. Eric threw his arms up to cushion the blow and shut his eyes, and a second later, he thumped into the bush. Dense branches cracked and snapped as he fell into them, but held, creaking as they bore his weight.

Cautiously, Eric opened his eyes. He still hung upside down, his weight supported by his shoulders, head, and neck. There were branches in his face, terrifyingly close to his eyes, but he hadn’t taken any serious damage; just bruises and scratches. He moved his limbs, checking for damage. Scrapes and bruises, all the way through. He’d lost a shoe in the tree in his haste to get free, and his socked foot pressed directly against the rocky incline.

He pushed, panting as he slowly extricated himself from the bush, scoring another few scratches along the way. He threw his weight downward, flipping himself around to fall butt-first, and slid the last few feet to the ground. Leaves crunched as he thumped down. For a second, he just sat there, stunned, and then he slowly pushed himself upright. He still wore one leather boot, but after a few limping steps without the missing shoe, he abandoned it to go socks-only instead. If he stepped on any rocks, he’d flinch for sure, but fighting with unstable footing from the start sounded even worse.

He looked up. The other shoe wasn’t unreachable; it just wasn’t tenable to retrieve it on the timeframe he currently had. He’d have to scramble up the cliff or try a second, more controlled drop, and he was no rock climber. It would have to wait. He sent his second shoe to his inventory and made a mental note of the location of his lost shoe, then took off at a jog through the woods in his socks.

No more damage bonus against bugs, he noted, then snorted. He’d be back for it. He couldn’t leave his precious bug-crushing shoes behind.

As he jogged, he pulled out two of the big rolls of snakeskin from his inventory. Each of the big rolls was about as wide across as his abdomen, and the same exact shiny black as the snakemen’s scales. Before he’d started this plan, he’d cut one of the smaller rolls up into strips with his dagger, then used the same dagger to poke holes in the bigger roles. Taking the strips, he’d tied the two bigger rolls together at his shoulders, letting them hang down in front and behind his abdomen like a sort of deranged tunic. Now, at the bottom of the cliff, he draped another small roll over his head and tied it under his chin to cover his chestnut hair with black scales. It wasn’t the best disguise, but it didn’t have to be. All he needed was to pass muster at a glance, from a distance, through the trees.

Piercing whistles sounded from the encampment, echoing across the ravine. The woods weren’t particularly thick here, and he could already see the first of the snakemen rushing down the path to the overlook. He put his back to a thicker tree and waited, counting the footsteps as best he could. Five? Ten? Somewhere in there. He peeked at their backs as they ran to the lookout to get a better count, and came up with eight. Eight snakemen rushing to his supposed location.

Eight fewer snakemen in the encampment.

He’d thought about looping behind them, cornering them on the death trap platform, and killing them there, but the problem was that all they had to do was send reinforcements and he was back in his original position, stuck between a cliff and a hard place (the ravine wall). So instead, he ran, eyes on his next goal.

Eric sprinted through the woods, crunching sticks under his socked feet. Occasionally, he stepped on a rock or a hard root that made him wince, but he put the pain to the back of his mind and kept running. Across to the second lookout. He reached the path, charged over it, and kept going through the woods, running as the bird flies without care for the routes through the woods. It was relatively flat territory at the bottom of the ravine anyways. He took a fair share of spiderwebs to the face and had to leap over fallen trees and streams, but it wasn’t that hard to cross.

He’d grown up in the woods. Grown up hiking with his slightly-crazy, distance runner mom and peaks-obsessed dad. Twenty-mile hikes were a normal day’s walk. He’d summited a 14,000 foot peak with nothing but a bottle of Gatorade, a bottle of water, a handful of granola bars, and a sweat jacket at the age of twelve. Sure, he was a pasty office worker now, who really needed to get back to that gym he was paying for at some point. But back in the day, he’d been a hell of a hiker.

True, he’d basically been dragged into every hike kicking and screaming, but that didn’t mean he hadn’t done it. He knew how to seal his mouth shut to save moisture when he ran out of water on mile four of ten in the blazing summer heat. He knew how to cross slick stone without slipping and how to clamber a fallen trunk without breaking stride. His body was flabby and pale now, but once, it had been toned and tan. He’d thought those days were behind him, that he’d rot away behind a computer happy and unhealthy in the comfortable A/C, but here he was, back in the woods again. And he remembered. He remembered like he’d never left.

His breath came fast, and his eyes opened wide. He kicked through the undergrowth, barely feeling the brambles even when his legs began to bleed. He grinned, almost laughing, and ran even faster. I used to hate this. So why do I feel so alive?

Behind him, the snakemen reached the first lookout and found themselves empty-handed. They milled around, confused, occasionally hissing at one another with loud voices. He glanced back once, leaning to see them better through the dense leaves, then didn’t spare them another look, hoping the canopy disguised his form enough for them to not notice him or otherwise mistake him for a snakeman with his shabby snakeskin disguise. It wasn’t much of a disguise, but this was exactly what he’d engineered it for: a quick glance through the leaves at a distance. Don’t notice me. I’m nothing. I’m another one of you.

More hissing, more angry snake grumbles. Someone shouted, and Eric’s heart lurched, but the expected sound of pursuit never came. If they’d seen something, it wasn’t him.

He closed in on the second lookout. Eric started swiveling, taking in the forest around him, and before long, he found what he was looking for: the well-worn route through the woods to the lookout. This path crisscrossed sharply up the ravine wall, doubling back on itself as it climbed the cliff face. It wouldn’t be as easy to ambush the lookouts, but that was fine. They were distracted, staring across the ravine and waving angry messages to the other snakemen. Keeping his head down, Eric climbed the path as quickly as he could, knowing they’d see through his disguise at some point. Just a little further. One more switchback.

Three switchbacks remained. His heart raced, but he walked calmly, pretending like there was nothing strange about this. His palms sweated into the grip of his bat. He glanced up. The snakemen were still distracted, pointing their spears at their compatriots on the opposite lookout. Two. One of them glanced down, and his heart lurched, but it just scratched its arm and looked back up, jabbing a clawed finger across the gap. One. He couldn’t wait any longer; his heart was in his throat. He lunged, running for the closer snakeman. At last, the monster turned. It screeched and pointed, and that cost it its life. Eric swung as hard as he could. Its head smashed into the ravine wall, and its body dropped. Behind it, the other snakeman jolted and fumbled for its spear. It never had the time to get a good grip. Eric smashed it into another red splatter on the wall.

No time to waste. The third lookout was whistling, and the eight snakemen on the first cleft were all shouting and pointing, the fastest of their number already sprinting down the path toward him. He vaulted from his switchback down to the next one, taking the path like a very tall set of stairs, leaping down one switchback at a time. He hit the floor and ran for it.

The third lookout was on the far side of the encampment. The fastest route there was right through the center, but that was suicide. Snakemen flooded toward him, running through the forest toward the second checkpoint from both the encampment and the first lookout, a few of the smarter ones going to cut him off on the pass for the third.

But Eric didn’t run for the third lookout. Instead, he ran from the second lookout directly back toward the first, skirting along the edge of the encampment. Snakemen ran for the second lookout, they ran for the third, but no one ran for the first. Some ran from the first, but not directly. It was still deadly to leap off the cliff at the end of the lookout. To leave the first lookout, they had to take the path back at least some ways, then run for the second. Eric, not bound by the height of the first lookout, was free to run straight there, and knew he’d encounter almost no snakemen on that route; so that was where he ran.

He didn’t run all the way back, though. Instead, he slowed as he reached the encampment, taking a moment to survey the chaos. Snakemen ran for the lookouts, some rushing for the paths, others braving the forest. A handful remained, no more than five or six, and they clustered around the giant tent in the center. It was clear as day: they were guarding the central tent.

He swallowed. This was it. His decision point. He could run around in the woods and exhaust himself playing guerilla, take out as many snakemen as he could, then try that tent. Or he could run straight in, take out the five or six guards with relative ease, then dive into the tent and pray that killing the boss ended the dungeon.

If there’s a boss, I’ll need my energy to face it. If I run around taking out twenty-plus snakemen, I’ll be exhausted before I even get to the boss. One way or another, the only way to beat this dungeon today is to rush the boss right now.

Eric took a deep breath. He wasn’t this kind of player. He liked to take it slow and cautious. Level up until he was confident he could take the boss on. But right now, Joe’s words echoing in his ears and the chaos he’d caused roiling the forest around him, he stared at the tent, unable to look away.

Joe wouldn’t have told me to beat the dungeon if he thought it was impossible. It has to be possible. Killing twenty snakemen and a boss doesn’t feel possible at my level. He knows that, and he told me to clear it anyways. It has to be an option. It has to… wait. Why don’t I just ask?

Hey, System. Can I take down the dungeon by beating the boss? Will I have twenty angry snakemen after my head if I kill it? Or is it the load-bearing type?

Objective: Clear the dungeon.

Clear options: eliminate all enemies; kill the boss; destroy the core.

Killing the boss was a “clear option.” In other words, a way to beat the dungeon. It was as clear an answer as he was going to get. He tightened his grip on the bat and narrowed his eyes, focusing on that massive tent. It was time to take on the boss.
