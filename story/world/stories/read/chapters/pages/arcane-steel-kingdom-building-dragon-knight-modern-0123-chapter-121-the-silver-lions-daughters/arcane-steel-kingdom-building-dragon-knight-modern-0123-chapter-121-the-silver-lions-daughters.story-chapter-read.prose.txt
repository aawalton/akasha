My mother finally let go.

She pulled back, her hands hovering inches from Ela’s narrow shoulders as if afraid the girl might shatter. The silence in the dining hall stretched thin, pulled tight over the raw, messy reality of the reunion.

Lyra broke the tension.

She launched herself from her chair, a blur of silver hair and pink fabric, skidding to a halt right in front of the terrified, confused girl.

"Hello!" Lyra yelled. "I am Lyra Wight! You can call me Lyra. I love booms! What's your name?"

Ela flinched. She shrank back, her shoulders curling inward. This was a girl used to making herself small, used to hiding from noise. Now she was staring at a miniature, aggressively cheerful hurricane.

Ela looked at Lyra, her sapphire eyes—so similar to mine—wide with a mix of stark terror and utter confusion. Her mouth opened, but no sound emerged.

"Um..." Ela finally managed, her small hands twisting the fabric of her borrowed, too-clean blue dress. "I... I am Eleanore. You can... call me Ela."

Looking at them side-by-side physically hurt. Lyra vibrated with the secure, insulated joy of a protected child. Ela, forged in mud and starvation, held a posture that screamed a desperate need to simply survive the next five minutes. They shared the same blood. They should have had the same life.

Lyra, oblivious to the tragedy, forged ahead. "Ela!" she decided, nodding as if approving a tactical plan. "And what do you like to do?"

Ela blinked. The question clearly didn't compute. She racked her brain, probably searching for an answer that wouldn't result in a beating.

"I..." she whispered, glancing nervously around the room. "I like... eating food."

Right on cue, Ela's stomach let out a loud, hollow growl.

The blood drained from Ela’s face. A furious, shameful red rushed up her pale cheeks. She ducked her head, her shoulders bunching up, bracing for the inevitable reprimand. For being greedy. For being loud.

My mother moved before anyone else could. The tears vanished, replaced by the Iron Duchess.

"Well," she said, her voice smooth, steady, and incredibly warm. "That is a very good thing to like. Come, Eleanore."

She gently placed a hand on Ela's shoulder and guided her toward the massive weirwood table. Lyra skipped alongside them, offering her own unprompted wisdom.

"Ah, yes," Lyra nodded seriously, eyeing a platter of eggs. "Food is good. But it's not as satisfying as a really good explosion."

Ela looked at my sister as if she had just sprouted horns. She clearly had no idea what this loud girl was talking about, but she let herself be seated anyway.

I took my own chair next to Nyxia. She had been sitting in stunned silence throughout the entire emotional breakdown, watching a filthy child materialize from a portal and turn my stoic family into a weeping mess.

I leaned over, my shoulder brushing hers. "I'll explain later," I muttered.

Nyxia’s neck turned a blotchy pink. She cleared her throat, grabbed her teacup, and stared very hard at the Earl Grey inside it.

I focused back on the table.

Ela stared at her plate. It held a basic meal—eggs, bacon, a thick slice of buttered bread. But to her, it might as well have been the crown jewels. She hadn't touched a thing. She just sat there, hands clasped tightly in her lap, looking absolutely terrified.

My mother leaned in. "Dear? Do you not like the food? I can have the kitchens make something else. Whatever you want."

Ela looked up, her eyes bright and wet. "Is... is this food for me?"

My mother reached out and gently stroked the girl's silver hair. "Of course it is. It's all yours. You can ask for seconds. Eat as much as you want."

I saw the problem immediately. The abundance of food was intimidating, but the room itself was the real issue. My father, my mother, Nyxia—they all ate with the quiet, practiced elegance of high nobility. Forks clinking softly. Measured bites.

Ela didn't know how to do that. She feared doing it wrong. She feared proving she was just a filthy peasant who didn't belong at this table.

She glanced sideways at Lyra. Their eyes met.

A silent, unspoken understanding passed between them.

Lyra, who had been using her fork perfectly five minutes ago, grabbed a fistful of bacon with her bare hands. She shoved half of it into her mouth, chewed loudly, and grinned at Ela, grease shining on her chin.

Ela stared.

A tiny, hesitant smile cracked the fear on her face. She reached out. Her small, trembling hand bypassed the heavy silver fork entirely. She grabbed a piece of bread.

She took a bite. Then another. Within seconds, she was eating with the messy, desperate intensity of a child who didn't know if this plate would suddenly vanish. She matched Lyra, bite for messy bite.

It was wholesome. It was cute. And it made me feel sick to my stomach.

We had failed her. My family, my massive fleet, my automated armies—all of it had been too late to stop her from learning to eat like a starved animal. I glanced at my father. He watched the girls, his jaw clenched so tight a muscle ticked in his cheek. He was thinking the exact same thing.

We were never going to fail her again.

…

Breakfast ended. The chaotic relief settled into a quiet, heavy determination. We moved out to the garden gazebo for tea. The simulated morning sun felt warm against the polished stone, contrasting sharply with the cold business we were about to conduct.

My mother set her teacup down. The clink was sharp. Final.

"I have something important to announce."

Everyone stopped. My father leaned back, crossing his arms. He looked supportive, but serious.

"I will be adopting Ela as our daughter," my mother stated. It was a royal decree. "Do any of you have any objections?"

My father gave a slow nod. "We discussed it. I am of the same mind. She is blood."

"No objections here," I added. "I already see her as a little sister."

Lyra practically vibrated off her cushion. "Yes! Yes! Yes!" A permanent playmate was clearly the best news she’d had all week.

My mother turned her gaze to the woman sitting rigidly beside me. "Nyxia?"

Nyxia blinked, startled by the inclusion. "Me? Um... do I have a say in this?"

"Obviously," my mother replied, her tone softening just a hair. "You are a part of this family now. You are like a daughter to me."

Nyxia stiffened slightly at that, but she bowed her head gracefully. "I have no objections, Your Grace."

Finally, my mother looked at the small girl sitting nervously on the edge of her oversized chair. "And what do you say, Eleanore?"

Ela looked around. She looked at the giant, scarred man with the sad eyes. She looked at the lady who smelled like safety. She looked at me, the guy who had dropped from the sky in scary armor and promised her food. She looked at the loud girl with the bacon grease.

She had spent her whole life hiding in the dirt, waiting for a ghost.

"I..." Ela started. Her voice shook. She looked down at her lap, then back up, her eyes swimming. "I want to stay. Please."

My mother smiled. It was a radiant, fierce expression that banished the last of the shadows from the gazebo. "Then it is settled. Welcome home, Eleanore Wight."

Cinderfall was losing its mind.

The attack on the capital’s naval docks by the Abyssal Fleet at dawn had shattered the illusion of a distant war. The impregnable fortress of the Hegemony had bled in its own harbor. The fires were out now, but the panic in the streets spread faster than the flames ever could.

The people were done. The bread bombs had shown them someone else cared; the burning docks showed them their King was weak. Cinderfall fighting a losing battle against its own starving citizens.

Then, the Oracle picked it up.

A massive, continental-scale energy spike originated from the bedrock of the capital itself. Something incredibly old, and terrifyingly powerful, was waking up.

A shriek tore through the city. It vibrated in the teeth, a high, piercing cry that shattered the stained glass in the upper wards of the palace.

Instantly, the royal aviaries erupted. Every lesser phoenix in the city answered the call. A chorus of hundreds of shrieks echoed the first—a chaotic mix of absolute subservience and pure terror.

From the base of the mountain, rising straight up through the grand, magmatic caldera of the Cinderfall palace, a shape ascended.

It tore into the sky like a tornado of fire, its wingspan easily exceeding three hundred feet. Its fire was blinding, majestic, pure gold.

It reached the apex of its flight, its massive wings casting a shimmering, golden shadow that blotted out the sun over the panic-stricken city.

This was the Guardian. The anchor holding the nation together. The progenitor that had spawned every other phoenix in the Hegemony.

The Tier 9 Mother Phoenix. The absolute power of Cinderfall.

The other phoenixes, the mounts of the elite guard, swarmed up to join it, setting the sky ablaze as they flew in wide, protective, frantic circles above the capital. The sheer heat of that golden sky seemed to evaporate the lingering smoke from the docks. More importantly, the sight of it began to quell the panic burning in the streets.

This was the first time in a century the Mother Phoenix had taken to the sky. Regular citizens lived and died without ever seeing the progenitor. It only showed itself when the kingdom was on the brink of collapse.

This was the very same beast that had flown beside the First King. It had forged the nation in fire. It was immortal. It had never lost a battle. Every time it appeared in the history books, it brought back victory for Cinderfall.

The majestic bird folded its massive wings and descended, landing heavily atop the great dome of the Cinderfall castle. It settled onto a specialized, reinforced perching spire built into the stone centuries ago for this exact purpose.

It folded its golden wings. Its eyes, burning with ancient, furious intelligence, stared out toward the coast. The Hegemony was bleeding, but its protector had finally woken up. And it was ready for war.

…

I watched the golden flare on the tactical display overlaying my vision. Even as I sat in the gazebo, surrounded by my family and the quiet joy of a new beginning, the war demanded my attention.

The Oracle feed was unambiguous. The Tier 9 had entered the board.

The heat signature radiating from the Cinderfall capital was staggering, dwarfing the combined output of the entire surviving Phoenix Knight roster. It was a localized, contained sun, a biological weapon of mass destruction that possessed centuries of combat experience.

I felt a cold knot form in my stomach. The first phase of the war was over. The surgical strikes, the bread bombs, the naval bombardments—they had succeeded in crippling the Hegemony's conventional forces. But conventional forces don't win wars in this world.

Tear nines do.

Tes, I projected, my mental voice flat, hiding the rising tension from my family. Status on General Goliath. How much longer until he extracts the cores from the Dominion?

[General Goliath is currently engaging the guardian entity of the second mature dungeon, Master,] Tes reported. [Combat efficiency remains optimal, but the density of the horde has slowed his advance. Estimated time to secure the second core and initiate transit to the extraction point: three hours.]

Three hours. An eternity in wartime.

We needed those cores to power the heavy planetary defense grids I intended to establish around the conquered territories of Aerthos. Without them, we were holding a vast stretch of land with a finite amount of mobile firepower. If the Mother Phoenix flew against my forward lines now, before the grid was active, the casualties would be catastrophic.

I looked at Ela, who was currently trying to teach Lyra how to build a small tower out of sugar cubes. Her laughter, fragile and new, was the most important sound in the world right now. I had just promised this girl a home. I couldn't let a giant, flaming bird burn it down before she even got a chance to sleep in a real bed.

I don't want to use another Icarus, I thought to myself, the memory of the searing white light and the silent devastation fresh in my mind. It's a blunt instrument. A last resort.

But if that thing takes flight toward my lines...

Tes. Divert twenty percent of the Omni-Forge's current output toward finalizing the orbital defense array. Prioritize the kinetic shielding modules.

[Acknowledged, Master. Reallocating resources. Production timeline for the Aegis Shield network accelerated by four hours.]

It would have to be enough. I closed the tactical display, forcing my attention back to the table, back to the tea and the sunlight. The war was coming, a clash of titans that would reshape the continent. But for these few, precious minutes, I was just a son, a brother, and a husband.

The Warlord could wait.
