Tavian waited for Valliroth to make his move. If the man was unwell enough that he was willing to fight Tavian and his men, then Tavian would back down.

Valliroth glared at him. He was still smarting from the loss of his mother and his home. It was the kind of pain that made men do irrational things.

“Valliroth,” Tavian said. “I’m not asking you to forgive me, or even to understand. Please—”

Valliroth didn’t move. His sword quivered, shining in the torchlight.

“I’m sorry for what I did to you,” Tavian said. “But I couldn’t lose another friend. Not like that.”

Valliroth’s eyes flicked off to the side.

Tavian followed them, and spotted Anella standing in the shadows, her eyes red, hair disheveled.

Valliroth slammed his sword back into the scabbard. His shoulders relaxed.

“What is wrong with us?” Valliroth gasped. “We have grown so divided.”

As Valliroth made the choice to have peace, the mist of miasma that Tavian saw on Valliroth’s mind… dissipated. It melted away into the shadows, like it had never been there.

Tavian wondered if his blow had been responsible for the miasma that had entered his friend. Then he marveled that the man was strong enough to reject it.

Valliroth gritted his teeth, then swallowed. Tavian recognized the action. His friend was putting aside personal grievance in favor of his duty to his people.

“I can't forgive you for what you’ve done,” Valliroth said. “But… Thank you for saving my sister’s life. She did not deserve to die with us.”

“No,” Tavian agreed. Never interrupt a man when he is convincing himself.

“My people are hungry, Tavian,” Valliroth said. “It’s my duty, as the head of House Tiren, to care for the physical needs of Ishtoran’s priests.”

Tavian nodded, slowly, watching the gears turning in Valliroth’s mind.

“You feel the same way,” Valliroth said.

“I do.”

“Then so long as we share a goal, perhaps we can work together. I would buy food from you with this gold.”

“I have a better offer for you,” Tavian said. “Swear fealty. Join the kingdom.”

Anella moved over to her brother, taking his arm and pulling him down so she could whisper in his ear.

Valliroth straightened, and looked Tavian in the eye. “I have here three chests, filled with gold. It is not much, for all these.” He gestured to his priests. Most of them were watching, now. “Will you accept them as our payment of your tax? Will you accept our oaths of fealty?”

“I will,” Tavian said. “House Tiren’s allegiance is worth the price.”

Tavian’s men grabbed the three chests. Tavian accepted the fealty of the priests.

“Yanin is dead,” Goren said, his face white as a sheet. “They pulled him down. I think they were eating him.” His voice was flat, matter of fact, but the boy was shaking as he spoke.

He’d sent these boys out to their death. He’d known some of them would die. And yet he’d dared to hope they would all live.
