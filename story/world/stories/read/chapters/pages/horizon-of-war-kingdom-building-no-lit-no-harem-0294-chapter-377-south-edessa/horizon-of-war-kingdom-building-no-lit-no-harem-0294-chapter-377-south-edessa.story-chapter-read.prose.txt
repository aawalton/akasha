Chapter 377

South Edessa

Lansius

The night of their advance toward Southern Edessa had passed. Bathed in the morning light, Lansius stood atop a low rocky rise with his entourage around him. From there, he had a clear view of the army gathered across the fields below.

Nearly seven thousand men-at-arms.

The number looked very different when found in records.

Below him, entire fields had disappeared beneath men. Even at rest, hundreds gathered within each part of the formation, sitting or standing in loose ranks. One group followed another, and then another beyond that. Standards rose everywhere above helmets and pikeheads. Behind them were tents, carts, servants, and beasts of burden, each taking up more ground.

A single thousand men would already fill a large square of earth when drawn up for battle. Here, nearly seven such masses were gathered before him.

It was as though the whole of Korelia had emptied itself into these fields.

And that was without counting the nearly two thousand horsemen spread across three separate columns. Horses took far more room than men, and among the Lowlandians it was common for a horseman to bring a spare mount, sometimes more than one. Even at a distance, their formations seemed immense.

Since his ascension as Lord Shogun, Lansius had grown accustomed to seeing such numbers in reports. Seven thousand. Ten thousand. Twenty thousand. On parchment they were merely figures. But standing above them was different.

Every number was a man.

Every hundred was a crowd.

Every thousand was a host in its own right.

And these numbers were no small army. By the standards of much of the medieval history Lansius remembered, it was a substantial field force.

During the Third Crusade, even Richard the Lionheart and Saladin often took the field with armies numbering only in the low tens of thousands. Many battles were fought with fewer than that. And many medieval armies were a mixture of feudal retainers, levies, militia, mercenaries, and other temporary contingents.

The Shogunate was different.

This force of nearly ten thousand men-at-arms, knights, crossbowmen, and dragoons was not made up of peasants hastily gathered and handed spears for a season. They were professionals, veterans, and career soldiers who had marched, fought, and survived together. Many had already seen more battles than most men would witness in a lifetime.

And among their formations were thousands of camp followers, men, women, and their children who had attached themselves to the brigade. They sustained the army in countless small ways, as cooks, barbers, peddlers, craftsmen, entertainers, and sellers of the little necessities fighting men often overlooked until they needed them. Among them were also the Edessans who had thrown in their lot with the Shogunate after being abandoned by the treacherous Margrave.

Looking over them, Lansius could not help but feel the weight settle on his shoulders.

Their lives depended on him and his officers.

That old sense of immense responsibility, long buried during his years of sleep, finally returned in full.

Looking back, Lansius knew this debacle likely would not have happened if he had remained awake. His vision for the Shogunate had always been to help, not to stand forever on the front line as a bulwark. His men had politically overextended themselves, too proud of what they had achieved. Still, he knew it had been born not from greed, but from honor.

They still saw Edessa as part of the Imperium, and believed they had a duty to protect its people. That had become their mistake. And the Margrave had used it against them.

In reality, the Imperium had already ceased to exist. What remained was little more than the pretense that it still endured. Even Lansius’ title as Count was only decoration on paper, carrying little real substance.

The morning wind blew across the plains, making the blue and bronze banners flutter strongly.

A group of horsemen approached, with Sir Harold among them.

Around supper the night before, the Banneret had brought Emma to see him. Their conversation had lasted for some time. Lansius had been especially interested in what had become of the Monastery, now known as Gregoria Castle.

On the same occasion, Captain Karl also introduced his mage, Alwyn. Lansius found him an interesting man. No longer young, he was still eager to prove himself. He kept a low profile and spoke with caution, but others said that when action was needed, he was decisive and brave.

Still atop the mound, Lansius watched as his physician climbed up to him and handed him another cup of medicine for his cough. The blood he had spat when he first awakened days ago remained a serious concern. As usual, Ocelot checked it first. Her sense of smell was as reliable as any cupbearer’s test.

Soon, Sir Harold arrived with Sir Stefan.

“My Lord,” they greeted.

“The army is ready to march,” Sir Harold reported.

“Let the men march at a comfortable pace,” Lansius said. “Don’t push them hard. Our destination, a town named Nona, isn’t far.”

Sir Harold gave a firm nod.

“And one more thing,” Lansius added. “I need you to prepare a few things. Subtle things, like what we did in the past.”

Before Sir Harold could answer, a stir ran through the far side of the formation. Men pointed toward the sky, others rising from where they rested, some shading their eyes as they looked upward. The commotion quickly spread through the nearby ranks.

Lansius followed their gaze only briefly before recognizing what had drawn their attention.

“Ah, the Lady of the House has arrived as well,” he commented. “She’ll lend a hand in our next move.”

The others looked up and saw the feared black airship approaching, its bronze bat ornaments unmistakable.

“The Lady commands that one?” Sir Harold asked.

“Indeed. As I said, the Lord of Dawn is busy with the Lord of Three Hills. They’re using the silver one.”

Another airship soon appeared behind it, much smaller, with ivory-colored skin.

Though talents from across the Shogunate had tried fitting them with a small steam contraption heated by the main burner to drive a propeller, the design was still weak and relied on small propeller. It was a far cry from the powerful yet complex pneumatic engine used by the silver airship.

Their speed was nowhere near that of the silver vessel, which could fly between Korelia and Korimor in less than ten hours.

A new propulsion system had been ordered from distant Ekionia and was currently undergoing tests in Dawn. But with the war still ongoing, there was no time to refit the airships.

For Lansius’ plan, however, these two were already enough.

Quietly, he reached into the inner pocket of his traveling cloak and found the earring. He did not need to take it out. Simply touching it and concentrating was enough.

Good morning to my beloved wife. Welcome to South Edessa.

Then a reply came, as if straight into his mind.

“Morning, my dearest Count of Midlandia. Did you think of me last night?”

Lansius could not resist a smile as he looked toward the leading airship, knowing she had already laid eyes on him.

Always.

The Walled Town of Nona

It was midday, but there was plenty of shade from the trees around the fields leading toward the walled town of Nona. Villages and fields heavy with crops surrounded the town. With the Margrave’s few hundred riders pinned far behind and screened off by the dragoons, who had every reason to fight hard against these allies turned traitors, there was no immediate threat to the tercio column. The command therefore allowed the troops into the nearby woods to gather firewood.

The army had drawn up at a measured distance from the town, close enough to display its full strength and put pressure on Nona, but not so near as to frighten away the men coming out to negotiate.

After the army sent a messenger with a calm and peaceful request for a meeting, three men emerged from the town gate and began walking toward the Shogunate camp. Along the battlements, townsfolk and guards had gathered to watch them go.

It was called an invitation, but no one in Nona could mistake it for a simple courtesy. Refusing might invite the wrath of the entire column. With the town’s crops lying outside the walls, the Shogunate would not even need to storm Nona. A few fires in the fields could leave the town facing the year without a harvest.

Though one of the three had the bearing of a veteran man-at-arms, all of them looked uneasy.

Beneath a colorful field tent, Sir Harold, Sir Stefan, and Mage Alwyn received them. Folding chairs and tables had been set out, along with bread, cheese, salt, pickles, and olives arranged on a large silver platter. A glazed earthenware jug stood beside several silver goblets.

“Good greetings from the town of Nona to the illustrious forces of the Shogunate,” the plump man in the red doublet began smoothly. “I am Luka, and these are Milos and Jovan.”

Milos was the old man-at-arms. His leathery skin, silvery hair, and neatly kept brown beard might have made him look like an aging farmer at first glance. But the scar crossing his chin, the strength in his gaze, and the way he held himself upright told a different story. He stood like a man long accustomed to armor, orders, and battle.

Luka continued, “We are pleased to receive you on your passage, and we hope your journey through this part of Edessa will be without trouble.”

The only answer he received was a curt, “Please, sit,” from Sir Stefan.

It was polite enough, but having their greeting ignored only made the three men more nervous. With little choice, they obeyed after a moment’s hesitation. Two made little effort to hide their nerves. Only Milos, commander of the town guard, seemed to have himself under control.

As the three took their seats, however, the men of the Shogunate remained standing. This baffled them. They had come expecting a negotiation.

Noticing their reaction, Sir Stefan made the introductions. “I am Sir Stefan, second in command of this force. And this,” he said, turning toward the man in the weathered traveling cloak, “is Alwyn of the Mage Guild.”

“Greetings.” The mage dipped his head.

The three men readily inclined their heads in return.

Sir Stefan continued. “And this is the knight commander of this force, Sir Harold, Banneret of the Shogunate.”

The three looked toward the imposing Sir Harold. He gave them a polite nod. “Gratitude for accepting our invitation.”

Again, the three inclined their heads in return. Luka wiped the sweat from his plump cheeks and asked, “What do you want to discuss with us?”

“Not yet,” Sir Stefan said. “But please, help yourselves to the bread, cheese, and olives.”

The three exchanged uneasy glances. Milos was about to reach for the food when Sir Harold stepped toward the table. Towering over them in a polished steel breastplate scarred by marks of battle, he quietly took up the pitcher and poured himself a drink. He drank from the goblet, then tore off a piece of bread and ate it with some cheese and olives, plainly showing that the food was safe.

The three men ate reluctantly at first, but soon found that the bread had been baked from fine wheat flour, the olives were good, and the goat cheese was of fine quality. This was no poor military ration.

After some time, with the three Shogunate officers still standing, Luka finally dared to ask, “Pardon, good sirs. Are we waiting for someone?”

Sir Stefan did not answer at once. He turned toward the rear of the camp, as though expecting something.

Luka swallowed. “We apologize if we have—”

Trumpets suddenly blared. Across the field, the three men saw thousands of men-at-arms stir as one. Backs straightened. Pikes rose upright. Shields were drawn close and held ready. The sight only deepened their unease. Who could make even the knight commander of such a force stand and wait? Nervousness crept over Luka and Jovan, and even Milos seemed troubled by the unexpected display.

Seven figures emerged from between the ranks, two of them cloaked and hooded. As they advanced, officers snapped to attention. Even men already standing seemed to straighten further. The movement passed through the lines like a wave.

Questions arose among the three as to who these figures were to command such respect from the troops.

Then, as if on cue, a great shape rose from behind a stand of trees, hidden from view until that moment. As it climbed higher, the three realized what it was. An airship, majestic and imposing, its skin and gondola glossy black and trimmed with brass, with a huge bat ornament dominating its prow. Flames burst from its mouth several times as it rose, making the vessel seem almost alive.

The sight stunned not only the three representatives, but the people watching from Nona’s battlements. They knew the stories of the black airship, the one said to make even the toughest men of the Empire wet themselves. Voices rose along the walls as people called to one another and pointed toward the sky.

Then it got worse. Another airship began to rise. There were two of them. The three men could only clutch at their clothes and swallow hard.

At last, the two cloaked figures reached the field tent. They pulled back their hoods, startling the three men even further. One was a half-kin. The other was a black-haired man with a thin black beard.

“Apologies for the wait,” said the black-haired man, speaking to the commanders with complete ease, like a man long accustomed to being in power.

By then, Luka and Jovan had gone pale, their lips trembling as they struggled to grasp who stood before them. The one man who had no reason to be anywhere near their humble town of Nona was now standing before them. They were so stunned that neither remembered to rise.

Ignoring their reaction, the black-haired man took the middle seat. The tall Sir Harold sat to his left, with Sir Stefan to his right. The half-kin remained standing behind him alongside the finely dressed young man and Mage Alwyn, while the guards stayed outside the tent.

“Gentlemen,” the black-haired man began, then stopped. His brow furrowed.

“You look a little pale,” he observed. With a small motion of his finger, his physician stepped forward. “I have an Elandian physician with me. Do any of you require his expertise?”

Then he chuckled. “I would rather not be blamed if one of you were stricken with illness after meeting me.”

Sir Harold and Sir Stefan chuckled with him.

“Are you... are you...” Luka, the plump representative in the striking red doublet, was the first to find his voice.

“He is the Lord Shogun, Leader of the Great Alliance,” Sir Harold said with pride, his voice carrying more weight than any herald’s shout, “the Count of Midlandia, the Lord of Korelia, the Protector of Korimor, the Lord of South Hill, the Lord of Corinthia, the Lord of Five Peaks, the Protector of East Nicopola, the Unifier Khan of the Great Plains of Lowlandia, the Bane of the Saint’s Undead, and the Slayer of the Lich Mother.”

The three representatives listened to the long list of titles in horror. Their faces looked drained of blood. Jovan’s mouths hung open as he struggled for breath. The day had begun so normally for them, until an army of ten thousand appeared at their gates without warning, not even using the road. And now the famed Black Lord himself, conqueror of many lands and last rumored to have slept for years, had suddenly appeared before them.

Luka muttered something unintelligible under his breath. His earlier bravado, telling the townspeople not to be afraid, that the Margrave was more frightening than the Shogunate, and that walls were walls and not easily breached, had turned into nothing but hot air.

Jovan, the wealthy son of a powerful merchant in Nona, looked close to fainting. Though not particularly heavy, he had a weak constitution. His family’s standing in Nona, however, meant he could not avoid taking part in the meeting.

The physician calmly checked Jovan’s pulse and spoke in an even voice. “Breathe deeply. Sit still for a moment. Drink some water. Slowly.”

Lansius

The meeting paused while the physician checked on one of the representatives. Lansius leaned back in his chair, trying to look relaxed. The entire display had been rehearsed for maximum effect. Sir Harold and Sir Stefan had added a few touches of their own, which pleased him. Normally, Lansius disliked having his titles read aloud and found the whole affair distasteful. Here, however, they were a weapon.

Southern Edessa was still largely untouched. Even in the previous war, the Margrave had spared its crops from the flames. Even in summer, its granaries were likely still plentiful. But Lansius had come for more than grain and provisions. He was here to inflict unforeseen damage on both the Margrave and the Imperium.

They had brought the battle to him. He would make them bleed for it.

Above them, the two airships maneuvered as they climbed over the town. He could hear the screams of children and parents from Nona’s battlements as the Bat passed overhead. They thought they were under attack.

The display brought a smirk to his face. Then he turned his attention to the other airship, the one ordered by the bank, with its yellowing ivory skin. It was noticeably smaller. He recalled being told its pilot was a Tedzeus.

That very airship had been a prototype and had crashed once, but the pilot survived. Better yet, House Tedzeus had paid half the cost of the repairs, which made Lord Avery and the Office of Works very happy to keep the same man as its test pilot. They had even allowed him to name the vessel. Thus, it became the Flying Orangutan, allegedly because it had nearly crashed again but was saved by an orangutan that later became part of its crew.

Lansius wondered whether his Shogunate had gone mad while he was asleep, or whether it had always been mad.

“Shall we discuss matters now?” Sir Harold asked, feigning impatience and keeping the pressure on the town’s representatives.

Lansius turned his attention back to the three representatives, keeping his expression relaxed while hiding his concern that the display of shock and awe might not be working. They had burned precious volatile oil just to keep the airships aloft. Still, at least the vessels had brought in much-needed supplies for the troops, including crates of his special medicines.

“Of course, of course,” Luka replied, his plump face flushed red.

Casually, Lansius turned to the mage. “Can you give our guests a nice cool breeze?”

The mage, Alwyn, nodded, and a cool breeze suddenly swept through the tent. Using a mage for something so trivial was itself a display of power and wealth. Meanwhile, Ocelot yawned as if bored and looking for something to kill.

All three representatives looked as if they knew they were in trouble. Nothing could have prepared them for this. Everyone had heard the stories. This was the leader of lords. The bringer of peace. The slayer of the undead.

Yet instinct for survival, along with their fear of the cruel Margrave, kept some steel in their spines.

“My Lord,” Luka managed, his voice barely above a squeak, “it is a great honor to see you come to our humble town of Nona. How can we assist you?”

“A fair trade is all I ask,” Lansius said, as though the answer were obvious.

Luka and Jovan carefully looked at each other.

Milos finally asked, “My Lord, you are not here to lay siege to our town?”

“A siege?” Lansius feigned surprise, then laughed. “No wonder you’re all so pale.”

His words prompted Sir Harold to laugh as well.

Lansius turned to Sir Stefan. “Was there some mistake in the message we sent?”

“I shall sort it out,” Sir Stefan said firmly, sounding as though he were ready to drag the messenger in and interrogate him on the spot.

That made even Milos swallow.

“No, no, he did nothing wrong,” Luka said hastily.

Lansius snorted sharply and asked, “Do we look like an army preparing for a siege?”

The three exchanged glances.

Before they could respond, Sir Harold muttered, “Well, it’s not as though we couldn’t.”

“It wouldn’t even be difficult,” Sir Stefan added flatly. “A town like this, with walls like those, we could take before supper without even using an airship.” He turned toward the three representatives with sharp suspicion. “You aren’t thinking of defending Nona against us, are you? That would be suicidal. We have men trained to take cities defended by 5,000 men-at-arms. Do you have that many men defending Nona?”

“No. Not at all, Sir,” Luka replied to Sir Stefan.

“You are greatly mistaken,” Milos insisted, sweat forming on his brow.

Sir Harold gave a loud grunt. “This brigade was much smaller back then, yet it still vanquished six thousand undead and a thousand Monastery guards. The Edessans probably underestimate our strength. The Empire has already felt our iron hand. Perhaps Edessa should feel it too.”

“The Margrave will,” Lansius declared coldly. This time, there was no pretense in his voice. “As for the good people of Edessa, much as I might wish otherwise, I have no desire to extend my wrath to them.”

The representatives could only nod, though they clearly did not understand what he meant.

Lansius furrowed his brow, suspicion crossing his face. “You people did not know?”

“Did not know about what, My Lord?” Luka asked carefully.

“You don’t know that the Margrave has betrayed everyone?”

“Betrayed...?” Luka asked. The other two looked just as confused.

“By the Ancients...” Lansius feigned a troubled look, then turned to the guards. “Gather fifty or a hundred of the refugees. Bring them here.”

“Yes, My Lord,” the leader of his guards replied. Several men quickly hurried off to carry out the order.

Soon, refugees young and old, men and women alike, were gathered before the three representatives. They told them of the tragedy during the retreat to Ostaria, of tens of thousands Edessans lost to the fires and the Empire’s riders.

The news stunned the three men.

Lansius did not stop there. He ordered the guards to bring the refugees closer to Nona’s walls. They did not need to enter the town. They only needed to speak with the people watching from the battlements.

With hundreds of refugees joining the first group and calling up their stories, word spread quickly that the Margrave had turned traitor and that the Empire had already slaughtered tens of thousands. A minstrel inside the town even recognized a peddler among the refugees. After that, the story was as good as truth to them.

Unease quickly spread throughout Nona.

At the field tent, Lansius spoke to the troubled representatives. “You may not know this, but we are withdrawing from the region. Defeating the Empire would be difficult with the traitorous Margrave at our back. We're only here because Nona lies on a road leading south. Since we were passing your town, we thought we might as well ask for trade. But seeing you like this, you had better return inside and prepare to leave.”

“To leave...?” Milos asked.

“We are abandoning our defensive positions because the Margrave attacked our rear. Without us, the Empire will be free to pillage its way here. You truly need to flee. Go as far west as you can while there is still time.”

Seeing their growing panic, Lansius pressed further. “I am saying this because I do not want to meet you or your sons in battle.”

That only confused them further.

Sir Harold exhaled deeply and spoke with authority. “I am sure you have heard that the Navalnians enslave both men and women. The men are kept in horrid conditions and trained for months, then goaded into battle as a slave army. If they escape or refuse to fight, their families are killed.”

Lansius nodded and said, "The more people we leave behind, the larger the Empire’s slave army will become.”

“The Empire,” Luka said, frightened even by the sound of it. “Are they really coming here?”

Sir Stefan answered, “Our men held Ostaria valiantly. But we have spotted a large number of horsemen riding toward the old bridge. From there, if they ride west...”

“Nona,” Milos muttered weakly.

Sir Stefan nodded. “Either they follow the road south to Tergeste out of greed, or they come to Nona in pursuit of the Shogunate. We estimate you have two days before they arrive.”

The three representatives turned grave. Jovan looked close to fainting again, enough that the physician had to attend to him.

“Two days...” Milos murmured, looking weak.

Not giving them time to think, Sir Stefan continued. “Our column still needs to buy some supplies. Please arrange what you can for us. We only need foodstuffs, and not much. Food is heavy, and our carts cannot carry too much. As My Lord said, we are withdrawing.”

“But the Margrave will kill us if we empty the town and leave the crops unharvested,” Milos said.

At that, all pretense was gone.

Lansius sighed loudly, drawing their attention. “You are fearing the wrong thing,” he explained. “No one even knows where the Margrave is. He may be hiding from my airships, or already a hostage of the Empire. What you need to fear is the Empire and its irregular riders.”

The three looked utterly lost. Even Milos, who had tried to remain tough, now seemed weighed down by what might happen in two days.

“I pity you,” Lansius said. “You have neither the Margrave nor my forces to protect you. Blame the Margrave for his treachery.”

The three were still at a loss for words.

Lansius looked past them, taking in the countryside, then added bitterly, “It is such a shame. This is beautiful land, with good people, but I must leave Edessa to its fate.”

From outside the tent, Captain Karl appeared and reported, “My Lord, apologies for the interruption, but there are still a few hours of good daylight left. The army is rested and has enough water and firewood for more than one night. Shall we prepare to march again?”

“Yes.” Lansius rose, prompting the others to stand as well and signaling that the meeting was already over. “Sir Stefan, handle the trade for us. Sir Harold, prepare the troops. We’ll march again.”

The officers he named saluted and accepted their orders.

Before leaving the tent, Lansius turned back to the three representatives, sympathy plain on his face. “I cannot fathom what kind of life awaits you under Navalnian rule.” He let the words settle before adding, “If it is any consolation, in war, the men die quickly. It is the women I pity. They will see the Eastern Empire and live horrendous lives there.”

“My Lord,” Milos called, already at his wits’ end, “can you spare some aid for the good people of Nona?”

“Help us,” Luka pleaded, while the physician tended to Jovan’s shortness of breath.

“Even my troops are withdrawing. I cannot condemn them to die for Nona.” Lansius rejected the plea firmly. “I will move my troops farther south, so you will know I have no ill intentions toward your town. With the money from selling us your surplus, you should have enough to start anew in another town. I pray that you and your families live safely through these ill tidings.”

He took several steps, leaving the three looking stricken as their last hope seemed to walk away with him.

Then Lansius stopped and muttered, “However...”

The three looked at him expectantly.

Lansius turned back to them. “There is one way.”

“Please,” Luka said, almost pleading.

Lansius seemed to consider whether to say it. “It would be difficult, and you probably wouldn’t like it.”

“My Lord,” Luka pleaded again. He looked ready to drop to his knees, the safety of his family and relatives weighing heavily on his mind.

“It would be a hard and costly undertaking, and it might wound your pride and honor,” Lansius explained.

“Please, enlighten us,” Luka said, with Milos quickly joining him.

Lansius inhaled, nodded once, and said, “The Margrave has sold you all to the Navalnians, but you can always refuse to be sold.”

“Refuse to be sold...?” Luka asked, as though unsure he had heard correctly.

“Yes,” Lansius answered, then turned his gaze toward the field beneath a cluster of trees. Milos and Luka followed it and saw dozens of children playing in the shade. Some wielded wooden swords, while others rode sticks as though they were horses.

“Do you know why I saved them?” Lansius asked.

“No, My Lord,” Luka replied.

“By now the children know my side can be trusted. They are young, but already familiar with the march. What better training could I ask for the future of my rule?” Lansius chuckled.

“But more than that, they have seen with their own eyes how the Margrave betrayed us and allowed the Empire to kill everyone. They have lost homes and relatives in this war. Even if their fathers are too scarred by what happened to take up arms, these boys will be different. The girls too, when they become wives, will tell their husbands to stand against the Margrave and the Empire. They will not need convincing to fight either of them. That hatred is already burning in their hearts.”

Lansius turned to the three men, looking at each of them in turn.

“Join me,” he said.

The offer caught the three so completely off guard that, for a moment, they seemed to forget to breathe.

Not giving them time to recover from their shock, Lansius turned to Milos. “I have seen past battles in you. Come with me, and I shall grant your family an estate fit for an armiger.”

“My Lord.” Milos dropped to one knee. It was a rare honor.

Lansius turned toward the town, with its old walls and grass-filled moats, then looked back to Luka. “Tell all of Nona. They may rally under my banner. I shall make the Empire weep. They shall find nothing but dust and dirt.”
