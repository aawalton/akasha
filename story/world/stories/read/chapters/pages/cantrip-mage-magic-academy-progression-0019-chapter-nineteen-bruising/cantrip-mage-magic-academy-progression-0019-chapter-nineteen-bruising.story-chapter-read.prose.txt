As the countdown to the midterm exams approached, the mood in Varin’s Academy shifted. The library’s first floor grew more and more crowded, and the number of students roaming Varin’s Town—or even grabbing a quick meal there after classes—plummeted. And nowhere on the school’s grounds was that feeling more intense than in the first-years’ rooms. The stress had even managed to push Eola’s quest to restore Varin’s diary into the background.

Practically speaking, there was more at stake for Eola, Colin, Patrice, or even Catrine than for any of the older students. Apprenticeships were up for grabs, after all.

Eola found herself in the Ideograms classroom one frigid morning, sitting near the back as Instructor Tarik laid out the process. “Unlike the end-of-year examinations, the midterms are simple. You’ll complete a series of tests—written or practical, depending on the professor—and will be given marks based on your performance. Using those marks, the school’s twenty-four professors will divide the first-year class among themselves, selecting their apprentices for the next two and a half years.

Tarik stroked his beard. “Given that this master and apprentice arrangement will follow you for the rest of your time at Varin’s Academy, it would be wise for you to do your best on these exams.”

“Sir, why?” one of the boys asked.

“Why what?”

“Well, why break us up like that? We’re all either Journeymages or well on our way to becoming one, and Ordered Magic’s all the same, anyway, so…“

“Mr. Oryole, Ordered Magic is, on its face, a simple and uniform thing. However, even setting aside attunements—which throw the entire system into chaos—there’s a lot of variance.” Instructor Tarik started sketching something in chalk. For a moment, Eola thought it might be a rune, but no—it was just a diagram. “Varin’s Academy keeps its enrollment small for a reason. Larger academies can produce more Journeymages and Truemages than we do, but by percentage, more of our mages reach Archmage than any other school. Why is that, Miss Lemiene?”

Eola flushed red in the back of the room. She hadn’t attended an Ideograms lesson in months, and now here Instructor Tarik was, putting her on the spot. Her hand closed on the stone cat figurine in her pocket. “Because, sir, of the apprenticeships?”

“Exactly.” Tarik finished his sketch. “The optimal learning environment is four to six students. Less than that, and the instructor tends to coddle their apprentices. More than that, and they don’t receive enough personalized instruction time. Hence, apprenticeships. Your homework, class, is simple. Study for your exams.”

Eola and Colin both looked glum by lunch. Madame Reyanna had given almost the exact same speech in Mana Studies, and she’d made it abundantly clear that her test was of the practical variety. Eola had no delusions about her ability to handle that test, while Colin was already making plans for after he failed the practical in Magical Dueling.

When Patrice sat down with them, she only made the mood worse. “I heard they don’t even offer apprenticeships to the bottom few students. Twenty-four professors, one hundred of us. Four left over.”

“That sounds made-up,” Colin said, but he didn’t eat his bread and soup. Eola stared at him, and after a moment, he nodded. “Go ahead.”

“It’s not made-up! My brother’s friend said it’s true!”

“I don’t believe you. Why wouldn’t they apprentice everyone?”

While Eola ate, Patrice and Colin argued back and forth. It was good-natured—mostly—but under it was an understanding that none of the three was at the top of any of their classes, and they were all near the bottom in at least one. Eola herself was a pretty good duelist, and she was motivated to know the different monsters in the library, but when it came to Mana Studies, she doubted she’d pass. Colin was possibly the worst duelist she’d ever seen. And Patrice…poor Patrice. She claimed to be the worst mage in Varin’s Academy, and she hadn’t given Eola any reason to believe otherwise.

If there were a few students who missed out on apprenticeships, it’d be at least one of them. Eola started thinking. Surely she could get Patrice through Magical Dueling, and maybe Introduction to Monsters. And as for Colin, he’d pass Ideograms and Mana Studies. Maybe.

Colin and Patrice were still arguing when Eola finished her—and Colin’s—soup and stood up. “It’s almost time for Monsters. I’ll see you there, Colin.”

“Not so fast,” a familiar voice said, and Eola turned to see the small, crimson-skinned Madame Reyanna bustling toward her, a look of mixed anger and triumph on her face. Eola winced when she saw who the professor had in tow: an almost as triumphant-looking Catrine.

Catrine glanced at Patrice, who—to Eola’s surprise—stood and offered a quick bow without making eye contact. Madame Reyanna didn’t wait for any formalities. “Miss Lemeine, Lady Andrese came to me after Mana Studies with an accusation. I simply must get to the bottom of it. Please come with me.”

The woman’s tone made it clear that it wasn’t a request, and Eola shrugged as she gathered her things. “Never mind. Take notes for me if I’m late.”

“Will do,” Colin mumbled, and Eola fell in a few steps behind Madame Reyanna and Catrine, looking much less smug than either.

Madame Reyanna’s office smelled like cinnamon, and Eola broke into a sweat within minutes. The gnome had a massive fire in the hearth, incense and candles burning on seemingly every shelf, and tapestries covering the windows; Eola and Catrine had needed to push through a thick, hanging blanket to enter the office at all.

After her previous office visit with Instructor Clearance, the warmth should have been comforting. Instead, it felt oppressive. So did the straight-backed wooden chair she was sitting in.

Reyanna gave her a long stare. “I assume you know why you’re here?”

“No.” Eola fidgeted in her seat.

“Lady Catrine Andrese, the youngest daughter of Lord Andrese, is accusing you of harassment and bullying—in my class!” The crimson gnome’s face went just a shade darker. “As a professor at Varin’s Academy, I can’t allow that to continue.“

“Harassment? You think I’m harassing Catrine?” Eola asked, dumbfounded.

“She provided me with evidence, and as both a Journeymage and the daughter of nobility in good standing, I don’t see any reason not to believe it.”

“This is ridiculous. I don’t want to be Catrine’s partner. She keeps seeking me out, even when I try to avoid her.” Eola’s eyes narrowed, and she balled her fists under her robe. What was Catrine playing at? What was she trying to do here?

“Madame Reyanna, that’s true. I was seeking Eola out.” Catrine smiled winningly, and Eola’s eyes narrowed even more. “It’s pretty obvious that she’s behind in everything except exhaustion training, and I was hoping I could help her catch up. I’ve got pretty good Mana control, and my Mana lines are the strongest in the class. I thought that maybe, since Eola seems unresponsive to what you’re doing, I could help her. I never expected the reaction I got from her, though.”

“I see,” Madame Reyanna said. She steepled her fingers dramatically and stared over them at Eola. “And what do you have to say about that?”

“Catrine never tried to help me. She was always showing off or trying to humiliate me,” Eola said.

“I’d never do that.” Catrine turned in her seat, looking Eola in the eye and grinning. “My father always says in his letters that it’s a noble’s responsibility to help the less fortunate, and I can’t think of a less fortunate person than Eola.”

“See? She’s doing it again!”

Madame Reyanna raised a hand. “I cannot allow a student in my class to treat another like this, especially when she’s trying to help. I’m going to have to remove you from Mana Studies for the remainder of the term. That means your final marks will be—

“If I might interrupt,” Catrine said sweetly.

“Yes?”

“I still want to help Eola. I’ve got that independent study time with you every evening, Madame Reyanna. It’s covering advanced Mana line techniques, but I’d be glad to use that time to help her catch up—with your tutelage, of course.”

“What?” Eola asked. Her nails dug into her palms.

“That’s a wonderful idea, Miss Andrese. It’s settled, then. Miss Lemiene will work with us every evening until I’ve decided she’s learned enough to pass the Mana Studies midterm. You’ll be her chief instructor, and she’ll help with the exercises I give you.”

“But—“

“No buts, Miss Lemiene. This is for your own good, you know? You’re wildly behind. You’re both dismissed. Run along—you don’t want to be late for Instructor Clearance’s class, do you now?”

Eola spent the entire Introduction to Monsters lesson grinding her teeth and looking for a way out of her brand-new evening lesson. She barely paid attention to the sweltering classroom in the greenhouse, or to Instructor Clearance’s lesson on Wood Trolls and their weaknesses.

Fire. It was always fire when it came to Trolls—and that went doubly so for Wood Trolls. Moving right along.

But by the end of class, she hadn’t come up with any ideas.

It was open floor in Magical Dueling—no First-Order spells, no attuned spells, no lethal spells, but aside from that, anything was fair game. Eola pulled her helmet on and let her gaze drift across the gymnasium, looking for Catrine. If she was going to get accused of bullying, she might as well give the noble something to complain about. But Colin found her first.

“Over here, Eola,” he said, waving her to a dueling strip at the far end of the classroom. He slowly moved himself into a perfect First Guard, then stood there while she mimicked him. “What happened with Reyanna?”

“Nothing.” Eola dropped into a lunge, and Colin’s defense was too slow, as usual. Her practice sword caught him dead in the chest. “Not here, at least.”

“Alright, fair enough.”

“Why don’t you attack, and I’ll defend? You could use the practice at it if you want to pass the midterm,” Eola asked.

The helmet on the other side of the pitch shook twice. “Nah. I’m not going to pass Magical Dueling. The Taron-Li and I don’t get along at all. I’m here to learn what I can so I can bring it to Instructor Clearance. All I need to do is focus on defense and the Safe Shield part of dueling, and rituals will take care of the rest.”

“Suit yourself. You’re a practice dummy, then.”

“What does that mean?”

For the next forty minutes, Eola taught him exactly what she meant. Her swordplay was too fast for his defense, and the way he placed his shields was predictable and easy to bypass. By the end of it, she was pulling her blows short, sweat pouring into her eyes as she danced the Taron-Li around him. It wasn’t as good as fighting with Catrine—she wouldn’t be pulling her blows against her—but the swordplay made her feel better.

When Maestro Yarrowbloom blew his whistle early, the only things louder were her pulse hammering in her ears and Colin’s groan as he slowly, gingerly pulled off his helmet. “Y'aer's Folly, Eola! What’d I do to you?”

“Nothing.” Before she could explain, the elven swordmaster waved everyone in. She mouthed the word ‘Later’ at Colin and joined the rest of the class, helmets peeling off and armor unbuckling as they assembled.

“I’ve taught you what I can in a half-term,” Yarrowbloom said. “In the interest of fairness, I’m going to explain exactly what Magical Dueling’s examination will entail. The Taron-Li is a dance. We will be putting on a recital.

“You will each be matched with three other students from the first year’s other classes, who you will spar with to demonstrate your skills at the dance. These bouts will be adjudicated by two other combat track professors and me. Grades for this practical will be awarded on the following metrics, in order: style and precision of your Taron-Li, your understanding of your opponents’ movements and attacks, and victories and losses.” Maestro Yarrowbloom paused to let that sink in.”

“So, it’s more important that we fight correctly than that we win?” Garreth Skaget asked.

“Absolutely. None of you is a master of the blade and spell yet. My concern is less with your skills at full speed and more with your understanding of the fundamentals under pressure.”

Eola relaxed a little. She’d been practicing those fundamentals for years, first with her mother, then with her uncle after she’d started living with him. If the test was fair, she had a good chance at earning maximum points both there and in the victories category, and close to maximum in the understanding category as well.

Even better, Colin had a chance at not coming in dead last. If he fought defensively and got good pairings, he’d probably earn a few points in style and precision, and he might be able to demonstrate his knowledge and understanding. It wasn’t his fault she’d been his sparring partner most of the time, after all.

She turned to say something to him, then stopped, staring. “I’m sorry, Colin. I didn’t realize.”

“It’s fine.” He flipped his cloak over his arms, covering the blossoming bruises that covered them.

“No, it’s not. I got carried away. I should have pulled my attacks earlier.” Eola’s stomach churned a little, and for the first time in a long time, she wasn’t hungry after Magical Dueling.

Colin waited until they got back to their suite and stepped into Eola’s room before he started casting. His familiar, the thin yellow weasel, stood by his feet and watched.

So did Eola. She sat on her bed, still feeling terrible about what she’d done to her friend. In fact, she felt worse because Colin had refused to talk about it the whole way across the snowy school grounds. She’d tried to apologize, only to find herself talking to a hand.

Even once Colin started casting his ritual, muttering words in Old Alemic as he slowly and methodically sketched out the First Order rune, she stewed about it. It was almost enough for her to forget the extra Mana Studies lessons with Madame Reyanna and Catrine.

Almost.

The ritual Colin cast seemed familiar, but Eola couldn’t place it at first. He stood by the shuttered, curtained window, chanting and sketching. Then, when it finally cast after thirty seconds, he pulled off his cloak and tossed it onto the bedframe. It slumped down onto the floor, crumpling in a wet, icy pile. He ignored it. The bruises weren’t gone, but they looked like they were three or four days old now.

Eola stared, fascinated. “You learned that from Clearance?”

“Yes. It’s a simpler version of the one she had putting you back together after you, you know, fell out of the third floor.” Colin held out his arm, pointing at a rapidly shrinking bruise. “I can’t heal major injuries yet—not enough Mana in my soul for Second Order spells like that—but this does well on bruises, cuts, and even the sprained ankle I got in Magical Dueling last week.”

“That’s amazing.” Eola meant to say it, but Patrice got there first. She leaned against the door, eyes wide and mouth caught mid-yawn. “Was it easy to learn?”

“Not really,” Colin said. “Rituals are tricky. They need a lot of precision.”

“How’d you get in here?” Eola asked.

“That’s too bad, Colin. You left the doors open, so I let myself in. Besides, I was curious about your meeting with Madame Reyanna. What’d she have to say?” Patrice asked. She flopped onto Eola’s bed, jostling it as she sat down. From above, Atta made a curious half-purring sound and locked her eyes onto the tall girl’s robes, and she huffed slightly and glared back. Atta didn’t look away.

“Stop that. Whatever she’s got, it’s not food. I’ll get you fish later,” Eola said. Then she sighed. “I’ve got a remedial with her every evening now. Catrine’s idea. I didn’t ask for it, and I’m already taking an independent study with Instructor Tarik. Madame Reyanna feels like she’s got it out for me, and putting me in a room alone with Catrine’s just asking for trouble.”

Colin let his ritual collapse, panting a little. His bruises were mostly gone—where there’d been fresh black and red shapes along his arm, there was nothing but a few yellowish discolorations. “See. All done. So, you were upset about that, and you brought it into Magical Dueling? And I made the mistake of partnering with you right after?”

“Yes. I’m sorry. I was trying to partner with Catrine.”

“Ah. Maybe it’s for the best, then. Just…next time, tell me what’s wrong before you beat me black and blue,” Colin said. He frowned and took the open desk seat, leaning forward onto its back. “I don’t think Madame Reyanna can do that. She can’t force you to take classes that aren’t part of the normal schedule.”

“What am I supposed to do about it, though?” Eola asked.

“Talk to Instructor Tarik. Or just don’t show up.”

Patrice nodded. “You’re one of his favorites, right?”

“No,” Eola said quickly.

“But you have an independent study with him already. You must have some leverage with him, right?”

Eola stood up and stretched, arms up to touch the loft overhead. Then she pulled her fingers back as Atta batted them. “Rude, Atta. I’ll talk to him tomorrow. But you’re right. She can’t make me do this. I’d rather spend this evening working on my runes. Speaking of which…Patrice, Colin, your company’s nice, but I need it to be quiet in here while I work.”

“I’d like to see what you’re doing sometime,” Patrice said as she stood up. “Not that I’d be able to do anything with it anyway. Magic and I don’t seem to get along.”

Eola nodded. “I’ll show you sometime later. For now, though…leave. Both of you—and your familiar too, Colin.” She shooed her friends out of the door, shut and locked it behind them, and got to work. But she didn’t work on any of the new modifying marks she’d gotten, or any runes she’d been tinkering with.

Instead, she slowly, painstakingly sketched the ritual rune Colin had cast, working as best she could from memory. She’d seen something in it—something she didn’t recognize.

She just needed to figure out what.
