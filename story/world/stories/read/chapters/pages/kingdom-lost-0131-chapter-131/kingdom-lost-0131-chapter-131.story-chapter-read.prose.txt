Chapter 131

The Clawborn wagons came down the road in a hard, driving rhythm, the horses at their fronts lathered where the harness rubbed and the harness dark with rain. The wheels rode the ruts they had cut a hundred times before. Rim on wet clay, rim on wet clay, and the road beneath the rim gave and packed and gave again. Each pass drove the earth tighter into itself so the ruts deepened by a hair with every wagon that came through, and every wagon that came through rode the deeper rut a little more surely, and the road took on its own character, that was familiar to this party.

The road climbed a low rise and dropped. Climbed and dropped. The rain kept coming down. The wheels turned hard and true as they had done before, and the time before that. The familiarity of this journey caused the members of the party to partially glaze over and the road ahead almost hypnotized them.

The near-side wheel hit the covered hole first.

It went in at the rim and as it went through the thin skin of mud, and the leaves under the mud gave way, the way leaves do, all at once, with a soft dry crush that made no sound anyone could hear over the rain. The wheel dropped a full hand's depth into the trough. The axle bit forward. The whole box of the wagon pitched onto its front-right corner and threw the driver hard against the front board and the crossbowman half off the box, and the horses screamed and lunged in the harness and pulled the wagon another half-stride before it could stop.

The far-side wheel took the second hole on the lunge.

It went into the trap on the left rut a stride later and it did not merely sink. The wagon was already pitched and the weight already shifted and the axle already under it all had not been built to hold at that angle, and the second wheel went into the second trough at the wrong moment in the roll of the box, and the spokes went at the hub with a splitting crack that carried in the rain. Two spokes went at once. The iron rim rolled off the shattered wood and lay flat in the mud in a slow half-turn and settled. The wagon dropped onto the broken hub and the hub took the weight for a heartbeat and gave, and the corner of the box came down onto the mud with a heavy wet thud that put the whole vehicle sideways.

The lead rider was already turning his mount. His club was already in his hand.

The older Clawborn in the wagon opened his eyes, motionless. Then he looked once at the tarp, and then looked at the trees.

Riley came down the burnt tower stairs two at a time because two at a time was still a habit her body remembered from home, and she had spent enough time at the top of the tower that her legs wanted the run. The stairs were sound under her boots but the walls on either side still carried the smell of the fire that had gone through them before, a low charred bite in the wet air that the rain had not yet washed out. Valrik was a half-flight behind her.

The depot yard was busy with tending to her orders. The captured Clawborn wagon stood in the line she had set for them along the north wall of the compound. It had been half-loaded with ore in the time she had been up the tower, the slate-dark chunks tumbled into the beds in the rough grade the Clawborn had used and stacked so the axles did not scream. A team of her own soldiers worked the loading with a pair of the Clawborn's own draft pulleys that had been left rigged over the ore hold. The chain sang under the load, and the men on the ropes had the rhythm of it, in and out, in and out, and nobody was standing around.

The remaining strongboxes from the depot vault stood in a short line in the yard under a tarp, the wax on the seals bright with rain. Four boxes. She had done the arithmetic in her head twice. It was more gold than she had ever seen in one place, and she was standing in a captured yard where it was the leftover of what had already gone home.

The soldiers were already at the depot gate. Riley eyed the HUD and the group was off.

As the last soldier went through the gate, Thorne pushed his way through. His eyes searched the yard and fixed themselves on Riley. He went to her without pause.

She watched him approach and opened the HUD then pinged him.

He stopped mid-stride. His head came up. His hand went to his temple in the small unconscious way it did when the HUD came alive on him for the first time in a stretch, and he stood in the yard for a beat with his mouth slightly open, and then he looked directly across the yard at her and his shoulders came down a fraction.

She watched him walk the rest of the way and did not smile until he was close enough that only he would see it.

"You didn't know we could do that," she said.

"No," he said. "I did not."

"You do now."

He nodded once.

"Warden."

Valrik. Coming across the yard from the direction of the depot's office with his arms full. He was carrying a stack of scrolls, and he carried them carefully as he approached.

"Warden. There is a back office in the depot behind the vault. I checked it after the first sweep and it was empty of anything but paper. I went back a second time to be sure. There is a shelf, high, at the rear of the room. There were scrolls on it. All of them are in the Clawborn tongue."

"How many."

"Twenty-three."

She looked at Thorne. Thorne looked at the pile of scrolls. He said nothing.

The scrolls were rolled tight and tied off with narrow leather cords, most of them a plain dark brown, a few in a paler tan. She lifted one. The paper was thicker than she had expected. The script on the outside was a hard, angled hand she could not read a stroke of.

She set it back. She lifted another. She lifted a third. Each of them was written in the same hand or in a hand close enough to it that she could not tell without a reader who knew the tongue.

Then she came to the one that gave her pause.

The cord on it was the same dark brown as the others. The paper was the same. What was different was the wax seal at the throat of the roll, a round of black wax pressed into an impression she had not seen at the depot. She lifted the scroll flat across her palm and turned it so the seal caught the grey daylight.

It was a claw. But not a Clawborn claw. The impression was of a single claw curving inward around a mark she could not fully read at this angle. A rune, she thought. The lines of the mark were fine. Fine enough that whoever had cut the seal had spent time on it.

She held the scroll for longer than she had the others.

Thorne was watching her. She felt it.

"Warden," Valrik said, quietly.

"I see it," she said.

She set the scroll back into the sling with the others but did not tuck it in with them; she laid it across the top of the stack so it would be lifted out again by whoever received the delivery.

She looked at a soldier, who was still in the yard working with the last of the men. She lifted a hand. He came at once.

"One more thing to go home with you," she said. "These scrolls. Wrap them keeping this one on top and delivery should be in a soldier's hands, not on a wagon, not on a saddle horn. Not opened on the road. Straight through the kingdom gate to Nikola, and Nikola puts it on the desk in my office. Not the desk in his own office. Mine. He will know why."

"Yes, Warden."

He took the scrolls and wrapped them with both hands and left with the bundle.

She opened the HUD and pinged Nikola. She kept the message short:

❖ A bundle of scrolls coming in with the gold escort. All in Clawborn. One of the men will bring it to you in hand. Put them on my desk, not yours. Do not open them. One has a black wax seal. Keep that one on top.

The tick came back almost at once:

❖ Understood.

She closed the HUD.

Thorne had not moved. He had waited through the whole exchange without interrupting and now that the exchange had ended he stayed a beat longer, because he was not done.

"Warden," he said.

"Go."

"A large Clawborn detail is on route. More than one wagon and a proper guard detail, more than a few."

She waited.

She did not move her face. She had been a leader long enough to know the trick of going in cold when something inside her was hot. She let the news sit for a moment, without clear reaction.

She looked at Thorne.

"How long until they arrive," she said.

"An hour. Maybe two."
