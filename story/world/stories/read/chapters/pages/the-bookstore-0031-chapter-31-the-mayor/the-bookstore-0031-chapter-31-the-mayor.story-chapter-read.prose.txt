Moruh had endured quite enough of Spring being treated like a miracle. His mistress had spent hours testing the strange blessing that allowed the not-quite-treefolk to cultivate essence plants. Every new discovery drew more attention, speculation, praise, and in Moruh’s opinion, far too much delighted staring. If this continued much longer, the blue feline feared he might soon be replaced by an overgrown bundle of vines with an alarming talent for producing useful things.

That danger demanded action. So, while Anne was still distracted by the latest questions raised by Spring’s golden wheat, Moruh chose the most uncomfortable subject available.

“Mistress,” he said in his wisest tone, adjusting his hat for emphasis, “should we not be more careful about drawing the mayor’s unfriendly attention? Mr. Rick and Mr. Dorgh have both mentioned his hostility toward anything connected to Riverend.”

Anne, who had been half lost in thought over a note-filled scroll, looked up with immediate annoyance. Not because he was wrong. But because he was right.

“We should,” she said. “But what would you have me do? Pretend Riverend does not exist? We need contact with every borderland town that has even half-decent adventuring activity, and that includes Riverend.”

Moruh took that as permission to continue.

“We might have sent someone quietly,” he suggested. “Discreet arrangements are often healthier than public ones.”

“Oh, yes,” Anne said sharply. “And that would certainly have gone well once he discovered it later.”

She set the scroll down with more force than necessary and stood.

“Moruh, as much as I dislike conflict, we may have to oppose the mayor on this matter. I know the risk, and I know it may complicate our work. But this is part of our duty. It is the reason we came to the borderlands in the first place. Otherwise, we would have gone to the Solar States.”

The last words came out heavier than the rest. Anne fell quiet after them, then let out a slow breath.

Mountainend had turned out better than she had hoped. More receptive and alive beneath its disappointment. When she first chose it, she had done so partly out of desperation and partly because it seemed like a place where she could stay out of the worst political struggles to come, gather strength quietly, and grow her craft before the borderlands truly ignited.

Now it seemed someone else’s game might drag her in anyway.

In a dark room lit only by a few candleholders, seven robed figures sat around a table and held a meeting that was not meant to exist.

Two were conspicuously tall and thin. Two others were short and broad enough to suggest dwarven blood or something close to it. One was both tall and solidly built, and unlike the others, made no attempt to hide his irritation. The final two had no obvious features worth remarking on, which in such a gathering might have been its own kind of talent.

The irritated one spoke first.

“What is it this time?” he said. “You had better not have called us down here for another minor inconvenience.”

“I called this meeting,” said one of the tall thin figures seated at the head of the table, “because our situation has changed. The Clows are sending an overseer to town. She should arrive within a week.”

The reaction was immediate.

“Why?” the irritated one snapped. “They never cared about this place before, so why now? Tientree, you bastard—her gift? Her gift awakened? Why weren’t we told?”

“No names,” the thin figure said coldly. “And that is a private matter.”

“Private?” the other roared. “Gods be damned, our lives are on the line. How is that private?”

“I did not know,” the leader shot back. “She did not tell me. Is that enough for you? Good. Then let us move on to what matters. A Clow is coming. We stop our preparations, and we hide our tracks.”

One of the more ordinary-looking figures, whose voice carried a calm feminine note, lifted her head slightly.

“What is her name?”

The leader hesitated for only a breath.

“Yana Clow.”

“Yana...” the woman murmured. “The third-generation holomancer? She has no real government post. She is practically an outcast.”

“She is still a Clow,” said the other tall thin figure, speaking for the first time in a voice too flat to reveal much of anything. “And now she is an overseer.”

One of the short broad figures frowned beneath the hood.

“Holomancer?” he asked in a heavy, rough accent. “Aren’t all Clows neuromancers?”

“They are,” the calm-voiced woman replied. “Holomancy is a rare branch of neuromancy focused on illusions and perception. Most of the family seems to despise it, which is probably why the branch is so poorly known. The girl is supposed to be talented, though. Very talented. My guess is that holomancy is all she can truly practice, which is exactly why they hold it against her.”

None of that improved the mood at the table. Regardless of craft, the problem remained the same. A Clow was coming.

The discussion turned quickly after that. Less shouting, and more calculation. What needed to disappear. What could remain. Which trails had to be buried first. Who might already know too much. The meeting dragged on for hours as they worked through fear and strategy in equal measure.

By the end of it, they had no good answers. Only necessary ones.

A couple of hours later, the thin figure who had led the meeting climbed through a tunnel that opened into a room beneath the town’s old warehouse. Once above, he removed the robe, hung it neatly on a wall peg, adjusted his clothing, and left through the proper door instead of sneaking into the street like a criminal.

Mayor Ehran walked out as though he had simply finished an ordinary inspection. He wore a pale red vest and matching trousers, both cut well enough that no one looking at him would mistake him for a common merchant despite the lack of obvious luxury. A golden pocket watch and a blue kerchief were his only adornments, but on Ehran even modesty carried a noble weight. He slipped an old scroll into his pocket and made his way through town at an easy pace, greeting people by name as he passed them.

No one seeing him would have guessed where he had just been.

When the villa finally came into view, Ehran drew a deeper breath than usual. The last two nights had been unpleasant ones. He had spent hours dismantling the stronger anti-scrying arrays around the house, leaving only the sort of protective measures any wealthy family might plausibly possess. Blocking scrying was not illegal, but too much protection invited the wrong kind of curiosity. Powerful hidden arrays practically announced that something within was worth seeing.

And at the moment, he could not afford that. Anything truly dangerous, or truly incriminating, had already been moved to a safe house outside town.

It would have been much easier if Asha had trusted them before going to the Seat.

That thought returned to him again, accompanied by a bitterness he could not quite extinguish. He still did not understand why the girl had hidden something so important. Was it Ailen’s influence? Her endless pushing against the obvious path? Her meddling? Ehran had tried to be patient with Asha’s restless fantasies, but there were limits. Even after her gift awakened, could she truly still be clinging to other possibilities?

He let the frustration pass. Blame was not useful now. What mattered was what came next.

Asha’s gift had long been expected. He had always known it would matter, even while pretending otherwise when necessary. In truth, it was central to what he hoped to build. That did not make the guilt easier to carry. Using his daughter as one piece in a larger design left an unpleasant taste in him, one he could neither deny nor cleanse with lofty reasoning.

Still, reasoning came anyway.

It is for her.She will never be free otherwise.A father must seek a better world for his child.

He could summon a dozen such justifications, but none erased the harder truth beneath them. Her gift fit his ambitions perfectly. Whether a father’s duty was to create a better future for his daughter or a daughter’s duty was to play her part in the grand scheme, the result remained the same.

Asha would have a role in what was coming.

From the balcony of the villa, Ehran looked toward the direction of the forbidden forest.

His thoughts shifted. Perhaps it was finally time to visit the bookstore.

Months ago, he had barely spared the newcomer any attention. Anne had seemed like one more well-meaning fool coming to fail in Mountainend—a young woman with an old dream and poor odds. His own plans for the town had not included her in any meaningful way.

Then she began moving pieces. Not with the subtlety of a court schemer, nor the care of a conspirator, but with something unexpected and in its way more dangerous. She awakened hidden desires in people. She showed them paths they had not believed available. She made neglected lives begin to turn with purpose.

Rick, the adventurers, and Asha.

Even his miscalculation with his daughter might well have been tied to that storewoman’s influence. He had considered forbidding Asha from going there, but Ailen had objected. His wife had hoped the bookmancer might help guide sense into the girl. Instead, Anne had helped her stray farther from the course laid out for her.

And now there was Riverend as well. Word had already reached him that Anne had sent adventurers there on business. That would complicate matters. Ehran’s rivalry with the town was not petty in his own mind, whatever others believed. It touched trade, leverage, long-term survival, and the shape of the borderlands to come. Anyone strengthening Riverend at Mountainend’s expense risked interfering with more than local pride.

He went inside and selected a book to take as a gift.

Then he paused.

An old memory rose, nearly three decades old now. A seer they had once trusted had spoken of the borderlands changing. Not merely the Wall falling—that had been foreseen by others as well—but something wider. The fall would bring a new sky to the land. New stars would be born, and those stars would guide the coming age. One of them, the seer had said, would rise in the small town at the foot of the great mountains.

At the time, the prophecy had seemed broad enough to be useless.

Now Ehran was no longer certain. Could the bookmancer be that star?

She did fit the guiding part. She was certainly gathering the unusual to herself. Still, prophecy was never reason to trust blindly. Whether Anne was a star, or merely a talented inconvenience, remained to be seen.

Either way, he would have to judge what she was to his plans.

Help, obstacle, or something worse.

Because even stars, when going against the inevitable course, could fall.
