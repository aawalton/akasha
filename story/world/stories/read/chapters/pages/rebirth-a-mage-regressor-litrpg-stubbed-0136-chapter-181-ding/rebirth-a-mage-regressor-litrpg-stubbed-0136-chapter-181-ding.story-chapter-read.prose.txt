What followed went quickly, once it found its rhythm.

Deidra wove the first few at Adom's side while he fed the work, and as the other healers shook off their disbelief, they fell in too. They lined the patients up and wove in turn, and Adom went down the row, a hand to each healer as the spell rose, lifting it past anything they could have managed alone.

And it wasn't only the weaving. When a healer reached for a draught instead, Adom dipped a finger into the bottle and fed the rune-influenced mana down into it, and the liquid woke up. Potions that had done next to nothing by the bottle now did the whole job in a mouthful. A man who'd been working through a flask of fever-draught for two days with no change drank one capful of the same stuff, enhanced, and was clear by the time he set the cup down.

Then the patient got up and walked out.

That was the whole of it, again and again.

A woman with a crushed hand, the bones gone to gravel under the skin, left flexing every finger. A boy who'd been burning with a fever that had beaten three healers flat that morning sat up clear-eyed and asked for something to eat. The grave cases, the ones Adom had stepped around on his way in, he stopped stepping around. He took them as they came now, and the worse they were, the more plainly the thing worked. A man carried in grey and barely breathing walked out on his own feet, looking faintly insulted to have been allowed to get that far.

And Sam ran the floor.

He sorted the room, called the next one up, and talked the frightened ones through the door.

"Next! Don't be shy, the magus hasn't lost one yet. You, sir, with the arm, up you come. Mind the step."

The line had thinned to a trickle when a very old man shuffled to the front of it, something large cradled in both arms.

Sam slipped back into his patter. "Afternoon, sir. What've we got? Where's it hurt?"

"Oh, it's not for me." The old man hoisted his bundle a little higher.

It was a cat. A fat one, grey, round and supremely unimpressed, hanging in the old man's arms.

"...The cat," Sam said.

"That's right."

"What's wrong with the cat?"

The old man gazed down at it fondly. "He's depressed."

Sam stopped. "Cats can be depressed? From what?" He caught himself, and softened it. "I only mean… forgive me, sir, but what's there to be down about? His whole day is eating and sleeping. What's weighing on him?"

"Oh I beg to differ, young man. They can, and terribly so. Won't play. Won't chase a thing. Sits at the window all day and stares out at it." He gave the animal a small encouraging jiggle. The cat swung a little and went on looking annoyed and sad in equal measure, which, Sam had to concede, rather supported the case.

"Sir." Sam summoned all the gentleness he had on hand. "I'm very sorry, but we don't really do… that. Cat feelings. We're more of a, you know. Wounds. Dying. That side of things."

"Let him in."

Adom's voice, from across the room, without looking up from the patient under his hands.

Sam turned. "It's a depressed cat."

"I heard you. Let him in."

Sam looked at the cat, and the latter regarded him with flat contempt.

Then Sam turned back to what was left of the room and raised his voice one final time.

"AND CATS!" he called. "Depressed cats, anxious cats, cats who've simply lost the will to go on. Bring them in! No ailment too small, no patient too furry! In you come, sir, in you come—"

"Sam?"

Sam turned from the thinning line.

Archibald stood at the mouth of the ward, Elena at his side, the two of them taking in a room that looked nothing like the one the family had been living above. The cots were emptying. People who'd come in carried were leaving on their feet. And his son stood in the middle of it, sleeves shoved to the elbow, calling the next name up.

"...Father," Sam said. "Elena."

Inside the room, tending to the cat, Adom heard it and kept his hands where they were. Not his moment yet. So he listened.

"Sam." Archibald came a few steps in, his eyes moving over the place. "What is going on here?"

Before Sam could find the start of an answer, someone got there first.

A man crossed the ward at something close to a jig, both arms up, grinning wide.

"A miracle, sir! A miracle!" He fetched up in front of Archibald and seized his hand, pumping it. "Twenty years, sir. Twenty years I've had the seizing in my legs. Comes on me in episodes, and no healer in this city could do a thing for it but tell me to rest and bear it. I came in this afternoon in the thick of one of the worst episodes I've had, couldn't hardly stand—"

He let go of Archibald's hand to slap both of his own thighs, hard.

"—and look at me! Gone. Clean gone. Twenty years, and gone in an afternoon!" He turned a full circle on the spot to prove it. "The Goldhand did it. The Goldhand himself, with his own two hands."

Archibald looked at him.

"...The Goldhand," he said. "And who is that?"

The man stared, plainly amazed the question needed asking.

"Why, the magus, sir! The Ghost of Arkhos! Adom Sylla himself!" He flung an arm back toward the room behind him. "Him! Who else?"

That was his cue, Adom supposed.

He gave the cat a last scratch between the ears, the grey head following his hand a moment before the animal remembered its dignity, and straightened.

"He isn't depressed," Adom said, and held the cat out to the old man. "He's humiliated."

The old man took him back, going very still. "...I'm sorry?"

"There's a bird. It comes to the branch outside your window most mornings, sits just out of reach, and makes a great deal of noise about it." Adom did his best to keep his face perfectly straight. "Your cat has been trying to catch it for the better part of a year. But he can't. The bird knows he can't. They both know he can't. And rather than go on losing every single morning, he's decided to stop taking part in mornings at all."

The old man looked down at his cat, and the cat looked away, at nothing, and declined to comment.

"Move his cushion to another window. One with no branch outside it. Somewhere he can sit that doesn't remind him of the war he's losing, and he'll come round inside a week."

The old man gathered the cat to his chest. "Thank you, Magus. Thank you."

"Don't mention it." Adom rose the rest of the way. "Good luck to him."

He turned to Deidra and the rest, who'd slowed at their work to watch him gravely take the side of a cat.

"I have to step out a while. Family." He held Deidra's eye. "Please carry on with what you can manage safely, and leave the grave ones for my return. I won't be long. You have my word."

Then he went out to face Archibald.

"Uncle Archibald." He gave him a small nod. Then, warmer: "Hey, Elena."

"Hi, Adom." Elena gave a small wave back, wide-eyed. Fourteen, and visibly working out whether she was allowed to grin in the middle of all this.

But Archibald wasn't looking at Adom's face. He was looking at the room, the empty cots, and the people on their feet.

"Adom." His voice was careful. "Don't tell me this is your doing?"

"Don't take my word for it." Adom tipped his head toward the ward. "Ask them."

So Archibald did.

He went to the nearest, a woman flexing a hand that had come in crushed, and asked her what had happened to it. Then a man. Then another. He went from one to the next, putting the same hard questions to each, waiting for the story to come apart in his hands.

But it didn't. They answered him gladly, every one, and every answer was the same thing. Crushed, and now whole. Dying, and now fine. The Goldhand. The magus. Adom.

Then it was Deidra who settled it.

She'd come up beside them while he worked his way down the room, and when Archibald turned to her, looking for the one person here who might finally tell him he was being taken in, she didn't.

"You work here," he said.

"Twenty-two years." She folded her hands. "And I'll tell you what I told the boy when he started. Unproven magic does not go near a patient. Not in my ward, not for anyone. It's the rule I've kept my whole career."

Her eyes moved over the emptied cots. "I broke it an hour ago. And I'd break it again this minute. I've felt the thing work under my own two hands. It's real. I don't understand it, and I don't have to, to know what I saw. I've never witnessed healing like it. Nor has anyone living."

That was what reached him. The cured had been overjoyed, every one of them, and none of it had moved him an inch. Deidra saying it flat, with no wonder left in her, only certainty, did.

Archibald turned to Adom.

"Do you think you can…"

He stopped, unable to put the rest of it into the air. He really was Sam’s father, hah.

"Yes," Adom said quietly. "I think so."

Archibald gasped all of a sudden. His hand rose to his mouth while his eyes filled and spilled before he could do a thing about it, and he turned half away, one big hand pressed flat over the lower half of his face, holding himself together by the grip of it.

Weeks of being told there was nothing left did that to a person, Adom supposed.

And just like that, they went up to Celene's room.

It was dim and quiet, the curtains half-drawn against the failing light. And it was the first time Adom had been all the way inside it. He'd stood in the doorway before, on visits, looked in, and looked away again. Now he crossed the threshold properly, and there was no looking away.

She was very small in the bed.

That was the first of it. The years had worn her down to almost nothing, the blankets scarcely lifted by the shape beneath them.

Her arms lay outside the covers, thin enough that the bone stood clear at the wrist and the elbow. Her face had fallen into hollows, the skin drawn papery over the bone, and her hair lay grey and fine against the pillow. She breathed, shallow and slow, and that was the whole of what she did.

The body had been leaving her by inches for a long time, and most of it was already gone.

A healer rose from the chair at the bedside, an older woman who'd plainly spent a great many hours in it. "Magus." She knew why they'd come. "You'll want to know where she stands."

"Please."

"It isn't an illness. And whatever you've been told, there's no spell on her now." She looked down at Celene. "There was one. Once. The day her son's magic woke."

"A bad awakening. When it goes that wrong in the young, the mana doesn't shape into what it's meant to, but into whatever it likes, all at once, and you get a freak spell. No telling what it'll do, because it's never twice the same." Her voice stayed level. "Sam's threw one off, and it caught his mother."

Sam's eyes went to the floor. He didn't say anything.

"What it did to her, in the half-second it lasted, was change her constitution. What the body's built on, underneath everything." The healer turned back to the bed. "The spell was gone before anyone could so much as name it. But the change stayed. It left her weaker than a body is meant to be, and slipping a little further every year since."

She nodded at the vial on the table.

"It's a strange enough affliction that careful people have given years of study to it, and that draught is the best they've come to. It shores up the constitution, mends back a little of what was undone. We've watched it bring her up, inch by inch." She sighed. "But it gives slower than she fails. So she goes on losing. Slowly. But losing."

"And this is the draught?"

She lifted a stoppered vial from the bedside table. The liquid in it was pale, a faint cloudy gold.

Adom crossed to the basin in the corner and washed his hands twice. Then he came back, took the vial, and worked the stopper loose.

He dipped one finger in.

And poured.

He didn't know how bad her condition was. So he gave it everything. He opened himself to the rune and fed the draught without measure, more and more, as much as the small vial would take.

The liquid lit from within, the pale gold deepening and brightening, a slow churn of light turning through it. The rune blazed up his forearm. He went past the point where most mages would have stopped, and well past that, because a first-circle reserve ran deep and he meant to spend as much of it as she could possibly use.

When he finally lifted his finger clear, the draught glowed steady in the vial, and his hand was unsteady around it.

Two-thirds of his reserves into a single dose. Perhaps more. He felt the absence of it at once, a deep cold drag behind the ribs, the floor turning briefly untrustworthy beneath him. His arm shook as he set the vial down carefully before he could spill any of it, lowered himself into the nearest chair, and sat with his head down a moment, breathing.

He couldn't remember the last time magic had cost him this much.

Sam was at his side before he'd finished lowering himself down.

"Easy. Not that one, come here." He got a hand under Adom's arm and steered him out of the hard bedside chair into the deep upholstered one by the window. "Sit properly. You look like death."

"Thank you, Sam."

"No." Sam looked at him, all smiles. "Thank you."

Adom stayed there. He hadn't much choice in it.

The healer had taken up the vial meanwhile, and she was looking at the steady gold light turning slow inside the glass.

"I won't pretend I know how far I lifted it," Adom said from the chair. His voice came thinner than he'd have liked. "I gave it everything I had. I don't know what she needs, so I didn't measure. I only hope it's enough."

The healer looked from the vial to him. "So do I." That was all she said.

Then she went to the bed.

A fine clear tube ran from the corner of Celene's mouth to a stand at the bedside, the thing that had kept her fed and watered through all the years she couldn't do it herself. The healer fitted the vial to it and let the glowing draught run down into her.

After that there was nothing to do but wait.

So they waited.

Archibald stood at the foot of the bed with Elena tucked under his arm. Sam stood close on the other side, not quite touching his mother, his eyes on her face. Adom watched from the chair, and the healer stood ready.

Celene didn't move.

A minute went by. Then another. The light went amber at the window. Below them the ward carried on, faint through the floor. Up here no one spoke, and nothing changed, and the longer it held the harder it became to keep hoping without saying so.

Then Elena suddenly gasped.

"What? What is it?" Asked Sam.

"Her finger. Her finger moved." Elena's hand was at her mouth. "I saw it. I swear I saw it, just— ah!"

She gasped again, because Celene's eyes had begun to move now as well, beneath the closed lids.

The healer was already there. Whatever stillness she'd been holding broke clean off her, and she moved fast, two fingers to the side of Celene's throat, leaning in.

"Her pulse." Her voice dropped. "It's come up. It hasn't been this strong in years." She looked at the gathering family. "Room, please. All of you, back a little. Let her have some air."

Nobody moved back. Archibald had both hands pressed to his mouth, while Elena was crying and Sam had gone white and still.

"Back," the healer said again, firmer this time. "She's surfacing. Let her do it slow. Sammenel, the tube. Help me."

That reached him. Sam moved, hands unsteady, and between them they drew the feeding tube free while Celene's breath caught and then deepened on its own, working without the stand for the first time in longer than any of them could easily count.

Her chest rose, then fell and rose again as her brow creased with it, the muscles of her face waking one by one.

And then, in her own time, Celene opened her eyes.

The room immediately came apart around her.

Elena got to her first, both arms around her mother, crying and laughing at once. Then Archibald, folding over the two of them. Sam held still a breath longer than the rest, and then he broke too, down onto the edge of the bed with his mother's hand gripped in both of his.

Adom stayed in the chair by the window. He hadn't the strength to rise and join them, and it wasn't his moment to join in any case. He only watched, hollowed out and faintly shaking and glad, gladder than he'd been in a long while, as the family he'd handed this to fell to pieces over it.

Then, into the thick of all of it…

Ding!

Adom's head came up.

He looked round the room. But no one had spoken, and no one was looking his way. The sound hadn't come from the room at all, he realized.

It had come from inside his own skull, and for a moment he sat there not trusting it.

Then his hand began to glow.

It wasn’t coming out of the rune. The enhancement rune sat dark on his forearm, spent like the rest of him. This was lower, in his palm, a light welling up out of the skin itself, gold and climbing.

He turned the hand over and stared.

The light was gathering at the center of his palm and brightening as he watched, but none of it was anything he was doing.

So he got himself up out of the chair, unsteady, a hand on the sill, and not one of them turned. They were lost in her. So Adom slipped out, down the hall to where it opened onto a balcony above the city, and no one marked him going, while the glow in his hand climbed brighter and hotter until he could no longer look straight at it.

He stepped into the open air. The wind off the rooftops moved his hair. And the light, having risen to where it blinded him, began, slowly, to dim.

It went down by degrees, the glare bleeding away, until at last he could look full at his own palm and see what had been set there.

It was a rune. This one new, where there had been nothing. Lines running out from a single center, even and many, a small sun pressed into the skin.

Then the words came.

[SYSTEM RECALIBRATING...]

[Anchor rune detected: Primordial Pattern — Sun.]

[Parsing user state. Please wait.]

Adom read it. In the six years of his second life, he had never seen the system do this.

Ding!

[Recalibration complete.]

[Welcome back, user.]

...Welcome back?

He held very still in the open air as a strange realization began to bloom in him. The rune was connected to the system.

He was about to chase the thought down further when another unbidden Ding pulled him back.

[Mission parameters updated.]

"Oh, for God's sake."

[MAIN OBJECTIVE] Survive the emergence of the World Dungeon. Reward: Fate Reversal — 100%. Failure: Death of user. Collapse of all surviving timelines. Terminal end state. Deadline: 10 years, 09 months, 11 days.

Adom read it as the wind moved his hair around.

...All right. There was a great deal to be said here. The last time the system had handed him a deadline was the day he came out of the past, and nothing since, not a single word, for six years. That much, on its own, he could have sat with. He'd have had questions, but he could have sat with it.

Had it stopped there.

Because below the main objective sat a second block.

[PRIMARY MISSION]

Remove Emperor Rayhan from the Sundarian throne.

Reward: +20% Fate Reversal Probability.

Failure: Death of user.

Deadline: 3 months, 05 days, 04 hours.

And beneath that, as though that weren't enough on its own, a third.

[SECONDARY MISSION]

Reach the Fourth Circle.

Reward: Fourth Circle attainment. +20% Fate Reversal Probability.

Failure: User too weak to carry the main objective.

Deadline: 10 years, 9 months, 11 days.

"...Okay," Adom said, to no one, in the air. "Okay."
