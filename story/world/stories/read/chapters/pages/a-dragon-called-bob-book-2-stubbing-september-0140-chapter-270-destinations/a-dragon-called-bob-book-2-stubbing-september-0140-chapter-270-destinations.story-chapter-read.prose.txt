I’d flown over this stretch of the Empire a dozen times but never really appreciated it.  The rolling green hills and small copses of trees took on a new reality when you were stuck on a barge full of limestone plodding along at the pace of a walking horse.

Well, a team of horses.  Barges full of rocks were heavy, and the team of four snorting and farting their way along the towpath looked like they were used to the work.  Their tails swished to keep the flies away as we slowly approached the city.

Having kissed Esme a fond farewell first thing, I’d taken the portal to Longbottom and had Harald install me as “crew” aboard the Lazy Lucy, the next barge heading for the city.  There had been a very clear understanding that I would not be doing any work, but that had turned out not to be an issue.

All the labour was concentrated in loading and unloading the vessel.  Between the origin and destination, the only work required was someone to walk alongside the horses during the day, and someone to man the tiller and keep the barge off the bank.  Pops kept that job for himself, never trusting anyone else to manhandle Lucy’s steering lever.

So I was added as the unnecessary fourth dragon on a three-man crew.   Ella and Sylis walked the horses in between sunbathing next to me, while Pops stood by the tiller.  He was stooped, permanently leaning to the side, just like he kept the rudder turned to stop the barge from grinding against the stone bank.

“You don’t talk much,” Ella said.  “What’re you doing here?”  She leaned back, her sleeveless shift following the curves of her body.

As a married dragon, I kept my gaze averted.

“I’m just following the yellow brick road, Ella.”

“The towpath is grey flags.  Granite, maybe?”

“They’re bottomstone.  Comes from Longbottom, same quarries as our cargo, but too hard to use for cement.”  Pops was keeping his ears open.  He hadn’t been terribly happy about my addition to his crew.

Things were fraught between Longbottom and Ankmapak.  The city had an insatiable demand for cement.  Repairs, mostly, but new construction also required huge volumes of the material.  When most of the buildings were hundreds of years old and weren’t built to the highest possible standards anyway, there was a constant need for refurbishment.

“Makes sense.  Are we nearly there yet?”  I suppressed a smile as I heard the old man snap something behind me.

“Don’t antagonise the old man.  He’s a good captain,” Ella whispered.  “Took me on when I was just a girl, and he’s done right by me.”

“I assume that means he hasn’t invaded your blankets and he feeds you properly?” I asked quietly.

“None of that!  Well, he does feed us proper.  And pays us on time and in full, which is more than most boathands can claim.  I don’t think you’ll last long, Bob.”

“Oh, I might stick around.”  I wouldn’t.  “What about Sylis? Was he a waif when Pops acquired him?”

“You won’t stick about if you don’t respect the captain.”

I lay back and stared at the clouds.  That one looked like a moose receiving an enema.  “I can live with that.”

“Boy, I’ve cruised this canal for seventy years.  Seen less sparkly men than you fall prey to the wiles of the village women and the temptation to swim in the waters,” Pops said.

“What is it with the sparkles?  Some kind of curse?” Ella asked.

“I'm blessed by the gods,” I replied.

It wasn't a subject I particularly wanted to dwell on. The blessing that I'd received had caused far more trouble than it had been worth, and it was going to make infiltrating the Thieves' Guild rather more complicated than it needed to be.  I could change my appearance at will, and had been building emotional control so I could minimise the extent of my glitterbombs, but I still dropped one every couple of seconds even when calm.

“It's a bloody strange blessing. What did you do to earn it?”

“You don't want to know,” I said with a soft chuckle.

I rather liked Ella. She was light-hearted and unserious. She did tend to ask rather a lot of questions, though.

“Boy, go and do your trick with the horses again.  We might make it to the last lock today.”

“Erm… That didn't work out so well last time. They really don't like me.”

“Don't argue with him, Bob. You know it's a waste of time,” Ella muttered.

I sighed and stood up, jumping from the barge to the bank. I made my way to where Sylis was leading the horses. I steered wide of them as I approached. Every time I went near them, they tended to become a little bit skittish.

“Pop says it's my turn,” I announced as I arrived next to the man.

His shaggy blonde hair fell over his eyes, but he turned to look at me and nodded silently.

“Just wait till I'm back on the barge before you bother the ponies,” Sylis said as he turned to jog back towards the barge.

I didn’t.  Ella was fine, Pops was a grumpy old man, but Sylis put my back up for some reason.  He hadn’t been rude or unpleasant, just off in some way I found hard to define.  The kid had an attitude.

Horses didn’t like dragons, even when disguised in our mammal suits.  Their eyes rolled whenever I went near them, and if I got too close, they kicked.  Upon discovering that a pair of hooves to the chest did little more than dirty my tunic, they tried to run.

Traditionally, the boathand in charge led the horses from the front, and spent most of their time stopping the horses from taking a break to crop at the nettles and ferns that lined the path.  In my case, as with all the greatest military leaders from history, I very sensibly led from the rear.  The beasts wickered and neighed as I got closer before breaking out into the best trot they could manage while dragging tons of rock behind them.

Timing was everything in life.  A pithy comment delivered three seconds late makes you look like an idiot.  Ask any woman, and they will be able to give you examples of terrible timing by men.  Too soon is just as bad as too late.  On this occasion, I nailed it.

Lucky Lucy surged as the horses tried to speed up.  Not much.  Momentum is too powerful to overcome so easily, but as Sylis’s foot touched the deck, the boat accelerated just enough to send him tumbling into the water with a curse.

I have many fine qualities.  Not being petty… that I would never claim to be.  The man caught a rope that trailed in the water by the stern and was dragged back aboard by Ella as Pops chuckled to himself, sending a crooked smile my way.

“Come on, girls,” I muttered, walking a little faster to keep pace with the suddenly enthusiastic ponies.

“You bastard!” Sylis called, shaking a sodden fist in my direction.

“I get you every time!  You’d think you’d have learned,” I shouted back.

A hoof flashed out and slammed into my chest, staggering me slightly.  Bloody horses.  Why don’t mammals understand their place in the pecking order?  I do the pecking.  Eating the animal was out of the question, unfortunately.  As much as I’d love to, we needed to approach the city discreetly, and me dragging the boat along would not be subtle.

I was off to meet some very dangerous men.  The Thieves' Guild was not a gentleman’s club.  Quite the opposite, in fact.  Cutpurses were the nicest of them, cutthroats were their run-of-the-mill members, and the best of them, or rather worst, were evil masterminds who would have given Dalgliesh a run for his money in the villainy department.

None of them had a Big Kenny, though.  They had some powerhouses among their numbers, but from what I’d half-slept through in the briefing, they relied on stealth and misdirection whenever the big dicks started swinging.

I jumped half a step forward, and the horses, who had been eyeing the nettles by the path in a way that didn’t look like it would get me to the city any faster, surged ahead once more.  An annoyed squawk followed by a splash behind me confirmed that my comedic timing was god-given and I didn’t even need to check to land it just right.

“You haven’t done that to me, have you, Bulb?  Or Denarios?  If you pricks are listening, I’d really appreciate it if you didn’t fuck with my life any more than you already have,” I whispered.  The horses' tails flicked back and forth angrily, but no divine reply was forthcoming.

Typical really.  The gods screw with you as they like, but then leave you read when it suits them.  Their lives were on the line as well, but they insisted a mere mortal do all the dirty work.  Vanity slapped the wall of my sanity in annoyance.  Fine, I wasn’t some mere mortal; I was scaly stupendousness, but it felt like saving the world was more of a god-tier issue.

As with all semi-corporate entities, shit rolled downhill, and I was knee deep in the stuff.  Not as badly as the elves were, granted, but their growing desperation was my gain.  When I wandered off for a dump, I pondered the orb and received updates from Kat, the terrible Mrs Sanderson, and Armand.

Things weren’t going well in the West.  We’d sent them as many troops as we could, and were holding our ground in places, but we couldn’t be everywhere, and the changer had long since dropped the furbie act.  It was more like The Thing, Carpenter movie not comic book, and was gradually creeping forward to infiltrate the defences the knife-ears had thrown up, except for when it simply swarmed over them.

The birds were cheeping, small furry things that made me want to chase them, scuttling around in the bushes.  It was hard to believe the empire was locked in civil war, or that some extradimensional invader was currently eating the woods that were the elves' ancestral homelands.  All I saw around me, besides a pair of horses' arses, was peaceful and serene.

And I was going to fuck all that up.  Not because I wanted to, but because it was the only way.  Mammals and probably even dragons were going to die in job lots.  The Changer hadn’t figured out how to deal with the dragons yet; the small flying forms it had tried out had been burned out of the air, but it would get the hang of it sooner or later.

Adaptability was a terrible enemy.  Anything we tried worked, for a while.  But the thing adapted so quickly: fire spells were met with metallic bodies that glowed cherry red but didn’t melt; water spells were met with chrome beings that didn’t rust and laughed at the liquid's abrasion.  Ice was met with fire.  It was like trying to catch a feather floating in the air.  The faster your hand snapped shut, the quicker the damn thing escaped.

Blooming Vale appeared on the horizon, beyond the tails and horse-farts.  A quaint little town, as unremarkable when seen from the ground as it was when viewed from above.  Maybe seven hundred people called it home.  They worked the fields, serviced the barges, and sold a passable ale according to Pops.

I’d seen no point in learning the horses' names; they were shit-machines one through four as far as I was concerned.  Stepping slightly closer to them upped their pace as our destination resolved from a blob stretching up a hill to a medieval town,  with warm beds and food free from Sylis’s sweat.  That guy was a terrible cook.
