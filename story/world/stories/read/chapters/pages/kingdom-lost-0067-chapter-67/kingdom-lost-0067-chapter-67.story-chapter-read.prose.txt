Riley stood before the newcomers, her voice steady despite the pressure against her chest. "You are right. There is no one to protect you from the Clawborn if you stay in Hoshin. If you stay, they will want more. They will murder some of you to motivate the survivors to meet ever-higher resource quotas. Those who don't die from their hands will die toiling for them. Many of you will watch your friends and neighbors die." She paused, gaze drifting to Nami and Mali where they sat close together on the floor. Tears gathered along her lower lids, catching the lantern light. "Many will watch family die."

A sharp, collective gasp rose from the group. Several hands flew to mouths. One older woman clutched the sleeve of the man beside her so tightly the fabric bunched and twisted.

Riley drew a slow breath through her nose. "Truth is, I am not giving you a future without a price. This realm is dangerous, and even if there were no Clawborn, without soldiers and a strong community, others like them would come. If you join us, the Clawborn will come anyway and try to kill us. But here you have a fighting chance. We are stronger together than alone. In Hoshin you are waiting to die; here you will meet the threat of death head-on, with a real chance to live."

She fell silent. One by one she met their eyes: the weary father whose callused fingers still curled as though gripping a hoe that no longer existed, a younger woman whose knuckles had gone white around the edge of her shawl, the elder whose shoulders trembled with the effort of holding himself upright. No one spoke. Only the soft crackle of the hearth and the distant murmur of night insects filled the room.

Riley straightened. "Stay the night and sleep on it. Give me your decision tomorrow."

She turned toward the doorway. Nikola stood just inside the frame, framed by the glow of torches carried by the soldiers at his back. His posture was calm, hands clasped behind him, yet the line of his jaw told her he had heard every word. She raised a hand in a small, tired wave. The soldiers stepped forward in quiet order. Two carried broad wooden trays laden with steaming bowls of barley stew, thick slices of bread, and metal containers of water. Another bore a stack of clean wool blankets folded into neat squares.

The scent of the warm meal drifted across the room. A few of the Hoshin villagers lifted their heads, nostrils flaring. An old man's eyes widening at the sight of the food.

Riley did not wait to watch them eat. Boots scuffing softly against the floor, she walked past Nikola without a word and slipped out into the cool late afternoon air. The heavy door thudded shut behind her.

Riley crossed the open ground toward the forge. Valrik stood near the weapon racks, speaking in low tones to a couple of the older soldiers. He straightened when he saw her approach, the conversation breaking off mid-sentence.

She stopped in front of him. "I need a few scouts, mounted, to ride out now with the remaining daylight. Meet me back here as soon as they are ready."

Valrik gave a single sharp nod. He turned on his heel and headed toward the stables, already raising his voice to call riders. Riley watched him go for a moment, then summoned the HUD. The translucent map bloomed in the air before her, edges flickering where the shroud still clung thick and gray.

Minutes later Valrik returned, boots scrapping the path. Behind him three riders led their horses from the stable doors: lean mounts already saddled, saddlebags light and spare. The scouts wore no armour, bows slung across their backs, short swords at their hips. They mounted in silence and formed a loose line facing west.

Riley studied the map again. Her eyes scanning the glowing interface, she selected each rider in turn. Small icons appeared above their heads on the overlay. She assigned their patrol paths: all three heading west spread apart, wide enough that on their return they would clear a substantial section of the map west of the community.

The three scouts looked to Riley and she gave a single nod. The riders turned and the horses hooves struck packed dirt in quick succession as the riders spurred forward and disappeared through the western gate.

She left the HUD open. Turning to Valrik, she said, "I will send them out a league then recall them. Once they turn, wait for their return."

Valrik folded his arms across his chest, gaze following the faint dust trail still settling in the riders' wake. "Smart, every bit of ground we uncover gives us eyes farther out."

"Beyond knowing what is around us," Riley said, "having more of this terrain scouted might give the watchtower greater reach and earlier alerts. At the least, if alerts come in, I will have more information about what threat is incoming. This is for more than just the Clawborn."

Valrik exhaled through his nose, a short sound that carried both agreement and something heavier. He shifted his weight. "We need to start drills for better defense. I have a few ideas."

Riley met his eyes. The lines around them had deepened since the threat of a probable Clawborn attack was realized. "Share them and let's start tomorrow."

The mood among the Hoshin villagers had shifted once the weight of Riley's words settled. Laughter drifted from one corner of the longhouse where a cluster of men traded stories over their bowls of stew. The sharp edges of fear had dulled for the moment, replaced by the simple comfort of full stomachs and warm blankets draped across shoulders.

Tama sat apart on a low bench near the hearth, arms folded tightly across her chest. She watched the group without speaking, letting the chatter wash over her. The choice still pressed against her ribs like a stone. She scanned the faces one by one until her gaze settled on Nami and Mali. The girl sat cross-legged on the floor, chattering to her cloth doll and making it dance across her knee. Mali's smile came easily, unguarded, the same smile she had worn when the gates first opened and she recognized familiar faces among the welcoming crowd. Nami sat beside her, one hand resting lightly on the girl's shoulder, her own expression calm and quiet. They carried none of the pinched worry that marked the others. Their choice already lay behind them.

Nami glanced up and caught Tama watching. She rose without hurry, brushed dust from her pants, and crossed the room to sit beside the older woman on the bench.

"Seems like Riley wasn't exaggerating about this place," Nami said.

Tama let out a slow breath. "A part of me wished she had been. This forces a choice many don't want to face: certain death, or a small chance at something better."

They sat without speaking. The fire snapped and sent sparks curling upward. Across the room someone laughed again, the sound bright against the low murmur of conversation.

Nami spoke first. "I left Hoshin for a future, more for Mali than for me. The time since we left, even though uncertain, has been the best I can remember. I think this is what freedom feels like. In Hoshin there was always that pressure, that powerlessness in the face of the inevitable."

Tama stared into the flames. "It is all we have known. It is the way of things."

"I would rather feel free and die," Nami said, "than suffer longer and die anyway. Our lives have no meaning as slaves to the Clawborn."

She reached out and touched Tama's hand, fingers light and steady, then stood and walked back to Mali. The girl had watched the entire exchange, eyes wide and serious, clutching the doll against her chest.

Tama remained seated. Nausea rose in her throat, sour and sudden. "How can I decide to leave Hoshin knowing some of us may die?" The thought looped again and again. Riley had forced her to face the truth: the Clawborn would be the end of Hoshin one way or another. But the real truth was simpler, this wasn't her decision to make. It was their lives, their futures. They should choose for themselves.

She pushed to her feet. The motion drew eyes. Conversations faltered and died until only the crackle of the hearth and the soft rustle of blankets remained.

Tama raised her voice just enough to carry across the room. "Look, this is the great decision of our lives. I have thought about nothing else for days, and I have come to a realization. I have failed you all."

Heads turned. Several people exchanged quick glances, brows burrowed in confusion. A man near the door set his bowl down slowly, the clay clicking against the floor.

Tama swallowed once. "As a leader I should have stopped trying to make this choice for all of you, trying to protect you. Everyone must make this decision for themselves."

Riley left Valrik standing at the edge of the western gate, arms crossed as he watched the horizon and listened for the sound of returning hooves. She turned away and walked the familiar path to the tower. Wooden steps creaked under her boots as she climbed. At the top she pushed open the heavy door to her room and closed it behind her with a soft click.

The small space felt quieter than usual. A single lantern burned low on the table, casting long shadows across the rough walls. Riley dropped onto the edge of the narrow bed and let her shoulders sag. The last few days pressed against her like stacked stones.

Her thoughts drifted first to the mine. The brimstone had been straightforward once tested: plain sulfur, sharp and unmistakable. The white powder remained the puzzle. She remembered the moment she touched a pinch to her tongue, the faint salty bite that yanked a memory loose. She sat with her dad on the worn couch in their old living room, the television flickering with grainy black-and-white footage of war documentaries. They talked about weapons and strategy, dissecting battles like puzzles. She had spent hours watching those films, and that was probably why she still loved playing her mobile war game. The memory brought a small, private smile to her lips, warm and steadying in the dim light.

The Hoshin villagers pushed the smile away almost as soon as it arrived. The Clawborn threat sat much closer now, real and immediate. She had spoken with confidence in front of them, but alone in this room the bravado felt thin. The settlement was not ready for open war. Walls stood tall and soldiers marched daily, yet every gap in the defenses showed when she closed her eyes: not enough archers on the towers, not enough stores of arrows, not enough trained hands to hold a line if the raiders came in force. She needed weeks, maybe months. A few days at most before the Clawborn noticed the empty village and followed the obvious trail of footprints and wagon ruts straight to her gates.

How to move the rest of Hoshin without drawing their eyes? The question circled tighter. Night marches would leave tracks. Smoke from cooking fires would rise like signals. A direct path invited ambush. She rubbed her temples, staring at the floorboards until the pattern of grain blurred.

An idea flickered at the edge of her mind, sharp and possible. She leaned forward, breath catching as the pieces began to fit.
