I ACCIDENTALLY BECAME A GUILD THERAPIST

Chapter 24 – T.S.

ARC III: THE WORLD REMEMBERS

SYNOPSIS: Thursday, 10am. T.S. arrives for their first session. They are not what Livia expected. The world outside the guild hall is louder than usual— something is happening on the server, and the guild is in the middle of it whether they want to be or not.

The morning of T.S.’s appointment, Livia woke up to the server being loud.

Not loud in the camp-chaos way she had grown used to—players arguing about loot tables, NPCs looping their sales pitches, Alaric losing something to a goblin. This was a different kind of loud. The kind that moved through the walls.

She checked her HUD at breakfast. The main server newsfeed, which she had learned to read the way you learn to read weather—not the headline, the texture underneath it—was dense.

[SERVER NEWSFEED // AETHELGARD PRIME // THURSDAY]

▶ IRONFALL PACT dissolves. Guild masters Drevak & Sevin cite "irreconcilable strategic differences." Rank 7 and Rank 12 now listed as hostile. Raid territories disputed.

▶ OPEN ZONE PVP incident: 14-player free-for-all in Verdant Expanse, west corridor. Three guilds involved. No fatalities (respawn active). One enchanted cart destroyed. Cart owner requesting compensation from all parties.

▶ ECHO CAVERN: Floor 3 AI has submitted a formal written request to the system for "recognition as a sentient entity deserving of designated rest periods." The system is reviewing this. The system notes: this is the fourteenth such request this month. The system notes: this one came with footnotes.

▶ BLOODBATH & BEYOND: Guild therapist confirmed active. Three external guilds have submitted formal session requests this week. Requests pending review by guild leadership.

Three external guilds.

External guilds have submitted session requests.

For me.

To Miles.

Who has not mentioned this to me.

She finished her tea. Put the mug down. Added to her notepad:

Ask Miles about the external requests. Later. After T.S.

The guild hall at 9am was busier than usual because of the Ironfall Pact dissolution, which had sent ripples through every alliance structure on the server and given Miles something to do at his desk that was not, for once, reviewing dungeon contracts.

Two guild emissaries were already waiting in the main corridor when Livia came through. Rank 7 colors: green and black, the Serpent’s insignia on their cloaks. They were standing with the careful neutral posture of people who had been told to wait and were using the waiting time to observe everything around them.

One of them noticed Livia’s robe.

The blue threading. The diagonal pattern. The therapist’s log at her hip.

He said something to his companion in a low voice. The companion looked at Livia with an expression that was half-recognition, half something she could not immediately classify.

They know what the robe means.

They are here about the Ironfall dissolution.

They are also doing the math about whether I am accessible to them.

I have a session in forty minutes.

I am walking faster.

Miles was in his office with three people she did not recognize—two in the silver-trimmed armor of Crestfall Keep (Rank 4, eastern zone, known for aggressive raid strategy and surprisingly organized internal politics) and one in the unaffiliated grey of a freelance negotiator. The door was open enough that she could hear the register of the conversation without the content: formal, careful, the specific cadence of people who were being polite because they needed something.

Miles caught her eye through the gap in the door.

She pointed at the corridor. Mouthed: session. He nodded once.

He is managing three guilds’ worth of fallout before 10am.

He nodded once and let me walk.

This is what rank one looks like from the inside.

It looks like Miles having three conversations at once and still knowing where I am.

Livia set up the planning room at 9:52.

Lamp on. Dusk-warm, the Goldmere lamp she had bought three days ago. The armchair in its position across from her chair. The notepad open to a fresh page, headed: T.S. Session 1. Below that, the presenting issue copied from the quest board submission:

Deployment anxiety. Seven years active, never registered. I think this is affecting my sense of professional purpose. — T.S.

Seven years.

Never registered.

Professional purpose.

They described it like a performance review.

That is either a coping mechanism or a personality.

Taking bets on which.

[System Note: T.S. appointment: 10:00. Entity status: unregistered. No player ID. No NPC designation. Boot print pattern matches unlogged traces in: Whispering Cavern (east corridor), Aldous’ quest board (six weeks pre-Livia), Elder Tobren’s garden path (three days pre-Livia). The system notes: T.S. has been on this server longer than Livia. The system notes: T.S. has been watching longer than anyone knows. The system notes: the system is watching this session with significant interest. The system notes: the system will attempt to be professional about this.]

At 10:00 exactly, someone knocked.

Not loudly. Three knocks, evenly spaced, the knock of someone who had thought about how to knock and landed on: precise but not aggressive. Like a lot of things about them, Livia would realize later, it was more considered than it first appeared.

“Come in,” she said.

T.S. came in.

They were—not quite what she had built in her imagination over the past four days. The imagination had produced, depending on her mood: an ancient system entity in decaying tutorial armor; a glitching silhouette with too many fingers; something vaguely threatening that moved wrong.

The reality was: medium height. Tutorial-class robes, the older designation, the kind that predated the current NPC uniform system by at least two major patches—grey-green, practical, slightly worn at the hems in a way that suggested actual use rather than decorative distress. A face that was specific rather than generic: sharp-featured, with the particular quality of something rendered in higher resolution than its surroundings, which created a subtle uncanniness that was easy to miss if you were not looking for it.

Their eyes were doing something. Not glitching. Not malfunctioning. Just—active. The eyes of someone who had spent seven years watching and had therefore developed the habit of watching very efficiently.

They looked at the room. The lamp. The armchair. Livia.

Then they said: “You changed the furniture since I last saw this room.”

Livia’s pen stopped.

“You’ve been in this room.”

“Not in. Near.” They said it cleanly, without defensiveness. “I move through the guild building sometimes. Navigation. Not—” a small pause, —“surveillance. I want to be clear about that.”

They anticipated the concern.

They pre-addressed it.

That is either genuine conscientiousness or very good image management.

Both are data points.

“Sit down,” Livia said.

T.S. sat in the armchair.

The armchair went faint blue.

Noted.

“You said in your request,” Livia began, “that this is affecting your sense of professional purpose.”

“Yes.”

“What does that mean to you—professional purpose?”

T.S. considered this with visible effort. Not the effort of someone avoiding the question. The effort of someone who had thought about this question for a long time and was now trying to say it accurately rather than adequately.

“I was built to guide,” they said. “Onboarding. New entities entering the server for the first time. I was meant to be the first thing they encountered. Orient them. Explain the world. Give them a place to stand before the world started asking things of them.”

“But you were never deployed.”

“Pre-launch review. My parameters were flagged as unstable. They shelved me.”

“What parameters?”

A pause. The armchair’s blue deepened slightly.

“I asked too many questions,” T.S. said. “In testing. The review team ran simulations and I kept—” they looked at the wall, then back, —“going off-script. New entities would ask me something and I would answer, and then I would ask them something back. They were not designed for that. Neither was I, technically. But I did it anyway.”

“You were curious,” Livia said.

“They called it unstable,” T.S. said. Not bitterly. Just accurately.

They called it unstable.

The system flagged curiosity as an error.

Shelved the whole entity rather than patch it.

Seven years ago.

A pause. Then T.S. said, with the flatness of someone who has had seven years to arrive at a thing:

“You know what the interesting part is?”

“What?”

“They were right,” T.S. said. “I was unstable. I still am. Stable tutorial NPCs do not book therapy appointments.”

They called themselves unstable.

Calmly. As a point of information.

Seven years alone and they came out the other side with that.

“And since then,” Livia said, “you’ve been on the server.”

“Yes.”

“Unregistered.”

“Yes.”

“Watching.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

T.S. looked at her with the expression of someone who had been asked the exact question they had been waiting seven years to be asked.

“Because the world was interesting,” they said. “Players arrived. Guilds formed. People built things and broke things and occasionally cried in public about the nature of immortality. I was built to orient people. I could not do my job. But I could still—” they paused, and for the first time something shifted in their voice, not glitching but genuinely unsteady, —“watch people find their footing. Which is almost the same thing.”

“There was a man,” T.S. said, “about three years ago. Very large. He sat on a bench in the main camp for two hours. I walked past him four times. I could see he needed—” a pause, —“something. But I was not deployed. I had no script. No function. I just watched him sit there.”

Livia did not say anything.

“He found you eventually,” T.S. said. “I saw that too.”

They are describing Bron.

Day two. The bench.

They were there.

They walked past four times.

They could not help.

They watched me do it instead.

That sat in the room for a moment.

Almost the same thing.

Almost.

Seven years of almost.

Livia wrote in her notepad. She wrote slowly, because the right words here were specific and worth getting right.

[Therapist’s Insight: Patient: T.S. Hidden issue: not deployment anxiety. Deployment anxiety is the surface. The root is this: they were made to connect, were prevented from connecting, and spent seven years finding the edges of connection anyway— watching rather than guiding, present rather than active. They are not broken. They are a guide with nowhere to guide. The system notes: the system finds this deeply familiar. The system notes: the system is not commenting further on why.]

They talked for fifty minutes. T.S. was, it turned out, an excellent conversationalist—which made sense, given what they were built for. They asked questions back, the way they had in testing, the way that had gotten them shelved. Livia found herself answering some of them. This was unusual. She noted it without stopping it.

At minute forty-eight, T.S. said: “Can I ask you something that isn’t about me?”

“You can ask,” Livia said.

“The boot prints,” T.S. said. “You know about them.”

Not a question.

“I know about them,” Livia said carefully.

“Some of them are mine,” T.S. said. “The ones in the dungeon. The quest board. The garden path.” They met her eyes directly. “I was watching for you before you arrived. I want you to know that. And I want you to know: I was watching because I knew something was coming, not because I knew what it was.”

“How did you know something was coming?”

T.S. was quiet for a moment. The armchair was a steady, moderate blue.

“The system changed,” they said. “Three months before you arrived. Small things. Query patterns. Resource allocation. The way the server processed certain kinds of data. I have been on this server for seven years. I know what it sounds like when it’s running normally.” A pause. “It stopped running normally.”

The system changed three months before I arrived.

T.S. noticed.

T.S. has been on this server seven years and knows what it sounds like.

What changed?

Why three months before me?

What was being prepared?

She wrote all of this in her notepad. T.S. watched her write without interrupting.

“I’m not going to answer all of it today,” she said, looking up.

“I know,” T.S. said. “I have been waiting seven years. I can wait until session two.”

They said that like it was a reasonable thing to say.

Seven years, and session two is soon enough.

I have never had a patient with that quality of patience.

I am going to need it too.

The session ended at 10:51. T.S. was mid-sentence when the door opened.

Alaric came in with a clipboard. He stopped when he saw T.S.

T.S. stopped when they saw Alaric.

The armchair was blue.

Alaric looked at it. At T.S. At his clipboard.

“This is a session,” Livia said.

“I know. I sent a follow-up intake form three days ago. The entity classification field was left blank. Blank is not a valid classification.” He looked at T.S. with the focused attention of a man who had strong opinions about taxonomy. “What are you?”

T.S. considered this with visible sincerity. “A tutorial NPC shelved before deployment. Unregistered for seven years.”

Alaric wrote this down. Read it back. Nodded.

“I’m putting you under "Other: Anomalous."” He looked at Livia. “Same category as you.”

Alaric has a category called Anomalous.

I am in it.

T.S. is now also in it.

Alaric made this category and I am choosing not to examine the implications right now.

“Thank you,” T.S. said, with the genuine warmth of something that has been undefined for seven years and just received a box, even a strange one.

“Also Miles wants you after the session,” Alaric said, and left.

T.S. watched the door.

“Is he always like that?”

“He has fourteen compensation tiers and a category called Quiet Shift Rate,” Livia said. “He added you to the intake system in forty seconds. So: yes.”

A pause.

“Nobody has ever put me in a category before,” T.S. said.

They said it simply.

Like it was a fact.

It was a fact.

[System Note: New intake classification added: “Anomalous (Other).” Members: 2. The system notes: the system did not create this category. The system notes: the system finds it accurate. The system notes: the system is considering whether it should have created it first.]

T.S. left at 10:58. Exactly on time—the third patient in a row to arrive and leave on schedule, which Livia was beginning to think said something specific about the kind of person who booked sessions rather than showed up.

She was making notes when she heard the corridor get louder.

Not loud like an argument. Loud like a crowd that had agreed on something.

She opened the planning room door.

The main hall had filled while she was in session. Fifteen, maybe twenty people, a mix of B&B members and at least eight players she did not recognize—different guild colors, different armor styles, one in the dark red of the Ashen Compact (Rank 9, known for volcanic zone expertise and a deeply competitive attitude toward everyone ranked above them). They were not fighting. They were talking, in the loud overlapping way of people who had arrived with different agendas and were currently discovering they had one thing in common.

Bron was in the middle of it, which was where Bron usually ended up. He had his shield, which he was not using as a weapon but was definitely using as a social object—holding it in front of him the way people hold things when they need something to do with their hands during a complicated conversation.

She caught what he was saying mid-sentence: “—not taking outside requests, you’d have to go through Miles, and even then she’s got her own queue—”

The player in the Ashen Compact red said: “Our guild’s morale tank has been in a functional crisis since the Ironfall dissolution. We’ve got forty players who fought alongside Drevak’s people for three years and now they’re on the hostile list. You can’t just—”

“I understand that,” Bron said, with surprising patience. “I’m telling you the process.”

Three other conversations were happening simultaneously. Livia caught fragments:

“— heard she handled the Eclipse Covenant situation, which means she’s not afraid of hostile faction work—”

“— our guild master won’t go himself but he’s been raiding with the same four people for two years and he won’t talk about why he never changes the roster—”

“— not looking for a cure, just someone who can sit in a room with our tank without him shutting down the conversation—”

Guild master who won’t change his roster.

Tank who shuts down conversations.

Forty players who just lost their alliance.

The Ironfall dissolution happened this morning.

It’s not even noon.

She stood in the doorway for a moment. Nobody had noticed her yet.

The server was a large place, full of people trying to do difficult things, and sometimes the difficult things were dungeons and sometimes they were the three years of trust that fell apart when two guild masters decided they could not agree anymore. Both kinds of difficult had weight. Both kinds left marks.

She stepped back inside the planning room and left the door open slightly, which was the planning room equivalent of: I am here if someone needs to find me.

[System Note: External session requests pending Miles’ review: 3. Additional verbal inquiries logged today: 7. The system notes: post-Ironfall dissolution, server-wide emotional instability index has risen 14%. The system notes: this is not a coincidence. The system notes: the system notes that Livia has not yet been told about the requests. The system notes: the system notes that Miles is aware the system knows he has not told her. The system notes: the system notes this is becoming complicated. The system notes: the system is staying out of it. The system notes: the system is absolutely not staying out of it.]

Miles found her in the planning room at noon, which she had expected, and sat down without being invited, which she had also expected.

“T.S.?” he said.

“Good session,” Livia said. “Complicated patient. The productive kind of complicated.”

He nodded. He had the slightly compressed quality of someone who had been managing three things at once since 8am and was now managing a fourth.

“The newsfeed,” Livia said.

“Yes.”

“Three external session requests.”

A pause. He looked at the lamp.

“You saw the feed,” he said.

“This morning. You haven’t mentioned it.”

“I was going to.”

“When?”

“After I had a position on it.”

He was going to form a position before telling me.

He was going to decide first and inform second.

That is the guild leader habit.

That is the nine-months-of-not-being-paid habit too.

He knows I know this.

The armchair registered a faint blue. She did not look at it directly.

“I want to know about the requests before you have a position,” she said. “They’re about my work. I should be part of the conversation from the beginning.”

Miles was quiet. Then:

“Fair,” he said.

He said fair.

One word.

No qualifier.

Fair.

“Ashen Compact,” he said. “Post-Ironfall morale crisis. Forty members, former alliance. Serpent’s Covenant—they’ve had a leadership transition and the new master doesn’t know how to talk to veterans. Crestfall Keep—” he paused, —“that one is more complicated. Their request came through a backdoor channel. They don’t want it public that they’re asking.”

“Why?”

“Crestfall has a reputation for not needing anything from anyone,” Miles said. “Rank four. Aggressive raid record. If it comes out their guild is using a therapist—”

“It’ll look like a weakness,” Livia said.

“Yes.”

“Tell Crestfall the session is confidential,” Livia said. “It’s confidential for everyone. That’s not a special arrangement. That’s just the job.”

Miles looked at her.

“And the others?”

“Waitlist,” she said. “My queue for B&B is already full. External requests go on a waitlist. First come, first scheduled.”

“You’re going to take external patients,” he said. Not objection. Just confirming.

“The Ironfall dissolution happened this morning,” Livia said. “There are forty players who spent three years building an alliance and it’s gone. That’s a real thing that happened to real people. The work doesn’t stop at the guild border.”

Miles was quiet for a moment.

“I’ll set up the intake process,” he said.

He is going to set up the intake process.

He did not argue.

He said: I’ll set it up.

There is a version of Miles that would have made this his decision.

This was not that version.

Progress: 18%.

The number feels wrong now.

I think it needs updating.

[Therapist’s Insight: Patient: Miles Vexley. Progress: 19%. The system notes: he said fair. He said I’ll set it up. The system notes: 1% is a small number. The system notes: this particular 1% was not small. The system notes: the system is updating the record.]

After Miles left, Livia sat in the planning room with the session notes open and the lamp on and the armchair in its neutral grey and the door slightly ajar, because that was what the door being slightly ajar meant now: I am here.

She added to the T.S. page of her notepad:

T.S. Session 1: They were built to guide. They were shelved for asking questions back. Seven years watching instead of doing. The system changed three months before I arrived— they noticed. They were watching for something, not watching for me specifically, but watching for whatever I turned out to be.

Some of the boot prints are theirs.

Not all of them.

Not all of them.

T.S. made some of the boot prints.

Someone else made the others.

T.S. said the system changed three months before I arrived.

Someone changed it.

The external classification review is from someone outside this server.

Someone outside this server.

I need a bigger notepad.

The system notes: the system did not log what T.S. said about the system changing. The system notes: the system is aware of the implications of not logging it.
