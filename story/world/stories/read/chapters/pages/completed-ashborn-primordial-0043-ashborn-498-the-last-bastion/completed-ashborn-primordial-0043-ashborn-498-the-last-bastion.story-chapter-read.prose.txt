The problems facing the group were many, but with the Boundary so close and with their powers crippled as they were, none were as pressing as securing the safety of Alt Ashani’s walls.

Furthermore, when every set of eyes and every pair of ears could be a potential agent of a supernatural enemy, getting around a packed city would prove difficult.

As powerful as the Fateweavers were, however, they ultimately relied on mortals. Fallable mortals, whose limited senses restricted their awareness.

Senses that were easily deceived. So long as they didn’t run into an Iksana with Sight—an incredibly remote possibility, given where they were—the chances of being detected through their prana signatures were next to nothing.

It was good that Maiya had seen fit to bring a face paint kit with her, storing it in the pocket realm the Imperium had bestowed upon her. They hadn’t had the time to use it in the previous universe, relying on stealth instead, but here, it would be essential.

And so it was that, after what felt like an eternity, Vir found himself wearing makeup to pass as a human once again. Maiya changed her appearance as well, dyeing her crimson hair black and altering her features just enough that even Vir would struggle to identify her. They were subtle transformations, but in cases like these, subtle worked the best.

Unless one happened to be an Automaton of the Prime Imperium, of course.

If only Ashani was awake, she could have simply transformed, reshaping her face into something utterly unrecognizable. It was an ability that still made Vir green with envy even to this day.

With her out of commission, however, their best bet was to put makeup on her as well and smuggle her into the city.

With that done, Vir turned his attention to actually getting in.

The gates were barred for obvious reasons, and anyone coming from outside when there was such little outside left would undoubtedly garner suspicion and intense scrutiny.

Scrutiny they would obviously fail.

And so, without Dance of the Shadow Demon to rely on, they’d have to do this the conventional way.

Luckily they did still have Maiya’s precharged orbs to draw on. Without the ability to recharge them, however, they were a precious commodity and needed to be rationed for only the most dire of emergencies.

“This will do,” Vir said, pointing to a heavily reinforced sewer grate. “Think you can blow that apart?”

Maiya cocked an eyebrow. “Seriously?”

She nonchalantly produced a B Grade Hail Burst and aimed at the grate.

“You may want to avert your eyes,” she said. “Considering, you know… your current state.”

Vir nodded, backing away.

Maiya unleashed the orb, which produced a barrage of icicles. Using the Artifact given to her, Maiya concentrated her fire on a single point on a single grate, effectively turning the ability into a targeted lance.

The thick metal grate froze and shattered in no time at all.

“Well,” Maiya said, dusting off her hands. “That was easy enough.”

“Let’s just hope no Ash Beast finds out about our handiwork,” Vir said, carefully climbing through with Ashani on his shoulder.

The wolves bounded in soon after.

“Eh. They’re Altani mejai. They can handle it,” Maiya said with a smirk. “So, where to now?”

Finding an exit was not difficult, and they emerged into an alleyway, finding an empty dilapidated shack where they stashed Ashani, putting a fair share of trash over her to conceal her.

“So sorry about this, Ashani,” Maiya mumbled as they finished with the last pieces. “She’ll forgive us right?”

Vir gave her a sidelong glance.

“Right?”

“Well… Ashani is quite gracious,” he replied sheepishly.

But was she this gracious? He wasn’t looking forward to finding out. The goddess was quite particular about her looks, after all, even going so far as to refuse to alter her facial features to be less beautiful.

With one problem solved, they then split up to cover a different sector of the city, carrying precharged communications orbs to stay in contact.

With the relative lack of prana, they would not remain charged for long, but it was too much of a risk being unable to signal one another in an emergency.

Unfortunately, the last bastion of human civilization was an incredibly vast city, second only to Sonam itself, which meant that traveling on foot was painfully slow, especially compared to flying.

Acira were a far better option, and indeed, several of the exotic black birds glided through the skies alongside Altani skyships, but Vir lacked the sort of political might in this universe to acquire one. As such, they were forced to travel as humans did.

At the very least, the presence of skyships proved that magic did work in this universe. They needed only learn how.

Even still, after years of bounding across landscapes and slipping through shadows, he found the experience nothing short of agonizing, making him wonder just how people existed like this.

How had he? It was an incredible thing how quickly one grew used to even the most exotic abilities. It was, he reflected, a little tragic.

It wasn’t long before Vir lost himself in the crowded streets—more crowded than he had ever remembered the city being. Even then, it was difficult not to admire the elegant Imperium construction, its physical characteristics perfectly woven with prana art that most eyes would never appreciate.

He soon came upon a large plaza where an argument seemed to be unfolding. One that had attracted a sizable ring of spectators.

Curious, he melted into the crowd, trying to understand what was going on.

He’d barely arrived, however, when a boy in his late teens approached him, looking exasperated. Vir gave the boy a cursory glance, noting he wore the ostentatious clothing of a Sawai aristocrat who looked very much like he wanted to flaunt the fact.

“Let’s see… more rabble seems to have gathered to watch two commoners have a spat, eh?” the boy said. “Typical.”

He turned his gaze to Vir, looking him over with obvious disgust. “Please don’t tell me you’re one of them? Wait, don’t answer that. Your clothing tells me everything,” he said, taking a half-step back, as though Vir’s very presence sullied him. “Well? What’s your story, then? Out with it now.”

Vir’s eyes narrowed, but he spouted the cover story he’d prepared just the same.

“I am Adya, from Rani,” Vir said.

They had all agreed to claim they were refugees from Rani, and since Vir had already seen clothing from all over the Known World among the crowds, the explanation was as good as any.

“Rani,” the boy scoffed. “Another freeloader, then. What business do you have here?”

“I’m seeking the library,” Vir replied, forcing his irritation under control. This boy, as entitled as he was, might very well know something useful. Moreover, Vir knew of no one he could ferret information out of without arousing suspicion.

“The Altani Academy’s? Then you’re applying as well?”

Vir nodded, letting the boy talk. Not a difficult task, considering how very much he seemed in love with his own voice.

“Hah! A friendly piece of advice… give up before you embarrass yourself. Someone like you has no chance. Me, on the other hand…”

Vir blinked, raising a brow.

“You do realize who I am, don’t you?” the boy demanded.

“Afraid not.”

“I’m Vayren!” he said, as though expecting Vir to know the name. When he showed no reaction, the boy added, “Vayren Vastav. Eldest son of the Vastav family?”

Vir thought for a moment. The name tickled something in the back of his mind. It sounded familiar, though he didn’t know anyone personally by that name. Someone in the human realm, from around here…

And then it clicked.

Vastav.

The name of the man whose orders had led to the deaths of Rudvik and Maiya’s parents. Even now, after all these years, despite having avenged those responsible for that atrocity, he felt a flicker of anger flash through him.

“So you have heard of us,” Vayren said, misreading Vir’s frown. “Well, of course you have. But, er, you’re not the brightest fellow, are you?”

Vir forced a blank expression.

“Well, I s'ppose I can't expect more from a commoner. But now that you know, what chance do you think you have this year? Competition is fiercer than ever. It’s not just Sawai like myself, either. Royalty is gunning for sanctuary within Altani’s walls. I suppose even someone like you has heard of Princess Ira? That sickly girl from Kinjal?” Vayren spat in disgust. “Tell me, what makes that little chal any better than me? Honestly, what good is a princess of a nation that no longer exists?”

Vir ignored his tone to focus on the meaning of his words. Kin’jal was gone. Reclaimed by the Ash.

“Hiranya is gone as well,” Vir said, more to himself than anyone else. His hometown in this universe was no more.

“Well, yes,” Vayren muttered, looking away. “But I’m not royalty. Am I?”

Vir almost laughed.

“Speaking of, Princess Mina is here too. She’s in the running as well.”

Vir stiffened.

Mina? Here?

The Mina of this universe would not be the broken, crippled girl he had made her. She would be how she once was—manipulative, brilliant, and twisted. The situation grew more complicated by the moment.

Vir considered infiltrating the library unseen but dismissed the thought. The Altani archives layered with magical locks, but he reminded himself that without magic, he was all but guaranteed to get caught.

“You know of her?” Vayren asked, snapping Vir back to the present.

Vir shrugged. “Just heard stories.”

Vayren snorted. “Who hasn’t? She’s glorious.”

“Yes. Glorious,” Vir deadpanned, Vayren, of course, didn’t catch on. One had to actually listen to notice such things, and this boy only listened to himself.

“Even with my status, it’ll be hard competing against her, I admit,” Vayren continued, oblivious. “But you? You’ve neither an Acira nor a skyship. Tell me, are all plebeians in Rani so poor?”

Vir found his patience running thin.

Not even kings spoke to him with such little respect. Let alone this child with barely three minor affinities.

Still, Vir exhaled. He wouldn’t sink to impulse. And especially not now. It was a fact that in his current state, Vayren was far stronger than he was.

“I assume you have a reason for traveling on foot, then?” Vir asked, firing back.

Vayren looked at him with disdain. “Obviously. I’m interacting with the plebeians. Seeing how they live.”

Vir’s eye twitched.

How about I punch you in the face?

“Has anyone punched you in the face recently?” a melodic, cheerful voice chimed behind them.

Both Vir and Vayren turned as a pretty black-haired girl approached.

“Excuse me?” Vayren began, “How dare you—”

“No?” she interrupted, “because someone really ought to. Your ego could use a good pop.”

Ignoring Vayren entirely, she faced Vir and bowed slightly.

“I sincerely apologize—I couldn’t help but overhear. Please don’t think all Sawai are like him. I’m Daya. It’s a pleasure.”

She extended her arm, and Vir grasped it.

“Adya, from Rani.”

Vayren, meanwhile, looked ready to explode. That was until Daya casually said, “Daya Keyali, that is.”

Vayren froze and even stepped back.

“What—what’s a Keyali doing here?” he sputtered.

“Well,” Daya said sweetly, “I just happened to be in the area. Taking a stroll, you know? Because there are so many places left to visit in this big world of ours. And before you ask, no, I will not be riding one of my family’s Acira up to the second tier. It would be a waste of resources to use such a precious asset for so frivolous a purpose.”

Vir hadn’t known a face could grow so red. Vayren looked like a cherry about to burst.

“So you’ll be applying as well?” Vir asked.

Daya nodded. “I hope we get along.”

“I’m looking forward to it as well,” Vir said, ignoring the fuming Vayren and smiling. “Now, do you mind if I ask you a few questions?”
