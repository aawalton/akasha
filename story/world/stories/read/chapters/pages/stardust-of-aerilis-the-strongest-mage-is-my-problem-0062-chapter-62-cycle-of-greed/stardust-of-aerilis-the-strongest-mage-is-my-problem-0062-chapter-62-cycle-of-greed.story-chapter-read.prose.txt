"The descendant of a God?" Aiven asked, his voice barely a whisper. "What is that supposed to mean? Are you saying... you’re immortal? That you’re some kind of divine being?"

Lucien let out a soft, amused chuckle and leaned back into the velvet sofa, crossing one leg over the other. "Immortal? No, Aiven. I bleed just as you do. I simply have a much higher threshold for what constitutes a 'mortal' wound."

"People use the term 'God' when they encounter something their minds cannot comprehend. It is a label for the impossible. In actuality, there is no 'God of the Skies' in the way your priests describe him. That 'God' was merely a man—a distant ancestor of mine.”

Aiven frowned, his mind struggling to bridge the gap. "How could a man perform such a miraculous act? To lift entire continents? To keep them floating for centuries? Even with a thousand archmages, the mana required would be... it would be impossible. Humans shouldn't be able to cast such a strong magic. It defies every law of the arcane."

Lucien’s golden eyes sharpened, pinning Aiven with a gaze that felt like a physical weight. "You are correct. Most humans cannot. The average person is a candle—bright for a moment, but limited by the wax of their own soul. But in the long history of our kind, there are always... exceptions."

He paused, his eyes drifting pointedly to Aiven’s left shoulder. "Exceptions like myself. And exceptions like you."

Aiven’s breath hitched. He thought of the "star in a bottle"—the nearly unlimited ocean of white light that lay behind the seal on his mana. He knows, Aiven realized.

Lucien reached out and took a slow sip of his tea, the steam rising around his handsome, calm face. "Let me tell you the truth, Aiven. The truth that is not found in your history books."

He leaned forward, his voice dropping into a somber, rhythmic cadence. "Down below, beneath the clouds and the mist you call the Void, is a world that has been abandoned. It was not destroyed, as your government claims. It still exists, after a fashion. It is a graveyard of continents, living off the scraps of whatever light filters down through our 'paradise' for centuries."

Aiven leaned in, mesmerized and horrified. "The world... used to be down there? On the ground?"

"There were no floating islands then," Lucien said, his eyes reflecting the golden light of the lamps. "It was a world of vast landmasses and deep oceans. People traveled across the seas in ships of wood and iron. They walked the earth, built cities in the valleys, and looked up at a sky that was empty of anything but stars. We all belonged to the land."

"Was life...prosperous down there?" Aiven asked.

Lucien’s expression turned bittersweet. "It depends on who you ask. Some lived in absolute luxury, while others lived in wretched suffering. It was a world of nations, of greed, and of beauty. In many ways, it was not that far off from the life you know now. Just... with a much higher population. Billions of souls, all clinging to the same soil."

"But then," Lucien continued, his voice growing cold, "the calamity happened. It didn't start with a bang. It started with natural disasters. Frequent earthquakes that leveled cities. Flooding that swallowed coastlines. Volcanic eruptions that choked the sky with ash for years at a time. The people thought the planet was simply angry."

Lucien’s eyes flared with a dark, prophetic light. "But it wasn't anger. It was structural failure. The disasters escalated until the very continents began to split apart like rotting wood. And from the cracks, the dark holes appeared—voids in reality that began to swallow the world whole. The earth wasn't just breaking, Aiven. It was collapsing into itself. The world was dying."

Aiven felt a cold sweat prickle his spine. The image of a world being eaten by "dark holes" mirrored the jagged, violet rifts Lucien had used to bring them here.

"My ancestor saw the end," Lucien whispered. "He realized that the only way to save even a fraction of humanity was to take them where the dark holes couldn't reach. He didn't bless the islands. He tore them from the dying earth and threw them into the sky, tethering them with the very mana that was meant to be the world's lifeblood."

Aiven leaned forward, his tea forgotten. The library of the Fold seemed to shrink as Lucien's words painted a picture of a world being eaten from the inside out. "The dark holes... the continents splitting... what could have caused that level of systemic failure?"

Lucien’s expression was grim. "It was the work of the dungeon cores. In that era, just as in yours, people were obsessed with growth. They exploited dungeons for their resources—crystals, artifacts,—and in their arrogance, they allowed the dungeons to grow unchecked. They didn't see the cores as parasites; they saw them as gold mines. But dungeons are not static treasures, Aiven. They are hungry. They were quite literally eating the world."

"Why not just keep the balance in check?" Aiven asked, his clerk’s mind searching for a regulatory solution. "If the cause was obvious, why not destroy the most aggressive cores and only keep the beneficial ones alive?"

Lucien let out a dry, mirthless laugh. "Because adventurers were not as organized or numerous back then. But more importantly... greed. People always want more. More resources meant more potential for wealth and power. Despite the warnings and the growing disasters, the ancient governments did the opposite of what was necessary. They restricted access to the most valuable dungeons—the ones with the most volatile and massive cores—declaring them 'State Assets.' No one was allowed inside except government officials and their chosen harvesters."

Aiven felt a cold sink in his gut. "So even while the world was physically breaking apart, they were still pursuing profit?"

Lucien nodded slowly. "Human nature is remarkably consistent. It wasn't until the first 'Great Catastrophe' occurred—an event that erased nearly a quarter of the planet’s surface—that the authorities finally realized the gravity of their mistake. But by then, it was too late to save the world. They used nearly every scrap of the world's remaining mana and resources to perform a desperate, final miracle: they lifted pieces of the earth into the sky."

Lucien’s gilded eyes grew distant. "But miracles have a price. Not everyone could follow. Only certain, handpicked areas were chosen based on their strategic and economic value. The rest? They were left behind to survive on a rotting husk of a planet, swallowed by the events below. The 'God of the Skies' didn't just save humanity; he performed a mass abandonment."

"But wait," Aiven countered, "if the disasters were tied to the planet itself, wouldn't they eventually impact the floating islands too? They’re in the same world, just at a different altitude."

"A perceptive question," Lucienreplied. "There was a limit to the spatial reach of that original Catastrophe. By separating the islands and lifting them beyond the atmospheric veil, the two realms were effectively treated as separate worlds. The tethers held... for a time."

Aiven stared at the man across from him, the puzzle pieces of history clicking into a terrifying new shape. "Does that mean... you are a descendant of that ancient government?”

"Yes," Lucien said simply. "Though I have no ties to the bureaucratic rot that calls itself the 'Aerilis Central Council' today."

Lucien stood up and walked to a shelf, pulling down an ancient, weathered ledger. "The current government is making the exact same error as my ancestors. They let dungeons grow in the name of 'economic stability' and 'national security.' They hoard the cores, feeding them with the very world they are supposed to protect. History is preparing to repeat itself, Aiven, and this time, there is no higher sky to flee to."

“My aim is simple: I intend to destroy every dungeon core in existence. I will starve the hunger that eats the world, even if I have to burn the current systems to the ground to do it.”

He turned back to Aiven, his eyes burning with a radical, terrifying resolve. "And if the Aerilis government insists on sticking to their greed... then I shall become the new ruler myself.”
