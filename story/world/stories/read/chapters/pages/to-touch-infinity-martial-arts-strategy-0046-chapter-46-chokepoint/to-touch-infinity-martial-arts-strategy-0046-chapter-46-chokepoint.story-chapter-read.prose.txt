Mu-cheon’s eyes grew wide, relief and anxiety warring with his exhaustion, but he refused to let the rest of his body show how affected he was. All around him, the remnants of the Tai-shan column trudged forwards in weary silence, their spirits as fragile as glass. Executing a panicked, mad dash through the ranks of these tired troops would be the worst thing he could do right now.

The young man clenched and unclenched his fists several times, before nodding slightly towards the ragged medic who had just delivered the news.

“Thank you for letting me know. You may return to your duties. I’ll…be by to see her. Shortly.”

The medic bowed and began stumbling back towards the supply train. For the next several ke, Mu-cheon continued marching at the front of the column, trying to play the unyielding Young Master for his men. Beneath his rigid posture, however, his heart was beating rapidly. Every step felt like it took him nowhere; every moment felt like an eternity.

Finally, the sun dipped behind the rocky western ridges, and the column halted to set up camp for the night. Only when the tents were pitched, the perimeter secured, and his departure would rouse no odd glances from the men, did he slip away into the deepening shadows and towards the medical tents.

When he saw her, his breath caught in his throat.

Chae-ha lay on a low cot, her bright white bandages and emerald silk contrasting sharply with the feverish flush on her cheeks. Loss of blood had left her features even thinner than usual, making her look impossibly fragile. But her eyes were open. The young man saw them follow him as he stepped into the dim candlelight.

A small, sickly smile grew on her face as he approached.

“Senior brother. I guess...we got away. How much farther until…we reach Laiwu?”

“Another two days,” Mu-cheon replied softly, stepping closer to her bedside. Seeing her so weak unleashed a torrent of buried guilt. He dropped to his knees beside the cot, his head lowering in shame.

“Chae-ha...I’m so, so sorry. It’s all my fault you got hurt. I should have led the retreat faster. I should have paid more attention, I–”

He cut himself off when he saw she was trying to reply.

“It’s not your fault, senior brother. My plan…failed. The enemy strategist countered it, and I...I left myself no other way out.”

Unbidden, a vivid memory flashed through her mind – the terrifying image of Mu-cheon on the battlefield, entirely consumed by his berserk rage. Instinctively, she turned her head away from him, her shoulder shifting slightly to put distance between them.

“I think we both learned a lot from this battle.”

“What happened in that battle wasn’t your fault, little sister,” Mu-cheon said, his voice pleading as he ignored her subtle withdrawal. He gently reached out a hand, although he hesitated to touch her bandages. “You’re just an Inspector. A part of the administrative staff! The responsibility of command…”

His voice trailed off as he noticed her head had turned to stare at him again, a coldness suddenly rising behind her eyes.

“Just…an Inspector, am I? Part of the administrative staff?”

Mu-cheon’s face paled. “That’s not what I meant. You’re not…I mean, you shouldn’t have to be the one who…”

This time she withdrew completely, turning her body fully away from him to face the dark corner of the tent. The motion caused a surge of sharp pain to flare up in her side.

“Thank you for your attention, senior brother,” Chae-ha whispered, “but I think…you should go. Right now, you should be seen amongst the troops. Not cooped up in here with an injured…Inspector.”

Mu-cheon remained on his knees, his hand hovering in the empty air. The silence in the tent was broken only by the distant crackle of the campfires outside, mocking the growing chasm between the sect’s prince and his shadow. After a few tense heartbeats, the young man slowly rose to his feet and turned to leave.

“I’ll do everything I can to make up for my mistake, little sister. Not because I think you need me to take care of you. But because it’s the right thing to do.”

The young man opened the flap to the tent, looking back briefly as he departed.

“You were right when you said that I learned something from this battle. I learned just how badly I need you by my side.”

After he was gone, Chae-ha continued staring at the corner of the tent, unable to name the complex emotions that plagued her.

“Not by your side,” she whispered into the corner of the tent.

“In your shadow.”

As time passed, and the march back to Laiwu continued to put distance – both literal and metaphorical – between the Tai-shan soldiers and their recent defeat, the pain of aching muscles slowly began to supplant feelings of shame amongst the troops. Chae-ha’s condition, meanwhile, remained mostly stable. Although she still couldn’t sit up and had a persistent fever, at least she wasn’t delirious. And the simple stew she ate every day did seem to be helping with the blood loss.

When the march was over and they were once again back within the walls of Laiwu, Mu-cheon proved to be a man of his word. Chae-ha was transferred to one of the best rooms in the local medical ward, and the most prestigious doctors available in the city were assigned to her care.

After a thorough examination, the head physician – an elderly man with a wispy white beard and a missing front tooth – provided his prognosis.

“The Young Master may put his heart at ease. The spearhead left a clean puncture wound. Had it sliced across the muscle or severed a major vessel, her condition would be much worse. As it stands, she will likely recover within the month.”

It would be another week before her fever finally broke. A few days after that, Chae-ha was finally able to sit up on her own again, even if she needed to be propped up against a mountain of folded linens. Her torso remained tightly wrapped with bandages, and her side twinged with a sharp ache whenever she moved suddenly or breathed too deeply. One of her very first requests was for a brush, ink, and paper to write on, which the medical staff happily provided.

Outside her sickroom, the war effort was floundering due to a vacuum of leadership. In the absence of Commander Mok, temporary control of the Tai-shan forces was balanced precariously on the thin shoulders of Lieutenant Shin.

Shin, Mu-cheon, and Tae-seok had spent the last several days pacing in the command tent, arguing about what to do. But now that Chae-ha had recovered enough to speak coherently, Mu-cheon stubbornly refused to make another move without her. Overriding the protests of the medical staff, he insisted on bringing her into the discussion. Or more accurately, since she couldn’t move – he insisted on bringing the discussion to her.

And that was how a high-level strategy meeting for the war effort came to take place in a VIP room at the medical ward.

“The Meng-shan will be on the move again within the next two weeks,” Lieutenant Shin grumbled, his lips pursed tightly with worry. “Yes, they will have been slowed by having to deal with the aftermath of the battle: consolidating their gains at the mine, setting up a proper garrison there, clearing the blocked road, and dealing with the ramifications of a dead High Elder. But while that’s all been going on, we’ve been wasting time here. We need to be ready to meet them!”

“You can get the men ready to march, but the outcome will be the same, boy,” Tae-seok admonished him.

“No, it won’t! This time, we’ll make better use of the local rangers to –”

Mu-cheon held up a hand. “We’ve been going in circles on this topic for days. I brought you both here because I wanted to hear what she had to say. She’s the only reason we’re all able to stand here and argue about this in the first place.”

All eyes turned to Chae-ha.

“Thank you, senior brother. Elder Eom is right; we can’t win on the roads, at least not like this. For the last three days, I’ve been working on a plan. As with any…wound…The first thing we have to do is stop the bleeding.”

Mu-cheon’s eyes involuntarily went to the bandages at her side, as Chae-ha held up a piece of paper and passed it over to Lieutenant Shin. Tearing his eyes away, he joined the other two men in looking it over.

On the paper were a few rough sketches. The first was a map of the Laiwu valley, with a large spot on the road marked with a circle. Beneath it was a sketch of a defensive fortification – a large wooden gate, with wooden spikes arranged in front of it, and a wooden wall extending out from either side.

“This is the chokepoint I identified earlier, the one Commander Mok so quickly dismissed. There is a steep cliff fairly close to the western side of the road here, and a sharp fall-off on the eastern side that’s close to impassable. We clear-cut the forest around this point up to those two obstacles, some fifty paces on either side. And we use the wood from doing that to build a wall, along with a gatehouse that sits on top of the road. The area in front will be covered with angled, sharpened tree trunks, to break up any column that tries to march through. And there will be space for archers up on top.”

Tae-seok nodded approvingly, his one good eye fixed on the paper, but Lieutenant Shin’s reaction was disbelief. He slammed his fist into a side table, surprising himself when he saw how much it jumped, and trying sheepishly to return it to its original state before continuing.

“Are you insane? Do you know how long it would take to make something like this?”

“If we deployed all the lumberjacks and engineers in Laiwu and had our troops assist with cutting down the trees, hauling away the stumps, and building the structures…well, I estimated it would take about a month. Four to five weeks.”

“The Meng-shan will be marching in two weeks! How are we supposed to protect the Deer Valley Mine?”

“We don’t,” Chae-ha replied simply. “We put up a show to slow them down, and make it look like we’re trying to defend the mine. We keep their focus there for as long as we can, so they don’t see what we’re building thirty li down the road. Then we eventually back off and let them take it. As before, they’ll need some time to consolidate their gains. Sacrificing the mine is how we’ll get the remaining time to finish the fortifications.”

Mu-cheon put a hand on the man’s shoulder. “We don’t have to give up on reclaiming our territory. We just have to stop their advance before we get back to focusing on that.”

Lieutenant Shin stared at the sketch, trying to bring himself to acknowledge the obvious truth. He glanced at Chae-ha, acutely aware of her contributions on the battlefield.

“Commander Mok would hate this,” the Lieutenant said after a moment. “If I agree, and he somehow turns up again after all of this, he’ll have my head.”

“I don’t think you have to worry about that, son,” Tae-seok replied, looking away.

“That reminds me,” Chae-ha cut in. “Lieutenant Shin, here.”

The injured girl rummaged through the papers by the side of her bed, removing one sheet from amongst the stack and handing it to him.

“I took the liberty of drafting this letter to the Sect Leader on your behalf. It explains the current situation, the plan to halt the Meng-shan advance, and…it requests that he name Eom Tae-seok the new Vanguard Commander, effective immediately.”

Tae-seok’s eyes narrowed, a threatening tone creeping into his voice. “Young lady, stop this right now. You don’t understand what you’re doing.”

Chae-ha ignored him. “Lieutenant, you are an excellent soldier, but we both know you aren’t ready to take command. Stamp this document, and you won’t have to take the blame for our failure to recapture the lost territory, or for the sacrifice of the additional mine. You can focus on what you do best – running day to day operations with the soldiers.”

Tae-seok stepped towards her, swinging one hand dismissively. “I asked you to stop once, girl. I won’t ask again. My duty is to guard the Young Master – nothing else.”

“So what will it be, Lieutenant?” Chae-ha asked softly. “Will you stamp it?”

Tae-seok looked furious. Lieutenant Shin stared at the letter, his hands shaking as he read it over. The man seemed almost relieved at the idea of ceding command to someone else. He looked up briefly, one final, weak objection on his mind.

“But…if Commander Mok returns…”

“Commander Mok won’t be coming back, son. He’s dead,” Tae-seok interjected, taking a deep breath and giving Chae-ha a long, sideways glance.

“I warned you, girl. You may think you’re smarter than everyone else in the room, and maybe you are. But I refuse to be manipulated. If you want my help, you ask me for it. I don’t appreciate being coerced into joining your scheme.”

“What scheme?” demanded Lieutenant Shin, puzzled. “And how can you be so sure Commander Mok is dead?”

“Because he was assassinated,” the veteran general growled, pointing at Chae-ha.

“By that young lady right there.”
