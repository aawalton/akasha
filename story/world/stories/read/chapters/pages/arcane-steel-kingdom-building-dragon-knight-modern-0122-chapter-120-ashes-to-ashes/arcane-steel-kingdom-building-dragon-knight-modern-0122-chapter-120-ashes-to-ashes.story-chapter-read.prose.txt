The freezing mud of Oakhaven clung to my boots, heavy and cold. I knelt before the small, soot-covered girl, my crimson armor a stark, alien presence in this ruined village.

Ela. My cousin.

I looked at her, searching for the features of the uncle I barely remembered. Beneath the grime, the silver hair and sapphire eyes were unmistakable. But it was the hollow, haunted look in those eyes that struck me. It was a look no five-year-old should possess.

A dark, cold sensation began to coil in my chest. It was the same feeling that had consumed me when I first thought my family was dead, a void of absolute, unyielding rage. For weeks, the warmth of my parents’ return, the chaotic joy of Lyra, the tentative connection with Nyxia—it had all acted as a balm, soothing the scars of my perceived loss. I had started to let the darkness recede, allowing myself to believe I could simply be a builder, a husband, a brother.

But looking at Ela, I realized the danger of that comfort.

My father warned me about blind rage, I thought, the memory of his words echoing in the silence of the village. But he didn't say I had to extinguish the fire entirely.

If I let the warmth of my restored family soften me, if I forgot the agony of loss, I would become complacent. And complacency in this world was a death sentence. I needed this darkness. I needed to remember the horror of Wighthelm burning, the smell of ash, the feeling of absolute powerlessness. I needed to forge that pain into a weapon, a beast I kept chained in my soul, ready to unleash on anyone who dared threaten my own. I had to build a world where no child would ever look at me with the hollow, starved eyes that Ela had right now. And to build that world, I couldn't be just a benevolent ruler. I had to be a monster they feared.

I refocused on the little girl shivering before me. She was staring at the silver lion crest embossed on my chest plate. The concept of a "cousin" clearly meant nothing to her. Family, to a child who had grown up hiding in the mud, was a foreign language.

She slowly lifted her hands, offering me the small, ornate wooden box she clutched so tightly.

"Nanny Lue told me to give this to you," she whispered, her voice rough from the cold and disuse. She glanced around nervously at the villagers peeking out from the dilapidated huts, then back to me. "She said... you can take me to my family."

Her eyes, huge and desperate, locked onto mine. "People who will care for me... like Nanny Lue did."

The words were a physical blow. The absolute, crushing loneliness in that simple request tore through my composure.

I gently pushed the box back toward her and reached out, ruffling her ash-stiffened hair. The gesture was clumsy, my gauntleted hand too large, but I tried to be as gentle as possible.

"Silly girl," I said, forcing a warmth into my voice that I hoped masked the cold fury boiling in my veins. "Like I told you, I am your cousin. I am your family."

A tiny, fragile spark of hope flickered in her sapphire eyes. "Really?" she breathed. "You will give me food? And clothes? And... and toys?"

She caught herself instantly, her eyes widening in sudden panic, as if she had just demanded the moon. She pulled her hands back, clutching the box tighter.

"I don't need toys!" she blurted out, her voice rising in a frantic, desperate rush. "Just food and clothes will be enough. I won't be a bother, I promise! I don't need toys!"

A fresh wave of pain gripped my heart, so sharp it felt like a physical wound. What kind of hell has she lived through? The thought that a five-year-old child was terrified I would abandon her because she asked for a toy... it made me want to find the nearest Cinderfall garrison and vaporize it slowly.

I didn't say anything. I simply reached forward and scooped her up into my arms. She was terrifyingly light, nothing but skin and fragile bones beneath the rags.

"Ela," I said, holding her securely against my chest plate. "You can have all the food you can eat. You can have all the clothes you want. And you can have any toys your heart desires."

She looked at me, her eyes searching my face for the lie. "Really?"

"I promise."

I turned and walked back toward the center of the clearing, where Kaelus waited. Varuna, still bleeding but conscious, was being carefully elevated onto a medical hover-stretcher by the sleek white automata.

As we approached the massive, starlit dragon, Ela’s fear seemed to vanish, replaced by a profound, wide-eyed awe. She stared at Kaelus’s shimmering scales, her small hand reaching out tentatively.

Kaelus lowered his enormous head, his sapphire nebula eyes locking onto her. He let out a soft, huffing breath that smelled of ozone, gently sniffing her hair.

Brother, his mental voice echoed in my mind, a low, contented rumble. I like her. She smells like Lyra.

I patted his snout. "Let's go home."

We took flight, the medical stretcher hovering smoothly alongside us, caught in the protective slipstream of Kaelus’s spatial magic. As we ascended, breaking through the low-hanging winter clouds, the horizon erupted.

A series of brilliant, expanding spheres of azure light bloomed in the distance, far down the horizon. The 3rd Fleet’s long-range supersonic missiles had finally found their targets, obliterating the Cinderfall defenses in a silent, beautiful display of total annihilation.

My HUD flickered to life.

[TES: REPORT. A PHOENIX KNIGHT IS TRAVELING AT HIGH SPEED FROM THE IMPACT ZONE TOWARD THE CINDERFALL CAPITAL.]

"They finally found a workaround for The Oracle's jamming," I murmured, watching the distant, golden streak of light through my tactical feed. They were turning a Phoenix Knight into a very expensive, very fast messenger pigeon.

The Hegemony was about to realize they were fighting a war on three fronts, and my generals were currently wreaking havoc on all of them. Let King Theron try to prioritize his defense now.

…

High above the clouds, Captain Ivela stood on the bridge of The Vengeance. The air around her was a chaotic symphony of screaming fighter engines and the deep, percussive thud of the carrier's main defensive batteries.

She was close. She could feel the single obsidian star of a warlord waiting for her if this operation succeeded.

Her mission was simple: punch a hole through the disorganized enemy airspace, rendezvous with the Lord Commander, and facilitate his extraction.

She looked at the tactical displays. The sky was an absolute mess. The 3rd Fleet, miles away on the ocean, had deployed every single Wyvern and Phantom in their arsenals. To a layman, or a terrified Cinderfall commander, the attacks looked random—a chaotic swarm of metal birds striking supply lines, communication hubs, and isolated garrisons with no discernible pattern.

But Ivela knew better. She was an officer of the Air Force; the sky was her domain. And right now, she had to admit, Commodore Theramore was orchestrating a masterpiece.

The "random" attacks were pulling the Hegemony's remaining aerial defenders apart, creating massive, localized vacuums in their airspace. The old sailor was using his planes like a conductor, drawing the enemy's attention everywhere except where it mattered.

And right down the center of that chaos, in the eye of the storm Theramore had created, The Vengeance sailed completely unmolested.

Ivela permitted herself a small, grudging smile. Show-off, she thought. But effective.

…

Off the coast of the Cinderfall capital, the Abyssal Fleet finally broke its silence.

Leviathan, Hydra, and Kraken didn't surface. They remained in the crushing dark, opening their torpedo tubes. An endless, silent volley of heavy, guided munitions tore through the water, striking the capital's fortified naval docks.

The explosions rocked the city, shattering the illusion of safety. An attack on the capital’s doorstep immediately overrode any concern for the border skirmishes. Theron’s remaining forces scrambled to defend their home, pulling even more troops away from the inland territories.

And in those inland territories, the shadows were taking their toll.

Nobles loyal to Cinderfall were dropping dead in their heavily warded manors. Supply caravans were found slaughtered, the food untouched, the guards eliminated with terrifying precision. The Hegemony had spent months building countermeasures against the Shadow Order, relying on House Black to manage the intelligence war.

Now that House Black was the enemy, Cinderfall was fighting blind. It was a brutal, bloody repeat of the purge that had secured Aerthos, only now it was happening in their own backyard.

…

Not long after The Vengeance arrived to escort us, I made a decision.

"Tes," I signaled. "Drop Varuna off with the medical bay on the carrier. Kaelus and I are heading straight back to The Aegis."

As we banked away from the massive ship, a persistent, blinking notification caught my eye on the HUD. It was an anomaly I had been ignoring during the extraction.

I opened the alert. It was a tracking beacon for General Goliath.

Bob was in the middle of the Maelstrom Sea.

I stared at the blinking icon, utterly bewildered. He wasn't on a ship. He wasn't in a transport. He was... swimming.

I accessed the log. After receiving the Level 3 alert, Bob had apparently decided that waiting for a transport from the Obsidian Dominion was unacceptable. He had simply walked his MECH into the ocean, summoned Aquarius, and started swimming across the continent.

Given that Aquarius was a Water Dragon, he was significantly faster submerged than in the air. Bob had managed to cross ten percent of the Maelstrom Sea through sheer, stubborn willpower and draconic horsepower before the alert could be canceled.

I quickly deactivated the Level 3 status. I would have to thank him later, but right now, I needed him to turn around. The carpet bombing had secured us a beachhead, yes, but we were pushing deep into the Cinderfall mainland now, stretching our supply lines and automated defense grids to their absolute limits. To hold that newly conquered territory against the inevitable Hegemony counter-attack, we desperately needed the massive energy output of those two Dominion dungeon cores. This was also an aggressive, dangerous push into a hostile wasteland, and I didn't know how long our forces could maintain the extraction operation without their commanding general leading the charge.

Tes, I projected, fighting the exhaustion that was beginning to pull at my edges. Inform the family we are returning. Tell them it's almost morning, and I have something incredibly important to discuss at breakfast.

…

The flight deck of The Aegis was quiet when we finally landed. The sun was just beginning to crest the horizon, painting the metal city in shades of pale gold.

Patricia was waiting near the primary lift, her posture rigid, her uniform immaculate. She looked as though she hadn't slept, but her face betrayed nothing.

I stepped off Kaelus and walked toward her, carrying Ela. The little girl was fast asleep, her head resting on my shoulder, one small hand gripping the edge of my armor.

I gently transferred her into Patricia's waiting arms.

Patricia looked down at the bundle, her professional mask slipping for a fraction of a second. She looked at the soot, the matted hair, the ragged clothes. Then she looked at me, thoroughly confused.

"Help her clean up," I said quietly. "And bring her to the private dining hall for breakfast."

Patricia nodded, though her eyes were full of questions. She thinks I picked up a stray, I thought with a grim smile. She was in for a shock when she finally washed the ash out of that silver hair.

…

The private dining hall in the New Wighthelm pocket dimension was a haven of warmth, smelling strongly of roasted coffee beans and fresh pastries. When I walked in, wearing a clean uniform that still smelled faintly of ozone from the flight deck, Lyra was already there.

She scrambled out of her oversized chair, a cheerful blur of energy, and launched herself at my leg. "Brother!" she chirped, hugging my knee tightly.

I smiled, a real, genuine smile, and ruffled her hair. "I have a surprise for you, fire-cricket."

"Yay! I love surprises!" she cheered, her sapphire eyes sparkling with innocent greed.

The contrast between Lyra’s boundless, secure joy and the haunted, starving look I had seen in Ela’s eyes just hours ago was a physical ache in my chest.

Nyxia was sitting at the table, stirring her tea. She looked restless, her eyes darting to me with unspoken questions. She was clearly wondering why her husband had vanished from their bed in the middle of the night without a word. I offered her a reassuring nod, but said nothing.

I walked over to where my father sat at the head of the table. His morning newspaper was spread out before him, detailing the latest troop movements. I didn't say a word. I simply reached into my pocket dimension, pulled out the ornate wooden box I had received from Ela, and set it gently on the table in front of him.

He looked at the box.

He froze.

His hands, which had held the line against dungeon hordes and dragon fire, began to tremble. He reached forward slowly, almost fearfully, tracing the howling wolf crest carved into the lid with a shaking finger.

My mother, sensing the sudden, suffocating shift in the room's atmosphere, moved closer. I stepped up behind my father, placing a steadying hand on his shoulder. My mother stood on his other side, her eyes widening as she recognized the crest.

With a soft, almost imperceptible click, my father used a tiny pulse of specific, family-locked mana to release the hidden latch. The lid popped open.

Inside, resting on a bed of faded velvet, lay a heavy silver signet ring bearing the wolf crest, and a single, folded piece of thick parchment.

For the first time in my life, I saw my father break.

He didn't sob. He didn't make a sound. But as he looked at the ring, tears simply began to fall, tracing silent paths down his weathered cheeks. I had never seen him cry. Not when our home burned. Not when we were reunited.

He gently, reverently, picked up the folded parchment. His hands were shaking so badly he struggled to open it.

He smoothed it out on the table. My mother and I leaned in over his shoulders to read the hurried, cramped handwriting.

Kaelen,

If you are reading this, I may be gone. I sense an imminent danger; the Cinderfall movements on the border are unnatural. I am sending my child away, in the desperate hope that she might find you, brother.

Please, take care of Eleanor, take care of my Ela. I will not waver in my duty. I will hold this fort, just as our father taught us, defending our people with my very last breath.

Beneath that, written in a softer, more deliberate script, was a second message, clearly intended for someone else.

My dearest Ela,

I am so sorry. All I can say is that I love you very dearly. I am able to leave this world with a clear heart only because I know my older brother will find you. He will care for you as his own, and he will never let you feel the pain of my loss.

Love,Dad.

My father looked up from the parchment, his stormy grey eyes wide with a frantic, desperate, impossible hope. My mother was openly weeping at his side, her hand covering her mouth to stifle her sobs.

"Alarion," my father choked out, his voice a broken, wet rasp. "Where... where did you find this?"

I didn't answer with words. I turned and gestured toward the entrance.

The heavy weirwood doors slid open.

Patricia stood there. Beside her, clutching the maid's hand with a white-knuckled grip, was a little girl.

She was wearing a clean, simple blue dress. The soot and ash had been washed away. Her silver hair shone in the morning light, framing a pale, painfully thin face with wide, nervous sapphire eyes.

Ela.

My mother let out a cry that was half-sob, half-laugh. She walked, then she dropped to her knees right there in the dining hall and threw her arms open, engulfing the terrified little girl in a fierce, desperate hug.

I looked at my father. The sheer, overwhelming relief and gratitude in his eyes was blinding. He looked like a man who had just been handed back a piece of his soul. I knew he wanted to rush forward, to crush her in a hug of his own, but he held back. He stayed in his chair, his hands gripping the edges of the table, afraid of overwhelming the fragile, traumatized child.

I stood there, watching my family piece itself back together. I had made a choice in the freezing mud of Oakhaven. I had chosen not to let the fire burn away my heart completely.

And looking at the timid, hesitant smile beginning to form on Ela's face as Lyra bounded over to excitedly introduce herself, I knew, with absolute certainty, that I had made the right call.
