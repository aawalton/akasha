As it turned out, he had been waiting. I found Dralos patiently standing by for me in the corridor outside the receiving hall. He had most certainly been close enough to listen in from that distance. It was either impressively loyal that he was always trying to remain in the know, or an indication of disloyalty, depending on how charitable I felt. I settled on loyal, given the hooks the Desire System had in him. And in all honesty, I would have let him attend the meeting if he'd asked. He'd attended the first meeting, after all.

“The conditions I spoke of,” I said to the Draconian, without stopping. He didn’t miss a beat and fell into step beside me. “I want them drafted and delivered to the Cardinals before midday. Make them airtight. I don’t want to be blindsided by any loopholes.”

“Are there any other conditions not yet mentioned, my lady?”

“Full disclosure of the convocation’s attendee list before I commit to a departure date. A written account of everything they know about the Church of Light’s activities in Lysoria.” I paused. “And in Cael.” I shot Dralos a look. “Make sure to mention that the descriptions must not be mere summaries. I want the entirety of the facts, accompanied by actual documentation.” I turned the corner toward the main staircase. “We must also have safe passage guarantees through Holy Kingdom territory from the churches, signed and sealed, not just verbal promises. I do not want to be dealing with violent fanatics while attending a convocation. And the name of every known person Mirabelle reported to in the Church of Balance or Church of Light, before I set foot outside this duchy.”

Dralos grunted in a way that was not quite agreement and not quite a question. “And if they refuse any of these?”

“Then I don’t go,” I answered simply, shrugging my shoulders. I paused at the base of the stairs and looked at him. “They need me there more than I need to be there. Make sure the language reflects that... in fact, say that. Those exact words. I lose nothing, really. They risk everything.”

“Of course,” Dralos said with another one of his weird grunts, a particular dryness in his tone that told me he had already been composing the letter in his head while I continued to talk.

“May I be dismissed?” he asked, not really waiting for permission before he started to bow. I just waved him away, and he left without another word.

In the meantime, I figured it was time to visit Lilliana’s eldest brother.

I decided to take the long way, wanting a bit of time to collect my thoughts, so I walked through the third spire’s Iron Wing before eventually arriving at where Raphael was recuperating.

Dame Carla had returned from delivering my report to General Marell only days after we reached Elyndor, and she had asked for quiet and consistency while she worked on Raphael’s mind. The Iron Wing could provide both well enough.

I leaned against the door to his room and pressed my ear to it. I heard nothing. When I cracked the door open, I saw only Raphael, still unconscious, lying in a medium-sized bed.

Someone had washed him since I’d last seen him. The blood was gone from his thick brown hair, which had been combed back from a face that was all sharp edges now, his cheekbones pressing against skin gone gray and thin as paper. Clean linen wrapped his hands where the stakes had gone through, and the outline of his ribs rose and fell beneath the blanket with every shallow breath. He looked younger asleep. He looked like Lilliana in all the ways that mattered, like the same small, starved girl whose body I had first woken in, before Reformation reshaped it into something closer to mine. The same brown hair. The same narrow chin.

Something shifted in my chest at the sight of him. I recognized that it wasn’t born of my own emotions. It was what remained of Lilliana’s feelings for him, stirring in the heart we now shared.

Dame Carla was absent, though I figured that made sense. It wasn’t as if the woman could work on Raphael for days on end without rest. I stood there a moment longer than I needed to, then moved on. There was nothing useful I could do in that room, and standing outside it accomplished even less. If Raphael’s mind would heal, Dame Carla would inform me when it happened.

So I left, heading back to my study, where a busy day and a stack of paperwork no doubt excitedly awaited my arrival.

The upper floors of the Duke’s Spire were always eerily quiet in the mornings. The administrative staff who had come with the duchy, the ones who had served the Alistar name for decades and were still quietly deciding what they thought of its newest bearer, tended to cluster on the lower levels where the work happened. Up here it was mostly my people, and my people knew better than to fill silence with noise for no reason.

My study sat at the end of the corridor, beneath the stained-glass warrior queen. I’d kept the plush chair, but the Duke’s original mahogany desk had gone back to storage once I discovered its drawer locks were all broken. The Duke, apparently, had had no need for secrets. I did.

Today, atop a lonesome new stack of unread paperwork, sat a letter. It was rather out of place among the rest. Where the reports beneath it were written on cheap, gray duchy parchment, this was a single heavy sheet the pale blue of shallow water, folded twice into a neat rectangle and edged in a thin line of silver leaf. One corner had softened and rippled, as if it had been carried a long way through damp air, and when I leaned closer, it smelled faintly of salt.

It wasn’t from Dralos. He was still downstairs composing my conditions with the careful precision of someone who enjoyed the work. Aldren, the quiet man who managed the Spire’s correspondence, must have left it there. The letter was sealed with a disc of pale blue wax the size of a coin, pressed with an anchor over two crossed swords and tied beneath with a short length of silver ribbon. My name and title were written across the front in dark ink, in a hand of sharp scrawls.

Duchess Lilliana Silverwater of the Alistar Duchy.

Though, technically, Silverwater was no longer my surname. The King’s decree had changed it to Alistar.

I looked at it for a moment before picking it up and breaking the seal.

The wax snapped cleanly, and I unfolded the letter and read it standing. I rarely sat to read anymore. Being a duchess involved far too much sitting as it was.

Fortunately, it wasn’t long. Three paragraphs, written with the economy of someone who chose his words carefully. Whether that came from training or experience, I didn’t particularly care to know. As I read, though, the answer came to me on its own, along with the identity of the author.

To Her Grace, Lilliana Silverwater, Duchess of the Alistar Duchy,

I, Prince Romeo, third son of King Aldric of Velora, write to you on my own behalf and with my father’s knowledge. News of the change in leadership within the Alistar Duchy reached our court some months ago. Please accept my personal congratulations on your ascension.

With his father’s knowledge, I noted. Not necessarily his blessing.

I couldn’t be entirely certain that this Prince Romeo was the same Romeo from Sealrite’s colosseum, but my instincts told me he was.

“That little shit wasn’t just a noble brat,” I muttered. “A prince?”

The second paragraph was where it really became interesting.

For the past year, Velora has endured a series of escalating difficulties with the Pandorian Empire. Trade convoys on routes that have gone uncontested for generations now meet imperial inspections that delay our shipments by weeks, and which have more than once ended with cargo detained on grounds never adequately explained. At the same time, Pandoria has made repeated formal approaches to my father’s court, asking that Velora sign a treaty of mutual recognition. Read closely, that treaty amounts to an acknowledgment of Pandorian primacy in any future regional dispute. My father has declined twice. The imperial ambassador has returned a third time regardless. Velora is not in the habit of selling its sovereignty for the illusion of safety, least of all to the very empire suspected of kidnapping not only myself, but several other nobles of our kingdom.

I could almost hear him saying that last line, in the same flat, stubborn voice he’d used to tell me there were innocents in Sealrite’s houses.

The third paragraph was the ask.

It seems to me that the Alistar Duchy, by virtue of its place within Lysoria, shares a common set of concerns with any kingdom that finds the Empire’s recent appetite for expansion inconvenient. I would welcome a meeting, at a time and place of your choosing, to discuss whether there is a basis for a more formal arrangement between our respective interests.

With respect,

Romeo of Velora

Interesting. If this was the Romeo I knew, it made sense that he wouldn’t want our connection in writing. But he had also never approved of my methods. So why reach out?

I read it a second time, more slowly.

I had not thought about Romeo in some months, though that was not the same as not remembering him. Nor Julius, who I now suspected had been some kind of guard, taken along with the boy. The colosseum had made itself quite unforgettable.

Romeo had fought his way out of the arena beside me, with Julius forever at his back. He had argued with me at nearly every turn in Sealrite: over the monsters I’d loosed on the city, over breaking and rebuilding his heart rings, over nearly everything. He had been sharp then, in the brittle way of someone running on anger and very little else. Not a friend. I didn’t have friends, and no one really made them in a place like that.

But there had been an understanding within our ragtag group, the kind that formed between people who were desperately fighting for the same reason and drowning all the same. And then, one day, he and Julius had simply vanished. There had been signs, in hindsight. I’d figured they had returned to wherever they’d come from, but I had not pegged Romeo for some lost princeling. Highborn, yes. Gronch had all but said so during the revolt, sneering that even enslaved, the pampered brat still had his guard.

Now he was writing to me on pale blue paper under a royal seal, in the hand of someone who had received an education that cost a great deal of money, requesting a meeting between our “respective interests,” whatever the fuck that actually meant.

I set the letter down on the desk and looked at it again, chewing absently on my bottom lip.

He knew it was me. That much wasn’t in question. Everyone knew I was the girl from the arena at this point. The change in the duchy’s leadership was public knowledge, and Romeo was clearly not someone who acted before he was informed. Yet he had addressed me by the name I’d fought under in Sealrite, not the one the King had given me.

I doubted that was accidental. For what purpose, I couldn’t say. But whether it was strategy or courtesy, it didn’t much matter.

I pulled a fresh sheet of paper from the desk drawer and sat down to respond.

My reply took three drafts, two more than I usually needed. The first was too direct and, if I was being honest with myself, a little rude. Political correspondence had never been my strong suit. Still, I didn’t want to rely on Dralos or Benedict to write something this personal.

The second was too careful and too... casual. I was trying too hard not to sound like a bloodthirsty conqueror.

The third was what I actually sent.

I began by acknowledging his letter and his congratulations. I noted, without elaborating, that I was familiar with the kind of imperial pressure he described, having encountered its effects at reasonably close range. I confirmed that the Alistar Duchy’s position within Lysoria did indeed give it common cause with any party that found Pandoria’s current direction inconvenient. And I proposed that, as I had reason to travel in Velora’s general direction in the coming weeks, I would be amenable to meeting in Velora on whatever date suited him, provided it fell at least two weeks before the Holy Kingdom’s convocation. The voyage from Velora to the Holy Kingdom took ten days, and I needed to leave room for it.

An official journey from Elyndor to Velora’s capital, Mariven, would take about a week. That left more than enough time in between to negotiate whatever treaty Romeo had in mind.

I chose not to mention that the Cardinals had visited me personally, nor what they had asked of me. I also did not mention Sealrite or the Church of Light or anything that had happened in the past two days, because none of that was his business yet, and information given before it needed to be given was information spent without return.

What I did do, at the very end, before the formal close, was write one line that existed outside the political register of the rest.

I look forward to speaking with you in person. It has been some time.

Nothing more than that. Nothing anyone who handled the letter in transit could read as more than simple courtesy. But enough that he would know I recognized him. And if it wasn’t the Romeo I knew, it could easily be dismissed as a mix-up.

I folded it and sealed it with the Alistar mark, which, even after half a year, I was still getting used to using as my own, and set it aside for Aldren or Dralos to dispatch.

Then I sat back in the plush chair and watched the morning light move through the stained glass of the warrior queen for a while.

The Pandorian Empire was squeezing Velora’s trade routes and demanding political recognition. It had almost certainly arranged for a Catalyst of Destruction to be used in Sealrite. I had little doubt that the Empire had also corrupted the Pope of the Holy Kingdom all those decades ago. And if all of that was true, I suspected it was behind the threads of control embedded in the minds of soldiers who were now, technically, mine.

Then my thoughts shifted to Andromeda, the brainwashed girl being used as my replacement at Niro’s side. The girl who had walked into my dreamscape and promised to be my death.

Poor, foolish thing.

Then I thought about the route from Elyndor to Velora, and from Velora onward to the Holy Kingdom, about how efficiently it could be traveled if the planning was done well, what I would need to bring, and who, and what it would cost to leave the duchy running in my absence.

By the time Dralos knocked on the study door an hour later to tell me the conditions had been drafted and the Cardinals had read them without visible distress, I had a reasonable outline of a plan and a considerably clearer sense of what the next several months would look like.

None of it was going to be simple. Then again, what part of my life ever was?

“Any issues?” I asked.

“None voiced aloud. They accepted every condition,” Dralos said from the doorway, a long nail scratching at one of his draconic face scales with the sound of steel on steel. “With one addition of their own. They would like a third member of their church to travel with us. A junior Cardinal, they say, for logistical support.”

“What are the chances they’re truly a junior Cardinal?”

“Low, my lady,” Dralos answered with a small sneer.

“Tell them the addition is acceptable, provided I receive the junior Cardinal’s name and a full accounting of their role before we depart. Let’s see what tale they weave to cover their backup plan, whatever it might be.” I picked up my reply to Romeo and held it out. “And have this dispatched today. Best to expedite it.”

Dralos took it and glanced at the name on the front, but had the apparent good sense not to ask. That, or the name had told him enough already. “Departure timeline?”

“Three weeks,” I replied, leaning back in the chair and grabbing one of the fae pastries I had delivered each morning. “We go to Velora first, then the Holy Kingdom.”

He nodded once and left, pulling the door shut behind him with a soft click. My core hummed after he’d gone, eager for the road and an end to the monotony.

I turned back to the window.

Three weeks to set the duchy in order, brief my generals, and prepare for a journey that would end in a room full of people who either wanted to use me or wanted to stop me, possibly both at once. It was not the worst position I had ever been in.

I had, after all, started in chains.
