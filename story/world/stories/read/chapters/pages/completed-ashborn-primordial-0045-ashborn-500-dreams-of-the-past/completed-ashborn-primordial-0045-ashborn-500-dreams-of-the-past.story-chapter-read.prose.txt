In the end, Vir was forced to endure Vayren’s company for hours—a miserable experience he swore never to repeat. The boy seemed entirely oblivious to the feelings of others, speaking at length of the grandeur of his own home.

Not even when Daya pointed out how said home was little more than rubble, having been reclaimed by the Ash, or mentioning how he was just as much a freeloader as everyone he ridiculed deflated his ego. Vir was convinced Ashani could reveal her identity to him and he’d still think himself superior.

The boy was young, however, and clearly sheltered. Vir could hardly believe his naivete had lasted as long as it had.

The experience was only slightly offset by the much more pleasant Daya, and when Vir finally returned to a waiting Maiya leaning against their appropriated shack, it was like taking a breath of fresh air after being drowned.

“You look like you swallowed a salmon,” Maiya said with a smirk.

“And you look drunk,” Vir replied with a raised brow.

“Well, guilty as charged. But I did learn quite a lot.”

“Oh? Such as?”

“People are on the verge of panic, despite assurances by the council that all is safe. Everyone knows the end is near. There’s this tension in the air. People feel the gods left them when the Vimana disappeared.”

Vir raised an eyebrow. “So its disappearance was a recent occurrence, then?”

Maiya nodded. “Seems like it, but no one was in any mood to provide details. Guessing you didn’t fare much better on that front?”

“Afraid not,” Vir said bitterly.

He’d hardly been able to get a word in edgewise with Vayren present.

“Y’know what? I could use another drink, after all. Tavern?”

Vir glanced at Ashani. She’d be as safe there as anywhere, and in fact, the less time they spent at the shack, the less attention they’d draw to her.

With a heavy sigh, he nodded. “Sure.”

Maiya insisted on going to a tavern far away, forcing him to walk across half the city.

“Well then, let us go over what we know,” Vir said once they’d settled at a table in a corner and ordered a couple of mugs of mead.

“Well, I guess I’ll go first,” Vir said. “I met a couple of Sawai Academy hopefuls who mentioned several interesting things. Firstly, that the library we’d hoped to access is restricted to trusted friends of the Academy, and to students and staff themselves. I imagine we would not have run into this issue back in our universe, given our standing there.”

“But here, we’ll be hard pressed to gain access,” Maiya completed. “Well, not unless we’re students or staff, anyway. That’s annoying.”

“Right,” Vir said. “And speaking of, it seems that Princesses Ira, Mina, and Tia are all here in the city. All vying to join the Altani Academy.”

Maiya frowned. “Why would a bunch of princesses want to have anything to do with the Academy? I can’t believe this universe is so different from our own that princesses aren’t given the very best training already…”

“From what I gathered, the shift in the laws of magic is a relatively recent development as they gained more power over the universe. The Altani seem to have figured out the secret, but with the Ash closing in on everyone and everything, the other countries had their hands full just staying alive.”

“So, re-education?” Maiya asked, raising an eyebrow. “Glad to know we’re not the only ones struggling, then.”

Vir nodded. “Which has meant that the Academy’s gained a lot of sway over the Altani council recently. I suppose the Academy saw this as a political opportunity and hoarded the knowledge for benefits. Seems everyone’s trying to get into the good graces of some mysterious ‘St. Amara’. They heralded her as the savior of humanity, but I wasn’t able to get much more on her.”

“Some savior, considering what’s left. But this could be problematic,” Maiya said, tapping her chin. “If we run into Ira here, let alone Mina, gods forbid…” she took a long sip of her mead.

“They aren’t the same,” Vir said. “Same faces, different people. We saw that clearly in the prior universe.”

“Be that as it may,” Maiya replied, “it’s going to be more than a little awkward. I’d normally say we avoid the Academy like the plague, but I’m not sure if we have that luxury. We may very well need their teachings to learn how to use magic again.”

Vir grunted. “That aside, I’m guessing you had more luck asking the locals about the Vimana’s disappearance?”

“Yes and no,” Maiya said, recounting her little escapade with Rishab.

Vir burst out laughing when he learned what became of the poor Sawai. “You really are terrifying, you know?” he said, shaking his head.

Maiya smirked. “Would you rather I’d entertained him longer? Let him take me back to his place?”

She’d been hoping to provoke a reaction he knew, but the only reaction she got was a rolling of his eyes. “The moment he realized what a monster you truly are, he’d have run. You did him a service, ending it there.”

“Well that’s not very nice,” Maiya shot back, feigning offense. “Anyway, it seems the Academy is working on some grand plan to save everyone. And the Vimana? It just up and vanished one day. This wasn’t long ago, either, so it’s not like it simply wandered off. Unless they flew it into the Ash without anyone noticing.”

Vir frowned. “Ashani mentioned something similar. You’re saying they made it invisible? Like your armor?”

Maiya shrugged. “We’ve seen them do it on a small scale. I’m guessing their Vimana has something similar.”

“That is as impressive as it is terrifying,” Vir said. “To think there could be more, invisible Vimana out there floating around… but that still doesn’t answer where it went.”

“No idea,” Maiya said. “And Rishab doesn’t either. It’s a real mystery.”

“Which means we’ll need to infiltrate the Academy to get some real answers,” Vir said with a sigh. “Perhaps it’s fortunate the entrance exams are in a few days.”

Maiya’s eyes narrowed. “You mean to suggest that we apply? As students?”

“I know how ridiculous it sounds,” Vir acknowledged. “But I do think this will give us the best excuse for wandering their halls with impunity. At least until I manage to figure out how to use Dance of the Shadow Demon again. And they might very well teach me how.”

“I suppose stealing their uniforms won’t work?” Maiya said hopefully.

Vir shook his head. “No. Eventually someone would realize we aren’t official students. Best case, we’re asked to leave. Worst case… well, I don’t need to tell you.”

“I admit,” Maiya said, letting out a long, dreamy breath. “It’s always been a dream of mine to attend the Academy. And these new laws of the universe would mean we’d gain something tangible from all of this. But as we are now, how are we expected to even get in?”

“We still have a few days, and I fully intend to utilize it. I once learned how to manipulate prana when the world told me I was doomed to live without it. I can do it again. Especially with you by my side.”

“Well… the idea does excite me a little,” Maiya said, gazing off into the distance.

“But even if we do manage to regain some of our magic, you still can’t use affinity prana. Won’t that be a problem for you?”

“That is a problem,” Vir admitted, “but one I think I should be able to solve. At the very least, with how crippled everyone seems to be thanks to their influence, I’m guessing the bar will be somewhat lower than normal. Shouldn’t be too difficult to fool the proctors into thinking I have some talent.”

With his extensive forays into the nature of magic, it would be more ridiculous if he couldn’t solve that particular riddle. Ash Prana, or Origin Prana as the Imperium called it, was the source of Affinity prana, after all. More potent and pure, but closely related. Just that he’d had neither the time nor the reason to investigate the issue in the past years, given the more pressing issues on his plate.

“Well, you’re not wrong,” Maiya said with a nod. “At this point, the Altani need every hand they can get. I doubt they’ll be especially picky when the city has less than a year to live.”

“Did Rishab tell you that?” Vir asked.

Maiya nodded. “Apparently it’s not public knowledge yet. Officially, the Council claims their inevitable demise is ‘far off’. Unofficially… it’s pretty bleak.”

“And then there will be nothing left,” Vir said quietly. “Not one trace of sapient life left. Exactly as they want it.”

“We can’t change that, Vir,” Maiya replied. “Not when they can rewrite the laws of the universe. Not when we don’t have a means of stopping the Ash in our own universe. They hold the ultimate control here. This isn’t some nation or ruler that can be killed.”

Vir bit his lip. He knew she was right. But his spirit rebelled at the idea that nothing could be done.

“At any rate,” he said at last, “it seems we at least know what to do next. Figure out how to regain at least some of our magic. Take the entrance exam and join the Academy. Then we search every nook and cranny for clues on the Vimana’s whereabouts.”

Maiya grinned. “On second thoughts, I think I might actually enjoy this. I’d always dreamed of joining the Altani Academy. It’d become a rather pointless dream, but hey, our enemies seem to have given it a new lease on life. Help me regain even a fraction of my manipulation skills, and I fear the Academy won’t know what hit it.”
