As a Goddess, a glowing, golden goddess, the first thing Gwen attempted post-pacification of her new city was the establishment of a regional Regent’s office.

She failed, after discovering that, impossibly, Quetzalcoatl did not run her nation on… paperwork. It was run on vibes and faith; through terror and sacrifice. That was the true miracle of Neo Tenochtitlan.

That or Malakath had kept a tight rein on things; only now, every tether had been slackened. Either way, the stress drove her halo to erratically splutter, turning her office into a disco at dusk.

By now, she had accepted the fact that she could not turn off the glow—at least without some explicit level of instruction in Faith Magic. As a victim of apotheosis without consent, she was doing the best she could using her existing Divination skill sets to tune out the murmuring prayers, as well as repurposing Sympathetic Life-Link to redirect the latent energy associated with Faith into her World Tree.

She hadn’t slept for a week—which was just as well, because the work was unending. Without Data Slates, running a city by command via word of mouth really did rely on faith.

“Take a break?” Slylth, thankfully, had seen enough of Suilven’s administrative methodology to shoulder some of her burdens. Golos and Ariel, on the other hand—

“I am okay.” Her temple throbbed. She had gotten the fervency down to a hum, at least. It was at a volume she could work through, unlike the flood that kept her sleepless and debilitated for hours whenever she relaxed. “We got… more work.”

She did indeed have more work. The work was a necessary distraction.

At the converted “office” that had been the old temple’s exterior, her new citizens begged for an audience to make their petitions.

Gwen tried to think of them as government employees—they themselves saw each other as competitive sycophants.

Elder Coatls, capable of rudimentary speech, slithered low with their great jade heads bowed; Jaguar Chieftains with their spotted arms crossed over their chests came to requisition SPAM and other holy objects that they may praise her in the Thunderer's name. A Cōātlīcue at the front, begging a blessing of her own, carried a child too young to understand why the sky had changed gods twice in one week.

“Goddess.” The snake-woman’s Nahuatl came out fractured, grief-thick, pushing the flower-wreathed child until she landed at Gwen’s feet. “What should we do?” Watching this gargantuan half-serpent, half-lady creature with four arms and no bra, she desperately wanted to tell them that she had been a god for a matter of days and did not know what she was doing either.

The Cōātlīcue had not brought her child to be baptised.The child was an offering.

“Well, NO MORE SACRIFICES, for one,” the Pale Priestess’ command radiated across the heavens. She gave the child a drop of Golden Mead, then sent the pair home as her emissaries.

Unfortunately, old habits died hard. Her new administrators were the old priest class, who were sure they had to somehow satisfy their new God's whims.

To themselves, they were saving their people. To Gwen, these psychopaths were sacrificing animals and people in secret, as if she might pop up out of the gore and praise them for really reading her mind.

After a dozen such cases, she simply Soul-Tapped the old priestesses. She watched the Serpent women convulse like fresh eels thrown onto the riverbanks before finally rising with an understanding that superseded language and culture. No More Sacrifices.

With sacrifices halting all over the city, a small riot broke out—until Ariel pacified them.

Then another riot broke out—in the underhives when some sod couldn’t be convinced, so Golos showed her people a new tenet—if someone tries to sacrifice your kids, sacrifice them to Golos.

Whatever the case, the Pale Goddess could do no wrong. Whenever she consulted people, they asked her what she wanted, praised her for being humble, and begged her to do as she willed—for the correct thing to do was whatever her edict demanded.

This, Gwen thought, was probably how tyrants, despots, and Godkings came about.

By day four, she stopped asking and started informing— “Xlealy! Take excavation teams one, seven and nine and rebuild the well on the third terrace first. Corix, take the troops and secure the main granary. Ixicur, clear the rubble, leave the debris. Someone will take care of it later. The buildings themselves must be ready to receive resupplies.”

She switched tones between request, demand, and command, ultimately settling on the language of edicts, and edicts were a language these people had never stopped speaking, even after their old order died screaming in a temple. She then watched relief efforts move through the city like the weather, and hated how smoothly Faith turned the tide in the city's favour.

Street by street. Wells before granaries, granaries before temples, temples into administrative offices. That was the whole of her plan until the Dwarves arrived, until her Tower crossed many thousand miles of open water and sent down the bureaucrats that would bring order and ordinance to a city.

And she still needed to go to Tryfan. She still needed to sit across from Solana and ask what the hell Malakath meant by the family drama.

Until then, she was stuck here, at a desk, in front of a “Pilgrimage”, doing administrative goddess work, and trying to survive her own divinity.

Shalkar.The Bunker.

Natalia Volkova had seen men work. She had also seen, insofar as the metonym could carry the fact, how men worked, from behind their eyes. Moscow Tower might have a reputation for corruption and kleptomaniac bureaucrats, but during the Great War, its Logistics Corps could break down a Mage Flight in full battle gear and restage the same dozen men and women across the continent in nine hours flat.

Even so, having picked the brain of many of Moscow’s finest, she had never seen anything like her lover’s ability to manage inventory.

Presently, she was watching Richard and his team—about two dozen of them: Humans, Dwarves, Rat-kin, Mermen, a Centaur, and, more recently, a lizard-kin who answered to Dhànthárian—orbit the situation table, each holding Message Devices and dataslates.

Richard was somehow running three Messages at the same time, and holding two conversations.

“Tell Hanmoul I want the fabricator engines cold-staged at Deepholm’s western gate by morning. Crewed and ready to move. Levitators, not legs—there’s a lot of water in that lake city.”

“Strun’s Exterminators need Liquid HDM replenishments, healing potions and Golden Mead. Send double rations. Get them there before the Tower arrives.”

Shalkar’s Majordomo unwound the Divination Magic— —then slumped into his mesh-patterned chair.Natalia slid over the armrest, applying a mind-massaging technique that relaxed the brain’s overstressed chemicals, simultaneously inducing a state of quickened clarity.

He gave her hand a squeeze, simultaneously keeping an eye on the living sand that made up the logistical terminal at the base of Shalkar’s World Tree, deep inside the Geo Front.

Dwarven convoys, rerouted to Neo Tenochtitlan. A week's grain reserve, re-flagged as disaster relief before London's customs could tax it as export. Rat-kin labour battalions, pulled off three unfinished projects and started on a fourth. Two hundred thousand healing injectors, requisitioned from the Ordo Bath clinics before they could protest.And dozens of entries, both to and from.

Natalia had heard that the Regent was a logistics wunderkind.

It would seem her cousin was no less talented.

He caught her looking once all the aides were gone. “What?”

“Just… admiring,” Natalia replied in heavily accented English. She could speak perfectly if Richard desired, but he said that he preferred her natural accent. “Where I come from, that kind of efficiency will get you killed.”

With a tug, he pulled her off balance. Feigning resistance, she fell into his lap.

The ease of working in Shalkar made her uneasy. She had spent a decade being taught that competence looked like a knife. Watching him work, she found she had to revise everything Moscow had taught her. Competence, it turned out, was a man who never needed to gloat, or shout, or mock his inferiors. Competence was the man who told people what needed doing without threatening their families, or cutting their rations when they fell short. Richard had effectively ruled Shalkar for almost two years now, and the city turned with a smoothness that was, frankly, admirable.

The whole thing would have been worshipful, almost mythological, had Natalia not seen her golden mistress in the mindscape, haloed and resplendent, and understood the true meaning of "mythological".

If Gwen was a deity now—what did that make Richard? What did that make her?

“Dick?”

“Yes, Nat?”

“Are we priests now?”

Richard chortled, exhaling jet streams of air through his nostrils. “Do you want to be a priestess? I think… you would make a very cute one. Naughty, too, full of sin.”

Natalia had lost the ability to blush a long time ago. Emotions like embarrassment and shame had been beaten out of the Sparrows with both rods of steel and maces of the mind.She blushed now just so that Richard could see that she was still human, that Moscow hadn’t taken everything out of her.

“I am serious.”

“Well, if we are priests,” Richard smirked at her, his hand growing inappropriate. “Then let us at least get a religious tax exemption out of it.”

Natalia laughed, really laughed.

“That's settled then,” Richard’s hands were very naughty indeed. “What do our hawks say from the north?”

Natalia straightened herself and swatted away his fingers. “Moscow is mobilising again,” Natalia sighed. “Any part of Shalkar is better than no part of Shalkar, I guess.”

“Well then…” Richard rolled his eyes with exasperation. A secret war was still a war, and that meant more work. “I guess it’s time your Sparrowhawks got to work. Do they have enough Golden Mead? The distilled stuff, I mean.”

Natalia nodded. She could almost see it with her eyes. All those oligarchs in Moscow, salivating over a steady supply of the very thing Shalkar had starved her former nation from ever possessing.

She wondered, for a moment, what would happen if her agents paired the youth-inducing liquor with evangelical pamphlets, perhaps a golden can of SPAM with her holiness' likeness.

Richard was right. Natalia’s lips went to his nape.

She really did make a wicked priestess.

Arica.

For almost a week, Oliver Edwards dreamt of a golden Regent with a glowing halo, worn upon a full head of piled hair with ringlets so voluminous that each strand moved in slow motion, like in those American hair commercials.

The commercials lied.

In the witching hours, he had dreamt of walking beside the Regent, his own hair long and luscious, flowing with the mana droplets in the wind, keeping the chill on his skull at bay. It had been a strange dream because there were others there as well, with faces he recognised. The most prominent of his companions had been Lei-bup, who was more defined than the rest.

Then he remembered that the Sea Witches had burst into his office and told him that Lei-bup had gone to the Great Shoal in the Deep, and he had grown inexplicably sad.

He hadn't spent much time with the Mer, but what they had accomplished in Shalkar and in Arica was nothing short of generational.

When his sadness grew excessive, Ollie woke up in paradise, to the sound of bird songs, salty air, and baldness.

His bed smelled like kombu, though Ollie had long since gotten used to the scent.This was because the rumours were true. Arica’s Regent had, in his loneliness, taken up with the Mermaids, who were certainly no maids, as Ollie would know. Nothing would come of this interspecies sortie, of course, even if Ollie was lorded as one of the Pale Priestess’ most trusted followers.

At first, he was worried about what his middle-class mage parents in London might think. But the mermaids never complained or cared about him having hair, because male Mer didn’t have growths that resembled hair, and being the Regent of Arica, he was very popular with the aesthetically pleasing Mermaid Mer.

He ate his breakfast at work, fed by the local girls, Mer, of course, with their dainty little fingers, feeding him spoonfuls of caviar with ponzu sauce on sticky rice—a delicacy that originated from the food stalls surrounding the Water Gate below Shalkar.

He was a dozen data slates deep when his office’s long-range Message device fired up, and Richard’s voice cut through the static, dry as ever, wanting confirmation that the Fifth Vel grain shipments had cleared his harbour.

They had cleared, of course. Ollie had personally made sure of it.

65,000 tonnes of it, re-flagged from Nagalese to Peruvian, to evade the US naval forces on the lookout for suspicious shipments to Central America. Yes, the Dyar Morkk network was reliable and safer—but really, there was no challenging the sheer bulk that was ocean freight.

Natalia’s projection came through a moment later, wiggling her perfect brows when she saw Ollie being pampered by a pair of mermaids. She told him to ready the harbour to receive Shalkar Tower. The total fuel expenditure for Pacific transit would require about half of Arica’s reserves, as well as food, water, and material replenishments. She also told him that the Tower had received a warning from the FedEx Tower in Hawaii, and that if Ollie felt like it, he could pass the message on to Lei-bup’s people.

At noon, while Ollie enjoyed a coconut, the LR Message Device flickered into life once more.

Lord Ravenport, the Duke of Norfolk, regarded him in his colourful, locally made shirt, his tropical fruit platter, hand-picked to entice healthy hair and nail growth, and the Mer-girls behind him, and grew concerned.

“You’ve… gone native?” The Minister of Foreign Affairs quickly collected himself. “Not… that it is any of my business.”

When Ollie tried to explain that it was hot and that his office was less humid without cooling Glyphs, or that men from Cuzco had unbelievably lush hair, Ravenport told him he didn’t care.

“Just… ignore any orders that come from anyone but the Ministry, or your Regent, for now,” the Duke pinched his brows. “We’re putting out fires here. The Ordos are in an uproar. Washington thinks that she’s trying to take the central continent for herself. Someone’s bound to make a move on you there, since you’re her largest deep-water port.”

“I’ll keep that in mind, milord.” Ollie raised a coconut, complete with a pair of paper umbrellas.

The Duke looked like he had something to say, but ultimately decided against it. The Message ended without further requests or questions.

Ollie stepped from his chair, into the wide open deck of his hilltop office, then lifted into the air over Arica.

Blessed Arica, white and bean-green, draped across the headland like a colourful towel over an ivory beach. To the north, past the breakwater, the deep-water port did its unglamorous work: cranes, gantries, the honest business of unloading Dwarven steel from Deepholm and Creature Cores from the Fifth Vel. South of the harbour wall, the town changed, transforming into white terraces cut into green hillside, a boardwalk strung with handcrafted lanterns, a resort built for guests who mostly hadn't arrived yet. Word, or so Richard said, was still spreading. Once the conflict died down and Shalkar’s Golden Mead could be spared, the place would be packed to the hilt.

About two nautical miles out from Arica’s waterline lay their new “lagoon.” Aristotle had journeyed north, and in its absence it had left the largest shedding on record: acres of living chitin and cast-off coral sloughing free of that vast hide, settling into the seabed, and blooming. What had emerged from the wreckage of a land god's old skin was, absurdly, a lagoon within the bay itself, a bioluminescent, jewel-toned underwater paradise more beautiful than anything his eight years of working with the Regent around the world had ever produced.

Even now, Mermaids chased each other through the shallows in a game with rules only they understood, tails flashing turquoise and gold where the light struck the water. Along the long wooden wharfs, Mer fishermen hauled up nets heavy with the lagoon's absurd new bounty, calling prices to each other in the easy shorthand of Mer who no longer worried whether the catch would be enough. Kelp farmers worked the shoreline in patient rows, bundling wet green ropes of harvest for the drying racks, and somewhere further down, a Sea Witch was training a pair of acolytes in their Siren songs, watched by a ring of Incan men with split-grins.

Ollie Edwards, Regent of Arica, ran a hand through his absent hair, feeling the breeze run through his Arica shirt made from breathable local plant fibres.

Life was hard, being the Regent of Arica. I should get a hat, Ollie thought. One of those hats from that island off the US coast—toquilla straw, a structured crown, with clean, understated lines. He'd have the band done in Shalkar blue.

Shalkar’s Tower arrived at its first destination.

It did not sail into the bay so much as shunt into empty space, foregrounding its arrival with a Mandala large enough to blot out the sun.

For the Mer and the survivors of the Mer’s rescue, the horizon simply stopped being empty. One heartbeat, open water; the next, a small tsunami below and a whirlwind above.

The room, large as it was, filled with golden amber light.

Waves of warmth washed over the crew, diffusing the fatigue of a week’s travel across the Pacific.

Strun reached her first, falling on both knees to supplicate before his Pale Goddess. The Rat-kins fell on their armour as one, creating an enormous clamour. The Dwarves, after a moment of peer pressure-induced faith, lowered their heads.

Lulan was about to kow-tow when the Regent stepped from the dais and demanded that they stop at once.

“Enough!” She snapped, uplifting the Sword Mage and himself from the floor with her mind. “This isn’t… I can’t help this. I can’t turn it off.”

Turn the light off? Strun’s ears twitched. Why would anyone want to turn something so deserved and resplendent “off”? Bathed in the Regent’s light, he could sleep like a ratling cub.

“Your Paleness,” Strun bowed from the waist, whiskers twitching. “Twelve battalions are onboard, ready to make peace. The Foundries are lit, and the Fabricators are ready to be deployed.”

The Dwarven Engineers gave their murmuring affirmation.

“Good,” his Regent relaxed at the good news.

“Regent,” Hanmoul offered their Demi-Goddess a salute. "Aye, got freighters on standby. Fabricators are hot-staged, as Commander Strun said, an' we're ready tae start rebuildin'.” He handed her a slate without preamble. “Richard's telt us the Great Shoal's pacified the whole coastal strip. We can start work soon as the Rat-kin Legions land an' secure the sites.”

“Thank God,” his Regent nodded, already smiling, already digesting the message from Richard.

"Report says two hunnerd an' thirty thousand pacified an' fed, near enough fer resettlin' intae aboot four major industrial ports," Hanmoul said, with the particular pride of a man reading out a job well done. "Fifth Vel crew lost aboot half the toons; there's two ports remainin', but the inland population's largely left intact."

His Regent held the smile for exactly as long as it took her to do the arithmetic.

Oh no… Strun felt his ears twitch. He knew that look.

“Two hundred and thirty thousand pacified?” their Regent’s glow flickered a little before hitting the crew with its full blast, bathing them in onsen water. The air suddenly tasted delicious, like salt lick.

She looked up from the slate, and the harbour, and the giant gouge carved into the town below where Her Paleness’ Aristotle had made landfall, and did not look quite so happy anymore.

“Hanmoul.” Her voice came out very level, and he could smell perspiration, which was how Strun knew she was secreting unhappy chemicals. “What do you mean, pacify?”
