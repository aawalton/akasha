Six Thousand Years Ago

“Tallas, you’ve gone as far as you can,” the God of Thunder said sadly.

He was sad. Tallas had been someone he’d had high hopes for. He’d understood the Stormsteel Path in a way that only a handful out of a thousand had. He’d nurtured the man for five years. Helped him through every roadblock on his Path. Taught him through trial and error how to walk his Path even as the deviations piled up.

But in the end, Tallas wasn’t strong enough. He was lacking. Not in talent. Not in the strength of his core. But in determination to strive for power beyond his place. Why else would he have failed to add a Law to the Stormsteel Path? It wasn’t the deviations. Even with all of those, the swordsman in lightning armor had enough of the Stormsteel Path in his core to contribute a new Law, and more than enough skill to forge it.

But he’d chosen not to.

“There’s a time in every pupil’s journey where they can’t go on. It’s normal. Still, you were one of the most interesting ones I’d had in a long time.” He paused briefly and reformed in his human shape, spear resting on his shoulder as he squatted down to speak with his favorite disciple. “Now, the time has come to explain the way of the universe to you.

“Every being in the universe longs for one thing. They seek escape. Some of them seek to escape from their portal worlds, pour onto a planet’s surface, and kill. I was one of those. I still am. Others seek to escape some thought in their heads, or some experience they’ve had. You, Tallas, and the family that wouldn’t accept who you were? That was you. And still others seek simply to escape anonymity and obscurity. To become someone.”

Tallas nodded slowly. “And you? You said you were one of the monsters who wants only to kill and kill. What happened?”

“I found something I had to escape more than that voice. A pressure. You know I’m SS-Plus-Ranked? What that means is that there’s an ever-growing weight, pushing me to escape. The only way out is to gather the strength to tear the shroud between universes open and push myself through. It’s more power than any being in the world can contain.” The God of Thunder waved at the dozens of pillars, now dripping with Mana, that hovered around them as they talked.

“That power, or some of it, is held here. I long to force the path between universes open and escape, but I respect you—and each of my previous pupils—far too much to treat you like lambs to the slaughter. So, here are my terms.

“I will give you three days. You will prepare yourself. Then, I will hunt you. If you survive, you can live on. If not, your core will fuel my ascension. Either way, you’ll die with honor.”

“And if I refuse?”

“What?”

“And if I refuse to participate? If I sit here, in your world, for three days? I’m not deluded enough to think I can kill you, Rodenick. I’d rather spend the time learning.”

The God of Thunder blinked. Then he slowly grinned, shifting to his lightning dragon form as laughter pealed from his throat like thunder. “If that’s what you wish, then so be it. You truly are a most interesting pupil, Tallas!”

Stormlord Nalthelas wrenched the gap between realms open, like a pupil dilating in the air. It slowly focused its gaze on the elf, and as it did, his cloak blew away, letting me see him properly for the first time.

He stood tall and thin, a dozen or so tools hanging from the bandoliers across his bare chest. Each of them looked worn and well-used, and they clattered slightly in the uprushing wind pouring through the eye between universes. Armor covered his shoulders and legs—a coppery-looking metal mixed with portal metal into a gray-bronze that gleamed dully in the flickering light. And the spear he carried…

I’d thought it was a staff at first. But no. No, it was definitely a spear. And not only that, but it was a familiar one. The God of Thunder had wielded it in battle when we’d hunted wolves. He used it in his man-shaped form.

“By my power, and the power I’ve acquired, I demand entry!” the Stormlord shouted. His voice, which had sounded weak and whispered before, boomed with power. His aura pulsed out, and as it did, I was driven a full step back.

And all around us, the trapped and caged Paragons howled. Cores shattered, their breaking accompanied by wails of pain or sudden silence as the monsters died, one by one, to fuel the attempt at ascension.

“What do we do?” Sarah asked.

I blinked. Refocused. Yes, it was horrifying. Yes, the Stormlord was incredibly powerful. But we had a mission here. “We do nothing.”

“Nothing?” Terrel Young asked incredulously. His face was twisted in anger and rage. I understood those emotions. “You expect us to do nothing?”

“Yes,” Ellen said before I could say anything. “This happened thousands of years ago. We’re trying to learn how to stop our monster, not stop this one.”

“Right…” The Portal Tyrant went silent.

“We spread out. See if you can figure out what the Stormlord’s device is doing, and how to interfere with it.” I followed my own orders and started poking around a cage that had contained a green-black lightning winged serpent. The monster itself had core-broken, and it lay on the ceramic floor, dying. I ignored it.

I was more interested in the dozen or so bronze needles hovering around it. One by one, with almost mechanical precision, they plunged into the broken core. It reminded me of my own core reforging, but in this case, I doubted that Stormlord Nalthelas cared about precision or accuracy. The needles punched into the dying serpent’s body, and a moment after the twelfth hit, they all shimmered. The monster’s core came loose like a ghost sliding out of a body. Electricity crackled across it.

Then it shattered completely.

A wave of Mana exploded outward. It surged into the connected conduit, then flooded, not to the Stormlord, but to the massive batteries he’d drained in the opening seconds of his ritual.

Dozens of high-ranking monsters died in the next minute, their cores squeezed for Mana like so many oranges. Every one of them fed the batteries, and every battery fed the Stormlord.

The iris overhead opened slowly. Wind ripped through it as pressure from two universes attempted to equalize. The Stormlord began to rise from the floor, cloak continue to flap around him.

Then something went wrong.

It happened suddenly. One moment, the device was humming along, murdering Paragons at an insane rate. A flash of white moved across the room as I blinked. The next, the battery-and-conduit array hissed as the eye closed overhead, and thousands of cores worth of Mana backed up into it. The process itself was reversing right before our eyes.

And Stormlord Nalthelas looked terrified.

“This is my right! You cannot take it from me!” he screamed, his voice cracking. The tools strapped to his chest came out. The cage around him opened. He rushed out, desperately working to keep the overloading device from falling apart further. Mana and lightning coursed through him as he worked, and the spiderweb of burns that criss-crossed his body glowed red-orange from the sheer energy. His eyes burned, too—but not with power. With rage, and with a desperate focus.

At the same time, the Thunder King changed.

The pillar of lightning was suddenly wrapped in a solid coat of liquid metal that burned and melted and reformed all at once. His cage disintegrated as he surged outward. For a few seconds, he stared at the Stormlord, and I thought he’d attack. Then he turned and vanished. One moment, he was there, the next? Gone.

I caught his name as he went, though.

The Thunder King: SS-Rank

He’d done it.

He’d pushed to SS-Rank in the chaos, and that had given him the strength to fight his way free. None of his fellow Paragons had, though; dozens were still dying, while the others desperately attempted to escape—or in a few cases, quietly waited for their master to harvest their cores.

I closed my eyes and thought.

And it hit me.

The God of Thunder had batteries much like the ones Stormlord Nalthelas had built. The pillars. They were his storage. But I’d spent time in his world, and I’d never seen so much as a hint of the madcap contraption there. The only thing even close to similar was the device he’d used to help me reforge my core, but it lacked the storage—or the features that would imprison a Paragon.

The God of Thunder…Eugene…could easily have replicated this. It clearly could work. But instead of building a device that could harvest cores from dozens of Paragons at once to help fuel his push, he’d opted for something different.

Why?

As much as I thought about it, there were only two answers, and only one made sense. Either he enjoyed the hunt, or something about this moment had shaken him to the very core of his being.

I shook my head as the Stormlord’s contraption tried to tear itself apart. Eugene was definitely the kind of dragon to be swayed by interesting situations. I could see him choosing to hunt Paragons for that reason alone. But I also believed I understood the pressure he was facing. Sooner or later, over the course of thousands of years and hundreds of pupils, he’d lose patience, or the pressure would become too much.

And if that was the answer, he wouldn’t have preserved this memory.

He’d preserved this memory and built those massive batteries in his portal world for a reason. This was a betrayal. He’d believed in the Stormlord, believed that he’d ascend alongside the SS-Plus-Ranked elf, and his faith had been rewarded with…this.

Eugene couldn’t escape the pressure on his core. He was too strong for this universe, and he had to escape.

But at the same time, he couldn’t repeat his mentor’s mistakes.

So he’d compromised. The three days. The hunt. The massive floating Mana receptacles in his portal world. It was all there to assuage his guilt over what he had to do.

I opened my eyes. The room pulsed with electricity, each wave getting closer to us. The others stood nearby, ready to fight but unsure what they could even do.

Ellen reached out hesitantly. “Did you…?”

“Figure it out? I think so. Let’s get out of here.”

Getting out of here wasn’t as easy as just leaving, though.

As we rushed down the stairs, a swarm of red-orange-robed cultists charged up at us. Their monstrous faces were distorted with rage and fear, and they shouted at us as they swarmed.

I started casting.

But Terrel got there first. He’d sheathed his Mana-linked axes and pulled out his massive warhammer; it thudded down on the first cultist like the fist of an angry god. Shockwaves rippled outward, forcing the A-Rank monsters off their feet and off-balance. Ellen filled in the space with a gigantic Abyssal Storm that rippled green-black electricity through the horde.

And my unempowered Hand of the Thunder God churned out a thin beam of concentrated, white-blue lightning that sliced across everything still left standing.

The door was just ahead. Cultists kept swarming up the stairs from below—I had no idea where they’d been, but there had to be hundreds of them. Every one of them was A-Rank or below.

And every one of them died in seconds as Ellen’s skin hardened and went purple-black. Our Shadowstorm Battery connection pulled on my core like an insistent hand in mine, and I let my Mana flow into her. The layered Shadow Cells that slammed down on the stairwell were both terrifying and beautiful, and I found myself grinning stupidly as my partner massacred monsters by the handful.

As she fought and I mopped up the survivors with the Storm’s Blade, Terrel Young got to work. The massive doors had been built to be imposing, and they held up well against his assault. At least, they did at first. But we’d fought through worse than this in the Workshop below Carlsbad, and he’d learned a thing or two. His hammer sent shockwaves up through the massive, decorated door, and every hit shook it on its hinges more than the last.

It buckled after maybe thirty seconds, and we surged out.

The two Stormlord’s Sentinels were waiting. Lightning crackled down on us as they opened fire. A split-second later, four winged serpents were in the air. Cheddar and Pepperoni opened up with their own energy attacks; a beam of sunlight ripped across one of the monsters, and a crackling burst of green-black lightning burned scorch marks in the other’s chest.

Then they met. Clawed wings and jaws ripped into long, snake-like bodies as they fought overhead. I shifted stances, multiple Lightning Charges crackling around the Storm’s Blade’s tip. Cyclone Forms. I free-cast a single, unempowered Hand of the Thunder God back into the oncoming wall of cultists, cutting into them with concentrated white lightning. Then I used Windfall.

The snares snapped shut around all four winged serpents as a second Hand of the Thunder God ripped across them. Ellen and I reacted instantly. Cheddar and Pepperoni vanished into their dimensional spaces before the beam had even finished firing.

The two Sentinels had nowhere to go, and the Windfall snare gripped each of them and stopped them dead mid-air. They started plummeting toward the forests far below.

As they did, I opened up with a full combo. Rolling Thunder. Brendan’s Storm Sigil and Ariette’s Thunderhead—copying the Thunderhead over and over. The Hand of the Thunder God hit, and when it did, it was truly massive.

And I followed it up with Stormbreak.

Exclusions opened around each of my allies—with the winged serpent familiars out of the way, they weren’t in any danger. Lightning flowers blossomed around each of the two wounded and snared Sentinels, and around the cultists still pouring from the Stormlord’s combination laboratory and slaughterhouse.

Lightning bloomed.

It flowered.

Pulses of electricity ripped across the entire portal world. Monsters died. Others survived. But none of them came away unscathed as seven massive pulses of energy rippled through the God of Thunder’s strange Ghostdream.

In the silence that followed, Ellen pointed across the thin bridge to the small building we’d arrived in. “Let’s get moving, Kade. We’re almost there.”

She was right. This portal wasn’t a threat. We didn’t have to clear it. And I’d learned everything I needed to know.
