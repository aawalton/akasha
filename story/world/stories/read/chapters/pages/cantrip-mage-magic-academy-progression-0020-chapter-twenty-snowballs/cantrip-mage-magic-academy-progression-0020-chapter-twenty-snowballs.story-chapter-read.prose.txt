In the week that followed, Eola didn’t attend Mana Studies—or the extra lesson with Madame Reyanna—once.

There was no point. The practical exam almost certainly had everything to do with Mana lines and attuned Mana. Eola didn’t believe in the first as anything but a learning lie for children, and while she had attuned Mana—sort of—it didn’t work like that in practice. It wouldn’t register for anything Madame Reyanna wanted her to do. She did ask Instructor Tarik for help in avoiding the extra lesson, though. “I’ll see what I can do,” he’d responded, but after that, she hadn’t heard a word from him about it.

So, if she couldn’t confirm it was safe to go to class, she simply wouldn’t. It’d be more helpful for her to focus on what she could control.

That meant the written exams in Introduction to Monsters and—to her annoyance—Ideograms. Instructor Tarik insisted that she had to take the test even though she wasn’t enrolled in the class. Even if she was grading second-year students’ work. Even if she was better at spotting errors in runes than the professor himself.

The most frustrating part was that, in her mind, it didn’t matter if she passed or not. She’d already done the agreed-on work to get through the independent study. This was strictly because next year’s instructors would want to know her scores. It was a waste of her time, just like Mana Studies’ practical.

Still, the Intro to Monsters exam would probably cover at least a handful of third-floor denizens, and Eola didn’t need any more motivation than that to study hard. And Ideograms was definitely something she could pass—probably with flying colors.

And then there was Magical Dueling and the Taron-Li.

Instead of visiting the library, she spent her time working through the sword dance’s traditional footwork and guards, making sure she not only understood them, but felt them the way Maestro Yarrowbloom believed duelists should. She refined her spell list down to a handful of quick-casting cantrips with some of the traditional modifying marks, the better to dance with. If the professor wanted a fundamentally sound dance, she’d provide it.

She didn’t dance alone, either. Colin joined her, desperation fueling his every textbook-perfect thrust and too-slow shield-summoning. He had the determination. The will. And, without spellcasting, he was actually an acceptable swordsman. Formal, stiff, and lifeless, but acceptable. But the moment he added a single spell, everything slowed down and perfection—no, order—took over.

Eola tried.

She really did.

In fact, they had one day before the midterms, and she was still trying.

“Why are you bothering with me?” Colin asked as she picked him up off the sparring floor for the dozenth time. “I’m hopeless.”

“You’re not hopeless,” Eola lied. “Again.”

But Colin didn’t even raise his sword—not even to block the lunge that bounced off his padded chest. He stared at Patrice, who’d joined them for practice. “Even Patrice is better than me.”

“I am not,” Patrice said from where she lay on the floor, eyes closed. “The smallsword is not my weapon. Too light and floaty.”

“No, you are,” Eola said. It was true, though. Patrice was like a bull in a china shop, flailing around with the blade like it was a mace or something. In a way, it was terrifying.

“Not in the fundamentals, I mean. I’m better in theory. But I can’t move fast enough for it to matter. You’re unpredictable, though. You might earn a few points for beating someone on sheer chaos.” Colin walked over to the sword rack and hung up his practice sword.

“Rude!” Patrice sat up and shot him a look.

Eola shrugged off her padded armor. “Rude, yes, but true. You have no idea what you’re doing. That makes you dangerous—to yourself and everyone around you. On the other hand, Colin’s predictable, and that makes him less of a threat than his familiar.”

“Also rude,” Colin said quietly.

“Worse, he knows it. He’s not even defending himself.”

Patrice stayed on the floor for a minute, then bounced to her feet. The tall girl moved shockingly quickly when she wasn’t half-asleep. “That was rude, too. My father would be disappointed in the friends I’ve made. Come on, you two. At this point, an extra few minutes of studying won’t decide whether we pass or fail. Let’s go do something outside while the sun’s out.”

Eola followed Patrice out into the academy’s grounds, past a hedge that looked more like a vertical mound of snow than a plant and into the school’s maze.

Every magic academy in Alemia had a maze, but unlike the dungeons they were built around, on top of, or in this case, inside, mazes didn’t serve any purpose Eola could see. It was near the greenhouse Instructor Clearance and a few other professors taught in, and she could see a corner of it from her window, but she’d never seen or heard of a class using it for anything. Every single academy had one. It was important. But no one knew why, and it was pretty much useless.

She was lost almost instantly, of course, but Patrice led the three of them through it like she’d been in it a million times.

“I’ve been in here a million times,” she said. “There’s a sort of hut in the middle that’s a great napping spot. Someone keeps it ensorcelled, and it’s always just warm enough to fall asleep in.”

“Are we going there?” Colin asked.

“Nope. We’re looking for a dead-end. Somewhere no one’s walked yet.”

Eola looked around. Everywhere she could see, the snow was untrampled and fresh. And bright; the sun was out, and it was shockingly comfortable this afternoon.

“There!” Patrice said. She burst into a sprint, kicking up snow behind her, then seemed to trip, jump, and spin all at once. When the snow settled, she lay in the snow, arms and legs flailing back and forth.

“What are you doing?” Eola asked.

“Making snow-seraphs. All the kids at Clerk Home made them when I was younger.” Patrice squirmed around a few more times, then stood up carefully. Where she’d be lying, an impression had been pushed into the snow.

Eola stared at it for a moment, the wing shapes and conical body reminding her of the monster she’d encountered on the third floor. But before she could ask about it, Colin spoke up. “Where’s Clerk Home?”

“Oh, it’s at the center of Foglight, almost as far up to the north as you can get.” Patrice lay back down in the snow, but this time, she didn’t move. Instead, she stared up at the deep blue sky overhead. “You, peasants, are standing in the presence of a noble lady. Bow at once.”

Eola rolled her eyes, and after a moment, so did Patrice. When she continued, she sounded a lot less pompous. “Yeah, I’m a noble brat. In fact, I know Catrine pretty well. Not by choice, though. I spent three years with House Andrese. House Clerk’s a tribute family to House Andrese—they pretty much own us, and since we run the city of Foglight, that means Catrine’s parents do, too.”

“You’ve known her a long time, then? Has she always been…” Eola trailed off.

“So noble?”

“Yes.”

“Always. It’s getting worse, though. She’s the youngest daughter, and she’s also betrothed to my brother. They’ll marry when she graduates, so she’ll be my sister.” Patrice rolled to her feet. “It’s a good move for both houses, but they’re not a good match. Latrine Catrine will eat him alive.”

“Latrine Catrine?” Eola asked incredulously. “How’d she get that name?”

“Can’t say, won’t say,” Patrice said, smiling devilishly. “Swore an oath not to.”

Colin had gone mysteriously silent behind them. “Hey!” he called, and as Eola turned, a ball of icy white snow caught her in the face. She spat snow out of her mouth as Colin stared at her and tried not to laugh.

Then Patrice was back down in the snow, gloved hands packing a snowball of her own. For a few moments, their corner of the maze was a war zone, and Eola’s cloak was covered in clumps of snow by the time she fell, laughing, into a drift. It was such a waste of time. She had magic to be learning, the rest of Varin’s diary to assemble, and her parents needed her. She was eighteen, Y’aer curse it. It was time for her to act like an adult.

But at the same time, her first three or four weeks at Varin’s Academy had been lonely, and this…wasn’t. She needed this.

She was about to say something sappy, but a snowball caught her in the chest instead. She glared at Patrice and Colin, but neither of them looked guilty. Then one hit Colin’s arm, and the three of them started packing their own snowballs to return fire as another group of first-years launched their own attack.

As the snow flew and the maze went from pristine to battle-ravaged, Eola had a brilliant idea. She stopped packing snow and, instead, drew a familiar symbol in the air. Two offset circles hung there for a moment, then filled with Mana and activated. A shimmering barrier appeared mid-air, and she ducked behind the Safe Shield.

“Hey, no magic!” someone called.

She stuck her tongue out and started packing another snowball.

The snowball fight ended as snowball fights do—with a few dozen freezing students stumbling into the dormitories, cloaks soaked through. Behind them, the maze was covered in defensive spells and hand-packed forts. Close to a hundred Safe Shields and other barriers glowed in the air, and one second-year student with an ice attunement had raised a miniature castle and rained down snowballs from his vantage point.

Eola, Patrice, and Colin had lost.

Obviously.

But that was fine. Exams were tomorrow, and not one of the first-years had thought about them for almost twenty glorious minutes. As she slipped into her room, Eola shed the sodden cloak and hung it on a hook, then cast a bit of Child’s Magic. It didn’t dry it, exactly, but it did wring most of the water out onto the floor. Then she toweled up the mess and sat down.

Patrice had told her a little more about noble parents as they’d fought, and the exchanging of children between tribute families and the Governing Houses. That horrified Eola; there was no way her parents would have abandoned her if they’d had any other choice. But it did put the nobility into a little more context.

And the snowball fight had been fun. No, more than fun. She’d needed that.

“Your friend didn’t cast a single spell,” Atta said.

Eola met the cat’s eyes. She’d hopped onto the desk and fallen asleep on the half-deciphered ritual Colin had used to heal his bruises. “Colin? I saw him casting. It was as slow as—“

“No, the other one. The tall girl.”

“She didn’t?” Eola closed her eyes and leaned back in her chair. Hadn’t she seen Patrice sketching a symbol? She couldn’t be sure; in the chaos of the battle, she’d lost track of both of her friends more than once. But it was possible that she hadn’t. “Now that you mention it, I’m not sure I’ve ever seen Patrice cast a spell.”

Atta stood and arched her back, then did a half-turn and lay back down. “She had to have successfully cast Call and Calm, though. Otherwise, how could she not have gotten kicked out by now? So she’s not as hopeless with Ordered Magic as you are.”

“Thanks for that.” Eola slid the parchment with Colin’s ritual out from under the cat and got to work on a sheet of tracing paper. It had two of the modifying marks sketched out already, but the third and fourth were being elusive. She let herself drift into an almost trance-like state and focus on trying to decipher the Forsooth Script and Old Alemic the ritual had used.

She’d been working for Y’aer knew how long when a claw poked into her arm. Atta looked at her, then looked up.

So did Eola. “Hi, Colin.”

“That’s my ritual, isn’t it?” he asked. “How’d you pick it up so fast?”

“I can’t cast it.”

“That isn’t what I asked,” Colin said. He ran his finger across the tracing paper. “You memorized it close enough that, if you cast this, it might actually work. Or it might explode in your face. These symbols are wrong here and here.”

Eola stared at the spots he’d pointed out. “Can you show me the ritual again?”

“No. It’s Mana-intensive, so unless you’re really hurt, I’d rather not. I can draw it for you, though.” He took a piece of chalk from Eola’s desk, picked up her blackboard, and got to work.

A minute later, the rune was done. What she’d been seeing as two clumps of tangled, nonsensical bits of Old Alemic stood out as clear as day. They were modifying marks in Forsooth Script. Two of them—plus a third she’d never seen before. It looked a little like a miniaturized version of the rune as a whole, with four smaller circles arranged around a larger one. She traced them onto her tracing paper.

“Why’d you take my ritual, though?” Colin asked. “I’d gladly have given it to you if you’d asked. It’s not like it’s a secret.”

Eola shrugged. “I wanted to see what I could do with it, and I’d been staring at something similar for a week while I recovered. Ritual magic’s slow enough that I could probably memorize them in a single cast if I practice a bit, and those two modifying marks stood out even while you were casting it. I didn’t mean to steal it, I was just testing myself.”

“Well, I’m impressed.” Colin handed the blackboard back to Eola. “But next time, tell me you’ve done it so I can check your work, okay? Spell backlash gets pretty bad with rituals, and I don’t want you blowing yourself up or something. Actually, that’s one of the reasons I like rituals. They’re lower-risk for me, since I have to be so precise.”

Eola nodded. “Sure, I’ll let you know next time.”

“It is interesting that you picked up the rune that fast, though. You should talk to Instructor Tarik about it. He might have some ideas for how to stretch that skill more. I’m going to hole up for the night. It was a fun day, though. One day of exams, and then we’re free for Midwinter. Good luck!”

“Good luck with your exams tomorrow,” Eola said absently. She was focused completely on sketching a copy of the mysterious, new modifying mark into her spellbook and making plans to broach the subject with Instructor Tarik. Colin was right—the professor would be more help than anyone else in getting better at identifying spells while they were being cast. Maybe Instructor Clearance, too, if she could get the hamadryad to cast rituals for her to examine.

As the afternoon turned to night, her four tests tomorrow were the furthest things from her mind.

~Patrice Clerk~

Patrice Clerk stared at the wall of her windowless room, eyes narrowed to slits to keep from crying.

The whole time she’d balled snow with her gloved hands and threw it at her friends, she felt that prickling sensation on the back of her neck—the one she felt when she was being stupid.

She’d felt it a lot over the last few months. She was wasting her friends’ time, and she couldn’t stop herself from doing it. A snowball fight? On the day before mid-term exams? Seriously? What in Cursed Y’aer’s name had she been thinking?

Honestly, Varin’s Academy was too much for her. She’d known it would be, too. Her attunement was beyond worthless here. Eola and Colin had magic. But Patrice? She had nothing.

She should have been studying—or at least not distracting her friends from their own studies. If she passed the theory exams well enough, she might buy herself a few more months at Varin’s. Surely, that’d be worth dropping the facade for a few hours to brush up on her ideograms.

Nah.

It was almost too much work just staying here. If it weren’t for her parents—and the stupid boy they’d betrothed her to—she’d quit now. It wouldn’t be so bad, being a noble who couldn’t do magic. Just walk away from this, hire some maids who knew all the spells she needed, and make a life with that idiot from House Urlach. House Clerk needed all the allies it could get, and it’d go a long way toward making up with her parents after their…spat. It might even be the right thing to do for Colin and Eola.

And it’d be the ultimate ‘bad girl’ move. She was tired of being the ‘stupid girl’ here at Varin’s. If she walked away right now, it’d send a message. A strong one. Patrice Clerk didn’t have to play by her parents’ rules—she’d already proved that—and if she left Varin’s Academy, she’d prove she didn’t have to follow magic’s rules, either.

But then again…

If she could just get access to the cells, she’d be able to cast. She didn’t have that at home, and magic was intoxicating. Almost as good as a bottle of port. Not quite, but almost. She took a big, shoulder-shaking breath, then pulled her robe off, changed into a sleeping shift, and climbed into her bed. Midterms were tomorrow, after all, and as long as she was here, she might as well put in some minimal effort so she could keep trying to figure out her insane attunement.

Besides, Eola and Colin were the first people she’d met that she’d actually liked, just because she liked them. No politics. No noble posturing. Just people. She didn’t want to lose that.

“I prooobably should have studied something, though,” she half-giggled into her pillow.

A moment later, the tears started in earnest.

And so did the prayers to Y’aer, the Saint of Gifts and Curses.
