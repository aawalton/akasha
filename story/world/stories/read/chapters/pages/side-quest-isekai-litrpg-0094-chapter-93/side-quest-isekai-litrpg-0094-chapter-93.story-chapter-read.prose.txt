In the library, Mishki held the device Senna created on an open palm. It looked like a compass with a glassy blue casing. Flick also sat on Mishki’s palm, poking the device with his clawed fingers.

Name: LoredexType: ToolLore: A custom compass that points toward books rather than north. Once attuned to an individual’s mana matrix, the loredex will respond when in the holder’s palm. If the holder focuses the needle on a specific subject, the dial will direct them to books of similar topics. This item bears an enchantment.

Currently, the floating dial twitched from one side to another, likely responding to so many tomes. That would change once Mishki focused the needle.

He brought it to the table, where he already had a book set out. It contained a story called The Battle of Sharraga, which Mishki recognized from his childhood. This was just one battle in the middle of the much larger Rift War, when incredibly powerful and intelligent monsters called titans plagued the land so badly that the gods returned to help mortals fight them.

Tons of heroes fought titans in the Rift War, many of whom ascended to godhood or as sages. While it didn’t surprise Mishki to find stories of the Rift War in the guild library, it was shocking to see the Battle of Sharraga in a non-goble colony library.

After all, the hero of this battle had been a goble.

Despite the story painting gobles in a good light, it was here, even if it differed a little from how Mishki had grown up learning it, which raised some questions. And hopefully, the loredex would help him find answers.

Holding the loredex in his palm, he began to re-read the story of the Battle of Sharraga for the fourth time.

Flick hopped onto the table beside him, tilting his head at the pages as if he was reading alongside Mishki.

The story began with a titan wreaking havoc on the people living near Mount Sharraga. From its high ground, it destroyed every contender who came to face it. The denizens of the Fold living nearby cast their search further and further until finally they contacted a distant goble colony. After all, gobles were still part of the greater society in those days.

Mishki smiled, and his mind wandered from the pages for a moment. He knew how the story would end. The colony would send a goble named Groshmelk, and he would succeed where so many others had failed.

Groshmelk was many gobles’ favorite war hero of legend, including Mishki’s.

Back then, gobles had appointed leaders, similar to warlocks of today, and Groshmelk was one of the most famous. He had earned the position because of his clever mind.

That was actually the reason Mishki himself had invested so heavily in Intelligence, despite his race being notorious for having some of the least efficient gains when investing in the stat. He always found it inspiring that the smartest recorded goble had written his name in history.

Unfortunately, with the story being so old, few details remained about how Groshmelk had defeated the titan. Even this version only mentioned that he showed incredible courage and overpowered the titan on his first try.

Most gobles told the story such that Groshmelk ripped the titan’s innards out from its rear end, and so they idolized Groshmelk’s strength. Even if that method were true, Mishki figured that for a small goble to manage that would have required a lot of precise planning. Groshmelk’s greatest strength, after all, had been his intelligence.

He shook himself from his nostalgia and returned to the pages. This was the part of the story that had given him something new to consider. Not the outcome of the battle, but the part about the people asking the gobles for help.

Apparently, Groshmelk wasn’t the goble leader yet.

When the troubled world sought the goble people, they requested their most clever citizen to aid them. And the gobles nominated Groshmelk.

Then the story continued just like Mishki had always heard it.

That tiny detail had stuck out to Mishi, though. The people living under the titan’s wrath could have gone to any race to find someone clever, but they had specifically sought the gobles.

If gobles historically were known for their low Intelligence, that made little sense. Unless, of course, that hadn’t always been the case.

What if Groshmelk wasn’t just an outlier in how clever he was? It sounded like the entire goble race were known for cleverness, and Groshmelk was the cleverest of all.

But no matter how many times he had re-read this version of the story or any of the others in this book, he couldn’t find any answers about how that might have changed.

He turned his attention from the book to the loredex resting in his palm.

The device’s blow casing glowed with a haunting light, and the needle suddenly jerked sharply until it remained fixed on a single location.

Mishki’s heart leaped with excitement. After days of fruitless searching, was he really about to unearth more goble history? Flick must have felt Mishki’s excitement because he chittered happily and hopped onto Mishki’s head and began tugging on Mishki’s ears.

Trembling, Mishki stood. As he walked to the main aisle, the needle slowly pivoted to remain fixed on whatever book it was leading him to. When he reached five aisles away, the slant of the needle finally showed he was in the proper row.

Unfortunately, it led him to a dead end where a bookshelf blocked his way forward.

Mishki and Flick backtracked through a few aisles, trying different offshoots, until they came to the back side of the dead end.

It continued like this, with Mishki having to wander through a maze. He wished the compass could show the path with the walls accounted for, not just the final location.

He knew he had to be getting closer. Then, he arrived at a place where the needle pointed directly in front of him. But it only pointed toward a gap between two bookshelves. He peeked through the gap and saw a bunch of old, dusty books. Surely it’s there.

After partially retracing his steps twice, he finally arrived in that aisle.

Now, though, the compass pointed behind him, once again pointing toward that gap.

Mishki frowned. That was impossible. He had just been there.

His eyes darted to the floor to see if maybe the book had just fallen into the gap. But the floor was empty.

Slowly, his eyes ascended higher until finally he reached the top of the shelves.

He smirked. Gotcha!

Like a bridge connecting the two bookshelves to another over the gap lay a book on its side.

He smiled at Flick, perched on his shoulder. “Can you do me a favor?”

Mishki idly rubbed the sleeping Flick’s chest as he read the second book. It had nothing to do with gobles at all, and Mishki would never have thought to check it out even if he had found it on his own.

Instead, it spoke about the gods. One god, specifically, who had been disowned by the others and had his name stricken from the records. Even in this tome, the god’s name was never mentioned for the shame and dishonor he had brought.

The events took place several centuries after the Rift War. Monster outbreaks still happened, but titans were no longer an actual concern, and so the gods had left the realm again. They left the sages in charge and only came back to check in on them once in a while.

One god, however, visited more often than the others, and he spent more time with the common people than he did instructing the sages.

At first, everybody respected the god for doing this because they valued him as a deity for the common person.

Before long, though, the sages noticed a pattern.

During his visits, the god often excused the sages from their protective duties whenever he was nearby. They, of course, obliged, and nothing bad ever happened to the people while they were under the god’s supervision. But often, within a year of his departure, wars and fighting broke out.

After several decades where this became a noticeable trend, the sages mentioned this to the other gods, who investigated.

The findings had shocked everyone from mortals to sages to gods.

After the unnamed god had ascended to godhood and experienced the influx of newly available divine skills at his disposal, he had become insatiable for more power. Knowing he was still young when compared to the other gods and that they might see through his power thirst, he instead turned to the denizens of the Fold. Under the guise of visiting them, he had experimented with tapping into their mana matrices for additional power.

The process imparted the faintest bit of his own mindset in the people under his alleged protection, and the divine tribunal judged that this was a side effect of the divinity meddling with the commoner’s mana matrices. His own thirst for power had rubbed off on them and grew like a parasitic mold until it influenced them enough to wage war amongst one another. Only when the sages intervened did peace return.

Since the power-hungry god had ascended to immortality, not even another god could sentence him to death. The next best punishment they could decide on was to split his soul into millions of fragments and bury them deep within an ancient site of power that had once birthed a titan.

This is the point where Mishki had two revelations. That site of power was none other than Mount Garraga. Because the mountain was to serve as the god’s chambered burial site, it earned the nickname of Barrow Mountain, a name Mishki recognized from other childhood stories, although he had never known it was the same as Mount Garraga.

But that paled compared to the second revelation.

The people were outraged when they discovered the unnamed god had manipulated them. While the impossibility of a death sentence disappointed them, they agreed to the gods’ solution of a fragmented existence far beneath the land, where the strength of the site of power would nullify his influence.

The people seemed to believe this specific site of power would add to the dishonor of the soon-to-be-fallen god, although the book didn’t say why. Also unexplained, the people assigned the gobles to dig the labyrinthine tomb.

From the tone of the book, this seemed to be a demand more than a request. And the gobles on the pages appeared both timid and eager to do this as a show of good faith.

Even though the book never mentioned the god’s name, Mishki believed it had to be Groshmelk. The story had taken place shortly after the Rift War. With how beloved Groshmelk had been during the war, he now wondered if Groshmelk had been so influential that he was one of the heroes who had risen to godhood. Even though Mishki had never heard such a story, if the gods had erased any stories of Groshmelk’s ascension from history, that could explain why.

Mishki didn’t think it was a coincidence that the very mountain Groshmelk had found victory upon was the same place to act as a shamed god’s burial site. And if the gobles felt guilty about a god raised from their own race, it made sense that they would try to atone for the sins of their predecessor. If that was true, it also seemed like the betrayal of a goble hero-turned-god could have been the beginning of lasting racial prejudice against the goble race.

The story didn’t end there, though.

When the great excavation of Barrow Mountain began, the gobles held regular status meetings with the surface to share their progress and announce how many of the secure burial sites had been dug. These meetings weren’t just for the people on the surface. It also served as a way for the thousands of gobles working on the giant project to coordinate with one another.

Mishki had to read that twice. Modern gobles didn’t rely on group meetings. Their hive mind allowed them to communicate with one another from afar. As he had learned, even the Boundary wasn’t enough to sever the communication ties, and so he doubted even the deepest depths of the Fold were a barrier for the hive mind.

Then again, none of these stories mentioned a single warlock. Therefore, gobles must not always have had the hive mind at their disposal, or warlocks.

In the story, the gobles’ meetings grew less frequent. The explanation given by the gobles was that, now that the gobles had reached such deep depths, extended spacing between meetings allowed them to stay underground longer and be more productive. Some of the surface dwellers doubted their truthfulness, but the gods confirmed that several of the burial sites had already been dug, and the gods themselves entombed several of the fragments of the shamed god.

As time went on, fewer gobles attended the meetings. The gobles who visited the surface assured people that, in the spirit of efficiency, they were holding conferences with one another at a halfway point beneath the surface to update one another, and then they only sent representatives to the surface.

Again, the gods confirmed the tomb progress was continuing as necessary, and the fallen god was nearly entirely put to rest.

When the gods confirmed they had entombed the last fragment of the divine traitor, the gobles of that era were never seen again. And the people seemed relieved by that fact.

Mishki had gained so much new information, he had to take a break from reading any more. He pulled a small blanket out for Flick and put it over his napping companion, then got up from his table and went on a short walk through the aisles.

As he walked past shelves and shelves of dusty books, he took comfort in the smell of old parchment and leather as he let his mind clear.

He wished he had someone nearby to whom he could talk.

Miriam, who also spent most of her free time in the library like Mishki, was nearby. As nice as she was, Mishki felt like it was a lot to dump goble history on her.

The next best thing would be to send messages through chat, but he knew Logan was getting ready to take on a quest, and Avalyn had her wings full with helping Razor pass the level 100 threshold.

Alden would be interested in all this, but he couldn’t chat with Alden. Mariv, though, might be available.

Mishki: Hi Mariv. Do you have a moment?

Mariv: Hello Mishki! To what do I owe the pleasure?

Mishki paused, unsure how to put words to his feelings.

Mishki: I’m in the guild library. I think everything I’ve ever known about my people has taken on a new light.

Mariv: Oh my. Do you think that’s a good thing or a bad thing?

Mishki considered Mariv’s question and decided he wasn’t sure. On the one hand, his ancestor had done something so horrible that the gods had intervened. On the other hand, this meant gobles might not be intrinsically vile, as most of the world believed. He had never felt that way about himself, but he knew others did.

When Mishki didn’t respond immediately, Mariv followed up.

Mariv: Do you want to talk about it?

Now that Mariv asked, Mishki discovered he did. He shared everything about the goble hero Groshmelk, and how ancient gobles might have been known for their sharp minds. He also shared his suspicion that Groshmelk had ascended to divinity and later tainted the entire race with his power-hungry motives.

Mariv: I am so sorry, Mishki. It is terrible how one bad person can ruin a reputation for so many others.

At that, Mishki’s chest tingled with emotion. That was exactly what was sitting so heavily on Mishki’s heart. It felt so unfair that all the scorn and distrust he had experienced from others all his life could have come from an event beyond his control.

Worse, a hero Mishki had always looked up to and modeled himself after might be the villain responsible for it all.
