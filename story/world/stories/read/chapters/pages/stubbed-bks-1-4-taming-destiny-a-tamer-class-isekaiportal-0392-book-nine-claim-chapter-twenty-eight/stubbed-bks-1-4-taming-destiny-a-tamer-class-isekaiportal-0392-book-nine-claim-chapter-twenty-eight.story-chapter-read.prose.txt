When I land on Sor’az’s ship, the soothsayer is obvious – a figure crouched over a pile of odd bits and bobs – bones, for sure, but it also includes wooden beads, stones, feathers, and other objects I can’t identify at a glance. She puts up a closed fist in an obvious request that I don’t interrupt her, so I close my mouth and let myself have a moment to examine her.

Her appearance takes me a little by surprise. I’m not sure why, but I’d imagined an old crone – perhaps I was influenced by fairy tales of witches always being old and ugly. But she looks younger even than Sor’az, who himself doesn’t look that old in comparison to how he acts.

Though, to be fair, no one I can see on the deck of the ship looks old by human standards, so perhaps they age more slowly – that would hardly be their only difference. Everyone on the deck looks like a burnished bronze statue, except for the hints of purple when someone’s shirt rides up. The statuesque impression is increased by the lack of hair on any of them. Even the soothsayer, whose build indicates she’s female, is bald.

But I’ve been invited to the ship for a reason, and staring isn’t going to calm the thrum of tension I can feel. The damayar aren’t happy having a human on their ship, invited or not. I certainly don’t want to overstay what little welcome they’ve offered me. I turn my attention back to the soothsayer who seems to have finished what she was doing and is briskly pushing the objects into a velveteen pouch.

“I hear you have read some omens and they’re not good…lady soothsayer?” I ask as soon as she stands up, my voice turning tentative at the end. It’s only when I recognise that the discomfort comes from not knowing how to respectfully address her that I realise how much I’ve adapted to Moriaxar polite society.

She smiles at me, so at least I haven’t messed up too badly.

“Well met, Blessed o’ Chaos. No need te call me ‘lady’. Ye can call me Soothsayer Murran – or jus’ Ranad Murran, if ye prefer.” Her voice is more like what I’d expect, a hoarse caw as if she’s been smoking too much.

“Very well, Ranad Murran.” The way she’d said it made it seem more like the Sera or Kera of Moriax rather than a first name. Then my forehead creases. “What do you mean ‘blessed of chaos’?”

I know plans seem to go to pieces around me, but I don’t feel like I deserve such a moniker.

Ranad Murran cackles a laugh.

“Ah laddie, ye’ll find out when time’s right. More’n me life’s worth te let on afore time. Now, yer here fer more’n bandying words with an old fila, are ye not?”

That sounds rather ominous, and less like she’s just referring to how we’ve been attacked twice already on the journey and it’s been less than a month. Chaos wasn’t one of the gods in the list I saw, I muse. Of course, perhaps it’s something specific to the damayar.

I consider objecting to being called ‘lad’. I haven’t been even legally a child for a decade. But, for all her lack of wrinkles, the look in her eye makes me think that I might not have been as far off as I thought when I expected someone old. There’s wisdom there that only comes from years of experience. I decide to let them both go – for now.

“I am,” I confirm. “Captain Sor’az indicated that you had found some…troubling information. Would you care to share exactly what you found? He was reluctant to go into details.”

“Not me place,” he mumbles behind me. Ranad Murran gives a sharp nod.

“He has t’ right o’ it. Many a slip ‘tween omen an’ interpretation.” She eyes me speculatively, then nods again, as if to herself. “Very well – ah’ll share t’ omens, though they be not clear, ah warn ye.”

“Thank you,” I respond politely.

Ranad Murran then launches into a simple but comprehensive explanation of what she means by ‘omens’. I find myself more fascinated than I thought I might be – I learned about Curses after the fight with Kyrian, and now I’m learning about soothsaying. They’re different, of course, but I would say those two areas have more in common than, say, Curses and Water-Shaping.

Soothsaying, it seems, is about drawing the energies of the world around into a medium, and then interpreting the effect of those energies on the medium. It’s a relatively gentle form of magic – far subtler than my Inspect – but it’s also less clear and requires interpretation. That’s where a soothsayer comes in – someone who’s practised and is able to interpret what to others would just be a mess of mixed signals.

It rather reminds me of divination back on earth where people sought omens in mediums like fire or tea leaves. Ranad Murran has used three different mediums and each has indicated the same disquieting findings.

“So, to summarise, if your soothsaying is accurate – and yes, I acknowledge your point that the energies of the roc might still be influencing the results, though I don’t believe you believe that?” I pause for a moment. She shakes her head fractionally, her eyes sharp, her mouth pressed into a thin line. “The results indicate that this current is wrong – that it should not exist. And you interpret them to indicate that it’s dragging us towards something. And you fear that whatever is causing it is something we can’t fight.”

“Aye, tha’ is t’ right o’ it. An’ forget not tha’ it seeks t’ roc – energies are drawn te energies.”

I’m able to make more sense of her cryptic-sounding comment now than I would have when I first arrived. It makes me grimace – none of us want to lose the bounty of the roc. Not when so many of us suffered and died for it.

“And what if we harvest the roc in its entirety?” I ask the soothsayer. “If there’s no roc, there are no energies that are drawn to each other.”

Ranad Murran’s mouth tightens further.

“Do no’ mistake greed for good sense, laddie,” she warns. “T’ closer we get, t’ harder it is te leave. For now ‘tis fine. Later?” She shakes her head doubtfully.

“And you don’t know how much time we have?” I press.

Ranad Murran hesitates then reaches into a purse and pulls out what looks like carven cubes of bone. She tosses them on the top of a nearby barrel, then closes her eyes, murmuring quietly with her hand held above them.

I wait in tense silence, doing my best to feel any stirring in the magic around me, but failing to detect exactly what she’s tuning into. Not even my magic sight spots anything.

Finally, Ranad Murran lets out a slow breath and gathers up the cubes, replacing them into her pouch.

“Ah cannot see,” she admits. “Hours, days…bu’ if we stay, we will suffer,” she warns, her eyes boring into me.

My lips press tightly together. I can already anticipate the reaction of the other lords, just based on our last meeting, but I believe Ranad Murran’s words.

I bow slightly towards her.

“Thank you for your guidance Ranad Murran. I’ll do my best to act on it.”

“Tha’ is all ah ask,” she replies solemnly.

With that, I nod a farewell to both her and the captain, and then leap into the air, wondering exactly how I'll break this to the other lords. I turn the idea over in my mind of pulling rank but dismiss it – it would completely undermine everything I’ve built until now. And we don’t even know how bad the danger will be, or how urgent it is.

I end up deciding to call for another fleet council meeting. Perhaps this time it would be better in person than over the mirrors.

Much as I expected, the rest of the lords aren’t best-pleased at the news.

“I’ve checked with my Water Mages – they agree that we’re in a current, but the ocean is filled with currents. And sometimes they change position for no real reason. We’ve fought for these resources – fought hard. Yet now you say we should give them up?” Although usually soft-spoken, it’s not entirely surprising that it’s Josiah of all people who is most angered by the idea.

“Josiah, I know that you had the greatest losses among your people–”

“Classers who have served my family for generations; sailors who have sailed the seas for decades,” Josiah snaps, his tanned skin flushing red in agitation. “And you’re asking me to turn away from the resources of a Tier four that they died to secure – that could save others? Purely on the word of some damayar soothsayer?

I wait pointedly for a moment, partly to make sure he’s not planning on continuing, and partly to avoid adding to the tension by hurrying the conversation. Josiah drops his eyes briefly, his body language calming a touch.

“I know that you suffered in that battle,” I continue soothingly, “and that we could well use the resources. But the soothsayer is clear – the longer we wait, the harder it will be to escape whatever this is. I do not wish to be too blunt–” that earns a couple of snorts from others around the table, “but our ultimate aim is successfully getting to the Lost Continent with those still alive, not avenging the dead.”

“How do we even know this ‘soothsayer’ is genuine?” Layton questions cautiously. “There are many charlatans who pretend to read omens in the clouds or tila dregs. Few of them are proven to be right in retrospect except by the most hopeful.”

Josiah’s angry nod proclaims his agreement with his cousin. As I glance around the table, I see that Elarion and Valence aren’t convinced either. Captain Garous has his head down and is a pace away from the table, still too nervous around lords to actually take part in the discussion.

I return my gaze to Layton, since he’s the one who asked the question.

“This soothsayer predicted the return of Captain Sor’az even when he was convicted unfairly and given a sentence of slavery.” I feel a bit bad for revealing Sor’az’s history when he’s not here to decide for himself if he wants it told, but the scars around his throat tell their own tale. And if it can be used to prove the accuracy of the soothsayer’s words and save us from whatever fate the current is pulling us to, then I think he’ll agree it’s worth the reveal. “And the crew believed her, even when they ran so low on money that they were almost reduced to eating their own boots. I’d say that their trust speaks volumes.”

“It does,” Layton agrees with another cautious nod, “but Josiah said that his own Water Mages didn’t notice anything strange about this current. Even if we accept the damayar crew’s trust as proof that the soothsayer is genuine, that does mean she might not be mistaken in her interpretation.”

“And you said it yourself,” Josiah breaks in. “Even if she’s right, she doesn’t know what the current is pulling us towards, only that it is something we cannot fight. Maybe it’s something that we can avoid instead.”

“Can we take that risk, though?” Valence asks, shifting forward so he’s leaning on the table. “After the battle with the Air Roc, our ships are damaged, our fighting forces depleted. If we face even another Tier four, the chances of us living through the encounter are slim.”

“This ‘soothsayer’ certainly didn’t warn us about that,” Josiah mutters bitterly. I might agree with him in principle, but I decide not to respond.

“That’s certainly a point we need to take into account,” I reply to Valence, nodding at him in appreciation. “Again, are the resources we can take from the roc worth it if the carcass pulls us into another battle, perhaps even worse? I would argue that we should leave while we can.”

“Easily said, not so easily done,” Elarion points out, joining the conversation for the first time. “Were we not in here only a short time ago speaking of the damage our vessels have sustained? Can we leave the current at this point?”

There’s a moment of silence – dismayed on my part. Because Elarion has a point. Possibly the Water Mages might be able to shift us out of this current, but possibly not. Everyone is tired from the battle, and the ships themselves are in bad condition.

“Not every ship has sustained equal damage,” Valence points out. “Garous’ ship is practically undamaged, for one. Couldn’t we tie one ship to another?”

“And then if a leviathan comes from below and drags one ship to the depths, what happens to the rest of them?” demands Josiah. “Lord Elarion speaks rightly – some of us,” his gaze darts to me and Valence again, “have repairs to make. Why not harvest at the same time?”

There’s a brief moment of silence as we consider that.

“If I might make a suggestion?” Elarion starts again, breaking the silence, then continues when no one objects. “Why must we make this decision now? The current at present appears to be normal. Even if this soothsayer is correct, we have time. Why not put it to good use rather than jumping to a decision and potentially putting ourselves in a worse position?”

I hate to admit it, but Elarion has a point.

“So, you’re advocating a wait and see approach?” I ask. Elarion inclines his head.

“And in the meantime, those who aren’t necessary for the repairs can help with the harvesting,” he agrees.

“But as soon as the repairs are sufficient to ensure that each ship is capable of at least some degree of independent piloting, we agree to leave, the carcass harvested or not?” I ask, glancing meaningfully at Josiah.

He looks mutinous for a moment and then I see his shoulders slump.

“I suppose the materials will have lost the majority of their benefit in less than two days anyway,” he muses, then sends me a sharp nod and looks up at me. “I can agree to this. You are right – the living are more important than the dead, though we will mourn them.”

I glance at Valence, and he nods in approval. I next look at Captain Garous, but he’s still staring at the floorboards. Well, the majority carries it anyway.

“Alright,” I sigh. “Then we’re in agreement. But we’ll get our mages to keep a close eye on the current. We may need to make a new decision quickly.”
