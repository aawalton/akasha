Armed with his Warm Fluffy Coat (or was it the other way around?) and various other winter gear, Eric set out bright and early, leaving the safe zone behind. Joe hadn’t reappeared since his stint as a toddler. Eric didn’t know if that was a good or bad sign, but it was certainly something. Nothing he could do about it now but get back on the grind and keep clearing dungeons.

Still, it’d been nice to just… take a day off. He was so used to having to make up excuses or drag his exhausted, sick body out to get a doctor’s note or getting told no, we need you today, it doesn’t matter how sick you are, get your ass in the office. Being able to manage his own time and listen to his body was such a godsend that it almost felt like salvation.

Let’s not exaggerate. The Game is still trying to kill me. But at least it was honest about that. He could almost respect it: the Game just coming out and outright saying, yeah, I’m going to kill you. It was way better than the implied work yourself to death, drone; oh, you don’t want to? There’s a dozen people begging to take your place, so do it or don’t bother coming in tomorrow that he was used to from his bosses. He was sure office work worked for someone; it never had for him. Sitting in a cubicle had felt like locking himself in a coffin and waiting to die.

“But that’s all over now. Now I just have to worry about Trinotaurs taking my head off,” he commented to himself. It really wasn’t an improvement to his situation, so why did it feel so much like one?

“Are you talking to the audience again?” Cy asked dryly.

“No, just me this time.”

Cy gave him his patented look. “You need more friends.”

“I’m the only human on earth,” Eric deadpanned.

Cy blinked at that, actually taken aback. “Right… that is… my apologies.”

Eric chuckled. “It’s fine. I’m coping so far, right?”

“Right…” Cy hunkered down on his shoulder, then scooted a little closer to his neck in a friendly sort of way.

Eric glanced at him. Rabbits die if they get lonely, right? It’s a myth, but nonetheless, I think Cy might’ve taken that a bit harder than I really meant it. He’d always been pretty solitary, so being alone on earth didn’t bother him too much. In some ways, he viewed it as an improvement: no traffic, no one yelling at him, no mystifying social mores to accidentally trigger or people having a bad day to choose to yell at him. At the same time, he did deeply value the few relationships he had, his friends and family. If people hadn’t been so terrifying, he probably would have sought them out. As it was, although he considered himself something of a humanitarian and truly, from the bottom of his heart, wanted to help as many people as he could, it wasn’t like he wanted to have a relationship with them or get to know them. He wanted to be a force of good. A helper. But he wanted to do it from the other side of the screen, with a big gulf between him and people.

He loved humanity, but people were scary.

That’s probably to the detriment of my plan, he realized. The best, most popular streamers—putting aside the ones who flashed money around and bought their clout that way—were the ones who maintained a parasocial relationship with their fans. The kind who, whether they realized it or not, made their fans feel like they were old friends who hadn’t seen each other in a while, who had that guy-next-door charisma. Even the fans who weren’t parasocial at all, who knew the streamers were entertainers who wouldn’t know them from Adam, would still fall into that easy charisma and feel that comfortable, oh I know him/her way about them.

Eric looked at his hands. He lacked that. Deeply and truly, he lacked it. He wasn’t handsome by any stretch of the imagination, and worse, he was unattractive in a way that screamed nerd, in a way that was distinctly uncool. His interactions with other people were usually short and stilted, and he could tell the other person was often feeling what’s wrong with this guy by the end. His career as a streamer was dead on arrival. He could spew memes with the best of them, but his actual personality was a bit lacking.

That’s what the guide is for. He didn’t need to be charismatic if he was just conveying information. Once he was established as a guide-maker, he could work on the charisma, and by then, people would “know him” and start tracking toward him, while he would’ve learned some form of charisma to draw them in… hopefully. He had no idea how goblin psychology worked, let alone the “higher beings” Joe had hinted at. All he could do was base things on the one form of psychology he kind of, vaguely, at a distance understood: human psychology. And he barely understood that.

All that, and what’s my biggest hit so far? Me taking out the Trinotaur, while doing absolutely no guiding. To be fair? He couldn’t possibly have done any guide-work in the moment. He’d been struggling just to survive. At best, he could take the footage and dub over it with his thoughts in the moment, but then it wouldn’t help anyone but a few mages who happened to have the very specific gear he had.

He took a deep breath. It was fine. He was figuring things out. Getting his big hit to draw attention to himself was the first step to getting somewhere. He’d do the guide stuff when he could, when it made sense to, but if his life was on the line, well, it was going to go by the wayside. That was just the way it had to be.

And if I think about it, that’s probably for the best? The people who want the guides aren’t going to be the same ones who enjoy watching me battle something way over my level and somehow win. It’s like Joe always reminds me: those are two different audiences. If I try to capture them both, I’ll grasp neither.

He came to a halt before the cold dungeon. Without the hot dungeon to beat at him from the other side—the dungeon was shut, with a thick wooden gate that simply hadn’t been there before blocking the entrance and a timer hovering in front of it that was stuck on 00:00:00 and not counting up or down—the cold wind blowing from the icy dungeon chilled him far deeper. Eric glanced at the hot dungeon, tilting his head. Were the linked dungeons linked in more than just loot? If he didn’t beat the cold dungeon, would the timer ever start moving?

It’s an interesting thought, but ultimately not important. I can ask Joe later. Without anyone else on the planet to contest him, it wasn’t a problem; it wasn’t like anyone else would want to use that dungeon, or try to use that mechanic to block him out of a dungeon when he was literally the only human on Earth. Was the potential for abuse intentionally built into the Game for drama? Or was it one of those accidents Joe would hastily update the second he brought it up? It didn’t matter, but he was curious.

In any case, right now, what he needed to do was defeat the cold dungeon. He quickly put on the pants he’d found, layering a few pairs for all the benefit he could get, then a few layers of ordinary clothes, the snakeskin jacket, and finally the fluffy jacket, boots, and gloves. He looked ridiculous, like a little kid dressed up by their mom, all puffy around the middle with his arms awkwardly out at a funny angle, and he felt even more ridiculous when he was standing there sweating away in the early-autumn heat.

“How do I set the coat to invisible?” he wondered aloud.

“Yes, thanks.” He wasn’t afraid of wearing a puffer jacket or anything like that; honestly, he kinda liked the cobra on the back. Call him cringe, but it was cool. No, it was more that he wanted to test the functionality. That, and he was kind of building a brand for himself by wearing the same general outfit (the jacket and the bandanna) day after day. If he wore the same gear consistently, it would become an outfit, and it could become part of his brand, versus constantly swapping clothes, where he had to build his brand on his face and physicality alone. He wasn’t hot enough for that, so uniform it was.

The puffer jacket vanished, and so did its bulk. He swung his arms experimentally. All the restrictiveness of the coat was gone. Its weight still wore on his shoulders and body, so it wasn’t like he could equip heavy armor, set it to invisible, and gain all the benefits for none of the downsides, but it was interesting that the physical restriction of wearing the jacket vanished. The layers he’d piled on for extra warmth still kept him from completely folding his arms tight and left him a little stiff-legged, but the coat itself was gone.

Gone… except for its warmth. He was still sweating his ass off, and the coat was no more breathable for being invisible. He touched his arms and felt his skin and the snakeskin jacket, not the puffer coat. Can it get damaged the way my jacket got damaged last battle? Will the snakeskin jacket be protected by the invisible, intangible (to me) puffer coat? Lots of questions to figure out, and none of them that he could figure out by standing here like an idiot.

“Neat,” he commented. He lowered his arm. It was time to get going.

Cy, who’d temporarily relocated to the ground, stood on his hind legs, and Eric set him back on his shoulder. He looked at the rabbit. “Do you, uh, need to keep warm too? I can put you in a pocket or—”

“I can extend my aura and ward off the elements that way,” Cy explained.

“Oh,” Eric said.

“Eventually, you’ll be able to do it too. You just need a little more training.” Cy thumped his shoulder in a reassuring way.

“How much more?”

Cy thought for a moment. “A month, maybe?”

Eric nodded. Not short, not long. A reasonable span. Forever by the Game’s timelines, but that was just how it was. A month’s investment to not have to worry about the weather (or at least the temperature) ever again sounded like more than a deal to him; it sounded like a downright bargain.

But that was for later. Right now, the dungeon laid ahead of him, and the only way to get stronger and gain the kind of skill that allowed him to ignore the weather forever was through it. He stepped forward, bracing his snake-gloved hands on the frozen wooden door, and pushed. With a great crackle of breaking ice and the groan of beleaguered hinges, the door swung open, and a gust of icy cold air burst out.

Eric braced himself, expecting to freeze, but the puffer coat did what it was supposed to. His legs felt a little chill, but that was all; the rest of him stayed warm. Beyond the door, vast hills of snow rolled off to the horizon, and a forest of barren deciduous and laden evergreens veiled his way ahead. It was cold and snowy, but that was all the more he could learn from where he stood.

“Let’s go,” he murmured to himself, and stepped into the dungeon.
