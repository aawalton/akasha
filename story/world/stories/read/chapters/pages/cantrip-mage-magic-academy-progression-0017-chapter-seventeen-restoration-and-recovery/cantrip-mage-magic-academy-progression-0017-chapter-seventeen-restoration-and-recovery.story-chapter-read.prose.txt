~Eddarn~

Eddarn watched through his mirror as the girl plunged through the air.

He couldn’t follow someone through the library’s third floor. That task was almost impossible, no matter how much the Scholar demanded it. Y’aer-cursed ever-changing maze. Scrying, though? The Scholar, praise be her, had figured out how to make that work, at least at short ranges. So Eddarn wasn’t there, but he had watched the fight. The girl had talent. She was wasting it, but she had it.

His eyes weren’t on ‘Tabby,’ though—he snorted at the idiotic fake name. Not anymore.

They were glued to the cat. Had been ever since it had ripped his leg open.

He’d seen that Y’aer-cursed cat before. It had stalked the fourth and fifth floors since before the Scholar’s Cult had inducted—he couldn’t help but snort at that—him. They’d lost dozens to it. It was worse than a Dalyn and Davek. Worse than a Srilla. It had eaten his lover right out of their bed—ate her, moth-eaten sleeping shift, threadbare blanket, and all, before she could even scream. The blue tiger was a horror story told around the fires at night, except it was real.

Real, and here.

Eddarn watched in horror as the collar around its neck loosened. Runes poured from its mouth. Its jaws opened wider and wider—impossibly wide—and he almost dropped the scrying mirror as they engulfed the falling girl from behind. His lover had died just like this, eaten alive. Now his recruit was going to follow her path. Y’aer curse—

No.

No!

Horror turned to fascination. The cat’s jaws slammed closed, and ‘Tabby’ was gone, but there was no blood. No gore. No ear-splitting yowls or floor-shaking purring. No horror. Instead, the blue tiger crashed through the barrier between floors, flailing to get its feet under it and hacking loudly, and the scryer went dead.

Well, then.

“The Scholar ent going to believe this,” he muttered into the chipped, tarnished mirror. “She ent going to believe this at all.”

The blue tiger was back. And it had bonded with a mage. That was a very…interesting sign. He’d have to report in to the Scholar and the rest of the cult, and the moment he did, he’d become one of its most important members. Maybe even second only to the Scholar herself. His mind’s wheels turned, forming the rough outline of a plan. It’d be a while before they could execute it, but better to get started now.

That Y’aer-cursed monstrosity would be theirs.

All it’d take now was some patience…

Eola woke up hurting.

And hungry. Desperately, ravenously hungry.

She tried to roll onto her side under the blanket, but there was a weight on her chest, and she couldn’t lift it. She struggled for a minute, then opened her eyes. A pair of bright yellow ones stared back at her, and she coughed once—painfully jarring something in her chest when she did. “What did you—“

Atta started to reply, then hacked loudly. “I can’t—not here, not now. Quiet, stupid girl.”

“What?”

The cat didn’t say anything, just glanced to Eola’s right, and she slowly, gingerly turned her head to look.

“Oh.”

She wasn’t in her bed. A single tree—more of a sapling than a proper yew—sat in a brown pot in the corner of a room that made hers feel spacious. There was barely enough room for the tree, the door, and the white-sheeted mattress, plus a single chair. Her armor and sword were nowhere to be seen, and she was dressed in a gray woolen gown instead of the linen shirt, leather boots, and thick wool pants she’d had on under her breastplate.

“Hospital?” she asked hoarsely.

Atta nodded, but didn’t say anything.

A moment later, Mana swelled in the air, and the tree’s branches started to bend violently. Before she could react, a woman stepped from the thin yew’s trunk, dusted her bark-like skin off, and looked down her nose at Eola. “Miss Lemiene.”

“Instructor Clearance!” Eola said.

She tried to sit up, only for the hamadryad to push her down gently, one hand on her shoulder. “Don’t try to move. This kind of healing isn’t simple—even for me—and the best thing you can do is give the spells time to work.”

Eola blinked, then stared up at the ceiling. Half a dozen different runes, none of which she recognized, pulsed with bright green Mana.

“Yes, those are mine. I rarely work with injuries—diseases are more interesting—but for some cases, they request my presence and provide me with a way to move around the place.” Instructor Clearance sighed loudly. “You’re a lucky girl, Miss Lemiene. Your friends said you were exploring the third floor, but the librarians found you on the first. That’s quite a fall, and you were covered in a thin, sticky liquid when they got to you. You should be dead. Care to explain?”

“I…I don’t remember much,” Eola said as honestly as she could. “When I remember more, I’ll tell you.”

“Please do. I’m also curious what has and hasn’t changed in the library’s upper reaches. I can’t go see, for obvious reasons.” Instructor Clearance’s branch-like arms folded over her chest, and she sat in the single chair. “You have visitors. Actually, you’ve had them for the last two days—“

“Two days?” Eola asked. She tried to sit up again, then fell back into the pillow.

“Yes, two days. Now that you’re awake, though, I need to perform an exam to make sure your body’s interacting with my rituals correctly. May I?”

Eola nodded, and for the next ten minutes, fingers that felt like smooth, carved wood gently squeezed, poked, and prodded her ribs, arms, and legs, then wormed their way up every inch of her spine from tailbone to skull. She couldn’t help but shiver, and Instructor Clearance nodded sympathetically. “I’ve been told this is unpleasant.”

“It’s just cold,” she said.

“Indeed.” The hamadryad kept working for another few minutes, then nodded slowly. “Your recovery’s proceeding according to my predictions. A trainee will be in to refresh the basic rituals and change your bandages in two hours. And, Miss Lemiene, if you do remember anything…?”

“I’ll tell you,” Eola said, nodding and wincing.

“Thank you.” A look of relief passed across Instructor Clearance’s face. “Do you want to see your visitor?”

“Yes.”

“Fifteen minutes, then. No more. You need to sleep.” Instructor Clearance walked, not through the door, but into the potted yew, leaving Eola alone.

“Be honest, Atta! What did you do?” she asked the moment they were alone.

The cat’s eyes narrowed. “I literally can’t tell you, and I wouldn’t if I could. You can’t rely on me to save you every time you get in trouble.”

“But—“

“No. You were stupid, and you almost got killed twice in there. It was a good attempt, and next time, you’ll be less stupid—and there will be a next time. Now be quiet and let me get some sleep before your friend shows up. We’ll talk more about it later.” Atta climbed back onto Eola’s chest and closed her eyes, and no matter how hard she tried to move her, the cat didn’t budge.

Nor did she open her eyes, no matter how much Eola begged.

She was still stuck when Colin showed up.

He looked like he hadn’t gotten much sleep the last two nights or so, and his cloak and robe looked even more disheveled and thrown on than normal. Still, he cracked a smile almost as wide as the door. “Hey, you’re up and at ‘em.”

“Not really. I can’t even move this cat,” Eola complained. “One of those rituals has to be…”

“Yeah, there’s a weakening spell up there. It’s pretty brilliant—keeps you from opening up half-healed wounds. But you’re also hurting pretty badly. Instructor Clearance’s pain delay spell’s helping with that.” Colin scooped Atta up, ignoring the yowl of protest, and sat in the only chair.

“Her what?” Eola’s eyes narrowed suspiciously.

“Pain delay. I don’t know exactly what it is, only that it’s a Third Order ritual. She wouldn’t explain more to me. Patrice was here earlier, but the professors are on her about failing Ideograms and Mana Studies, so she had to go.”

“What happened?”

Colin shrugged. “I was hoping you’d tell us. Tagg came and got us a couple of hours after you went through the gate. Otherwise, we’d probably still be waiting there. No idea how he knew where we were, but there you go. Since then, we’ve just been waiting for you to wake up and collecting your homework.”

Eola sighed. “There are only two constants in the hospital, apparently. Starving to death, and homework piling up.”

"It’s covering half your desk.” Colin laughed, and Eola grinned slightly. It pulled on her cheek and sent a spike of pain across her face, and she winced. Colin winced too, in sympathy. “Is there anything we can bring you?”

“Other than a triple breakfast?” Eola stopped to think. What did she need? She squeezed her eyes closed, then opened them wide. “What happened to my gear?”

“We brought your sword, wand, and armor back to your room.”

“And my bandolier?”

“It’s there, too.”

“Great. There should be two books in the pocket. Can you bring them to Tagg—or one of the other librarians—and check them out in my name? The Records of Lord Card’s Greenarbor is for me, but I grabbed Magick at the Dawn for you. It’s a mythology, but that’s close to history, right?”

Colin’s smile only widened. “I’d hug you, Eola, but…”

“Please don’t. You’d probably re-break my ribs,” Eola said. She yawned loudly. “How have classes been?”

For the next ten minutes, she lay back and listened as Colin told her all about how Magical Dueling, Mana Studies, and Ideograms were going. The conversation was low-effort on her part, which was fine with her. Everything hurt, and she couldn’t put the energy into doing more than making responding noises and occasionally asking a question.

It was a relief when the nurse knocked on the door. “Time’s up. Miss Lemiene needs to rest, and she can’t do that if she’s talking your ear off, Mr. Tremory.”

“Sure,” Colin said. He stood up and walked to the door. “I’ll get those books taken care of for you, Eola. Anything else?”

“Well, I’m still hungry,” Eola laughed, then winced again. “But aside from that, no.”

Over the next week, Eola gradually realized just how miraculous Instructor Clearance’s ritual magic really was.

Her pain peaked on the third day as the rituals winked out one by one, leaving only a pair of spells still running overhead. She could practically feel the magic pulling her bones together and forcing them to heal in her arms and back, clicking and popping as they went. She didn’t get any sleep that night, and for the first time, she was thrilled not to have eaten anything; the pain was bad enough that she spent the night on her side, wrapped around a basin for vomiting into and checked on every five minutes or so by an exhausted-looking healer-in-training—and, of course, by Colin.

Her whole body was fixing itself, and that didn’t leave her with the energy to so much as hold The Records of Lord Card’s Greenarbor open and skim its pages—but it did give her plenty of time to think.

Atta helped with her first revelation. Her familiar spent their first several days drilling into her head exactly how reckless and arrogant she’d been. The third floor wasn’t a place for an eighteen-year-old girl who wasn’t even a Journeymage. It was a place for packs of second-years, and it’d be a long time before Eola was ready. The cat was adamant about that, and after some thinking, Eola agreed.

“I won’t go back up there until we both agree that I’m capable,” she promised.

“Good. Now that you’ve realized that, let’s work through what you’ll have to handle to be ready,” the cat said from the foot of the bed. “And you will be ready. You and I are going back.”

Eventually, they narrowed it down to three problems.

First, there was the problem of the Scholar's Cult. If they controlled the third floor, Eola needed to learn more about them. All she knew was what Instructor Clearance’s lectures had told her—and what Eddarn himself had. That wasn’t enough to make a plan to handle them, and Eola was pretty sure the rotten-toothed, pale man wasn’t much of a high-ranker in the cult.

More worrisome to Eola was the powerful feral book. It had to be one—Instructor Clearance hadn’t mentioned anything else that took that form—but it was much, much stronger than the professor had claimed they usually got. And there were other things that didn’t add up in its behavior, like the glowing purple core, or the fact that not one of the shelves it had been near had needed weeding.

Come to think of it, none of the shelves Eola had seen in that room had needed weeding. That couldn’t be right; the third floor was supposed to be unweeded and overgrown. Instructor Clearance had also said that feral books were supposed to be rare, and even Eddarn had been more concerned about Srillas and demi-Srillas, whatever that second one was.

That was a mystery. Eola suspected it might have something to do with the Scholar's Cult’s presence, but she couldn’t be sure. There wasn’t any other reason for Eddarn to have been in that section of the library. Was there?

And third, Eola needed more magic. Whatever the Scholar's Cultist had been—and Atta was convinced that he wasn’t a Truemage—he’d been strong enough to beat her even with Atta’s help. She just didn’t have the Mana or the spells to compete against a mage like that. And he’d said something about being an ‘eight-bar Truemage.’

“Eight-bar?” she said the words over and over, feeling them out with her mouth. She’d had bars at home, back in Greenarbor. She’d filled all four. How could he only be at eight, though? That didn’t make sense. Her head still hurt, and the thin, tasteless, and worse, fruitless porridge she got for breakfast, lunch, and dinner in the hospital wasn’t helping her think straight, either.

Then, finally, the pain faded, and Eola started gaining strength as the last two rituals guttered out and the magically-induced weakness that had left her unable to so much as sit up in bed faded.

On the morning of the seventh day she’d been conscious in the hospital, she reached for The Records of Lord Card’s Greenarbor. But before she could open its cover, Atta started hacking and coughing.

“What now?” Eola asked. Her familiar had gone through this routine a disturbing amount as Eola slowly worked through the less than an hour she’d been on the third floor and the consequences of it. It felt like Atta wanted to help her, but couldn’t. She usually got her coughing under control after a few moments, though.

This time, though, the blue cat didn’t get it under control. Just like when the cat had coughed up a single page of modifying marks, her mouth opened far too wide, and a slime-covered ball of parchment fell out of her mouth and onto the clean white blankets covering Eola’s lap.

“Let me guess. Another gift?” Eola asked.

“Don’t be ungrateful,” Atta said as she licked her flank. “I’ve had to carry that for a week, and you’re lucky I got that much. The book it came from is part of that monster now.”

“Ah.” Eola reached out and—with as few fingers as humanly possible—spread the page out on her bed and let it dry.

“Good choice. Now, I’ve been snooping around while you sleep, and Instructor Clearance is probably discharging you in a few hours. It’ll be back to classes and working for that professor for you, and back to regular meals for me. What are you going to do once you’re free?”

“I think I’ll start with Instructor Clearance. I owe her the truth, or some of it, and I have a lot of questions about the library’s third floor. So does she. Maybe we can trade information or something.”

“And then?”

Eola rolled over and wriggled herself to a sitting position. “And then, Maestro Yarrowbloom for advice on fighting monsters my smallsword can’t handle and Instructor Tarik to see if there are any cantrips I’m missing—or to help track down anything Yarrowbloom says I need. I can’t do anything about my Mana except keep exercising it and growing it, but I can make suremy spell list is as strong as possible.”

She fell silent, and so did Atta. Then the familiar opened her mouth. “Reasonable. Surprisingly un-stupid. And, of course, there’s all the homework.”

“So much homework,” Eola agreed, groaning.

When the discharge came, Eola tucked the folded, mostly dry paper into the cover of The Records of Lord Card’s Greenarbor, got dressed in her robe and cloak, and hurried to the cafeteria for roasted vegetables and seared fish. She was one of the only students in there, and ate three servings in complete solitude, marveling at the slight lemon and dill flavor. It had been a long week of nothing but gruel.

Then, finally, she slipped into her suite and cracked the door to her room open.

There was plenty of homework on her desk, but all of that could wait. She pushed the stack of parchment aside and sat down in her comfortable, familiar chair. Then she opened The Records, unfolded the dry, stiff paper Atta had hacked up, and started reading as the cat fell asleep on her bed.

This being the diary of me, Varin, apprentice mage to Archmage Frelanne, I—

Her breath caught in her throat. Every muscle in her body stiffened, and she spun out of the chair and glared at Atta. “I destroyed the diary of the Great Mage Varin, and you didn’t tell me!?”

Her Y’aer-cursed familiar didn’t even open her eyes. But she did lick her lips, and her purr was the most self-satisfied, taunting purr Eola had ever heard.
