Sap gave a low neigh as Kalsin led him farther out from the stables, his saddlebags softly clanking in the cold evening air. It was dark outside, with no sign of the moon breaking through the stormy sky any time soon. Distant thunder rumbled across the plains.

Few men were out at this late hour, and Kalsin didn’t try to avoid them. Almost far enough away to blend into the darkness, a fellow with his cap pulled over his eyes snoozed next to a fishing line. The grass around him was cut low enough to show a hint of the bottle peeking out from between the strands.

Kalsin clicked his tongue quietly, nudging Sap to avoid the hairline fracture just wide enough to trap an ankle if you stepped wrong. The thunder growled louder overhead, and Kalsin glanced up toward the horizon, seeing only darkness and distant flashes that lit up tiny pockets of the skyline. Normally, he might have called it pleasant weather. Just not when there was distance to cover.

He rechecked the knot holding his shield to his saddle, gave Sap’s scaly flank a reassuring pat, and continued walking further inland.

The wind picked up the farther he went. It came in steady bursts from the west, tugging at his coat and pulling loose grit into the folds of his collar. The red grass here was denser than near camp. It shushed underfoot, the dark plains dancing in a uniform pattern, leaning, bowing, revealing scattered rocks half-swallowed by the earth on occasion.

The plains sloped downward into a basin where old irrigation ditches had left behind shallow grooves in the ground. Like scars on a close-shaved pate, no grass sprouted inside the grooves, giving the basin an artificially checkered pattern. Kalsin guided Sap down the slope, then back up toward the other end. The leather of his gloves creaked as he adjusted the reins.

Up ahead, the silhouette of the rock rose like an upside-down tooth jutting out of the earth. Half-tilted, worn smooth from centuries of wind, and just wide enough to blot out the flash of distant lightning. It was one of the few natural landmarks around this part of the border region—a small break in the flatlands.

Kalsin slowed Sap to a stop and listened. He glanced back the way he came.

Nothing. Just the faint roll of thunder and the flapping end of his coat.

From up close, the rock formation was large enough to tower over a man his height eight times over, and maybe half that in diameter. Kalsin circled the rock once before settling into the lee side, where the wind broke against the curve. A nook beneath the outcrop formed a crude shelter, just enough room for two men to sit and keep dry if it started to rain.

“Sorry I haven’t been there for you lately,” Kalsin murmured, rubbing the side of Sap’s muscular neck. The rock pressing against his back was cold. “They’ve been feeding you alright?”

His bahten snorted quietly. Kalsin smiled. Not a big conversationalist, the scaly chap.

“Look,” he gently nudged on the reins, back toward the Republic proper. “Look, there. It’s dark now, but thataway is where the fractures are wide and the swimming is easy for a fellow like you,” he said. “If you have a horse-memory of a horse-childhood, it’s probably there. Past the Inner Line of Defense, from Újtavak and Grenstel, to Határ and Araz… it’s a good place to be a horse. At least, I hope it was for you.”

Kalsin let his eyes blur out of focus for a bit. “Hm. The source of my woes lie thataway, too. Where I’m hunted for what I could be and what I believe in. Hunted for what I am, now, I suppose.” His fingers felt the little grooves between Sap’s scales as he scratched idly. “I did something today. Something impulsive. Something irreversible. You know? I took a risk with little to no payoff. And here I am now, left wondering if I really have what it takes to become someone people will want me to become. I didn’t heal her because I’m selfless, Sap. I healed her because I simply happened to grow a bit fond of her,” he whispered. “The plight of the innocent doesn’t move me. Not as a concept, I don’t think. Do you understand? It was selective, and transactional, and, and self-indulgent. How can—how can anything noble come from a bastard like that?”

Sap offered no answers. The lightning had crept closer in the night sky. The man and his beast listened to the thunder that followed.

It was some time later when a snap of gravel caught his ear.

Kalsin stepped away from the rock and moved toward the sound, his hand resting on his scabbard for a moment before dropping to his side. Even if it was someone else, he was confident he could talk his way out of any trouble.

A shape approached from the western plains. Tall, wide shoulders, and leading something four-legged behind. Kalsin waited until the figure neared the rock formation and caught the spill of lightning-glow for a moment.

His bodyguard had ditched the golden Clenn uniform for her old hunter’s gear.

She came to him slowly, stopping just out of arm’s reach. Her hair normally pulled in a tail was down on one side, covering half her face in a way that almost hid her uncertain expression. She gave him a hesitant little wave, barely more than a twitch.

Kalsin calmly extracted his hand from the reins. He stepped forward and silently embraced her.

Jack stood stiff for a heartbeat before leaning into him, arms wrapping around his back. One of her hands climbed to his neck, and her towering height meant that when she pulled, it smushed him right between the valley of her breasts.

Kalsin snorted right into her, struggling with the squishy pair before pulling back enough to say, “Suffocation by my favorite pillows. Not exactly how I pictured going out.”

Jack tilted her head down disapprovingly and flicked his nose. Then back into the boob-grinder he went.

Kalsin let it happen. It’s not like he had much room to complain. Literally or figuratively.

Eventually, she loosened her arms and took a step back. Kalsin adjusted his coat, pretending he hadn’t just been smothered in a way that would’ve made a lesser man lose dignity and clarity both.

“Evening, Hollow,” Kalsin nodded toward Jack’s bahten, the warlander standing tall and enduring as Sap sniffed around his ears. He carried enough bags to almost count for another person in the saddle.

Jack pulled his gaze back to her with a finger against his chin. She raised a brow and patted one of his pockets.

“Yeah. I brought everything,” Kalsin said. “Took some liberties with the food and packed some fishing sets instead. So long as we have fractures to travel by, we won’t go hungry.”

Jack held up a hand and did some reading motions, then poked him in the chest. Questioning look, again.

“Uhuh. I mean, I’m no mathematician, but I can manage some basic arithmetics. Supplies plus income minus consumption, equals; we should be in the green.”

Jack opened her mouth, paused for a moment, but then just shook her head looking exasperated. She pressed a smooching kiss on his forehead and let him go.

“Oh,” Kalsin said, at long last struck by enlightenment. He rubbed at his arm. “You mean… You meant your letter.”

Jack’s expression was equal parts amused and vindicated. She watched him squirm with folded arms and a cocked hip.

“I uuuh… Right, so, about that.” Kalsin muttered, aware of the beautiful woman’s gaze. “You know, the thing is that, well. In light of recent events, when it comes down to it…” He thought of Ringtail on top of him. “All things considered, the reality is…” He recalled his power and his choice to play hero and the marital customs his high station might demand in the future. “That is to say...”

Jack massaged her temple.

“I would like to give us another try.”

When her hand dropped, it revealed the most shit-eating grin Kalsin had ever seen on his bodyguard's face.

“But. But!” Kalsin reflexively stepped back from the overly affectionate giant and her smothering peaks. “This is not, uh, an immediate thing. We can’t be playing newlyweds just when the hunt is about to pick back up.” Kalsin gestured back toward the way he came. “Right there, somewhere in those clay huts, sleeps an agent of the Civil House. Agents, maybe. And the moment they realize we’re gone, they’ll stop biding their time and come after us in full force.”

Jack’s face turned serious at that. She tapped an index finger against her throat and looked at him in question.

“They only tried once. Poison. I’ll… tell you about it later,” Kalsin quickly waved her concern away. He paced a few steps away from the rock, then back. The first few drops of rain started pelting his coat. “My guess is that they were waiting for the Veiled to do the dirty work. A man waking up with a slit throat is awfully suspicious. But when a soldier dies in the line of duty—nobody questions that.”

Jack huffed something like agreement. She made a magicky-exploding motion and shook her head, almost sadly. ‘None of the Clenn fuckers died’.

“Figures. We buried three in just our squad alone. They were good men, too.” Kalsin stood next to Jack, facing the storm slowly creeping closer in the night sky.

Jack gave his shoulder a reassuring squeeze, and they watched the horizon together. She nodded east, absently arranged her loose hair to cover her face, then lifted it up as if unveiling a secret.

Kalsin gave her a curious look. “Nope. I never bothered to look underneath their veils. Why?”

The only glimpse he’d gotten of the Veiled during the battle had been bloodied, mangled, and sliced clean off.

Jack raised a forearm and used her pinched fingers to mime writing a long, flowing script on her skin.

Huh. They covered their bodies because they wrote on it? That was effectively the opposite of the Republic at large.

“Odd,” Kalsin muttered. “I can’t rightly imagine…”

He trailed off with a frown. Wait a minute. Script inked into your own skin… that sounded like something he had encountered before. Somewhere during Blindlight. Not too long ago.

He slowly whispered, “Veiled—inside the Republic?”

His musings were interrupted by a sound that was not his bodyguard, their mounts, or thunder. It was the sound of voices. Human voices. Speaking and coming closer.

In one swift motion, Jack pulled Kalsin behind her, faced the direction of the sounds, and rested her dominant hand on her sheathed sword.

Kalsin cursed, trying to step around his brickhouse of a bodyguard. “Stay calm. We’re just two soldiers meeting for a quick lay. Nobody has a reason to suspect us of desertion. Play it composed and everything will be fine.”

Kalsin believed his own words, too.

At least, until the voices came closer. Until the figures—a damn near dozen of them—climbed up the basin and arrived at the rock.

Until the petite figure of Ringtail stepped into their rocky shelter, bag in one hand, something wet in the other.

“Found him!” she said over her shoulder, then turned to him with a grin. “Hey there, saviour boy. You really thought you could get away without letting me repay my debt?”

Paper. She was clutching wet paper.

She had stolen Jack’s letter. Leading her right to him.
