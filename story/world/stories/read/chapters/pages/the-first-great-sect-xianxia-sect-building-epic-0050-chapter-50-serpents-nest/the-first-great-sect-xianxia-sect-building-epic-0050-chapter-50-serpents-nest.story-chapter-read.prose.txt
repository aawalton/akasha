The Jolly Rangers fell with six swings of a sword, one swing each for those who ranged too far and explored the fields beyond life. Their final tether to the living, a rope dangling from a tree, was cut down. Each body was caught in either a hemp blanket or a bundle of linen filched from once warm beds.

Neither Hua nor Weiji had thought to bring medical supplies enough to prepare six corpses for a possible burial. They made do with what they could find, fishing hooks threaded with string sed to stitch smiles made of gutted torsos and held back tongues of intestines that had once served as noose.

It was disgusting work that they did. Smartly, they had stripped bare and performed their work without a chance for their clothes to be stained and risk carrying the pervasive scent of decay when they next ranged out for the bandit camp. Hua still got viscera on her wrappings as she brought down the body of the third Ranger. By the time they had stitched up the last Ranger, she felt grimy with blood, frozen guts, and too-sweet rot.

They wrapped the corpses of the Jolly Ranger in bedspreads and placed them on a cart meant to be pulled by a team of donkeys or oxen, a cart for all the village’s trade when they ventured out to the nearest large city.

It was in the structure that hid the cart that they found a girl who had been murdered. Bruises on her neck and face and body. A single stab wound through the heart. A mercy killing and an unmerciful act. Hua and Weiji shared a look. This was likely why the bandit had his head crushed to a pulp.

“When we return, we’ll bring them back,” she told Weiji, told Qing sitting on the edge of the cart, told the empty village square and its indifferent tree that anchored the desolate village.

“She would appreciate it,” Weiji said, speaking of the foolishly pretty Ranger recovering at Hua’s camp, protected by well-trained soldiers. Blood had splattered across his chest and shoulders. Coloured the pale hairs on his chest. “Thank you for choosing to be kind.”

“Kindness is a weakness.”

“Often it is. But if such visible weakness is the privilege of the strong, then should a lord not be kind on occasion?”

“Your moralising is ridiculous. Leave it be for your stories. We’re here to find bandits to slaughter. Anything else is needless prattle.”

Saying that, Hua covered the cart with a tarp and brought it beneath the shelter, where it would stay cool and be hidden from scavengers who would never refuse a free meal.

They pulled water from a well after breaking the layer of ice by dropping a heavy stone into the darkness. With only frigid well water, the weak glow of the stars, and Hua’s golden gaze to light their way, they washed themselves clean. The dead lingered in odd ways; as ghosts, as memory, as tacky blood under your fingers and the pungent smell of rot that never left no matter how many pails of freezing water you threw over your head.

Dressed, distressed, and disturbed, they set off once more, in search of the camp the Jolly Ranger spoke of. Dawn would arrive in two hours. It was not the late dawn of deep winter that came closer to noon and gave so little time to sunlight that working in the dark was the norm. But it was late enough that they would still enjoy the cover of the grey murk.

“Why did you tell me to count to ten?” Weiji asked, breaking the dead silence. “Before we fought the bandits. You said count to ten and strike.”

“Just advice Grandmother gave me when I was younger. Weaker. Before I learnt to love a fight. No matter how afraid you are, count to ten with each breath and, on the last, you strike. The fight will be over then.”

“That’s suicidal.”

“No. It gives you a plan. Forces you to stay calm. And sometimes, Weiji, you strike even if only to kill the weakness inside of you. I know you have a lot—”

“Don’t—”

“—of internal weakness, but it’s good advice. Maybe if you tried it more often you wouldn’t lose to random bandits.”

“I tripped!” he shouted whilst whispering. Hua was surprised he could manage that minor impossibility. “I could have killed him just fine.”

“You were about to be stabbed.”

“So were you,” he countered, which was unfair. Hua had suffered a Qi deviation and killed more besides. She said as much. “That makes it worse, not better. It means you’re reckless.”

“I remember when you were nice to me. It was only an hour ago.”

“Thankfully, I am not a lord and so kindness need not be a permanent virtue.” She let out a breath and for a moment, she felt she could ignore the grime of such senseless deaths. “Come, let’s go find our bandits.”

Kang was utterly uninterested in hiding his camp, that much Hua could say. His was safety in audacity. Bloody serpents painted upon tree trunks. Flags flying proudly on branches. A veritable mass of skulls, human and animal.

She found it tasteless. But it served a different purpose than flying kingfisher banners upon looming walls. Overt displays of power and violence might not be the weaknesses Hua thought they were. If it got the mortals to behave, and obey, then maybe she needed to decorate her home with a few more skulls.

Not the serpents, though. That was an idea Kang could keep to himself.

Hua and Weiji made their way through the grey murk. The Ranger’s directions had proved fruitful and now it was simply a matter of verifying numbers and location. Hua watched carefully, seeking any patrols, and found none. They were crouched low in the shrubbery, a distance away from one another, keeping in line of sight. Hua suppressed her presence and kept her steps quiet, but each step still felt painfully loud.

In the corner of her vision, Weiji gestured silence in the sign language all clan fighters knew. Hua slowed gracefully so her silhouette would not judder aggressively. Slowed her breathing and let her senses spread out, Qi like static permeating the area around her.

She felt it a moment before Weiji acted.

He reached down and swept his hand through the dirt. Hua saw a shift in the murk an instant before Weiji drew back his arm and threw the stone he stole.

It whistled and struck home with a meaty thud. Hua dashed forward and caught the collapsing bandit, covering his mouth in case he thought to scream. Up close, she could see he wore the simple leathers and hemp of the many bandits she had encountered so far. The mark on his exposed arm was the same serpent. Hua placed her hand over his mouth and found him still breathing that stutter-step breathing of the violently unconscious.

“You have good senses,” she told Weiji as he approached, examining the body. The bandit carried a gourd attached to his belt by a rope and had a sabre at his waist.

“Sentry or bad luck?”

Hua shrugged. “He’s underdressed for a mortal. Bad luck, I say.”

She gestured. Weiji shouldered the body and the two of them continued onward, tracing the path he had come from.

It was a well-worn path, trod upon by many bandits. They avoided it, taking a path through the thicket adjacent to it. Expecting more sentries and finding none, only more skulls chiming in the wind where they were hung and serpents painted in blood.

The reason for the seeming lack of security revealed itself soon enough. Fires dotted the valley below, leaving sleepy sentries aglow. Some dozed. A few were fully awake, walking between the paths made by tents and exposed sleeping mats. Protected on two sides by river and mountain, the angles of approach were few, and they all required a trek of three li across open ground. Any army would be pelted by slingers and archers before they drew close, and by then, any competent commander would have lines of spears aimed at their approaching enemy.

One did not need security when they had such numbers and a terrain advantage. Nothing short of a group of foolish Cultivators would battle a bandit army this large.

“Hey, Hua, what happened to it being just a few bandits?”

She elbowed him. The bandit moaned. Ah, Hua had forgotten they were dragging a body with them.

“Thank you for volunteering to join me as I go kill the small group of bandits that isn’t a camp or army.”

“We are not walking into the middle of that camp,” Weiji hissed, pulling her back down as she rose, jostling the bandit. “No. As your head strategist, I am vetoing that plan.”

As if Hua would truly walk to her death. Even she understood that numbers were a force multiplier. There were enough mortals to kill her. Against fifty, perhaps, she might endure alone. Assuming she struck from stealth and killed a good number of them before they woke. Assuming she didn’t play with her prey. Assuming, assuming, assuming a dozen other things.

“I don’t have a head strategist.”

“You need one if you mean to keep your head.”

“Fine, we’ll add it to your job titles without a pay raise. Congratulations, Strategist Weiji, what do you say we do now?”

“First, we get rid of this corpse, get rid of our tracks, and get far away from her. We can plan an attack later. And in the meantime, hope they don’t move. I doubt they would. This place is too easily defended.”

They made their way back, still alert for an errant patrol or a straggler coming back to camp. Found none and returned to where they had felled their bandit. They searched for a good spot and found a rocky ditch overgrown with vines and long grass, easy enough for a mortal to stumble over.

They poured out the bandit’s gourd of beer and placed it so that it would look absently discarded. Just a few things remained to make it look believable.

“Well, Weiji, do what needs to be done.”

“Why do I need to do it? I could be the one to kill the bandit.”

“Because you don’t have to squat down,” Hua pointed out very reasonably, “and because I can beat you in a fight.”

“The next time we need to do this, you’re doing this—squat or no squat.”

As Weiji relieved himself to better sell the story of a drunk bandit who died from the unfortunate mix of inebriation and a slope, Hua carefully observed the pattern of stones in the ditch, figuring out a believable trajectory for him to fall. When she had it, she broke the bandit’s neck, applying pressure fully along one side to better simulate a sudden impact against the ground.

He was still wheezing even after Hua twisted his head about.

“You missed his spine,” Weiji said, closing his pants. “How did you miss his spine? No, don’t try to fix it. Just let him drown in the puddle.”

They placed his body facedown in the puddle of piss and waited for him to drown.

“Have you considered a courtesy name?” Weiji asked as the bandit choked to death.

Hua looked around, checking for any shadows moving in the dark. “I already took one new name. I can’t think of another so soon.”

“Can I pick it then?”

“If you impress me enough, then sure, you can pick it. Between the two of us, you mastered more of the Six Arts.”

“That is perhaps the nicest thing you’ve said to me since we left.”

“I just tell the truth as I see it.”

“You go out of your way to be as malicious as you can if it provides you some humour. The truth has no bearing on any of your actions.”

“That too. But between us, you studied useless things like the classics of literature and poetry.”

“There it is, that familiar malice. You know, one day you’ll regret not studying your classics and I won’t be around to save you.”

“Doubtful.”

“Do you think he’s taking a bit long to die?” he asked, nodding to their drowning bandit.

“How am I meant to know? I’ve never killed a person like this.”

The bandit gave a final rattle, which Hua found was answer enough.

He was just the first of hundreds they would have to kill.
