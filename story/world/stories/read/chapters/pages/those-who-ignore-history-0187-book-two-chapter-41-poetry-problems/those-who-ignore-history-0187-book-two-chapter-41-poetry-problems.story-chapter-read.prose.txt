The dryad’s eyes shimmered like dew catching dawnfire. Her voice—if it could be called that—rolled across the marble chamber like the whisper of wind through hollow wood, every word carrying the rhythm of rain against glass. The air in the estate shifted, heavy with the scent of earth, old parchment, and something faintly metallic, as if the walls themselves remembered forests that once were.

She raised a hand, and the mana-lamps dimmed in response. The faint motes of green and amber light that gathered around her pulsed in time with her words, forming transient shapes—roots, branches, and leaves made of pure luminescence, gone as quickly as they appeared.

Her gaze fell upon the first of the two newly formed Hydra-born—one of my reflections, still pale and disoriented, the weight of new existence pressing down like wet clay. The dryad spoke, her tone a woven melody of blessing and decree.

“Art of sunset, no reason nor rhyme,

I grant you the turning clock of Thyme.

Rivers of leaves and lakes of ash,

Where forest and mountain in fury clash.

Be not mere shaper, nor lesser weaver,

You are born from the Lord of Paper’s fever.

Smoke shall crown you, roots shall guard,

Oaken might shall be your shard.

Never alone, though never free,

I name thee—Duke of the Tree.”

As she spoke, thin trails of gold and green mana spiraled upward from the marble floor, coiling around the clone like gentle vines. They didn’t take physical root—only shimmered faintly, marking him with the echo of the forest rather than its form. When her final words fell silent, the air stilled, and the glow sank into his skin.

His eyes opened slowly—soft amber at first, then deepened into green flecked with gold, as though light from unseen leaves moved behind them. He didn’t speak, but the air around him felt heavier, older, alive in a way that made the hairs on my neck stand on end.

Then, the dryad turned. Her body fragmented into drifting petals of pale green light that flowed like ink through the air before reforming in front of the second Hydra-born.

This time, the scent in the air changed. The sweetness of sap gave way to something sharper—the ozone of storms, the tang of wet stone. Her tone deepened, layered with echoes of rain and distant thunder. The very mana in the room rippled, bending slightly toward her.

“Water and fire, the steam’s delight,Heaven shall tremble with your might.You fear not hunger, nor yield to tire,For you bend both river and wire.Hot and cold—remedy and blight,Paradox walking, dusk and light.Like the one who came before,Born from the Lord of Paper’s lore.To you I decree—through storm and sea,Rain shall always answer thee.”

When the final word left her lips, a fine mist bloomed outward from the Hydra-born’s body, spreading across the polished floor like the breath of a hidden tide. It glistened faintly, not water exactly—too thick, too luminous. It left no wetness, but its scent lingered: clean, sharp, alive. The chandeliers above flickered as though caught in a passing storm, then steadied.

The dryad’s expression softened, but only for a moment. Then she turned toward me—exhaling in that universal way that said I warned you about this.

“Alexander,” she said dryly, rubbing at her temples as if warding off a headache that was more spiritual than physical. “Do you remember when I told you that if you didn’t resolve your spiritual instability, I’d have one of my daughters bind themselves to your estate’s heartwood to stabilize it?”

I blinked. “...Yes?”

“Well,” she said, gesturing broadly to the two groggy figures struggling to orient themselves. “I’m retracting that offer.”

She looked up at the ceiling—though it was clear she wasn’t really looking at it, but beyond it, as if trying to stare down whatever pantheon had decided my life needed to be an ongoing experiment. “I don’t care how beautiful a star crystal tree is, nor how much mana your property can sustain. Whatever fate the gods, the Grandis, or the creatures above them have tied to you, I will not have my daughters involved in it. I value their sanity.”

She sighed again, long and slow, though a small, crooked smile tugged at her mouth despite her words. “You attract too much chaos, young Duarte. And worse, you make it look poetic.”

Her eyes drifted back to the two Hydra-born. “For what it’s worth, the first one’s Arte seems rooted in growth. Not manipulation in the way your paper bends—but true genesis. He doesn’t just shape trees; he births them. He is—quite literally—the Duke of the Tree. It’s rare to see that level of origin-tuned creation outside of elemental lords.”

She gestured toward the second clone, who still knelt quietly, the faint shimmer of mist coiling around him like ghostly rain. “The other one… his Arte is more complex. The aura around him carries the resonance of condensation, but not purely water. Something warm, viscous—like a cross between oil and ichor. Living, volatile, potent. I can’t classify it. All I can say is: the element responds to him with recognition.”

That last word sent a shiver through me. Recognition. As if the world had looked at him—and remembered.

I hesitated before asking, “This might sound naïve, but… do dryads not know what Arte you awaken in us? I thought that was your gift.”

The dryad turned her gaze on me, amusement glimmering faintly in her eyes.

“Wrong phrasing, child. We don’t awaken your Arte. We witness it. We step into the threshold between what is seen and what might be, and we describe what your soul reveals to us before it closes again. It isn’t a science, Alexander. It’s… divination through empathy.”

She began to pace slowly, her footsteps silent on the marble. “Each awakening is a reflection—of power, fear, longing, and the fragments of what was left unsaid in your soul. We speak those truths into form. But that doesn’t mean we understand them.”

She paused, then added quietly, “Sometimes, even you don’t.”

Her hand swept once more over the Hydra-born, and faint embers of light fell from her fingertips, vanishing before they touched the floor. “Those two? They’re not offspring. They’re memories given autonomy. Choices you didn’t make, paths you didn’t walk. Living echoes.”

Her eyes met mine again, calm and ancient. “So make sure they remember who they came from.”

And with that, her form dissolved—light scattering into the air, fading like candle smoke in the stillness of the estate.

For a long while, no one spoke. The twins—my twins—sat still, their breathing shallow, their eyes faintly glowing with different hues of life. The only sound was the soft hum of the mana conduits embedded in the marble and the faint flutter of Fractal’s feathers in the next room.

I exhaled slowly.

The Dryad’s words lingered, heavy and alive: Never alone, though never free.

And I realized she wasn’t just talking about them.

***

The sun was already low, dragging the last of its golden light over the sandy ridges that stretched beyond the estate. The air here always smelled faintly of dust and wool — the pens were close enough that the bleating of sheep came and went like waves. It was quiet, windless, except for the occasional metallic hum of the mana fences that kept the livestock from wandering too far.

We’d come here because it was open — far enough from the estate that any mana surge wouldn’t send Barbra into another lecture about “containment protocols.” I’d taken a seat on a flat sandstone outcrop, the kind that held heat long after sunset, and opened my system interface.

And promptly wished I hadn’t.

I stared at the glowing text for a full ten seconds before my throat went tight and I choked out a half-laugh, half-sob.

“Really? You’ve got to be kidding me.”

[ERROR. STATUS TEMPORARILY UNAVAILABLE. PLEASE CONTACT YOUR LOCAL ADMINISTRATION.]

That line blinked back at me, flickering faintly like a dying lantern.

I rubbed my eyes. “Yeah. That’s just… that’s just great.”

Cordelia tilted her head, glancing at me from where she was lounging on a slope of sand. “Mine’s perfectly fine,” she said matter-of-factly. “I think that’s just you.”

Ten grunted. Fallias hid her smile behind a gloved hand, and the others just nodded in varying shades of awkward sympathy.

I dropped my hands in my lap and exhaled. “Of course it’s just me.”

“Sire—if I may propose a hypothesis.”

The voice came from above my shoulder, calm and crystalline. Lumivis, my ever-helpful spirit, hovered like a floating wisp of molten glass — shifting between amber and white as he spoke. “Your sense of self is currently… strained,” he said delicately. “The status system cannot determine which parameters belong to you. Especially after your, ah, infusion of Hydra blood.”

I blinked. “Infusion?”

Lumivis turned in slow, almost apologetic loops. “A poetic simplification, perhaps. Regardless, it would explain the disruption. Did the Domini who assist not explain one of the key abilities a Hydra possesses?”

Before I could ask what he meant, the air twisted.

A sudden ripple of starlight bent across the sand — and then Gin, Barbra, and Leraje materialized with the kind of authority that made everyone else jump. Sven yelped and nearly toppled off the fencepost he’d been sitting on.

“No,” Gin said smoothly, brushing sand from his white gloves. “But we were about to. Without your input, apparently.”

“Without my— you literally appeared out of nowhere!” I said, gesturing helplessly.

Barbra ignored the complaint. She had that professional, measured tone that made everything sound like a formal evaluation. “Alexander, pay attention. This is critical.”

Leraje stepped forward, folding his arms. The sun caught the metal lining his uniform, making him look like he’d been forged from dusk itself. “Hydras,” he began, “possess three defining traits. Most of which your body now… partially shares.”

He raised one finger. “First: regeneration. Most compare them to trolls, but that’s a misconception. Troll regeneration halts under heat or fire. Hydra regeneration does not. You can recover from almost any wound — though external healing will overtax your system.”

I frowned. “So, if someone heals me—”

“It will hurt,” Leraje said simply. “And it will slow you down.”

“Great.”

He raised a second finger. “Second: the infamous ‘many heads.’ Again, a myth, though not entirely false. Each head, as you’ve seen, carries its own Arte, personality, and spirit fragment. They are separate entities — but still you.”

Barbra picked up seamlessly, her tone colder. “And that means all of you technically share the same status and skill pathways. Combine that with the external modifiers you already possess — namely, the Mysterious Stranger system — and multiple networks are currently overlapping and attempting to reconcile their data structures.”

Fallias gave a quiet whistle. “In simpler terms: you broke your own soul’s code.”

“Thank you,” I muttered. “That makes me feel so much better.”

Gin’s grin widened, sharp and knowing. “It’s not all bad, Alexander. You’ve essentially reached a point of quantum instability. Multiple versions of your being are syncing — and unsyncing — at once. It’s messy, yes, but…” He spread his hands, as if revealing a magician’s flourish. “It also means potential. A lot of it.”

“Potential to do what? Explode?”

“That depends,” Leraje said flatly, “on whether your will remains intact.”

That word — will — hit harder than I liked. The air seemed to cool around us, the sun dipping lower behind the dunes.

“Your regeneration,” Barbra added, “isn’t just physical. It’s conceptual. Each head, each fragment, represents a version of you that could exist. And because you’ve already split twice, your sense of identity is… diluted. The system doesn’t know which Alexander Duarte it’s reading.”

“So it’s not broken,” Fallias said softly, “it’s confused.”

“Precisely.” Lumivis floated closer, his glow pulsing. “Your status reflects you. And right now, even you don’t know who that is.”

I stared down at the error message again. The words flickered, then briefly changed.

[ERROR—SELF-DEFINITION INCONCLUSIVE.]

Then back to static.

My chest tightened. “You’re saying my own system doesn’t know if I’m… me.”

Gin shrugged. “Identity is relative. You’re not the same person you were yesterday.”

“That’s not what I—”

Barbra cut in smoothly. “What Gin is saying, if clumsily, is that your structure isn’t static anymore. You’ve crossed into a state of perpetual evolution. The moment you attempt to measure yourself, you’re already changing.”

Fallias stepped closer, her eyes soft with concern. “And if he can’t measure himself… he can’t anchor.”

“Correct.” Barbra’s tone softened, but only slightly. “Which is why we’re here. If this continues unchecked, the system will eventually fail to reconcile your fragments — and erase one. Maybe more.”

I swallowed hard. The sand beneath my boots suddenly felt very unstable.

Leraje’s eyes met mine. “The Hydra is a creature of excess, Alexander. Infinite heads, infinite hunger. Its curse is not destruction — it’s indulgence. Each head believes it is the true self. The more you divide, the more you risk believing the same.”

For a moment, no one spoke. The sun had nearly vanished, leaving the sky a wash of lilac and smoke. The wind picked up, dry and hollow, carrying the faint bleats of the sheep back toward us.

Finally, Cordelia broke the silence. “So. He’s got an identity crisis so severe the universe can’t process it.”

Gin smirked. “In fairness, that’s very on-brand for him.”

I let out a slow, uneven laugh, rubbing my temples. “Yeah. Sure. Funny.”

But beneath the humor, a thought lingered like grit between my teeth.

It wasn’t that I feared power — I never had. I feared what happened when it stopped feeling like mine.

And judging by the blinking line still frozen in my vision, that moment might already be closer than I wanted to admit.
