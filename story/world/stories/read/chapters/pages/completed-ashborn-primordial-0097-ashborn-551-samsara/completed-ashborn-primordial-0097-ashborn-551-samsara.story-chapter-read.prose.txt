Raja Jagath Aindri leaned back in his throne, one arm draped over the gilded armrest, listening to his runner narrate the latest news from Camar Gadin.

He had messengers crossing the realm, flying in at every hour of the day to report everything they saw and heard, and the picture they painted was both grim and fantastical.

In the course of a single day, a human-commandeered Vimana had struck Camar Gadin and been repelled by the Akh Nara, who had then proceeded to raise the dead, of all things.

Were it anyone else, Jagath would have dismissed such reports as exaggeration and embellishment, and perhaps even punished his messengers for failing to take their task seriously. Yet having once lost to that very foe, Jagath knew not to underestimate him, and that was before he’d reunited the realms, becoming what many believed a living deity.

A claim that was becoming increasingly difficult to deny.

Jagath ground his teeth. If, indeed, he had resurrected the dead, then who was anyone to say otherwise?

Strangely, the Akh Nara had not wielded his new army as a commander might, nor had he even enlisted them in Camar Gadin’s reconstruction efforts, for which they would have been amply suited.

Instead, he had disappeared to some valley in lands that had until recently belonged to the Ash and its many terrors.

“And you say he has not moved since then?” Jagath asked his messenger.

“No, my Raja,” the demon replied. “The scouts monitoring him all report that he has taken a seat and appears to be meditating, not moving an inch. Not even for sustenance.”

“A being of his power does not require any,” Jagath replied, staring off into the distance.

Puzzling, to say the least. Puzzling, and yet more than a little tempting. He glanced down at the lengthy note the Pagan Order had sent him some weeks prior. Regarding a proposal for closer cooperation and mutual support.

It was no secret that those extremists desired. Before contact with the human realm years ago, Jagath might never have believed there was a faction of demons in the human realm more fanatical than even the biggest zealots among the clans, and yet, Fate always had a way of humbling one’s self.

And there could be no doubt that Jagath had been humbled, despite the Akh Nara’s words. Even now, he seethed at the loss he’d sustained, back when his adversary sought to restore the Garga.

True, Jagath had been oathbound to honor a prior promise to Raja Adiman. A ruler for which he bore no love, what with his foolish mismanagement of the windfall that had been Samar Patag and the Gargan clan territories.

Yet the fact remained that he had lost. Not just a battle, but the war itself, and he was not blind to the damage his reputation had taken among his people. He saw it in the way they looked upon him and the way they reacted. Loyal, yes, but no longer reverent.

Like salt in the wound, Jagath had then been forced to watch as the Akh Nara dismantled demonic culture, piece by piece, in the years that followed.

His desire for peace. For rewriting the warrior instinct that ran through the blood of every demon. His pushes for cooperation.

It was worse than blasphemous. It was unnatural, crippling the very core of that which gave demons their strength. Not their inborn physical advantages, but rather the pursuit of personal strength. Risking one’s life in the face of true danger. What would demons become without the drive to constantly test one’s self?

Jagath had no doubt that ridding the realm of the Ash would indeed save countless lives, yet he couldn’t help but mourn its loss. For millennia, it had proven the ultimate proving ground. A land in which survival was unlikely, where the mere act of entering proved one’s courage and mettle. For until the Akh Nara, there had been no guarantee of ever returning.

Yet those who returned, returned changed. Reforged and tempered. Greater than they had ever been. Even the Akh Nara himself had undergone such a transformation, granting him the strength required to reshape the realm.

None of that existed anymore, and for the longest time, Jagath had resigned himself to that reality. Now, however, he saw opportunity, for in the Akh Nara’s absence, much was possible.

The realm required revenge for this atrocity humanity had wrought upon them. Demonkind required reinvigoration and a return to the old ways.

Jagath crushed the note in his hand and commanded his messenger. “Contact my friends at the Pagan Order. Tell them Clan Aindri wishes to discuss plans. Tell them that time is of the utmost essence.”

“As you wish, my Raja,” the demon replied, bowing deeply before hurrying off.

Jagath stared at his back as he left, but his eyes were elsewhere. Be it a day or a year, he would not waste this seric opportunity so neatly delivered upon his lap.

For what better avenue for his people to have their revenge than all-out war?

What better legacy for Raja Jagath Aindri than to command the greatest cause demonkind has ever known?

It had been a day since she’d negotiated terms with Roshan, but to Maiya it felt like it’d been a week with all she’d done in that time.

The Sarvaak’s command center echoed that sentiment, with the projection table that had shown the Vimana’s heading now displaying three different maps. The demon lands, the human countries, and the vast expanse of new land that separated them.The whole place was a hive of activity as aides bustled about the room, relaying reports and messages, the communications array on the wall pulsing as new transmissions arrived from various cities in the realm.

Luckily, Maiya wasn't alone. Two aides of her own supported her. The first was a young Altani named Saanvi. Her personal scribe for three years could anticipate her instructions before Maiya had finished saying them, and Maiya didn’t know what she’d do without her.

The second was Hema. Her faithful ally ever since she had still been Ira’s right hand.

Back when they’d all fallen victim to Andros’ trap that had nearly killed them all.

She’d gone missing after that along with the Head Handmaiden, Neeti, but they’d both come into Maiya’s service after the long leave she’d forced upon them, serving her faithfully through the long year when she’d reigned as the interim Rajni of Clan Garga while Vir dealt with his fractured soul. They'd pulled her through countless critical times since.

Which meant that they’d grown effective enough for Maiya to offload far more than was responsible. Everyone hailed Maiya, but no one realized it was Hema and Neeti practically running Maiya’s affairs in the Human Realm these days.

“Thanks to the combined efforts of the Asura and the Bairan emergency relief forces, we finally have an accurate count of Camar Gadin’s casualties,” Saanvi said, reading from a long piece of parchment. “Initially, the count sat at eight hundred-fourteen injured and three hundred and seventeen dead. After the Akh Nara's miracle, the death toll dropped to zero. The others who were injured are being tended to by Naga healers. As for the raised demons, all were accounted for in the procession that left the city.”

Maiya idly tapped the map table, staring into it like it would give her all her answers if she simply stared hard enough. “What about the city itself?” she asked.

“The impacted areas will require significant reconstruction, of course. Crews with the appropriate Aspect Arts have mobilized to at least put up temporary shelters and Asuras are helping the Bairans in that effort as well. Also, Chitran relief forces have been dispatched by Raja Matiman. We expect them to arrive overnight.”

“Good, good,” Maiya mumbled, reflecting on how incredible it was that the Chitran, of all people, were sending anyone to help others. They’d changed so very much under Matiman’s guidance these past years, and not all in a bad way. “What is the current situation in the Human Realm, then?”

As important as these events were, her duty was first and foremost to humans, where there had been plenty of chaos to begin with even before all this.

“The news is spreading faster than anticipated. By demons with communications crystals, we assume.”

That was another new thing. Demons from the Demon Realm outside Human Realm enclaves like the Pagan Order. Under Ira’s rule, demons had been warmly welcomed, and for years now, they’d roamed the streets of Sonam and Kin’jal’s other cities.

Hiranya and the other nations were far less progressive in that regard, and even in Kin’jal, demons still weren’t a common sight, even if more and more visited every year.

Maiya had always championed the effort… until now. Now it made her life a massive pain.

“They have likely spread to most nations by now, though we’ve yet to confirm just how far that news has traveled.”

“And how is it being taken?” she asked, knowing fully well the story would be warped and dramatized well beyond reality. Even if that reality was plenty fantastical in and of itself.

“Well, they’ve started calling Vir’s resurrected ‘The Samsara’,” she replied. “As you can imagine, demons are beyond ecstatic, while pretty much the opposite is true in the human realm.”

“Figures,” Maiya replied. “But Samsara, huh? Who started that?”

“Likely a demon,” Hema said. “It is, after all, a word from their myths.”

“Even still, the progress we’ve made with human-demon relations these past years is helping immensely,” Saanvi said. “Panic has yet to break out, and while it is obvious some are scared, it seems most are taking it well for now.”

“That’s a relief,” Maiya replied, rubbing her temples. “But it won’t last. We’ll have to act, and soon.”

Further discussion was put on hold when a great bear of a demon walked through the doors, stooping so his massive frame would fit through the oversized door designed with Bairans in mind.

Maiya hardly needed to see his face to know who it was. Only one demon was that large.

Thaman was alone, which was beyond unusual. That itself told Maiya quite a bit about the nature of this little visit.

“Saanvi. Hema? If you will.”

Her aides nodded, and within moments, the chamber had emptied, leaving Maiya and Thaman alone.

“You’ve been busy,” he said, nodding with his chin to the untouched platter of food on the table.

“That’s an understatement,” Maiya laughed.

“I truly appreciate all you’ve done for my clan, Maiya. Please, before we discuss anything else, tell me. What has become of my son?”

Maiya tensed up, deliberating how to break such strange and unprecedented news to him. She’d been dreading this moment for hours.

“So he is beyond help, then,” Thaman muttered, mistaking her silence.

“What? No,” Maiya replied hastily. “He’s… still him. It’s just… complicated.”

“Whatever it is, tell me. I must know.”

So she did. Everything from when they’d met to everything he’d said.

Thaman didn't interrupt her, despite already having heard it all. He’d undoubtedly received reports of his son leaving with Vir, even if he hadn’t seen it himself.

Maiya then told him the details he didn’t know. Of the tether and the fact that Roshan refused to leave Vir’s side. That Vir was fighting an unending battle in a field and would remain there until he either failed or found a solution.

She told him about how Roshan had taken on the role as the leader of the revived and that he had asked her to tell his father in person that he was sorry he could not come himself.

Thaman closed his eyes, clearly struggling to take it all in.

“Tell me then what you and he agreed to.”

Maiya did exactly that, filling him in on the details of their accord.

Roshan would remain with Vir as the coordinator of the revived. He'd manage the camp there with support from the Asura. Both so they could monitor the situation and to render aid to Vir if needed.

He had also promised to report to Maiya daily via communications orb.

As for the revived, any who wished to return to their homes would be able to, though not one had shown any desire to do so thus far. And though she’d never admit it, Maiya was secretly pleased they all stuck together.

She wanted nothing more than to keep their existence private for as long as possible. The realm had rumors that Vir had resurrected the dead, but they had no idea that the Samsara, or whatever they ended up being called, did not require food or rest. Or that they appeared to be effectively immortal and far stronger than they’d ever been.

That wasn’t even mentioning the really hairy stuff. That they weren’t truly alive. That their bodies only functioned on account of that tether.

Despite wishing to keep that part to herself, she forced herself to say the words to Thaman.

He’d done far too much for Vir to deserve at least that much.

“I admit, I struggle to digest all of this. You mean to say that he will never age… that if he is struck down, he will rise again?”

“Effectively,” Maiya said. “Their condition depends entirely on the tether. Whatever happens to Vir happens to them. So long as he lives, it seems Roshan cannot die. Siya could find no mechanism by which a tethered demon could be killed short of severing the tether itself, and the only way to do that would be to stop it at its source. The quantity of prana is so immense that no prana or Chakra attack, even from a Vimana, could hope to sever it.”

Thaman slammed a hand on the table to keep his balance, nearly toppling it in the process.

“I’m unsure if I should cheer that my son has become something greater than anything I could have hoped… or weep in sorrow at his fate.”

“I know it’s a small consolation,” Maiya said with a wry smile, “but I feel exactly the same.”

Thaman let out a breath. “How did he seem to you?” the Raja asked, looking into her eyes. “Tell me truly.”

“The process has… clearly affected him,” she said, choosing her words carefully. “Not physically, but in bearing. I admit I did not know Roshan well, but before this, he didn’t strike me as the sort of demon who’d worship Vir, let alone carry him as he did.

“Whatever the revival did to him, it left its mark. Something more than mere gratitude for saving his life. Almost like he knew he was different now. He seemed to be at peace with it though. This is just a guess, but I think he’ll be a better leader to those revived demons than anyone we could have chosen.”

Thaman looked dazed, but far more relieved than when he’d walked in.

“Then any changes have only been in degree, and not in bearing. He always was an ambitious boy, and noble to a fault. I feel the revival has only brought more of it out.”

Maiya suspected the Raja said the words more for himself than to her, but she nodded her agreement nonetheless. For all she knew, he could be right. Why add to his already substantial worries?

“Well then,” he said in the confident, booming voice Maiya had always known. “I shall wait until he is ready to speak to me. Demons are long-lived creatures, after all. What is it to me if he takes a few months, or even a year? Now come. Let us discuss what you hope to achieve with this meeting. Before the others arrive.”

Maiya smiled. “I’d like nothing more, Raja Thaman.”

Gods knew she’d take all the help she could get.
