I follow the blue line on the floor, and eventually a scribe meets me. She’s a well-tanned woman with the head of a hippo with a surprisingly high-pitched voiced.

“A Visitor? Here to join the games?” She asks, looking at a rather modern-looking clipboard in her hands. A scroll is unfurled on it.

“Yes. I’m looking to earn entry to the Royal Market.”

“Well, we’ll start off with the mathematics challenge. Follow me, please.”

After several minutes of walking, we step through a pair of large double doors. With everyone being around my height, maybe a few inches shorter on average, everything is sized for me. I don’t have to bend over to walk through doors. It’s surprisingly nice. I guess you don’t appreciate the small things until you lose them. Like properly sized doors.

The warmly lit corridors open up into a massive, sandy arena. The entire circular clearing is half a mile across. Quickly counting, there’s enough seating for half a million people across six levels. The largest stadium on earth is the Narendra Modi Stadium in India, capable of seating 130,000 people. It’s blown out of the water by the Colosseum of Horus.

Gold filigree along more than a thousand support pillars make up a complex network of reinforcement glyphs. They’re likely the only reason a stone structure of this size and design could stand. Even modern construction techniques and materials would have trouble with the sheer scale of it.

“Wow,” I whisper, and the scribe beams.

“It’s magnificent, isn’t it? The Great King ordered a project that lasted years to develop enough of the knowledge needed to construct it. Out of all the worlds that have come to us, not a single Visitor has ever seen anything like it.”

“And that trend continues. This blows the largest stadium we have out of the water.”

The arena has been divided up into hundreds of smaller circles, delineated by rings of papyrus poles. Most of them are combat oriented ones, though combat of all types. Some look close to MMA, while others remind me of sumo wrestling. All of them have some smaller bleachers constructed around them. Not all have viewers, but a scant few have some. Usually family members or friends, I imagine.

The air is heavy with the smell of sweat, sand, and some blood. Some arenas have the participants wielding weapons. They appear to be dulled swords with flat tips. Sharp enough to leave thin cuts, but not sharp enough to carve deeply into flesh. I watch one fighter hit another with a strike strong enough to carve through an IFV’s armor like butter.

His opponent takes it on the cheek, and I watch as glyphs beneath the sand surge to life. What should have taken the falcon headed-man’s head clean off instead sends him spinning to the ground. A referee rushes into the arena and breaks up the fight. The falcon headed man tries to get up, but fails. Each time he slumps over. After a count of 10, he’s placed on a stretcher and taken out of the arena.

“Don’t worry,” the Hippo scribe says happily. “The Arena’s magicks will make sure you aren’t slain. We’ll also give you a preferred weapon that has been dulled, assuming you use one at all. But that’s for later. There’s no weapons needed for math!”

Eventually we arrive at a table with a shade constructed over it. There’s a smaller viewing stand, though there’s only room for about ten people. Thutmose is there, and he gives me a wave. Around the shaded table are three stone benches. One is already occupied by a woman with the head of a duck. She’s fiddling with a charcoal stick, but perks up when she notices us.

The scribe leading me here sits down in the third bench, at the head of the table. With some trepidation, I sit down in the remaining one, mentally waiting for the crack of shattering stone. It doesn’t even creak or groan.

“Now,” the scribe says. She pulls several documents out a satchel at her waist. “My name is Hathorsakmet, and I’ll be proctoring today’s numerical challenge!”

She gestures to my opponent, the duck-headed woman.

“Here we have Shoshan the Record-Keeper—” I hear Thutmose suck in a breath. Hathorsakmet gives him a glare and he gives her an apologetic look.

“—And here we have, hm, I’m afraid your name is in a different language?”

“Machina,” I say, and she repeats with a nod. She passes both myself and Shoshan a document, and I’m pleased by what I see.

Earth’s version of Ancient Egypt had fairly basic mathematics—at least by modern standards—though they did have some. They were known to have had the math needed for architecture and civil engineering, like surface area, volume, and algebra. They even had fractions, though only unit fractions—fractions with a 1 in the numerator.

Khetmenet has math that’s actually much closer to modern day. While they don’t use the same Arabic numerals, the structure of the math I’ve seen so far is almost identical to ours. It’s strange that it’s so similar, but less so than an entire world—I hate to call it a Fracture at this point—so closely resembling Ancient Egypt. At least this section of it is. It’s hard to say exactly how different the rest of Khetmenet beyond the Kingdom of Amun-Ra is.

The translation thread I started earlier has completed enough work to allow me to read the math problems. Hanging out with a merchant meant I’ve been exposed to manifests and prices more often than I normally would have.

The scroll has a set of five basic algebra problems on it. The kind fourth or fifth graders might be asked to solve.

“You have fifteen minutes to solve them. Begin!”

I pick up the charcoal stick I was provided. It’s sized for someone closer to my size, though my rather bulky gauntlets make it a little clumsy to use. Not nearly as bad as a pencil from Earth. It’s odd that I’m more at home in another world than Earth itself. I don’t like that thought.

Thread terminated.

I write down the answers immediately, having calculated them in nano seconds. I didn’t even need a computer’s mathematical capability to solve this, and I hand it over. Shoshan is not much slower than I am. It really wasn’t that difficult.

After confirming our answers are right, Hathorsakmet hands us something a little more difficult after flipping through a series of other worksheets. This time it requires us to calculate the square root of a number. There are a multitude of ways to calculate square roots by hand. I personally was taught Newton’s method, which interestingly enough, is how calculators do it too.

I ask how many decimals of accuracy she wants.

“As many as you can calculate,” she says with an odd look. Thutmose leads forward, visibly excited.

I figure she’d probably want me to show my work. I spend the next fifteen minutes writing down each successive step, not stopping until our proctor calls time. I managed to get down seven hundred decimals worth of accuracy. Shoshan sighs when she sees my paper crammed with calculations. She only managed eleven decimal places, which is still amazing for doing it all in her head.

“You’re a Visitor, are you not?” She asks me, tilting her head. I confirm her suspicions.

“What did you do?” She asks. Hathorsakmet has pulled out a small abacus and is furiously clacking away at it.

“I was an engineering student for a long time. I was studying how to build machines and devices.”

“Ah,” Shoshan hums, nodding. “Was there a lot of math involved?”

“Oh, you have no idea,” I said with a chuckle. “We made machines that could do math for us.” I point at my head.

“When I changed—it’s a long story, but I wasn’t always like this—I gained the ability to do math like our machines do.”

“What do you mean?”

“I’m a machine now. Artificial. I have many of the abilities machines do. Being exceptionally good at math is one of them.”

I shift uneasily.

“I hope that isn’t considered cheating…?”

Hathorsakmet looks up for a second.

“Would you consider a strong man joining the fighting games to be cheating? It is what you are, correct?”

“I couldn’t remove it from myself even if I tried.”

She shrugs and returns to her counting.

Shoshan coos sadly.

“Such a shame to be paired up against such a monster,” she says.

The rest of the match—first to three points, with the square root calculation being the first to me—is pretty much a formality. Both myself and Shoshan are aware of just how out classed she is. Not of any fault of her own, but just by sheer computational ability. Computers have a hard time with some kinds of math, but Hathorsakmet doesn’t include finding analytical solutions to differential equations in the game’s list of challenges.

I was good at those in college anyway. For the most part.

The next couple of hours I spend demolishing challenger after challenger in the realm of competitive math. Sometimes Hathorsakmet tries to throw me a curveball with geometry, trig, and what I suspect to be an early iteration of fluid dynamics. It’s exceptionally crude as they don’t have calculus to truly describe it as it should be. Good enough for their low-performance requirements, I’m sure, despite how rough the approximation is.

I’m tempted to break out the Naiver-Stokes Equations more than once, but they might just claim I’m making shit up. I felt much the same way the first time I saw Diff EQ.

I watch the last challenger depart dejectedly. He was a fish-headed man with light brown skin, the lightest toned person I’ve seen. His ink and charcoal stained fingers place him as a chronically indoors scribe of some kind.

Hathorsakmet neatens the stack of papers before her and they disappear into the satchel at her side. It must be magical, because the amount of stuff she’s pulled out of it wouldn’t fit inside a military rucksack. She rummages around in the satchel of holding before pulling out a medallion. She hands it to me after checking it.

“Here, bring this to the office in six days. That is when the main games for math starts. You’re slated for tomorrow’s combat entry bracket. Be here just after dawn.”

I take the medallion. It’s a piece of bronze about four inches in diameter. For a human, it’d be a large and heavy coin, coming in at over a pound. For me and everyone else here, it’s the same size as the gold coin, which appears to be their currency. I have yet to see anything else be used.

Hathorsakmet guides me out of the arena and I meet back up with Thutmose. The crowd outside the Colosseum is smaller than before. Unlike what most people think, the hottest time of the day isn’t high noon, but actually a few hours after it. It’s not until the sun drops lower that temperatures will begin to fall.

The sub beats down on all of us, and it’s hot enough that a human would suffer a heat stroke in the shade. The air is nearly a hundred and forty degrees. Even Thutmose looks put off by it.

“Ah, let us get out of the sun, yes. Back to my wagon. We shall have drinks. Then we decide what to do next, yes?”

We hustle off, making much better time. The streets are similarly sparse as the exterior of the Colosseum. We get back to his wagon, and he starts to unfold various parts of it. In just a few minutes a large shade extends out from it. He pulls out several papyrus benches and a table. A few gold coins to a young lion-headed boy later, and we have a pitcher of lemon water.

He presses a finger against a glyph along the bottom. After a brief moment, blue light flashes from it, and mist pours from the pitcher’s mouth. Thin fingers of frost grasp the ceramic jug, and he beams at me.

“There, refreshments for any and all weary people looking for a place to rest for a moment.” His eyes flash. “And, perhaps after a few moments, their eyes find something they like, yes?”

His wares are arranged in such a way where someone who’s sitting down can see them all, but without being obviously set up for that seat. Thutmose expertly used color and perspective to allow the eye to identify and dance over everything. He’s clearly very experienced at this specific tactic.

He gestures to another seat, one angled in a way that wouldn’t block the view of a potential customer. When I give the bench a studious glance, he cocks his head.

“Ah, friend, what is the problem?”

“Are you sure it’ll hold my weight? I am extremely heavy.”

“Yes, yes, please sit!”

The bench creaks and flexes under my weight, but doesn’t break.

“You often check places before you sit and move. You even move slowly, why?”

“I do not fit on Earth anymore. The average human is a little more than half my height and a twentieth of my weight. People from my world are so fragile that I could hurt or kill them just by bumping into them.”

My thoughts turn to the gala that Morgan organized for the Academy some time ago. On how l had to gently weave through the crowd slowly. There was a reason I left for the roof as soon as I could.

“I’m used to breaking all kinds of things. I even damaged roads while running.”

I feel the cloth of my ever present cloak between my fingers.

“A friend recently made this for me. It’s enchanted so I don’t break things by walking on them. You don’t realize how much you miss the indoors until you can’t go inside. Not without big metal plates to help spread out my weight.”

“Ah, I am sorry my friend. You fight so hard for a world that hates you so.”

A woman with the head of a crane interrupts us, asking about the chair and a drink. Thutmose turns his attention to her smoothly, and offers her the seat. She keeps giving me odd looks. Eventually she buys a small bag of spices from Thutmose’s stock, and moves on.

Watching the exchange gets me thinking about the Royal Market and purchasing a sun crystal—or many of them, if possible. All the money I have is the handful of gold coins Thutmose gave me for pulling the cart here.

“Thutmose, how much money can I expect the crystals to cost? Assuming they exist at all.”

“I have good news and bad news. Good news first: The crystals do exist! There are a few cases of them for sale. Each case has twenty crystals. Bad news: extremely expensive. Each case is worth far more than my entire wagon, even if I sold every last item I brought, yes.”

“I don’t suppose there’s a reward for winning the tournaments?”

“There is,” he says with a clack of his beak. “Enough to buy a case, yes. Two, if you have a silver tongue. Sun Crystals, as you call them, are expensive, but not rare.”

Forty crystals would be a good start, but would that be enough? Technically even one would be if we can actually make more, but we don’t know how long that would take, or how powerful the duplicates would be.

I pull a Fractal Shard from my storage space and offer it to him.

“How much would this—”

“Put that away!” He hisses, eyes wide and nervously clacking his beak. It disappears in a flash of light.

“What’s wrong?”

“You have God Hearts?! Those are… I have no words! Valuable is too small, too small! Dangerous to have!”

He looks around and leans in.

“The Laws of Karnak are strong indeed, yes? Protected by both Guard and Great King. But while both are powerful, they are not perfect. For something as valuable as a God Heart? Many would be willing to test their mettle against even the Great King.” He shivers.

“Pray no one saw that, yes. Or the both of us shall have much more trouble.”

“Could I trade them for the sun crystals?”

Thutmose opens his beak to say something before closing it with a clack.

“Maybe. Yes? I do not know. I have never traded in God Hearts before. God Hearts are… far beyond me. God Hearts are the realm of kingdoms. The Great King perhaps has traded with them in the past. I hear he is using some for the great ritual that will power the Unveiling.”

“Maybe he’ll buy more? I have… a lot.”

While I hand over a vast majority of the Fractal Shards—God Hearts here—to Morgan or Captain Rains for research, I still have several on hand. Ones I haven’t gotten around to giving away yet. Considering everything I store away stays in some kind of stasis, it’s not a problem to go several Fractures before offloading stuff in bulk.

“Maybe, maybe,” Thutmose says. “But I cannot help you with this my friend. It is hard to say just how upstream such things are of me. Upriver and beyond The Heart, hm?”

“Could the shards allow me to gain entrance to the Royal Market?”

Thutmose clacks his beak thoughtfully.

“No,” he says slowly. “Ah, when I said entry to the Royal Market must be earned, I meant it. More than money is required. Standing, honor, glory, many things. But simply holding valuables? No, no. Access to the Royal Market is tradition, and money will not sway the Great King. Even for something as a God-Heart. He is too honorable for such a thing.”

“I guess I have to win the tournament, then.”

“I have no doubt you will!” Thutmose says with his normal cheer.
