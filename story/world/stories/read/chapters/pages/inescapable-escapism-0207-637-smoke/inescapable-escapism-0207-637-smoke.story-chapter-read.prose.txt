I dropped into a crouch as my eyes snapped to the hole in the front of the building, scanning the street for the source of the metallic clanking that came from behind me. It sounded almost like someone kicking a can or a tin, but the thing was heavier. The noise had been slightly deeper than it should have been if it were just a can.

But I couldn’t see anything. There was no sign of movement, no hint that anyone was hiding behind the abandoned cars, but I didn’t trust that. Something or someone must have caused the noise, and I wasn’t in the centre of the city anymore, but I doubted it was an animal. It must have been a person.

Slowly, I crept behind one of the dust-covered remains of a long counter, watching closely. My heart was hammering in my ears, making it hard to listen for footsteps, and my hand strayed towards the knife I kept strapped to my leg, preparing to pull it out if I needed to.

Taking a deep breath to steady myself, I paused. I wasn’t sure what to do. Part of me wanted to call out, to speak to whoever it was out there, but I knew that was stupid. Instead, I carefully slipped my backpack off my back, moving as slowly as I could to avoid making any noise, but the soft brush of the strap against my jacket made me wince. I froze, my muscles tensing in preparation for the flurry of movement I expected to hear, but none came.

Uncertainty pulled at me, but I batted it away as I unstrapped the bow from my bag. I didn’t want to fight the person or people. There was only one of me, and I didn’t know how many of them there were, so I could easily be outnumbered. They might be able to overpower me too, especially if they had guns.

All I had was my bow and arrow and a couple of knives. We had a couple of guns back at camp, but I couldn’t bring myself to take one and leave everyone even less protected. I could have used it, though. Since running away, I’d come across quite a few people with firearms of some kind, but bullets were always hard to find. There were a couple of loose ones floating around the bottom of my bag that I’d scavenged along the way and picked up, either to trade or in case I got my hands on a gun, but I didn’t have enough to truly be of use.

My bow was better. It was more useful. I didn’t need to worry about running out of bullets because I could carve my own arrows, and I’d only just replaced the string. It would probably hold up for a while longer before needing to be fixed again.

Still, I couldn’t stop myself from pressing down on the fishing wire, making sure that it was still taut. It felt fine, but anxiety continued to pull at me as I unscrewed the top of the quiver I took from camp and pulled out an arrow. Straining my ears, I swung my backpack onto my back and risked a glance back at the quiver. It was in the same place it always was, slotted into the pocket on the side of my bag, so I knew I’d be able to grab the arrows when I needed to.

Pain burst in my shoulder as I notched an arrow and took another deep breath. No more sound had come from the street outside, and I couldn’t continue to crouch there and wait for people to surround me. I needed to check what was happening out there.

My body felt tense as I slowly rose and peered over the counter, prepared for the inevitable flurry of movement that would erupt as soon as I came into view, but nothing happened. The city beyond the destroyed bank I was in remained just as still and lifeless as it had been moments before.

I scanned the street again, standing slightly taller. My gaze flitted from car to car, searching for a hint of hair sticking up from behind or a shoe poking around the corner, but there was none. The buildings seemed empty too. I could see nothing of concern.

It was probably just the wind, I told myself as I let my bow drop slightly, and my shoulder cried out in relief. Someone had most likely dropped a can at some point. It was probably full of rainwater, or something grosser, and I’d probably just heard it rattling along the road. It didn’t seem particularly windy, but the poster had been blowing in it, so there must have been some. I just hadn’t noticed it.

Relief washed over me, and I let out a breath before standing up fully. My back ached from being hunched over. My shoulder throbbed in pain, and I gripped the bow and arrow with one hand, lifting the other to rub my sore joints. It was almost bad enough to take the last of the pain medication I’d traded far too much for, but anxiety stopped me. If anything else happened, if my shoulder got worse or I got hurt, I’d have nothing, and the pain was bad, but I’d dealt with worse. At least rubbing it helped a bit.

But my brief moment of reprieve was shattered when a hissing sound caught my attention. The noise reminded me of the smoke machine at the school discos I used to go to as a kid, but I knew it was not that. The version of me who had grown up in that world recognised it instantly, and I dropped behind the counter again.

Either it was a smoke grenade or tear gas, but god, I hoped it was a smoke grenade. They were annoying yet harmless, but tear gas was not. Especially because I did not have a mask. I hadn’t had one since I’d left camp, so all I had to protect myself was the bandanna tied around the lower half of my face. That would help a little, but I knew it would not be enough.

Readying my arrow again, I peeked over the counter, and immediately, my heart sank. I was certain I’d only heard one noise, one grenade being thrown, but thick smoke billowed from multiple places out in the street. There must have been a few out there, and I didn’t know what to do about that.

It hadn’t reached the building I was in yet, though. Part of me wanted to hunker down and stay there. The bank felt somewhat safe, and the counter I was hiding behind seemed thick. It was sturdy enough to protect me, should I be shot at, and there if I stayed, there was a chance that the smoke might never reach me, but if I left, I’d be out in the open and probably expose myself to whatever it was.

Anxiety rose within me as I eyed it, noticing the strange green tinge that the still-growing wall of smoke had. That concerned me. Normally, when I’d seen smoke grenades or tear gas being used, it was white. It was usually just thick white smoke. There was never any other colour to it, but the stuff I was staring at had a distinct green hue, and I didn’t know why.

It must have been something else, I realised. Another chemical must have been added to the smoke, and I had no idea what it was, but it could have done anything. I didn’t want to find out, though, and that meant I couldn’t stay where I was. The smoke was steadily creeping closer.

Looking around, my eyes found a door at the back of the room I was in, but I didn’t need to move any closer to see that I couldn’t go that way. A pile of bricks and plaster blocked it, and there were no other doors. I couldn’t see any other way out of the building. The only exit was back through the collapsed front of the building, but that was where the smoke was.

And the people were out there too. Whoever had thrown the grenade must have been out there, and the thought of facing them terrified me. I couldn’t help but wonder whether they’re the people who have been searching for me for so long. Perhaps they’d spotted me sneaking away from that building, and they’d been following me ever since. They might have been waiting for the perfect moment to strike and realised I was cornered when I stepped into the bank.

My hands began to tremble, making it even harder to hold my bow, and I gritted my teeth, trying to force myself to calm down. They might not have known where I was, I told myself. It seemed like they didn’t, anyway. If they had, surely they would have thrown the grenades into the building to flush me out rather than setting them off further away.

I hesitated, my eyes flitting between the approaching smoke and the rest of the street as I tried to work out what to do. Whatever it was, I knew that I needed to make a decision fast. Either I had to stay where I was and pray that the smoke didn’t reach me and I wasn’t found, or I had to risk going out into the street and being ambushed.

Glancing back over my shoulder, I counted the paltry number of arrows in my quiver. I’d been running low for a while, but there was no point in making more before I found more fishing wire. I’d just be blunting my knife and wasting time, so I planned on making more once I got out of the city, which meant I wasn’t sure that I had enough to take the people down, regardless of how many of them there were.

But I couldn’t stay. I knew that and had done pretty much as soon as I saw the smoke, but I didn’t want to do it. I was so sore and tired, and I didn’t want to have to fight anyone. I just wanted to lie down and do nothing, but I didn’t have that luxury. Perhaps one day, I’d be able to relax and not be constantly scared of being snuck up on or ambushed. I hoped so.

Tilting my head to each side to stretch and crack my neck, I took a deep breath, checked the street again and slowly edged around the counter. I moved carefully, keeping my bow at the ready and watching for movement, as I crept towards the front of the building.

As I drew close, I caught the scent of the smoke. It was sharp, acidic almost, and immediately, my eyes watered. An intense itch began in my throat, as if I’d swallowed sandpaper and then vinegar, and my chest spasmed. The urge to cough, to try and rid the foul substance from my body, seized me, and I pressed my lips together, desperately holding it back. Any sound could alert the people to my presence.

I wasn’t even in the thick of the smoke. It had barely reached me, but the effect was so strong that I could barely see into the street. I crouched behind a stack of rubble and peered out, but my vision was blurry and obscured by tears.

It wasn’t just the pain in my eyes, throat, and chest that was making me cry. My nostrils burned too. It felt as though two white-hot pokers had been plucked straight out of a fire and plunged deep into my sinuses. I could feel pure agony singeing the inside of my nose, dancing around my eyes, and making every breath almost unbearable.

Even though I couldn’t see properly and wasn’t sure whether the people were waiting for me just beyond the bank, I stumbled out over the mostly collapsed wall. My head whipped back and forth as I stared in both directions, trying to work out where the majority of the smoke was coming from, but it was hard to tell. It seemed like grenades had been dropped all around me, but I could just about see a building through it on the left. On the right, I could see nothing through the dense, green-hued wall. I turned left and ran as fast as I could.

My steps were clumsy. The smoke and pain were making my head spin, and my mind was consumed by the horrible burning that was starting to consume me. It had begun to attack my skin too, making my arms and face itch so intensely that I longed to rip my skin off. It felt like the only way to make it stop.

The bow almost slipped from my hand, and my breathing hitched. Panic thrashed even more frantically in my heart, and I tightened my grip, terrified of what would happen if I dropped it. I wasn’t sure that I’d be able to find it again. My vision was obscured by the greenish smoke, and I could barely see the pavement beneath me. If I let go of it for even a second, I may have never seen it again, and then I’d have to fight the people off with just my knife.

My breathing was fast and shallow as I looked around, trying to inhale as little of the unknown gas as possible, whilst also making sure I wasn’t about to be attacked, but I saw nothing. It seemed like I was reaching the edge of the smoke, though. It was becoming less dense, and I could make out more shapes on the other side.

Tears streamed down my cheeks as I ran forward, my lungs still burning. I was almost to safety, almost out of danger, when a shout sounded behind me. My stomach dropped, and my heart seemed to leap into my throat, but I was too scared to look back to see who it was or how far away they were. My head was still spinning. I was dizzy, and I needed to focus on where I was going because if I took my eyes off my path, I knew I’d trip.

Another shout sounded, but it was much closer that time. I gritted my teeth and pumped my legs even harder, trying desperately to get away from the people, but I barely sped up. I was already pushing myself as hard as I could, and the flare of adrenaline helped, but not enough.

Shots came from behind me, and less than a second later, bullets slammed into the pavement beside me. I stumbled out of the way as the tarmac exploded, sending a spray of stone fragments up at me. One caught the exposed skin on the back of my hand, immediately drawing blood, and I couldn’t help but gasp.

That was a mistake. The gas plunged into my lungs, and I couldn’t hold back the cough. Foaming saliva sprayed from my mouth as I fought for control and threw myself off the pavement, diving behind a car. I couldn’t stop, though. There was too much smoke around me. I couldn’t see well enough, much less breathe, so I couldn’t stop and start picking the people off. I just had to keep moving.

I ran forward, weaving back and forth erratically as another volley of gunshots came from behind me, but I didn’t hear them hit the road around me. I wasn’t sure if the person thought I was still on the path or if their shots had just completely missed me for some other reason, but they didn’t seem to know where I was.

But I couldn’t feel relieved yet. I was still in too much danger, and I continued to run, finally managing to get my coughs under control. My chest still spasmed. My lungs still felt like they were twitching, but I kept my lips pressed together, refusing to let any more noise escape me.

Another shout came from the smoke, and I dove to the side, but no bullet slammed into the road or cars. I didn’t hear them hit anything, and despite my fear, confusion began to nudge me. They had to be shooting at me still. I hadn’t seen or heard anyone else.

Maybe there was someone, though. They could have been hidden from view by the smoke, and perhaps that was why the gas had been set up so far away from the bank. I might not have been the target the whole time.

The urge to stop and look back gripped me. I wasn’t sure whether I’d be able to see anything through the smoke, but I longed to check. It could have been the girl from the poster that I’d just found, I realised. The people could have surrounded her. They could have been slowly closing in, and she might have needed my help.

It would be foolish to stop, though, I told myself as my steps slowed a little. I still felt like I was dying from the smoke, and I didn’t know for certain that there was actually someone else there. Even if there was, I didn’t need to help them. I shouldn’t. I would just be putting myself in danger for someone that I didn’t even know, so I should keep going. If there is someone else there, they might be causing enough of a distraction that I could slip away, and I knew I shouldn’t waste that opportunity. I might not get another.

But as that thought crossed my mind, another shout sounded, and my steps faltered. There was no doubt in my mind that it was a shout of pain, and I hesitated for just a moment before diving behind a car. Holding my bow close, I looked around, checking that I was out of the smoke before taking a gulp of air.

My chest rebelled immediately. A cough welled up inside me, but I choked it down and wiped my streaming eyes before glancing around the car. My heart thundered in my ears as I stared at the thick smoke, catching sight of shadows moving in the depths.

That was all I could see. Indistinct shapes moved back and forth, the movements darting and sudden, until suddenly, someone staggered out. My eyes bulged as I watched them stumble, a hand pressed to their face. Blood poured over their fingers, pouring down their body as weak cries spilled from their mouth.

Half of their face had been torn off, I realised with a start. I could see some exposed bone glinting in the dying sunlight, and I had no idea what had happened to them or what could have caused it, but fear pulled at me. If it was the girl or someone else, I was no longer sure I wanted to meet them if that was what they were capable of, but that didn’t stop me from lifting my bow once more and sliding an arrow into place. My arms shook, and my vision swam in and out of focus as I aimed before firing.

An almost silent curse spilled from my mouth as the arrow thudded into the ground a few feet away from them, but thankfully, the man didn’t seem to notice. I needed to try again, to aim higher, I told myself as I retrieved another arrow and let it fly.

The man’s body jolted as my arrow embedded itself in his eye. He fell still, his other eye widening before he tumbled to the ground, and I let out a sigh of relief. I wasn’t sure whether I was helping whoever was fighting the people or if I was just trying to put that man out of his misery, but I was glad my aim had been good that time, even if I’d wasted an arrow the first time.

Another person emerged from the smoke, their gun raised, and immediately, I knew that it was not the girl from the poster or any ally. The person was wearing a gas mask, and their movements were too… practised. They swept their gun from side to side, clearly scanning for someone, before turning in a careful, deliberate circle.

They didn’t seem to notice me, though. I wasn’t sure if they expected me to be closer, but it seemed like they were searching for someone nearby, and I refused to lose the opportunity to take another one down.

Adrenaline dulled the pain in my shoulder and chest as I retrieved another arrow and lined up my shot. I waited until they turned away from me again before letting go, and the arrow flew through the air before piercing the back of the person’s throat. They lifted a hand to the arrow, glancing down at it before beginning to turn. But before they could see me, a giant black shadow emerged from the smoke.
