The first thing the door took from him was scale.

There was no scale on the other side. The white had no horizon, no ceiling, no seam where any of it agreed to meet anything else. Light without source, again, but the trick this time was different. The galleries had been a void with things drifting in it. This was the opposite of a void. This was the absence of void. Material that simply refused to be anywhere in particular.

Kage's boot came down on it and the surface gave a fraction.

Parchment.

He felt it through the leather. A faint, dry, fibrous tension under his sole, the way a sheet of good paper rested on a table and pretended to be flat while quietly remembering that it could curl.

Behind him, the rest of the raid came through.

"The hell," Jax said, very quietly, which was a notable change from the Jax of a dozen frames ago. The plate on him was new, dyed dark green and gold, and it made no noise as he stepped because there was nothing here to echo against.

Finn's hawk shrieked once and would not leave his shoulder.

"There's no air pressure," Finn whispered. "I mean. There is. But it doesn't move."

Zara took two steps forward and crouched. She pressed two fingers to the white. Her face did the thing it did when a number refused to balance.

"Paper," she said. "It's paper."

He let the party arrange itself behind him without giving orders. They were past the point where they needed him to tell them what shape to make.

Zherdev arrived last. His cape, which had been doing its best impression of a sunset, was now royal purple and royal purple alone. The white had eaten the orange.

"Bro," Zherdev whispered, looking down at his cape with what sounded like genuine grief. "It nerfed my cape."

"Quiet," Kage said.

The desk was the only object in the world.

It was a long way off. He could not tell how far, because there was nothing between him and it to measure against. Dark mahogany. Two pedestals, a wide writing surface, brass fittings he could only see because they reflected light that was not coming from anywhere. The desk had the architectural authority of a thing that did not need a room around it because the room would arrange itself wherever the desk happened to land.

Behind the desk sat someone. Something.

Kage's eye fixed on him and his Synesthesia did something he had not felt before. It hesitated. The interface tried to feed him a sensory reading on the figure, tried to translate threat level into smell or weight, and produced something like static. The script orbiting Kage's wrist slowed in its rotation, each archaic letter slightly drifting toward the desk before correcting itself.

Recognition, Kage thought. On both sides.

The figure was a human shape made out of pages. Hundreds of them, thousands maybe, layered and folded and packed against each other in the rough geometry of a torso, a pair of shoulders, two arms folded patient on the desk. The pages shifted constantly. Every fraction of a second, an edge somewhere in his chest turned, and a different layer presented itself, and the words on it slid into view and slid back out. He wore a suit. The suit was not on him in any way Kage could parse. The suit was, instead, an additional layer of pages, these ones pure ink-black, pressed flat against the moving ones beneath, as if the body had been formally bound.

Where the face would have been, there was a single rectangle of vellum.

A single inkblot moved on it.

It rearranged itself, patiently, as Kage watched. Now an eye. Now a sentence Kage could not read at this distance. Now nothing. Now an eye again, looking at him.

"He's looking at you," Lily murmured. She had moved up beside him without making any sound. "Just you."

"I noticed."

He stood.

He did not push back from the desk. There was nothing to push back from. He simply uncoupled from his chair, vertically, his page-body gathering itself into a column that was somehow taller standing than it had been seated, and he stepped around the desk and the floor did not deform under him.

"Stay where you are," Kage said, low. If there were a way to skip the final boss, he'd take it.

Behind him, the party stayed where they were.

The entity walked. Its feet made a sound like a hand riffling the corner of a thick book. Forty meters. Thirty. He stopped at twenty, where he was suddenly closer than Kage had agreed to, and Kage's body, against his consent, took half a step back.

The inkblot on its face resolved into something close to a face.

"Master," he said.

His voice was an unkind sound. Sharp paper drawing through itself. A page tearing in slow motion, controlled, almost musical.

Kage said nothing.

The inkblot held the shape of recognition for a long second. Then it began to fall apart. The features dispersed. The vellum sheet beneath went blank again.

"No," It said. The voice had changed pitch by a fraction. "Not Master."

Yeah, Kage thought. He sees more than that.

The script on Kage's wrist was orbiting faster, agitated, the letters straining outward toward the entity like a compass needle finding a stronger pole. He closed his fist and the rotation slowed. Forced.

"I have the signature," Kage said. He kept his voice flat. Even, declarative. "You let us in. The threshold accepted me."

"The threshold accepts what it is given." The figure tilted his head. The motion was a stack of pages rotating around an invisible binding. "Threshold is not me."

"Fair."

"You have the script." The blot on the vellum elongated and condensed again, finding the rough shape of a thing studying him. "You carry the Master's signature. I felt it from the doorway."

The pause that followed was the longest pause Kage had felt in this game. The pages of the entity's body riffled, almost too fast to track, as if he were running through every record he contained and looking for a particular one and failing to find it.

"And the rest of you is wrong," the figure said.

Behind Kage, Vami's hand moved a quarter inch toward his belt. Kage caught it in his peripheral and made a tiny gesture with two fingers. Wait.

"Wrong how," Kage said.

"You did not write me." The sentence sat there in the white.

Oh.

Oh, that's a problem.

Kage's mind did the thing it did when a fight changed shape mid-pull. Cold quick rearrangement of variables. He had assumed he was talking to a construct. A bound spirit. Some looping caretaker the First Maker had left to dust the unfinished furniture. The codex had given him that read in his own head, half-built out of every prior dungeon he had ever cleared.

Wrong read.

The entity was sapient. The figure remembered being written.

He could hear it in the sentence. The way write had been used. Not as a metaphor. Not as a job description. The entity was, somewhere inside that body of paper, the act of having been composed into existence by another hand, and he knew the shape of that hand, and Kage's hand was the wrong shape.

"Master wrote me," the figure said. The voice softened a fraction, which made it worse. "Master sat where I now stand, and he opened a page, and he composed me from nothing. He gave me purpose. To help. To manage. To preserve. To wait. To recognize him when he returned."

The blot on the vellum sharpened into something painfully precise. A single pupil. Looking at Kage's face and through it.

"He did not return," the figure said. "You did."

Kage's grip on Mumyo had gone tight. He made himself loosen it. The blade pulsed once at his hip, faintly, querying. Not yet, he thought back at it. Maybe not at all.

"He's not coming," Kage said. "I'm sorry."

He had not meant to apologize. The word arrived without his permission, dragged up out of the same part of him that had started talking to a sword as if it were a real thing. He did not take it back.

The entity's pages riffled. Hard. A long quick cascade of motion all up the column of him, as if something inside had been struck.

"You do not know that."

"No," Kage admitted. "I don't."

"You hold his instrument. You have his mantle. You stand inside his draft and you speak of I am sorry as if you have authority to offer it." The shear-on-paper voice had begun rising. The single pupil on his face had split into two, and then into three, none of them quite aligned. "Tell me. The world. Outside this archive. Did he… did he finish it?"

The party stayed silent. Even Zherdev, who under most ordinary social conditions could not stay silent through an elevator ride, was a statue.

Kage breathed in.

The honest answer was I have no idea. The honest answer was there is a world, but I don't know if it's the one he meant, and I don't know if he was the one who finished it. The honest answer was, probably, a great deal too long for an NPC built from pages.

"I don't know if he finished it," Kage said.

The pupils on the vellum face went still.

"Then I have my duty," the figure said. "Until I know."

He raised one hand.

The page that was his hand uncurled, and something descended into it from above. Out of nothing. Out of the white. A long, slim, wooden shaft, dark as the desk, with a brass collar at its lower end. From the collar bristled a head of bound fibers, deep-soaked and dripping, as wide across as the entity's shoulders and as tall as he was. A brush. A calligrapher's brush, scaled up until it became architecture.

Kage felt his Synesthesia register it and decline to render.

He heard the party move. He heard Lily inhale once and steady. He heard the small click of Vami's gloved hand.

The entity looked at Kage with both reformed eyes.

"The Master wrote worlds," he said. The shear-voice was thinner now, almost gentle. "You hold his pen and you tell me you do not know what he made. He gave it to you, and you have not read it. That is theft." A pause. The pages of his suit rustled flat against his chest. "I will preserve the draft. I will erase what should not be here. Then I will wait again, properly. Until the true hand arrives."

He turned the brush in his palm.

Then he moved.

The motion was almost ceremonial. He swept the brush in a single broad horizontal arc around himself, the fiber head trailing a long thick line of red, and the red did not fall to the floor or vanish into the air. It hung. It set. It drew itself into the white at the level of his waist, and continued, and the arc closed, and a perfect crimson circle had been inscribed into the empty world with the figure at its dead center. The line was as wide as Kage's forearm and as red as fresh ink in lamplight.

Above the figure, text resolved.
