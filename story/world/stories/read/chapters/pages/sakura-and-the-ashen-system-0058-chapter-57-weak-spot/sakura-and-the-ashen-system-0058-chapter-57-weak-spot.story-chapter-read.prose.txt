Right before the service entrance, Sakura jumped down from the driver’s bench one more time, as if checking the harness. In the green mist, she could not be seen, but Sakura still played it safe and acted as if the prison staff might be watching her.

A few seconds were enough. She crouched by the rear wheel, covered the movement with the edge of her cloak, and loosened the retaining pin, but not enough for the wheel to come off at once.

Then Sakura climbed back onto the driver’s bench and tightened the reins. The wagon rolled out of the green murk almost right at the gates. The guard on duty in the same green protective suit first saw the horses, then the wet tarp, and then the figure of the driver in a protective suit and mask.

"Finally!" he shouted. "Where’s Lieston? Today is his shift."

Sakura turned her head. The mask and filter made her voice dull and unfamiliar.

"Sick."

The guard on duty frowned.

"And who are you?"

"Replacement."

"Who assigned you?"

"The storeroom supervisor. They told me to deliver, so I’m delivering."

He was still looking too closely, and that was bad. Her name. Sakura could not let him learn her name. Before leaving, she had still smeared Gray Veil over her wrists. Thinly, just as Gilbert had taught her, while trying not to think about how unreliable it was. In an ordinary place, that kind of interference could raise questions by itself, and in the Castle even more so. Any guard on duty who saw System static instead of a normal name would immediately understand that the person in front of him had something to hide. Especially when that person was a delivery driver.

But it was still better than letting them see her real name at once. "Sakura Arai" was an instant death sentence. System static, at least, would buy her a couple of extra minutes before they realized who stood in front of them.

"Wait a second..."

Sakura did not wait to hear what he would say next. She snapped the reins and led the horses forward, as if she were simply obeying the general order not to delay entry. The wagon passed the gates and rolled into the inner service yard, where people were already running around with buckets and fussing.

"Hey!" the guard on duty shouted after her. "I said stop!"

"Don’t stand at the gates, don’t block the passage, stop, don’t stop," Sakura muttered through the filter, testing her acting skills. "Maybe you people should make up your minds!"

She veered a little to the right, as if going around wet crates and buckets abandoned on the stones. The rear wheel hit the edge of an uneven slab and cracked. Then the wagon jerked sideways, and the horses snorted and backed up. The bundles under the green tarp slid heavily toward one side. Sakura grabbed the reins and cursed loudly through the filter.

And the left rear wheel suddenly decided it would continue on its own. It flew off the axle, rolled briskly across the yard, bounced on a stone pothole, and crashed into a rack of empty buckets. The buckets came pouring down with such a racket that it sounded as if an entire cart had collapsed in the yard. One bucket rolled to the guard’s feet and stopped right in front of his boot.

For several moments, everyone fell silent. Sakura shifted her gaze from the guard on duty to the wheel, then looked at her wagon, now sitting crookedly to one side.

"Great," she said. "Just great."

"What did you do?!" the guard on duty shouted.

"Me?" Sakura turned her head toward him. "This is your yard, and your ‘faster, don’t stand at the gates.’ You wanted faster? The wheel is already faster than everyone!"

Sakura spoke loudly, angrily, and so confidently that she really sounded like a worker offended to the depths of her soul, whose wagon had just been broken by someone else’s rush and a terrible yard. In reality, everything inside her was ringing with tension. But right now, she had to play it through to the end. The harder she protested, the less the people around her thought about who she was. And it seemed to be working.

"Unload the bundles!" someone among the workers shouted.

"Hands off the shipment!" Sakura snapped even louder.

The worker froze.

"What?"

"Are you blind? The wagon is leaning. The load inside shifted. If you unload it from the wrong side, it’ll collapse completely, and the whole shipment will be written off! This isn’t potatoes. There are filters in there!"

The guard on duty stepped closer.

"You were supposed to check the wheel before entering," he ground out irritably.

"I did check it. Before you made me drive through your broken yard."

"Our yard is fine."

Sakura silently looked at the wheel lying among the overturned buckets. The guard on duty looked there too. He clearly did not agree with Sakura, but he had nothing to say.

"Fine," he said angrily. "Fix it already. And faster."

"And while you fix it, I need the storeroom supervisor to sign that the shipment was not opened," Sakura declared.

"Like hell."

"Then sign it yourself. But when they ask me why your signature is where it doesn’t belong..."

The guard on duty fell grimly silent. Sakura and Gilbert knew one thing about the Castle for certain: it had plenty of bureaucracy fanatics. Bureaucracy bloomed here like mold in a privy, and that was exactly why any paperwork problem quickly became a real one. No one wanted to be the person who signed in the wrong place, took responsibility for the wrong thing, and then had to explain to the superiors why everything had gone against procedure.

"Get the supervisor," the guard on duty snapped angrily at one of the workers. "And the levers. Quickly."

"And the wheel?" the worker asked.

"And the wheel," the guard on duty looked at Sakura as if she had personally insulted him, "can stay lying where it apparently likes so much."

Sakura said nothing. She really wanted to answer, but right now it was better not to overdo it. A good scandal also had to be left to breathe at the right time.

The workers began to move. The guard on duty stayed nearby, grim and angry, but he was no longer looking at Sakura as closely as he had at the gates, because he was busy with something else. Apparently, he had never tried to learn her name through the System, otherwise he would already have raised the alarm. His thoughts were occupied with something else now. And the longer they stayed that way, the longer Sakura herself would remain safe.

She gripped the reins more tightly and forced herself not to look around too often. She could not search for Gilbert with her eyes, look at doors, passages, and walls, or try to guess where he might appear from. She did not know if he would appear at all. She did not know whether he had found Lir. She did not know whether he was even alive. Since she had received his code, enough hours had passed for everything to change sharply.

Her part of the plan was simple: get inside the Castle with the wagon and everything necessary for the escape, stay inside, and avoid being exposed too early. So far, she had managed that.

There was some kind of commotion in the Castle. Even Sakura could tell that something unusual was happening inside, and that gave her hope that Gilbert had indeed caused chaos. Still, he needed to hurry.

Elvin Thorn hated Gilven Neld with all his heart. During his service, he had often met criminals he hated, but that kind of hatred usually flared up a couple of times during a shift and was forgotten by dinner. Elvin also hated mages, dangerous beasts useful only in chains. But even that hatred was nothing like what he felt for Gilven. Gilven stirred true black rage in him. Especially after he pressed a knife to his side and spoke about his grandchildren.

About Marna and Tiv. This year, Marna had learned to read signs at the market, and now she read everything out loud, even in places where it would have been better to walk past and not embarrass the adults. Tiv collected and carried pebbles, bits of buttons, and dried beetles in his pockets. His parents had shaken everything imaginable out of his little jacket before a big wash. Every evening, Elvin grumbled that the children had become completely spoiled, that their mother was too soft, and that there was now noise in the house as if there were horse races inside instead of a little brother and sister.

But then Elvin still pulled two honey cakes from his bag for his restless grandchildren.

Tonight, Marna and Tiv were supposed to be waiting for him too. Surely they were counting again on tasty honey cakes and the old man’s funny stories before bed.

But today, Elvin was not sure whether he would return to them.

Gilven Neld pushed the door with his shoulder, and the cell of the young valuable prisoner finally opened.

With grim anger, Elvin thought that the mage was still lucky. Lir was being kept in the lower block for valuable prisoners, but still not where the Castle kept its true fears. That was an entirely different level: deeper, colder, and far more protected from other people’s pity, accidents, and old runic wardens with knives at their sides. Truly valuable and the most dangerous criminals of Veyr were kept there. The ones no one went down to without an order and an entire guard convoy. The ones who were locked behind not one, but two, or even three runic locks at once.

Ironically, just a few days ago, the prison leadership had actually discussed whether to transfer this stupid fisherman’s son there. Elvin had overheard the conversation in passing. Someone upstairs had decided that since the boy was connected to Sakura Arai, it would be better to be safe. Then they had dismissed the idea themselves, because it was too much for a village boy who looked more like a frightened puppy than a threat. The Castle, after all, was confident enough in itself and its protections. Escapes had not happened here for many years, even from the least protected blocks.

Now Elvin understood that the Castle was too confident, and they should not have brushed it off like that. If Lir had been hidden there, on that lower level, Gilven Neld would never have reached him so simply or so quickly.

The guard stood by the wall, feeling the skin under his collar grow cold. There was commotion in the Castle. He could hear it in the distant noise and even feel it in the vibration of the place itself. The Castle was like a living organism, one Elvin had studied for a long time and knew how it was supposed to behave.

The mage entered the cell first.

"Lir," he said shortly.

The boy sat by the wall, knees pulled to his chest. He was very much a village boy: tall, awkward, with a simple frightened face and dirty hair.

At first, Lir flinched at the voice, then saw the figure in guard uniform and pressed his shoulders into the wall.

"I didn’t do anything," he said hoarsely. "I already told you. I just..."

"Get up," Gilven interrupted.

"Please don’t." Lir tried to crawl farther away, though there was only a damp wall behind him. "I don’t know anything. I swear. I wasn’t even supposed to..."

Gilven stepped toward him and grabbed him by the collar.

Lir jerked, but he had no strength left. Elvin saw that at once: the boy had not been beaten half to death, but the Castle knew how to break a person without leaving too many bruises.

"Quiet," the mage said sharply. "I’m not a guard."

Lir froze.

"What?"

"Can you stand?"

"I..."

Gilven yanked him to his feet in one sharp motion and dragged him out of the cell. But then footsteps grew closer in the corridor. Elvin heard them almost at the same time as the mage. Gilven froze sharply, then, without explanation, shoved Lir back into the cell and stepped in after him. He pulled Elvin by the sleeve so roughly that the old man nearly hit his shoulder against the doorframe.

"Quiet," the mage ordered with only his lips.

He pulled the cell door toward himself. After the lockpicks, the lock could no longer latch properly, but from the outside the door still looked closed if no one came too close.

The footsteps approached, and someone passed by the cell.

Gilven stood right by the door, almost merged with the shadow. He held the door with one hand and still held the knife with the other. The mage’s gaze was fixed on the gap between the door and the frame, his attention turned to the footsteps of the guard who could stop and turn around at any moment.

For the first time all this while, Gilven was not watching Elvin. And that was the small opening where something could be done.

Elvin had never considered himself a hero. Heroes died beautifully, and then their names were forgotten by the same people who had demanded a feat from them yesterday. Elvin had always preferred to live ugly, but long. His life consisted of routine, things like work, wages, small arguments with superiors, and family.

But now, if he simply went along, this mage would lead out the valuable prisoner, open the emergency passage, disappear, and then the Castle would begin its investigation. And who would be the first to be blamed? The old runic warden who had opened all the doors for them and had not tried to raise the alarm. The superiors would not listen about knives and threats. Superiors always believed only paper and whoever shouted loudest for someone to blame.

Elvin swept his gaze around the cell. Of course, there was nothing useful in the cell itself. The Castle was not stupid enough to leave a valuable prisoner potential weapons within reach. But by the door, in the narrow niche between the frame and the door, there was an old service mount.

Once, small hand lanterns for night inspections had been kept on such mounts, before runic lamps were installed in the lower block. The lantern itself had been gone for a long time, and the half-ring looked like nothing more than a rusted part of the door. In essence, just a decoration impossible to tear out with bare hands.

But Elvin knew one useful little detail. He himself had once advised them not to remove such mounts “in case of interruptions with runic light.” Back then, one young repairman had laughed that the old man was still preparing for the days when the Castle would have to live by soot and fire again.

And even now, Elvin remembered that the short retaining rod in that mount had not been riveted, because it had been made removable for cleaning and replacing the bracket. All one had to do was press from below, turn it a quarter of a turn, and pull straight toward oneself. A delicate procedure, which was why prisoners would not have guessed it. They would have pulled and jerked at the rod, not knowing it could be taken out if one followed the pattern.

While Gilven was distracted, Elvin carefully reached out. His fingers settled on cold iron. The rod sat tight, rusted at the edge, but it could still come out. Elvin pressed from below, turned it with a barely noticeable movement, and pulled.

The metal did not yield at once. While Elvin worked it loose, the guard beyond the door was already moving mercilessly farther away.

Elvin pulled harder, and the rod finally came out of the bracket. It was short and thin enough to hide in his clothes. Elvin lowered his hand along his body, and the rod slid under the wide sleeve of his work jacket.

"Let’s go," the mage said quietly right then, releasing the door.

Lir stood by the wall, white as chalk, and still looked at him like someone who might be saving him or might be leading him to new torture.

"I don’t understand," he whispered. "Who are you? Why..."

Gilven grabbed him by the shoulder again and led him out of the cell. He whispered something in his ear that Elvin had no chance of hearing. Lir froze so sharply that the mage had to yank him forward by force.

"From Sakura?" he asked loudly, and his voice changed. It became more alive, almost childlike. "She’s alive?"

Gilven grimaced as if someone had slapped him. “Sakura.” He had not wanted Elvin to hear that name, but it was already too late.

"Alive." The mage said nothing else.

Only then did Lir understand that he had to walk, and he moved awkwardly, stumbling.

"She’s really alive? Is she here?"

"Questions later," Gilven snapped.

Elvin walked ahead, feeling the weight of the rod in his sleeve. Farther on, the corridor narrowed and led toward the service door, beyond which began the passage to the healer’s wing and the emergency section. Elvin knew this route too well. Over the years of service, he had opened runic locks here hundreds of times: for guards, for healers, sometimes for those quiet people from the upper offices whose names were better left unasked.

But today, he had to open doors for the strangest company in all his time: a mage and a valuable prisoner. However, they did not know that an iron rod was hidden in Elvin’s sleeve.

"Faster," Gilven threw quietly.

Elvin obeyed. He had not given up. He was simply waiting for the right moment to strike. Last time, he had failed to outsmart the mage, but now he absolutely intended to do it.

Lir walked unevenly. The young man sped up, then stumbled, then tried to turn back toward Gilven again. He was clearly waiting for the mage to properly explain everything, but the mage only stayed silent. The fisherman’s son seemed very simple and stupid compared to him.

Now the guard understood perfectly that he was not dealing with ordinary criminals. All of them were accomplices of that same Sakura Arai, whom only the deaf had not heard of by now. Elvin already hated her as strongly as he hated Gilven. Because of that girl, the world was collapsing, the Castle was losing, and Elvin’s grandchildren risked never seeing their grandfather again.

The first runic door waited for them around the turn. Pale lines shimmered faintly in the damp half-darkness. An ordinary guard would see only iron and stone, but Elvin saw much more. The closing knot, the access circuit, the weak spot at the lower curve.

"Open it," Gilven said.

The knife was closer to Elvin’s side again. The old man slowly raised his hands to the ring and took out the white bone key. For so many years, this object had been part of his work, but now it felt like betrayal.

Elvin pressed the key to the lock, and the runes responded at once. Elvin traced a short curve with the key, then a second, pressed the hidden point below, and the door clicked with familiar obedience.

Lir flinched at the sound. For him, any click here probably already meant something bad.

"Move," Gilven said shortly.

They passed inside. Elvin walked and counted steps. To the next turn. To the second door. To the place where the corridor narrowed and the ceiling dropped lower. There, Gilven would have to walk to the side, almost behind his shoulder. There, Lir would probably stumble again, because the floor by the turn was uneven. There, he could strike.

At the end of the corridor was the turn to the emergency section. Very little remained. The rod against his skin seemed to grow colder. Gilven walked behind Elvin, holding Lir with one hand. The mage was clearly in a hurry, but not so much that he had completely lost his head. He was still on guard.

And yet he was tired.

Elvin could hear it in his breathing. The guard knew how people breathed when they were tired and in pain. He himself often breathed like that when his back locked up yet again and he could not straighten, feeling as if he was about to break.

Beyond the turn, voices sounded again. This time very close, but not in their corridor. Behind the wall, in the healer’s passage. Someone was arguing with one of the junior servants.

Lir involuntarily turned his head toward the voices, and Gilven also looked away for a fraction of a second. In that moment, Elvin struck. He simply pulled the rod from his sleeve and turned with his whole body, the way he had done long ago, back when he had not yet been an old warden, but a young apprentice taught that even a runic mage had to know how to survive if something went wrong.

The iron went in under the mage’s collarbone. It struck and tore through the skin with a wet, crunching sound where the collar had already shifted slightly. Gilven sharply drew in air, but did not scream. He only swayed, and in that short movement, Elvin saw the very thing he had risked everything for.

Something dark slipped from the wound, like a shard of black stone with living light inside. It fell to the floor, struck the slab, and rolled toward the wall, leaving a thin dark-red trail.

Lir stared.

"What is that?.."

Elvin was already drawing breath to shout and call the guards. He still held the rod in his hand, ready to defend himself. He opened his mouth, but Gilven struck first.

The pain should have bent him over and distracted him properly. But the mage did not lose even one extra second.

He lunged forward so sharply that Elvin did not even manage to shout. The blow landed in his throat. The cry stuck somewhere inside, turning into a pathetic rasp. His neck burned like fire.

Elvin recoiled and still tried to strike again. Gilven intercepted his hand. Fingers closed around his wrist, twisted it aside, and pain flared so sharply that the iron rod fell on its own. It struck the stone with a ring and rolled toward the wall.

Elvin tried to strike with his other hand. A pitiful old man’s attempt, with more anger in it than strength. Gilven dodged almost effortlessly, though his face had turned gray from pain and the torn collar was quickly darkening with blood.

"Old fool," the mage breathed.

And he struck Elvin in the temple with the hilt of the knife.

The world swayed. Elvin saw the floor too close. Saw his own hand twisted with pain and the white bone key still hanging at his belt.

Elvin wanted to think of Marna and Tiv, of how they would wait for him by the fireplace in the evening, of what their mother would cook for the whole family for dinner. But his thoughts were spreading apart. The voices in the corridor sounded farther and farther away, as if the Castle itself were moving from him somewhere into the depths.

And then darkness covered Elvin, heavy and dull, like a closed door.
