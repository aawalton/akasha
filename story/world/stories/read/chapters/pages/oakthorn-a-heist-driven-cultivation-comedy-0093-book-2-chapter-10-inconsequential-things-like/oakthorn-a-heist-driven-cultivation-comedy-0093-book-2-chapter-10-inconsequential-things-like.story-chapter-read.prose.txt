The moment Nash’s eyes locked with Cade’s, a flicker of recognition and panic sparked between them like a struck match. Time expanded in that single breath—the tavern’s raucous laughter slowing to a distant roar, the clinking tankards fading to muted echoes as adversaries measured each other across a sea of oblivious patrons.

Then Cade watched Nash bolt, and time crashed back to normal speed.

The Son of Ruin lunged toward the back door, but his path was immediately blocked by a pair of drunken Arbiters who’d chosen that exact moment to stand and sway toward the bar. His core surged again, an angry tempest beneath his skin that threatened to tear free with every labored breath.

“Oi, watch it,” one of the Arbiters slurred, hand dropping instinctively to his sword hilt. “We’re Arbiters. Show some damned respect.”

Cade might’ve considered the command more if it came from someone who didn’t hiccup between each word.

Unlike the three that had risen from their seats next to Nora and the others, these two had clearly inventoried the bottom of over six or seven drinks. Cade was dimly impressed they were both upright, given how dilated their pupils were.

Orro materialized beside Cade, eye patch discarded, hand firmly on his companion’s shoulder in what looked like camaraderie to observers but was actually restraint.

“Apologies,” the assassin grunted, steering Cade toward the main entrance where Nora was already hustling Evie and Chubbs out the door. “My friend can’t handle his drink. Excuse his rude interruption of your well-earned drinks. Here, on us.”

Orro slid several coppers over to the Arbiters in their path.

“See to it he learns how to better hold his drink,” the drunker of the two managed to get out. “And respect. Teach… respect… of his betters.”

Through gritted teeth, Cade hissed, “He’s getting away.”

“And you’re about to explode,” Orro muttered under his breath. “Surviving first, spies second.” He nodded at the two Arbiters. “We’ll be on our way. Honor be with you both.”

Orro steered him around the pair and toward the exit. The unpleasant churning in Cade’s gut told him Orro was right. Whatever was happening inside him took precedence over chasing Nash—especially when chasing the spy might trigger whatever catastrophe was brewing in his core.

They stumbled out into the cool night air, Orro maintaining his grip on Cade’s shoulder with the precise pressure needed to look casual while actually guiding him. The rest of the crew waited in the shadows across the street, tension evident in their rigid postures.

“Move,” Nora ordered as soon as they approached, already leading the way out of town. “Quickly but naturally.”

No one spoke until they’d reached the abandoned mill at the settlement’s edge, a crumbling stone structure that had apparently surrendered to time and weather decades ago. Only when they were safely hidden among its ruined walls did Rayka break the silence.

“What just happened? You both look like you’ve seen Death himself playing cards at the bar.” Her hands were on her hips, blonde hair pulled back into a high ponytail. “Well? Did you?”

Cade assessed her, eyes blinking hard against the sting of the chilly evening wind.

“Worse,” Cade managed, leaning against a moss-covered stone as another wave of magical turbulence rolled through him. “Nash was there.”

“Wait, which one was Nash?” Jer asked, positioning himself at the broken window to keep watch.

“He’s Bernard’s go-to infiltrator,” Gav answered before Cade could, his hands tightening the pink ascot he was retying around his neck. “He’s—not someone you forget.”

“And the fact that he’s here means our competition knows exactly where we are,” Cade finished, closing his eyes as he fought another surge of rebellious magic. “Which is just fantastic.”

“The real question,” Nora interjected, setting Chubbs down on a relatively clean patch of earth, “is how much he heard before Chubbs pointed him out.”

The toddler goddess beamed proudly. “Bad man was listening! His eyes are gross.”

Orro’s expression darkened further, if that was even possible. “If he heard about the breathing apparatus—”

“Damn it! Then Bernard’s crew will be racing us to the outpost,” Cade finished, pushing off from the wall. “We need to move faster than planned.”

“What about the recon mission?” Rayka asked, concerned eyes fixed on her brother. “We were going to observe for a day first.”

Cade shook his head. “No time. We need to hit that outpost tonight.”

“Are you insane?” Elena exclaimed. “It’s an Arbiter outpost. You know, the people who literally make their living hunting down criminals like us?”

“She has a point,” Evie agreed softly. “Perhaps a more measured approach—”

“No,” Cade interrupted, the decision crystallizing in his mind as he spoke. “Nash’s presence changes everything. If he reports back to Bernard, they’ll make their move immediately. We need those masks before they get to them.”

A tense silence fell as the crew exchanged glances. Finally, Nora spoke, her voice level. “So what’s the plan?”

Cade took a deep breath, fighting another wave of discomfort as his magic roiled. “Jer, Gav—tonight you’re our advance team. Scout the outpost, find where they store the masks, and report back. Don’t engage, don’t get spotted. Just intel.”

Jer grinned, already bouncing on the balls of his feet. “Finally, some action.”

“Stealth, Jer,” Cade emphasized. “This is reconnaissance, not a performance.”

“I’m hurt,” Jer replied, clutching his chest dramatically. “When have I ever been anything but the soul of discretion?”

“The Whispering Temple incident,” Elena said immediately.

“The governor’s daughter’s bedroom,” Rayka added.

“Last week when you wanted to invite Emily to our secret hideout,” Orro rumbled.

“Alright, alright!” Jer threw up his hands in surrender. “Message received. I’ll be a shadow. A ghost. A—”

“Quiet,” Gav finished, placing a restraining hand on his companion’s shoulder. “We’ll be quiet. When do we leave?”

“As soon as it’s full dark,” Cade replied, glancing at the deepening twilight visible through the collapsed roof. “One hour, tops.”

He ignored another ripple of pain through his core, focusing on strategy instead of discomfort. “Nora, take Elena and secure the eastern road. Count every Arbiter patrol. We need to know exactly how many we’re dealing with.”

Nora nodded, her hand already on her concealed blade. “And if we encounter Nash?”

“Observe only,” Cade replied firmly. “I want intelligence, not confrontation. Not yet. We have no idea what tricks he and Bernard’s crew might have up their sleeve. But since Bernard is gold rank, he might have one or more on his crew too.”

“Rayka,” he continued, turning to his sister, “food and supplies. We might need to move quickly after tonight.”

“On it,” she confirmed, but her eyes lingered on him with unmistakable concern.

“Evie, you’re with Chubbs. Keep her quiet and, if possible, asleep.”

The toddler goddess, as if on cue, let out a massive yawn before protesting, “I’m not sleepy! I want to help catch the bad man!”

“The most helpful thing,” Cade told her patiently, “is getting your sleep, little one. We’ve got a big day tomorrow.”

“But I’m not—” another yawn interrupted her “—tired.”

“Of course not,” Evie soothed, lifting the child who was already blinking heavily. “We’re just resting our eyes, aren’t we?”

Nora gave him a lingering look as she moved away, concern flickering across her stormy eyes. But she she seemed to bite down on whatever she was going to tell him. Instead, she gave him a quick nod and a thin smile. “See you in a bit.”

“Yeah.”

Within moments, the rest of the crew had dispersed, leaving Cade temporarily alone. He pressed a hand against his chest, feeling the erratic pulse of magic beneath his palm. Like something clawing for escape. He’d felt this way before, and it wasn’t just when Destruction’s Remnant merged with him below Scorn’s temple.

No.

He’d felt this way before then. Before Hugh, even.

A memory knifed through him—sharp, unwanted, yet inescapable:

His father’s face contorted in the firelight, fingers digging painfully into Cade’s small chest as he branded a strange sigil. “This will hurt,” he’d whispered, voice breaking. “But it will hurt more if I don’t. The truth in your blood demands recognition, son.”

The smell of burning flesh. His own screams.

“I’m sorry,” his father had repeated, tears streaming down his weathered face. “Some legacies can’t be outrun.”

The memory shattered as another surge of magic drove Cade to his knees, and a strangled cry escaped through his clenched teeth.

He tasted blood.

“Fuck,” he gasped, hunching over as magical energy coursed through his veins like liquid fire. His core, normally a wellspring of controlled power, felt like a cracked vessel barely containing a tempest.

He needed space. Away from the others. Away from where an accident could hurt someone.

Stumbling to his feet, Cade ducked through a crumbling archway into what must have once been the mill’s storeroom. Moonlight spilled through the collapsed ceiling, illuminating a small, enclosed space perfect for his needs.

Just in time. The magic surged again, stronger than before, and this time Cade couldn’t hold it back. He directed the release upward, toward the open sky, as a chaotic blast of energy erupted from his outstretched hands.

The sight was bewildering—not the pure destructive force he’d come to expect from his core, but something impossible. Flames spiraled upward, freezing into crystalline patterns mid-air before shattering into thousands of glittering ice shards that melted into droplets of water, which then evaporated into steam that twisted into miniature cyclones.

It was as if multiple magical dominions were fighting for control, cycling through manifestations too quickly for any to stabilize.

“That’s new,” Orro’s dry voice came from the shadows behind him.

Cade whirled, surprised despite himself. Even after all these years, Orro’s ability to move undetected never ceased to amaze him. The assassin leaned against the archway, arms crossed, expression inscrutable in the dim light.

“How long you been lurking there?” Cade rasped, one knee still pressed to the dirt, sweat dripping from his brow.

Orro didn’t answer immediately. He watched, assessed, calculated—as he always did. Finally, he moved forward, footsteps deliberately audible. “Your magic’s trying to kill you.”

Not a question. A statement of fact delivered with the blunt force of Orro’s typical candor.

Cade barked a laugh that turned into a wince. “Probably.”

Silence stretched between them, thick with ten years of shared history. Orro finally settled on a fallen beam nearby—close enough to intervene, distant enough to give space. Classic Orro positioning.

“You’re not telling the others,” Orro said.

Again, not a question.

Cade pushed himself up, staggering slightly before finding his balance. “And burden them with another thing they can’t fix? What good would that do?”

“They deserve to know if you’re going to explode and take them with you.”

The accusation hung in the air, sharp-edged and honest in a way only Orro could manage. Ten years ago, Cade might have thrown a punch for less. Now he just absorbed the blow, recognizing the concern beneath the brutality.

Cade’s fingers curled into fists at his sides. “And tell them what, exactly? ‘Hey everyone, my magic’s having a civil war and I might detonate at any moment, but don’t worry about it’?” He laughed, the sound hollow. “They’d never let me out of their sight.”

“That would be the point,” Orro countered.

“It would be a distraction.” Cade pushed himself up, swaying slightly before finding his balance. “They’d be so busy watching me that they’d miss the real threats.” He gestured vaguely toward the town. “Like Nash. Like whatever’s happening in those marshes.”

Orro’s expression didn’t change, but his silence spoke volumes.

“What?! Are you gonna tell me your pissed-off expression isn’t because you secretly agree with me? You know that heads that aren’t fully focused on a job have a nasty habit of falling when things go wrong. We can’t have any more distractions. I—I can’t…”

“I’m not pissed.”

Cade’s anger flared. “Then what are you?!”

“I’m scared,” Orro admitted, the words torn from him like a physical wound. He didn’t look at Cade as he spoke, eyes fixed on his own scarred knuckles. “Fucking terrified, actually.”

The admission hung between them, weighty and rare. Orro admitting fear was like a mountain admitting weakness.

“Orro…”

“You just got over that damned soul curse, and now there’s something else trying to kill you. Something I can’t stab or strangle or intimidate. It’s just…” Orro’s knuckles went white as he strangled his fist into a tight ball. “I can’t help you, and I hate it.”

“Orro, I—” Cade faltered, his anger replaced by remorse and concern for his best friend. “I had no idea. Things just happened so fast, I didn’t even stop to consider how it all affected you. I’m… I’m sorry, friend.”

Cade took a step closer.

“Would it have mattered?” Orro countered, finally looking up. “Would knowing have changed anything? Made you more careful? Made you stop?”

The unspoken accusation hit its mark. Cade winced. “Probably not.”

“Exactly.”

Another silence. This one thicker, filled with all the things they never said to each other. Years of risking their lives together, saving each other countless times, and yet some truths remained too raw to touch.

“It’s my father,” Cade finally admitted, voice barely audible. “The memory that hit me. He branded me when I was a kid. Some kind of... family legacy bullshit.”

Orro’s expression didn’t change, but his posture shifted minutely. Alert. Attentive. “That emblem you hide.”

“You knew?” Cade’s head snapped up.

“I’ve seen the scar.” Orro shrugged, a gesture deceptively casual. “Never asked. Figured if you wanted to talk about someone burning a mark into your chest, you would.”

“Hells.” Cade ran a hand through his hair, uncomfortable with how exposed he felt. “I thought—”

“That I missed it?” A ghost of something like amusement crossed Orro’s face. “I notice everything about the people who could kill me in my sleep.”

“Charming.”

“Practical.”

Another pause, this one less tense. The familiar rhythm of their banter creating a safe space amid the chaos.

“It’s getting worse,” Cade admitted quietly. “Whatever’s inside me... it’s fighting against the goddess fragment. Like oil and water, but with more explosions.”

“Do you think it’s the brand?” Orro suggested, his voice carefully neutral. “Legacy magic runs deep. You can’t outrun blood.”

The words hit too close to a nerve. “Says the man who’s spent his entire life trying to do exactly that.”

Orro’s lips curled in a tight smile, but Cade saw the pain hidden behind it. “Low blow, Stormhollow.”

“Sorry.” Cade meant it. “That was—”

“True,” Orro interrupted, surprising him. “Which is why I know what I’m talking about.”

The assassin stood, moving with that liquid grace that made him so lethal. He approached Cade, stopping just close enough that Cade could see the tiny scar bisecting his left eyebrow—a childhood injury from before they’d met, one of the few stories Orro had never shared.

“Rayka’s worried,” Orro said abruptly.

The shift in topic made Cade blink. “She always worries.”

“Not like this.” Something in Orro’s tone changed, became almost defensive. “She sees things in you the rest of us miss.”

“Including you?” Cade couldn’t resist the dig, watching Orro’s jaw tighten.

“Especially me.”

And there it was—the tension that always simmered beneath their friendship. Rayka. The one person they both loved more than anything, in entirely different ways.

“You and her...” Cade started, then stopped, unsure how to navigate this territory they’d spent years carefully avoiding. He rubbed the back of his neck, really hoping Nora or one of the others would barge in and save them from this conversation.

“Is none of your business,” Orro finished for him, expression shuttered.

“She’s my sister,” Cade countered. “That makes it my business.”

“Does it?” Orro’s dark eyes held his, challenging. “Or is this just another way for you to avoid talking about whatever the hells is happening to you?”

Cade’s eyes hardened. “That’s not—”

“It is.” Orro’s voice was quiet but firm. “You’re deflecting. It’s what you do when you’re scared.”

“I’m not scared,” Cade lied automatically.

Orro just looked at him, saying nothing. The silence stretched, becoming its own accusation.

“Fine,” Cade finally conceded, the word scraped raw from his throat. “I’m fucking terrified, okay? Happy now?”

“Ecstatic,” Orro replied dryly, but something in his posture eased slightly. “Now we can actually talk about what we’re going to do.”

“We,” Cade repeated, the word catching in his throat.

“Unless you’ve somehow convinced yourself I’d let you handle this alone.” There was an edge of genuine offense in Orro’s voice. “Ten years, Cade. Ten years of your idiotic plans and my brilliant rescues.”

Despite everything, Cade felt a smile tug at his lips. “Whoa there, half-blade. I think you’re revising history a bit there.”

“I think your memory’s selective.” A ghost of something like affection crossed Orro’s face. “But that’s beside the point. And half-blade? We’re digging that up now?”

“You fight with a hilt, Orro. It has a tiny orange decoration on top. You’re practically begging for someone to ask you about other issues you might have with size,” Cade replied with a genuinely wicked smile.

“I know how to kill a person in over a hundred ways. Want to be my practice dummy? I seriously can’t tell the difference between you and them sometimes,” Orro shot back, his own smile returning.

“I can talk, Orro. That’s the difference.”

“Don’t I know it,” the assassin moaned playfully.

The tension between them eased, morphing back into the comfortable camaraderie that had sustained them through countless trials. This was how they functioned best—oscillating between brutal honesty and protective deflection, always finding the balance that kept them both sane.

“So,” Orro said after a moment, “what’s the real plan for tonight? Not the one you told the others.”

Cade grinned, unsurprised that Orro had seen through him. “That obvious, huh?”

“To me? Always.”

“Fair enough.” Cade leaned forward, lowering his voice despite the fact that they were alone. “I’m going with Jer and Gav.”

Orro’s eyebrows rose fractionally. “With your magic acting up? That’s riskier than usual, even for you.”

“I can control it,” Cade insisted, though the recent display suggested otherwise. “And I need to see the outpost layout myself. Something about this whole setup feels off.”

“The masks.”

“Exactly. Why would Arbiters need special breathing apparatus for the Blightmarshes? They’ve patrolled that boundary for decades without issue.”

“Unless something’s changed,” Orro suggested.

“That’s what I need to find out,” Cade agreed. “And I can’t do that from here.”

Orro studied him for a long moment, then nodded once. “I’m coming too.”

“No,” Cade said firmly. “I need you here, protecting the others in case Nash decides to pay us a visit.”

“Rayka and Nora are more than capable—”

“Of fighting, yes. Of anticipating a spy’s tactics? That’s your specialty.” Cade paused, then added the argument he knew would convince Orro. “Rayka needs you here.”

A flash of irritation crossed Orro’s face. “Using my feelings for your sister against me is low, even for you.”

“I prefer to think of it as strategically allocating resources,” Cade replied with a wink. “Besides, I—”

The rest of his sentence died in his throat as a distant sound caught his attention. Both men froze, heads turning toward the mill’s entrance.

“Was that—” Orro began.

“Screaming,” Cade confirmed, his enhanced senses picking up what others might miss. “From town.”

Another sound followed, this one unmistakable even to normal hearing—a thunderous crash followed by shouts of alarm and the distinctive roar of fire.

Cade was on his feet in an instant, Orro beside him. “I smell smoke,” he said, his silver-ranked senses detecting the acrid scent even at this distance.

They exchanged a single look—no words needed—before sprinting toward the mill’s entrance where the rest of the crew was already gathering, faces tense with concern.

“What’s happening?” Cade demanded as they emerged.

The young thief’s gaze locked on the orange glow now visible above the town rooftops, the distant screams growing louder as panicked figures began spilling onto the road.

“The tavern,” Rayka said grimly. “It’s on fire.”
