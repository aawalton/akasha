Expert Meta Steel Spatha (Uncommon)

Weapon type: Sword

Material: Steel

Aspect: Meta (moderate)

Effects: Improved ability to cut steel and retain its edge while doing so.

Moderate! Caleb realized, wondering what could be different, but the answer was immediately apparent. My enchantments must be polluting the purity of the steel.

This along with his realization that stats need not come from equipment but could be placed on items like ingots opened up a host of possibilities.

He pulled himself from his thoughts and wiped the blade clean before handing it to his master.

His master looked at the blade, and then back at Caleb.

The layering had produced a beautiful watered pattern all along the blade’s bevel.

Gingerly he took the blade from Caleb and held it up to the light. Holding the tang, he swung it, feeling the weight of the unfinished blade.

“This will do,” Master Dimel said.

The gathered apprentices broke out in cheers of congratulations at the praise, but Caleb felt that something was missing. He was used to impressing his master, and his reaction didn’t seem impressed. Almost like his master had expected even more from him.

Or maybe I’m just seeing in him what I feel.

He knew he could have done better if he’d not had to hide his abilities. How could his master know Caleb could do better?

It’s just in my head, he told himself, allowing himself to smile.

When the cheers settled down, Dimel spoke. “I’ll send this to Evans, he will see a proper handle made for it. Would you like to keep it or sell it?”

“Keep it,” Caleb said automatically.

He planned to level, and to do that, he’d need to fight. It was about time he got around to creating proper equipment.

His fellow—former fellow—apprentices dragged Caleb away to the tavern, where he was given his first opportunity to test what an extra 2 points in constitution would do for his ability to hold his liquor.

It turned out that it did a lot, but not enough.

***

Caleb woke, very aware that something was wrong but not sure how he knew.

Cautiously, he opened his eye to see Kelia glaring at him from the chair in his room at home.

“That didn’t count,” she said.

“What? What didn’t count?”

The last thing he remembered was going out to celebrate, and from the splitting headache he had, whatever benefits his new constitution had had been defeated by copious amounts of alcohol.

“You don’t remember?” she said, relieved.

“Remember what” Caleb asked, sitting up, getting tired of the guessing game.

“We… got married,” she said quickly.

“We what?!” Caleb jumped out of bed and realized he wasn’t wearing anything.

She didn’t repeat herself.

“Please tell me what happened,” he said, sitting defeated and covering himself with his blanket.

“Gris and I came back to town and there was another party going on. You…” She stood and began pacing. “…were very drunk by then. Then you conviced me to drink… and Gris stole our constitution ingots.”

“Oh…” He understood now how he could have gotten to such a state.

“Yeah… So, you proposed to me in front of everyone. You pulled a nail out of a table and formed it into a ring,” she said, holding the ring up to show him. “Using you magic, but no one noticed.”

“Well,” Caleb said, unable to poke some fun at the dire situation, “what did you say?”

“I said yes, of course!” She quickly added, “But I was also very drunk. Anyway, then everyone insisted we get married that instant, so… we did.”

“Wow,” Caleb said, at a loss for words.

“Wow,” Kelia repeated.

“Does it count, though,” Caleb asked. “Like, are we married.”

Kelia collapsed into her chair.

“I think we are,” she admitted.

“That’s great!” Caleb said, standing up again, this time holding a blanket.

“How is this great?!” she demanded.

“Because we are married!”

“Huh,” Kelia said, as if she hadn’t considered that.

She hadn’t, after all, wanted a traditional marriage ceremony or gift. Those had been Caleb’s insistences. In fact, if she could have chosen how they were to have gotten married, this was—while not exactly how she’d have preferred it—not too far off. In her ideal wedding, the drinking wouldn’t have preceded the ceremony.

“Right?” Caleb said.

“But you wanted it to be special. You wanted to make me a gift,” she said, concern for him clear in her voice.

“I can still do that,” he said. “You did get a ring, that’s sort of traditional—it’s not knife, but it’ll do. How did the town react?”

“Surprisingly well, actually.”

They talked for a while longer, working themselves off the ledge they’d thought they’d been on, and both came around on the idea.

“What would you like for breakfast my dear wife?” Caleb said, after getting dressed.

“If you’re going out, bring back eggs,” Caleb’s father’s voice shouted through the door. “And while you’re out, maybe find a new place to stay, since you’re a married journeyman and all that.”

His voice was threatening, but Caleb knew his father was joking—at least partially.

He blanched, however, not because of the threat, but because of the implication that his father had heard their whole conversation—and who knew what else.

“Elders,” Caleb said in prayer. “We need to find a place to live.”

***

To avoid any extra gossip—not that there was any chance they’d avoid it altogether—Kelia snuck out the back window of Caleb’s room like when they were kids. Caleb got dressed, left through the front door, and waved sheepishly to the neighbors, who were all sitting outside watching him. They all seemed disappointed that it was just Caleb emerging.

Don’t these people have anything better to do? he thought.

It was nearly noon, and as he walked through town, he realized the back-to-back celebrations might have been a bit too much for the village to handle and other people were taking the day off.

Caleb should have taken the day off, but there was too much to do. So much had changed in so short a time and he felt he was playing catch-up. Just a week ago he was an uninitialized, unmarried blacksmith’s apprentice. Now none of that were true and he had a baby dragon he’d been neglecting.

Thinking of that baby dragon, Caleb went to his former master—though he’d always call him Master no matter what their relative ranks became. This was one man who wouldn’t be sleeping off a party.

Just as he expected, Dimel was at work at his forge, hammering out a breastplate in an attempt to replicate the one Caleb had repaired. Caleb gazed at the man’s instinctive, if clumsy, mana control. It was like a blind man trying to copy a drawing by feeling for the lines on the original—not impossible but extremely difficult, requiring a level of skill in drawing few could achieve.

“Good morning,” Caleb said, drawing Master Dimel’s attention.

Dimel greeted him warmly, only teasing him a little about the night before.

Once they were through the pleasantries, Caleb said, “I wanted to offer a trade.”

“And what exactly would you trade?”

“I’ll give you the items I found in the cavern in exchange for a fair amount of steel,” Caleb proposed.

“Ha. ‘Fair’? I’d have to give you all the steel in my forge and keep supplying it to you until I died.”

“That’s kind of the point,” Caleb said, then quickly amended. “Not you dying. I just want to secure a source of metal. I’m going to make myself a lot of tools to really turn my mill into a forge. I won’t be selling anything for a while and can’t afford to wait for business to come my way.”

Not to mention I have a hungry steel-gorging dragon.

“Bah, don’t worry about that last part. I’ve already had people asking about when you will start taking orders. But still, I’d be in your debt for years.”

His master paused to think about it for a while, and then decided.

Caleb gathered the items he’d made and handed them over to his master. Once more Dimel studied each with a practiced eye.

“I always wanted to give you this back,” Caleb said, handing Dimel back the hammer he’d used for years. “I don’t need it anymore, and I think it’s best if you give it to another apprentice.”

Dimel narrowed his gaze at Caleb, then looked from him to the hammer, to the spear he held and then the sword still waiting for a handle.

“Alright, but… I’m adding a condition,” Dimel said after some consideration.

He got up and moved to his back room and his rack of special steels, where he pulled out another billet.

“Make something from this,” he said, handing it to Caleb. “Really make something from it.”

Caleb sensed it was another piece of meta steel, this one with far less flaws. The way his master said “really” struck him.

He knows, he realized.

“How?” Caleb asked.

Dimel guessed the correct intention behind the vague question.

“You think I wouldn’t recognize the work of my own apprentice?” he scolded him.

He pulled Caleb in close and asked, “You didn’t do it, did you?”

“No,” Caleb said honestly. “I didn’t pick Blacksmith, but… I didn’t pick Frost Dragon Hunter either.”

To give more meaning to his words, Caleb used his Imperium to lift the billet from the table to his hand.

His master’s eyes grew wide, but he made no other signs of his shock.

“You best be careful,” Dimel advised.

“I know,” Caleb agreed, “I will be. And… later, let’s talk. I might be able to give you some… pointers. I can see things now. I understand so much more of what’s happening beneath the hammer strokes.”

“I’d like that,” Dimel said, not taking offense at being offered pointers from a journeyman.

He was a man who’d studied his craft his whole life, slowly and methodically making up for a lack of Magical Perception with trial, error, and persistence.

He would not pass up a new perspective.
