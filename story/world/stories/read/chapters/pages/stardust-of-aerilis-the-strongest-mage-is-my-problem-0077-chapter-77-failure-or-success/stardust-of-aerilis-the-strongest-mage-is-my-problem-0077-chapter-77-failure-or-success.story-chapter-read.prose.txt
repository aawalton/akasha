Virelle moved.

Her hand snapped up. A dome of pale lavender mana sprang into being around Aiven and her, a clean, smooth sphere a pace across.

It lasted half a heartbeat.

CRACK.

The dome shattered. Not gradually. Not with the slow give of a wall under pressure. It simply ceased, the way a piece of glass ceases when something stronger than glass passes through it, and lavender shards dissipated in a fine glittering mist around them.

Aiven felt his breath leave him.

He had not even seen Lucien cast, but he could guess. It was the gravity magic used to immobilize his targets. Virelle knew it could be blocked despite it being invisible, so she casted the barrier to protect them.

Virelle was already moving again.

She did not curse. She did not hesitate. Her right hand snapped down to her side, lavender light gathering at her open palm in a tight, vibrating column, and then she fired.

A beam of pure mana, the width of a man’s wrist, lanced across the hall at Lucien.

It struck him.

It did not strike him.

Aiven could not tell which. The beam reached Lucien, and Lucien was still there, and the beam was not. The light simply ended a foot from his coat, with no flash, no impact, no scatter. It was as if the hall had quietly decided that the beam had never existed at all.

Lucien tilted his head.

“Ah,” he said, in a tone of mild academic interest.

Aiven’s body moved on instinct.

He pushed off the floor of the hall with a flicker of flight, angling himself toward Lyra, toward the dais, toward the only thing in this room that mattered. The wind around him gathered at his back. He covered three paces in the time it took to draw a breath.

Then the floor took him.

Not the floor itself. The weight above him. The air. Something heavy closed onto his shoulders, and his knees gave out, and his obsidian palm slapped down against the polished stone of the dais ramp with a crack that traveled the length of his arm.

He could not lift his head.

He could not lift his ribs to breathe.

“Master!”

Virelle’s voice was sharp and high. He had never quite heard her say it like that. The two syllables came out together like a struck bell.

He heard her snap her hand up.

He heard the air sing.

Three mana spears, each the length of a halberd, each humming with the pitch of a stretched wire, hurtled across the hall in a tight diagonal spread, aimed at Lucien from three angles.

All three simply stopped existing a foot from his body.

“Hm,” Lucien said. “An old trick.”

Aiven’s teeth were grinding.

Gravity. He had spent the past three days learning gravity, in its small, polite, lift-a-stone form. Lucien had simply weaponized it the way a master uses a chisel where an apprentice uses a hammer. Direction, intensity, locus.

He could not push out.

He could not push up.

But he had also, over the past three days, learned to take a wind at his back. That had nothing to do with mana being lifted off him. That had to do with the air around him being moved.

He reached for his flight, not as flight, but as wind.

He felt for the underside of his arm.

He nudged.

His arm rose, just an inch. Enough to put his palm to the dais ramp.

He asked the dais.

FWUMP.

A six-foot pillar of dais-stone erupted directly under Lucien’s feet at the speed of an angry catapult.

Lucien’s legs went out from under him.

The gravity on Aiven’s back vanished.

He was upright before the air had finished settling.

Lucien was twenty feet up and rising on the pillar, his robe whipping around him, his hand already raised to do something about it.

Aiven did not give him time.

He pivoted, brought his obsidian arm forward, and triggered the palm vent.

The mana net unfolded out of his palm in an unfurling lattice of cyan-white threads, expanding mid-air into a wide rectangular weave that sang as it crossed the hall. It reached Lucien.

It ceased existing a foot from him.

The threads simply unraveled into nothing, the cyan light going out the way a candle goes out, all of them at once.

“Damn it,” Aiven hissed.

Lucien was already firing.

He jumped down from the pillar, opened his hand, and a beam of light the colour of cooling iron lanced down at Aiven.

Aiven threw himself sideways.

The beam carved past him close enough that the small hairs on his cheek crisped. It struck the floor where he had been, and there was no impact. The floor where he had been was simply gone, a clean disc of absence three paces across, the stone neatly removed as if it had never been there.

Aiven’s heart hammered against his ribs.

If that had hit me, he thought.

He did not finish the thought.

Lucien landed lightly at the floor and raised his hand again, calmly, like a teacher pointing at a chalkboard.

Before the gravity force could hit Aiven, a mana barrier appeared on top of him and shattered.

Virelle had bought him a half-second.

He took it.

Aiven dropped low, twisted his shoulders, and reached for the wind behind him.

Not flight. Not lift. Just a hard, focused shove of air at his back.

FWOOM.

The wind hit. He shot forward across the floor of the hall at the speed of a thrown spear, the obsidian arm leading, his coat snapping behind him in the rush. Lucien was twelve paces away. Then nine. Then six.

Lucien’s hand was already coming up.

But Virelle was faster.

Three glowing red sigils bloomed in the air between Aiven and Lucien.

They snapped open like flowers.

WHOOSH.

A wall of fire poured out of them, broad and chest-high, a roaring orange curtain that filled the hall between Lucien and the dais. Lucien jumped back, his robe whipping, his hand pausing for a fraction of a second longer than it had paused for anything yet.

The fire did not absorb.

It was elemental. It was not pure mana.

Aiven’s lips peeled back from his teeth.

He did not slow down.

He shifted the obsidian arm forward, triggered the discharge vent, and the cyan etchings along the back of the knuckles flared up into a single hot line of light.

He hit the fire-wall.

He swept through it.

The flame parted around the obsidian, hissed off his coat, and on the other side of it Lucien was there, mid-step, hand still up, eyes widening for the smallest possible fraction of a second.

Aiven punched him.

CRACK.

The obsidian fist took Lucien squarely across the jaw, and Lucien, who had not been touched by anything in this fight until this moment, who had absorbed beams and unraveled nets and shrugged off two domes of mana barrier like a man brushing snow off a shoulder, finally felt something.

He went down.

He hit the floor of the hall on his side, hard, his hood flying back, his hair spilling loose. He was, for a brief and beautiful moment, undignified.

Aiven straightened, breathing hard.

His mind was racing, but his clerk-brain had already filed the relevant rows. The beam. The spears. The net. The barriers. All pure mana. All absorbed.

The pillar. The fire. The fist.

All gone through.

There was no time to tell Virelle. He did not have the breath for it. He simply lifted his palm and fired the first thing that was not pure mana.

ZRRT.

A jagged white-blue lightning bolt cracked across the gap at Lucien, where he was still half-down.

Lucien’s hand snapped up. A dense, layered lavender shield, three deep, sprang up between him and the bolt. The lightning struck it. The shield held.

But Aiven had what he needed.

The shield had held against the lightning. It had not absorbed it.

Lucien was using mana defensively. Which meant the absorption was not a passive aura. It was a directed effect. Which meant it cost him to maintain. Which meant.

Which meant.

Virelle was already two steps ahead of him.

She had seen the same thing he had, and she had not needed words for it either. Her hands rose. Both of them, this time. Above them, the air of the hall ignited.

Spell circles. Dozens. They unfolded in the air around her like petals, each one a different size, each one glowing the deep red of a furnace, each one a focusing rune for the same simple element.

Fire.

She did not say a chant. She did not need to. The circles snapped into focus and discharged at once.

Dozens of fireballs erupted across the hall, each the size of a man’s head, each homing on Lucien with the lazy malice of a swarm of hornets.

Lucien moved very quickly.

He rolled, came up to one knee, and threw both hands out. A much larger mana shield, fully spherical this time, bloomed around him at a radius of perhaps two paces.

The fireballs hit.

Each one struck the shield and broke against it in a wash of red and orange. The shield held, then warped, then began to bow inward under the weight of the bombardment.

By the fifteenth fireball, it was visibly cracked.

By the twentieth, it was a lattice of fractures held together by Lucien’s will alone.

Aiven was already moving.

He dashed forward through the smoke and the heat, the obsidian arm pulled back, and as he ran he reached again for the place inside his chest where the lightning lived. Smaller, faster, angrier. He found it. He grabbed it.

He channeled it into the Armvil.

The cyan etchings on his arm went white, and by the time he reached Lucien’s broken shield he was holding, essentially, a lightning bolt with a fist around it.

He punched.

The shield exploded.

Lucien staggered back three full steps, his shoulders rocked, his composure cracked across the middle for the first time today.

Aiven did not pause.

“Now!” he shouted.

Virelle was already in motion.

She had been waiting for the word. Her hand was already up, her fingers already crooked in the specific small claw-shape she used for lightning, and she fired.

A bolt of pure white-blue lightning, fatter and brighter than any of Aiven’s, snapped across the hall and struck Lucien squarely in the chest.

Lucien could not move.

His arms locked up. His back arched.

He was, for the small precious window of perhaps three seconds, paralyzed.

Aiven was already past him.

He hit the dais ramp at a sprint, took the steps two at a time, and reached Lyra in three strides. She had not moved. She had been watching the fight with that same wary uncertainty on her face, her hands at her sides, her brown scarf hanging loose at her shoulder.

He grabbed her hand.

“Lyra,” he gasped. “With me. Now.”

She did not resist.

Her fingers closed around his.

Her eyes flickered, just briefly, behind him at Lucien. Then back to him.

She said nothing.

His mind was already running the steps.

Get to Virelle. Touch all three. Use the teleportation artifact. Out.

He pivoted, gathered the wind at his back the way he had minutes ago, and shoved off the dais. Lyra came with him. He flew, half-controlled, half-falling forward, the obsidian arm tucked at his side, Lyra’s hand in his, the floor of the Fold blurring beneath him. Virelle was perhaps fifteen paces away, hand still raised from the lightning strike, her eyes already on him as he came.

He saw her face as he closed the distance.

He did not have time to read what was in it.

He reached for the silk-wrapped talisman in his coat pocket with his free hand.

And then the Fold turned.

It was not a flash. It was not a tear. It was a slow, soft, deeply wrong dropping, the way a floor drops in a dream when you are not expecting the next step to be missing. The hall greyed. The air thinned. Aiven’s lungs forgot, for a moment, how to draw breath, and his ears filled with a low, pleasant hum that was the sound of two layers of reality folding past each other.

Then sunlight.

Grass under his hands.

The smell of salt and burnt wood.

Aiven hit the ground of the islet hard.

He rolled. His shoulder caught a thirteen-day-old earth pillar and slammed into it with a thump that knocked the rest of the breath out of him. Lyra landed beside him, lighter, the way a person lands who has been thrown by a teleport rather than tackled.

He scrambled up.

His ears were still ringing.

“Lyra,” he gasped. “Lyra. Are you all right?”

She was already pushing herself upright on her elbows. Her hair had fallen forward over her face. She brushed it back with a shaking hand.

“I am,” she said. “I am, Aiven. I’m here.”

He grabbed her shoulders. He looked at her. The brown scarf. The deep blue eyes. The honey-blonde hair. He had her. He had her.

He almost laughed.

Then he stopped.

Something was wrong.

He looked down at his free hand.

He had not pulled the talisman out of his pocket. He had been reaching for it as the Fold dropped, but his fingers had not actually closed around it. He had not had time.

He had not used it.

His other hand went to his coat pocket. The silk wrapping was still there. He could feel its outline through the fabric. He pulled it out and unfolded the silk with shaking fingers.

The talisman was inside.

Whole. Intact. Unused. Its single charge sitting in it like a small held breath.

He stared at it.

His head turned.

He looked around the islet.

The grass. The pillars. The cliff. The scout-ship, sitting at its dock. The pale haze still on the sea.

Pelka, who had come running halfway down the ramp at the sound of their arrival, was standing frozen at the foot of it, both hands clapped over her mouth.

There were two people on the grass.

Aiven, on his knees, talisman in his hand.

Lyra, beside him, brushing her hair back.

Virelle.

Virelle was not there.

Aiven’s mouth opened.

No sound came out.

He turned, slowly, in a full circle on the grass. As if turning might place her somewhere his first look had missed. As if there might be a corner of the islet she was simply standing in, hands clasped behind her back, hovering an inch off the ground, that he had failed to check.

There was no corner.

There was no Virelle.

The islet was very quiet.

A single seabird called once, far below the cliff, and went silent.
