When I lift my head and look at more than just the roc’s back, I easily answer my own question. Seeing might be believing but I still blink for a moment at what is before me. And then a force blade slices into the roc’s flesh only a foot away from my head and I realise that we can’t stay here. Exchanging a quick look with Noir, we both leap into the air to avoid friendly fire.

It seems incredible, and I don’t think even the roc had realised we’d sunk so quickly through the air, but the way it’s partly submerged in the sea is undeniable proof of the success of our efforts. Noir circles over me as I hover in midair and watch in tired amazement as the battle moves into its final stage. The roc is down and everyone is determined to keep it that way.

Several ships have sent grappling lines to hold onto the roc, their sharp claws digging into the Tier four’s corporeal flesh. At the same time, Water Mages have wrapped ropes of water around its wings, tail, and neck. Every time it bites through one of the restraints holding it down, they replace it with another. Even Elarion is joining in, his vine-like ice creeping over the roc and making its movements weaker wherever it touches. Cold will probably work better than the heat of my lava, thinking about it.

Of course, it’s not like the roc has just given up – it’s fighting as much as it can. I hear Layton’s ship groan loudly as the roc rears up against the grappling hooks embedded in its flesh and threatens to drag the ship over. At the same time, one of its wings buffets another ship, a loud booming, splintering sound filling the air.

However, at least it can’t bring its claws into play, stuck on its front as it is, and the different ships have wisely stayed well away from anywhere it can reach easily with its beak. Its neck dexterity is far less than I would have expected, fortunately for us. Perhaps that was something it gave up in an Evolution. It also seems far weaker than it was up in the air – whether due to the rock, the water, or the cold from Elarion’s ice, I don’t know. Probably it’s a combination of them all.

With the roc now within reach, everyone who had previously been helpless on the deck as it attacked is finally free to exact vengeance.

Arrows, spears, fireballs, chunks of earth, blades of wind or force, patches of eerie-looking mist that rots the corporeal features it comes into contact with…I can’t identify all the attacks being used, but their impact is obvious. Individually, they would have little effect. Together, though, is another story.

The thought crosses through my mind that perhaps I should feel pity for the roc – we’re ants taking down a giant. Yet even as I ask myself the question, I realise that I don’t feel sympathy in the slightest. We didn’t seek this fight, and it’s taken far too much from us. I might not cheer when it finally gives up the ghost, but I do feel immense satisfaction at surviving what seemed at first to be an impossible situation.

We’ve done it, I tell Noir tiredly. My channels feel raw in a way I’m only acknowledging now – the amount of mana I’ve pressed through them is more than I’m accustomed to. But I don’t think that anyone will complain if I stay as an observer right now. And if they do, next time they can be the one to pour lava over a Tier four from above.

I frown as I see a dark green feline jump down from the deck of my ship and start ripping into the flesh of the roc, leaping deftly to the side whenever the flesh turns incorporeal to avoid falling through and into the water below. Trouble? No – Ninja. And then my frown deepens as a black shape seems to split from her.

Swooping closer, confusion goes through me. Ninja is bigger, her wings wider. And her slashing attacks slice through the reinforced flesh of the roc far more easily than they should.

Ninja…you Evolved? I ask incredulously. I can’t believe I missed that.

I did, packleader, she agrees, feeling more at peace than she has for a long time.

Congratulations! I tell her, delighted on her behalf. When did it happen?

She proceeds to tell me a tale that has me staring at her in bemusement. I didn’t even know that beasts could move while in a state of Evolution. I can only hope that it hasn’t done her any damage – I make her promise that she’ll let me check her over as soon as the most urgent things – like killing this roc – are done.

As for what she’s gained from it, her magically-enhanced claws are definitely an improvement, but it’s this whole shadow clone thing that is a real game-changer. It’s like what we were practising with Bond Teleport, but better, because Ninja can swap with her shadow clone without cost or build-up time. We’ll have to test exactly what she can do with the clone – already I see that it has a physical presence of its own, even if its attacks are far less effective than Ninja’s. I also wonder if it has a maximum range to which the shadow can be sent away from Ninja.

Other people from the ships are emboldened by Ninja taking the fight to the Tier four – and the presence of Kalanthia who is still in the middle of the roc’s back and using her earth to pierce the roc deeply in its body – and leap down to join in too with their weapons bared.

Unfortunately, without Ninja’s ability to teleport away from flesh that suddenly becomes incorporeal, several of the fighters fall into the body of the roc. The lucky ones fall straight through and are pulled half-drowned a minute later from the ocean by the Water Mages. The unlucky ones don’t survive being trapped in the roc’s body when its air-magic shifts back into flesh. I wince at one cut-off scream and the way the hands that had been grasping at the solid flesh around suddenly go limp when rescuers are only a couple of paces away.

I expect their enthusiasm to be daunted by the risk, but I’ve underestimated how frustrated some of our melee fighters have been in this battle – and probably in the previous too. Instead of giving up, they just try to make it safer.

A squad from Layton’s ship tie themselves to their vessel with ropes, their compatriots above pulling them sharply out of danger whenever their footing disappears beneath them. Vines of ice solidify into an ice floe near Elarion’s ship and the fighters pilot it like a raft, the floe too big to disappear into any of the incorporeal patches. Maxen leads his own group of fighters, cooperating with Kalanthia to use sections of rock she creates for them. The fighters from the other ships are no different: all find their own means of overcoming the difficulties they face.

During all of this, the roc writhes, flails, knocks people over and sends ships rocking, but is unable to free its head or its wings from the sea. The combined might of all the Water Mages on our ships has to all be concentrated on this single aspect of the battle, but they are utterly indispensable, as can be seen when their bindings accidentally slip once and the hole in Elarion’s ship doubles in size with a single thrash of its wing.

It’s slow work, but ultimately inevitable. The roc’s flesh shows our progress – from healing every wound in moments, its regeneration slows and then begins ignoring minor wounds in favour of major ones. The alchemists get into the battle now, directing potions down into the raw and open flesh. Acids, poisons, and probably a good amount of mana-inhibition potions enter the roc’s bloodstream.

Its attempt at magical attacks – limited as they were under water – cease entirely and its physical attempts grow weaker and weaker.

Trusting that the others have it under control, I fly towards my ship. I need to investigate the damage there – because even from where I am now, I can see it’s extensive.

Noir flies with me, though fear and reluctance leaks through our Bond. It increases as I redirect our flight to the quarterdeck – or what remains of it after it was crushed by the mast.

I land lightly on the splintered deck; Noir circles nearby, not seeming willing to actually land. And I think I know why – leaking out around the crushed mast where the wheel used to be, there’s a large pool of partly-dried blood.

Noir…. What happened to Captain Tolly? I ask gently, doing my best to keep my dismay from leaking over to him. I’d suspected that something bad had happened – that Captain Tolly hadn’t been with Noir when I’d caught up to him in the tornado had been an undeniable sign. But it had been possible that he’d fallen into the waves instead – and if he’d managed to survive the choppy water, we might have been able to rescue him. But Noir’s sense of failure and the quantity of blood I’m seeing here are not good signs.

Still, I start carefully digging in the wreckage. It’s always possible that the captain is clinging onto life.

I…I tried to get him out, Noir tells me, wracked with guilt. But he was tied so tightly…. I couldn’t cut through his ropes fast enough and the wooden tree was falling and–

It’s alright, I cut him off, doing my best to press reassurance into our Bond. Though there’s the mean-spirited part of me that wants to blame him for the death of my ship’s captain, the much greater part of me knows that he did what he could. Tolly was dedicated to the good of the ship and everyone aboard – you didn’t tie him to the wheel, nor did you cause the mast to fall. If he’s dead, it’s not your fault.

Grief swells within Noir, not for Tolly himself, I don’t think – they didn’t know each other well enough – but for the death of someone Noir had tried to protect. I send him my understanding, and share with him my memory of Spike. He, too, was someone I tried to protect, and who I utterly failed. And, unlike Noir, I had far more culpability in that than the alcaoris did in this matter.

It’s hard work to lift the mast – it’s wider around than my arms can reach, and more than thirty metres long, making it awfully heavy. But among the assortment of useful and unusual items in my Inventory, I have a magical equivalent of a jack, bought from the carpenter who agreed to mentor Loran.

I wasn’t sure if it would be capable of lifting something as heavy as a mainmast, but its enchantments glow steadily and it works, albeit slowly. I would feed it with more magic if I weren’t afraid that I might burn it out. Instead, I just have to be patient – a big challenge for me.

Little by little, the mast lifts. Even as the mast rises, the scent of blood in the air intensifies. Swallowing, I set my lips in a grim line and just keep on pumping the jack.

It soon becomes very clear that the wheel and control hub of the ship have taken extensive damage. I don’t even need to see the details to know that they will both take a good deal of time to repair. That doesn’t even take into account the masts that have been either knocked over entirely or are hanging on by a few splinters.

Ninja’s story of a chunk of ice piercing the ship’s hull is worrying because it indicates the physical shielding was completely down. Now, seeing the ruins of the wheel and the crystals that Tolly used to control the shields, I have to wonder whether it was deactivated by that rather than worn away by impacts.

The former is actually more problematic – a shield that’s overwhelmed will regenerate itself over time. A shield that’s deactivated will stay like that until it’s reactivated – and at present, the control to do so is destroyed.

Then I remember that I have a secondary control in my cabin and I breathe a sigh of relief – that had better be my next step, and quickly. There are still chunks of ice floating around and every impact just worsens the damage and risks another hole.

Finally, I’ve managed to lift the mast high enough that I can see past the ruin of the control hub of the ship – and to the person who had been piloting it.

I don’t even need to use magic to know that Tolly’s gone. The descending mast forced the wheel on top of him and pulverised him. The only thing still entirely intact is one hand, reaching desperately outwards. A single loop of bloody rope wraps around his body, the last thing between him and freedom.

I’ve seen enough blood and gore to no longer feel nauseous over the sight; I just feel a deep sadness. Not grief – though he was a competent and capable captain who I respected, we were never friends. But we might have been by the end of the journey – if he’d survived.

Frustration also grips me. We’ve just lost our captain – who is going to pilot our vessel now? Good captains don’t just grow on trees, especially not in the middle of the ocean.

But even as I stand raging at what we’ve lost, I’m distracted as the sensation of the roc’s domain flickers and then vanishes. A ragged cheer goes up from every ship, but I can’t bring myself to celebrate right now.

We might have won the fight, but we’ve lost far too much in the process.

A pyrrhic victory.
