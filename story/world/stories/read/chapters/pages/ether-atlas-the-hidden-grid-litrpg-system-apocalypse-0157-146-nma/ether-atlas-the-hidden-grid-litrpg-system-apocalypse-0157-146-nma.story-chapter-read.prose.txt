Chapter 146

At Messene, the quarry boys marked the good stones with red chalk before the masons came down from the city. The finished walls bear no record of their names.— Codex of the Long Watch, A Field Observation, 3rd Century BCE, Messenia

The cups and saucers were still on the table where Donna had placed them. The olives that had stood for firing positions had dried and darkened since the afternoon, and nobody had cleared them away. The salt cellar had shifted since Donna’s placement.

Arthur sat with his back to the wall and watched Sarah pace.

She had been doing circuits of the kitchen for the better part of an hour, stove to window and back, and each time she would pause at the sill to look towards the street.

Stavros came in from the yard with a jug of water and set it on the table though none of them had asked for water. He had barely spoken since Eleni left, no doubt thinking of his boy on the hill with the rest of them.

“They will be back soon,” Arthur said, trying to reassure himself as much as the others.

It was another thirty minutes before that reassurance turned concrete as Eleni pushed through the front door, unwinding her scarf. Spyros followed, closing the door carefully behind him.

“They will see you. Tonight, after the neighbours are quiet. The mother and the boy, Dionysis,” Eleni said.

"Katerina knew where they live, two streets behind the chandler's on the west. A house with a green shutter."

“And the mother remembers you, and both Dionysis and his mother remember what you did for them,” Spyros added, lowering himself into a chair. “Thanasis was there when we arrived. He will come back tonight. The two are close.”

"They agreed?" Sarah asked.

“They agreed to hear you. This is not the same thing. Her son said he would listen, not that he would do anything,” Eleni said.

“All four of us going will not be a good idea. Someone is bound to notice. Sarah has to go, being the miracle healer and all that. I will go with her,” Donna said.

“The two of you will stay tucked inside.” She nodded towards Arthur and Steve.

“You do not have to convince them tonight. You only have to tell them the truth and let them decide what to do with it,” Arthur said, moving closer to Sarah. “And if anything feels wrong, you come straight back,” he said.

Arthur bent and kissed her forehead as Sarah’s fingers tightened around his. It was a small gesture made bigger by being made in the middle of the kitchen while Eleni and Yiayia watched.

• •

They had left their weapons at the house. Donna wore a plain dark coat and Sarah had changed into an old wool cardigan Eleni had found in a chest upstairs. Without Arthur or Steve behind them, they looked less like a delegation sent to tear down a gate and more like two women making an ill-timed household visit, and that was the point.

“Three ahead, by the bakery. Just standing,” Donna said.

"Patrol?"

“Could be, or could be men who like lounging at doorways at midnight for no apparent reason.” Donna took Sarah’s elbow and steered her around a stack of crates. “We’re not finding out either way.”

At the junction by the chandler’s yard, Donna stopped and put a hand against the wall. They could hear boots on stone. Steady and unhurried. Four soldiers came round the corner at the far end of the street carrying spears and a lantern.

Donna pulled Sarah into a doorway. The overhang was deep enough to hide them both if they pressed flat against the door. The lantern swung closer, its light caught the wall above their heads, held for a breath, and passed.

They waited until the lantern rounded the next corner and moved.

A shutter closed as they passed a house on the residential lane. Someone had been watching the street and decided they did not want to be part of whatever trouble was walking through it.

Dionysis’s house was narrow and painted yellow that the years had turned to mostly cream. The door was opened by a thin woman of perhaps forty, before Sarah had finished the second knock.

She ushered them in hurriedly and gently closed the door behind them. Dionysis’s mother took them into a kitchen at the back, where heavy curtains covered the windows and a towel had been pushed against the gap beneath the yard door.

“Dionysis,” a young man said, getting up from the dining table and giving Sarah a wide smile.

Sarah smiled back. “It is good to see you standing.”

"I am walking, carrying sacks and standing on the hill until my feet go numb.”

His mother turned from the stove and said something rapid in Greek.

"She says you are still favouring the other leg when you are tired," Sarah translated for Donna.

A shadow moved past the lamp and Thanasis stepped into the light with the same easy confidence he had worn at the Council door, though his constant glances at the window undercut that confidence somehow.

He shook their hands and seemed to be searching for something.

“Axe?” he asked.

“Left it at home,” Donna said.

“Then the night is safe already.”

Dionysis's mother said something sharp in Greek.

“She says the night will be safe when we have gone and the neighbours are still asleep,” Sarah said.

“My aunt is a great comfort in difficult times,” Thanasis said, and got a glare from the woman.

Dionysis's mother set five cups on the table and began making coffee nobody had asked for. Sarah and Donna sat down around the table.

"We need something from you," Sarah began. "And I will be honest about what it costs, before anyone says yes to it."

“I thanked God for you, I thanked the Panagía. I thanked your mother. You saved my boy’s life, but you did not buy him,” Dionysis’s mother interrupted.

Dionysis shifted beside her. "Mamá."

“I kept his leg working because he was hurt and I could. He owes me nothing. If he says no tonight, I will still be glad I was there,” Sarah said.

“I’ve come here tonight not to collect a debt, katalaves, I’ve come to ask for help. My brother is inside the Acropolis. His wife is there. Stavros’s son is there, the Council leaders are there and many people whose families do not have anyone strong enough to climb the hill and demand them back.”

Sarah turned the cup in front of her before continuing. “We cannot reach them safely because we do not know which rooms hold them or what the soldiers guarding those rooms will do when an alarm sounds."

“They say the people there are working for the city,” Dionysis said.

“They say many things. You don’t keep people under guard when they are working out of their own free will,” Donna replied.

Dionysis’s mother spoke little English and in practice, the conversation moved mostly in Greek, with Thanasis filling the gaps and Sarah carrying Donna’s words across whenever they became too important to approximate. Donna waited through each translation without impatience.

“Before we can get anyone out, we need to know what is inside,” Donna said.

"You want me to carry something in," Dionysis said.

"A message, a verbal message, no one will have anything to find. It tells him we are here and trying to free the civilians and asks him to send back what he sees. Where the detainees are held. When they move. Guard numbers and shifts. The alarm procedure."

“The answer is more than a verbal message,” Thanasis said.

“No, but that is not what we need right now. To start with, I want one of you to carry one message if there is an ordinary, safe opportunity to do it. If there is no opportunity, you do nothing and come home,” Sarah said.

“There is no safe opportunity,” Dionysis’s mother said.

“And that is the cost. If the wrong person finds a message, he may question the man carrying it, then his family, then anyone the message was meant to reach. Mavros may change where the detainees are held before we can act. He may punish people we do not even know are involved,” Sarah translated for Donna.

“You are not very good at asking for help,” Thanasis said.

“I am good at making sure people know what they have agreed to,” Donna said.

Dionysis’s mother poured the coffee and took a chair next to her son. “Why do this? People on the hill are fed and clothed, some are better than the people in the harbour at night.”

“Because eventually a tyrant will consider power first and people second. That is how it is. Hippias started peacefully until he was not.”

Steve had drilled the story of Hippias of Athens into both of them. How the last tyrant of Athens started as a cultured peaceful patron of the arts and turned into a brutal dictator after one act of political violence.

Thanasis looked towards the curtained window again. “And if Petros has these answers?”

“Then he sends back what he safely can. Not everything in one message, small pieces that mean nothing useful by themselves,” Donna answered.

Donna waited until everyone else had started on their coffee before continuing.

“Tell me what you actually do on the hill, not what you think might help us. Your ordinary duties.”

Dionysis rubbed his palms down his trousers. “After the wolves, they took me off the perimeter. I could walk, but I could not run the steps for long. They put me with rations and stores. The sacks do not care if I limp a little.”

“Where does that take you?”

“We collect from the lower storehouse. Flour, beans, oil, sometimes water. There are four men and a corporal. We take loads to the kitchens, the gatehouse and the barracks stores.”

Sarah leaned forward. “Have you seen Petros?”

“Once or twice, he helps with the supply books, sometimes. Deliveries, counts the sacks, resolves shortages. I saw him when he signed for a few sacks. He told me he was sorry I could no longer be Giorgos Karagounis,” Dionysis said, smiling.

“I work the outer checkpoint and sometimes the controlled entrance above it. I see the duty lists before the supply details come through. I know which officer is checking carts and which one searches men because he is bored. If the rota changes, I see it,” Thanasis added.

“Can you see when the inspection has changed?” Donna asked.

“A different officer, extra men, opened sacks. Yes.”

“Then that is your part. You watch. If anything is different, Dionysis keeps the message and comes home with it. You do not alter a name, change a number or make an error for someone to remember.”

“How do I warn him with the corporal standing there?”

“Ask about the leg,” Dionysis said, looking at his cousin.

Thanasis frowned.

“You never ask about the leg. You tell my mother I am complaining, and then you laugh,” Dionysis said.

“Because you complain.”

“If you ask whether it is hurting, I will know something is wrong.”

Donna considered the code. “A family question at a checkpoint where everybody knows you are cousins. Nothing written and nothing invented. Good.”

“And when it is hurting?” Dionysis’s mother asked, staring at Thanasis.

“Then he answers honestly. The warning is the question, not the answer,” Sarah said.

For the first time that night, Sarah saw the woman’s mouth soften.

Thanasis rubbed both hands over his face. “Then I watch the post. If anything changes, I ask about the leg.”

“You do not have to,” Dionysis said.

“Re, I know this. That is how choosing works,” Thanasis said, slapping his cousin on the shoulder.

“Verbal message is easy, find when no one is close, say a line. What about written message? What Petros sends back. Written message not so easy,” Thanasis asked.

Donna did not have an immediate answer and the table sat around thinking of a way to smuggle more complex messages than hang on we are trying to figure this out.

Thanasis finished his coffee and took a small tobacco pouch from his coat out of habit and placed his hand on it.

“Do they search you?” Donna asked Dionysis.

"Coming in, they pat your sides, check your pockets, look inside anything you are carrying that is not a sealed ration sack. Coming out is lighter, they count heads and wave you through."

"Do they search cigarettes?"

“Cigarettes?" Dionysis frowned.

"Does anyone on that gate unroll a man's cigarette to check what is inside the paper?"

"No." He looked at Thanasis for confirmation and got a shake of the head. "No, they do not."

“Paper can also look like something else,” Donna said, staring at the pouch. She reached for the pouch and Thanasis pushed it across the table.

Inside were loose tobacco and a thin packet of rolling papers. Donna removed one sheet and laid it on the table beneath the lamp.

“I write on the inside in Greek, small enough to leave the edges clean. The tobacco rolls over the words. Until somebody opens it, it is one cigarette among the others.”

Dionysis picked up the paper by one corner. “And I give Petros a cigarette.”

“Only if the moment is ordinary. During unloading, during a pause, with nobody paying attention. If you have to create the moment, it is the wrong moment,” Donna added.

“And how will he know the message is from you? Any soldier can write a message on cigarette paper,” Dionysis’s mother said, folding her arms.

“Because I will be the one writing the message. My father wrote his sigmas with a hook at the top. Petros and I picked up the habit. I will also add something from our family that no one can guess from just listening at a door,” Sarah said.

“And if I reach the store and Petros is not there?” Dionysis asked.

“You come home with the message undelivered. A missed opportunity is not a failure,” Donna said.

Dionysis's mother put her hands on her son's shoulders.

“Say no. You can say it.”

Dionysis watched Sarah fold the unused paper back into the packet.

“Kostas from the next street is on that hill,” he said. “Milos is there. People whose mothers did not have a doctor when the soldiers came for them. She saved my life in the middle of a wolf pack when she did not have to. If I can carry one cigarette past a door I already pass, then I will carry it,” Dionysis said, his face firming up in resolve.

His mother closed her eyes.
