Two weeks after my response to Romeo's letter went out, Benedict called for me. He had cleaned himself up to a level I'd never seen of him before, which I took as a sign that the news was bad.

He was waiting for me at the bottom of the stairwell beneath the dungeons, hair slicked back, robes pressed, with his hands folded behind him like a schoolmaster expecting a pupil who had not done her reading. The stench of the lower lab still rolled out of the open obsidian door behind him, but at least he no longer smelled like he'd been dragged through it. Somebody had, apparently, finally convinced him to bathe. I suspected Dralos. I suspected many, many threats involved.

"You look almost human," I observed and sniffed. “Almost smell it too.”

“Can’t say the same for you, Your Grace,” he grumbled, his tone a mix of irritation and exhaustion. “You look like the waking dead. When was the last time you slept?”

That was the first time in a month he'd used my title, I realized, causing an eyebrow to lift at the usage as I came to a stop on the last step.

"Must be truly bad news for you to fake such politeness, Benedict."

He merely grunted in response. “See for yourself.” He turned without waiting and led me inside.

The circular chamber hadn't changed much since I'd last seen it. The glass cylinder still stood at its center, the golden-eyed corpse still floated in its bath of preservatives, and those ominous golden eyes still seemed to stare right through me.

What had changed, however, was the table beside it. Benedict had cleared away his clutter and laid out some more useful clutter. Probably. There was a jar of luminous gold liquid collected, I assumed, from the corpse somehow I didn’t really care to know, a sheet of parchment covered in his cramped, slanted script, and the borrowed storage ring I’d taken from one of the knights during our venture into Silverwater.

The ring that held the silver chains.

The topaz storage ring sat in a shallow dish of black sand, wrapped in three layers of rune-inscribed linen. Even from the door, I could feel the wrongness of the chains within. It was like a whisper that was just quiet enough I couldn’t quite hear what it said.

My palm itched where the topaz ring had burned me. The scorch mark had faded to a pale circle over the past weeks, but it had never fully healed even despite my natural regenerative abilities. The wrongness of the chains was like a stench embedded into the burn mark that refused to come off no matter how hard I scrubbed at it.

“What did you find out?” I asked, noting the rippling of the air around the ring and the scattering of the black sand.

"I opened it," Benedict said, his eyes still wild despite his cleaned appearance. Rather than a Marquess, the man was beginning to look like some mad scientist in a drunken stupor of panic. "Once. For exactly eleven seconds. I lost two fingernails and a very good pair of gloves." He held up his left hand. The tips of his first two fingers were bandaged. The same scent of wrongness that permeated my palm flowed from under the bandages. “It’s still eating away at the fingers.” He picked up the parchment, then set it down again as if he'd decided it was more theatrical to recite from memory. "All energy and all mana carry a signature. The same way a voice carries an accent, or individuals have different personalities. Fire mana from a Caelian mage and fire mana from a Lysorian one are both fire, but if you know what to feel for, they don't feel the same at all." He gestured at the jar of gold. "I've spent the better part of two weeks learning the feel of the mana signature of the liquid. Then I did the same with the chains in that topaz ring."

He let the silence stretch for a moment and I let him have it, doing my best to keep the impatience off my face. He'd earned one dramatic pause, I supposed.

"They match," he finally shouted, throwing his hands into the air. "The chains in the topaz ring are filled with dense, corrupted mana. It desperately tries to consume whatever touches it. The mana in these eyes is thin but tame, bound into living tissue as distinct from the mana in the chains being bound to inanimate material. But the mana signatures underneath are identical, Lilliana. They feel the same. Their pressure is the same." He paused. "Their wrongness is the same. I would stake my life on the fact they originate, somehow, from the same source. It is as if someone took the chains you found in that dungeon, diluted its mana a thousand times over, and stuffed it behind a man's eyes."

I looked at the corpse again, even though it continued to stare through me at nothing.

"And you think that whatever was being held prisoner in Silverwater is the source of this tainted mana?" I asked him, running a finger gently along the glass tube.

Benedict nodded fiercely, his fingers tapping against his thigh like he was playing the piano. "I don't know what was in that cell. Your Grace might, or at least you may have a better guess than I do. But whatever it was, a piece of it is in your knights."

I thought of the young knight in the Silverwater dungeon, the one with the brilliant gold eyes who'd found the chains and reached for the fluid before I slapped his hand away. He'd nearly touched it despite the obvious danger of it.

Had it been calling to him?

"The dragon fire," I remembered. "Was it able to burn the gold out?"

His mouth thinned. "I... tested it." At my frown, he hurriedly continued. "On the condemned only, of course. The Paragons brought a few dozen traitors and prisoners back before pausing the Scarlet March to return here. Four of them had gold eyes and had once been knights of the duchy. They were going to hang regardless. I thought their deaths might as well be useful."

I nodded, accepting the logic. "What did your testing show?"

"Well, only one of the four ended up surviving Vespera's dragon fire." Benedict grinned. "But he did survive. In a way. His eyes are brown now. A plain, ordinary brown." Benedict exhaled and removed the spectacles he'd begun to wear increasingly often. "The other three unfortunately weren't able to withstand the flames and burned from the inside out. The dragon fire burned through the gold wherever it had spread. Behind the eyes, into the brain, down the spine in one of them, even into the heart. It burned everything between it and the spreading gold mana."

"Was the spread less in the survivor?"

Benedict nodded. "I am not sure why the gold mana's spread was less in him than the others. I have some people looking into the man's background now, though my guess is he was only knighted a few years ago."

One in four was extremely low. Much too low to actually begin implementing a purging of the gold mana infestation.

Every knight sworn to the Alistar Duchy had those eyes. Every one. I did not need to do the arithmetic aloud to understand what burying three of every four would mean. Even the survivors would abandon the duchy if I tried something like that.

"The survivor," I asked. "Is he himself?"

"I am not sure of that either," Benedict admitted. "We haven't yet located anyone who knew him before the golden mana removal, much less reunited them. He did weep for hours after he awoke though. He claimed what we did caused something to let go of him, though we haven't been able to really understand what he meant by that." Benedict's voice had gone uncharacteristically flat.

"Can you tell which knights the infection has spread deep within before the burning? We could focus only on those with surface level infections."

"Not yet." He rubbed his eyes with his good hand. "I'm working on it. Give me months, and I could maybe give decent estimates. Give me weeks, and I'll give you educated guesses."

"I'm leaving in a week," I grumbled. "And I will likely be gone for months."

Benedict shrugged. "Are you worried the knights will turn traitor in that time?"

I walked around the cylinder and laid my palm flat against the cold glass, the same way I had the first night. The corpse's eyes didn't move. But the moment my skin touched the glass, I felt a faint pull from somewhere very far away, like a fishing line drawn taut in dark water.

I snapped my hand back, though the feeling lingered for another moment.

"Whoever holds the other end of this," I wondered aloud, "do you think they can see through the infected eyes?"

Benedict didn't answer for a long moment. He took a seat and dropped his head into his good hand, rubbing the bridge of his nose with the other. "I would very much like to believe they cannot."

So do I, I thought.

"Keep the survivor alive and comfortable for now but keep him quiet. He needs to stay under observation until I return." I turned for the door, back to Benedict. "Keep this a secret for now, even among the Paragons. No one but Nida, Dralos, and Ethan should know."

"Your Grace," Benedict called, his voice quiet and small. I looked over my shoulder to see that the man hadn't even raised his drooped head. "What are you going to do with the rest of them?"

"I'm not sure," I answered truthfully, heading toward the exit door again. "I would prefer to keep them alive, but you know as well as I do that right now, I have what amounts to hundreds of daggers pointed at me from all directions. That is dangerous to us all."

I found Dralos that afternoon on the walls above the eastern gate, where I'd gone to watch the last of the supply wagons roll in for those who would join when the March resumed. I told him only part of what Benedict had found, enough that he would be able to carry out my orders.

"A liaison," Dralos repeated, in the tone of someone confirming the name of a poison.

"It is to be an honor," I said, the dramatic flourish of my gesture not matching the seriousness of my gaze. "Every company of knights in the duchy receives a liaison officer to share its command. Every garrison with a knight in it too, down to the smallest watch post. The liaisons will drill with the knights, eat with them, sleep in the same barracks, do everything the knights do. To the knights, I hope it appears that the Duchess is attempting to merge her personal forces with the Duchy's."

Dralos snorted. "They are more likely to view it as a lack of trust. What is the real reason to embed the Paragons into the knight units?"

I blew out a breath. "In truth, they would be right. The Paragons will be installed to watch and observe how they act with the infection of golden mana." Below us, Elyndor went about its afternoon. Carts. Hawkers. Children chasing a dog through a market that hadn't existed six months ago. It was easy to forget, up here, that I'd nearly let the whole city burn. It had been for a purpose, of course, but the more of the city I encountered, the more I grew uneasy with the recollection. "Every knight in this duchy has gold in his eyes, Dralos. Every single one Collin Alistar ever made. Every knight any Duke of this duchy ever knighted. As far as I am aware, only the common soldiers are clean since they were never linked to the House Coin. But the knights are the spine of the army, I can't simply cut them out of it without the entire duchy collapsing."

Dralos leaned on the parapet beside me, his long, scaled fingers drumming on the stone. "The Paragons are few, my lady. Much too few to put one in every barracks."

I nodded in agreement. "We will have to rely also on the freed slaves I brought out of Sealrite. And, if we must, a few of the officers who rose through the ranks without ever being knighted." I glanced at him. "Aargorn, for one. He rose to be third-in-command to the Duke's military, yet he was never linked to the coin. His eyes are as plain as a farmer's." I watched the dog vanish beneath a fruit stall. "Speak with Nida about choosing who goes where before we leave. And there will be no more knightings until I return." I turned to him, my frown deepening. "Make it known. Not a single knighting is to happen."

"Knighthood in this duchy is centuries old." The draconian shook his head. "There will be massive pushback among every rank within the Duchy, Your Grace. Down to the very last peasant boy who dreams of knighthood."

"It doesn't matter, Dralos. This is not something I simply wish to do. It must be done." I twirled a strand of hair around my finger in thought. "Tell them the Duchess intends to revise the ceremony. Which is true, in a way." I tapped a finger from my free hand against the stone. "I must make sure the knights are mine. Not some foreign power's."

Ironically, Dralos' own golden irises narrowed at that, his diamond pupils thin in the afternoon sun.

I waved away his comment before he could make it. "You are not even from this Kingdom, draconian. I do not suspect your loyalties simply based on the color of your eyes." Then, I offered him a smile and gave his shoulder a pat. "Besides, I have understood the signature of your core since I resurrected you. Had that golden mana spread to you, I would have noticed and dealt with it. You are, after all, far too central to my plans to let your knowledge leak to whoever is behind the golden mana."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he laughed, low and rasping, the kind of laugh I'd learned meant he was genuinely amused. "You are a terrifying woman, my lady."

"So, I've been told." I flashed him another short smile.

"And your captain," Dralos said, after a pause. "Marcus. What are we to do about him?"

I'd watched Marcus closely these past two weeks since Benedict first discovered the disease that was the golden mana. "I asked Lady Eliza after one of the council meetings last week how her brother had linked his knights to the House Coin for the knighting, feigning a moment of curiosity. She claims to have watched him do it more times than she can count. First, the knight kneels upon the stage of his chosen church, then the Duke presses the coin to his brow and speaks their full name. When the knight rises, his eyes are gold. She said it was like watching a candle catch."

"That's all?"

I nodded and shrugged. "At least it was all she was willing to share. From what she explained, the exact method of utilizing the House Coin has always been passed from Duke to heir, though I find that unlikely given the age of House Alistar. I find it hard to believe that no Duke in its history ever died before teaching his heir the knighting method." I slipped a hand into my pocket, where the House Coin sat cold against my fingers. "According to the Lady Eliza, the coin is actually older than House Alistar. Older than even Lysoria. The first Alistar found it right here when he first began to build Elyndor."

"The elves," Dralos whispered, voice barely audible, eyes going wide with understanding.

I nodded again. "That is what I suspect."

Nasq had believed that, before Elyndor was settled by humans, it had been home to elves. From what he'd told me before his coma, and what little I'd been able to learn since the Hydra's rampage, he may well have been right. Elyndor's walls were carved with runes no one could explain. Not a single rune artisan in the city could tell me even the basics of how they'd been drawn. Its spiral stairs grew like the inside of a tree and, whenever I focused on them hard enough, they almost felt alive.

If that was true, then for generations the Dukes of Alistar had been pressing an elven coin to their knights' brows and calling it an honor, never knowing what else it tied them to.

The most worrying part was that I had used it myself, before I understood the first thing about it. I'd tapped into the House Coin against Benedict's summoned horrors to command Duke Alistar's army, and again in Sealrite. I had felt hundreds of mana and energy lines run out from that coin and never once wondered where else they led.

"Will you leave the captain behind when you leave for Velora and the convocation?" Dralos asked, straightening from the wall and tapping his black nails against the stone material again.

I shook my head and pushed off the wall as well. "No. I think I will keep him close, for now. Until whatever is behind the golden mana decides otherwise, the knights should still be loyal to me. I might as well test how deep that loyalty runs."
