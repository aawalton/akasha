Early the next morning, the legion gathered for parade and ventured out on a final, short march towards Nerithon’s walls. About them rose the blackened skeletons of the city’s suburbs; the buildings had long been dismantled by the Fifth Legion to bolster their palisade defences. What the legion had left, the Ürkün had burned.

As the column wound around a thicket, Skippii caught sight of the city. The eastern fields rose to the precipice of seaside cliffs and collapsed out of sight upon the backdrop of the blue sea. At the land’s edge rose an enormous tower, thirty metres tall—a grey shield that melded with the cliff’s face. Atop the tower was a beacon, but the lighthouse was cold, and northward from the tower stretched Nerithon’s mighty walls.

At the tower’s feet, the masts of a dozen ships bobbed like brown mallards with red and white streaks–colours of the Imperium—the blockade. Many more lay obscured behind the cliffs, but rumour was that none had managed to penetrate Nerithon’s fortified harbour.

The harbour itself was hidden from sight, but above it, a disparate flock of seagulls circled. However, they were outnumbered by their black cousins: the crow. Carrion gathered like mites upon the lighthouse and the tall towers of the adjoining wall. Those battlements stretched westward for one mile until they veered sharply eastward at a right-angle. Grey and hard, the walls loomed over the Fifth Legion’s encampment with a cold, overshadowing scorn. There was no weakness in its design that Skippii could see. It was built by Philoxenian hands with the help of Auctorian mason-masters in the days of old. Now, its blemished rock was adorned with crude banners made from the scraps of a brownish-red cloth.

Gazing northward, he saw that the walls dipped slightly with the landscape, finishing at a cornerstone tower, second only in magnitude to the lighthouse at the cliff’s edge. From there, the walls ran eastward away from them, built close to the foothills of a remote mountain. Skippii had noticed the mountain once before, as he previewed the land from afar. Lush forests crept from the mountain’s base to meet the walls, and a union of masonry and the wilds was met. Like a lake of green, the flourishing forest ran up the hillside, foaming at the mountain’s apex, only to fizzle out before its flat peak. There, a bald, black rock formed its zenith, cast in unflinching shadow.

His eyes lingered on that peak, and felt a trickle of excitement enter his heart; magia came next, quickening his step, and he grazed the heel of Drusilla in front.

“Hey, watch it,” Drusilla said.

“Sorry,” Skippii said, shaking himself and turning his attention to their immediate surroundings.

The head of Legion IX’s column marched between the first ring of palisade walls that encompassed the city.

“They’ve seen some fighting,” Orsin said as they drew close, noting the disrepair. The spiked trunks had been flattened or burned in sections. Their bases were overgrown with weeds, and their towers had long since been abandoned by the Fifth Legion. Arrows prickled their battlements like sticky burdock pods. Abrupt mounds littered the earth; bodies, long since discarded. No legionnaires manned the defences. No banners flew.

“Remind you of something?” Orsin said over his shoulder.

“Tyrlon Hill,” Arius murmured.

“Hespera, guide them,” Orsin prayed grimly.

“The Fifth Legion shall be glad of our arrival,” said Tenoris.

“Yeah,” Drusilla said. “Looks like they’ve had a rough time of it.”

“Might not have held out without us,” Kaesii added, a note of pride in his voice.

“The Ürkün were this close?” Fulmin asked.

“They had the Fifth surrounded,” Skippii said. “Pressed between here and the walls. The besiegers under siege themselves.”

“But the enemy couldn’t chase away our ships,” Orsin said. “And they’ve been bringing in supplies for the Fifth.”

“So it formed a stalemate,” said Skippii.

“This again,” Cur sighed. “The talk of tactless tacticians.”

“The Fifth is a veteran legion, right?” Fulmin asked, ignoring the old man’s grumblings. “Must take one to do their job. Surrounded on all sides with just these flimsy wooden walls to keep the enemy out.”

“A legion of veterans,” Cur marvelled. “That would be nice.”

“Not for long,” Orsin said. “I bet their casualties are pretty bad. They’ll need re-bolstering with velvets once this is all over.”

“Maybe they can have some of ours,” Cur said.

“They can have you,” Drusilla said. “I’ll beg ‘em to take ya.”

They passed a mound littered with nettles and brambles, speckled with faded red fabric and the dead gleam of bronze. A frenzy of flies flew above the mound, joined by crows hopping through the wreckage.

“Oyaltun, guide them,” Skippii whispered, but the prayer felt clumsy on his lips.

Before long, they entered the second perimeter of palisade defences. Here, the walls stood strong. The gate’s towers were manned–banners flying the legion’s reds. Scant legionnaires stood aside and watched the Ninth enter. Kaesii saluted a small group who were setting axes to the sodden timber remains of a derelict battlement. Unmoving, they returned only indiscernible scowls on dirty, war-hardened faces.

“Ungrateful sods,” Kaesii muttered.

“Hey,” Cur snapped. “None of that.”

Ragged tents tore at their poles; cones of legion spears stood alone, with no men to bear them, and ballista siege engines bent their heavy necks to the ground. In the churned mud, nothing grew.

Finally, they approached the Fifth Legion’s innermost palisade walls. Smokestacks rose from campfires within, blown on a gust of sea wind, carrying a confusing concoction of scents: the tang of salt, the grime of livestock and the putrid ditches of legionnaires. Skippii held his breath as a gust assaulted his senses.

Marching beside him, Kaesii and Drusilla coughed, but Tenoris took a deep breath through his nostrils. “Ahh. Smells like home.”

“You were raised in an outhouse?” Kaesii said.

“A farm, where all the fields are an outhouse in early spring.”

“Look,” Fulmin pointed with his spear. “What is that marking? An eye?”

Skippii followed his gaze to Nerithon’s wall. Each of its towers rose at least twenty metres tall, their rock as flat as a grey gown. Closer now, he could see that what he had taken for brownish-red banners were in fact legionnaire’s cloaks–hundreds of them–faded by the elements, marred with black inscriptions, hanging from the battlements. Upon the towers of the gatehouse, many cloaks had been woven together to create large banners painted with cosmic swirling vortexes. One vast tapestry eye from the battlements of the cornerstone tower, its baleful eye gazing unblinkingly on Legion V encamped below.

“How many cloaks are there?” Drusilla said. “Must be hundreds.”

The column marched towards the inner-most defences and veered north along its edge. As Skippii neared, he regarded the depth of its ditches and solidness of the palisade walls, supported by many towers–at least one every twenty metres. This had been the Fifth Legion’s last line of defence against marauding Ürkün, and it had held, yet it was meagre compared to Nerithon’s grey walls beyond. The path beneath him was churned with mud. He planted his spear to stop himself from slipping as their formation loosened, struggling to keep pace.

Custos Maritor raised his voice at the head of their tonnage.

“Look sharp, lads. Keep step. Show the Fifth we’re not just a bunch of velvets. We’re proud men of the Ninth.”

The palisade to their right was arrayed with the faces of legionnaires from the Fifth. Their grimy faces bore the weight of many months of toil, all grit and gaunt. Forcing his head straight and his eyes from wandering, he marched in time, following the column as it took a wide berth around Nerithon, coming ever closer to the flat-topped mountain beyond.

Skippii’s eyes wandered to its dark crown, closer now, that he could almost make out the details of something lurking within its barren, shadowy peak.

Something jolted within him–a knot coming loose. Without meaning to, he summoned a fraction of his power, and felt the earth open up beneath him. His eyes were drawn to the mountain. It was not very tall, but expansive, like a great bear laid low over the landscape. His heart hastened, eager to bring him to its base.

Rather than camp all together, the legion’s cohorts spread out. Some bolstered Legion V’s defences, but his cohort and five others ventured into the foothills at the base of that mountain. From their slight vantage, they were roughly level with the tops of Nerithon’s walls half a mile away. Scouts had picked the spot, and slaves from Legion V had constructed it ahead of their arrival. Ditches and palisades ringed their grounds.

The legion divided into cohorts, and again into tonnages, and finally, Skippii and his companions found a spot and erected their companeight’s tent. Skippii caught one last view of the siege’s layout before the legion obscured his vision. Strangely, the palisades ended at the mountain’s foothills to the north, where Nerithon’s walls stretched out to meet them.

“The encirclement is broken,” he observed. “Right where the city walls meet the forest.”

“What could break it?” Tenoris asked.

“An Apertrox,” Kaesii said confidently. “They must have had a few to smash through the palisades.”

“I doubt that,” Drusilla said. “Anything can break a wooden wall.”

“The point isn’t the walls,” Skippii said. “The question is, why hasn’t the Fifth retaken that area? What kept them out?”

“Marauders,” Drusilla said. “The ones we defeated at the river.”

“You on about the leak?” Cur said, approaching them. “Yep, they’ll put us to work plugging it. Ditches, ditches, ditches. Hope you like digging, boys.”

“What does it matter if there’s a small hole in the siege?” Kaesii said. “We’re still here, still surrounding them. They cannot escape the city.”

“But if they broke the siege, the’ll have gotten supplies in,” Skippii said. “There’s probably trails through the mountains. The best weapon of a siege is starvation, and the defenders have managed to blunt it.”

“So it was all for nothing?” Fulmin said.

“Maybe,” Skippii mused. “But why there? Why was that the legion’s weak spot?”

“Furthest from the sea?” Orsin suggested. “No support from the ships.”

“Or something in the forest,” Arius said.

Their gazes wandered to the lengthening shadows of the forest.

Kaesii broke the silence. “Like I said, an Apertrox.”

“Maybe,” Skippii sighed. “Or some other monster.”

A gloom spread within him. It was difficult for him to accept that anything could oppose the might of an Auctorian legion. As the sun set, and their slaves prepared their camp, Skippii struggled to shake the storm clouds from his mind. He had learned at an early age to fear the faces of a downtrodden legionnaire, and the squalor in a man’s heart who had given in to desperation. In them festered a meanness. All too often after a hard battle, legionnaires had snapped at him–thrown things or smacked him–and punished him for their own hard luck. He’d had no father to protect him, merely a collection of older brothers and surrogates, whose temper he had been at the mercy of.

But that was long in the past. The only legionnaires he had to fear now were his superiors. His companeight ate in a tired silence, except for Tenoris, who marvelled at the city walls before them.

“When duty calls, shall we be chosen to scale the walls? I can think of no greater honour.”

“Have your honour,” Kaesii said. “But if we’re on the ladders and towers, I’m pushing to the front. Formation be damned. I want that crown.”

“What crown?” Tenoris asked. “They will make you king?”

“The Corona Muralis,” Fulmin said, as he ran a nit-comb through his coarse orange hair. “Awarded to the first legionnaire who breaches the walls.”

“You haven’t heard of it?” Skippii said. “I thought everyone knew about the Corona.”

“I am a simple farmer,” Tenoris said, holding his hands up. “Whence the word crown brushes my ears, they close shut like a snail whose head has been poked by a stick.”

“So what?” Kaesii said. “Anyone can claim it, if those are there to witness and testify. And it guarantees a promotion.”

“If you survive,” Orsin muttered.

“Don’t bother, Kaesii,” Drusilla said. “They can’t forge the Muralis big enough for your head. It’s never been done.”

Kaesii scowled. “Laugh all you want, Agrippa, but when I’m your superior, I’m going to make you do all sorts of dirty deeds.”

“Just as long as I’m not in the same tent,” Cur said.

Drusilla laughed. “That’s what you would do with command, force me to be your lover?”

“No. Disgusting.” Kaesii grimaced. “You shall see.”

After a lull, Skippii spoke. “How long do you think before the rumour spreads to the Fifth Legion?”

“Of us?” Orsin said. “Of you?”

“Yeah. How do you think they’ll take it.”

“Not well,” Cur said.

“Why not,” Tenoris challenged. “Our Imperator has vouched for Companeight Four’s champion.”

“Because their whole Coven was destroyed by a heretic magus,” Cur said. “That’s the rumour, right? They’re probably shit-scared of any magia that’s not, you know… pantheon.”

“Yeah,” Skippii said. “I don’t blame them.”

“Once they hear that you slew a heretic in the fields, they shall understand,” Tenoris said. “How could they not? It is simple.”

“Depends who is telling the rumour,” Arius said. “It may sound different from different mouths.”

“Then perhaps we should tell it ourselves.” Tenoris made to rise, but Skippii put a hand on his thigh.

“Don’t worry about it,” he said. “It’s not of our concern. Not really.”

The Gris came with meagre rations, and Cur stirred up a flavourless stew.

“Here,” Skippii said, handing him a pouch. “Salt and luger root.”

“I’d love to give that a climb,” Drusilla said, marvelling at the mountain before them. “A fine flat-top. Good for sightseeing.”

“The Sleeping Mountain,” Arius added. “Its name.”

“Put a ballista up there, and we’d be able to shoot over the walls easily,” Fulmin said.

“Don’t go giving the superiors any ideas,” Cur said. “You wanna lug a ballista up them slopes?”

“What good would that do anyway?” Skippii said. “We’d just be shooting at houses.”

Fulmin scowled to himself. “I guess we could aim for the temple.”

“And destroy it?” Orsin said. “That temple’s older than the Imperium. I’d rather liberate it, don’t know about you.”

“Yeah, you’re right,” Fulmin said.

“Do you think these foothills yonder will be appropriate for your training?” Tenoris asked.

“Yeah,” Skippii said. “If I get time away from duties.”

“Don’t bet on it,” Cur said. “I’m not picking up your slack anymore.”

“Don’t worry,” Skippii said. “I don’t intend for you to.”
