A fresh herd of digger inklings pulled themselves off of the ground, the brush-strokes composing their bodies solidifying as they took their first steps. The constructs didn't need much time to orient themselves, instead lumbering forwards and beginning to tear into the iron-rich stone surrounding them with their teeth and claws.

They didn't... really need the iron. Not urgently, in any case. Their supply line was giving far more of everything than they used at effectively every step of the operation. The diggers extracted more ore than the transport carts moved down the Ironroad, they got more ore at First Tower than their Blast furnace processed, which also created more raw iron than their hammer-station could process into billets, which still managed to create far more iron than Oliver and Jacob were capable of turning into actual products.

But Henrietta made sure everything kept flowing anyway. A lot of that was simple inertia. If they let it lapse, it would be far harder to get it restarted both socially and technologically. While things were running, if something broke they could notice immediately and fix it, instead of trying to fix an unknown number of issues which might accumulate as things sat and rotted. And socially, habits like keeping things running could be easy to break but hard to remake.

The other part... well, she expected that their surplus wouldn't last much longer.

She wanted to say that they were approaching a tipping point, where their progress would become exponential, but they'd already reached that tipping point many times. Technological progress simply was exponential by its very nature. The more advanced the tools, the easier it was to make even better tools. They'd hit milestones of progress when Oliver first built a stable environment for spellcasting, when they had set up an inkling-manned brick-making factory, when they'd gotten System access, when they refined iron, when Oliver had made motion slides, when Alyssa had made their Blast furnace, when Clark had trained sniffers to do most of the work for their Charcoal Furnaces, when Oliver had figured out broadcast glyphs, when they had created a lathe and clockwork heart, and again when Oliver developed spellcord.

Each of those developments had resulted in more time and better tools, creating the best kind of positive feedback loop.

But with Clark's newest subskills... they were on the cusp of something truly massive. Exponential automation. Not truly self-replicating machines, but something adjacent. They'd already been decently close, but with Oliver working on turning Metalpress into a form transmutation engine capable of turning raw metal into complex parts with only a hologram to guide it, they would effectively be there.

Hells, he'd already created himself one automata in the form of his familiar. Industry was more a certainty than a likelihood. They would be able to literally make themselves more manpower, giving yet another factor to their feedback loop.

And so, the iron had to keep flowing. They'd be needing literal tons of it soon.

A dragon pounced over the walls, specifically a scalewolf, and before it could even land, Henrietta had snapped an ink-flail tendril around its torso, slammed it into the rocky ground, and then flung it skyward.

She unfurled her wings from around her shoulders and took to the air with just a couple of flaps. There were indeed a few dragons here and there, but four of her big dragons were keeping them in check, so it wasn't anything to be too concerned about. They'd had to disable the ballista around the pit on account of them being incapable of distinguishing the inkling guards from the dragons and other wildlife they were supposed to keep out, but at least the enormous draconic inkling defenders didn't need to be reloaded constantly.

She monitored the diggers for a few minutes more, ensuring that they were tearing chunks of ore out of the ground, and depositing them in the ore-pile for her notter inklings, with a role expanded beyond just gathering clay from Tower Stream thanks to how many she had, to transfer into their transport carts. Then, she hopped onto the back of one of her big dragons and headed home.

She needed to make a brief stop to fix some dragon-damage to the Ironroad, filling in a gouge in the hard-packed dirt and picking up a transport cart that had been derailed as a result. But the trip was mercifully fast, and she briefly stopped over to see Oliver - he was surrounded by disassembled and broken pieces of Metalpress while muttering himself - before checking in on Jacob.

"How's your quickdraw progress coming along?" If their Warrior could be said to have a weakness, it was how long it took his [Frostblade] to be cast. That made him dependent on precasting the skill, and thanks to being magically half-crippled from fighting a Tyrant during The Binding Wars, doing so consumed most of his mana Generation. It also made his ⟨Rimeforge⟩ not particularly usable under normal circumstances - there was no time for him to change weapons once he saw what he would be fighting, and the risk of being completely weaponless when a dragon stronger than their passive First Tower defenses could handle attacked meant he couldn't use it for custom tool purposes either.

They had a workaround in the form of a sword enchanted to let him activate the skill quickly on it, but there were several issues with that approach, which a proper Skill or Subskill would remedy.

"It is my perception that I have done all I can do in preparation," the Warrior answered. "I shall merely require an appropriate foe against whom I may sharpen and wholly coalesce the technique."

So next time something big attacked, effectively. Henrietta didn't bother to ask why he still kept his blade at the ready, if he was confident he could spontaneously summon it the next time it would be needed. She knew the answer: well-earned paranoia.

Alyssa was out on a scouting mission, exploring the Underground cave system she'd found back in the early days of the Jump now that she had reliable access to magical light. She'd be gone for a couple of days at a time, returning once her supplies ran low.

Clark was making some rounds repairing things that had broken.

All in all, none of her team needed her help right now. Henrietta took advantage of the opportunity to take a look at all of their sub-Factories, producing lumber, iron, charcoal, bronze, copper, bricks, sand, and even full spellcord. Glass, clockwork hearts, motion slides, and other parts took more manual intervention and as such weren't being actively built. Paper too, but they hadn't had much use for it since ⟨Universal Canvas⟩ allowed her to practice her skills on other substances, and the Window of Words and Winds became capable of holding her notes.

What they did have use for was cloth.

Fabrics had been one of humanity's earliest civilizational level technologies, with some evidence that the very first domesticated plants had been not for food, but for the making of clothing. And while Clark's work and her own inklings made it slightly redundant for clothing, it was hardly something they could ignore indefinitely. Too many types of magic required threads and woven cloth, and Forerunner Expeditions didn't assume that any given type of fabric would be available.

Copper and iron were multiversal constants. Cotton and silk were not.

That was a large part of why so much of their training was on general theory, such that they could adapt to local conditions and teach people new skills that built upon what they already knew, instead of wholly supplanting their existing daily rituals with 'superior' ones. The First Empire of Humanity was a bit... touchy on the subject of 'higher powers' arbitrarily imposing their will and insisting on specific behaviors. It also just... overall wasn't a useful approach.

As a result, there was a lot of emphasis on ensuring that their first contacts would be able to demonstrate the value they could bring, and were to guide people into overall better patterns rather than simply giving a large set of arbitrary rules and directives. The radical improvement people tended to see in their daily lives thanks to the social and mechanical technologies they needed to implement just to have a chance of making a portal tended to be far more convincing than any degree of rhetoric anyway.

That flexibility, of taking what was present on a world and working with it, still only stretched so far. There were different general portal designs, which could utilize a number of different magical disciplines based on what was most possible on the foreign worlds, but many of them required fabric of some kind to work. The world-tree Henrietta had used last time didn't, but the knife-and-veil method, where the portal-making ritual culminated with a ritualistic blade cutting a tear in a physical curtain, was practically the 'default' if such a thing could truly exist for something so intricate and custom.

All in all, fabrics were a decidedly non-negotiable material for The Factory to be capable of producing.

Fortunately, Alyssa had been practicing with [Rustlewind] a little while hence and managed to find a certain blue-ish coiling vine that was 'good for the making of ropes and cloth.' Oliver had done some divinations to confirm the initial impression, and now Henrietta was working to turn that into a reality.

It was a bit unfortunate that the reeds that grew everywhere, and which Henrietta used for paper-making, wouldn't work for cloth, but they were too sticky to make for a smooth fabric The same property which made it great for papyrus or paper... didn't translate well to smooth clothes-making.

However, their new threadvines were somewhat more promising, and Henrietta had worked with Oliver to divine a proper processing method. First, a trio of pseudowyvern inklings she had set to the task harvested the vines, after which they were spread out in direct daylight for a week, until what traces of green the vines originally had faded, leaving them a dusty blue hue and relatively dry.

They'd reached that point now, so Henrietta took a few handfuls of the vines - each about the same thickness as her finger - and started running them through a pair of wooden rollers, crushing them. It took several passes to fully work, but doing so eventually broke down much of the internal bonds the vine used to stay together, but still resulted in a number of distinct clumps of string, where the threads tangled between one another, winding and snaking through what had previously been the plant's internal structure.

And at this point, Henrietta needed to do more experimentation. She tried using weights to encourage the vines to unwind, to no avail. She tried to make a comb and straighten out the threads like it was a particularly tangled clump of hair. To the thread's credit, it held up to the abuse admirably, and didn't break no matter how much force she tried to apply. Her comb actually broke at one point, but that was more because she'd cobbled it together out of reeds than anything. She couldn't spin it for that very same reason, the fibers were bunched up together too much to be drawn out.

She wound up finding the solution by complete accident, after a bundle of crushed vine fell in one of her paper-washing pools of water without her realizing. When she noticed it several hours later and dried it out by hanging it over the brick kiln 'exhaust', it had fluffed up immensely, with the longer threads weakening and fraying, giving her a very fluffy, dusty-blue clump of threadvine fibers that held together decently well but could be gently teased apart if she put some effort into it.

Perfect distaff material, in other words.

Henrietta of course knew how to spin thread, having first learned it as part of the preparation for her first Expedition and then getting lots of practice as she interacted and socialized with the locals during that Expedition. Spinning endless amounts of thread was an ubiquitous pastime on preindustrial worlds across practically all social classes.

Mostly because it was incredibly time-consuming.

The absolute simplest form of spinning, and by far the most common across history, was done with just two pieces of 'tech' known as the distaff - effectively just a stick wound with a clump of fibers - and the spindle, which would be wound with the actual thread.

In order to make the thread, fibers from the distaff needed to be pinched off, slowly teasing out more and more of the material while it was, literally, spun into existence. Turning the spindle at a constant rate, twisting the fibers into a more finished project. The twisting played two roles, both in winding fibers around themselves and one another into string and in providing a semi-constant amount of force to the distaff, making it easier to keep the thread thickness consistent.

To aid in that goal, most spindles were what was known as a drop spindle. There, the spindle had a weighted disc on the bottom, so that it could be wound up and then dropped, with the momentum of the spindle responsible for doing the spinning-work itself. However, it had to constantly be reset, leading to an activity that Henrietta had found quite tedious every one of the few times she'd had to actually engage in the process.

And it took... absolutely forever to make any appreciable amount of thread. Just a genuinely, mindbogglingly large amount of time to Henrietta, who had of course grown up in a fully postindustrial society and therefore barely even had to think about how fabrics were made right up until she was in training for the Forerunner Program.

But! Industrialization would make that far simpler, and could even completely automate it... in due time.

Actually creating the machines involved would involve an absurd amount of high-precision parts, and even more if they didn't want to spend entire days setting up the machines for a run. And not only would those parts be far easier to produce once Oliver had his Metalpress upgrades completed, he would realistically be needed to make a bunch of them anyway, and the entire point of Henrietta taking on the weaving project was that it was something she could do while the rest of her team was busy.

So for now, she was going to do an older, more manual version of weaving tech - the spinning wheel. It was the larger cousin of the drop spindle, the spinning wheel. It still followed the same basic principles of spinning - a spindle with a hook, gradually pulling out fibers as they were fed into the machine and twisting them into thread - but instead of relying on gravity and momentum to do the work, the spindle was connected to a much larger wheel via a belt, such that when the larger wheel was spun - usually through a foot-pedal system or the like - the spindle would spin much faster.

There were a lot of other minor details that were different, but overall it was still a project which Henrietta could do on her own. And once that was done, she could see about making the basic structure for a loom in preparation for later fabrication of the more fiddly pieces. But for now... thread would suffice. String would be useful in a host of minor ways - the thin vines they used were fine enough for lashing, but a more flexible cord would be a welcome addition for their individual carry-around kits.

And so... she set about her work, figuring out how to spin threadvine.

It was a long-strand fiber, so it didn't need particularly aggressive twisting - that was good. Reeds, processed like she was making them into papyrus but left as a long strip, could be used as a belt for the spinning wheel. And then came the details.

She needed some way to adjust the position of the spindle to account for the belt stretching over time.

She needed to develop a system for swapping out spindles on the spinning wheel.

She needed to create spindles - reeds were useful, but at the thickness needed for the tip of the spindle, insufficiently stiff. Feathers wound up being useful for that.

She needed to figure out a better way to decant the thread from the spindle that was less tedious than hand-unwinding all of the thread spooled around it. Preferably one which could be retrofitted into rewinding the thread more tightly onto a different spindle that would be more purpose-built for usage on a loom.

The work never ended. And for all that it could be tremendously annoying...

It still beat drop-spinning.
