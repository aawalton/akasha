A merchant I knew paid his ferryman twice over at a crossing where no fare was owed past the first. I asked why he threw good coin after a debt already settled. A settled debt buys you the crossing, he said, the coin you did not have to give buys you the man. When the river came up in flood a year later, and every other boat stayed tied to the bank, I understood he had bought well. — Codex of the Long Watch, Field Observation, the Isthmus

The second pass was already aimed at the boat. Shark, it had to be a bloody shark. Arthur knew in his head that a shark was no more dangerous than a column-throwing man-wolf, and yet… Thanks, Mr Spielberg.

Spyros had the tiller jammed under one arm and was shouting for them to get down. Most of the words were drowned out by the noise of the water, but his frantic hand-waving got the message across. Instead of being down, Arthur was braced against the mast with one of Spyros's long spears in his fist, three metres of ash with a leaf of iron lashed to the end. He did not remember picking it up.

The smooth patch of sea came at the hull again.

"It comes under. Do not lean out. You lean out, it takes the arm," Spyros said.

Arthur could make out some features now that his eye had the trick of it, a long grey weight rolling just below the surface, keeping pace with the boat. He tried to set his feet as he would on stone, and found that there was nothing to set them against. His body was trying to do what it always did at the start of a fight: plant, find the line of force from his boots through the surface into the substrate and lock it. In this case, however, the surface was wet timber and the substrate was the Saronic Gulf. There was no line to find.

The deck rolled and lifted under him and moved his weight onto the wrong foot, so when he threw, the spear went where his balance went, which was not where the shark was. It was all shoulder and no wrist, a hammer-thrower's motion with a javelin's weapon. It skated off the water two feet wide and the sea swallowed it.

"Bloody hell."

"One of four, re, and gone to the bottom. You have three left. Think, before you send them after it," Spyros said.

Donna already had the second spear aimed, but she did not throw at the shark. She threw at where the shark would be, ahead of the grey shadow, and the timing was better than Arthur's because timing was what she did. The spear struck the shark behind the fin and stood there. The shark did not so much as change its course. It still came at them with three metres of ash sticking up from the sea. On that mass the spear was a pin in a bullock. Then the shaft snapped against the hull as the creature rolled, and that was the second of four spears gone.

Arthur tried again. He waited longer this time, trying to mirror Donna's posture. The throw was better, from the point of view of hitting the target. The angle, however, left much to be desired, and the spear skittered off the back of the shark and joined the other two spears at the bottom of the Aegean.

"Steve," Donna said.

Steve had the rod up and Arthur couldn't tell if he was concentrating on keeping his footing or aiming the rod. Then the thermal lance leapt at the creature, the same lance he had put through goats and wolves and guardians. It hit the water over the shark, and the sea snorted in the form of hissing steam. Steve swore.

"It's water. It's bloody under water. Heat's for air," he muttered to himself. "Fire down there is an idiot's game. Fine. Fine. Concussion then."

Arthur suspected that Steve was so used to putting fire lances through targets that he had reached for the spell instinctively.

Arthur saw the problem the same instant Steve did. The shark was against the hull, and the hull was two centimetres of pine between all of them and the sea. A full concussive blast at that range would go through the shark and the planking and open the boat like a tin.

"I can't give it everything. The boat's in the way," Steve said.

"Then give it what you can and mean it," said Donna.

He controlled the blast and caught the shark along the spine. The force was half of what it generally was, but for the first time the grey weight below flinched. Half the force was still double the impact when there were no actual bones in the body. It slewed off the hull and turned under the keel.

Then it came back for the third pass and hit them properly.

The strike came up through the garboard on the starboard side, and the boat threw them about. Arthur crashed into the mast. Spyros went down at the tiller, and the loose spar he had never got round to lashing came off the gunwale and hit him across the lower leg on its way to the bilge. Water came in along a seam that Spyros had worked shut with his own thumbs days ago.

"Sarah, the bow," Spyros screamed, clutching his leg. "Weight forward. Balance her."

Remarkably, Donna had kept her feet through the collision. She took the fourth spear from its lashing, cracked it short to a length she could better manage, and put it into the hole her first spear had made.

A second later another tight concussive blast hit the creature, and the backlash pushed the boat back slightly. Steve was preparing to send another when the calm patch of water in the roiling sea disappeared.

The shark had made the same decision that generations of lions had made when faced with a healthy, alert giraffe. The meal had got dearer than the hunger. The grey weight rolled and then went straight down. The fin did not come up again.

For a moment they stood watching the horizon, wary of other fins.

Then Spyros shouted something in Greek that broke the spell, and everyone was moving at once.

"Bail. Bail now, the pot by your foot," Spyros said, and dragged himself up the thwart with his leg trailing. "The pot."

So Arthur bailed. Steve jammed a folded overcoat and the flat of the shield against the cracked seam. He held it there while Arthur and Donna threw water over the side. Between them the level of water in the bilge stopped climbing. Steve shoved another garment from the pack into the split. It did not stop the leak, but slowed it to something they could stay ahead of.

"Sit down and let me see the leg," Sarah said to Spyros.

"The boat first."

"They have the boat. Sit."

Spyros grumbled, but sat down, and Sarah ran her hands down the shin over the trouser.

"Cracked fibula." She set her palms, and the blue lattice glowed under her skin. "Hold it still. In ten minutes you will be able to put some weight on it. It will ache for a few days, but you will be fine to walk in an hour."

"I do not complain," Spyros said, complaining.

Arthur straightened over the bailing pot and looked at the split seam.

"You'd fixed her only days ago for this journey. I'm sorry," Arthur said.

Spyros waved his hand. "A week. The seams worked, the rotten sheet replaced and the sea does not care." He looked at the stuffed overcoat and the water still sloshing in the bilge. "Kala. She floats. A boat that floats is still a boat."

"We were severely limited there," Steve said. He was still holding the shield to the hull. "Everything we've built is for the ground. Arthur can't plant on a deck. Donna can't outrun a fish. I can't throw a fire lance into the sea, and my tool could sink the ship."

"We need range in our kit. Proper range. Not Arthur throwing his maul or me throwing my axe. I have never once been the useless one, and I did not care for it," Donna said.

"A bow needs a year to learn and two to be worth carrying. But yes. We're a fist. Maybe crossbows?" Steve said. "Theoretically we are a balanced party. A tank, a healer and two damage dealers."

"For when you can chug down a health potion after every encounter or take a long rest," Arthur said.

Arthur narrowed his eyes and asked, "Say, what happened to the yarrow we found under Kastella?"

Steve snapped his head back as if he had just remembered something.

"What did you do?"

"Nothing. I took it out to give it to Yiayia, but she was with her coterie of would-be healers. So I might have planted it in the backyard. I was concerned it might die," Steve said defensively.

"And then you forgot about it, didn't you?" Donna said.

"Probably."

"Well, I would give it even odds for the plant still being alive when we get back. Steve is hardly Mr Greenthumbs," Donna said.

"Bail, chatty foreigners. Bail," Spyros said, and they went back to bailing.

They limped the last stretch into the shelter of Aegina, Arthur still bailing and the shield pressed to the hull. A hundred metres off the beach, in the calm waters, something came up beside the boat that was not the shark.

The creature surfaced without any apparent hurry or concern, a broad domed shell the colour of old bronze, a blunt head lifting on a wrinkled neck. It turned one eye up at the boat and the humans in it. Then it hung there in the green water and looked.

Loggerhead. Caretta caretta. The individual is mature, the carapace approaching one metre. The lineage predates the primate order by a hundred and fifty million years, Archie said.

"A turtle. That's a wild loggerhead. There are barely any—" He was cut short by Spyros.

"My grandmother used to say that the sea does not send them for nothing. A turtle that comes to a hurt boat, it is the sea choosing to carry you the rest of the way in. An old soul, guiding you to the shore." Spyros watched it for a long moment. "Bad luck to touch it. Good luck that it came. You take the good and you leave the animal alone."

The turtle floated for a few minutes, unimpressed by all of them, and then tipped its head down and slid soundlessly under.

"These were on the coins, you know," Steve said.

"Aegina minted the first proper coins in Europe. The silver staters. And stamped on every one of them was a sea turtle. Right here. This island was the mint of the whole Greek world, and the turtle was the mark that said this weight of silver is good, I stand behind it."

"And then Athens conquered them," Donna said, from the tiller.

"That they did." Steve nodded. "And after that the coins changed. The sea turtle comes off it, and a land tortoise goes on. Same island, same silver, but the animal on the money crawls up out of the water and onto the dry land, because the sea wasn't theirs any more. You can read the whole defeat in the shell they chose to stamp the coins with."

Nobody said anything as the turtle-less water slid past.

"It's the same as cores," Arthur said.

"Hmmm?" Steve looked at him.

"Cores are now the same thing. You take a value, and you stamp it into an object small enough to carry. They put a turtle on a lump of silver and it was worth a cart. We pull a bead of light out of a monster and it's worth a week's food." He nodded at the field pack. "It's coinage. That's what a core is now, till we get back to any standard currency."

"A turtle on the silver meant Aegina stood behind the silver. Somebody's word made it good. Who stands behind a core? There are no organised cities any more, or organised systems," Donna said.

"Nobody stands behind it, and that's what makes cores better as currency. Silver was only ever worth the man who stamped it. Aegina fell, and the turtle went from a promise to a picture on a lump of metal. Only the intrinsic value of the metal remained. A core can't fall like that. Nobody mints it, so nobody can debase it, and you don't have to trust a soul for what it's worth."

"Huh," Steve muttered.

"Land in front of you. Philosophy on the shingle, not in my boat. My boat has heard enough today," Spyros said.

They beached the caïque in a cove south of the harbour town. The hull ground onto the shingle and Spyros went to the split plank and knelt beside it. He put his hand on the wood the way you touch something alive that is hurt.

"I will stay with the boat and try some repair. But she will hold for the return if we don't meet another shark," he said.

The party shouldered their packs and moved inland. The shore gave way to scrub and then the scrub to pines. The ground tilted upwards, and soon after they were met with a welcoming party.

There were five of them. Raptor-derived but built to griffon-vulture scale. Their wingspans were easily over three metres, and the talons gleamed in the sun.

But this was land.

Arthur planted, and where the deck had given him nothing the shingle gave him everything. The first one stooped at the shield and he barely felt the impact. Donna triggered Reflex Action, and the first bird was down before it had finished its dive, the second a heartbeat behind it.

Steve no longer had to throttle his output, and he put a thermal lance through the third at fifteen metres, and it fell out of the air already dead. The fourth broke off and turned only to meet Arthur's maul mid-air, and that was that. The fifth came for Sarah and met the axe Donna had planted in its path, and that was five.

The whole fight ran perhaps twenty seconds. Nobody was even breathing hard.

"That's more like it," Donna said, nodding to the rest.

"Forty-six. Forty-six, off the next level. We are forty-six short. I could kill one very small extra bird and we would all go up," Steve said with an injured look on his face.

"Except there are no more birds," Sarah said.

"I am aware there are no more birds. I am registering that the universe has a rather ungentlemanly sense of humour."

Sarah was on her usual core duty and found three cores. Arthur was looking at the cores as she handed them over to Steve.

"What do you think about giving these to Spyros, for the boat?" Arthur asked, looking around.

"I don't care, I'm not binding any of those to my gear," Donna said.

Steve and Sarah both shrugged when Arthur looked at them.

They walked back down to the cove where Spyros was busy tending to the boat. He got up with a concerned look on his face when he saw the party coming down.

"Trouble?" he asked.

"Not really," Arthur said as Steve held out the three cores towards Spyros. "For the boat."

Spyros looked at the three cores, but did not take them. "The fare was four. Four cores on boarding. You paid the four."

"This isn't the fare. This is for the hole in your boat, and the week you will have to spend fixing it and not fishing. And who knows, we might need a boat again," Arthur said with a smile.

There was a long silence while the proud man decided. Then Spyros took the three cores and put them away in a pocket without a word.

"Kala. The boat I can mend. Now go do what you came to do, and then we go back to safe harbour," he said.

Arthur turned and looked up the slope, past the pines to where the ground rose towards the island's high shoulder. Somewhere up there, under the peak, was the node. Arthur had felt the pull for the last hour, and on ground the pull was steady and sure. It was not far now.
