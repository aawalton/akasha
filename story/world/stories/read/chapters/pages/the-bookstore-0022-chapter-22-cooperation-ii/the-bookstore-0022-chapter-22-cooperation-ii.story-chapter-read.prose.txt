In a rare surge of courage, Rick threw himself at the wolf instead of away from it.

The decision felt foolish the moment he made it.

He drove the spear forward at the panicked beast and landed an awkward strike against its foreleg. The wolf howled and twisted. Rick yanked the weapon back immediately, unwilling to repeat the mistake from the boar hunt and lose his spear with it still buried in flesh. That instinct saved him. The wolf snapped where the shaft had been a heartbeat earlier.

Without thinking, Rick attacked again.

This time the point only scraped through fur.

The beast had been torn between two urges from the start—escape the fire or bite the man in front of it. Pain tipped the balance. Its lips drew back. Its body lowered. Then it sprang.

Rick had no time to set the spear properly.

He reacted on reflex, jerking the weapon sideways and blocking with the butt end. The impact shuddered through his arms and almost knocked him back, but the wolf was lighter than the boar had been, and the fear in it had stolen some of its force. Rick managed to shove it away.

They circled each other for a breath.

Rick held the spear tightly and tried not to show how badly his hands wanted to shake. The wolf grinned at him, tense and ragged, its eyes flicking everywhere except where they ought to. It was not thinking clearly anymore. Fire, pain, and panic had made that much obvious.

Then the beast lunged again.

Or rather, it began to.

Halfway through the motion, it faltered. The flames behind Rick had shifted with the wind, brightening just enough to catch the wolf’s eye. Instinct screamed louder than bloodlust. The beast hesitated, turned away from him, and tried to retreat.

Rick did not waste the opening, and drove the spear into its neck.

The wolf let out another cry and stumbled back. Whatever it had thought of Rick before, the beast had now judged him too dangerous to challenge directly. It tried to break away, angling toward the nearest patch of shelter and shadow beyond the fire.

That would have been a mistake even if Rick had let it go. But he did not.

He chased it for two quick strides and struck again, this time into its back. The wolf collapsed in a knot of limbs and sound, then sagged into the ground.

It was over. Rick stood over the body, panting, and only then realized how hard his heart was pounding.

So this was what it felt like to fight alongside others.

Iris kept her attention on the fire lines.

That was her job. The cantrip itself was simple. Reaching for the prepared grass lines at this distance was the harder part. Once her spell touched them, the ignition took almost nothing—only the right flow of essence, a word of power, and enough control not to set the wrong part of the forest ablaze.

That last concern had not been theoretical. When Rick read about the wolves’ weakness to fire, the four of them had immediately started searching for ways to use it. They had thrown ideas back and forth with increasing desperation until Anne, who had been listening silently, stepped in and offered the final piece.

Flammable roadside grass.

People paid attention to it every dry season because all it took was one careless spark, or the wrong lightning strike, to set a whole area in flames. Anne had seen plenty of it growing between town and the bookstore and pointed it out to them.

From there, the plan had become almost obvious.

Build a grid, draw the wolves in, ignite the lines, and then fight whatever ended up trapped inside.

No one had expected to catch the whole pack. Two wolves would have been enough. Three would be fortunate.

Rick nearly dying before she could cast had not been part of the plan.

Still, once the grid came alive, the plan began working about as well as such things ever did. The wolves did not understand how shallow the fire lines truly were. They only saw flame cutting their paths apart and reacted exactly as the beastiarium had promised they would.

Cyrus moved first. He crossed one of the burning lines with his sword already drawn and went after the nearest trapped wolf before it could settle into a direction. The beast did not engage him immediately. Like Rick’s, it wanted distance more than battle. But Cyrus kept coming, straightforward as ever, giving it less and less room to avoid the choice.

Then the wolf struck.

Iris stopped breathing. The bite caught Cyrus’s arm. Not deeply enough to cripple it, but more than enough to make her stomach drop. As usual, the idiot seemed to require one real wound before remembering that fights were supposed to be taken seriously. He bellowed, cursed, and finally answered with a proper slash that opened the wolf’s side.

Only then did Iris force herself to breathe again.

She kept the spellwork going.

One wolf had broken toward her side of the grid, though not quite toward her directly. Hakon stood close by with a handful of stones and a knife, which was not especially comforting, but it was what they had. The foxkin was a healer first. No one here expected him to become a warrior simply because circumstances were impolite. Still, Iris trusted that he would not fail her if it came to that.

And if things became truly dangerous, she had one last answer.

The acid potion.

She had not mentioned it to Cyrus, partly because he would somehow find a way to be cheerful about the possibility of her dissolving something’s face, and partly because she preferred to keep one unpleasant surprise in reserve. Between that and her fire cantrip, she could at least make a wolf regret choosing her.

Another trapped beast was nearing one of the weakening lines.

Iris noticed it and bit back a curse. The grass there had already burned low. She would have to refresh it soon or lose that side entirely. She shifted her focus, prepared the next pulse of fire—

And saw Rick do something unexpected.

The hunter crossed past one of the dying edges and moved toward the wolf instead of away from it.

For one brief, astonished moment, Iris thought he had lost his mind.

The beast crouched, lips peeled back, ready to spring. Rick did not wait. He flung a small rope net at it and threw himself sideways almost at the same time. The wolf lunged through the air and bit straight into the netting. Teeth tore through part of it, but not fast enough. Its own momentum dragged the rest over its body, and it hit the ground in a snarled knot of rope and limbs.

Iris stared.

The maneuver was so simple, so primitive, and so effective that it shocked her into forgetting the fire line for a heartbeat.

Only one heartbeat. But in a fight like this, one was enough.

By the time she realized the mistake, the weakened section had collapsed into smoke and glowing embers.

She cursed herself and snapped her attention back to the flames.

Too late for that side.

The trapped wolf reacted instantly. The moment the fire died low enough to stop frightening it, the beast launched itself through the gap in a desperate escape. Fortunately, Cyrus had already seen the same opening. He turned, stepped into the path, and brought his sword down in a single hard slash.

The wolf dropped and did not rise again.

Cyrus looked over at Iris with entirely too much satisfaction.

“Hey, Iris,” he called, grinning despite the blood on his arm. “You seemed a little out of your game today. Thank the Gods I was here to save the day.”

Iris closed her eyes for one slow breath.

This was, unfortunately, what happened when one gave Cyrus even the smallest chance to feel heroic.

Anne was in the middle of three different tasks when she heard customers coming in.

She set aside the restoration notes, capped the ink, and left the storage room quickly. Anyone who entered the bookstore during business hours deserved proper attention, even if she had been cataloging valuable inventory just moments earlier. Besides, customers arriving in a group were often more interesting than customers arriving alone.

This time, they were also dirtier.

Rick and the three adventurers stood in the front area wearing the unmistakable expressions of people returning from a successful hunt. Rick looked mildly torn about the face and hands, Cyrus had a bandage around one arm, and the others carried the bright energy of a job completed.

Anne was pleased before they said a word.

The success did not directly enrich her, at least not today, but that hardly mattered. Her guidance had produced results again. A hunter had advanced. A party had worked with him. The bookstore’s influence had pushed one more step into the forest and come back carrying proof.

That was good business even before it became actual business.

“Miss,” Rick said after the greetings, his expression turning oddly embarrassed, “there’s something outside we need your help with.”

Anne raised an eyebrow.

That was not the sort of line one heard every day, and certainly not from a young hunter who had recently begun learning the joy of competence. Still, she nodded and followed them out.

The answer became clear at once.

Several pieces of meat had been hung on a rough wooden drying frame. Wolf materials, by the look of them. Not an enormous haul, but respectable, especially for a first joint expedition. Her eyes moved across the arrangement, taking in hide, blood traces, crude but functional processing, and the general shape of success.

Then she noticed the living creature.

A grey wolf lay tangled in a rope net near the yard, still struggling, snarling, and trying to find some weakness it could exploit. The beast looked healthy enough, which in this case was the most troublesome part. A dead beast was a solved problem.

“You caught one alive,” Anne said.

Rick rubbed the back of his neck.

“We did. More or less.”

Cyrus snorted. “Mostly him.”

Iris looked tired enough not to argue.

“Can you help us with it?” Rick asked. “I mean… do you know a safe way to keep it bound? We tried threatening it, kicking the ground near it, even dropping water on it. Nothing makes it settle for long. It’ll be worth more alive and healthy, so we don’t want to injure it.” He glanced at the wolf and then back at her. “But if it keeps going like this, I’m afraid it’ll eventually get loose.”

They had come expecting a direct answer.

Perhaps a sleeping draught. Perhaps a restraint spell. Perhaps some clever bit of bookmancer knowledge that would let them sell the beast without risking their hands in the process.

Anne studied the wolf in silence.

Then she studied them.

A live wind-essence beast. Young adventurers short on money but rich in need. A hunter already learning routes, habits, and terrain. Customers who had just proven they could cooperate under pressure. A bookstore that, by its nature, was supposed to guide people toward the knowledge and opportunities that suited them best.

The answer came together so quickly that Anne almost smiled.

Instead, she kept her face perfectly straight.

“Hm,” she said. “I see.”

Then she looked from the tangled wolf to the four stunned young people around it and asked, with complete seriousness:

“But instead of selling it, why don’t you keep it?”
