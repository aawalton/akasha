Pocket glanced over at me as she reached up to clip onto the zipline.  We were all dressed in black, and my hatred of thieves was warring with my hatred of ninjas.  We weren’t invisible, but it was the principle of the thing.

“Remember, I’ll create the breach in the aura detector, but it will only last a few seconds; the rest of you need to be right up my ass, otherwise we’ll be rumbled.”

“I’ll be happy to be right up your shapely—”

“Shut up, Lughole.  Bob, are you comfortable with this?”

“He’ll be comfortable right up—”  Pocket kicked out with one foot, hitting Lughole in the stomach and cutting off any further innuendo.

Zip-lining looked like fun, although it was probably a downgrade from actually flying under my own power.  I reached up and plucked at the steel cable that Pocket had launched across the street.  A satisfying twang rang out, earning hisses of annoyance from my companions.

“What?  The distraction is already in full swing,” I muttered.

The Bank of the House of Lucratis, which was an unwieldy name for the organisation and was clearly lacking a satisfactory number of Bob’s in the wording, lay across the other side of one street and three rooftops.  We were perched on a ledge a hundred metres above the roads, halfway up some noble's coping mechanism for his small penis syndrome.

Infiltrating Baron Guthrup’s property had been a cinch, especially for the precocious thieves accompanying me.  They had scaled the outer wall like rats up drainpipes and dropped down the other side to find me waiting for them.  Jumping ten feet high wasn’t exactly tough.  Locks had been picked, guards subdued but not killed, and we had walked up a long spiralling stone staircase to find ourselves perched on a small balcony overlooking the bank.

“They’ll deal with the urchins soon enough,” Burglar said.  “But for now, Bob is right.”

We formed an awkwardly intimate line behind Pocket, all clipping on.

“Lughole…” she said in a warning tone.

“That’s not my finger.”

“Gods you’re disgusting.  We go on three.  One… Two—urk!”

Lughole had shoved her off the edge and leapt after her.  I was behind Lughole, carefully trying to avoid physical contact with the man.  Whether or not spirit animals were a thing on Helstat, I couldn’t say for sure.  If they were, he was some kind of blend between rodent and rabbit.  As I sped out into the night, I heard Burglar launch himself almost as soon as I started moving.

The smog worked in our favour.  We were high enough up that we should only show as black smudges from below, and with the street lights between us and any terrestrial observers, we ought to be invisible.  Pocket passed the raised ledge at the edge of the bank’s roof, and blue light flashed.  A circle opened up, around which a pale cyan field was being repulsed.  I hurtled towards the break in the magical barrier.

My natural instinct when moving too quickly through the air was to flare my wings, but in my mammal suit, that was not an option.  I slammed into Lughole, knocking him into Pocket.

“Sorry!” I whispered.

Burglar smacked into my back, but I didn’t budge.  There was another thud and an oof as the fifth member of our raiding party arrived.

“I need to get to a locus.  If anyone was watching their orbs, we’re screwed,” hissed Particlics.

He was the magical defences specialist, Pocket was the lockpick, Burglar was the muscle, and Lughole was the safe breaker.  With me present, I had felt that bringing Burglar along in case anyone needed putting down was a bit of an insult, but I had been forced to accept their counterarguments.  You could never have too many dudes on hand capable of knocking heads together in case we ran into some unexpected guards.

Pocket looked up from the door she was fiddling with.  “Ten seconds.”

I counted to ten under my breath, and sure enough, as I reached the final T, the door clicked open, and Particlics jogged down into the darkness.  I followed them, bringing up the rear, down three flights of stairs.

“After you,” Particlics said quietly, sweeping his lank blond hair back from his eyes, as we arrived at a solid-looking door.

Thieves made me nervous.  No way was I going in first.  I didn’t have anything in my pockets, but I didn’t know for sure these bastards couldn’t nick stuff out of my pocket dimension.

“No, you first, I insist.”  I deployed my toothiest of grins.

“The security team is on the other side of the door.  Don’t piss about.  Bob and Burglar, you go first.”

Was this a ruse?  Honour among thieves is all well and good, but I was not a thief, so unlikely to fall under their “brotherhood” in their minds.  As I hesitated, Burglar slipped past me and swung the door open silently.

I ducked in after him.  Three men, all broad and strong-looking, sat in neat grey uniforms in front of desks covered with orbs that they were constantly scanning with their eyes.  Burglar had pulled a cosh from his belt and moved silently towards the nearest victim.  As the lump of lead wrapped in leather fell, I lunged, covering the distance between myself and the other two.

I grabbed a skull in each hand and brought them together hard.  There was a meaty thunk, and possibly a minor cracking sound, then the pair fell to the ground between their desks in an untidy heap.  A crack rang out as Burglar rendered his target unconscious and possibly brain-damaged.

“Did you kill him?” I asked as the man slumped from his chair, a trickle of blood dripping from one ear.

“Did you kill yours?” he snapped back.  “Particlics, here you go, do your thing.”  Burglar pointed at a cabinet in the corner of the dimly lit space.

The mage hurried over in his midnight pyjamas and began fiddling with the door to the cabinet.  “Pocket, I need a hand, please.”

“I told you I’d be useful beyond gaining access,” she muttered.  She moved over, knelt down, pulled a complicated-looking tool out of her pocket, and began playing with the lock.  It swung open a moment later, and she stepped back, slipping her pick into a pocket on her thigh.  She patted the aforementioned pocket, then scowled around the room.

“Give it back.  Tools are off limits.  Only cosmetics,” she growled.

Lughole tossed the pick back to her, and she slipped it down her top for safety.

Within the cabinet was a complicated arrangement that I could only describe as gubbins.  Glowing crystals, wires made from weirdly iridescent material, and small cuddly toys.

“Why is Winnie the Pooh a thing on this world? And where does that wire plug in?” I muttered.

“Who and what?”  Particlics asked absently as he knelt, pulling wires out and rearranging crystals.

“The teddy you just shoved a gemstone the size of a tangerine up his… Jesus, you’re violating my fondest childhood memories.”  I turned away, unable to watch anymore.

“It’s an array.  It channels a bunch of the ambient mana and a chunk taken from a mana crystal into the detection and defence spells.  All I’ve done is set it on a loop for a few hours.  The system won’t notice anything anomalous because nothing weird happened in the last few hours.”

“We did just knock out the observation team,” I pointed out, waving a hand at the unconscious men.

“Well, this helps as well,” Particlics said defensively.  “I don’t know why I bothered to come along when all I’m getting is crap from an outsider.”

“He’s not an outsider anymore.  This is his first job.  He’s a thief now,” Pocket said.

“Hold up.  I am not a thief!  I just happen to be helping you out on one mission in exchange for your contractually bound loyalty when I take over the city…  And a solemn oath to never go anywhere near my pile of shinies.”

“If you’re here, you’re a thief, Bob,” Burglar chuckled.

“I—No, I’m bloody not.  Party tricks, are you done yet?  Can we get on with this already?”

“Yeah, I’m done.”  He closed the panel carefully, and it clicked into place.  “We’re clear for the next phase.”

“On me,” Burglar growled.  I’d rate it a four out of ten.  Mammals sucked at growling, but he got bonus points because the emotion behind it came through clearly.

We filed back out into the corridor and made our way along it, keeping an ear open for any footsteps approaching us.  The thieves all had a funny way of walking.  They laid their feet down in a rolling pattern, heel to toe, that muted almost all the sound they made.  I did my best to mimic them, but clearly, I hadn’t spent enough time at thief school.  Ignoring the dirty looks they kept shooting at me was no hardship.

Burglar leaned around a door and then waved us forward.  Steps leading down into darkness that shifted as the lightorbs faded.  Pocket was waving a device in the air in front of her.  It looked like a walkie-talkie, but every time it moved, more lights faded.

We must have spiralled down six floors' worth of stairs.  They let out onto a wide marble-lined room.  At the far end, beyond the windows, I could see the security guards fighting with urchins and larger, more burly-looking men and women.

“Distraction is holding well,” I said cheerfully.

“Shhh!”

The rest cat-walked away from the entrance hall, and I trailed along, doing my best not to sound like a herd of elephants.  We put down two more guards on the way.  I was fairly confident mine would live, but Burglar didn’t seem to have restraint when it came to coshing some poor unsuspecting bastard.

One broken nose and concussion, and one fractured skull later, we arrived at the vault.

“You lot keep watch.  And keep quiet,” Lugholes ordered.  The vault door was huge, fifteen feet across.  It was a gleaming metal plug blocking me from the vast wealth of my enemies.

“Suck it, Angtirm.  I will get the last laugh.  Stealing my goddamn idea… this is my revenge,” I muttered.  Wrath and Greed shared a high five in my mind at the realisation that the revenge I’d postponed in exchange for peace at Fidler’s Mill was finally at hand.

“Shhh!” everyone hissed at me.

“Fine, fine.  I’ll keep watch over there.”

I moved back to the corner that led to the entrance and watched the guards holding their own against the mob outside.  It was impressively vicious.  Neither side was holding back.  The mob was outlevelled by a considerable margin, but they made up for their individual lack of power in weight of numbers and sheer, bloody-minded vindictiveness.

Suppressing a wince as one guard got lifted off his feet from a kick to the family jewels, I watched as his colleague brought down a cudgel to drive off the attackers.  The now infertile man staggered to his feet and pushed open the door.

“Guys…” I whispered.

“Shhh!”

Lughole had his ear pressed to the safe next to a complex three-dimensional dial that he was twisting and turning.  His tongue was sticking out between his teeth and his eyes were closed, lost in concentration.

“I think we’re out of time,” I hissed.  “The guards are retreating!”

“The watch will be along shortly.  We bribed them to hold off.  Any second now you’ll hear the sound of—ah, there we go,” Burglar said.

Whistles rang out on the street, and the mob turned at bay.  The injured guard was nursing the sources of his never-to-be-born children, oblivious to me as I poked my head around the corner again.

Fists and stones rained down on the armoured glass in a last spurt of violence, then they began retreating to the right, reluctantly giving ground to the armoured guardsmen who came in wielding heavy-looking staffs.

“Hurry up,” I said, a little too loudly.  Even in his mind-rending agony, the guard on the inside looked up, tears streaming down his face, and opened his mouth to squeak an alert.
