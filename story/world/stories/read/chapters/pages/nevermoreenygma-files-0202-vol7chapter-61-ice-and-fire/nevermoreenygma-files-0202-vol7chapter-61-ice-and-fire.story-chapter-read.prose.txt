Chapter 61

Ice and Fire

At the beginning of January 2095, tensions continued to rise worldwide.

Although some progress had been made through the use of specialized weapons, the Fractus threat seemed endless. Despite ongoing efforts to integrate humans, espers, and feys into unified military structures, the war remained unpredictable. The sudden and seemingly random appearance of portals, opening and closing like brief blinks in the fabric of reality, made it impossible to predict where the next incursion might occur.

Given the positive results achieved in the field, several trials had been conducted to determine whether certain feys could assume command roles during operations. Mixed squads were already active across the globe, and if everything continued as planned, full deployment was approaching faster than anyone had expected.

Meanwhile, Mai continued participating in the ongoing trial missions.

Following the success of the first operation—chaotic though it had been—three more missions followed within a single week.

A happy New Year celebrated not with fireworks, but with electrical weapons.

The second and third missions had at least been easier than the first, in the sense that she had not been electrocuted on the front line. Unfortunately, that improvement had been replaced by a chill that seemed to crawl through her entire body.

The reason: The Fractus were much smaller than before, but vastly more numerous.

This time they belonged to the same armored species previously encountered in the African deserts, except these new specimens had arrived from the sea. The creatures, roughly two meters in diameter and nearly three meters long when fully extended on land, had rolled across the ocean as though they weighed no more than feathers. During a single night they invaded a fifteen-kilometer stretch of coastline in Lebanon, between Beirut and Sarba, in what historians would later call the Battle of the Fifteen Kilometers.

More than fifteen thousand people disappeared in a single night against barely two hundred Fractus.

At least, it was assumed they had died.

Any biological lifeform touched by those organisms vanished completely.

The electrically charged rifles had finally shown promising results after receiving new calibration systems capable of measuring output and intensity while delivering synchronized double shots. Even then, several soldiers were usually required to bring down a single one of those armored creatures. Once destroyed, they left behind spherical cores with rough surfaces and a strange diffuse reflection that seemed incapable of reflecting biological forms.

Mai arrived on the second day of the invasion alongside Rainer and the others. For nearly thirty-five exhausting hours they participated in two consecutive operations, hunting down surviving Fractus and searching for any that might still be hidden.

The campaign was accompanied by a second wave, much smaller than the first and occurring far from their operational area. Their squad managed to locate only seven of the creatures while other divisions dealt with the rest.

The city itself showed surprisingly little physical damage. What haunted everyone was not the destruction.

It was the knowledge that more than twenty-five thousand people had simply ceased to exist within two days along that narrow coastal region.

Not a single drop of blood.

Simply gone.

Those operations marked the end of Mai's assignment with Reiner's division.

She and two other feys were reassigned elsewhere.

Only a few days had passed, yet it felt as though she had spent months alongside them.

Still Mai did not return to the island.

Instead, her next destination was a military vessel operating in the Atlantic near the Canadian coast, serving as part of the newly established joint coastal defense and ground-force command.

The moment she arrived and was summoned to headquarters, she nearly felt her soul leave her body.

A general asked what she thought about serving as mission commander for a field trial.

Mai stood speechless in front of a general carrying enough medals and decorations to qualify as a living Christmas tree.

For a brief moment she was certain cat ears and a tail had grown from her body, and she could practically feel a hairball climbing her throat.

She hated leadership.

Unfortunately, the recommendation had come from Rein on the island and Leon within the Council. It even appeared that Gehirn had written favorable evaluations about her and expressed interest in having Mai oversee field testing of several experimental ZAIEN weapons systems.

Naturally, that came attached to a confidentiality agreement.

Mai wondered what terrible crime she had committed for all of them to do this to her. She attempted to protest, insisting that she was not prepared for command responsibilities.

General Briggs responded by pulling a thick file from his desk.

The document contained records of the missions she had carried out through Nevermore, particularly rescue and recovery operations involving feys.

Mai swallowed.

The level of information sharing between military departments made the hair on the back of her neck stand up. She sincerely hoped certain details would never become public knowledge. Otherwise she suspected she might find herself standing before a tribunal once the war ended.

Then again, how exactly did humanity intend to sentence someone who did not age?

Mai sighed. Eventually she accepted.

Not because she had changed her mind, but because after several minutes she noticed Briggs's eyebrows slowly becoming more and more threatening.

Regardless of her personal opinions, she had no desire to become a pain in the ass.

In the end, she surrendered.

The mission assigned to Mai and her team took them to a remote region in northern Canada, to a snow-covered lost city known as Hothmar.

Loaded with new exoskeletons, they set out for the mission. New weapons too... The fifth version in a week. Mai wondered who were the poor souls who tested those things in the lab. Or were they the guinea pigs for that, too? She’d have to ask Rein if the Council was using them for that as well. She hoped not.

Hothmar had been a prosperous city during the 20th century, before extreme temperatures and solar storms of the 21st century left it forgotten. Now, intelligence reports indicated that something had taken up residence there: a group of fractus had settled in the ruins of the city, and attacks on nearby areas were becoming increasingly frequent. Worst of all, according to the latest data, the fractus dominating Hothmar were a bit more strange than the previous ones the army groups fought in recent days and weeks, but also seemed to be changing shapes or evolving.

Mai’s team was about to be deployed to investigate, neutralize the threat, and recover any data that might explain how rapidly the fractus were adapting. The situation was further complicated by the location: the Arctic’s extreme climate was an enemy in its own right. Mai didn't like the cold, but she liked being in charge even less. Given the situation, she didn't want to be the one to go against the grain. Teams all over the world were testing the waters for the feys, and she didn't want to be the one to tip the scales by arguing that it was a bad idea. Sure, she still thought so… a little. But what did she know? She was just a girl who had spent nearly a hundred years bouncing around the world.

The group arrived in Hothmar at dawn. The city was blanketed in thick snow, and the icy wind cut like freshly sharpened blades. The old buildings gave her the impression of being ghosts that, instead of sheets, were hidden from head to toe under a thick layer of snow. The streets were empty, yet the feeling of being watched hung in the air.

Mai and the Alpha-7 squad disembarked from the Nevermore transport five hundred meters from the designated point, taking cover behind the remains of a collapsed building. Thermal sensors registered erratic readings across the area: barely residual heat, but no human presence. Zaien's new equipment had a magical feature that allowed them to detect the ley lines in that place. There weren't too many of them, and they were really faint.

It was a trap—or something worse. She didn’t believe it. They had no intelligence, right?

“Delta formation,” Mai ordered, checking her visor.

The exoskeleton responded well, though the thermal compensation system still had flaws, temperature indicators fluctuated every ten seconds. To her right, Tina, another human Nevermore agent, now turned soldier, calmly adjusted her Gauss rifle with professional precision. Behind them, two espers maintained psychic surveillance, while the rest of the team advanced with detectors active.

“There’s nothing here?” she asked.

They both shook their heads.

Just to be sure, she would have liked to take off her helmet and use her antennae, but she didn’t want to. Who knew if echolocation might not be a bad idea that would attract them toward them.

The fractus were not visible. But they were felt. That pressure in the air, that dull vibration in the bones. Like an electric buzz that existed only inside the skull.

“Movement, northeast sector,” reported Kaito, the reconnaissance specialist.

Mai raised her arm.

“Two teams, cross-coverage. Tina, left flank. Do not fire until the target is confirmed.”

The wind whipped shards of ice like shrapnel.

Through the fog, a red figure emerged out of thin air, gradually taking shape. It was not entirely humanoid. But the fact that it bore a certain resemblance caused them to exchange glances. They knew this from the reports, but seeing it was different. Had this fractus tried to copy an anthropomorphic form?

It resembled a segmented mass over five meters tall, with limbs resembling crystal and metal, moving unnaturally. It reminded Mai of those exercises young children are given, where they describe objects by drawing them using geometric shapes. Although the body was moving, it seemed to be composed of rhomboidal and triangular shapes joined at their vertices. Given its color and apparent composition, someone else might almost have thought it was a piece of abstract jewelry. Its surface shifted constantly, as if reconfiguring itself. Long, thin legs ending in sharp points were its only point of contact with the ground.

“Visual contact,” Mai whispered.

The creature turned. In an instant, the air thickened, charged. The weapons fired. A burst of electromagnetic projectiles hit the fractus’s torso, but the impacts dissipated, absorbed by a transparent layer that shimmered like liquid crystal. It was quite different from the basilisks or the round ones.

“That thing has a shield,” said one of the Espers.

That was new.

“It’s not penetrating the barrier,” Tina said. “It changed the frequency.”

Mai gulped. “Let’s try these gloves, then. Turn off the weapons and restart them after three seconds.”

Mai activated the V3 pulse module integrated into the exoskeleton glove.

“I'll take the right side. Tina, take the left. Everyone else, cover us and open fire if it charges.”

The two of them secured their weapons to the exoskeletons' magnetic locks and broke into a run. The creature stood roughly fifty meters away, but they only needed to close the distance by twenty before launching the attack into the ground.

A bluish wave spread across the terrain, distorting the surrounding magnetic fields. The Fractus reacted immediately, its shield flickering.

Mai knew the effect would only be temporary. She had read the instruction manual thoroughly before agreeing to use the device. The glove was an experimental system designed to generate an electromagnetic pulse. It was nowhere near powerful enough to destroy a Fractus, but that was never its intended purpose.

Field tests conducted by other teams suggested that the pulse created a strange anomaly throughout the creatures' structures, something that propagated through their entire bodies. If the reports were correct, it might disrupt them long enough to lower their defenses and allow the squad to strike with the new weapons and expose the core.

“Now!” Mai shouted.

Three plasma discharges struck the creature. For an instant, it seemed to dissolve. Then two smaller forms emerged from its body.

The division occurred almost instantaneously, like a cellular process unfolding in fast forward.

“Replication confirmed!” Mai reported.

The BOC's voice echoed through the command channel. Although Mai led the squad, someone else remained in charge of overall operations. “Priority: isolate and destroy through thermal exposure.”

Mai moved through the remains of a collapsed building.

“Tina. We're forcing critical temperature.”

“Understood. Charging thermal launchers.”

The new exoskeletons included controlled-fusion ignition modules. They were experimental systems designed specifically for extreme combat scenarios.

Mai adjusted the output, aimed at the ground, and fired.

A column of white fire erupted upward, instantly melting snow and vaporizing condensed frost. The two creatures attempted to retreat, but the sudden heat disrupted them. Their internal structures began vibrating violently while the icy plates beneath their bodies cracked and shattered like tempered glass under pressure.

Mai and Tina repeated the maneuver, advancing another ten meters before directing a second pulse into the ground. The two Fractus hesitated, seemingly disoriented. That opening was all the rear elements needed.

The soldiers behind them advanced and fired again from much closer range. One of the espers landed a direct hit.

The projectile pierced the weakened shield, sending crimson crystal fragments flying in every direction. Moments later they repeated the tactic against the second creature.

This time the shot struck a rhomboid section in its chest. The surface fractured.

For a brief moment they caught sight of what appeared to be the core, glowing with an iridescent radiance beneath the broken layers.

Two minutes later—though to Mai it felt closer to ten—they finally succeeded in destroying one of the cores.

Everyone expected they would still have to deal with the second creature.

Instead, the remaining Fractus simply dissolved. The replication had not included the core.

In fact, it was possible that the duplication process was merely another form of manifestation rather than true reproduction.

Regardless, they had eliminated it far more quickly than anticipated.

After that just silence and the cold wind remained for several seconds.

“Targets neutralized...” Tina murmured quietly.

“Not yet,” Mai replied.

Because something on the ground was moving.

From the glowing core where the Fractus had stood, a black filament began extending outward like a living root searching for air.

It moved really fast.

Mai fired another burst.

The filament spasmed, twisted around itself, and disintegrated into black particles.

Finally, it stopped moving.

The squad maintained their positions for several seconds. Then Kaito broke the silence.

“Commander, sensors are still detecting activity underground. It isn't gone.”

“Collect samples,” Mai ordered. “We'll need every piece of data we can get before we pull out.”

Tina looked at her through her polarized visor.

“What the hell are these things?”

“I don't know,” Mai replied. “But I don't like it when they take humanoid forms.”

A distant sound echoed through the ruins, immediately putting everyone on alert.

Of course, one kill was never going to be enough.

More would come.

They would have more than enough work for the rest of the day—and possibly for several more before the entire area could finally be cleared.

The first casualties in Alpha-7 occurred on the third day.

By then, they had already destroyed several Delta Types, was the name they would give them for the time being, and believed they had at least a partial understanding of their behavior. Their shields could be temporarily disrupted through electromagnetic pulses. Extreme heat appeared to destabilize their structures due to the sudden thermal shock between intense cold and high temperatures. Their cores remained the primary weak point, but those black filaments had to be neutralized before anyone could safely approach.

Mai was moving through the ruins of an old railway station when the reconnaissance team detected activity beneath the ground.

It was not a strong signal.

Merely an energy anomaly connected to one of the thin ley lines running deep beneath the city, with traces of that energy rising toward the surface.

It was enough to justify an investigation.

“Possible contact beneath the northern platform,” Kaito reported.

Alpha-7 deployed immediately.

The sensors revealed something motionless beneath several meters of ice.

It appeared to be just a core and the faint energy of the ley line seemed concentrated around that point. It did not resemble a Fractus.

It looked like nothing more than an abandoned core.

Mai was not convinced.

Two soldiers advanced with specialized recovery equipment.

They were the youngest members of the squad, barely in their twenties. Ethan Mathesson and Ivonne Williams.

They established a two-meter perimeter and began excavating while the rest of the squad maintained security. It was the first time during the mission that they had needed to dig a core out of the ground.

Had a Fractus somehow died underground?

As they worked, the snow began to vibrate.

Mai felt a chill run through the antennae she could barely move beneath her helmet.

“Get back!”

The warning came a fraction of a second too late.

The supposedly abandoned core exploded.

Dozens of black filaments burst outward in every direction like living roots escaping from a seed, throwing snow and frozen earth into the air.

The two soldiers never had a chance to move.

The filaments pierced the gaps between the exoskeletons and their neck seals before anyone could react. The suits lost power instantly. The armor collapsed in on itself as though the entire system had been drained from within.

They barely had time to scream.

The filaments tore through metal, flesh, and bone alike. The entire squad opened fire while rushing forward.

By the time Mai reached them, it was already too late.

The filaments that had impaled them withdrew in a final violent motion, slicing both soldiers apart and scattering the remains across the snow.

The silence that followed was unbearable.

No one spoke too much as they recovered what remained.

Not even Mai.

Unlike the armored Fractus or the basilisks, this type did not freeze or erase its victims. The bodies remained where they fell while blood spread across the snow like crimson roses scattered upon a white canvas.

Mai bit her lip and struggled to maintain her composure, as she used insulated recovery gloves to collect both Fractus cores and pieces of human remains from the battlefield.

The transports arrived five minutes later as technicians and military personnel moved into the secured area.

There was still work to be done.

That night, Mai barely slept at the forward operating camp established near the site where they had destroyed the first Delta Type.

The following day, she almost joined the casualty list herself.

A group of three Delta Types had been located inside the maintenance tunnels beneath the city.

The battle unfolded in cramped spaces between frozen pipelines and partially collapsed corridors. For nearly twenty minutes Alpha-7 managed to maintain the advantage, destroying two of the Fractus before the last one changed.

Its body split apart into multiple geometric segments that began rotating around one another.

“Fall back!” Mai ordered.

For a brief moment, the image of Ethan and Ivonne's mangled remains flashed through her mind.

The creature lunged forward.

The metal plates of the exoskeletons groaned as the Fractus's energy field swept past them.

Mai fired and the shot destroyed part of the exposed core, but it was not enough.

The creature exploded into a cloud of crystalline fragments and from that cloud emerged new filaments, spreading through the tunnel like a living black net.

One of the soldiers was caught in the leg. Mai reacted on instinct.

She threw herself forward and shoved him clear of the attack.

The decision saved his life but it nearly cost her own.

The filaments wrapped themselves around her exoskeleton.

The alarms began blaring immediately.

Power loss. Structural failure. Structural failure. Structural failure.

For a brief instant, Mai thought she was going to die.

The energy drain caused the exoskeleton's actuators to lock unevenly. Armor plates began folding inward as systems failed one after another. One section of the right leg rotated several degrees out of alignment, and Mai heard a sickening crack followed by a wet, crushing sound as the metal compressed the flesh of her foot.

With trembling hands, she activated the emergency mechanical release.

The locking mechanisms disengaged with explosive force.

Entire sections of the exoskeleton broke away as she collapsed into the snow.

The others destroyed the filaments only seconds later.

That night, while running a fever and enduring as best she could the mixture of medication and her own regenerative abilities, Mai sat in the infirmary tent watching the snowstorm through a frost-covered window.

There, she realized something she had already known for a long time.

Leadership was not about deciding who lived.

It was about carrying the names of those who never came back.

And that was a responsibility she was not sure she wanted to bear. Not because she was afraid, but because she believed someone else could do it better. If she had done better herself, those two young soldiers would still be alive instead of lying in body bags.

There was still work to be done in the area, but Alpha-7's part of the operation was over. After four days in Hothmar, it was finally time to return.

During the trip back to the ship, nobody spoke much. That was the price of war, and a tragedy that was undoubtedly repeating itself across countless battlefields around the world. Yet Mai knew one thing with certainty.

She had been right when she tried to avoid that responsibility.

For that reason, the following day she went to the general's office and requested that she no longer be placed in command of other people. She could follow orders without complaint, but she did not believe she was capable of carrying the burden of leadership on her shoulders when such a short mission had already cost the lives of two soldiers under her command.

Briggs let out a long sigh and sank back into his chair.

"During the time of the Uprisings, do you know how many people I lost under my command?"

"No, sir."

"In my entire life, I've lost eighteen people. Young men and women. Even so, if I were in your position, I have no doubt I'd still be out there. In the past week alone, I've received reports of more than a hundred dead soldiers and marines. Global casualties are already being counted in the millions, and I'm afraid this is only the beginning."

He paused and leaned back slightly in his chair.

"We used to fight one another over food, water, resources, and money. Now we're fighting for our planet. What better reason could there be for those who possess power to use it under circumstances like these?"

"I don't feel like I want power for that," Mai replied quietly. "I only want to do what I can."

Briggs scratched the side of his head and frowned.

"I'll admit it, I find feys difficult to understand. Most of you look like us, but with that kind of lifespan, you'd think wisdom would come with it."

"Wisdom comes only from understanding, and knowledge comes through reflection."

The general stared at her for a moment.

"Using that as an example, I understand you even less, young lady."

A faint smile appeared on his face. Mai could only respond with a sad expression.

"You're probably much older than I am. You've spent most of your life moving from one battlefield to another, and you have the experience to lead. If you don't want to be part of an army, I can understand that. You've probably seen more than your share of terrible things. But you can save a lot of people."

"With all due respect, General, I think you're right, but there's a nuance to it. I can save more people without being in command. I can follow orders, but I'm not qualified to lead others against an enemy we're only beginning to understand."

"Yet according to your record, you've survived by improvising on the battlefield more than once."

"That's different when I was alone, or with companions capable of regeneration. We can choose to risk our own lives. What I don't want is to be responsible for putting others in danger because of the way I am. I know myself well. As I said, I can follow orders. But sometimes I'm impulsive, and my body moves faster than my thoughts. Under those circumstances, I don't want to put other people at risk because of a poor judgment call in combat."

Briggs looked at her for several seconds before folding his hands over his stomach.

"Very well. I won't pressure you any further."

"Thank you. May I ask for something?"

"Go ahead."

"I'd like the addresses of the two soldiers who died during the mission."

"The families were notified yesterday."

Mai was about to speak, then stopped herself. A few seconds later, she tried again.

"Even so, I'd like to write to them. They were under my command. That's my responsibility."

"I'm sorry, but that won't be possible right now. I assume you can imagine why."

Mai nodded.

She could.

It wasn't the general's fault, and the reason was obvious.

"Following your request, I'll arrange for you to be assigned as a regular squad member from now on. The next rotation will probably arrive within a week."

Mai nodded again.

"Where am I going next?"

"The Pacific, most likely. The western coast of South America. We're trying to deploy feys across different latitudes, and you already have experience from several engagements."

"May I be dismissed?"

Briggs nodded.

Mai turned and walked toward the door.

"Izumi."

She stopped and looked back.

"You did a good job."

Mai blinked.

The general continued before she could answer.

"Perhaps... in a few weeks, I'll be able to send you the information you requested. If, by then, you still want to write to their families, you'll be able to do so."

At those words, Mai's pupils widened slightly.

She knew exactly what that meant.

The day was approaching.

"One more thing," Briggs said. "If this war drags on, it won't matter how much you dislike it. If others recognize your abilities, you'll have to make decisions whether you want to or not."

"Thank you," Mai replied, lowering her head slightly before leaving the office.

In the final report, the mission log was classified Crimson-Zero.

The details regarding the filament and the anomalous readings beneath the ice were omitted from the official records.

Weeks later, in the laboratories of Nevermore, that fragment would receive a name:

Delta Type.

The first of a new category discovered among the more than one hundred known Fractus variants.

Good luck trying to classify creatures capable of moving through an extra-spatial dimension.

The reason for the secrecy was simple: research.

In the middle of the war, rumors were already spreading about corporations profiting from Fractus fragments. Nevermore and several other organizations were simultaneously attempting to identify infiltrated operatives and ensure that certain cores recovered from recent battles did not fall into the wrong hands.

While all of that belonged to the realm of wartime corporate espionage, Mai had only one objective.

To destroy Fractus wherever they appeared.

The deaths of those two soldiers continued to weigh heavily on her mind, but she knew she had to keep moving forward.

After the mission in Canada, a few more followed, and Mai had been reassigned to a new team in South America: an experimental squad combining espers, human soldiers, and a few specialists in advanced weaponry. This time, their destination was an abandoned mining complex deep in the Peruvian Andes.

The area had been a key site for rare mineral extraction during the 21st century, in the era of resource wars under dictatorship, but had lain forgotten for decades. A report from Miskatonic had noted that rumors circulated in that area about a strange cult that had existed in the last century, one that extended beyond Salapunco and was linked to the occult world—but these were only rumors. It had happened so long ago that only legends remained of those events.

However, in recent weeks, signs of Fractus activity had been detected. New satellites recorded thermal anomalies and electromagnetic fluctuations in the mining complex that did not correspond to any natural activity. On the day the planet was bombarded by those fragments huge rocks had rained down in various places, and that area had also been hit by the shower. Moreover, the few explorers and scientists who had attempted to investigate had disappeared without a trace.

The mission was descend into the mines, assess the situation of the missing researchers and the fractus, and eliminate any threats.

The trip had taken one hour from the Puna Island Base in Ecuador to reach the mountainous region of southern Peru. They had been assigned the mission to investigate the area just the day before, after it was confirmed that a group of scientists from a research center in Cusco had gone missing. The scientists had been attempting to survey the area by vehicle and on foot after sending drones proved unsuccessful.

The squad arrived in a VTOL aircraft at dawn, landing on the cargo platform of the old complex. The air was cold and dry and at that altitude, the air felt a little thin, with gusts of wind slapping their cheeks, even though it was summer at that latitude. Luckily, the suit and exoskeleton kept them warm enough. If the mission dragged on too long, they’d have to switch the thermal system later, because the sun would heat things up in just a couple of hours and they’d be sweating buckets.

At first glance, the place was dead, but something in the atmosphere felt off. Mai could feel it in her bones.

The team consisted of eight members: Captain Eric Vargas, a combat strategist with extensive experience in covert operations, Sheep, a high-level esper with the ability to manipulate and detect magnetic fields, Janet, a sniper with advanced optical implants, Dmitri, a specialist in heavy weaponry, Harper, an infantry soldier with reinforced gear for hostile environments, Laura, an expert in tracking and survival, and Mai, the only fey in the team. The eighth member was an aeon aboard the ship, named Twe77Y, which would be flying over the area to transmit data on the surroundings while they explored on the ground. There were three auxiliary drone soldiers who would be keeping watch at the site while they went inside to explore the mine. There were five other members who, because they had colds, had been left behind at the base.

She had met them just a week and a half earlier, during an exchange with the teams training on platforms in the middle Pacific. Considering that she had been in cold places, the move to the equatorial region was at least a significant change. It was a miracle that the Fey had such ironclad immune systems, otherwise, between Australia, Europe, Canada, and the Atlantic, Mai would have caught colds and the flu—which were currently a problem in the north and among the human and Esper troops traveling back and forth.

Even so, no one in the squadron was young, they all had years of experience. That was a relief. Days had passed, but she couldn't shake the images of what had happened in Canada. Because she was part of the team, Vargas knew what had happened, but he hadn't said anything to her when he saw Mai's somewhat somber expression. She was still sad and still had trouble sleeping, but she tried not to show it.

The small group on land adjusted their equipment once more and cautiously surveyed the area using night vision. The entrance to the mines was abandoned but open, like a gaping mouth in the rock ready to devour anyone who approached.

Captain Vargas adjusted his communicator and spoke in a serious tone: “Alright: we go in, explore and try locate the anomaly, and ensure no further fractus activity remains in the area. If we find survivors, we evacuate them. I want tight formation and zero mistakes. If these fractus are as dangerous as the reports claim, we have no room for error.”

Janet looked around, searching for signs. “If they're fractus, we don't even know what kind. Rumor had it that they had seen something with many legs.”

“Maybe it's my wife,” Harper laughed.

Sheep sighed, adjusting his gloves. “With the amount of residual radiation here, I doubt there’s anything alive. But if there is… we’ll know soon enough.”

Mai said nothing, but an increasing unease grew within her. Something in the depths of the mine was calling to her, like a distant echo only she could hear.

At that spot, there was an abandoned backpack containing clothes and some notes. They also found other clothing, but no blood or anything else to suggest that the team of scientists had died a violent death. Some stakes in the ground outside and a sleeping bag that had been blown away by the wind revealed that this had been the spot where they had disappeared or been moved elsewhere after camping.

“Do you detect anything, Mai? With those antennas of yours?”

Mai took off her helmet. “I'm not sure.”

Although they had the necessary equipment to detect movement, due to the reported instrument malfunction, they couldn’t be sure the electronic equipment was reliable; having someone with magnetism-related powers and sonar abilities could be useful in that situation. Beyond that, when Mai made the antennas vibrate, she detected absolutely nothing, though she couldn’t shake the feeling that something was off. It could have been the slightly strange air or the altitude; beyond that, the instruments showed no variations from the readings they’d obtained before landing, and no new reports of anything unusual were coming in from the ship either.

“The whole area has strange readings, to be honest.”

“Let's find out then,”

Mai put her helmet back on. In that situation, she wondered if, when she came back later, she could ask for a custom helmet that would allow the antennas to stick out.

“No fucking around,” Dimitri declared.

The main entrance to the mine was open, but ten meters inside was more blocked in some parts by collapses, but Sheep’s sensors detected a side tunnel descending to the lower levels. They had found some papers from the high school in Cuzco, a tablet with a cracked screen that no longer worked, and a map of the area. They looked at each other and scanned the ground for footprints. There were some tracks that went back and forth, leading nowhere, and some that disappeared into the darkness of the cave.

The descent was slow and silent. The temperature began to rise abnormally as they moved into darkness. Laura’s thermal sensors registered inconsistent readings, with pockets of extreme heat alternating with areas of absolute cold. It caught their attention because the tunnels in certain areas had formations made of softer rock rather than rough walls. There were even completely smooth crystalline formations that gave the impression of being in something a little more organic than an abandoned mine. The rail tracks and conveyors were melted in certain places, but there was no mention of that in any report.

“This reminds me of how lava flows,” Dimitri said.

“This makes no sense,” Laura murmured, adjusting her visor. “It’s like the environment is… fluctuating between extremes.”

Sheep checked his tactical tablet and tested the rock walls by putting his hands on them. “Electromagnetic readings are off the charts too beyond this point. Something down here is generating an absurd amount of energy and screwing the compass.”

Laura knelt, touching the ground. “There are markings… not many, but recent. Something came this far and then retreated.”

Suddenly, a sound rumbled in the distance. A deep, guttural noise, as if something gigantic and metallic was breathing and clashing in the bowels of the mine. Sheep looked at Vargas an nodded.

Vargas raised his fist, signaling everyone to halt. “Get ready.”

Just seconds later, the ground shook. A reddish glow lit the tunnel far from them as a colossal figure emerged from the darkness. The fractus… was unlike any Mai had faced before. Its body… seemed composed of molten rock and liquid metal, as if it had been born from the very core of the Earth.

Because that’s what it seemed to be. “Oh, shit,” Mai muttered and Dimitri echoed the expression.

“DAMN, IT’S HUGE! I’ve never seen a fire one!” Harper shouted, opening fire with his plasma rifle.

“Goddamn it…” Mai muttered. “It’s not a fractus! It’s a fire elemental!”

Everyone stared at her.

“We don’t have the right weapons for this. We need someone who can use magic!”

The elemental advanced, ignoring the gunfire—or perhaps curious about the free snacks. Its limbs radiated extreme heat, and every step melted the ground around it. Then, with a brutal movement, it unleashed a burst of incandescent fire.

“Take cover!” Vargas roared.

The team scattered, barely evading the wave of heat.

The air inside the mine had become unbreathable. The heat was unbearable, the metal beneath their boots melting, and the sensor readings resembled a raging electrical storm.

“We have to get out of here, now!” Vargas shouted as a jolt collapsed part of the ceiling. The crash was so violent that the team almost lost their footing. Behind them, the elemental roared, its voice like an ancient forge awakening in the dark.

Mai turned for a moment. That creature was not an enemy… but neither was it an ally. These types of elementals were very rare. Its incandescent body oscillated between matter and energy, its form barely contained by the mass of molten rock surrounding it. Every breath made the air vibrate.

“Captain, tunnel C-3 is clear!” Laura reported, consulting her drone.

“Let’s move! Harper, cover the rear. Dmitri, drop containment grenades every ten meters.”

“In a mine, really?”

They advanced at full speed through the narrow corridor. The air smelled of ozone and molten stone. Behind them, the elemental roared, trying to force its way through the debris. Its heat melted metal like wax. But the explosions had at least given them precious seconds to flee from the elemental's reach. The problem was that the fallen material would become part of the elemental's mass.

Sheep paused for a second, watching the fluctuations on his HUD. “The magnetic field is being disrupted… That thing is drawing all the thermal energy from the environment!”

“Then all the more reason not to stick around and find out,” Mai replied, gripping her energy rifle. “Move.”

They emerged to the surface just as dawn painted the mountains red. The cold highland air was a relief… until the ground began to tremble beneath their feet.

“Movement… a lot of movement,” Janet murmured, focusing her visor. “Multiple contacts! Twelve—no, twenty—forty—what the hell?”

From the rocks emerged the creatures. Fractus, but different: dark, metallic bodies with four segmented legs like those of a spider. Their skin seemed a blend of crystal and iron, reflecting the sunrise in iridescent flashes. They moved fast—too fast.

“Defensive formation, closed circle!” Vargas roared.

The plasma rifles opened fire, the blue beams illuminating the field. The creatures leapt between rocks, evading shots with impossible movements. One landed on an auxiliary drone soldier outside, piercing his helmet.

“Sheep, generate an EMP, now!”

The used his glove and and he crouched down, touching the ground, and a bluish wave swept the terrain. The creatures staggered for a moment, vibrating, their bodies humming metallically.

“It works! Regroup and fall back!”

Behind them, the elemental emerged from the mine—a living mountain of fire and stone. The shockwave knocked everyone down. Dirt and rocks were flung out from the cave's entrance, and now that opening—which had been just a few meters wide barely half an hour ago—had transformed into an opening of twenty-meter radius.

The spider-like fractus turned in unison, drawn to the burning mass rising before them. They did not hesitate. They leapt onto it.

“God…” Laura murmured. “They’re going for the heat.”

The elemental roared, unleashing flames that incinerated dozens of the creatures, but it was useless against the fractus. Hundreds. The swarm covered it, climbing its body like a mantle of living shadows. Each time one of those beasts touched it, a fragment of its body went dark, solidifying into gray stone.

“Everyone to the VTOL, now!” Vargas ordered.

The team ran among the rocks as the ship’s engines roared at full power. The ship’s automated turrets opened fire, sweeping the terrain. The spider fractus exploded, but for every one destroyed, three more appeared.

Mai was the last to board. From the open ramp, she saw the elemental kneel, completely covered by the swarm. Its body flickered like a dying volcano. The fire in its chest slowly extinguished, leaving only a colossal statue of fractured stone.

The swarm paused for a moment, seemingly satisfied, and then began to disperse, sliding back into the cracks of the terrain.

“This doesn’t make sense…” Sheep said, watching the monitors. “The weapons can disintegrate a fractus in seconds, but that lava thing is being devoured.”

“Because its caloric energy doesn’t repel them enough to destabilize them,” Mai replied, almost in a whisper. “These fractus seek heat, they absorb it, they must be unstable. But our weapons aren’t heat—they’re concentrated discharges. They strip them of form through concentrated destabilization.”

Sheep nodded, frowning. “They want stability in three dimensions, and that thing… was pure thermal stability.”

Janet nodded. “The elemental forces dissipate as they rise in dimension, right? These things must be really small in their own dimension, but strong enough here.”

“Even an ant has the strength to lift several of its kind, my friend,” Dmitri said. “Our forces here are easily scattered in high dimensions, but here we are the breadcrumbs they carry to their anthill. The entire planet and its energy sources are food for them unless we hit them with high-voltage insecticides.”

Vargas looked at everyone. “The problem is that insects eventually become resistant to insecticides.”

“Ask the cockroaches,” Laura said.

“Not even the elementals are safe…” Mai murmured, her voice breaking. “Not even the ones tied to the Earth’s ley lines…”

Vargas looked out the window, his expression grave. “Then there’s nothing immune anymore.”

The VTOL ascended silently, leaving the battlefield behind. Below, the petrified corpse of the elemental crumbled under its own weight, while the sun—distant, indifferent—rose over the Andes.
