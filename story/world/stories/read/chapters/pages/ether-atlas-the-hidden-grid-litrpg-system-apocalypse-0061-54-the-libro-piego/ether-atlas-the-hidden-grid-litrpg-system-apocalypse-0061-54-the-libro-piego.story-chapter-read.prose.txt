Chapter 54

Information asymmetry is the oldest weapon in the human arsenal. Before your species developed bladed tools, you developed secrets. The individual who knew where the water source was controlled the group. Thirty-five thousand years of observation suggests this has not changed. - The Ether Atlas: Marginal Notes of Archimedes (Guidance System, Earth Assignment)

Steve was surveying the plateau before dawn.

The cart ruts ran south across the exposed limestone in parallel grooves, spaced 140 centimetres apart, too regular for erosion and too old for any vehicle Steve could name. He’d been following them since first light, crouching every thirty metres to run his fingers along the grooves, checking depth, checking angle, checking whether the spacing held. He kept going and it held every time.

The ruts didn’t lead to the quarries where the temple stone had been cut. They didn’t lead to the harbours where boats had launched for a thousand years before Rome existed. They ran between the temples. Ħaġar Qim to Mnajdra, and then south toward the coast where the plateau dropped away in limestone cliffs.

Some of the grooves went off the cliff edge.

“The standard narrative,” he said to Arthur, who’d followed him out because Steve on a plateau edge at dawn was a supervisory concern, “is that the temple culture collapsed around 2500 BCE. No evidence of invasion. No famine. No mass graves. The population just vanished.”

“Vanished.”

“Yes, vanished, not collapsed.” Steve straightened from the cliff edge. “People who collapse leave bodies. Broken tools. Burned granaries. The archaeological signature of a civilisation that died where it stood. The Maltese builders left finished monuments and empty homes. A complete infrastructure and no forwarding address.”

He looked south along the ruts. “These aren’t transport tracks. They connect nodes to each other. Surface markers for something underneath.”

Arthur looked at the grooves running off the cliff into air. His mind filed the data alongside the Seeder transit chambers, the conduit network beneath Cosquer, the Grid architecture Steve had been mapping since London. Infrastructure. Markers. A system built to last and then abandoned by people who left no explanation for where they went.

“Archie,” Arthur said. “The cart ruts.”

Archie’s geometry rotated at the edge of the plateau. “The grooves are consistent with surface indicators for subsurface conduit routing. The methodology is crude relative to Seeder standard but functional.”

“Crude?” Steve said.

“The Seeder specification for conduit marking involves embedded resonance tags at forty-metre intervals. The intent was identical. The execution was limited by available technology.”

“So the builders knew the conduits were there.”

Archie’s geometry slowed and he did not answer. Steve wrote “conduit markers: Archie silent on builder knowledge” in the guidebook margin.

* * *

Sarah came back at midday with the same escort who stood again by the tent flap. She knelt beside Donna’s cot and put her hands on the shin without preamble. The violet glow traced the fracture line, fainter now, the bone ends closer to alignment than yesterday.

“The callus is bridging,” Sarah said. “Two more sessions. You’ll be able to bear weight by the day after tomorrow.”

“The rate of tissue reconstruction has increased by eleven percent since yesterday’s session,” Archie said from above the folding table. “The practitioner’s channelling efficiency improves with repeated exposure to the same injury. This is consistent with Nadi Matrix pattern recognition.”

Sarah looked up at him from her work. “I know. I can feel the difference.”

“Your self-assessment is accurate.”

“Thank you for confirming what my hands already told me.”

Donna watched Sarah’s hands work the bone line. The hesitation was there again, the fraction of a second before contact. Then gone, swallowed by professional focus.

“How long have you been doing this?” Donna asked.

“Since it all started,” Sarah said without looking up.

“How many people?”

“I stopped counting after the first week.”

The guard shifted his weight and Sarah’s eyes flicked toward him, a reflex so practised it was nearly invisible. Donna saw it.

They talked in fragments after that. Short questions, shorter answers, the guard a metre and a half away hearing everything. Donna asked about the injury. Sarah answered in clinical detail. Donna asked about the compound. Sarah said it was well-provisioned. Donna asked if she was okay.

Sarah’s hands stilled on Donna’s shin. Her left thumb found the edge of the fracture callus and pressed steady counter-pressure along it, the kind of positioning and expertise that came from holding limbs together in triage tents with no anaesthesia.

“I’m fine.”

Donna had spent a career reading the gap between what people said and what their bodies told her. Sarah’s body said the thing her mouth couldn’t, and the reason her mouth couldn’t was standing by the tent flap pretending to look disinterested.

“My sister works in a restaurant,” Donna said. “Emma. Near Shoreditch. Or she did.”

The glow between Sarah’s palms pulsed once, steady.

“I hope she’s safe,” Sarah said.

“She’s resourceful. Stubborn. She’d like you.”

Sarah looked at Donna for the first time. Really looked at her. Her jaw loosened and her shoulders dropped, the brace coming off after weeks of holding. The guard, a metre and a half away, saw two women looking at each other and nothing worth reporting.

“Same time tomorrow,” Sarah said. She stood, rolled her wrists once, and left.

Donna lay on the cot and stared at the canvas ceiling for a long time after that.

* * *

The Baron arrived at the camp in the late afternoon.

He came with four fighters and the unhurried posture of a man visiting a property he considered his own in all but name. Azzopardi met him at the camp’s edge. They spoke in Maltese, their voices low. Their body language talked of two men who tolerated each other because the alternative was worse.

The Baron found Arthur and Steve by the watchtower. Steve had been explaining conduit theory to three of Azzopardi’s soldiers using a stick and a patch of dirt, drawing the Grid architecture as he understood it. Lines between nodes. Intersections where the Ether concentrated. The soldiers had been taking it in with focused attention. These people had spent two weeks fighting creatures and understood that knowledge was the difference between surviving the next fight and not.

The Baron watched the lesson from ten metres away. He waited until Steve finished before approaching.

“Mela,” he said, pulling a chair from the watchtower’s shade and sitting without being offered a seat. “You’ve been busy, my friends.”

“People have questions,” Steve said. “We have some answers.”

“I can see that.” The Baron looked at the dirt diagram. The lines between nodes. The intersection markers. “And you’re giving those answers freely.”

“Every civilisation that tried hoarding knowledge got overtaken by the one that shared it faster,” Steve said. “Libraries, printing presses, the Royal Society. The open model wins. It always wins.”

The Baron smiled, the professional warmth calibrated. “Do you know of the Libro Piego?”

Steve’s head tilted toward him. “The encoded navigational charts? Accessible only to the Doge and his inner circle?”

“Exactly.” The Baron leaned back. “The Venetians built an empire on knowledge they controlled, my friend. Spice routes. Trading passages. Navigation charts that only a handful of men could read. The information was the empire. Without it, Venice was a collection of islands in a swamp.”

He looked at the soldiers who’d been listening to Steve’s lesson. “Information is power, my friends. And while I appreciate your efforts for the local populace, it also destabilases a fragile arrangement.”

“Meaning you want to control the power by controlling the knowledge,” Donna said. She’d been carried to the watchtower shade an hour ago and was sitting upright on the cot with her leg elevated, watching the Baron the way she watched everything: measuring the distance between his words and his intentions.

“Meaning,” the Baron said, turning the smile toward her, “that it is obvious to me you know far more than you are willing to share. I have shown you hospitality. I have sent my healer. Mela, all I ask is that generosity be repaid.”

“Generosity with an expectation of payment isn’t generosity,” Arthur said. “It’s a transaction.”

“Semantics, my friend. Semantics.”

Arthur studied the Baron’s face. The charm was a tool. It existed to do a job.

“And what stops you from killing us once you have your information?” Arthur said. “While Donna is still healing.”

The Baron spread his hands. “Nothing. I have forty fighters, which should be enough, though you are considerably higher level than my people.” He paused. “But I am not a barbarian, Arthur, nor am I stupid. I prefer to work with people when it’s possible.”

“As long as you come out ahead,” Donna said.

“As long as I come out ahead.”

“And if we refuse?”

“Then my personnel will be instructed to concentrate their efforts on people who are more... flexible.” The words were reasonable, even warm. The implication sitting beneath the words was like a structural fault beneath a foundation. “The Doctor would understand that the days of selfless acts of kindness are over.”

The air in the shade changed. Donna’s expression didn’t shift but her hands went still on the blanket. Steve stopped writing. Arthur felt the implication land in his chest and sit there.

The Baron was offering a deal. Heal Donna in exchange for information. And if they refused, Sarah would be pulled. Donna’s fracture would heal on its own over two weeks. Two weeks immobile in a camp on an island controlled by a man who was explaining, calmly and politely, that compassion was a currency he could withdraw.

“Fine,” Arthur said. “We’ll share what we know. But we share it when Donna is healed and walking. Not before.”

“Of course, my friend.” The Baron stood. “Let Donna heal. We’ll have a civilised dinner once she’s well and discuss matters properly.” He straightened his cuffs. The left sleeve. Then the right, over the forearm that wasn’t all his anymore.

“Issa,” he said. “I look forward to it.”

He left with his fighters. Unhurried.

“Archie,” Steve said. “The Libro Piego. The Venetians’ navigation monopoly. You must have been watching when they built it.”

“The Venetian Republic’s information-control model was effective for approximately three hundred years,” Archie said. “The model failed when competing powers developed independent intelligence-gathering capabilities. All information monopolies follow this trajectory. The question is never whether the monopoly collapses, but what fills the vacuum.” He paused. “The Baron’s model is approximately six hundred years less sophisticated than the Venetians’. The timeline to collapse will be proportionally shorter.”

“That’s comforting,” Donna said.

“It was not intended to be.”

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

Ħaġar Qim Plateau, Malta

THE GAUGE

One hundred and forty centimetres. Groove to groove. Held to the millimetre across thirty-metre samples, held across the eight kilometres I walked before dawn, held across every site I have ever measured. The number is the problem.

The standard model says cart ruts are evidence of wheeled transport. Wheels were invented somewhere around 3500 BCE in Mesopotamia. The Maltese ruts at Misraħ Għar il-Kbir have been dated, conservatively, to before that. Some of them run off cliff edges into open air. The wheel-vehicle hypothesis requires a vehicle, a cargo, and a driver willing to push that cargo into the sea. We have been politely ignoring the cliffs for two centuries.

Then there is the gauge itself. The Phrygian rock-cut ruts at Mira and Sagalassos. One hundred and forty. The Anatolian Bronze Age road sections at Boğazköy. One hundred and forty. The Etruscan basin tracks at Vulci. One hundred and forty. By the time Rome standardised its roads on the same spacing, the gauge had already been the international convention for two thousand years.

A standard that consistent does not propagate through trade. Trade fragments standards. Local geography rewrites them. The Iberian wagons in the same era ran at a hundred and twenty, the Gallic at a hundred and thirty-five, the Phoenician at variable widths depending on the colony. Where the Curators were maintaining infrastructure, the gauge holds. Where they were not, it drifts.

The hypothesis I have not yet been able to disprove. The ruts were never cuts made by wheels. They are surface markers. Someone cut them deliberately into the limestone at a fixed gauge to denote the conduit pathways beneath. The wheels are the cargo cult. The wagons are a folk explanation invented by people in the second millennium BCE who walked over the markers and tried to reverse-engineer a purpose.

If that is right, then every measurement of cart-rut wear I have ever cited was measurement of intentional artefact, not transport damage. Which would explain why the wear patterns never quite match the load profiles a draught animal would produce. We assumed the data was noisy. The data was not noisy. The data was telling us we were measuring the wrong object.

I wrote a paper in 2014 arguing that the Maltese ruts were ceremonial transport routes for ritual stone delivery between paired temple sites. I cited eleven sources. I won an early-career prize for it. Every one of my eleven sources was working inside the same wrong framework, which means none of them could have caught the error. The literature did not protect me. The literature confirmed me.

✎ The carts are the cover story. Someone catalogued the conduits with a chisel and a fixed gauge, and we have been writing about the cover story for two centuries.
