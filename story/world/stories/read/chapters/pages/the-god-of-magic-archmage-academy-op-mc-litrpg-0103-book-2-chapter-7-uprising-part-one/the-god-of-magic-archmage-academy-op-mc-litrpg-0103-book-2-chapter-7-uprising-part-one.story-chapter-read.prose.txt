After The Uprising Begins

Agra

He paced across the sandpit, each step only making the ache at the base of his horns worse. “Drussan’s man isn’t coming. Something must have gone wrong,” he said.

Everyone was quiet at the truth of his words. He’d said what no one else had the horns to. Freedom wasn’t coming.

Shir held up a hand. The old dreadbeast man had the face of a warthog, and one of his eyes was milky white. A scar started at the base of his ear and draped down his neck. He was the oldest here, maybe the oldest of all the slaves. He’d been born one, and it seemed the promise of freedom might still slip from the old man’s grasp in his old age. Agra felt for him. “There is still time. Let’s be patient. Do not lose hope. Drussan expressed confidence in this ally of his. The night is not over.”

Does the old man even believe those words? Agra grunted. “No, Shir. No more waiting,” Agra said. His voice carried throughout the sandpit, where the slaves slept at night. “We’ve done our duty to our people. We did not take opportunities to escape, and we abandoned our pride at every opportunity. Tonight. Tonight, let us set ourselves free. Many of you have seen the guards asleep or drunk at their posts. If we act decisively, we can free ourselves. No more waiting.” Agra spun slowly as he spoke, reading the expressions of those around him. Many agreed. Many already dreamt of the taste of freedom. Yet Shir was asking them not to take a bite of the meal already prepared for them.

His people whispered amongst themselves. Others nodded their approval. Tonight, they would draw blood and escape.

Shir stood, and the whispers fell silent. Those who supported Agra turned to acknowledge their elder with respect. In another life, Shir might’ve led a warpack or served as an Elder of the Horde. A real elder, not just the title dreadbeasts used to honor their old. People respected Shir, and though he rarely offered his opinion, when he spoke, they listened. “I admit, there was a time when I was skeptical of Garvhadda. Many of us were. There are those of us who still remember the stories our parents told of the fall of Korsca. So when rumors and whispers reached us of a man who would carve out a new home for us, I doubted. But look around you. I’m an old man. My body grows weaker each day, and I’ve got one good eye. Still, somehow, I hope.” Shir smiled.

Many others smiled in response. Even Agra felt his own heart stir. “Isn’t that all the more reason to make our move, Elder Shir?” he asked, taking a step forward.

Shir placed a hand on Agra’s shoulder. “I believe in the cause we’ve waited for. The stories we’ve heard all end in the same way. Garvhadda will come to free all his people in a single night. Even this old skeptic of a dreadbeast has come to believe. But maybe something did go wrong. Maybe the agent of Drussan was captured or killed on the road. If that’s true and we try to escape on our own, without guidance or knowledge of the routes established for us. There’s a chance we are caught, or word gets out and it ruins the escape of our brothers and sisters.” Shir stared Agra in the eyes and then looked at the room around them.

“We must remain here, as we always have. Even if it means abandoning freedom. Is that not a sacrifice worth making for the good of dreadbeasts everywhere? If we alone remain slaves in the name of freedom for every other, is that not better than attaining freedom at the cost of every other?” Shir asked.

Agra’s shoulders sank. Would his plan truly risk that much? He wanted to fight. To free the children of this village. To free the woman he loved. But was it right to do so at the expense of every dreadbeast child? At the cost of every dreadbeast man and woman still in chains? He sighed. Maybe Shir was right.

“Okay, Shir. Then we’ll—”

A human man with long white hair fell into the sandpit.

Many of the dreadbeasts jumped to their feet. This human had heard them all talking. If he told the others that they were planning to escape, that would be the end of them.

Agra’s eyes locked with one of the dreadbeast behind the human. They needed to capture him now.

The man eyed the sand beneath his feet. Then looked up and met Shir’s eyes. “Gentlemen, I applaud your decision. It’s a noble sacrifice, really. But let me make counter argument if you don’t mind. We must never give up. We must never surrender to evil men and the society in which they fester. Our surrender is their goal. No, tonight, on my word, every dreadbeast in this town will be free.”

Shir glanced at Agra before looking at the man. “Who are you?”

“Me?” The man pointed at himself. “My name is Mori. I’m the one they send when mistakes are unacceptable. You my friends, are in good hands.” Mori grinned. His teeth were a perfect shade of white.

“Drussan sent you?” Agra asked before he meant to. He shook his head at the dreadbeast creeping up on Mori.

Mori turned and walked up to the stone doors of the sandpit. The sand around him swirled around his leg, and then Mori kicked the door. It took several humans, or at least two dreadbeast to open that gate. But under Mori’s kick the gates not only opened, they tore off the hinges and slammed into the ground outside.

Agra shook his head in disbelief. “The guards will have heard that!”

Mori turned and looked at him. He shrugged. “I’ve been sent to free you. The guards won’t be a factor. Come now, we’re on a tight schedule.”

Shir shook his head. “What—what do you need us to do?”

The others looked on with hope. If Shir accepted this eccentric man, perhaps the others weren’t crazy to hope as well.

Mori pointed down the streets of the desert town and toward its edge. “I want you to walk straight out of this place and leave town. Don’t falter, don’t stop, don’t even flinch. Put your trust in me. Of course, I’ll protect you even if you don’t, but my feelings might be hurt a tad bit.” He smiled.

Now Agra was the one who felt unsure. “He’s a strange one, Shir,” he whispered.

Shir placed a comforting hand on Agra’s shoulder. “I will go first. If this is a trap or if it goes wrong, then watch over them and blame me for this. I will pay the price. For what it’s worth, Agra. I’m very proud of you. I know you will escape this place one day. You will be a Khan one day. I believe that in my heart. But today, walk behind me, and let this old man take the first arrow if everything goes astray.”

“Shir…” Agra started. The elder had honored him with his words.

Shir hesitated at the fallen doors for only a moment before crossing the threshold to freedom. The guards were already reacting. They were setting up on rooftops and formed a semicircle in the streets to surround the sandpit where the dreadbeast slaves were kept.

Mori nodded encouragement and then faded from reality.

Shir looked taken aback by that.

“Don’t move!” one guard called out.

“Get back inside, now!” another cried.

Shir started to look back, but stopped himself. He walked forward, and he hummed an old song of their people.

As one the guards fired. Arrows whizzed through the air.

Agra tensed. “Shir!” he bellowed, charging forward to pull the old man out of danger.

The arrows struck.

Agra’s breath caught. “No…”

Shir didn’t falter for even a moment. The guards looked at each other in confusion as the old dreadbeast stepped under a mana-light torch. A blue barrier around him shimmered in response as the arrows fell away.

The man’s words echoed in Agra’s mind.

Don’t falter, don’t stop, don’t even flinch.

This was it.

“With me,” Agra called out. He followed Shir out of the gate, followed by the hooves and feet of the others. It was time. Drussan’s man had come. Garvhadda was waiting for them.

More arrows flew, as others around Agra took up the hum of Shir, joining the old man in his song. Barriers kept them safe, and they quickly caught up to the old warthog dreadbeast.

They walked, and the guards retreated, still firing and shouting for reinforcements. The ground opened beneath their feet and swallowed the human slavers. Stitching itself back together as the dreadbeasts kept moving.

Agra held his head high.

They were free.

Samara

She walked through the encampment. She carried her greatsword in one hand, with her other she gripped the slave trader by the back of his head. He whimpered as she dragged him past the corpses of his men, closer and closer to the cages where the dreadbeast slaves were kept.

“Please, I can pay you—agh!” he groaned as she gripped him tighter.

“If the mood strikes to talk to a scumbag, I’ll let ya know. Until then, hush up,” Samara spat.

Moonlight reflected off the cages. The dreadbeasts slowly rose as they saw her approaching. The soldiers had all rushed off to their master’s home when his house guards signaled the alarm. Now the only thing keeping the dreadbeasts here was the magically reinforced cages. Samara dropped the slaver and brought her sword crashing down on the lock of the first cage. The dreadbeast inside eyed her for a moment, as if trying to decide if she was friend or foe.

Behind her, a man cried out. “Brother! We’re here to help you!” She turned and saw a group of men standing behind her with swords at the ready.

“Huh, guess I missed a few.” She threw the slaver to the freed dreadbeast and turned to fight.

An audible crack made her glance over her shoulder. The dreadbeast stepped over the corpse of the slave trader she’d thrown at him. He left his cage and took a stand beside her. He nodded at her, and then they charged together towards the last of the slaver’s forces.

Aiden

He liked night walks with his mom. He always slept better afterwards.

“Come on, Aiden, it’s time to go home,” his mother cooed. She looked tired. She’d been working all day.

“Okay!” Aiden smiled. He gripped his mother’s hand to share his energy with her. Then he looked up at the stars. It was a cloudless night, and the stars were really visible. Mom said they were even more visible away from the mana-lights in town, but Aiden had good eyes. He could see the stars just fine!

He squinted when he saw it.

A bunch of tiny, see-through bolts of lightning floated in the air above, somewhere above the rooftops but far below the stars. His head tilted at that. “Mom, look!” He pointed up excitedly. He’d never seen anything like this.

“I know, Aiden, the stars are pretty tonight,” she said without looking up.

“No, you have to look!” Aiden pointed again, as if it might show his mom how important it was.

He watched one of the bolts fall from the sky, slowly, and go right into the body of a guard. It wasn’t one of the city-guards. Aiden was supposed to go to the city-guards if he needed help. No, this was one of the guards dressed in black. Aiden wasn’t supposed to go near them. He didn’t get why, and mom never explained. Maybe it had something to do with the lightning bolts?

Most of them protected the mine where the animal-men worked. Aiden thought the animal-men were cool looking, but mom always said he wasn’t allowed to visit them. The guards in black would get angry if they tried.

They kept walking, and he turned back to see a woman walk past them in a white robe. She was pretty and cool-looking too; she had red eyes and long white hair. She smiled at him as they passed her.

Aiden waved, then remembered the lightning and looked up. The lightning bolts were gone. He looked all around, wondering where they had gone.

“Aiden, don’t pull. What’s wrong?” his mom asked.

“The lightning—” He paused when he noticed the woman was gone. His eyes fell on the guard in black. He wasn’t standing anymore. He was on the ground, and smoke rose from his clothes. The other guards in black were lying down too. All down the street behind them. His nose wrinkled at the sudden burnt smell that filled the air. Aiden tilted his head. Now it made more sense why he wasn’t supposed to go to the guards in black for help. They were asleep when they were supposed to be working.

Aiden shook his head and smiled. “I love you, momma!”

She looked surprised at that, but smiled all the same.

“I love you too, Aiden.”

Bara

The dreadbeast sat with his legs crossed in the center of the room. His wife played with their children beside him. She cautioned his youngest about being mindful of his horns. In the morning, his children would be taken away. The merchant had sold them. He was taking Bara’s children away because that was what humans did. They were evil for the sake of being evil.

This was not the first time the trader had taken the children of his slaves. Each time their parents had wanted to fight. But Bara had convinced them not to. They had to wait for Garvhadda’s signal. Each time they had listened.

Tomorrow morning, he would prove himself a hypocrite. Tomorrow morning he would kill the merchant.

“You’re quiet,” his wife said, finally intruding on his meditation.

“It’s my last night with them,” he spoke plainly. He would protect his children. It would almost certainly cost him his life.

She frowned. “Then spend it with them instead of getting lost in your head.”

The sound of a key unlocking their door made his head snap. The other dreadbeasts turned to look as well. There were no windows, but his instincts told him the night was still young. It was too early for their work to begin. He turned to look at his wife. This could only mean one thing. The merchant had come early.

He had to act now.

He rose and moved toward the door with conviction.

“Bara?” one of the others whispered.

They were supposed to sit when the guards came. They were supposed to move further away from the door.

Bara couldn’t do that. He intended to crush the head of the first human he saw when that door opened. They would not take his children.

When the door opened, only a single man stood outside. It was so surprising that Bara stopped reaching out the moment he realized it.

The guards never came alone.

The man looked up at him and blinked a few times. “It’s really you, brother!” The human ran forward and hugged Bara.

Bara looked back at the other dreadbeasts with confusion. They all watched, equally confused as he was.

“Brother?” Bara asked.

The man nodded. “We have to hurry. I didn’t know they were keeping people here. We need to move before they realize.” As the human looked up at Bara, the dreadbeast noticed his eyes were unfocused. The man looked like a sleepwalker.

“What’s going on?” Bara asked. He pushed past the human and looked down both stretches of hallway. The doors to the other dreadbeast holding rooms were closed.

Some of the other dreadbeast warriors rose. Four men and two women. They approached slowly. Their minds were the same. Could this be the work of one of Garvhadda’s or Drussan’s agents? Was tonight the night? Or was some other force at play?

Another human rounded the corner, and Bara’s eyes lit up. “Hey! You there! Don’t move!”

This was bad. The humans would think they’d tried to escape. What could he do here? Kill both the humans and try to hide the bodies? Or maybe —

The human charged. But just as Bara reached out to crush him, the human tackled the man who had freed him. He punched the man over and over. “I know what you did with my wife!” he yelled.

“What’s going on, Bara?” his wife asked. His youngest hid behind her. The oldest had moved to join him and the other warriors. That filled his heart with pride. Bara shook his head. One of the women warriors reached over and pulled the human back from the other. He turned to look at her. His eyes were glazed over as well.

“They’re dreaming,” a woman’s voice called out.

But this wasn’t from the hallway. The voice came from the center of the room where the dreadbeasts slept at night. She was translucent; her form and clothes were green and traced in a pink outline.

“Who are you?” Bara asked.

“You can call me Milim. I’m here to help. All the guards inside the compound are sleeping. Take the keys and free the others. Do what…do what you need to do and then make your way outside. I’ve secured a path out of town already. I’ll be waiting.”

“Who sent you?” he asked. He’d heard Drussan had human allies, but it was difficult to believe. What human would help a dreadbeast?

“I’m a friend of Drussan’s.”

That she even knew Drussan’s name was all he needed to hear for now. As she faded away, he took charge. Bara pointed to the surrounding warriors. “You have the kill-list. Go to the barracks. None of the men who knew about this place get to live. Leave the others.” He knelt before his son. “Take the keys off the human there. Open the other doors and free our people. I’m entrusting this to you. Can you handle it?”

“Are you leaving, father?” he asked.

Bara looked up and locked eyes with the dreadbeast parents he’d once asked to make sacrifices he himself hadn’t been willing to make. He owed them now. “I’m going to fetch the merchant. We will have words about his actions.”

A few of the parents stood up to join him. Tonight, the merchant would pay a blood price for his sins.

Eriq

He scrabbled backward down the hallway. Around the corner, the other guards screamed. He flinched as blood splattered the wall. Behind him, a mass of swirling, writhing vines had blocked the hallway. He could only hope the creature responsible turned away instead of finding him.

Each step the horned creature took shook the building. Each second more of his comrades were silenced forever. Eventually, the slaughter was over.

Thud.

Thud.

The creature’s shadow was massive, stretching even into this section of the hallway from around the corner. It undulated as the vines that followed it wherever it went spread. But before his eyes, the giant creature’s shadow shrank just as it rounded the corner. When it was finally within sight, Eriq could hardly believe his eyes. That horned monstrosity was gone. In its place was a beautiful woman with small deer antlers and reddish-brown hair. She tilted her head when she saw him.

“Oh, I missed one.”

“Where—where’s the monster? Do you work for it?” he stammered.

She chuckled. “The monster? That’s not very nice to say.”

His eyes trailed down her dress until he saw the blood at its hem. He slowly realized the monster hadn’t gone anywhere.

It had just changed forms.

He scrambled back some more.

She smiled.

“Why…why are you doing this?” he asked.

“Because my master asked me to.” She spoke as if it were the simplest thing in the world. So casual about killing all those men. And she spoke with such a sweet, melodic voice. How could she really be the same creature that had ripped his friends apart?

More importantly, what kind of person could control a creature like this? A monstrosity like this?

Something wrapped around his wrist, and he looked down to see a vine. “No...please!” Another wrapped around his waist and pulled him toward the wall of vines behind him. “Wait, I can help you! I’ll do anything you want!” he pleaded.

The deer-woman frowned. “How many dreadbeast begged for mercy? How many did you help escape, or have sympathy for?” she asked.

This was about dreadbeast? She had done all this to help dreadbeasts?

That was his last thought before the vines overtook him and he was lost.
