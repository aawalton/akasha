The slime farming village was a relatively new settlement built around a small meteor crater where the metal slimes had been seeded, as the mana there was deemed optimal for their growth. Compared to older villages in the region, it still looked rough around the edges, less like a permanent town and more like an ambitious project that had grown faster than its founders could have expected.

A wooden palisade surrounded the settlement, reinforced with sharpened stakes and watch platforms built at uneven intervals. At the moment, however, the village’s modest defenses were not the most striking feature.

It was the army surrounding it.

Our group stood on top of a grassy hill overlooking the entire situation from a safe distance. We were still far enough away that no one below could realistically interfere with us, but close enough to get a clear view of the siege camp sprawling around the village.

Dozens upon dozens of tents had been erected in a loose semicircle around the settlement. Campfires burned lazily, wagons were parked in clusters, and armed men moved about in organized chaos.

“There must be at least a thousand mercenaries down there,” Raik muttered, narrowing his eyes.

“Only six hundred and thirty-seven,” Calr corrected calmly while adjusting the spyglass pressed to his face. “And more than a hundred of those are camp followers, not actual fighters.”

Raik turned toward him.

“You counted them?”

Calr lowered the spyglass slightly and gave us a grin.

“Of course."

That was the least surprising thing he had done with his powers since he joined the team.

“We could take them,” Katar said casually.

Ja’a slowly turned toward him.

“I swear,” she said with terrifying calm, “one day I am going to kill you myself, steel-for-brains.”

“You are welcome to try.” Katar looked mildly amused.

“That was not a joke,” she insisted.

I ignored them and continued peering through my own spyglass.

“The village seems fine for now,” I observed.

From here, there were no obvious signs of panic inside the walls. I honestly expected smoke, or maybe a breach on the wall as the defenders desperately scrambled to block it, or maybe men rushing the walls with rams and ladders while others covered them with arrows and siege weapons. Instead, the two sides were just watching and waiting.

“Yeah,” Katar said, folding his arms. “Most mercenary companies like this only have maybe ten people strong enough to be an actual threat to Loumpa.”

He tilted his chin toward the palisade.

“With the walls, he and his wife can probably hold it indefinitely.”

“Couldn’t they use their number advantage?” I asked.

“Couldn’t they just burn the walls?” Vena asked at the same time.

“And walk into arrow range while trying?" Raik scoffed; he shook his head. “Not a chance.”

Katar nodded.

“They could breach the wall eventually if they committed everyone to an assault,” he admitted. “But they would probably suffer thirty percent casualties doing it.”

“More, honestly,” Raik said. “And mercenaries are not paid enough to die for some noble’s property dispute.”

“Not these cowards, at least,” He stared at the camp with obvious disdain.

“They do not need to breach it,” Calr interjected.

He pointed his spyglass toward the road, and all of us followed his line of sight.

A merchant caravan had been stopped by a group of around twenty armed soldiers stationed where the road branched toward the village.

“They are enforcing a blockade,” Calr explained.

I focused harder and finally understood the layout. The main road toward Ectamel was still open. Merchants and travelers were allowed through after inspection, but the smaller road branching toward the slime village was being actively controlled.

“No one is allowed to enter the village,” I realized.

“They are starving them out.” Calr nodded.

“With the road sealed, they cannot receive supplies,” he continued. “No food imports, no trade, limited farming, and reduced grazing space for livestock.”

“So they are not besieging the walls,” I muttered. “They are besieging the economy.”

“That is significantly more efficient,” Calr agreed.

“Give me that.” Ja’a snatched the spyglass from his hands and immediately began inspecting the road checkpoint herself.

Her expression darkened visibly.

“Oh, those bastards are charging tolls too,” she said.

“What?” I asked.

“They are taxing merchants trying to pass through.” She lowered the spyglass in disgust.

“That cannot possibly be legal,” I said.

“Legal according to whom?” Ja’a gave me a very unimpressed look. She gestured dramatically toward the camp.

“We are standing in front of six hundred mercenaries hired by a noble to illegally starve an independent village into submission.”

“Fair point.”

“This road is partially maintained through Hano tax agreements,” Raik grumbled. “Even if Veraden has regional authority, this is still an abuse of jurisdiction.”

“Well,” Calr said dryly, “I imagine Lord Verra would simply deny everything.”

“He probably would,” Raik grimaced, crossing his arms, looking visibly unhappy.

“I will report this to the freelancer guild,” he added.

“But?” I prompted.

“But I do not think we should personally interfere.”

Ja’a immediately looked offended.

“But where am I supposed to offload all the ore we have gathered?” she asked.

“That is your concern?” Raik stared at her.

“It is a very legitimate concern,” Ja’a argued. “This is the only place where it would be worth something.”

“Who cares about a few silver coins?” Raik dismissed.

“At least three gold worth,” she protested.

“At minimum,” Calr confirmed. “The slimes need to eat, so they would buy them at a price closer to processed metal.”

“I could teleport in and offload them,” I suggested.

Everyone turned toward me, Raik pinching the bridge of his nose at that suggestion.

“We are not starting a conflict with a noble over a few ingots.”

“I do not care about the money,” I said.

Ja’a still looked like she cared deeply about the money.

“It is just a convenient excuse,” I admitted.

I raised the spyglass and looked toward the village again.

“I do not like doing nothing.”

That was the real issue. Maybe Raik was right. Maybe this was none of our business. Maybe interfering with regional politics was an objectively terrible idea. But I liked Loumpa. He had been helpful, welcoming, and his village was doing something genuinely innovative.

“I am also not saying that I would kill people over a conflict I understand very little about,” I finally added. “But if anyone is starving or in need of medicine, I would like to help.”

Also, if this kind of extortion worked once, it would absolutely happen again.

Raik studied me for several seconds. I could practically see him debating whether allowing me to do anything was worth the inevitable headache.

“I already know that look." Finally, he sighed.

“What look?”

“The one you had before summoning a goddess.”

“Hey.” I blinked.

Before I could protest further, someone else spoke.

“The one you had before stabbing a troll in the back,” Katar added.

“The one you had before introducing new medical knowledge," Vena said.

“The one you had before making a rat trap deep in Hano’s sewers,” added Calr.

“The one you had before starting the pizza business,” Ja’a contributed.

“The one you had before standing between me and a spider matriarch,” Kan said.

We all turned toward Shingo instinctively.

He made a series of gestures involving exaggerated arm movements and miming washing.

Calr frowned.

“The one she had before buying the bathtub?”

Shingo nodded enthusiastically.

“That one was anticlimactic,” Ja’a said, shaking her head.

“Yeah, that was weak. Shame on you, Shingo.” Calr looked disappointed.

Shingo gestured in visible frustration.

“Do not blame us for taking the best ones,” Calr argued back. “You have only been present for two of those.”

“Hey now, do not fight over who gets to claim my craziest moment,” I protested loudly.

That somehow made everyone laugh.

Kan finally spoke.

“Just let her go. She will probably be fine.”

“Not reassuring,” Raik muttered.

“You are not to engage anyone.” He rubbed his forehead.

“I know.”

“You are not to draw attention to yourself.”

“I know.”

“You are absolutely not starting an incident.”

I hesitated; that hesitation lasted less than a second, but apparently it was enough.

Raik narrowed his eyes.

“Alice.”

“I said I know.”

“Fine, fine,” Raik grumbled.

“Thank you,” I said.

Ja’a perked up immediately. “Shingo, Calr, your bags of holding.”

Before I could react, she had already started handing me inventory instructions.

“This one has twenty baskets full of ore,” Ja’a said, pointing at the first bag. “You can sell the individual ore at forty copper each, give or take.”

She pointed at the second.

“This one has the pig iron and scrap metal. This is processed metal, so three bronze per kilogram minimum. Or do not sell it if they lowball you.”

Then she hesitated.

“And do not sell the cobalt.”

She immediately shoved a hand into the bag, pulled out six ingots, and hugged them protectively.

“Actually, I am keeping these here so you do not accidentally sell them.”

I rolled my eyes at her theatrics and grabbed the bags.

Raik was still staring at me. He pointed a finger directly in my direction.

“You go in, assess the situation, offload the metal, and come back immediately.”

“Understood.” I nodded enthusiastically.

Vena looked concerned.

“Be careful.”

“I will.”

“If you decide to start a fight, come fetch me,” Katar grinned.

Raik turned toward him in horror.

“She is specifically avoiding that,” he admonished.

“A man can hope,” Katar shrugged.

Ignoring that deeply unhelpful contribution, I stepped away from the group and summoned my spear into my hand. The familiar weight settled comfortably into my grip.

“I will be back soon,” I said.

I closed my eyes and whispered.

“I wish I could safely teleport to Loumpa’s village. Preferably somewhere discreet.”

Then my wish activated, and I vanished.

Less than a second after I appeared, something landed behind me… no, someone. My training kicked in, and I spun around immediately with my spear already in hand.

Loumpa stood a few meters away in a low fighting stance, shoulders tense and ready to explode into motion. His dark skin glistened faintly with sweat, and although one of his legs was missing below the knee, replaced by a simple wooden peg leg, he somehow still looked like he could absolutely ruin my day.

Honestly, the missing leg somehow makes him look more intimidating, despite how painful it was to have landed on it.

I immediately raised both hands.

“Hey, hey, it’s me,” I blurted out. “Alice. We met a few days ago.”

Loumpa narrowed his eyes suspiciously. For one awkward second, I genuinely thought he might attack anyway. Then recognition clicked. His posture relaxed slightly.

“You were in the Agame-boy’s team,” he said. “Raik, was it not?”

I nodded enthusiastically, deeply relieved that his conflict resolution strategy was not “punch first and ask questions later”.

“Yes. Glad you remember me.”

“You startled me.” He straightened fully, though he still looked mildly annoyed.

“I was flaring my aura outward to detect disturbances,” he explained. “Then suddenly, I felt your magic tear through space behind me.”

“It is not subtle.” He gave me a look.

“Yeah, stealth is not really my specialty.” I grimaced.

“But in my defense,” I added, “it is not like I came here to spy or anything.”

“Then why are you here?” Loumpa folded his arms.

“Originally?” I shrugged. “To sell you metal.”

His entire expression changed instantly. It was honestly kind of impressive. A moment ago, he had looked like a battle-hardened militia leader under siege. Now he looked like a man who had just been handed salvation.

“You have metal to sell?” He stepped forward so quickly that he grabbed both my shoulders.

“Yes?”

“Ion the Phoenix sent you.” He exhaled in visible relief.

“Maybe I am Ion,” I said, referencing the dreamer meme that everyone is in on as a joke.

Loumpa ignored that completely. Tough crowd.

“Come.”

He started walking, and I hurried after him. As we moved deeper into the village, I looked around properly for the first time. Despite the siege, the settlement itself looked relatively calm. People were moving about with purpose. A few guards stood on elevated platforms watching the walls, but no one seemed especially alarmed. There was some tension, certainly, but not chaos.

That felt a little strange if I was honest.

“I do not understand,” I admitted as I followed him. “Do you really need metal more than food or medicine right now?”

Loumpa gave me a tired look over his shoulder.

“The blockade only started four days ago.”

He tapped his chest with one hand as he walked.

“We have food stores for approximately three months.”

That was significantly better than expected.

“As for medicine,” he continued, “we have had barely any real fighting.”

“Really?”

“We lost one guard during the initial assault, as well as a few wounded.” His expression darkened. “But we punished them tenfold for that mistake.”

I grimaced at the loss of life on both sides. What a waste.

“Since then, they have barely engaged us.”

“So what is the actual problem?”

Loumpa let out a long breath.

“The slimes.”

That explained his earlier reaction.

“They intercepted a major scrap metal shipment from Ectamel three days ago,” he said. “Without it, our feeding schedule is disrupted.”

I frowned.

“Did you not have metal reserves?”

“We did.”

“Then what happened?”

Loumpa grimaced.

“For some reason, the slime population increased significantly faster than expected.”

“That sounded like a good thing,” I said.

“We decided to sustain the growth.”

“Meaning?”

“We fed them all to improve quality rather than harvesting the excess monsters into lesser cores.”

That is greedy... but I kind of get it. since core prices increased exponentially with size.

“With Ectamel nearby and skeletons constantly bringing rusted tools and scrap metal into the region, I did not consider supply shortages a realistic concern.”

Famous last words.

We arrived at the center of the village shortly after.

The village had been built in a ring-like formation around the meteor crater, which made the entire settlement look vaguely donut-shaped from a bird’s-eye view. Standing here in person, the crater was even more striking. A shallow bowl in the earth filled with bouncing gray slimes.

A worried-looking man stood near the crater edge, arms crossed tightly as he watched them.

“Chief Loumpa,” he called out immediately, “The slimes have started cannibalizing each other at a higher rate.”

He gestured toward the crater.

“We either feed them soon or reduce their numbers before we lose too many of them.”

I stared. Inside the crater, the gray blobs bounced around erratically. Occasionally, two would collide and remain attached for a second too long before separating again. One of them would usually emerge slightly larger.

The last time I had visited, the crater had been full of rusted tools like broken weapons and random scrap metal. Now it was almost empty.

Loumpa turned toward me with the intensity of a drowning man.

“What do you have?”

I hesitated. This was technically a fantastic opportunity to squeeze them for maximum profit. Their desperation was obvious. And yet... that felt a little too morally questionable.

Instead, I asked the question that was still bothering me.

“Why are you not more worried about the army outside?”

“The army will be dealt with.” Loumpa waved one hand dismissively. That was said with a suspicious amount of confidence. There was not a hint of worry in his voice.

Okay. If he was this unconcerned about the siege, then maybe I did not need to feel guilty about asking for fair compensation.

Also, Ja’a would absolutely murder me if I returned having donated everything for free.

Plus, this was not my bounty alone. Lowballing the price would be like stealing from Calr and Shingo, who desperately needed the funds. This was not just about me.

I slid both hands into the bag of holding and heaved one basket filled with ore out with a grunt of effort.

The worried man visibly brightened.

“With that alone, we can probably stabilize feeding for a day.”

“Only a day?”

He winced.

“Yeah. Only a day. Not enough.”

Well.

I opened the bag wider and pointed it toward Loumpa.

“You are probably going to need to carry the rest out yourself,” I said. “I am not strong enough for twenty baskets.”

Loumpa froze.

“Twenty?”

I smiled.

“There are twenty in total.”

Both men stared at me as if I had personally descended from heaven.

“Lady Ja’a told me to charge eighty copper per ore,” I lied.

Loumpa did not react.

“However,” I continued, “considering the siege, I am willing to lower the rate.”

“How about one bronze per ore?”

Technically, that was still more than Ja’a’s original estimate. But honestly, these people could afford it. I had seen what high-quality slime cores sold for when Lady Satori made my armor.

“Deal.” Loumpa did not even blink.

He answered so quickly that I immediately realized I could have charged significantly more.

Ah, well.

The slim attendant was already dumping baskets into the crater as I opened my bag wide for Loumpa to offload the baskets.

The moment the ore rolled toward the center, the slimes became dramatically more active. Gray blobs bounced excitedly toward the metal, engulfing chunks inside their gelatinous bodies. The ore began dissolving almost immediately.

That was honestly kind of adorable in a mildly terrifying way.

“You are a lifesaver,” Loumpa said.

“I also have more metal,” I added. “Though this next batch is processed, and we were technically supposed to bring it to Hano.”

Loumpa turned toward me so fast that I was briefly concerned he would break the sound barrier.

“Name your price.”

So naturally, I offloaded everything.

Fifty ingots of pig iron, rusted swords from skeleton grunts, pickaxes from the mining skeleton, scrap metal, doom ball rings, and various random metal debris from the forge.

By the time we were done, I had somehow acquired seven gold coins.

Seven, that was enough money to buy a house in the temple district of hano or a small farm outside the walls of hano.

Even Ja’a’s most optimistic estimation had only predicted three.

I stared at the coins in my palm.

“Are you sure you can afford this?”

“What use is money if no merchants can enter the village?” Loumpa snorted.

“I guess.”

“Better to preserve the slime farm until the siege is lifted.”

“You seem very confident the siege will end.”

“Of course.” He said it so matter-of-factly that it started to get irritating, if I was honest.

“I sent for help the moment I learned an army was marching toward us,” he finally explained.

“They should arrive any time now.”

“Who?” I asked.

Loumpa only winked, for some reason, he was acting absolutely insufferably. So I tried another angle.

“If reinforcements are that close, why buy this much metal?”

“Even if the siege ended today,” Loumpa shrugged, “it would still take another week for proper shipments from Ectamel to resume.”

I nodded at that.

“One last question,” I asked. “Do you need anything else? Food? Medicine? Supplies?”

He smiled.

“We are fine, thank you.”

“In that case,” I said, “I should probably head back.”

Loumpa shook his head.

“I recommend you remain nearby.”

“Why?” I frowned.

A grin slowly spread across his face.

“The spectacle will be worth watching.”
