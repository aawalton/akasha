“I think I tore something…”

Finn took two hops with his arm draped around Toar’s shoulder before slumping against the nearby desk.

“Argh!”

He clutched at his ribs, coughing in a shallow rattle. Finn looked to be struggling to keep himself upright. Meanwhile, Jackal sat cross-legged, staring at his crushed forearm. He was still conscious, but he looked pale, and was slowly adding to blood pool that Mansol had left behind.

“What are we gonna do?” Jackal asked, his eyes still fixed on his injury, on the body before him.

Toar found himself staring too. Mansol had taken a couple of minutes to stop gasping and twitching, and Toar had scarcely taken his eyes off of his cousin for all of that time.

Well, he’d spent some of it with his eyes closed, withstanding a migraine. Some of it shaking, feeling anemic and lightheaded. But he’d spent a good deal of that time watching his cousin die.

How had they won? They hadn’t done so without consequence. Finn couldn’t walk unassisted, Jackal’s arm was ruined, and Toar had exacerbated his existing injuries—those were just the issues that had presented themselves in the last three minutes.

“Hey… Toar?”

But how had Mansol died so easily? He was so strong, so imposing. How could a man like that leave his neck exposed?

Toar thought he understood the answer, but it felt strange to even consider:

Mansol wasn’t a fighter.

His cousin had a Tier 2 core. He was a large and physically impressive beastkin. He was a Halfshade…

But he was a doctor. He likely hadn’t received combat training since he was a child. Whatever combat instincts Mansol possessed, they were basic. He’d demonstrated as much in their fight.

It was either that or overconfidence. Perhaps both. Maybe Mansol didn’t believe that three children so weak would be able to actually threaten him.

Or maybe his cousin expected him to stand and watch as he killed his friends.

Were they my friends?

Had I killed my cousin to protect people who hated me?

It didn’t matter.

“Toar!”

Toar snapped to attention. He was starting to feel woozy, his eyes closing. The shout caused his lids to flutter and his eyes to swivel. He remembered where he was, tasted metal in the air, and fought bile rising in his stomach.

“Toar, Jackal is…”

Toar’s eyes snapped to Jackal, who was now laying on his side in the pool of blood, only a couple of feet away from Mansol’s body.

Toar hobbled his way over to Jackal, squinting at him through double vision, and only then began to realise that he too was profusely bleeding from his stab wound.

“I need a tourniquet,” Toar murmured, his throat dry.

“A wha—”

“A belt,” Toar explained, panting where he stood. “Give me your belt.”

Finn did as he was told, sucking air through his teeth as he unbuckled his tool belt and grunting, hopping, and swearing as his attempt to throw it over resulted in him almost losing his balance.

Toar took the belt and tilted it upside down, empting the contents and then removing the holsters. Once Toar was left with only the strap and buckle, he began wrapping the leather around Jackal’s forearm, as close to the elbow as he could go. Toar squeezed tight, tugging until the wound moved from gushing to seeping.

“Come and put pressure on this,” Toar demanded.

“I… okay. Just give me a second…”

“Now, Finn!”

Finn started as if he were going to hop over, but either saw the wetness of the floor or felt the uncertainty of his balance and thought better of it. He slowly lowered himself to the floor, grunting and clutching at his side as he did so. Once there, he began to shuffle his way towards Toar and the barely-conscious Jackal, who was murmuring nonsense as Toar clutched onto the belt.

“Can you move any faster…”

Finn covered his hands, legs, and behind in varying shades of scarlet as he trudged through the sodden floor. Multiple times, he gasped, shouted, or groaned. When he finally reached his companions, he grabbed onto Jackal’s arm and held the belt tight. Tight enough that the blood finally stopped.

“Okay… you’re not gonna pass out, right?”

Finn nodded.

“Talk to me.”

“I won’t let go.”

“Okay. Okay.”

Toar rose to his feet and began scanning the area for things he could use. He racked his brain, panting as he stared through the various medical instruments and tried to recall anything Mansol had taught him.

Gauze. He needed gauze and dressing for the wound. Something to clean it with. Drugs to numb the pain…

Then he needed to assess how bad it was. Jackal wasn’t bleeding as fast as Mansol had been, but he was still losing blood rapidly. His arm had been half-crushed, it looked mangled and destroyed. There was a good chance that if Mansol hadn’t ruined an artery when he’d stabbed Jackal, he had when he’d squeezed his arm.

Was Toar going to have to repair that? Did he know how?

He thought harder. He cursed that the only person in the room who definitively could’ve healed Jackal’s wound was the one who caused it, the one who was dead now.

Then Jackal’s shoulder began to spasm, and Toar knew he needed to act faster.

“Stay with him.”

Toar limped from the room and went in search of a saw and a Heating Gem. If he could find those two things, he was confident he could perform an amputation. Cutting off Jackal’s arm was hardly something Toar wanted to do, but things looked bad and he might not have an alternative. He’d try dressing it first, but if that failed, the arm would need to go, or Jackal would die.

And, honestly, he might die regardless.

Toar was on his way to the operation room, ready to bring out everything he needed to assist Jackal and cart it to where he was, when he spotted something that made him think twice.

The back room of the tent. Mansol’s personal storage. Toar was never allowed in there.

Checking could be a waste of time. Toar knew everything he needed to amputate Jackal was going to be available in the operation room…

But, worst case, he found something strong he could give Jackal to numb the wound. Best case, something that would help stabilise him.

One minute.

Toar hurried into the room. It was eerie inside. The area carried a strong herbal scent, but the backdrop was horrifying. Pickled jars aligned the far wall, containing various limbs, appendages, and organs from various creatures. Some of them certainly belonged to humans, beastkin, and orcs…

What was this? A trophy collection? Research material? Both?

Toar walked further into the storage room, soon coming across a tapestry that seemed to be made of dried skin. It was bare, and he couldn’t discern its purpose, but once he was able to tear his eyes from both that and the collection of bones that sat near a stained workbench, his eyes were drawn to one of the other prominent displays in the small room.

A collection of medicines, poisons, and potions. The doctor had been well-stocked.

Toar limped over to the shelf, staring through blurred vision and attempting to read the labels.

Ferrous Sulfate, Drasil Venom, Moorland Root, Major Respite Potion, Methylprednisolone, Iodine Melascus, Superior Health Potion, Abydroxil—

Wait.

Toar reread the label once more. Then a second time to make sure he wasn’t confused.

He pulled out one of the potions in question. Then a second. And a third. One was half full, the others completely.

It wasn’t anger that bubbled inside of Toar as he held the potions in his hands. Rather, it was disgust.

He didn’t let himself linger on the emotion. He knew what these things could do.

He held off on treating himself, despite the immense temptation to take a swig. He knew these things were potent, but he might need all of them combined to save Jackal’s life.

Toar placed the three potions on a tray, alongside two major respite potions, and made his way back to the office.

Finn was still holding on, and Jackal’s body seemed to have stilled, but his breathing was laboured and he no longer looked conscious.

Toar wasted no time administering the potion. He didn’t know exactly what to expect, but considering what a small amount of this very same potion had done for him at death’s door, he could only hope things would work the same way for Jackal.

He unstoppered the potion and pressed it to Jackal’s lips, lifting the top and feeding him a small gulp.

The effects were instantaneous. Toar could see the wound in Jackal’s arm beginning to stitch over and heal. Not only that, but bones snapping into place, his limb realigning itself. The experience seemed to shock Jackal awake, who stared down at his arm in awe, gasping like he’d awoken from a terrible nightmare.

Maybe he had. Maybe it wasn’t over.

“Wh-what’s…”

“You went into shock. You were—”

Toar stopped himself. He needed to breathe for a moment. Seeing that Jackal seemed to be stabilising was somewhat relieving, but only made Toar more aware of the dizziness and pain he was going through.

He didn’t know how he was still standing. And soon enough, he wasn’t. He crsahed to his knees, as meanwhile, Finn continued to hold the tourniquet.

“Why have you got that thing around me?” Jackal asked. “My arm’s completely numb.”

“Try…” Toar took a couple of deep breaths. “Try relieving the pressure a little.”

He watched as Finn did so, and saw that the open wound didn’t begin to bleed again. Not only that, but it was still closing, albeit more slowly now.

It took a minute, but eventually, Jackal began to tense and flex his arm, feeling returning to it.

“Doesn’t feel that bad,” he said, twisting it as he spoke, squeezing his fingers open and closed. “A bit numb, maybe.”

“Have a bit more,” Toar instructed, passing him the bottle.

He’d just taken a sip himself, and he could feel his most recent wounds already beginning to close and his head becoming clearer by the second. Not only that, but old injuries from his spider fight felt as if they were closing. Healing sprains and fractures seemed to jump towards their conclusions in a terrifying snap.

Before long, Toar began to feel as if he was most of the way to a full recovery, and as if his life-or-death fight just minutes before had been an intense workout that had left him with aches and bruises, rather than fresh breaks and a forming concussion.

And with that clarity of mind came acknowledgement of a dawning realisation. Well, two.

One, and still this was difficult to swallow: Mansol had access to superior health potions all along. He could’ve given them to Toar in the first place if he’d really thought he deserved them. But Toar supposed it was a test. That he was meant to show his resourcefulness by claiming the resources that had been made available to him.

But that was hardly the point. Toar had sat in immense pain, fighting death for days. Recovering for weeks. Barely able to walk until now.

And Mansol hadn’t thought to give him any of this medicine. Not even a single drop.

Toar’s second thought tied into the first, and it was a lot more simple…

Mansol was sick. He didn’t know how he hadn’t better seen it in the past.

He could think up many excuses. A whole slew of reasons that he hadn’t suspected his cousin’s true nature.

But most of them were bullshit, and none of them disparaged the truth of the matter.

Mansol was twisted. The world was likely better with him dead.

But, even with that being said…

“What are we going to do now?”

Finn had asked it right after finishing the remains of the opened superior health potion. It was the question on everybody’s minds. And none of them seemed to have an answer.

So it was down to Toar to invent one. To be a leader to his group.

“We need to clean up any evidence that we were here.”

“They’re gonna know, though. Even if we manage to dump him, they’ll know he went missing,” Jackal argued.

“We can’t move the body,” Toar told them. “Too heavy. Too big. We’d get caught moving it.

“They’re gonna know there was a murder,” he continued. “And they’re going to look for the culprit. There’s nothing that any of us can do to get around that.”

The others looked at one another. Toar could sense their silent fear, as thick as the odor hanging in the air.

“All we can do is clean this place up. Remove any evidence it was us.”

Both of them nodded. They were listening to Toar. They were panicked, and so was he, but he knew he needed to present a strong face now. There’d be time to fall apart later. For now, their lives were still on the line here.

Toar passed around the respite potion to the pair of them, then took a long swig of it himself, draining the contents.

“We need to work fast. Clean up every trace of blood and make sure you don’t leave anything behind. Jackal, that means your fur. Mine too.”

“So that’s it?” Jackal asked through shallow breaths. “Make this place spotless and go?”

“Not spotless,” Toar corrected. “We don’t have time for that. People could turn up here at any minute, and if we’re not gone by then, we’re probably dead.”

Toar looked between each of them in turn. “Deal with the blood. Then any hair or fur you see. Then we leave. Got it?”

A pair of acknowledgements, and the three of them soon set to work.

It was a grim and grueling task, but with the help of the potions and the knowledge that they didn’t have time to waste, the three of them made short work of cleaning up the crime scene, checking on each other’s work in between.

When it came to checking over Mansol himself, Toar let Finn and Jackal take point. They cleaned blood and dirt from under his nails and stripped him of his apron, but otherwise left him untouched.

Toar would’ve been more than happy to call it quits at this point, to leave, but…

“We need to clean him.”

Jackal stared at the fresh corpse. “Fuck, dude…”

“There’s no way around it.” Toar grabbed one of the scrubbing brushes as he spoke, throwing it to Jackal. “If there’s traces of our blood on him, and they find them—”

“Do we even know if they can identify blood?” Jackal asked.

“You wanna take that risk? I fucking don’t.”

“I know, but…”

“Stop bitching. I saved your life. Now take a sponge and clean him.”

Toar continued scrubbing the floor as his two group members did the nasty deed. Toar didn’t like putting them through something so stomach churning alone, but he couldn’t bring himself to help. He didn’t even want to acknowledge who it was laying there, how the pair of them grunted as they struggled to flip his dead weight, how pathetic the grown tiger looked being scrubbed through as he laid face down in the carpet.

It took forty minutes for the three of them to be at the very least minorly confident that they’d done a thorough job. Another twenty to confirm it, and that was really pushing it. All of them wanted to make a run for it and not look back, but it was all for nothing if they got caught a day later.

After just over an hour of work, and a sniff of the air revealing nothing besides the heavy stench of cleaning products, the three of them determined that they’d done enough.

Or rather, that if they stayed any longer, they’d likely be dead regardless. Each of them knew they were already cutting it thin.

Toar went to the ledger and forged a note of his recovery dated for yesterday. After that, the three of them left through the back.

None of them spoke on the way back to the camp. Maybe they should have. At least to devise a plan of how to explain things to the group.

From here on out, things were going to get a lot more complicated.

“Mom?”

I blinked and found myself at home. It was a simple shack lacking interior walls—one large room made up our dining, eating, living, and sleeping quarters, and within it was every bit of wear and shabbiness one might associate with a residence in the slums.

But our house wasn’t derelict. The woman who kept it neat enough to live in, who toiled maintaining and tidying this place was my mother.

And she was staring at me like a hawk might a mouse.

“Don’t track mud into the house,” she instructed, her voice somewhat shrill. She used her mop to point to the mat that had been placed just outside the door.

“Use it.”

I did as I was instructed, knowing there’d be hell to pay otherwise. Once I’d cleaned up and removed my boots, I stepped onto the fresh floor, still wet from the mop.

I tiptoed across the wet spot, hurrying towards the bed on the far side of my room, looking to deposit today’s score. The smell of lavender hung heavy in the air, and across the way, shouts, screams, and giggles echoed.

“Want to tell me where you’ve been all day?”

I stopped in my tracks. Somehow, I’d assumed that if I didn’t dirty up mom’s cleaning, she wouldn’t make a fuss about me dipping in and out like this.

“Just exploring,” I answered non-committally.

Mom squinted, betraying a bit of wrinkle around her eyes. “Exploring with who?”

I scrambled for a lie. “Summer, obviously.”

“And if I ask her, she’ll confirm that?”

“Of course.”

Mom rolled her eyes. “If I asked her if you were with her last morning, when you were in bed, do you think she’d confirm that too?”

Across the room, my brother and sister chased each other around a large, padlocked chest. I hung my head as mom stared me down.

“Where were you really?”

“Shining shoes.”

“Empty your pockets.”

I reluctantly did as I was told. Within, there were three silvers and a handful of coppers.

“You made all that shining shoes?” My mother asked.

I opened my mouth to tell another lie. Her face told me I should quit while I was behind.

“No.”

“Were you spending the day with Fazo again?” mom asked, leaning into me as she spoke. “Be honest.”

The way she said his name was similar to how one might utter a curse. It wasn’t his country of origin, she had multiple Assani friends.

No. It was because she had friends from Assan that she knew all about Fazo and his preferred line of work. Hence the weight of her accusation.

“I wasn’t,” I answered honestly. “I went and did a job for Garlan. It was nothing!” I quickly added, seeing the way her expression shifted. “Just delivering boxes.”

“You got paid three silvers for delivering boxes?” my mother asked me. “Boxes of what? To who?”

Obviously I didn’t look inside. I might’ve had my own curiosities, but sealed product was meant to be delivered sealed, and whether I suspected, or even pretty much knew that I was transporting illegal goods, having them unopened and being the age I was tended to be enough to mitigate most trouble.

I didn’t tell my mom all of that. Just that I didn’t know and that I didn’t ask.

“Garlan is a gangster,” mom hissed, lowering her voice. She placed the mop to one side and pulled me in by my arm. “You might be too young to be convicted for smuggling, but what happens when another criminal sees you walking around with a box of contraband?”

“How is anyone gonna know, mom?” I questioned. “It’s just a box. They’re light, they’re small… I was done in an hour.”

“There is magic in this world,” mom stated plainly. “Some people can see things that we can’t. You know why you’re being paid so well to do something so simple, Adam?”

“Because Garlan’s known to the guards, and they harass him, and—”

“Because it’s dangerous!”

Mom shook her head, curly locks bouncing across her forehead.

“Stop rationalising what you did. It’s not okay. Your father might not have the foresight to tell you, so I will. Stuff like that gets you killed. It’s how I got this.”

Mom pulled on the top of her dress, exposing a long, jagged scar that ran just below her neck.

“I almost died when I was only a little bit older than you, because I decided to work for a gangster. All I was doing was pouring drinks!”

She lowered her voice. “Then, one day, the tavern got raided, and I got cut down for calling for help.

“That’s what hanging around with criminals gets you. I knew they were bad people, but it was good money, and I wasn’t doing anything illegal. I didn’t think I was in any danger.

“You’re always in danger.”

My brother and sister had stopped playing now. They were looking over at us. Thankfully, I think the conversation was low enough that they didn’t pick up much of it, but the seven and eight year olds both knew that something was going on. They knew mom was angry.

She glanced at them for a moment, then snatched the coins out of my hand.

“Hey!”

“I’m holding onto this until you prove to me that you can behave yourself.” She placed the coins in a satchel hanging off a chair. “No more schemes. No more hustles. And definitely no more working for criminals. Society has rules, and you need to start following them, or you’ll be dead before you’re twenty.”

“Okay,” I nodded quickly. “But I earned that, you can’t just ta—”

“I can and I will,” mom told me. “It’s my house. When you’ve gotten your class, if you want to move out and live your own life, I can’t stop you, but in the meantime—”

“What’s wrong with your father’s job?”

“Uh, I dunno. Maybe that I just made his monthly wage in an hour?”

Her eyes shifted between me and the satchel.

“Your father makes more than this.”

“Not by much.”

“Well, at least I know he’s coming home every day!”

“Yeah, because the machines in those factories are so safe,” I shot right back.

“What, so the way we live isn’t good enough for you?” my mother accused.

“It’s not like you could do more.” I shrugged. “You both grew up with no money and got common classes. I think you both do great with what you’ve got.

“But I don’t wanna be stuck like this. I want to make money. I want us all to live somewhere better. I want dad to be able to do work he likes. I wanna buy these two gifts. Help them go to school somewhere nice even.”

I pointed at my younger siblings as I spoke. My sister smiled, while my brother just stared up at me like he wasn’t sure what to make of things. I probably shouldn’t have been involving them in the conversation, but I wasn’t really thinking.

“I have faith in you, Adam. You’re my oldest boy and you’re smarter than you ought to be. I’m sure the gods will smile on you. And that you’ll do well in life, regardless of your class.”

She raised a finger; my stomach moved inversely. “But, that doesn’t mean I believe in any of this. All these dreams and aspirations you have, they sound lovely, but they’re unrealistic. You’re going to make so much your father can retire? That you can send your siblings to school, that you can lavish everyone in gifts?”

I opened my mouth to respond, but I never got that far.

“You don’t need to do all of that for us. I want you to succeed in life. That’s why I raised you. Hoping to provide for your whole family alone, it’s no wonder you’re doing bad things. How else would you hope to make enough at your age?”

I didn’t respond this time. Just stared at her, teary-eyed and embarrassed.

“If you went and got yourself killed, or jailed, or barred from any chance at a good career, all because you wanted to provide for your family at the grand age of eleven, and I just let that happen, then what kind of a mother would that make me?”

I sniffled. “It’s not your fault.”

She sighed. “It isn’t yours either.”

She walked forwards. She loomed over me, still taller.

She patted my head.

“There’s nothing wrong with being ambitious. But I want you to be safe. There are plenty of ways for a bright boy like you to make money. Why don’t you try selling those rat catchers you made? They work a charm. I’ll even have your father pick up some more materials if you need them.”

I thought about it for a second. I hadn’t considered selling them before. They’d just been something I’d made to keep our pantry safe.

“I bet the bars and kitchens around town would love to buy some from you.”

I considered it a moment more.

“Thanks, mom.”

She kissed me on the forehead. I felt accepted. It was soothing.

“You’ll be here for dinner tonight I assume?” she asked me, looking down at me.

“I… Yeah. Of course.”

“Okay. No repeats of today from now on. I mean it.”

“Okay…”

“Good. I love you.”

“Love you too.”

That was it. I broke away from the semi-embrace and walked over to my bed. I didn’t really know how to feel about all that mom had said to me, but it upset me to feel like I was disappointing her. I really was trying to help everyone. I didn’t resent my parents’ lot in life, but I knew I could do more. That I could be someone bigger than the slum I’d been born in.

Maybe she was right about taking risks. Maybe I’d be able to do everything when I was older without putting myself in harm’s way if I just carried on working hard.

But my way was faster. It’d earned me multiple gold coins already and I was still alive and carried relatively few scars. Even today I’d made a handsome amount, which I was still annoyed that mom had taken.

It was all for the class. It was all well and good being able to make more when I was older, but if I didn’t have the right offering…

I wasn’t sure what to think.

My sister told my brother about a funny rhyme she’d heard as I fished through the underside of my bed until my fingers brushed a drawstring purse. I pulled it out and inspected the contents, feeling reassured by the golden glint inside.

Garlan had left me with something other than payment. He’d told me that if I wanted to earn more money this evening I could swing by his place and he’d give me another job. He hadn’t specified what or how much but after how much he paid me for an hour of delivery I expected the pay to be good.

But my mother’s warning was playing heavy on my mind.

She asked me where I was going as I left the house, and I simply said ‘I was going on a walk’ and that I’d be back soon. I didn’t know if I was lying. Neither did she. I could see the concern on her face.

I walked for a long time. I passed Garlan’s place twice, and both times I saw kids around my age and a bit older coming and going, as well as older teenagers.

I dunno. He might ask me to burgle a house. Or to make drugs. Or… who knows what.

What if I said no?

I’d only known the man a little while. Fazo had been out of town and I’d needed someone to sell to. He’d seemed reasonable enough and given me a good price for the things I’d found, stolen, and scavenged, but that was about as far as my knowledge of the man went. That and that he was clearly a criminal who was happy to use kids.

I think my mom scared me. I think I’d scared myself by thinking about this for so long.

I eventually turned around and returned home, cursing myself for my uncertainty.

“Well, look who decided to turn up!”

I recognised my father’s voice, but that was about all I could recognise. The man had soot on his face, his hands were cut and bruised, and he wore a completely exhausted expression.

Still he sounded happy to see me. A man who worked himself to death twelve hours a day to provide for his family and put food on the table.

“Hi, dad.”

My mother only smiled at me. She seemed relieved that I’d decided to show.

“Adam, Adam!”

My sister started to chew my ear off, telling me about this thing and that as eventually dinner was brought out. It wasn’t some noble spread of a hundred dishes or whatever fancy dinners looked like, but my mom still put in effort. There was bread and soups and meat and cheese and olives and mushrooms and spinach and cauliflower, all of it fresh and ready.

I wasn’t often happy with my lot in life. I often wanted more. More money, more opportunities, more of everything.

Right now, it was enough. I ate happily as dad told a story about his boss and complained about his fellow line workers. I let thoughts of the eternal, uphill climb that I was always so obsessed with drift from my mind, and for once, spent a moment in the ‘now’.

And I was happy. I loved my family. And they loved me. And despite my dreams of a perfect future, all I really needed was—

[Wake up, Adam.]

All I needed was—

[You need to wake up.]

Fuck.

This wasn’t my family. None of this was real. I didn’t have a family.

My parents were dead, or I’d never known them. The woman sitting opposite me was…

I had to keep fighting.

I wrapped my hands around my mother’s neck. False or not, I felt sick doing it. I grabbed at the looser skin around her throat and began to squeeze hard, fighting with every ounce of persistence I still held.

I felt that same pain all over my body, my organs punctured, my heart only beating by conscious effort, my mind fixed on a single, inexorable objective, one I could not relent from no matter how many wounds I endured…

Don’t pass out. Don’t die.

Survive this.

Survive this nightmare and wake up.

It was hell at first. The worst pain I’d ever experienced. More traumatising than any fight I’d been in, more terrible than any conflict I’d endured in the waking world. All of the worst sensations I could imagine were inflicted upon me over and over again.

[You’re almost there. Just a little longer.]

I wasn’t sure when it was that I began to recognise the fakery of it all. After a minute? Two?

The pain felt real until it didn’t. The woman I’d called my mother was a pretender. None of what she did to me hurt, not really. I was completely whole on the outside. I knew I could survive this. This world didn’t have power over me.

Once I began to realise that, the failsafe became weaker. Its blows began to really not hurt, and then I took the advantage. I pulled the knife from its hand, and found that my wounds had healed before I could even consider their existence.

I took the knife and slashed in a fell arc, down the neck until the breastbone.

The thump as she fell was sobering. The scream of my fake siblings and the sob of my imaginary father reminded me just what this dream had been. Whose image I had just killed.

The whole thing made me feel sick. As the dream fell apart, I was more than ready to leave.

And the moment I woke up, coughing, sputtering, falling out of my pod, I realised I could scarcely remember exactly what had just transpired.

I remember Maya. I remembered her dreams. But after that…

Maya!

The girl in question wasn’t in her pod. Instead she was at the console.

Slumped over by it, at least.

As I walked over to her, listening to her haggard breathing, I realised with a start that she was hurt. Hurt badly.

I didn’t know what was wrong with her. I tried giving her some potion, but nothing I administered made a difference. She was gasping harshly with each inhale; her whole body was burning up.

“Maya…”

I tried a Recovery Stone next, jamming it into her arm to not waste time.

Still no difference. It wasn’t mist exposure, or at least, that wasn’t all of it. There was something else wrong with her.

Had the pod stasis been keeping her healthy, somehow?

I needed to find a way to fix her. She’d risked her life to save me when she could have killed me and ran home through that portal. The barrier was finally down. And she was dying.

[Quest complete! Major advancement has been—]

I needed to take her through the portal.

I needed to bring her to her world and get her to a doctor.

It was the only way to save her, and I was running out of time. I couldn’t just leave her here.

I picked Maya up, which was surprisingly easy. She didn’t weigh much. I started to walk, out of the control room and towards the transport room.

As soon as I arrived, I felt the difference. Lack of a barrier led to the energy around the portal swirling, unnatural wind biting at me as I stepped my way forward.

I steeled myself. I’d done this before. I’d gone through one of these and came out the other side. Maybe I hadn’t been on my way to a whole new planet, filled with questionable natives, but—

[Do not go through that portal.]

At first, I didn’t register the message.

My every step forward was met with ten more.
