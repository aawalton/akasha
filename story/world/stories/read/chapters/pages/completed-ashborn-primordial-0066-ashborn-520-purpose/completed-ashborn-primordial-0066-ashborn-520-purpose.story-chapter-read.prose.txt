Before Vir could ask what the god meant by that, he had already turned and was leading them out of the chamber in which he had revived Ashani.

Glancing at Maiya, who shrugged, Vir followed, with Ashani bringing up the rear. She averted her eyes, and it was clear they would have to have a chat later to—!?

Vir froze in his tracks, realizing that with all the revelations, he’d forgotten one crucial detail.

“Ashani?” Vir said slowly. “Did you say you were Janak’s wife?”

To his surprise, she actually chuckled, covering the gesture with her hand as though it were something to be ashamed of.

“My predecessor’s consort,” Janak clarified from ahead as they walked back into the main chamber. A set of spiral stairs suddenly materialized out of the ground, leading them down into the building’s bowels. “Though we have grown closer to one another during our long cooperation.”

“Janak retains his predecessor’s memories,” Ashani said. “At least up to a certain point. As such, their personalities are nearly identical. Is it any wonder I would fall for him again?”

“All this time,” Vir muttered, descending into the staircase after Janak, “I assumed you had no interest in such matters. To think you harbored such love…”

“My apologies,” Ashani said with a playful smile. “But I assure you that if it weren’t for your love for Maiya, and if you were just a few hundred years older, I would have fallen for you several times over. You are, after all, his incarnation.”

Vir flushed with embarrassment, his eyes jumping to Maiya in guilt. Her own eyes narrowed at the goddess before meeting Vir’s gaze, as if to warn him not to dare. After holding the gaze for a second, however, she sighed and rolled her eyes.

“As someone who’s known him his whole life, Vir’s too devoted to chase other women. Even goddesses, no matter how unfairly pretty they might be.”

Vir was taken aback for a moment at Maiya’s vote of confidence, but his chest bloomed with something warm, and he smiled back at her. She knew him so well.

“Well, then I must thank Maiya,” Janak said with a chuckle, “for allowing this old relic some companionship in his old age.”

Vir frowned. “I understand that Ashani has been dormant for long periods to conserve her energy, but have you been active this whole time?”

“Every moment,” Janak admitted wearily. “Our chances to interact were few. Even fewer once she was indoctrinated by my enemy, but I cherished every moment we had together.”

“Janak has been trapped within this tower,” Ashani explained. “We were never able to communicate directly, and since I gained my Gate creation abilities only after the realms split, I could not free Janak from his prison.”

So the bit about her being trapped was true, but the part about her not having any contact with Janak was a lie.

Though he’d not gotten over the shock of what she was, Vir nonetheless understood she had done it for the same cause he’d given everything for. There was a limit to how angry he could be with her, given all that she’d done for him.

Especially with that look of longing in her eyes whenever she looked at Janak. She loved him so dearly, he was surprised she hadn’t thrown herself upon him by now. He certainly would have, had he been separated from Maiya for that long. Even the relatively brief years they’d spent apart had nearly driven him mad.

Subconsciously, he reached for Maiya’s hand, gripping it tightly within his own. She didn’t protest.

“This doesn’t explain how you came to be Janak’s consort in the first place,” Vir said. “The original Janak. Or was the part about being created as Siya’s protector a fabrication as well?”

The Imperium automaton shook her head. “No, that was no lie. Janak knew he’d have less and less time to spend with Siya in his pursuit to find a cure, but the thought of leaving her alone in her final days nearly drove him mad. I was his solution. Yet, as life often does, things did not quite go the way he had planned. I befriended Siya, of course, and we got along splendidly. Soon, I came to regard her as not just a friend or even a sister, but more as my own daughter. I think Janak saw that, and in time, came to see me in a different light.”

“It did not hurt that Ashani greatly aided my predecessor in his research as well,” Janak said. “They grew close to one another.”

“If only I contributed anything of significance, perhaps none of this would ever have happened,” Ashani said, gesturing at the air.

“I’m sure he enjoyed having you around, regardless,” Maiya said, looking on the verge of tears herself.

“Janak had lost his wife to the same illness that plagued his daughter,” Ashani said softly. “He was both the greatest and the loneliest being I ever knew. At first, I simply wished to ease his pain. Not even I could have predicted the depths our relationship would achieve.”

Vir fell silent at her words, scarcely fathoming what such a relationship must have been like. Though there must have been great joy, he could only see the pain. The pain of watching Siya wither away while Janak slaved away searching desperately for a cure. The pain Ashani must have felt when the world ended and her Janak passed. Of being indoctrinated as an agent of the beings who had orchestrated it. To say nothing of the millennia she’d spent after, never knowing if Janak’s plans would come to fruition and if the Akh Nara would ever save her…

It was only when Maiya’s grip on his hand tightened that he realized they had descended to the bottom of the spiral stairwell, entering a colossal underground chamber.

One that Vir recognized immediately.

For standing before him was the very same unknowable contraption he’d seen a hundred times or more.

The black contraption of metal ten stories in height that plagued his nightmares. In which he died.

No, was supposed to die.

“So it’s true, then,” Vir said, but his words came out as barely anything more than a whisper. “My destiny… the moment of Fate your predecessor sealed within me… is to die in this machine.”

For what felt like an eternity, no one said anything, thereby confirming Vir’s statement.

“Your sacrifice would have saved three realms,” Janak muttered, looking forlorn as he gazed up at the tall structure, standing like some caged Ash Beast with uncountable chains, hoses, and who knew what else shackling it to the walls, floor, and ceiling. Pipes and other protrusions jutted up and down its length at haphazard angles, entirely unlike any Imperium creation Vir had ever seen.

And within it all, Vir spied a central shaft that from his nightmares, he knew would ferry him up to the top, where his body and soul would be ripped to shreds.

Vir frowned at his choice of words. “Would have? Do you mean to say that without Iksana’s Reality Inversion, all is lost?”

Despite his words, Vir was sure that Janak had some sort of plan. Otherwise, why bother leading him here? Why bother fighting at all in the centuries since the Bloodline Art was lost?

“I felt that way once. Even now…” Janak’s jaw clenched, and his features contorted into a grimace. “Over the years, our enemy eroded the essence of the Akh Nara’s reincarnations, and each incarnation was a little less than the last. Less able to affect the threads of Fate, and thus more predictable. I thought all was lost, that without a plan, only a miracle spun by a future Akh Nara could bring back any chance of success.” He stopped, and something like wonder crept into his voice. “It is difficult to admit, but I was merely going through the paces, stubbornly refusing to accept the inevitable… until your soul shattered.”

“That was their doing?” Vir asked, instinctively bringing a hand to his chest. “Annas’ attack?”

Janak laughed. “Nothing so crude, I’m afraid. That would be far simpler to deal with. No, their doing was to have Annas be born in the first place,” Janak said, pacing in front of the machine. “And it was my doing to enable you to win the war against the Chitran. Their doing to have Annas open the Crown Chakra when by rights he should not have. Would never have, had he not stumbled upon a most fortunately placed clue that happened to be left by the great-grandson of a demon they had once touched briefly with their power. They laid a thousand and one pieces across countless centuries, all of which culminated in the shattering of your soul.”

Coming to an abrupt halt, the ancient god met Vir’s gaze with such intensity his eyes may as well have peered into his soul.

“They tug upon a single thread of the tapestry and a mother dies during childbirth, killing the child that would have become king. Unable to save her life, I give the seer a prophecy to ensure the childbirth happens in the Ash, thus saving the baby, and later, when he is old enough to venture into places he should not be, project a well-timed message, thereby setting him upon the path of reuniting the demon clans.

“For millennia, we have been embroiled in an unseen war. A never-ending battle of causality where the smallest tug has the potential to unravel the universe or save it. Yet I am little more than a trapped and broken man from an era long forgotten, fighting an enemy that weaves the very tapestry of an infinity of universes. There is only so much I can do.”

The words hung in the air, each one a revelation that rewrote everything Vir thought he knew about his life. They had killed his mother. He’d always blamed the Ash, and in a way, the Fateweavers were the Ash. They’d created it.

Perhaps, if she hadn’t died, his father might be alive as well. Perhaps the Chitran would never have taken over Garga to begin with, and Vir would have grown up in the demon realm with a real family.

In which case, I would never have met Maiya, he thought, glancing at the love of his life, whose face was scrunched up in anguish and sympathy.

How was he supposed to weigh the life of his mother against the love of his life?

Causality was such a curious and profound thing, he reflected. One set of tragedies might open the doors to uncountable enriching experiences, or vice versa, as they’d witnessed in the alternate universes. As Janak said, the smallest changes can cause realm-altering changes, given enough time.

Perhaps it was for the best that the beings who wove its fabric bore no resemblance to mortal beings.

They would have lost their minds long ago if they had.

“I thought all was lost when your soul shattered,” Janak continued, and now there was something almost like amusement in his voice. “Thought they had fired the final arrow.” He shook his head slowly. “But you survived. Somehow, against all odds, you went against their every prediction and reforged your soul through sheer bloody-minded stubbornness. It should not have been possible. They should have seen and accounted for it. And in so doing, you purged their hooks entirely. Became a truly free agent for the first time in five thousand years.”

Janak laughed—a sound that was bitter and triumphant in equal parts. “It made them panic. Made them fear for what might very well be the first time in their infinite existence. They focused on our world, trying to destroy it before you could realize what you’d become.”

Maiya drew in a sharp breath. “The Ash Tears,” she said, her voice tight. “The surge in activity, the way they suddenly became so much worse—that was all because Vir reforged his soul?”

“In a word, yes,” Janak confirmed. “They threw everything at this universe. Tried to overwhelm it, to force you into a corner where you’d have to make choices they could predict and counter.”

The scale of it all staggered Vir. This wasn’t a battle between nations or even realms. This was a war fought on a different plane of existence entirely. A battle between forces that mortals couldn’t even perceive, invisible to the world.

How many people had lived and died never knowing they were pieces on this board?

“It didn’t work,” Vir said, the pieces falling in place one after another. “Because all the work they’d spent undoing the Akh Nara over the millennia suddenly came undone. And all the people they had in place…” He looked at Maiya and Ashani.

“The Akh Nara’s effect spreads to all those close to him,” Ashani said gently. “It’s why your allies thrived despite their attention. Why Maiya could resist their influence even as the Blessed Chosen, and why I was able to keep my mind.”

Janak nodded. “I’m but an observer. I’ve thwarted fate where I could, nudged probability in small ways, but I lack the power you possess.” He looked at Maiya. “Both you and Ashani were supposed to be poison pills, meant to unwittingly corrupt his efforts. Yet not even they expected him to reforge his soul. It was what enabled our realms to survive so long despite waging a war against them. As you saw yourself, most universes that gain their ire succumb almost immediately. Your arrival here has at least given us a chance…”

He paused, his expression darkening. “Even if they nearly managed to foil my plan at the last moment.”

“I take it we were not supposed to visit that last universe?” Vir asked.

“Oh no, you were,” Janak replied. “That series of Gates was the only way I could bring you here. Only, none of the universes you visited should have been corrupted. They learned of my plans, as they always do, and countered it.”

Vir blanched when he understood Janak’s meaning. “They countered it… by corrupting an entire universe?”

“It is their way,” Janak said with a humorless chuckle. “The Vimana were originally all mine to activate and command,” Janak said. “But our enemy has wrested control over the millennia, one by one. I thought myself safe, using only the ones I fully controlled to function as Gates to bring you here.” His jaw clenched and Vir could almost hear him gnashing his teeth. “But alas, they saw which Vimana you would take in that alternate universe and corrupted it.”

“Alright,” Vir said, working through the implications. “I think I understand the general picture, but what exactly is this machine? What was your predecessor’s grand plan that required my death to save the realms? How does this all work?”

“That,” Janak said, pointing to Vir’s chest, “is where your tattoos are required. For this machine might very well be the most impressive creation my people have ever built.”

Janak turned, proudly sweeping his arms at the towering contraption.

“For this machine is designed to consume every ounce of prana in the Ashen Realm… and, with you as its conduit, use it to collapse the three realms. Back into one. To the way it once was. The way it should be. With Siya turned back into her true form.”

“Her… true form?” Maiya asked, echoing Vir’s own confusion. “What does that mean?”

“You might think the greatest cruelty inflicted upon me is this prison,” Janak said, gazing into the distance, past the walls of Mahādi’s central spire. “Or perhaps all the time I’ve spent contemplating my mistakes. But you would be wrong. That title goes to the twisted irony that my prison guard…”

Janak trailed off, his words catching in his throat as his body trembled.

But he didn’t need to finish that sentence.

For, with dawning horror, they understood.

“It’s your daughter, isn’t it?” Maiya whispered, hands clasped over her mouth. “They turned Siya into the Prana Swarm that keeps you here.”
