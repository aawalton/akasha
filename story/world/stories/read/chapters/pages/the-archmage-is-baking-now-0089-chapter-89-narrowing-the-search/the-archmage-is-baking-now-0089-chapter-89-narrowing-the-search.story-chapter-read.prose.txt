They climbed back into the carriage and rode for the few minutes it took to reach the inn, a large barn-shaped structure set just off the main thoroughfare. Stables flanked the main building, and lamplight was already glowing in the windows. The smell of hot food set Jane’s stomach to groaning, and when the carriage stopped, she was truly glad to be out of it.

The innkeeper hurried over, a round sort of man who looked surprisingly unsurprised at the arrival of a small army.

"How many rooms will you need?"

"Six,” Captain Harris replied. “One for the lady, one for myself, and four for my men. We'll need stabling for the horses and meals for everyone."

"Of course. I’ll have everything ready within the hour. Will you be needing anything else?"

"No. That will be quite sufficient.”

The innkeeper nodded and hurried off, shouting instructions to someone inside the building. Jane watched him go, then turned to the captain.

"Shouldn’t we ask him what he's seen? He's on the main road. Dave and Brand would have passed right by here."

"He's already been questioned. My riders spoke to him on our first and second passes. He doesn't know anything. Believe me on this, at least.”

Jane accepted that. The captain knew his business. If he was satisfied, she’d have to be as well.”

They made their way inside. A handful of other travelers were scattered around the tables, but left the well-armed force alone and kept to their own business. The captain guided Jane to a table and flagged down a serving boy to bring them food and drink.

"You should eat.”

“I will, believe me.” Jane sat back on her wooden chair and tried to keep her stomach’s complaints from becoming audible to others.

“Good. Now, if you don’t mind, I've been meaning to ask you something. About your aunt."

"Cecelia?"

"Yes. I've met her a few times over the years. At official functions, mostly. But I saw her in action once. Really saw her work, I mean."

"What happened?"

"There was a landslide of sorts, a place where a flood took out a whole section of an important road. The engineers said it would take weeks to rebuild. Your aunt showed up and put it back. I mean that. She used her magic to shove the soil back into place like she was sliding a piece of furniture across a room. It was truly frightening."

Jane nodded. "That sounds like her."

"How does someone even do that?” the captain asked. “Could you?”

"Remember what I said earlier? About archmages needing either overwhelming power, the ability to improvise, or both?"

"I remember."

"She would qualify for archmage on raw power alone, even if she only knew a few spells. She’s that strong.” Jane reached up and grabbed a plate of meat and vegetables the serving boy had brought, taking a large bite and swallowing it down almost before she had chewed it. “I’m strong too, of course. But nothing like her.”

"You seemed powerful on the road.”

“A few more castings and I would have been completely drained. My aunt could have done the same thing a hundred times without breaking a sweat. If she could cast the spell at all, I suppose. I’m not sure that she would have approached the problem in the same way at all."

The captain took his own starting bites of food, and they had their plates nearly cleared by the time he decided to talk again.

“I'm curious. Between the two of you, who is the better mage?"

"Honestly? I’m the wrong person to ask. I can't even assess that."

"Can't, or won't?"

"Can't. My aunt is my aunt. I grew up seeing her do impossible things. I’m sure she’s larger in my head than she really is, but I have no idea where it stops. It’s hard for me to even think of myself as having the same title as her, though I know I do.”

The common room gradually emptied as the other travelers retired to their rooms. Jane took another plate of food, then found herself nodding halfway through it. It had been a busy day, and travel took most of her energy even in the best of times.

"You should sleep," the captain said. I'll wake you at first light."

"What about you?"

"I’ll be up for a few hours yet. I'm more used to this kind of life." He stood and offered her a hand up. "Come on. I'll show you to your room."

Jane let him guide her up a narrow staircase to the inn's second floor, where a row of doors led to individual rooms. Hers was at the end of the hall. She thanked the captain and closed the door behind her, leaning against it with a sigh.

Where are you, Dave? Where are you, Brand?

She undressed and climbed into bed. It was not hard to sleep. She had just a few moments of visions of disaster dancing in her head before she drifted off.

—

Jane woke before the captain's knock the next morning, dressed, and was ready to go before the moment she and the guards finished their breakfast. They were on the road within the hour.

The first stop came after about forty minutes. Jane drew her circle, cast her spell, and found Dave's carriage among the ghostly images.

"They came through here." There was little else to say. “And they were safe.”

"Good. I saw the same myself. Let’s keep going."

By the time they were four stops in, Jane was much more in agreement with the captain over the necessity of rest. Her magic was back, and that translated well to her increased control over the spell. The images were clearer than they had been the night before, and the cost was lower. On top of that, they were covering an enormous amount of ground. The royal carriage moved faster now that the horses were fresh, and by midday, they had already passed the point where Jane estimated the halfway mark should be.

We're getting close. Whatever happened has to show itself soon.

But as the miles rolled by, and each casting showed Dave and Brand still traveling safely, Jane found herself starting to worry that her methods were somehow broken or missing some aspect of the greater picture. She pushed the doubt aside almost as quickly as it arose.

It’s working. You know that. Just keep it up.

Still, she wasn’t the only authority on this trip. Even her aunt sought guidance from experts. She took that page out of her aunt’s book and applied it between one of their afternoon stops.

"Captain, are you comfortable continuing on as we have?"

He looked up from the map he had been studying. It had been marked all over with what Jane assumed were notes regarding the search.

"What do you mean?"

"I know this is unconventional, and you have more experience with this kind of thing than I do. You outrank me in this sort of work. If you think we should be doing something different, I want to know."

The captain laughed.

"Not many people would think a guard outranks an archmage."

"You have more experience than me," Jane repeated. "I have to acknowledge that. I've never searched for missing people before. You have."

"Maybe so. But your method is sound, Miss Jane. We're covering ground faster than we could any other way, and we know for certain that they passed each point we've checked. That's more than my riders could have told us."

Jane found herself accepting the reassurance. It helped to hear someone else say it.

"Thank you," she said.

"Just keep doing what you're doing."

The day continued in much the same way. Jane's reserves held steady. They stopped, she cast, and they moved on. The brief rests between castings were just barely enough to compensate for what she was spending, but they were proving to be enough overall. She sustained the pace all day, verifying the lost duo’s progress again and again.

The sun was beginning its descent toward the horizon when the town came into view.

Jane recognized it almost immediately. She had passed through this town on her way to Glenfall from the capital, and had slept and eaten before moving on for the last long leg of her journey. It wasn’t a remarkable place, but the time had been remarkable to her, and it left its impression.

The town was bigger than Willowbrook, but not by much. Jane estimated a few hundred people lived here, maybe four hundred at the most. From what she could tell, the town dealt mainly in timber. She could see sawmills working, and stacks of cut lumber lined the streets waiting to be carted off to buyers in other cities.

"This is the destination," the captain said, consulting his notes. "The merchant Dave was delivering for has a warehouse here. Our riders confirmed he never arrived at that warehouse."

"Then whatever happened, it happened somewhere between here and our last stop." Jane was already climbing out of the carriage, reaching for her etching tool. "Let me try one more casting. Maybe I can narrow down the location."

She found a clear patch of ground near the edge of town and drew her circle. She pushed a bit of power into it, now expert in the use of the spell in a way that would never leave her. The road above them flickered into ghostly life.

It was chaos.

Jane had expected the town to have more traffic than the empty stretches of road they had been checking, but she hadn't been prepared for this. Dozens of figures moved through the image, overlapping and blurring into each other. There were merchants, farmers, wagons, all smushed together into a writhing mass of activity.

She tried to focus, tried to pick out Dave's carriage from the crowd. She couldn’t, at least not with any true level of confidence..

"Anything?" the captain asked. “I didn’t see them.”

“There are too many echoes to sort through." Jane rubbed her temples. "I can't pick them out from the crowd, and the spell doesn’t work twice in the same place."

"I thought that might happen. It’s hard to track through a busy town, even without magic." He looked up at the sky, where the first stars were beginning to appear. "It's getting dark. I'll find rooms for us. We can start questioning people in the morning."

"I want to walk around a bit first." Jane straightened, forcing her tired body to cooperate. "Stretch my legs. See what I can learn."

"Don't go far."

"I'll be fine."

There were food stands scattered throughout the town, more than Jane would have expected. She stopped at the first one she found and bought a portion of river-caught fish, eating it with her fingers as she walked. It was good.

Jane worked her way through the evening market, stopping at each food stand she passed and buying small portions. The vendors were friendly enough, happy to chat with a paying customer, and Jane steered each conversation toward the same topic. Had anyone seen two young men, traveling together in a wagon?

Not everyone remembered them, but Jane quickly realized the riders hadn’t questioned the entire town. A few people had seen them, after all.

"The young one was asking about the mills. Wanted to know how we cut the timber so straight. I told him about the water wheels." The man selling Jane a bag of fire-roasted nuts was happy to share what he saw as simple gossip. “The other one said they had those in Glenfall. He knew a bit about how they worked.”

About every fifth person had noticed them, or at least a wagon that fit their description. Jane was confident they hadn’t actually made it to the warehouse, or the guards would have uncovered it. Somewhere in town, their course had changed.

Nobody had seen them leave. Every person Jane talked to remembered the arrival, but none of them could say when or how Dave and Brand had departed. It was as if they had simply vanished from the town, there one moment and gone the next.

Jane’s questioning grew more rapid and direct as she followed the trail, but also more frustrated and frantic. She had hoped someone would have seen something useful. What she got was a handful of amused memories about two nice young people and nothing actionable.

She was eating fried meat and just about to report back to the captain when she noticed the laundress. The elderly woman had set up her operation in a small lot of the main road, and had shirts, pants, and unmentionables galore laid out on grids of rod iron suspended on the inside of a three-sided stone chimney. The clothes were steaming slightly as moisture rose off them, clearly being dried by some means Jane couldn’t quite understand.

"Excuse me," Jane said, approaching the woman. "Do you mind if I take a look at your equipment?"

"At my dryer? Sure, go ahead. I’m surprised someone wants to see it. I think it’s a neat piece of equipment, myself, but most people don’t find it too interesting."

Jane stepped up and examined the contraption more closely. It was ingeniously designed, the kind of thing Allen might have put together once he put his mind to a problem. The grids of rod iron were positioned to allow air to flow freely around the clothes, while a magical mechanism at the base produced a constant stream of warm air. It wasn’t hot enough to damage the fabric, but the constant flow of warm air forced the clothes to dry in a fraction of the time they would take on a normal clothesline anyway.

"This is wonderful," Jane said. "Where did you get it?"

"Had a traveling tinker build it for me, oh, must be fifteen years ago now. The enchantment was the most expensive part. Cost me half a year's savings, but it’s paid for itself by now. I can turn around a full load of work clothes twice as fast as anyone else. It’s good for business.”

"It's very clever."

"It is, isn't it?" The woman finished folding the shirt and set it aside. "You look bothered, if you don't mind me saying. Something on your mind?"

Jane hesitated, but the old laundress had a kind face, and Jane was learning that talking about things often helped her feel better. She felt she could use that, just then.

"I'm looking for some people. I haven’t been able to find them yet, and I’m getting a bit downhearted about it.”

I know just what you mean." The laundress set another in a long line of folded shirts on a neatly stacked pile. "It helps to let things go, you know. For instance, the other day I asked two boys to pick something up from my sister. She lives out in the woods, not too far from here. Should have been back just that afternoon. I was counting on them to be.”

Jane went very still. "They were going to your sister's house?"

"That's right. She gives me spices she grows herself, and I told them I’d clean up their traveling clothes if they went to pick them up for me. Seemed like a good trade." The woman frowned slightly. "They should have been back that same day. Never did come back.”

Jane's mind was racing. The older woman obviously didn't realize that she was casually describing the last known movements of the missing prince of the realm. She was just a laundress, annoyed at the unreliability of today’s youth.

"Your sister," Jane said, keeping her voice carefully calm. "How do I get to her house?"

The woman looked up, surprised by the intensity in Jane's tone. "You want to go out there? It's getting dark."

"I know. But it's important." She frowned. “I think those two boys might be the people I’m looking for. I’ll check after them for both of us.”

"Well, all right." The laundress set down her work and grabbed a pencil. “Here are some directions. Follow them and you’ll get there all right, so long as you don’t get lost in the dark. Is something wrong, do you think? Did something happen to those boys?"

"I don't know yet." Jane moved back towards the street. "But I'm going to find out."

She found the captain at the inn, just finishing arrangements for their rooms. He looked up as she burst through the door.

"I know where they went," she said. “Get us horses. I’ll take you there.”
