After Kelia left, Caleb settled down for the night—well, the day—napping in the cot he kept in the old mill for just such occasions. Occasionally Bog woke him when the bloomery needed tending, and he dozed until noon, when once again Kelia woke him with food.

Once awake, he got back to work. While he was not the most vaunted initialized member of the community, he still had responsibilities. Initialized were given leave to spend part of each day pursuing whatever goal they thought might allow them to level, and not even a master could keep their apprentice from doing so—not that any would, if the apprentice truly was pursuing their class. Levels were what kept the village alive in times of crisis, after all.

Caleb was certain his work wouldn’t actually help him level. He’d already figured out something about crafting classes allowed them to gain essence without killing. He just didn’t know what that was or if he could replicate it. He did know, however, that his work would be for the benefit of the village, so he had no qualms about lying so he could have extra time in his forge. Before he’d foolishly left the village alone in search of metal from the sky, he’d been require to spend most of the daylight hours working in his master’s forge. Now he’d be able to leave halfway through the day, or work every other, to pursue his own goals.

Grisim already essentially lived out in the forest. He didn’t have a formal apprenticeship before and had just been learning the trade of a woodcutter—or Lignator, if you asked some of the more prideful of the lot. Allegedly there was a woodcutter class by that title, but no one in Caleb’s village had ever had it, and Caleb thought it foolish to claim the High Tongue name for such a humble job. When Grisim got initialized, he quit and was able to support himself by selling the fruit of his hunting labors—with a little help from Kelia, who would often tag along while Caleb was busy and teach him about the herbs and wild plants he could collect to supplement his income.

The three of them agreed that Caleb should tell his master that he’d be hunting with Grisim on his time off, when he’d really be here at his forge working on his craft.

Caleb spent the remainder of the day forging a complete set of ingots for himself, Kelia, and Grisim. They each had two plus-3 ingots for strength and one plus-2 for constitution. Near the end, he figured out how to get the plus-4 enchantments into the smaller bars, so he spent a while trimming the originals down and mending the enchantments.

When he was finished, he found he was out of iron. He’d need to part with some of his “found” gear to replenish his stock, as he had no money worth speaking of. Any he earned went toward buying the discounted scrap from his master. He had begun buying that, but looking at it with his metal senses made him balk.

“I can’t believe I used that mess and was proud of the product,” he said to Bog.

The scrap was a mess of dissonant, chaotic mana strings. He could make something visibly pleasing and even up to his master’s standards if he spent the time, but nothing he knew of could salvage the tangle within save for smelting it anew—even then, he wasn’t sure what would happen.

“Looks like we are out of coal too,” Caleb said as he went to get more for the bloomery.

He reached out to the forge with his metal sense and found the little magical ball of energy within. He had Bog push the scorching hot lid aside, lifting the blue lump of metal out with his mind.

“It seems like it’s metal,” Caleb said cautiously.

Bog nodded emphatically, licking his lips and making a horrible rasping sound in the process.

“Here you go,” he said, sending the metal toward his dragon. “Remember to give this piece back after you figure it out, or I’ll tell Kelia you stole her present.”

Bog nodded seriously, snatched the lump out of the air, and curled up in a ball to meditate on the metal with.

It looked a lot like a nap, but Caleb could tell through their bond that the dragon was concentrating.

Exhausted from his own day of repetitive labor, Caleb went to bed, readying his mind for a return to normalcy.

Things were not normal.

Caleb knew he had improved in his forging, but he had already forgotten how slow he’d been before. He realized this as he watched his fellow apprentices work as he walked through the smithy—a few hours late. Their hammer swings were inaccurate and moved little steel. Even from far away Caleb could tell their metals had either been under or over heated, and they had to take breaks from the work to rest their arms.

The thing was, physically each of these apprentices had been better than him before, and it was his technique that had set him apart. Now he was leagues past them and he had only just begun.

In the back he saw his master working on a piece and felt a tinge in his Magical Perception. Normally it lay dormant, as he found the mana a distraction, but he was beginning to think that was a mistake.

With his skill active, Caleb saw his master had a mana pool around him—smaller than Caleb’s by far, but still there it was. He watched as Dimel worked the steel in front of him just outside his mana pool, and with each strike, the master smith pushed a little bit of mana out of the steel. Whether or not he knew how he he was doing it, Dimel was purging the metal of the natural mana within and allowing its aspect to manifest.

“Look who decided to show up,” Dimel announced cheerfully, stopping his work when he saw Caleb. “Late night partying?”

Caleb blushed and rubbed the back of his head.

“Working, then,” Dimel said, smiling. “That’s what I figured. Let’s go talk in back.”

The other apprentices and strikers had stopped their work to listen in on the conversation. Their rhythmic tamping still continued, as they didn’t want to be too obvious about the listening in, all conveniently at the same time choosing their steel needed more heat.

He followed his master into the back office, a large bundle of equipment over his shoulder.

“Show me what you found,” Dimel said, excitingly rubbing his hands together.

Caleb unloaded his bag. He’d removed the spearhead from the spear to avoid carrying the conspicuous item around town. The shaft itself had been no loss, as its construction had been crude compared to that of the head itself. Next to the spearhead he laid the helmet and then the repaired breastplate.

To Caleb’s surprise, his master reached for the battered breastplate first and started examining it closely.

“Amazing that you were able to repair this!” he said as he looked it over. “Was all this repair work done by you?”

He continued on with a line of inquiry that made Caleb grow increasingly uncomfortable with the lie. Once satisfied with the breastplate, he moved the spear and helm over. Dimel asked less questions, but he kept looking up at Caleb appraisingly as he examined.

“Are you ready?” he asked, suddenly putting down the helm.

“For…”

In lieu of an answer, Dimel rose to his feet and went over to a small rack of steel he kept at the back of his office. He picked up billets one by one, tapping them with his hammer and listening to the ringing until he settled on one.

“This will do,” he said, placing the rare—though now ringfallen—metal before Caleb. “It’s time for you to submit your journeyman piece.”

Caleb’s heart skipped a beat. He’d thought he’d been ready for a while, but his master had not agreed. It seemed that the initialization had been enough of a shake-up that the stubborn old man finally agreed Caleb was ready. By regular standards, he had only just reached the proper age to perform the trial, but in Caleb’s not-so-humble opinion, he was above regular standards.

When Caleb didn’t speak, frozen in shock, his master continued.

“This is high-quality meta steel, have you heard of it?”

Caleb nodded before realizing that maybe he ought not to have.

“It’s aspected for metal itself. If the aspect can be maintained in the forging, this will cut through steel with far greater ease than the cold steel you are used to working with. Use my forge. Make something memorable from this steel and you will be a journeyman.”
