The forest Tarek had chosen for our meeting had given up on maintaining any pretenses of life. Crumbling, leafless trees creaked under a lead-gray sky, with clouds moving fast and low overhead. A chill accompanied the wind, tugging at my breath as it turned white with vapor.

Olethros was leaning against the trunk of one of the more stable trees with his eyes closed, meaning he was paying attention to everything around us.

A few feet away, a gaunt Prince Mito was leaning heavily into a quiet conversation with what appeared to be a filthy beggar in rags. He was shaved bald, hunched over slightly, looking like someone you’d discreetly step around on a city street.

The beggar kept glancing over at Olethros, his eyes narrowed in anger.

I squinted. There was something about the beggar that I couldn’t place.

Mito said something in a low voice, and the beggar nodded. Then Mito pulled him into a brief, firm hug that the man accepted with stiff surprise. His Highness then stepped back, squared his shoulders, and cleared his throat.

“Your Majesty,” he said to Olethros, not quite looking at him, “I believe you have something that belongs to Father Irijah.”

My eyes widened.

Father Irijah?!

Irijah turned toward Olethros.

“My staff.”

“Mm.” Olethros didn’t move from the trunk. “Alright. It’s not really my style,” he agreed pleasantly. He straightened, and the stolen gold staff appeared in his hand. He held it out for a moment, then drew it back. “Before I return it, I’m curious. Are you willing to help me get back mine?”

Irijah looked at him for a long, irritated moment. Then he glanced at me.

“I am no longer a priest,” he said, his voice stripped of inflection, flat but not so much as to fully hide the bitterness underneath. “Presumably, Relias sealed my abilities after he learned of your escape. I would be nothing but a burden to you.” His jaw tightened, and he threw another dirty glance at the demon king. “And yet here I am, supposedly at the behest of both of you.” His gaze returned to mine. “Tell me. What use is there for me now?”

I scratched my cheek. “Honestly… I was worried about you,” I said. “I told you to hide and that I couldn’t help, and then I left. But now I’m in a position where I think I can.” I paused. “Tarek found you because Olethros asked him to, because I asked him to. I admit that confirmation of your well-being makes my guilt a little easier to bear, so I suppose I’m being a bit selfish here, too.”

Irijah stared at me for much longer than he needed to.

What was wrong with my answer?

It’s not like I was asking him to do anything in particular.

Is this another trap?

Then he laughed—a solid, loud cry that was probably directed at himself as much as anyone else.

“What?” I asked, looking around in confusion.

He shook his head, still laughing, pressing one hand over his eyes. When he lowered it, his expression was balanced between rueful and relieved. “In the underground, you told me to hide,” he said. “You don’t even remember the part where you told me to find anyone you know along the way, and help them to disappear too.”

“Oh, I remember that too. Sort of.”

“And I did what you advised.” He straightened slightly. “Twelve priests. All of them were originally sent to the underground dark mage laboratory with me. Their abilities are intact—Relias only sealed mine; it seems he couldn’t be bothered with the rest.”

He turned to Olethros once more. “If what His Highness says is true, you’re lacking healers in your court.”

I looked past him into the trees.

Figures emerged from between the trunks, a dozen in all, in various states of dishevelment. They stood rigid, eyes hardened from what I could only imagine was abandonment and traumatic survival. All of their robes had markings of the General Assembly in Chairo.

“You do realize I’m going back there?” I shouted to the group. “If you come with us, you’ll—”

“No man is a God!” they shouted in angry, slightly disjointed unison. “Free Chairo!”

It was clear they knew Relias had sent them to the lab only to die in his name.

I exhaled slowly and turned to Irijah. “And you. Do you normally take advice from someone you don’t trust?”

“The advice itself was sound.” He paused, meeting my eyes. “I just needed time to accept its source.” He looked at Olethros, then back at me. “Now will you return my staff? If nothing else, it holds sentimental value.”

Olethros handed it over without comment.

After checking in with Galenus and Amos, Chester led me urgently through a mirror to the training yard, where Nora was with a trio of military personnel. Captain Armand of the Order of Blue was the closest, his posture waning from the years pushing down on his shoulders. In his hand was a saber. To my surprise, Ex-Captain Alban of White was next to him, examining his own sword.

Beside them both, with her arms folded and an expression full of opinion, was Jessamy, twin to Captain Sonea of the Order of White.

She gave me a withering glance, yet another saber sheathed at her hip. “You’ve caused a hell of a lot of trouble for us all.”

Behind them, three horses stood saddled and waiting. Aurum was off a bit to the left, ignoring the rest.

Ah. Nora must have gotten them up to speed.

But who was going to give me their backstory?

I coughed. “I would start by explaining—”

“Don’t,” Jessamy interrupted. “I hate speeches. Just tell us your orders.”

I looked at the three of them. Armand seemed content, even patient. Alban was a bit guarded, perhaps merely careful. Jessamy was already moving.

“How are you all this calm?” I asked. “You’re in a demon’s magical bastion, separated from the rest of Speranza by a denounced mage, and you’re—fine with it?”

Jessamy tilted her head. “Would you prefer we weren’t?”

“A little, to be honest.”

“Armand was summoned to Chairo,” she said with a shrug. “Alban and I got out before lockdown. We ran into each other.”

“And?”

“We advised against reporting for duty.”

Armand added, mildly, “I’m not prone to shirking orders, but they convinced me that there’s a time and a place to override protocol. Besides, what use could they have for yet another Captain in Chairo?”

“War’s coming to Chairo,” Jessamy affirmed. “We want to be on this side. Now, orders.”

No questions about me not being dead?

I looked at Aurum, who flicked an ear.

“Mounted saber work,” I said with a grunt. Then I hesitated, trying to keep my explanation neutral. “I need to learn it fast.”

The silence that followed was sharp. Jessamy’s expression didn’t change, but her eyes flickered first to Armand, then to Alban. Armand’s jaw tightened slightly. Alban exhaled, slow and deliberate.

They know I’m asking for a miracle.

Proper training takes time.

Jessamy stared at me for a long moment. Then she gave a single, grim nod. “Right,” she said. “Get on your horse.”

The first thing Jessamy established, once I was on Aurum and she was circling slowly around us, was that everything I knew about fighting on foot would work against me.

“Ground instincts,” she said. “You plant, you brace, you recover. On horseback, however, there’s no recovery. You have to redirect. The swing follows your body, not the other way around.” She stopped in front of Aurum and considered him.

He returned the gaze silently.

“At least he’s well trained.” Jessamy pointed to the training circuit. “Let’s see how you ride first.”

Armand took up a position at the fence rail, quiet and watchful, occasionally offering a single word of advice that seemed deceptively simple. Alban worked the other side, observing how I held the reins in one hand and the angle of my shoulder.

Sitting came first. Then, how to walk. Then, trotting, all without the sword, until Jessamy was satisfied that I wasn’t going to pitch myself off at the first change of pace.

When I complained that I already knew this, she corrected me sharply. My form wasn’t terrible—it just wasn’t good.

Apparently, I slouch?

Will was unsheathed after our third full circuit.

The first swing was wrong. I was too committed, and felt myself leaning too far out over the side of the saddle. Startled by my sudden loss of balance, I jerked back, nearly overcorrecting my slash into his neck.

I nearly overcorrected into his neck.

“I’m so sorry!”

One of his ears swiveled back at me.

“Again,” Jessamy shouted before I could give up entirely.

By the end of the first hour, I had managed three clean swings in succession, although at what remained unclear.

“Not terrible,” she said.

I felt my chest rise. “High praise!”

“It isn’t.” She glanced at Armand. He pushed off the fence rail and walked over, and whatever conversation they’d had before I arrived had been settled.

“The reach problem,” Armand started. “General Ragnerus has a greatsword. On foot, you’re too short to close the distance effectively. On horseback, you gain range, but the gap doesn’t disappear. And he can translocate.”

“And turn to mist,” I said, the small victory of those three clean swings already feeling hollow.

I knew it. This was a stupid idea. But why hadn’t they told me that from the start? Was all this to drive the point home?

Armand coughed. “Conventional cavalry won’t touch him.”

Jessamy and Alban nodded in sober agreement.

“Alright, I get it,” I said with a deliberate scowl. “If you’re all so smart, then give me the answer! How do I fight him and win?!”

“It’s simple,” Jessamy said with a wicked grin, lighting up her aura. “We’re going to teach you how we cheat.”
