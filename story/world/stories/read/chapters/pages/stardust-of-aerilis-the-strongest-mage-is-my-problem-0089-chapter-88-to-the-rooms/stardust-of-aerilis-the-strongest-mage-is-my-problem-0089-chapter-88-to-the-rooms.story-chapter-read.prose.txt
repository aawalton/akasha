The Cinder-Fox set down in Dryadalis just after dawn.

Aiven stepped out onto the landing platform behind Cyria, with Lyra at his shoulder, and for a moment he simply stood.

The platform was white; it was the white of a thing that had been made once, correctly, and had been kept correct ever since. There were no stains. There were no cracks. Where vines had grown along the balustrade, they had grown in even, deliberate spirals, as though someone had spoken to them politely and asked them to do it, and they had agreed.

Beyond the platform the building rose in tiers; tall, spired, its roofs steep, the pale stone catching the morning light and softening it. It was not the fortress of a people who expected to be attacked. It was the residence of a people who did not, at this point in their history, particularly consider the possibility.

Farther out, past the roofs, the forest fell away in green terraces down toward a river that caught the sun in scattered pieces. Waterfalls spilled in thin white ribbons between the cliffs. The whole of it looked less like a kingdom and more like a well-kept idea of one.

He had read about Dryadalis. There had been three chapters on the elven continent in the geography primer he had been made to memorise in his second year of schooling. The elves were reclusive. They did not welcome visitors. They did not particularly acknowledge the existence of other kingdoms unless obliged to. And yet, when the leaders of the world faced questions about the nature of magic, questions about deep enchantment, or old ecology, or the long slow drift of the world's mana, it was to Dryadalis that emissaries were quietly sent.

Aiven had never expected, in any version of his life, to be standing on a Dryadalian landing platform.

His attention was pulled back by movement across the terrace; a group of elven women were approaching. Four of them, in loose formation, all of them tall and pale and slim in the way of their kind. Their hair was silver.

Something moved in his chest, small and unexpected, and looked away before he could stop it.

Silver hair.

He watched them for a moment as they crossed the white stone. It struck him, in a way he had not thought to think about before, that Virelle had been made in the elven register. Her body. Her form. The pale-lavender wash in her hair, the small pointed ears, the fine bones of her face and hands. Cyria had told him, once and in passing, that Lucien had made Virelle's body, but Cyria had not said how much of that shape had been Lucien's decision and how much had been Virelle's.

Had Lucien chosen the elven look for a reason? Had it meant something to him? Or had Virelle, in whatever moment she had first begun to construct herself, reached for the shape she felt most correct in, and if so, why?

The thought spiralled outward almost immediately.

How is she now? Is she eating properly? Has Lucien sent her out to do something already? Has he begun the next phase of his grand plan to reset the world?

The questions had come every hour of every day since he had woken up on the Vulpine bed. They came now, unbidden, on a white terrace in a kingdom he had never expected to see, and they came harder for the silver hair moving toward him across the stone.

Cyria and the elves were discussing about things he could not really understand, something about conservations. His attention had drifted mid-exchange, and by the time it returned, some arrangement had already been reached. Cyria turned to him.

"You two will be escorted to your rooms," she said. "I have to speak with the Elder. About the arrangements. Rest. Eat. Do not wander. Someone will come for you when we have something."

He nodded.

Cyria and two of the elves peeled away and were gone through a side arch before he had finished processing the goodbye. The two remaining elves gestured, politely, toward the tall doors at the far end of the platform.

Aiven glanced at Lyra. She glanced back. They followed.

The hall on the other side of the doors made him stop again.

It was tall enough that the ceiling was in shadow. The floor was pale grey marble, polished to a soft satin gleam. Great columns rose along both sides at even intervals, wound with the same careful vines, and between them, tapestries of soft green and pale silver hung from bronze rods too high to see the top of.

There was no clutter nor dust. There was, Aiven noticed, no sound, apart from the small quiet clicks of their own boots on the marble.

Beside him, Lyra had begun looking at everything at once.

Her eyes moved from the tapestries to the columns to the far archway to the small floating lights that turned out, on second inspection, to be actual tiny lanterns suspended from filaments so fine they were almost invisible. Her mouth was very slightly open.

Then she caught him looking at her. She looked away, quickly, before, as if she had realised the looking-away had been the more obvious motion, she turned back toward him and leaned in.

"Sorry," she whispered. "I was just — a little nervous."

He was fairly sure the elves ahead of them could hear it. The hall was very quiet, and elven hearing was one of more well-known things about them.

"Well," he murmured back, "you're not alone. I've never been to the elven kingdom. I have definitely never been walked through a castle by them."

Lyra huffed a small laugh into her sleeve.

Ahead of them, one of the elves—the taller of the two, whose silver hair fell in a straight sheet nearly to her waist—spoke without turning her head.

"No need to be nervous, dear guests. We do keep to strict rules. And you may perhaps have heard the rumors that we distrust humans." She glanced back over her shoulder, a small courteous smile. "Vulpine has assisted Dryadalis in a great many conservation efforts over the last several decades. Any guests of Lady Amberfang are treated as guests of the kingdom."

She turned forward again. "You may rest easy."

Aiven, for reasons he could not quite justify, felt more nervous than before.

They walked on.

The corridor turned. It opened into a smaller, softer wing, the ceiling here lower, the light here warmer — recessed lanterns tucked into pale niches, casting a honey-coloured glow along the walls. The two elves stopped before a heavy pair of doors set into a small alcove.

"These are the rooms." The taller one gestured. "They are the same. Please pick whichever you prefer. Food has been prepared and set inside. When Lady Amberfang and the Elder have concluded their discussion, we shall come to inform you." She inclined her head to both of them.

The two elves turned and walked away without further ceremony. Their footfalls made almost no sound against the marble. Within twenty paces they had rounded a column and were gone.

Aiven and Lyra were left in the alcove, alone, in front of two identical carved doors, in a wing of a castle built by a race neither of them had ever met. They looked at each other. It was, unexpectedly, very awkward.

"Okay." Aiven cleared his throat. "Um. I think—I think we should rest up."

"Are you sure you're okay?"

"Of course I am. I'm not a kid."

Lyra tilted her head at him. Her voice dropped. "You've been so stressed lately. And—Aiven. I've been thinking. I shouldn't have told you about the—my thing. Not yet. Not on the airship. I could have waited until—"

"You should have told me." Firm. "It's your life, Lyra. It's endangering your life. I have—I still have so many questions to ask you about it. I want to. When we sit down. But you did the right thing, telling me. Please don't take it back."

Lyra hesitated.

"Okay." She let out a soft breath. "Okay. Later. We'll...we'll go through it later. For now, just—" a small gesture toward the door "—rest. I'll come by your room in a little while."

"Right."

"Good."

She paused.

"I wonder what food they have prepared. It's been a while since I've had a proper—"

A soft, unmistakable growl. They both looked down.

It had come from Lyra's stomach, and Aiven kept his face very carefully still.

"I didn't hear anything," he said.

Lyra's ears went faintly pink.

"That's—that's something someone who heard something would say."

"It's not like I haven't heard your stomach growl before."

"Yes, but it's still—" She swatted the air between them. "Whatever, Aiven. I'll see you in a bit."

She turned toward the door on the right.

A small, unbidden smile had settled on her mouth by the time she reached for the handle. She had not planned it. It came, briefly, from a corner of her that had gone quiet for a long time—from the version of them that had run barefoot through the low grass when they had both been nine, and had bickered about who had eaten the last honey-cake, and had made a whole afternoon out of nothing, then from the version of them who had exchanged fun banters for an hour or two before Aiven had to depart for the next delivery. For those few embarrassing seconds she had, without meaning to, felt like they were back there. Before any of it. Before the burdens.

She reached for the handle. She glanced left, and Aiven was still standing outside his own door. Watching her.

She stopped. "What are you doing?"

"Waiting for you to go in."

Her brow furrowed. "That's weird."

"Isn't it the polite thing to do?"

"It's—no. Or maybe. I don't know." She waved at him. "I'm going in now. So you go in too. At the same time. Okay?"

"Will do, ma'am."

She snorted.

They paused, one at each door, in the honey-coloured hush of the alcove. Then, together, they opened the doors and stepped inside.
