Chapter 99

Khal-Vor stood at the forward position and watched the valley, while the cold came up through the soles of his boots.

The light that morning was the wrong colour. It was not the grey of a winter dawn, or the white of high cloud, or anything he knew from the campaigns he had run before. It seemed to come from the valley itself, or from the vortex at the head of the valley. It lay across the snow, the camp, and the men like a film over the eye that could not be blinked away. He had stopped trying to blink it away on the second day. By the eleventh day he no longer saw it as a colour. He saw it as the air.

The valley narrowed at its head into a kind of throat between two cliffs, and at the throat the vortex stood. It was a vertical tear in the air, taller than three men and as wide across the middle as a doorway, the edges of it turning slowly in a way that he had spent more hours watching than he would have admitted. Cold poured from it. Not weather cold. The cold that came off the opening was the wrong kind of cold, the kind that did not warm at fires. Men who slept too close to it did not wake up. He had stopped letting men sleep close to it on the third day.

He counted the army from his position, by habit. The forward line, five hundred at the cliffs on either side. The main body, eight thousand in the bowl of the valley behind him, in companies arrayed by the standard order. The rear, two thousand at the southern mouth of the valley with the supply train. Eleven and a half thousand men, give or take the dead. They had marched in with twelve. The valley had taken five hundred of them in eleven days, mostly to the cold and mostly at night.

He had been told this morning was the day.

He did not know what the day meant. He had been told by Edrin Kavos, who had been told by the Empress, and what the mage had told him was that the army was to be at the cliffs and the bowl and the southern mouth in those exact dispositions by midday, and that he was to send no scouts forward of the forward line, and that he was to wait. Khal-Vor had asked why. Edrin had not answered. Khal-Vor had asked again. Edrin had said the Empress wishes it, in the tone the mage used when he wished the conversation to end, and Khal-Vor had ended it because the Empress did wish it, and he had been a general long enough to know which orders were given to be followed and which were given to be understood.

He did not like the mage. He had never liked the mage. He liked the mage less this morning than he had on any morning previous.

A movement at the edge of his vision pulled his eye. Edrin was crossing the snow on foot, alone, from the camp toward the throat of the valley. He carried a staff Khal-Vor had not seen him carry before. The staff was nearly as tall as he was, iron-shod at the foot, and at the head of it a fist-sized stone was mounted in a setting of dull metal. The stone was the colour of old blood, dark enough at the centre that it absorbed the light of the morning rather than reflecting it. Khal-Vor watched the mage walk the length of the open ground between the forward line and the throat. He did not hurry; he walked like a man who knew exactly what he was going to do.

He walked the way a man walked who knew what he was going to do at the other end of the walk.

Edrin reached the throat and stopped twenty paces from the vortex. He drove the staff into the frozen ground. The shaft went deeper than it should have, the iron foot biting through earth that should have been hard as iron itself, and the staff stood upright when he let go of it. In a dramatic fashion, he raised both hands.

Khal-Vor could not hear what the mage said. He saw Edrin’s lips move and then the cold air at the mage’s hands begin to do something he had not seen air do before. It thickened and gathered. It began to draw toward the stone at the head of the staff in slow streams and as it drew the stone began to brighten.

The light came up through the dark of the stone in pulses. Once, twice, then a third pulse. The stone became brighter, and then the stone was lit from within. A beam of light the colour of pale fire ran from it across the twenty paces of open ground and struck the vortex full on.

The vortex shrank.

Khal-Vor watched it shrink. The vertical tear in the air narrowed by a hand's width, then by another, the edges drawing in toward each other in the same slow rotation they had always held. The pouring cold from it diminished. He could feel it diminish; his face began to ease; and he could breathe a little easier.

He thought, the mage is closing it.

He thought, the army marched four hundred leagues to close a hole in the air.

The vortex shrank by a third before it stopped shrinking.

Then it resisted.

The first sign of the resistance was not visible. It was a sound, combined with a low vibration. A note came out of the vortex that was below the bottom of what Khal-Vor's ear could properly hear, and he felt it in his ribs and in the bones of his face and in the teeth in his head, a slow long deep note that did not belong to any instrument and did not belong to any throat. It was a sound that announced. It was the sound a horn made on the wall of a city when the city saw the army coming. It was the sound of something on the other side of the opening knowing that the opening was being worked.

The note held for a count he did not measure.

Then the shape came into the vortex.

The form came like something from the inside of a window being unclear until the eye from the outside of the window refocused past the glass. Now it was visible. A massive shape in shadow, the edges of it too straight to be a thing of nature, the faces of it too flat. A cube. The cube filled the visible portion of the opening, and the part of it that was visible was a corner, and the corner alone was the size of a fortress. Khal-Vor could see one face running off to the left and another running off to the right and the edge between them running away into the depth of the vortex's perspective, and the edge was straight along every inch of its length.

He had been a general for many years and he had seen siege engines, he had seen towers and he had seen the great wall of the eastern marches as it stood at the end of the empire's first expansion. He had never seen anything that was a cube. The cube was wrong, it was not natural.

Edrin saw the cube the moment it came through the focus of the opening and his breath stopped. He had worked portals for years. He had opened them, closed them, read them, walked through them. Every portal he had ever worked had opened onto somewhere he could place on a map of the world he understood. He had stepped through portals into other kingdoms, into other forests, into the back rooms of houses he had been paid to enter. Every opening he had ever made had led to something familiar. This one led to nothing he had ever even conceived.

The cube was not coming through. It was beyond the opening; at a distance he could not measure because his eye had no scale by which to measure it. It was visible in the way mountains were visible from the coast, the way a city was visible from the next valley over. It was simply present on the other side.

He closed his eyes.

Seeing the cube was taking something from him. He shut his eyes and put his attention back into his hands, and his hands were trembling. The sweat had started across his back and along his forehead in the cold air. He pushed the trembling feeling down. He focused all of himself into the task before him.

The stone at the head of the staff grew darker.

He felt the shift in his hands before he saw it through his eyelids. The light he had been channelling had been the colour of pale fire. The light he was channelling now was a colour for which there was no name in any language he had ever heard. It was darker. It was not the absence of the light, it was an inverted version of it, the same shape but pulling instead of pushing. He fed it everything he had. The stone took it. The stone had been made to take it.

He opened his eyes.

The dark light from the stone was wider than the pale light had been, a band rather than a beam, and it struck the vortex and spread across its surface and began once more to drive the edges of the tear inward. The shrinking resumed. He had it. He had it again. The herald note from the opening was still sounding, low and ancient, but the cube was beginning to blur as the vortex closed around it, the corner growing less distinct, the edges softening into the rotation at the rim. He pushed harder. He gave the stone more than he should have given it. He felt something break in the back of his mind, and he pushed through it because there was nothing else to do.

The vortex shrank to half what it had been.

Then it pulsed.

Khal-Vor saw the pulse before it reached him.

It came out of the vortex in a wave across the snow. He saw the wave the way a man sees a wind move across a field of grain from a distance, the ground itself rippling in a line that travelled outward from the throat of the valley along the flat of the centre. The wave reached the forward line first. The five hundred men at the cliffs went down where they stood. He saw them go. He saw the line of them collapse in the order the wave reached them, the men nearest the throat first, then the men beside them, then the men behind, the falling moving along the line in the same wave-shape as the pulse itself.

The wave kept coming.

He had time to think Edrin. He had time to think the Empress knew. He had time to think one thing more, which was the centre of the valley, and this was where eight thousand of his men stood in their companies, and the wave was already there.

The wave reached him.

The cold inside it was the cold from the opening, but concentrated, the same wrongness that had been pouring out of the vortex for several days condensed into a single layer of air that travelled at the speed of a fast horse and carried with it the silence of the place on the other side of the opening. He felt it strike his face. He felt it strike his chest. He felt his knees go before he had decided to bend them. He felt his hands lose the spear he had been holding. He saw the snow come up toward his face very slowly and he did not feel it when his cheek met the ground.

The last thing he saw before the dark took him was the bowl of the valley.

The companies were going down in their order.

Edrin stayed standing.

He stayed standing because the working held him up. The dark light from the stone had drawn the pulse through itself on its way out of the vortex, and the channel he had cut to push the light into the opening had reversed for the moment of the pulse and become the channel by which the pulse passed back across him, and the stone had taken the cost.

The stone broke.

It did not shatter. It cracked across its centre with a sound like a small dry stick breaking, and the dark light went out, and the staff stood upright with the cracked stone still in its setting and no light at the head of it at all. The vortex closed. Not fully. It closed to the shape it had been before he had begun, the same vertical tear at the throat of the valley, the same slow rotation at its edges, the same cold pouring out of it at the same rate it had poured for several days. The cube was gone. The herald note had stopped.

Edrin dropped to his knees in the snow.

He stayed there for a long time. He did not look behind him. He did not need to. The valley behind him was silent in the way only ground full of bodies was silent, and he had been a mage long enough to know the silence when he heard it. He knelt in the snow with the broken staff in front of him and the cracked stone above his head and breathed in the cold air that no longer poured from the opening because the opening was at rest, and he thought about what he had seen.

A cube the size of a fortress, visible at distance through an opening to nowhere he knew.

He thought, the Empress knew there was something on the other side. She did not know what.

He thought, I will not tell her what I saw.

He thought, I will tell her the working succeeded and the army paid for it, and I will tell her the opening is closed enough that nothing further will come through, and I will keep the rest.

He got to his feet.

He pulled the staff out of the frozen ground. The shaft came free easily, as if the earth was no longer holding it the way it had held it before. He carried the staff with the cracked stone at its head across the snow toward the centre of the valley, and his boots made the only sound in the valley, and the slow rotation at the edge of the vortex continued behind him as it had continued for eleven days.

He found Khal-Vor among the bodies of the forward command.

The general was on his side in the snow with his face turned toward the cliffs. His chest was rising and falling. Edrin crouched beside him and put two fingers against the side of the general's neck and felt the pulse there, slow, even and stubborn. Khal-Vor was not dead. The pulse had knocked him unconscious instead of killing him, like it had done to some of the men in the centre of the valley and all the men in the rear. They would wake up in the cold and discover that thousands of the Clawborn army were gone.

Edrin looked at the general's face for a moment.

"You were not meant to see this," he said, quietly, to the unconscious man.

Then he stood, and he turned, and he walked back across the snow toward the camp, and the cube did not come back into the opening, and the herald note did not sound again, and the valley held the silence.
