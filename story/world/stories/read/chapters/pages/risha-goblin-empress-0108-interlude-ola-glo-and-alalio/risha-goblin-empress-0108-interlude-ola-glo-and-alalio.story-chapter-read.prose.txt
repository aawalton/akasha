Ola looked at the small child, studying the green of her leaves. There were vines in the deep, but none of them looked as vibrant as the small child’s.

“A Lin child.” The Spark’s words brought her mind away from the strangeness of the child.

Ola turned to the Spark curiously, “What’s a L-”

“Do not speak it aloud.”

Ola’s mouth snapped closed, and the two guardians of the child gave her a strange look. Ola withered under their attention, but waited for the Spark to explain before she translated for the others.

“This is clearly a secret Mother Risha wants to keep. For now, she is a simple vine child.”

Ola nodded, and turned to the three of them.

The vine child looked between Ola and the large chained crystal curiously. The Shade only had eyes for Ola, his eyes narrowed at her slip up. The Kobold, thankfully, seemed to be distracted with observing the come and go of the Under Goblins that traveled through the tunnels.

The four of them sat on stone chairs with spidersilk cushions, arranged in a semi-circle facing the chained crystal heart of Koliekan.

“Speak for me, Ola, to the child. What did the Mother want her to learn from me?” Ola repeated the words of the Spark, then waited with her hands folded in her lap for the child to respond.

“The Sapling who teaches me taught me about the history of the Lin. The splintering of the God of Life, and the awakening of Mother Earth. He told me of the power that I wield, and that I have more value than others.”

“A Sapling? What other poisons did the old servant place in your mind, little one?” Ola glanced at the Crystal heart, but repeated the question anyway.

To Ola’s surprise, the young girl considered the question for a long time “He didn’t like Talo or Leaf-Watcher. He told me that they were made to be servants, not family.”

“I see. And did he guide you to connect with the earth?” There was a tenseness there that worried Ola.

“Not yet. He wanted to start next week.”

Relief flooded from the spark, palpable even to the guardians of the girl. “Good. Do not let him interfere with that process. It is a sacred one. Do you believe his words about your friends, little one?”

Aliya shifted in her seat, “No, but… they are different. They don’t have the connection.”

“A wise observation, but that does not make them lesser. Their connection is simply different, and I would not underestimate the power of the old servants-” The Spark paused. “Do not use that word, Ola; servants. It is an old ideology that does your people a disservice. You are Monsters, and the earth itself has recognized your people as part of the Family.”

At the Spark’s request, Ola did not share that last part as she translated for the girl.

Aliya smiled wide, “I thought so. The way he talked about everyone, I didn’t like it.”

“There are ancient reasons for the Sapling’s fervor, but that does not excuse it. His people are blinded, and their blindness will only bring ruin to the family you have here. It is true that you will need guidance to connect with the earth. It must be someone you trust with not only your life, but your very spirit. Mother Risha may be capable, but I can think of few others.”

“What about you, Koliekan?”

“I am honored, but you should not trust so blindly. I am not deserving.” The crystal radiated a sadness that made Ola’s heart drop into her stomach. “Instead, I will tell you more about the power that has been granted to you through your ancestors. The power of Creation. Could one of your guardians send for one of the plants you keep close to you?”

The two guardians shared a glance, then Leaf-Watcher stood, and motioned to one of her pack that rested some distance away in the large cavern.

Aliya hopped up and down in her seat, “Are you going to teach me magic?”

“Of a sort. You have the power to change the world around you, I will simply show you how to guide it.”

Glo stood at the stern of the boat, feeling as it shifted with the waves. Beside him, an Ancient blue-skinned Goblin General stood. He looked over the waves fondly.

“You spent your life on these waters?” Glo asked, swaying with the movement of the boat.

“I fought against the great one called Hwahwei.” The Ancient Goblin Spirit said, his expression frowning. “When she betrayed us to fight with the Sparks.” He turned to look at one of the unevolved blue goblins who hurried about the rigging of the boat, his small body allowing him to climb up and down easily. “Our people payed for her betrayal.”

“Why did you answer my call, General?” Glo asked, turning his gaze to the endless horizon of the ocean. His heart twisted with fear every time he looked upon it, but he crushed the feeling and lifted his chin with forced confidence.

“A favor for the First Servant. The wars of the ocean are different from the wars of land. If you are going to face the monstrosities of the watery deep, and the gods beyond, then you will need my knowledge.” The Goblin paused, then looked at Glo with hesitation, “I have heard tales of her. Is she as great as they say?”

“She is undefinable. You can feel her spirit through mine, can’t you?” Glo asked.

“Yes.” The Ancient Blue Goblin’s strong expression fell away to be covered with sadness, “I did not think it was possible. The price we payed for our war was so great, I did not think we would ever recover.” He took a deep breath, then smiled, his teeth shining with silver and gold. “The first thing you must know about the sea, is that it is not only the monsters you must fear, but the very sky itself.”

Diel, first of Risha’s Deathbringers, and servant of the Spider Empress, stared at the elf as he ordered another drink from the bar. The Elf looked into his drink like it held answers to the many troubles on his shoulders.

The door to the mostly empty tavern opened, and Diel shifted in his armor, his hand on the handle of his blade.

Two Dark Elves walked to a nearby table, and ordered something.

“You look so grim, Orc.” The Elf’s voice brought Diel’s attention back to him.

“I take my duties seriously, Tender.” Diel responded, bowing his head slightly.

“Hmm.” The Elf tilted his latest drink back, then placed it gently on the counter. “I’ve never worked with an orc before. I’ve only seen you slaughter. I didn’t think you’d be so… studious.”

Diel held the Elf’s gaze for a long moment, then snorted, “When all you know is war, honor becomes more than a simple word.”

“And so well spoken too. You’re full of surprises.”

“You wish to offend me.” Diel smiled, “A puny elf’s insults are nothing when I spend my days surrounded by goblins.”

“You find goblins insulting?” The Elf smiled, as if he’d discovered something big.

Diel laughed, the nearby conversation of the monsters in the tavern quieting. “Incredibly. They like it that way.”

The Elf’s smile disappeared as something deeper entered his eyes. “I see.” He pushed his chair back and stood, then stumbled slightly. “To think, orc drink would affect me so.” He shook his head, then walked towards the door.

Diel fell into step behind him, sensing as two presences stood from their own table. He did not look at them as they left the building.

Two more followed from a nearby store. Elder Kulos’s caped goblin was still there on a nearby roof, giving him some comfort.

“How does it feel, to follow a goblin?” The Elf asked.

Diel stopped.

The Elf walked a few extra steps, then stopped in confusion as he looked back at Diel. Diel just stared at the Elf.  The Tender frowned in confusion, then stepped closer to Diel.

Diel moved, grabbing the Elf’s shirt and pulling him close. Diel’s red eyes glared into the Elf’s green eyes as he held him close. “I do not care if you insult me, Elf. But any insult to the Mother is one I will pay back twelvefold. Do you understand?”

The Elf’s eyes held no intoxication, clear as day. He tilted his head, studying Diel, then simply nodded his head. “I understand and apologize. I wouldn’t suffer any insult to the Ancient Mother.”

Diel let the Elf go, then stepped forward, “Good. Then your life is worth saving.”

The Elf blinked in surprise, then followed the orc as they walked through the streets. “What do you mean by that?”

Diel stopped in the middle of the road, drew his sword, and cut through a passing figure in a single fluid movement.

A dagger fell to the ground as the Dark Elf dropped to the floor.

Energy flowed from the dead Dark Elf into the Deathbringer, filling him with power as three more figures ran at them.

Diel smiled wide as his sword met another. Diel twisted past the strike, and stabbed forward through the opponent’s heart, as his master had taught him.

He kicked the dead Dark Elf off of his sword and into the third, then moved his head slightly as an arrow flew past him and into the fourth’s neck.

The third managed to push the body off of him, but Diel was there, his sword pressing against the skin of the third’s throat.

“We have some questions for you.” Diel growled, the power of those he killed further fueling him as the specter of death raised its scythe behind him.

The Dark Elf pulled a knife, and stabbed it into his own chest before Diel could stop him.

Diel frowned as he pulled his sword back. He glanced over to the shocked Elf, confirming that nothing had reached him.

“Ah. That’s what you meant.” The Tender frowned as he looked at the orc. “Would you have let them kill me if I hadn’t apologized?”

Diel shrugged as he wiped his sword on the robes of the dead Dark Elf. “We’ll never know.”

The Elf gave a dark smile. “I think I’m going to like you.”

Diel paused, and looked at the Elf with narrowed eyes.

The Elf stepped past Diel to look over the dead Dark Elf. “My name is Alalio, not Elf.”

“And mine is Diel, not Orc.”

The Elf reached down to a dropped dagger, studying the purple liquid that dripped from its edge. “I will remember that, Diel.”
