After the earth broke and devastated the city of Liaojiangkou, allowing mortals to run uncontrolled, the authority of the Liao Clan may have been called into question. How much power could they truly claim to hold if the city that bore their name was in such disarray? Their Patriarch had gone into closed-door Cultivation and even this devastation had not been enough to rouse him. Did they still hold the right to rule through the strength of their Cultivation?

Today, they would remind the mortals of that power.

The great banners of the Liao Clan were raised high, bluer than the sky that had been stained a sickly yellow for days now. The kingfisher flew proudly at the head of the convoy, carried aloft by flagbearers on horseback.

At the western gate of Liaojiangkou, a convoy of hundreds had been assembled to accompany Lord Liao Weilong on his journey to Western Reaches. There, the Lord Liao meant to confiscate every tael of silver and gold. Not that anyone beyond Lord Weilong’s newly instated deputy and the frazzled scribe from the Magistrate’s office knew. All that the soldiers and servants knew was that their service had been requested and that they would fulfil their duty to the clan that owed their fealty or be hung for treason.

Hua rode near the front, behind the flagbearers, in a bubble of space flanked on one side by Weiji and the other by Captain Yao. Behind them were orderly rows of cavalry, dozens of horses plodding along, resplendent in the armour. The captain had been a requirement from her grandmother. They’d worked together technically on both trips to the Magistrate, but Hua knew nothing of importance about him.

She had also expected to ride with Liu Xin, but he’d claimed a fear of horses as an excuse to ride in the caravan shielded from the elements. It had stopped Weiji’s complaints and so she’d allowed the lie.

It was a great convoy, one worthy of this new saga the Lord Weilong would tell for his clan, and it was stuck in the mud for the second time that day.

Freezing cold rains had swept through during the first hour. It was almost sleet with how hard it pelted Hua’s conical hat. Without the veil and the thick winter coat, she would be miserable. As it was, Hua was only extremely annoyed.

“This is ridiculous,” Weiji said, shouting to be heard over the rain. They had been forced to divert off the road as part of it had sunk into the earth. They and the others on horseback were waiting for the baggage train dragged by the donkeys to make it around. “It’s going to take us all of winter just to get to Coaltown at this rate.”

“Do we have enough supplies to last us that long?” she called back.

“Not at all.” She glared at him. He brought up his gloved hands that were soaked through in apology. “There was no reasonable expectation or method to carry six months' worth of supplies with us. I expected us to buy what fell short after a month or two.”

“I’m letting the soldiers eat you first if we run out of food.”

They had expected the roads to be in bad shape. A few sections swallowed whole, perhaps. This was a nightmare. Thus far, they had seen deep waters where the river overflowed and waterlogged the lands. Deep pockets of earth that were functionally lakes. So much of the roadside, usually furrowed into dirt paths and kept free of plants by grazing animals, had been churned to mud by what few travellers had braved the path these last few days. Where the roads hadn’t been churned to mud, they were forced to take long detours around deep pockets of earth. And they couldn’t go fast with the thick mist that had rolled through. Sometimes, Hua could glimpse the golden edges of a wheat field though just as often it was darkened by burnt fields.

“Start with the imperials, they look particularly plump, and there are very many of them,” he said with a grim chuckle, directing his horse onward as the last cart made it over the ditch. Hua’s followed along placidly. “Or maybe that attendant of yours.”

In the middle of the convoy was the Imperial contingent. A dozen Imperial soldiers, led by a Captain Ciao who had been tasked to protect the Magistrate’s scribe who was coming in an official capacity. Hua remembered him as he was a fellow Cultivator and not a mortal. She still hadn’t bothered to learn the scribe’s name. It surprised her that they had the forces to allow a Cultivator in Qi Gathering to guard a relatively minor functionary.

The Magistrate himself was joining Grandmother on the journey to Seamouth, a much larger city that had its own Magistrate who would bow to the change in power or, he’d be accused of some malpractice, killed, and then his second would do the job of signing the paperwork to seize all the resources, silver, and gold the Liao Clan desired.

The Magistrate will also be accompanied by Cultivators, she thought, raising her arm to shield against a hard gust of wind. He was due such a right, but Hua would have truly believed that Captain Ciao was their highest-ranked soldier. I underestimated the resources the Dragon Throne placed in the Amber Sea.

“I’ll let Liu Xin get the first bite of you. Make sure your spirit can’t pass on.” She parted her veil just so he could see her sharp grin. The sleet striking her face was worth his shudder. “I’ll even send your corpse north to the Jurchen tribes.”

“My Lord truly is a spiteful lord. How I must have been a monster in my past lives to deserve your cruelty. If you ever send me to the Jurchens, I will find a way to haunt you. If you ever make me work with more than that barely civilised savage of yours, I will renounce my oaths.”

“Then you had best make sure we have supplies or grow so far beyond me that I can’t do anything about it.”

“Dealing with supplies it is. That’s achievable, even if I have to hunt the animals of the West to extinction.”

“Weiji.” She said his name chidingly. “If you give up now, you’ll never have a chance to surpass me.”

“Good. I’m not trying to compete with you. I’ll leave being a prodigy to you and live a nice, quiet life where the most exciting thing I’ll do is organise your trips. A nice, peaceful life. I’ll have gardens and music and poetry and none of the Jurchens you pick up on a whim.”

“If you impress me, I’ll reward you with an estate.”

“And what’s the poison you’ve added to this tea?”

“It’ll be to the far north. At the border.”

North was the border with the Jurchen tribes. The land there was often contested. It was probably cheap. Maybe she could get Weiji a full castle overlooking the border. He’d have gardens and music and poetry and all the time in the world to deal with Jurchens.

“My Lord, this threat doesn’t work for the simple reason that you don’t know how to purchase a plot of land.”

“Yes, I do,” she said, diverting her horse around a dead ox. It had two broken legs and if the mud trail was telling the full story, it had dragged itself before succumbing in the road. Free meat. It would be added to one of the carts and prepared on the move. The mist was so thick she nearly lost Weiji in that short time as he had taken his horse the other side.

When they rejoined each other, Weiji was already speaking.

“The process of buying land involves mortals. Many of them, in varying configurations, all requiring verification from one mortal to another. You might find a Cultivator in the web, but that would likely be the major landowner of the region. But he wouldn’t notarise, stamp, or seal the paperwork. So, if my Lord required a plot of land be purchased, my Lord would speak to this lowly Weiji, and this Weiji has no interest in buying land in the desolate north populated by savages.”

“I could ask one of our many cousins.”

“Go ahead, I wish you luck. See if anything gets done within a decade without me around.”

She shot him a look hidden by a veil and sleet. The resentment was new. “How did the elders take you dropping their work in favour of mine?”

“I made sure to complete it or leave trustworthy cousins to handle it. Harder than you would imagine to find cousins with any skill in logistics who won’t run away the moment someone approaches them. Suppose that’s why I get saddled with it. I couldn’t just abandon my work. It would wake me in the middle of the night with worry.”

“Did your grandfather take no issue with me stealing you away?”

“It was always going to be me you chose. If not me, then maybe one of the Qi Generation Elders. But after your… the way you exercised your authority against the elders, it was unlikely. Grandfather told me to expect your summons and not to shame the clan.”

A grandparent with high expectations. They had more in common than Hua thought. This journey was a test on her grandmother’s part. An opportunity to let her exercise power with no supervision. She’d either succeed and cement the name Liao Weilong as a worthy lord, or she would fail, and they’d all pretend it had never happened. It was a low-stakes journey unlike the trip to Seagate out east. The worst that could possibly happen was killing some merchants, maybe a few angry miners revolting. They’d be lucky if they even got a bandit or two.

She left it at that, and they continued through the rain as the day faded. If not for her gloves, her thick cloak, and the Qi circulating through her limbs, Hua would be freezing. She made use of her frustration by helping move a set of stone statues that had fallen over, the gods depicted as they deserved. There was a stretch of relatively stable road that lasted them nearly five li, one that had Hua optimistic.

That was until one of the horses fell into an abrupt sinkhole.

It had been a frenzied few minutes dealing with that problem. Startled yelps, the horse loud neigh before the sound of a snap and a scream moments later. In the mist, it had caused confusion and unrest. The soldiers showed great discipline, she was proud to say, and the horses were well trained. She soothed hers easily and Weiji’s only trotted a few steps away from the direction of screaming, butting into another horse just behind. That had almost set off more chaos if not for Captain Yao’s firm orders that carried over the din.

In the end, the horse would be fine, if with a sprain. The flagbearer had broken his collarbone in the fall. He was loaded onto a cart at the back for a healer to look at. Hua was finally getting her wish of fewer flagbearers.

“Weiji and I will ride ahead,” she said. “My eyes are better. Take a twenty-minute break before you resume the journey.”

“Yes, lord.”

Using her cousin as a message relay was a novel idea. She could see problem areas and send him back to warn them. By the fifth such instance, he was riding a new horse, claiming his old when could do with a bit of rest.

The rain lightened after another hour. Sleet that stung became a mist that sprayed. It unfortunately deepened the feeling of the mist. Hua could only see the closest extents of the farming fields that gave way to forests. And where there were trees growing, there would inevitably be trees falling.

They moved the first tree in the road easily between them. It was a moderately young pine, only the length of five men. Weiji kicked up a fuss about the mud. Hua kicked a fuss of mud at him. Just enough to splatter the hem of his travelling robe. It was too hard an impulse to resist. Reminded her of being children.

He was sulking when they reached the second fallen tree and made her do the work. Hua spent the boring moments moving a tiny cypress by suggesting a series of increasingly elaborate estates for Weiji in increasable obscure locations with an ever-expanding range of hostilities that would be present. Weiji offered a devastating counter by asking her to name the Land Registry Office, a place she only knew existed because Weiji spent half an hour talking about it and where it existed in the Imperial bureaucracy. Hua concluded that it wasn’t a real place because no one should have to suffer the tedium of so many different offices just to buy a piece of land.

After the third collapsed tree, she was reaching fury. When she spotted the fourth in the distance, she leapt off her horse and made her way there on foot at a fast run. The great pine tree was scorched black, filled with so much yang Qi from a lightning strike she almost expected it to become a jiangshi of some sort.

With a groan, a shout, and a heave, she threw the old pine clear of the road. It landed with a grave thud, rocking the muddy road, and splintering along the seams that sudden heat expansion had created.

“We’ll wait for the convoy,” she growled, glaring past the stupid tree and to the sky. It was getting late.

It took half an hour for the captain to emerge from the mist.

“Captain Yao, what’s our pace looking like to you?”

“Slower than I am comfortable with. If the next three days don’t prove themselves favourable, we will have to consider turning back.”

“There are villages and towns on the way, no?”

“Yes, but I don’t know how highly I rate their chances to have come out of this chaos unscathed. We aren’t seeing enough travellers be it fleeing destruction or regular traffic.”

“We’ll pluck that kingfisher when we get to it. How much riding can we get in before we set camp?”

“Another hour is the most I would counsel.”

“So be it. An hour’s ride and then camp. I’ll leave the organisation to you. Oh, and Captain Yao, make certain that the maps are being updated. I want a full list of areas of damage by the time we reach the town and a summary every evening.” Captain Yao watched her carefully but said nothing. “Is there an issue?”

“No, my Lord.”

“Then get to it,” she ordered, before leading her horse forward.

“Do you think he dislikes me?” Hua asked Weiji once they had put some distance between them.

Weiji gave her a deeply disquieted look. “Hua, please don’t use such a thin excuse to kill the commander of your army, no matter how small it is.”

“I don’t want to kill him,” she shot back. “He just seemed very displeased.”

“Because you asked him to do cartography work. That’s not his job in the slightest.”

“I didn’t expect him to personally do it.”

“How is he supposed to know? You have a reputation for a short temper that’s only gotten worse.”

“Lord Weilong doesn’t have a reputation yet. That’s half the reason for this trip.”

“You are very wrong. You are the Golden-Eyed Lord who purified the dead. That’s what I hear the vassals calling you. That’s not even considering your reputation from before. You’re known for beating your kin so badly we develop some colour. They call us ghosts until you get your hands on us.”

“That’s just training.”

“And the times you rampaged through the city because someone insulted you and you destroyed a building or two?”

“I’ve only ever destroyed three buildings in the city.”

“All the dojos you have broken over your knee?”

“They were occupying the three buildings I destroyed,” she pointed out. “Valid targets if you ask me.”

“You named yourself after a dragon but you were already trampling through the city like one. Just accept that people will think you are capable of biting their heads off from now on. I don’t get why you’re so upset. You don’t even like mortals.”

“I don’t like handling the random mortals. But the people in our clan are different. They wear our colours. They don’t need to be afraid of them.”

“You say as if you didn’t threaten the Elders and then make a display of your power. Face it, not a single one of our vassals or mortal servants will ever stop being afraid of you.”

“Most of my household servants aren’t afraid of me,” she said petulantly.

“How little time do you spend at home?”

Weiji was wrong. They had to like her. She was sure of it. “They were never afraid of my father.”

“Our Patriarch spent the last few decades without conflicts. Most people don’t remember his power. They rarely saw how easily he wielded lightning. He was close to a god, but he never felt the need to show it. You never stop reminding people how far you’ve advanced in so short a time.”

“Don’t call him that. He was never as fickle as a god.”

“But he was that fearsome. No one would ever wage war against the heavens, and no one would wage war against him. That your father’s reputation is enough to stop war breaking out. Our vassals might not fear him but to our rivals, his legacy is written in the blood of their forebearers.”

And now that legacy would fall to Hua.

“I’ll surpass it,” she promised, gazing up at the starless sky where her enemies hid. “And I’ll kill any gods in my way.”

Nothing less could be accepted.

“That’s what I’m afraid of.”
