The Maharani cried like someone who had no practice doing it.

Her face barely moved. Her shoulders stayed straight. Her hand, however, clutched Shayura's back with enough force that the girl made a small sound into her mother's dress.

Shayura did not pull away.

She cried harder instead.

I stood above the broken wetlands and let the Third Flower dim.

It did not vanish completely, doing that would be pretty difficult like holding my breath for too long. I was a Transcendent being now, and this was part of my life. So threads still ran through the battlefield in thin lines, but they no longer pressed into my mind with full force. If I kept it fully open much longer, I had a feeling my head would stop knowing which thoughts belonged to me.

Avyaan's devotion, Bagha's rage, Shayura's hurt, the Maharani's cracked emptiness… all of it had passed through me inside Event Horizon, and I wasn't entirely sure where any of it had gone.

Transcendence had not made me immune to consequences. It only made the consequences arrive in shapes I could survive.

Probably.

Below me, nobody else moved.

Bagha Hamid stood with one arm missing, his body held together by stubbornness and the last smoke of Bonbibi's power. He was breathing heavily, shocked as he stared at the crying Maharani and her daughter.

Dhumravarna hovered behind him, four arms bloodied and third eye half-closed. Jaya leaned on a broken spear. Maricha, who’d fallen unconscious earlier, was awake again and had one knee planted on empty air, and her illusion-cloak kept failing in little strips around his shoulders.

The Deva-Sena survivors watched their queen hug her youngest daughter.

Avyaan watched too.

He tried to look away once. He tried and he failed. The thread between him and the Maharani trembled in a way it had not before. In a very simple, familial way.

Bagha stared at the scene, expression strange, filled with rage and whatnot. And yet, the same man let out a loud laugh.

The sound came out rough and wet. It made him cough blood, which made him laugh harder for half a second before he had to bend forward and brace his remaining hand on Kal-Danda.

Nobody joined him.

He wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist and stared at the Maharani. "Well," he said, voice scraping, "I suppose that counts as a miracle."

The Maharani did not answer.

Shayura's fingers tightened in her mother's dress.

Suddenly the demigods around me flinched, looking around. Only the Maharani and I were unbothered. For she was the reason they flinched, and I’d already sensed it.

Threads across the continent started moving too fast, too many at once, like a thousand rooms had opened their doors at the same time. If I hadn’t noticed it earlier, I might have thought that it was the Third Flower acting up.

Everyone had noticed. That the Lucent Air was gone.

Not just over Vanga.

The Crown of Emptiness, and the Sage of Thousand Eyes wearing it, didn’t bother to hide what she was feeling. So I got a glimpse of everything too from the threads. Farther west, a tax clerk in Suryavansh threw down his ink brush and started screaming at his superior about thirty years of stolen wages. In a fishing village near Nagadipa's coast, a woman kissed the man she had been pretending not to love since childhood and then slapped him because apparently the emotion arrived in a confusing order. In Amravati, a masked official removed his silver face covering, stared at his own hands, and began laughing so hard his servants backed away.

In another house, two brothers started fighting over a dead father's inheritance that had been settled twenty years ago.

In a temple, a priest of Luminas started crying during morning recitation and couldn't explain why.

The continent had not become free in some clean and holy way. It had become loud.

The Maharani felt it the best.

She’d chosen to let the Lucent Air fall, but that didn’t mean her Crown of Emptiness was malfunctioning. It caught the returning emotional noise from billions of people, and her head turned slightly as if she had heard a door break somewhere far away.

Bagha stopped laughing. He slowed down, one breath at a time, before his expression grew strange again.

This was what he had wanted. It was also bigger than what he had wanted. Wanting a dam broken and watching the flood hit villages were two different experiences.

"Dhumravarna."

At Bagha’s call, the Rakshasa straightened despite the blood running down his side.

"Go east. Any lord using this as excuse for revenge gets one warning. After that, break the hand holding the weapon."

Dhumravarna bared his teeth. "Only the hand?"

"Today, yes."

The Rakshasa looked disappointed.

"Jaya."

The woman lifted her head.

"Take the shrine roads. The priests will panic first, given the confusion. Keep them from declaring the end of the world until tomorrow."

Jaya gave a tired laugh. "They'll hate that, you know?"

Bagha ignored that. Maricha looked up before Bagha called him. "You know what to do, Maricha?"

"Dreams and messengers, yes," Maricha said. "I will put the same lie in every noble's head." Bagha's eyes narrowed. She smiled through blood. "Fine. The same warning."

"Better."

They moved without waiting.

The Maharani watched them go. Her eyes followed Dhumravarna first, then Jaya, then Maricha as she peeled herself into a dozen false bodies and sent them scattering across the sky.

For the first time since I had met her, she looked at Bagha Hamid like he was more than a beast with a club. Bagha noticed. He straightened as much as his ruined body allowed.

"You see?" He nodded toward the continent beyond the broken trees. "They were never peaceful. They were sedated. I’ve been trying to make you understand all this time, but…"

The Maharani's tear-streaked face turned toward him. "And what if they tear themselves apart after this?"

Bagha's mouth twitched. He almost smiled, but the pain stopped it halfway. "It isn't a what-if. Some will."

Shayura turned her head from her mother's chest.

Bagha looked at the girl, then back at the Maharani.

"That is also life."

The Maharani's fingers tightened again on Shayura's back. She seemed surprised by the answer but not pleased. "So that is your answer. To simply let the weak bleed because pain is honest?"

"Don't put your court words in my mouth, Maharani.”

"Then speak better."

Bagha laughed once. "I am missing an arm. Give me some slack."

"It looks to me that your tongue survived just fine?" That line made half the battlefield look between them like children watching parents argue over a burning kitchen.

I cut in before they rebuilt the war from the bones of the last one.

"Enough."

Both of them looked at me.

Two rulers, one crowned and one savage, turned to me. I cleared my throat. That was not the tone I intended to use outside my Worldforge. I had no love for death. Even so, a few hours ago I had been a useful nuisance with an absurd Class. Now they had to include me in the shape of the room.

I did not let myself enjoy it. That kind of enjoyment got people killed.

"I just want to say… The Stillness is gone, and you, Maharani, will have a ton of ideas and arguments over whether that was a good choice or not. And if you should bring it back or not," I said. "If my voice counts, I just want you to give the continent time to breathe before you try to fix it again."

The Maharani's eyes sharpened.

"You are negotiating with me?"

"...Yes?" that confused me a little. “Can’t I?”

Bagha looked delighted. I continued before either of them could make it worse. "Bagha has been governing Vanga without the Stillness for a while, so I think your people should observe Bagha’s people work. Learn how Bagha’s people stop villages from burning each other. Basically, his people will stop the first wave of riots before they become massacres and you guys learn. In the meantime, just don't clamp the Stillness back down tonight because you're scared of noise."

The Maharani's voice cooled. "You speak as if you have authority here, Heavenly Demon. Isn’t it already a reward to let you breathe?"

"It is. But I also have leverage. Today, that is close enough."

The Maharani parted her lips to speak, but then Shayura pulled back slightly and looked up at her mother. The Maharani looked down at her. Then she sighed.

The Maharani turned to me. "How long?" she asked.

"Thirty days," I said.

Bagha frowned. "Seven will be enough.”

I looked at him. "You sure? You just got everything you claimed you wanted, and your first instinct is to underbid your own rebellion? Give yourself some extra time man, you might not get the chance later."

"My people can stabilize faster than your flying city can pour tea."

“Okay, that part’s not true at all.”

The Maharani's gaze moved between us. She looked tired now, which was strange on her face. "Fourteen days," she said.

Bagha shrugged. I rubbed at my brow. The Third Flower had dimmed, but the pressure behind my eyes remained.

"Twenty-one," I said. "Nobody likes it, so it is probably fair."

Bagha grunted. “I don’t mind.”

The Maharani looked at Shayura again.

"Twenty-one days," she agreed. "Observation only."

Bagha's ears twitched. "Observation from where?"

"From my castle."

"No."

The word came from Shayura.

The Maharani stilled. Shayura wiped at her cheeks with both hands, then looked from Bagha to her mother and finally to me. She looked exhausted. She also looked like a princess for the first time today.

"Not Amravati," she said. "If you observe from there, every report becomes a mirror of what you already believe."

The Maharani did not answer.

Shayura swallowed.

"Use Nagadipa. Sathari's family can host the neutral table. Mother, if you want me and everyone else to trust you, then do this somewhere we are not trapped under your sky."

To be fair, every sky occupied by an Arcane King is a trapped sky. But oh well, what does she know? I held back from making a comment. The Maharani's expression barely changed, but the thread between them pulled tight.

"Very well," she said.

Bagha snorted. "The snake king will love this."

"He will survive the honor," the Maharani said.

Bagha laughed again, harder this time, and almost fell out of the sky because of his injuries. Bagha coughed and wiped more blood from his chin.

"Ah shit. Honestly, if I knew making you cry would stop the war," he said, "I would have kidnapped two daughters."

Nobody moved. Shayura gasped and Avyaan closed his eyes like a man hoping death might show mercy and take him right now.

The Deva-Sena survivors looked ready to attack on principle, but none of them had enough intact bones for it.

The Maharani stared at Bagha. Her face gave nothing away. The Crown above her brow flickered once, as if trying to decide whether this counted as insult, tactical provocation, criminal confession, or attempted humor.

Then she laughed.

It was a small sound, but compared to her dry laughter before, this felt genuine. It was not performed at all. It did not fit her face at all, and the shock of hearing it struck the battlefield harder than some techniques.

The Maharani looked faintly confused by the sound. Shayura stared at her mother with fresh tears gathering.

Avyaan opened his eyes. Bagha's grin softened into something he would deny later.

I exhaled through my nose.

The continent continued waking.

The sound did not literally reach us from every city and village, but the threads carried enough. Lovers confessed. Old grudges surfaced. Children cried without knowing why their parents cried too. Soldiers laughed in barracks where laughter had been treated like a defect. Somewhere in Amravati, a magistrate screamed into a cushion for ten full seconds and then returned to work with shaking hands.

This was not salvation.

This was a withdrawal symptom on a continental scale.

I looked at the blue sky and felt the size of the problem settle over us. Bagha had won more than he expected. Shayura had reached her mother. And me? I had forced a god-queen to cry and somehow lived.

In reality, we had just woken a continent that had forgotten how to feel without hurting itself.

But that sounded like victory if a drunk bard told it. I chose to believe that so far.

The Maharani looked up at the sky. "So this is what disorder sounds like," she said. "It has been a long time."

"No," I said. "This is what real life sounds like."

She lowered her gaze to me. The wetness remained on her cheeks. The Crown of Emptiness still sat above her brow, but it no longer looked quite as clean.

"Say, Iskandaar Romani."

“...What?” I did not like that tone.

"You’re quite young but… Have you thought about becoming a father yet?"

"...?"
