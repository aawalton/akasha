The brewery was already in motion by the time Alric stepped fully into it. Six boilers stood in their two triangle arrangements, their copper catching the light in uneven bands where heat had begun to leave its mark, the newer ones to the left still holding onto a degree of shine that suggested newness. Currently only five were lit, each one breathing a steady warmth into the room while the sixth remained dark, clean, and waiting.

He paused just inside the threshold, not because anything had gone wrong, but because nothing had, and that in itself had become something worth noting. The rhythm held. The staff moved without hesitation, each task flowing into the next with the sort of quiet certainty that had taken far longer to establish than it had any right to, and for a moment he allowed himself to simply observe it, tracking the movement of a barrel being guided into place while another was being sealed, already considering what would come next without needing to ask. It was the kind of order that looked simple from a distance.

Moreen had made an appointment a few days before, to meet at the brewery and he was bringing a friend. Alric turned, noticing a purple carriage making its way over the cobbles toward the brewery, the colour alone announcing that whoever was inside did not concern themselves with being overlooked. It was being pulled by one of the large six legged crocodilian things Alric had learnt were called swamp dragons, which moved with the slow, deliberate confidence of something that knew it would be considered the debate champion anywhere. The creature had a rider as was typical of them, guiding it with reins.

Swamp dragons, he had learnt, were kept by nobles as they lived on a strictly meat diet, had to be raised from egg, and their eggs were highly prized, which meant that the creature in front of him had likely consumed more coin in its lifetime than most men would ever see.

Alric watched as the carriage pulled up, the rider on the swamp dragon giving him a small nod, just enough acknowledgement to mark him as neither important or in the way. Before the door could be opened, Alric noticed the scent, sharp and clean in a way that did not belong to the brewery at all, cutting straight through the layered familiarity of wort and yeast and the faint lingering sourness that. It was a fresh scent, specifically of grapes, and it arrived with the quiet certainty of something that did not need to ask permission to be noticed.

The door was opened by an attendant who Alric hadn’t noticed before, which in itself suggested a level of competence that was difficult to fault. He placed a small stand by the doorway, opened it, and stepped back with the sort of practiced invisibility that implied he had been doing this long enough to know that success was measured by not being remembered. Moreen stepped out without ceremony, though not without presence, moving over to Alric with a brief shake of his head before turning toward the carriage and offering a bow. The shift in the room was subtle but complete; hands paused for just a fraction longer than necessary, posture straightened, and one by one the staff inclined their heads in a manner that was far more practiced than anything he himself had ever managed.

Moreen’s bow struck Alric as strange, not because it was incorrect, which he had no authority to judge, but because it appeared to require a degree of coordination that suggested prior rehearsal. One foot forward, the front foot on its toes, knee slightly bent, torso twisted at an angle that seemed to imply both respect and mild discomfort, one arm down, the other placed carefully at the small of his back.

Alric blinked, unsure if he should copy it. Seren had told him there was no standard and bowing was as individual as people made it, which had sounded helpful at the time and considerably less so now. He opted for a stage bow, shifting his left leg back, keeping his back straight, tilting to a forty-five degree angle, his left hand over his chest while his right hand gestured toward the door in what he hoped was a reasonable approximation of intent. He kept his gaze lowered as a well dressed man stepped out of the carriage and approached, aware that whatever he had just done was now done and could not be improved upon without making it worse.

The man who stepped down from the carriage did not rely on colour to announce himself in the way most merchants did, though there was no shortage of it in what he wore, each layer arranged with deliberate contrast rather than excess, deep navy blue set against lighter blues. His coat sat over a fitted underlayer of softer fabric, the cut precise enough to suggest it had been made for him rather than acquired. His hair was black, kept short and slicked back with some kind of oil that caught the light just enough to be noticed without appearing wet, and a neatly trimmed goatee framed his mouth in a way that made his expressions seem more intentional than they might otherwise have been. As he approached, Alric became aware of something else, something out of place in a brewery that had long since given up competing with itself for scent; the Baron smelled… good, not strongly so, but clean and composed, like someone who had decided that even the air around him should behave properly.

“Well,” the man said, glancing briefly toward Moreen before turning his attention to Alric, “that is a new one.”

“A pleasure, I’m from very far away,” Alric said, keeping his gaze lowered.

“I’m Baron Izewald, no need to stand on formalities. You’re the brewer, yes?” he said, his mouth pulling into a grin that settled somewhere between amusement and approval, his gaze lingering on Alric with the sort of interest that suggested he had not quite expected what he was seeing but had decided, in the space of a moment, that he did not dislike it.

“I am called that, most call me Alric,” he said, straightening. Behind the Baron, two attendants were approaching, carrying between them what Alric considered a packaging crate. It looked deeply out of place with its simplicity.

It was the sort of container that might just as easily have held tools or samples or anything else that required transport without ceremony, yet the scent that followed it made its contents unmistakable long before the lid was lifted. It was not simply that it was grapes, though it was that, but that they carried with them something else as well, something that spoke of timing more than quality.

Alric straightened, resisting the urge to look around for confirmation that he had not, in fact, done something deeply incorrect. He realized Seren would probably admonish him later anyway, which at least provided consistency. “I find it… may I see them?” he asked, gesturing to the box.

The Baron’s grin widened slightly. “Of course, you bought them all already,” he said, with an easy certainty that carried no weight of command behind it.

Moreen, who had yet to say anything at all, allowed himself the smallest shift of expression, something that might have been agreement or might simply have been acknowledgement that the conversation had begun and was proceeding according to a plan he had not felt the need to share.

The box was opened without announcement.

Inside, arranged with a care that suggested they had been selected rather than gathered, lay grapes, the box was full with seven different varieties. Alric assumed because some colors were a bit different. Their skins were taut and unblemished, the deep colour of them catching what light the room allowed and holding it. Alric moved to the box, reached for one, turning it lightly between his fingers, feeling the resistance beneath the surface, the firmness that spoke of timing more than ripeness, and for a moment the noise of the brewery fell away.

“These will be great,” he said, not looking up as he spoke. The bunch in his hand were white grapes but there were reds and deep purple within the box, each one carrying its own set of possibilities and problems in equal measure.

The Baron watched him, not the grapes.

“And how,” he asked after a moment, his voice carrying easily over the steady work of the room, “do you intend to turn them into something worth drinking? Moreen says you make the best beer in the region. Do you know some trick I don’t?”

“Do you want to try the beers?” Alric asked, and the Baron shook his head slightly. Alric kept his face neutral, though it took a small amount of effort, as he would have liked a noble’s opinion if only to confirm that taste did not improve with title. “Well, come through, I’ll show you the fruit press,” he said, gesturing toward the doors.

Seren had appeared from somewhere, as she tended to do, holding a proper bow and gesturing toward the back yard where the press waited, her execution of the movement making it abundantly clear that whatever Alric had done earlier did not qualify.

Alric set the grape back into the box, wiping his fingers absently against his palm as he turned, following after the Baron, who was currently being led by Seren with the quiet efficiency of someone who understood both the path and the expectations attached to it.

They entered the back yard, which had become a utility space over time rather than by design, benches, tables, and a few fenced vegetable patches occupying the space in a manner that suggested practicality had won several arguments. The press stood closer to the doors, solid and functional, its construction more practical than impressive, the wood worn in places where hands had found purchase again and again.

“We press it,” Alric began, moving past the Baron to the press, though he continued without pause, describing the process as he walked them through it, how pulling on the bar put pressure on what was inside, how that pressure translated into yield, and where the limits began to make themselves known.

The Baron followed, listening, though it became increasingly clear that he was not listening for the process itself but for something within it. He nodded along.

“Unless…” he said eventually, glancing once more at the box, “is there some trick to this press? Can it handle something in the region of forty wagon loads to follow?”

The number did not land loudly. It settled instead, the way numbers tended to when they represented work rather than theory, finding its place in the minds of those who heard it and refusing to move.

Alric blinked rapidly.

Somewhere behind them, a worker slowed just enough to hear it properly, another glanced up, and though the motion of the brewery did not stop, it shifted, the awareness of scale settling over it in a way that could not easily be undone, like a weight that had always been there but had only just been acknowledged.

Alric did not respond immediately.

Instead, he looked to Moreen.

The merchant was no longer watching the exchange, his attention seemingly fixed on the sky, though the slight curve at the edge of his mouth suggested that he was aware of the effect all the same and had no intention of interfering with it, which, in its own way, was a very specific kind of involvement.

Understanding, when it came, but settled into place.

“We will run the press,” Alric said at last, turning back, his tone even in a way that concealed more than it revealed, “as hard as it will allow, we will find a way to make it work.”

Alric internally winced. Summer hadn’t quite broken. If all the grapes arrived at once, they would not be able to process them fast enough.

The Baron studied him for a moment longer, then gave a small nod.

“In that case,” he said, his tone shifting almost imperceptibly, “I would like to secure two barrels for my own use, when the time comes.”

“That can be arranged,” Alric replied without hesitation, “I did want to ask though, do you maybe have older wine barrels?”

The Baron laughed.

It was not unkind, but it was immediate.

“My ancestors used those,” he said, the amusement in his voice carrying no room for negotiation. “I am not in the habit of selling them, but I’m glad you see the worth of seasoned barrels. Your wine should be interesting.”

Alric inclined his head slightly, though the motion carried more acceptance than agreement, and as the conversation moved on, the shape of the problem settled into place.

By the time they left, the brewery had returned to its rhythm, though it was no longer quite the same as it had been before, the awareness of what was coming sitting just beneath the surface of every movement.

He found Vale not long after, the man already halfway through something that looked like a list and felt like a calculation, though he stopped as Alric approached, setting it aside with a glance that asked the question before it was spoken.

“Why,” Alric began, the words arriving already shaped, “why weren’t we told?”

Vale did not answer immediately.

“Moreen didn’t tell us because he didn’t want to, simply,” he said eventually, choosing his words with the same care he applied to everything else, though it did little to soften the underlying reality. “Moreen secured a vineyard. Entirely, from what I can gather. This was not arranged recently. The only comfort is the wagons can’t arrive all at once, they cannot harvest that fast.”

Alric nodded once, slowly, the pieces aligning in a way that made the earlier conversation feel less like a surprise and more like the final step in something that had been moving for some time.

“We will need more barrels,” he said, the statement simple enough to carry the full weight of what it implied without needing to expand upon it.

Vale’s expression tightened, just slightly.

“Urgently,” Alric added, because there was no value in pretending otherwise.

Outside, the air had begun to shift in a way that was difficult to notice unless one was looking for it, the heat of the day no longer settling as deeply into the stone as it had before, the nights beginning to pull it back just a little faster than they had any right to. Inside the brewery, the pressure eased almost without anyone marking the moment it happened, the space becoming more bearable by degrees so small they might have been imagined.

It did not feel like relief.

It felt like the beginning of something else.
