Once the two saboteurs have been taken off to be confined, I insist that Lathani and Kalanthia go separately with Hunter and River to interview everyone else on the ship. They’re armed with a small number of questions, designed to bring to mind any intentions or inclinations towards sabotage.

Elarion wasn’t keen on the idea, but given recent events, he doesn’t have a leg to stand on. I do assure him that I won’t punish anyone just for their thoughts. Though I have companions capable of mind-reading, I don’t intend to police what remains theoretical.

It will, however, give me an idea of who to keep an eye on both here and on the Lost Continent. Actually, I should do this fleet-wide, I muse. Even if the other lords have less animosity towards House Titanbend than Elarion does, individuals might have had bad experiences with my family. Or, they might be spies for our enemies, Torrent in particular. While we’re on the ships is the perfect time to weed all of those out – there’s nowhere to run.

Though, I need to remember that Kalanthia and Lathani aren’t infallible and remain cautious even after. They can’t hear what someone doesn’t think on their surface. Or rather, Kalanthia could but shouldn’t because of time, effort, and the damage that’s left behind. River and Hunter are asking pointed questions that should bring any treacherous thoughts to mind, but it’s quite possible they’ll miss something. Hopefully the stunning combination of a humanoid crocodilian asking blunt questions about their loyalties while a nunda looms over them will be enough to weed out the majority of the troublemakers.

While they do that, I return back to my ship and call a meeting of the lords and captains. Sarran brings along the second mate of our ship who, due to the death of Captain Tolly and the disappearance, probable death of the first mate during the battle with the roc, has become captain by default.

“Sabotage?” Valance gasps with his eyes wide. “Who would be stupid enough to challenge you like that?”

I send him a flat look.

“Not everyone has had a change of heart in an arena,” I remind him. He colours slightly and his eyes drop to train themselves on the table. “And I’m glad you did,” I follow it up with, feeling like I’ve misstepped in reminding him of what our relationship used to be like. I blame my fatigue and general soreness.

I’d have liked to sleep before this meeting, or at least have some time to soothe some of the strain to my internal matrix. But, as always, there are more urgent things to do than simply recover. Namely, get a status report from everyone and then, if we’re able to, set sail again, even if at reduced speed to account for everyone whose ships are still not fully repaired – my own in particular.

“What will you do to them?” Layton asks, a troubled look in his eyes. He could be troubled by the idea of sabotage, or its potential consequences.

“Once I have discovered if there have been any other saboteurs, we will have a public trial. You will know then,” I reply firmly.

“Is that wise, Lord Markus?” Josiah asks a little tentatively. “Revealing the sabotage might hearten other potential malcontents.”

“I doubt it will do anything that won’t already happen – it’s not like this incident happened in secrecy,” I point out. Gossip has a curious ability to leap even separating stretches of water. “Better, to my mind, that the consequences of it are well-known too,” I point out. “And that leads me to my next point.”

I continue onto my firm ‘request’ that my team of investigators are allowed access to everyone on all the ships. This, as expected, meets with more than a little discontent and polite attempts to refuse, but when I start making pointed statements about whether they have anything to hide, and that I will be requiring everyone on my own ship to submit to the same questioning, most of the attempted refusals fall away, though I can tell that Layton and Captain Sor’az are definitely not happy about it.

In the case of Captain Sor’az, he tries to pull the ‘we’re from a different country’ card, but I counter with a pointed reminder of the previous consequences to him and his crew of being treated differently. I continue pointing out that if he wants himself and his crew to be treated like Moriaxans, he needs to accept these security measures like everyone else too. In the end he accepts it with a grumble, insisting that no humans except for me come to his ship. Since the investigative team is made out of two nundas and two samurans, that’s something I can easily agree to.

Elarion remains a mostly silent presence in the meeting, his face blank but worry in his eyes. Even when we move onto discussing the state of the ships, he only contributes what’s absolutely necessary. I wonder, is he more concerned about what might happen to the two saboteurs, or what their actions might mean for how much I trust him going forward?

Overall, we’ve come out of the encounters with the Tier five far better than we had any right to expect. We still have all seven ships – mostly intact – and the two fighters who fell out of the rowboat were the only casualties. It’s ironic that the battle with the lower level beast was so much more detrimental to us. Then again, the Tier five wasn’t actually targeting us – it would have been a very different story otherwise.

And even with that lack of directed interest, we’ve taken damage we really can’t afford.

As well as my ship’s main anchor chain being broken, Valence’s ship is worse off than it was a couple of hours ago – when the Beast Emperor lost patience and destroyed the barrier, his ship was lagging behind like mine was and ended up being rather battered by some chunks of ice that fell from the icebergs. Fortunately, since there wasn’t any sabotage there, Josiah was able to pull him out of the mess.

Our Mages are still almost entirely depleted and will require a long, restorative sleep before they’ll be good for anything – and yes, I’m including myself in that category. My ship is still at less than half sailing capacity, and across the fleet we’ve lost between ten and twenty percent of our personnel in the last couple of days to either death or injury, the losses disproportionately affecting the fighters and sailors – both vital groups for when we land on the Lost Continent.

I’m not the only one concerned about that.

“What if we reach the Lost Continent successfully, but then don’t have enough people to actually settle land there?” Josiah asks, a worried crease between his brows. He lost the greatest percentage of fighters in the battle with the roc – almost twenty percent of his force.

Glancing around the table, I see the same question asked on several faces. Elarion’s notably, is blank, his eyes trained on the table. He’s definitely troubled, but not necessarily about this subject.

“Even with our reduced numbers, I think we’ll do fine. But we do have an option for replacing some of those lost,” I point out. That earns me several confused glances. “The slaves I know you all brought with you.”

That is met by uproar, voices entwining as several speak at once.

“—not fighters, they’re labourers—”

“—kill us in our beds—”

“—why should they want to help?”

I hold up my hand and silence reluctantly falls.

“Let me ask you this question – what are you really afraid of? That the slaves are so violent by nature that they will go on a murderous rampage at the first opportunity, or that they hate you all enough that they will choose to attack you even if their own survival depends on yours?”

The silence becomes uncomfortable. Layton and Josiah exchange glances; Valence avoids my eyes. Captain Sor’az, however, eyes me with a burning, far-away gaze. I know this topic is a difficult one for him in particular.

“I’m not suggesting we just shove weapons at all of them and say ‘have at it’. But I’m saying that we shouldn’t discount what they can offer. Look at Captain Garous’ ship,” I emphasise, gesturing at the captain who is in the room but still doesn’t dare actually approach the table. He ducks his head uncomfortably as attention is brought on him. I feel bad for that, but I need to make this point.

“They haven’t sailed off into the open sea. They haven’t attacked us. Heck, they had the chance to sever the connection between their ship and Elarion’s while pulling us out of there; they didn’t. Captain Garous, would you mind explaining why you didn’t?”

I lightly touch the Bond between us and feel his intention to dissemble before his mouth opens.

“And do please be honest,” I preempt him casually. “There will be no consequences for your words, I promise.”

That gives him pause and he sends an uncertain look at me. I flicker reassurance down the Bond and feel more than see his resolve firm.

“I…It’s better like this,” he admits. “On our own…we know we couldn’ get far.” He waves vaguely at the wall of the cabin. “Yon beastie made that more’n plain. An’...we’re bein’ paid. More’n some have ever earned, even ‘fore the collar. Mood is te see how it goes.”

“And what if you decide that you can gain more by murdering and stealing than working for pay?” Layton asks sharply. Captain Garous shuffles his feet and looks down.

“Well, me lord…I couldn’ rightly say,” he murmurs.

Layton makes a scoffing sound and then looks at me, crossing his arms over his chest.

“I can answer that question well enough – we’ll be murdered in our beds, with barely enough time to regret giving convicted criminals such freedom.”

“I recognise your concern, Layton,” I reply calmly. “But please remember that they were not all sentenced for the same crime. I don’t know about you, but the majority of the contracts I hold are for non-repayment of debt. I would also point out that the outcome you fear would be more likely to occur if we don’t give them a path by which they can better their conditions.”

“That might be giving them more credit than they merit,” warns Elarion evenly, his shuttered gaze not allowing any of his true feelings through.

I look around and see several others are still unconvinced too. I decide to leave them with a single thought.

“Just consider this: I haven’t had issues with the slaves I brought with me and they're all training either for a trade or as fighters.” Even Thalon’s been keeping his head down since he tried to bully another slave and ended up being put on his back by an unexpected rebuttal – and was then docked pay to add insult to injury. “The slaves I took from Torrent and his fleet are also all working – as sailors, fighters, and crafters. You’ve heard from Captain Garous that none of them are plotting rebellion. It’s working out for me – why not you too?”

I let that linger a moment longer, then change the subject.

“Now, the last two encounters have indicated some issues with our tactics. I’d like to propose that, once the ships are repaired, we work on our group battle strategy. Do any of you object?”

Predictably, no one does. I’d have been worried if they did – it would have indicated an unwillingness to improve.

“Good. Now, what did you notice didn’t work well and what did?”

The ensuing conversation is awkward and stilted at first, but over time everyone relaxes into it. Even Elarion contributes a couple of comments about how his own tactics combined – or didn’t – with those of others.

“Communication was challenging,” Layton notes, with an apologetic look at me. “The Bonded you left on each ship helped, but it was still heavily delayed. We did not know what was happening with the roc until it fell into the ocean with you riding on its back.

“Seconded,” Josiah agrees. “That you had previously disappeared from view after your masts were knocked over was also more than a little worrying.”

I nod thoughtfully.

“I understand the issue.” I hesitate, looking around the faces of my allies. Can I trust them with this? I wonder. And then I consider the reverse: what if I don’t trust them with this?
