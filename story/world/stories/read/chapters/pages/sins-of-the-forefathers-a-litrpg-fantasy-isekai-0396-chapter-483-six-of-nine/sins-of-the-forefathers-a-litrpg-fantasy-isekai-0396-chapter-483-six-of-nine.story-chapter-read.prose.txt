The important thing, I dimly realized, was that all of the nine spires around the lake hadn’t collapsed. Not all of them, at least.

Just most of them.

Somehow, the collapse of the initial tower across the lake had started a chain reaction where six of the spires had either collapsed in on themselves or into each other. Like a line of dominoes arrayed by a child, one after the other, they spelled doom for the next. Some of them looked like they had outright collapsed inwards, crumbling from a central point deep within themselves. Some, still, almost looked like they had exploded, flinging rocks every which way to ricochet out over the entirety of Karag Korvok. And the rest appeared to have snapped off somewhere along their lengths, and listed to the side like the very first one. That, I think, was what had started the entire chain of events.

How? It made no sense. This was, literally, nonsensical. Some of the towers that had collapsed weren’t even close to each other. It was like they had spontaneously self-destructed when another one did-

I paused, there in the doorway of the flooded tower we had only just barely survived, as a curious whisper from Middle reached me.

Surely not.

I cast out a new gaze at the circle of spires, the lake upon which they were crowded, and the central one in the middle, from which the great Serpent Calamity that I suspected was Nehushtan was suspended.

And stopped thinking like a military commander for one moment, and started to think like the Magi Grey had trained me to be.

“This entire place…” I whispered aloud. “Is a ritual.”

“Nathan?” I heard Sylvia ask in a confused tone next to me. I only spared her the briefest of distracted smiles before I stepped out of the doorway and onto the stairs. I needed a better look at everything around us as the realization dawned on me. I had seen things like this before. Hell, I’d even been taught things like this. I ignored the near knee-high pool of blood that had spilled out onto the shores of the lake and waded into it.

As I did, I lifted an arm and started pointing out individual components. I was barely aware enough to realize that Sylvia, Walter, Renauld, and, curiously enough, Meia had joined me in my wading.

“Focusing agents…” I mumbled, pointing a finger at several of the different collapsed spires. The runic carvings I had spotted upon the needle-tipped towers took on a new meaning. Instead of mad scribbles that, frankly, I still couldn’t translate, they became another dialect of runes.

Something with purpose.

“A sympathetic reagent…” This time, I gestured toward the blood itself before shifting my aim up to the serpent. “A power source. It was even arranged in a thaumaturgically significant symbol. The spiral designated a pathway that Aether could flow, and the heart was the spigot. And then…”

Finally, my pointing figure reached the central tower.

“The destination point. The target of the ritual,” I shook my head at the scale of it all, honestly overwhelmed. “Even fucking shape of the mountain itself is part of the ritual, isn’t it?”

I wasn’t expecting an answer. At that point, I was reeling from the realization of what this place was. However, I had forgotten I wasn’t the only trained ritualist among us.

“The walls of the cavern aren’t just carved,” Meia mused from behind me. “They’re polished and shaped. Any Aether that escaped the focusing of the ritual would be directed back into the central spire. Honestly, this is…impressive, for Veredanians. I’m not sure even the Court would have been this audacious.”

My head snapped over to stare at Meia Itzelan as my own unvoiced thoughts were aired. The Half-Orc woman met my gaze calmly and evenly, even as the rest of my companions joined me in staring at her.

That’s right. The Orcs were notorious for their Blood and Sacrificial Magic. They incorporated it into nearly every facet of their Mysticism. It…actually made perfect sense that she might have recognized what was happening here. The ‘Court’ she was referring to must have been the Court of Iron and Blood, which, from what I understand, was the personal attendants of the Orcish God-Empress, she that was the indisputed ruler of Orc kind.

Lyneia, the Goddess of Prosperity.

A frown grew on my lips as I glared at Meia. “When did you realize?”

The Half-Orc wasn’t fazed by my irritation. She just shrugged at me. “Roughly at about the time we were entering the cathedral. Honestly, Hart. This isn’t my specialty, but I am familiar enough with blood Rituals to recognize the feel of one.”

“Why didn’t you say anything?!” I nearly screamed at her. “You know as well as I do the danger of stepping into an unknown ritual!”

“Because we were busy!” Meia finally snapped back. “Did you expect me to just interrupt that flayed monster as it pontificated at us?! Maybe spill its own secret to its face and taunt it with the knowledge? Use your brain, Hart! We were locked in from the moment we stepped into that hall. No, more than that. We were locked in the moment we started this fight.”

I seethed impotently for a moment, as I realized she was right. It was just frustrating, damnit.

“Nathan, what’s the problem?” Sylvia asked, a calculating look on her face as she looked at Meia.

I recognized that look, and while I was frustrated with Meia, I didn’t exactly want her to get knifed. I also didn’t like the way my fellow Abjurist jutted her chin out in challenge at the Sculpted woman.

I forced myself to calm down and raised a hand to stop Sylvia. “No, it’s…fine. She’s right, and we can’t afford to start infighting now,” I heaved a heavy, frustrated sigh. “This just explains a lot. We just need to figure out what to do next. Meia, do you have any ideas on what might have caused the initial collapse?”

That was the one thing in this entire theory I didn’t understand. The first tower had fallen only minutes after we had killed Rhogann.

The Half-Orc woman furrowed her brow and tapped her fingers along her staff, uncaring how they stuck to the wood from all the blood. “If I had to guess…” She said slowly. “There was some kind of feedback in the first tower. Something that caused it to collapse. But if you consider the scale of this ritual, and the amount of Aether that must have been flowing through it, the moment one of the towers touched another, that was what caused the entire cascade. It’s like…a lightning rod, for lack of a better term, touching another when they’re charged. A massive blowback would have occurred through the entire system, and I believe it caused the destruction of four of the other spires. And then…” She gestured out at the tableau of destruction arrayed before us.

Our entire group gazed out at the mostly destroyed lake. In the distance, a hunk of stone from one of the more half-destroyed towers tumbled into the lake. It was large enough that it caused a wave to erupt on the surface of the blood lake and travel far enough to lap at our knees.

Renauld was the one to break the silence. “Did we fuck this up?”

I winced at his bluntness, but…

“We don’t know if this was something we did,” I said weakly. I winced at the looks I got from everyone else, but continued. “No, really. I know that we were kind of…assaulting them, but this entire setup is rife for volatility. I don’t know how long they’ve been doing this-”

(“With that much blood?” Renauld muttered, staring at the lake.

“Probably centuries, judging by the volume,” Meia whispered back thoughtfully.

“How can you know that?!” )

“-anything involving blood in Mysticism is delicate,” I continued, ignoring the peanut gallery. “Rituals and sacrifices are far more likely to blow up in your face if you mess up or your concentration slips for an instant. It’s not just a moral reason that they’re heavily discouraged in modern academia. They’re dangerous, and you need an entirely specialized discipline of Magic and Cultivation, with its own specific guardrails, if you want to take advantage of it.”

Which is what the Orcish Dominion had. There was a great deal of power you could eke out of such an approach. Likely more than what traditional Veredenese methods of Mysticism could manage, judging by the relative strengths between our two worlds. But in my opinion, it just wasn’t worth it. As much as I loathed the messes that the Netherim had left for us, their approach to advancement was strictly superior in my mind.

And part of my plans for Vereden going into the future, through the use of the Archive left to me by Travers, locked up in a vault in Herztal.

All of that only mattered if we could prevent the fucking apocalypse that was the Skyfall in time, though.

“Enough of this,” Sylvia cut in irritably. “This isn’t the time. There are other matters that demand our attention.”

I took the rebuke in the spirit it was intended, because she was right. Academic questions about how and why this entire ritual space had collapsed in on itself could wait till later.

Right now, we had a campaign to continue, and our soldiers were literally waiting for us to stop deliberating on the step behind us. No doubt they had been listening to every word.

I nodded sharply. “You’re right. Sylvia, I want you to take ten of our best scouts and try to figure out what happened to our other groups. Stop in with Rafael and Gustave along the way, make sure they’re fine, and then I want you to find Bleddyn or Olag. Preferably both, if it can be helped. Make sure they’re fine, and assist them as needed. In the meantime, we’re going to continue with what we set out to do. This, at least, is one of the last remaining spires. Presumably, it has a Rendarch inside.” My gaze shifted over Fade, waiting patiently on the step just above the blood line. He cocked his head at my regard. “Fade told me he can extract some information from one of these guys, and that’s just what we’re going to do.”

Expressions all around me firmed, even those on the faces of my soldiers who definitely weren’t listening in. Meanwhile, Sylvia searched my face for a moment intently before nodding firmly. She reached down and grasped my hand for a moment and squeezed. “I believe in you, Nathan. You can handle them.”

I couldn’t stop a smile from rolling over my face at her words. I returned the squeeze, in lieu of the kiss I wanted to give her.

As much as I loved Sylvia, we were both covered in blood from head to toe. Including our lips.

“I do,” I said, with a confidence that wasn’t entirely feigned. “I have their measure now. Now, get out of here, will you? For all we know, Bleddyn is drowning in a pool six inches deep.”

A smile ghosted over Sylvia’s lips, and she disappeared up the steps. She knew exactly who to go for, considering her entire personal squad had mostly been scouts in the first place. In moments, my love and her squad had dashed away, leaving a trail of distorted blood in her wake.

Meanwhile, I took a deep breath, turned, and nodded at my own soldiers. “Let’s go! Don’t pretend you weren’t eavesdropping! We’re going in!”

My soldiers hurried to obey my orders, and in moments, the doorway had been cleared. Before I could follow after them, I was stopped by the feeling of a broad hand clamping down on my shoulder.

I turned, and found that it was Meia with a troubled frown on her lips. “One thing, Hart,” She said with a furrowed brow, shooting an almost paranoid look over her shoulder at the tower. “You were wrong about one thing earlier.”

I gave a quick nod to the waiting Renauld, and he vanished into the spire, leaving only Fade, Meia, and me out here. “What was I wrong about?”

“The central tower isn’t the target of the ritual,” Meia said bluntly, causing my eyebrows to shoot up. “Rather…it’s just another focus like the other ones. As far as I can tell, the target of the ritual is something below it that the spire is directing everything toward.”

“Below it?” I murmured in confusion. I paused. “You mean, below the lake?”

Both of us gazed out at the central spire, upon which a limp Calamity almost appeared to be deceased, impaled through the heart as it was. Only the occasional pumping of its heart was enough to indicate life was still in one of the people I had come to rescue.

Presumably.

I couldn’t help but think that the tower was even more ominous, now that I knew there was something beneath it.

I shook that off. This wasn’t the time for dread. “C’mon, Itzelan. We can worry about that later.”

“We have work to do.”
