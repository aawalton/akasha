“How can you be so blind!” Thaman fumed, pacing around the courtyard of Camar Gadin’s royal palace. “You, who have been granted a second life? Who worships the Akh Nara so!”

“I understand the Akh Nara’s sacrifice better than most, father,” Roshan replied. “Certainly better than you. I understand that he sits alone even now, fighting his own war to prevent the realm from coming apart.”

“Then, why?” Thaman roared. “Why spit in the face of all that he has worked so hard to achieve? Why go to war?”

“Because demons from every clan clamor for revenge, and at least by joining the fight, I can ensure that the worst does not come to pass.”

“The worst,” Thaman spat. “I can scarcely think of anything worse than total war between our peoples. Now, of all times, when the realm has scarcely recovered from the blight of the Ash Tears and the surprise of Reunification.”

“As terrible as war is, father, it is not even close to the worst outcome. Not for the Akh Nara. That would be what Jagath wants. Not total war, but total annihilation of humanity, or barring that, their subjugation at our hands.”

Thaman stopped pacing. “Jagath had indicated no desire for this,” he said.

“And yet, it is what he craves in his heart of hearts. To erase the smear of his loss against the Akh Nara years ago by annihilating the people our god holds dear.”

“All the more reason to cease this madness,” Thaman said. “Don’t you understand? You, Jagath, and everyone who goes to war plays into their hands! Our enemy is not humanity! It is the foe that controls the very threads of Fate itself.”

“See, father, that is where you are mistaken. Siya has informed me about them. However, I am no longer part of their grand design. I am Samsara.”

Thaman scoffed, throwing his hands up. “To think you know better than gods. Your resurrection has changed you more than I’d feared.”

“It has changed me, this I cannot deny,” Roshan replied. “Who could go through such a thing and emerge unscathed?”

“That is… I did not mean it that way, Roshan. Just that you could be aiding Maiya instead. Putting a stop to this war in the first place.”

“I’m afraid Jagath will ensure that war happens no matter what any of us say or do,” Roshan said with a sad smile. “All I and the Samsara can hope is to reign him in once his bloodlust has been satiated. To ensure humanity remains as intact as possible by the time the Akh Nara returns. But as strong as the Samsara are, we number merely one-hundred and eight. Having the Bairan army would go a long way toward ensuring we succeed.”

But Thaman shook his head. “No. I will not command our clan to participate in this madness.”

“You cannot stop them,” Roshan replied. “If you issue an order to stand down when all the other clans are mobilizing, you’ll have a mutiny on your hands.”

“You think I do not know this, boy? I’ve ruled this clan for centuries. I know my people. I will issue no such order. I will, however, publicly decry this war. I will plead with every Bairan to respect the Akh Nara’s actions until now. I will ask them to question whether he would approve of t his madness.”

“Do what you must,” Roshan said as he turned to leave. “Droves of Bairans will volunteer regardless.”

Thaman watched his son’s back as he left the courtyard. It was no lie that he knew his people better than any. It was why he feared that his son, young and brash though he might be, was right.

Maiya made sure she schooled her expression into a mask of calm confidence, striding atop the parapet in front of the fifty thousand soldiers. It was exactly the act they needed to see, and they they stood a little straighter as she passed, their legs shaking just a bit less.

Not a single one saw the same terror that hid behind her expression.

Terror that far exceeded their own. Having never once fought demons, they thought they held every advantage, but Maiya knew better.

The wall was truly a marvel of human engineering. Eighty paces of thick stone, built in just months, it spanned the width of the recently dubbed Kanthaghati pass, where the valley narrowed to just three hundred paces across. Every art of war of humanity had, built into a stronghold that ought to be untakeable.

While the Ashlands were still being charted out, it had quickly become clear that a vast and near-continuous range of jagged peaks ran north-south, as if dividing what had been the human realm from what lay in the middle now.

Given that similar geography existed on the demonic side, Maiya was all but sure Vir had designed it this way. He’d seen war in the realm’s future after all. Maybe he was trying to give humans an advantage.

Even so, he must have known it would hardly have been enough. She herself had known that well before the first stone of the wall was laid.

To call it a gateway to every human city was no exaggeration. If it broke, nothing lay between twenty thousand demons and all of humanity’s cities.

Some, like Sonam, would fare quite well, she thought. The city had been built first and foremost as a fortress to reign above all fortresses. But what of those who tended the fields outside? What of the countless towns that had no walls at all?

Maiya didn’t have to imagine too hard. As much as she loved demons, she was acutely aware of their thirst for battle. With two living gods on their side, the result would be nothing short of pure devastation.

She stopped to look down at the tents arrayed below, stretching off to the horizon in neat rows behind the wall.

The closest banners belonged to Matali, with Riyan's Saians and the small Ranian contingent behind them. In what could only be seen as a show of power, the Altani had chosen to fly in semi-permanent structures with their skyships, and so their encampment had no tents at all. Their two-story buildings lined the side of the valley, white-and-blue flags adorning every surface they could stick them on.

The other half camped a quarter mile back with twenty thousand Balarian Warriors and an additional five thousand Balarian Guard. Behind them were two thousand Asura.

Her people, and among the few who still answered to her and not Tia. Above it all, her Vimana loomed high in the gray sky like a guardian, Asura skyships circling its hull.

Four months. Four, long months since she had last heard his voice. Forget meeting him, he’d ceased communicating altogether, and she didn’t blame him one bit.

Not one morsel of his attention could be spared on her, or anyone else for that matter. At least she knew he was well. Siya ensured Maiya stayed apprised of his current situation, if not his exact whereabouts, not that it would have done Maiya much good.

The two had traveled more in these months than in all the years prior, seeking out Janak's dead laboratories, looking for a way to stop the realm from ending.

It was more than simple heartache and loneliness that bothered Maiya, though there was plenty of that too. No, there was another, far more real fear beneath her feelings. One she’d told no one.

The farther she drifted from Vir, both in distance and spirit, the more she drifted into their sphere of influence.

Nothing she felt, of course—the Fateweavers did not work in such crude ways. Just that her actions had begun to feel futile. That her efforts didn’t matter, or worse—actively worked against her goals.

Was it them? Or was it simply that Maiya was one person whose influence upon the world had simply waned as of late?

“You built too many stairs.”

The words brought Maiya out of her thoughts, and she blinked to find Ira standing at the top of the stairwell, red in the cheeks and absolutely winded, the quilted Kin'jal coat making her look like some young girl rather than the ruler of the most powerful country in the human lands.

“You do realize there is a hoist on the south tower, yes? For ferrying people and cargo? To say nothing of the airships you have at your disposal?”

“It's… bad for morale,” she huffed with a small pout. “Watching me arrive in a rubble bucket or an airship. I might as well declare to the world I’m too frail to even climb a few stairs.”

Maiya very much wanted to tell her that she’d done exactly that by huffing and puffing her way up here, but knew when to keep her mouth shut. After her training at the periphery of the Ash, she was not nearly as fragile as she’d once been, and she felt much of it had to do with Ira’s mind, rather than her body. Regardless, she welcomed the sight of her friend. One of the few she had these days.

And besides, Ira would never have even dreamed of climbing stairs as she’d been before her training in the Ash’s periphery. She’d come far, even if she still lagged behind most others.

“It is good to see you, Ira,” she said instead, embracing her friend, who squeezed her back.

“You as well,” Ira replied with a small smile. “I know we keep saying this, but we really ought to sit down and catch up. Once all this,” she gestured to the tents, “is done with.”

“I’d like that,” Maiya replied, returning her smile, fully aware that they both knew that wasn’t about to happen anytime soon.

“When it begins,” Ira said, looking down at the impressive camp, “I'll go up to the ship. I'm no use down here. But first you should know I've spoken with every general I’ve sway over. My elite Balarian Guard will remain at the rear until forced to engage. We will not be the aggressors here."

"Thank you," she breathed in relief. As much as Maiya wanted to ask Ira to have her entire army stand down, she knew it was an unreasonable ask. The sort of order that could very well get Ira ousted from her throne, despite her popularity. She was not a military commander, and everyone knew it. She must have burned quite the goodwill dictating strategy to her generals even as it was.

Maiya only hoped having the realm’s best vanguards relegated to the rear would be enough to convince Tia and the others to stop this madness before it began.

Futile though it might’ve been… she had to at least try.

The long table was ringed by every major commander the human realms had left. Due to their relative individual weakness, the Ash Tears had hit humans harder than demons, and several high ranking officers had been lost along the way.

Tia sat at the head in her brigandine armor, mud still on her boots, looking every bit the warrior princess she’d fashioned herself to be.

King Riyan Savar beside her, sitting with his arms crossed, as silent and stoic as always.

As for the Altani, they’d actually sent one of their elusive council members for this meeting, which was something Maiya hadn’t ever expected. An S rank mejai by the name of Ravan, whom Maiya knew personally, though unfortunately not well enough to get him to boycott this ridiculous war.

Maiya sat to Tia’s right, in a message that couldn’t be missed even by a blind woman.

And yet, for all of Tia’s soaring popularity, Maiya still had her allies and her cards to play. One of them floated above the battlefield at that moment, as a matter of fact.

Tia wasted no time calling the meeting. "The demonic vanguard's dispersed, as we’d guessed,” she said. “S rank pre-emptive strikes will be of limited use, and I would rather us focus on starving battlefield prana than taking potshots at ragtag columns.”

An Altani general scoffed. “The brutes have some sense, at least.”

Tia’s eyes narrowed. “Don’t underestimate them. They know all about our magic, and I know firsthand just how strong they can be,” she said, glancing briefly at Maiya. “We believe they will strike at dusk.”

“Dusk?” a Ranian commander asked. “What fool would attack a fortified position at night?”

“Fools who see, smell, and feel better than you ever will,” said the cold, even voice of King Riyan Savar, and Maiya wholeheartedly agreed.

Tia nodded. “They’re leveraging their physical advantage, which is smart. Or it would be, if we weren’t so well fortified.”

She placed a marker at the valley mouth on the giant map that covered the table. “When the vanguard passes the maw, we’ll unleash our S rank spells. Each of the four spells will fire at their designated targets, with my own coming after.”

Maiya bit her lip. That Tia of all people could hurl S rank magic was still something she couldn’t believe. It made sense, of course. One did not spend more than a decade in the Ash and not become that powerful, but it was so at odds with her image of the princess from before.

No matter her strength, however, Maiya still far outstripped her. That was even before her boons the Imperium of the other universe had gifted her.

And yet all the power in the world made no difference. Maiya wasn’t Tia’s target, after all.

“Don’t even bother trying to recharge your S ranks after,” Tia continued. “We can’t spend the hours needed, so they go first, while the valley's thick with prana. Then we open fire with every A grade we have. Eleven thousand spells in three waves, while the column's bunched in the narrows."

“A standard strategy,” Ravan said in approval. “Deprive the field of prana as soon as we can. Only this time, we’ll not have to worry about any S rank spells coming our way.”

“Indeed,” Tia replied, moving several markers as she spoke. "After that the valley’s prana will be spent. No large scale magic can hope to reach this wall, and we’re more than equipped to handle B-grade equivalent arts. We’ll bleed them in the funnel until they rout."

Voices of assent went around the room, but Maiya killed it with a single question.

“An excellent plan,” she said, speaking above the noise. “But what of the soldiers who cannot die? What army could possibly defeat them?”

The room went silent.

“They number a hundred, give or take,” Riyan replied. “Not a number that can make any real difference. If our information is correct, they take several minutes to resurrect. More than enough time to deny them from the fight."

How could he possibly know about that? Maiya wondered. Only a tiny handful of Samsara had died over the past months, and Maiya had covered up any details of their resurrection herself.

“Even without that,” Tia said with a cough. “A hundred is a small enough number to keep suppressed. We’ll need mejai and Warriors to do it, but as you can see, we have a slight advantage in numbers.”

Quiet chuckles went around the room as Tia perfectly stole back the momentum Maiya had just killed, but she wasn’t done.

“Before this goes any further,” Maiya said loudly, killing the chatter once more. “Let me be brutally clear with all of you. I've planned my fair share of battles. I’ve won most of them, and I won't sit here and ridicule yours. It’s a solid plan. We have the numbers, the magic, the tactics, and the wall…”

Nods of affirmation went around the room. Confident expressions on every face, except for two. Riyan knew Maiya well enough to know where this was going, and Tia had known of Maiya’s stance on this battle all along.

“And you as well,” Maiya continued, addressing the room. “You are best of all that humanity has to offer. When have this many S rank mejai ever convened? I think never! Magic that we’ve only recently seen annihilate an entire city will be used upon a far smaller foe.”

Riyan stiffened at that. For as much as he had usurped the throne, he truly did care about his people. Losing the better part of that town had been a hard blow for him.

“And I'm telling you,” Maiya said, “that in spite of this, no one will win tonight.”

“That’s… difficult to believe,” Ravan of the Altani high council said, frowning. “Considering all our advantages as you yourself just said. You can’t mean to tell us that a hundred demons, immortal though they might miraculously be, will be our end?”

“What many here forget, Councilman Ravan,” Maiya replied, “is that I ruled the Garga for an entire year, and I’m telling you that what you fill soon face isn’t like any army you’ve known. It’s a warband of demons, each with their own arts. Each entirely unpredictable, and completely different from another possessing the very same inscriptions. “Besides which, you’ve all focused on the wrong threat. You worry so much about the Samsara, but tell me… how will you defend against a demon wielding a Warrior Chakra? A weapon that bypasses all armor, which can cut down every single one of you here, no matter your standing? “I know this because I have opened that very Chakra myself. I’ve seen my husband—the closest being to a god in this realm—spend a year catatonic trying to pick up the pieces of his soul. No healer alive, no S rank magic, can help. And I can assure you that nearly every single demon on that field tonight has this power."

The hall went quiet, as not one of them had an adequate reply. Yes, they’d try to win the fight from range. With magic and airships, and to be sure, that would inflict many casualties, but every commander assembled knew all too well that war always devolved into chaos. Into a bloody contest of swords and spears. At that close range, they’d be nothing but fodder.

“I'm not asking you to give up the pass or this fortress,” Maiya continued, now firmly in control of the conversation. “All I'm asking is that you stay your attacks and consider going back to your homes should the demons do the same. Let me go out to them. I have sway with demonkind. Several of the Rajas are my friends. I will convince them not to fight.”

“And if you fail?” the Ranian commander asked.

Maiya shrugged. “Then you lose nothing. But if even one Raja hesitates, the odds stack in your favor.”

No one spoke for a long time, and Maiya saw their strategic minds come around to the idea. What military leader would say no to fighting a weakened enemy?

Hardly Maiya’s goal, but if it meant giving her the opportunity…

It was Tia who broke the silence. “No one here wants this war, Maiya. Least of all me,” she said, meeting Maiya’s gaze for the first time since the meeting began. “But you've already tried. You plead in front of all of them, and you couldn’t even convince your so-called friends. A few more hours change nothing. Make no mistake. They’re the ones pushing for this war. They’ve left us no choice, and if we have to fight, I’d rather it be here, at the throat of the realm, rather than the streets of Daha or Sonam.”

“So you agree, then?” Maiya said. “If demons do not attack, nor will you.”

Dissenting voices erupted, but Tia silenced them with a hand.

“We will not,” Tia announced loudly, albeit with a sad smile. “You know I no longer hate demons as I once did. I was not lying that none of us want this war. We must, however, defend ourselves if attacked. Will you aid us with the weapons of the Gods if that were to happen?”

“What I will do is leave you with the same notice I intend to leave the demons,” she replied. “As you mentioned, my Vimana stands between the human and demon armies. My Vimana. Crewed by the strongest demons you will ever see. And I tell you this now. The first army who moves will experience firsthand the full extent of the Gods’ fury. I will unleash every weapon that thing has and there will be nothing left when I am done.”

Without waiting for a response, Maiya rose from her seat and walked out, leaving the room full of leaders behind her.
