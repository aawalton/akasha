It was a windy, overcast day when General Silas Nevor arrived in the city of Parlest. He felt awkward in his casual attire, the weight of his armor that had become part of him absent. People eyed his sword as he walked through the crowds, no doubt wondering if he was a military man, or merely someone who didn't give two shits about the laws of Araz. In truth, it was both; he was never particularly fond of the weapon regulatory laws in this far region of the Ember Republic. It wouldn't stop people from carrying blades on their persons, only ensured that when a strike eventually came, it came from an unseen place.

Once he had to ask for directions, but he managed to find his way otherwise. Terrin's home looked exactly the same he remembered; a large two-story house with beautifully crafted wooden beams that made it stand out from the regular brickwork uniform the surrounding structures wore. At the front, a small section was fenced off, empty save for a single cart with a bundle of animal hides on top.

Silas was contemplating whether he should open the fence gate and knock, or call out from there, when he saw movement from above. Up on the balcony, a young woman was leaning on the railing with her eyes on Silas. Next to him, a much younger boy had his back to the street, busy with something in his hands.

Before Silas could shout a greeting, the girl jumped on the railing, caught onto the house walls, and proceeded to climb down, quick as a squirrel. She landed on the yard kicking up dust. Only after straightening her shirt did she address Silas, wordlessly raising a brow.

"Stairs. They collapsed?" Silas asked, hands clasped behind him. "No? I understand. You see, me and those devils don't get along much. We do not. A real pain on the joints." When he got no answer, nor did the youth come any closer, he reached out a hand as if trying to grip a ghost's forearm. "I'm General Silas Nevor. I've come to see Terrin Zares. Terrin. Good man, good friend of mine. Last I've heard of him, he lived here with his wife Mera and their newborn. You must be Cinder then?"

Cinder was the splitting image of her parents. Wispy blonde hair, an athletic frame born of hard work, and searing eyes that only comes from protectiveness towards something one loves fiercely.

Seventeen years. That's how long it took for a brand new life to turn into a woman. And it takes a single strike to take that all away. Silas shivered in the face of the shocking reminder, etching this moment in his mind so that he may draw from it in times of need.

Dig the well now, dig before drought sets in, and dig it deep.

"General, huh." Cinder looked him over with a lopsided smile. "Could be a general, I guess. An Arch could also pay us a personal visit, and I wouldn't be able to tell otherwise." Her eyes wandered to the sword at his side. The pommel, in particular, which carried the seal of the Republic, one of the seven ever created.

Silas drew his sword with the blade facing him, handle extended toward Cinder. Seeing her hesitate, he grunted. "Come on, lass, while the invitation's open. These things, not everyday you get to see one of these. Use that head your father gave you. If I wanted you dead, I'd have brought a crossbow. Bloody things require pissant training."

Her smile never wavering, Cinder opened the fence gate and approached. She slowly reached out, brushing a hand over the seal. The genuine grin that spread on her face was a hundred times more bright than her previous expression. After another look, she turned her attention back to Silas. "Know any war stories?"

"I do. Stories, yes." Too many of them, Silas silently added.

"Alright then." Cinder nodded at last. "And don't worry about our stairs. On your account, I'll forward a hard stomp next time I come across them."

Silas followed her inside. The hall was spacious enough to contain said staircase, a set of doors leading to the dining room, with an opening to the study further back. In short order, he was directed to the diner and had his sword belt hooked on the chair he was seated at. He faced Cinder over the table.

"Your parents. They aren't home, I take it." Silas said, more a statement than a question.

Cinder waved an impatient hand. "They should be back soon. But until then, I've questions to ask. Actually… One moment." She got up, dashed out the door and stormed up the stairs. No liar, that one.

When she returned, the boy Silas had seen earlier was with her. "Let me introduce my squire, Kalsin. I'm sorry, sir, but I doubt you'd be his match in a fight."

“I’m your brother, dummy,” Kalsin muttered.

Cinder just smiled slyly.

Silas looked from one to the other, wondering if he was being toyed with. The boy was nothing like Cinder, and definitely nothing like either of his parents.

Where the family's mane ranged between Terrin's brown to Mera's blonde, Kalsin's hair was raven black. His face was angular and grave, or as grave as a young boy at his age could manage. But his most distinctive features were his eyes; cloudy gray so bright they were bordering on silver. It borrowed him an ethereal visage, as if he could stare right into your soul, and not be in the least impressed by what he saw.

Silas gave him an encouraging smile, one his subordinates wouldn't get to see in a lifetime if he had any say in the matter, and even his wives rarely did. He nodded toward the book held tightly to the boy's side. "Studying. Good, that's good. Apologies. I didn't mean to interrupt."

He wasn't great with children, but he must've said the right thing. Kalsin perked up. "It's not a book. Well, maybe it is, but I wasn't reading."

As he was fumbling for words, Silas interrupted him. "That right? Let's see it, then."

Cinder snorted from the seat she'd thrown herself down at, legs propped up on the table. She watched the scene unfold with wordless amusement, as if the general just got himself caught in a bear trap without any means to disarm the mechanism.

Both younglings watched with rapt attention as Silas reached for the book, one with mischievous eyes, the other squirming. Wondering what the hell he got himself into, but playing along nonetheless, Silas propped the book on his lap and opened it around the middle.

On the right page, two stick figures stood apart from each other, holding swords that were hard to differentiate from their arms. They were closer when Silas turned a page, but still locked in the same position. He licked his lips, stalling, unsure what to say. Searching for any good comment he could latch onto, he turned to the last quarter of the book, but coming up empty when he couldn't even tell the two stick figures apart, let alone from the swords they were supposed to be swinging.

"Ah. That's interesting. Yes." Silas said. He leveled a glare at Cinder, whose face was reddening by the moment, her shoulders shaking with laughter that wanted to burst out. For a moment he was worried he'd insulted Terrin's son, but surprisingly, Kalsin only rolled his eyes.

"Just watch," he said, standing beside Silas. Then he bunched up the pages of the book between his fingers, and slowly started to let them roll from below his thumb. The stick figures came alive under his hands, clashing together in a theater that, while lacking the realism of true combat, was a fine artistic depiction of it. He could even make out when one of them pulled out a dagger, getting around his opponent's defenses. The fight ended in a rather dramatized struggle on the ground, and even with the childish exaggeration he could make out the squirming deathrattles of the defeated party. Or perhaps he was only imagining that last detail.

Considering the eye for detail that was required to create the little mock-fight, Silas found himself impressed. Dedication, no matter what form it comes in, tends to leave an unmistakable mark. His feelings must've shown on his face, because Kalsin was glowing with pride, shooting a smile to his sister.

"Silas?"

Silas looked over to see Terrin standing in the doorway. He stared at his old captain as if he'd seen a spectre, his expression mirrored by Mera who stood next to him. Both their once lean forms had filled out during the elapsed years, putting on a healthy weight that was the common price of familial comfort. Terrin's beard had grown long, and Mera's golden locks were flowing free instead of the bun she used to prefer.

Silas stood. His hand instinctively twitched, but he held the gesture, nodding to them each instead. "Colonel Zares. Huntress."

As if his words were quarrels sent into a wall of glass, the tension shattered. Terrin rushed to embrace him, followed by his wife. Silas grunted under the pressure, but returned them with equal strength until it was hard to keep the smile from breaking up his expression. They assaulted him with questions that would've been considered improper back in the army, and he kept his answers short so that he could satisfy most of them. Yes, family was doing well. No, the city wasn't being invaded. Of course, he still held his position, though the chain of command had changed considerably.

Silas held up a hand, forestalling the onslaught and gesturing at the kids. "There, I've had the pleasure to meet your children." Cinder and Kalsin had grown quiet, observing the reunion from their seats. "Bright, the both of them. Bright. Just the right amount of wits and insolence, the exact features I wouldn't want anywhere near the Inner Line of Defense. Or the Old. Or anywhere in the army, for that matter."

Terrin laughed. "Good. With all respect, sir, I'd sooner decorate your backside with a ring of fletchings than to let you pit 'er together with a band of conscripts dodging law's arm."

Perhaps it was his imagination, but Silas swore he heard Terrin say 'her' instead of 'them'. No one else seemed to notice, however. He probably misheard. Those cursed ears. The gears of time spared no man.

"Father," Kalsin said, his hands fidgeting on the soft cover of his book. "I've finished the drawings, and it’s a duel between a hunter and a disguised mythos. They fight to settle a feud between them. And treasure, too, sitting atop a dragon's hoard." He sneaked a look at his sister, miffed, "And, and a magic sword is also involved, I think."

"Great. That's great, Kalsin, can't wait to see it." Terrin smiled at him, but Silas could tell his mind was elsewhere. "Give us a minute, rascals, eh? Old people's got some catching up to do."

"Sure, Dad." Cinder said, standing with a mock salute. She took Kalsin by the shoulders, leading him outside and closing the door behind them. Silas could practically feel the heat from Mera's gaze burn a hole through Terrin, but the man himself didn't appear to notice—that much better for his health. He moved about the room, producing a bottle of wine and filling three glasses without spilling a drop.

Silas accepted the drink, only taking a single sip before putting it down. If it was a good harvest, he couldn't tell. He never basked in alcohol of any kind, not during a campaign, not even off-duty. A muddled mind? He just didn’t see the appeal.

"So. This place. Children. Hunting small game, eh?" Silas said, noting the various trophies hung on the walls. "Just as well. You and your obsessions. You still visit that shrine you couldn't keep your mouth shut about, Terrin?"

"No, place got overgrown a long while ago. Got better things to keep me occupied nowadays."

"Why are you here, Silas?" Mera asked. She had her arms folded, her glass untouched on the table. Silas considered her question, but she got ahead of him. "Is this about the mythos sightings we've been hearing so much about?"

"In part," Silas admitted. He was a prism with his own manifestation, far above the power of an abyss, and yet she could always make him feel like a bug pinned under a sharp knife. Nearly as sharp as the blades she used to carry with herself. He didn't let his discomfort show, though. He had a reputation to uphold, even among friends. The chair creaked as he leaned back in his seat. "Lately, our armies have been stretched thin. The East is under constant pressure, which, as you know, wouldn't be a problem in and of itself. We've dealt with the Veiled before, and even with weakened forces, we can continue to do so. For now."

"For now," Terrin echoed, not quite able to hide his foreboding tone. He shared a look with Mera.

"Letters. Mhm. Damned things make my head hurt before I even open them. Imagine. I'm sitting in the command post, boy comes in running with a letter. A wyrm infestation to the north, provide three nail battalions minimum, by the suggestion of His-High-Fuckin-Archness. So I call a good man of mine—overworld, mind you—and put him in command, then I send the units on their way." Silas waved his hand, as if batting away a fly. "So now there's a gaping, three thousand man sized hole in the Inner Line that somehow I'm magically supposed to fill. Naturally, I start reorganizing. Mhm. Then comes in another letter, this one about some damn-if-I-know sort of dragon burning up shitville and their neighbors, military support expected. Starting to see a pattern here?"

"A pattern that shouldn't exist," Mera pointed out. "Hunters should be more than capable to take care of the mythos problems. We were, in the past."

Silas snorted. "You mean those self-proclaimed opportunists? No offense, Mera, I owe you much. But first and foremost, it was always the military who shielded humanity from the monsters at our gates. Not the Civil House, not the Arches, and certainly not your past vagabond associates."

"Show some gratitude, Silas." She said with a shake of her head. "You know hunters work for free."

Silas suppressed a grimace. An argument as old as time itself. Technically, yes, hunters worked for free, but instead they helped themselves to whatever wealth the mythos' victims left behind. Graverobbers, the whole lot of them. "Be that as it may, the increased number of non-humans wasn't followed by an equal number of hunters to match. And there's no guarantee the incline is stopping any time soon."

Mera switched tactics. "I never took you for someone who danced to the tune of the Civil House, Silas. The Arches—aside from their personal little pretend-armies—have no legal jurisdiction over the military. Never have."

"See how you specified 'legal', hm? Well, legality nosedives through a three-story window when it's the lives of innocent citizens in mortal danger. Pure politics, I don't rightly care about, but the safety and goodwill of the Republic’s inhabitants is another matter. The common man pays taxes threefold; to the Civil House, to the churches, and to the military. Three systems of power in which they can decide to put their trust into, to defend them. I can't afford to lose the support of the population, and the Arches know it."

Terrin absorbed it all in silence, tapping a finger on the table. A war of his own raged in his eyes, and Silas could understand the origin of his conflictions. Terrin looked out the window, eyes on the cart and hides resting on top visible under the gray tinted sky. "Look, I'm sorry but—"

Silas pointed at Terrin, interrupting him. "I need my best men. The best. Flattery aside, you're one of them, even if your surge only reaches root. The fight, it's moving to too many fronts. You could make a real difference there."

"Really. Really?" Mera said. "That was your plan? Barge in here after nearly twenty years of absence to wave your flag of righteousness in a call-to-arms, expecting us to leave behind friends and family."

Silas didn't take his eyes off Terrin, waiting.

Seeing her words find no foothold against the general, Mera turned her attention to Terrin, stepping behind his seat. She smoothed a hand over the back of his neck, caressing. "Don't tell me you're considering it, dear." Silas barely restrained a shiver. He failed to see the appeal of a significant other who might slit your throat on a whim. He even felt a whiff of the abyss' morale-influencing surge.

Terrin took Mera's hand in his, kissing it. His words were directed at Silas. "I miss it, you know. The fights, the danger. But my responsibility rests with my family now."

"I understand," Silas said. He’d expected their refusal from the start.

He received a look of surprise from them both. Their reaction nearly made him laugh. "You served me well. A strong sense of duty never fully disappears, only shifts to other things. Of course. Yes, I understand. Children, hm? How old are they? If memory serves, your eldest should be seventeen by now. Has the looks of her father, she does."

"That's Cinder," Terrin grinned. "You just missed her birthday a week ago. We rented an entire tavern for the evening, free drinks, threw one hell of a party."

"And the other, Kalsin? What is he, ten, eleven?"

"Nine. Good kid. Smart, eager to help out." Terrin hesitated, something clearly nagging him. Mera picked up the thread. "We're just a little worried. Kalsin hasn't come into his surge yet."

Silas raised his brows. Strange. The first stirrings of the surge usually appeared around the age of seven, the sense fully developing by the time of puberty. It was a basic part of humans, just like having two arms and legs.

He let the thoughts disperse. After all, his question served a different purpose, one that might help him salvage the situation. If duty shifted once, it might shift again in the future. "Raising your kids is a higher priority for you than anything I could offer, and I respect that. I do. So you will raise them. They'll grow up. Like all adults, they'll find something to do, as independence dictates. Once that happens, I'd like both of you to join me as leading officers."

He waited. It appeared as if the world was holding its breath. Terrin was absently stroking Mera's hand, who in turn was standing with her lips pursed. "We're not the fighters we used to be," Mera said quietly. "And years from now, we'll be even less so."

"I don't need you to fight. A root and an abyss? No, your surge isn't what I need, I've overworld under my command. What I need is people I trust to get things done. Nothing more."

It wouldn't be easy to convince them. But there was a chance. Sometimes an idea only needs time to take root, waiting for the harvest later down the line. After all, people seldom want to die a meaningless death, and so he trusted each elapsing year to make his offer more and more appealing.

At any rate, he'd done all he could do. Silas stood, fastening his sword belt with brisk motions. "Think on it. If I still happen to be wasting good air and not feeding the worms, visit the nearest military headquarters. They'll expect you, and they'll know where to find me."

An hour later Silas was walking out the city gates. His job was done, onto the next. It went about as well as he could expect. He hoped they would come around, eventually.

But even as he went, as he made his way through the throngs of people shifting in and out of the city, a pair of silver eyes remained in his mind like an unsolved puzzle.
