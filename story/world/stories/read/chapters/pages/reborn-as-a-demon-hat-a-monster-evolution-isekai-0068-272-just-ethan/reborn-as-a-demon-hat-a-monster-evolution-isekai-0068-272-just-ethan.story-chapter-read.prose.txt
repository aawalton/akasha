The youngest master of magic in Argwyll locked eyes with the last Dragon in the world and found that, oddly, she was not gripped by fear.

Rather, her mind was mulling over the fact that the creature had deigned to reveal itself to her and her alone.

Maybe it is simply curious, she thought. Or maybe it wants simply to toy with me. Master Fauna suspected he’d try to toy with us.

The great neck of the dragon rose slowly, steadily, into the sky, and it opened its mouth in a silent yawn that displayed all its rotted, yet still sharpened teeth.

“Peculiar”, it said. “My mind touches yours, and yet I cannot wrest an answer. Could it be the mortals of Argwyll have so guarded their minds against my powers after all this time?”

Mara swallowed the trepidation in her throat and stepped forward with a gracious bow.

“Forgive my intrusion, Master Architect.”

“Architect,” the dragon huffed, scoffing at the phrase as though it was more an insult than a moniker. “It has been some time since I have been called that name. Already I have some idea of what you want. If your desires are truly what I suspected, then you are just as dull as those creatures you travel with.”

Mara kept her head held high. She resisted the urge to call Master Fauna and the rest. If the creature’s demeanor was any indication, he specifically wished to avoid them. Perhaps out of fear, perhaps out of spite.

“If that was what you thought of me,” she said. “Then you would not have revealed yourself to me as you did.”

She watched the gnarled mouth of the Architect curl and into something that chilled her.

It was a smirk.

“An astute little rabbit, I see,” it huffed. “You have intrigued me. Come then, we shall converse.”

Mara cocked a fluffy eyebrow as the creature retreated into the mist, seemingly waiting for her to follow.

“That is what you and your gang wanted, was it not?”

She decided to simply follow him instead of replying, though she kept her spell wards on standby. If this beast decided to end her life, it could have done so already. But all the same she couldn’t be sure that it would not simply attack her on a whim. She’d defend herself – yes. But she would probably fail to best it.

And yet still, she did not hesitate as she levitated up to its craggy lair at the peak of the tallest mountain in the range. Dotted among this place were more pieces of crumbling runic architecture wrapped with glowing flowers and covered with moss. The dragon crouched upon a stone dais at the center of the ruin, its great tail coiling round it in the same manner of a dog that had found a quiet spot to nap in. She got the sense, though she couldn’t say why, that this place had been important to the creature in some way.

From up here, one could actually see past the fog and cloudcover to all of Argwyll’s Southern reaches below – where the mountains blended into a series of mossy fingernails jutting out of the earth, and gave way to the verdant fields of Eastmarch and its blossoming forests.

And far, far to the West, Mara could make out the monolith of Arcona standing tall in the night sky. She’d lost track of time. She had no idea that the stars were already out, staring down at her and the dragon that seemed so fascinated by her presence here.

She waited for it to address her. From what she knew of the great wyrms, they liked to be waited on.

“Well?” the creature huffed, stretching out its batlike wings. “I have a question that remains unanswered.”

Mara gulped, steeling herself. She walked towards the dragon’s great snout and sat cross-legged before him, laying her staff on the ground quietly, and calmly.

“You disarm yourself before me,” the Architect snarled. “That, itself, provides me an answer.”

“A creature as powerful as you must know that I am not truly disarmed,” Mara replied. “My gesture is symbolic. I think it is better to meet new people with open hands, don’t you?”

The Architect let out another bestial snort.

“A rabbit with wisdom beyond her years,” he whispered – if such a being could truly whisper at all.

“I am just a student,” Mara replied. “I apply the teachings of my masters, and those of the world.”

“Hm,” the dragon pondered. “So that is why you did not slay the stone guardians? That is why you, and only you, have saw fit to spare such sorry creatures?”

“They are alive,” Mara retorted – with a little bit more feeling than she’d intended. “I have no right to take their lives.”

“Hah! The rabbit-girl speaks of ‘rights’. An easy thing to do for one who so easily can bend others to their will. Tell me, child, with all the might you sense within me, do I also not have the ‘right’ to slay whomever I so choose?”

“No,” Mara answered. “You do not.”

She met the dark eyes of the dragon and did not flinch. Fauna had taught her to master her breathing, to control her fear. Fear was just an impulse of the body. The mind could be trained to master it.

Though that didn’t mean she didn’t feel it. Right now, she felt it more than she ever had her in entire life.

“A brave girl, too,” the Architect said. “But a liar. I have watched those you call your ‘teachers’ as they lay waste to my stone forest below. Yet you do not follow them. Why?”

This time, Mara hesitated. She dared not attempt to breach the dragon’s mind, lest she anger it with the attempt. But she got the sense that it was…testing her, somehow. That its questions were somewhat premeditated, as though it was checking off her answers one by one.

If that was the case, then what happened if she failed?

She met its dark eyes and flaring nostrils and decided that her best bet was to simply tell it the truth:

“I do not begrudge Mistress Fauna, Mistress Tara, or Master Klax the path they take,” she said. “It is the path they have chosen after seeing the horrors of the world, and its wonders. But it is not the path I walk.”

The dragon cocked its scaly head at her, running its tongue over its rotted teeth.

“The path you walk makes a weak world.”

“Perhaps. But does it make a weak person?”

For a moment, the Architect said nothing more. Its eyes stared unblinkingly, as though daring her to make a move.

And then it showed all its teeth in a smile that was equal parts terrifying as it was amusing.

“You have given me my answer,” he said. “In return, I shall not burn the flesh from your body.”

“You mean that you will not try.”

“HAH!” the dragon’s throat boomed with laughter. It threw its neck up in a hoarse chuckle that shook the very foundations of the mountain. Then, curiously, it started to cough.

“A brave little rabbit, indeed!” it roared between bouts of coughing, moving away from Mara as though it didn’t want her to catch whatever ailment plagued it. “Now I truly have seen all that this world has to offer!”

It sputtered again, creased its great scaly brows as though it was in pain, and then lay down by the side of the mountain peak.

Mara got up and followed it, her own curiosity now piqued. Because now she understood the thing that she had sensed within the creature’s great heaving breast when it had appeared to her. It was not the raging storm of a hellfire that was about to be unleashed, but a smattering of fading embers.

“You are dying,” she said.

The dragon’s old eyes glanced sidelong at her.

“A pitiful way to go,” he snorted. “A common cold. ‘Common’ to your kind, of course. Exposure to air that I had not experienced in eons has left me withered and barren. And yet, the poetic justice is not lost on me. I shut out this realm for so long, and when I finally have a taste for it, the air itself rejects me.”

Laughter spilled from the dragon’s old throat again, hoarse and guttural, like an animal that truly was gasping for its last breath.

“We have many healers in Arcona,” Mara offered. “Mistress Fauna herself is quite skilled.”

The dragon huffed. “And what would that buy me? A few more years of this?” he swept a claw over the world below. “I have seen plenty that this ‘new world’ has to offer. I have seen what has been built on the ashes of the Last Archon. And I must admit my disappointment. Even with all the lofty claims of new order and justice and equality that was promised, still there is discord, still there is hate. And still there will be blood. Even you cannot deny this, little rabbit.”

“I do not deny it.”

The dragon nodded.

“The world you see with your tiny eyes out there – it is not beautiful, is it?”

“It is not beautiful.”

The Architect gave a satisfied snort.

“…but that does not mean it is not worth living in.”

The great dragon turned its face toward the Hopla mage and noticed that she too was looking out over the world that stretched out before them both. Her eyes were focused. Her mouth – stretched in a warm smile.

“There’s a lot of pain, and terror, and sickness, and hate,” she said. “It’s just like you said. But even knowing all that and seeing it with my own eyes, I refuse to believe that that’s what makes us who we are.”

“You would deny the truth of this world? The reality of suffering?”

“No,” Mara replied with a little nod. “I would acknowledge it like I would acknowledge any bump on a road. And then, I would continue walking.”

“Hmpf,” the old dragon snorted. “You are young. Perhaps still naïve.”

Mara didn’t take the bait. She sat down and dangled her legs over the side of the mountain, listening to the dark, hearing silence amidst the pangs of the Architect’s heavy, increasingly labored breaths.

“We are our choices,” she said. “I have chosen to try to make this world a place that others would love as I do. And I only came to that decision after doing exactly what you have been doing for these past five years – traveling the world and seeing it with your own eyes.”

She looked up at him and found that his amber slits were already fixed on her.

“What have you chosen?” she asked him.

The dragon regarded her with detachment, looking across at the very same world she did and yet seeing something so much more distant.

“Choices,” he muttered. “And so do we come to the point of this entire exchange.”

Mara couldn’t help but notice that he’d avoided her question.

Voices suddenly assailed them from down amidst the shadowed boughs of the crag-trees and rocky outcroppings. Master Fauna was calling out to her apprentice, as were Tara and Klax.

“I know why you have come here,” the dragon told her. “I know why you have been seeking after me. And I cannot grant you what you wish.”

Mara said nothing. She got the sense that the dragon had been practicing the script of this during their entire conversation.

“He came before me, once,” the Architect said – a slight smirk touching his aged lips. “Like the others before him, he was powerful. Fierce. Determined. Yet unlike them he was astutely aware of his limitations. He doubted himself, as he had been trained to. And that was the reason why I gave him an audience instead of all the rest. Because that doubt, expressed by a being who held such power within its hands, intrigued me.”

Mara stayed silent, though her ears perked up.

“He offered me a deal,” he said after another short bout of coughing. “My freedom from the curse placed upon me in exchange for a Skill of my own making that could provide him with a contingency, should he become exactly what he feared he would. Naturally, such fear was well-placed, though his enemy was not quite what he expected it would be.”

The dragon let out a pained yawn, looking as though he could swallow the moon itself. At this point, Mara wasn’t listening to the calls of her friends anymore. Now she was fixated on the Architect’s every word. They did not seem like the words of a terrifying world-eating wyrm anymore. Now, they had more in common with the lamentations of an old man approaching death’s door.

“…but then, our enemies never are what we expect,” he continued. “I took up his offer to see the world he was endeavoring to build. And I found that it no longer holds any meaning for me. In fact, it seems to have rejected me outright.”

As Mara made to interject, the Architect held up a bony nail.

“Do not fret, child. I knew this destiny would befall me eventually. When one measures time in eons, death becomes less of a fear and more of a promise left unfulfilled.”

He turned to her with sudden conviction.

“And that is why I cannot bring him back. He chose the moment of his demise. He made what he deemed to be a worthy sacrifice for – this,” the dragon spat up phlegm at Argwyll. “I shall not pretend to understand it. For there are some mysteries your mortals hold within you that even I cannot fathom, so illogical are you in your wants and your fancies. But I accept his sentiment. A deal is a deal. And I see no reason to bring him back simply because his old friends grieve his passing.”

Mara said nothing in response to this at first. Instead, she felt the chill wind that had started to blow through the mountain realm touch her bones, and, realizing that it must be well past midnight by now, and that her Masters were clearly not going to leave without her, she simply stood and brushed down her robe, ready to depart.

The dragon, meanwhile, cocked a scaled brow down at her.

“Oh? That’s it? No retort? No attempt at changing my venerable old mind?”

“I don’t have the power to change your mind,” she said. “Nor do I have the authority to try.”

She started to walk away, head held high, still feeling the cold nip at her shoulders as she tried to keep them from shaking.

“So much for the determination of Hybrids!” the Architect scoffed at her back. “You would abandon your quest without even a debate?”

“Would that I could debate an ancient dragon out of his own mindset,” Mara called over her shoulder. “You made your decision before you even knew we were looking for you. Why would you listen to anything I have to say on the matter?”

She walked away from him knowing that he was staring after her, his nails practically digging into the dirt of his mountain. She knew that, right now, she had him much more captive as a listener than if she’d opposed him directly.

Just like you always say, Mistress Fauna, she thought. Patience and a little bit of pressure sometimes work the greatest magic.

If she was being honest, she wanted to see him again. Of course she did. But she knew that her Master wanted that even more than she did. She knew it every time she looked in her eyes and saw that something had been lacking for her since that fateful day five years ago.

Yet she knew too that such desires were ultimately selfish, perhaps even antithetical to the very society they were trying to build. That’s what this Architect also thought. That’s how he saw them all – as profoundly selfish creatures when all was said and done.

And something about that…pissed her off.

“I do have a question for you,” she said before she left the mountain peak. “If you will indulge me.”

His eyes hadn’t left her. He threw back his head and laughed again just has he had when they’d first come here together.

“My, but you are a bold little rabbit, aren’t you?” he chuckled through more coughs. “You would ask a dying beast to satiate your curiosity before it leaves this realm?”

“You don’t have to answer,” she smiled. “But I think you will, won’t you?”

“That depends on how stimulating the question is.”

Mara’s smile only grew.

“Have you ever considered, even for a moment, that you might be wrong about him?”

Silence followed Mara’s question, punctuated only by the dragon’s continued ragged breaths.

He cocked an eyebrow at her as if goading her: ‘well? Go on.’

“You have lived longer than any being on Argwyll,” Mara continued. “You claim to know the hearts and minds of mortals, though you can’t fathom why we do the things we do. I wonder why that is the gap in your knowledge that you admit. Surely you won’t let us defeat you?”

That conjured a wicked smile from the dragon. She conjectured that, had he still the fires of strength in his bosom, he might have just roasted her alive right then and there.

“You claim to know what was in his heart?” he asked. “Merely because you were one of the beings he possessed?”

“Actually,” Mara chuckled. “I was one of the ones who never did wear him on my head. And to be honest, I can’t claim to have known him best. Mistress Fauna has that honor. Her, and the others who journeyed with him.”

“And yet you are the one who stands before me, claiming that his intentions are worth considering.”

Mara thought about that for a moment, looking out again over the star-studded sky that had draped itself over the Argwylian night. She thought of all the people down there in the valleys and hamlets – people she’d never known and would probably never meet – who had woken to something new on the day Ethan died. A choice. An endless stream of new rivers.

And she thought of the one person who’d never truly had that in this world.

“I knew him as ‘Mr Ethan’”, she said quietly. “I’d never met one of the Archons before. Gyko’s reign was before I was born. And the others were just legends. But I’d always imagined them to be beyond comprehension. Like they would be totally alien to me. But he was different. He was goofy, kind – and just a touch more prideful than most. He would play with the children of the farmhouse not because he had anything to gain from it, but simply because he found it fun. And sure, maybe he liked the way we looked at him as a savior to our people. But inside, I don’t think he ever really wanted to be a hero. I think that’s what we wanted him to be. I think that’s what he thought he had to be in a world like ours.”

“And what makes you say this, little rabbit?” the dragon asked. “You admit yourself that you barely knew him, and far less than your companions did.”

She’d been wondering about that very question for these past five years. During lulls in her training with Mistress Fauna, or when listening to Agna’s melodies and songs in the Arcona market-square, her eyes would wander over the statue of the Demon Hat in the city center and linger on its dull, stony eye.

She’d never spoke with him alone like her companions had. She’d never ventured out into the world with him on his adventures, and she hadn’t been with him just before the end. Fauna had told her of what had transpired long after his death, and she had listened, mute, trying to understand why he’d done everything he had. Trying to understand the real human that he’d been underneath the fabric and stitching of the form the old prophet had granted him.

And her mind always came back to that final moment when she’d touched him as the dark angel died.

In that moment, she’d seen much – felt much – of what he felt right as he passed on from this world. She’d seen his eye flutter in recognition of who she was for the briefest of instants, and couldn’t resist the urge to reach into his mind and see what his final thoughts were. And all these years later, she was still trying to make sense of what she’d seen in that moment.

She’d at first thought that they were simply his memories – images of his friends, of Argwyll, of the world he left behind, of the joy he’d felt and the pain and the terror and the strange satisfaction that he’d felt in the end. She’d thought that he had no control over what these final thoughts of his dying mind were. She’d looked into him during what should have been a moment of privacy, and she’d felt shame at having done so all these years. It was one of the reasons she couldn’t face Mistress Fauna down there.

But now she thought that, in that final moment, he knew that she was there. And he’d shown her something meant only for her.

“Call it intuition, if you believe in that,” Mara replied with a little wink. “But I think he’s the only one in this world who really never got to live a day as himself. As just Ethan. I think that’s all he ever really truly wanted – to be himself in a world that didn’t force him to be someone else, that didn’t need him to be anything except who he was. He died to give us all that gift. And it just seems unfair to me that he never got a chance to experience it himself, and that you never got a chance to really meet the real him. Not the hat, not the Archon, not the hero with all the Stats and Skills. But just Ethan.”

She let her words sink in – or at least she hoped they were. The fact that the dragon stood there, practically on death’s door, and had listened to her without interrupting told her that at the very least he’d heard what she had to say. Whatever he thought about her and her simple, perhaps even puerile desire, she couldn’t say.

“I’m not going to ask you to bring him back,” she added. “I’m asking if you would try to understand him. Not for me. Not for Mistress Fauna. Not for everyone down there in Argwyll. But for yourself. Because you might find that there is something to this world after all, if you could get out of your own head just before the end comes.”

She thought she might have pushed it with that last line. Now she was almost certain that the dragon would have rushed her for daring to imply fault with its character.

But as she lingered there on the moonlit mountain, eyes never straying from those great amber slits of the beast, she didn’t register rage. She didn’t register anything at all. So she bowed and took her leave, saying just one more thing before she left the Architect’s peak:

“Forgive me. I never did tell you my name. It’s –“

“No need, little rabbit,” came the dragon’s response as it turned away from her and curled up in a sleeping position once more.

She watched him in silence, frowning at the odd smirk that had smeared itself across his face again.

“I know who you are.”
