“The Bonne Mère has watched this harbour for eight hundred years. Some guardians are built. Some are found. The distinction matters less than you think.” - Codex of the Long Watch, Field Observation, 19th Century, Marseille

They started with the crack of dawn.

The hill was steeper than it looked from the harbour.

Arthur felt it in his calves by the third switchback, the old road climbing through terraced gardens gone feral. Bougainvillea spilled over walls in tangled drifts, their colour now matching the violet sky. The city dropped away behind them, Marseille stretching south and east under a damp grey morning.

Steve had been talking since the first switchback. Steve always talked on approach, and Arthur had learned to let it run because buried in the torrent there was always something useful.

“The hill is a hundred and forty-nine metres of Urgonian limestone. Cretaceous period, a hundred and twenty-five million years old. This rock was a seabed when dinosaurs were the dominant land species.” He paused long enough to breathe. “A priest named Pierre built the first chapel up here in 1214. Eight hundred years of continuous worship on a rock older than flowering plants.”

“Steve-” Donna started.

“But here’s the thing. It’s not really a church. Francis the First built a military fort around the chapel after the siege of 1524, the same year he built the Château d’If on the island. The Count of Monte Cristo’s prison and the Bonne Mère are sister fortifications. The church is inside the fort. They lowered the drawbridge during peacetime so pilgrims could reach the chapel, and raised it during war. A church you can only enter when someone decides to let you.”

Arthur filed that, and another thought arrived on its heels. A king built a military fort around a chapel on the highest point in the city. The Templars had done the same at Temple Church, building a fortified compound around a node they didn’t know was a node. Nodes had been wrapped in human construction that humans believed they’d built for their own reasons. Did Francis the First decide to fortify this hill, or did someone whisper in a royal ear that this summit needed protecting?

Donna was reading the walls. “Bullet scars,” she said. She ran her fingers along a line of pockmarks in the stone. “Small calibre. This is a strafing run, not shelling. World War Two?”

“Yes, August 1944,” Steve confirmed. “The Liberation of Marseille. The Germans held the summit. A French tank called Jeanne d’Arc led the assault up this road. One of the soldiers knew a hidden stairway under the basilica that the Germans hadn’t found, came up behind them through their own fortifications.”

“A hidden stairway,” Arthur said.

Steve turned and met his eyes. The same thought arriving simultaneously. A military fort, eight hundred years old, built on Cretaceous limestone, with passages the occupiers didn’t know about.

“Let’s activate the node first,” Arthur said. “One thing at a time.”

They climbed the last switchback through the monumental staircase and passed through where the drawbridge had stood. The basilica rose above them in striped stone and gilded excess, red and white in alternating courses, Romano-Byzantine, designed by a twenty-three-year-old Protestant architect who’d died of pneumonia that he got in his own crypt.

“Your species builds its defensive architecture and its devotional architecture on the same hilltops,” Archie observed. “This is either strategic efficiency or theological confusion.”

“Both,” Steve said, without looking away from the stonework.

The golden Madonna caught the morning light from sixty metres above, arms open, nine and a half tonnes of gilded copper assembled in four sections at the summit because they couldn’t get her up the hill whole.

Steve’s head tilted back, still talking. “The Madonna faces the sea. She presents the child to the world. Every ship entering the harbour for a hundred and sixty years has seen her face first.”

Arthur looked up at the golden figure watching the Mediterranean with the patience of something that had been standing in the Mistral since 1870 and intended to keep standing.

The éperon, the stone spur at the western end, held the orientation table. A circular stone platform with a brass compass rose set into its surface, directional markers pointing toward every visible landmark. Arthur’s Resonance had been pulling since the second switchback. Up here, the pull was a hum.

“The riddle,” Steve said. “Touch the brass-bound star where the sea-winds blow. The star is the compass rose. The mistral wind blows to the south. This is the marker.” He pointed.

Steve reached for the southern point and touched the Rose. Nothing happened.

“Did we not agree not to touch things we don’t understand?” Donna chided, her eyes darting around.

“Candidate-gated,” Archie said. “And trail progress needs commitment.”

Steve looked at Arthur and gestured towards the compass. “Well it seems, it waits for the savant.”

“Savant is too generous for the current state of the trial,” Archie intoned.

Arthur stepped forward and placed his palm flat on the southern brass marker. It was warm from the sun and a deeper power. The Grid recognising the hand it had been waiting for.

The compass rose lit under his palm. Ether light traced the brass inlays outward through every directional marker and flowed down into the éperon’s stone, illuminating channels hidden in the limestone for a hundred and seventy years. The light travelled through the fort’s old walls, turning the hilltop into a diagram that Arthur wanted to read but couldn’t yet parse.

Then the air above the terrace split.

The guardian came from the sky.

It manifested in a burst of compressed Ether thirty metres above the terrace. A raptor’s form with wings spanning five metres tip to tip. Its plumage was crystallised energy and its eyes were points of concentrated light.

Arthur’s shield came up and he tightened his grip on the hammer. Donna’s eyes had gone sharp, her new dexterity baseline reading trajectories in the approach.

“Banking left,” she said. “Three seconds between the turn and the commit. Southeast.”

As Donna had predicted the guardian dived from the southeast. It hit Arthur’s shield at full speed and the Ironhold’s channels did what they were designed to do. The force propagated across the entire disc instead of concentrating at the point of impact. Arthur slid back two metres across the stone. His arm rang but his frame held.

The guardian climbed. Thirty metres in three seconds, already banking for the next pass.

It came again on a different angle. Donna had moved during the climb, cutting wide to put herself on the far side of the raptor’s line, axe ready. Arthur saw the setup and committed to it. As the creature dived past him, he swung the Ironhold up and across, a shield-bash meant to knock it off-line and drive it straight into Donna’s waiting blade.

The raptor was faster than anything they had fought so far. The shield edge clipped plumage with a glancing contact, nothing more. The guardian twisted in mid-air and was already climbing before Donna’s axe got anywhere close.

Donna’s blade cut empty air. She pivoted and tracked the climb.

Arthur’s tabletop instincts parsed the encounter. Open terrace, no cover, no chokepoints. An enemy with total aerial freedom that chose when to engage. Two melee fighters and one unreliable caster with burned hands.

“It’s a high-Dex build,” he said. “Open terrain is a total party kill.”

Steve let out a laugh despite himself. Donna was already scanning the architecture.

“The bell tower arches,” she said. “Funnel.”

Arthur followed her eyes to the basilica. Twin stone arches cut under the bell tower, narrow at the shoulders and tall at the apex. They were defensive proportions, the kind that forced attackers through single-file.

“We can’t funnel it if it won’t come down,” Arthur said. The guardian was circling, patient, reading them the way the alpha boar had read them. Learning. “It dives, hits and then climbs. We get one window per pass and it’s not enough. We need to ground it.”

He looked at his shield and then looked up at the guardian overhead. His mind ran a calculation that had nothing to do with buildings and everything to do with physics.

“Steve. Next dive, heat the air in its approach. Twenty feet out. Make it flinch, slow it down, even a fraction. Can you manage that with the burn on your hands?”

“I can do that.” Steve’s hands were shaking. “What are you going to do?”

“Arthur. What are you doing.” Donna’s voice was clipped.

“I’m going to ground it. Structural Reinforcement makes me immovable. An immovable object on a flying creature is a grounded creature. I’m going to make it carry something it can’t carry.”

Donna’s head snapped toward him. “Arthur. Tell me you’re not about to do what that sounded like.”

“It’s either that or death by a thousand claws. When it comes through, you’ll have seven seconds. Maybe ten. Make them count.”

They started across the terrace toward the basilica entrance, Arthur keeping the Ironhold high and angled against the sky, scanning for the next commit.

The guardian found them halfway across.

It dived fast but Arthur saw it coming and got the shield up, ready to take the impact dead-on. And then it feinted. The raptor read the shield and adjusted. Its wings folded a fraction and its body rolled in mid-air, the dive angle sliding around the shield’s face and past Arthur’s left shoulder.

It hit Donna.

She was a half-step behind him. The raptor’s leading edge caught her across the torso and she rolled with the impact, turning into the spin, the reflex of somebody who’d taken body-shots for a living. The Scaleguard Vest took the rest. The overlapping plates absorbed a hit that would have opened her chest, and still the force carried her two metres sideways before she could plant her feet.

Steve caught her elbow before she went down. “Go, go, go-”

They ran the last ten metres to the bell tower arches while the guardian climbed for its next pass, wings beating fast for altitude, recalculating its next approach.

Arthur set the Ironhold down on the flagstones inside the arch.

The guardian banked, tucked its wings for a dive and committed.

Steve raised his blistered hands and pushed. The air between the guardian and the terrace rippled with excited molecules, heat distortion like over tarmac in summer. The guardian came through it and its dive angle tilted, unable to avoid the cone of blistering heat. Its speed dropped a fraction as it tried to reorient.

Arthur jumped.

Strength twenty-four launched him higher than any human was capable of before the integration. He caught the guardian’s neck as it passed, both arms locking around crystallised plumage that burned cold against his skin. The guardian shrieked with a sound like tearing metal, and tried to climb. Its wings beat once. Twice.

Arthur activated Structural Reinforcement.

His body locked, every joint going rigid at once. Ninety-seven kilograms of Ether-reinforced human became a fixed point in space that happened to be wrapped around a creature’s throat. The guardian’s wings strained against the sudden impossible weight. Physics won. You cannot fly when the load exceeds the lift.

The guardian dropped with Arthur hanging on it like dead weight. It twisted, trying to shed him, wing clipping the edge of the bell tower arch as it careened through the opening. Stone chips exploded from the impact. Arthur felt the collision travel through his locked frame, his body endured and the technique held.

They hit the terrace floor inside the arch. The guardian thrashing, wings pinned against the stone walls on either side, Arthur locked around its neck like a millstone that had decided it was never letting go.

“Donna! Now!“

And she was there. Her arrival looked like a cut in a film, one frame she was outside the arch and the next she was inside it with the axe in motion. The Refined blade found the wing joint where it met the body and drove through. The guardian shrieked again. Donna struck a second time, lower, where the crystallised plumage thinned along the ribcage. A third strike. A fourth. Each one precise, each one finding the structural line that Arthur’s weight had exposed by forcing the wings wide.

The guardian’s movements became erratic. Its form was destabilising, the Ether construct losing coherence under sustained damage while carrying a load it was never designed to bear. Arthur felt it fragmenting beneath his arms and he held on. Nine seconds. Ten. Eleven.

Donna’s fifth strike went through the neck.

Arthur’s Structural Reinforcement cut out and the recovery hit him all at once. His joints screamed and his muscles spasmed. The transition from stone back to flesh was rough enough to leave him gasping on the terrace floor amid a shower of dissolving Ether crystal.

“Technique duration exceeded safe parameters by four seconds,” Archie said. “The recovery cost scales exponentially beyond the threshold. This is not a recommendation to repeat the approach.”

Steve called from outside the arch. “Talk about a millstone around your neck.”

Arthur couldn’t laugh. His diaphragm wasn’t cooperating, and he managed a sound that might have been acknowledgement.

Donna stood over the dissipating remains, the axe hanging at her side with the Refined edge luminescent with residual Ether. She was breathing through her teeth. The repeated strikes had twisted her torso and the cracked ribs were collecting their debt. Steve was on one knee outside the arch, hands curled against his chest. Again.

Marseille - THE MADONNA IS AN ANTENNA

The engineering on the Madonna has been bothering me all afternoon. She wasn’t cast. She wasn’t hammered. She was grown - galvanoplasty, a new electroplating technique in 1867, where copper is deposited molecularly onto a mould by running electric current through a copper-salt bath. Christofle in Paris essentially grew her in four sections, shipped them to Marseille by train in late 1869, and stacked them on Espérandieu’s iron armature.

Nine and a half tonnes of copper at the highest point in the harbour, produced by what amounts to running electricity through a chemical bath until the shape appears. I am not suggesting anything. I am noting that somebody in 1867 chose a brand-new electrical process to manufacture a figure that now sits on top of a Grid node, and that the word antenna keeps occurring to me, and I cannot make it stop.

Arthur has never ridden a creature into the ground before and I have seen his bookshelf. There is no manual for that. His body produced the solution before his mouth had finished explaining it to us.

What I also want to note, because nobody said it aloud, is that the creature had three passes to learn us and by the third pass it was targeting the unarmoured member behind the tank. Seeder guardians are not set-piece encounters. They are adaptive systems.
