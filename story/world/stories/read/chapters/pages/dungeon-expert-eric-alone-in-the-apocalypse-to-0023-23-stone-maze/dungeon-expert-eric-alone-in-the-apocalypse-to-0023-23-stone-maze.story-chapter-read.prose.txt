The inside of the maze wasn’t hugely different than the outside. It was hot, dry, and dusty, with red rock everywhere. The only difference was that it was a little shady, thanks to the close stone walls standing tall beside them. A few little scorpions wandered around, but they fled as Eric approached. Somehow, a tumbleweed blew down the narrow corridors, bouncing along an intersecting path. A skull with three horns laid in the corner of the nearest dead-end, three hollow eye sockets gazing at nothing. Two of the horns curled; the third thrust straight up, like a unicorn.

Don’t want to encounter whatever that is, Eric thought, eyeing it as he turned down the first intersection, taking the traditional turn-left traversal route. And then he paused and backed up. He walked over and picked up the skull.

Widehorn Ox Skull (crafting material)

Eric grinned. Score. The skull vanished into his inventory. He didn’t know what it was used to craft, but it never hurt to have more crafting materials. The snakeskin jacket was already paying dividends (even if it looked a little silly with the t-shirt and gym shorts). He was looking forward to getting that upgraded to full-body snakeskin (which would probably look even sillier on him, though the practicality of the situation outweighed looking frighteningly fly) and maybe even acquiring the invisibility buff later if he found whatever he needed for that.

Dusting his hands, turned. The skull was nice, but he needed to be strict about his traversal method if he wanted to map out the entire maze. He needed to get back to the entrance and—

Uh. Where’s the entrance?

From where he stood, he should’ve been able to turn around and see the exit. It was right behind him. The only thing he’d done so far was walk straight forward and pick up a skull. And yet, a solid wall of red stone blocked his route back, resolutely closing him in as though he’d taken several turns and wandered deep into the maze. In fact, it closed off everything but the very final intersection before the dead end. He could turn left or right, but he couldn’t return to the entrance of the maze.

Welp. So much for the classic left-handed traversal. He’d have to do this the toddler-in-a-cornmaze-way and wing it.

“That’s on me, guys. That was my bad,” he admitted aloud. He hadn’t checked if the maze’s walls moved. To be fair? Why would he? They looked as solid as—well, rock. Something to check in the future, I guess. Not that he knew how; maybe sending some mana forward or throwing an object? He’d have to figure out what triggered the wall movement first.

He lifted his hand, but before he could try sending a mote of mana ahead the way he had with the gnomes, rabbit claws bit into his shoulder. “Don’t be foolish,” Cy commanded, suddenly wide awake.

“Yeah?”

“The walls trigger on mana, yes. What do you think happens to the spaces behind the walls?”

Eric paused. “I… don’t know.”

“Precisely. Do you want to find out?”

Eric looked at the brand new wall. In his mind’s eye, he stepped forward to just before the intersection, then sent a mote of mana ahead of him. Rock shot out of the wall beside him, picking him up and smashing him into the opposite wall. Who knew if the entire space from here to the entrance was filled with rock? He shuddered. “Good point.”

“Be prepared. After they lock you in is usually when they send in the swarms.”

Eric blinked at Cy. “The swarms?”

“Eyes forward!”

Scorpions flooded toward him, black and yellowish tan bodies skittering over one another as they poured down the route ahead of him. He checked over his shoulder, but the route behind was open. Shooting a firebolt backward, he ran down the route behind him. Scorpions sizzled and popped, dying quicker under the +5 bonus against bugs from his shoes. Ahead, the path twisted, then came to an intersection. The left intersection had another intersection not far down it, while the right intersection went straight for a long time. He ducked down to the left, then leaped around the next corner and put his back to the wall.

The scorpions rustled by. He couldn’t see them. The rock walls blocked his mana vision. But he could hear them. Some went right, some went left, but most of them ran by, ignoring him where he stood. A few rounded the second corner and hissed at him, but few enough of them rounded both corners that he was able to dispatch them with a few well-placed stomps. More poured around the corner, and he had to speed up, stomping faster. For a little while, it felt like he was playing one of those old-fashioned dance games, but then the scorpions scurried off, and he was alone once again.

Eric peeked around the corner. The way he’d come from was sealed off. He could follow the scorpions to the left or the right, but he couldn’t turn back.

He weighed what he’d heard, then took the left route. If nothing else, he’d squashed a good number of the ones that had gone this way when they’d rounded the corner at him. Assuming the scorpions split roughly fifty-fifty, there would be fewer this way as a result.

“I don’t know if this is a good bet, but if you’re wondering…” Eric described his thoughts as he walked. He didn’t know if this was entertaining or not, but it was what he’d initially conceived of: a walkthrough. If he didn’t explain himself, he wouldn’t be able to guide anyone else.

“I just hope this is getting through,” he muttered, mostly to himself.

“How so?” Cy asked.

Eric gestured. “I’ve been watching the Tutorial. It’s a big lump of garbage. It’s drama and bullshit. Sometimes they do some weapon training, but that’s it. If that’s what they do for a whole year while the dungeons get harder and harder, it’s going to be a slaughterfest when they get here.”

Cy rolled onto his side and primly licked his forepaw. “That’s the idea, yes.”

“Why?”

“The Game demands blood. Its audience cries out for it. They enjoy the slaughter. Relish in it. If your people were simply left to face the dungeons from day one, they might stand a chance. Instead, they’ll give fantastic ratings on “Release Day” when they’re all released from the Tutorial and into the dungeons, like lambs to the slaughter.”

Eric frowned. “That’s not what Joe said.”

“Joe doesn’t say a lot of things.”

“What about the ones who like to watch people win? Watch good fights, and all that?”

Cy sat upright. “Have you ever cheered for a paper boat going down the stream, knowing it will get subsumed by the impending waterfall? Is it not more exciting to choose one and cheer for it, to hope it makes it over the waterfall, even when you know it will dash upon the next rock even if it survives the plunge?”

“But that’s…”

Hey, kid. That’s the kind of shit we need Cy not to say. I can’t air any of that.

But is he right?

He’s not totally wrong… a bit too cynical for my blood, though. Remember: he’s a loser. He lost. His whole world lost the Game. Everyone he knows and loves was devoured by it. Everything he says, everything he believes, even the truths he holds in his heart, are filtered through that reality.

Eric frowned. That was unusually heartfelt and honest for Joe. The guy usually wore the veneer of a slick showbiz recruiter like a skin. For him to admit that Cy wasn’t totally wrong… to allow something bad about the game…

Hey there. I’m not totally heartless. Look, kid. I’m a corporate drone, so I’ve gotta echo the company line. That’s the way it goes, bucko. You get it. You were a drone too, right?

A corporate drone? Eric wrinkled his nose. Joe was right: he had been one of them. He’d had to say things he didn’t believe and pretend he liked or agreed with decisions that were outright idiotic because the CEO thought he’d stumbled upon the next genius maneuver to suck more profit out of his workers for private equity without his workers noticing. Anything to keep his job. Anything to pay rent and keep his cockroach-infested roof over his head, all while lying to himself that he’d get that raise, save up, and finally move into a nicer apartment.

Wait. Does that mean Joe’s being exploited, too?

It raised questions he didn’t have the answers to—questions he wasn’t sure he wanted answered. As much as he hated it, Joe was right. Right now, he needed to get noticed. He needed to be aired and gain followers, no matter the cost. To survive—to win this Game, for everyone, and save his planet—he needed to play to the audience. And that meant not discussing the uncomfortable truths of his situation.

It was like saying “unalive” instead of “die” or “suicide” on the most popular video platforms, where using the wrong word or showing the wrong events could get your account silently suppressed and dumped from the algorithm. It wasn’t up to your audience to decide if they wanted to keep watching your content or not; the algorithm silently, one-sidedly, removed you from everyone’s feeds, and since it was nearly impossible to go looking for a particular person’s content—or rather, most people simply wouldn’t bother—that was the end of you. Even on the “more tolerant” platforms, the wrong content would get videos demonetized all the time. He’d heard his streamers complain about it endlessly. Sure, sometimes they said an Australia word (dropped a c-bomb) or showed 18+ content from a video game on accident, but sometimes neither he, nor they, nor anyone else could figure out why a particular piece of content had been demonetized. It was simply the name of the game.

In the Game, where pleasing viewers and the all-oppressive silent algorithm was a matter of life or death, he couldn’t afford to so much as take a risk on a “naughty word.” He had to play it safe. That’s what Joe is for, he realized abruptly. Joe was sculpting his runs to make content out of them, sure, and that was a job and an art in of itself; but the most important thing Joe could do was cut out the naughty words and bad topics that he didn’t even know were bad. He didn’t know the algorithm. He didn’t know what was acceptable and not. Joe did. That was what a manager was for: keeping him from getting silently oppressed by an algorithm he didn’t and couldn’t know.

Man, I hate this. He really did. Selling his soul for this bullshit Game… but it was that or die, and he wasn’t ready to give up yet. He took a deep breath and pushed his irritation down, burying it deep. He played the Game. He walked the line. And when he beat it, destroyed this Game and this insane situation and this ridiculous scenario, then he’d destroy that, too—the oppression that forced him to act this way.

“What about the scorpions?” he tried, going for a change of subject.

“What about them?” Cy asked haughtily.

“I took out that big one at the entrance, but it’s been little guys since then. What’s up with that?”

Cy harrumphed. “You must not be deep enough in the maze. Keep going. You’ll find them.”

With no other option at hand, Eric kept going.
