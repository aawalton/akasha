Some choices cannot be unmade.

Ironwine's antenna twitched the tendrils around his mouth, flexing with an ache he felt deep in their fibers. How long had he stood there, bent over the forge? Days? Or was it weeks? The Thrysalid could only recall replacing the medicinal bath eight times. Each pour had taken longer to absorb than the last, but the process had not yet stopped.

The boy was still alive.

The newest medicine simmered in the coals. Ironwine had been forced to change his recipe more than once as the human absorbed it, for while the medicinal mudbath weakened, the human elixir did not. It was a truly baffling concoction, not the strongest he’d seen, but harsh beyond measure. Ironwine matched it, turn by turn, increasing its potency as the boy continued to survive—no longer was he drowning amid an infant's prescription, but a solution that only a few Thrysalid children could survive.

What boiled in the cauldron now, however, was a stage beyond that entirely. Fit for an adolescent Thrysalid, the medicine belched clouds of heavy fumes that barely escaped the lip of the pot. It oozed down the side, banished only by the banked heat of the forgebed’s coals. It was not yet ready, but when the human called out—as he always did—Ironwine would be there.

Only a piece of his mind was spared for such activities however. The rest of him was focused on the Making.

Whether it was the struggle of the human child to survive or the mixing of Age-old medicines, Ironwine wasn’t sure and did not care—he heard the Call. It was not strong, more of a whisper or a dim light piercing his dark hovel. Somewhere between the third batch of medicine and the fourth, Ironwine’s Stars had returned to him. Before he could do more than flatten his antennae, nimblefingers swung his hammer. After that, the passage of time had become far less important.

He brought his hammer down, sparks scattering in the heated wind. Claw turned the metal before him, aligning its face against the anvil’s curved horn. He struck, each time driving free the dross that infested the slab. Each time, his soul reaching toward the ineffable that had drawn him there.

Ironwine held the Call of Making between his fingers as gently as a songbird. Hammer and steel were in ironfists, but the Call was not for cages. Twice it tried to flee, to escape toward some brighter Star, but Ironwine folded the metal and let his soul widen beneath its light. Reflection upon reflection, wind upon wave, he let it guide him back toward confluence.

His claws twisted, iron carapace carved with alai, lit by molten steel and Stature’s blaze. Metal pressed tight, hammered flat and curved. The world hung between the sparks, suspended amid the rise and fall. The Three That Were One broke open: Stone, Stars, and Sea.

In the first hours, Ironwine was unsure what he was Making. This was not unusual. Such things were slow to progress and he was out of practice. But to be Eidhrin was to bear witness to the turning of the Three, and he had seen so much. He was a Wright, born of dark confluence and clad in his people’s bane. Each strike was a bolt to his memories, toward turnings that had once been his to spend. To be a Wright was to Make, and a Making was not done without the Intended.

The human cried out—not with voice or Talent, but with the deeper pieces of himself. Higher and far below, between Sea and Star.

There it is.

The metal in his claws had twisted three times, folded until its bulk was layered deep enough to drive its weaknesses to shed amid the anvil’s foot. He set it into a barrel of effervescent Water, and steam jetted upwards to pool across the mossy rafters. Fungus flared, set ablaze with an internal might before dimming back to quiescence. The twisted hunk of steel sank carelessly to the bottom. What remained was nothing but remnant dross—an effort to find the center line of the Intended. He had that now.

Ironwine reached, pulling at the Talent that spun invisibly through the air all around them. It was a strange power, so different from the pompous jays that paraded before his home, eager for a Making from him. It was quiet—far more than Surafce should be capable of—Ironwine could only sense it through their Bargain, and even then it eluded him at times. It shifted like his antennae, prodding inward more than out. A reflection of his growing soul, one that—

He shuddered, mandibles rattling. Black eyes blinked across a gulf between them, for a moment unable to understand its significance.

The human’s soul was broken.

Stature was all that held the boy’s center together. It bound his Soul Lock within the expanse of his tributaries and surprisingly small reservoir, the glut of his Bargains somehow making up for the gilded cracks that riddled his Lock’s sides.

Stillwater. How many Bargains has this child made?

Ironwine seized his horror and discomfort, bundling it along with the shape of the boy's limited soul. Stature was part of the Making too. Everything was, so long as it was significant enough. Pieces of the boy sparked across their Bargain, a hammer strike that hurled meaning across the Intended’s soul.

Fear. Compassion. Intransigence. Truthfulness. Features of Harker Shoalborn, the child who fought so desperately to survive. The details stacked up against Ironwine’s anvil, eager to be shaped into something…anything.

Now, the true Making began.

"Stone, Star, and Sea. The Three Who Are One.” His voice was a whisper in his cavernous home. A Wright was more than a smith or even the thaumist that the humans saw them as—they were a conduit to the foundations of the world.

Ironwine felt it through the bones of the earth. Felt it amidst the unquenched blaze of the forge. Heard it through the rush of river waves. The world spoke and meaning followed. The Making sifted through the Intended, touching upon his soul, Talent, and Stature alike. Each piece altered it, shifted its shaping toward ends unseen. Ironwine let it flow through him, slipping through the alai across his chest and shoulders until they too lit beneath its influence. He—

There was more.

A burr scored the boy’s soul. Gold flashed, a bolt that struck from clear skies. Ironwine threw up his heavylimbs, alai alight, as an astounding measure of violence descended upon him. It washed over him like tempest waves, a storm of soul rather than the physical. Violence was followed by dominance, and a sharpness that sank deep, riven through carapace and soul alike. A descending blade without purpose.

Just as swiftly as it had begun, it faded.

What are you, Harker Shoalborn? He lowered his heavylimbs and stared toward the basin. What will you become?

Ironwine was eager to find out.

“Stone, Metal, Loam Is Called.”

Nimblelimbs pulled from cabinets, drawers, shelves, and boxes—everything was within easy reach, and nothing was left untested. Crystal, rock, and slate leapt to hand, dropped almost as soon as they were inspected. The rumble of their matrices told much, and Ironwine cast them aside, one after the other, until he found a stone no bigger than the human’s head. He set it upon the anvil and gestured. All at once the rock was shorn of its sediment. It fell to the sides like dirt crumbling beneath the press of his fingers, revealing the lumpy facets of hardened ore.

“Star, Flame, Shadow Is Called.”

He gripped the ore with his heavylimb, the dark color almost glossy compared to his carapace. Thrust above the forge, a pale yellow flame coursed upward—a sword of scourging heat. It bathed his claw, the flame intensifying as it turned orange and red, then blue, before shifting to a brilliant white. Iron claw turned a cherry red along the bottom-most edge as the flame wrapped itself around his heavylimb. The alai burned brilliantly against the charred black, soaking into the ore with every incandescent flare.

Within his ridged grip, the ore was another matter entirely. It heated until it shone like the stars themselves: white and too vivid to even glance at directly. Ironwine did not flinch. His black eyes soaked in the earthbound star, seizing the other end with his second heavylimb and pulling. It stretched beneath his forge-won strength, until it spread before him on the anvil.

“Filter, Sift, Sound. All Must Move Through Spark, Bargain, And Soul Unseen.”

The metal called for the hammer, and Ironwine obliged. Sparks flew. Metal flattened and folded. Again and again, each strike driving imperfections to the surface, where their charred remains flaked off and crumbled to dust.

Sound stretched thin. The air quivered.

It called for heat, and the reflection of the heavens shone down upon the bones of the earth. Pliable again, he folded it. Heat, hammer, and the press of the human's small soul. All of it encompassed Ironwine, drawing him inward. He was the hammer, the fire, the metal itself as he shaped it.

A piece of the Eidhrin, a piece of human, all of it folded into the metal. Turning it into what it must become.

Ironwine's sense were muddy, but his thoughts were clear—far more than they had been in years. He felt the Making, down to the deepest thrum of his second heart. His tail lashed against the forge floor as near-forgotten skill answered his hands.

"Sea, Oil, Salt Is Called."

The heated metal screamed as it hit the basin of Water. Blue-green light leapt upward, turned in an instant to steam that curled into a language even the Thyrsalid had forgotten. Oil was next, and the metal was not cool. Its molten edge lit the liquid aflame, even as it bubbled against its surface, evaporating the entirety of the oil barrel.

The last was salt.

A small dish of the refined mineral sat in the unsepel, flanked by wavelets shaped of lapis and polished malachite. The bowl contained no more than would fit in a nimblepalm—enough to finance a mansion in human standards—but its value went beyond currency. Each flake was condensed from the idle tides, a dangerous treasure that no Wright could do without. Deadly to Talent, it drew out impurities from all that it touched.

Metal included.

Ironwine pressed the Making into the dish. He expected it to deform, the surface bubbling to eject those imperfections he could not cast out—but it did not. The Making thrummed, a low noise that rattled his carapace, and the salt beneath it gave way. The dish’s content melted, but unlike the Water or oil, it did not boil away. It was pulled inward, absorbed by the dark Making in seconds. A fortune in minerals, devoured.

Why?

Ironwine bent toward the problem, testing the Making against surface and tool alike. Time melted just as surely as the refined salt, and the chamber whirled about him as his feet carried him back and forth across his workstation until a haze clouded every pulled material. He swallowed. Blinked, antennae thrashing side to side. Somehow, he stood near the river. The water lapped against the edge of his house, soft but insistent.

His heavylimb held up the boiling cauldron, its belly empty as remnants dripped from its leding edge. Had he replenished the boy's basin already? When? Why couldn’t—

Shadow caught his eye. A piece of night, set against the stone floor dragged his black eyes toward it like a lodestone. A black circle, small and flat and featureless, pressed between the seams of his Wright-made floor. He reached a nimblelimb for it, only to draw a spark across his fingers.

The edge…it’s razor thin. He tried again, this time leveraging it carefully, and held it up against the glowing fungus. It bore neither reflections nor markings, and as he turned it about, that spark returned. Only this time, it was not an edge against iron…but an undeniable flash of gold flickered behind his eyes.

The Making.

It was complete.

But what had he Made?

The basin rang out, a meaty clapper against an enameled bell. Fingers, slick with foulness, tightened on the edge, the squeak of wet palm loud in the sudden quiet. A sharp, percussive hissing joined it—slow at first but gaining speed. The human was alive.

He was laughing.
