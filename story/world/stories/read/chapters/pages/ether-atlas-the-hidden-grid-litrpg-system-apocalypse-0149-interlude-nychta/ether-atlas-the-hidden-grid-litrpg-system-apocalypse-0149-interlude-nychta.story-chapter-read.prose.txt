Interlude

The clumsy tyrant comes with fire and gives the city someone to hate. The patient one comes with a lamp and a folded paper. He spares the city and takes those who run it, and the city wakes not to a conqueror but to an absence, and cannot understand what it has lost until it is too late. — Codex of the Long Watch, Field Observation, 9th Century, Morea

The paraffin lamp in the long room on Akti Miaouli had been turned down to a small bead to save oil, and General Dimitrios Mavros studied the maps by what little light it gave. He drew a green pin out of the Kastella shore, held it for a moment between finger and thumb, and then pushed it back in.

Lieutenant Fotis stood just inside the door with his cap under his arm. He had been standing there long enough to say his piece twice over but had not been given leave to begin.

"The boat," Mavros said, without turning from the maps.

"Cleared the point before dark, sir. It is a caïque, captained by Spyros, out of the Mikrolimano berth. The three foreigners and the Vassilou woman were aboard, with no other cargo."

Fotis had a paper with the information against his thigh though he had read it enough times to no longer need it. "The harbour men confirmed our information that it is headed for Aegina. With this wind they do not put back before tomorrow afternoon at the earliest. To come home tonight they would have to row it, and no man rows that water at night."

Mavros stood motionless as he listened to the report, then lifted his thumb off the map. His back cracked as he straightened himself. He stood a while longer looking at the map, his hands loose at his sides.

"Who saw them go?"

"Corporal Nikos, sir. He and one other followed them from the house to the quay. The woman with the axe carried her weapon openly, and the big one with the shield put the packs aboard himself.”

"Karras. Sotiriou," Mavros said, still looking out of the window next to the maps.

The two of them came in from the outer room. Captain Karras was a short, thick man who filled half a doorway sideways. Major Sotiriou was the oldest man in the building, and the only one whose tunic still had all its buttons.

“Fotis, come inside and take a chair. Karras, Sotiriou, you too. Close the door.”

Fotis gave a quick nod, closed the door and sat on a chair next to Karras and Sotiriou.

Mavros himself did not take a seat. He stood looming in front of the three men, his hands behind his back, and met each man’s eyes.

"Do you know what the politicians in Constantinople were doing while the Ottomans were casting the cannons that would shatter their walls?”

Nobody answered. They knew from experience that these were not meant to be answered by them. And they knew what this night was really about.

“They were arguing over legalities and religious texts. They debated until the swords were at their throats. Our Council is doing the exact same thing while the monsters breed outside our gates."

Mavros finally took a seat and took out three sealed envelopes from his jacket. He kept them on the desk and continued.

“Even great Rome knew that democracy is a luxury of peacetime. Lucius Quinctius Cincinnatus was appointed as an absolute Dictator when Rome was surrounded by enemies. When the enemy is at the gates, debate is treason. They created the office of Dictator because they knew a single, absolute will is what keeps a republic alive."

With one finger Mavros pushed the first envelope towards Karras, the second towards Sotiriou and the third towards Fotis.

"Karras, you have Antoniou. Take a squad. You give him the paper and you wait. He will want to talk. He will offer you coffee and warm words and a chair. Let him and do not be rude but when he has finished, bring him to the garrison."

Karras picked up the envelope and put it inside his jacket. "Málista."

"Sotiriou, you will go to the Archbishop. Take Sergeant Elias and a squad. Nikephoros will invoke his office and God and every authority he has ever held. His household will wake and try to intervene. Do not raise your voices and do not answer any of his questions. Hand him the paper. When he has read it, walk him to the garrison."

"And if he is unwilling to walk, sir."

"Then you stand and wait and politely assert your authority. He will see reason soon enough. The other option is force and he would not want that."

Sotiriou picked up the envelope without another word.

“Fotis. Papadakis. You will take a squad as well, but I don’t think she will be difficult. Of the three she will give the least resistance, she is a smart woman.”

“General.” Fotis waited for Mavros to nod before continuing.

“Shouldn’t we get Vlachos? He is the Academic wing leader.”

“Vlachos is an old fool. Talks too much and means very little. He will rant for a day then settle back into his dusty tomes. Papadakis is the one the wing listens to. Once you bring her in, get someone to bring in her papers as well.”

Fotis reached forward and took the envelope.

Sotiriou looked through the book he carried, confirmed something and said, "And the Council, General. It sits on Thursday."

"There is no Council on Thursday, Sotiriou. There is no one to sit it,” Mavros said.

"No, sir," Sotiriou said and shut the book.

"All three at the same hour. You go when the bell at Agios Nikolaos strikes midnight. Not before." Mavros looked at his officers. "Questions."

“I want you and your squads to remember that we are not tyrants. We are the shield. Rome survived because it knew when to silence the politicians and trust the iron. Constantinople fell because it forgot. Every minute the Council spends debating resource allocation, someone else is butchered. Their democracy is paid for in our soldiers' blood. Today, we will stop paying that tax. We bury the Council's ink and its paper, so that we do not have to bury any more of our men."

“Yes, General,” all three said in unison.

"Then go."

***

Antoniou's house was in the upper quarter, behind the old customs building. Karras knocked on the door three times and stood back. A light came on behind the upstairs shutters. There were footsteps on the stairs after about a minute and the bolt was drawn back.

Christos Antoniou stood in the doorway in a shirt and trousers pulled on over his nightclothes. He carried a small lamp that he set on a side table next to the door. Antoniou looked at the squad of armed men on his doorstep and then looked at Karras.

"Captain, it is after midnight," he said.

Karras did not respond and instead held out the envelope. Antoniou took it, broke the seal and read it by the light of the lamp.

"This is not a valid instrument," Antoniou said, straightening his glasses. "The Council has not authorised a transfer of executive authority. I would know. I keep the minutes."

"The paper is signed, sir."

"I can see who signed it, and one signature does not make it a valid instrument, Captain."

“It does for me and my men,” Karras replied.

Antoniou folded the paper, held it by his side and stood looking at the captain for a long time.

"Karras. Come inside. Let me put the coffee on. I will telephone the — no. Let me send a runner to the Archbishop, and we can resolve this in the morning. There is no need for armed men at this hour. Come in, I have a little left of something better than coffee as well."

Neither did Karras move nor give a response to the invitation.

"You are a career soldier, Captain, and I have always respected the garrison. Tell the general I am prepared to discuss whatever concern he has at the next session. It is Thursday, just two days. That is how governance works."

"The general asks that you come, sir," Karras said.

"Of course he does, and I will come, gladly, on Thursday, when the Council sits. We can settle the whole business of the wall and the men across a table like reasonable people. You are a reasonable man, Captain, and you should not be running errands in the dark. What do you owe him, that you carry papers for him at such an hour?"

"The general did not send me to discuss, sir. He sent me to bring you to the garrison."

“Then you take a message and I follow at first light with my own two feet, you have my word,” Antoniou said with as much authority as he could muster.

“The squad was sent to provide protection. You should come with us.” Karras paused, and then added, “For your own safety.”

Antoniou looked past Karras at the squad of soldiers in the street. A few were looking down at the cobblestone, but most were looking at him. Each carried a weapon.

He stood in the doorway for several seconds without speaking. Then he went inside and came back with his jacket and the small leather diary he carried to every Council session. He locked his door and walked ahead of Karras down the street without looking back. Karras and the squad fell in around him.

***

The Archbishop's residence was three streets from the Church of the Resurrection. The ground-floor windows were still lit. Nikephoros kept late hours and the household knew it. Sotiriou sent Sergeant Elias around to the garden gate to secure it and went to the front door himself.

The housekeeper opened the door, looked at the uniforms and went upstairs without being asked. Nikephoros came down the stairs in the black cassock and the plain wooden cross he wore at home. The lines in his face looked deeper under the sparse candlelight in the hall.

"Major Sotiriou, what brings you at this hour?"

Sotiriou held out the paper in response. The Archbishop took the envelope and read it standing in the hallway, under a painting of Saint George and the dragon. Even in the low light Sotiriou saw the priest's face turn red.

"This is an outrage against the Church and against the lawful authority of this coast." Nikephoros spoke using his pulpit voice, pitched to fill a room. "I am the spiritual authority of the Saronic diocese. I answer to God and to the people of this city, and the Council has not sanctioned this."

"Despóta," Sotiriou said.

"Yiorgo," the Archbishop said, motioning to one of the squad soldiers. "I married you at Ayía Iríni myself. And you, at the back, you carried your father's coffin in the spring, God rest him, I said the words over him and you wept in my church."

Nikephoros came out onto the step in his slippers among the wet leaves. "And now you have come in the dark to take a bishop. Do you know what it is that you are doing? That you are being made to do? There is not a man among you your mother did not bring to be blessed. I have the oil of your christenings still on my thumb."

"Your Beatitude. You are asked to come to the garrison, for your own safety. It is written here," Sotiriou said, his voice a little higher than he wanted.

"It is written, many things are written. Pilate wrote a thing and had it nailed up over a man's head." Nikephoros looked at each of them again, one after another and waited for them to make the right decision, to put their cap back on and go home.

Nobody moved. A man did have his cap in his hand and stood turning it in his hands. The boy Yiorgo was looking down at the ground. But none of them moved.

“Mavros does not have this authority, not in the eyes of the Council and most definitely not in God’s eyes,” the Archbishop spat.

“We have our orders, Despóta, and in the army the general’s word is the word of God, your Grace,” Sotiriou said.

The Archbishop folded the paper and placed it on the hall table next to a set of house keys. He gave Sotiriou and the men behind him a last look and went back inside. When he came down again he had put his panagia on over the cassock, and his cross was on his breast.

He walked out through his gate ahead of the squad. At the corner he stopped, turned and made the sign of the cross over the sleeping street. Then he let them take him up the road and to the hill.

***

Papadakis did not keep them waiting at the door.

Fotis knocked and almost immediately Papadakis opened the door in a dressing gown with her reading glasses pushed up into her grey hair. She read the first two lines of the paper and then stood aside letting the squad enter. None of them had expected that. The men came in awkwardly and stood among her furniture, not knowing what to do.

"Madam, I have been ordered to—" Fotis began.

"I know what you have here," she said, holding up the envelope. “I also know what your orders likely are, who gave them and what you are expected to do.”

She turned the lamp down till it went out and took her coat from the hook by the door. She stepped outside and closed the door behind her.

“I hope you and your men realise that what you are doing is undemocratic and unlawful. Yes?”

“The general has signed the orders, Madam,” Fotis replied as if that simple fact overrode everything else.

***

The bell at Agios Nikolaos struck three. Mavros was at his desk waiting for the information the night would bring in.

Karras came in first.

"Christos Antoniou is in the garrison, sir. Ground floor, the room behind the armoury. He came without resistance. He asked to send a message to his wife and I told him he could do that in the morning."

"Good," Mavros said.

Sotiriou came in next.

"The Archbishop is in the room beside Antoniou, sir. His household is quiet. I posted Sergeant Elias at the residence."

"Sotiriou. Come, sit."

Fotis was the last to come in and he came in with a leather satchel in one hand and a stack of notebooks under his other arm.

“Dr Papadakis is in the garrison, sir. Third room, the one with the barred window." Fotis set the satchel and the notebooks on the table. “As instructed, I had two men go in after we had left and bring all the papers in the house.”

“And all of them are comfortable?”

“Yes, sir, I inspected the rooms myself,” Sotiriou said.

Mavros motioned for Fotis to take a seat and pulled the stack of notebooks towards himself. He opened the one on the top and turned several pages. The pages were filled with maps, drawn in ink in a precise hand. He turned to another page and found a diagram of the Acropolis drawn from the north, marked with distances and symbols and annotations in a shorthand. Beside it there was another diagram of the Acropolis from above, with lines radiating from a point near the summit to five other locations around the Attic coast, each one labelled. There were notes on other monuments and lines drawn from one ruin to another.

He closed the notebook and set it aside with his hand resting on the cover.

“Karras, I want the harbour checkpoints doubled before first light. Our men only. Nobody enters or leaves the port district without their names being written down in the log. Sotiriou, draft a notice. The military authority assumes interim governance of the Saronic coast, effective immediately, until the security situation permits the restoration of civilian oversight. Date it today. I will sign it at dawn."

Sotiriou took out a pen from his jacket pocket and slid a blank sheet of paper towards himself from the stack on the table and began to write.

"One more thing.” Mavros pulled a drawer open and took out another sheet of paper. It had Requisition of skilled persons, for the works and the supply of the garrison written on the top and had several names on it.

Karras leaned over and read the list from the top.

"Conscription, sir?"

"Infrastructure, Captain. Infrastructure and support. The garrison provides the safety and these people make the garrison function. We all have to play our parts for the safety and security of the country."

Mavros slid the paper towards the three soldiers.

"Smiths, boatwrights, harbour hands, leather workers. The people who know where things are and how they move. Start with the circled names tonight, before first light. The rest can come in their own time in the morning, with a day's clothing and whatever tools they own. Make sure that they are informed that the military will take care of their and their families' food and lodging."

Karras read the names and his finger stopped on the third.

"Petros Vassilou, sir. His sister is one of the four, the healer," Karras said, looking up from the list.

"I am aware of whose brother he is, and that is why his name is on the list," Mavros said, not explaining any further.

"Málista." Karras nodded, folded the conscription order and put it inside his jacket.

Mavros leaned back. “Good work tonight. Now see to the rest of it.”
