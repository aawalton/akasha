I arrived at the barricade to the same prevailing sentiments as before. Wide eyes and cautious movements, hesitant smiles and quick whispers.

During the day, the bustling activity around the barricade stood out more than at night. Not driving past at breakneck speeds also helped. Instead, I took my time to watch the ongoing repairs and was struck by how normal the work seemed. Construction workers yelled at each other as they erected a crane to clear away some debris, while others jackhammered the road nearby to refurbish the ruined pavement. The Domain would have seemed almost peaceful if it hadn’t been for the hushed conversations, the scared glances, and the grim orange glow of cigarettes flaring inside far too many crowds.

The scenes I got to watch could have been taken from any town, anywhere in the United States. Though it was road construction, so probably Michigan or Illinois. Only subtle details, like tools manifesting directly in people’s hands with flashes of cyan light kept reminding me that the last 150 years of my life hadn’t been a dream.

Gathering intel from the city watch was easy, once I found back into the patterns I’d developed as Saint Samantha. While I technically wasn’t part of the chain of command, tales about the woman who’d walked into the breach with cold iron in her hands and returned with blood on her face had already spread like wildfire, loosening tongues. However, there was a reason I hadn’t waltzed into the city as the Torchbearer, besides the obvious. Authority bred isolation. Even though I learned what I needed to know, there was a certain distance—either cautious or disparaging—that dominated any conversation I struck up.

Jacky arrived half an hour later, saving me from running out of things to do. When I asked her if the gangsters had given her any trouble, she just snorted a laugh, then grabbed my arm.

“Alright, shopping time!” she said, all matter-of-fact. “You’re going to love Avant Street, but first we have to go Downtown to pick up some groceries.”

“Actually, I should probably stay here to—”

“Shut up.” Jacky said, laughing, so I clicked my teeth together. “There’s always a warning before a surge, silly. Blue light, then red, then the Ferals. Or, well, lately it’s been the other way around, but who cares?”

I pressed my teeth together a bit harder and forced a smile.

“Anyway, no! I won’t have your only memory of Musington be the ruins of Northside and a barricade made of trash. We will go shopping, right now.”

Surprisingly, my smile never wavered. “But I need to—”

“…live a little!” Jacky interrupted. “Let’s go, you’ll love it, I promise.”

She pulled my arm, but I hesitated. The woman was relentless, and truth be told, I did want to learn more about the city, and learn more about how Domains worked when they weren’t under attack. But what about Latirae?

“Look, can we at least move up the barricade a little? Give ourselves a bit more breathing room?” I asked.

Jacky frowned, so I added, “I’m just worried things will go pear-shaped while I’m not here. It’s less than a two-minute sprint from here to the center of town. Not a lot of leeway to rally people.”

She looked over my shoulder, toward Hart Hill, as several thoughts passed behind her eyes. Eventually, she said, “We need to defend the tower… but we— Ah, who cares. You’re here now, you’ll handle it, right?”

“Sure…?” I said, without any clue of what exactly I’d be handling. But before I could ask, Jacky had already stormed off in the direction of a nearby construction crew. A few sentences later, and she waltzed back with a big grin plastered on her face.

“Shopping,” she declared.

“Shouldn’t we stay and—?”

“Can you mix mortar?”

“No…?”

“Pour concrete?”

“…No.”

“Let’s go. I’m driving.”

A short while later, we pulled into the parking lot of a medium-sized grocery store. It wasn’t a chain, or at least none I recognized. Only about half the spots were occupied, which made it easy to find one close to the entrance.

“Not a lot of customers?” I asked as I opened the passenger side door. It was surprisingly heavy. Steel, not fiberglass.

“Not since Northside got sacked,” Jacky replied, donning her sunglasses like a diva.

“Right.”

“Come on, let’s go, slowpoke!” She laughed, dragging me along and out of my thoughts.

The inside of the grocery store was much like I remembered grocery stores in the U.S. People huddling along without paying much attention to anything but their shopping list. Long shelves stocking mostly the same stuff for meters on end, but from different brands. Softly buzzing halogen tubes that battled with the not-quite-sand colored walls. And, of course, a soft jingle echoing through the aisles.

“Huh,” I said, recognizing the tune. “Isn’t that the song Darryl played this morning?”

“Hmm?” Jacky was picking out pork chops, packaged in styrofoam trays and wrapped with plastic. “Oh yeah, of course. They only play his stuff here.”

My eyebrows rose. “Wow. I knew he was good, but—”

Jacky came up and tossed several packs of meat into the shopping cart. “He is that good. He needed publicity, though, that’s where I came in.”

“So that’s your job, then? You’re his manager?”

“Kind of. I have this knack for taking charge, and use it as best I can.”

“I can see that,” I had crossed my arms on the shopping cart handle and dutifully pushed it forward as Jacky wandered along the aisles.

“Yeah, and promoting Darryl is a part of that.” She grabbed a couple of bags of flour and threw them into the cart without even looking. “I also coordinate the City watch and some other community groups.”

“Lots to do.”

“Yep. Wish I had the time to sleep in.”

“Ouch,” I said, deadpan.

“Stop pouting. Milk is next, they don’t deliver anymore, if you can believe it.”

“Outrageous,” I said.

“Right. Stop the snark, too.”

I smirked, but bit down another reply.

“How is all this stuff even made? I didn’t see any farms when I flew in.”

“What, the food?” Jacky asked, still forging ahead.

“Mhm,” I said, because she hadn’t turned around yet.

“Depends on the store. Meat at the butchers’, Dairy at the local distributor, bread from a bakery downtown. The same it’s made in any other town.” Finally, she turned around with a bemused smile. “You should know all that.”

“Works differently where I’m from,” I shrugged.

She went back to browsing with an unreadable smile, but as we went from the spice section to the drugstore, she explained they had several businesses in town that took Cash and transformed it into food. Apparently, the ruined factory up north had produced a lot of the building materials and furniture the town used.

I still had trouble imagining exactly how the process worked. The way Jacky made it sound was that you just poured bills into a slot, cranked a lever, and received a finished product at the other end of the machine. But when I asked why they still had specializations, and why they didn’t just have one of those machines in every home, she just scoffed and shook her head.

Once our shopping cart was so full it took effort to not let it careen into a shelf, Jacky counted off something on her fingers as she muttered to herself, then nodded.

“Done?” I asked.

When she nodded, I heaved the cart in the direction of the entrance, thankful for my new CPU the entire way.

Still, Jacky parked me at the line to the cashier and left, because she had forgotten something. She was gone for a long time, and the line proceeded at a steady pace. When she came back, I had already packed all our stuff on the belt and was next in line. The problem was, I had no idea how to pay. Jacky might have been able to summon a wad of cash on demand, but I had no idea how to do that.

By the time she returned, I was sweating bullets.

“God, I feel like a child,” I said, feeling sheepish. Why was I telling her that? I blamed the relief.

“How so?” Jacky was going over the mountain of items on the belt, oblivious to my thoughts.

“My mom used to do the same thing. Leave me in the queue to get something last-minute. She never gave me any money, though, and I was terrified of holding up the line.”

“Don’t be a fool, you have money now!” There was no force behind the words. She was going through the items to check if we’d forgotten anything.

“Anyway, find what you wanted?” I asked.

“Hmm?” Jacky looked at me as though she’d just realized I was there “Oh, no, they didn’t have it.”

The cashier rung us up, and smiled when she saw Jacky. It was hard to tell, but she must have pressed a button on the register, because suddenly, our total had sunken by about 25%.

Jacky just pulled the Cash out of her coat.

The cashier kept staring in our direction while the boy behind the checkout bagged our purchases. Was she nervous about the favor? No. It was my presence. If I hadn’t been sure I’d cleaned Arx this morning, I would have checked myself for bloodstains. As it was, I gave her an encouraging smile as we grabbed our bags and left. She pretended not to notice.

“You don’t seem like a knife,” Jacky said as we walked across the parking lot.

“Sorry?”

“A Dagger. You don’t seem like one. You’re too nice, too soft.”

“You sound like my old boss. He told me to sharpen up, all the time.” I smirked. I stood behind the trunk, waiting for her to unlock it so I could toss our groceries inside.

Jacky shook her head as she dug through the pockets of her cardigan for her car keys. “You got it wrong. I like it when you’re nice. Here, hold this one second—.” She passed me the bags she’d been holding and searched with both hands. “I’m just saying that… Ah. Here it is!”

She held the key aloft like a trophy kill and said, “Your real name isn’t Dagger.”

I didn’t respond, she unlocked the car, and that seemed to be the end of that conversation. But after I put the groceries into the trunk and walked around to the passenger door, she was waiting for me. Her eyebrow was raised inquisitively, her hand planted against the door to hold it shut.

“Your real name isn’t Jacky, either,” I said.

“You’re deflecting, that’s bad for you,” Jacky said, with a smile that felt far too kind for this situation.

“Who cares, Jacky? I didn’t pick the name if that’s what you’re asking, but it suits the job.” It was a half-truth, and we both knew it.

“Dagger, that’s a weapon. And the job, that’s mercenary. I don’t want a weapon or a mercenary living under a roof with Darryl.” Jacky’s hand shifted, still splayed against the car door like a spider’s web. “And I certainly don’t want both.”

“Well then why’d you invite me to stay?” I asked. I’d crossed my arms, but found myself listening more closely than I’d thought.

“Because clearly, you need guidance,” Jacky said. Her hand left the door and found mine. “And in these times, I could use all the friends I can get. Loyal friends.”

“So?”

“Loyalty doesn’t lie. Friends tell friends their name.”

In that moment, standing in a parking lot full of vintage cars, feeling another woman’s fingers curl around mine as the autumn leaves whirled and come to rest, perfectly camouflaged, in her hair, it was easy to forget that magic existed, and names carried power.

“Sam,” I said.

Jacky laughed. “Like the Torchbearer?”

“The same.” Why did my cheeks grow hot? Disconcerting.

“Well, then get in the car, Sam. I’ve made a couple of catches today, but there’s still shopping to be done, and you will tell me your opinion on these shoes I’ve been eying.”

We returned home after sundown, with me carrying bags full of loot (mostly hers), and Jacky’s arm slung around my hip. With the car parked near the gates of the old fort, the only sounds left in the damp air were the wind brushing against the Tower and the distant, lonely prattle of the city.

That changed as we approached the house. The music sneaked in like a tired parent at night. At first I couldn’t even tell it was music at all, just a sound that I instantly liked. Then, as the porch came into view, and the notes became more obvious, I still couldn’t tell exactly what I was listening to. Only when I saw Darryl sitting on the porch, his little tin of drugs closed in front of him, did I realize what I was listening to.

He was humming, harmonizing with the cicadas and the electric buzz of the streetlights.

“Darryl!” Jacky said, and his head snapped up. The smile he’d been wearing brightened, then withered as he saw me. Finally, he noticed the placement of Jacky’s hand. His stare turned to daggers.

Channeling my namesake, I stayed unfazed. The porch creaked under my boots as I carried up the various bags we’d brought. Jacky had run ahead and pulled her boyfriend in for a kiss, so by the time I set down the spoils, the man didn’t look like he was going to strangle me.

“Alrighty!” Jacky said. “I’m going to go cook. You guys play nice.”

“I’ll help,” I said, but before I could so much as grab the first bag, Jacky had already gathered them all up like it was nothing.

“No. You did enough already. Find some moderation.”

“But—”

“No.” With that, she vanished inside.

“Better listen to her,” Darryl said. “Woman always gets what she wants, in the end.”

I didn’t really know what to say to that, but if the only thing I was good at were making enemies, Novus Apex never would have been built. I could recognize an olive branch when I saw one.

Darryl started rolling up one of his joints, and reluctantly nodded at it, offering me the first pull.

“That shit really isn’t good for you.”

He shrugged. “Life isn’t, either.”

“Maybe. But we can make it better.”

He scoffed, a sound like the hinges on a broken screen door. “Don’t see how.” I didn’t push any further. How could I, given everything?

So with an inward sigh, I nodded at the Tower. “Jacky tries.”

Darryl frowned as he licked the paper of his cigarette. “I guess. Lots of instruments, mics, new, fancy recording deck. State-of-the-art. Antenna amplifies every signal, clear as crystal. Great view, up high, far from the hustle and bustle of…” he trailed off with a sigh. “It’s a good studio.” He closed the little tin with his drugs, and it shut with a metallic snap.

“There’s a studio up there?”

“Where do you think we’re recording the show?” he mumbled around the joint in his mouth. He was patting his pockets for a lighter.

“I dunno, I thought you had a room upstairs or something.”

He just snorted a laugh, punctuated by the clink of his lighter. The first pull he took was greedy, the second and third—following like chained inmates—he savored. Then he began glitching. The stench of burnt plastic and copper permeated the air.

“I’m going to take a shower,” I said.

He chuckled, and his head snapped around in a glitch of motion. His eyes were wide, the curl of his lips unhinged. “Sure, sure, sure. See you later, Sam.”

Standing with one foot in the door, I hesitated, but when he began cackling, I went inside. The pained howls of his drug-extorted laughter haunted me all the way to the bedroom.
