Chapter Thirteen: Crucible

Crucible came into sight as Vash's ship descended. Apparently, we had been hovering far above the world for some time now. I held Elisa's hand as we descended, studying the planet through the wide viewport in the ship's observation deck. The sight was breathtaking, though not in the way Earth's blue-green splendor might have been. This was alien beauty, harsh and uncompromising.

"It's so... red." Elisa said, her small face pressed against the transparent material, breath fogging the surface slightly.

The observation was childishly simple yet perfectly accurate. Crucible was indeed red, in every conceivable variation of the color. Crimson plains stretched toward russet mountains. Vermilion clouds swirled in a scarlet sky. Even the large bodies of water appeared to be the color of freshly spilled blood.

Once upon a time, I may have laughed. I found there was little laughter to be had in the world now. Not since that night in our home, not since Lynn and Jackson had been taken from us. But Elisa was still able to admire things. Even when she woke up in terror during the night, reaching for my arms, for comfort, she could still see something new, and find wonder in it.

I envied her that capacity. To witness horror and still find beauty afterward seemed a gift I had lost somewhere along the way. Perhaps it had been stripped from me along with my family, or perhaps it had been deliberately excised during my training under Vash. Either way, I looked at the crimson world below us and saw only a new battlefield, a new challenge to overcome.

The death of innocence is another pain, one I have often observed. It can be lost in a moment, but often it is an unseen death. A gradual fading, like color bleaching from fabric left too long in the sun. Sometimes you don't realize it's gone until you try to recall the last time you felt true wonder, true joy untainted by cynicism or fear.

Try and remember when you lost yours. When you stopped looking at wonderful things and marveling at them. When you realized how truly fallen our world was. I bet you'll have a hard time placing it. Was it a specific moment? A particular realization? Or did it slip away day by day, eroded by accumulated experience, by witnessed cruelty, by understanding of how things truly worked beneath the pleasant surface we all pretend exists?

Innocence is a gradual death. Much like life. We begin dying the moment we're born, cells breaking down even as others multiply. We lose pieces of ourselves along the way, small deaths that prepare us for the final one. Innocence is perhaps the first significant loss, the first step toward the grave.

The ship continued its descent, passing through layers of atmosphere, each deeper layer revealing more detail of the world below. Crucible was a world covered in every shade of red one could imagine. A subtle darkness wove through all of it, and Flux storms waged a constant war against the planet. I could see them from our approach—vast cyclones of visible energy, crackling with power, sweeping across the landscape like living entities, hungry and searching.

Dark spires, and buildings with architecture I couldn't think of building dotted the landscape. Impossible angles, materials that seemed to absorb light rather than reflect it, structures that appeared to defy gravity and conventional engineering principles. Nothing resembled the houses I had built back on Earth, the simple, functional, welcoming structures meant for family living. These were monuments to power, to dominance, to alien aesthetics that cared nothing for human comfort or comprehension.

Ships, sleek, black, and pulsing with runes flew through the blood soaked skies, landing on various platforms in a huge arrival port built for the purpose. The vessels varied in size and configuration, but all shared that distinctive Nyxen characteristic of appearing both organic and mechanical simultaneously, living metal shaped by will as much as technology.

Our landing was smooth, almost imperceptible. A slight shift in gravity, a subtle change in the ambient sound of the engines, and we had arrived. Vash appeared at the entryway to the observation deck, her perfect features as impassive as ever.

"Prepare for disembarkation," she said simply, before turning and gliding away, her movements liquid and precise.

The crew sprang into action at her command, a choreographed routine they had clearly performed countless times. Equipment was secured, systems checked, protocols observed. I watched with detached interest, still clutching Elisa's hand as if she might disappear should I release her.

Lyra, my daughter's caretaker during our journey, appeared at our side. Unlike other Nyxen, whose expressions ranged from cold indifference to outright contempt, Lyra showed genuine warmth toward Elisa. Her smile reached her eyes, her voice softened when she spoke to the child. She was an anomaly I had not yet deciphered.

"Come, little one," she said to Elisa. "Let's get your things. We have arrived at your new home."

Home. The word rang hollow in my mind, a concept that had been shattered along with so much else. Home was gone, buried with Lynn and Jackson in the ruins of our old life. This place, this red world of alien architecture and Flux storms, could never be home. It was simply the next arena, the next battlefield in my ongoing struggle for survival and power.

We departed as a group, Lyra whispering to Elisa as we walked, pointing out various buildings and sights to her. I found it a bit odd how kind Lyra was for a Nyxen. She seemed to genuinely care for my daughter's well-being, taking time to explain things, to answer questions, to provide reassurance. It was behavior I had witnessed from no other member of their species, not even those who treated me with some measure of respect after I had killed Zorin.

I would learn it was because she was not Nyxen at all, but rather a Har'Ji slave. The Har'Ji, a faction that embraced different aspects of the Flux than the Nyxen, particularly the Vital and Echo aspects, were philosophical opponents to Nyxen ideology. Their members occasionally ended up in Nyxen service through various circumstances, bringing their different temperament and values with them. Perhaps if I had known that then, events may not have unfolded as they had. I might have asked different questions, made different choices, found different allies.

No matter now.

The spaceport was a cacophony of sounds, smells, and sights that assaulted the senses. Creatures of various species moved purposefully through the vast complex, some humanoid, others decidedly not. The air carried scents I couldn't identify, spices or chemicals or biological markers entirely foreign to my human olfactory system. Announcements in languages I couldn't comprehend echoed from hidden speakers, occasionally interrupted by what I assumed were names or flight designations.

Vash led the way, crowds parting around her, heads bowed low, muttering in that strange language of theirs. Her reputation clearly preceded her, generating fear or respect or some combination of the two that manifested as immediate deference. No one made eye contact, no one approached, no one impeded her progress in any way. It was as if she moved within a bubble of authority that repelled all who might delay or inconvenience her.

I walked just behind her, Elisa and Lyra at my side, the remainder of our party following in formation. I noticed the stares we received, the curious or calculating gazes that tracked our progress through the terminal. Being human marked us as unusual here, specimens to be assessed and categorized. I met those stares with a cold gaze of my own, challenging any who looked too long or too intently. After killing Zorin, after embracing the darker aspects of my nature, I found it easier to project a dangerous aura, to communicate wordlessly that I was not prey.

Soon, we came to a huge building, a mansion of black, and pulsating crimson. It sat on an elevated plateau overlooking what appeared to be a sprawling city, though "city" seemed an inadequate term for the vast complex of structures that stretched to the horizon. The mansion itself was imposing, dominating the skyline, its architecture a statement of power and privilege.

The grounds were immaculately maintained, with what I assumed were gardens consisting of red and black vegetation I couldn't identify. Creatures that resembled insects but were easily the size of small birds flitted among the plants, their carapaces reflecting the crimson sunlight in iridescent patterns.

We entered through a gate, opening for Vash as if they were not there at all. No guard checked credentials, no security system scanned our party. The gate simply recognized its master and yielded, as it was designed to do. I wondered if it was technology or Flux-based, then realized the distinction might be meaningless here. The Nyxen seemed to blend the two seamlessly, using Flux to enhance and direct what might otherwise be pure machinery.

Huge doors opened as we neared the mansion, and I saw my first alien. Not humanoid like the Nyxen, who despite their cold perfection and inhuman grace could still pass for human at a glance, but truly, undeniably alien.

A draxi they were called, a lizard like people distantly related to dragons. Kobolds in earth mythology were inspired off of them. He was dressed in a dark suit, eyes yellow, with a crimson line for pupils. His scales were every shade of yellow you could find, from pale cream to deep gold, arranged in patterns that might have denoted age or status or simply genetic variation.

"Welcome home, Lady Vash," his voice was a rippling, discordant hiss. Like an instrument out of tune. The words were perfectly comprehensible, but the sound grated on the ear, unnatural to human hearing.

Vash said nothing, striding by him as if he were not even there. The draxi showed no reaction to this treatment, no offense or resentment. It was clearly the expected interaction, the natural order of things in this household.

We followed, and more staff lay beyond. Draxi primarily, but also other species I couldn't identify. All moved with the efficient precision of well-trained servants, attending to tasks without being instructed, anticipating needs before they were expressed. The mansion's interior was as imposing as its exterior—vast chambers with soaring ceilings, furnishings that appeared both ancient and cutting-edge, artworks that depicted scenes of conquest and dominance.

Soon, we were led to our rooms, with Elisa sharing one with me. The chamber was larger than the entire first floor of our house back on Earth, with a sleeping area, sitting area, bathing facilities, and what appeared to be a small training space, complete with weapons rack. The furnishings were luxurious but subdued, comfort clearly secondary to function.

Elisa explored the space with cautious curiosity, testing the bed's softness, examining the strange fixtures in the bathing area, peering out the large window that offered a panoramic view of the crimson landscape. She seemed neither intimidated nor impressed, simply accepting this new environment as children often do, with remarkable adaptability.

"Is this where we'll live now, Daddy?" she asked, her voice small but steady.

"For a while," I answered, unwilling to make promises I couldn't keep. The future was too uncertain, my path too unclear, to offer her false assurances.

She nodded, as if this made perfect sense, and continued her inspection of our new quarters. I watched her, marveling at her resilience, her ability to continue functioning despite everything she had witnessed, everything she had lost. Children are remarkably adaptable, I supposed, their minds not yet rigid with expectations and attachments.

Once we were settled, Vash appeared. She entered without knocking, without announcing herself, her presence suddenly filling the doorway like a predator materializing from shadow.

"I require you in my office, initiate. Come along. The girl will be fine on her own for now."

Her tone brooked no argument, no negotiation. This was not a request but a command, delivered with the absolute certainty that it would be obeyed. I had learned by now not to question such directives, not to show hesitation or reluctance. Those were weaknesses in the Nyxen view, and weakness was not tolerated.

I kissed Elisa on the hair before departing with Vash. A small gesture of affection, of reassurance. A promise without words that I would return, that she was not alone in this strange new world. She smiled up at me, a brief flash of the child she had been before our lives were shattered, before continuing her exploration of the room.

Vash led me through corridors that seemed to stretch endlessly, past chambers and galleries and spaces whose functions I could not determine. The mansion was a labyrinth, designed perhaps to confuse visitors or to provide multiple routes of escape in case of attack. Or perhaps the Nyxen simply preferred complexity for its own sake, a reflection of their convoluted nature.

We arrived at last at a huge office of dark wood with veins of red. It was hardly decorated, everything there served a function. A massive desk dominated the space, its surface bare except for a single device that resembled a computer terminal but without any visible input methods. Shelves lined the walls, filled with objects that might have been books or data storage devices or something else entirely. The floor was polished to mirror brightness, reflecting the crimson light that streamed through tall windows.

"Soon, you will start the Initiate Games. It is time for you to learn what that means. Sit down."

I complied, taking one of two chairs positioned before the desk. Vash did not sit immediately, instead moving to stand before the window, gazing out at the crimson landscape as if searching for something specific among the alien cityscape.

"The Initiate Games are not a competition as you might understand the term," she began, still facing away from me. "They are a culling. A process by which the weak are eliminated and the strong are identified. Many enter. Few survive. Fewer still prosper."

She turned then, fixing me with those dark violet eyes that seemed to see through flesh to whatever lay beneath.

"You will be placed in a Flux Dungeon with other initiates from across the multiverse. Some will be like you, newly awakened to the Flux. Others will have trained for years in preparation. All will be seeking the same prize—advancement, sponsorship, power."

Flux Dungeon. The term was unfamiliar, though I had heard Vash mention it briefly during our journey. I imagined some sort of labyrinth, a maze to be navigated, but sensed there was more to it than simple navigation.

"The dungeon is a construct of pure Flux energy, shaped and controlled by the masters of the Games. It contains challenges, obstacles, rewards... and dangers. Flux beasts roam its corridors. Traps await the unwary. And of course, the other initiates themselves are often the greatest threat."

She moved to her desk then, activating the device with a gesture. A holographic display sprang to life, showing what appeared to be a map or schematic, though of what I couldn't determine. The image shifted, zoomed, highlighted certain areas in pulsing red.

"The objective is simple in concept, difficult in execution. In the center of the dungeon lies the Cathedral. To enter it, you must obtain four Keys, hidden throughout the complex. Once inside, you must defeat the Guardian and claim the prize."

The holographic display shifted again, showing what I assumed was this Cathedral—a massive structure of impossible architecture, pulsing with energy, surrounded by four distinct symbols that must represent the Keys.

"Sounds straightforward enough," I said, though I knew it would be anything but. The Nyxen never made anything simple or easy. There would be complications, twists, hidden challenges beyond the obvious ones.

Vash's lips curved in what might generously be called a smile, though it contained no warmth, no genuine amusement.

"Indeed. So straightforward that ninety percent of initiates die in the attempt."

The statement was delivered with clinical detachment, as if discussing rainfall statistics rather than mass mortality. To Vash, to the Nyxen, this was simply the natural order. The weak perished. The strong survived. The system functioned as intended.

"And those who succeed?" I asked, already suspecting the answer.

"They become apprentices, sponsored by Lords and Ladies who see potential worth cultivating. They receive training, resources, opportunities denied to others. They take their first true steps on the path to power."

She deactivated the holographic display with another gesture, the image collapsing into nothingness.

"You already have an advantage most initiates lack. You have my interest. I have invested in your preliminary training, prepared you for what is to come. This is... unusual. Traditionally, Lords and Ladies choose their apprentices from among those who complete the Games, not before."

I had suspected as much, had puzzled over why Vash had selected me from my dying world when she presumably had access to countless potential initiates across multiple worlds. The answer had something to do with my bloodline, with whatever the status screen had identified as "Nexus Bloodline," but Vash had been frustratingly vague on details.

"So why the Games at all? Why not simply declare me your apprentice now?"

Vash's expression hardened, the temperature in the room seeming to drop several degrees.

"Tradition. Protocol. Politics." She spat the words as if they tasted foul. "The Games serve multiple purposes beyond simple selection. They are entertainment for the masses. They are political currency for the Lords and Ladies. They are a showcase of potential, a market of talent. And they are a test, a final verification that my assessment of your worth is accurate."

She moved closer, her perfect features just inches from mine, her voice dropping to a near whisper.

"Do not disappoint me, Varus Thorne. I have eliminated one failed investment already. I will not hesitate to do so again."

The threat was clear, the reference to Zorin unmistakable. I had replaced one apprentice; I could be replaced just as easily. In the Nyxen hierarchy, everyone was expendable, everyone replaceable. Value was measured in power and potential, not loyalty or sentiment.

"When do the Games begin?" I asked, refusing to show intimidation despite the implicit threat.

"Tomorrow," Vash replied, straightening and resuming her more clinical demeanor. "You will be transported to the dungeon entrance with the other initiates. From that point, you are on your own. Alliances, strategies, methods—all are yours to determine. The only rule is survival."

She moved back behind her desk, a clear dismissal.

"Rest. Prepare. Consider your approach carefully. The Games last until completion or death, however long either may take."

I rose, processing this information, calculating odds and strategies already. I had survived the loss of my family, the destruction of my world, months of brutal training under Vash. I would survive this too, would use it as another step toward power, toward ensuring Elisa's safety and my own advancement.

"One last question," I said, pausing at the doorway. "Elisa. Will she be safe while I'm in the Games?"

A flicker of something crossed Vash's face, too quickly to identify. Irritation? Amusement? Calculation?

"The child will remain here, under Lyra's care. Her safety depends entirely on your performance in the Games. Succeed, and she prospers. Fail, and..."

She let the implication hang in the air, unspoken but unmistakable. Another threat, another leverage point. The Nyxen understood motivation, understood that different individuals required different incentives. For some, the promise of power was enough. For others, like me, the protection of loved ones provided stronger motivation.

I nodded once, accepting both the information and the implicit threat, before turning to leave. The path ahead was clear now, the challenge defined. Enter the dungeon. Find the keys. Reach the Cathedral. Defeat the Guardian. Survive at all costs.

For Elisa. For power. For the future I was determined to create from the ashes of my past.

As I walked back to our quarters, I thought about what awaited me in the Flux Dungeon. Dangers, certainly. Opportunities, perhaps. A testing ground where theory would become practice, where training would be applied in life-or-death situations. A crucible of sorts, much like the planet itself, where impurities would be burned away, leaving only the strongest, most valuable elements.

Crucible. The name seemed appropriate now, in ways I hadn't appreciated before. This world, this situation, this challenge, all were designed to test, to transform, to refine through fire and pressure. I had entered one crucible when I lost my family and my world. Now I would enter another, by choice rather than circumstance.

What would emerge from this refining fire remained to be seen. But I was determined it would be something powerful, something capable, something that would never again be victim to forces beyond its control.

Whatever it took. Whatever I had to become.
