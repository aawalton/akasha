General Ragnerus was sitting in the center of the magic circle when I arrived. He wasn’t contained by it, yet he filled the space in a way that made it feel as if he was restraining himself. There was still the occasional pink crack appearing along his chest, but they were more hairline fractures than the giant ruptures I had initially caused. Even his long, black, and chain-bound leather trenchcoat had them, mostly around the frayed edges.

He still needs more time to heal, but he must know there’s a timing issue, too.

Bodhil and Beigaldi stood at attention on either side of the door. They acknowledged me as I entered, putting in an obvious effort to appear professional before their glorious leader. I nodded, then walked to Ragnerus and took a seat on the floor across from him.

“You wished to speak with me,” I said.

He looked at me, and I looked back.

One second. Two. Five.

I’m sure he’s collecting his thoughts.

Ten seconds. Fifteen.

Seventeen seconds is actually a long pause. Studies suggest most people can’t handle seventeen seconds of direct eye contact. Or maybe it was eight seconds. You’d think I’d remember, since they were my own studies conducted for Psych 101.

I made it what, 3 seconds normally?

It’s a good thing I kept switching majors.

A minute.

I’m not going to be the first one to speak. I already did!

Ninety seconds.

Maybe I should stop blinking? Except staring contests are stupid. I’m not letting my eyes dry out for no reason. This is a test, and I absolutely cannot lose.

Two minutes fifty seconds, by my count.

I lost it twice, but it seems about right.

What food haven’t I eaten lately? The replicator magic thing only makes Speranzan dishes. Since demons don’t need to eat, they’re not particularly helpful when I want to find a new recipe. And spices are seemingly non-existent. Or maybe that costs too much animus.

Four minutes.

What if I start laughing? Will everything fall apart then? But this is so ridiculous—

“I have observed your actions,” he said finally, his voice low and controlled.

“Alright,” I said, because what else was I supposed to say?

“You allowed me to speak to my subordinates without your presence.”

“Yes.”

He paused again, and I feared another long wait. Luckily, my fake patience earlier was rewarded.

“I will help you get into the Holy City. I will be the distraction you need on the battlefield in Chairo. I will even let the residents escape once you bring the holy crystals down.”

He narrowed his eyes, the left one gleaming around the scar that still ran into its eyelids. “If you grant me one request.”

I glanced at Beigaldi and Bodhil, but their faces were trained on the General.

What exactly had they briefed him on?

I turned back to the General. “What is it?”

He looked over my left shoulder. “Tell her my Purpose,” he commanded.

I spun around to catch them straightening in unison.

“Epiales, forever bound in place by the chains of Euphridia,” Bodhil began, her tone oddly reverent.

“Gave life,” Beigaldi continued, his hands clasped, “by ripping free his right arm.”

“Thus was General Ragnerus created, Firstborn of Epiales! Given Purpose by our King to be the sword and shield of all Demonkind! To destroy those who would seek our destruction. To protect and serve!”

They both saluted sharply and shouted. “All Hail General Ragnerus!”

General Ragnerus gave a single, satisfied nod. “Go and advise the Otherworlder of my earlier suggestion. Dismissed.”

Wait—what suggestion? Nora will surely talk to me about it first, right?

They both faded with a deep bow.

“That was quite a story,” I murmured, deciding to focus on him for the time being. “But I didn’t catch your request in it.”

“A necessary fiction,” he replied, inhaling and shutting both eyes. “The truth is different.”

And then he told me.

Epiales, bound and desperate, descending into the madness that would define the rest of his existence, had torn off his own arm trying to destroy himself. Ragnerus had emerged from his arm, even as the chains brought Epiales back together.

He wasn’t created with intent, as the story went, but he was assigned a Purpose by Epiales—and not the one shared with his subordinates.

Go to Euphridia. Pray for what’s left of my soul.

By this time, Paradise had been sealed, and Relias had overseen the migration of humanity. Chairo had been built, its High Temple recently dedicated to The Goddess herself.

Chairo’s defenses, however, were already in place. Holy Orbs, predecessors to the Holy Crystals, kept him out for the thousands of years to come. He had tried sneaking, then breaching the walls, each failure driving him to create more armies, to amass more animus, to keep trying.

He didn’t say it, but his face hardened, and I could only imagine his desperation growing, all to fulfill the one request that might have brought his accursed father peace.

“My demand,” he said, before I had a chance to make a fool of myself, “is exactly that. When Chairo’s doors open, I will pray in the High Temple. Unaccosted.”

“That’s it?” I asked.

“That is all.”

I took a deep breath. “I’ll be honest with you. Euphridia may not even hear you, let alone grant your request.”

I wonder... Would Euphridia have relented once he heard his pleas? What if Relias hadn’t intentionally blocked Ragnerus?

The outcome might have been the same, but it must have worried Relias enough that he took steps to ensure their meeting never took place.

The General eventually shrugged. “I am aware of this. Regardless, I will fulfill my assigned Purpose. The response, if any, is not within my authority to control.”

This is how he can get closure regarding his father.

“You have my word,” I said. “Help us, and you are free to present yourself.”

He nodded once, his face drawn.

“You’ll need to speak with your brother about—”

“I have no brother.” Though loud, the words were automatic and flat. They were worn smooth by him, either by saying them to his subordinates or by merely thinking them to himself over the years.

“I’m going to insist that you—”

His left eye flared, blazing crimson. He howled, clasping his hands over it, but the eye seared through his palm.

I flinched as the chamber filled with red light and the runes along the walls began flashing.

Ragnerus disappeared, and I found myself in ancient darkness.

The shaggy, maddened beast that was Epiales was there in the illusion of the past, and then Olethros appeared before him, already kneeling.

Epiales roared. “You chose to spare him.”

Olethros’s head dipped lower. “You gave me the choice, Your Majesty.”

“You made the wrong one.”

A bolt of lightning struck Olethros, flashing in place as he resisted, but ultimately succumbed to a scream. When it ended, he said, “I did as you commanded, Your Majesty. Without his eye, Ragnerus cannot enter Chairo.”

“Give it to me.”

Olethros steeled himself, still on his knees. “If I, as weak as I am, could take it, what will you gain from absorbing it? Only his weakness.”

Lightning crackled above him again, threatening to strike, but it disappeared as quickly as it came.

“Leave. Take your hollow trophy with you, Phorutos. And do not think that I am at all pleased with you, for trash is only pleasing when it is eradicated.”

The darkness flickered. Although I had not returned to Delta XII, I was in a familiar stone room. There was fresh, black blood dripping down the walls, with deep gouges running across the stone. A table in the middle of the room was burning, all but its center reduced to red coals.

The Tower of Olethros.

Olethros, transparent and flickering, lay on the floor in one of the larger pools of blood.

He groaned and turned his head slowly, one hollow green eye opening.

“Why did you hide from him?”

A crimson-irised eye formed from errant black mist, only to stare at him.

“You’re stuck here now, too.”

Its pupil constricted.

Olethros turned his head away, his eye closing. “Begone from my presence. I’m not happy with either of you right now, and I doubt I ever will be.”

Delta XII swerved back into place around us. The General’s left eye was dim now, squinted, and leaking a clear liquid.

If I were to call it tears, surely he would object.

“It seems,” he said slowly, his face twitching, “that there is a part of me that wishes to speak with my brother.”
