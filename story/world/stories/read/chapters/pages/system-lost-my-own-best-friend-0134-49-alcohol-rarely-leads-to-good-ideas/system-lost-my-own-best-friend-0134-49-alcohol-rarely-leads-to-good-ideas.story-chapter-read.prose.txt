The party isn’t nearly as structured as Rara led me to believe. Occasionally, the hostess will introduce some new entertainment, and I gather from observation that it’s considered polite for the guests to spend at least a few moments giving the entertainers their full attention, but otherwise guest participation is kept to a minimum, allowing us to wander around and mingle to our hearts’ content.

The entertainments themselves are also not quite as varied as I expected. Music, song, and poetry are all pretty common, and it seems like the “surprise” has more to do with the specific performer and whether their performance is novel. Also, music and song are very different things. Fa’aun instruments are often wind-based and have a distinct reverberation to them that’s interesting but really different from the sort of music I’m used to. Music is never accompanied by song or vice versa, and the songs don’t have any lyrics. Instead, Fa’aun singing is a throaty, guttural thing more akin to yodeling—though their different vocal chords are capable of making some really neat sounds.

I don’t know if it’s just the nature of this party, or if there’s a broader cultural implication, but it’s mostly solo performers. Aside from those classic forms of entertainment, there are quite a few cases of what I’m mentally grouping together as “skill demonstrations.” Like a talent show, but...more. A chef turns an entire huge fish into neatly-sliced and bite-sized pieces of sashimi in less than thirty seconds, including plating, and by the time the serving staff has collected the plates to bring them around for tasting, the waste is gone, the stage is spotless, and even the knife sparkles as though it’s never been used.

The fish is good, too. I was worried that eating sashimi would bring back some unpleasant memories, but the texture is soft and pleasant, with a subtle savory flavor that practically melts in my mouth. Compared to the nasty, chewy, gamey lizard meat, it’s night and day. And the less said about rock snail meat, the better.

It goes nicely with my drink, which is some kind of spirit with a slightly fruity aftertaste. Aftertaste because I can’t taste much of anything at all while it’s in my mouth. The fumes singe my sinuses and make my eyes water with how ridiculously strong it is. Not usually the sort of drink I go for, but if I’m going to be nursing one drink all night I may as well make it count.

The drink being so strong means that I can take tiny sips of it while I nibble on the fish and watch a man recite a poem that seems to be mostly silent apart from the occasional halting syllable. Evie’s auto-translation does us no good trying to understand it, but I get the impression that he’s playing with Fa’aun’s system of compound words to intentionally cherrypick the stops and glue them together into something that’s technically grammatically sound, but utterly incomprehensible. It does draw a few laughs from the crowd, though, so I guess it still makes a little bit of sense.

I cast a sidelong glance at Draga, who is engaged in his own conversation nearby, but still making sure he has a clear line of sight to both Rara and I. He’s been making sure I don’t wander off too far.

“How can I help you?” I ask politely. “I don’t believe we’ve been introduced.”

“It’s no trouble,” he says. “I asked around for your name before approaching. It was the least I could do after what you’ve done for us.”

I blink. “After what?”

“Ah, forgive me—I should introduce myself. I am Ssri Keeno.”

His name is really odd, and I furrow my brows as I try to make sense of it. His pronunciation of Ssri sounds more like “Siri” but I can tell that he’s actively suppressing his urge to break up the vowels. It reminds me of when Fa’aun try extra hard to pronounce my name. Then there’s his clan name—Keeno, or “Ke’eno’o” which shares the same root as “Ke’ena’a” or Clanless.

“Clan...forsaken? Rejected? Abandoned?” I try, the words all sounding very similar in Fa’aun.

“Any would be true, but ‘forsaken’ is the correct one,” he provides. “It means that I was born to Clan Foren, but gave up that status, along with my original name.”

“Oh. Why?”

Seriously, she never even gave me a hint that she was married. Or that she’s a woman. Wait—I’m making bad assumptions here. He’s been using gender neutral language for them. I probably shouldn’t be drawing conclusions.
