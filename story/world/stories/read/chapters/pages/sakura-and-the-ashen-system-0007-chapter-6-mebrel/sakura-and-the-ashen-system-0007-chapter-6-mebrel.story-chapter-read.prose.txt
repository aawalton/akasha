Sakura woke early, with the dawn. For a few seconds she simply lay there, looking at her hand with the strange mark on it and listening to the nearly burned-out wood crackle in the hearth. The night had not brought her any real rest. Sakura kept slipping into a heavy half-sleep, then jerking back out of it, startled by memories of the subway, the basement, Crowlis’s words, and the ash that had sunk into her skin. But in the morning, strangely enough, it felt a little easier. For the first time since all of this began, she had at least one simple task: get to Mebrel and not say too much to anyone.

Her cloak lay folded beside her, still damp at the edges and sagging on one side where the hidden pocket held the book. Sakura looked at it for a second before the thought finally settled properly in her head.

The Book of the Obsidian Hierophant. She had kept it in the cloak because, at the time, she had not known what else to do with it. Now that she actually knew she had an inventory, leaving the most dangerous thing she owned inside a piece of old fabric suddenly seemed almost impressively stupid. Too easy to notice, too easy to steal, too easy to lose.

Sakura sat up carefully, pulled the book out, and glanced toward the door before thinking of the inventory. The translucent window opened at once. She focused on the book, and a moment later it vanished from her hands.

INVENTORYSlots Used: 6/12

Book of the Obsidian Hierophant x1

Sakura looked at the empty space between her fingers for a few seconds, then let out a quiet breath. Much better.

Voices drifted in from the next room. Sakura did not move, only listened. It was Brein and Lir, and from their tone it was obvious the conversation was not a pleasant one.

“I’m taking her there anyway,” Lir said, a little louder than usual. “She won’t make it there alone. You can see that yourself.”

“What I can see is that you were born with too much pity and not enough brains,” Brein answered darkly. “That’s what I can see. And if you die on that road, I’ll kill you.”

“Yeah, yeah.”

“And if you start playing hero, I’ll kill you a second time. Just to make sure.”

Sakura almost smiled to herself. Brein, it seemed, knew how to express concern only by making it sound like a threat.

“Nothing’s going to happen to me,” Lir muttered. “This is Mebrel, not the southern borders.”

“For now,” his father cut in. “Fine. Go.”

After that came the sound of a bench being shoved back, footsteps, and the creak of the door. Then the house fell quiet for a moment. A few minutes later, Lir knocked lightly on the doorframe of the room where Sakura had slept.

“You awake?”

“I am now,” she answered, turning toward him.

The morning was colder than she had expected. The air had grown even damper overnight, and it was chilly even inside the house, as if autumn had no intention of ever ending. Sakura tidied herself up, braided her hair, and stepped out.

Brein was already standing by the door with the expression of a man personally sending them off to execution rather than to the next town. He shoved a short knife in a rough sheath into Lir’s hands, gave Sakura a long look that made her want to speak as little as possible all over again, and only then grunted,

“You’ll get to Mebrel by the lower trail. Don’t go through the old ravine. And don’t dawdle. I don’t like letting you into the forest as it is.”

“Yeah, yeah, we got it,” said Lir.

“You got it. She’s just nodding for now.”

Sakura really had already nodded several times. Then she had to add, “Yes, I understand. We take the lower trail.”

They left when the sky had already begun to lighten, though the sun still could not be seen behind the thick clouds. From the start, the day looked as if it meant to break into rain, but for some reason it kept holding back, gathering weight and darkness instead of letting go.

At first they walked in silence. Lir moved ahead easily and quickly, clearly used to these forest paths, while Sakura kept close beside him, trying to match his pace without looking too helpless on the uneven ground. Things stayed calm for a while, and it was probably that calm which finally made Lir speak first.

“The truth is,” he said without turning around, “I’m not as stupid as my father thinks.”

Sakura tensed a little, but said nothing.

“And I can see you’re hiding something,” he continued more quietly. “He’s not wrong to be suspicious.”

Now she really did tense. A cold heaviness gathered in her chest at once, and the hand hidden beneath the cloak clenched harder than it needed to. Sakura thought that whatever came next would probably be a question she had no good answer for, but to her surprise, Lir started talking about something else entirely.

“You’re not from here,” he said. “And it’s not just the name.”

Sakura asked carefully, “Why do you think that?”

Lir huffed.

“Just an observation. You don’t act like someone from here, and that’s a fact. I’m guessing you’re from the south.”

Sakura looked at him more closely.

“We get a lot of refugees here,” Lir continued, lowering his voice as if the forest might overhear and take offense. “Most of them come from the south. That’s where the obsidian beasts crawl out like flies, and people don’t really live there anymore, they just fight them off. Whoever can leave heads west, closer to places like Mebrel. People don’t like them much, honestly. They think they bring someone else’s trouble and someone else’s sickness with them. And... the refugees themselves are usually ashamed. They don’t like talking about where they came from.”

Sakura nodded slowly.

“I see,” she said quietly. “So you decided for me who I am?”

“Yep,” Lir answered without a trace of embarrassment. “I did.”

“And what if you’re wrong?”

At last he turned and grinned, not unkindly. At that moment, Sakura thought he had more than enough self-satisfaction in him, the way young men so often do.

“Then you’re very strange. But honestly, southerners always seemed strange to me. Not quite of this world, like half their head is still somewhere worse.”

Sakura nodded again, almost accepting his version. Arguing with a theory that convenient would have been foolish. Lir took it as confirmation, stopped asking questions, and seemed almost encouraged, as if he had done a good deed and helped her save face.

The rest of the journey passed in a strange mix of light talk, long pauses, and the rustling of the forest. From time to time, Lir told her things about the road, about where old hunters’ posts had once stood, about where the trails got washed out after spring. There were a lot of words. Lir spoke about the region with enthusiasm and absolute loyalty.

“We’re lucky, actually,” he said at one point. “Compared to other places, it’s still almost calm here. Well, as calm as anything can be now. There are beasts here too, sure, but not as often, and the guard still rides the roads. Settlements don’t die out every week. Down south, or in the Black Lowlands, it’s been worse for a long time. Sometimes you can find a whole village standing empty, with only doors banging and chickens walking through the mud. At least people here are still living, not just waiting for whatever gets to them first.”

Sakura listened in silence. The longer he talked, the clearer it became that her terror yesterday, the ruins, the monster, all of it was only the beginning.

And how easily Lir talked about that kind of life, as if surviving under those conditions were no different from going to the market. Too casually. Could people really adapt that well to something so awful?

By midday the forest had started to thin, and the weather grew worse still. The clouds sank so low it looked as if they were hanging right over the treetops, and the light turned steel-gray, dim and heavy.

They stepped out of the last line of trees onto a rising road, and at last Mebrel appeared ahead.

Sakura slowed. The city was nothing like what she had imagined. After all the talk about “the west, where things are still bearable,” she had built a picture in her mind of a small town, poor perhaps, but still almost cozy, somewhere a person could catch their breath. Instead she saw a high, solid wall of dark stone, far too massive for a “quiet place,” wrapped in dry pale roots like ivy, only dead. The roots climbed up the masonry, caught in the seams, wrapped around the towers, and in places hung down in long stiff strands, making the whole wall look both grim and beautiful, like something that had spent too long learning how to survive. Dark arrow slits gaped above the gate. No festivity, no trade bustle at the entrance. Only weight.

“There’s Mebrel,” Lir said, with a kind of awkward pride.

The closer they came, the clearer the feeling became that poverty and fatigue had grown into the place for good. Right by the walls stood carts with cracked sides and sacks. The people at the gate were dressed sturdily but poorly. Boots had been resewn, sleeves patched, cloaks darkened by rain and time. Even the horses looked worn down.

When Sakura and Lir were finally let inside, she felt it even more strongly in the streets: Mebrel lived hard. There was no festive hum here, none of the sense of motion a city usually had. People walked quickly with their shoulders lowered, as if they were carrying not baskets and bags but constant exhaustion. Their faces were grim. The houses crowded tightly together, wooden, dark, some of them leaning, with narrow windows and roofs reinforced with whatever was available.

What unsettled Sakura most was the gatekeeper. He let them through after a brief exchange with Lir, but he looked at them too carefully. Not like a man checking strangers, but like someone already waiting for people like them. His gaze slid over Sakura’s cloak, her face, her hands, lingered a little longer than it should have, and the feeling made something unpleasant tighten inside her.

“He looked at us strangely,” Sakura said quietly once they had gone a little farther.

“They look at everyone like that,” Lir said dismissively, but there was less certainty in his voice than he wanted to show.

They went a little farther along the main street, past a roughly built awning where something like dried roots was being sold, past women with buckets, and Sakura had already begun to think that maybe her nervousness was only getting in the way. She needed to relax.

But...

The guard did not appear all at once. First two men with spears stepped out ahead from around a corner, cutting off the road, then two more moved in from the side, then behind them boots rasped heavily, and before Sakura had even managed to take a proper breath, she and Lir were surrounded. The spears were not leveled for a strike, but they were angled low enough to make it clear Sakura and Lir were not supposed to move.

Lir reacted first.

“Hey, what is this...?”

The senior guardsman stepped forward. He was tall, with a hard, tired face and a short-cropped beard. His gaze settled on Sakura and immediately turned colder still.

“By order of the head of Mebrel,” he said evenly and harshly, “this girl is to be taken into immediate custody. We need to establish the circumstances of her appearance within the city limits and her connection to confirmed testimony placing her at Crowlis’s castle during recorded anomalous activity. Until further inquiry, she is to be treated as potentially involved in a prohibited incident and as a possible threat to public safety.”

For one short moment it grew so quiet that Sakura could hear her own heartbeat. Lir stared at the guardsman with such disbelief that it looked as if the man had just announced the sky now belonged to the head of the city.

“What kind of nonsense is that?” burst out of him.

The guardsman did not even look at him.

“Step aside, boy.”

Sakura did not move. Everything inside her went cold, but this time it was not panic; it was the terrible clarity of understanding that the city had been waiting for her. Someone already knew enough to mark her as dangerous and connect her to the ruins, the red glow, and the thing she herself still did not fully understand.

She slowly lifted her eyes to the spearheads, then to the faces of the guards. In the dull light, Mebrel suddenly stopped being a poor city a person could reach in order to rest and think about the future. It had become another trap.
