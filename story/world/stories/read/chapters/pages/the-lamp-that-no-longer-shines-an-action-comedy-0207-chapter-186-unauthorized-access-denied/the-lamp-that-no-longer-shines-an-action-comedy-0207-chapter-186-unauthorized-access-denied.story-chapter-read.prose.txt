The front door clicked shut behind Hathaway, and the amber warmth of the foyer wrapped around her: roasted garlic and Anna's evening tea.

Hathaway contributed exactly enough syllables to register as present, excused herself the moment the plates were cleared, and went upstairs.

She spread the [Iron Will] feat scroll across her desk, flooded the arcane matrix with mana, and pressed her palm flat against the parchment.

A sharp spike of silver light drove directly up her arm and into her cerebral cortex. Her fingers dug into the edge of the mahogany desk as the scroll dissolved into fine, cold ash.

Every piece of fantasy media she had ever consumed in her previous life assured her that a Will save was a matter of grit. Stubborn, screaming, psychological endurance against an invading force. You gritted your teeth. You held on.

As the feat compiled into her neural architecture, she understood exactly how wrong that was.

She felt a cold, crystalline structure settle over the outer layer of her consciousness: a boundary defense layer snapping into place with the frictionless precision of enterprise-grade security infrastructure. When a hostile matrix attempted to hijack her emotional state or rewrite her perception, she didn't need to fight it. The firewall simply checked the incoming packet, confirmed the complete absence of administrative credentials, and dropped the connection.

Unauthorized Access Denied.

She exhaled, rolling her shoulders as the phantom pressure faded.

That, her developer brain noted with profound, structural satisfaction, is a patch worth eighty-five thousand Solars.

The delivery arrived via an arcane logistics courier precisely on schedule.

For the next two hours, she sat on the floor of her bedroom, surrounded by heavily warded crates, unboxing the systematic financial ruin of her summer operating budget with the focused, reverent care of a museum conservator.

Alchemical calipers forged from cold-iron and star-silver, calibrated to a tolerance of five decimal places. Compounding crucibles whose interiors were coated in a seamless, heat-resistant enamel that felt exactly like touching liquid glass. A micro-scale inscribed with anti-gravity runes to negate the interference of ambient air pressure on its readings, because an alchemist who blamed humidity for a failed measurement was an alchemist who deserved to fail. She lifted each component out of its packaging with its specific, immaculate weight: the weight of equipment engineered to survive catastrophic detonations while remaining accurate to the fifth decimal.

It was, without qualification, exactly like unboxing a rack of ultra-high-end server infrastructure. The hardware demanded respect.

She arranged everything across her reinforced workstation with meticulous, obsessive geometry, following Sharon Curie's Chapter Three diagrams to the millimeter. When the setup was complete, she pulled Volume One toward her, opened it to Chapter One, and began to read.

Ten o'clock passed. Eleven o'clock.

The townhouse settled into the deep, rhythmic quiet of a sleeping household. Hathaway turned pages of foundational alchemy theory, her eyes scanning dense paragraphs on harmonic resonance and chaotic suppression.

But her mana radar was running a continuous background sweep of the street beyond the front gate, and had been for some time.

Her developer brain logged the discrepancy with clinical precision: You are experiencing the exact cognitive and behavioral signature of a 1950s domestic housewife waiting in the living room for her corporate husband to return from a late shift at the firm.

Hathaway suppressed the observation with extreme prejudice and aggressively turned a page so hard the corner nearly tore. I am engaging in rigorous academic preparation. My sleep schedule is merely flexible.

At 11:47 PM, a familiar, chillingly precise mana signature crossed the perimeter ward of the Ludwig estate.

She closed the book, smoothed her shirt, and walked out of the room.

She descended the staircase just as the heavy front door clicked open below.

Victoria stepped into the foyer. She looked impeccable, dark jacket sharp and unwrinkled, but the faint bluish shadows beneath her pale eyes told the full story: not the fatigue of physical exertion, but something quieter and more compressed.

Victoria looked up and saw Hathaway standing at the bottom of the stairs.

Something infinitesimal relaxed in the pale blue eyes.

"You're still awake," Victoria said.

"I was studying," Hathaway replied, defensively fast. "The Curie manual. The foundational theory requires extensive review."

The corner of Victoria's mouth moved, a faint, knowing curve. She began to unbutton her coat. "Of course."

They walked up the stairs side-by-side, the silence between them comfortable, undemanding, filled only with the soft rhythm of their boots against the carpeted runner.

"Any explosive insights?" Victoria asked.

"Mostly warnings about explosions," Hathaway said. "Extensive, detailed warnings. Several of them appeared to have been written from direct personal experience."

They reached the top landing. Victoria stopped in front of the guest room door, rested her hand on the brass handle.

She turned.

Her gloved hand reached out, sliding behind Hathaway's neck, fingers tangling lightly in the hair at the nape. Before Hathaway's nervous system could issue a proximity alert, Victoria leaned in and kissed her.

Hathaway froze perfectly solid.

It was the second time. Both times, Victoria had initiated the sequence and entirely bypassed Hathaway's internal processing queue, and both times Hathaway's operational readiness had been caught completely flat-footed. There was no Vissel spice this time to chemically anesthetize the sensory array. She felt the impossible, velvet softness of the contact. Victoria's scent flooded her olfactory sensors: the sharp, pristine clarity of a freezing winter morning. But buried beneath that frost, barely perceptible, was a trace warmth, like a sun that had sunk to the very bottom of a frozen lake, radiating a microscopic heat through the ice.

Victoria stepped back. Her pale eyes held Hathaway's for one quiet, unhurried second.

The brass handle clicked. The door opened. Victoria slipped inside, and the heavy wood closed behind her with a soft, definitive thud.

Hathaway stood in the empty hallway.

Her enterprise-grade firewall had not engaged. It had simply bypassed every existing defense layer through a completely unprotected access point, and her developer brain had crashed to desktop.

She stood there for three full minutes before she remembered how to walk to her own room.

The remaining sleep evacuated Hathaway's biological system in a single, violently ejected breath.

The Elders.

Her operational understanding of the "Main House Elders" was compiled entirely from transmigration literature, which constituted a reliable, extensive, and completely consistent body of evidence: a council of rigid, ancient, supremely powerful fossils whose sole narrative function was to obstruct the protagonist's romantic progression via draconian ancestral laws and ritual disapproval that could not be argued with because it predated argument.

Survival protocols engaged. Clothes make the woman. You cannot walk into a hostile administrative tribunal looking like you rolled out of a dorm room. You need armor.

She tore her wardrobe open.

She bypassed the casual wear, the comfortable linen shirts. She reached into the absolute back and extracted the coat.

The deep crimson velvet, the gold lion-head buttons, the Ludwig crest on the cuffs with the dark rose patterns shifting along the hem: the coat she had worn on her first day of enrollment and again the day she passed the A1. She pulled it on.

But her eyes didn't glow.

Could I cast a low-tier [Light] spell directly onto my corneas? she thought, briefly. Just a faint, localized glow. Purely for situational purposes.

Her internal safety protocols flagged this as an excellent method to permanently destroy her retinas approximately ten minutes before walking into a hostile tribunal. She abandoned the glowing eyes, adjusted her collar, and sprinted for the door.
