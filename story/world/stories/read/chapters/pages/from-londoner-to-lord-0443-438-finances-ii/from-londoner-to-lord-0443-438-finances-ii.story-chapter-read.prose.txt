“That is before deductions though,” Duvas said. “Those wage numbers include all the people living in the manor—apart from Leah who lives with Cedoron, and the newly hired guards staying in the longhouse blocks—but they do not yet account for food, clothing, and similar costs, while lodging is given freely as a perk. So we deduct the cost of food and clothing from their wages, which comes out to around 4 coppers per day on average, at last year’s food prices. The currently high food prices are supposed to be temporary and that cost is being absorbed by the manor for the guards and servants, since you didn't want their wages to be reduced even more after this deduction of 4 coppers."

Kivamus nodded. "Yeah, that's fine—otherwise they will barely get anything. Once the wheat crop is harvested in autumn, we will basically get a huge surplus of wheat for no other cost other than what we have already invested in buying the seeds and the wages for the farmers. It will work out in the end."

"I do hope the harvest will be good, although our ever increasing population means it won't last nearly as long as we thought. Coming back to the topic, those deductions will add up to around 165 gold a month for all the manor residents and guards—including those who are not living here currently." He lowered the page slightly. “That means the net wage expense for the manor residents and guards is 344 gold a month."

Duvas hesitated for a moment. “But this is before we count the cost of buying food and regular supplies for the manor." He looked back down at the figures. “For food alone, the manor residents and guards—and I am including all the guards here, even those staying at the longhouse blocks currently, since they will eventually move to the barracks here anyway—will need around 20 sacks of grain a month for three meals a day. That comes to roughly 120 gold a month for grain at the current prices. Once we add the other provisions, it rises to around 150 gold a month. So together with wages, we would be spending about 494 gold every month just on the manor residents and the guards.”

Kivamus exhaled through his nose. “That is a huge amount. I didn’t expect we would have to spend nearly 500 gold just to feed and pay the manor only.”

"This is how it's going to be if we pay everyone, and anyway, we do have above a hundred guards now, compared to just around a dozen last autumn.” Duvas looked at the ledger again. “Next, there are the senior level village workers, since you said you want to keep all the craftsmen on our own payroll. The foremen like Yeden and Pinoto are at 20 copper a day, while the longhouse supervisors get an extra incentive of 5 coppers a day apart from their usual wages for wherever they work, and the 3 master craftsmen—Cedoron, Darora and Taniok—remain at 25 coppers a day. Altogether that comes to 66 gold a month.”

He turned the page.

“That leaves the regular village workers. Diggers, coal miners, loggers, farmers, and the rest. Unlike the guards and the manor servants who are our full-time employees, these workers are paid daily wages—mostly at 8 to 10 coppers a day—as long as they continue showing up to work, though the payment will be given weekly. Last year we could only give miners 25 days of work in a month. Now there is more than enough work to keep all of them employed every day, but people still lose a few days to sickness, injuries, or family needs. The average comes to around 27 workdays a month as of now. Based on the latest worker list—including the 57 refugees we got yesterday—and with men and women paid similar wages based on their tasks they do, that would amount to 778 gold a month.”

Kivamus stared at him in surprise. “That is an enormous amount. All of these numbers are higher than I expected... At this rate I'm sure we won't break even. And we still need enough left to pay taxes and buy supplies for winter when trade stops.”

“I know,” Duvas sighed. “I was surprised too. So I checked the numbers again because I thought I had made a mistake. But the estimates are right. The population of both the village and the manor has risen drastically, and so has our revenue, but the expenses have obviously grown with it."

Kivamus frowned uneasily. “Even so, it doesn’t feel right... It's not going to be easy to start paying the villagers in coin if we do not actually earn enough gold to cover it.”

“Of course,” Duvas said. “But until now we've been buying the food from visiting merchants ourselves and giving it to the villagers for free as weekly rations. We do not need to keep doing that in the same way once wages begin. We have 2 options here. One way is to deduct the estimated cost of food and continue issuing weekly rations the way we've been doing. The other option is to let the village merchants buy grain and sell it directly to whoever needs it.”

He shook his head before Kivamus could say anything. “The problem with the second option is the longhouse blocks. Food is still cooked communally there. Everyone living there would try to save part of his own share he needs to contribute to the communal rations or argue about who contributed what. It will become chaotic within a few days and will likely lead to fights breaking out."

“You’re right,” Kivamus said. “Until villagers are living separately with their own families, that won’t work."

He leaned back slightly. “We've promised that everyone will have regular food and shelter here to entice more refugees to move to Tiranat. That is what the longhouse blocks are for now. Many new refugees come here with elderly relatives or young children who can't work, and those groups will also stay in those blocks without having any way to earn their own food. Other workers who contribute to the communal rations from their own wages will start a riot for that, saying why should they pay for those who can't work. So the manor will have to continue providing food and free shelter for the blocks no matter what.”

He paused for a moment, then continued. “On the other hand, once we begin building those apartment or tenement blocks—or whatever form they take—the families who can afford to move out to a separate place can start handling their own purchases. So we can keep the longhouse blocks for new refugees, the disabled, the old, orphans, and anyone who cannot support themselves yet. They will still get three meals a day and a roof over their heads, but no extra coin unless they work for it. Those who do go out and work as laborers or miners would then receive wages on top of that, after the appropriate deductions, of course.”

Duvas nodded slowly. “That does sound more reasonable.”

“As the village grows,” Kivamus went on, “people will slowly build some savings from that extra coin and gain confidence that work will remain steady. Then they can move into the individual apartments—paying some rent for it—and buy their own food and supplies. That will become the new middle class of the village. At first it will only be craftsmen, their apprentices, and merchants who can afford that rent, but the number will grow slowly.”

He gave a faint smile. “Unlike the longhouse blocks, which earn us nothing beyond keeping people safe, those future housing blocks will also bring in income. I’ve already been sketching some designs for them. Most of the better ideas will have to wait until we can make brick and, later, cement in large amounts. But I am close to finishing a design that can still be made from wood while providing living spaces which are much bigger, safer and far more private than a bunk in the blocks. When brick becomes available, we can build much larger apartment blocks with proper kitchens and bathing spaces. Until then, this earlier design of a tenement block should still be a worthwhile step up—enough to charge some rent for it. It will still serve the same purpose as an apartment, but it doesn't feel right to call it a real apartment without any place to cook or shower. So naming this intermediate housing as tenement blocks—which are a step below apartment blocks—feels better.”

Duvas nodded. “It makes sense, I guess... So for now, we continue providing enough rations and supplies for those living in the blocks, and in return we deduct the cost of food?”

“Yes, that is the only way which will work for now without causing fights,” Kivamus said. "By the way, how will you decide the amount of that deduction? Do you have an estimate for the expenses of a laborer who earns 8 to 10 coppers a day? Assuming they have to buy everything from the market and the manor isn't providing anything—whether food or shelter."

The majordomo explained, "Any new laborers or miners start at eight coppers a day here. That amount is just enough for a single fit worker to be able to feed his family of four. For reference, a copper coin can buy a full meal in the alehouse, along with a mug of ale. That means three coppers a day is usually enough to feed someone who doesn't have anyone else to take care of—at the grain prices of two years ago. For those with families, let's say a family of four, they will spend around seven to eight coppers a day just to buy enough food for their family. It will be much easier for them if both the man and the woman of that family can go to work, but that is not always possible, especially if the kids are young, or if there are elderly who can't work, which increases the food cost further."

Duvas added, "But by the time someone gets married and has a family they usually have the experience to earn 9 or even 10 coppers a day, and that surplus is used by them to save for a rainy day, or to buy other occasional necessities like soap or clothing. Of course, with the grain prices having increased by a half compared to two years ago, that's not quite how it will work right now if we let everyone buy their own meals. As for housing, until last year they all lived in makeshift huts or shacks they had built themselves from fallen branches and vines they gathered from the forest. That didn't cost anything to them, but it also didn't provide much protection from the cold—and some of them froze to death every winter—compared to the relatively comfortable life in the longhouse blocks."

Kivamus nodded slowly. A daily wage of 8 coppers a day would probably not even buy enough food for a large family at the current prices. He sighed and looked at Duvas. “Well, then the only way to continue feeding the villages to make sure they can work at their full efficiency is to continue providing rations to them at the blocks, and we'll pay a deducted wage on top of that for those who work. Do you need time to work those numbers out?”

That drew a tired smile from Duvas. “I know how your mind works. I expected you might choose that option, and I was ready to suggest it myself if you did not. So I already made the calculations.”
