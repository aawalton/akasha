Last time, when the team had entered the Wickenberg fields in pursuit of the Craftsman’s automaton monsters, we’d sneaked in through a gap in their wall.

The Guardians’ ‘shoot first, ask never’ attitude toward trespassers had mattered at B-Rank. But I was S-Rank, and while Ellen wasn’t quite there herself, she was probably one of the strongest A-Rankers in the city.

This time, Ellen and I took a more direct approach.

I stood on the 303 Wall, looking down at the fields. Deimos had dropped us off at the elevator two minutes ago. “I don’t see any patrols,” I said. “Looks like the Tyrants’ intel was right.”

“That’s good. Ready?” Ellen asked.

“Almost.” I peered into the dusk, squinting. My S-Rank body was better at perceiving things, after all, and the purple portal in the distance was clear enough.

So were the half-dozen human-sized figures, the handful of hulking monstrosities, and the spells and weapons—all silhouetted against the portal. I watched just long enough to confirm what we’d been hoping. The Wickenberg Portal was, after all, an active portal break. It had been since the day the Guardians had secured it. Even now, with the guild’s strongest fighters no doubt engaged with the Portal Tyrants, they’d been forced to leave a team behind to keep the portal contained.

That’d work to our advantage.

“Ready,” I said. I grabbed Ellen by the waist and threw myself off the 303 Wall.

Lightning Chain caught my fall before I hit the ground far below, and we swung away from the wall, hit the ground in a roll, and popped to our feet. An alarm blared across the field. Ellen raised an eyebrow.

“Stealth was never an option,” I said with a shrug. The Storm’s Blade ignited in my hand, and I barreled down the rows of corn and wheat. The humidity pressed in on us immediately—but with it came something else. Static. The electricity in the air. It wasn’t just from the storm in the southwest, either. No, there was an energy in the air, like things were about to happen.

We rounded a corner, cut left, and got in line with the portal containment building’s walls. I’d never gotten this close—only seen it from the 303 Wall; it was impressive from up there, but this close…

Three massive cement-and-portal metal walls, each a good thirty feet thick. A similarly-sized roof covering the top, and another slab that reached almost fifty feet from the ground to the portal itself. Water poured down from the blazing red gate, filling a sluice with two ramps, one on either side. Each was big enough for a pair of semi trucks to pass each other, though one was covered with a set of tracks instead. Two gigantic steel gates—one at the top of the sluice, and one at the bottom—filtered rubble from the water, while a pair of massive, stationary back hoes were clearly positioned to keep them clear.

The Wickenberg Portal had been mined heavily, and the rails were rusted and broken.

My phone buzzed.

Jessie: Portal Tyrants report. Vern Kellen in Phoenix. Clear to go.

Kade: Already going.

Jessie: Be careful.

Kade: Go to sleep. Quit hurting yourself trying to coordinate this. We’ve got it.

Then I tucked the phone away, ignoring her response messages, and my maelstrom and lightning armor covered my body.

It was time to get Ellen to S-Rank.

Jessie’s plan had been simple.

It was also something straight out of a chess match I’d watched last year.

Black had lured White into an attack by leaving their rooks exposed on the left side, then swung in with a knight and bishop. They’d checked the king, taken the queen, and set up a vicious hook that’d leave one or both of White’s own bishops dead—all at the cost of their own bishop and a pawn. White had played three more moves, then resigned.

Jessie didn’t want the Guardians destroyed. Her entire goal had been maintaining Phoenix’s stability. And I didn’t want them gone, either; if they disappeared, it’d cause chaos, and chaos was my enemy right now. The storm might be chaotic and unpredictable, but I was already juggling too many balls.

And Ellen…well, Ellen needed S-Rank.

We could accomplish all three of those goals with a single move.

The second we hit the base of the left-side ramp, Ellen started casting. Shadow Shapes came first. Gigantic tendrils of darkness lashed out, thrashing against the plated, walking squid-monsters and A and B-Rank delvers alike. Then Shadow Box, cutting into our enemies’ armor. As the gap between Ellen and me opened, I launched into a reckless assault on the closest enemy.

Gina Smithers: A-Rank

The platinum-blonde archer wasn’t ready for me. The Storm’s Blade cut through her weapon even as she turned to try firing at Ellen, and my next attack forced her to throw herself onto the concrete. She rolled, coming up with a pair of daggers.

“You don’t want to do that,” I said.

All Gina did was bare her teeth and growl almost ferally.

“Okay, maybe you do.”

Stormfall accelerated my attacks, and within seconds, one of Gina’s arms hung limply, and both daggers were on the ground. One more blow—this one to the back of her knee—put her down but not out, screaming in a combination of pain and impotent anger.

Then I looked around.

Ellen’s Shadow Shapes were ripping into the monsters and delvers, forcing them back. Half a dozen figures were already down, as were the swarms of C-Rank shrimp-looking things that had been coating the ramps’ tops a moment ago. Fear gripped my stomach for a moment before the Stormkin Avatar reflexively activated, and Dad’s hand pressed it back down. The portal break’s defenders weren’t so lucky. “Come on, Kade. Quit playing with your food and let’s go.”

“Fine,” I said. “Gina, you’re lucky. We’ve got business in the portal, and with its boss.”

Ellen stepped past me and ducked into the massive red portal, and I followed, leaving Gina and her delving team behind. None of them were in shape to chase us—and even if they were, we’d beat them again.

Their only option was to call for back-up.

Just like we’d planned.

The first part of Jessie’s plan was a blatant, almost suicidal attack across the Wickenberg Fields—just like the bishop and knight.

The goal wasn’t to take pieces, though. We didn’t care if Gina Smithers, her team, or any of the other Guardians died. In fact, the fewer who did, the better. No, we had our sights on something bigger: the portal boss.

Or at least, we did on the surface.

Ellen and I stepped into the Wickenberg Portal, the red gate shifting from in front to behind us, and my feet sank calf-deep into thick, sticky mud. Ellen had it a little better; she’d come in on one of the dozens of maze-like land bridges, and only her boots’ soles had gotten soaked. I pulled myself out of the mire with a sick squelching sound, and an even grosser-smelling stench filled the air. “Pretty sure the mud itself is rotting,” I muttered.

“I expected an aquatic portal,” Ellen said. “Everything I’ve read said this one was aquatic, not a morass.”

Morass. I’d been in a couple. The first had been my one-hour rush job to get ready for Ellen’s egg, which had become Cheddar. I summoned the winged serpent reflexively; he’d be more than a match for the monsters here. Ellen’s Pepperoni was a little weaker and a little slower, but not much. They’d give us more than fighting power, too. We could use their eyes in the sky.

Then I sniffed the air and looked at the root-bound islands dotting the morass portal world. Every one of them was lined almost completely with tall, multi-trunked bushes—small trees, really—and the land behind them seemed to be solid. Everything else was mud. Mud…and salt water. “This is an aquatic world, Ellen. It’s just a very different one. Almost a hybrid.”

“Oh? How do you figure?”

“There’s a place down past the Texas cities that’s supposed to look like this. It’s on the coast. And I smell salt.”

She sniffed, too. “You’re right. So, any idea what to expect?”

“From the Wickenberg Portal? No idea. They’ve kept this thing locked down,” I said, checking my Charges. I had only a single Lightning Charge to play with. “Let’s get moving.”

We moved—but we didn’t move far. The first of the root-bound islands was only a hundred yards away, and we covered that distance in less than five seconds. I hit the solid ground first, sword at the ready in Thunderlord stance. Aggression was the key here. We needed to put as much distance between us and the portal as possible before the Guardians reacted.

“Threats?” Ellen asked.

I shrugged. “Not sure.”

Then the root-bushes erupted around us.

Dozens, or even hundreds, of wooden tendrils lashed at us like whips. The blows came in fast enough to crack the air, and hit hard enough to knock me back a pace before I dropped into Torrentborne. The Storm’s Blade surged back and forth, leaving trails of lightning in the air, as I parried the monster’s attacks. “The islands are traps!” Ellen yelled.

“I figured that out!” I shouted back.

Mangrove Treant: A-Rank

The island shook below our feet as it shifted, and in that moment, I re-evaluated the monster. It wasn’t root-bushes. This was the top of the thing—and that made it massive. Three more parries as the tendrils came in. I glanced at Ellen. She was already casting something. Shadows swirled around her in a vortex. Her skin, normally an off-white tan, had gone negative. So had her eyes; they burned yellow-orange. The spell finished, and Ellen reminded me that, while I was powerful in my own right, a good mage’s offensive prowess was not to be underestimated.

Shadow—hyper-concentrated shadow—blasted from her outstretched hand. The portal world flickered, colors strobing between normal and negative. The ray of shadow sliced into the thrashing tendrils around us, severing their shadows from the roots themselves. Ellen’s spell wasn’t done, either. It grabbed the shadows and wrapped them around their former casters, then dragged them down. Not all of them died instantly, but enough did. The Mangrove Treant rose slowly from the water, dozens of thick trunks supporting its weight, and screamed in agony.

Then the beam hit the ‘ground’ below my feet, and those very trunks’ shadows turned against their casters, too. The monster died slowly, but it died, leaving us once again mired in the salty swamp.

“Shadowed Doubling,” Ellen said. She breathed heavily. “A-Rank spell. It’s a new one. I picked it out while you were training, but this is my first time casting it. It’s a good one, though.”

“Sure is,” I said. My boots were already sticking in the mud, but I didn’t move. Not yet. “So, we’ve got an aquatic world that’s behaving like a morass. That’s gotta be part of what’s causing the Wickenberg fields to be so productive, right?”

Ellen nodded. “I don’t know much about farming, but this mud seems like great fertilizer.”

“Great,” I said. And it was great. Every single thing we learned from this world was one more reason the Guardians couldn’t let the Wickenberg Portal fall. Fertilizer, water, and who knew what else; this place was critical to Phoenix’s future. It was a lifeline in a storm of dry, cracked earth.

They’d have to react.

“Come on,” Ellen said. “We’ve got a lot of work to do here.”

We did, and I started walking toward the next island of roots. Every kill gave Ellen a little more experience, pushed her just a tiny bit closer to S-Rank—and that was our main goal. Not killing the monster in charge of this portal world. Not drawing the attention of Vern Kellen and the other Guardians. Not even saving the Portal Tyrants, although I’d gladly have Terrel Young on my side for the upcoming conflict.

S-Rank for Ellen. Nothing else mattered.

We ripped into the next island before we’d even made landfall, and it was dead by the time I’d counted to ten.

“Mana?” I asked.

“Yes, please,” Ellen said. “I’m at five hundred out of eight hundred ninety.”

“That’s a hefty pool,” I said, but I fired up Shadowstorm Battery and started pouring Mana into Ellen. I’d done this before, right after my own core had been reforged, but this time…this time felt much more intense. Our bond was growing, and the sheer amount of Mana that poured through it was…significant, to say the least.

“I’m just about ready to push Shadowstorm Battery to A-Rank,” she said after a minute.

“Let’s get you to S-Rank first. Then we can push them together.”

“Got it.” Ellen squished down into the mud and started trudging toward the next island, and I followed. So far, the plan was proceeding perfectly.

Magda, the Paragon of the Sliding Surge, had been mid-core bonding when the intruders had arrived in her portal world.

Not that she’d ever publicly claim it as such. She hadn’t even slain the portal’s boss and usurped the giant, armored crab. Xanavos Kankar was a plenty imposing monster to rule the swamplands in her stead, after all, and she had more important things to worry about.

For one thing, her loyalties.

Magda was an old Paragon. She was far older than the Placid King, though he’d converted her into a servant with such ease it was almost shocking. The God of Thunder was, perhaps, older. She wasn’t sure. And the Crone definitely was. But unlike their Paths, the Sliding Surge had ended at A-Rank. She was an old Paragon, but an almost pathetically weak one, by comparison.

That weakness mattered to the Placid King. He was disappointed in her efforts so far, and he’d made that fact known at their last meeting. But it didn’t seem to matter to the God of Thunder. Their core-bindings were as intense and deeply revealing as ever, and their time afterward, though short, was always pure joy.

But she wasn’t loyal to the thunder dragon, and he knew it. The Placid King was her master. He’d ordered her to keep a close eye on Earth, and she’d done it faithfully for well over fifteen years. She hunted the swamps, killing portal monsters as she saw fit, but never slid through the portal herself. So far as she knew, Earth considered her portal world to be a boon—one that she was more than happy to manipulate. After all, a world without water would desire nothing more than an infinite flow of it, and that, the Sliding Surge could provide. Information for water—a fair trade, but one that required constant visitors.

The Guardians had found her a half-dozen times before she’d talked to one of them rather than killing her. The woman had been…informative. They’d come to an agreement. The portal would stay open. Xanavos Kankar’s monsters would break free periodically. The water would flow—and so would the information.

Magda was used to intruders.

But the Placid King was already beyond frustrated with Magda’s long-standing mission on Earth, and these intruders…they seemed familiar. If her world were to fall—and not only fall, but fall to a servant of the God of Thunder—it would mean her death at her master’s hands. Transgressions of the core, the Placid King could overlook. Failures on the scale to disrupt his plans? Not so much.

No. Magda would have to get creative in order to stop the S-Rank delver who, along with a single A-Ranker, was shredding her Treants.

But Magda had always been creative. She’d find a way.
