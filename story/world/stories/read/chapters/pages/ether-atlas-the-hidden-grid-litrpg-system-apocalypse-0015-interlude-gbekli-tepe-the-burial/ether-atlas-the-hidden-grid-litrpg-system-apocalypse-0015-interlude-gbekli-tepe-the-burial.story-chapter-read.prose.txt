10th Millennium BC - Göbekli Tepe, Anatolia

They were burying the temple.

For three generations the stone circles had stood open to the sky, massive T-shaped pillars carved with animals that didn't belong together. A fox bared its teeth beside a crane. A boar faced a knot of snakes. Now the workers filled the spaces between them with rubble and earth, sweating under the Anatolian sun.

A young man paused, leaning on his mattock. The pillar beside him was twice his height, its surface carved with a fox and a crane in a configuration that looked more like a diagram than decoration.

"Why bury what took so long to build?" It came out harsher than he had meant. "My grandfather set this stone. His father cut it. And I carry dirt to cover their work so that no one will ever see it again."

The Curator ran her fingers along a groove connecting two animal figures in a line so precise it might have been drawn with instruments that wouldn't exist for another ten thousand years. She wore a plain linen wrap and her feet were bare on the hot rubble.

"Because the stone itself was never the point." The woman did not look up from the groove. "The stone is a container. What it holds is already in the ground under your feet, anchored, aligned and waiting. The pillars did their work the day they were placed. All that is left for them is to survive."

"And my grandfather's name?"

"Will be lost. What he made will outlast every king who is not yet born. It will outlast the language you are speaking to me now. And no living soul will ever know his hands were on it."

She straightened from the pillar and looked out across the hilltop, where rings of pillars were disappearing beneath loads of rubble. She glanced at the sky and seemed satisfied by whatever it told her.

"This is not the only one. Across the world, other hands are covering other doors, and none of them will ever learn that the others exist. Fire will come to this land. Empires will cross it. Armies will camp on this hill and never once wonder what sleeps beneath them. That is the design." She looked at the young man with eyes that a monk in India would recognise ten thousand years later. "Bury it deep. Bury it completely. And tell no one what lies underneath."

The young man nodded and went back to his work. Within a season, the hilltop would be smooth and featureless. The oldest temple in human history would vanish from memory for twelve thousand years.

It would be found again in 1994, by a German archaeologist who would spend the rest of his career trying to explain why a civilisation that predated agriculture had built a monument that predated everything. And how people who raised these temples kept no farms, fired no pottery, worked no metal, and raised pillars of sixteen tonnes with rope and muscle and no wheel at all.

He would never find the answer. The answer was sleeping in the bedrock, exactly where she had left it.
