Chapter 106

Watch the burdened ones. A species that sheds everything not useful to it by nightfall moves quickly and arrives, in the end, with nothing. — Codex of the Long Watch, Watcher’s Commentary

Arthur lashed the dead shield to the outside of his pack where his hand could find it.

“You’ll carry that?” Steve asked.

“A shield sheared off at the bottom is still better than no shield,” Arthur replied and stood up. The sun was not over the water yet. The temple around them was a ruin of fallen columns and one long stain on the marble that the tide would never reach to clean.

Donna had the trident roped diagonally across her pack. Seven feet of barnacled black iron rode at her shoulder like a ship’s spar. Sarah stood with her left arm bound across her chest, her face already tight at the thought of the road.

“Right,” Arthur said. “Athens then. We’ll let Sarah set the pace.”

“The pace will be slow,” Sarah said.

“Good thing the scenery is nice then.” Arthur smiled. He hooked his thumbs under his chest straps and turned down off the headland, leading them into the long, grey country they had dragged themselves through the day before.

It took the trident nine minutes to become a problem.

The path off the cape was nothing more than a goat track winding through fractured limestone, and the rear prong, despite being wrapped in strips of canvas, caught on every gap in the stone and every low branch of the wind-stunted pines. Donna walked straight through the first three snags without a word, her shoulders twisting to wrench the iron free. On the fourth, the long haft caught a thick root, levering her sideways into a dense patch of thornbush.

“Bloody hell,” she muttered, tearing her arm free from the brambles.

“Seven feet of scrap iron a guardian was swinging at our heads yesterday,” Steve said. “I’m only observing that the romance of the trophy and the physics of the trophy are pulling in different directions.”

“It was your bloody idea,” Donna shot back, hauling her pack straight with a sharp jerk of her hips. “Loot, you said.”

“And you want half of it and I have ten strength,” Steve said with a smile.

“It is twenty kilos of good metal,” Donna agreed ruefully, her breath hitching slightly as she reset her stride. “You wanted a focus, Sarah needs a bracelet, and I want a weapon that does not look like a standard prybar. That is all currently sitting on my spine, so I would thank the pair of you to keep walking.”

That, at least, had been settled at the temple the day before, the guardian still cooling beside them. The trident was raw stock, and three people had a claim on it, and the only argument left was carriage. Arthur was carrying a shield and Donna was the spare.

They walked.

By the middle of the morning the sun had come up off the water on their right, and Sarah had gone quiet, wincing with pain every now and then.

“Sarah.”

“I am fine.”

Arthur came up level with her on the wide part of the track. “I can carry you. It is not complicated. You weigh nothing.”

Sarah’s face went a deep and immediate red, all the way to her ears, and she looked very hard at the path. “Absolutely not.”

Donna looked back and chuckled.

“It is the efficient solution.”

“It is not happening.”

She took four more rigid steps, the colour refusing to leave her cheeks. When she spoke again, the words were hesitant. “But you can give me your arm. On the right side. If I have something to lean against, then I run Ether from the bound hand into the injury.”

Arthur extended his right arm. Sarah hooked her good hand into the crook of his elbow, letting her weight slump into his side. Her eyelids fluttered half-closed as the bound shoulder took on the faint, dull violet undertone of active Ether.

They kept moving in silence. Arthur kept his eyes locked on the road ahead, deliberately ignoring the proximity of her face, and filed the sudden, small warmth of her hand against his forearm somewhere to be dealt with at a later date, possibly never.

“We could build her a travois,” Steve said brightly, from behind. “Two poles, the spare bedroll, and we drag her along behind Arthur. The Plains nations moved entire households that way. Dogs, then horses. Sarah, you would be a household.”

“No, Steve, I don’t want to be dragged across the Attica shoreline,” Sarah said, without opening her eyes.

“Steve,” Donna said. “Eyes on the road, they have it handled.”

The travois is structurally sound, Archie said. The doctor’s objection lacks a logical foundation.

“Thank you, Archie,” Sarah said. “Stay out of it.”

They covered what Arthur estimated to be ten kilometres before the midday heat forced a halt. They slouched into the narrow strip of shade offered by an abandoned filling station, its rusty pumps long since dry, and chewed in silence on salty olives and pieces of the rock-hard paximadi.

It was here that Steve started being Steve. He sat with the ledger open, his eyes scouring the dead forecourt, the rusted sign and a lizard frozen on the baking concrete. It was the restless attention of a man whose brain had nothing to work on and was therefore dismantling everything in front of him.

“That lizard,” he said, shifting his weight. “I will say Dexterity twelve, easily. Constitution four. No Resonance to speak of. A solitary ambush feeder, fundamentally a glass cannon.” He tilted his pencil stub toward a crow sitting on the sagging overhead wire. “That one’s smarter. Acuity’s doing something there. They can work a problem.”

“Steve,” Donna said from the shade.

“The station itself is interesting, structurally. If you read it as a dungeon room, you have one clear entrance, two exits, unobstructed sightlines along the pumps and that office at the back for low-level loot, maybe secured in a cabinet. I’d put a low-level encounter in there.”

“Steve.”

“The bread is standard Constitution food. A slow metabolic burn. The olives are providing the salt our bodies have been begging for since the climb, which is a Resonance thing. The biology knows what it is short of before the brain does, which is interesting, because…”

“Parr.” Donna had not raised her voice. She had lowered it, which was worse. “If you assign one more random stat block to one more random thing, I am going to assign one to you, and you will not enjoy the result.”

Steve closed the ledger with a small, dry sigh. “It passes the time,” he said.

“It does not pass mine.”

Arthur, who had been turning the dead shield over in his rough hands while they rested, set the warped metal down flat on the concrete. He looked across the forecourt at the iron trident balanced across Donna’s knees.

“Donna. Hand me the trident a second.”

She slid the heavy iron over. Arthur peeled back the canvas wrap from the prongs and held the tip over the sheared, ruined edge of the shield. The others watched him, the quiet curiosity overriding their fatigue.

“The trident aged things it touched,” Arthur said, his eyes scanning the prongs. “Turned the shield to powder, degraded the cliff, rotted the stone floor it stood on. Stavros is going to put this thing in his forge, and I want to know if we are handing him raw material or a curse.” He looked up, meeting Donna’s eyes. “The shield is done for anyway. We need to find out what the weapon does now that the hand wielding it is gone.”

“That,” Steve said, shifting his ledger to sit upright, “is a properly isolated test. Carry on.”

Arthur swung the trident, driving the flat of the centre prong hard against the face of the shield. He struck it twice, then a third time for good measure. The iron rang with a dull clatter in the dead air of the forecourt. He stopped, and they all leaned over the metal.

The shield remained as grey and degraded as before. The struck face showed nothing but a bright, raw scuff, with no new pitting or chalky powder gathering on the edge.

“It was the guardian,” Steve said, nodding as his pencil found the paper again. “Not the weapon itself. The creature was channelling the degradation effect through the strike, and the metal was just the conductor. The trident is inert.” He gestured at the seven feet of dark iron. “It is just a very large, very ugly and very useful bar of infused iron.”

“Which means Stavros can work it without his forge turning to ash,” Arthur said, handing the weight back to Donna. “Good. I wanted that answered before we asked him.”

You could have asked me, Archie said.

The four of them looked up at the violet geometry turning slowly in the shade.

“Would you have told us?” Donna asked.

No.

“Do any of you sometimes feel that Archie is trolling us?” Steve asked, looking up at the floating geometry.

They made camp the first night in a half-built villa above Anavyssos. Four walls, a roof and no doors, the kind of luxury shell that had been everywhere on this coast before the world ended and was everywhere on it still, emptied. Sarah lowered herself against the masonry with deliberate care, her face drawn, while Donna split a wooden window frame to build a small fire.

“I keep turning over what Archie said,” Steve said, his ledger balanced on his knee as they chewed through their remaining rations. “Closed circuit. No dead channels. A frame with nothing dragging can reach the Ether field around us.”

“You will close yours next level,” Sarah said, her voice quiet but steady. “We both will. It is just one point into Dexterity.”

“That is not what I was getting at. There is a pattern here that has been chewing at me. The concept of wholeness. It is ancient, it is everywhere, and I spent my entire career filing it under primitive nonsense.”

“Here we go,” Donna murmured from across the hearth.

“No, listen, the correlation is solid,” Steve said, sitting forward. “Every single culture that ever sacrificed an animal demanded it be completely without blemish. Leviticus is explicit: a beast with a fractured bone or a blind eye is rejected at the altar. The Greeks maintained the exact same standard.”

He flipped a page in the ledger, the firelight catching the movement. “The Romans would not let a priest serve if his body carried a defect. Sine vitio, they called it: without blemish. The flamen Dialis, the high priest of Jupiter in ancient Rome, lived strangled by complex taboos because his physical person had to be structurally flawless.”

“There is an Irish myth about King Nuada, who loses his hand in battle and forfeits the throne that very day, because a maimed man cannot rule. He only reclaims the crown when a physician builds him a fully functioning silver arm. A whole-bodied king for a whole functioning kingdom.”

“We have a word for that concept,” Sarah said softly, her eyes closed as her good hand rested flat over her bound shoulder. “My grandmother used it. Not to describe physical perfection, but to define a person who is entirely themselves.”

“Kalokagathia,” Steve said, nodding. “Beautiful-and-good. Balance between beauty and virtue.”

He spread his fingers toward the small flickers of the fire. “To the ancients, the fit body and the fine soul were a single integrated unit, because a complete physical frame was the outward proof of an ordered internal structure. I used to stand in front of lecture halls and explain that it was nothing more than aristocratic self-flattery, a way for the wealthy and well-fed to claim inherent virtue. Class prejudice with a classic Greek paint job. I genuinely believed that.”

“And now you have run into a contradiction,” Arthur said, shifting his weight against the wall.

“And now we know that a body with no dead channels can draw power straight out of the ambient field, and a frame with a choked channel suffers systemic drag.” Steve stared into the embers. “What if it was never prejudice? What if every single one of those cultures was remembering a technical truth, or a protocol they were taught thousands of years before the integration?”

He looked around at the circle of faces. “What if all those myths and rituals were just degraded metaphors about something the ancients were taught but could not understand because the System was dark? They kept the parameters long after they lost the functional reason for them. The rules drifted into superstition because there was no System and no Grid.”

Nobody spoke for a moment. The dry wood of the window frame popped, throwing a small cluster of sparks into the dark.

Your reconstruction is incomplete but not entirely inaccurate, Archie said.

It was on the third night, inside the hollow shell of a beach taverna with the plastic chairs still stacked in the corners and the paper menus curling on the walls, that Donna brought up the other thing.

“Archie said a strange thing back on the cape,” she said, running a piece of leather down the edge of her axe. “One tier away from a System unlock. One portal away from your final destination.”

“He did,” Steve said.

“We have taken three portals so far.” Donna held the sharpened edge up to the firelight, checking the bevel, before looking toward Archie. “London to Marseille. Marseille to Malta. Malta to here. So when you say one portal away, you are not being mystical.”

Mystical carries a connotation of a lack of understanding, which is not central to my method, communication or choice of syntax, Archie stated in his usual flat register.

“Right. You are just being obtuse,” Sarah said with a faint chuckle.

“Greece is the last one,” Steve said slowly. “It is the final door.”

“And after Greece is somewhere that is the actual destination,” Sarah said, “and not just another step toward it.”

They all looked up at the violet geometry turning in the gloom.

I will not tell you what is on the other side, Archie stated. I have given you only a relative measure of your progression. You may find that you would have preferred not to know even that much.

“Why would we prefer to be blind?” Arthur asked, his boots shifting slightly against the gritty floorboards.

Because a man who knows he is near the end of a road walks the last of it differently, Archie said. And rarely better. I have observed this behavioural deviation across more species than your history has names for. You asked me to be less of a closed book. This is what the open page says.

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

The Attic coast, on the road back

THE UNBROKEN BEAST

I have spent four days walking on a choked circuit behind two people drinking out of the air, and it has forced me to apologise to a great many dead cultures I spent my career condescending to.

The rule is everywhere once you see it, and I taught it as superstition. The sacrificial animal must be without blemish, says Leviticus, and says the priest at Olympia, and says every altar from the Ganges to the Tiber. The Roman priest must be sine vitio, sound in body, or the rite does not take. The king must be whole: Nuada of the Tuatha Dé forfeits the throne of Ireland the day he loses his hand and recovers it only when Dian Cécht forges him a working one of silver, Nuada Airgetlám, Nuada of the Silver Arm, a whole king for a whole land. Maim the sovereign and the country goes to waste behind him; the Fisher King bleeds and the fields die. And the Greeks compressed the entire intuition into one word I have publicly dismissed as snobbery, kalokagathia, the beautiful-and-good, the sound body as the visible proof of the ordered soul.

I called all of it a status game. The handsome and the whole declaring themselves blessed and inventing a theology to keep the lame and the scarred out of the temple and off the throne. I believed the wholeness was a symbol the powerful chose because it flattered them.

I no longer think it was a symbol. I think it was a measurement they had lost the instrument for. The System rewards a frame with no dead channel and starves a frame with one, and it has been installed in us for thirty-four thousand years, running below the threshold we could read. They could not see the circuit. They could feel, dimly, that the unbroken body could hold something the broken one could not, and they built that feeling into law and liturgy and the words for virtue, and then the power went quiet and left the rule standing with the reason scrubbed off it.

This is the same shape as the healers feeling the channels they had no name for. We have been carrying the operating manual for this System for longer than we have been farming, folded into our oldest taboos, mistaking it for prejudice because the machine it described was switched off.

I marked an undergraduate down once for arguing kalokagathia might describe something real in the body and not only in the politics. I would like to find him and concede the point.
