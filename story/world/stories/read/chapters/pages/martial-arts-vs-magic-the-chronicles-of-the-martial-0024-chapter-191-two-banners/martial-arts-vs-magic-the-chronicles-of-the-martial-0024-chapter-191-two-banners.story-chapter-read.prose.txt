The sign went up before breakfast.

There was no ceremony to it. I climbed a ladder, hung the lone-star sigil over the door, and then climbed back down to help unload crates. It was made of black silk, crimson thread, with a star wrapped in flame. It looked small against the building but that was fine. Everything looks small until people start walking under it.

Senna's advance team had come through the Bifrost overnight, five of them, all wearing plain Goryeo robes because I'd told her the branch wasn't a parade. Lailah stood in the courtyard directing traffic like she'd been born holding a clipboard, which was funny, because a year ago she'd been a farmer's daughter who'd never seen a city bigger than Scorpion's Kiss.

"That crate goes to the clinic," she said. "No, the other one. Hey Solara, please stop opening boxes with fire."

"But why? I'm being efficient."

"The last one you opened is ash. Just use your hands."

Solara wasn’t listening; she was already elbow-deep in someone's ruined shoulder. A porter had wandered in before the door was technically open, dragging a joint that had been dislocated for three days because he couldn't afford a real healer. She'd set it with a burst of controlled flame that cauterized nothing and healed everything. The man had nearly wept.

He'd been hauling cargo one-armed the whole time because a healer cost more than his week's pay. We didn’t. So I made a note to ask Lailah what porters actually earned in this district, because if a dislocated shoulder was a luxury expense here, that meant the clinic was going to be very busy.

I watched from the frame of the doorway. A widow was arguing with one of Senna's instructors about the breathing lessons. She’d been skeptical about the purpose of a breathing technique, and the instructor had done a fine job destroying the skepticism. Now she was angry that it's free.

"No really, it's free," the instructor said again.

"Nothing is free!" the widow said. "What's the catch?"

"There isn't one." He said it like he'd said it nine times already that morning.

"Look boy, I am your mother’s age! I’ve lived long and seen the world for what it really is. What do you mean there’s no catch? There's always one!"

She left without signing up.

She'd be back within the week. People who distrust something for free usually come back once they've watched their neighbor benefit from it twice.

Children dared each other to touch the banner. One kid got close enough to brush the silk with two fingers before sprinting back to his friends like he'd touched a live wire. They all laughed. Nobody had told them what the star meant, which was the point. Let the meaning arrive slow.

The children touched the silk because it was still only silk to them. By evening, they would have seen a porter leave with his shoulder working, old men eat rice under the support beams, and adults stop their ominous whispering and just ask for help.

I stood there and thought about the game.

In Chronicles of the Heavenly Demon God, the cult had been both villain and hero, and the difference depended entirely on who was telling the story and how much of the world the cult already owned. Win big enough, and the label stopped mattering. Nobody calls it a rebellion once the empire survives it. Nobody questions the war hero whose decisions killed a few million people along the way, not really, not in any way that changes what gets carved on his statue. Back on earth, the general public wasn’t even made aware of Winston Churchill’s other side.

My goal wasn't anything that grand or that bloody. So I just stood there and enjoyed the fact that my cult's first branch had opened with a woman demanding to know why free medicine was free.

Lilian was on the roof, sitting with her knees drawn up, watching the street the way she always did. I climbed up to check on her once. She glanced at me, confirmed I wasn't trying to annoy her, and went back to the street. I left her to it. She had her own fights coming. This one wasn't hers.

I was halfway back down the ladder when Vyrn found me.

The owl came in low and fast, wings beating hard. Few of the newer instructors flinched at the aura that the creature carried. He landed on my shoulder with more weight than he usually bothered to use, and he didn't settle. His head kept turning, tracking something that wasn't there, or wasn't there yet.

"What's wrong with you?"

He didn't answer. He never did, not in words. But he sat unsettled, weight forward, talons flexing at nothing, and my Heavenly Demon’s instincts prickled for a second before I shook them off.

Problems. If they really were big enough to register as problems, then I’d find out sooner or later. For now, I just wanted to watch the day unfold.

Ha-Yun had attended court sessions since she was seven years old. She had learned to read a room the same way her fox-blessed senses read scent, layers underneath layers, truth buried under performance. She had watched her father negotiate grain tariffs, marriage proposals, and border disputes without ever once raising his voice.

She thought she understood what danger in a formal setting looked like.

Lord Fang Yizhen proved her wrong within the first ten minutes.

He entered the throne hall with twenty men in perfect formation, and every motion he made was just correct. Correct like water in a riverbed, nothing stiff in it, nothing that announced itself. He bowed to the exact depth protocol demanded, no more, no less. He praised Goryeo's recovery from the war in language that sounded sincere because it probably was.

Baolian's Emperor, he said, had watched Goryeo's resilience with genuine admiration.

Her nose told her even less than her eyes did. People's scents argued with their faces all the time. That was half of how she read a room. Fang Yizhen's scent and his face said exactly the same thing, meaning either he believed every word he was saying, or he had been doing this for so long there was no seam left to find. Either way, the one advantage she'd carried into every negotiation of her life was useless against him. That made sense; he was a demigod as much as her own mentor was.

Then came the gifts.

Baolian silk, dyed in five colors that Goryeo's own weavers couldn't replicate. A jade set for her father, carved with the sun crest worked so subtly into the pattern that a careless glance would miss it entirely.

And something for her.

A hairpin. Silver, inlaid with a single dark pearl, in a style one shade off a betrothal gift. Every noble in the room noticed. Nobody could have called it that out loud.

Ha-Yun accepted it with both hands and a bow exactly as deep as her father's had been.

The pearl lay cold across her palms. Fang Yizhen did not look at the hairpin. He watched the space between her fingers instead, as if the gift mattered less than whether her hand learned fear before her face did.

Her stomach went cold at what it was testing. Would she flinch? Would she glance at her father and hand Fang Yizhen a crack to read?

She gave him nothing.

The proposal came after, delivered in the same even voice Fang had used to compliment the palace gardens.

"His Majesty wishes to extend an offer of deeper friendship," Fang said. "A permanent advisory office, established here in Hwangcheon, to assist Goryeo through what remain unstable times. Be it trade facilitation or cultural exchange. Shared prosperity between neighbors who have already proven their bond in blood."

Ha-Yun's throat tightened before she understood why. Then she registered every noble's spine straightening at once, dozens of small movements that added up to the whole room recalculating in silence.

Fang kept speaking, and his voice never changed pace.

"As is customary in such arrangements, the office would maintain joint oversight of foreign organizations of uncertain provenance operating within Goryeo's borders, to ensure the safety of all parties involved."

There it was.

Buried in the middle of a sentence about trade routes and cultural exchange, wrapped soft, built to slide past half the room before anyone caught the shape of it.

Ha-Yun's mind went straight to the Divine Cult’s branch. The lone-star banner that had gone up that morning. The clinic full of people her father's own tax offices had never bothered to visit.

Foreign organizations of uncertain provenance.

She looked at her father.

His golden aura had not moved. Not a flicker, not a hum against the stones. That stillness had frightened her once, back when she was young and assumed silence meant calm. She knew better now. It meant he was holding something down with both hands.

Minister Zhou had gone completely rigid beside the throne, his mouth slightly open, the words he usually had ready nowhere to be found.

Nobody in that room could refuse the proposal outright. Refusing meant insulting the Dominion in front of twenty of Shengzong's men. Refusing meant handing Fang Yizhen exactly the grievance his empire specialized in collecting.

And worst of all…

Fang Yizhen was strong enough to collect it right away, in this exact hall, the moment a “no” might leave her father's mouth.

Her father rose from his throne.

"Goryeo thanks the Emperor of the Sun for his generosity," he said, his voice level and formal, "and will study this proposal with the care it deserves."

Fang Yizhen bowed.

He looked satisfied, like a man watching a transaction proceed exactly on schedule. Triumph would have meant he'd ever doubted the outcome. Delay was an acceptable answer.

Delay meant the clause existed now.

It was on the table. It had been read aloud in front of the full court, witnessed, recorded by three separate scribes.

It would not leave that table again.

Fang and his men departed with the same flawless procession they'd arrived with. After she'd heard the report that Fang Yizhen had met Iskandaar already but a fight hadn't broken out, a flicker of hope had risen that maybe a fight would be avoided this time around. But now…

Ha-Yun stood very still.

Iskandaar built with timber.

Baolian built with sentences.

She was no longer sure which frame went up faster.

Ha-Yun found the branch of the Divine Cult – no, the Divine Sect publicly – full when she arrived that evening. She was still wearing her court dress, because the clause was not something she was willing to trust to a messenger.

When she said “full” she meant the way a home gets full, not the way a market does. It wasn't too loud like people had actually decided to stay instead of grabbing what they came for and leaving. Old men sat on benches eating rice from bowls, and a pair of children chased each other around a support post while their mother watched with an expression Ha-Yun hadn't expected to see on anyone in this district. Relief, mostly. Rationed so long the woman looked like she didn't know where to put it.

She stopped in the doorway.

An ache of guilt filled her heart. Had… had her own capital held this many hungry people, this close to the palace, and she had never once walked far enough to see it?

Iskandaar stood near the far wall.

He leaned against the wall with his arms loose at his sides. He held no clasped hands behind the back, none of the posture that a sect leader was supposed to perform. How odd… He was just watching the room like it was the only thing happening in the world.

He didn't notice her at first.

That struck her harder than she expected. A man who could sense a thread of intent from miles away hadn't noticed her walk up beside him, because he was too busy watching an old woman finish her bowl of rice.

He actually cared about this. She searched the scene for the angle, for the audience it was staged for, and couldn't find one.

"...You'll catch flies," she said in the end.

He blinked and looked at her. The quiet focus folded away, and the easier version of him arrived in its place, half a beat late.

"Ha-Yun. How was court today?"

"Complicated."

She told him about it. There was no need to hide anything, not from him. So she told him about the gifts, the pin, and the proposal, word for word, as close as she could remember, ending with the clause about foreign organizations of uncertain provenance.

The softer version of him left again somewhere around the word oversight.

"....."

She watched him work through it, and it wasn't dramatic, just a man connecting dots that he didn't like the shape of.

"Hmm, the Sin of Pride,” Iskandaar Romani said. “So much theatrics when he could have just simply attacked me. But no, he wants a show. Ego is truly the death of efficiency.”

He looked past her as he spoke, toward the benches, the instructor showing a widow how to breathe without pain. Ha-Yun understood his frustration, a sword would have been easier to answer but words and paperwork with the intention of making the other party scared was not the style of the Heavenly Demon.

To begin with, he was not someone who could be scared. Ha-Yun had never seen him like that. Even now, when a demigod roamed the block across, waiting to gut him open.

"So what do we do?"

"Well. If your father signs this, Baolian gets a legal right to walk through that door and ask questions." He nodded toward the room behind them, across the old men and children. "My branch stops being my shield and starts being a shared jurisdiction question. Which is annoying, but not lethal. The Emperor does not understand me.”

The logic was simple. Sure, that if her father refused, that would imply that Gorye has chosen the demon over the Dominion. In writing.Which would give the Emperor and his people reason to cause trouble. But…

Iskandaar and the Goryeo royals had already accepted that fate.

While that didn’t erase Ha-Yun’s and her father’s fear, it did for Iskandaar. Mental games of this level did not affect a man who was so bold to call himself the Demon of Heaven, being hunted by half the Arcane Kings of the world, and even going against the Demon King as well.

Ha-Yun thought of her father on his throne, holding his aura still with both hands.

"But yes,” he sighed. “Given such needless theatrics, it proves our initial hypothesis that an empire such as this doesn't burn the rival's banner. It makes the banner require a permit first. Then it burns it."

She didn't have anything to say to that. Somewhere in the palace, three scribes' copies of the clause were already drying.

"Tell your father I'd like the proposal studied slowly," he said. "Very slowly, actively, obviously slowly. I'm about to make the question a lot more complicated."

"How?"

He didn't answer that directly. He turned his head toward the door.

"Lilian." He called, and she dropped down from wherever she'd been perched, landing without a sound despite her size. "Send word to Nebula," Iskandaar said. "Tell her to bring Valeria."
