As much as Vir wanted to tell Maiya everything right then and there, she was already nodding off, and so Vir instead guided her to the bed and helped her slip out of her clothes before sitting cross-legged on the floor nearby, cycling prana through the night.

Cycling made a better cure for tiredness than sleep, and the more prana he had flowing through his veins, the better off he’d be.

Maiya awoke bleary-eyed the next morning and groggily made her way to a nearby chair, setting her arms down on the table.

“All right,” she said, still not fully awake. “Tell me everything.”

And so he did. Vir recounted his infiltration of the Vimana complex, covering everything from the mejai working on their grand project to the conversation with Amara and her warnings about the gate, finishing with her refusal to let them help.

With each word, Maiya's expression grew darker.

“Who does this Amara think she is?” Maiya muttered, tapping her finger on the table in irritation. “Acting so high and mighty, above our help? We've fought demons and gods and everything in between. And she thinks we're going to just sit here twiddling our thumbs while she works some secret plan that might not even succeed?”

“I don’t think it's about thinking we're beneath her,” Vir said, frowning in thought. “She seemed genuinely concerned that our involvement would cripple their chances. Her words made it sound like I’d already doomed them by just showing up.”

“She’s worried you’d tip them off?” Maiya asked, crossing her arms. “You're telling me none of the hundreds of mejai you saw working at the Vimana were their agents? That not one of them might accidentally draw attention to the plan?”

Vir’s eyes narrowed. “The thought had crossed my mind, but I assume she has a mechanism for vetting people. She clearly has means she shouldn’t. Just like how she knew about us, and how she appeared and disappeared in front of me. I couldn't even read her prana signature.”

“Well, it’s not like we wanted to end up here,” Maiya countered. “And we’ve been doing our best to hide our identities.”

“We have, but even still, if Amara knew about us, I have to wonder if our enemies do too.”

Maiya was quiet for a long moment. “Well, we can't simply sit around and do nothing. What's the plan?”

“At the very least, I'd like to gather more information,” Vir said. “Understand what Amara is actually trying to do. If we know the goal, maybe we can find a way to help that doesn't trigger whatever she's afraid of.”

“I'm coming with you this time,” Maiya said immediately.

Vir nodded. “I assumed as much.”

The city was already awake at this early hour, but as they walked toward the Academy, Vir noticed how few students they passed.

“Odd,” Maiya murmured. “Shouldn't everyone be rushing to classes?”

Vir checked the position of the sun. “We're a bit early, but I felt the same. The halls should be packed.”

After Vir’s revelations about the Vimana, sitting through lectures on basic prana theory felt absurd, so they’d planned on skipping classes today. Everyone else, however, should have been in attendance.

“Darsh! Maiya!” Daya called out, jogging over to them with a bright smile. “Good luck with your exams today!”

Vir and Maiya exchanged confused glances.

“Er, sorry?” Maiya asked, beating Vir to it.

“I know you two will do great!” Daya said, as if it were the most obvious thing in the world.

Vir felt a chill run down his spine. “Daya, the battle exams were yesterday.”

Daya laughed. “Don’t we wish. Honestly can’t wait to get them over with.”

“Right,” Vir replied, entirely unsure of what to make of the situation. “Sorry, forget I said anything.”

“You’re just nervous,” Daya said. “Don't be. You'll both do wonderfully. I need to run, gotta get some last-minute practice in before my own exam this afternoon. See you later!”

She waved and hurried off, leaving Vir and Maiya standing in stunned silence.

“That was...” Maiya trailed off.

“Odd,” Vir finished. “Really odd. She didn’t seem like she was joking, either.”

They continued into the Academy, descending into the underground complex where the battle exams had been held. Sure enough, students were streaming toward the arena rooms, chattering nervously about their upcoming duels.

Vir recognized several people he'd seen celebrating their victories last night at the Bard and the Brew, not a trace of relief on their faces. They all looked as anxious as he’d have expected the morning before their exams.

“This doesn't make sense,” Maiya whispered. “What is this, some elaborate prank?”

Before Vir could respond, a familiar voice called out behind them.

“Darsh! Maiya! Fancy meeting you two here!”

Vir turned to find Vayren approaching, his expression friendly rather than the seething resentment Vir had expected after their duel.

“Vayren,” Vir said cautiously.

“Heading to your exam room?” Vayren asked. “I'm so excited. Finally get to show what I can really do. The examiners paired me against some commoner. Should be an easy victory.”

Vir stared at him. How Vayren had known that beforehand, Vir couldn’t say, though it likely had to do with him being Sawai and sweet-talking the teachers into it.

The boy showed no recognition of their previous fight. After how thoroughly Vir trounced him, he’d have expected nothing less than blind rage—maybe even a punch from the boy.

“The battle exams were yesterday, Vayren,” Maiya said.

Vayren looked at her, confusion crossing his face. Then he laughed. “Good one, Maiya. Almost had me there.” He clapped Vir on the shoulder. “Good luck out there. May the best mejai win!”

He strode off, leaving Vir and Maiya frozen in place.

“Something is very wrong here,” Maiya said, her voice tight.

“Agreed,” Vir said. “Come on. We need to get to that door.”

Vir barely registered the crowd of students as they made their way down.

Had everyone's memories been altered? Or was this some kind of mass illusion? As much as Vir wished to believe there wasn’t anything powerful enough to force that upon an entire city, there unfortunately was.

At long last, they rounded the corner to the final corridor where the guards had been posted in front of enormous double doors that led to the Vimana complex, and when his eyes took in the stonework at the end of the hall, his stomach dropped.

For instead of guards and a door, there was nothing but a solid wall.

“It was right here,” Vir muttered, approaching the wall and running his hand along the smooth stone. “The doors were here. The guards, the…”

“Maybe Amara sealed it after you left?”

“Maybe,” Vir said, though he already knew the horrible truth. Had known it long before he arrived at this floor.

For Prana Vision remained dim the entire time. No vast cavern, no ocean of prana beneath their feet as there had been yesterday.

Activating Dance of the Shadow Demon, Vir sank into the shadow realm, peering out at the innumerable shadows around him.

Shadows that led to arenas and Thaumaturgical labs and hallways…

And not a single one that led into a vast chamber beneath.

No Vimana. No mejai working around the clock. No Amara.

Vir resurfaced, his expression grave.

“That bad?” Maiya asked.

“It's gone,” Vir said quietly, nodding. “All of it, as though it were never there at all.”

Maiya's face went pale. “That's impossible. You saw it. You spoke with Amara down there. How can something like that just… cease to exist?”

“I don’t know,” Vir replied, wondering if the masked woman had something to do with this. It seemed likely, and yet, this was power on a scale even Vir couldn’t comprehend. It wasn’t possible. Shouldn’t be possible.

They stood in silence for a moment, both grappling with the implications.

“Could it have been a dream?” Maiya asked. “Some kind of illusion cast over you?”

“It seems unlikely,” Vir said. “It was real. And besides, that’s not the only thing wrong, is it?”

“No, but that only makes this all the more confusing. What even could…”

She stopped, her eyes widening as the same terrible thought occurred to both of them simultaneously.

“What if,” Vir said slowly, “them being in control of this universe doesn't just mean that things go their way?”

“You mean rewrite reality itself?” Maiya finished, her voice barely above a whisper.

The implications crashed over them like a wave. If the Fateweavers wielded such an iron grip over this universe that they could alter not just future probability but history itself, then everything they thought they knew was worthless. Yesterday could be erased. Events could be undone. People could be made to forget, or cease to be entirely.

“But we remember,” Maiya said. “Why us?”

“Because we're not from this universe,” Vir realized. “We're anomalies. Whatever mechanism they used to rewrite the world, we seem to be immune. Ashani, too, thankfully, though it doesn’t seem like she’ll be waking up anytime soon. There’s something else that bothers me, though.”

“What doesn’t about this situation?” Maiya muttered, pacing around the corridor, frowning in deep concentration.

“Doesn’t this seem weird to you? Nothing changed until today. At least, not that we knew.”

Maiya’s eyes widened. “But then you met Amara. Wait, are you saying what I think you’re saying?”

Vir nodded. “I think Amara was compromised. And… I think she knew she was compromised. Otherwise, she wouldn’t have had that reaction to my arrival. Her claim that I’d doomed them by showing myself… her unwillingness to work with us. It all makes sense in that light.”

“That’s terrifying,” Maiya said, hanging her head. “If this universe’s only hope was nothing more than a puppet… What do we do, Vir?” Maiya asked. “If they can just erase things, how do we fight that? How do we plan for anything when they can simply undo it?”

Vir had no answer. For the first time in years, he felt truly humbled. He felt like he had in Brij. Normal. Powerless.

What was the point of gathering strength, of rising above all mortals, when their enemies operated on another realm entirely?

They might as well be ants in the face of a Prana Swarm.

Around them, students continued streaming toward the arena rooms, chattering about exams they'd already taken, lives they'd already lived. None of them knew that yesterday had been stolen from them. That their memories had been altered or that they were living the same day twice.

Were they? Were these even the same people?

The thought made Vir feel sick. Had his curiosity caused this? Had he brought down the Fateweavers' ire, bringing to bear the true extent of their power?

“Hey,” Maiya said, pulling him into a deep embrace. “It’s not your fault, Vir. You couldn’t have known.”

Vir’s eyes widened. “How did you—”

“Know? C’mon, Vir, we’ve known each other all our lives. I’d be a pretty terrible friend—let alone wife—if I didn’t. Besides, isn’t this just like the old days? Unreasonable odds, the universe stacked against us?”

Vir chuckled, finding the crushing weight of these revelations slightly abate. “I suppose you’re right. Thanks, Mai.”

He broke from her embrace and took a long, slow breath.

“We need to think about this carefully,” Vir said. It was his turn to pace around the corridor. “If we did indeed precipitate this, we have to assume they know of us. But that doesn’t mean they know everything. We know they operate through people. We know they’re not omniscient.”

“So where does that put us?” Maiya asked.

“We need every advantage we can get,” Vir said, stopping his pacing. “That means getting you to your full power and me to mine. Past that… we need to learn more of our enemy.”

“You make it sound so simple,” Maiya laughed. “How do we learn of an enemy with no face and no body?”

“We observe the changes. We learn from them. We figure out the rules of this game before we try to play it.”

“And if there are no rules?” Maiya asked. “If they can simply change reality whenever something inconvenient happens?”

Vir had no answer to that. They stood in the corridor, surrounded by students who didn't know they were living a lie, facing enemies who could rewrite existence itself.

For the first time in a very long time, Vir felt genuine terror settle into his bones.

Not the fear of death—he'd faced that many times. But fear of something far worse. That nothing they did would matter, that they were insects trapped in amber, struggling against forces so vast and incomprehensible that resistance itself was meaningless.

That if their enemy could do this to one universe, they could very well do it to another.

To his.

“Come on,” Vir said quietly. “We need to get out of here. Somewhere we can think.”

They turned and walked back up the corridor, leaving the arena rooms and the students behind. Neither spoke, for what could they possibly say?

Reality itself had become their enemy, and Vir had absolutely no idea how to fight it.
