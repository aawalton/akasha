Sakura did not immediately understand what exactly had snagged on Gilbert’s last words. Then it clicked, and unease crept over her.

“A dead mage’s core?” she repeated, still standing by Crest’s wagon. “Are you serious right now?”

Gilbert looked at her calmly, as if they were talking about a rope, a candle, or a piece of bread.

“I’m only using precise words.”

That, of course, did not help at all. Sakura immediately pictured something utterly revolting, half-dried and carved out of someone’s chest, and grimaced. Apparently that showed on her face a little too clearly, because Gilbert added quietly,

“It’s not exactly what you’re imagining.”

“That’s already something,” Sakura muttered. “Though only barely.”

Fortunately, or maybe unfortunately, the conversation had to be postponed. The wanderers were already getting ready to leave. The horse had fully calmed down, the wagon had been set right, the knocked-over bags and crates had been put back in place, and the music seemed just about ready to return to the forest, as if to prove that no trouble in the world could ruin these people’s mood.

Crest came up to them one last time, still shining as if he saw nothing in the road but romance, not mud, monsters, and death.

“Well then, marvelous Lady Sakura, gloomy Sir Gilbert,” he announced with mournful ceremony, “our roads, alas, part here. We still have to haul this magnificent pile of clutter to a place where people will underpay for it.”

“And we, alas, are not headed for cheerful travels with music,” Gilbert said dryly.

“Oh, that’s fine.” Crest waved it off. “That only means you’re currently in the stretch of life after which people usually start drinking and writing memoirs.”

He laughed at his own line. Ria, already sitting in the wagon and still wincing a little after her fall, said,

“That’s if there’s anyone left to write them.”

“What is this gloom?” Crest protested. “Personally, I’m betting all of us survive our beautiful grand adventures and definitely meet again. The world loves throwing people back together, especially when they didn’t get enough time to properly annoy each other the first time.”

Sakura smiled anyway.

“Then I hope your horse doesn’t try to kill anyone next time.”

“And I hope you won’t come flying out of the forest so suddenly next time,” Ria said, completely without malice. “I almost thought you were some local witch.”

“That still hasn’t been ruled out,” Gilbert said evenly.

“You see?” Crest brightened. “The perfect first impression is already in place.”

“And still, thank you again. For calming the horse,” Ria added with a faint smile.

Sakura only nodded modestly in return.

They wished each other luck, and the wanderers’ well-wishes dragged on so long that if Sakura had been writing them down in a notebook, they would have taken up a full page, maybe more. Especially from Crest, every farewell sounded like a toast. Sakura was much more practical in her choice of words, while Gilbert didn’t seem especially interested in forcing out anything more than, “See you.”

When the wagon, the cart, and the voices finally began to fade between the trees, Sakura stood there a little longer, watching after them. Bright, loud, strange people. They still seemed too calm and too kind for Veyr. Or maybe they simply liked believing that if they believed in the best, everything bad would pass them by.

“That’s enough,” Gilbert said quietly. “Time.”

They waited until the wanderers were far enough away, then headed back into the forest and quickly found Pampli. He greeted them with the look of a creature that was already tired of sitting in bushes and now had a lot to say about it. But as soon as Sakura reached toward him, he suspiciously lifted his head and stared at the new case in her hands, then at Gilbert, as if trying to decide whether the two of them had brought back yet another problem from their latest outing.

“No, it’s not food,” Sakura said. “And not something bad. Not yet.”

Pampli gave her a deeply suspicious snort.

They walked on without saying much until Gilbert finally came back to the subject that had been hanging between them ever since he mentioned the dead mage’s core.

“We can’t go after it right now,” he said.

Sakura frowned at once.

“Because of Faerd?”

“Because of him too. But first of all because of you.”

She shot him a dark look.

“That’s not a criticism,” Gilbert continued calmly. “It’s calculation. A dead mage’s core is not just loot. It’s a residual magical node where too much compressed power, too many traces of skills, and too much unstable background remain after death. Things like that don’t lie by the side of the road. And you don’t go after them at a level where a couple of basic spells can still kill you.”

Sakura looked at him sideways.

“Thanks for believing in me.”

“I do believe in you. That’s exactly why I don’t want you going after it at a level this low.”

He said it too evenly for Sakura to argue with him emotionally. And unfortunately, he was right again.

Gilbert continued in an almost instructional tone, though without sounding preachy.

“The higher your level, the higher your stats, the steadier your channels, and the easier it is for the System to hold your class and new skills in place. The more mana you have, and the more basic durability. You’ve already run into the fact that your anomalous nature comes with restrictions. That means you need a proper reserve in your parameters before you work with dangerous magical objects.”

Gilbert paused briefly.

“In the best case, you won’t even have to touch the magical core, because in our plan I’ll be the only one dealing with it directly. But even then, the methods we’ll need to use to get it may be very dangerous for a beginner. Especially if luck turns against us. Especially if there are too many obstacles in the way.”

Sakura digested that in silence.

“So,” she said at last, “you just spent a very long time explaining that I need to level up before we go after something that important and that disgusting.”

“Exactly.”

“And you couldn’t have just said that from the start?”

“I could have. But I wanted to make sure you understood it completely.”

She snorted, though without any real irritation now.

“All right. So where exactly are we going to get me this new level?”

Gilbert looked ahead.

“I know a place.”

He said nothing more, and that was pure Gilbert. First he would toss out a mysterious line, then make her walk through half a forest, and only then show her what he meant.

They did not have to walk long. The forest gradually thinned, becoming lower, drier above but wetter underfoot. Soon the trees opened up, and a low hill appeared ahead. They climbed it, and Sakura immediately saw why Gilbert had brought her there.

Below them stretched marshy flats. Not one solid swamp, but a network of sticky pools, black inlets, rocky patches, and low dark grass, with black stones jutting up here and there, glowing faintly red from within. Sakura noticed movement almost at once.

There were two obsidian creatures down there. Small compared to the horrors they had recently faced, but still unpleasant. Each one was about waist-high on a person, with broken, angular bodies that looked assembled out of black glass and hardened resin. They wandered along the edge of muddy water, stopping now and then to turn their sharp heads. These two, like that family of obsidian centipedes before them, were not trying to attack each other, which again made Sakura think that these creatures were not always hostile toward their own kind. Maybe they turned aggressive when they got too hungry. Or maybe they fought over territory. She had no answer to those questions yet, though it wasn’t as if she urgently needed them. Her head was already crowded enough with much bigger mysteries she would have liked to solve.

Sakura tensed, and lines from the System flashed before her eyes:

OBSIDIAN SCAVENGER Level: 6

OBSIDIAN SCAVENGER Level: 6

She exhaled.

“Only two?”

“For a start, that’s enough,” Gilbert said.

Then he pulled out the familiar case of scrolls and held it out to her. Sakura understood right away what he wanted, but still asked,

“You mean... me?”

“You,” he confirmed. “No help from me. No help from Pampli. Your own strength, your own decisions, and your own scrolls.”

That sounded disturbing. Just like that, go out there alone against monsters. Pampli immediately let out an offended grumble, clearly insulted that anyone had dared exclude him from something this interesting.

“No,” Gilbert told him at once. “You’re already too big. You’ve had enough food for the near future.”

Pampli flicked an ear angrily. Sakura looked from him back to the case, then down to the creatures below.

The tension, of course, had not gone anywhere. After everything they had been through, the idea of go deal with it yourself sounded a little mocking. But only a little. Mostly, she understood why it had to be this way.

If they really were going after Lir, if the Castle of Justice, Faerd, the dead mage’s core, and who knew what else were ahead of them, she could not stay behind Gilbert forever and hope he would handle everything.

Sakura crouched down and opened the case. The scrolls lay there in a familiar stack, whispering dryly under her fingers. When she concentrated hard enough, she could feel their faint vibrations of energy, of magic. But now was not the time for anything like real meditation, so she flipped through the titles quickly, trying not to panic and not to grab the first thing that looked usable. The obsidian creatures were only level six. Not too powerful, but still dangerous if she got too close and handled it stupidly.

There were plenty of scrolls, but right now all of them suddenly seemed useless. A cutting scroll, for example, was too risky if she missed. It would only make them angry. A fire scroll didn’t fit this wet, swampy terrain very well. She had already used the restraining net once before, but now she wanted to consider more options. Conjured daggers could be useful, but not as her main card. The crafting scrolls looked doubtful.

Sakura couldn’t help remembering how she had already stood with this case over a battlefield once before, back by the river, making decisions in a matter of seconds. Now she had more time. And somehow that only made it harder, because she was thinking too much and getting in her own way.

“Don’t rush,” Gilbert said, as if he had read her mind. “Don’t choose what looks impressive. Choose what keeps you alive.”

“Nice line, Gilbert,” she breathed quietly. “Did you rehearse that one?”

She thought he would snort or snap something back, but instead he only answered dryly,

“I didn’t rehearse it. I’ve just survived often enough.”

For some reason, those words made her uneasy all over again. That mage had too many scars, and every time she ran into that fact, something inside Sakura flinched.

So she huffed, forced herself not to think about it, and went back to the problem of the fight. Pampli made another dissatisfied sound and shifted from paw to paw, still clearly hoping that at the last second they would let him join in.

“No,” Gilbert repeated without even looking at him. “Stay.”

Pampli turned away with the expression of someone profoundly disappointed in the fairness of the universe.

Sakura finally chose several scrolls and stood up. Inside, she felt tense. Not paralyzed with fear the way she had before. Instead, her mind had gone unnaturally sharp and clear, picking every tiny detail out of her surroundings. She was tightly wound, the way a person gets before something they have to do themselves, because too much will depend on it afterward.

She looked down once more at the marshy flats and the two obsidian scavengers wandering aimlessly through the dark water.

Then she tightened her grip on the case.

“All right,” Sakura said. “Looks like a new level won’t earn itself. It needs a little help.”

And after one deep breath in and out, without giving herself time to change her mind, without even looking back at Pampli and Gilbert, she started down the slope with steady determination.
