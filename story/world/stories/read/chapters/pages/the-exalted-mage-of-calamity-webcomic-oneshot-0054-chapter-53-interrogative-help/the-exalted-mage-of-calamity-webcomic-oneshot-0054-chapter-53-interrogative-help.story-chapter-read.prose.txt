“So? What did you want to talk about?” Helm asked as he took the seat across from her.

Veronica held his gaze.

“I heard you’re in here because of High Crates,” she said evenly. “Because of a shipment gone wrong.”

His eyes stayed sharp. “Yeah. Shipment to Lilybed. Attacked by dozens of bandits. Great stuff,” he replied, voice laced with sarcasm. “What’s it matter to you?”

Veronica rested her hands on the table and folded them together. “I’ve recently become acquainted with a merchant named Galland.”

A flicker of recognition passed through Helm’s eyes.

“I heard the two of you were… partners,” she continued. “Both tasked with advising on a winter route for a shipment. But things went wrong, and you were punished for it.”

He leaned back in his chair.

“Good enough summary, I guess,” he said casually. “Though I’d say he got the better end of the deal. Gets to walk free while I sit here and rot for another year and a half.”

A year and a half… so that’s when he gets out.

“I’m here today because I wanted to understand what happened,” Veronica said.

He raised a brow. “Why? Case is three years old. I’ve been in here all that time, and ain’t nobody come by askin’.”

“I came because I intended to hire Galland for a private arrangement,” she explained. “However, when I visited High Crates… that’s when I learned about his debt.”

Helm said nothing, studying her.

“I also spoke with a senior merchant named Dain.”

Helm’s brows drew together, and he clicked his tongue. “You spoke with Dain? Tch. Head up his ass, that one.”

Veronica kept her expression neutral. “According to him, you and Galland were punished due to the perceived risk level of the chosen route. An unusually large bandit force attacked the caravan, killing most of the crew and the Scavenger transport guards.”

Helm crossed his arms but did not interrupt.

She continued her recount.

“Because the actual threat far exceeded what was reported, people lost their lives,” she said. “You were punished for failing to identify a bandit encampment along the route. And in your office, there was reportedly a news article you both overlooked. By the time the truth surfaced—”

“That’s bullshit,” Helm cut in.

Veronica paused. “Excuse me?”

He turned his head slightly, lifting his chin. “Ain’t no news article. That’s a pile of bullshit.”

She raised a brow. “What do you mean?”

“There was never any article like that in our office.”

“Then are you saying Dain lied?”

He clicked his tongue again. “He didn’t lie… but he did at the same time.”

“What?”

Helm bit down, jaw tightening. He leaned forward, elbows braced on the table. “We had a news article, yeah. Simple stuff. Check the news for anything happening in the local area along the route,” he explained. “But bandits? Fucking bandits? A whole encampment? Are you telling me we would’ve missed something that obvious?”

Veronica remained silent, allowing him to continue.

“We read multiple different publications. News of all types. There’s only two major presses in town. The paper found in our office later? It didn’t mention any bandit camp. Not a word.”

“What do you mean, not a word?” she asked.

“They seized most of our belongings, including that paper,” he said. “But me and Galland went over it a dozen times. It was a major contract. We wanted everything to run smooth. We were as diligent as anyone else. And I can tell you this—the paper we had mentioned no bandits.”

“What about other copies? It’s a new article, right? Did anyone else have them?”

He shook his head. “The other articles in town had it correct. Each one had a section mentioning bandit activity. It was only ours that didn’t. Almost like it was on purpose.”

“What are you insinuating?” Veronica asked.

Helm stiffened with a rough exhale, crossing his arms again. His gaze drifted aside.

“Someone tampered with things,” he said. “Planted evidence. Things altered or swapped. Stuff like that. But I know there was nothing in that article about bandits. If there had been, we would’ve chosen another route. Hell, I'm sure the other guys knew about it, too, but ignored it.”

Veronica hummed softly. “And you believe that,” she said. “That’s why you refused to testify to being guilty.”

He looked back at her. “Of course not. I know what I read. That busybody Galland does too… or did.”

His voice roughened to stone. “But his confidence cracked. He started doubting his own memory. Said he couldn’t swear there wasn’t a section about bandits. Couldn’t say for certain. So he took the fall.”

Helm leaned forward again, pressing a finger firmly against the tabletop.

“And when he fell,” he said, voice low, “he dragged me down with him.”

The room fell silent.

Veronica studied him, weighing every word, every flicker of expression he made.

“If someone altered the evidence,” she said slowly, “then this wasn’t negligence.”

Helm’s jaw tightened.

“No,” he replied. “It wasn’t.”

His eyes became heavy, tone shifting lower, almost to a deep whisper.

“It was a setup.”

Veronica remained seated, quietly studying the man across from her—his posture, the set of his shoulders, the tension coiled beneath his stillness.

Helm carried resentment like a second skin. That much was undeniable. And the longer she spoke with him, the more certain she became that he was the catalyst behind the Scavengers’ eventual downfall.

But she couldn’t afford to let events unfold at their original pace.

She needed to accelerate them.

“I know you’re innocent,” she said.

Helm turned toward her. His expression remained unchanged, but his eyes sharpened, weighing her, measuring intent.

“And why’s that?” he asked.

“I find everything that’s happened to you and Galland a bit too… convenient,” she replied. “The pieces fell into place too quickly. Just like you said—you had an article warning of a bandit encampment, and yet neither of you remembers it mentioning anything of the sort. I also don’t believe Galland to be capable of such a simple mistake. Your contracts too, it almost feels like they were designed for a situation like this.”

She leaned forward slightly.

“Helm, I think something else is moving behind the scenes. And it isn’t High Crates.”

That got a reaction from him.

“What do you mean not High Crates?”

“I think the Scavengers orchestrated all of it,” she said. “I believe they’re engaged in illegal operations that connect to several businesses throughout Ronswick. Some individuals from High Crates may be involved—but not the house as a whole.”

His brows knit together. “Scavengers, huh?” he repeated. “The transport company. What makes you say it’s them?”

She shook her head. “I can’t explain how I know. You’ll have to accept that they’re behind everything—why you’re in jail, why Galland is burdened with a decade-long debt, and why I need your help to set things right.”

“My help?” he echoed. “And how exactly am I supposed to help you?”

“Do you know anything—anything at all—that could help expose the Scavengers?” she asked. “They’re a transport company. They must keep records, manifests, route logs… something.”

Veronica straightened.

“I arrived in Ronswick only a few days ago. I’m unfamiliar with the city. But you—you worked with them closely for the shipment, right? There has to be evidence somewhere that could prove your innocence.”

His eyes widened slightly at her words, shock flickering across his face before slowly receding.

A knock sounded at the door. Both of them turned.

“You have three minutes left,” came the officer’s voice from outside.

Helm looked back at Veronica, lips pressed thin. After a moment, he spoke.

“I’ve been in here three and a half years,” he said. “Plenty of time to think. About how it all happened. How everything fell apart. What exactly screwed me and Galland over.”

Veronica waited, silent and attentive.

“And you know what?” he continued. “Everything kept circling back to that damn newspaper.”

“Do you think it was fake?” she asked.

He shook his head. “No. The article was real. Galland and I read it several times. But…”

“But?”

Helm released a frustrated breath.

“The mention of bandits was printed on the final page—the back page,” he said. “When Galland and I read it, that page was blank. Completely blank. And yet, by the time it appeared in court, it wasn’t.”

Veronica’s eyes narrowed. “Could someone have swapped the page?”

“No,” he said. “There were clear indicators it was the original paper. Our fingerprints. Pen markings we’d made while reviewing it. It was the same copy from our office.”

She held his gaze, watching his eyes flicker as he sorted his thoughts. “Then what are you suspecting?”

Helm pressed his lips together before speaking.

“False ink.”

“False ink?” she repeated.

“The last page must’ve been written with it,” he said. “Invisible at first, then revealed once certain conditions are met—water, sunlight, heat. That sort of thing.”

Veronica considered that. “And how does this connect to the Scavengers?”

“The Scavengers’ boss is a man named Tarenick Pines,” Helm replied. “His brother serves as one of the chief ledger record keepers here in town—the office that handles private correspondence, legal records, notarizations… even documentation for the nobility. They work essentially as a private City Hall. They’re the only establishment in Ronswick with access to specialty compounds like false ink.”

“And you think the Scavengers planted a newspaper for you using false ink through that office?”

He nodded. “It’s the only explanation that fits. And if records were falsified, that’s where the evidence would be stored. Not anywhere obvious, though. Hell, they might not even be in the office—but something there would lead you to the real treasure trove. It would be the first place I’d look.”

His gaze returned to her, his demeanor sharpening.

He clicked his tongue. “Problem is, it’s a city-sanctioned office. They’ve got close ties to the Scavengers, and some of their own men are stationed there as guards. And even if you find proof… it might not be enough.”

“Not enough?” she asked.

“I planned to plant recording devices once I got out,” he said. “Place them around the Scavenger headquarters and the ledger office. Have someone try to make a deal with them, catch them talking and record it all in a magic crystal. Make them hang themselves with their own words.”

Recording devices…

So it was Helm. In the future, he was the one who brought them down.

“I see,” Veronica said. “That’s something I should be able to handle.”

He leaned back in his chair with a skeptical huff. “You, huh?” His eyes swept over her, assessing. “You’re young, miss. You’d be going up against a mercenary company and a guarded ledger office. Is Galland really worth whatever you’re after?”

She chuckled softly. “Are you trying to talk me out of securing your freedom?”

He snorted. “Hah. If you’re that confident, go ahead.” He turned his head toward the wall. “If you don’t, I will—once I get out of here.”

A knock sounded at the door. A moment later it opened, and the city officer stepped inside.

“Time’s up.”

Veronica rose and moved toward the exit. As she reached the doorway, she paused and turned her head slightly, looking back at Helm.

“You won’t have to wait that long,” she said quietly. “I’ll bring them down like the world depends on it.”

For the first time, Helm didn’t respond with sarcasm.

He watched her leave in silence, something cautious and newly lit stirring behind his eyes.
