Ten Years Ago:

“Fighting isn’t only about strength and speed,” Roger Gerald said.

He and his stepson sat on the couch. Jessie had her head on his lap; the six-year-old hadn’t stopped sobbing for four days. Alessa had left after two, and that had only made it worse; now, Jessie wasn’t just crying from pain. Alessa had been leaving a lot lately, but usually only for half an hour to re-center herself.

Two days were enough time for Roger to be pretty sure she wasn’t coming back.

So, he had a stepson who needed him, and a daughter who did, too. All he could do for her right now was be a pillow and offer a comforting back rub. But he could teach Kade. Today, they were watching a high school fencing tournament, being broadcast live from the rebuilt Suns stadium.

“That one’s going to lose,” Kade said. He pointed. One of the girls was being driven back across the pitch. Her parries seemed sloppy and slow, and her ripostes and thrusts were hesitant.

Roger nodded. “It looks that way. Her technique is worse, she’s slower, and the other girl’s got a good two inches and, oh, fifteen pounds on her. It’s probably all muscle, too. But I’m not so sure she’s losing.”

The right-hand girl stepped back. Back again. And then she couldn’t step back another time. She was out of space and unable to maneuver. Roger saw it first. The moment the left-hand girl realized she’d won. She launched into a committed lunge. Her foil tip snapped forward.

For the first time, the losing girl’s parry was perfect. Her riposte was almost casual, but the left-side girl couldn’t react in time.

The point was called.

The fight reset.

“What happened?” Kade asked.

Roger didn’t pat the boy’s shoulder, but only because Jessie moaned in pain, and he had to focus on her for a moment. When she’d settled down, he waved at the screen. The fighters were evenly matched now. “The girl on the right set a trap and got the first point of the bout out of it. The one on the left fell for it. It’s not enough to win, but it was enough to gain the initiative. Right-side is weaker, slower, and her technique is sloppier, but she’s going to win, because her strategy was better, and because she grabbed the initiative and won’t give it up.”

This Year:

I hadn’t asked for Stormbreak.

People had died because of it.

But it was the perfect weapon, and as the negative zone appeared around me and the strongest positive zone I’d ever seen formed around Queen Mother Yalerox, I had no other option but to embrace the trap I’d set and hope it’d be enough.

“What is this?” Yalerox asked.

And I laughed through my agony. Yalerox’s positive zone expanded. It filled almost the whole Eye of the Storm. The pressure was overwhelming as she tried desperately to cast a finishing spell—but it all meant nothing. The Eye of the Storm was open, and its Mana poured into her. And, as it did, Stormbreak drank deep.

The Eye of the Storm wanted to fill Queen Mother Yalerox’s Mana. Stormbreak was designed to burn it off.

Two flashes of light. One that lasted no time at all, and one that echoed eight times, on and on, redoubling as it did.

Portal Collapse In: 59:54

She was dead. I’d won.

The rest of it didn’t matter.

I used Stormbreak.

Exclusions on Ellen and Tanat. They both had to be in fighting shape for this. Flowers began blossoming around me—and around the Placid King’s many tentacles and the core of his body. I pushed my will into the lightning flower blooming around the Placid King as Mana poured into me from the Eye of the Storm.

And oh, what Mana it was. To describe it as a flood wouldn’t do it justice. It filled me, then overflowed my core and poured out, even as Stormbreak lapped it up in an attempt to drain me completely. The bond between Ellen and me reversed; Mana surged into her Voidwalker-enhanced body and core, and her presence shifted as she realized what was happening. She started casting, trying to burn through all the Mana, too.

That wouldn’t be enough. It wouldn’t be anywhere near enough. Stormbreak fired. My own flower pulsed with energy and didn’t stop, the Mana flooding down from the Eye of the Storm too much for it to consume. The Placid King’s tentacles weren’t so lucky. Pulses of lightning ripped into them, searing flesh and evaporating his saltwater blood. A cloud of steam rippled across the monster’s body as the central blossom fired a few seconds later—just as the SS-Plus-Ranked monster withdrew his tentacles from the Lightning Tower.

Just as I’d hoped.

I lunged at the Placid King. Stormbreak was still pulsing around me, and as I made contact with the Paragon’s body, the lightning arced over to him, too. Pain. Massive waves of it. But that didn’t matter; I’d reforged my core to handle it. I could do this. I had to do this.

Lightning poured through my hands and feet. It rippled across the Placid King’s body. The Eye of the Storm fed into me, and I used the extra Mana to fuel my attack. The Storm’s Blade glowed a hot red, so much lightning pulsed around it. I thrust it into the Placid King’s body, and at the same time, used Stormfall.

The shockwave, coupled with my will releasing its hold on Stormbreak, was massive. It rippled out, knocking the monstrous octopus’s tentacles away from his body. I went flying, and gigantic chunks of flesh splattered across the portal world. My Unique skill was still feeding, though—still drawing from the Eye of the Storm.

Then it stopped.

Tanat closed the Eye just enough to cut the flood of Mana off, and my Unique skill stopped. I had nothing left. No Mana, and essentially no Stamina.

But a hundred yards away, the Placid King lay. Watery blood flooded outward in an arterial spray, and three more tentacles had been severed. His countless eyes stared at me, and his body twitched. Health was already repairing the wounds, but even for a monster of his rank, what I’d accomplished would take more than a handful of seconds to heal off.

We had a window. A few seconds—maybe half a minute—before his nerves and muscles recovered, and before the pain stopped being overwhelming.

I looked up. The Storm’s Blade waved in the air. “Now!” I yelled.

And Ellen leaped from the tower as Tanat re-opened the Eye and flooded us both with Mana.

I summoned Dad. Tanat was only a few seconds behind Ellen, and the four of us ripped into the Placid King’s wounded form. Shadows poured across the battlefield, green-black lightning filling the space between storm and shadow maze as she cast Abyssal Storm and Shadow Puppeteer together. The shadow clone tore into the Placid King alongside Dad, whose longsword cleaved into the base of a tentacle. We had twenty seconds—or less—to do as much damage as possible.

I didn’t hold back, either.

The only time I stepped out of Cyclone Forms was to generate more Lightning Charges for Rolling Thunder. Hands of the Thunder God rippled out in a roaring, crackling barrage, boring into the Placid King’s body like needles searching for something in a haystack. None of them was sufficient to kill the monster. I didn’t care. The goal was damage. Do as much damage as I could—and try to find the Placid King’s core.

I was going to destroy it, so help me god.

Magda was…not dying.

She wished she was, though.

The God of Thunder had saved her life, but she wasn’t sure if the act counted as a kindness. Instead of fading off into oblivion, she was watching…something.

It wasn’t possible. The Stormblade was strong, yes. He’d obliterated her in battle, then turned around and defeated Kalorwan with only minimal trouble. That was…surprising…for a brand new S-Ranker. But it wasn’t shocking. Ranks only measured power levels so well.

What she was witnessing as the God of Thunder held her close to his body was…well, shocking was both apt and horrifically understating it.

The Placid King was…losing.

The God of Thunder had started a core-bonding with her. She’d enjoyed it at first. The process was comforting and healing, and yet, even though it felt right, she couldn’t relax like she usually did. Her emotions were a sea of chaos such that she’d never felt in all the millennia she’d served the Paragon of the Stillwave Path. Her thoughts were a torrent of fears, anxieties, and pain, and the God of Thunder, as wonderous and terrifying as he was, couldn’t compete.

She disengaged from the core-bond. “I need to focus on this, my lord. My master is…”

“Dying? Yes. The Placid King is going to die if he doesn’t escape.” The God of Thunder made no indication that he was disappointed in Magda’s choice, and she relaxed fractionally. Now that she was…more recovered, if not whole…she was more confident that she’d survive this encounter with the SS-Plus-Ranked Paragon, and that her core wouldn’t be harvested.

“It’s interesting, isn’t it?”

“My lord?” Magda asked.

“When I found Kade, he was dying after a fight with a C-Rank boss. Can you believe that? A C-Ranker killing the man who’d become my disciple. I think I found him more of a novelty than anything. You see why, don’t you? He’s incredibly headstrong, too determined to let himself lose, and willing to suffer as much as it takes to win. Right now, he’s in worse shape than he’s ever been in, with the exception of when his core shattered and he kept on fighting. I told you about that, right, Magda?”

The God of Thunder’s hand rested on Magda’s flank, right below her ruined fin. He’d shifted into his human form. She shook her head to clear it. The boy—no, the Stormblade—had taken horrific injuries. His back bled under his armor from reopened wounds. His casting hand was all but destroyed. And every inch of skin, exposed or not, was blackened, charred, and smoking. The dark smoke swirled around him.

He hardly seemed to notice.

“My master’s aura has collapsed,” Magda said after a moment.

“Exactly. Without that aura, the Placid King can’t suppress Kade’s killer instinct. The fight is over. Either he retreats, or Kade kills him.” The God of Thunder went silent for a long time, and Magda relaxed into him. Not all the way, though. She hadn’t forgotten that feeling of fear and unease when she’d realized that the God of Thunder would just as easily harvest her as anything else—and that he only cared about her core.

Then he continued, his voice musing almost idly. “I wonder how he got that portal world set up so quickly…and if he has any more tricks up his sleeve.”

The Placid King recovered.

We’d done an incredible amount of damage, but even so, the Paragon of the Stillwave Path had half a dozen massive tentacles. His body bled, but we hadn’t found his core, and his healing had overwhelmed us.

The plan had been for me to fire a second Stormbreak, but speaking honestly, I couldn’t survive another Eye of the Storm-empowered activation. My body should have already been done. I had no Stamina left; every drop went into healing the wounds I’d given myself or the ones the Placid King had inflicted. The Shadow Cell closed around a tentacle, ripping rectangles of flesh clean off of it, as Ellen continued to fight. She echoed it with Arcane Resonator, then reactivated it.

She was still following the plan. So were Dad and Tanat; their swords flashed and cut into another tendril.

But the Placid King was up and moving again.

His eyes focused on his damaged body; four tentacles were gone. The wounds from my barrage of Hands of the Thunder God still poured saltwater blood onto the portal world’s dirt.

“We need to back off!” Ellen shouted over the roar of spells and howling wind. “He’s getting back up!”

It wasn’t possible. Nothing could survive the sheer amount of damage we’d done. But we hadn’t hit anything vital, and he was too big for decapitation. I doubted that’d stop him, either.

Like it or not, we were in trouble.

And we were in it for the long haul.

The Placid King spent the first thirty seconds—an eternity—trying to process what had happened.

He’d been winning. No, not winning. He’d been inevitable. Any yet, somehow, things had gone wrong. Lightning coursed through his nerves. His movements were slow, and his body numb to his efforts. His aura wouldn’t push out. Nothing he did worked.

The Stormblade and his friends had caused actual damage.

Not merely trimming a tentacle. He routinely did that as they aged, replacing them with younger, more functional versions. But real damage. The kind that would be a setback.

He couldn’t allow himself any more setbacks. His obsession with the God of Thunder had been foolish; let the old lightning worm ascend! See if he cared! The God of Thunder was irrelevant, and so was his maniac of a disciple. The sooner he extricated himself from this mess, the sooner he could focus on what really mattered. Not his feud, but his ascension.

As lightning and shadow ripped through the Placid King’s body, he gradually came to a realization. His attempts to maintain his aura weren’t working, but they were getting in his own way.

He stopped trying to use it. Let it collapse.

And the fear hit him.

The Stormblade and his friends had caused actual damage!

Four tentacles. Lances of electricity that had bored through almost to his core. Explosions of fountaining gore from thousands of square-shaped holes in his limbs. He was hurt!

He had to flee. To retreat. To fight another day. The maniac Stormblade and the Shadowstorm Witch didn’t matter. The God of Thunder didn’t matter. Even ascension didn’t matter.

His body extended outward, his half-dozen major tentacles grappling with the world, and the Placid King slowly pulled himself back toward his escape portal. Saltwater blood trailed behind him, and his aura pulsed out and cracked in waves as he retreated. He had to get to his portal and close it behind him. Once he was there, he could recover—and more importantly, he’d be safe from this lunatic. No one would dare attack an SS-Plus-Ranked Paragon on their own turf. That’d be suicidal.

Yes, retreat. If he could just leave, that’d be all he needed right now.

The wind howled around him. Behind him, shadows blossomed, and lightning cracked in the sky overhead.
