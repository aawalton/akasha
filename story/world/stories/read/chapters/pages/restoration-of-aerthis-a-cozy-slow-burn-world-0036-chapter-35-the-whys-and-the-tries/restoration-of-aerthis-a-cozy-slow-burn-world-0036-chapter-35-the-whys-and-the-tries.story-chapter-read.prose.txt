The road stretched on without hurry. Dust clung to her boots, sunlight filtered through shifting leaves, and Elynara walked with a steady pace that didn’t quite match her thoughts.

She tapped her staff now and then to keep rhythm. The same rhythm she tried – and mostly failed – to match inside her head.

Meditation, as she understood it, had a shape, a process to it. Sit, breathe, focus. Let the body rest while the mind reaches inward. Easy in theory. She had done it often enough to feel proud of her progress.

So why not while walking?

Her legs knew what to do. Her eyes scanned the road out of habit. There was no one else around. It should work.

Except it didn’t.

The first time she focused fully on gathering mana, her pace slowed to a crawl. She blinked, startled to find her staff rooted in the dirt and both feet stopped entirely.

The second time, she kept moving, only to feel the mana unravel, slipping away before she could shape it. Every attempt after that followed the same pattern. Too deep, and her feet stalled. Too loose, and her grip shattered.

By midmorning, her shoulders sagged from more than just walking.

Starpuff hovered behind her ear, trilling quietly with no particular sympathy.

None at all. It probably just wanted more mana to eat instead.

Though it had done enough for the day, having spent the morning attempting to balance a leaf on Elynara’s head while drifting upside down.

“You try training while moving,” she muttered. “See how long you last.”

The puff ignored her. Typical.

Still, Elynara refused to let the frustration root. That was the trap. Doubt clogged everything faster than bad footing.

And why doubt when there is so much beauty and magic to explore still?

Even if the process is a little tiresome, it just means she needs more practice.

So she changed her goal.

If she couldn’t cycle mana while walking, maybe she could gather it? Just draw it in and hold it.

That too, as it turns out, wasn’t easy.

With her reserves already dented – both from spell flaring during the Bonefang ambush and from Starpuff-misuse-fun-time – the air offered little comfort.

Drawing in ambient threads felt easy enough when stationary. But it fell back to feeling like she was trying to gather mist with a net. Some came. Most slipped through.

But it helped, and was easier than meditation. Her focus sharpened. Her steps regained their pace.

It wasn’t yet efficient, or elegant. But it kept her moving. And thinking. Always thinking.

Why didn’t this work? Why did it feel different now than it had back in Oltenar, despite having more mana around?

Mana moved differently when she moved, that much was clear. The push and pull of breath didn’t line up with steps. The body wanted one rhythm, the mind another. And her staff added a third. Everything tugged out of sync.

Elynara wondered, for a moment, how others trained. And how they overcame this issue.

But maybe no one had.

That thought wasn’t discouraging. If anything, it made her grin.

She could be the first! The pioneer.

Or well, she supposed if there was nobody now, there might have been in the past. But not being a ‘pioneer’ didn’t feel any less of an achievement to her.

Her next attempt lasted all of twenty-five seconds before Starpuff collided with her ear mid-drift and sent her flailing.

She staggered, caught herself, and glared at the puff now spinning innocently just out of reach.

“One of those days I’m going to turn you into a lantern.”

It pirouetted in place, utterly unfazed.

By midday, she gave up on perfection and settled for partial success. A half-cycle here, a pulse of gathered mana there. Enough to keep her skills in motion. Enough to keep her thoughts organized.

Her notebook, sitting safely in her satchel, rattled softly as she walked.

Elynara had pages to write later. About rhythm disruption. About the odd way the mana swayed while she tried to gather it in motion.

And about how to maybe fix all of that.

But even without writing it down, the pieces were forming. There was something here, even if it hadn’t taken shape yet.

And if she managed to crack it, she would no longer need to stop every few steps just to practice her magic.

It was such a wonderful idea all the frustrations simply melted away at it.

She would make it work!

Eventually.

But for now, the road kept going. The light shifted golden. Her legs ached a little but kept moving.

When her shadow stretched out long across the packed trail, she paused just once to gather breath and mana together. She let it sit in her chest for a moment, warm and steady.

Yes. Progress was being made.

The path bent until the trees opened just enough to reveal a split ahead. Packed earth gave way to two narrower tracks, one disappearing straight ahead, the other curling left around a rise where the grass grew taller.

Elynara slowed and tugged her map from the satchel. Not that she didn’t already memorize the road before, but it felt right to check.

The ‘main’ road going straight ahead would take her straight to another crossroads and then the farther villages.

The left reached the closer stop first, then she would need to backtrack to the main route again further on. Alicia had written both names on the guild slip.

No real choice at all, either way, as she would visit both. But she still weighed it, tracing the lines she had drawn herself.

Starpuff drifted over her shoulder, humming faintly, head tilting at the map. Its glow brightened once, then dimmed again, as if approving her careful study.

She folded the parchment neatly and tucked it away. “Left,” she murmured to herself. “Then back here again.”

Movement on the far stretch of the main road caught her eye.

She straightened, staff shifting lightly in her grip.

Silhouettes were coming into view. Four, maybe five figures, specks against the pale road. Too upright for merchants. Too even in pace for wanderers. Gear caught sunlight in glints – metal edges, polished leather, a shield or two. Adventurers. Marcus had mentioned most would be heading back soon.

Elynara lifted her staff in a small, casual wave.

One of the figures lifted a hand back. A simple acknowledgment, nothing more.

They passed at a comfortable distance, boots crunching over the dry edge of the road. A faint smell of oil and travel drifted on the breeze. For a moment she caught a scrap of a laugh, someone telling a story she couldn’t quite hear.

Then they were gone, their voices fading back into the hum of insects.

Elynara smiled faintly at the simplicity of it.

She shifted her satchel to a more comfortable position before turning her boots toward the left path. The forest’s shadow followed a little way, but sunlight reached across the grass beyond, spilling into a low meadow dotted with white and purple blooms. The scent of them mixed with the warm dust rising from the trail.

Starpuff zipped ahead colors changing in quick bursts. It darted between tufts of grass and skimmed the surface of a puddle left from last night’s rain, leaving tiny rings of ripples behind.

Elynara adjusted her grip on the staff, pace steady.

“We’ll reach the village before dusk if we keep moving,” she said softly.

The puff trilled back, drifting just ahead.

She glanced over her shoulder once, catching a last glimpse of the adventurers on the main road. Their figures were shrinking into the shimmer of heat above the earth, shields and cloaks blurring until only the rhythm of their steps remained.

They had their work. She had hers.

She found herself walking faster without meaning to. Her mind calmer for having chosen a path – even one chosen for her. Each step gave her more space to plan, to sort her thoughts.

Elynara wondered briefly if the adventurers had guessed she was the one running all the errands in Oltenar while they had been away. Probably not. That thought made her grin. Less attention suited her fine.

Starpuff looped back and forth, trailing faint glows that vanished quickly in the daylight. It was practicing again. Circles, corkscrews, half-finished symbols she still couldn’t name.

She scribbled a note about that last night; she would add another tonight.

The rise ahead offered a better view of the horizon. She climbed it in slow, deliberate strides, staff planted firmly each time. At the top, she paused for a breath.

The left path curved away through rolling green and the first scatter of rooftops peeked above a distant copse of trees. Smoke curled thin and pale from what looked like a small communal oven. Children’s shouts floated faintly across the open air.

Even from here she could feel the presence of their way of life.

She shifted her pack again, nodded once to herself, and began the descent.

Starpuff drifted lower and perched briefly on her shoulder, before it zipped forward again to lead the way.

By the time the village came into view proper, the sun hovered low, casting stretched shadows over the field’s edge.

Her mana core sat heavier in her chest again. Not quite full yet, but she had made progress.

It hadn’t come easily. Her steps had stumbled more than once, and her breath had caught enough times to make her frown, but she had done it. She had learned how to gather mana while walking.

Progress!

Elynara jotted a quick line into her notebook without breaking stride, then clicked her tongue and tapped the staff against her shoulder to annoy Starpuff, who had been smugly circling overhead since her last Starpuff-failed attempt.

The spirit let out a whiny trill, which she took as a full admission of guilt.

The village wasn’t large, but it wasn’t just a scatter of farmhouses either. There were fences here, proper paths between buildings, and a small square where a weather-worn well sat at the center.

It felt organized. Tired, but holding together.

She passed through a gate that was more symbolic than defensive, her boots thudding softly against the packed earth. Eyes watched from windows and doorways, cautious at first, then curious. A boy carrying a basket of onions ran past, then doubled back and ran off again after spotting her staff.

Not long after, an older man approached, posture straight despite a limp, gray hair tied back with a rough cord. He didn’t bother with pleasantries.

“Guilder?”

“Elynara. From Oltenar,” she replied, holding out the folded request slips. “Here on errand work, mapping, and shrine surveys.”

That earned her a long look. Then he nodded once.

“Elder Maren. You’ll want the corner house with the red roof. My daughter keeps the spare room there. It’s small, but the floor won’t eat your feet.”

“I appreciate not having my feet eaten.”

That got the faintest snort.

“You’ll find dinner by the fire if you’re settled before dusk. Just don’t step in the grain drying racks. That’ll get you ran out faster than a thief.”

“Understood.”

Introductions continued, a little less stiff now. He took the guild papers, asked questions with more weight than most, and didn’t seem to like vague answers.

Elynara held her ground, giving him the facts plainly: the mines near Oltenar had collapsed, some deep things crawled out, but the town was safe. The guild was stretched thin. This visit was part of an ongoing plan to check and preserve spirit sites.

He nodded in understanding.

“We’ll make space for you. And if you’re mapping shrines… then yes. Someone should be doing that.”

Another nod, and with that, Elder Maren turned and limped back toward the square. No fuss or formal farewell.

She wasn’t sure how to feel about that.

Starpuff dipped lower, circling lazily once before vanishing under a roof eave. Probably off to explore.

Elynara followed the directions toward the red-roofed house, which sat tucked between two barns, a short wooden fence half-collapsed around it. A girl barely older than herself greeted her there – bright smile, sleeves rolled high, and fingers stained purple from berries.

“You’re the guilder?”

“Yes.”

“Good. You get the left cot. I’ll take care of food, but you wash your own dishes.”

“Deal.”

They shook hands, and Elynara dropped her pack just inside the room she was given. It really was small – just two beds, a stool, and a table with one corner permanently tilted.

But the window faced the fields, and there was a shelf just wide enough for her notebook.

When she returned to the village square an hour later, dusk had thickened the air and drawn most of the villagers toward the center fire pit.

The scent of stewed grains and roasted root vegetables pulled at her stomach in earnest.

No one stopped her as she joined the edge of the group. A bowl was handed over without fuss. Another woman pressed a slice of bread into her hand without a word.

She ate quietly, watching the flames and listening to the hum of shared stories. It was a different rhythm than Oltenar, slower, more self-contained. But the warmth was there, in motion and routine.

Afterward, she took a quiet walk near the fence line, not far from the grain racks she had been warned about. One of them had a small wooden post near it, barely marked, but the shape of the wood and the faint mana clinging to it made her stop.

“Shrine?” she muttered.

The hum of presence there was weak. But as a little spirit puff peeked out at her approach, she knew she had the right place. She knelt beside it, and fed it as much mana as it could eat.

Starpuff got jealous and had to be given its own share too.

Still, she smiled. It was another dot on her map with a living spirit to call it home.

Elynara rose slowly, brushing dirt from her knees, and made her way back toward the red-roofed house.

There would be more work tomorrow.

But tonight, she had a roof, a fire-warmed belly, and her first village on this leg of the journey already half-understood.

Not a bad start.

The spare room smelled faintly of herbs and wool. A clay lantern flickered on the corner shelf, casting soft light across the low ceiling beams. The walls were bare, the blanket thin, but the quiet made up for it.

Elynara sat cross-legged on the straw-stuffed bedding, staff balanced across her lap, the lantern already dimmed to its lowest wick.

The house had gone still some time ago. Her only company the wind breathing through the shutters, and beyond that, the heavy hush of a village without lights.

Out here, the dark felt deeper. Oltenar always had a bit of glow – lanterns hung at corners, warm firelight leaking through windows, the quiet buzz of activity, even at night.

But here, once the cooking fires died and the shutters were drawn, the village gave itself over to the dark entirely.

No moons tonight, either. It was just too cloudy. Or she just had bad luck.

Elynara hadn’t thought to ask anyone about them before she left either. Too caught up in errands, in Alicia’s grin and the promise of ruins.

Another mystery she likely would not be able to do anything about for a long time still.

But curiosity didn’t care for that!

But there was nothing tonight. Only ink-black sky and the soft breath of wind across her face.

So she sighed, shut it again, and turned back to what she could control.

Training.

Her breathing evened out as she gathered mana slowly, pulling threads inward, holding them still. She let the flow settle first, then began to push just until she felt resistance.

This method was still the best. Tedious, yes, and dull when repeated so often, but effective. Elynara focused on the steady loop of draw and shape, watching the feel of it slide beneath her skin like warm mist. Her muscles stayed still. Her heart kept time.

Ten minutes passed. Then fifteen. She increased the pull.

The next marker came slower again. Her reserves weren’t at their fullest from the travel, and today had drained more than she had thought. The Bonefangs, the training, the map-scribbling, even the magic play with Starpuff – it added up. Even if she recovered most of it by the time she got to the village, it still meant she had to fill up the rest before starting.

It didn’t take too long for the routine to assert itself.

And by the end of it, her capacity has increased by 5.

Doubtlessly her mana breathing making it possible, despite harsher cycling requirements every time.

She let out a long exhale and tipped her head forward. She gave her stiff legs a shake and leaned back on her palms.

Progress.

Starpuff hadn’t been a presence during the training, which struck her only now. She glanced around, wondering if it had tucked itself under the bed again or drifted off to snoop around somewhere.

Before she could investigate, it floated down from the ceiling beam, upside-down and glowing faintly. It booped her on the forehead, then spun once and curled into her lap like it had been there all along.

“You’re a terrible training partner,” she whispered.

It vibrated slightly in reply.

Smugness incarnate in shape and glow.

Elynara nudged it aside and laid down slowly, pulling the blanket up to her chest. The room felt cooler now, the night deeper. But the quiet held steady, and her thoughts no longer scrambled in too many directions. Tomorrow there would be shrines to chart, villagers to ask,  and work to do.

Tonight, there was rest. And warmth. And one more thread of strength added to her growing repertoire.

When sleep came, it came fast and without dreams.

The sun rose clear and steady, brushing warmth over the rooftops as Elynara stepped out with her satchel slung firm across her back. The village was still rubbing the sleep from its eyes, and early clatter echoed from the small square where baskets and ropes had begun to pile.

She started with the shrines.

There weren’t many. Barely more than a handful. But each had been marked on the elder’s rough sketch, so she knew just where to go.

The first she checked the spirits held strong. At another, tucked behind the carpenter’s home, a single small one still held domain.

She offered small bits of mana at each one.

Most of the rest were unfortunately quiet and empty.

When she returned, map in hand, the elder met her with a small nod, eyes already weary with village matters.

“Three still have spirits around,” she reported, tapping the symbols. “The rest no longer has any watching over them.”

He looked down at the marks and sighed.

“Even if some are gone, it’s not a bad thing to know where to leave offerings best. The little ones will appreciate it.”

Elynara also shared all the tips she gathered so far on caring for the spirits.

His lips tugged into something like a smile, and he bowed slightly in thanks.

From there, the talk shifted.

Forest work. The village relied on timber, not crops – though they had just enough for themselves – and their felling grounds were separated by deliberate distance from the deeper woods nearer the mountains.

Their own forest was almost self-contained, cut away from the rest and spreading away from and along the river

But despite that there were always more pests than hands to deal with.

And she was here to help with that.

Yet before she so much as moved to go, a man stepped forward at once. Stout, middle-aged, eyes lined from squinting against wind and sun. He held a woodcutter’s axe over one shoulder and a length of rope looped at his hip.

“Such a pretty lassie shouldn’t be wandering the forest all alone,” he said, voice straightforward.

Elynara blinked.

Then she snorted, brushing a hand across her face.

“Is that how we’re introducing ourselves now?”

His grin deepened.

“Figured it’d get your attention.”

“It did.”

“Good. Name’s Barden. Shall we get going?”

“Elynara. And sure.”

And that was that.

They walked without ceremony, past the cleared paths and down toward the southern edge where old stumps and regrowth gave way to underbrush.

Barden didn’t fill the air with talk. He pointed occasionally, explaining the felling business.

Marked trees that had been left for regrowth. Noted scuffs on the trail from boars.

But even his remarks had grown fewer the deeper they went in.

When the first growl came – sharp and low from the bushes – he stepped aside without hesitation, readying his axe just in case.

Elynara raised her staff.

The beast that lunged was thick through the neck, fur patchy, its legs twitching with unnatural angles. It resembled a Bonefang, if one were to be unfaithful with a prickly bush.

The firebolt burst through its chest clean, and it collapsed before Barden could even lift his axe.

He gave a low whistle.

“Fast hands.”

“I’ve had practice.”

“Not arguing that!”

Another weird one came out not long after, after Elynara made some noise with her favorite method.

This one had a stony crest over its skull, a trait she didn’t recognize. She waited until it charged, then drove an explosion of flame straight beneath it. The burst cracked through its underbelly.

It didn’t get back up.

Barden pulled a knife and stepped in to check the body.

“Haven’t seen one quite like that before.”

“Any clue what it could have been?”

“Nope. Probably wandered in from deeper woods.”

He didn’t sound surprised in the slightest.

They kept going. Two more small nests. A single burrow with signs of gnawed roots. Elynara marked each down, her notes crammed into the edge of her notebook with practiced shorthand.

Barden took the pelts and whatever trinkets he felt were usable, stashing them in the sack tied to his belt.

When they finally circled back toward the village, the sun had climbed higher, and the air carried that late-morning stillness that came after early labor.

Barden stretched his shoulders.

“Not bad work.”

Elynara flicked a smear of ash from her sleeve.

“Could have gone worse.”

He gave her a sharp grin.

“You’re alright, lassie. Now, excuse me, gotta get the boys to bring back all that meat we left in there. Not leaving free meals for the wild ones!”

She rolled her eyes, but it tugged a smile from her anyway. And against her better judgement, offered to help with that too.

Was she a glutton for punishment? Or did she just like helping people so much?

It didn't matter in the end.

She would just do what she felt like doing, and that’s it!

Starpuff, who had followed from above in lazy spirals, swooped down just in time to slap her shoulder with a tiny celebratory headbutt.

By the time they returned, the sun had dipped low and the weight of the hunt pulled steady at Elynara’s shoulders.

Eleven carcasses. Eleven.

The rope tied to her belt cut faintly against her hip, dragging the last two behind her. Barden and two others hauled the rest in a makeshift sled rigged from a battered fence board and some twine. Dust trailed behind them, thick with the sharp scent of sweat, blood, and dirt.

They reached the village gate, and the call went out before either of them could speak.

“Back! They’re back!”

Voices rose, boots thudded on packed earth, and soon they were surrounded. Wide eyes stared. Then a few boys let out cheers, running to help pull the sled forward. Laughter cracked across the square, a woman clapping her hands over her mouth in delight.

The elder stepped out last, eyes wide, then slow to smile.

“Well,” he said, rubbing his jaw. “That’s… more than expected.”

Elynara grinned through her exhaustion.

“They didn’t want to run, so I convinced them.”

The snort Barden gave was somewhere between amusement and pride.

“Convinced with fire.”

“That counts.”

The next hour blurred into work. Carcasses laid out. Butchers fetched. Salt brought in by the bucket. Knives flashed. Buckets filled.

Elynara helped with the sorting until her arms began to wobble. Someone shoved a cup into her hand – watered fruit wine, sweet and faintly tart – and patted her back so hard she nearly toppled forward.

“Careful,” she muttered, catching herself on her staff.

But even then, she was laughing.

They turned it into a feast.

Not immediately. There was meat to preserve.

But offcuts and fresh strips were tossed straight into pans or onto skewers. Fires lit fast in three corners of the square. Smoke climbed into the twilight air as spices were hurriedly fetched from homes and root cellars.

The elder gave her a seat on a flat log. Barden disappeared, then returned with a plate balanced in one hand and a half-charred sausage in the other.

“Cooked it meself.”

She gave him a deeply suspicious look.

“It’s fine, really,” he insisted.

The bite she took proved him right, to her dismay. The next vanished even faster.

Starpuff darted overhead, weaving through firelight and laughter.

The scent of food stirred it into a frenzy of loops and flips, and at one point it dove low, nibbling on a tiny morsel from an unattended plate, and vanished behind a pile of baskets.

Elynara pretended not to see it. The trickster earned its scraps.

She leaned back, letting her muscles uncoil. The air held a dry chill now, brushing sharp across her cheeks. But the fire warmed her front, and the food sat heavy and good in her stomach.

The villagers moved lazily now, full and smiling, voices a gentle hum around her.

Someone started a half-hummed tune and others picked it up without fanfare. It wound through the night soft as thread, anchoring everyone with a shared rhythm.

Elynara didn’t know the words, but she mouthed along anyway.

At one point, a girl no older than Mina, and probably a couple years younger, climbed into her lap without warning and offered her a half-eaten bit of flatbread with fruit jam.

Elynara blinked, took it solemnly, and gave her a nod of thanks. The girl stayed curled there for five minutes, humming happily, then wandered off again, no explanation offered or needed.

It was that kind of night.

Later, Barden clinked his cup against hers.

“You do good work,” he said.

“You too,” she replied, then added, “except your cooking posture. That needs help.”

He laughed, deep and rumbling.

The fire cracked louder as more wood was thrown on. Somewhere in the dark, crickets began to sing, their slow chorus layering over the last of the humming voices.

She closed her eyes for a breath. Then two.

And simply let herself feel the warmth.

Only greedy hunger answered, and a circle of people who were, for tonight, safe.

Starpuff nudged against her cheek and settled on her shoulder with a low prrt. She reached up to scritch it without opening her eyes.

She could leave tomorrow. She would. But not tonight.

Tonight, the stars were hidden behind the smoke and the firelight curled golden across the dirt, though she could just barely make out the shapes of the moons flitting in-between the clouds.
