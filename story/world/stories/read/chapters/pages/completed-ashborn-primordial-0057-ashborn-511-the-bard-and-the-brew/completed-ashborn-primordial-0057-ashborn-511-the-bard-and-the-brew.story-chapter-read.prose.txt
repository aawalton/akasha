It was with heavy steps and mixed feelings that Vir returned to Alt Ashani’s streets.

On the one hand, he’d located the Vimana—a fully powered Vimana, no less—and he doubted very much Amara could stop him from going through if he so chose. On the other, if her warning that doing so would doom both their universes wasn’t simply a bluff, going through that Gate would be the worst thing Vir could possibly do.

Even if it was half a bluff—if only her universe was doomed—Vir wouldn’t do it. Even still, he couldn’t waste half a year in this place. Something had to be done, but what?

He initially considered simply heading home but changed course halfway, heading for the Bard and the Brew—the venue where he’d agreed to meet Maiya after their respective exams. While he would have normally taken her along, ferrying people with Dance of the Shadow Demon only increased his chances of detection. Besides, if it had all been for nothing, he would have forced Maiya to fail her exam by default, which seemed unwise if they wished to remain within the Academy.

And so, despite wanting to internalize all that he had learned, he found himself walking through Alt Ashani’s higher-end residential streets, past gorgeous Imperium architecture that once stood proud and gleaming, now covered in soot like everything else.

It was an odd sensation, walking through the city like this. It reminded him greatly of Mahādi, only on a smaller scale. Alt Ashani bore the same tall spires and wide streets, as well as all the other thaumaturgical marvels—the inverted waterfalls and doors that materialized out of walls when one approached, only to disappear after use.

Alt Ashani, however, was different in one crucial way. Where Mahādi was dead, this city had yet to perish. It bustled with life, with the grim determination that shone in the eyes of those who resolved to do whatever it took to weather the incoming threat, as well as hopelessness in the expressions of the ones who had already given up.

That anyone at all held hope that they could turn back the forces that besieged them surprised Vir. It spoke volumes about the faith they placed in Saint Amara.

Vir thought back to the mysterious, fox-masked woman and wondered if they might be correct. Between her knowledge of alternate universes, of those who wove Fate, and her ability to revive the Vimana, maybe her plan did have a chance of working.

Which made it all the more galling that she utterly refused to allow him to help.

Lost in his thoughts, Vir soon arrived at a three-story building that housed the pub. While he had never set foot in here, he knew it was a popular establishment among the student base, and he could see why when the wall disappeared in front of him, admitting him through.

The soundproof barrier had hidden a din of raucous activity, with shouts and yells coming from every table and students clapping each other’s backs in merriment or drowning their sorrows away in drink. A slam from a nearby table had that whole section of the pub hooting after a round of cards. Bartenders flew every which way, and the whole place was a hive of activity.

Vir already found himself perking up as he threaded through the warm, crowded space in search of Maiya, taking in the faint scent of cinnamon and alcohol—no doubt a seasonal special cocktail.

“Darsh! Over here!” he heard a familiar voice call.

It took him a moment to realize the pretty, tan, black-haired girl was Maiya, what with her completely altered complexion.

She was sitting at a square table by a window, and to his surprise, she wasn’t alone. Across from her was a silver-haired, emaciated-looking, mousy girl that Vir almost failed to recognize.

Ira?

The princess was nothing like he remembered her. Frail, but more importantly, hunched over, eyes darting every which way, and entirely lacking the confidence he had known she possessed even before she’d fixed her physical ailments in his universe.

Vir simply couldn’t reconcile that the two were one and the same.

“Darsh! Welcome! I was just chatting with Prin—”

“Ira,” the princess said, cutting Maiya off.

“Right,” Maiya said awkwardly. “With Ira. Why don’t you come and join us? Ira, this is the friend I mentioned.”

“A pleasure to make your acquaintance,” Ira said, bowing her head far too deeply for a princess, even if the nation she belonged to no longer existed.

“The pleasure is mine, Princess Ira,” Vir said with a smile.

To his amusement, her eyes bulged and she looked around, almost as if scared to hear the word.

“Please! I’d rather not advertise that bit of information.”

“And why is that?” Vir asked, taking a seat.

“Well, I’ve no talent for magic, for one, and the moment people know of my pedigree, they seem to think I’m some sort of Kin’jal warrior like my father. The first thing they do is challenge me to a duel. Which, needless to say, never ends well.”

Vir laughed. “Your father—I take it you mean Imperator Andros?”

“Late Imperator,” Ira corrected, and if Vir wasn’t mistaken, he could swear a spark of something flashed through her eyes. Was it anger? Or revenge?

It seemed that even in this universe, Ira detested her father. It didn’t take much to guess why. Andros had always valued strength, and his sickly, frail daughter with no magical talent to speak of would have been an eyesore for him. It seemed she’d received an even shorter stick here.

Vir suspected she wasn’t even allowed to develop her mind. He supposed that in a universe that was crumbling as quickly as this one was, every nation’s top priority would have been defense, to say nothing of the Kin’jal. Anyone not suited to being a warrior would have been cast by the wayside, royalty included.

It spoke of the chaos that had plagued this realm. This was not a slow crumble like his own universe, not a process that took thousands upon thousands of years. No, it seemed that the ash’s encroachment had accelerated once the Fate Weavers had fully asserted their control over this universe.

“’Tis something of a sore point, so I would very much prefer it if you kept it to yourselves,” Ira pleaded, hanging her head.

“Of course,” Vir said, fighting to keep the pity out of his voice and expression. Fighting and failing, apparently, by Ira’s sad smile.

“It’s okay. I’ve gotten used to it. To be honest, being in a place like this is more than I could have ever dreamed of.”

“Oho? And why might that be?” a rough-sounding woman asked.

All three turned to find a certain freckled girl with messy, golden hair down to her shoulders, clad in battle armor. Without asking for permission, she took the last open seat at their table, flipping it around and sitting on it backward, resting her arms upon the backrest.

Well, this keeps getting more and more interesting, doesn’t it? Vir thought.

“Princess Tiyana Matali,” the newcomer said, staring each of them in the eye as if challenging them to disagree. “You might have heard of me.”

Vir blinked in surprise at the brash introduction, though in hindsight, he shouldn’t have.

He’d now seen three different iterations of Talia, after all. Each wildly different from one another. The Tia he knew had been a tormented girl trying to find her calling in life, harboring a hatred of demons that might have been at least somewhat justified based on her past. In the other universe, she’d been a freedom fighter with no such past, working hand in hand with demons in the demon realm to better the world, hopeless as her cause might have been.

And then there was this.

“Princess Ira, right?” Tiyana said.

“Y-yes?” Ira squeaked. “But please, just call me Ira.”

Tiyana scoffed. “I see that you are as sickly and as frail as they said. And as weak, too.”

“Well, I can’t deny that,” Ira replied, looking away.

But Tiyana shook her head. “Not just in body—in spirit. No princess should ever deign to hide her heritage. To do so is an affront to your kingdom and everyone who has ever fought and died for it. You spit on their sacrifice.”

Ira bit her lip. “Perhaps you are correct. Just sometimes, maybe it’s not such a good thing for a country to live. Not when it is run by—”

“See?” Tiyana interrupted, throwing Ira a smirk. “You disagree with the way Kin’jal did things.”

“Yes, I—” Ira began, but Tiyana cut her off again.

“Truthfully, it’s a pity your country folded. The Altani seem so content to sit on their haunches here, twiddling their thumbs waiting for the end of the world. Your father at least sought to do something about it.”

“Which killed him and empire both,” Ira muttered, almost too softly to hear.

Vir raised an eyebrow at that. “And what of the Matali? How do they fare in these dark times?”

Tiyana’s grin widened. “We don’t sit idle, if that’s what you’re asking. Our land might be gone, but our fight continues. Every day, we send raiding parties to the Boundary, supplementing the Altani defenders to strike at the beasts before they can organize.”

“A losing battle,” Vir observed.

“Perhaps,” Tiyana admitted, taking a long drink from a mug of mead she’d ordered. “But I’d rather die on my feet than live on my knees. Wouldn’t you agree, Darsh?”

The way she said his name made it obvious she’d meant the words as a challenge. For a princess to even talk to a commoner as he claimed to be was already unusual—almost like she could sense there was more to him than met the eye.

“I would,” Vir said. “Though I prefer to find a third option. One where I neither die nor kneel.”

Tiyana laughed hard at that. “A diplomat’s answer! You know, you don’t strike me as an ordinary commoner, Darsh. There’s something about you…”

Vir felt Maiya tense beside him, but he kept his expression neutral. “Is that so?”

“Indeed. It’s the way you carry yourself. The way you speak, too.” Tiyana leaned forward, studying him intently. “Do you know how many commoners soil their pants when they learn who I am? You remind me of someone I knew once. A warrior from before the collapse. He had that same look in his eyes. Like he’d seen it all, lived through horrors most couldn’t fathom.”

Perceptive, Vir thought. Far more than the Tia he knew.

“We’ve all seen horrors,” Vir said. “That’s the world we live in now.”

“True enough,” Tiyana conceded with a nod. “But some of us bear them better than others. Take poor Ira here. She’s been crushed by hers. While you…” She gestured at him with her mug. “You’ve been forged by them, no?”

The conversation continued with Tiyana extolling the Kin’jal ways, making Ira increasingly uncomfortable, which Vir assumed was her aim. Vir soon found himself studying not her words but her expressions, the pure confidence she emanated.

It was fascinating, really. In his universe, Talia had been broken by her circumstances. In the demon realm, she’d become an idealistic freedom fighter, working side by side with demons she’d sworn to kill in his universe. Here, she’d become a proud and haughty warrior princess.

What makes a person who they are? Vir wondered. Is it their choices? Their circumstances? Or something deeper, some fundamental core that remains constant across realities?

He looked at Ira, cowering under Tiyana’s verbal assault, and despite that, saw echoes of the confident princess he’d known.

“You’ve gone quiet,” Tiyana observed, pulling Vir from his reverie. “Am I boring you with tales of Kin’jal glory?”

“Not at all,” Vir said. “I was simply thinking about how differently people can turn out based on their circumstances.”

“Philosophy?” Tiyana grinned. “Now I’m certain you’re no commoner. Come, Darsh—the night is young, and I grow tired of sitting. Dance with me.”

It wasn’t a request.

Vir glanced at Maiya, who gave him a subtle nod, her eyes full of amusement.

“I’m afraid I’m not much of a dancer,” Vir said sheepishly.

“Neither am I,” Tiyana admitted, already standing up. “But I find that half the fun is making a fool of oneself. Besides, you intrigue me, and I wish to know more about the mysterious commoner who thinks like a philosopher and carries himself like a king.”

Before Vir could protest further, she grabbed his hand and hauled him toward the dance floor, where several other students had already begun dancing to some upbeat melody being played by a small band in the corner.

“You’re quite, er, forward,” Vir observed as they took their positions.

“Life’s too short to be otherwise,” Tiyana replied. “Especially in times like these. Tell me, Darsh, what do you really think of our situation? Of Altani’s chances… of Saint Amara’s grand plan?”

Vir chose his words carefully as they began to dance. “I think that hope is a precious thing, and I think that Saint Amara wouldn’t claim to have a plan if she didn’t think it would work.”

“A political answer,” Tiyana said, clearly disappointed. “Come now, I saw the way you looked when I mentioned the Altani sitting idle. You don’t approve of their methods.”

“I think,” Vir said slowly, “that there are often forces at work we don’t fully understand. Sometimes what appears to be cowardice is actually prudence in disguise.”

“And sometimes,” Tiyana countered, “prudence is just another word for fear.”

They danced in silence for a moment, and Vir found himself appreciating this Tiyana’s directness. At least she was honest about her beliefs, even if they were misguided. That was a far cry from the one he’d known.

“You know what I think?” Tiyana said suddenly. “I think you’re hiding something. Something very important.”

Vir’s heart skipped a beat, but he kept his expression neutral. “Aren’t we all?”

“Perhaps,” Tiyana agreed. “But yours feels… bigger somehow. Like you’re carrying the weight of more than just your survival on your shoulders.”

Oh, if only you knew, Vir thought ironically.

The song ended, and Tiyana stepped back, studying him one last time. “You’re an interesting fellow, Darsh. I hope we have the chance to speak again before the end comes for us all.”

With that, she strode back to their table, leaving Vir standing on the dance floor.

When he returned, he found Maiya grinning at him. “Making new friends?” she teased.

“Something like that,” Vir muttered, ruffling his hair.

The rest of the evening passed in a blur of drinks and laughter, with Maiya dragging him back onto the dance floor at some point. Even Ira was coaxed into joining, though she looked terrified the entire time.

By the time night rolled around and Vir and Maiya stumbled back to their room hand in hand, Vir was both exhausted and strangely satisfied.

“That was fun,” Maiya said, leaning against him. “Really fun.”

“It was,” Vir agreed. “Though I think Tiyana suspects something.”

“Of course she does,” Maiya replied. “That you’re hiding some secret Sawai title or some powerful connection. Besides, what’s she going to do about it?”

Vir thought about that. In truth, probably nothing. They’d be gone from this universe soon enough, one way or another.

But the encounter had reinforced something important.

These weren’t just shadows or echoes of people he knew. They were real, living beings with their own hopes and dreams and fears.

And Vir couldn’t stand idly by while their world burned.

It was time to fill Maiya in on Saint Amara. It was time to take action.
