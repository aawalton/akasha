Chapter 19

If the candidates fail, there will be no second attempt. The Grid runs once. The Fence drops once. The Silent come once. There is no revision. There is no second draft. This is not a rehearsal. - Codex of the Long Watch, Watcher's Commentary

They stopped at the loading bay.

"Points first, we're not walking into that with unspent allocation.” She motioned at a squat shed next to the printworks. “In there.”

They moved cautiously towards the shed and tried the door and found it locked from the inside. Arthur gave a gentle push, the inner latch rattled against the frame.

“Anybody in there?”

There was no reply, though they could hear a faint shuffling noise and Donna nodded to Arthur.

“Come on, mate, we can hear you. You all right in there?”

“Go away,” came a thin voice from inside.

“We only need the shed for a minute. Then we’re off.”

“They got Jimmy and Troy. Nobody is coming in. Now piss off.”

“Mate, you either undo that latch now, or I'm taking it off the door. I swear you've got nothing to fear from us.”

There was a long silence followed by shuffling. The latch was thrown back by a heavy middle-aged man wearing overalls. There were two others behind him.

“What the fuck do you want?”

“As I said, just to use the shed for a minute.”

“Find your own fucking shed.”

“Mate, we are going in there.” Donna motioned at the loading bay. “We need to get ourselves sorted first. I would appreciate you not being a twat about it.”

“That’s where they got Jimmy and Troy,” a thinner man said from inside. “The belt got 'em. I swear it did. You'd be mad going in there. The whole world's gone fucking mad.”

“All right, listen. You’ve got a shed...” Arthur craned his neck. “Two bits of pipe and a few tins of beans. How long exactly do you reckon you can hold out?”

“Till the coppers come.”

“The coppers aren’t coming. Not for three blokes hiding in a random shed.” Arthur shook his head. “You need proper shelter and more people. Down the road, they’re already barricading themselves in.”

“Jared, I said we should go find others. I bloody said it, didn’t I?” the third man called from behind him.

“Shut the fuck up. They want us outside so they can nick our stuff.”

“Your stuff?” Steve looked around the shed. “What, precisely? The beans or the pipe? I don’t mean to be an apocalypse elitist, but have a look at us, then have a look at yourselves. What on earth would we rob you for?”

“Jared, mate, we’re not here to nick your stuff. But I’m serious about the shelter. This place is useless. The road back’s mostly clear, so go find somewhere you can actually defend. Now step outside for a minute.”

“See? I fucking knew it. You want us out.”

“Oh, for fuck’s sake,” Donna said, loosening the axe. “If you want to spend the rest of your life sitting in this shithole cuddling tins of beans, that’s your business. We need the shed for one minute. If you’re still in it five seconds from now, I’m going to beat you senseless.”

The axe made further discussion unnecessary. The three workers shuffled out, still clutching their tins.

“Wait there. We’ll be out in a tick,” Arthur said, and the party ducked inside.

Donna bolted the door and set her pack down.

Arthur allocated fast, putting two in Strength and one in Constitution. The restructuring hit hard but was a tad shorter this time, the body learning the process. About half a minute of grinding pressure for each allocated point and then release.

Donna did hers standing and clutching her fists. All three went into Dexterity. When it was done, she bounced on the balls of her feet to test the change. The movement was fractionally faster. Real, if not dramatic.

Steve sat with his eyes closed and his teeth clenched. He followed the same pattern as before, putting two in Acuity, one in Resonance. When he opened his eyes, he blinked several times. "Everything's sharper," he said, getting up.

"Good. Use it to spot things that want to kill us."

It took them about five minutes to finish the allocation and come outside.

“It was more than one fucking minute,” Jared grumbled.

“You know it full well, a minute is an expression,” Arthur said, clapping his shoulder. “We’ll be off now. If I were you, I would head for where people are.”

The loading bay opened into a corridor that shouldn't have existed. The building's exterior was single storey, about six metres from floor to ceiling. The corridor descended at about a twelve-degree gradient, and after thirty metres they were below the foundation level. The walls were industrial brick, but the mortar had darkened to blue-black, and faint lines of Ether moved through them in patterns that looked almost organic. It seemed like the building had developed veins.

The air was dense and each breath tasted metallic, the Ether concentration was so high that Arthur could feel it pressing against his skin. This energy was raw, unlike the clean signal of the Grid. It felt unrefined, heavy.

Donna cracked a chem-light and tossed it ahead. The green glow revealed industrial shelving holding yellowed paper reams, a fire extinguisher at regulation height with red paint flaking, and a notice board.

REPORT ALL ACCIDENTS, WASH YOUR HANDS

The mundane infrastructure of a workplace that had been absorbed into something alien. And right below the signage were two mutilated bodies. Probably Jimmy and Troy.

"The floor plan should end here," Arthur said, pointing where the building's footprint terminated outside. The corridor continued twenty metres beyond it and bent left into darkness. "We're well past the building's edge."

“How deep does it go?” Steve asked.

“Unknown,” Archie stated. “Anomaly interiors are not pre-mapped. Local Ether saturation has produced a spatial extension.”

Steve stopped walking. “A spatial extension.”

“The interior occupies more volume than its external boundaries would ordinarily permit.”

“I know what the words mean.” Steve stared down the corridor. “I’m having some difficulty with what they do to several thousand years of mythology.”

Donna looked back at him. “How have you made this about mythology already?”

“The oracle of Trophonius. It a—well it's a hole barely wide enough for a man to crawl through, but people returned having seen things that could not have been inside the chamber. Or the Oweynagat at Cruachan in Ireland, the stories say it was a cave beneath a hill that opened into an inhabited kingdom, they called it the gateway to Otherworld.”

Steve did a full three sixty looking at the space. “Then you have the Daoist grotto-heavens. Entire landscapes inside mountains, with their own sun and moon and passages connecting distant peaks.”

“Stories,” Donna said.

“Yes, but essentially the same story. A small entrance with something impossibly large behind it.”

“People find caves frightening. It isn’t a remarkable coincidence.”

“Quite, and a frightening cave should become tighter and darker. But these stories do the opposite. The world beyond it expands.”

Arthur looked back towards the loading bay. It was still visible behind them, framed by an ordinary industrial doorway. Ahead, the corridor continued past the point where the building had ended.

“What are you saying?” he asked.

“We thought D&D was the manual,” Steve said, chewing his lip. “What if…mythology was the primer instead?”

“That’s mental, you are saying the Curators planted those stories?” Arthur asked.

“Maybe. Or people encountered anomalies before and the reports survived after the evidence disappeared which then turned into myths. I don’t know.” Steve shrugged and gestured at the impossible corridor. “But we have just walked thirty metres beyond the outside wall of a building.”

“Because it is a dungeon,” Donna said.

“Yes, and maybe the stories reached us before the real thing did.”

“That’s a bit of a stretch, innit?” Arthur said.

“We are quite literally standing in a room that would send Newton into catatonia.”

“Cultural narratives can reduce cognitive resistance when an individual encounters a phenomenon that contradicts established physical expectations,” Archie added.

Donna drew her axe. “You can rewrite the history of mythology after we get out of the monster building.”

“Assuming outside is still where we left it.”

She frowned at him.

“Too soon?”

“Considerably.”

***

Arthur read the space like he'd read an encounter map spread across a pub table on Tuesday nights. The corridor was narrow and about two metres wide. That was a chokepoint. Anything coming from deeper in the building had to funnel through it. If they held formation here, numbers didn't matter. One at the front with a shield, one flanking behind, one at range.

"Formation," he said. "Same as the street but tighter. I hold the corridor width. Donna, you're behind my shield-side. When I block, you hit whatever I'm blocking. Steve, stay two metres back. Watch the rear. If anything comes from behind us, you call it."

"Classic dungeon formation," Steve said grinning.

Donna looked at him. "It's a corridor, Steve. Not a campaign encounter."

“It's a corridor in a dungeon. That rather makes it a campaign encounter.”

They moved deeper. The corridor levelled out into a wide press room. But the presses had changed. Massive rollers and conveyor belts were still recognisable as industrial equipment, but the metal had darkened and thickened. Organic growths had erupted along the frames, chitinous ridges and grey tissue that pulsed with blue light. The machines looked half-alive.

Archie's geometry brightened fractionally. "Organic-inorganic symbiosis under high Ether saturation. The machinery is developing circulatory function. Your species' industrial infrastructure makes a remarkably efficient Ether conduit.”

“I suspect the original engineers would find this gratifying and then very quickly horrifying," Steve said, scribbling in the guidebook margin. Arthur caught a glimpse: Ether + dead tech = inorganic mutation. Machine biology. Archie confirms: circulatory function.

Steve had just put his book away when they heard sounds from the far end of the press room. It sounded like multiple sets of claws on concrete.

"Contact," Donna breathed. "Multiple. Small and fast."

They came from behind a press in a coordinated rush. Five rats, each the size of a border terrier, their fur replaced by grey chitin plating and their teeth elongated into curved puncturing instruments. They moved as a pack rather than a mob, flanking wide, driving towards the party's edges like a wolfpack splitting a herd.

"Pack coordination exceeds the single-organism baseline by a factor of three. The Ether density is producing collective tactical behaviour," Archie said.

Arthur read the encounter the way he'd designed encounters for Tuesday-night campaigns. Five hostiles with pack tactics. They'd try to surround, get behind the shield, and overwhelm through numbers. The counter was the same one he'd used to save a party of Level 2 adventurers in the Caves of Chaos: don't let them surround you.

"Back to the corridor!" Arthur barked. "Chokepoint. Now."

They repositioned in a hurry. Donna understood instantly; she'd built enough rogues to know that open ground against pack enemies was death. Steve backpedalled, the billhook sweeping in wide arcs to discourage anything from getting behind them.

Arthur planted himself at the corridor mouth, shield filling the width. The first rat hit the Bullguard and bounced, the shield's enhanced density doing what it was designed to do. A second came at his legs beneath the shield rim, and Arthur stamped down, pinning it against the concrete. The crowbar came down a heartbeat later.

Donna worked from his right side, the gap between shield edge and wall just wide enough for her to swing. The rats had to come through Arthur to reach the party, and when they did, Donna was there. Her axe caught the third rat mid-lunge with a lateral strike that used the corridor wall as a backstop. The creature hit brick and didn't get up.

"Two left!" Steve called from behind. His Resonance was earning its points. He could feel the creatures' Ether signatures, two bright sparks of hostile energy, circling. "They're holding back. They know the chokepoint's a kill zone."

"Smart rats," Donna said.

"Sanctum rats. Enhanced instinct."

The two surviving rats tried a different approach. They attacked together, one high and one low, through the same gap Donna was using. Arthur read the timing and angled the shield to block the high one, forcing it into the wall. The low one slipped through; it was fast and vicious and went for Donna’s legs. But her Dexterity had been 14 before the last allocation and was 17 now. The gap between the rat's strike and her dodge had gone from impossible to merely terrifying. She sidestepped the lunge, the axe came down, and it stopped moving.

The last rat scrambled over the bodies of his dead mates. Arthur met it with the flat of the shield and drove it backward. Steve's billhook came in from the right, the curved blade hooking behind the creature's neck before it could reset from the shove. The pull was ugly but effective.

The corridor fell silent. Five dead rats and nobody seriously injured. Arthur had a scratch on his shin where the second rat's claws had found the gap beneath his shield, and Steve's hands were shaking, but the compression bandage on his fox-bite arm was still dry.

"Seventy-two," Steve said. "On the surface, those foxes gave us forty-five. The density multiplier in here is significant."

Archie's geometry rotated once. "Shield angle during the second contact was four degrees steeper than the engagement in Deptford. An improvement. The chokepoint application was efficient."

Arthur blinked. That was about as close to a compliment as Archie was likely to get.

"You used terrain control to negate pack tactics. You ran a dungeon encounter in your head before the first one reached us,” Steve said, looking at the five rat corpses stacked at the corridor mouth. “This…the chokepoint, focus fire, taking them on one at a time."

"You held a corridor the way you hold a session. You DM'd the apocalypse," Steve said with a wide grin.

They moved deeper.

The press room opened into a larger production floor. Two industrial printing presses dominated the room, each the size of a delivery van. The conveyor systems between them had fused into something that Arthur could only describe as an arterial network. Metal tubes were wrapped in organic tissue, pulsing with blue light, carrying Ether through the machinery like blood moving through veins. The air was thick enough to chew.

And then between the presses, something moved.

It was larger than the rats. Considerably larger. And it looked as if it had been built from pieces of the building: a chassis of press components, rollers and gears and steel plate, wrapped in the same chitinous growth that covered the machinery.

But it moved on six insectoid legs, each one a hydraulic arm from the press mechanism that had been co-opted by whatever biological process the Ether had triggered. It was neither a machine nor a creature but something in between that made Arthur's instincts scream at the wrongness of it.

Steve's breath caught. "It's a fused creature. The machine and the growth, they're not separate systems anymore."

"It's a press amalgamation," Archie stated. "Ether-fused industrial entity. Classification: elevated threat."

"So, it might drop a core," Steve said. "Arthur, the legs. Six of them but they're not symmetrical. Most of the weight is on the front two. The rear four are locomotion. If you take out a front leg -"

"The whole structure tips forward," Arthur finished. "Like a building losing a column."

"Conveyor to the left is not fused. Arthur, hold its attention from the front and I’ll circle behind it,” Donna said.

"Flanking. Classic rogue positioning."

"It's not a game mechanic, Steve. It's common sense."

"Both can be true."

Arthur raised the shield. "Donna, go wide. Steve, stay at range and call its movement pattern. I'll hold aggro."

"Hold aggro," Steve repeated, grinning despite the terror. "You actually said hold aggro."

"Move, Steve."

The amalgamation turned towards them. Its optical array was made of three printer scanner heads fused into a cluster of blue-glowing sensors. They locked onto Arthur's shield. When it charged, the sound was of grinding metal and shrieking hydraulics. Arthur braced.

The impact was different from the bull. It was harder in some ways. The creature was metal and chitin, not muscle and chitin. But less overwhelming because Arthur was stronger and better equipped now. Level 3, Strength 14, the Bullguard Shield distributing the force across his entire forearm. He slid back two steps instead of six and his shoulder held.

The amalgamation pressed forward, its front legs driving into the concrete with mechanical force. Arthur held the centre, keeping its attention, keeping its sensor cluster focused on the shield while Donna circled behind the conveyor belt.

"It attacks in a rhythm!" Steve called from the rear. "Two strikes, then it resets the hydraulics. There's a window after the second strike where the front legs lock."

Arthur waited. He caught the first strike on the shield and deflected the second strike sideways, the metal scraping across the Bullguard with a sound that set his teeth on edge. Then there was a pause, just as Steve had mentioned. The hydraulic legs were locked, pistons cycling.

The amalgamation's tail, a fused conveyor belt, whipped and caught Steve across the ribs.

He hadn't seen it coming. He'd been watching the front legs, counting the cycle and calling the pattern. The tail came from the blind side and hit him like a length of steel cable. At Constitution seven, Steve's body had the impact tolerance of a man half Arthur's weight. The blow folded him in half and he hit the concrete floor shoulder-first, the billhook clattered away. He couldn't breathe. His diaphragm had locked and his intercostals had decided they were done.

Arthur saw him go down and stepped sideways to cover Steve's body with the shield while keeping the amalgamation's sensor cluster focused on him. The creature's front legs were still cycling. Three seconds. Maybe two.

Steve was on the ground behind the shield, curled around his ribs, dragging air in ragged gasps. Donna was circling on the wrong side of the conveyor. Fifteen metres away. Too far.

Steve rolled onto his hands and knees. His vision was grey at the edges. The rats and foxes hadn’t prepared him for this. One hit from the tail and he was on the floor for ten seconds. Constitution seven was a number so far. He'd just learned what the number meant.

One second. Arthur braced himself, he would have to take another two hits. Deflecting was out of the question with Steve behind him.

The amalgamation finished its reset cycle and came again; Arthur tucked his shield arm in against his chest and braced his right arm with his left. The front legs hit in quick succession, pounding his shield. Arthur went down to a knee but held.

"Now!" Arthur shouted.

Donna came in from the blind side. Her axe found the joint where the front left leg connected to the chassis, the weak point where biological growth hadn't fully bonded with the machine frame. She struck twice in quick succession and the leg buckled.

The amalgamation pitched forward, its weight distribution catastrophically compromised, and the remaining front leg couldn't take the full load. Arthur jumped up and drove the crowbar into the sensor cluster. It was a hard and precise blow like driving a chisel into a cracked bearing plate. Glass and chitin sparked blue and shattered outward.

The creature collapsed. The hydraulic legs twitched once, twice, and went still.

The flat of the blade developed a faint, angular texture that caught and bounced light. The billhook looked lighter. More precise. Less like a farming tool and more like a surgical instrument mounted on a pole.

Steve tested the weapon and his eyebrows rose. "Sensory feedback," he read. "When I hit something, I can feel its internal structure through the blade. That's... that's sonar. That's echolocation through a weapon."

"Can it kill things better?" Donna asked.

"It can kill things better while telling me exactly where to kill them."

"Then it was worth the core."

Arthur looked deeper into the production floor. Beyond the dead amalgamation, the space opened further. And they could hear a faint grinding noise from further in. A heavy rhythmic note of something massive.

"There's something bigger in there," Donna said.

Arthur nodded. He could feel it too.

"The boss," Steve said. He'd stopped grinning.

Arthur checked his shield and then scanned the party. Donna was bruised but moving freely. Steve was shaking but seemed focused. Archie's geometry was oriented towards the dark, scanning the gloom. He hooked the nearest carcass away from the centre of the corridor, then cleared the others with his boot. If they had to retreat at speed, five bodies across the choke would turn the corridor against them.

"Same rules," Arthur said. "I hold. Donna flanks. Steve reads the pattern." He met their eyes. "And if it's too much, we fall back to the chokepoint corridor and reassess. No heroics."

"No heroics," Donna agreed.

"None whatsoever," Steve said. "I have just been personally introduced to my Constitution score, and I would like to avoid a second—” He pressed his ribs and decided the sentence wasn't worth finishing.

They moved towards the grinding dark.
