For a heartbeat, the Divine Hall held its breath.

On one side of the ruined doors, Zora and his small storm of men; on the other, Decima standing alone at the base of the throne, wand in hand, five eyes fixed on him. Between them lay a long carpet and a longer history.

Zora was sure the carpet was red.

Enki lifted his rifle first, and the diamond barrel scraped softly against the air as he did, but Zora held out a hand and shook his head once.

“Leave her to me.”

Hesitation. Silence. Then Enki’s rifle dipped by a fraction, and Zora supposed that was as much trust as the boy ever showed.

With that, Zora stepped forward, tapping his staff once.

“Strike the witch.”

The word snapped out like flint on steel. Wind surged from his lungs, zipped to the tip of his staff, and then swirled into a rippling lance of wind that hurled itself across the hall.

Decima didn’t move.

“Boring,” she said lazily, flicking her wand. The wind he’d sent at her shattered sideways as an identical lance of wind matched it. Both attacks smashed into a pillar along the right-hand wall instead, exploding masonry in a shower of dust and broken stone, and the impact rang around the hall.

Zora walked forward with measured steps.

“From the old stone plains where giants buried their wars, let the earth remember its teeth.”

The Divine Hall shuddered. The marble flagstones beneath their feet buckled, stone heaving upward in jagged spears that stabbed towards her from below. Her wand traced a tight circle as she sighed once again

“Block.”

An invisible floor interposed itself under her, dulling the spikes’ ascent and breaking their tips like harmless chalk. She didn’t shift from her spot.

Zora flicked his staff again.

“When rebellions rose in the dawn of the Attini Empire, even the banners learned to refuse their poles—so tear yourselves free, and bind the wicked.”

The silk and brocade that hung along the hall’s flanks answered. Dozens of embroidered standards tore themselves free of their poles with a hiss of fabric, then twisted into coils mid-air and shot toward her like strangling serpents.

Decima raised a brow. The banners struck her lingering ‘block’ spell again, fluttered, then slid harmlessly away, collapsing around her feet instead of around her throat. Decima still hadn’t taken a single step back.

Zora’s next breath left his lungs hot.

“The banners burned when the First Empress marched. Let history heat itself again.”

Fire leapt from his words. The discarded cloth at her feet ignited in a ring of sudden flame, tongues of heat licking up her ankles and robes, heat rolling across the floor in a wave that even the men behind Zora could feel on their faces.

“... So poetic,” Decima said lazily, waving her wand and extinguishing the flames with another gust of wind. Not a single fold of her jewelled garments caught fire, and she was still standing exactly where she had begun. “But there is an advantage in poeticism, in that it takes me a while to understand just what, exactly, your spells are trying to do.”

Zora kept coming. With each step and each new ‘fire’ spell, the temperature in the hall climbed. Heat and flames gathered under the ant-head throne. Cracks spidered outward from the trails of stone he’d torn up as giant earth spikes. Even the Spore Knights shifted quietly as the pressure in the air thickened around their armour.

Decima laughed, unbothered by his constant barrage of spells. She made no attempt to attack, and she only held her ground with basic defensive spells.

“You’ve grown far stronger than when you first killed Nona,” she called, voice carrying easily over the crackle of flames. “Just this repertoire of basic spells of yours—earth spikes and wind lances—would demolish any weaker Insect God with sheer magnitude and frequency, not to mention your impossible ‘fire’ spells. They are impossible to contest for most bugs like us. We are weak to heat, after all. No wonder my compatriots call you the Thousand Tongue of the Long March.”

Zora angled his head slightly.

“And you,” he said, “are still talking.”

Her grin widened. “They also call me ‘Reverberator’ Decima,” she said lightly, “and they do not call me that for nothing.”

He was halfway across the hall when she flicked her wand to the side.

“Backstab.”

The air behind Zora warped. A compact spear of wind manifested at his back, honed and silent as it thrust towards his neck, but he heard it twist into being—the tight coil of pressure, the sudden narrowing of sound at his rear—so he swept his staff back.

He batted the attack aside. The gust veered, carving a deep gouge into the stone floor beside him instead.

“Jab,” she spoke again.

A thin whip of pressure stabbed toward the side of his head. He flicked it away with the staff’s tip.

“Lash.”

Three quick slices of wind lashed at his legs in succession. He stepped through them, staff spinning, parrying invisible strikes that others would’ve only felt when they cut.

“Is this all your spellcraft amounts to?” he asked, voice dry. “Petty, boring tricks and nips from the dark? Spells meant to agitate and harry and unsettle, rather than strike true?”

Decima’s laughter rang from all five of her eyes.

“Of course. I am the Witch of Adults, after all.”

Her next word came softer, almost tender.

“Rally to me.”

The Spore Knights lining the hall jolted as if strings had been yanked in their spines. Thirty heavy visors lifted in perfect unison, and thirty pairs of boots stepped forward together, forming a solid ring in front of her dais. Sawtooth greatswords rose, teeth glinting in the light.

Then:

“Rage and agitate, my warriors of the earth.”

The Spore Knights threw their heads back and howled.

It was not a human sound. It tore out of their chests like something dragged from the bottom of a pit, and then they surged as one—a charging wall of armour and blades—the pounding of their boots shuddering through the hall so violently that the burning banners trembled where they lay.

Zora exhaled slowly.

“Earthen wall.”

The floor in front of him convulsed. A massive barrier of stone surged up from the ground, thick and high enough to block the charging phalanx. The hall rumbled as if the temple itself had clenched its jaw.

But the Spore Knights just smashed into it. Their momentum cracked fissures through the wall, and the stone splintered as they howled again, sawtooth greatswords biting into rock and brute force beginning to chew their way through.

Zora thumped his staff a few more times, repeating ‘earthen wall’. Wall after wall rose before them, overlapping slabs of stone that forced the Spore Knights to slow, batter, and push through layer after layer of resistance. Each impact was a thunderclap. Dust rained from the ceiling, bits of carved ant mandible shook loose around the throne, and Zora watched them come with reckless abandon. He could hear their laboured breaths inside their helmets—that muffled anger, that strain on their joints—and it was wholly… ‘unnatural’, if he had to describe it in a word.

“... Tell me something, Decima,” he said. “What do you think an ‘adult’ is?”

The Spore Knights tore through the last of his walls in a shower of rubble. They were one charge away now, thirty bodies of rage about to crash into him at once.

Then space distorted in front of him, and every charging Spore Knight plunged into a giant wormhole. Half a second later, they erupted out of other wormholes across the hall: halfway up marble walls, sideways above pillars, feet tangling in torn banners and broken stone. Armour slammed into ceilings. Sawtooth blades bit into columns, into railings, into one another, as the knights stumbled and collided into a mess of limbs and deranged snarls.

Without a word, Enki lifted both of his arms, and thin cryogenic mist began wafting off his limbs. Winter breathed across the hall. The mist rolled along the ground, curling around the floundering Spore Knights’ knees and then hardening into ice, and when they tried to stand, their boots were frozen in place. Gloves scraped uselessly at frost. They snarled, inhuman and enraged, but they were no longer mobile. They wouldn’t be for a while.

Zora didn’t look back.

“There are liars and cheats among adults, I will admit,” he said, resuming his walk through the frost and flame. “There are those who manipulate, who use cleverness to strip others of choice, and who call it ‘wisdom’ when it is only fear in a fine coat, but much like how Nona believed human children were small and cowardly—that they would always run and tremble and cry—she devoured that lie and made it her creed. That was her ultimate undoing.”

He took another step. The stone under his feet ran hot.

“And you, Witch of Adults—you have devoured the lie that adulthood means deception. That the only way to live as a grown human is to trick and to control,” he continued. “In my long march across the south, I have met lords willing to bleed for their people. I have met soldiers willing to die for a banner that has forgotten their names. I have met princesses who stand in front of tides she cannot possibly stop alone. They are good and honest adults too, wouldn’t you say?”

Decima began to spit more spells, her voice sharp and clipped.

“Strike. Slash. Break. Shatter.”

Wind claws slashed toward him. Blunt blows of compressed air hammered at his sides and chest, all meant to push him back, stall him, and dent his forward momentum.

He lifted his chin.

“Burn it all.”

Flame seared out from his words, a sudden blossoming heat that incinerated every incoming wind attack. Invisible jabs and lashes burned to nothing before they could touch him. Shockwaves hissed and vanished in the wall of heat.

He kept walking.

And by the time he stopped, he stood directly before her.

The space between them was three paces, then two, then one. Her five eyes bore into his empty ones, and he lifted his staff, holding the pointy end right over her chest.

“Tell me, Decima,” he said softly. “In your understanding, what does it truly mean to be an ‘adult’?”

She opened her mouth—

He stabbed.

The staff punched forward, the point driving into her chest with a hard, ugly sound. Chitin cracked. Flesh parted. The blow went in deep—straight into her heart—and Decima jerked.

All five of her eyes flared wide, and then she coughed. A hitch of surprise fluttered in her throat. Her four arms spasmed for half a heartbeat—fingers curling as if she meant to reach for him, then dropped lifelessly to her sides as she began to stagger back.

Across the hall, his team reacted in a single, jolted intake of air. Kita’s armour rasped as her body tensed. Baya’s stance shifted. Even Machi’s breath skipped. Only Enki’s pulse remained steady, but that was more a testament to Enki’s calm than everyone else’s surprise, because even Zora himself flinched.

He hadn’t expected her resistance to crumble under the first solid strike. Not from her. Not from a bug he’d been hunting for two years, and not from a Magicicada Witch whose name carried ten years of fear.

Decima sagged off the end of his staff with a shuddering gasp. Chitin cracked. Fluid dripped. She collapsed to her knees and pressed one trembling hand—only one—against the widening hole in her chest.

Then she fell onto her back and began to crawl.

Slowly, steadily, she dragged herself up the steps toward the Divine Empress’s throne, her limbs trembling in a way that was almost—almost—pitiful. Her jewellery clinked faintly with every movement as she pulled her body upward. She wheezed and choked, her breath rattling like a dying cicada’s hum, and… all Zora could do was watch her climb.

Something was wrong.

Something felt wrong.

He’d been so certain that she had some final trick, some hidden escape route, some layered deceit waiting to spring once he met her face to face. She’d said it herself that in a direct confrontation, he’d win incredibly easily, but he hadn’t expected her to just stand here and take it. If she knew she couldn’t defeat him, then why?

Why hadn’t she moved?

Why die here?

What was so special about this hall?
