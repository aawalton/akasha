After asking a city guard for the location of the private notary office in Ronswick—the one owned by Tarenick Pines—Veronica made her way toward the northwestern quarter of the city.

It was afternoon now; the city was lively with people going about their day. Schools also let out around this time, and she saw many children walking home with their parents.

Some of the older students wore uniforms. A few were dressed in fitted white attire trimmed with blue, while others wore darker blue cloaks that resembled robes.

According to the city map she carried, Ronswick maintained several institutions dedicated to general education. However, there were also two specialized academies: one devoted to swordsmanship, the other to the study of magic.

She had little sense of how rigorous the magical institutions were in Ronswick, but according to Viscount Leopold, the strongest mages in the city rarely exceeded the fourth tier. Respectable for a settlement this far along the kingdom’s border, though hardly exceptional.

At least, not to someone who had once reached the tenth tier.

Along the way, Veronica made a brief detour to a street vendor and purchased a small hand-held basket of sugar-coated raffies: tiny, chewy red fruits dusted in crystallized sugar. She popped one into her mouth as she continued down the road, enjoying the sharp sweetness.

Eventually, the notary office came into view.

The structure was larger than she had expected—substantially bigger than what the word office typically implied. At the front stood a sturdy wooden door bearing a simple crest: a sheet of parchment with a pen resting across it vertically.

Guess this is the place.

Veronica tossed another raffie into her mouth before pushing the door open and stepping inside.

The interior had a rustic kind of smell, much more than that of ink or parchment.

It was noticeably cooler indoors. Sunlight streamed through tall windows along one wall, casting soft light over several neatly arranged tables where stacks of documents rested in precise, orderly piles. Shelves lined the far walls, each filled with bound ledgers or wooden document cases sealed with wax.

The furnishings were unexpectedly comfortable. Instead of simple wooden chairs, upholstered couches were arranged for visitors, and long woven rugs guided the way through the room.

Compared to City Hall, the atmosphere here felt quieter… and more spacious. Cozy, even.

Her gaze swept across the interior.

Only a single clerk occupied the front desk.

She appeared to be in her mid-thirties, with chestnut hair neatly tied behind her head. She wore long gray robes of simple design and a pair of large round glasses resting upon her face. The lenses magnified her attentive eyes as she noticed Veronica approaching.

Veronica stepped up to the counter.

The clerk offered a polite smile.

“Good afternoon. My name is Lissa. How may I assist you?”

Veronica placed the small basket of raffies on the counter and returned the smile.

“I was hoping to inquire about a particular service,” she said. “I need to send a letter, but the contents are… somewhat sensitive.”

The woman nodded. “We offer notarization, secure record keeping, private document registration, and courier verification,” Lissa explained. “Which of these services were you interested in?”

Veronica tilted her head slightly.

“Well, actually… I was wondering if you sold any specialized papers.”

“Papers?” Lissa repeated.

“Yes,” Veronica confirmed. “Something… unusual. Perhaps a document that could alter what appears on the page? For instance, if someone intercepted a letter containing directions to my home, it might instead show them something entirely different.”

Lissa hesitated. “Hmm… I’m not sure if—”

Veronica leaned a little closer across the counter, lowering her voice conspiratorially.

“Ah—actually, perhaps it isn’t paper,” she said thoughtfully. “It might be something like invisible ink. Or enchanted ink that reacts to magic.”

The clerk blinked once, then her brows slowly drew together.

“I apologize,” Lissa said, “but I’m afraid we do not sell materials of that nature.”

Veronica hummed quietly, her expression shifting into mild disappointment.

“Oh? I already checked with City Hall, and they didn’t have any special inks available either. I’d heard that notary offices sometimes keep compounds like that—for security purposes.”

The woman shook her head gently.

“Inks like that do exist and are occasionally used for certain legal documents,” she explained, “but they are typically handled privately, most often among members of the upper nobility. Our office does not provide services of that sort, nor do we maintain any specialized inks of that nature.”

Denying it, huh?

Veronica kept the thought to herself, allowing the disappointment to linger across her features.

“I see,” she said lightly. “Perhaps I misunderstood. I thought I’d heard that merchants occasionally used your services for matters like that.”

The clerk tilted her head slightly.

“Heard? From where?”

“Oh, nowhere in particular,” Veronica replied casually. “Just something I overheard while I was out. Maybe a few merchants chatting on the street—or perhaps someone in a tavern.”

She reached into the basket and popped another raffie into her mouth.

Lissa hummed softly, a faint crease forming between her brows.

“Rumors do tend to circulate and become distorted as they pass from person to person,” she said. “Perhaps what you heard referred to magically sealed documents. Those are commonly used for secure deliveries.”

Veronica shook her head lightly. “Ah, that’s alright. I do have another method for sending an encrypted letter. I simply thought I might stop by here to see if I could obtain the ink as an extra precaution.”

Lissa nodded with quiet understanding.

“Of course. Matters of privacy are important.”

Veronica offered a small, polite bow of her head and retrieved her basket, which now held only a few remaining raffies.

“Well, I appreciate you taking the time to answer my questions,” Veronica said.

Her gaze drifted briefly around the room again.

A staircase stood to the left, ascending toward the second floor where additional shelves lined a broad balcony. On the ground level, a hallway extended toward the back of the building, its entrance partially obscured by a wooden partition—likely leading to storage rooms or private offices.

“In most cities I’ve visited, City Hall typically handles things like notarizations, confidential documents, and record keeping,” Veronica continued conversationally. “Having a separate office like this… feels somewhat unusual. It’s my first time encountering it.”

Lissa folded her hands neatly atop the desk.

“Ronswick sees a considerable amount of trade activity,” she explained. “City offices can become overwhelmed with paperwork, especially during the warmer seasons. Our establishment helps manage the overflow.”

“Ah, that makes sense,” Veronica said.

Her eyes moved casually across the room again.

“So merchants come here fairly often, then?”

“Quite frequently,” Lissa replied. “Some prefer a quieter environment than City Hall. And since we work directly with merchants on a regular basis, our services are often faster and more convenient.”

Veronica nodded thoughtfully.

“Well, I suppose I won’t take up any more of your time,” she said with an easy smile. “Thank you again for answering my questions.”

“You’re welcome,” Lissa replied politely.

Veronica gathered her nearly empty basket and stepped out of the building.

There hadn’t been much to gain from the conversation. Still, she had managed to confirm one thing—the clerk had been very deliberate in stating that their office did not deal with false ink. More importantly, the woman had seemed subtly uneasy when the topic surfaced. It had been framed as a harmless question, yet asking about such materials clearly struck her as unusual.

Unfortunately, that alone wasn’t much to build upon.

As Veronica descended the front steps, she noticed a man in light armor walking toward the entrance. A crest was emblazoned across his chest.

A wolf.

The man offered her a casual nod and a polite smile. Veronica returned the gesture before continuing down the street.

After passing him, she allowed herself a brief glance over her shoulder.

He entered the notary office.

“A wolf…” she murmured under her breath. “Scavenger…”

The door to the notary office swung open.

Lissa glanced up from the ledger she had begun filling out.

“Oh, Roderic,” she said. “You’re here. I actually need to speak with you.”

Roderic shut the door behind him before turning toward her. “Yeah?”

Lissa’s eyes narrowed slightly as she flicked her gaze toward a small drawer near the entrance.

He understood immediately.

Without another word, Roderic stepped over and opened the drawer. Inside rested a small wooden sign suspended by a thin string: Closed for Lunch.

He took it out and opened the door again, briefly scanning the street outside to ensure no one was nearby. Satisfied, he hung the sign on the handle and closed the door once more.

Turning back toward the counter, he shrugged. “There. So what’s going on?”

Lissa exhaled quietly. “You just missed someone leaving.”

“Did I?” he asked. “That girl with the basket?”

“Yes. Her.”

He chuckled under his breath. “Hard to miss. Quite a looker.”

Lissa’s expression tightened. Her lips pursed as she shot him a sharp glance over the rim of her glasses.

“Focus,” she said. “Do you remember the message Dain sent this morning?”

Roderic frowned slightly. “The one about someone snooping around and asking questions about… what was his name…?”

“Galland,” Lissa supplied.

He snapped his fingers and pointed toward her. “Yeah. That guy.”

Lissa tilted her head toward the door. “That was her.”

“What?” Roderic said, straightening. “You sure?”

Lissa tapped the edge of her desk thoughtfully. “She came in asking about invisible ink.”

Roderic blinked. “Invisible… ink?”

“Yes.”

“What exactly did she say? Why does she need it?”

“She claimed she needed to send a private letter,” Lissa explained. “One that required more secrecy than usual. She mentioned she had already asked City Hall first, then came here afterward.”

Roderic scratched at his jaw as he walked toward the counter.

“That’s… odd.”

Lissa nodded slowly. “That’s exactly what I thought.”

He leaned against the counter, brows furrowed.

“How did she even know we have invisible ink?” he asked.

Lissa adjusted her glasses, pushing the rim slightly higher along the bridge of her nose.

“She said she heard rumors,” she explained. “That notary offices like ours sometimes keep materials like that on hand.”

Roderic scoffed. “What? No, they don’t. Invisible ink is something you find in underground markets or among thieves’ guilds.

“I know,” Lissa replied calmly. “That’s exactly why I found the question suspicious.”

Roderic’s head turned, his gaze drifting toward the closed door.

“You think she knows something?”

Lissa paused, considering it.

“No,” she said at last. “Perhaps something superficial. But she didn’t give the impression that she understood what might actually be happening.”

“Still,” Roderic lowered his voice as he turned back to face her, “that’s an oddly specific request. Almost nobody uses invisible ink anymore—especially not for letters. Even at thieves’ guilds, it’s rare and archaic. No ordinary person needs it when magical seals exist.”

He studied the spot where Veronica had stood moments earlier.

“And she didn’t look like a noble. Nobles don’t come asking around for it anyway. They have that sort of thing delivered directly.”

He leaned forward, resting his elbows against the counter.

“So what did you tell her?”

Lissa closed the ledger in front of her.

“I told her we didn’t carry anything like that,” she said. “And that materials of that nature are usually reserved for noble clients.”

“Good.”

A brief silence settled between them.

Then Roderic pushed himself upright. “I’ll mention it to Tarenick,” he said. “Just to be safe.”

Lissa frowned slightly. “You think that’s necessary?”

He shrugged. “Maybe not. But he should hear it from us rather than finding out later.” Roderic crossed his arms. “Besides—rumors about our office carrying invisible ink? The last time we used any was years ago.”

His brow furrowed.

“And with Dain’s warning earlier today…”

Lissa nodded slowly. “She’s definitely looking into something. Checking our contracts, asking questions. Could be simple curiosity… or an investigator.”

Roderic let out a short laugh. “She looks a bit young to be one. More likely she had some business at High Crates and started poking around afterward.”

He tapped the counter lightly. “But I wouldn’t rule out surprises.”

“Should we keep an eye on her?” Lissa asked.

He shrugged again. “I’ll see if I can have someone follow her,” he said. “Though it might be smarter to act first.”

“The prison?” Lissa asked.

Roderic nodded. “If she’s in town asking about Galland, there’s a good chance she’ll try speaking to him—or that other man who was locked up. I’ll see if I can stir something up at the jail.”

“A prison riot?” she asked.

A slow smirk spread across his face. “Something like that,” he said. “Maybe a certain someone has an unfortunate accident before she gets the chance to ask too many questions.”

He turned his attention toward the dim corridor at the back of the building.

“Did we hear anything from the crystal seller?”

Lissa shook her head. Reaching beneath her desk, she retrieved a small notebook and opened it.

The pages were filled with short entries and vague phrases—notes written in a way that concealed their true meaning from anyone unfamiliar with the code.

“No,” she said. “He never showed up last night.”

Roderic clicked his tongue in irritation. “Tch. One problem after another.”

“We still can’t reach him either?”

“No,” Lissa replied. “We tried contacting his communication crystal, but there’s been no response.”

Roderic exhaled sharply and began walking toward the back hallway.

“Give it another day,” he said over his shoulder. “If he still doesn’t make contact, assume something went wrong. Seal the records and move the meeting location.”

With that, he disappeared down the corridor, leaving the front lobby quiet once again.

Lissa lowered her gaze to the ledger before her and began writing a new entry. For a long moment she worked in silence, occasionally adjusting her glasses. Yet the faint crease between her brows never fully disappeared.

She drew a thin line between two sections of the page.

One entry she labeled the early rooster’s call.

The other—the missing golden egg.
