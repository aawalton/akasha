The troll maiden sat up with a deep groan, debris scattering off her battered body. The groan turned into a wail as her eyes roamed across the devastation, at the remains of the tavern that was more sky than room. The tavern’s broad chimney had survived, and the part of the floor around it, and the ceiling above it. The bar was intact likewise, the remains of the once merry establishment clinging to the pillars and underside of the bridge in quiet desperation, as if the stones might let go and join their brothers and sisters in the chasm far below at any moment.

“This is going to take forever to fix,” she growled.

Leaning out myself, I could see River far below, turning somersaults in the rapids. She gave me two wet thumbs up when she saw me looking and blew me a kiss. I lifted a paw in greeting, still in a bit of a daze.

“Stones and spirits can be replaced,” Mama said, stoutly.

She turned to me with concern gleaming in her eyes. It was true, my body would take more than a bit of grooming.  I looked down at the ragged tears, the visible teeth marks and the exposed bits of bone, and shuddered. But none of us had come out of this fight unscathed. The living would heal, however. My options were limited, even with my Maud’s stitching skills.

“I’ll think of something,” I said, vaguely. “Death is not an ending, but a point on the cycle of transformation.” I felt very wise as I said this, and everyone was suitably impressed with my wisdom. Of course, at that point, fresh from my victory over the demonic king of rats, I was still far from coaxing anything resembling healing from my poor bruised and battered flesh. Yet, still I was content with the possibilities. They existed. That was enough.

We spent the remainder of the night in a comfortable pile, with plans to set off home in the morning. At some point a pain in my stomach gave way to great wracking convulsions, so loud and so violent that they could not be contained.

“Oh no,” I arched my back in agony, yacking and vomiting but only empty air came up my throat. There was something in there that needed to be expelled. The remains of For-Molsnian’s core? I thought I had absorbed it into my dantian?

“What’s wrong?” asked Modde with some concern, as the others gathered around.

“Probably just a hairball,” said Hush.

“That’s some hairball,” said Thimble.

With a last great heave, it came out. I spat the cold, knobbly thing onto the grass, and stared down at it with great pride. Everyone gathered round to admire the rat-king’s skull. It is oddly shaped, which is perhaps unsurprising after his unnatural life, and topped with a metal crown that seemed fused to the ivory. Clear, twinkling diamonds filling the eye sockets. Interesting.

“A fitting trophy,” said Mama.

I nodded in agreement, entranced by the shininess of the eyes. I decided I liked For-Molsnian much better this way. He would look fine at the back of my cupboard with my other Special Things.

We make our farewells, leaving Mama and the troll maiden to their rebuilding. Fortunately the pixies could not abide mess, so they had more than enough helping hands, and in some cases paws. I promised to visit again soon.

Those of us who lived in the forest set off towards home, slowly, taking our time to savour the trip. There was no need to rush. The rats were gone. The necromancer was gone. Things were different, they would never be the same again, but I knew that the future will bring only new adventures. I was looking forward to those. Flying was surely on the agenda, I was sure of it. Probably by the time I entered the Skyrunner Realm. I would figure out how to dream again. Sleeping was a part of my heritage after all. Once I had figured it all out then… then I would ascend to the Starwalker Realm. Then I would travel to see the stars, and Moon. How surprised they would be to see me! After Starwalker, I would ascend to the heavens and be a god amongst cats!

For the moment I was looking forward to some quiet time, and to properly explore my newfound ability with death qi. Yes, all was good.

We parted in the forest, with many promises to visit each other soon, and I went on my way alone and happy.

As I passed the grove where some of the dead rats’ demon core’s were buried, I paused to sniff at them. Their corruption was nasty and clearly evident. I did not like the way it seeped into my forest, with blackened, oily stains, crawling through the soil, into the plants, the trees, rotting everything from the inside out. Some stinking mushrooms had sprung up on the edges, where dead met living, but the stench of them made me sneeze. The silence around it was broken only by the buzzing of carrion flies feasting on the remains of whatever forest creatures were too stupid not to give the place a wide berth.

I would deal with it. I would grow, and I would figure out how to clean it up. My death qi would be able to purify the mess, I was sure of it. Once I had the skill. As the owner of the forest, and its primary guardian I would make sure things stayed… nice. Death was fine, we had come to an understanding which I’m sure would only deepen with time and familiarity, but corruption was not welcome.

Pleased with all my thoughts I headed towards my cottage. Home. I knew one other thing for certain: my Maud was going to need my help. She had been busy in my absence. As I approached I eyed the newly dead trees and plants of the forest. Under a grove of birches I paused and stared up at the silver fork that was spinning above me in the breeze. A new hobby, apparently. My human had been digging pits and there were strings of strings of jingling cutlery between the trees. Decorative? Or to warn away unwelcome guests? Knowing my Maud, possibly both.

Our reunion was suitably passionate and lengthy. She wrapped her arms around me and I purred furiously into her collarbones and nipped at her fingers. She leaned in and as her skeletal face loomed for one brief second I wondered if she was going to try and eat me, but no, she was merely planting copious amounts of kisses atop my head and tickling my chin with her bony fingers. Her bony fingers were very good at tickling.

Nothing else mattered for a while.

That evening my Maud and I sat together on her chair in front of the hearth, telling each other about our adventures, of which we had both had a great many. Despite the lack of Fire, it was still cosy. She’d spread a blanket over her femurs, and I sat on her lap while she knit.

“‘I’m going to do lots of  crafting, Jenkins,” she told me, her needles clicking, “I’m going to build up the village, all sorts of things, and oh yes, quite a bit of murdering if people continue to bother me. And I think I should make you a lich, Jenkins.” Her needles moved back and forth. “Just as soon as I figure out how to do that. Then we can live together forever, and I won’t have to worry about you getting hurt.”

“That is acceptable,” I said. “Although I have my own ways of becoming immortal. But if I’m going to be dead, I might as well be as powerful as possible. And you will need me. I don’t trust that Whisperer-god.”

She tickled under my chin.

Everything decided, I stretched out my paws, and then curled up, drifting off into deep meditation by the melodic clack of her needles and the patter of raindrops on the window panes.
