The portal—a bright white gate that contrasted with the shattered rubble, dark water, and flailing shadows—finished opening as we approached, and two figures stepped through it. One wore a blue dress so dark it was nearly pitch black. Her four arms hung from her wooden shoulders and ribs, folded across the suggestion of a bust and waist. Her movements were simultaneously jerky and silk-smooth, like she was on strings being fought over by two puppeteers.

The other leaned on a cane, her decrepit body completely moonlit, with black eyes that stared at me with an intensity I couldn’t match. I wasn’t fooled by her age; the Crone and Mind Maiden Enolda were, individually, enough to end us here and now.

“Oh, dwellers of this portal world, rejoice, for Mother Mine hath—“ Mind Maiden Enolda began.

The Crone’s gnarled hand raised to cut her off. “Enough, fool girl!”

I relaxed. Not much, but the tiniest bit.

“Your sister has spent the last hour trying desperately to get me to intervene here, on your behalf. I finally agree, and what do I find but corpses and devastation. You actually killed him, eh?” The Crone laughed, the sound like a squeaking gate in the wind. “How, pray tell, did you manage that?”

The Crone was here. The most powerful SS-Plus-Ranker I knew of. She could kill us both and take the Placid King’s core. It’d be simple. But even so, that sense of relaxation washed over me—not like an aura, but true relaxation. We’d killed the Placid King, and now we had time before the God of Thunder attempted his own ascension.

“Why are you here?” I asked.

A single finger rose, and the Crone’s eyebrow raised over a black eye. “Oh, you know why. I’m here to collect.”

“The core,” Ellen said.

“Of course, dearie. The core.”

I pulled it out of my pocket. She knew we had it, so hiding it was pointless. Then I waved it at the Lightning Tower. “Let’s talk about this core. Why do you care so much about it?”

“We’ll walk and talk. Enolda, guard the portal.”

“Thy wish is my reason for being, Mother Mine,” the puppet woman said. She collapsed into a pile a moment later, her strings seemingly cut.

The Crone, for her part, started walking. She leaned on her cane as she did, long, tangled mats of hair parting around thin legs. “You know what you have there, right, boy?”

“An SS-Plus-Ranked core,” I said.

She nodded, but made no move to take it from me. We strolled along a path that seemed to dry before the Crone’s footsteps could land, water skirting away from her in sheets. The SS-Plus Paragon didn’t seem in a hurry. Why not?

“An SS-Plus-Ranked core. One that belonged to a being beyond ready for ascension. If he hadn’t allowed his emotions to cloud his mind, he would likely have been successful—and his passing to the next realm would have likely meant your world’s destruction, regardless. I’ve already alerted the Ghostmarket to the existence of the Placid King’s world and its undefended state. When it collapses, it will have already been stripped to the walls, and his stored power distributed like sand in the desert.”

I thought about that as we entered the Lightning Tower and found a quartet of seats. Tanat had, evidently, expected us; she sat in one, but stood as the Crone entered. Her eyes locked on the Paragon’s for a moment. Then she broke her gaze, staring at the floor, and sat back down.

“A girl who knows respect! You keep good company, Stormblade.” The Crone sat, and Ellen and I did as well. “Now, the core, please?”

I hesitated, the watery core still in my hand. Giving it to the Crone would be the easiest solution to the problem of what to do with it. Its mere existence was a threat; if the God of Thunder got a hold of it, he’d be able to ascend immediately. But…”I have a few questions first.”

“Ask away. You’re the one with a time limit, after all.”

“Fine. You said earlier that you were opposed to any further ascensions while you tried to learn a better way. Why didn’t you stop the Placid King? Why did Jessie have to bother you for an hour before you decided to help?” My eyes narrowed slightly.

She saw it. Her old, thin lips curved upward in a grin. “Two reasons. First, this wasn’t my war to fight. Quite the opposite. If an idiotic Paragon is too consumed by revenge to simply ascend, far be it from me to stop it from using its stored strength for other purposes.”

“And the second?” Ellen asked. She glared at the Crone with more fury than I could muster.

“I was outmaneuvered. The God of Thunder and the Placid King shouldn’t have been at the cusp of ascension together. With both of them in this position, I couldn’t bring my power to bear stopping one without offering the other the chance to ascend.”  Her hand raised before I could say anything. “No, I don’t believe the two of them had conspired. They hate each other as rivals. If either ascended, it would make the other’s path forward that much harder. It was simple bad luck.

“No matter the case, though, I have the strength to defeat either of them, and to resist the other, but beating both at once would be taxing beyond reason. That core? I would expend a dozen like it to achieve victory—and that cost isn’t one I can stomach.” The Crone held her hand out, more insistently. “Now, the core?”

“Wait,” Ellen said. “Kade, we need to keep it.”

“You dare pit yourselves against me?” The Crone’s eyebrow raised again, and a grin crept across her face. “You three are bigger fools than Enolda out there. At least she shows proper respect.”

Tanat looked horrified as her eyes flicked from the Crone’s face to mine, then to Ellen’s. I cleared my throat after a moment. “The deal was for cannons.”

“And, as I asked your sister an hour ago, where are my cannons?”

Ellen stood. “On their way. But the core isn’t part of that deal, and we need it to stop the God of Thunder.”

“How?” the Crone asked. Then she shook her head, tangled mats shifting. “I don’t care. You’re willing to fight me for it?”

I glanced down at the sword still in my hand. My resources were regenerating, and my body was…hurting, but not broken. Ellen’s injury was almost completely healed; she’d clearly been focusing on the puncture wound. Yes. Yes, I was willing to fight the Crone over this.

I nodded.

“Fine. Fine! I won’t pry it from your lifeless hands. Not yet. But your friend Eugene—“

“The God of Thunder,” I said quickly.

“The God of Thunder, then. Your friend, the God of Thunder, won’t be so merciful. When he destroys this portal world, takes your core, your pretty little partner’s, and this one, and uses all three to annihilate half the universe in his attempt to ascend, you will wish I had.”

The Crone stood, and so did I. Her black eyes flickered across my body, and I braced myself. “Thank you for the visit, Crone.”

“Bah!” The Crone strode back to the Lightning Tower’s exit and stepped into the air. She hovered in place for a moment, then nodded. “You be careful, Kade Stormblade. You’re playing with lightning, and that’s a good way to get shocked.”

I nodded back. “So is he, Crone. So is he.”

She snorted, collected Enolda, and disappeared from my portal world.

Tanat waited until the moon-white portal actually disappeared before she said a word. “Kade Noelstra, you keep incredibly dangerous company.”

I rolled my eyes. “She’s harmless.”

Ellen shook her head. “You sure about that?”

“Well, other than that she’s turned at least one monster as powerful as Retan inside out. And that she’s beyond strong enough to do that to us all. But I wouldn’t treat her like an equal if she hadn’t done so first.” We’d have that conversation about the nature of ascension, after all, and we were…not friends. But allies of convenience.

The enemy of the Crone was also my enemy.

“It’s not a permanent arrangement, though, Kade,” Ellen said. She pulled me back down to my seat, fingers woven through mine, and I realized she’d put her own chair next to mine. “At some point, we will become her enemies. Either if we decide on ascension for ourselves—“

“Not happening.”

“—or if she breaks and attempts to ascend. She said the act would be incredibly destructive. We’d have to try to stop her.”

I nodded. “We’ll burn that bridge when we get to it.”

“Then what now?” Tanat asked. “You still have a monster every bit as strong as the Placid King to fight, and…”

“The God of Thunder is likely stronger. The Placid King, for all that he was an SS-Plus-Ranked Paragon, seemed to prefer to fight from the shadows, using his pupils and minions—like Magda.” I shivered, thinking about the Paragon of the Sliding Surge. She was with the God of Thunder now, no doubt conspiring. “Euge—the God of Thunder is a combat monster. I only ever saw him deploy a minion a handful of times, and that was always as a test for me, not to achieve one of his objectives.”

I closed my eyes, thinking about both the lightning dragon form and the form he’d taken when training me, first in my push to C-Rank and then again in my attempt to get Stormbreak under control. Either would be more than a match for me in a one-on-one fight. Either could probably handle Tanat, Ellen, and me together without breaking a sweat.

“We’re going to be outgunned, but not outnumbered,” Ellen said.

Tanat waved a thin hand at the tower. It was in shambles; the Eye of the Storm had collapsed outside, and portal metal creaked ominously around us. “This will not repel a similar assault. Not again.”

“Not even with this as a power source?” I held up the core. “It’s not a lightning core, but we can probably use it to create clouds, and from there…”

“From there, the storm,” Tanat finished. Her hand closed on the portal, fingertips grazing mine, and she nodded. “I will do what I can with it, but I will no doubt need your help. Both of you.”

Ellen cleared her throat. “This is useful. But we still have most of our final day of time. We need to use it intelligently. First, by making sure our allies understand what’s at stake, and making sure they’re willing to fight. Second, by bringing the tower up to speed. My…Bob’s cannons should be showing up soon. We’ll give the Crone what we can spare, but the rest need to be in our portal world as soon as possible.”

“Right. Let’s head back and see what the damages truly are,” I said, and together, we walked through the portal back to Earth.

The damage was bad.

Nothing as catastrophic as what the Placid King had done to our portal world, but somehow, this felt more permanent—and more personal.

The Desert Wind building almost certainly wouldn’t be livable any time soon. Sirens blared all around it, their high, piercing wails a counterpoint to the thunder and storm’s howl that still echoed in my ear. The gym’s wall had completely collapsed, blown outward by the force of my fight against the Placid King’s minions. The library had partially flooded, and a fire crew battled a blaze that had started in the kitchen.

No doubt because Jessie had been making pancakes or something.

I pocketed the keystone to our portal world, and Ellen and I picked our way through the rubble. Two bedrooms seemed to have collapsed into the gym. Here, a mattress lay, sheets half-off, sprawled over a weight rack. An entire drawer of socks had landed in the flood zone, and they half-floated like jellyfish.

It was a miracle when we ran into the fire crew. I raised my hand as they moved toward us, assembling stretchers like we’d been hurt. We had, but not by the building’s destruction. “Kade Noelstra and Ellen Traynor, S-Rank. We’re fine.”

“S-Rankers?” one of the firefighters said.

“Yep. This building was—“

“Attacked. That’s what the briefing said,” the man said. His voice was muffled by his helmet and breathing mask, but he pointed back over his shoulder. “I’ll lead you two out of here. The rest of you, keep looking for survivors!”

“That’s not necessary. We evacuated the building…an hour ago? Two?” I closed my eyes, rubbed my palms against them, and regretted it. Dust and grit coated my hands. “I need to talk to Jessica Gerald. She’s the guild leader in charge here.”

The helmet dipped for a moment. If I didn’t know better, I’d say the man looked embarrassed. He started walking, head still down. “She said you’d say that.”

“Who did?”

“Councilwoman Myers. She’s taken control over this operation.”

“Why?” I asked. The guild building’s destruction looked like a clean-cut emergency services situation—maybe with GC oversight, but not with GC control.

“You can ask her. She’s right past the tape.”

We emerged from the wreckage, and I blinked against the Phoenix sun. Someone shoved a cup into my hand. I smelled it: coffee, black. Piping hot, too. I drank it, wincing a little at the flavor. Ellen only held hers as the fireman escorted us past a line of yellow tape and toward the hawklike woman staring us down.

“Where’s Jessie?” I asked the second I was close enough.

Councilwoman Myers only narrowed her eyes. “You have the nerve to ask that after she—you know what? No. No. Your sister’s as much a maniac as you are. She opened up a portal in the middle of the Portal Tyrants’ keep and went through. It closed behind her. The rest of your guild was able to explain an eighth or so of what happened, but not enough for us to replicate it ourselves. We believe she’s visiting—“

“The Crone, yes. We know. Take me there.” I stared Myers down. The woman stared back. “Listen, Councilwoman, I don’t care about whatever you’re trying to prove here. We’ve got a limited amount of time to rally. An SS-Plus-Ranked monster just attacked Phoenix. It’s going to happen again, and next time, it’ll be worse.”

For the first time, the councilwoman seemed to falter. She nodded. “Fine. I’ll take you both there. But first, there’s the matter of those.”

Before I could ask what she meant, she gestured to a line of semi trucks, parked on the side of the road, in the shade of the 303 Wall. Each of them carried a stack of crates on their flat beds, and each truck bore a familiar logo. The Traynor Corporation. And, at the head of the convoy, a jet-black limousine idled, its windows rolled up.

Ellen stiffened. Her hand slipped into mine, and I squeezed it reassuringly.

Bob Traynor had come to the Desert Wind Guild.
