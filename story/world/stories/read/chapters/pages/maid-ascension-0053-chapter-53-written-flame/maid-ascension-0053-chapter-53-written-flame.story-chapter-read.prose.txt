When Lira first considered converting one of her existing spells into a runic equivalent, her instinct was to begin with one of the first spells she had learned: her fire manipulation spell, a spell she already understood well enough to cast comfortably and whose structure was similar to four other spells she knew.

In theory, it seemed like a reasonable starting point. In practice, it proved frustrating almost immediately.

Sitting cross-legged in her alcove, Lira began breaking the incantation apart piece by piece in her head, testing substitutions mentally before even touching ink to paper.

The problem revealed itself quickly. If she wanted to anchor the spell onto a written symbol, then one component of the original incantation would need to be replaced by Kisar, since that was the entire foundation of runic casting, transcribing the magical effect onto a symbolic medium.

That meant something had to be sacrificed, either Pika, Fila, or Farshum.

Eliminating Fila was nonsensical, as that was the elemental core of the spell itself. Removing Farshum was arguably even worse, as that more or less defined the magical effect.

That left only Pika, which at least made some degree of sense. Rather than actively guiding the spell with her mind, she could, in theory, allow the symbol itself to serve as the guiding structure. It was not an unreasonable concept. But that would mean setting the spell into a static configuration rather than something she actively controlled.

For a brief moment, she also considered expanding the spell into a Tier One variant by adding additional words instead of switching them.

But the longer she thought about it, the more the idea irritated her. Every spell built around Pika was fundamentally dependent on the active control aspect to be useful. That was the entire point of the word. It granted flexibility precisely because the caster remained an active participant in shaping the magical effect as it occurred.

Attempting to convert that kind of spell into a passive written form was structurally awkward. It was like replacing a pair of skilled hands with wooden stumps and expecting a comparable level of dexterity.

Lira groaned softly and rubbed her forehead, annoyed at herself for approaching the problem incorrectly.

She forced herself to stop thinking in terms of convenience and instead think in terms of structure. What she needed was not a spell built on versatility, but something more straightforward, predictable, and conceptually simple enough to naturally lend itself to symbolic anchoring.

Almost immediately, her thoughts returned to the heating enchantments Edward Emberborn had installed throughout the library.

That spell was elegant, practical, and simple. More importantly, she was already familiar with it. She had not only observed it being cast but also possessed the warm pocket spell, which was an even more complex variation of the same principle.

Lira placed a fresh sheet of paper on the stone floor before her and carefully created the rune for Fila on it. Then she settled herself into a meditative state.

“Pika. Selan. Tureth.”

The world softened around the edges as her breathing slowed and her awareness sharpened. Magic became easier to conceptualize in this state, so she focused on reviewing the spell structure mentally.

Kisar. Fila. Coopar.

Fila. Fire. Coopar. Contain.

Together, they produced heat. This was not something fundamentally new. When missing a word for a concept, combining words was often the solution, and defining heat as fire that was bounded and controlled was almost elegant. A contained flame was heat.

It made sense, which perhaps explained why the spell had worked so well.

She had felt the effects of that combination all winter, first with her warm pocket spell, then with Edward’s enchanted plates. She had concrete confirmation that it worked.

She studied the rune once more and thought about language, about writing itself.

Runic magic seemed to care about symbolic meaning more than ordinary spellcasting. It was not merely about issuing instructions through words, but about embodying concepts through symbols. Writing as a concept was already an abstraction, turning sound into sight and word into meaning.

Runic magic simply weaponized that principle, turning spells into enchantments.

Kisar. Fila. Coopar.

Click.

Lira smiled as the spell imprinted itself into her understanding. She focused on the rune at the center of the paper and cast:

“Kisar. Fila. Coopar.”

The spell activated immediately.

Warmth radiated from the paper, subtle but unmistakable. She reached forward and touched the rune carefully, expecting perhaps the sharp, dangerous heat of fire. Instead, it felt more akin to hot metal left near a hearth, uncomfortable but not harmful.

She waited for the inevitable.

Her previous experiments with fire-related runic effects had all ended the same way, with the paper burning after a brief success.

But this time, the result was different.

After a minute of stable heat distribution, the magical effect began fluctuating rhythmically. It weakened slightly, then strengthened again, then weakened, then returned, pulsing like a breathing heart.

Lira leaned forward, immediately fascinated.

The heat output remained stable enough to warm the alcove gradually, but the magical intensity clearly oscillated in a repeating cycle. It was almost like the spell had reached an imperfect equilibrium.

She narrowed her eyes thoughtfully.

Perhaps the issue was not the spell itself, but the symbolic structure supporting it.

That thought immediately inspired her next attempt. If the symbolic aspect mattered, then perhaps a different layout could improve efficiency.

She drew another Fila rune, but this time surrounded it with eight smaller Coopar runes arranged in a symmetrical circle around the central symbol, almost like containment barriers.

Then she cast again.

“Kisar. Fila. Coopar.”

The difference was immediate.

The heat became softer and more evenly distributed, while the fluctuations almost entirely disappeared. The warmth lacked the sharper intensity of the original version at its highest peak, but the overall effect was considerably more stable.

Lira grinned.

That was enough proof of concept to send her spiraling into experimentation.

This field of magic collided directly with another area of expertise she happened to excel in: calligraphy. Despite her youth, Lira believed herself to be already a master of decorative writing.

Her father had taught her from childhood with relentless precision, drilling discipline, stroke control, and structure into her hands until penmanship became as natural to her as breathing.

Her later work as a forger had refined those instincts further, sharpening her hand-eye coordination and forcing her to understand the concept of writing, as well as style, intention, and how the form itself conveyed meaning.

Later still, her granduncle’s book, which she had discovered in this alcove, has reignited her passion for the art of calligraphy.

So if runic magic cared about symbolic efficiency, then artistry should matter as well.

Using the Common script’s naturally cursive nature, she began elongating and curving the letters in the word fire until they formed the shape of an actual flame.

The result was beautiful and barely legible, the sort of calligraphic design one could only read by tracing it carefully with a fingertip from beginning to end.

She admired the result for a moment before casting.

“Kisar. Fila. Coopar.”

This version produced more concentrated heat, strongest at the center and weaker toward the edges. The fluctuation returned, though this time the cycle was shorter, rapidly switching between hotter and colder states, stronger but more unstable.

Ten minutes later, the paper finally ignited.

Lira noted the result carefully, then moved on to the next test.

The next design involved repeatedly writing the word fire in tight cursive loops until the entire arrangement resembled the rune Fila itself, a symbol composed of language recursively imitating another symbol.

“Kisar. Fila. Coopar.”

This version produced a heat level similar to the normal rune, but with nearly double the fluctuation frequency. However, the time it spent near the peak was longer than the time spent near the trough, which probably indicated a slight increase in efficiency.

Now fully invested, Lira decided to attempt something far more elaborate.

Writing it manually would have been tedious for a design of this complexity, so she was fortunate enough to rely on magic instead.

“Asatan. Tir. Kisar.”

Ink shifted fluidly under her control as she manipulated it across the page with painstaking precision.

Using repeating Coopar runes, she drew the structure of a small enclosed furnace, the kind used to heat homes during winter. Inside its frame, she densely packed repeating Fila runes arranged to resemble a living flame.

The final illustration was absurdly detailed, so intricate that she nearly ran out of usable ink before finishing and had to replenish it manually halfway through.

When complete, the page was almost entirely black with layered symbolic density.

A furnace containing fire, built from containment itself.

Concept reinforcing concept, used to create art.

She stared at it with mounting anticipation before finally casting.

“Kisar. Fila. Coopar.”

The result was immediate and excellent.

Warmth spread steadily throughout the alcove. It was not as intense as the cursive flame, nor as weak as the first stable attempt. And it had no noticeable fluctuations.

Most importantly, it did not combust.

Lira touched the paper cautiously, then waited.

One minute passed. Then two. Then five. It was still stable.

Her smile widened into something deeply smug.

This was by far the most successful configuration she had created so far. It was stable, practical, visually elegant, and entirely paper-and-ink based.

In fact, Lira strongly suspected her version was now conceptually superior to Edward’s copper plates in heat distribution, though admittedly, his possessed the distinct advantage of durability and not being made of flammable parchment.

Still, superiority was superiority.

She carefully placed the enchanted sheet on the stone floor away from anything remotely combustible and arranged two paperweights over it, mostly to soothe her own lingering paranoia. She also destroyed the inferior version so she would not overheat at night.

The true test now was longevity.

Would paper prove too unstable a medium for sustained enchantment over longer periods?

Would the spell eventually degrade or combust overnight?

There was only one way to know.

Lira yawned suddenly, the accumulated exhaustion of late-night experimentation finally catching up to her.

She sat down on one chair and arranged the other chair as leg support.

“No exploding while I sleep,” she jokingly told the paper before finally lying down in the tight makeshift bedding.

As she drifted toward sleep, one final thought crossed her mind.

Runic magic was far too much fun and dangerously addictive.
