Gin left me, and for the first time since waking, the world didn’t feel right. My perception of time was fractured—not normal. Not even close.

If I stopped focusing on any one person, their movements slowed to a crawl. Voices warped into deep, syrupy tones, drawn-out and alien, as if the air itself had thickened. It was like watching reality breathe in slow motion, each exhale heavy enough to press against my skull. The harrowing part wasn’t that it happened—it was how natural it was starting to feel.

Then, a hand touched my shoulder.

I didn’t need to look to know who it was. The faint scent of wild spice, the soft click of claws against marble, and the whisper of her tail cutting through air—all of it announced her presence long before her voice did.

I turned anyway, forcing a smirk. “The Panthress Barbatos. How may I assist you this fine afternoon?”

Her tail flicked once, sharp and deliberate. Golden eyes met mine, pupils narrowing to slits. The expression she gave me wasn’t anger exactly—it was correction.

“Tsk.” A single sound, disapproving but not without amusement. “Alexander,” she said, her voice low and resonant, “I assume you’ve recovered some memories. Do you mind telling me what they were?”

There was no real way to refuse a Dominus—not one who ruled the very space you breathed in. Still, I tried to hold her gaze without flinching.

I sighed, rubbing the back of my neck. “Yeah. I did.”

The words came slow, dragging the weight of everything I’d seen. “Some memories I didn’t know I had. Didn’t really want to come to terms with them yet, but…” I exhaled, a humorless laugh slipping out. “Yeah. I’ll say them.”

Her eyes didn’t move. She was patient—predatory patience, the kind that waits for truth to hang itself.

“It started at fourteen,” I said quietly.

And with that, the silence between us deepened, like a curtain being drawn before confession.

***

I was living my life in Marr then—if you could even call it living. A kind of self-imposed exile, really. I told myself it was my choice, that I preferred the quiet, the distance, the solitude. But the truth was less noble. Part of it was my own anti-social nature—this innate discomfort with crowds and conversation—and part of it… was her. My mother.

Juliet Duarte.

Even when she wasn’t in the room, she was there—an ever-present pressure, a gravity that bent everything around her. To the world, she was the unrivaled matriarch of the Duarte line, the legendary Septuplication Witch who could command seven selves at once, each one sharper and colder than the last. To me, she was the constant whisper in my mind telling me what I should be, who I must become, and what I was failing to do.

So I withdrew. Hid from the world. Hid from her.

It was during that isolation that I stumbled upon a new profession. Or maybe it stumbled upon me. A whisper at a tavern, a pamphlet carried on the wind, a fragment of conversation about the Otherrealms—the places beyond the veil of our world, where power and mystery bled into reality like ink through paper. The stories spoke of endless skies, of monsters, of riches, and of those who dared to walk between realms. Walkers, they were called.

Everyone in Marr knew of them. They were the ones who guarded trade routes, escorted caravans across both mortal lands and alien soils, negotiated with the peaceful denizens of Otherrealms, and fought off the abominations known as Others. To most, they were mercenaries or glorified travelers.

To me, they were something else entirely.

They were freedom.

Freedom from Marr. Freedom from the house of Duarte. Freedom from her.

I remember telling my mother about it once—naively thinking she’d understand. Her expression was kind, almost warm, her tone carefully measured. She told me she would help. That she would use her influence to secure my recommendation, to ensure the examiners looked favorably on my application. And she did.

At least, that’s what it looked like.

Behind the curtain, her other selves—the ones I never saw—were already moving against me. Whispering in the ears of officials, altering documents, redirecting correspondence. Each small sabotage was so precise, so deliberate, that I never noticed. A form misplaced here. A letter of recommendation delayed there. Minor inconveniences that piled up until my progress slowed to a crawl.

She called it protection. She always did.

Whenever I confronted her—rarely, and never without trembling—she’d smile and say, “You don’t need to chase danger to prove yourself, Alexander. The world will come to you in time.”

But I knew better. She wasn’t trying to protect me. She was trying to keep me.

Keep me where she could see me. Where she could control me. Where I would never leave her shadow.

That was when I realized—freedom wasn’t just a dream. It was a rebellion.

The training my mother gave me was supposed to be a gift. That’s what she told me. That’s what everyone told me.

“You’re fortunate,” they said. “Juliet Duarte herself is guiding you. Most Walkers would kill for a tenth of that instruction.”

And I believed them, for a time. I believed her too. Because when you’re young—when your world still orbits around your mother’s approval—you mistake cruelty for discipline, and confusion for complexity. You assume that if you don’t understand the lesson, it must mean the lesson is beyond you.

It took me years to understand that the lessons themselves were designed to make sure I never succeeded.

Juliet’s training sessions were labyrinthine—by design. They never ended where they began. She would start with meditation, turn it into endurance drills, and then demand I recite theoretical formulas on energy displacement while blindfolded and standing on one leg. I thought she was testing my adaptability. But every test was flawed, every method deliberately skewed toward failure. She’d ask me to shape mana in geometries that contradicted basic principles, to repeat gestures that cancelled their own effects. And when it failed—as it always did—she’d sigh and shake her head as if I were the one who misunderstood.

“Again,” she’d say. “You’re not listening, Alexander.”

Of course, I wasn’t listening. There was nothing coherent to hear. She was teaching me to build towers without foundations, to sculpt air into stone. Every success I had was met with subtle correction that steered me back toward mediocrity.

She wanted me to think I was progressing. To keep me occupied, frustrated, hopeful, but never dangerous.

There were days when she seemed kind. When she’d brush a hand across my cheek and say she was proud of me. That I was special. That the world didn’t deserve me yet. But that tenderness was always followed by a new “lesson”—one that undid the progress of the last. A regression masked as refinement.

I didn’t understand it then. I thought she was simply eccentric—brilliant beyond my comprehension. I thought her methods were meant to break me down before building me up again.

But they only ever broke.

It wasn’t until a year later—on my maiden voyage out of Marr—that I began to see the truth.

The journey was supposed to mark the Coming of Duty, a rite of passage every young adult took before their Awakening. A simple custom, a trial by distance and responsibility: you joined a trade caravan, drove the wagons, bartered in the border towns, proved you could stand on your own feet beyond the gilded walls of your home.

For me, it was supposed to be a test of independence. For her, it was something else entirely.

We departed Marr at dawn, the horizon painted in the orange haze of burning incense from the gates. The road wound like a vein through plains of silvergrass, and for the first time in my life, I felt the air move without my mother’s shadow following behind it. No copies of her voice whispering lessons in my ear. No expectations binding my spine straight. Just the sound of wheels against stone and the rhythmic beat of Fractal’s wings overhead.

It should have been liberating. And for a while, it was.

The caravan was manned by a dozen apprentices—alchemists, healers, artificers, and fledgling warriors. Everyone with potential but without purpose yet. We traveled together for weeks, trading goods and laughter, learning what it meant to be small and mortal under an infinite sky.

It was on the third month that I found it.

The grimoire.

It wasn’t buried or hidden. It was just there—wedged between a crate of furs and a broken chest of coins, as though it had been waiting. Its cover was carved with looping sigils that glowed faintly when I touched it, pulsing in response to my mana. The letters rearranged themselves, translating into a language I somehow understood instinctively.

The Testament of the Asura-Machina Del Diablo.

At first, I thought it was myth. An old scholar’s attempt to romanticize the Age of Awakening. But the further I read, the more I understood that it was no fiction—it was a chronicle. A record of when humanity used pseudo-spirits to power constructs we now called Machina. Not just tools, but echoes of our own souls, given form.

The text spoke of the balance between soul and steel. Of binding mana into pattern. Of how willpower, if given proper rhythm, could shape reality itself.

It was everything my mother’s teachings weren’t—clear, methodical, patient. It respected the learner instead of punishing them.

For the first time, my mind clicked with what I was reading. I began to apply the principles quietly at night. Tiny things at first—manifesting paper sigils that would float and spin with minimal mana cost, folding barriers from will and ink. Fractal seemed intrigued, sometimes landing beside the grimoire, her iridescent feathers rippling as if the words resonated with her too.

The progress was fast. Too fast. I began dreaming of structures—geometric arrays of light, mechanical wings, symphonies of motion that looked less like spells and more like designs. I woke up feeling invigorated, like I was pulling something from another world.

Six months passed like that. Six months of discovery, of joy. Of being me.

When I finally returned home, I felt ready. Confident. I thought—naïvely—that Mother would be proud.

She was waiting in the courtyard when the caravan pulled in, her seven selves standing like statues in a crescent. Her real body—the center one—smiled. It was a small, delicate smile that could mean anything.

I remember holding up the grimoire like an offering. “I found something incredible, Mother. It explains everything—the Asura-Machina, the old methods of mana structuring, how—”

That was as far as I got.

A wave of mana hit me before I even finished the sentence.

It was silent, invisible—but it hurt. It wasn’t like fire or lightning; it was a rewiring. My mana lines spasmed, my vision fractured into prisms. My knees hit the cobblestones as blood leaked from my nose. I heard her voice distantly, layered across seven mouths. “Shh. Sleep, my son. You’ve gone too far.”

Then blackness.

When I woke, it was dusk. I was in my old room, the curtains drawn, the grimoire gone. My pulse pounded in my ears like a metronome, but everything around me felt too alive. The walls breathed faintly, and the floor hummed under my feet. I could hear the mana, like a heartbeat under the world. The sensation was intoxicating and wrong all at once.

But the worst part wasn’t the sound or the light. It was the emptiness.

I tried to recall the text I had read so fervently—the sigils, the techniques, even the title page—and nothing came. Not a single line. I could remember reading, could remember feeling, but not what I had read. It was like a book with every word ripped out.

Panic gave way to nausea. I grabbed at my head, tried to reconstruct anything, but all I found were blanks. Smooth, clean blanks.

That was when suspicion turned to certainty.

She hadn’t just sabotaged my training. She’d sabotaged me.

The techniques she taught me, the contradictions, the deliberate obfuscations—they weren’t failures. They were containment. Each incorrect lesson was a chain designed to ensure that when I discovered something real, something beyond her control, I wouldn’t be able to keep it.

Juliet Duarte didn’t want a son who could surpass her. She wanted a weapon she could recall when she needed it.

Over the following weeks, the symptoms worsened. I’d wake with nosebleeds. Hear voices humming in equations. My mana felt knotted, pulsing irregularly as if something inside me was struggling to breathe.

One night, I found her in the observatory. She was staring through the telescope, a cup of red tea in hand. I asked her—directly this time—what she had done to me.

She didn’t even turn. “Something you’ll thank me for, one day,” she said quietly. “You found knowledge that was never meant for our Domain. ou don’t understand what it would have done to you.”

And maybe she was right. Maybe it would have broken me. But the truth is, I’ll never know, because she made that choice for me.

From that night on, I stopped calling it training. I stopped calling her Mother.

Every whisper of mana I feel now, every flicker of that paper-starlight under my skin—it’s a reminder. That once, I was closer to freedom than she ever allowed me to believe. And that she burned it out of me in the name of love.
