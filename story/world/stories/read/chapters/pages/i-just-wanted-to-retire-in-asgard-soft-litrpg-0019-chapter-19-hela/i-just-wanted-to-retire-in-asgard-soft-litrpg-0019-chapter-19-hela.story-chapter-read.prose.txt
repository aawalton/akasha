Barry had lost everything, but he knew one thing would still survive. He stood in the ash and ran every diagnostic he had left.

The familiar blue light flickered, then slowly steadied.

[System still operating]

The system had survived the crash, and he resented it. Why had it made it through when the rest of his inventions hadn't?

"Okay, you cockroach of a system, analyze the surroundings."

[Analyzing...]

[Atmospheric composition: Highly incompatible.]

[Material classification: Deceased organic structure.]

[Mist analysis: Unavailable.]

"What? That's it? Give me something more, something useful."

He tried again. The interface hung half a second longer than it should have, then repeated the same three lines in colder text.

Six lines, and every one of them was either obvious or useless.

He stared at the display. "Great. The one thing that should've died with the rest of my life is the only thing still talking."

Nothing answered. As he went to swipe it away, another panel slid into view.

[Book analysis: 23% complete.]

"It's still... analyzing," he muttered, then paused. "Wait. I used my own scanners, not the system."

He hadn't asked it to analyze anything. It had started on its own. He had questions, but he knew there wouldn't be any answers.

Around him, the wind kept coming, fast and cold, and he could feel it in his bones. Ash skated across the ground in thin sheets and settled again. Barry shut the diagnostic window for good and turned the same pressure on the only other person in sight.

Baldr stood a few meters away with his arms folded, watching the gray horizon like a hiker taking in the view. Barry walked over.

"You know I'm going to die from this constant wind, right?"

Baldr turned toward him. "Ha! You can't die here. This is where the dead go, mortal." He paused, his smile thinning. "You're alive, and you're not a god. That's actually new. I wonder what the wind does when it finally takes something that still has a pulse."

"You tell me," Barry said, still shivering. "You've been here for centuries. Explaining this place should be your job. How did those dead projections of me form? Why is the air toxic? Why does the wind never stop, and why is it so damn cold?"

Baldr's eyes slid over to him, unhurried. "You talk a lot for someone who just got here. You know that?"

"I need data, Baldr. Not your half-assed guesses."

"And you need to shut up for a second. Are all mortals like you these days? I thought you lot were a race of hope and good and change."

Barry didn't shut up.

"Yeah, yeah, go check our history. Read about Hitler, Alexander, or even Genghis Khan, and you'll see a lot of hope there." He said, walking back. "And the ash packs like wet sand under my boots, but the wind pushes it around like it weighs nothing. Same material. Pick one."

"It's Helheim," Baldr said.

"That's not an answer. And the sound. It carries way too far, then dies like someone hit mute. Real air fades a voice out. It doesn't just cut it off."

This time there was no answer at all.

Baldr's posture never changed, but the line of his mouth tightened the longer Barry pressed. That was the difference between them. Barry needed to understand things before he could fix them. Baldr had lived long enough to stop asking.

"Come on, Baldr. What's the logic behind this infinite cold wind with no storm anywhere near it? I can keep talking until your ears melt."

One moment Baldr was standing still. The next, his hand closed around Barry's throat. The grip was precise, firm enough to cut off speech but not to crush. He lifted Barry clear of the ground.

"Enough."

Barry's boots left the ash. Baldr rose with him, wings of pale light unfolding from his back with a low hum, like a transformer coming online. The ground dropped away, the wind hammered Barry's face, and the gray sky rushed closer.

"Or you could just shut up," Baldr said. "The old me would have sliced you into a million pieces. Did you know that?"

Baldr flew them in a hard, straight line until the air thinned and the land below blurred into a smear of black and bone. At the edge of the sky, he stopped and held Barry out so he could see.

"But I've become more tolerant. More generous. So look." He pointed ahead. "There's one of the answers to your nagging questions."

Far off, a single colossal vulture stood on a column of stone that rose from the world tree's exposed roots, where the bedrock had cracked and buckled under the strain. It was big enough to dwarf mountains. Its wings moved in slow, measured strokes, and each beat didn't just push air. It simply made it.

"Is that where—"

"Yes," Baldr cut in. "Every wind from Asgard to Niflheim comes from there. From that thing's wings. Don't worry, it's bound here for all eternity."

Barry went still. For the first time since he'd arrived, his face changed. The flat, analytical set of his mouth eased, and something like focus replaced the frustration.

Baldr noticed. "Are you... smiling?"

Barry didn't look away from the bird. "Calling that a bird is like calling Yggdrasil a tree. Shouldn't it be an eagle?"

The grip on his neck loosened. Baldr studied him, a little in awe, and watched the smile widen.

Why wouldn't he smile? Barry had just found the most sophisticated natural system he'd ever encountered, and it was the first thing in Helheim that felt like it belonged in his world of engineering.

Infinite wind.

His mind went straight to practical application. If the vulture held the realm boundaries in place, then a single feather, or a controlled sample of its energy signature, could supply the missing component for most of his equipment. The calculations started on their own. Yield estimates. Containment parameters. Extraction vectors that wouldn't kill the source.

"Baldr, take me," he said, forgetting he was still dangling by the neck. "Take me to that bird."

Baldr answered almost before the words were out. "Oh, you're crazy. First you talk about making the Allfather pay, and now you want to fly straight at that thing?"

Barry turned his head, his voice still rough. "You already know what I'm thinking. By now, you should have some sense of who I am."

"I have a guess. The so-called engineer." Baldr kept his voice level. "Even if you're the one who fixed the Bifrost, this is a whole new world and a whole new problem. If that bird stops moving, the Nine Realms start collapsing into one another. It's more than an animal. It's a structural component of reality. Take from it and you don't get power. You get cascade failures. You've already watched one take everything you owned. This one wouldn't stop at you."

Barry looked back at the creature and ran the numbers again, slower this time. The math didn't lie. The scale was wrong for harvesting. Any meaningful sample would create a local instability, and that instability would spread. He could engineer a temporary bypass, but not a permanent one, not without becoming the problem he was trying to escape.

He let it go.

Baldr watched the decision settle. He didn't need to ask. It was right there in Barry's eyes. "So you're leaving it alone."

Barry's jaw tightened. "I'm not that stupid."

They drifted down in silence. Baldr set him on a ridge of black stone overlooking the corpse sea. The wind settled back into its constant scrape, and Barry rubbed his throat once before dropping his hand.

He didn't want to play this card, but he had no other choice. So he offered the only leverage he had left.

"Odin is dying."

Baldr's eyes twitched for half a second, but his expression didn't change. "Huh..."

"Yggdrasil is consuming him. Golden tissue growing through the muscle, merging with the tree itself—"

"You lie. Anyone can say that." Baldr hadn't moved. "The Allfather has outlived worse than a tree. Give me one thing you couldn't have made up."

"The gold under his skin." Barry answered without a pause. "It pulses, and not with his heartbeat. I timed it. Same rhythm and the same resonance as the tree."

Baldr stared at him. His skepticism held for several seconds, then cracked under the detail.

"Cognitive changes," Barry said. "He isn't just getting weak, Baldr. He's clearly being overwritten."

Barry kept going: the rate of tissue conversion, the progressive loss of independent decision-making, the way the Allfather's remaining will had started to echo the tree's own patterns. Baldr's shoulders shifted once.

"If what you're saying is true, and that's a very big if, how long?"

"I don't know."

Baldr was quiet for longer this time. Then came the sharper question. "Why tell me?"

Barry met his eyes. "Because I need something from you, Baldr, and you're the only one who can help me."

It was a pure transaction, and Baldr knew it. Helping Barry would bring him more news, more knowledge of Asgard. But even so.

"No."

"He's your father, isn't he? Don't you want to save him?"

For a moment there was only the wind.

"And?" Baldr's voice stayed flat. "If Odin is dying, then Odin is dying. You keep talking about fixing things as if everything that breaks wants to be repaired. That old man should have died in the final wars."

The words landed harder than any blow. It was a way of looking at the world Barry had never seriously considered, and his frustration boiled over before he could weigh the cost.

"You've spent centuries hiding in a graveyard because you're too afraid to go back to the life you used to have. Is that it?"

Baldr moved. The punch was short and precise, and it carried the full weight of a god who'd had enough. It cracked against Barry's jaw and threw him sideways into the stone. Pain flared white. Blood flooded his mouth. He stayed down on one knee, breathing through it.

"Ow! Fuck," he groaned. "Come on, aren't you supposed to be the god of joy or something?"

The wind changed at once. The constant scrape of ash stopped for a heartbeat. The dead along the shoreline halted mid-step. The corpse ship on the black sea drifted to a stop, sails frozen. Even the colossal vulture on its distant column ceased beating its wings, and nothing moved at all.

"Duck," Baldr said, shoving the bleeding mortal down just before the giant captain's glowing red eye swept across the spot where they'd been standing.

Baldr looked at Barry, his voice low. "You shouldn't have spoken of certain things. Do you know if any of the creatures that roam Helheim heard you?"

Barry spat blood and answered through his broken jaw. "I didn't say her name."

Baldr's gaze moved past him toward the horizon. "You didn't need to."

The ground began to shake, lightly at first. Both of them scrambled to their feet as the tremors grew stronger. At the edge of the sky, the vulture lowered its head and covered itself with its giant wings. Out on the sea, the giants began to bow, one by one.

Above them all, the gray sky crackled with green thunder.

"What's happening?" Barry asked.

"She's angry."

"Who?"

"My niece," Baldr said. "The queen of Helheim. Hela."
