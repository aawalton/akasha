“Now? I’m kind of—”

“NOW.”

The word cracked through the clearing like a snapped bone. It wasn’t loud in the way shouting is loud. It was commanding. The kind of sound that doesn’t argue with your ears, it just informs them. Mana shuddered. Even the bioluminescent growths dimmed for a breath, as if the forest itself had flinched.

“The rest of you,” Ratatosk continued, voice grinding low and brutal, “report that this Otherrealm is a boundary point. Ergo, no citizens live inside. While it isn’t mine, I’ve been given authority to use it.”

I turned fully toward him then, and gods above and below, he was worse up close.

Ratatosk wasn’t merely large. He was excessive. Built like the violent idea of a man rather than a man himself. His body was wrapped in layer after layer of pelts—wolf, bear, something reptilian I didn’t recognize—stitched together with sinew, wire, and bone. Skulls hung from his shoulders and belt, some cleanly picked, others still bearing cracks from how they’d been taken. Antlers jutted up and back like a crown ripped from a forest god’s corpse. His chest was corded muscle under leather scarred by claws and blades alike, every mark telling the same story: he survived, and others did not.

He smelled like blood that had soaked into dirt and never washed out. Like smoke clinging to fur. Like a predator that had learned patience.

And his eyes—his eyes weren’t wild. That was the worst part. They were focused. Calculating. The gaze of something that knew exactly how many ways it could kill you, and was calmly deciding which lesson would hurt the most.

Everyone else felt it too. I could tell without looking. Fallias pressed closer to my side, tense and ready, his grip tight enough to ground me. Fractal’s feathers prickled and shimmered, her illusions twitching reflexively before she forcibly stilled them. V’s hand slipped halfway into his pocket, fingers already ghosting the tools he’d need if this went to hell. Cordelia didn’t step back, but her posture shifted, subtle and defensive, like a chessboard reconfiguring itself under threat. Ten… Ten stood rigid, jaw clenched, legs braced, like she was one bad word away from launching herself at a goddamn mountain.

Sadrin, of course, looked pleased. That infuriating, knowing smile never left his face.

Ratatosk rolled his shoulders once, pelts sliding against each other with the sound of distant thunder. He loomed. He didn’t need to move closer to feel oppressive; the space bent around him regardless.

And something in me finally fucking snapped.

“Listen,” I said, my voice cutting through the tension sharper than I expected. My heart was pounding, but I didn’t slow down. Didn’t soften it. Didn’t dress it up. “I don’t know about you, but I’m fucking tired.”

Ratatosk’s brow twitched. Interest, maybe.

“I am fucking tired of people stronger than me deciding they get to pull my strings. Tired of Domini. Tired of gods. Tired of fucking heralds and watchers and whatever cosmic assholes think they get to poke at my life like it’s a goddamn experiment.”

The air felt thinner with every word. I could feel Lumivis stirring faintly in my core, tense, uncertain, ready to interject and already knowing better than to do so.

“So unless your plan is to break me,” I continued, stepping forward despite every instinct screaming that it was a stupid idea, “burn me down, and toss what’s left into the dirt—”

I met Ratatosk’s eyes head-on.

“Then fuck off.”

Silence slammed down like a dropped gate.

For a moment, I thought I’d just signed my own death warrant.

Ratatosk stared at me. Really stared. The kind of stare that strips away excuses, pretenses, titles. The kind of stare that weighs marrow.

Then, slowly, something changed.

His mouth pulled into a grin—wide, sharp, and feral. Not mockery. Approval.

“Good,” he rumbled, voice thick with something dangerously close to satisfaction. “There it is.”

Sadrin inhaled softly, amused. The others didn’t move. Couldn’t.

Ratatosk stepped closer, close enough that I had to tilt my head back to keep eye contact. I could feel the heat radiating off him, the sheer physicality of a creature that lived by the rule of dominance and survival.

“You think the Wild breaks those who submit?” he said. “No. It breaks those who pretend.”

He leaned down just enough that his shadow swallowed me whole.

“If you’d bowed,” he continued quietly, “I would have crushed you. If you’d begged, I would have burned you out of mercy.”

His grin widened.

“But you told me to fuck off.”

The forest breathed again, slowly.

“You told me,” Ratatosk said, his voice no longer thunderous, but absolute, “to fuck off. I outpower you physically. Magically. Spiritually. By all bars.”

He leaned closer, and the world bent around his silhouette. “So, Alex,” he finished, almost gently, “it’s time for you to wake up.”

***

When consciousness returned, it did not come softly.

It slammed into me like a door kicked open.

Paper. That was the first thing I noticed. The smell of it. Old paper. New paper. Ink that had never quite dried. The sound of pages whispering against one another without wind. I opened my eyes to a ceiling that wasn’t a ceiling at all, but an endless vault of bookshelves curving inward like ribs. Hundreds of thousands of volumes hovered, stacked, suspended, drifting in slow, lazy orbits as though gravity had been replaced by indecision.

My head throbbed.

My body felt wrong. Not injured, exactly. More like it had been paused and restarted poorly.

Only four people stood near me.

Gin was the only one I recognized immediately. Same lazy posture. Same infuriatingly calm expression, like the universe itself had just told a bad joke and he was savoring it.

The others blurred until my eyes focused.

“I told her,” Gin said, exhaling through his nose, arms crossed. “You and Fallias together would complete her tasks. I also told her it wouldn’t end well.”

“Shut up, Gin,” snapped a thin, lanky man in a pale coat that looked less like clothing and more like a concession to decency. His fingers flew across a floating slate of glyphs and numbers. “Patient A7121-DA. Consciousness returning. Logging event.”

That designation made my stomach twist.

Before I could say anything, the lanky man was bodily shoved aside.

A woman stepped into my field of vision. She looked my age. Or close enough to be unsettling. Scales shimmered faintly along her neck and jawline, catching the ambient light like oil on water. Her eyes were sharp, alert, and very much awake.

“Alex,” she said, gripping my shoulders firmly. Not gently, but not cruelly either. “What do you remember? What do you know?”

Her touch grounded me.

I swallowed. My mouth was dry. “I was…” I forced the memories into order. “In a nexus. A convergence. Or something like that. A forest. My team got separated. A big brutish guy tried to bully me and—”

Gin snorted, then outright laughed.

“No,” he said, shaking his head. “That brute can’t do that.”

I frowned at him. “Excuse me?”

“Demeterra lies,” Gin continued easily, as if explaining the weather. “And she lies well. But she still has to borrow from reality.”

I stared at him, blank and unamused. My head was still ringing. “Gin,” I said slowly, “if this is one of your riddles, I swear—”

“Okay, okay,” he interrupted, holding up his hands. “Let’s explain before you pop a blood vessel.”

The floating books subtly rearranged themselves, drawing closer, as though leaning in.

“Demeterra,” Gin began, “is the Dominus of Earth and Law. That part you know. What most people don’t know is what else she is.”

The scaled woman’s jaw tightened.

“She is also an Archmage of Illusions,” Gin continued. “Not cheap tricks. Not glamours. Laws-as-illusions. Her domain doesn’t just enforce reality. It rewrites perception.”

I felt a cold thread slide down my spine.

“Every person within her domain,” Gin said, tapping his temple, “is granted an illusion tailored to them. A life they believe is real. Rules they accept without question. Stories they live inside.”

The thin man muttered something under his breath and continued typing.

“And the price?” I asked quietly.

Gin smiled without humor. “Access.”

The word landed heavy.

“For every person living inside her illusions,” he went on, “Demeterra gains access to their Artes. Their mana affinities. Their potential expressions of power.”

My breath caught.

“And Star mana,” the scaled woman said, picking up the thread, “isn’t just another affinity.”

I nodded slowly, dread pooling in my chest. “It’s a fusion furnace.”

Gin pointed at me. “Exactly. Infinite input. Infinite conversion. Infinite permission.”

The room felt smaller.

“So,” Gin said, voice softer now, “when you saw Ratatosk, when you saw Sadrin, when you saw your friends tested and separated and challenged—”

“It wasn’t just a trial,” the woman finished. “It was a diagnostic.”

I squeezed my eyes shut.

All of it replayed at once. Cordelia’s debate. V’s hunt. Fractal’s gauntlet. Fallias’s isolation. Ten bleeding and unbowed. And me. Always me at the center, pretending I was choosing, pretending I was leading.

Demeterra’s illusion hadn’t just shown me who I was.

It had shown her what I could become.

“And the brute?” I asked, voice hollow.

Gin shrugged. “A construct. A metaphor with teeth. Something she thought you’d either submit to…or break yourself against.”

“And instead,” the scaled woman said quietly, “you told him to fuck off.”

Silence stretched.

I laughed once. It came out wrong. Thin. Tired.

“Great,” I muttered. “So what now? I wake up? Pretend none of it mattered?”

Gin’s smile faded entirely.

“No,” he said. “Now you get to decide whether you’re still willing to live inside someone else’s story.”

I looked around at the impossible library. At the hovering books. At the people watching me like I was both a patient and a weapon.

“And if I don’t?” I asked.

Gin met my eyes. “Then you stop being an asset… and start being a problem.”

I leaned back, rubbing my face with both hands. I sighed. I sighed deeply. Because somewhere, buried beneath the exhaustion and anger and revelation, one truth burned brighter than the rest. I hadn’t woken up.

I’d just realized how long I’d been asleep.***

Seeing Patient Duarte awaken was… nice.

That felt like an inadequate word, even as it formed in my thoughts. Nice was what you said when tea was warm or when a book ended without tragedy. This was heavier than that. Denser. Seeing Alex’s eyes finally focus, watching the fog of Demeterra’s layered dreams fracture and peel away, was like watching someone surface from deep water without realizing how close they’d come to drowning.

I had been trying to wake him for a long time.

Longer than anyone else in the room realized.

From their perspective, this intervention was recent. A calculated maneuver. A risky negotiation. Gin’s cleverness, Danatallion’s grudging permission, the sudden insertion of a “foreign variable” into a sealed illusion.

From mine? It had been years.

Not years as humans counted them. Years as dragons do. Slow. Stretched. Measured not by days but by cycles of memory, by how many times you watch the same soul repeat the same mistake from different angles.

At first, I was only an observer.

That was the role assigned to me. A stabilizing presence. A sympathetic anchor inside Demeterra’s dream lattice. Someone whose emotional resonance aligned closely enough with Alexander Duarte to keep his psyche from fracturing under the weight of recursive law-illusions.

I was not meant to interfere.

I was meant to witness.

The problem with witnessing, though, is that you see everything.

I saw him wake up in Demeterra’s illusion for the first time, blinking in confusion, already carrying more responsibility than anyone his age should have been allowed. I saw him accept titles he didn’t want because refusing them would have hurt someone else. I saw him smile through exhaustion, apologize for things that weren’t his fault, and shoulder burdens as if that was simply what people like him were supposed to do.

I watched him be shaped.

And slowly, against my better judgment, I stopped thinking of him as Patient Duarte.

He became Alex.

Not immediately. That change was subtle, insidious. The way he’d mutter to himself when he thought no one was listening. The way he treated his Machina like a person rather than a tool. The way his mana reacted not to threat, but to care. Star mana wasn’t just destructive or infinite. It was connective. It wanted to bind, not burn.

That should have frightened me.

Instead, it made me protective.

I tried to keep my distance. Truly, I did. I reminded myself that I was older. Much older. That dragons aged differently, yes, but four times his lifespan was not a trivial gap, no matter how immature humans liked to be about such things. I told myself that emotional attachment compromised objectivity, that this was unethical, that Danatallion would never have allowed me this close if he’d known.

Then Demeterra escalated her illusions.

The dreams stopped being passive narratives and started becoming tests. Stressors. Controlled failures designed to extract more data, more mana compatibility, more leverage. Alex began to suffer in ways that no amount of passive observation could justify.

So I started pushing.

Small things at first. A comforting presence in a dream that should have been empty. A voice reminding him to breathe. A moment of warmth where there should have been cold. Each intervention cost me. Demeterra’s illusions are self-correcting. They notice anomalies.

Every time I helped him, I felt her attention drift closer.

It wasn’t until Gin noticed that something was wrong.

He came to me quietly, the way he always does when he’s pretending not to care. Asked questions that weren’t really questions. Noticed patterns he hadn’t before.

“You’re invested,” he said eventually.

“I’m assigned,” I replied.

He stared at me for a long moment, then sighed. “Those aren’t the same thing.”

That was when he told me his plan.

Bartering with the real Dominus Danatallion was not something one did lightly. Danatallion does not give permission. He gives conditions. And one of those conditions was that if I entered fully, if I stopped being a background resonance and became an active participant, then whatever happened next would be on me.

On my conscience.

On my heart.

I said yes anyway.

That was the moment it stopped being theoretical.

Being inside Alex’s dreamscape like that, unfiltered, was… dangerous. Demeterra’s illusions were elaborate, layered constructions of law and expectation, but underneath them was a very human mind struggling to reconcile who he was with who everyone wanted him to be.

I saw his fear up close.

Not fear of death. Fear of failure. Fear that if he stopped moving, stopped leading, stopped being useful, the people he loved would disappear. That he would be abandoned. Forgotten.

It hurt to see.

It hurt worse to realize how badly I wanted to be someone he could lean on.

I didn’t plan to fall for him.

That’s the lie people always tell, isn’t it? That love is an accident. A slip. Something that happens to you. The truth is uglier. Love is a series of decisions you don’t stop in time.

I chose to stay longer than I had to.

I chose to speak when silence would have been safer.

I chose to let him see me, not just the role I played.

By the time I realized what I’d done, it was already too late.

Watching him wake now, in this impossible library of suspended knowledge, my chest ached with relief and something far more dangerous.

Hope.

He looked smaller out here. Not weaker. Just… unarmored. Without the weight of Demeterra’s expectations pressing him into a shape that served her. His confusion was raw, unfiltered. When he sighed, deeply, like the world had finally caught up with him, I had to resist the urge to step forward and take his hand.

I didn’t.

Not yet.

Gin was talking. Explaining. Always explaining. The thin man was logging data, treating Alex like a specimen even now. The scaled woman, sharp and protective, hovered close enough to intervene if he faltered.

And me?

I stood back.

Because now came the hardest part.

Awakening someone is one thing. Staying with them afterward is another.

I didn’t know what Alex would do once he understood the truth. Whether he’d rage, retreat, or burn everything down out of spite. I didn’t know if he’d forgive me for being there so long without telling him everything.

I didn’t know if he’d want me anymore once the illusion stripped away the version of me Demeterra had allowed him to see.

But as he rubbed his face, exhausted and angry and very much alive, I felt something settle in my chest.

Whatever happened next, it would be real.

No dream-law. No scripted trials. No borrowed metaphors wearing the skins of monsters.

Just him.

Just me.

And the consequences of choosing each other in a world that would absolutely try to tear that choice apart.

For a dragon, falling in love with a human is supposed to be foolish. Brief. A spark compared to a lifetime of embers.

But watching Alex breathe, watching him wake, I realized something quietly, dangerously certain.

I wasn’t thinking in sparks anymore.

I was thinking in vows.
