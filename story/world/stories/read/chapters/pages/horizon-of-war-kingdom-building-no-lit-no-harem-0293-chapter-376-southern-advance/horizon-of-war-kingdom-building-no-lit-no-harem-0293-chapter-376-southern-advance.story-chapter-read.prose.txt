Chapter 376

Southern Advance

Plains of Edessa

Amid the scorching heat, the cornu blared across the plains. The column of 6,700 men-at-arms stirred back to life. Men gathered their gear, packed and lashed it to their rucksacks, drank from their waterskins, and pulled makeshift cloth coverings over their heads against the sun, leaving their helmets secured to their breastplates. Pikemen shouldered their long pikes as commands to advance passed down the line. The column spread across the open plains and gradually wheeled south.

The change of direction surprised many, but discipline came first. They maneuvered now and asked questions later.

Behind them, the 1,900 horsemen remained in place for a time as ordered. They would form the rear and flank screen, taking the burden from the exhausted rear guard.

The carriages and carts of the camp followers and refugees followed the army south. There was no road to follow, only open country, but with news of Derenza’s fall and rumors of the Lord’s return spreading among them, they pressed on. Fortunately, the summer heat had baked the ground hard enough for carts and carriages to cross the plains without too much trouble.

As they marched, word of the Lord of Korelia’s return continued to spread through the ranks, feeding both disbelief and excitement. While some remained skeptical, thousands felt a surge of relief and renewed hope.

Thousands were thrilled. The Lord was well loved, but more than that, he had become a legendary figure. His victories, his inventions, his leadership, the peace he had brought, and the undead he had fought drew thousands of pilgrims to Korelia each year. A fortunate few were even allowed to visit his resting place in the White Chamber.

Those chosen visitors returned with the same account: the Lord’s body had not decayed. That gave further credence to the belief that he was blessed, something more than an ordinary man. Their testimony kept hope alive that one day he might return.

And now, he had.

Here on this dusty plains, surrounded by hostile forces and hunted by both the Empire and the Margrave, at a place where many had already begun to lose hope, the Lord had returned to the land of the living. For men who had long yearned for his leadership, the joy was difficult to contain.

Word soon spread from the half-kin Ocelot that only days had passed since his awakening.

The news struck them even harder. Many felt their chests swell with immense gratitude. The Lord had not forgotten his men. He had barely risen from his long sleep, yet he had come straight to join them in hostile territory. To many, it seemed almost as if the Ancients themselves had sent him back at the hour they needed him most.

That made the column march with renewed confidence.

With lively talk passing from rank to rank, even the march seemed a little lighter.

But not everyone was so easily convinced. Skeptics remained among the column.

Slowly, as the euphoria faded, the scorching heat, low-hanging orange dust, and sweat dragged them back to the harsh reality of the march. Within an hour, questions began spreading through the ranks.

“Why did they sound the cornu? Was the signal wrong?” asked a man farther back in the column.

“Yes, that’s strange,” another answered. “Advancing where, exactly?”

“This isn’t the road to Corna,” a young man remarked, unwilling to be left out.

His friend was quick to reply, “Derenza has fallen, you idiot. We can’t keep marching west.”

“We’re moving south, away from any possible ambush,” their sergeant, leader of the fifty-man group, explained, trying to calm them.

“But why south?” another asked.

Questions and murmurs spread through the ranks.

Elsewhere in the column, the discussion had turned to the Lord of Korelia himself.

“Is the Lord truly back?” one man asked those marching beside him.

“A man up front said he saw him,” one replied.

A skeptic beside him snorted. “Or someone who looks enough like him to stop men from deserting.”

That drew immediate argument. Everyone knew hair and beards could be blackened with dye. Expensive, perhaps, but hardly impossible.

“But the officers recognized him,” another man pointed out. “Sir Harold wouldn’t make that mistake.”

“That proves nothing,” the skeptic replied. “They could all be part of it.”

A few men grumbled at that, but the doubts did not disappear.

Then someone farther along the line raised a question that quieted those around him. “What if it really is the Lord... but those two years changed him?”

That was harder to dismiss. Nobody had ever heard of a man sleeping for nearly two years and waking again.

Soon, darker whispers began passing through the ranks. It was common knowledge that the Lord had been cursed by a lich. Now, some quietly wondered whether the curse had changed him into something unnatural. A fell creature.

Those who knew what had happened at the Monastery feared something darker still. What if he meant to lead them into disaster, just as the Saint had once sacrificed others in his attempt to become a lich?

Then someone challenged the thought. “If he meant to sacrifice us, why bother with all this? Why not just send us west toward Derenza and Sirmona?”

That quieted the men around him.

Exhaustion and wariness had a way of making men imagine the worst. Even so, speculation continued to spread through the column, and many still questioned what the Lord truly intended.

As they marched farther south, deeper into the vast open plains that increasingly resembled their own Great Plains of Lowlandia, fear and doubt continued to fester like untreated wounds. Farther ahead, they could see the looming mountains that separated Edessa from Salceslia.

Two hours into the march, the sun had begun to drift west. A cooler wind now swept across the plains, but the sting of the sun remained every bit as harsh.

Behind them, a great cloud of dust rose into the sky. Their horsemen were returning.

Scouts and patrols became more active as well. Some rode toward the vanguard, likely to make their reports.

The men marched on warily. Troubled speculation passed through the ranks that the enemy had finally found them.

They were right. Before long, word spread that their scouts had spotted pursuers.

“How many?” one sergeant asked a senior sergeant who had just returned from the command staff.

“I heard at least several hundred horsemen.”

“That’s not many. Our cavalry can take them.”

“No.” The senior sergeant shook his head. “More will come. Now that they’ve found us, they’ll shadow us from a distance and wait for their main force to catch up.”

Many of the men around them swallowed hard.

“How many men do you think the Margrave has?” the younger sergeant asked.

“If they took Derenza that easily, I’d say fifteen to twenty thousand, with plenty of horsemen among them,” the older man answered.

“They fielded that many last year too,” another man added.

“And they’ve got the nomad tribes with them as well,” a third remarked.

Suddenly, trumpets rang out across the column. Lieutenants rode hard toward their companies, and the speculation ended at once.

“Assume tercio formation!” one lieutenant howled as he rode up, relaying the order from the commander.

The sergeants immediately took up the cry, shouting orders down the ranks.

“This is it. This is it,” one man muttered as the loose marching column began drawing inward, men reforming into dense square formations.

“To battle,” his friend answered, eager despite the weariness in his voice.

“Crossbowmen!” the lieutenant shouted again. “Ready your quivers. Make every shot count. Don’t waste your bolts!”

Along the forming ranks, crossbowmen checked their strings and release mechanisms, while others adjusted the quivers hanging at their hips or shoulders. Pikemen shifted their pikes upright and closed the gaps in the line as the formation took shape.

As the tercios continued to form, a messenger came riding hard from the vanguard and pulled up beside the lieutenant.

What he heard made the lieutenant stare. “What? Say that again.”

That drew the attention of everyone nearby.

“The Lord said to halt and rest,” the messenger repeated.

Men traded uneasy glances.

“Is what you’re saying correct?” the lieutenant asked, still seeking confirmation.

Already expecting the doubt, the messenger continued, “He has ordered the dragoons and tribesmen to guard our rear and flanks.”

Amid the murmuring, he added more firmly, “He also ordered that the refugees be brought inside the formation.”

“Did the Lord truly say that?” The lieutenant was still incredulous. “And what did the knight commander say?”

“He only nodded,” the messenger replied.

Troubled looks passed between the men, but moments later the trumpets sounded again, this time with the signal to halt. There was nothing left to do but obey.

All they could do was hope that the man who had returned to them was truly the Lord of Korelia they remembered.

Plains North of Derenza

Amid the low-hanging dust, six hundred Edessan cavalry rode across the plains. They formed a mobile brigade drawn from Calissia, and within its ranks rode Mihal, son of the Margrave, dressed like any ordinary light cavalryman. Bruises marked his face, partly hidden beneath his hood and a length of cloth wrapped across his mouth and nose against the dust.

His plan had saved their House, and half of Edessa, yet the old man was still furious. He had beaten Mihal for the humiliation he had endured after being branded a traitor to the Imperium.

Ironically, his father had not only known about the plan, but had wholeheartedly agreed with it. He hated the usurper Lansius and the abhorrent league of lords he had named the Shogunate.

If his father had not consented, nothing would have been done. His grip over the domain was absolute. The reason for the beating was simpler. The old man could not stomach that it had been Mihal who arranged the favorable deal with the Empire.

The beating itself hardly bothered Mihal. Compared with what he had endured as a child, his father’s fists two days ago had almost felt like a gesture of affection.

Though he was the Margrave’s legitimate son, born of his lawful wife, his father had never truly liked him.

As the cavalry crossed the empty plains in a seemingly endless ride, Mihal’s thoughts drifted further into the past. The old man had always preferred his firstborn bastard son, who had eventually died drunk. That half-brother had been boisterous, tall, and square-jawed, resembling their father far more than Mihal ever had. In those days, when Mihal was still known as Michael, he was smaller in frame, reserved by nature, and more inclined toward books and thought than reckless bravado.

As soon as Michael had been old enough to ride, his mother’s family and their allies had helped him escape his father’s grasp. He spent years roaming the realm, staying with minor lords, baronets, and knights as a guest at their tables, all under the respectable guise of receiving an education and being made more manly.

He continued that life until his father summoned him home for lack of another heir. Though the old man remained physically strong, age had caught up with him, and he had failed to sire another son. There had only been a sickly bastard daughter by another mistress, and Michael’s mother had been more than happy to make certain the girl never reached adulthood.

Michael returned, but he kept the relationships he had built among the nobles of the realm. Among them were Navalnian agents who had long sought to secure the favor of Edessa’s future lord.

Those ties later proved far more important than either side had expected. Despite last year's invasion and the open hostilities, Michael maintained backchannels to certain high lords in Navalnia. Earlier that year, after the Empire’s disastrous retreat, they contacted him to discuss a truce, and eventually, a peace treaty.

The Empire would keep what it had already taken, along with a route for further expansion westward and northward into Midlandia and Northern Midlandia. In return, the Margrave would retain his remaining lands and be recognized as one of the Empire’s high lords.

The same arrangement also saw Michael take the Navalnian name Mihal, as he was recognized as a Bey, meaning lord or vassal commander. This mattered, as he would also be expected to marry into a Navalnian noble House, perhaps even take more than one wife. He had been told that many powerful families saw Edessa as a valuable foothold toward the heartland of the Imperium.

Mihal was nearly thirty. He loved his wife, but Edessa’s inclusion in the Empire was greater than either of them. He might even have to annul the marriage. She had borne him two daughters, but no son. With millions already dead in the conflict, the suffering of his own family, or the bruises on his face, seemed insignificant by comparison.

“Mihal Bey,” his lieutenant called as he rode up beside him, a wooden box strapped to his back. From it rose a long, flexible bronze stem, thin enough to sway with the horse’s movement, with small feathers fixed near the tip. Even the trusted officer was still not entirely accustomed to using Mihal’s new name.

“Speak,” Mihal replied, loosening the cloth wrapped across his mouth and nose against the dust.

“A message just came,” he said in a hushed voice. No messenger had approached their column, yet none of the officers nearby seemed surprised. The Margrave’s House had been entrusted with such items. “They’ve spotted the Shogunate force.”

“Our men are in Derenza, so the Shogunate must already be near the city. They’re moving quickly—”

“No, Mihal Bey. They’re heading south.”

“South?” Mihal frowned. The officers around him exchanged surprised looks.

One of the knights, an old mentor of his, spoke first. “Could they know our forces are in Derenza and be trying to evade us?”

“The message thinks the same,” the lieutenant replied.

“How about Ostaria?” his mentor asked.

“We hear they’re still under siege.”

“Not abandoned, I see,” his mentor muttered, brushing orange dust from his beard.

“Do you want us to attack from the other side?” another officer asked Mihal directly.

“No need,” Mihal answered.

“Ostaria isn’t ours?” the officer asked.

“It can be, but I don’t want to race the Empire for it. They’ll brood if they lose out. Sadly, it would be unwise to insult our new overlords,” Mihal jested.

His officers snorted and chuckled.

Mihal was glad to be surrounded by loyal men who could respect his vision. Originally, many had opposed his plan to save Edessa. Some called it a betrayal of the Imperium, but after a swift purge that saw three baronets and twenty-two esteemed knights removed from command, there were no more dissenting voices in his father’s court.

He glanced at his friend, Buran. Recently knighted, the man was only a few months younger than Mihal, but broad-chested and strong-shouldered, with a fierce face to match. Mihal had been introduced to Buran years ago. Many dismissed him for his below-average height, but what he lacked in stature, he more than made up for in boldness and strength.

The younger man had first served as his page, then later as his squire. They had not always gotten along, and that was precisely what made Buran valuable. He was loyal, but he never hesitated to speak his mind. He was not as educated as the others, but he trusted his instincts like a hunter. While other young nobles learned to recite poetry, Buran hunted for food.

Mihal looked to him. Buran answered with a shrug.

That was enough.

Mihal came to a decision. “We have spare horses. We ride straight on. Send a fast rider to Sirmona and a message to Derenza. Tell them to send their columns and riders ahead of us.”

The 6,000 men who had taken Derenza should still be in good condition. The city had fallen through subterfuge, with infiltrators opening the gates from within and sparing his force the need to assault the walls. Another 9,000 men remained at Sirmona, along with 1,400 horsemen and roughly 3,000 Edessan nomads loyal to his father.

“At once.” The lieutenant veered out of the column and rode off.

Mihal looked to the officers around him. Aside from his old knightly mentor, several were veterans who had served his father for years. One by one, they nodded. There was no objection.

The Shogunate’s desperate retreat was plain enough. Their men might be well trained and well equipped, but exhaustion would catch them eventually. Trapped deep inside Edessa, their fate was as good as sealed.

Lansius

There were still three hours before sundown, but the column had already halted . The tercio formed a hollow square, thick with pikemen on all sides, with four separate mobile groups attached to the formation. Much to the disbelief of his officers, the Lord had also allowed several thousand camp followers and refugees to enter the square.

As half of their 1,900 dragoons chased after several hundred enemy riders, the column was ordered to march a little farther. It was not because of any enemy movement, but because the scouts had found a better place to camp near a farming village, where a narrow creek ran through the fields.

Lansius chose a stretch where the grass grew thinner, reducing the danger of fire. Nearby, several low rises offered better ground for observation and for setting up the command post.

Despite the village’s proximity, they did not move into it, fearing that doing so would break up the formation. Instead, selected groups were allowed to search the village and the nearby woods for food, fodder, and firewood. Harming the villagers was strictly forbidden, and the two bailiffs accompanied the parties to keep order.

As expected, the village was empty. Yet the wells had not been ruined, nor had the vegetable patches been destroyed. That told them something important. The Margrave had not expected the Shogunate to march this way. The villagers had likely fled only when they saw the army approaching.

Lansius ordered the bailiffs to leave a fair amount of coin tucked into the beams of every house from which food or fodder had been taken, payment for whatever the army used.

Once the column settled into its new position, the troops were allowed to rest and take turns drawing water from the creek. Washing was limited to a single bucket per person, lest the shallow stream run dry.

Before long, a work party had raised a simple field tent on one of the low mounds to serve as the command post.

Now, Lansius sat on a folding canvas chair, listening as the officers gave their reports and received their orders. Ocelot remained close by, while his personal guards rested outside. They would need to be awake and ready should an attack come during the night.

“Have the dragoons and tribesmen keep watch on our flanks, but don’t let them commit too heavily. The column can defend itself,” Sir Stefan finished.

The officers saluted and filed out.

The field tent quickly fell quiet around Lansius, Sir Harold, Sir Stefan, and Ocelot.

“Sir Stefan,” Lansius called.

The vice turned and approached. “My Lord?”

“Once it’s safe to do so, let the men light fires and cook whatever food they have.”

“Cook?” Sir Stefan blurted, not expecting such an order with the enemy lurking nearby.

“Yes,” Lansius confirmed. “Let them cook if they have anything worth cooking. Make hot gruel from the hard biscuit, or at least warm their bread over the fire.”

“But, My Lord...” Sir Stefan wiped the sweat from his face with a small cloth. “The enemy is nearby.”

“They’re only a few hundred riders. What can they do?” Lansius replied. “Our horsemen still outnumber them several times over. For now, we have little to fear. Besides, they won’t dare camp too close. They’ll keep their distance for fear of our cavalry.”

Sir Stefan did not agree at once. He glanced toward Sir Harold, who stood beside Ocelot. Sir Harold gave a firm nod.

“Understood,” Sir Stefan acknowledged.

“Also,” Lansius added, “have the men pitch their tents.”

Sir Stefan swallowed dryly. Halting here felt far too dangerous. “While I understand that the men need rest, wouldn’t it be more prudent to put as much distance as possible between us and the enemy while we still can?”

Lansius snorted and asked, “Do you think the Margrave will risk a night battle after chasing us through a long night march?”

Sir Stefan began to see the situation a bit more clearly.

“The Lord is right,” Sir Harold agreed. “Throwing exhausted men into battle after marching through the night would be suicidal even for the Margrave.”

“Moreover,” Lansius added, “they already have the advantage over us. In their eyes, victory is all but guaranteed. There’s no reason for them to gamble on a night attack. They’ll play it safe.”

Lansius looked at Sir Stefan again. “You look exhausted. That night march must have taken its toll on you.”

Sir Stefan inhaled deeply, but his troubled expression remained.

The young Elandian physician quietly entered the tent carrying Lansius’ medicine. Ocelot took the cup, sniffed the contents, then handed it to Lansius, allowing the physician to withdraw without a word.

Lansius drank from the bitter herbal concoction before turning back to the wary vice. “Listen to my counsel. You’re afraid of the Margrave’s army. But the truth is, the Margrave’s army is still made of men. They march on two feet like everyone else. Do you understand what I’m saying?”

The vice’s eyes widened as the simple truth finally struck him.

“Just because they’re chasing you doesn’t mean they suddenly grew wings on their feet and can march faster than us.”

Even Sir Harold nodded at that and admitted, “We’ve rarely had to fight a retreating action. Our inexperience is showing.”

Lansius glanced at him, pleased by his banneret’s humility. “The nearest threat is at Derenza. We were a few hours from the city, and then we marched three hours south before their scouts found us. Their riders still need time to carry the news back. After that, their commanders have to gather their men, prepare their column, and march after us.”

Lansius looked past the lines of men toward the north. “I’d say that even if they force-marched through the night, we would still have half a day’s advantage. But I doubt they’ll risk it, knowing we might lay an ambush.”

“But what about their horsemen and nomads? They might still catch us,” the vice warned after a brief moment of thought.

“I’m aware of the threat,” Lansius said. “But our tercios carry pikes specifically meant to deal with them.”

The vice was not easily convinced. “Marching in formation will slow us. Their main force could catch up.”

“That is a risk I’m willing to take,” Lansius said. He shifted in his seat before continuing. “Put that aside for a moment. Knowing your enemy is important, but first you need to know the strength of your own men. Right now, all I see are exhausted soldiers barely able to keep their eyes open. I’m sure you know better than I do that they need plenty of rest.”

Sir Stefan said nothing for a moment before finally giving a reluctant nod. “Very well, My Lord. I shall relay the order.”

As Sir Stefan motioned to the guard outside, about to summon his captain, Lansius spoke again.

“Tell everyone to treasure this sleep. It may be the only proper rest they get for days.” He glanced toward the open plains. “Tomorrow will be a different game.”

As Sir Stefan saluted and left the tent to carry out the Lord’s wishes, Sir Harold took a folding canvas stool and sat before him. Leaning forward, he asked, “My Lord, are you against night marches?”

“I appreciate that you marched on foot. It means you know the exhaustion of your men. Still, I could argue that most of your baggage and heavier gear were carried on the horses. But that is beside the point.” Lansius paused to finish the herbal concoction, then drank some water to wash down the bitter taste.

“What matters more,” he continued, “is that marching at night exhausts the men without much gain. Even with lanterns and torches, people naturally stumble and move more slowly in the dark, and in the end may cover only half the distance they could manage by daylight. They’ll be sluggish and sleepy for days after a single night march. I cannot afford to march them through two nights in a row, not in hostile territory. That would be suicidal.”

The banneret stroked his chin, now covered by a beard. “The Edessan nomads and their archery would be disastrous for us.”

Lansius snorted.

That caught Sir Harold’s attention, but Lansius waved it off. “It’s nothing. I was just thinking that we both look proper now. More like commanders of men than we did years ago.”

Sir Harold’s lips drew into a thin smile. Lansius now wore a thin beard and mustache as well. Back then, both of them had usually been clean-shaven.

“How is your family? Clementine, was it?” Lansius asked.

“Yes. We had a baby daughter. And...” The banneret cleared his throat. “You may also need to know that I took a concubine.”

Lansius blinked twice but withheld judgment. “That is entirely your affair. I can only give you my blessing. Is she from a good family?”

“She is Emma, the former Saint Candidate. I believe you’ve met her. She had a great deal weighing on her, and with the other former Saint Candidates needing protection, she came to Clementine asking for help. She believed that marrying into my House would keep her sisters at the Monastery from being unjustly persecuted. In return, she gathered the sisters and brought them into our ranks as healers, and even mages.”

Lansius exhaled and lowered his gaze for a moment, considering it. Then he nodded twice. “Never mix politics with romance. Still, I have to admit, it was a good move. We need the sisters’ loyalty.” A faint smile touched his face. “And you are fortunate. I remember her being as beautiful as a flower, even among the sisters. Be sure to make both of them happy.”

The words pleased him. “She is here, My Lord. Would you like to see her?”

Lansius’ eyes sharpened. “Here?”

“She joined the medical group. Clementine refused to let me leave without a healer at my side.”

Lansius settled back more comfortably and chuckled. “It seems I’m not the only one who has to contend with a shield-maiden for a wife.”

The two men laughed freely. Ocelot nodded along with them, clearly amused.

Beyond the open tent, the troops had settled into their early rest without breaking formation. Many sat or lay where they had halted, while small groups were called forward in turn to draw fresh water and refill their waterskins. Elsewhere, fires were being prepared and tents raised for the night. The promise of sleep had put some spirit back into the weary column.

Meanwhile, the camp followers gladly set out their wares and began preparing food to sell to the troops and refugees alike. Given the chance, children ran about within the formation, playing and giggling. For those who saw them, it offered a brief respite, a small contrast to the threat looming beyond the lines.

Sir Stefan entered with two officers in tow.

“My Lord, these men are in charge of the provisions. They say they can offer only bread, cheese, carrot pottage, and pickles tonight.”

“That is plenty,” Lansius replied. “Tell them that by tomorrow night, we’ll have a feast.”

His answer surprised everyone, even Ocelot.

Sir Harold was the first to ask, “Is the Lord of Dawn’s airship arriving tomorrow?”

“No, he’s occupied with Lord Jorge.” Lansius shook his head. “The feast. The enemy will provide it for us.”

Then he laughed.

The officers exchanged puzzled looks. But they knew their Lord well. There was a good chance he meant exactly what he said.

Slowly, careful grins spread among them. Edessa was about to witness one of the Black Lord’s schemes.
