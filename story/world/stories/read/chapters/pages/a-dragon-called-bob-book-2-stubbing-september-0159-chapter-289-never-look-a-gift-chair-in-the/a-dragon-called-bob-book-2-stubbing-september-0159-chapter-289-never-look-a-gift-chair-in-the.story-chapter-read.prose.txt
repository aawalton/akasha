“There’s got to be some good ‘two newts and an axolotl walked into a bar’ jokes, but for the life of me I can’t think of any,” I whispered.

It had been a long swim, and my frogman team had been disappointing;  no frogs, for a start.  Blurp and Glump were newtmen, and had provided my initial means of transport.  That was until the tails I’d been holding onto detached and started thrashing around in the water.

My initial panic gave way to wry amusement as they both confirmed it was fine and they’d regrow the appendages over a week or so.  By that point we had been just outside the harbour, speaking in quick gasps between waves that kept submerging us.  My ability to hold my breath for half an hour or so was limited to my glorious dragon form, so the mammal suit had been struggling.

“Shh,” Nolixyl hissed.  She had produced three bottles of an ointment after we slipped ashore in a dark corner of the wharfs, and passed one to each of her subordinates before beginning to anoint herself with the contents of the jar she kept.

She was combing the greasy liquid through her gill fronds, and as she did so, they began to fade from visibility.  The newts began slathering the stuff over their slimy skin, and fading out like oily Predators.

“You don’t need to do that.  I’m good from here.”  My damp towel went back into storage, and I began pulling out drab grey clothes and the heavy black cloak I’d borrowed.

“Not gonna miss this,” Glump muttered, his tongue stretching out to lick his left eye and smooth out the ointment.

“Your stealth potion has one obvious weakness,” I observed.  Nolixyl had finished her head and shoulders, and everything above her collarbones was invisible, bar her large orange-pupilled eyes.  “And mouths.  You guys are the creepiest Cheshire Cats I’ve ever seen.”

“Slime Team Six is at your disposal for this mission.  She who swims in foam made that clear to us.  Our lives are yours,” Nolixyl whispered as she worked on her chest and torso.

“Great,” I lied.  “It should be a simple in and out.  Well, an in, drop off a bunch of bombs, then portal out.”

“We have other options available to us.  Gragfish juice to go in water systems and block them, Varlspine venom to poison wells and food stores we can’t destroy, Oriballis’s Ooze of Obviation to mess up magitech enchantments—”

“We aren’t going to poison any bloody wells!” I hissed.

“It is standard practice on any infiltration mission.  Harming a besieged city’s infrastructure is a vital element in destabilising the defenders' morale.”

“Nolixyl, we are not poisoning wells.  Except… do you think we can get into the Imperial Palace?”

“Course we can.  Whether we can get out again is a different matter,” Blurp said cheerfully.  Death in the line of duty apparently not phasing the insane newt at all.  I reconsidered my earlier complaint about the team.

Was it worth it?  The Immortals, the Emperor’s finest troops, were all barracked in the palace.  They were high-level, tough bastards.  Weakening them might actually be worth it.  But there was that damn spymaster to worry about.  She knew everything that happened in the palace, and I didn’t fancy my chances of sneaking in undetected.  Besides which, most of the palace’s inhabitants were servants, functionaries, and menials; not soldiers.  It was also the size of a small town, so finding the right water sources to poison might be tricky.

“I’ll bear it in mind.  For now, let’s go have a chat with the Thieves and get the lay of the land.”

With the bulk of my sparkles suppressed and the ever-present smog of the city, we didn’t stand out too much.  The six eyes, two orange and four blue, floating along behind me were the most remarkable thing about our little troupe.  Slime Team Six knew to shut their eyes whenever anyone got too close, and they kept close to the walls, staying out of the way of the handful of people out on the streets.

You could tell things were bad when the ladies of the night and pickpockets had largely packed up shop in the dockside areas.  The city felt tense and fearful.  People moved quickly, eyes down and shoulders hunched.  The murmurs of conversation I caught through open windows or the occasional group on the street were all about the fleet that lurked on the horizon.

Prices had already jumped for simple things like bread; as usual, the people to suffer first were the poor.  Mothers soothed upset children and tried to explain why the portions were smaller today than yesterday.  As a being who tended to eat one very large meal every month or so, I developed yet another layer of appreciation for the beauty of being a scaly tank of a lizard.

Vanity swelled, but Regret also became more prominent in my internal menagerie.  Greed teamed up with Regret to take Vanity down a peg, then put the boot in once the idealised version of myself was on the ground.  Every civilian who died was one less future taxpayer after all.  My priorities were all in order.

The stench of tanners filled the air as we approached Watling Street; the amphibikin complained quietly due to their ability to smell through their skin.  It was like being bathed in foulness for them.  I simply kept my mouth shut until I reached a familiar, plain oak door and knocked.

“Fuck off, we’re closed.”

“Open up, Alad,” I said, being careful not to inhale with my mouth.

“Sign?”

“The moon laughs at the squirrel, and the janglebonk lows in the night,” I ground out.

“That’s an old password.  Fuck off spy.”

“It’s me.”  I raised my hood a little so he could see my face.

“Never met you before.”

“Ah, my bad.”  My face flowed back to its normal appearance.  “Remember?  The dragy-onnay guy?”

“Shapeshifter.  Could be anyone.  Piss off.”

The downgrade in vulgarity he employed in his order for us to depart suggested I was making some headway.  I did mentally tag him as edible, though.  Officious guards got the teeth.

“The second sign was about barnacles and shadowcats.  You know you made out like a bandit after the heist at the Lucratis bank, for basically doing naff all,” I muttered, moving closer to the hatch in the door.  “I could make it worth your while just to have a quick chat, but we can’t do it in the street.  Off the books, you wouldn’t need to give the Guild its cut.”

As painful as it had been to even lie about paying for information, his eyes narrowed then filled with a look I recognised only too well.  If Alad had a mental menagerie, his Greed Goblin had just taken over.

“Make it quick,” he hissed as the hatch shut and bolts slid aside.

He tried to swing the door closed behind me, but my slimy ninjas barged it back open and moved in before closing it themselves.  The orange eyes blinked down at the cowering thief while the blue pairs floated up the walls and swept upstairs.

“Clear left,” Blurp called.

“Clear right,” Glump answered.

“What the—”

“They’re friends.  Can we sit for a minute?”  I asked.  I sat down on the stool that was his usual resting place.  A little too hard, unfortunately, as it cracked under my weight and I nearly fell.  “Oh, sorry.  Here.”  I produced two chairs from my pocket dimension, one simple wood, one padded with velvet cushions.  I offered him the fancier one.  “You can keep that.”  It still hurt, but it wasn’t actually gold, which was some small consolation.

He cautiously lowered himself into his seat and immediately relaxed.

“Comfy, right?” I asked.

“Very.  Never seemed right that they never gave me better accommodation.  You’re still a scaly bastard, though.”

“That I am.  I need to know about the granaries.”

“My dad used to take me there as a kid.  He was a great thief, my old man.  And a drunk and a womaniser.  Dirty old git couldn’t keep his hands to himself.”

“Do you mean the thieving or the ladies?”  Philandering was reprehensible; if it were nonconsensual touchy-feely stuff, that would get a man a fast track down my gullet.

“Both?  He used to seduce noblewomen.  Played the rakish soldier of fortune and poet until he got into their bedrooms, then he took what he wanted.”

“Just their stuff?”

“Yeah.  I mean he’d do the deed first, then when they were asleep he’d rob them blind.  I was always a disappointment; I got my mum's looks,” he said morosely.

“While this is all very interesting and your mother must have been a handsome woman—”

“Then when I was fifteen, he put me up for the Guild.  Six months later, an irate husband caught him and split him from shoulder to hip.  Me mum died when I was seven, so I was an orphan, and the guild became my only family.”  A single tear traced a line down his cheek, leaving a clean streak on his grubby skin.

The demon-fueled truth chairs were amazing devices, but getting people to stop talking about their childhood trauma once they sat down was a major drawback.

“So you know a lot about the granaries?” I prompted and prayed.

“Yeah.  The bulk of them are underground, in an expanded space.  One of those fancy places that’s way bigger on the inside than it looks.  Four points of entrance, all heavily guarded, plus the loading bays and the Cornmarket.  That’s where they sell the stuff.”

“How did you get in?”

“Dad used to—”

“How would you get in now, if you wanted to?”

“This chair is sooo comfy.”  He leaned back and relaxed, all the tension flowing out of his body.  “I wouldn’t and I don’t want to.  With the siege, anyone caught stealing food is dying ugly.  Not worth the risk.  It’s part of the deal anyway.  The Guild doesn’t steal food during city emergencies, and we don’t get hunted too hard the rest of the time.”

“But if you needed to for other reasons.  Like someone you loved had been kidnapped and was being held inside?”

“No one loves me, and I don’t love anyone,” he said, his voice breaking with emotion.

“But if you did… or if there wasn’t a siege and someone had hidden some gold or something in there.”

“Oh, that would be different.”  He suddenly cheered back up.  “The Cornmarket has a lot of entrances.  They’re all guarded, but there is a crack in the enchantment.  Old Prenderghast was a bit paranoid; whenever he did some work, he left a backdoor.  Something that he could use to slip in and out if he needed to.  I know the one for the Cornmarket.  One of the last things my dad taught me…” Another tear trickled down his face.

“And what is it?” I prompted.

“The very last thing he taught me was never to trust a woman.  Either they cheat on you, or they die, like ma.”

It took an effort of will not to eat him, but I controlled myself.  “What was the chink in the enchantment?”

“You need a chicken feather, three litres of baby lotion, a three-foot stick of solid hardwood, and a drop of human blood.  There’s a corner on the riverside of the building; you can get to it if you slip through the gap between the market and the stables, then turn left.  What you do is you tie the feather to the stick- doesn’t matter what with- then dip the feather in the oil.  Oh, you’ve gotta put the blood in the lotion as well.  Then if you slap four bricks in the right order, and redip between each slap, a secret portal will open to a closet inside the market.”

“Which bricks and in what order do they need to be slapped?”  I asked with a broad smile.
