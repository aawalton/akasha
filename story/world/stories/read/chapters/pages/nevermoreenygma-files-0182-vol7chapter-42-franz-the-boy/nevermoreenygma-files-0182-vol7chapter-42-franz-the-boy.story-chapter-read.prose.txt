Chapter 42

Franz, The Boy

It had been weeks.

News had already started to forget few weeks later.

The news had changed topics. But Mai still woke up hearing the ringing in her ears.

She hadn’t gone back to the place of the disaster. Kii insisted she rest, but Mai couldn’t stand being indoors for too long. The silence in the house made her stomach twist.

So she walked.

One afternoon, she ended up in Tetsugakudo Park again with its stone philosophers that looked like they were really tired of thinking. The last weeks of summer air was still heavy, sticky. Cicadas screamed like they were announcing the end of the world.

Mai sat on a bench, drinking cold canned coffee, trying not to think. That was when she noticed them.

Three figures in black, moving with the kind of precision only bodyguards have. They weren’t pretending to blend in. Suits, gloves, earpieces. Foreigners, probably. Who on earth could wear gloves in that heat?

They stopped a few meters away. The tallest one spoke in English.

“Mai Kanzaki?”

Who? Kanzaki had been one of the false identities she sometimes used for work. It was necessary at times, and the registry offices had her registered under that name. It was also the identity she had used during the Shinjuku disaster.

“I'm sorry. Izumi. Mai Izumi?”

Her pulse spiked. She stood up slowly.

“Depends who’s asking,” she said.

The man’s hand went to his jacket. Mai reacted instantly —faster than she thought she could. She twisted aside, grabbed her bag, and the blade inside it sang halfway out of its sheath.

Then someone spoke behind her. A voice too calm for the situation.

“Please, stop. They’re with me. I told you guys  it's  her.”

Mai turned.

A boy stood near the path’s entrance.

Looked like fourteen, maybe fifteen years old.

His slightly round face. Pale, perfectly dressed in white, hair combed in a style reminiscent of that still worn by some gangsters in the red-light district, but with Western features. His eyes were gray —not cold, but old. In some way he looked like a photograph that had stepped out of its frame.

The guards immediately lowered their weapons. The tall one bowed his head slightly.

Mai didn’t move.

The boy smiled, just enough to look human.

“Miss Izumi. I apologize for the sudden approach. I suppose you remember me?”

She blinked. Of course she knew him.

The boy from the conference. The one she’d carried out of the debris.

“You’re the kid from the hotel, Franz Rackhenhell,” she said.

His face seemed to twitch when she said “kid.” Mai understood. Franz Rackhenhell was much older than her. “ Boy” was just a nickname because of his round face.

“Yes,” he replied. “And I’m alive thanks to you.”

He walked closer. The air around him felt colder. Not dangerously so —just unnatural, like the temperature dropped out of respect. He stopped two steps away.

“I wanted to thank you personally. You risked your life that day.”

“I didn’t exactly have time to think, I wasn't the only one who helped,” Mai said.

“I know. But that’s precisely why it matters. You lost someone important that day too, and still you helped me. My deepest condolences.”

For a moment, they just looked at each other. Franz had that way of staring that vampires sometimes did —like he was reading through you, not at you. But there was something different in his expression. Guilt, sadness maybe. Or curiosity.

Mai nodded and bow. “I give you my condolences too.”

“Would you walk with me, just few minutes?” he asked finally.

“Now?”

“It’s safer than it looks,” he said, with a faint, ironic smile. “My guards are discreet... Usually.”

Mai looked at the vampire gorillas and hesitated, then sighed and sheathed the blade.

“Fine. But if one of them tries anything weird, I’ll put them in the fountain.”

Franz almost smiled. “Understood.”

They began walking down the stone path, the sound of cicadas swallowing the city noise.

For the first time in weeks, Mai felt something close to normal—and that scared her more than the men in black.

They walked along the park’s central path, beneath the trees that filtered the fading light of sunset. The guards followed at a respectful distance, though Mai knew exactly where each one was.

Franz strolled without hurry, his hands clasped behind his back—the posture of someone who had forgotten what it meant to be a child.

“I must admit,” he said after a long silence, “I’m a little ashamed I didn’t do this sooner.”

“Do what?” Mai asked.

“Thank you. Or at least… speak with you.”

The boy’s gaze drifted up toward the branches.

“Things within the family are… complicated. Since the incident, no one trusts anyone. I suppose you’ve heard something.”

Mai nodded slowly. “I understand. You’re not the only ones.”

Franz glanced at her from the corner of his eye, more intrigued by her tone than her words, but he didn’t press further. Up close, Mai studied him with more attention. Beyond the legends surrounding vampires, the Franz she had seen on the day of the disaster had seemed healthier than the one standing beside her now. Maybe it was the short distance between them, but he looked exhausted—far paler than before.

They walked a few more steps until the sound of an artificial stream reached them. Franz stopped before one of the stone bridges.

“Even so,” he continued, “I feel I owe you an explanation. At least the little we know.”

Mai crossed her arms. “Go on.”

“The investigators from the Council and our own specialists all agreed on one thing: what happened wasn’t a conventional attack. It was… a ritual. A ritual for a curse.”

The word hung between them.

A curse.

“A ritual?” Mai repeated.

“Yes. The magical analysis and the bomb-disposal teams found a high concentration of iron in the air, the walls—even in the water used to extinguish the fire. But the iron didn’t come from any metallic device. It came from… blood.”

Mai stared at him without answering.

“They used blood to create the explosion,” Franz went on with an unnatural calm. “The blood of everyone present the moment the ritual was triggered.”

For an instant, even the cicadas seemed to fall silent.

Mai remembered the hotel.

The heavy air.

The sticky feeling on the floor.

The impossible sight: blood rising from the bodies, lifting as if given a will of its own.

A chill prickled down her spine.

“I saw it,” she whispered. “Right before everything collapsed… the blood was going up. As if the air was calling it.”

“Exactly. The residual energies confirm that the detonator was an invocation sealed with a biological component. Something that only activates when sacrifice is involved.”

Mai frowned.

“Sacrifice?”

“Yes. One that extends to everyone sharing the same living space during the ritual. It didn’t matter if they were vampires or humans. We were all linked to the same matrix.”

Could that be why she and Miho, standing outside the trigger zone, had avoided falling into the same state as the guards?

Franz leaned against the stone railing. For the first time, he seemed his age: a child who had seen far too much.

“We don’t know who initiated it… yet,” he added. “But we’re certain someone infiltrated the site before the meeting. The guards’ blood was the first to react. From that moment on… there was no turning back.”

Mai folded her arms again, watching the current slide beneath the bridge.

“So… it was planned from the inside?”

Franz lowered his voice. “There are signs that part of the security personnel belonged to an unknown group. A seal found among the debris bore the emblem of a circle divided into eight sections.”

Mai looked at him.

“Octogonus.”

The boy nodded slowly. “It could be—but maybe not.”

Silence stretched out. The wind rustled the leaves, and one of them drifted down into the water, spinning until the current swallowed it.

“If that’s true,” Mai said at last, “then the attack wasn’t just against you or the occult world. It was against everyone.”

“Exactly. And that’s what worries my family the most. No one knows who the ritual truly served. Or what it awakened.”

Mai took a deep breath. She could still smell metal, even after weeks.

“You should be careful, Mr. Rackhenhell. If no one trusts anyone… someone must want it that way.”

The boy smiled—an expression far too serious for his face.

“In that ritual…” he began, “they summoned something that shouldn’t exist here. The oldest records only mention it as a legend. They called it the Star Vampire, or The Shambler.”

Mai frowned. “Vampire?”

“Not in the traditional sense,” Franz clarified with a slight tilt of his head. “According to the documents, it was an astral entity. It absorbed blood, yes, but not for nourishment… blood was its means of manifestation. The more it obtained, the more tangible it became.”

Mai listened without interrupting.

“The problem,” he continued, “is that to keep it under control, the summoner needed overwhelming spiritual strength. Otherwise… the creature would devour them.”

“And that’s what happened?” Mai asked.

“Probably.” He nodded. “But the strangest part is that no invocation core was detected. There wasn’t a single trace of the mage who controlled it.”

Mai leaned slightly toward him.

“How do you know all this?”

Franz opened the leather satchel at his side and drew out a small sealed envelope. He didn’t hand it to her—just held it between his fingers.

“Because the family’s analysts found clear evidence,” he said. “In the wounds of the dead, on the walls of the building, and in the rubble. Fragments of parchment, scraps of paper inscribed with the spell’s characters. It was an invocation designed to create a barrier to hold the creature long enough to act.”

“How exactly was it supposed to act?”

“By absorbing the victims’ blood. Once the cycle was complete, the barrier self-destructed and released all the accumulated energy. That’s why the explosion was so large.” Franz paused, lowering his gaze. “It was designed that way—to look like a bomb. What the analysts found wasn’t a conventional explosive, but something that could almost be explained through applied physics… mixed with the arcane. The creature acted as a catalyst: it absorbed the blood energy of those around it and concentrated everything inside its own body. The ‘rituals’ investigators discovered weren’t decorative symbols. They were formulas of containment and channeling. The invocation ritual is ancient, but the explosion is not. The entire operation is a hybrid ritual, like when a magic user performs a new type of magic. The old documents described how the victims’ blood and energy flows, the new part is that would be converted into a controlled shockwave. It wasn’t that the creature exploded by itself—rather, it released all the energy it had absorbed at once, like a chain reaction of magic and biology. That’s what gave the blast its extraordinary destructive effect—impossible to reproduce without both the creature and the precise rituals.”

Mai stayed quiet for a few seconds, understanding what that implied.

It wasn’t just a massacre—it was a demonstration.

“They were aiming for spectacle,” she murmured.

Franz lifted his eyes. “Exactly. They wanted the investigators to witness the impossible. To document it. To fear it. A message for those who know how to read between the lines.”

Mai studied him for a moment, her expression hardened.

“And do you know who could did it? Any suspect?”

Franz shook his head, but it wasn’t ignorance—it was caution.

“We know it was someone with access to forbidden texts. Someone who had the means and the strength to act without drawing suspicion.” He paused briefly, meeting her eyes. “But we still don’t know who. Or perhaps… someone doesn’t want us to. There are many suspicions, but I don't want to assume my suspicions until I have solid evidence.”

Mai didn’t reply. She simply watched him.

Franz’s face shifted. The calm he’d maintained cracked just slightly—and for a heartbeat, Mai thought she saw something in him that didn’t belong to a child: an old shadow.

“One of them…” he said at last, “was part of my family.”

Mai looked at him carefully, saying nothing. There was no pride or anger in his voice—only a controlled, cold sadness.

“He wasn’t the one who planned it.” Franz clenched his small fists, the black gloves creaking faintly. “He was just a puppet. Someone used him to channel the invocation. When it was over, they found him at his estate. Without a single drop of blood left.”

“And the hand behind it?” she asked.

Franz shook his head slowly.

“It still hasn’t been revealed. But I’m certain of one thing: whoever did this wasn’t motivated by politics or family rivalries only. It wasn’t for money, or economic power—not even revenge. It was a message.”

“A message? For who?”

“Yes.” The boy’s gaze hardened, fixed on something far beyond the trees. “A warning to the hidden world. A reminder that there are still forces capable of breaking any pact whenever they wish.”

Mai felt a chill despite the still air.

“That’s not the kind of warning someone makes on a whim.”

Franz nodded.

“No. Whoever did this wanted to be seen—and feared. Fear is the greatest weapon of all. They wanted everyone to know they can do it again, whenever they choose. And that no one can stop them.”

In that moment only the distant sound of the stream and the faint rustle of leaves could be heard.

“Someone capable of controlling a Star Vampire… isn’t someone you’d want to meet alone, right?”

Franz smiled, though there was no humor in it.

“Exactly. Not even the great families would dare attempt it. We are talking about people with enough power to summon entities from who knows where in space. The Star Vampire is an extraterrestrial entity, and there are many of them. They are leeches from somewhere in space, and in the past, a way was found to summon them.”

“Then…” Mai studied him with growing concern, “what are you going to do now?”

The boy looked at her with disconcerting calm.

“Wait,” he answered simply. “And watch. Some believe it’s over—that it was just a mystical accident, a minor catastrophe. But we know it isn’t.” His eyes shifted to her. “And you know it too, don’t you? You were there.”

Mai didn’t answer immediately.

She thought of the blood floating in the air that night, just before everything blew apart.Of Miho’s body crushed beneath the rubble.

Yes. She knew.

Things like that don’t fade.

Franz seemed to understand her silence and nodded once more.

“Thank you for what you did,” he said, softening his tone. “If it weren’t for you, I probably wouldn’t be here. I even went to see the police officer… I think I scared him a little.”

Mai let out a brief, tired smile.

“I was just in the wrong place.”

“Or maybe the right one,” he replied, eyes still fixed on her. “Sometimes fate disguises itself as coincidence.”

She huffed lightly.

“You talk like an old man.”

“I hear that a lot,” Franz said, shrugging.

Silence returned, this time less sharp. A flock of pigeons flew over the pond, and the fading sunlight cast a golden haze over the park’s walls. Franz took a step back. His bodyguards—who had kept their distance until now—finally approached.

“I must go,” the boy said. “But be careful, Miss Izumi. If you see something out of place again, don’t act on your own.”

Mai raised a brow.

“And what am I supposed to do? Call you?”

Franz allowed himself the faintest smile, as if the idea wasn’t entirely ridiculous.

“Perhaps,” he replied before turning slightly. “Build yourself a circle of people you trust. Ella and I tried to handle everything on our own.”

Mai looked at him. “Look where that left you. You were together, right?”

“Yes,” Franz said simply, and extended his hand. “It was a pleasure meeting you, even if the circumstances were less than ideal.”

“Likewise,” she said, shaking it.

“Miss Izumi?”

“Yes?”

“If I ever learn anything else, I’ll let you know.”

“Thank you.”

“Just… well. Among our kind, these matters tend to take a long time.”

“I know. I suppose that comes with living so long.”

Franz nodded. “Until next time, then.”

He turned away, and the men in black followed him in silence. Franz’s small figure gradually faded along the park’s winding paths, swallowed by the maple trees.

Mai stood still, staring at the spot where he had disappeared. The wind stirred, whispering softly as it swept through the fallen leaves.

She sighed.

She knew more than before, yes. Some pieces were falling into place—but what they formed wasn’t comfort. It was a warning. This was only the surface of something much larger, a fracture in the veil of what people were never meant to see.

For a moment, she thought of the creature. Its name. Star Vampire or The Shambler. And the idea that someone, somewhere, might summon it again.

The distant sound of a train cut across the city. The sky had already darkened, and the park was beginning to empty out.

“A wake-up call, huh?” she murmured to herself. “Looks like someone really wants us to listen.”

She headed back, knowing the calm she felt wasn’t peace at all, but the quiet pause before something else— maybe even something worse—happened again.
