Rarely did anyone come to the river, and so, I considered it part of my own domain. This was the only place in this new world where I could go for some quiet contemplation. The sun's shade broke over the many trees, and the river water that flowed here was cool on the skin. Yes, I quite liked this place. Perhaps even more than my spot by the low wall.

I sat cross legged at the bank and extended my left hand toward a patch of dead grass. "Bloom," I said. White light moved from my palm to the grass, and the dead grass began to straight and color. Before long, there was only a circle of green in the dirt.

And there was an impression, as there always was. It was the memory of rain, and of something that had only ever wanted to grow, and was thankful that I had let it.

Grass was simple. I had restored plenty of dead or dried grass before. The set of wilted flowers I found were harder. Flooding them with an aimless use of the Cradle could certainly revive them, but it cost far more mana than it did with the grass. It was far more effective to use my power more gently, with more precision.

The first flower straightened. The second followed. And before long the flowers were whole and unbothered, any memory of their withering long since erased. The impression that came was strange, or perhaps no stranger for some flowers. It was the soft warmth of sunlight peaking in through a canopy.

These memories of the living were getting clearer the more I used the mark, and I did not know if I liked that. No, I did know, and I did not like it.

That was two tests done. Next, I turned my attention to a dead tree sapling that lay between its healthier kin. The thing was thin and small, barely any taller than I was. The whole thing looked like it would crack under a stiff breeze. I extended my left hand, letting brilliant white light meet the dead wood.

Mana drained from me in a single sharp pull, and no small amount of it. I almost pulled back the Cradle entirely. The grey bark darkened, and a single leaf unfurled from one of its branches. The leaf was pale, and so thin I could almost see through it.If this was life, then it was the shortest step up from death.

My arms were shaking. I sat back on my heels and let my breathing steady. There was a purpose to this. I needed my mark to advance. There were stages even past the Line, and I wished to reach them. So far, the Line had not so much as budged. It had not grown dull either, like the Spark had eventually. Perhaps each stage took longer than the one that came before it?

The river moved past me. It glowed with the first light of the day. The wind blew through this place, and it was cold. My gaze fell to the other side of the river, to the line of trees. Something moved there. No, it was two somethings. They were small brown creatures. They darted along the floor, before climbing the trunk of one of the trees. I had not seen these beasts before.

Both of these strange creatures had brown fur, and were oddly round. Their tails moved behind them, almost as long as the rest of them. One of the two stopped climbing. It reached out with tiny hands and began to groom its companion. The other leaned into the touch.  I watched them for a very long time, trying to guess at the purpose behind this act. At the selfish motive. I tried, until I reluctantly acknowledged that there was none.

I would not have seen such a thing in the Demon Realm. There, every beast was a predator or prey, and most were both. Parents ate their children, and often the children grew strong enough to eat their parents. There were no beasts like these there.

Zera would have wept if she were here to see this.

Zera had been missing from her post for two hours.

I had noticed her absence at once, for I felt and saw everything in my fortress. Every stone was as much a part of me as my own flesh. And so when General Zera slipped back through a side passage in the lower eastern wing, I was waiting for her.

Zera was tall for a demon. She was only a foot shorter than Zarvok, and had a scar that ran from her jaw to her collarbone. Height aside, she bore every resemblance to any other woman.

Zera's arms were pressed against her chest, cradling something beneath her cloak. She glanced around everywhere, before she slowly stepped inside the halls of my fortress proper.

"Where have you been, General Zera?"

Zera froze for just a heart beat. She looked at me, and she had the nerve to look surprised. "Your Royal Darkness." She bowed quickly, so low her head almost reached the floor. But she did it awkwardly. She was trying not to jostle whatever she was holding. "I was conducting a perimeter survey of the molten core, and I was not aw-"

"Show me."

Zera's jaw tightened. I saw her consider telling me another lie. After a moment, she straightened her back and pulled back the cloak entirely, letting me see what she held. And what she was cradling was…a strange creature.

The thing in her arms had six legs, and four of them bent in strange directions that made no sense. The body itself was a dark brown, with strange circular patches of what might have been fur. The beast only had one eye, set in a face that had a mockery of a snout. When it breathed, the sound of it came out as a rasping wheeze. A line of dark drool hung from the edge of its maybe-snout. It was, without question, the most repulsive living creature I had ever beheld. It was also the only thing of its kind I had seen.

"Where did you find this?"

"I found it in the tunnels, my Queen." Zera answered quickly. Her voice was steady, her hands were not. "In the lowest of the lowest tunnels. I'm not sure how long it was there. There were others, but they were all dead. This was the only one of its kind left."

"And in your wisdom you brought it here, to my fortress." My voice was hardly above a whisper.

"Yes, my Queen. I did."

There was a plop as the creature's drool fell onto my floor. The drool was a dark purple, I saw that now. The creature's single eye slowly opened and closed, as if the effort of even seeing was too much for it. Just what was this strange monstrosity? Even I could admit to some morbid curiosity now.

“[Inspect]”, I said, arm raised towards the beast.

The System screen split the air in front of me. A quick glance told me that this beast had no proper title, but the System called it a [Sighmaw]. An appropriate name, as that was what I wished to do right now. My hand was still raised towards the strange beast.

I could use any one of the hundreds of spells I possessed. One thought, and this Sighmaw and its miserable, wheezing existence would be over.

Zera looked at my outstretched hand and her face had nothing but acceptance. Then her arms tightened, just slightly. I might not have noticed at all if I had not been watching her so closely. Perhaps it would not be tactically wrong to keep this beast alive. It might be the last of its kind, and even this beast might-

The pressure of the Class came, as it always did. I felt it grow in my skull, and within moments it was a pounding thing that almost made me flinch. My mana stirred without my bidding it to. It wanted me to erase this thing. Zera watched me, and something in her eyes was strange. Still, she said nothing.

"In the future, you will inform me first," I said, and the words came out through gritted teeth, "of these indulgences, General. You will inform me before you make them. Every single one. Is that understood?"

Zera's eyes widened. Then she bowed deeply, and this time her head did reach the floor. I turned, and left my General and the ugly stray she had brought to the halls of my beautiful fortress.

I blinked, and the memory faded. The brown creatures were nowhere to be seen. I looked for them, and found them again. They were much higher up on the same tree, until they almost reached the canopy. They were both hanging upside down from the same branch by their tails. Why they did this, even I could not say.

Zera would have known what they were called. She might have even known why they hung so. She had known the name of every beast in every realm, even the ones she had never seen. Even the ones she never would. She would have already climbed the tree, and might have joined them in this hanging had she a tail. The fool.

The Cradle pulsed along my left arm. I looked down at it and frowned. A moment later, I returned to my training. This little rest had already lasted far too long. Perhaps I would try one of the larger dead saplings next. Perhaps I would try something with Ruin.

My training in peaceful silence lasted all of four minutes.

"Lysanfia!"

I turned. Sara stood behind me, holding a stick. The girl was out of breath, which meant she had been running. Who would dare betray where I was to this girl? This space in the forest was close to the village, but surely not safe enough for a child to come alone. "Lysanfia is not my name."

"Hi, Lysanfia!"

"Ly-san-thi-a." I said each syllable with great care. Enough for even a foolish human child to understand.

Sara nodded seriously. It seemed she understood. "Lisanthia."

"No." She had not understood.

"Lissanthy-a?"

"That is further from correct than when you started. How is that even possible?"

I turned away from the girl. "Lys!" She said loudly. I looked over my shoulder and was ready to correct this error as well. This was the most egregious of all the errors by far. This was a shortening of my mighty name to a mere syllable. I closed my mouth and turned back to the river.

"What are you doing today?" Sara sat down beside me without invitation. The stick lay across her lap. "Naming more tech-niques?"

"No. I am training."

"Can I watch?"

"You are already watching."

"Can I watch moreee?" She said, leaning closer.

I did not know how one could watch a thing 'more', but I did not wish to find it. I ignored the girl and extended my left hand toward more dead grass. White fire left my palm, and within moments, the grass greened.

The girl clapped next to me. The sound was entirely too loud for a place I had chosen only because it was quiet. "Ooh! Do another one!"

"I am not performing for you, fool child. I am refining my technique against varied biological targets."

Sara pointed at a drooping flower by the bank. "That one!"

I looked at the flower, then at the girl. It was a reasonable test. Flowers were far different from grass, after all. I had done this test once already, but there was wisdom in confirming something already known. I extended my left and used Bloom again. The flower's stem straightened, and the petals filled with a deep red.

There was an impression, a faint one. One I could and did ignore. At least I was getting better at that.

Sara's eyes were wide. She looked around, and then ran a few paces beside the river. She stopped and then pointed at it. "Can you do the fish?"

I rose, stepped forward and followed her gaze to the shallows, where a small silver shape drifted against a stone. It was a fish. Dead and caught against the rock by the current, turning slowly.

Plants were one thing, an animal was quite another. Animals had minds and selves, and I had not yet tested the Cradle against one besides the Phoenix. This was a rather good test.

I rose and waded into the river. The water was cold against my legs, soaking the hem of the dress Martha had given me. I picked the fish up with both hands. It was small, and I felt it twitch faintly. The fish was still alive, but barely so. It would not be for long.

I bathed it in white fire. And then it pulled mana. It pulled so much mana from my core that I almost fell to my knees. And there was an impression, a strong one. One I couldn't ignore. There was cold water, the flash of silver scales in a fast current. There were other fish, as small as myself. Finally, there was the instinct to turn and flee from shadows larger than myself.

I blinked, and the impression went away. I looked down at the dead fish. Its tail twitched in my hands. The dull scales caught the light and for an instant they shone. Only then did I realize that I had dropped to my knees after all. This thing had drained far too much mana.

I set the fish back in the water. I expected the thing to begin moving again. It did not. For several long moments it did nothing at all, until the river water began to carry it downstream. My power had not worked. Something in my chest pulled tight.

Then I saw it. Its tail flicked, just once. The thing thrashed, and then it was moving. It turned against the current and darted away, vanishing into the deeper water.

"Oh," Sara said. It was barely a whisper.

My hands were shaking and I put them behind my back. I had taken a near-dead thing and had given it life. I, who had only brought endings, had given a thing a new beginning. It was not the first time. There was an egg that I had made sure was secure where I had left it.

I rose and waded back to the bank and sat down. The girl was still staring at the river, where the fish had gone. Perhaps she was tracking it.

"He liked the fish here. He s-said the silver ones were fastest." She said.

“Who?” The girl must have been speaking of her father.

“Marin.”

I said nothing. Anything I would have said would have done her little good. I returned to my tests. I chose to use grass, though I already knew how my power would work with them well enough.

A minute passed. The river moved. "He used to throw rocks with me," Sara said. "That was fun."

I had no answer for this either. I was doing more tests, but my focus was gone. For I could feel the question building in the girl. The way an army built outside the gates of a great castle. I knew what she was slowly circling towards. I could have stopped it. I did not.

The girl took a full minute to gather the courage for what she was about to ask. "Lys?" I did not correct her either. "Can you make Marin…bloom again?"

My left arm pulsed.  I stared at her for a long time, until she shifted under my gaze. Until she hunched in on herself and trembled. I had seen that look before, in retainers who had asked too much of their Queen and knew it. It had never looked like this.

I opened my mouth to answer.
