Eric spent the rest of the day alternating between dozing and checking to see if the gas station was still standing. At sunset, he was startled awake by the same automated voice that he heard at the door announce: “Closing for the night cycle.” Metal grates rattled down over the window and door, and when he jumped up and tried the doors—all of them, including the fire exit—not a single one gave. He was locked in.

Okay, what the hell? He wandered the gas station, looking for the speaker or whatever was operating the grates, but there was nothing in sight. “Hey, whoever’s doing that… come out, please? I just want to talk to someone about… all this.”

Nothing. He hadn’t expected anything, but he was disappointed anyways. After another few laps of the station just to be sure, he settled back in his spot somewhat hesitantly to wait the night away. He ate a little more, walked a few laps in the gas station to wear himself out, then went back to sleep, waiting for daylight to return.

He was a little shocked at how well he was adapting to this strange situation, but what else was there to do but adapt? The world had changed fundamentally. There was nothing left of its original form. Even if he broke down and gave up, it wouldn’t make the monsters disappear or the rest of humanity reappear, so he did the only thing he could, and kept truckin’.

I love truckin’, he thought sleep-drunkenly to himself, then chuckled. He’d considered getting real-drunk on the 31-DEGREE ICE COLD BEER in the refrigerated glass cases along the wall, but one, he wasn’t much of a beer drinker, and two, he didn’t think getting drunk in a confusing situation replete with monsters during the total breakdown of human society was the best idea. Once he had a handle on things, maybe then, but right now, it just felt like a good idea to be as sober as possible at all times. If one of those cockroaches got in here, drunk him would just get eaten. Sober him would at least be able to beat the thing’s head in before a thousand of its buddies tore him apart.

I’m really not sure if that’s better or worse.

He woke up to rhythmic beeping and the rattle of grates lifting from the exterior of the gas station. Eric sat up, his whole body sore from sleeping on hard tile. He’d spread t-shirts under him so he wasn’t sleeping directly on dirty hard tile, though coming face-to-face with trucker shirts emblazoned with problematic slogans first thing in the morning wasn’t his ideal awakening. The gas station smelled like gasoline, roller dogs, and—oddly—fresh coffee.

Beep. Beep.

He grimaced. What the hell is that infernal beeping? Body groaning and aching, very much displeased with sleeping on the hard tile, he pushed himself to his feet and staggered over toward the sound.

Cigarettes sat on the counter, aligned with the scanner so that every so often, the scanner registered them and beeped as it rang them up. A second identical box of cigarettes sat beside them, nudged just far enough that the scanner no longer registered their existence.

Eric blinked. Respawning cigarettes…? It’s too early for this. He nudged the second set of cigarettes out of the way of the scanner and stumbled over to the coffee machines. Even day-old, stale cold coffee was better than nothing.

He grabbed a cup and pulled the little lever, prepared for sludge, but instead, fresh hot coffee poured out, letting off delicious steam as it splashed in the bottom of the cup. He paused, releasing the lever, then leaned forward and took a sip. It wasn’t stale or disgusting. It was… well, it was gas station coffee, but it was at least fresh gas station coffee, hot and new. Eric frowned, sniffing it, then took a second sip. No, it’s definitely fresh.

Second box of cigarettes. Fresh coffee. He turned slowly, taking in the gas station. The dented shirt stand he’d destroyed yesterday stood proud once more, fresh and clean. There were no shirts missing from the rack, nor snacks missing from the aisles where he’d ransacked the long-lasting foods.

Am I going nuts, or is the gas station respawning all its contents? He looked at his coffee cup, then back at the coffee machine, then shrugged and went back to filling it up. “Am I going nuts” applied to so much of the last twenty-four hours that he didn’t even feel anything anymore. But there was fresh coffee, and he knew what to do with fresh coffee, so he took a sip and wandered the store yet again, marveling at all the things that’d been replaced.

And then a blue box popped up in front of his face.

Welcome to the System!

Many apologies, but due to your unique energy signature, you were not recognized as a sentient being on the System’s first sweep of your planet. Consequently, you were unable to receive any messages until this moment. If you can see this message, please choose yes.

YES NO

Unique energy signature…? He scanned the line again, then raised his brows. Not recognized as a sentient being…? He knew he was the team’s coffee gofer, but did that really disqualify him from being sentient?

A part of him very much wanted to choose NO, but he’d already been abandoned on the planet by something very strange which this appeared to be related to, so he clicked YES. Given the consequences associated with… whatever this was so far, he didn’t want to risk anything by being cute.

The standard Thank you for your acknowledgement! text box popped up, followed a moment later by a much longer message.

Dear denizen of Planet (designated 721.004), to our great regret, you were not recognized during our initial planetary sweep. We offer you a belated welcome to the System and the Great Game.

Win, and prove your planet is worthy of integration. The System’s benefits will be extended to every citizen of your planet. Lose, and your planet’s lifeforce is forfeit.

(The Great Game will be televised. Please look forward to the coverage!)

You may choose to participate in the Great Game as a Player or choose to forfeit your lifeforce to the System and become an Audience member. Players will be inducted to the Tutorial. Afterward, they will be loosed upon the newly built World Dungeon.

A box popped up as he read that part of the message. Unfortunately, you will not be able to enter the Tutorial. The Tutorial takes place in a pocket dimension that cannot be reopened once it is closed. If you choose to become a Player, you will not enter the Tutorial. You will have to immediately face the World Dungeon without any instruction.

“Sure,” he muttered. Is that what’d been happening this whole time? The setup of this… “World Dungeon?” That was why the cities, roads, and everything else had crumbled away? That was why he’d almost died to falling skyscrapers?

It was an answer, at least. After so long, just getting an answer, no matter how ridiculous and impossible, felt reassuring.

The popup faded, and the message continued.

Audience members forfeit their right to take part in the Great Game. Their lifeforce is considered staked on the outcome of the game, and in the case that no Players remain from your world, all Audience members will be terminated.

I don’t like the sound of that, Eric thought.

Another message popped up. It was getting annoying reading the letter around all the popups.

We are legally obligated to offer you the role of Audience member, and to fulfil your request to the greatest extent reasonably possible if you are to select this option.

“No, that’s okay,” he said. The idea of sitting back while the fate of the entire world, and his own fate, was decided by someone else wasn’t his idea of riding out the end of the world. If the world was over, he’d rather fight to survive than let someone else decide his survival. The no-Tutorial thing wasn’t great, but he was the kind to skip tutorials in video games anyways. He’d figure it out. Probably.

The popup vanished, allowing him to continue reading the letter. Audience participation is only recommended for the young, elderly, and infirm. We cannot emphasize enough that your world is at stake here.

“And who asked you to put it at stake, huh? It was doing… it wasn’t actively dying yet,” Eric quipped at the letter. It certainly wasn’t in the state of immediate death for the entire population, which was where this Great Game put it.

He got the feeling that whoever had written this letter did not care. If anything, they were probably just doing this for their amusement. Whether his world survived or not was immaterial to them. They just wanted a game to watch.

The letter continued.

Those who do well in the Game will be rewarded. Those who do not can expect punishment. The Game will be shown to your own population as well as that of worlds so far beyond yours as to be incomprehensibly advanced. If you perform well enough, you might receive prizes.

“Oh, neat.” He waited for more, for another popup or something, but that was it. The message faded away.

Eric looked around him, then clapped his hands. This was it, then? The World Dungeon. And for some reason, the gas station was part of it, but not his office building, apartment, or city. He still had more questions than answers, but at least the big ones had been answered: where was everyone else, why was he here, what was going on—all those had answers, now. Not satisfactory answers, and sometimes answers that outright insulted him, but at least they had answers.

This was the end of the world, and he was trapped in the World Dungeon all alone while everyone else got to practice in the tutorial, playing a self-described Great Game at the whims of he-didn’t-know-what. The letter hadn’t described the Great Game, per se, but it didn’t have to. Dungeon. Monsters. A “game.” Eric could put the rest together himself.

If that wasn’t enough, everything he knew was dust, and he was stuck in a gas station with killer cockroaches outside ready to eat him—and they were probably the weakest monsters around. Not to mention that if the Players lost the Great Game, all of humanity died, though from the sound of it, for them to lose the game, all the Players would have to die anyways, so it was mox nix on that one from his perspective as a Player. Also, he apparently didn’t qualify as sentient. He was pretty sure fish qualified as sentient, let alone the average human.

It was a lot to take in. He took a sip of his coffee and waited for it all to make sense, and when it didn’t, he shrugged and kept going.

“So… what now?”

As if in answer, new messages began popping up in the lower right of his vision, reminding him of old-fashioned achievements.

Found first safe room! Added to map.

First to find this safe room! Reward granted.

First kill! Reward granted.

Vehicle discovered! Reward granted.

“Reward… where?”

As he asked, a new window appeared before him. It was divided into tabs, including Status, Rewards, Inventory, and Map. There were a few other tabs, but they were grayed out for now.

Abruptly, one lit up. Class Selection, it read, and a message popped up before Eric’s eyes. As consolation for skipping the Tutorial, you may choose a class early.

“Oh. That’s nice.” He clicked on it out of sheer curiosity. Rather than taking him there, a popup appeared.

Congratulations for completing the Tutorial! Now that you’ve had a chance to try the common weapon and battle styles, it’s time to choose your class!

“Uh huh,” he muttered to himself as understanding came over him. This was less the reward the popup was selling it as, and more “this is what you get after the tutorial and we didn’t know how to give it to you, so here you go.” He also hadn’t had a chance to try fighting with anything but a short wooden club, so this offer was actually just a debuff. He shook his head. He was really playing the death game on Hard Mode, huh?

“I guess that’s what us non-sentients get.”

He dismissed the popup, then paused. Rather than go straight to choosing a class, he should really check his status first. He couldn’t pick a good class for himself unless he knew what he was working with. It was like Dungeons and Dragons. He’d make a poor wizard if he didn’t have any Int, and a terrible fighter if he lacked physical stats like Str or Dex.

Unfortunately, the stats weren’t the familiar D&D stats he was used to.

Still, it wasn’t impossible to figure out the stats. Strength, magic, stamina, dexterity, defense, and… mdf… He frowned, squinting at it.

A tool tip appeared. Magic Defense.

Oh. Made sense. He wasn’t happy about the 2 in it, but then, he wasn’t happy about a whole lot of his stats. The only one he liked was the one he didn’t know he had at all: magic. It was his highest stat, and not by a lot, but it was the only stat he had where he could round up to double-digits if he really squinted at it. He’d been working out. Doing cardio. Sure, he was an office worker, but he’d been doing his best. He hadn’t felt like he had a 4 in strength, but then, he didn’t know what kind of scale this System was using. Maybe 10 was the strongest man on earth. In that case, sure. Pasty office worker Eric could be a 4.

He took a deep breath. Enough feeling sorry for myself and my sorry stats. He clicked on the rewards menu. If stat-boosting items were a thing in this game, and he had some stat-boosting rewards, that could shape his class selection as well.

Once again, instead of entering the tab, he received a popup.

Rewards are tailored to classes, and will not be allotted until after Class Selection.

Makes sense, I guess. He was tempted to click Map just to see if it spawned a popup, too, but instead, he went to the Class Selection tab. A list of options appeared before him.

Fighter

Berserker

Rogue

Scout

…and so on and so forth. There was no obvious pick, no Dragon Sorcerous Warrior 9000 or God Tier Cheat Class, nor anything particularly exciting, just a list of basic classes that he might see in any RPG. For some reason, he felt a spark of disappointment, but quickly swallowed it. Any class was better than no class.

Taking in the list, he twisted his lips, considering. Given that there was no obvious pick, the question became: what did he want to be?

I don’t really want to be locked into close-range battle, he thought, remembering the swarming cockroaches. If he could kill something from afar, that was far superior than being right on top of it where he had to deal with the fallout of killing the monster. If bigger monsters had the same kind of built-in on-death trap that the cockroaches did, and he was a melee fighter, then he wouldn’t last very long out there. Especially not with 4 strength and stamina.

On the other hand, he realized abruptly, 4 strength and stamina was still enough for him to kill monsters. The weakest, basest monsters—literal cockroaches—but he wasn’t helpless. He might’ve missed the Tutorial, but he wasn’t utterly ruined. He was playing Hard Mode, but it wasn’t impossible. If he had to farm cockroaches, he could, even classless and level 1.

Okay. Melee is out. That leaves ranged units and casters. He didn’t really have to think long about that one. If he was picking between shooting a bow and shooting a fireball, well, there was only one correct answer, and it didn’t involve fletching. That, and aside from magic being awesome and literally something he’d dreamed about having his whole life, it was also versatile. A bow killed things efficiently from a distance. It was the hunting weapon of choice for centuries and a sought-after weapon in battle for a very good reason. He had absolutely nothing against bows, and in fact, a lot of respect for them. In a world without guns, bows reigned supreme.

But bows couldn’t start cookfires. Bows couldn’t create smokescreens, light up a dark cave, or raise the dead. Bows couldn’t teleport, heal, or change him into a wolf. So, for all those reasons, he was picking magic.

Which still left him with a wide array of choices. The System might have only offered him basic classes, but on the other hand, it offered him pretty much every basic class imaginable. He poked around, trying to get a feeling for the upsides and downsides of each, and eventually, divided them into three categories: Linked, Specialist, and Generalist.

“Linked” classes, as he categorized them, referred to the ones that were specifically linked to “deities” or “higher beings,” like Cleric or Warlock; he dismissed those outright. He didn’t know what the Great Game considered deities and higher beings, but he knew he didn’t want to start off enthralled to someone else. He’d gotten enough of that from corporate America; he didn’t want to immediately dive back in to having a boss in the apocalypse. Not having a boss was one of the few clear upsides to this somewhat shitty situation.

The “Specialist” mage classes, like Summoner or Necromancer, were specialized into a subclass of magic. He hadn’t been able to investigate particular subclasses before picking thanks to his lack of tutorial, so he shied away from those as well. As much as he loved necromancy—who didn’t?—there were games where necromancy was weak and a total mistake, and it was the same with any particular type of magic. Sure, sometimes particular subclasses were overpowered, but he lacked the knowledge for how each subclass worked, and that was the most important part: he didn’t know what was strong and what was weak in this System. Therefore, it was better to generalize.

It could be just as much of a mistake to generalize versus pick a particular subclass and lean in, regardless of how weak the subclass was; he knew that as well as anyone. Leaving all the buckets open sounded amazing until you were face-to-face with a level 99 fire mage who could summon a volcano on your face, while a level 99 generalist mage might only have access to Fireball. But right now, with an entire World Dungeon ahead of him and no one else to help, generalizing sounded pretty nice.

For example, there was Healer. All the System gave him pre-binding the class was this:

Healer

A class that allows you to heal. Gives access to healing spells. Passives are healing-oriented. Boosts MAG and STM on initial bind. High MAG growth, low physical growth.

With nothing but that to go off of, he couldn’t pick Healer and hope someone else picked a damage-dealing class, or hope that Healers had a way to dish out hurt, too. If he picked Healer and it could only heal other people, but not himself, and it had no way to damage monsters, then he was truly stuck with nothing but 4 strength and a wooden club. While something like Firemancer was a better bet, he didn’t know what laid ahead. He could very well find himself in a water dungeon or in a lava dungeon where fire wouldn’t do very much at all. It would be fine if he was in a party or had someone else to lean on, but he didn’t. Lighting cookfires, illuminating dungeons, opening doors, healing wounds, removing hazards and fighting monsters; he had all those duties. It was just him, and because it was just him, he had to generalize.

That’s fine. I like the idea of being able to do anything, anyways.

Generalizing left him with a few choices yet: Mage, Sorcerer, Runic, and Wizard. He quickly pulled up the different options to compare and contrast.

Mage

A class that grants access to magic. Can cast any spell, but class progression gives access to no spells; spells must be located in the environment. Passives boost magic. Boosts MAG and DEX on initial bind. High MAG growth, low STR growth.

Sorcerer

A class that allows one to use and boost one’s innate magical power through the System. Spells and passives are aligned with one’s innate element and alignment. Boosts MAG and STR on initial bind. Natural growths are boosted.

Runic

A class that grants access to runes, which allow you to cast spells in exchange for health instead of mana. Gives access to runic spells. Passives are health-oriented and give a larger HP pool. Boosts HP and MAG on initial bind. High HP growth, low defensive growths.

Wizard

A class that grants access to a spellbook. You can bind spells into your spellbook and use them so long as you have access to your book and can speak the chants. Passives are casting-speed oriented. Boosts MAG and MDF on initial bind. High MAG growth, low DEX growth.

Sorcerer was the obvious loser of the bunch. As far as he was aware, he didn’t have innate magical power, so he wouldn’t gain anything by picking it. A part of him wanted to pick it to find out if he did have innate magical power, but the rational part of him held the stupid part of him back.

He liked the idea of Runic from a distance, but he didn’t like it so much from close up. Using his HP to cast sounded cool, and he was sure it was cool in a video game, but in a survival scenario it was nothing but downright dangerous. The System could call it HP, but what it was, whether it was flesh, blood, pain, or something more metaphysical, was the line between life and death. If he used HP to cast, he was basically selling his life for power. Again, very cool, but probably also very painful, and if nothing else, definitely dangerous. The cockroaches could chew through steel. If one of them bit him, he wanted to have as much padding between him and death as possible.

Which left Wizard and Mage. The two felt like the most generalist classes of all the generalists from the start, and they still felt that way now that he’d narrowed it down to two. The big difference was that Wizard needed a book and a chant to cast, whereas Mage didn’t have those downsides, but in return, he started at actual 0. The improvement from nothing to Mage was that he gained more of a stat he couldn’t use (and DEX). The improvement from nothing to Wizard was magic. Spells. Fireballs and stuff like that.

On the other hand, the long arc of Mage was far more appealing to him. Wizard just had so much overhead baked in. Having to hoist a book around and read chants was less than ideal. What if he wanted to cast on a ladder, or somewhere else he needed to hold on with both hands? What if someone took his book, or he dropped it somewhere? Mage, meanwhile, just “gained access to every spell.” He could just cast things. No unwieldy book, no lengthy chanting, no obvious and glaring weakness that anyone could exploit by taking something from his bag while he slept.

He pinched his chin. Short-term suffering, or long-term, inescapable anxiety hell over losing his book? I can kill them. With nothing but the bat, I can kill those cockroaches. I can level up. Get strong enough to roam around and find spells. And I have my rewards. One of those could be a spell. They aren’t allotted until I pick a class; that implies they’re influenced by the class I pick.

Besides, nothing else promised access to every spell. Only Mage. Only Mage.

He took a deep breath. His stomach churned like he was about to make a mistake, but he was pretty sure it would do that no matter what he picked. Aloud, he said: “Mage. I choose mage.”

The options dissolved and the tab vanished. Golden light swirled around his body, twinkling like the stars, then melted into him. He lifted his hands, looking at them. He didn’t feel much different. Maybe… a little? He closed his fingers, then opened them. There was something there. Warmth he hadn’t felt before.

Or maybe I’m imagining it? He shrugged and pulled open his status. Choosing a class was supposed to give stats. If his stats had changed, he could at least confirm that something had happened.

It was time to get smashing.
