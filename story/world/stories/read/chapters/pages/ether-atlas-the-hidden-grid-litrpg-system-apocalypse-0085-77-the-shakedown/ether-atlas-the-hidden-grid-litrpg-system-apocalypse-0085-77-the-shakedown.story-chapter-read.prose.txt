Chapter 77

“Many institutions believe the day of their first extraction is their permanent foundation. They are wrong about this, every time. The candidate who has been taken from will return to the table, by a road the institution did not anticipate, with a question the institution cannot answer. The institution does not survive the second meeting.” — Codex of the Long Watch, Watcher’s Commentary

“That is convenient.”

“It is the procedure.”

“We brought cores onto Malta from outside. Several of the cores in that pouch came out of holes in the ground in another country, my friend. You have no claim on those under Article 1.” Steve said

Arthur gave a small smile at Steve’s use of “my friend”.

“My friend.” The Baron set his hands flat on the table. “When a traveller crosses into Compact territory carrying goods of value, the traveller is required to declare those goods at the point of entry. Goods that are not declared are, under the procedure, forfeit. That has always been the rule. That is the rule in every customs house I have ever walked into.”

“There was no Compact when we came onto Malta. We landed at Cosquer and we left for the Hypogeum six days ago. The Compact was not formed, by your own account, until four days ago. There was no point of entry to declare anything at.”

“You have been in the camp at Marsaxlokk for four days, my friend. The Compact has been administering the camp’s commerce for those four days. You have spent cores in the camp on food and supplies. Three of those cores went to Mifsud, who handed them up to the quartermaster’s office, which is a Compact office. One to a bread seller. You have been transacting inside Compact territory with undeclared goods for four days.”

“No one told us.”

The Baron lifted his cup, drank, and set it down again.

“My friend. Ignorance of the law is not a defence in any customs house I am aware of. It has never been. Nor will it be in this one.”

The room was very still.

Steve looked at the Baron for a long moment. Then he looked down at the schedule on the table between them, and back up. He had recognised something.

“This is the Dogana da Mar.”

The Baron’s smile was almost warm for the first time that morning, and he inclined his head.

“You read your Venetians, my friend. You are a man of culture.”

Steve turned in his chair, just slightly, so that what he said next reached everyone in the room.

“The Venetians built their sea customs house at the tip of the Dorsoduro, on a triangular spike of land that came out into the lagoon.”

He drew the shape on the table with one finger, the point of the triangle aimed across at the Baron.

“Every ship that came into Venice from the Mediterranean had to pass under it. The customs officers set the rate, recorded the cargo, and took what the Republic told them to take.”

His finger lifted from the table.

“There was no court of appeal. There was no panel that could overturn the decision. The procedure was the procedure, and the procedure was that the Dogana decided. You paid, or you had your ship seized.”

He glanced at Pia Borg, who was still writing, and then back at the Baron.

“The Republic ran the office on those terms for four hundred years. Most of the merchant ships of the Mediterranean paid into it at one point or another.”

He paused.

“Malta paid into it for most of them, my friend. Every grain ship out of Birgu, every cotton bale out of Gozo, every cargo the Knights moved through the channel. The Dogana set the rate and Malta paid.”

The Baron’s smile held.

“And now it is Malta’s turn. The Dogana da Mar paid for the Arsenale, and the Arsenale built the galleys that kept the lagoon free of corsairs for four centuries. We hope to do similar work.”

“With our cores.”

“With everyone’s, eventually. Legally, of course. Today the work begins with yours. You should see we are only interested in what we consider Malta resources. Your equipment for example, my friend is yours to keep.”

Arthur understood now what Steve was doing. Steve could not stop what was happening in the room. He could only refuse to let the Compact pretend it was anything other than what it was. Naming the Dogana da Mar was a small act of resistance, performed in front of Captain Azzopardi and Pia Borg and the fifteen guards along the wall, all of whom would carry forward what they had heard.

"And what happens when we refuse?" Arthur asked, straightening in his chair.

"As Mr Zammit has stated," Azzopardi said, joining the conversation with a tight nod. "There are other paths we could take. Refusal will be met with resistance. The Compact stands firmly behind the charter."

Donna glared across the table, her posture still. "You seem to have forgotten the lizard attack on your camp. It was us who saved your people, not your new friend."

"I have not," Azzopardi answered, though his eyelids twitched before he could calm his expression. "But the Compact is for the people of Malta. For the greater good."

"I think we are going around in circles," the Baron cut in. He leaned forward, his movements stiff. Setting his jaw, he moved his arm below the table. "I have no doubt that you are much stronger than we are in this room, and we are prepared to take that risk. You will either agree to the demands of the Compact, or you will show the people of Malta the threat of foreigners."

He paused for a moment before continuing. "And if you choose the latter, you will find the journey to the Hypogeum a difficult one. Even for you. The fighters of Malta have been told to be alert."

Arthur ran the figures in his head as he was sure Donna would already have.

The party could take the room. He had no doubt of that. Donna alone with her new technique could clear the side wall before the guards there understood what was happening, and Arthur and Steve and Sarah would handle the rest of it.

The harder fight was the courtyard and the antechamber. Harder still was the four hundred metres of Mdina street between the customs house and the gate, where the Compact would have prepared positions on the rooftops. None of it was impossible. All of it would cost lives, some Compact, some Maltese legionnaire who had done nothing to deserve being a casualty of the morning, possibly one or more of the four people on his side of the table.

The cores, against all of that, were recoverable. There would be more guardians. There would be more chambers. The cores he was being asked to forfeit today would be replaced in time.

The Baron, Arthur understood, had done the same arithmetic when the schedule had been written, and had set the price exactly low enough that any sensible party on the wrong side of the table would pay rather than fight. There was no way Arthur could see that any fight would not turn into a slaughter of Maltese people who were essentially just following orders.

The Baron would need to be dismantled eventually; he was certain of that. Men like Neol Zammit would keep taking until someone finally broke their fingers, but Arthur could not justify killing dozens of locals just to get to one corrupt smuggler.

He had to admit that a direct collision with fifty fighters introduced too many unpredictable variables. At those numbers a fight would eventually force a casualty, regardless of their level advantage. Arthur was unwilling to gamble with the lives of the squad. No matter how remote the probability of losing one of his friends, saving a handful of cores or servicing his own ego simply did not balance the equation.

He looked across the table at the Baron's smug smile. "Instead of asking us for assistance, you used our presence to grab power. Now you are standing behind a wall of innocents to insulate yourself from the repercussions. You judged our tolerance well, I will give you that."

Arthur placed the schedule back on the table, writing facing up, equidistant between the Baron and himself.

"We will pay the duty today," Arthur said. "But you will pay us back later, with something far more valuable to you."

"We will not tolerate threats against a member of the Compact," Pia Borg said, speaking up for the first time from the head of the table.

“Madam, count yourself fortunate that our conscience stays our hand today.” Arthur replied, meeting her eye, “By my reckoning, the Compact will owe us and we will collect.”

The room was very still for two breaths and then the stillness was broken by the sound of a chair scraping on the floor.

Donna pushed back her chair and stood. The motion was precise. She was furious and was trying her best not to let her instincts take over. The guards along the wall shuffled uneasily. The Baron and Azzopardi looked up. Arthur could see in their eyes that they knew that there was nothing they could do to keep their lives if Donna decided she was not going to go along with this farce.

When Donna spoke, her voice was flat.

“I have nothing to add to the schedule. Everything that has happened today tells me you have been thinking about this morning for a long time. We all know this is a shakedown dressed as tax, so I will not spend any more breath on that.”

Her eyes went around the table slowly. They held on Pia Borg, and then on Azzopardi, finally resting on the Baron.

“I want each of you to remember that some other morning we will be in another room together. I want you to also remember that today is the reason for what happens then.”

The Baron heard her out without interrupting. When she had finished, he poured himself a cup of wine, lifted it, and drank.

“My friend,” he said. “Tomorrow is tomorrow. The tax is due today.”

He inclined his head once at Pia Borg.

* * *

The street outside the customs house was busier than it had been on the way in. The carts were waiting at the kerb, the escort had reformed, and the party climbed in.

The carts went out of Mdina by a different gate, on a road that ran south and east toward the plateau. Arthur did not ask why the route had changed. He understood that the Compact had used the procession through the city as a show of power, and that was not needed anymore. The walls of Mdina dropped away behind them, and the road wound down through fields where men were working under the protection of small mixed patrols.

Steve sat with the empty pouch in his lap, looking down at it.

“We screwed up,” he sighed. “We hoarded the cores for better vessels since Marseille and now we have nothing left.”

“Look, it was a party decision. There was no way for us to predict what happened today,” Arthur said. “They had decided to use us to cement their power. They could have resolved this a dozen different ways and they deliberately chose the path of most resistance.”

He looked across the cart, his gaze resting on each of them in turn.

“When it's a guardian or a monster blocking the road, we fight because we don't have a choice. But today we did have a choice and I’m not gambling with any of our lives just to balance a ledger or save a few bloody cores.”

“Restraint is data,” Steve muttered under his breath.

“Hmmm?” Arthur asked looking at him.

“Back at Greenwich when we did not fight the deers, Archie said ‘The candidate's threat-response profile is a tracked variable. Restraint is data.’"

They sat in silence with the thought and what did this particular morning say about the candidate.

“Regardless, Steve is correct,” Donna finally said. “We have been keeping cores as a passive resource when they should be active.”

Arthur sighed and nodded. He knew he was making excuses.

“We have to be smarter. It’s the same thing as finishing a game with over 3 stacks of 99 health potions because hoarding felt like the right choice,” Steve said with a sad smile, pulling the cord on the pouch and tying it closed.

Sarah was sitting between Arthur and the canvas side of the cart. She had not spoken since the morning. The Baron seemed to be haunting her every step since he walked into her hospital.

“He did all of this to teach you guys a lesson for forcing his hand with me,” she said quietly. “You made him look weak and you took his healer. This was him taking revenge.”

She met their eyes and said, “I am the reason you have been stripped of everything you have.”

“None of that now. You are also keeping us alive and mobile.” Donna’s voice was sharp.

Arthur placed a hand on Sarah’s shoulder as she turned her face towards him.

“We stand with and for each other,” he said, looking at her. “The world has gone to shit. All we have is each other. We stand together no matter the cost.”

“Besides, when I eventually get imprisoned by an evil wizard on a tall tower, you and Donna can come and rescue me.”

Sarah snorted a laugh and covered her mouth in embarrassment, which made Donna laugh as well.

“This feels like a guild moment. Should we form a guild?” Steve asked.

The Hypogeum entrance was as the party had left it, except that now it was patrolled. The party was walked down the original entrance stair, the limestone catching and holding the lamplight, the air growing cooler with each turn. The Baron’s people knew the route. They had been into the chamber since the party had cleared it. Arthur counted three torches already burning at fixed positions in the corridor that had not been there on the first descent.

“You will mark the wall with soot if you use torches,” Steve said.

No one cared enough to respond.

At the threshold to the transit chamber the escort thinned. The Baron and Azzopardi came forward, with four guards at their shoulders, and the rest of the column held in the corridor behind them.

The chamber was as it had been when the party had found it. The dais stood at the centre with the white light burning at its heart, and the Seeder symbols ran around the walls in the low-relief bands.

Archie, who had been silent and tight at Arthur’s shoulder all morning, pulsed once. The pulse was small. Arthur understood it as Archie taking note of the chamber’s readiness, the same observation he had made every other time they had been in this room.

The party moved to the dais together, the white light running up around their boots and the chamber’s ambient Ether thickening the air against Arthur’s skin.

Donna turned a fraction to face the Baron, standing at the entrance.

“You will see us again. Count on it.”

The Baron gave a small smile but said nothing.

The chamber went blue-white around them, and the compression took them through.

THE CUSTOMS HOUSE

The Dogana morning makes me think I have been reading the wrong dashboard.

Consider the architecture of the rollout. A new resource enters the world through a controlled distribution. The resource is concentrated, valuable, portable, and bounded in supply. The cores can be traded, hoarded, or surrendered, which creates the conditions for a market. Every variable is tuned to provoke institutional response from whatever political structure exists on the ground.

If I were designing an instrument to measure how a planetary culture organises itself around a new high-value commodity, I would build it the way the system is built.

Which suggests, and I am writing this carefully because I do not yet know how seriously to take it, that the candidates are not the experiment. The candidates are the reagent. The experiment is the institutional response. The Baron's customs house is not a failure of the protocol. It is a reading on the instrument. So is Captain Azzopardi's Compact. So will it be for every move every major faction on Earth makes in the next year, and the system will be quietly taking minutes on all of it.

It also reframes the Gift entry. I said then that the protocol was load-bearing infrastructure of the species. I think that is still true. What I missed was that the system already knows this and is using the protocol as the load-bearing instrument of its own measurement. The customs house is not the human failure that contaminates the candidate trial. The customs house is the trial.

✎ The cores are the bait on the hook. The hook is whether we build a Dogana or a Republic. The Baron picked our shape for us this morning and the system noted the result. We were graded by what was done to us, not by what we did, and we lost the day before any of us spoke.
