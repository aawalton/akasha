I watched each person enter Sadrin’s room one by one, stepping through the shimmering threshold like survivors pulled from different nightmares. Cordelia was first, pristine as ever on the surface, but her eyes—a little too still, a little too bright—betrayed fractures beneath the porcelain calm. V arrived next, shoulders squared, jaw braced, movements clipped as if every muscle were fighting to keep from trembling. Fractal came in a beat later, feathers dulled, illusions flickering weakly around the edges like smoke refusing to become fire. Fallias slipped inside with gentle steps, her usual library-born composure strained thin, relief blooming across her features the moment she saw me.

And then Ten limped in.

I’d thought seeing the others disheveled was bad. I had not been prepared for her.

Cuts trailed her legs and arms like chalk marks on a map of suffering. Deep bruises swelled under her skin, turning the entire right side of her face into a storm cloud of swelling. She favored her left leg, every step weighted with pain. Her clothes clung to her in torn strips, scorch marks and gouges revealing glimpses of hardened muscle—and the sickening remnants of battle.

She didn’t look defeated.

She looked like she had won fifty times over and had been forced to keep fighting anyway.

My breath jammed in my throat.

Sadrin waited until the door sealed behind her, until all of us stood in a crooked half-circle, ragged and shaken but upright. Only then did he smile. A slow, curling crescent of delight sharpened at the edges by something hungry.

“Each of you were given a challenge, an opportunity, and a choice,” he said. His voice dripped with pleasure, the kind that should never be near a group so clearly injured. “This nexus is a crucible. It does not test at random.”

He lifted a hand, indicating Cordelia.

“To Cordelia, the nexus gave a battle of the mind—and a chance to break away from your past as a ‘potted plant,’ as your kind call it.”

At the term, we all saw it: a microscopic flinch across her lips, a ripple under her serene mask. Cordelia refused to let the emotion bloom, but the crack she tried to hide glimmered like a bruise under amethyst light.

Sadrin’s grin twitched wider.

He gestured toward V next. “To V, the nexus offered a chase. A hunt. A mockery of your friends, each one sculpted from the darker corners of your fear. You demolished them with explosives and salt.”

V barked out a humorless laugh, rubbing a hand across the back of his neck. “They weren’t you guys,” he muttered. “Didn’t have your… vibes.”

“That is hardly comforting,” Cordelia murmured.

“It wasn’t meant to be,” V shot back.

Sadrin flowed on as if their exchange were a gentle breeze passing through him.

“To Fallias,” he continued, “you were given time in isolation. The question was simple: are you happier alone, in the library you once called home… or with your friends?”

Fallias didn’t answer with words.

She simply moved to me, slipping into my space with fluid urgency. Her arms wrapped around me, anchoring herself, her breath brushing my collarbone. And then she kissed my neck—soft, quick, but firm enough to broadcast her answer to the entire room.

My face heated, and even Ten blinked at that.

Sadrin chuckled.

“And finally,” he said, turning his attention toward Ten, “to @!#$!@%&.”

The name mangled itself in our ears. A jagged scrape of iron nails dragged across a chalkboard, a sound that lanced through my skull like a spike. We all winced—hands to temples, teeth grinding, shoulders tensing.

Everyone except Ten.

She stood still. Almost preternaturally. That deep, grounding sigh that left her chest was neither exhaustion nor defeat. It was something colder. A release of tension so ancient it didn’t belong to the moment.

Sadrin eyed her with interest bordering on reverence.

“You were granted an endless gauntlet of foes,” he said, “each sculpted as simulacra of your companions. Not to punish you, but to push you. To peel back each defensive instinct until you answered the question buried beneath your rage.”

Ten’s fingers twitched once. Just once.

Sadrin seemed delighted by whatever he saw in her stillness.

Then the god-being finally turned to me.

“And to Alexander,” he said, “I gave a challenge not to the self, but through the selves of others.”

My stomach twisted. “Meaning?”

“You watched them,” he said, stepping gracefully into the center of the room. “You saw each struggle, each doubt, each moment of choice. Not to torment you. Not to inspire pity. But to reveal something vastly more interesting.”

His gaze slid across the group, lingering on each person.

“You were each given the choice to walk away,” Sadrin continued. “To abandon him. To leave this path. To declare your independence from Alexander’s orbit.”

My heart slammed against my ribs.

I looked around, suddenly terrified at what I might see.

Cordelia met my gaze calmly, as if the idea itself were unthinkable.

V stared at the ground, cheeks pink with stubborn loyalty.

Fractal’s feathers fluffed up, her illusions forming incandescent halos of silent defiance.

Fallias only tightened her arm around mine.

Ten…

Ten finally looked up, meeting my eyes—not with fear, nor shame, but with a fierce, exhausted kind of recognition. A survivor acknowledging another survivor.

Sadrin’s smile sharpened into something triumphant.

“This was to prove to him,” he said, sweeping an arm toward me as if unveiling a painting, “that he above all others has the right to Walk.”

The capitalization drummed in the air like a ritual bell.

“He has the right to lead.”

I felt heat crawl across my skin. Disbelief. Horror. Awe. All tangled.

“You all believe in him,” Sadrin said softly, almost tenderly. “And yet he believes not in himself.”

Silence fell like a velvet curtain.

Ten broke it first, her voice hoarse. “We didn’t walk away.”

“No,” Cordelia agreed. “We did not.”

“You couldn’t get rid of us if you tried,” V said with a lopsided grin.

Fractal nodded, eyes bright. “Never.”

Fallias tilted her chin up. “You’re ours. And we are yours.”

My throat felt tight. Too tight. I inhaled, shaky.

Sadrin watched me with the satisfaction of a craftsman admiring a freshly carved statue.

“You asked for answers,” he murmured. “This is the truth.”

I had no words. Only the overwhelming realization that every nightmare they had endured—every trial—had been tied back to me. And yet not one had chosen to let go.

Sadrin’s smile had always bothered me. It wasn’t the grin of someone amused or entertained. It was the curl of a page torn halfway from a forbidden tome, something that shouldn’t be open, shouldn’t be read, shouldn’t be allowed to speak. Yet it had a mouth and it enjoyed using it.

“Sadrin…” I forced my voice to be level, even though every nerve in me still trembled from seeing the others’ trials. The weight of what he’d shown me still clung to the inside of my ribs. “My mission won’t be complete until I deliver our goods and establish a trade contact with the peaceful residents of this Otherrealm. But from what you’ve told me…”

He was already shaking his head, long before I finished the sentence.

“There aren’t any,” he said. “This realm isn’t exactly stable enough. It manifested here only because someone asked me to. Someone asked me to deliver you into this world, and to give my honest appraisal of one Alexander Duarte, the Star Writer.”

The others stiffened. Even Fractal, perched on my shoulder, feathers puffed with irritation. Ten, battered but still breathing heavier than all of us combined, clenched her jaw. Fallias only squeezed my hand tighter, the warmth in his fingers stubborn and grounding, like he was daring the air itself to try separating us again.

V didn’t wait. Of course he didn’t.

“Who?” he barked, already reaching into his right pocket. I knew that pocket. It held three bombs, two vials of counter-dust, and a single very questionable jawbreaker he’d been aging like wine.

Sadrin chuckled. “We know the Catlamity watches the Star-Writer. But another wished to train the boy. I know he has a retinue from the Lost…” His eyes glimmered as if savoring a private joke. “But has he been exposed to the Wild?”

Before I could demand an explanation, the ground trembled.

Not a polite tremor, not a warning, not even the harsh quakes of the simulacrum battles. This was a slow, dragging heave, as though something enormous and very tired had decided to pull itself upright after centuries of pretending to be inert.

A low bellow rolled through the chamber. It wasn’t a roar. It was more like a mountain clearing its throat.

V immediately stepped in front of me, arm out, stance tight. Cordelia slid into a position opposite him, daggers drawn so smoothly they may as well have appeared there. Ten inhaled sharply, locking her legs into a bracing stance. Fallias moved behind me—not hiding, but anchoring.

Fractal hissed, colors sharpening into an aurora of alarm.

And then he appeared.

Massive didn’t begin to describe him. The figure that pushed through the corridor was a walking menagerie of dead eras, wrapped in pelts that weren’t just from animals but from myths. A cloak of direwolf fur hung from his shoulders, but beneath it I could see layers of scaled hide stitched into bark, feathers fused with chitin, antlered skulls bound by sinew to form a grotesque mantle. His skin was the color of soil fed with old bones, marked with symbols too sharp to be natural and too wild to be crafted.

His face—what could be seen of it—was half-covered by a mask made of the fused jaws of several beasts, tied together by braided roots. His exposed eye glowed like a coal in a snowstorm.

He carried no weapon.

He didn’t need one.

When he finally spoke, his voice shook dust from the ceiling.

“I am Ratatosk,” he rumbled, each syllable like a tree falling. “Herald of the Wild, Messenger of the Roots, the One Who Runs the Spine of Yggdrasil.”

Sadrin, the smug bastard, bowed.

“Alexander Duarte,” Ratatosk said, focusing entirely on me. His gaze was heavy… but curious. “Star Writer. Wanderer. Walker. The boy who carries stories in his shadow.”

My lungs forgot how to work.

Ratatosk stepped closer, and the room shrank around him. V tensed so hard his shoulders nearly clicked. Ten shifted her weight, ready to explode into motion. Cordelia’s daggers flashed with a subtle shimmer. Even Fallias’s breath stuttered against my ear.

The creature—no, the being—studied me as though examining a half-finished statue.

“I requested this nexus,” Ratatosk said. “I requested the trial. And I requested you.”

“Why?” My voice cracked. Perfect. Just what I wanted: to squeak at a living avalanche.

Ratatosk let out a sound that might have been a laugh. Or a warning growl. Either was possible.

“Because the Wild has taken an interest in you.” He leaned forward, the pelt mantle shifting with the sighs of a hundred creatures. “You have been molded by Order and tempered by Loss. You have walked with ghosts. Played hands with Fate. Bartered words with Color.” His gaze flicked toward where the Card Knight’s dormant card lay tucked against my chest. “But you have not yet met us.”

I swallowed. Hard.

“The Wild?” I asked.

He nodded slowly, a tree bowing under the weight of a storm.

“The Wild,” he repeated. “The force that grows without permission and devours without apology. The beginning and the end. The part of creation that refuses to be civilized.”

My heartbeat thudded like it wanted to run away on its own.

“I don’t understand why you’d want me.”

Ratatosk grinned, revealing rows of interwoven teeth behind the bone mask.

“That,” he said, “is why.”

I hated that answer. I hated that it made sense.

Sadrin clapped his hands like he’d just hosted a very successful dinner party. “Well,” he said brightly, “that’s your next teacher. Or your next mistake. Or your next destiny. Hard to tell with the Wild.”

Ratatosk didn’t blink. “Alexander Duarte,” he said, voice dropping low enough to vibrate my bones. “Will you hear the Wild’s call?”

Everything in me locked up.

Fractal’s claws dug into my shoulder.

Fallias held me a little tighter.

V didn’t move but I could feel him ready to blow a hole in reality if needed.

Ten’s stare burned.

Cordelia’s daggers didn’t waver.

And yet none of them could answer for me.

Only I could.

I took a breath that felt like swallowing a storm.

“What,” I asked, “does the Wild want with me?”

Ratatosk’s smile widened.

“Adventures,” he said. “Many of them. And the first begins now.”
