The chaos was worse than Thaman had ever witnessed. Considering how he'd seen more than most demons would in ten lifetimes, that said quite a bit.

He stood at the top of Camar Gadin’s highest watchtower, gazing down at the streets as rivers of demons clogged every major thoroughfare and grinding traffic to a halt. This despite orders not to leave their homes until they were told. That each batch would be evacuated in turn.

As Thaman had fully anticipated, every able-bodied person had promptly fled for the gates upon hearing of the evacuation from their friends or family.

He’d even tried to inform small batches rather than making a broad announcement. That worked for the first few waves, but word got around as it always did, and chaos ensued. Bairans ignored his decree, thereby ensuring their own doom.

The Asuran airships that were meant to take on passengers at designated pickup points were instead forced to descend into those clogged plazas to ferry away whoever could fit, nearly flipping over as the writhing masses trampled over each other in a bid to get on.

Only the impossible level of power wielded by the Asura crewing them managed to keep the order. Had they been any normal demon, they would have perished to those mobs long ago.

Thaman could hardly comprehend their bravery as they threw themselves and their ships into the fray, again and again. All to help the panicked masses of another clan.

An exhausted officer came up beside him with a stack of reports, having clearly run here at max speed. “I bring… the latest casualty figures, my Raja.”

“Well? Out with it, then,” he commanded, bracing for the bad news.

“Twelve dead from a stampede, with thirty-seven more injured. Three were killed in a fight that broke out at the western airship plaza. A family tried to cut the line, which sparked a small riot. Our Warriors have contained it for now, but similar events are occurring throughout the city. It’s too much for us to handle!”

Thaman closed his eyes. Twelve dead already, and the Ash-damned Vimana hadn't even arrived yet. In a stroke of genius, their enemies had managed to kill them before firing a single volley.

“And the Panav?” Thaman asked. “What of the Nagas?”

“They are tending to the wounded as best they can, sir, and while more arrive with each passing hour, I’m afraid we won't be able to rely on them. They are already overwhelmed.”

Hardly a surprise, though no less difficult to hear. Nagas were in short supply everywhere, let alone this far south.

“I see,” Thaman replied. “And what of our progress on the evacuation? How many souls have managed t o leave the city?”

The officer hesitated for a long moment, which was never a good sign. “Fifteen thousand by our best estimate, my Raja. Perhaps another few thousand who managed to leave the city on foot or atop Ash’va. Mostly those who got out before the panic began to spread.”

Thaman massaged his temples, forcing himself not to groan.

Four whole hours into the evacuation and only fifteen thousand had left. Fifteen out of almost two hundred. Thaman did not need to be a logistics specialist to understand the implications. Not only would the city not be evacuated in time, not even half would escape.

“My Raja...” someone said in a much older and considerably more weathered voice.

The chief logistician of clan Baira was a demon Thaman had trusted with the clan's defense through countless crises, and a single glance at the old demon’s face told Thaman that the bad news had yet to end.

“Speak, Bodhin.”

“At our current pace, my Raja, I fear we will evacuate less than a third of the city before the Vimana arrives. I have been considering measures that might increase the rate. Prioritizing the young and the able-bodied over the elderly for example. Perhaps embrace the airships overloading past their safety thresholds, which seems to be happening regardless.” He cleared his throat awkwardly. “Each of these would increase the total number evacuated.”

“Each of those will also cause more deaths, yes?” Thaman asked.

“As you say, Raja. But when faced with the choice of saving even some...”

Thaman sighed, looking down at the city.

If he gave Bodhin permission to push harder, the casualties thus far would seem quaint by comparison. He would evacuate more people, true, but he would also kill more in the process.

Their blood would be on his hands, rather than caused by necessity.

Nor did Thaman have any particular reason to believe that Vir would find a solution in time. The Akh Nara himself had told him that the best minds in the realm had yet to come up with one. By all reasonable expectations, he ought to believe those words.

Yet Thaman chose to trust him anyway. He had watched that young demon defy the impossible too many times now to believe that this would be the moment he failed.

Not now. Not after reuniting the three realms—a feat that no being in five thousand years had ever even thought of, let alone dared to attempt.

He’d disappeared, and when he’d returned, the Ash Tears had stopped. The Ash itself was gone, and the blight that had defined every Bairan’s life since time immemorial had been wiped from the world by a single demon who, against all odds, had defied Fate.

Compared to that, what was stopping a single Vimana?

“No,” he said at last, turning to Bodhin. “Do not hasten the process any further. Continue the evacuation with as much order as possible. Focus instead on minimizing casualties.”

Bodhin’s brow furrowed. “My Raja, with respect. That decision will leave the majority of the city in stranded when the Vimana arrives.”

“I am aware of the implications, Bodhin.”

“My Raja…”

“I have a feeling,” Thaman said quietly, “that this will all be quite unnecessary soon enough.”

Bodhin opened his mouth to respond before reconsidering. He looked at Thaman for a long moment, searching for some response that might convince his stubborn Raja otherwise. But Bodhin knew Thaman just as well as the Raja knew him, and he realized quickly enough that there would be no winning this battle.

Instead, he bowed and went to relay the order, while Thaman turned his attention back to the doomed city.

Whatever it is you plan to do, Akh Nara, he thought, for all our sakes, do it soon.

“You can't be serious,” Vir said, staring at Siya’s projected face in disbelief.

“I know it sounds outlandish,” Siya replied. “But everything we’ve found says it should work!”

“You’re telling me that the only viable option is to wait until the Vimana opens fire on Camar Gadin, then attempt to overpower its main weapon with a counter beam?”

“Y-Yes,” Siya muttered, averting her eyes.

“Surely, this cannot be your only plan? What about Chakra vulnerabilities you mentioned? Surely there must be something there I can use?”

Siya shook her head. “ Saunak has briefed me about these Chakras of yours. While the Vimana has no defense against Chakra, your attacks simply cannot penetrate far enough into the superstructure to reach the humans inside. Sheer distance functions as armor all on its own. Such an attack would dissipate entirely after having traveled that far through the superstructure.”

Vir couldn’t argue with that. He’d spent enough years with his Chakras to know exactly what their effective range was, and while his outstripped all other demons, the prospect of pushing one through such dense Imperium metal was not promising.

“Before you ask, there is no way to open the doors once the operators have locked them from the inside,” Siya said. “We considered that as well. The locking sequence overrides the external door controls, and we have no way to access the override without being inside. Even Saunak agrees.”

Vir somehow suspected there had been an intense debate before the Mad Thaumaturge had come around to that.

“This will be the end of me,” he sighed, closing his eyes and massaging the bridge of his nose. “If I even survive the sort of firepower capable of destroying an entire city, it seems that I will have only the tiniest sliver of an opening to return its attack with a beam of my own. An entire city is at stake, Siya.”

“It is the only way,” Siya said gravely. “But there is hope if you can manage it. The Vimana’s weapons are the largest and most prana-intensive system aboard. If you can overpower its discharge the moment it fires, the feedback should damage the array beyond repair. They have not yet been able to restore the external emplacements you destroyed earlier, so once the main weapon is disabled, the Vimana becomes effectively harmless.”

Vir nodded. At this point, he'd take all the good news he could get.

“Unless they trigger the self-destruct,” Siya added softly. “But we needn't worry about that.”

Vir’s eyes widened. “Sorry? Its self-destruct?”

“All Vimana have them,” Siya said hastily, twiddling her fingers. “The designers needed a failsafe in case one ever went out of control. However, the self-destruct requires authorization codes that only the original operators would have possessed. The humans on board will not have them. I verified this in the Imperium archives aboard our Vimana. Not even I have those codes. It will not be an issue.”

Vir didn’t like the sound of that one bit, and by the way the unflappable girl was squirming, she didn’t fully believe that either.

Regardless, there was little they could do either way. At least the weapon, he had some influence over. However small that might be.

“I’ll get in position,” he said exhaustedly. “And let us pray this works as you expect.”

Pray to whom, Vir didn’t know.

There was not a single being working to counter the Fateweavers. No one of similar power, anyway. Janak had been the closest to that, and even he'd struggled to make a dent in just one universe, let alone any others. Merely delaying an unwinnable battle.

But Janak was gone, which left just Vir. His woefully inadequate successor.

Everyone in the realm had full faith in him.

And yet, what celestial being watched over Vir? What god of luck or weaver of Fate was t here to spin the tapestry in his favor?

No, there was only Vir and the actions he and everyone else took.

Against an enemy that saw all outcomes, past, present, and future. That had spent millennia countering plans exactly like this, learning from every failed attempt in a near-infinity of universes.

The thought terrified Vir more than anything else had in the entire ordeal.

He gripped the orb, bringing Siya’s projection closer.

“Stay safe,” he said. “If this goes wrong, I want you and Saunak off that Vimana the moment Cirayus tells you to move.”

“Vir…”

“Promise me, Siya.”

She held his gaze through the projection for a moment.

“I promise,” she said, before dejectedly cutting the connection.

Vir turned to the helmsman.

“Ready my personal airship and set course for Camar Gadin, maximum speed. I need to be in firing position before that Vimana reaches the city, and we have less than two hours to manage it.”

“Your will be done, Akh Nara.”

The skyship banked, and below them, entirely new lands blurred past. Lush, fertile land that had just been covered in Ash.

Vir barely noticed. For if he failed in the next two hours, Ksaia's tragedy would seem quaint in comparison.

With one final look at the horizon, he turned and headed for his ship.
