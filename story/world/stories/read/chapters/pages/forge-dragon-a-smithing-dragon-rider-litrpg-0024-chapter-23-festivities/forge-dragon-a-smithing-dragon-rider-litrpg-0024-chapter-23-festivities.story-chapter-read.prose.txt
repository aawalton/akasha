Cavil, Caleb, and Grisim’s father, hadn’t kept word of his son’s survival to himself. After stashing Bog at the river mill forge, the trio went into town to find a feast in the works.

“All this because I’m not dead?” Caleb asked.

Kelia suddenly grew quiet and avoided eye contact.

“What did you do?” he demanded.

“Nothing, just… I may have told your father that you were initiated.”

“Why?!”

“You should have seen him when he thought you were dead. I just thought he deserved to know,” she said, guilt written on her face.

“It’s true, Dad was really sad,” Grisim said. “It almost hurt, because I don’t think he’s been that sad when he thought I was dead. Honestly, if he wasn’t, I might not have went to save you.”

“Fine,” Caleb said, forgiving the pair.

He was actually slightly relieved he didn’t have to tell everyone. He wasn’t sure how to do that without making himself the center of attention—though a feast for his initiation wasn’t any better, but that would have happened regardless. Now he saved a day of being fawned over.

The distraught father in question came running to meet them as soon as they crested the ridge Trisken sat on. The village was small, or at least that was what people said. Caleb had never seen anything but other similar-sized villages, so he had no frame of reference. It sat on a plateau below the mountain tree line. Cold-aspected timber was the village’s main export, and most of the villagers worked as loggers. As such, all the houses were made of cold-aspected wood.

If you looked out from their plateau with your back to the empire and the giant ring that dominated the sky above it, three ice volcanoes of the Great Ice Range could be seen, slowly erupting with their glacial flows to fill the Glacial Basin that fed the rivers down which they sent their logs. Their village sat on one such volcano, and it was inside there that Caleb had spent the better part of a week.

Caleb’s father Cavil looked nothing like him, and exactly like Grisim. Both men were large and heavily built with wide shoulders, and had thick brown hair. Caleb was wiry, fair-skinned, and certainly took after his mother.

A mother who was hiding a bloodline from us, Caleb thought sullenly.

He was certain his mother had the same bloodline he had. It explained her skill in weaving magical cloth. He only wished she’d told him about it. He probably wouldn’t have risked his life trying to find a stupid piece of metal.

“Hi, Dad,” Caleb said mid-embrace. “I’m back.”

While some fathers may have put on a show of bravado and made a joke to hide their true feelings, Cavil was not one of them. As he always told his sons, “The world is dark enough to bury joy under pride.”

Both boys took it to heart, though Grisim didn’t hold back on the pride bit; he just didn’t use it to hide his happiness.

“Don’t you ever do that again,” he said softly, and then gave his son a pat on the back. “Come on, there’s a party waiting for you! Kelia, please tell me all about how you saved my idiot sons.”

And that was how the day went. People came to Caleb, embraced him as the town’s newest “Frost Dragon Hunter” and sat enraptured as Grisim recounted the story of his “heroic” rescue.

Kelia excused herself, ostensibly to go visit her father but in reality to get away from the judgemental looks of the villagers. Kelia had always been an outcast. He features bore a striking resemblance to those of the Frigidi, and what started as children’s teasing when they were young turned into shunning as an adult. Rumor had it that her father had found her abandoned in the forest and raised her as his own. Caleb knew that wasn’t true, as Kelia had told him stories of her mother, but he’d never asked her for an explanation of her Frigidi features.

He suspected that her mother was at least partially Frigidi. They, like all the other aspected humans, were enemies of the empire. And, as the nearest people under the empire’s ostensive control, that made them enemies of Caleb’s people. The hostilities took the form of occasional raids when things were desperate for the Frigidi, and trading when things were desperate for Trisken.

Officially they were not allowed to interact with the enemies of the empire, but when the empire ignored pleas for aid, the Frigidi were always willing to trade for steel.

As soon as Kelia left, a line formed of older women.

“Caleb, you look so handsome, have you met my daughter Sena?” the first said, and the rest went about the same way.

Being the newest initialized made Caleb the newest eligible bachelor—no one seemed to care that he wasn’t actually single. He liked to think that they would have been like this for anyone who was taken, but he knew the truth. When Grisim had initialized, he’d been seeing someone, and no nosy mothers had badgered him on his return. He fended each off with polite disinterest.

Each left affronted as if he were the one being unreasonable.

“You did it, boy!” a familiar older voice said.

Caleb turned to see his Master Dimel waiting patiently for the line of busybodies to leave. He smiled and embraced his master with a hug. Dimel was a shorter man in his late fifties, strongly built with graying hair. His voice was often happy, though loud, as the years in the forge had made him hard of hearing.

They sat together, and Caleb told the story once more, complete with fictional accounts of finding enchanted equipment. In the end, he decided to tell a bit of the truth and admit to having repaired the breastplate.

“Amazing! I always knew you would be one of the best if you ever got initialized,” his master said, patting him on the back.

Quieter he came in close and asked, “You didn’t do it, did ya?”

Caleb knew the “it” in question was picking up the Blacksmith class.

“It was offered,” he admitted. “But… in the end I went for the highest-rarity class.”

“My boy!” Dimel said, patting Caleb hard on the back in relief. “I couldn’t have the damned empire come and take away my star pupil.”

Wishing to change the topic, Caleb pulled the white ore and the metal flecks out of his bag to show his master.

“I found the meteorite,” he said, not mentioning that he’d fed most of it to a dragon. “And this white metal. It absorbs ridiculous amounts of heat. It’s extremely cold-aspected.”

Dimel lifted both and examined them, tutting and humming to himself.

“This white stuff, I’ve seen it before but never this much. I wouldn’t waste time trying to forge it. You could throw it into a bloomery and all it would do is waste a forest of wood for no result. This white ore… I’ve never seen the likes. You think it’s magic?”

Caleb nodded. He was certain it was magic, just not certain what it did.

“Bring it by later and we will try to smelt it,” Dimel said, handing it back reluctantly. “There’s not much, but if it’s suitable, we can use it to make the edge of a blade.”

Originally that had been Caleb’s hope, but recent conversations had made him realize he needed to finish his engagement gift and propose to Kelia. This small bit of metal might make a ring, and who knew what benefit he could reap if he kept the aspect pure.

Not all were happy with Caleb’s return. Others his age resented the fact that he and his brother had killed two dragons alone, not giving others the change to initialize.

“How about next time, I just let them kill me?” Caleb suggested in a helpful tone, which killed their grumblings.

All in all, Caleb was quickly erasing all the good will he’d gained from being initialized. He’d always been an odd one out due to his smaller size, but he’d never been ostracized for that. It was his relationship with Kelia that made him somewhat of an outcast. They pair had met as children. Caleb had started out as an apprentice in the bloomeries, where all their iron was smelted, before being taken on by Master Dimel. Kelia had accompanied her father when he delivered wood to Dimel, and they’d quickly become friends.

The thoughts of Kelia got him to realize she had left. Caleb suddenly had the urge to be away himself, and excused himself politely to go to an outhouse. Once there, he slipped around the back and left the feast behind him.
