Anne allowed a hopeful smile to bloom for the briefest moment before she buried it beneath disappointment.

Dorgh saw it.

Of course he did. That was the point.

Twelve gold was close enough to hurt. Close enough to tempt. The smile had told him that much. She could almost see the chain of thought moving behind his eyes. The price was within reach, but she still hesitated. Which meant the problem was not the money alone. It was the place. The distance. The risk. The fear that buying this oversized wreck would be the same as burying her last chance beneath warped planks and old dust.

“Damn...” Anne let out a breath and shook her head. “Sorry. You really do know how to close a deal. I want to say yes, but if this fails, I will not even have enough left to try selling books on the street. Such a pity...”

She had to be careful not to overplay it. Too much despair and he would suspect manipulation. Too little and he would hold firm.

Then, as though struck by a thought she had only just had, she hesitated and looked back toward the interior of the building.

“Could I take another look?” she asked. “Alone, I mean. No offense, but I cannot really imagine the possibilities with... interruptions. The town still does not suit my business, but perhaps I might think of something. As you said, the place is large. I may have an insight.”

The reluctance remained in her voice, but now it sat beside a thin, dangerous thread of interest.

Usually, no sensible seller would allow a potential buyer to roam a property unsupervised. But Dorgh had already started treating her like a customer he had nearly lost. Under those circumstances, another look could hardly make matters worse.

So he agreed.

Anne walked away at an unhurried pace, pretending to reconsider room uses, entrances, renovations, and furniture placement. She paused once by the tables, once by the stairs, once near the counter as if measuring the size of the room with her eyes.

The moment she was sure the dwarf could no longer see her, she gathered her coat and hurried.

The staff corridor seemed narrower when taken with purpose. The old floorboards complained beneath her boots. She ignored them, pushed past the obstructions, and reached the far room in little more than a rush of breath and suppressed impatience.

Then she stopped.

The rift still hung in the air exactly where it had been before, colorless and shifting, its edges folding over themselves in silence. Now that she could study it properly, without Dorgh talking beside her, it looked worse—more dangerous and unstable. The light around it bent strangely.

Anne lifted a hand to the blue cat talisman hanging from her neck.

“Come out, Moruh. It is time to work.”

Smoke spilled from the talisman in a curling ribbon of blue, thickening in the air before taking shape. A large feline landed lightly on the floorboards, his fur a deep vivid blue, an old-fashioned black hat perched between his ears with far more dignity than such a creature had any right to possess.

At the same time, Anne slipped a book from a hidden fold inside her coat.

It was not large enough that it could not be carried concealed, yet it should not have fit where it had been. Its cover was old, dark, and marked by a weight of history that no ordinary grimoire ever truly carried. The moment it left her hands, it floated before her and began to turn its own pages with a dry whisper.

She found the one she wanted.

Then she cast.

“Restrigere, Spatium!”

Power surged through her in a sharp, familiar rush. Her eyes shone blue. Loose strands of dark hair lifted in the current of released magic. She moved both hands, guiding the spell outward, and translucent lines of force spread from the grimoire like a second geometry overlaying the room. They rose along the walls, sealed the doorway, and curved overhead into a contained shell of dim blue light.

A barrier of isolation.

Not strong enough to stop a real assault, but more than enough to dull prying senses and smother suspicious fluctuations. If Dorgh, or anyone else, noticed magic here, her entire gamble would collapse.

“Quickly, Moruh,” Anne said. “We do not have much time.”

“Yes, mistress.”

The familiar sounded as composed as ever, but he moved immediately.

Above him, several grey scrolls appeared one after another, floating in the air like summoned records from some unseen archive. One would unfurl, spill symbols and diagrams into the room, then roll itself shut and vanish while another replaced it. Without a proper circle anchoring the work, he could only maintain a few at a time, but that was enough.

Their interest in this building had never been accidental.

A year earlier, buried among academy reports and boring event summaries, Anne had found a record describing the appearance of this rift. Stable. Unusable. Not worth further resources. That had been the official conclusion. But official conclusions were often written by people too lazy or too unimaginative to chase strange things to the end.

More importantly, the report had stirred another memory—an uncertified academic speculation on stable rifts with no formed tunnel.

Anne had chased that trace of theory ever since.

She had not lied to Dorgh. This truly was a desperate attempt to save her craft. If this rift connected to some worthwhile location in the forbidden forest, then the building might become more than a doomed bookstore by the roadside. It might become a chance. If it did not, then she was still one failed gamble away from hauling books through Riverend’s streets like a peddler.

All spatial rifts connected two places. Usable or unusable changed nothing about that truth. Somewhere, there had to be another side.

The academy had not cared enough to find it, but Anne did.

While Moruh sorted the scrolls, she took out a rough grey stone and began inscribing a mark into its surface with focused, practiced strokes. The rune was small and crude compared to the arrays filling the floating scrolls above them, but it only needed to do one thing, and do it properly.

A few moments later, Moruh froze on one of the active scroll sets.

“Mistress.”

Three scrolls remained open together now, filled with symbols, measured arcs, and spatial diagrams dense enough to make an ordinary scholar weep.

Anne stepped closer, checked them, then swallowed.

This was it.

She looked once at the rift, muttered a short prayer to every god that had ever tolerated desperate people, and threw the marked stone forward.

It vanished the instant it touched the shimmering tear.

Not swallowed. Not broken. Simply gone.

Anne exhaled and moved at once. She spread another scroll on the floor, this one marked by a spiral diagram. At its center, she pinned a small metal needle, then tied a necklace around the pin so that the stone hanging from it rested within the drawn pattern.

Its inscription matched the one in the stone.

The grimoire turned a page.

“Ad Suum Par!”

This spell moved differently.

Instead of flaring outward, the power narrowed into a clean line. Anne guided it into the necklace-stone. The rune answered in a brief pulse of silver-blue light, then the pendant jerked violently westward, dragging the necklace taut.

It began to circle the pin.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Anne dropped to one knee, watching with fierce attention.

The stone spun faster, then slowed, then found its line again with tiny deviations so slight another person might have missed them. At last it stopped, trembled in place, lost its light, and dropped.

Moruh’s tail lashed.

“Nine turns to the west,” he said quickly. “Two fingers north deviation. One finger high. Mistress...”

“Only one finger high...” Anne murmured, already reaching for her map. “So the other side is only slightly above this level. Not farther up the mountain, then. Good. Give it to me.”

Moruh passed her the roughly drawn map she had paid far too much to acquire.

Together, they marked the estimate.

The problem was clear.

The forbidden lands were still too poorly known for certainty. Even a map this incomplete had been expensive. The directional pull gave them an angle and a relative measure, not a precise position. When Anne finished the calculations, only two plausible locations remained.

She stared at them in silence.

One possibility lay on this side of the mountains.

Useless.

The other crossed beyond.

A door into everything she had come here to seek.

Anne’s fingers tightened slightly against the edge of the map.

A gamble. Again.

Her life had been reduced to too many of those lately.

Still... it was enough. More than enough. The rift was real. Meaningful. Maybe even worth the building and the risk.

Worth pressing harder.

She dismantled the working quickly. The scrolls disappeared. The barrier unraveled into blue motes that faded into the dusty air. Moruh returned to smoke and blue metal, sinking back into the talisman. The grimoire vanished into concealment once more.

By the time Anne left, her heartbeat had steadied.

She emerged with a brightness she did not bother hiding at first. Hope came too naturally after a discovery like that. But the instant she saw Dorgh waiting and noticed the expectation lifting his expression, she caught herself and let that excitement collapse into visible regret.

The shift was almost painful.

Perfect.

“I...” Anne began, then stopped, as though fighting herself. “I really wanted it. But I cannot take that risk. If only this place were in town...”

Dorgh stared at her.

He had already all but given up. She could see it. The long wait had drained his patience, and the failed sale was already settling into his thoughts as an unpleasant report for his superiors. But now he saw something—the customer had wanted the building. Truly wanted it. Which meant the deal had nearly closed.

He made his decision in a rush, as though speaking before caution could stop him.

“Miss Anne, wait. I am truly going too far here—far enough to invite complaints, perhaps worse—but I cannot bear to let you walk away from this opportunity.” He dragged in a breath and winced as if the number itself hurt him. “Seven gold coins. That is practically giving it away.”

Anne did not blink.

“Deal!”
