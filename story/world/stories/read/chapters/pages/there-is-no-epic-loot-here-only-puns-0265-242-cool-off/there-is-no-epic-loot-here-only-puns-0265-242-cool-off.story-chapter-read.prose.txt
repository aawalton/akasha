"We need to leave."

Nickels had suggested it three times already. Each time, the Maxers had found another reason to keep walking, as though the Dungeon would be embarrassed into giving up its secrets if they stayed long enough. 

He was beginning to think they would rather die down here than return empty-handed.

Two of them nodded, though neither moved towards the exit. Someone at the back muttered about all the time they had wasted getting here. Nickels looked around for a single person willing to argue on his side and found none.

They could still walk out, sell what they had learned, and come back better prepared. Unfortunately, information was difficult to acquire in a tavern, especially when the next table had a new sword to show off or some never-before-seen loot.

"The door still works and so do our legs," Nickels said. "Both seem like advantages."

"And wanting to leave teleports us out," someone said with a shrug.

"Then we should be the boss. No one knows what it could be on this stage," another offered. Several heads turned his way. Nickels stared at the floor until he trusted his face again not to be too judgemental.

A proper report on this floor would sell. Even a rough account of the changing boss could earn them enough to make the expedition worthwhile. 

"This floor has a slime boss," Nickels said. "Or something that behaves like a slime. Sometimes it turns into a knight. Sometimes a beast. People reported even weirder things, like on-site legal advice for messy divorce or property disputes, so it's a mixed bag." He paused. "No one ever provides clear notes. Mainly because they were too busy grabbing loot to say please or thank you."

"Ain't thanking a Dungeon for loot. It's like thanking a tree for air," someone scoffed.

"Then you'll feel very foolish when the trees get tired of you and let you suffocate," Nickels replied. Someone told him to stop being ridiculous. Someone else demanded a vote, and before long half the party was shouting over the other half.

Caution lost by two votes. Nickels counted them again, hoping he had misheard, while the others started towards the boss door. Long grass scraped at their boots along the cracked walls.

What bothered him was the stage system. Maxers could tell you how to raise a stage and what rewards to expect, but ask why the Dungeon changed its encounters and the answers grew vague. 

There had to be more to it than a number. What made the number go higher? Sometimes people stole everything not nailed down and barely got above stage 2. Others fought every monster that appeared and didn't get past stage 3.

Most were content to push that number higher, collect whatever appeared, and call the expedition a success. It didn't sit right with Nickles.

This Dungeon had put up with far worse than them.

There had been warnings, too. More than enough for anyone paying attention.

Nickels had never visited a Dungeon that gave adventurers so many opportunities to change their minds. In fact, far too many were intent on drawing people in deeper.

They stepped forward, and the statue above the door cracked apart. Stone fell in clean sheets, dropping away from the figure inside. The creature landed in a deep crouch, one fist planted on the floor, wings unfolding to form a firm barrier between the group and the door.

Nickels's mind supplied the landing pose was… really impressive, but he didn't say it aloud.

Stone slid from the figure's shoulders as he straightened, nearly eight feet tall. A greatsword hung across his back. Nickels took one look at its width and decided not to be the first person to draw a weapon.

"Weapons!" someone began, already raising a hand.

The gargoyle raised both hands, palms out.

"I seek only one of you for combat. The rest may pass," he said. He spoke quietly enough that the Maxers stopped moving to hear him.

The gargoyle had not even reached for his sword. That seemed to trouble the Maxers more than a threat would have.

Nickle's mind raced, his hands twitching to grab the guidebook. No notes covered this. No helpful scribbles from past adventurers who might have bothered to write something down instead of bragging about loot or complaining about the Dungeon. This was new, and new things in a dungeon were usually bad.

He wondered if that was true here.

Still, they had wanted to learn something about the boss. Nickels doubted any of them had expected the answer to be eight feet tall.

A few Maxers began whispering about who should volunteer. None used their own name.

"Just one? Not to the death?" someone asked. Nickels noticed he had already stepped behind the others.

The gargoyle looked from one adventurer to the next before settling on Nickels, his imposing eyes narrowing as if he had been looking for someone just like Nickels all along.

"I wish to fight you alone. Will you surrender your way of passage for the others to continue?" he asked, voice solid as the stone he emerged from.

Nickels looked at the others, who were suddenly very interested in the gargoyle's proposal. He had spent the better part of the day keeping them alive. Apparently, that also qualified him to be left behind.

He could refuse. He could demand another vote, though he had seen how the last one went.

Every warning he had given them had bought perhaps five minutes of good behaviour. Then someone spotted a chest or heard a rumour about a hidden room and off they went again. Nickels was tired of pulling them back.

If they wanted to face the boss without him, they could. He was finished voting on their behalf.

He stopped watching the others and faced the gargoyle.

"I accept. You can continue. Go be legends," Nickels said, managing a thin smile. The others slipped past the gargoyle, careful to give his wings plenty of room.

The gargoyle waited for the last Maxer to pass before turning back.

The door shut with a heavy clunk. Nickels glanced at the gargoyle's sword, then at the space around them. There was room to dodge, though not nearly enough room to feel good about it.

"I do not think I can match you in direct combat," Nickels said. There seemed little point in pretending otherwise.

"You underestimate yourself. You have speed and a mind that adjusts. Those traits decide many fights," the gargoyle replied. He left his sword where it was, then looked down at the floor as though he needed to admit something awkward.

"I may have misled you and your group. I didn't plan to harm anyone. The Dungeon asked me to delay your advance. Nothing more," he admitted.

Nickels looked at the sword again, then back at the gargoyle. He had spent several minutes preparing to be cut in half for nothing.

"Huh?" he managed.

"Delta is grateful. She noticed you trying to adjust and learn how things work here. The problem is the system. It keeps a whole group in one stage. No one gets lowered on their own. The only workable option was to hold you in place and let the others move forward," the gargoyle explained.

Nickels scratched his cheek. The Dungeon had arranged all this because it had noticed him trying? That sounded like it was always watching, able to pick people apart without issues… not a feat for a place with only four floors.

"This Dungeon isn't normal, is it?" Nickels asked. He already knew the answer from the rumours outside.

"I cannot say. This is home. It has always been home. But the people who enter bring trouble. Many arrive with rude habits and no respect," the gargoyle said. His wings pulled tight for a moment before he settled again. Nickels was rather happy he hadn't been rude. That greatsword was still within the gargoyle's reach.

"Delta tries to guide them. Some listen. Many do not. She hopes for better outcomes, but hope only reaches so far. Your effort stood out. Most visitors rush through every hall and every room without thought." The gargoyle folded his wings closer to his back.

Nickels rubbed the back of his neck. "Didn't think anyone paid attention to that."

"Delta did," the gargoyle said. "She wanted you to know."

A warm breeze touched Nickels' neck. He twitched and looked over his shoulder, but there was nothing there. The warmth stayed for another moment before fading.

"What's going on in the boss room?" he asked, changing the subject before he had to figure out what to say to any of that.

The gargoyle looked towards the door. A dozen bolts secured it once it closed behind the rest of the Maxers, each fastening like a vault rather than a prison, and something dark leaked through the gaps around the frame.

"Your comrades are learning a valuable lesson," he said, voice oddly calm.

"Like math or history?" Nickels asked slowly, knowing it wouldn't be anything so simple.

The gargoyle considered the question. His mouth curved into a rather worrying smile.

"Science. The lesson that every action has an equal and opposite reaction in equal response," he said. Nickels glanced at the Doctor's mark on the door and decided he did not want the details.

"Will they be alright?" he asked. Something hit the other side of the door hard enough to shake the bolts.

The gargoyle took his time answering.

"They'll live."

Well, what else could Nickels really ask for?

Beyond the door, the cold had worked its way into every stone.

Frost crept along the floor and ceiling in thin lines, and a few icicles hung from the broken arches. The Maxers filed in, their breath rising in pale streams. Some wished Nickels luck under their breath. Others vaguely promised to meet him upstairs, already thinking about bruises and stories.

No one offered to risk going back for him, using their superior numbers. This dungeon had 'mercy ', and Nickles should abuse that. Anyone would really.

A draft blew through the broken arches. The room had once been a throne room, judging by the pillars and the raised stone at the far end, but much of the ceiling had collapsed and snow covered the rubble, falling from somewhere above, flakes landing on the once-throne with a solemn silence.

A round hole opened in the floor beyond the ruined dais. One Maxer threw a pebble into it and waited. Nothing came back.

As they moved toward the centre, a Maxer's boot clipped something buried in a small snow mound, sending it slightly skittering out into the open. He bent down, brushed it off, and lifted it with a burst of noisy triumph.

"Loot!" he shouted, holding up a battered crown. A crack ran halfway around the band, and several of its points had been bent flat; it looked warped, like the head that had been wearing it suddenly grew too large for its band.

"Melt it down or wear it and see if it blocks the boss' attacks," someone said. That set the rest off. Hands dug through snow, tossing aside clumps in the hope of hitting another prize.

Snow blew around their boots while they searched. The Maxers began pulling apart a second mound when one of them finally looked toward the empty throne.

"Speaking of which, isn't there supposed to be a boss here?" a Maxer asked, brushing snow off her gloves.

Wind erupted from the pit, flinging snow against the walls. Frost raced up the nearest pillars and spread across the ceiling. An icicle grew above the party, cracked under its own weight, and smashed against the floor.

"What happens," a voice called from the pit, "when a king finds his crown too small for what must be done?" The Maxer holding the crown lowered it.

"What does a king do when his people, his family, his home no longer need a just ruler?" Something scraped against the pit's inner wall, and the Maxers backed away.

"Formations!" someone shouted. Boots scraped for position. The floor shifted slightly beneath them, and only then did they notice the slight downhill tilt leading straight toward the pit.

"You have destroyed my family. You have stolen our treasures. You have hurt my mother!" the voice roared. Snow fell from the arches, and a crack shot across the floor near the pit.

A long, azure neck rose from the pit, bone plates overlapping from skull to shoulders. More of the creature followed, heavy coils scraping against the sides as it climbed.

No one rushed it. The Maxers shuffled backwards, some with weapons still half-drawn, while frost crept over the toes of their boots.

"You deserve not the judge and his verdict. You do not deserve the nurse or her cures. You do not deserve the mercy of a court; your crimes are beyond excusable," the beast spat. Droplets of venom struck the floor, freezing the snow into hard white crust wherever they landed.

"You do not even deserve the damnation of a king,"

The serpent raised its head above them and fixed its orange eyes on the crown.

"Your reward is the rage of a tyrant," the serpent announced, and its eyes flashed a deep orange.

There was a slight pause.

"I'm… sorry?" someone said hesitantly. The giant snake tilted its head, and it somehow smiled, all teeth and no warmth.

"Not as sorry as you're about to be," the boss replied.

Then ice and venom rushed down at them.

The crown lay forgotten in the corner of the room, slowly buried once more under a frozen mound.
