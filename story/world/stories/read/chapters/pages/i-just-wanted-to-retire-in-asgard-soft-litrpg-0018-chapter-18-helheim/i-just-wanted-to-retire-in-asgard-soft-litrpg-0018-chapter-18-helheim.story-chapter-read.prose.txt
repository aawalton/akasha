Barry searched for his stuff, for anything he could use or salvage, but the only thing he was standing on was ash.

If there had been any doubt before, there wasn't now. Everything he had ever built was dead. He could taste it, literally: carbon polymer and aluminum oxide, the same compound he mixed for his own insulation, coating his tongue. He knew exactly what that meant. His instruments had gone dark the second he arrived. No telemetry, no charge, nothing at all.

A few paces off, the golden man waited, silent and perfectly still. Observing him.

Barry spat out a mouthful of grit and wiped his chin with the back of his hand. "Okay, so in Helheim, dead people walk around in my armor? And who the hell are you?"

Baldr glanced around before he answered. "No. Those were your projections, not the dead. A side effect, I suppose."

"Side effect of what? The corruption of gods?"

"Of a living creature. Of you. The living are not meant to stay."

That was it. That was the whole answer.

Barry reached for his usual sarcasm, then stopped. The copies hadn't just borrowed his armor. They moved in it the way he did: the slight lean forward when he checked a joint under load, the left shoulder that dropped when he was tired. Habits he had never once tracked on purpose. Watching them play out on bodies with nothing behind them stirred something in him, and he wasn't about to look at it too closely. He turned his eyes up to the gray, misty sky instead.

"He really hated me from the start."

He'd always figured the work was the point, that no one would resent him for it. He'd figured wrong. Even gods held grudges, especially the powerful ones.

The golden man still hadn't moved, apart from his eyes, which kept sweeping the mist.

"So, why are you here? You look... brilliantly alive. Seriously, it's making me nauseous. Dial it back a few million lumens, will ya?"

Baldr turned his luminous eyes on him. "I am the same as you. I was kept here through trickery. That is why I came flying down, thinking you were another god. But no, you look like..."

"I think the word you're looking for is human."

"Yes." Baldr paused. "You look.. human."

The ash kept falling, and Barry's lungs had started to burn. "Thanks for the acknowledgment. I needed that. Knowing my lungs are gonna die on this air while you stand there like nothing happened? Cheers me right up."

The golden man smiled. "haha...You are funny, for a mortal. I might keep you as a pet."

"Pet, huh." He said, his right eye twitching with restraint. "If I had my tools, you'd be dead by now," Barry said evenly.

Baldr burst out laughing.

"I'm serious."

That only made Baldr laugh harder. "You are funny! Yes, I will keep you." He reached out and flicked Barry's forehead, and a golden haze spread across Barry's body.

"Okay," Barry said, and for the first time since he'd arrived, the air going into his lungs didn't burn. "Suit yourself."

He made himself take inventory, starting with what he could pull from his dead selves. The jacket was compromised but holding. A few power cells were drained, but they'd hold a charge again if he could get any power into them.

The comm array was dead. Only basic life support still worked, running on whatever pressure was left, and that wouldn't last. Helheim, apparently, had no patience for innovation. Everything here just rotted, and fast.

Baldr hadn't taken his eyes off him. "So what did you do to the Allfather that he sent you here?"

"I didn't do anything," Barry said. "But I'm gonna, and it's not gonna be pretty."

***

In Asgard, the throne room was as still and silent as ever, until the doors crashed open. Thor strode in, Yggdrasil's residue still smoking off his body.

"Father!"

Odin felt it as soon as Thor walked in, and a smile tugged at the corner of his mouth. Somehow his son had found a way to Yggdrasil and survived it.

"Yes, my son. What is it?"

There was white at Thor's temples now, and Stormbreaker rode across his back, quiet against the dark leather of his cloak, though the air around it still hummed faintly. He laid it all out in order: the vortex over the workshop, Barry dragged into some kind of black hole, the instant the whole structure was scoured clean.

Odin heard him out without a word. He leaned forward on the high seat, one hand on the arm of the throne, the other hidden in the fold of his cloak. When Thor finished, Odin said only one thing.

"Hela. It has to be her. I can think of no one else."

He turned to his wife. "Isn't that right, dear?"

Freya stood at his side, sweat beading on her forehead. She nodded, and the finality of it settled over the court. Nobody argued. The guards at the doors shifted their weight and stared at nothing. A few of the lesser gods traded glances, then decided the floor was more interesting.

"My niece?" Thor said. "Hela? Father, are you sure she's strong enough to attack Asgard?"

Loki had been in the room the whole time, invisible. She'd meant to stay that way until she heard her daughter's name, and now she had. She waited until the last echo died, then let herself be seen.

"Every unexplained disaster," she said. The cold in her voice cut deeper than shouting would have. "Every convenient failure. Every time this court needs a name to blame, it all goes to my daughter. Doesn't it?"

No one answered.

Odin didn't so much as shift. His stare said it plainly: leave, or go back to being invisible. Loki looked from face to face and saw the room for what it was: a place that had already settled on its story and only needed someone to play the villain.

"You're all corrupt morons," she said, "and I'm the one they call the goddess of mischief."

She walked out, and the doors swung shut behind her.

The corridor outside was quieter. Servants slid past with careful efficiency, none of them bothering to nod or show the goddess any respect. Loki didn't care. She never had. Something was wrong, badly wrong, and she could feel it in her bones. The way her mother had reacted only confirmed it.

She didn't need to ask where Freya had gone. Some habits are older than any crisis.

Once court had broken up, she found her mother alone in a side chamber above the lower courtyards. The yard below was empty. Whatever Freya had been doing, sorting reports or just standing where the light fell right, she stopped the moment Loki walked in.

"Hela had nothing to do with Barry's disappearance," Loki said. "You know that, don't you?"

Freya held her ground for a second, out of training or long habit. Then something in her face gave, just slightly.

Loki didn't waste the chance. She stepped close and took her mother by the shoulder.

"Mother, this is about your granddaughter. Please. Tell me something. Anything."

Freya turned her face away. She couldn't look at Loki's jade-green eyes, the same eyes her granddaughter had.

"You're right. She's innocent." The words came out flat. "It's Odin. Your father. I don't know how, but Yggdrasil is consuming him." She paused. "His left hand. The skin from the wrist to the second knuckle. I watched him cover it at the last council. He thought I wasn't looking." Another pause, shorter. "And it's not only the flesh. It's his mind, too. This has been going on much longer than he'll admit."

She offered nothing more. She didn't need to.

The chamber was small: stone walls, a single high window letting in the pale light of the realm. Freya stood with her back to it, the light catching the edges of her hair and leaving her face mostly in shadow. Loki watched her hands, perfectly still at her sides. No fidgeting, no stray gesture. It was the discipline Odin had always demanded of his court, and Freya was turning it against him now.

"Why?" Loki said, gripping her mother's hand tight. "Why didn't you tell the court, or my brother? Why didn't you try to do anything at all?"

Freya felt the sting of that grip and let it be. "I tried, Loki. I really did. But he knew. And then came everything you and your brother went through. After that, I stopped."

It sounded like the end of something.

"But please, don't tell your brother. I don't want Thor fighting Odin while he's unstable. I don't know what Odin might do to him."

Loki nodded.

"No, a nod isn't enough. Swear it." Freya took Loki's hand and pressed it to her own head. "Swear in my name."

"I... I swear."

The words were simple, but Loki felt them bind, settling into place like a knot pulled tight.

"Now save the mortal," Freya said. "He's the only one who understands the mechanics well enough to slow what the tree is doing. And it's long past time you met your daughter properly, before she decides the state of the realms is reason enough to march."

Loki saw the trap before Freya had even finished. Go after Barry, and she'd walk into Helheim already carrying a secret about Odin's condition. Meet Hela, and she'd be standing beside the daughter the court had spent years using as a convenient scapegoat. Either way, she'd end up somewhere the court could afford to lose her.

She looked at her mother. Freya looked back, and nothing in her face suggested she hadn't seen it too.

The silence stretched.

"You knew," Loki said. It wasn't a question. "You knew Father would come to this eventually. That we'd end up here, me asking you for answers, you sending me back to Helheim."

Freya smiled, just barely. "I am the first witch, daughter. Knowing is my job." Her voice stayed level. "But I needed someone who could still move without being watched. That is you. That is why I chose you."

Loki almost laughed, but the sound caught in her throat. "And if I fail?"

"When the tree takes Odin, your father, and he dies, the nine realms will see Ragnarok."

"...Another great war," Loki muttered. Then she turned and left without another word.
