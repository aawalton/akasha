CHAPTER 9

“We seeded survival knowledge in institutions because institutions outlast individuals. We did not account for the possibility that all institutions would fail simultaneously. This was an error in our modelling. The candidate must function without institutional support. Adjust the selection parameters accordingly.”- Codex of the Long Watch, excerpts from ‘the fall of planet illis’

“Wait a minute.” Donna said and stopped walking.

Arthur turned. Steve, mid-sentence about the astronomical alignment of Mithraic temples, took another three steps before realising he’d lost his audience.

“Before we go haring off after some underground temples in the middle of the apocalypse, can we acknowledge the bleeding obvious? We are three random people who got caught in this mess like everyone else,” she said.

Arthur knew what was coming. The thought had been sitting with him ever since the stone went dark. A riddle was not a briefing, and three people with kitchen knives were a long way from being a response team.

“We should tell someone. There are people far better qualified to deal with this than we are,” he said.

“Exactly.”

Steve rubbed the back of his neck. “Shame. For a second there, I thought we were about to embark on an epic quest.”

“We just killed a monster with a multitool and a bit of pipe, Steve.” Donna gave him a look. “I’m fine with quests. I’d just prefer one where the authorities know what’s going on and can provide backup with proper weapons.”

Arthur nodded. “Let’s find the nearest police station. We tell them what we’ve seen, show them the stone if we have to, and let people with resources handle it.”

The air beside them bent and thin blue lines folded into a familiar geometry. Donna exhaled audibly.

“Authority response capacity is compromised. Also, your theory about more qualified people for this particular test is incorrect.” The construct stated.

“Is that so?” Arthur asked without turning his head.

“Emergency infrastructure demand exceeds functional thresholds. Institutional response probability is low. Furthermore, they do not have the candidacy markers needed for activation.” The construct replied.

“‘Furthermore’... well, that's me convinced,” Donna said.

“Going to the MET is still the sensible thing to do, and frankly, I don’t trust your tosh enough to skip due diligence. Furthermore……” Arthur paused, watching for any reaction from the construct. When none came, he continued. “....I haven’t the foggiest what you mean by ‘candidacy markers’.”

"I do not possess motivations that would warrant distrust."

“Everyone says that right before they start having them and the next thing you know, you are tied up in a basement.” Donna said.

The construct had assessed the probability of institutional failure. It had not, Arthur noticed, suggested what they should do instead.

It remained a moment longer, then dissolved. Arthur shook his head and turned east towards the station.

The police station was six streets east, near the junction with Bishopsgate. The walk took thirty minutes through streets that had deteriorated further in the hour since they’d last crossed them.

More survivors had emerged from the buildings and were gathering in frightened knots along the pavement, inside shops and beneath awnings. Arguments flared and fizzled out. A woman sat cross-legged in the middle of the road, crying quietly amid a scatter of dead phones. An elderly man in a suit strode off with his briefcase in hand, as though sheer commitment to routine might somehow reverse whatever had happened to the sky. The Ministry had offered much the same advice the last time the world went to shit: keep calm and carry on.

They passed two more bodies. Arthur had stopped checking pulses. It was the same result every time, and each time he knelt and found nothing, the weight of it compounded. Donna noticed him pass the bodies without stopping and said nothing.

The police station announced itself by smell before they turned the corner.

Blood, sweat, and the sharp chemical tang of an overturned first-aid cabinet. The crowd outside the station had spilled across the road, seventy maybe eighty people pressing towards the entrance while a handful of officers in high-vis jackets tried to maintain a perimeter with nothing but their voices and their bodies.

The barricade across the road was improvised with overturned benches, traffic bollards, and a section of metal fencing dragged from a construction site.

Something lay in the street directly in front of the station. The creature was smaller than the one they had faced in the alley, its body hacked apart with a fire axe that still protruded from the mess. Two officers stood near it. They were looking everywhere except down.

Arthur studied the barricade. The officers had decent instincts. The perimeter was tight and the weight distribution of the fencing section was mostly sound. But the south side had a gap where the bollards didn’t quite meet the wall, it was wide enough for anything low and fast to break through.

Inside the station, things were worse than the pandemonium outside. The lobby was a portrait of human crisis. Swearing, shouting and crying all mingled together to an extent that it was now just noise. There were injured people leaning against every available wall, some holding makeshift bandages to wounds that were still bleeding. A woman argued loudly with an officer who had nothing to give her except empty assurances.

A child sat alone on a plastic chair near the entrance and every time an adult passed, she looked up; none stopped. Arthur looked away from the child before his mind lost its grip on the rest of him.

The power was out here too and the glowsticks cast a flat green light that made everyone in the lobby look like they were already dead. An officer behind the desk was writing on paper with a pencil, actual paper, actual pencil, logging names and injuries because every computer in the building was now a black screen and a lump of plastic. Arthur wondered how many hours it would be before someone started asking whether anyone knew where to find a typewriter and how to use one.

Arthur looked at the scene and saw what any structural engineer sees when a system is at capacity. The point just before failure. The officers were the load-bearing elements, the civilians were the live load, and the ratio was horribly wrong. Too much weight on too few supports. One more surge through those doors and the whole thing would collapse into a building full of frightened people with no one in charge.

They took a place at the back of what was trying to be a queue and moved up it at about a foot every few minutes.

Arthur listened, because there was nothing else to do with the time. A man in a courier’s uniform was explaining to everyone within reach that it was definitely gas, and that there had been a main under Moorgate since before the war and the council had known about it for years. Lazy bastards, the lot of them.

A woman with a bandaged hand told the courier it was the Russians. The woman behind her said it was a solar flare, which had killed the phones and changed the sky, and that the animals were coming up because the sewers were too warm. An older man stood against the wall with a rosary and did not join the argument. He had the settled look about him as if he had been expecting this since he was a boy.

Two places ahead, a young man in a suit was trying to tell the officer at the desk about the writing. "Blue writing. I saw— there were blue words. Floating," he kept saying. The officer asked for his name and wrote it down.

“Everybody saw blue words, you git, now move on.” Someone said from the right.

A woman with him put a hand on his back and moved him away from the queue.

Steve in a queue was never a good idea. A queue did not offer enough stimulation so he would normally end up manufacturing some. This time the object of his attention turned out to be the officers’ caps.

“Red and white caps, the Met wear black and white. This lot are the only force in the country who don’t,” he said.

“Because this is the City of London Police,” Donna said. “One square mile and their own commissioner. They are not the Met and they never have been.”

“The pattern is Scottish. A chief constable in Glasgow put it on his men’s hats in 1932 because the public kept mistaking them for bus drivers. He ended up running MI5.”

“And you have been standing in this queue for twenty minutes deciding when to tell me that.”

“I was waiting for a natural opening,” Steve said.

The officer at the desk had a system going. Names on the left, injuries in the middle, a tick or a cross on the right. He never looked up.

Steve watched him do it for a while with something close to admiration. “That is the first competent piece of data management I have seen tonight.”

“He is writing names on paper, Steve.”

“He is writing names on paper that will still be legible in four hundred years. Every hospital record in this city is currently a black rectangle. That book,” he said, pointing at the officer's notebook, “will outlive all of it.”

“Our turn to get in the book and become immortals,” she said as they reached the front of the line.

When their turn came, the officer asked for occupations without lifting his eyes. Arthur gave his. Donna gave hers. Steve gave his, and then added that it might be worth noting down the British Academy, since eventually the question was going to come down to credibility.

“He doesn’t need your CV,” Donna said.

The officer wrote archaeologist and asked what they wanted. Arthur said he wanted to talk to whoever was in charge, and the officer replied, without malice and without much hope, “So does everybody else.”

It took forty minutes to catch the attention of someone senior enough to listen. The sergeant was a broad-shouldered man in his fifties, his shirtsleeves rolled to the elbow with a smear of someone else’s blood on his forearm that he either hadn’t noticed or had stopped caring about. His eyes carried the exhaustion of a man who had been holding a position for hours and was running out of things to hold it with.

“Sir,” Arthur said, leaning over the counter. “Three things. Quickly. I’m a structural engineer. She’s a security consultant. He’s an archaeologist. We’re not concussed, hysterical or making this up. There’s a dead creature in an alley two streets east, killed forty minutes ago, and it matches the thing your officers hacked down with a fire axe outside. Phones are out across London. The casualties were simultaneous. Whatever this is, it isn’t a chemical attack or a gas leak.”

The sergeant looked at Arthur the way you look at someone who has just told you something you cannot afford to engage with.

"There's more. A stone on Cannon Street activated about an hour ago. Well, activated, maybe the wrong word…. it started glowing and projected text, a specific reference to Roman infrastructure under the City. We think it's a location and there is something underneath the city that's connected to whatever is happening. We can take you to the stone now or we can take you to the corpse. Either one. Both."

“Son,” the sergeant rubbed his eyes and said carefully, “I've had a hundred people come through here tonight telling me the sky's talking to them. We're treating this as a mass-casualty incident, and stress reactions or hallucinations are part of the picture.”

"It wasn't a hallucination, I study Roman London for a living. The text referenced the Walbrook river, the Temple of Mithras, and the relationship between the two. That's not a stress reaction. You don't hallucinate academically accurate archaeological riddles." Steve said.

“Did you see any notifications?” Steve asked quietly. “After the sky changed. Blue text. Calibration event. Anything like that.”

The sergeant paused, his eyes flicking to his hands before they came back to Arthur. It was brief, a fraction of a second, but Arthur had his answer before the man spoke.

This man had seen it and filed it away in a place where you put things that can’t be real because you have a lobby full of bleeding civilians and admitting that floating text appeared in your vision is not compatible with maintaining command authority.

“No,” the sergeant said firmly.

“Look, at the minute we're treating this as a coordinated attack. Chemical, biological... maybe terrorism. Until we know what we're actually dealing with, the priority is keeping people calm and keeping them off the streets.”

"And the creatures?" Donna asked.

"We're aware of the… animals," the sergeant said, his voice betraying the barest bit of hesitation. "Armed response has been deployed where possible but communications are down and we're operating blind."

“Then I want this logged,” Donna said, her voice calm. “The officer at the desk's got our names, but he'll have a stack of others by now. We're heading for a possible secondary site near Cannon Street. If we come back with information, I want a proper callback route, not another forty minutes sat in your lobby.”

The sergeant looked at her properly for the first time. Whatever he saw in her stance made him pause longer than he had with Arthur.

"You're going to investigate it yourselves. This riddle that you have seen?"

"We're going to look. We won't engage anything we don't have to. When we're back, who do we ask for?"

He held her gaze for a beat, then lifted a pencil. "Names."

Donna spelled their names. Below their names the sergeant wrote a number, paused, crossed it out, then wrote his surname and a unit designation.

"Sergeant Garrick. Ask for me or for whoever's holding command if I'm not on shift."

"Garrick." Donna nodded.

"So you're not going to investigate the stone." Her voice was not accusatory. It was a professional recognising the limits of another professional's capacity.

"I'm saying that if there's a glowing rock somewhere in the City, it's going to have to wait until I've dealt with the people in my lobby who are actively dying."

Arthur couldn't argue with that. The man was doing his job with the tools he had and the information he was willing to accept. That the tools were inadequate and the information was incomplete wasn't his fault.

“Sergeant.” Arthur waited until the man looked up from the counter. “Your south side is open. Where the bollards run out against the bank wall there is a gap at ground level, about half a metre. If anything gets through there it comes up behind your men.”

“Turn the bench over, that long one your people are sitting on.” Arthur continued when the Sergeant did not object. “Turn it on its side across the gap, then drop the fencing section on top of it and weigh the foot down with a bollard. One man, ten minutes.”

Garrick tapped the pencil, considering the suggestion, then he turned his head and called out to someone. A constable came over, and Garrick told him about the bench.

They turned to get out of the station, but Donna went to the main desk instead of the door.

"The girl by the— the one in the green coat. She's been on that chair the whole time we've been in here, and nobody has taken her name."

The officer looked up at her, then past her at the chair, and turned to a clean page.

Donna crossed the lobby and crouched in front of the chair until her head was lower than the girl’s. It took a while to get an answer out of her and Donna waited for it without hurrying her. Then she went back to the desk and stood over the officer until he had written it down. Only then did she leave.

“Well, that went exactly as the geometry predicted.” Steve said, once they were outside.

Donna glanced back at the station doors. “It’s not their fault. They’re drowning.”

“Doesn’t change our situation,” Arthur said.

The air folded beside them and the construct’s geometry assembled itself with what Arthur interpreted as unhurried precision, the measured pace of a system that had never once been in a rush.

Steve flinched. “You really need to stop doing that. One of these days you’re going to materialise and I’m going to swing the pipe before I think.”

“Projected outcome of institutional escalation: unchanged,” the construct stated.

“I told you so is shorter,” Arthur said.

“I previously indicated the probability of successful external intervention was minimal.”

Arthur stared at the construct. The thing had told them the police wouldn’t help, and they’d gone anyway because that’s what you do. You try the system before you abandon it. The fact that the system had nothing left to give didn’t make trying it wrong, it just made the next decision harder.

He looked back at the station. Through the glass doors he could see the sergeant, dealing with the next crisis in line. The man would hold his lobby together for as long as he could, and when he couldn’t, he’d hold it a little longer anyway, because that’s what people like him did. Arthur understood it and respected it. It was the same instinct that made him shore up failing buildings.

But this had not made the decision any easier. Follow a riddle from a glowing rock into an underground Roman temple, guided by a floating geometry that won’t explain itself, armed with kitchen knives and a pipe.

Or find shelter and wait for all of it to hopefully blow away.

Donna was looking south, towards Cannon Street. Her brain had processed the options and arrived at the same conclusion Arthur had. There was no cavalry or backup, and no institutional safety net. It came down to the three of them and whatever the stone was pointing towards.

“If we’re doing this, we’re not doing it with kitchen knives.” She said.

“There’s a Toolstation on the corner of Eastcheap. Two streets west from here. If the world wants us to go dungeon crawling, we’re going properly equipped.”

Steve blinked. “Did you just say, ‘dungeon crawling’?”

“I said what I said.”

Arthur smiled. It was the first time since the sky changed that anything had felt like a plan rather than a reaction.

"Toolstation, then the temple and whatever's waiting underneath it," he said.
