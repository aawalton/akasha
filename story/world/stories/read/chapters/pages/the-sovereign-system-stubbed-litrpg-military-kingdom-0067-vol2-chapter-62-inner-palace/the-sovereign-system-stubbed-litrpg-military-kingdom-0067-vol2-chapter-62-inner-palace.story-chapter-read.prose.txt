A tall, effeminate man strode through the open courtyards of the inner palace, his hands tucked neatly into the sleeves of his long, flowing robe. Everywhere he passed, servants and maids stole furtive glances—quick, cautious, as though afraid their gazes might draw his attention.

Their eyes shimmered with awe and reverence, but beneath that, something else stirred—something half-hidden.

Zhao Lianying walked with unwavering poise, each step carefully measured. His high cheekbones and soft brows contrasted with a sharply defined jaw, giving him a beauty so balanced it made his gender difficult to determine at first glance.

An androgynous presence, Zhao was well known within the palace walls. The inner palace, populated entirely by women, admitted only eunuchs among its male ranks—himself included.

And that was why each reverent glance carried a hint of pity… and longing. Without his manhood, Zhao Lianying was seen as little more than a pretty face—adored, but never truly desired.

He was not blind to this. After years of such glances, he had long since understood the minds of those who cast them.

As always, Zhao kept his chin raised as he approached the Western Palace. The building stood apart from the others, constructed in traditional Chinese style—an elegant contrast amidst the palace’s otherwise western refinements.

Its courtyard was a delicate mosaic of color, where a carefully maintained garden flourished. Indigenous flowers bloomed along winding stone paths, painting the scene with vivid brushstrokes of red, yellow, and violet.

Two eunuchs dressed in pale blue robes stood near the entrance. As Zhao approached, they cupped their fists and bowed low in greeting.

“Greetings, Lord.”

“Is the Noble Consort inside?” Zhao asked, his delicate voice smooth, devoid of warmth or inflection.

“She is within… with Taijian Gong. He just arrived.”

Zhao's eyes widened. A frown crept across his face.

“The Grand Supervisor?” he repeated, his tone suddenly sharp. “Move aside!”

A wave of unease rose in his chest as he pushed past the two eunuchs. Neither dared stop him.

What is that old bastard doing here? he thought, heart pounding. This can’t be good.

Gone was his usual calm. Panic gnawed at him as he rushed through the palace corridors. The echo of his footsteps bounced off the walls, and just as he rounded a corner—

The sharp sound of shattering porcelain brought him to a halt. A wail followed—guttural, raw, and unfiltered. The kind of sound that made the hairs on the back of one’s neck stand on end.

Dread pooled in his stomach.

Without hesitation, Zhao darted around the corner and entered the main bedroom.

There, on her knees, clinging to the robe of a familiar elderly man, was a figure he knew all too well. The older man wore the same courtly robes as Zhao himself—distinctive, austere. The Grand Eunuch.

“Taijian Gong… what is the meaning of this?” Zhao asked, voice taut with alarm.

His eyes darted between the old man and the woman groveling before him. Her figure was petite, yet gracefully curved. Powdered makeup streaked her tear-soaked cheeks, and her once-pristine hair had begun to come undone.

It was Lady Bei—the Noble Consort. The very woman Zhao had sworn to serve.

“Z-Zhao!” she cried, her voice breaking. “Tell me it isn’t true… TELL ME!”

She shuffled forward on her knees, dragging herself across the polished floor. Tears spilled freely, washing away the hours of careful preparation.

“My Lady, what are you doing!? Get up at once!” Zhao gasped, his composure cracking. He bent low to help her, glancing nervously at the Grand Eunuch looming nearby.

They were in the presence of the Queen’s own right hand.

To act in such a way was unsightly. Zhao could already imagine the whispers that would follow—the snide remarks the other eunuchs would exchange behind his back.

Still, he tried to lift Lady Bei to her feet. But the petite woman was like dead weight, her knees refusing to support her as she clung to the floor, her grief only growing more irrational with each passing moment.

Frustration simmered in Zhao’s chest. He turned sharply to Taijian Gong.“What is she talking about?” he demanded. “What have you done?”

The older eunuch was silent for a long moment. His gaze, once filled with cunning and superiority, now seemed… hollow.It was strange—Taijian Gong was known for arrogance woven into his very bones, yet now, none of that remained.

Only solemnity.And sorrow.

“The Second Prince is dead,” the Grand Eunuch finally said. “Killed while retrieving our troops from the Qin Empire.”

Zhao’s mind went blank.

The room spun. Lady Bei’s wails became distant, like echoes from another world.

Prince Lyon is… dead?

Impossible.

After everything we did... everything we endured?

His legs gave out slightly. He staggered, barely keeping himself upright.

He met Taijian Gong’s gaze—this man he’d sparred with for years in the shadows, each trying to secure influence as the princes came of age. There was no smugness now. No glint of triumph. No trace of guilt, either.

Yet still, a tide of fury and bitterness surged in Zhao’s chest.

“Leave us,” he whispered.

Taijian Gong gave a faint nod and departed without a word.

Now only Zhao and Lady Bei remained. She had curled into herself, sobbing in ragged gasps on the floor. Her grief echoed through the chamber, raw and real.

Usually, Zhao would help her compose herself—apply fresh powder, adjust her robes, prepare her to receive the King if he chose to visit.

But not today.

Today, he didn’t care.

All he felt was resentment. Cold and heavy.

“Th-The King is coming!” came a shout from outside, a maid’s panicked voice echoing into the room.

Zhao turned sharply. The urgency in her tone struck him like a slap. A shadow passed over his face—but then his expression hardened.

Without hesitation, he dropped to one knee beside Lady Bei and seized her gently by the shoulders.

“That man is coming,” he said, his voice low but firm. “So listen to me carefully…”

A few minutes later...

Lady Bei sat upon the lounging chair in the center of the room, her posture weak, her expression hollow. Streaks of ruined makeup marred her powdered face—tears still clung to her lashes, framing her grief in a portrait of fragile beauty.

She was the very picture of heartbreak.

Zhao stood nearby, composed once more. His gaze held no warmth as he watched. Only cold detachment.

Then came a soft voice.

“Bei…”

A rotund man entered, dressed in black robes trimmed with gold. His figure was heavy, his presence unmistakable.

The King.

A flicker of disgust passed over Zhao’s face before he forced it away.

The King approached Lady Bei and gently took her into his embrace. The sight was almost comical—his large belly made the gesture look awkward and stiff.

Sliding into the seat beside her, he pulled her close with one arm, stroking her hair.

“I’m so sorry, Bei,” he murmured. “You were right… I should have let Lyon stay in the kingdom.”

The King’s voice was heavy with grief, but it carried an unmistakable plea—one that begged for forgiveness.

His subservience before his Noble Consort was nothing new to Zhao, nor to the maids who had observed the two together in the quiet halls of the inner palace.

It wasn’t exactly a secret that Lady Bei had the King wrapped around her finger.

Due to strict rules governing the inner palace, no uncastrated men were allowed entry. As a result, much of the court remained ignorant of what truly transpired behind these gilded doors.

But Zhao knew.

Lady Bei sat stiffly as the King tried to pull her into his arms. Yet despite her unresponsiveness, he did not react with frustration.

“What can I do to make it right, Bei?” he murmured, his tone desperate. “How will you ever forgive me?”

“There is nothing that will bring back Lyon,” she replied, her eyes hollow, fixed on nothing. She looked utterly broken—lost in a sea of grief.

With her son’s death, her influence had died with him. Though she still held the title of Noble Consort, it now held little weight. At any moment, she could be cast aside on the King’s whim.

Affection could not protect her forever. As time wore away her beauty, so too would it wear away the King's favor. That was the fate of a consort who failed to produce an heir.

Zhao met her gaze briefly.

She gave a small, nearly imperceptible nod.

He bowed his head in silent acknowledgment, then turned to the nearby dresser. Upon it sat two ornate bronze cups beside a flagon of wine.

With practiced care, he retrieved them and placed them on the table in front of the lounge. The King, still focused entirely on Lady Bei, barely noticed his approach.

Zhao stepped back, eyes shifting toward the entrance—as if anticipating something.

Moments later, a maid arrived with a bundle of incense, her expression flustered. Zhao accepted it without a word and calmly arranged it on the table beside the wine and ceremonial cups.

Only then did King Julius seem to realize what was happening.

“…Thank you, Zhao.”

The King reached out to lift the flagon, but a sudden hand stopped him.

“No…” Lady Bei whispered. “Let me pour first.”

Sliding from the lounge, she knelt before the table. Her hands trembled as she poured wine into the first cup, then passed the flagon to the King.

A fresh tear traced down her cheek, making her appear even more fragile—an image of grief that pierced the heart.

King Julius followed her lead, kneeling slowly and pouring his own.

Zhao approached once more, his expression unreadable. From within his robe, he produced a jade token—delicate, intricately carved—and handed it to Lady Bei.

She took it with reverent care, her gaze fixed on it as if the token carried her son’s very soul. She stared at it, unmoving, lost in memory.

The King’s features tightened as he watched.

He raised his cup with trembling fingers. Wine spilled, cascading down his hand. But he didn’t flinch. He didn’t even seem to notice.

“My son…” the King began, his voice trembling. “The heavens have called you to an untimely rest. I should have protected you—kept you close in these times of turmoil. But it was my own expectations that sent you to your death.”

A heavy sigh escaped him, and a look of grief set onto his features.

Lady Bei gently placed the jade token between them on the table, her gaze never leaving its surface. Then, wordlessly, she picked up her ceremonial cup and held it in both hands.

“My sweet child,” she whispered, “you should have outlived us. A mother should never have to bury her son… nor be asked to continue on after such loss. You were my heart… my soul. Now that you're gone, I don’t know how I will endure.”

Tears burst forth once more, breaking the stillness with her sobs. As her shoulders shook, a fat hand reached out, resting gently on her arm.

She flinched, startled, spilling some of her wine.

Then, slowly, her sobs faded. She bowed her head, allowing the silence to settle.

King Julius raised his cup to the ceiling, his eyes lifting as if they could pierce through the firmament and reach the heavens.

“I offer this cup as tribute,” he said solemnly. “Lyon… may you watch over us—and over the kingdom—from beyond.”

He turned slightly, waiting.

Lady Bei lifted her chin. Her hand trembled as she raised her cup, her voice like cracked porcelain.

“Take this cup, my dear son… If this lets me hold you again, then let it take what little remains of me.”

Without hesitation, she drank deeply.

The King frowned, but only for a moment. Then he too brought the cup to his lips and downed its contents in a single motion. Wine dribbled from his chin, but he paid it no mind.

Instead, he reached for the jade token—

“Huh?”

He wavered slightly, his fingers hovering over the carved surface. A dazed look crossed his face.

His eyes shifted to Lady Bei, who now stared directly at him—tear-streaked and unmoving, her gaze burning with accusation.

“You killed our son, Julius,” she said coldly. “You sent him into enemy territory, knowing he wouldn’t return. You wanted him gone. You wanted to remove him from the line of succession.”

“What?” Julius gasped, blinking rapidly. “That’s… nonsense…”

His words slurred. His pupils were unfocused, his breath shallow as he tried to remain upright.

“It is done now, my foolish husband,” Lady Bei said, her voice eerily calm. “We will cross the Yellow River together. There, you may beg for forgiveness… and perhaps, in death, you can make peace with the child you betrayed.”

A soft smile curled at the corners of her lips.

“Lyon… Mother will be there soon.”

And with that, Lady Bei collapsed backward, her lifeless body settling against the polished floor.

Panic overtook the King. His hand clutched at his chest, wheezing. He turned toward Zhao, gasping for air.

“G-Get the apothecary…” he stammered, his voice little more than a croak. “P–poisoned…”

His strength drained rapidly. Moments later, the large man crumpled beside the Noble Consort—motionless.

There was no flailing. No desperate struggle.

Only stillness.

It looked like a grieving ritual—a double suicide. A scene that, while tragic, would not be unfamiliar within this place.

Zhao had watched the entire ceremony unfold in silence.

His expression remained impassive.

“Such a pity…” Zhao murmured, releasing a small sigh as his eyes fell on Lady Bei. She looked as if she were merely sleeping—peaceful, serene.

But when his gaze shifted to King Julius, his expression twisted.

“Useless bastard… right until the very end,” he scowled.

A sharp breath escaped him as he spat onto the polished floor, venting the contempt he had long kept buried.

Without another glance, he turned and walked to the far side of the room. There, he knelt before an old chest, his sleeves brushing the dustless floor. From within his robes, he retrieved a small bronze key.

With practiced familiarity, he slid the key into the lock and turned it.

A soft click sounded. The lid creaked open.

As light spilled into the chest’s depths, it illuminated fine layers of silken fabric—folded and arranged with care. Zhao moved with purpose, brushing them aside until something solid came into view.

A small stone statue.

Coiled around a downward-facing sword was a serpent, its mouth slightly open, fangs bared. Despite its size, the sculpture exuded an ominous presence.

Zhao reached out and traced a finger along the serpent’s spine. The texture was cool beneath his touch. A grin slowly formed at the corners of his lips.

“Master,” he whispered, voice low and reverent. “It is done.”
