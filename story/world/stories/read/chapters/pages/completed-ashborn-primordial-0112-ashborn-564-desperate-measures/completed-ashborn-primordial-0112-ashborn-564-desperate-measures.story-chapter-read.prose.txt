“Please,” Maiya pleaded. “You must trust us!”

“Trust demons, milady!? You ask us to trust demons?” the mother cried, clutching her two children tightly, glaring at the Asura rescue ships as though they were some sort of menace.

In a single week, demonkind had made it as deep as Hiranya, ransacking and torching every village, town, and city along the way.

Saran had once been a place of wonder for Maiya, back when she’d just left Brij with Vir. It was the first town she’d ever set foot in, and as a village girl, she’d found it magical.

The seaside port was every bit as idyllic as she’d remembered… but all of that was about to change. The town she knew was already being destroyed as demons breached its eastern gate. The very same gate she’d come through all those years ago.

“These demons aren’t the same!” Maiya said for what had to have been the thousandth time over the past week. She’d said it so many times that her throat had gone hoarse. “They’ll be here at any moment. Please, you have to hurry!”

And yet, the mother still refused to take Maiya’s hand. Despite knowing who she was. Despite knowing what she stood for.

And every second Maiya wasted on her was one not spent on getting another victim to safety.

She’d learned that lesson the hard way, having long ago given up attempting to convince anyone who wasn’t willing to leave.

But one look at those children and Maiya found herself unable to leave them to their fate.

“They will not hurt you,” Maiya said as soothingly as she could manage. “They answer to me. Every last one of them.”

“You truly vouch for them?” the mother asked skeptically.

“With my life. These demons will take you to safety and protect you with everything they have. I’ve already saved four hundred people with those ships today. Children even younger than yours. Not one of them has been hurt.”

The woman glanced at the square, where yet more townsfolk refused to board. An old farmer even swung a hoe at one of the Asura herding people toward the ramp, for all the good that did.

When the Asura didn’t even turn as he took the blow to the back of his head, the farmer to dropped the implement and fled in terror.

Likely right into the arms of the assaulting demons. Right into his death.

“Where are these skyships headed?” the woman asked.

“South, over the sea to Rani,” Maiya replied. “We’ve set up camps where you’ll have food, water, and shelter.”

“And then?”

“We’ll return you to your homes as soon as this war is over.”

The woman scoffed. “More like those demons’ll kill every last one of us, is what’ll happen.”

“That won’t happen,” Maiya replied, but the woman saw the utter lack of confidence in her voice.

“I can only pray you’re right, milady,” she said at last. “Fine. Arjun, Stuti? Stay close. We’re leaving.”

Maiya took the youngest of her children so she could move faster, and they finally crossed the square toward the ramp.

The boy was thankfully quiet in her arms, staring up at her in fear.

“It’ll be alright,” she said with what she hoped was a soothing smile. “Everything will be alright.”

They were ten paces from the ramp when the sky turned to fire and night became day.

The first volley of Aspect arts came down less than a hundred paces away.

Maiya turned away from the explosions, protecting the child from the blistering heat with her back.

The house they’d just left was gone, reduced to rubble that immediately caught fire.

It was, tragically, not the only thing. Arjun’s mother hadn’t been fast enough.

They’d been fifteen paces behind. Just fifteen paces was all that had been the difference between life and death.

In the span of less time than it took to blink, Arjun had lost his mother and only sister, doomed to grow up without them.

The boy stared blankly at his burning house, saying nothing.

Maiya wondered if he even knew what had just happened.

Better he doesn’t, she thought.

As much as she wanted to grieve, every moment wasted was one she wasn’t saving another soul.

And so, with a heavy heart, she Blinked to the airship, handing the lost boy to the Asura.

“Take him and head for Rani!” she called to the Asura at the ramp. “Get airborne as quickly as you can. Don’t wait for me.”

To his credit, the Asura didn’t argue or ask questions, responding to her order with only a nod.

He knew what she could do. They all did. They’d seen it a dozen times or more over the past week.

Maiya needed no protection.

If only she could say the same for everyone else.

If Maiya had gotten through to the mother even two minutes earlier, she and Stuti would have lived.

Fire, it seemed, did what an hour spent pleading could not.

The townsfolk who’d outright refused to leave now ran for the ship, scrambling over one another in desperation to reach the ramp.

All of them too late.

The Asura hauled humans up well past Saunak’s intended limits for the craft, but there were still so many when the Aindri riders came charging over the hill with their Ash Mules, the rest of their warband not far behind.

Maiya turned her back to the airship and drew her talwar, her hundred-odd Asura forming up on her without an order.

Against the thousand they faced, a hundred was plenty to utterly dominate under normal circumstances. Especially since Jagath wasn’t even present, having established a forward camp in the human realm.

Incapacitating a thousand demons armed with Warrior Chakras, however, was another matter entirely.

But that didn’t mean she was entirely toothless.

A single B Grade Fire Barrier stopped the charging beasts in their tracks, causing them to rear up, throwing off several riders.

One demon charged through, but she caught his spear on her bracer and deflected it. A quick yank had the demon falling off his mount, and an Empowered chop to the back of his neck put him out of the fight.

A Chitran swung an axe at her head next, but she just broke his elbow, forcing him to retreat.

It was with mixed feelings that Maiya realized the demons were not fighting at their full potential.

Though her actions had certainly soured her relationship with demonkind, the effect had been far less pronounced than she’d feared, and there wasn’t a single demon who dared strike her, let alone fire off any Chakra attacks at her.

Perhaps they feared Vir’s divine retribution were he to learn who crippled his wife.

A well founded fear, if she was honest, but it wasn’t just her who got that treatment.

All around her, Asuras occupied as many incoming demons as possible, putting them down nonlethally.

The ones who did fight them treated it more as a duel, backing off with respect the moment they conceded defeat.

If only they’d extend the same courtesy to everyone else…

Those who made it past Maiya slaughtered everyone they came across, and they certainly did had no compunctions about using their Chakras against them.

Maiya had had to end more than one innocent soul’s suffering, and she knew her Asuras had been forced to do the same.

At the very least, their actions bought the refugees time. Time enough for more of her airships to land and take on passengers, saving yet more lives.

Then the rest of the enemy warband caught up, and the arts began to fall in earnest.

A volley of red streaks soared in an arc high into the air.

Having seen this exact manifestation of Aspect of the Inferno before, Maiya countered by launching two spells of her own.

First, the B grade Water Spear, which fired on an intercept course.

Focused by her Imperium Artifact down to a pinprick in diameter, it skewered the spell midair, detonating it prematurely, bursting into a thousand streaks of fire that rained upon the heads of the fleeing crowd, setting ablaze every thatched roof upon which it landed.

It was then that her A Grade Deluge fully manifested, bringing with it the wrath of water.

Except where she’d used her Artifact to focus the first of her two spells, she did the exact opposite now, diffusing its sheer destructive potential to cover the better part of the town, turning it nonlethal and utterly ineffective against the attacking demons.

But destruction was not Maiya’s aim. What she’d wanted was rain, and rain was precisely what she got.

The burning roofs doused immediately and the people set ablaze were saved by the squall. It passed as soon as it had arrived, leaving a soaked—but otherwise safe—town behind.

Dozens would have perished were it not for her intervention. As it was, only three failed to rise again.

Those, her Asura carried away. Naga dissidents and human healers were aboard every airship of hers. If they could be saved, they would be.

Maiya had averted this little crisis—one of dozens—and it was abundantly obvious to her and the attacking demons why her strategy was doomed to fail.

A single demonic art had taken both B and A rank spells working in tandem and augmented by Maiya’s unique abilities to thwart, in addition to a coordinated rescue effort on the part of the Asura.

It hadn’t even been all that powerful an art. A mere Aspect of B grade-equivalent strength.

To save everyone across the realm, Maiya would need a thousand equal to her. Perhaps ten thousand.

In the distance, another Aindri charge tore into the town, its riders whooping as their spears and their mounts’ hooves cut down humans fleeing for the boarding ramps.

Maiya looked on with horror in the realization that she wouldn’t reach them in time. Even if she did, what of the people here? She could only be in one place at once.

Her chest clenched, the scene playing out at a fraction of normal speed thanks to her Haste.

And then, faster than everything in that slow-motion world, fell a red meteor from the sky.

Maiya’s heart nearly stopped… until she recognized the falling object for what it was. Then she roared in elation.

A Bairan giant slamming down upon the ground had all the effect Maiya hoped it would, and then some.

The small crater his landing made threw dirt in every direction, making the Aindri mounts panic in fright.

Several reared, throwing off their riders while others toppled over, unable to slow themselves in time.

And just when they’d recovered, when their riders had crawled out from under their fallen beasts and drawn their talwars… Balancer of Scales crushed them.

Pinning the attacking demons… and only the demons, leaving every man, woman, and child between them entirely untouched.

Maiya did not know a single demon who wielded that ability more masterfully than Cirayus. Not even Vir.

That was the sort of skill that came from centuries of relentless practice, and he wielded the cudgel of an art with as much delicacy as a master painter did with her brush.

“Cirayus!” Maiya cried, running up to him.

“Go, lass,” he said, already moving to deal with more attackers. “I apologize for my tardiness. A new front broke out near Daha or I would have come here sooner. Get those you have into the air. I’ll keep them off the ships until you’re safe.”

“I’m not leaving you to—”

“Lass…” the giant gave her an exasperated smile.

Maiya choked back her next words when she realized just who she was worrying about.

Cirayus drew Sikandar and flared his Crown, a metaphysical giant ten times his already impressive size appearing behind him.

“Go. I shall reunite with you as soon as I finish here.”

Cirayus held the enormous blade off to his side and his avatar mirrored the motion, daring the demons to attack.

When not one of them took his challenge, he bellowed a deep, terrifying laugh that drove home just why he was called the Ravager. Why the Bairans had named an entire art after him, and why no demon was stupid enough to fight him in open combat.

If the Asura were each the equivalent of a battalion, then Cirayus was an army, and there was no spell better at controlling large crowds than Balancer of Scales.

To the point where even if Maiya had a hundred Asura with the ability, she might well have had her way.

Alas, of all the forces loyal to her, only Cirayus had that art, while their enemy had Roshan and Raja Thaman. Maiya thanked the gods that the Raja hadn’t directly participated in the war.

She gave Cirayus a single nod before dashing to an Aindri who was trying to creep up on the fleeing villagers from behind.

A C Grade Arc spell sent him spasming into the land of unconsciousness.

Launching herself onto the deck of the last airship that was already struggling to the air, she turned to see one of the few Altani mejai she’d brought with her struggling to catch up.

She’d been about to jump back off to help the mejai when an Iksana rose from his shadow, carrying a prana suppression collar that he snapped around his throat.

One of Saunak’s more recent inventions. One that he created in secret cooperation with Jagath, entirely unknown to her or Vir.

While not as powerful as the Artifacts upon which they’d been based, they were more than capable of sealing a mejai’s abilities long enough to kill them. It was what had spelled the S Rank spellcaster’s doom at Khantaghati Pass.

It was what killed this poor woman now.

Maiya knew it was too late, even before the Iksana’s katar slid through the mejai’s chest, her every protection nullified by the collar.

Maiya swore under her breath, stifling her urge to look away as the mejai fell.

It was happening in every battle now. In every engagement in which mejai appeared, so too did the Iksana, either snapping the collars on themselves or ferrying through the shadows Pagan Order infiltrators who did.

The airship rose to the sky, and already the demons below had begun looting the Hiranyan town they’d just sacked.

The very first town she’d ever visited in her life. Where she and Vir had been conned by some street urchins.

And she could do nothing to save it.

A week of toiling relentlessly, spending every hour of every day saving lives, and all she’d done was save a few thousand from an army that had already killed at least ten times that number, ransacking their way nearly uncontested toward the realm’s core cities.

Daha, Sonam, and the likes still stood, but at this rate, it would not be long before they, too, fell.

And no mejai army in the realm, no Kin’jal wall would be strong or sturdy enough to stop the reckoning that was yet to come.
