I ACCIDENTALLY BECAME A GUILD THERAPIST

Chapter 26 – Alondra, Session 2

ARC III: THE WORLD REMEMBERS

SYNOPSIS: Alondra Fayeblossom returns for session two with thicker armor. Livia waits. Something falls. Glimmer enters. Behind Glimmer: someone who should not be there, with a recording crystal that is absolutely on.

Livia was at the planning room door when she heard it.

Not a conversation. Two names. That was all.

From the main corridor:

“Alondra.”

“Miles.”

Then nothing.

She stayed in the doorway and did not look directly at the corridor. You learned, in this job, when looking was the wrong thing to do. She looked at the floor instead, which gave her just enough peripheral vision to register: two people, stopped mid-corridor, facing each other at the distance of people who had once stood much closer.

Miles had the posture of someone who had known this was coming and had decided how to hold himself for it. Contained. A specific kind of contained—not cold, but careful. The way you carry something breakable.

Alondra’s posture was different. Hers was the stillness of someone caught mid-step, recalibrating in real time. Her chin dipped. Just slightly.

One second. Maybe two.

Then Alondra walked toward the planning room. Miles walked in the other direction. Neither of them looked back.

Seven months in one corridor.

Two names.

Whatever happened between them has a shape I can almost see.

I am not going to ask about it today.

Today is session two.

Session two has its own work.

Alondra came in and sat down.

She had notes. More than last time. A proper binder, this time, with dividers. The kind of binder that said: I have done significant work since we last spoke and I would like that to be visible.

The armchair went blue immediately.

Alondra did not notice the armchair.

The armchair noticed Alondra.

“I’ve been reading,” Alondra said. She opened the binder to the first divider. “Rogerian Person-Centered model. Unconditional positive regard as the core mechanism. I’ve been cross-referencing it with the attachment literature—” she paused, —“specifically the work on anxious attachment and caregiving systems. I think that framework might be more applicable to my presenting issue than the standard competency-gap model.”

Livia looked at the binder. At the dividers. At Alondra’s hands, which were steady on the cover.

She prepared.

She built something between last week and this week.

Something she could stand behind.

“That’s a lot of work,” Livia said.

“I wanted to come prepared,” Alondra said.

“For what?”

A small pause. The binder stayed closed.

“For—” Alondra stopped. Tried again. “For whatever you’re going to ask that I don’t have an answer to.”

She knows I’m going to ask something she can’t answer.

She prepared a binder anyway.

The binder is not for the answer.

The binder is so she has somewhere to put her hands.

Livia let the binder sit on the table between them. She did not touch it. She did not dismiss it. She just said:

“Before the frameworks—last week you said there was something you weren’t ready to say. Is it still the same thing?”

Alondra looked at the window.

“Yes.”

“Bigger or smaller than last week?”

A longer pause.

“Bigger,” Alondra said. “I’ve been thinking about it. It’s bigger than I thought it was.”

[Therapist’s Insight: Patient: Alondra Fayeblossom. Session 2. She came prepared to manage this session. She is already off the plan. The armchair is reading moderate-high. Something in her is both relieved and terrified that she came back.]

[The system notes: what she is carrying is not only about methodology. The system notes: the system can see the shape of it. The system notes: the system will not say what it is. That is Alondra’s to say.]

The knock came at minute twelve.

Three knocks. Specific rhythm. Alaric’s rhythm.

Not now.

Absolutely not now.

“One moment,” Livia said to Alondra. She went to the door and opened it six inches.

Alaric was there with a clipboard. He looked past her at Alondra with the expression of a man checking something off an internal list.

“The intake form,” he said. “I sent it four days ago. She’s here, which means she’s here, which I calculated at eleven percent. The form still needs to be completed.”

“Alaric,” Livia said.

“Eleven percent,” he repeated, in case she had missed the significance. “I told Miles she wasn’t coming back. I told Bron she wasn’t coming back. I had it on the board. Eleven percent is—” he stopped. “Anyway. The form.”

From inside the room, Alondra said: “I heard that.”

Alaric processed this.

“The eleven percent isn’t an insult,” he said, to the room generally. “It’s the model. The model said eleven. The model is often wrong when Livia is involved. I have been updating the model. The form is still required.”

Alondra was quiet for a moment.

Then: “Leave it outside the door.”

“There’s a section on previous guild affiliation that requires—”

“Outside. The door.”

Alaric set the clipboard on the floor outside the door with the careful precision of someone complying under protest. He looked at Livia. “The model,” he said, with dignity, “has been updated.”

He left.

Livia closed the door.

Alondra was looking at the window again. But there was something at the corner of her mouth that had not been there before. Not a smile. Adjacent to one.

“Eleven percent,” she said.

“His model is usually accurate,” Livia said. “He’s been updating it.”

“Since when?”

“Since therapy started working.”

The thing adjacent to a smile became more adjacent.

They found a rhythm after that. Alondra was, as she had demonstrated in session one, genuinely intelligent about her own patterns once she let herself look at them. The binder stayed closed. She talked about the guild—not as a professional case study but as a place she had been. Phina before the wedding. Bron before whatever Bron had become in the months she had been gone. Nyx, who she described as "always in a corner, always watching, always pretending he wasn’t."

“That sounds familiar,” Livia said.

“He’s better?” Alondra asked. Not casually.

“He’s different,” Livia said. “Different is usually better, for him.”

Alondra nodded. She was quiet for a moment. Then:

“I told myself I left because of my practice,” she said. “Because I had identified a gap. Which was true.”

“But?”

“But there was something else.” She looked at the window. Not avoidance—more like she needed the light to be somewhere specific while she said this. “Something I did. Before I left. That I haven’t said to anyone.”

Livia’s pen was still. This was the moment. You learned to recognize it—the specific quality of silence before someone said the real thing.

“I—” Alondra started.

The air in the room didn’t just go quiet; it went thin. The kind of thin where you can hear the leather of the armchair sigh under the weight of a secret.

I didn't breathe.

In this job, silence isn't empty space; it’s a bridge.

And Alondra was halfway across it.

“I didn’t mean for it to become what it became,” she whispered. Her voice was different now—stripped of the professional armor. It was just a girl in the middle of a wreckage. “The thing I did... before I left... I—”

Something fell.

It was a ceramic plant pot. Small. It had been on the windowsill. It was now on the floor.

Glimmer came through the gap under the planning room door—which, it turned out, was just wide enough if you were a slime with determination—and pulsed apologetically in the direction of the broken pot.

Behind Glimmer, the door opened.

Livia turned around.

Her brain performed, in rapid sequence, the following operations:

That is a skull.

That skull is wearing a crown.

The crown has stars on it.

The skull has eyes.

The eyes are heart-shaped.

The eyes are pink.

The skull is wearing a coat.

The coat has multiple layers.

The coat has hearts embroidered on it.

The coat also has skulls embroidered on it.

There is pink fur at the collar.

There are skull ornaments on the belt.

The skull is holding something.

The something is a recording crystal.

The recording crystal is glowing.

The skull is whispering.

Livia opened her mouth.

Closed it.

Opened it again.

Her brain, still processing, produced one final output:

Is this the entity that has 847,000 followers on social media?.

This entity lives in this building.

I live in this building.

This entity has a crown.

This entity is a skeleton.

The skeleton has a social media presence larger than most guilds.

The skeleton is currently live.

Morty “The Cute” Calvarium, Level 92 Pastel Lich, was pressed against the doorframe with the body language of someone who had intended to be less visible than he currently was. His layered coat—pastel blue over pink over ivory, hearts and skulls in equal measure, the fur collar slightly crushed from pressing against the doorframe—was doing nothing to help him blend in. The star-tipped crown on his skull was askew. His heart-shaped magenta eyes were wide in the specific way of someone who had made a decision they were currently reassessing.

He was holding a recording crystal on a lanyard and whispering into it.

“Chat,” he whispered, “this was not the plan. The plan was corridor footage. The plan did not account for Glimmer.”

Glimmer pulsed.

“Chat is saying interrupt,” Morty whispered. “Chat is wrong. Chat is often wrong. But—” he looked at the room, at Livia’s expression, at the broken pot, at Alondra who had turned to look at him, —“okay chat was right, we are already interrupting.”

Chat.

He is narrating to chat.

He is live.

He is a skeleton.

He is live-streaming into my therapy session.

He has a crown.

I have so many questions and none of them are in the right order.

“Morty,” Livia said. Her voice came out more composed than she felt, which she attributed to nine months of professional practice with people who routinely defied reasonable expectation.

“Hi,” Morty said. His voice was bright, hollow in the warm way of resonance inside a skull, and deeply, genuinely apologetic. “The crystal is—”

“On,” Livia said.

“On,” Morty confirmed.

“You are live.”

“Technically.”

“Into my therapy session.”

“Glimmer opened the door,” Morty said, with the energy of someone distributing responsibility fairly.

Glimmer pulsed in a color that read as I opened the door because there was a pot falling and I was trying to help. The pot was still on the floor.

“Chat says sorry,” Morty said.

“Chat is not in this room,” Livia said.

“Technically chat is—”

“Morty.”

The crystal went off. He held it in his hand, sheepish, the magenta hearts dimmed slightly with something that read unmistakably as genuine remorse.

From the doorway, Alaric’s voice: “What happened? I heard something fall.”

“Nothing,” Livia said.

“Was that Morty’s voice?”

“Go away, Alaric.”

“The intake form is still on the floor—”

“I know where the form is.”

Footsteps retreating. Then, a beat later, Bron’s voice from somewhere further down the corridor: “Was there a session happening?” Alaric’s voice: “There was.” Bron: “And Morty went in?” Alaric: “Morty and Glimmer.” Bron, after a pause: “Live?” Alaric: “Live.” Bron: “How many viewers?” Alaric: “I don’t know, I’m not—” Bron: “I’m checking.”

Bron is checking the viewer count.

This is my life.

Morty picked up the pot. It had broken into three pieces. He looked at them, then at Livia, with the expression of a skull that was very sorry and had been very sorry before and was aware that being sorry was insufficient.

“I can fix this,” he said. “I have a restoration spell. I also have—” he reached into a coat pocket and produced a small crystal that was identical to his lanyard crystal except with a blue cap, —“an offline recording of the corridor footage. Which was what I was here for. Not the session. The corridor.”

“You were filming the corridor.”

“For ambience,” Morty said. “The guild hall has good afternoon light. I document the guild. I have for three years. I mostly don’t intrude.”

“Mostly.”

“Glimmer opened the door.”

Glimmer pulsed again. The same color as before.

Alondra, who had been quiet through all of this, said: “You document the guild.”

Morty turned to her. His heart-eyes brightened slightly with the reflexive warmth of someone who default-presented as welcoming. “Three years. Mostly corridors and common areas. Some raid footage. Some—” he paused, —“quiet moments. With permission.”

“With permission.”

“Usually.”

Alondra looked at the broken pot on the table where Morty had placed the three pieces. At Glimmer, who was now sitting in the corner emitting a very small, very guilty glow. At the recording crystal in Morty’s hand, which was off but still visible. At Livia.

“Are all your sessions like this?” she asked.

“No,” Livia said.

A beat.

“Some are worse,” she added.

Something in Alondra’s face did something unexpected. Not the almost-smile from earlier. Something older. More tired. The specific expression of someone who has been carrying something heavy for a long time, and has just seen, unexpectedly, a reason to set it down.

“I miss this,” she said. Quietly. Not to anyone in particular. “Not the chaos. The—” she looked for the word, —“the being somewhere that’s actually happening.”

Nobody said anything.

Glimmer pulsed very softly.

Even Morty was still.

[Therapist’s Insight: Patient: Alondra Fayeblossom. The thing she was about to say before the pot fell: the session will not reach it today. But she came back and she stayed through the chaos. The armchair is reading something new: not just distress. Something underneath the distress. Guilt with a specific shape. Not the guilt of a mistake. The guilt of a choice.]

[The system notes: the system knows what the choice was. The system notes: the system will not say it. That is Alondra’s to say. The system notes: she will say it. Not today. But she will say it.]

Morty fixed the pot with a restoration spell that worked perfectly and left a faint shimmer on the repaired surface, which meant it was fixed but visibly fixed, which Livia decided was fine. He apologized three times. He left the offline corridor footage crystal on the table. He said Livia could watch it or not. He said he understood if she preferred not.

He left before she could ask the question she was forming about how long he had been in the guild and why they had never met directly. That question could wait. She had, apparently, unlimited questions for later.

Alondra stood to leave. At the door, she stopped.

“The thing I was going to say,” she said. Not turning around. “I’m going to say it next session.”

“Okay,” Livia said.

“It’s bad,” Alondra said.

“Okay.”

“I’m not—” she stopped. “I want to be clear that I know it was bad. I’ve known for seven months. I just haven’t said it to anyone.”

“That’s a long time to carry something alone,” Livia said.

Alondra’s hand was on the doorframe. Her knuckles were not white. She was holding herself, not gripping.

“Yes,” she said. And left.

After Alondra left and the corridor was quiet and Glimmer had returned to the garden looking sheepish, Livia sat in the planning room with her notepad and the repaired pot and the offline crystal Morty had left and the afternoon lamp casting its non-intrusive light across the table.

She wrote on the Alondra page:

Session 2: She came prepared to manage this. She didn’t. Something is coming in Session 3 that she’s been carrying for seven months and hasn’t said to anyone.

She said: I know it was bad. I’ve known for seven months.

Not: I think it was bad. Not: maybe it was bad. I know it was bad.

Guilt with a specific shape. Not the guilt of a mistake. The guilt of a choice. There’s a difference.

She looked at what she had written.

Then she wrote, underneath, smaller:

The corridor. Miles and Alondra. Two names and the specific quality of people who have history that involves more than professional disagreement. He was careful. She was recalibrating.

Whatever she did— it involves him. Or it involved him. Or it was about him.

I don’t know what it is yet.

But I know the shape of guilt that involves another person.

I know what it looks like when someone has done something to someone, or almost done something, or not done something they should have, and they’ve been living with the weight of whichever one it was.

She put the pen down.

Looked at the armchair, which was neutral gray now, the session long over.

Session 3.

She said she would say it.

I believe her.

I also believe it is going to be very hard to hear.
