To call Liao Hua’s foray against bandits overwhelming would be underselling the force with which the power of Liao fell upon the bandits. The bandits were not prepared for the strength of Liao falling upon them. Their weapons were a hodgepodge of spears, swords, and slings. Her soldiers had steel, armour, and cavalry. Wealth and training and equipment always made the difference in war.

They also had two powerful Cultivators.

A bandit was fleeing, running as fast as he could. Far behind him, the cavalry were running down the last of his allies, hooved galloping down the plains loud as any thunderstorm. But right behind him was the lightning that always preceded thunder. On his heels, Liao Hua gave him the impression that he could escape, staying just out of reach. Letting him exhaust himself and—

He stamped his foot hard on the earth to stop. His momentum carried him, creating a deep groove in the mud. And even as he slid through the channel of earth, he pivoted harder than he stopped and swung his cudgel behind him in a wide arc.

Light glittered off the steel spikes embedded in the steel wrapping around the long cudgel. Golden light, sunlight, and perhaps her widening eyes as those damned spikes sought her head.

Hua raised her blade to block the blow. Stepping around cudgel would be far too much effort to expend and oh fuck fuck fuck that hurts!

Hua had forgotten a single key fact. She was left-handed. She wielded her blade with the left. It was her sword arm that her deviation had been expelled through.

Against the other mortals, it hadn’t mattered.

This man had tempered his body. She felt the strength of that in his swing. It made her arm twinge. Made it shake and twang. The bruises hidden beneath her bandages flared bright with pain. Hua gritted her teeth, unable to send Qi to strengthen it. That had been where the impurities violently exited her body. Pushing Qi to it could cause her wounds to burst open.

She was being pushed back. By a mortal. How embarrassing. She would need to use both hands and live with this shame.

“Hua!”

In the corner of her vision, appearing as if from nothing, was a man with a spear. A man with no presence for he was no Cultivator. A man of such little importance she hadn’t paid him attention. She remembered his top knot for she had seen it, but she had not committed him to memory. And now his spear was coming straight for her, eager to skewer her through the side.

With a spark, a push, a thought, her dantian spun. Qi surged through her, unrestrained and wild. Power without form rose within her as she reached with her right hand for the hilt of her sword.

I created this technique with nothing more than the Trigram Zhen, brought together twice over to form the greater Hexagram Zhen. I created it to overcome my shortcomings. It is nothing truly special. A girl as hard-headed as you will never truly learn it despite it being possible in Qi Condensation.

Her grandmother’s words came to her. Hua had never formed this technique. Had never been trained in it.

But if Hua knew one thing, it was how to create the Trigram for Thunder. Her raging Qi split into three parts, two to yin, one to yang. Through both arms the Qi went, even as the left felt like molten metal was being shoved through it. She felt her bruises burst open, felt her bandages soak through with blood.

It did not matter.

From her left hand, strongly-yin and weakly-yin Qi met her blade. From the right came an overwhelming mass of strong ying Qi. They rejoined the moment her right hand reached the hilt. Zhen was formed throughout the blade.

For a brief instant, her blade hummed.

So sharp was it that it cut through the steel bands of the club. Cutting through steel and wood, and then the arms swinging the club, chewing through flesh and bone. Viscera followed the trail of her blade. Her arc continued unabated and hit the tip of the spear, cutting it clean off.

I did it, she thought wonderingly.

And then her blade shattered.

Hua dropped the screaming hilt as shards flew about, strengthening her Qi barrier. Metal shards cut through her robes and flesh anyway. One lodged into the spearman’s eye and then out the back of his head. He died instantly.

She stared at the blade that lay shattered in the mud. It still howled in agony. I didn’t even have you for a week!

Before she could forget about another mortal, she turned on a dime and snapped out a kick to the armless bandit. His head twisted sharply, neck crunching as it broke. Head turning till it faced the opposite direction. He fell to his knees, hands reaching up for a moment before he tipped over.

A yelp drew her attention. Weiji had managed to trip on mostly flat ground and was now rolling away from spear thrusts. She loved her cousin, really, she did, but sometimes he was an embarrassment. Hua crossed the distance to the bandit who was desperately trying to kill her cousin, and kicked him with her full strength, sending him flying.

Hua bit down on her tongue and swallowed her yelp. Her shin twinged painfully. She could feel a bruise blooming already. His body was Tempered enough to harm her. Hmm, maybe Weiji struggling wasn’t so pathetic then.

And, well, he had maybe, possibly, potentially been paying attention to Hua instead of his own fight.

“Thanks,” Weiji said breathlessly, scrambling to his feet as the bandit skidded across the ground. “I can handle—”

She shook her head sharply. “No, I’ve stopped having fun with this.”

The bandit she’d kicked was rising, staring at her with the quivering dread of someone who knew they were about to die. Or someone who’d been violently winded, had a few ribs cracked, and was forcing themselves up. They were very similar expressions she had encountered a dozen times in the last twenty minutes.

“Don’t—”

Hua was behind him in an instant, drowning out whatever Weiji was going to say. He had no chance to react as she grabbed the bandit by the chin and forehead. With a twist, his neck snapped.

She let his body drop, wincing as another wave of pain hit her arm. Blood was soaking her bandages, seeping through the many layers of her robes.

Weiji was glaring at her. “Damn it. We could have questioned him.”

“They’re just bandits.”

“It’s never just bandits. It’ll be a whole camp of bandits. An army of them.”

“You should spend less time reading your epics and more time in the real world.”

“No I—what happened to your arm!? You were just fine.”

Blood was licking down her fingers, dripping to the ground. A small puddle had formed. Hua blinked, finding things very bright all of a sudden.

“Nothing a healer can’t handle.”

Weiji worried too much. Hua batted his hands away when he reached for her arm. In the distance, Captain Yao and his men had cut down the last of the fleeing bandits.

“Oh, and Weiji.”

“Whatever it is, can it wait until after you’re not bleeding to death?”

“I was trying to bring you a bandit to question. Then you carved open her guts. So really, this is a problem of your making.”

“Your very existence is my problem!”

“Also, I need a new sword.”

“I’ll give you one of the spares if you let someone look at your arm.”

That was a trade she could accept.

***

The next three days passed without incident. The healer, who surprised her by being Young Ren, insisted Hua not take part in strenuous activity, so it fell to Captain Yao and his soldiers to ensure the road was clear. The road was gradually improving as they had only lost a quarter of the riding day so far, compared to half for the first two. Hua rode her horse, though he was rather distressed with her injury and kept butting his head against her shoulder.

The convoy had stopped for a short break. Hua’s arm was in a sling. With her free one, she brushed her horse’s neck. I’m going to give you a name, she decided. In the ranking of her favourite things in the world, it would soon edge out Weiji if he didn’t stop hovering.

“Do you miss him?” she asked after finally giving up and eating the pills he forced on her. A mix of healing pills and weak Qi replenishment pills. They tasted terrible even hidden in pan-fried dumplings.

“Who?” She gave him a significant look. Outrage coloured his face as he read her. “My father?! Why the fuck would I miss him?”

“No, I meant my brother. You two were always close.”

He looked terribly pained. Then he sighed, white misting the air. “Please, don’t misunderstand me, I would happily serve any Lord Liao, but I am glad it is you. Few would be as… reasonable with my time and efforts as you are.”

“We barely spent any time together the last year. And we hardly train together.”

“I am grateful for that limited attention, and I humbly beg it remains that way. For the sake of my work if nothing else. And my sanity,” the last part he muttered and when she tried asking any further, he quickly excused himself.

Hua left it at that.

That evening, after dinner, Captain Yao once more made good on her order to update her on the road’s condition. The report was intentionally tedious. It had to be. No one could make a report this boring unless they meant to kill their lord through sheer boredom.

“My lord, there is something very wrong. We should have reached the first village by now even at our reduced speed.”

They were outside, enjoying the surprisingly pleasant evening. Warm, with little smog choking the air. A handful of stars shone through the thin layer of ash in the sky. Warm rice wine was entertaining her alongside the distant notes of Weiji’s flute work. He truly was an excellent player. It brought her back to more youthful days.

If not for it, she surely would have accused Captain Yao of attempting to murder her with such a dry report.

She realised he was waiting for a response from her. “Could the village have been destroyed and swallowed by the ground? There were areas in Liaojiangkou that were completely swallowed. Village huts are barely worth calling buildings.”

“It is possible, but we should have seen a greater indication of prior habitation. The farms have been painfully empty and untended.”

“Perhaps the bandits were particularly vicious,” Liu Xin suggested from where he had been talking to the Magistrate’s Scribe. Hua still could not remember his name. “Bandits don’t depopulate a region this extensively, but they also lack any means to hide this level of ruthlessness. Realistically, if for some strange reason, they had killed the villagers, they would be living in their huts for the winter.”

Hua shrugged. Winced as it jostled her left arm. Said, “We’ll find out soon enough which unnatural cause has left this area devoid of mortals,” and downed a long draft of her wine.

She slept well that night despite the aching of her arm which woke her at odd intervals. Qing was always watching her when she woke. Dark gaze disappointed. Hua was becoming used to that feeling.

Violent rains had swept through overnight. Both used to and expecting persistent rains, the camp was set up such that they were not sinking in the morning. But as the ground turned to churned mud that got everywhere, the entire was miserable by afternoon.

“My Lord, please know that Scribe Tao, Captain Yao, and I all suggest against travelling today,” Liu Xin said, standing in her tent, wet curls clinging to his forehead. He had needed her seal for something she’d signed without remembering.

Hua, who had a fire, thick furs, tea, snacks, and a very comfortable padded bench, pointedly, aggressively, raised her eyebrow at him. “Do I look like I plan on going anywhere?” she asked, waving her arm still in a sling.

“Thankfully not, but those documents you signed and sealed were orders confirming that.”

“I’ll forgive this minor treason.”

The rains would peter out late in the afternoon. By then, everyone had settled in for another night in the same muddy puddles. Hua’s tent had sprung a leak and necessitated a servant coming in to change the bucket every few hours. Once it was dismantled, it could be stitched shut on the road.

Morning came and with it, a clear day. The sun was out and actively fighting the frost that crept across the ground to the relief of everyone in the convoy. They left a sichen after dawn. It was the first truly pleasant day since they had left the city.

They got two full sichen of uninterrupted travel through the undulating farmland before they broke for a quick lunch. The Liao River had become a distant speck by now, a glitter you sometimes saw out of the corner of your eye.

Hua gulped down water. Someone had mishandled the wine and it was buried too far deep to pull out for a quick meal. Hua suspected sabotage. Just a question of which of her underlings was stopping her from drinking.

“Play us a song, Cousin. Let us enjoy our trip if only a little bit.”

Weiji had always taken any excuse to play his flute and this time was no different. Music ran in his lineage. Elder Shenyu was a powerful operatic singer, and his mother had been the reason Hua fell in love with the plucked chords of the guzheng in the first place. He would occasionally pause in his play and recite the words to a poem, his words drifting between the plod-plod of the horses.

“The sun is in the east / And that lovely girl / Is in my chamber. She is in my chamber/ She treads in my footsteps / and comes to me.”

That was from the Shijing if Hua was remembering her classics well. That song was perhaps not entirely appropriate for a man to play for an unmarried woman, but then again, she was playing her own role. Liao Weilong, Lord and heir. Not Hua. Not in public.

She stretched out her arm and he paused.

A commotion was building at the vanguard. Hua sat up in her saddle, directing her powerful gaze forward. There was a group of mortals speaking with one of the scouts. She assumed them civilians because the scout hadn’t cut them down yet.

Well, isn’t this interesting.

“My Lord, is something wrong?”

“I believe we will have answers on the matter of missing villages.” She glanced at a soldier.

***

It was a group of impoverished mortals in thin clothes that the scouts returned with as the mists lightened. Their clothes were well-maintained but of low quality. They looked to have missed two in three meals this season, stick-thin bones showing clearly. Mud clung to their hemp shoes, dust and dirt to their cloaks. One was bruised with lesions across his body. Illness, she suspected.

Hua sat on a bench that had hastily been unfolded from her tent supplies. With the mat, carpets, canopy, food and drink, it almost had an air of formality. Around her was what she coming to term her command staff: Weiji her deputy, Captain Yao her military leader, Liu Xin her secretary, and the Magistrate’s Scribe who was purely decorative.

The scout spoke. “You approach the Lord Weilong who acts in the name of the Radiant Lightning Body, Patriarch of the Liao Clan, and ruler of all lands between the Western Reaches and the open sea. State your case.”

The mortals fell to their knees before her at that pronouncement. Hua glared down at them imperiously. None were brave enough to maintain eye contact. None were foolish enough.

Something was wrong. The atmosphere had turned eerie. Far too quiet for her liking. Why was it like this?

“Lord, please, we’re good sons of your land, loyal sons, and we need—”

She turned to face her cousin, interrupting the explanation with a raised hand. “Did I say you could stop playing? Keep going. If I must listen to mortals, I’ll do so with some entertainment.”

It had taken her a moment to figure out what was wrong with the mortal distracting her. Her cousin gave her a look of such profound disappointment Hua’s descendants were cringing for her. Unfortunately, Hua’s descendants didn’t exist, so it was ineffectual. He sighed and returned to his flute. The song he played was somber, melancholic, and threatened to move even Hua’s heart.

Once she had musical accompaniment, she spoke again. “For what reason do you seek this lord’s aid? State your case.”

The leader of the group, the young man with the lesions, raised his head for a moment before ducking it again.

“We’re running, lord. We got sent ahead to make sure the path was safe for our convoy. Being hunted by a big-shot bandit, the biggest name in the west. Calls himself Kang and he’s been a right terror for years. But these last few months, something's happened, and he’s fallen in love with bloodshed.”

“Why did you not simply rally together and kill him?”

“He’s a strong Cultivator, same as you. Was no hope of us defeating him.”

This made less sense. A Cultivator running a bandit group wasn’t unheard of. Doing it so close to the Liao capital with no word reaching them was absurd.

Hua scoffed. “This lord will judge his Cultivation ability. He does not find reason to believe mortals are capable of such.” The notes of Weiji’s dizi brightened, became faster and more insistent. He wanted her to question them further. “How long have you travelled?”

“Got my home a few weeks northeast of Coaltown. Those I’m with all came from villages nearby. Tried sending for help but the mayor kept sending back whoever we sent. Even after we petitioned the Boss Deng—he’s the Justice, him, highest with the Dragon Throne. Even after reaching out to him directly, we got no help from no one. Ain’t do nothing to get us justice. He claimed to have sent word to the Magistrate in your city, but nothing came of it.”

Hua glanced at the Magistrate’s Scribe who Hua still could not recall. It has to be a spell. There is no other way a man becomes so forgettable.

The Scribe said, “I read through all mail sent to the Magistrate before he receives it. To ensure there is no danger to the missive and that it is worth his time. We received no message from Justice Deng.”

“Lord, please, believe us. Ain’t got no reason to lie. Ain’t got no benefit to piss you off.”

Hua gestured their silence and turned back to the Scribe. “Do you often remain in communications with the Justices of the Province or does that role lie with the Governor?” Weiji’s made a discordant note. Ah, Hua had made a mistake. She added quickly, “This lord would hope this breakdown in communications allowing bandits to roam is simply a mistake and not an intentional act.”

There, good. Turn the flub into a threat. The Scribe, who had seen Hua drenched in the blood of her enemies three days ago, paled.

“I assure you that it is not. It would be treason to hide such an event. No Justice or Magistrate in service of the Dragon Throne would dare. The consequences far outweigh the potential benefit.”

The Justice might be corrupt. His letters might have been intercepted. Either way, there was a mystery afoot. Bandits and a mystery. Hua might not completely die of boredom in the west. She hoped her grandmother would only have tedium on her end.

“How did you attempt to solve this issue?” Hua asked the mortal, squinting beyond him as orange flickered in the distance. Now what was that?

“Got all us together, one and all we pooled our money. Sold our jewellery and our lives just to make the money to send out the message. Thought it was worth it when the Jolly Rangers arrived. But we haven’t seen them since the world started breaking.”

Hua looked around at her command apparatus and saw equal blankness. “Who are these Jolly Rangers?”

“They’re heroes in the hinterlands,” he said with reverence. “Wandering Cultivators who help out people like us. They fight evil wherever it exists and uplift good when they find it.”

“But they charged you, yes?” She nodded. “And you still consider them heroes? I would call them mercenaries. How much exactly did you pay?”

“Got them a hundred silver taels.”

“A piece?”

“All in.”

Hua blinked. Weiji’s flute cut off. Captain Yao coughed violently.

“Either they were saints or thieves. If the former, this lord apologises for impugning the character of your Rangers because not even a wandering Cultivator of Qi condensation would accept so low a payment, let alone a group of them. Have you heard from them since?”

Hua had paid Liu Xin more for three days of work and his job was talking to mortals. Going to war against a Cultivator leading a supposed bandit army—one hundred silver taels was such a nonsensically low figure it made no sense.

“No, lord.”

Hua gazed out past them, considering everything. If they were wrong, then she could just string them up and have them executed. But if they were right, she could face a powerful Cultivator and have a good time.

“More pressingly, did your Jolly Rangers wear orange clothes and straw hats?”

“Yes. How did you know?”

Hua pointed at the copse of trees in the distance. It was hard to see, but for someone with golden eyes, it was possible.

“Because I think I see one of them impaled on a tree.”
