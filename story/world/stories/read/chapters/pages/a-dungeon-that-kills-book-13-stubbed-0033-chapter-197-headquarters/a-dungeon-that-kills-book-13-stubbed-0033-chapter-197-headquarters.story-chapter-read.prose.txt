Viktor pushed the door open and stepped out of the room, the hinges giving the faintest creak of protest before settling back into silence behind him. The outside was dimly lit, the only source of illumination a small lantern hung crookedly on the wall. Yvonne was standing next to it, and at the sound of the door opening, her head turned in his direction. Her eyes widened in shock the instant she recognized who had just come out—the very same man she had lured into her bedroom with the promise of something he would never actually get.

To her credit, she regained her composure admirably fast. She took a deep breath that lifted her shoulders and let them fall again.

“Is that you, my lord?”

“Yes.”

Without delay, she lowered her head in submission. Her body was completely still as she stood there, waiting for the next instruction.

“Well done,” Viktor said. “You may now come inside and begin the cleanup.”

Not that there was much to clean anyway. The one item that most needed to be removed had strolled out on its own two legs, while Viktor himself had already returned to his house, so there was nothing left in that room except a pool of water leaking into the floorboards.

“As you wish,” the woman said, her tone carrying none of the warmth she had used on that man, as though that voice belonged to someone else entirely.

Viktor walked past her, preparing to depart. But then, he froze mid-step, because there in front of him was a thing he had forgotten to account for.

Stairs.

Well, of course, there were stairs. He was on the second floor, after all, so unless he intended to fling himself out a window, stairs were precisely the thing he had to walk down if he wanted to leave.

The problem was, the body he was controlling was far less cooperative than his real one. He had practiced a lot, yes, so walking on flat terrain or flying through the air would give him no trouble. But descending a staircase was another beast altogether. One misstep and he could tumble all the way down. Sure, the fall itself would not do a thing to either him or the dead man. But such a disgraceful performance before a subordinate would undoubtedly cost him quite a bit in terms of dignity.

Oh, well.

Clenching the dead man’s teeth together, he took hold of the rail and began his descent. Here we go. One step, then two, each one a negotiation between his intentions and the body’s sluggish response. Slowly, steadily, he told himself. No need to rush.

Only when he had finally reached the first floor did he let out a breath of relief, a breath that had been held inside the dead lungs for what felt like an unreasonably long time.

Without sparing a glance at his spy, whom he trusted to handle whatever remained of tonight’s loose ends without needing any further input, he opened the front door and stepped out into the streets.

There were not many people about at this hour. It was already dark, after all, so most sensible folk had long since retreated behind locked doors. And once he crossed into the west side, the streets grew even emptier. He saw absolutely no one, save for occasional patrol groups. They yelled at him to stop when they first spotted him, but as they neared and got a proper look at his face, they recognized him as one of their own and let him pass through without a fuss. So just like that, he made his way toward a large wooden building standing in the middle of the neighborhood.

He had heard it was once some sort of marketplace-warehouse hybrid, a place where the people of the west side used to gather and trade with each other whatever passed for goods in this part of town. The monster attack eight years ago had seen many of them leave for good, and the building had been abandoned, at least until his dungeon emerged and brought a fresh wave of newcomers who were looking for somewhere, anywhere, to put a roof over their heads. For a while, it had served that purpose well enough. But then, Zubayr and his men came. They threw everyone out, took it for themselves, repaired and reinforced its crumbling walls, and converted it into their headquarters.

Viktor had watched the place plenty of times before, always from a safe distance. He had sent Galatea to keep an eye on it too, once or twice. But he had never risked sending her inside, since there was a chance a barrier had been set up around it, similar to the one at that outpost, to warn them of an intrusion. Now, though, wearing this body, he could simply walk in without any problem.

Crossing the threshold, he found himself in a large hall. Rows of tables and benches filled two sides of the space, the whole arrangement looking like something that was both a main hall and a mess hall, a hub where the Iskora adventurers gathered to eat, drink, and socialize. Ahead, stairs climbed up toward the doors above, where Zubayr and his lieutenants kept their rooms. Down here, more doors lined the walls, leading off to wherever the rank and file laid their heads.

Not many people were still around, though. A handful scattered across the tables, hunched over their half-empty tankards, and more than one of them looked to have given up on sobriety entirely.

“Hey, Kevan!”

Ah, yes. It was this guy’s name.

He turned and found a woman seated near the far wall. She was very tall—he could tell even though she was sitting—and broad-shouldered, several visible scars tracing her muscular, bare arms. Her hair was shaved close at the sides, while the top was left long and dark, falling loosely down her back.

And he knew who she was, thanks to his prior reconnaissance trips.

Valeska.

She was one of Zubayr’s top enforcers, much like Dimitria. Come to think of it, there was a suspiciously high number of tall, large women among those the guy had chosen to be his lieutenants.

Since she had called him by name, it would look a bit fishy if he ignored her outright, so he made his way over instead.

“I thought you went to that girl’s house? The one who works at the caravan station?” The woman grinned as he neared. “Why are you back so soon?”

Viktor arranged a disappointed look across the dead man’s face.

“It didn’t go anywhere.”

“Figured.” The woman barked a laugh, before stopping herself. “Sorry.” A beat passed, and the grin came creeping back. “Hey, if you’re wanting, I could offer a bit of comfort myself.”

“I’ll pass.”

“You’re no fun,” the woman said, bringing her tankard up to her mouth and taking a long swallow. “Actually, it’s me who could do with some comforting tonight.”

“That so? What happened?”

“Zubayr just told me I’ll be taking part in that stupid contest.” She snorted. “Going into the dungeon is one thing, but doing so under Xander’s command? Ugh.”

Oh?

“Who else will go?”

“Not decided yet,” the woman said with a shrug. “I’m the first one he’s picked.”

Viktor turned that over as he stood there nodding along. So, only two names were certain so far: Xander and Valeska. Nothing on anyone else. Not even, by the sound of it, whether Zubayr would be among the participants.

“Anyway,” the woman said, pushing herself up from the bench, revealing her full height that towered over him. “Last chance. Yes or no?”

“I said I’d pass.”

“You’ll die a virgin,” she said as she turned away, her long strides carrying her toward the staircases.

Well, about that...

Viktor stayed where he stood, waiting until the woman disappeared behind one of the doors on the second floor.

That was a nice bit of information to walk away with, but it was not why he had come here tonight. He reached into his pocket, opened the ink pot he had brought, dipped a finger in, and smeared a thin line of it along the underside of the table. Then, he strolled around the hall at a leisurely pace, doing the same thing at table after table. A hall like this lived and breathed on gossip, and that meant there would be no shortage of material to gather. Celeste was going to have her hands rather full soon, but hey, multitasking had always been her particular talent.

The ink pot was, of course, the same Reliquary he had gotten from Yvonne back when she joined the ranks of the dungeon’s agents in the outside world—a great organization that rivalled the Children of the Abyss itself, numbering a grand total of two people, with her being the first.

The artifact was a set of two items: an inkpot and a quill. One could pour any ordinary ink into the pot, and it would do the work, provided that they smeared it straight from the pot itself onto whatever surface they wanted—no, you were not allowed to decant the ink into some other vessel and leave the original pot at home, more was the pity.

Once the smear was there, the quill would then automatically write out whatever conversation it overheard. The surface on which the ink was placed could be moved a short distance afterward, though not too far. That was why Yvonne had been able to take a picture frame down off the wall in Brynhildr’s room, put a single drop on its back, and then hang it up again. What she could not do, however, was smear the ink onto some portable object like a piece of paper, crumple it up, and toss it wherever convenient. It would definitely have been far easier for him if it worked that way. But it didn’t. So Viktor had been forced to carry the Reliquary itself, while walking another man’s corpse straight into the heart of enemy territory like this.

And so he walked the length and breadth of the hall, smearing ink wherever he deemed a spot suitable. The tables, the chairs, the bases of pillars, the rims of flower pots. Nobody paid him any mind. The few souls left awake were far too occupied with their cups to spare a glance at a man dawdling near the furniture. He left a few drops by the doors leading off toward the barracks as well, before dumping the rest of the ink under the staircases.

Going anywhere beyond this hall would be pushing my luck, Viktor thought.

It had been fortunate that few people were here tonight, but the longer he lingered, the higher the risk that someone with sharper eyes would notice something was off. Better to leave now than to grow greedy and undo the entire night’s work. This place, where everyone would pass through, where everyone feasted and gossiped and let their guard down over drink, should be more than enough. While putting an ink drop somewhere like Zubayr’s private quarters would certainly yield richer secrets, it was simply not feasible to do so at the moment.

So he turned and walked back out through the main doors.

Stepping out into the streets once more, he tilted his head back and looked up at the pale moon hanging overhead.

Five days. That was the time he had left.

Then, the confrontation with that bastard would finally begin.
