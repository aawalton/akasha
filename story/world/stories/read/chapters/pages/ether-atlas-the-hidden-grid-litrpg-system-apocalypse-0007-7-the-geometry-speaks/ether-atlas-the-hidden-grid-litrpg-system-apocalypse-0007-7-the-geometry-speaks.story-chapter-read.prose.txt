CHAPTER 7

"The Guidance System is not your ally. Do not attempt to instruct it, befriend it, or negotiate with it. It has observed more civilisations than yours has produced generations. Your opinion of its methods is not relevant to its function." - Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

“Candidate evaluation complete.”

Arthur stared at the rotating structure. Up close, it had no surfaces he could read. It had no material, no mass and no point where one element bore the weight of another. All he could see was angles folding into angles which in turn folded into patterns that dissolved and reformed in a continuous loop that his eyes could follow but his brain couldn’t quite understand. The blue light it cast was sharp, throwing thin geometric reflections across the wet pavement.

Nobody in the restaurant behind them had reacted to what seemed to be origami made by someone on acid. Through the glass door they could see Emma and her staff continue their quiet, urgent conversation while a man near the window stared at his dead phone. None of them so much as glanced at the hovering shape.

Arthur glanced at Steve who was looking at Donna. Donna’s attention was on the construct, her expression suggesting she was calculating whether a kitchen knife would work on geometry.

"Both of you. The floating shape. You see it?" Steve's voice was careful. “I seem to be asking that question a lot today.”

“I see it,” Arthur said.

“I see it and I don’t like it,” Donna said.

Steve edged closer to the floating shape, his head tilting the way it did when he was examining a carving or a scroll. The unconscious habit of a man trying to read something that wasn’t written in any language he knew.

“Candidate who and Candidate for what, exactly?” he asked.

The construct’s internal lines shifted and several angles aligned towards Arthur.

“Interface suitability confirmed. Viable candidate chosen.”

Steve waited but nothing followed the statement.

“Is that it? That’s the whole answer?” he asked.

The construct continued rotating in silence. Arthur glanced back at the restaurant staff, who were still debating something.

Donna guided Arthur and Steve around the corner, being careful not to alarm anyone in the restaurant. Outside, the street had begun to organise itself in small, frightened ways. A knot of office workers were ducking one by one under the half-raised shutter of a Sainsbury's Local. On a balcony across the road a woman stood wrapped in a duvet, her face turned up to the violet sky. Somewhere above them a window scraped shut.

When they were out of eyesight Donna turned to the construct.

“What are you?” she asked, folding her arms.

The floating construct went through a brief reconfiguration and then answered. “Seeder-placed auxiliary intelligence. Planetary guidance subsystem.”

Steve let out a frustrated breath. “Seeder-placed auxiliary intelligence. Of course, naturally. That explains everything and nothing simultaneously.”

Arthur kept his eyes on the construct. The way it was oriented towards him made him believe that the construct was looking at Arthur specifically. Steve and Donna were in the room, but Arthur was the subject. The thought of a god-knows-what taking a special interest in him for god-knows-why was rather disconcerting.

“You said you evaluated me. For what?” Arthur asked.

“Interface compatibility.”

“What happens if I’m not compatible?”

“Alternate candidate selection.”

The answer was immediate and clinical. The construct had evaluated him like Arthur would evaluate a wall, could it hold, yes or no. If the answer had been no, it would have moved on and found one that could. Arthur would have preferred this thing to have chosen someone else. Richard from the gym, maybe?"

***

“And what made you choose him?” Donna asked, her eyes narrowing. She had categorised the construct as a potential threat until proven otherwise.

The construct answered without delay. "Observed behavioural patterns demonstrate consistent prioritisation of non-self survival outcomes."

Steve's lips moved silently as he repeated the words in his head. Arthur could almost watch the sentence being disassembled in his head, each word tested against normal speech. "Observed behavioural patterns... prioritisation of non-self..." Steve's face cleared, after a few seconds "It's saying you keep running towards danger to help people."

Donna gave Arthur a look. "Which you absolutely do."

“I wouldn’t call it a qualification, more a character flaw. Ask anyone who’s worked for me.” Arthur said.

The construct pulsed faintly. “Inaccurate classification. Statistically advantageous trait for interface function.”

Arthur held whatever approximation of gaze a rotating geometric shape could produce and realised that the thing had been watching him. In the pub, in the alley, on the street. It had watched him check pulses on dead strangers, pull a woman from a car, put himself between a creature and an injured man. And it had run those observations through whatever passed for its decision-making process and concluded: this one will do.

He didn’t know whether to be reassured or disturbed. He settled on both, since it was proving to be that kind of a day.

"What are you, exactly? Some kind of AI?" Steve asked.

“Your species’ artificial intelligence systems are statistical prediction engines dressed up to resemble thought. They possess no independent reasoning capacity.” The construct’s geometry tightened by a fraction. "The comparison is not applicable."

Steve looked at Arthur. “Fine, not an AI. But it was put here in advance, it evaluates the candidate, and I bet it wants something from us. From you, more specifically. Every game I've ever played, that's a quest-giver. The NPC who stands in one spot, runs you through the tutorial, then refuses to tell you a bloody thing until you've jumped through the right hoops.”

"Does the label change what we do next?" Arthur asked.

"Not even slightly. But a quest-giver means there's a quest."

"Right," Donna said quietly. "We're standing around arguing taxonomy with a floating shape from god knows where." She looked at Arthur, then at Steve. “Anyone else slightly concerned by how quickly we've stopped finding that completely mental?”

"Would it help if we panicked?" Arthur said.

"It would help if someone acknowledged the situation."

Arthur smiled faintly. A part of him said that smiling was wrong with what he had seen today while another part of him was relieved that he still could.

"What happened to the world?" he asked.

The construct’s geometry slowed a fraction at the question. Its internal structure seemed to be processing something larger than a single query. The lines reconfigured while the angles tried their best to give Arthur a headache.

When it spoke, the voice was the same flat, clinical monotone, as if the answer deserved no more emphasis than a weather report.

“Planetary system activation event.”

This was followed by a long silence while the three stared at the construct.

“That is the single least helpful sentence I have ever heard in my life. And I’ve sat through departmental budget meetings.” Steve said slowly.

The construct offered no further elaboration.

Donna stepped forward. “People are dead. A quarter of the people in that pub behind us dropped and didn’t get up. Is that part of your ‘activation event’?”

“It was not my activation event. This is ‘an’ activation event. Biological compatibility variance during initial Ether saturation is an expected system parameter.”

“Oh for fuck’s sake! What the hell do you mean by ‘expected.’ You’re telling me that those deaths were expected?”

The construct’s silence answered enough. Donna’s expression flattened as she recalculated where it sat on her private scale of trust. The construct had just moved from ‘unknown’ to ‘not an ally.’

***

Steve, predictably, took a different approach. Where Donna hits walls, Steve looked for doors.

“You said Seeder-placed auxiliary intelligence,” he said. “That implies something was seeded or placed in advance. Something was prepared.” He was talking faster now. “And ‘planetary’ means this isn’t local. This is happening everywhere, isn’t it? Not just London. The whole world.”

“Correct. The activation event is global in scope.”

Steve absorbed that and Arthur watched his face cycle through three or four expressions in rapid succession. Horror at the scale, then fascination at the implication, then guilt at the fascination, then fascination again because Steve’s brain genuinely could not help itself.

“The Ether,” Steve continued, gesturing at the faint lattice beneath his skin. “That's what the notification called this stuff we're taking in. Is that what did all this? Changed the sky, killed those people, started turning the animals into... whatever the hell they're becoming?”

“Ether saturation is the primary catalyst for all observed environmental and biological changes.”

"And exactly what is Ether?" Arthur asked.

"A higher-dimensional energy substrate currently saturating the planet."

“Christ, you sound like a damn solicitor. Any chance of an answer that actually tells us something?” Donna said, staring at the construct.

The construct did not appear to consider the question worthy of a response. It kept rotating in silence.

“So what about the people who died, they couldn’t process this Ether? The Ether was incompatible with their biology?” Steve asked, filling the silence.

The construct’s lines tightened into what Arthur was beginning to recognise as its processing configuration.

“Biological integration of Ether requires specific genetic markers. Approximately seventy-five per cent of your species carries compatible sequences.”

Steve went very still as his brain made the connection. "Seventy-five per cent compatible. Twenty-five per cent not." He looked towards the street, at the bodies on the pavement. "That's the number. That's the exact ratio."

His voice had lost its excitement. The explanation for a mass death event was a percentile on a compatibility chart.

"And who decided the compatibility? Who decided which people could process this Ether and which ones dropped dead?" Donna asked, heat rising in her voice.

At the question the construct's geometry went still. The rotation stopped and the facets locked in place. Stillness, total and deliberate, as if the intelligence behind the geometry had chosen to hold its breath.

Donna nodded once, as though the silence had confirmed exactly what she'd expected. "So someone designed this. Someone chose the criteria, knowing that twenty-five per cent wouldn't make the cut."

The construct remained silent.

"And they bloody did it anyway," she finished.

Steve pivoted, pulling the conversation back towards the analytical before the emotional weight could settle. "So someone knew this was coming…...the Seeders, whoever they are and they implemented criteria for Ether compatibility and they also built you. This isn't random, there's a structure behind it."

"Your interpretation is within acceptable parameters."

“‘Within acceptable parameters.’ Is that a yes?” Donna repeated.

“It is an acknowledgement that the stated interpretation does not contain sufficient inaccuracy to warrant correction.”

Donna looked at Arthur. “I already hate this thing.”

"So, who are these Seeders?" Steve asked.

The construct's geometry contracted into tight compressed lines and folded into a configuration Arthur hadn't seen before. Then it resumed its usual rotation and said nothing.

"Are they aliens? Gods? What do they want with Earth?" Donna pressed.

The same contraction followed by the same silence.

Arthur had been listening. He’d let Steve probe and Donna push, tracking how the construct responded, what it answered, what it deflected, and where the silences fell.

There was a pattern to it: specific technical questions got specific technical answers. Broad questions about meaning or purpose got clinical non-responses. And anything that touched on the larger plan, who the Seeders were, what they wanted, why any of this was happening, produced silence.

It knew much more than it was saying, Arthur was sure. Whether that was by choice or by design, Arthur couldn’t yet tell.

“You picked me for a reason. Which means you want something done. What is it?” Arthur said.

The construct’s geometry shifted and several internal lines aligned in a new direction, towards the street behind them. Like a compass finding north.

“Guided Infrastructure node activation evaluation,” the construct replied. "Proximity alert. Infrastructure element detected. Six hundred metres west. Candidate interaction required."

Arthur waited for more context but nothing came.

"What kind of infrastructure? What happens when I interact with it?"

"That information is proximity-dependent."

Arthur followed the alignment down the darkened street. Somewhere in that direction, past the stalled cars and the scattered bodies and the purple darkness pressing down over the city, something was waiting for him. The scary bit was that he had started to feel it, a faint pressure behind his eyes. A pull, steady and precise, pointing down the street.

“If you think,” Donna started, “that we’re following a floating geometry puzzle through a city full of dead people and monsters to a location it won’t describe for a purpose it won’t explain..…”

She paused and let out a sigh.

“....then you’re right, because I don’t have a better option and I’m furious about it.”

Steve looked at her. “Was that a, yes?”

“That was an acknowledgement that the stated option does not contain sufficient stupidity to warrant refusal,” she said.

Steve barked a laugh and then looked down ashamed.

The construct rotated once more, its blue lines sharpening into a configuration Arthur hadn’t seen before. This was tighter, far more focused, the geometry condensing towards a single directional axis and then the damned thing dissolved.

The lines folded inward and vanished, leaving nothing but the wet pavement reflecting the sky above and the faint metallic taste of Ether in the air.

Steve took a step back. “Bloody hell! Where did it go?” Donna had her knife in her hand.

Arthur couldn’t explain how, but his mind told him the construct was around, just not visible. It was something he felt, and he had no scientific explanation for it, but then that particular bar had hit a new low today.

“I think it is still around, just not visible. I think it can decide when to show itself.”

The pull behind Arthur’s eyes had remained. Clear, steady, and pointing west away from Fenchurch Street. “I feel a pull, like a compass. West. It’s pointing west.”

Arthur stood on the pavement outside his friend’s sister’s restaurant, in a city that had stopped working, holding a kitchen knife and a bin lid, and considered his options.

They were, as Donna had noted, limited.

The phones were dead. The emergency services, assuming they still existed in anything resembling a functional state, were probably drowning in a catastrophe that made no sense to anyone without a floating geometric intelligence dispensing cryptic instructions.

The only thing in the last two hours that had come close to an explanation was the construct, and even that had told them almost nothing while somehow making it clear that the few admissions sat on top of something enormous.

They had two choices. Follow the pull and find the node, then deal with whatever came next. Or stay here waiting for the world to start making sense again, which it didn't seem particularly inclined to do.

“We go and see what this damned Infrastructure element is.” He said and started walking west.

Somewhere ahead, something old and buried waited in the wall of an unremarkable building on Cannon Street. Something that had been there for longer than London had existed.
