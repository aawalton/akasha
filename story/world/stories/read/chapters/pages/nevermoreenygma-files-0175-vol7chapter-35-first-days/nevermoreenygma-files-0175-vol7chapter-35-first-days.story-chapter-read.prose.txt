Chapter 35

First Days

After going downstairs, letting her wash up, and after Mai confirmed she could use a toilet, Miho finally exhaled a little in relief.

"Do you want me to help you wash a bit?"

Mai shook her head, then bowed politely. “I-I can do it myself. Th-thank you very much for your hospitality.”

She had started speaking—actually speaking—and Miho leaned one arm against the wall, staring at a blank spot with visible relief.

“I'm so glad we can talk. I thought I'd have to teach you… I’ve gone through that before…"

Despite her relaxed, almost disarmed demeanor, the woman was obviously studying her carefully.But what unsettled Mai the most was how she herself knew things without understanding where that knowledge came from? She truly had no memory of anything—except for that strange dream of the pyramid. And then that collage of memories of herself as a child. Everything else in her mind was a mystery.

But if it was about knowledge, she knew more than she imagined. She felt as if someone had hammered an encyclopedia into her head.

When they left the bathroom, Mai waited for a moment in the hallway and saw Miho walk toward what looked like the front door and wave her hand. “She's fine, no problem!”

“Are you sure?” someone responded.

“Yes. Everything's fine. Let me take care of it.”

“You don't need any help?”

“No, it's okay. Thank you so much for everything.”

“See you around, Miss Sagara. Thanks for the breakfast.”

Mai heard a vehicle start up and drive away as Miho closed the door. “That was the police. They just wanted to make sure everything was in order.” Mai stood there watching her. “Lets have breakfast.”

Mai timidly followed Miho through the house.

“Don’t be scared, come. You must be starving. I am too.”

The place was huge, beautifully furnished, filled with all kinds of objects and furniture. Mai looked around curiously as she followed Miho’s back to the dining room, where there was a low table. The sliding doors were open toward a garden, letting the morning sunlight spill in. There was a strange odor that Mai recognized for some reason: a faint cigarette smell mixed with ambient deodorant.

“Wait here. Sit down, I’ll bring the food.”

Miho left Mai alone, and Mai seized the moment to look around. She was still nervous, but calmer now. She sat on the tatami floor, legs folded beneath her on a cushion, in a small, simple, but cozy room. Miho soon returned with dishes and glasses of orange juice and tea.

“Th-thank you very much,” Mai said, bowing her head humbly.

“With everything that happened last night, you didn’t wake up, so it’s normal to be hungry. Tell me if you want more.”

The rice was warm, the grilled fish released a comforting aroma, and the tea steamed in its ceramic cup. Mai held the chopsticks between her fingers, feeling the smooth, light wood, but her movements were clumsy—as if her body remembered something her mind still couldn’t reach.

“You have memories—at least some muscle memory—and you can understand me. That’s already a great start. Eat slowly, there’s no rush.”

Mai looked at Miho. She had taken off her glasses, yet somehow she seemed able to tell exactly what Mai was doing by sound alone.

“What is it?” Miho asked.

“I-I’m confused…”

“Of course you are. Well, I should start at the beginning.”

The morning light filtered through the shoji screens, painting long shadows across the tatami. Miho was eating with the serenity of someone who had repeated this routine a thousand times. She didn’t seem hurried or tense. She simply waited.

Then she set her chopsticks down on the edge of her plate and spoke.

“You’re a fey, Mai,” she said calmly, watching her with that gaze that was both patient and firm.

“F-fey…?” Mai repeated. The word didn’t sound Japanese to her, but she knew it.

“In the past, people would’ve called them fairies, spirits, or supernatural beings. ‘Fey’ is the term most accepted today, though in Japan they’re also known as marebito. Some would call you yokai, but that’s not entirely accurate. Well… in the end, in the supernatural world it all sort of blends together.”

Mai stared at her. She understood the words—each one clicked into place in her mind like pieces of a puzzle. She knew what a fey was now, she knew what it meant, but she couldn’t remember ever learning it. It felt as though the information had always been there, waiting to be unlocked.

For some reason, the image of her mother flashed through her mind.

Miho went on. “There are others like you in the world, but most hide. Centuries ago people feared them, worshipped them, or hunted them. Stories changed with time, they became myths, horror tales, forgotten legends. But they’re still here. They’ve always been here. Governments knows, though they’ve never made it public. Imagine what would happen if people learned the old stories about immortal beings, shapeshifters, or creatures with impossible abilities were real. The panic would be uncontrollable.”

Mai pressed her lips together, feeling her thoughts swirl in circles. There was a void in her memory, a shadow where something solid should have been.

“Japan is one of the few countries that recognized the issue years ago,” Miho continued, taking a sip of tea. “Here they established entry zones for feys and others like you or me—places where we can live without fear of persecution. Not many, but they exist. Some cities hide them within certain districts, others disguise them as closed communities. They’re places where our kind, the hidden world, can exist without fear. Though in my case, well… I’m a bit more tied to it because of religious matters.”

“You are…”

“No, I’m not a fey. I’m just a human with psychic and supernatural abilities, to put it simply. Do you understand me?”

Mai shook her head, and she finished her glass of juice..

Miho scratched her chin. “Hmm. The easiest way to explain it is this: feys live hidden from the public, and people like me belong to the esoteric traditions—what’s known, what’s been recorded. For example, since I’m an itako, I can lend my voice to spirits and see through them. And I also have psychic abilities that sometimes help with certain cases. My case is particularly unusual because it is uncommon for a person with mediumistic abilities to also have psychic abilities. Part of it is tradition, which fits into cultural and religious context… and part of it is paranormal ability, even if most people nowadays dismiss it as superstition. I’m not sure if I’m making this more complicated for you.”

Mai nodded. “I think I understand.”

Miho’s brows knitted slightly. “Then you must not be an Ancient. If you can understand the term ‘psychic’ and everything I’ve said.”

“Ancient…?”

“Oh! Don’t worry. I just mean that if you can understand my words and definitions, then you’re lucky. Because other feys can barely communicate when they first arrive—at least not all of them. Some have different dialects or speak other languages, but in archaic forms. You can speak well, you can understand.”

Mai placed her chopsticks on the plate with a faint click. Her throat was dry. She wanted to speak, but the words slipped through her fingers like sand. After sipping her tea, she finally managed to whisper:

“Was I… a-always… that? A… fey?”

Miho fell silent for a moment, her expression difficult to read. When she finally spoke, her voice softened.

“There are theories… but the only certain thing is that you are one now. And you will remain one, whether you like it or not.”

Mai looked into the ceramic cup and saw her own reflection.

“I’m teasing a little—don’t make that face. To be honest, I’ve heard theories that say different things. I can’t give you a concrete answer.”

Miho set her cup down on the low table, fingers interlaced over her lap.

“Most of what we know comes from private research, but there are people who make sure those studies don’t endanger feys. There are types that has lived in secret for centuries,at least those that did not disappear in the nineteenth century, and there are all kinds. Not all have human form like you. Some resemble animals, others shadows, or even elemental entities. There isn’t a single mold. But all of them arrive without memories.”

Nineteenth century? Mai thought.

She let the words float in the air: “There isn’t a single mold. But all of them arrive without memories.”

A knot formed in Mai’s throat. The echo of the dream pulsed inside her: her mother’s laughter, the bow in her hands, herself dancing, the flash of glass piercing her chest.

Things that should not exist in her mind.

Things no fey should remember.

She opened her mouth, as if she were about to speak… but something—an unnamed feel—closed her voice around her throat. It was more than fear: it was certainty, dark and absolute, that revealing those memories would mean losing something essential, or worse, inviting a disaster she could not withstand.

Yes. That was it.

She didn’t know why, but the moment she even thought of those dream-memories, she felt invisible hands tightening around her neck.

And something echoed inside her mind, like a command spoken by her own voice:

Never say it. Don’t even think about saying it.

It was as if her unconscious had leapt up, clawing at the idea of revealing the truth.

She fixed her gaze on the steaming tea, avoiding Miho’s eyes. She pretended to study the swirl of vapor, as if refuge could be found there.

No one could know. Not even the woman who had saved her.

The dream had to remain buried, locked in the depths of her mind.

Even if it burned inside her, even if it woke her at night, even if it rattled through her mind like wind chimes that would not stop ringing.

Mai listened quietly, a little distant, as Miho’s explanations continued, feeling the words settle into her thoughts like pieces of a puzzle whose full image she still couldn’t see. But truly, it felt as if she had just escaped the guillotine. She couldn’t say why, but she felt she had narrowly avoided her own death.

“Are y-you a psychic police officer, then? Or a priestess?”

“Oh, that.” Miho smiled lightly. “Well, as I said, I’m not a fey. I’m human—but I have certain abilities. Psychic and extrasensory capacities, and I’m an itako. I help the police with special cases, though this time it was the reverse. I wasn’t the one helping them… I asked them to help me find you before someone else did.”

Mai frowned slightly and tilted her head.

“I dreamed you,” Miho explained, with a small smile. “I saw that a girl would appear in the alleys of Ikebukuro, but it still took us more than twenty minutes to locate you. We almost arrived too late.”

A chill crawled down Mai’s spine.

There was something in that sentence that unsettled her deeply.

“Curiously, a few years ago another fey disappeared around there. But he was a tall man, frightening in appearance. He never surfaced again, from what I heard, and it happened almost in the exact same place where we found you.”

Mai looked at Miho and asked the question she felt she should have asked long ago. “And now? What will happen to me?”

Miho drew a slow breath and rested her hands on her knees.

“You can stay here as long as you need. I don’t mind. I’m not going to pressure you to make a choice right away, but when you do, I want you to know you have options. You can collaborate with government investigations, if that’s something you’d want. Or you can choose something completely different. It’s your decision. But you need to be careful. Even in Japan there are people who would go after you just for being a fey. Even if you're a fey, like everyone else on the planet I suppose, or at least most people, you should do the same as all of us.”

“And w-what's that?”

Miho smiled. “Do your best, little one.”

Mai lowered her gaze to her plate. The rice and tea were gone. The fish had vanished.

A choice.

The idea felt distant, belonging to someone else—another girl, another life.

But still, it was there. Waiting for her.

“Do you want more?” Miho asked, slipping her glasses back on as she glanced at Mai’s empty plate.

As if answering for her, Mai’s stomach growled again. She flushed red, the sound wiping away the anxious thoughts that had been pressing on her.

“Y-Yes, p-please.”

Over the next few days, Mai watched Miho with a curiosity that grew slowly—like a plant in half-shadow, reaching for light it didn’t fully understand.

The woman had something unusual about her: a calmness, and at the same time a looseness that didn’t quite match her age. Mai had studied her face more than once, trying to decipher the years hidden behind that unperturbed expression. Inside, she suspected Miho must be past thirty, yet something in the way she moved, the way she spoke—so sure, so casual at home—made Mai think she could be older. Much older.

It intrigued her. And Miho made no effort to dispel that quiet mystery.

She spoke little of her past despite being cheerful, relaxed, and openly humorous. Yet what she communicated to Mai wasn’t only in words—it could be seen in her gestures, in the way she watched her, as if she had seen too much to be young… too much to be exactly what she appeared. But her personality was so lively that was difficult to say she was too older. She like music, drink beer in the night, and also make silly jokes.

The house Miho lived in was an echo of the woman herself—caught between the ancient and the modern.

Mai could feel the past in every corner: dusty books, age-worn wood, the soft creak of floorboards that whispered that some parts needed repair even though they gleamed with polish.

During those first days, Mai explored like a cautious cat, rarely venturing outside, drawn by the house’s strange atmosphere, a quiet mystery that seemed to cling to everything. The property even had a wide yard with a second building in the back.

What had begun as simple wandering through rooms soon became a small explorations—something to occupy her hands and her restless mind.

Every corner seemed to hold its own story, and her questions about Miho multiplied with every little discovery.

In her earliest expeditions, Mai followed every hallway, stepping into rooms, climbing the stairs that led to the upper floors. The house resembled an antique shop in some way due the amount of stuff everywhere—but not an ordinary one.

It felt like time had halted there.

Between the shelves crowded rare, peculiar artifacts: metal figurines, porcelain objects with inscriptions Mai couldn’t read, ancient clocks with long-dead mechanisms that somehow still held an eerie beauty.

The walls in the living were lined with bookshelves, filled with volumes on magic, old grimoires written in languages Mai didn’t know.

Miho’s house wasn’t only a home.

It was a refuge for forgotten things, a place where the past breathed in every corner.

And a place where certain objects were forbidden to move.

Miho seemed strict about feng shui and oracles—understandable, given her work.

The front section of the house looked like what one might expect from a modest but spacious old property. It held two studios.

The one near the entrance was where she received clients, conducted small ceremonies, and performed readings—nothing requiring more than her desk or an oval table, also that study had more bookshelves.

The kitchen and living room sat connected but intentionally separated from that front study—Miho’s attempt at keeping her work “somewhat professional,” as she put it.

Beyond that, the second studio—facing the garden—was a personal sanctuary, with a wide window overlooking the overgrown greenery, as if the garden had decided long ago to rule that part of the world.

And then there were the upper floors, furnished with antique pieces and old floors maintained with meticulous care, despite the house’s secret tenants: long-legged spiders nesting in quiet forgotten corners. Miho insisted they should be left alone. “They’ll leave when they feel like it.”

Mai didn't like spiders, but she couldn't seem to figure out why. Miho told her that the reason must be simple. Mai was part moth fairy. She must be reacting because spiders sometimes hunt moths. She shouldn't be surprised if she was afraid of bats and other birds as well. Mai certainly didn't like the idea.

Beside that Mai could see the contrast between what Miho showed the world and what she kept hidden.

Those private corners seemed to be where Miho found her real peace—a place where words were unnecessary. Where the scent of wood and old books had been her only company long before Mai ever arrived.

There were no photographs in the house. But there were paintings.

As the days passed, Mai began noticing additional details that made Miho’s house feel even more enigmatic. Like how the neighbors sometimes crossed the street instead of walking past the front gate. Even so, they spoke to Miho casually—except for one nosy woman crossing the street who seemed to live for spying on others.

Mai felt a chill the first time she saw her. Watching through the curtains, the woman looked like one of those pale ghosts from old paintings. To her relief, the neighbor was no ghost—just a busybody. Apparently, there had been incidents in Miho’s house before… the spiritual kind.

And as it turned out, the house itself carried a bit of supernatural history. To reassure her, Miho explained that she had exorcised the spirit long ago—right after moving in. Thanks to that ghost, she had actually gotten the place at a bargain.

Around the house, in certain corners, Mai found protective seals, shikigami, and mirrors arranged like a surveillance system. Miho warned her not to touch them, they were “for emergencies.”

Mai wondered if a regular alarm system wouldn’t be simpler—but then again, maybe these were the kind of emergencies one couldn’t exactly call the authorities about.

Miho also warned her that although there were no spirits of the dead in the place, there were familiar spirits that remained connected throughout the house by means of talismans. Whenever anything happened, these spirits would communicate to Miho where the problem was. Apparently, a certain fey priestess, who was a friend of Miho's, had created them. After a few days, once Mai had gotten used to the place, the spirits would show themselves. They were a kind of wolf and fox that flitted around the house when they were bored, although most of the time they remained invisible. She got used to them, and some mornings when she woke up, there would be one floating above her bed, looking as if it were sleeping.

One afternoon, in a corner of the entryway, right beside the door, she noticed a worn, wrinkled sign.

It was pinned neatly, almost as if it were part of the décor, but its message was anything but subtle:

“Assistant wanted. Experience in cleaning (and gardening) and handling phone calls required. Must be available all day.”

Miho had no time for small daily tasks.

Her life was marked by absences—sometimes for days—and Mai soon realized those absences weren’t random. Miho often vanished for long hours, even in the dead of night, and when she returned, she sometimes brought someone with her. The house received a surprising variety of visitors: stern-looking police officers, Buddhist monks, Shinto priests, and even well-dressed civilians—a strange blend of the sacred and the mundane. Miho used to get angry with the monks sometimes, telling them to at least obey the rules and wear civilian clothes instead of drawing attention to themselves.

Mai, aware that her presence was an intrusion, avoided those moments whenever possible. She retreated to the living room or one of the quiet corners of the house, where Miho rarely disturbed her, and buried herself in the books Miho allowed her to touch, along with the spirits that played around her. There weren’t many books that she could touch, and most were about history, philosophy, or ritual manuals—dry enough to put her to sleep. There were only a few novels, and some tourist guides about Japan and China.

The other books were off-limits. Not only books but also scrolls. Miho warned her that some of them were dangerous, filled with secrets not meant for just anyone. Mai accepted the warning and kept her distance, she felt that Miho had already done more than enough for her by offering shelter, clothing, and food. She wasn’t about to disobey.

Some of the restricted texts were dark, often incomplete, as if someone had tried to erase their contents. Even so, Mai couldn’t shake the feeling that something within those pages was calling to her. The curiosity simmered, but she wasn’t ready to risk uncovering what Miho had labeled “dangerous.”

Fuyu no Makimono, The scrolls of Hsan, The Great One, The Sign of Signs, The Dark Version of Bao Puzi, The Ten Thousand Curses of the Hour of the Ox, The Scrolls of the Cursed Verses of Omoikane, Futago Hoshizora no Kiroku, Alchemical Study of Hypnerotomachia Poliphili, Recipes and Invocation that could be performed through the influence of Liao, Zhouyi Cantong Qi, Vermis Mysteriis by Ludvig Prinn, among thousands of other obscure books.

Miho even warned her that a couple of the books bit. Mai wasn’t sure if it was a joke or not. Some tomes, bound in leather with monstrous designs, really did look like they might lunge at the reader for a good chomp.

Just in case, she poked those with a stick—from a safe meter away.
