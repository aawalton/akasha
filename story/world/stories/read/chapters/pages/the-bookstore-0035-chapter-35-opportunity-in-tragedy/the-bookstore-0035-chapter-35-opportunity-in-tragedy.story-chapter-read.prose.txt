Anne refused before the trio could place the coins back on the counter. They had not completed the full list, but they had followed the priorities correctly and brought back the materials she needed first. The missing items were convenient, not urgent. Ceres could retrieve the rest when she next passed through the region.

The reason for the interrupted commission stood only a few tables away, in the form of a weary dwarf and a half-elf girl trying very hard not to fall apart. Anne noticed the connection immediately but chose not to press.

Rick’s order had also arrived with the supplies, so the hunter would soon be able to leave on his next mapping trip. Anne took more satisfaction from that than from the rest of the haul. She listened carefully while Cyrus, Iris, and Hakon recounted the wild dog hunt on the road, interrupting now and then with a practical question. Their success had been small, but encouraging. More importantly, they had returned with her goods instead of running with her coins.

Anne remembered such things. With the road behind them and coin in hand, the three seemed ready to spend their little treasure on reading credits before exhaustion settled over them again.

Anne understood the temptation. She also understood the numbers.

She had paid them forty silver for the supply run. They had earned another eight from the wild dog. Ten had already disappeared into food and potions, and another day in Riverend would have eaten more. The borderlands bled money from anyone careless, and the trio still stood close enough to poverty that one poor decision could undo several good ones.

When they collected Rick’s armor and spear, all three had apparently lingered near the edge of buying equipment for themselves as well. Anne was relieved they had resisted. Advancement remained the wiser use of coin, and this time they had remembered before the excitement carried them too far.

They had still allowed themselves three purchases, none of them foolish. Potiomancy ranged from ordinary usefulness to absurd luxury. In fair conditions, prices rose by tier, quality, rarity, and trust in the maker. The trio had stayed cautious. Their largest expense had been a high-copper comprehension potion for five silver. The other two, a fire essence potion and a healing essence potion, both low-bronze in quality, had cost two silver each.

Anne’s attention lingered on the comprehension potion. She was only mildly fond of such things. Used too often, they encouraged wrong habits. People who grew used to comprehension in a bottle rarely learned how to wrestle honestly with difficult knowledge. Fatigue and temporary mental strain were manageable. Dependence on effortless progress was the true danger.

Cyrus might be one of the rare exceptions. His struggle with literacy had become too obvious to ignore. Left alone, it would eventually slow not only him, but the entire party. If one potion gave him two hours of enhanced grasp, enough to consolidate what he had already fought to learn, then the expense might prove worthwhile. Used carefully, it could become a push rather than a method.

The other two potions were simpler. A temporary rise in power or control was no miracle, but sometimes one brief advantage made the difference between returning home and dying on the road.

Anne called for Moruh to take the materials away and see them properly stored. The blue familiar appeared almost immediately, and with him came the first ordinary reaction Alanis had shown since entering the bookstore.

The half-elf stared. Riverend’s poorer quarters did not often place people near magical companions of Moruh’s sort. When such creatures appeared, they usually belonged to the very rich, dangerous, or people one should avoid offending. Alanis looked from Moruh to Anne, uncertainty cutting through her exhaustion. The bookseller who had offered water and shelter was suddenly harder to understand.

Anne noticed the stare and ignored it. But Moruh did not.

He leapt onto the counter, rubbed his head against Anne’s sleeve with exaggerated possession, and glanced toward Alanis as if making sure she saw every moment of it. Then the purchased materials rose behind him in a neat, controlled procession.

Cyrus, Iris, and Hakon scarcely looked up. They were long accustomed to his performances. Moruh gave them a deeply offended look, lifted his chin, and stalked away with his floating burden and a dignity no one had granted him but himself.

The trio turned back to Anne almost immediately and began asking about the next step in their studies.

Anne reached for the note scrolls she kept for proper follow-up guidance. Cyrus needed work that reinforced letters without humiliating him. Iris needed references that would improve her planning before the next hunt. Hakon would benefit from something defensive and practical, the sort of text that rewarded patience and meticulousness.

Before she could choose, voices from deeper inside the building caught her attention. They were not loud enough to suggest trouble, only curiosity.

Spring, she guessed, had found their new guest.

Anne excused herself and rose. Not everyone would recognize something treefolk-like for what it was, and Spring’s current speaking abilities were still too limited to carry a first meeting alone. Better to smooth the matter before curiosity turned into discomfort.

She passed through the storage room and found them immediately. Dorothy stood facing Spring with the alert, respectful stillness of someone meeting something unusual without insulting it through fear. Spring, meanwhile, was trying very seriously to conduct an introduction.

“I, Spring,” she said, pointing at herself.

After a pause filled with concentration, she added, “You?”

Dorothy did not laugh.

“I’m Dorothy,” she said with complete sincerity. “Nice to meet you, Spring.”

Spring gave something very close to a pleased smile. Anne took in the scene and approved of the dwarf’s sense. Fear would have been understandable, and mockery would not have been forgiven, but Dorothy had chosen neither.

“Oh, I see you’ve already met,” Anne said lightly. “And how do you like the freshening up, Dorothy? Feeling a little more like yourself?”

The transformation deserved appreciation. The bath had lifted much of the road from the dwarf’s face and posture. Without the grime, fear, and exhaustion weighing so heavily on her, Dorothy looked less like a fugitive dragged in by disaster and more like a sensible, experienced woman who had survived one. She had also done admirable work with the plain yellow cloth Anne had provided, turning it into a simple knee-length dress laced in red at the waist and breast. It was modest, practical, and somehow made her seem more distinctly herself.

Anne had expected no less from someone who braided her hair that well.

“I hope Spring didn’t startle you,” Anne added as they walked back toward the main room.

“Not at all,” Dorothy said. “The girl’s just a bit enthusiastic.”

Anne caught the choice of words at once. The dwarf had not pried or pretended ignorance in some clumsy attempt at politeness. She had simply accepted Spring as a girl and moved on.

Insightful, respectful, and tactful.

When the two of them re-entered the study space, Cyrus, Iris, and Hakon blinked in visible surprise at Dorothy’s transformation. Alanis sagged slightly with relief at seeing the dwarf restored to something closer to herself.

The half-elf had been holding herself upright through shame, fear, and very little else. Anne could see the strain in the set of her shoulders and the way her eyes kept lowering before anyone could meet them for long.

Anne turned to her gently.

“Why don’t you go and wash up too, Alanis?”

The girl immediately looked as if she wanted to refuse. Her instinctive resistance only made the offer more necessary. She needed privacy, a bath, and a few quiet minutes where no one expected her to speak. Dorothy gave the smallest approving nod, and Alanis finally agreed in a timid voice.

Anne led her away. The girl was only a little taller than Anne, which made things easier. Anne returned briefly to her own room, then came back with a bucket of clean water, a fresh cloth, and a change of clothes that ought to fit well enough.

“These should do,” she said, setting them down carefully.

Alanis accepted them with a wary look. Anne did not force gratitude from her. She only nodded once and withdrew with Dorothy, leaving the girl alone.

Neither woman commented on what Alanis would likely do once the door closed. The girl needed the chance to cry without witnesses. Some grief had to be given privacy, or it became another humiliation. Anne closed the door gently behind them and returned toward the front of the bookstore with Dorothy beside her.

Whatever tragedy had delivered these two women to her door, trouble would follow. Anne had no doubt of that. Fear, desperation, and disruption had already entered with them.

But trouble was not the only thing she saw. Dorothy had dignity, skill, and the kind of tact Anne valued. Alanis needed safety and direction before shame hollowed her out completely. The bookstore had always grown by turning need into work, knowledge, and opportunity.

This time, the need had simply arrived in a more painful shape.
