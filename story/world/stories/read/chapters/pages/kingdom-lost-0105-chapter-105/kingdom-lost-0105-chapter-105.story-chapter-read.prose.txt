Chapter 105

The body of Korr-Va lay where the horse had brought him, the leather cord still wound at his throat and the long knife still buried in the hollow above his collarbone. His two Clawborn knelt at the gate with their hands bound behind them and a soldier at each of their shoulders. The horses stood quiet. The wind had not yet returned through the gate.

The Hoshin did not move.

They stood along the edges of the open ground in the loose clusters they had occupied when Korr-Va had walked his counterclockwise prowl. Their heads were no longer lowered, and their hands were no longer hanging at their sides, but they did not move. They looked at the body. They looked at Tama. They looked at the soldiers, and at Riley, and at one another, waiting for what would come next.

The old healer had stepped back a pace from Tama. She kept one thin hand on Tama’s forearm. The tracks of tears had dried on Tama’s cheeks now, and her breathing had returned to the slow, measured rhythm it had held before, but a small tremor still passed through her shoulders at intervals, the way the last shivers move through a body after the cold has gone.

Riley walked back across the square to her and stopped a pace short of Tama.

She did not look only at Tama. She turned her head as she spoke and let her voice carry to the edges of the square, and the Hoshin lifted their faces to listen.

“This is the end of the old way,” Riley said. “This culture of servitude, of giving yourselves for the betterment of others. Now you will invest in your own futures. This is the new way. Defeating the Clawborn is only part of the journey. Freedom and community are the goal, and they will be our future.”

She looked at Tama for a moment, then back out across the square.

“You are the last to join the others. They are waiting for you.”

The Hoshin looked at her. A few of them nodded, but most only watched.

“Gather your things,” Riley said. “We cannot bring the wagons through the trees. What you carry, you carry on your own back or on a horse, so bring what matters. Bring the small things and the things that cannot be replaced. Leave what can be made again. Meet back here when you are done.”

No one moved.

Tama cleared her throat.

“Everyone,” she said.

Her voice was quiet, but it reached the corners of the square.

“Gather your things. We meet back here. We have one last thing left to do.”

The Hoshin nodded, then broke from the edges of the square in twos and threes and went toward their huts.

Thorne moved.

Riley had not given him a word. She did not need to. He turned toward the gate where the soldiers stood with the bound Clawborn and the horses, and he instructed them to help the Hoshin with their belongings.

Riley watched for a moment, then turned back to Tama.

“You are right,” Tama said. “About the future.”

Riley looked at her. Tama was not looking at Riley. She was looking past Riley’s shoulder toward the open door of her own hut.

“We have a lot to do,” Riley said. “But we will get there.”

Tama looked at her.

“The Hoshin have been lost for a long time,” she said. “This will be the true beginning of the transition.”

Riley nodded.

“What do you need from me to help with this?”

Tama drew a small breath.

“Nothing more than what you have already given,” she said. “And maybe more time.”

“You’ll have time.”

They stood for a moment. A soldier passed carrying a roll of bedding beneath one arm and a small woven basket in the other hand. He nodded to Riley without slowing and continued toward the gate.

“We will make it together,” Riley said. “Your people and mine.”

“Our people,” Tama corrected with a slight smile.

Riley and Tama let the idea settle between them. This was the end of something they had begun together, and the weight of that needed time to rest.

“Before the Hoshin can move forward,” Tama said, “there is one last thing we have to do.”

She turned and walked across the open ground toward her hut at the northern edge of the square, past the empty mat, past the long smear in the dirt. She did not look down as she passed it. She stepped through her doorway.

Riley watched the hut for a moment, then turned and walked back across the square toward Thorne at the gate.

It took the better part of the morning.

The Hoshin emerged from their huts one at a time and in pairs, carrying their belongings. A bundle of clothing rolled tight in a piece of oiled cloth. A small wooden box with a carved lid. A cooking pot with the handle wrapped in twine. The soldiers took the heavier pieces and lashed them to the horses. The Hoshin carried what could not be carried for them.

The old healer brought out a basket no larger than a loaf of bread. She would not allow a soldier to carry it. She held it against her ribs with both arms, and she neither looked down at it nor met the eyes of anyone who looked at her while she held it. After a moment, the soldiers stopped offering, and she stood at the edge of the square with the basket pressed against her ribs and waited.

Thorne came to Riley’s shoulder once and said, “The Clawborn at the gate.”

“Take them out now,” Riley said. “Far enough that the village does not see, but close enough that we can ride past them when we leave. Two soldiers on each.”

“Yes, Warden.”

The two bound Clawborn were lifted to their feet and marched out through the gate with soldiers surrounding them. They disappeared down the path leading into the trees.

The body of Korr-Va still lay in the dirt. No one had moved it.

The horses were loaded. The Hoshin gathered again along the edges of the square, though more loosely now, their packs at their feet or strapped to the horses behind them, their hands free.

Tama’s door opened.

She came out empty-handed. She crossed the open ground to where the rest of her belongings had been stacked at the edge of the central group: a small pack, a folded cloak, and a wooden bowl. She stopped behind them and looked up at her people.

“Before the Hoshin can move forward,” she said, “we have to say goodbye to the past.”

She turned and walked back to her hut. This time she did not enter it. She reached inside the doorway, and when her hand emerged it held a lit torch.

She turned and faced the square.

“This is the end of the old way.”

She pressed the torch to the thatch at the corner of her hut.

The thatch caught at once. Flame raced up the roofline in a quick orange seam, and the dry timber beneath it ignited a moment later with the soft, hollow whuff of old wood taking fire.

Tama stepped back.

The old healer stood at her shoulder. Tama held out the torch without looking, and the old woman took it from her hand. She walked to the next hut and touched the flame to the corner of its thatch, and the second hut caught as well.

She passed the torch on.

It traveled from hand to hand around the square. A young man took it from the old healer and lit his father’s hut. A woman lit her grandmother’s. The torch moved around the ring.

Riley caught Thorne’s eye across the square and gave a single nod. Thorne turned to the soldiers waiting beside the loaded horses, and they began leading the animals through the gate at a slow walk, down the path toward the treeline. The packs went with them. The square emptied of everything except the Hoshin, the burning huts, and the body in the dirt.

The torch came back to Tama.

She lowered it toward the ground and let it fall. It landed in the dirt and lay there with its flame still burning, small now against the larger fires behind her.

She picked up her pack, her cloak, and her bowl. She did not look back at her hut.

The Hoshin began to move. They went toward the gate in twos and threes. They passed the body of Korr-Va in the dirt without slowing and without looking down.

Riley walked beside Tama. They went last.

At the gate, Tama stopped and turned to look back.

The huts were burning in earnest now. Fire had leapt from roof to roof, and smoke rose in a tall pale column straight into the cold air because there was no wind to bend it.

Tama watched for perhaps ten breaths.

Then she turned and walked through the gate.

At the treeline, the Hoshin had gathered. They had stopped there on their own, the soldiers and horses standing slightly apart, and they faced the village in a loose half-circle with the dark wall of trees at their backs.

The old healer began to sing.

It was the same thin, wavering voice she had used the morning she danced for Korr-Va, but the song was different now. A low note held beneath it without moving, and the melody rose and fell above that note in a slow, mournful pattern.

Riley listened.

A second voice joined beneath the low note, then a third, then four or five more at once, and the song spread through the group the way the fire had spread across the huts.

Riley stood and listened. Thorne stood at her left shoulder. The soldiers beside the horses had removed their helms and stood holding the reins in silence.

The song ended the way the wind had died earlier in the square: without warning.

The final phrase descended into the low note. The low note held for the count of three breaths, then released.

The Hoshin turned.

They put their backs to the burning village and their faces to the way ahead, and began to walk. They disappeared into the trees. All that was left was the burning huts. It was the end of the past lighting the Hoshin’s future.
