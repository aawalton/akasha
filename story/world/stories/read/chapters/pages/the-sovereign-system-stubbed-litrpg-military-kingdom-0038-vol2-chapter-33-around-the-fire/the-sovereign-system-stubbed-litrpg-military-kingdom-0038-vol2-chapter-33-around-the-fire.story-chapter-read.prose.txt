After leaving the unconscious minister behind, the group of fifty men—among them the second prince—traveled south along the river toward the southern border. Without the burden of a carriage, they moved quickly, navigating the rough terrain with ease.

Prince Lyon rode with practiced ease, likely trained from a young age. Not only did he keep pace with the others, he hadn’t voiced a single complaint during the four days of hard travel.

On the fourth night, as they set up camp beneath a blanket of stars, General Drakon announced that they would reach their destination by the following day. He summoned Richard, Tristan, and the second prince to a strategy meeting, leaving the rest of the men to gather around the fire.

Stanley Prichard was an unremarkable-looking soldier, his features weathered by age but his frame still strong—much like the men around him. A veteran of over twenty years in the king’s army, the lines on his face and the gray streaks in his beard spoke of countless campaigns.

Even here, in a rough makeshift camp under threat of war, the other soldiers treated him with deference.

“Stan, how are your old bones holding up after all this riding?” one soldier asked, flashing him a cheeky grin.

Stanley scoffed. “I was riding horses before you were a tadpole in your father’s ballsack. You think four days in the saddle means anything to me?”

Laughter erupted around the fire. These kinds of crude jests were common among soldiers—levity forged from the ever-present shadow of death.

But the current camaraderie had taken time to build.

After witnessing General Drakon attack the second prince and threaten the minister, morale had plummeted. The men were wary, dreading the temper of a boy who wielded the title of general.

Stan had felt that dread more keenly than most.

He’d seen his share of reckless youths, obsessed with glory and blind to the costs paid by their men. In his experience, teenage commanders usually left trails of dead soldiers behind them.

At first glance, this young general seemed no different—sharp eyes, cold face, and the quiet intensity of someone who hadn’t yet known failure.

But as the days passed, Stan’s judgment softened.

The boy—no, the general—proved to be careful, measured. He watched his men closely, adjusting the pace when fatigue showed. Even today, he’d called for camp earlier than expected, noting their exhaustion without a word of complaint. It was a small gesture, but one that meant everything to soldiers used to being treated like tools.

Stan’s gaze drifted toward the largest tent, where the general, the second prince, and the others were deep in discussion. Lantern light flickered within, shadows moving across the canvas walls.

“What do you think they’re talking about?” a voice asked beside him.

“It’s not for us to know, squirt,” Stan replied, ruffling the dark hair of the young soldier at his side.

The kid barely looked older than the general himself. Stan still couldn’t understand why someone so green had been included in this mission.

Even so, the boy grinned at the attention, cheeks red with embarrassment.

“You got a sweetheart back home?” Stan asked, his tone gentler now, the firelight dancing in his eyes.

The young soldier smiled and nodded slowly.

“We’re set to marry in the spring.”

Stan’s expression shifted—just slightly. Something flickered behind his eyes. After a brief pause, he managed a weak smile.

“Good. You’re a lucky man. Most of us spend so much time whoring we forget what it means to settle down.”

One of the other soldiers let out a laugh.

“Nothing beats the courtesans in Ralis City. We’ll take you to the Jade Silk Pavilion when we get back.”

“No, you damn well won’t,” Stan said, standing abruptly. His glare cut through the laughter like a blade.

The soldier only shrugged.

“Even if we don’t go, do you think you can stop all the other soldiers, old man?”

Stan didn’t answer. His face turned unreadable.

“I need to piss,” the man muttered, turning away from the fire.

As his footsteps faded into the darkness, the young soldier leaned toward the others, whispering with a confused look.

“What’s up with him?”

No one answered at first. The older men exchanged glances, unsure whether to speak.

Finally, one of the veterans broke the silence.

“Stan’s relationship with the Jade Silk Pavilion is… complicated.”

The fire crackled as discomfort settled over the group. Most already knew the story. But the kid, full of curiosity, seemed on the verge of asking more—until he wisely held his tongue.

“It’s not exactly a secret,” said the same soldier who had teased Stan earlier. He stepped closer to the flames, rubbing his hands together for warmth before launching into the tale.

“Back when Stan first joined the army, he had a sweetheart. When he came back from his first expedition, he found his hometown wiped off the map.”

He spoke without emotion, as if recounting someone else’s pain.

“He didn’t take it well. You can imagine. But he stayed faithful to her. Even when everyone believed she was dead.”

“Wait—believed?” the younger soldier cut in, eyes wide. “She wasn’t?”

The storyteller ignored him and continued.

“For fifteen years, the old man kept his vow. Never strayed, never even looked at another woman. Then one night, after we got him properly drunk, we dragged him along to the Jade Silk Pavilion.”

He paused, eyes flickering with memory.

“And that’s when he saw her…”

The fire popped. No one spoke.

“She was alive. Even after fifteen years, he recognized her the moment he laid eyes on her.”

Just then, Stan returned to the fire.

The others quickly shifted, pretending nothing had happened. But the youngest turned toward him, tears brimming in his eyes.

“So… did you reunite with your sweetheart?” he asked, unable to contain his hope.

Stan’s expression darkened, but when he looked at the boy’s face, he let out a long breath. He walked over, placed a hand on the kid’s shoulder, and sat down heavily beside him.

The others were surprised—Stan wasn’t usually so tolerant of personal questions. But the old soldier ignored them all.

“Things aren’t as simple as they seem, young one,” he said quietly. “After the attack, Rose fled to the capital. She was taken in by the Jade Silk Pavilion.”

The young soldier leaned in.

“But now that you’ve found her, can’t you… take her back?”

The innocence in his voice hung in the air.

Stan’s expression stiffened.

“As I said, it’s not that simple. Anyone who wants to marry a courtesan from the Pavilion has to pay a dowry. For the past five years, I’ve been saving every coin I can…”

“How much is it? Maybe I can help!” the kid offered, his voice bright with sincerity.

Caught off guard, Stan let out a dry laugh and ruffled the boy’s hair.

“It’s a thousand gold coins. Even if you handed over your next month’s wages, it wouldn’t leave a scratch.”

“A—a thousand gold!?” the boy stammered, eyes going wide. “But we only get a single gold coin a month!”

“Aye,” Stan said with a chuckle. “That’s why I said it’s impossible. But I’ll pay it off myself. I don’t want to owe anyone anything.”

The soldier who’d told the story earlier piped up with a wicked grin.

“You think you’re the first one to offer? This stubborn old bastard’s gonna die alone at this rate.”

The others chuckled, but the young soldier looked unconvinced. His brow furrowed with determination.

“But… how can you raise that much by yourself? Even if you’ve saved everything these last five years…”

He glanced down, counting on his fingers, trying to make sense of the impossible math.

“Don’t strain yourself, boy,” Stan said, grinning.

Laughter rippled around the campfire, the heaviness lifting just enough for the men to breathe easy again.

“Alright, that’s enough for tonight. Get some rest,” Stan ordered, using the boy’s shoulder to help himself to his feet. “I’ll take the first watch.”

The soldiers didn’t argue. With practiced ease, they rolled out their bedding and curled up around the fire. Soldiers had a knack for sleeping anywhere, in any condition.

The kid hesitated for a moment, but Stan gave him a gentle nudge toward his bedroll. Eventually, he relented and lay down, leaving only Stan and another soldier still awake.

“You take that side. I’ll cover the north,” Stan instructed, gripping his spear as he moved to his post.

His eyes drifted toward the makeshift command tent where the general, the prince, and the others were still locked in discussion. It had been over an hour since they entered.

Stan tried to listen in without drawing attention.

Guilt gnawed at his conscience, but he pushed it down. Everything he’d said by the fire was true—he had been saving to pay the dowry. But as the kid had pointed out, it was a hopeless task on a soldier’s pay alone.

So Stan had turned elsewhere.

He had made a deal with the Black Tiger Gang. In exchange for coin, he would pass on what scraps of military information he could gather. It was dishonorable—treasonous even—but for love, he had crossed that line.

Even now, he hated himself for it.

But Rose was the only thing left in this world he cared about.

Such was the curse of love.

“We must first ascertain the situation at Xiandong…”

Stan listened intently, trying to catch the voices from within the tent while keeping his eyes on the distant treeline. Unfortunately, beyond a few scattered words, he couldn’t make out enough to understand the discussion.

Letting out a quiet sigh, he gave up for the night. This wasn’t his original task, after all.

His real assignment was something far more delicate: protect the second prince. And the one who’d issued that order was none other than Ming Hu—the leader of the Black Tiger Gang. Strangely enough, the king had given Stan the same directive.

Stan was more than willing to comply. It made his betrayal easier to justify. But what puzzled him was why someone like Ming Hu would want to safeguard the brat.

At first, it seemed a simple job. With so many loyal soldiers in the unit, what could possibly go wrong?

Then, less than an hour after they’d left Ralis City, the young general had nearly committed regicide.

Just the memory of that moment made Stan shudder. He remembered how helpless he’d felt as General Drakon stalked the second prince like a predator eyeing its prey.

There was something terrifying in the way the young man moved—graceful, silent, unflinching. Stan hadn’t been able to lift a finger. It was like standing in the presence of a beast untamed by nature.

His thoughts scattered as the tent flap rustled and three figures stepped into the night. The second prince, the quartermaster, and the knight—Richard Boyd—emerged. The knight’s eyes immediately found Stan.

Stan straightened and gave a respectful bow.

But Richard didn’t react. Instead, the hulking man strode toward him with heavy, deliberate steps. Stan’s heart picked up pace.

“You’re Stanley Prichard?” the knight asked, his voice deep and unreadable.

“Yes, sir,” Stan replied, doing his best to sound composed. “How may I be of service?”

The words came out uneven. Being under Richard Boyd’s gaze was intimidating enough—but with the weight of guilt pressing on his chest, it was nearly unbearable.

If the knight suspected anything, he didn’t show it.

“The general wishes to see you,” Richard said flatly, gesturing toward the tent.

“Yes, sir.” Stan dipped his head, then turned toward the makeshift shelter, forcing himself to control his breathing.

He stepped inside.

The candlelight inside flickered with every breath of wind, casting shadows that danced along the tent walls. The air was still, thick with smoke and tension.

General Luke Drakon sat on a low stool, hunched over a crude map drawn into the dirt floor. The lines etched across the soil marked the southeastern border—each stroke firm, decisive.

Even in the dim light, Stan could see the youth in the general’s face. He looked younger than the green soldier Stan had spoken to earlier.

But there was no mistaking the aura that surrounded him.

Stan didn’t dare let his guard slip.

“You wished to see me, General?” he asked, cupping his fist and bowing low.

“Mmm. Come. Sit.” Luke’s voice was calm, almost disinterested, and he didn’t lift his gaze from the map.

A knot formed in Stan’s stomach. His instincts screamed that something was wrong.

Still, he obeyed. He had no other choice. After taking a seat, the tent flap moved, revealing the hulking knight who stood sentinel at the only exit. Stan’s gaze flashed back to Luke who was still studying the makeshift map on the ground.

“So tell me, Stanley,” the young man said, finally lifting his gaze. “Who are you working for?”

Stan froze.

Those hazel eyes stared straight through him—intelligent, calculating, and disturbingly calm. For a heartbeat, he couldn’t breathe. The tent, the candlelight, the map etched in the dirt—all seemed to blur around the singular presence in front of him.

It felt as if he were staring into a galaxy, one that threatened to pull him into its depths and never let go.

A groaning sound of metal cut through the silence.

Stan’s head snapped around. Richard Boyd stood just behind him, unsheathing his massive greatsword. The weapon gleamed in the low light, and the knight’s hulking frame radiated a cold, murderous intent.

“I suggest you tell me the truth, Stanley,” the young general said, his voice calm despite the situation. “Your life—and thousands more—depend on it.”
