Panic broke loose in layers.

First came the scramble of boots and hands as the soldiers surged towards their fallen lords, dragging them back and away from the fighting. Then came the sound of armour trying not to rattle—and failing, of course—because when it came to Insect Gods and their auras, fear always found gaps in discipline.

Underneath the fallen lords’ skin, Zora heard all the wrong little notes of necrosis sinking in. They were all lucky to only have been hit on their sides by the wave of zombie ants. Any more and they’d have been dead already.

Still…

Kita, too, hit her knees beside her father.

“No—no—” her voice cracked on the second syllable. “You can’t… father, this isn’t—”

Machi tried to pull Kita back, trying to pry her from possibly getting caught on the necrosis as well. Baya’s breath, alongside the other lords’, had already become rasping bellows, half of their lungs working harder to make up for the other half that were failing—but that didn’t stop him from lifting his head just enough to look at Zora sternly.

“... Thousand Tongue,” he rasped, and the words came out thin. “This isn’t over, is it?”

Zora’s fingers tightened around his staff. The tips still throbbed where the zombie ants had bitten him, but he didn’t shake his head.

As long as Enki was here…

“You and the Worm Mage must retreat,” Baya continued. “Take them away. Take my daughter. Take my men and get them to safety.” Then he angled his head at Regalia. “We will hold her here and buy you all a little time to regroup.”

Kita jerked her father’s good arm, furious in a way grief often became.

“No!” she snapped. “No! I’m not leaving you, father!”

But behind the throne, Regalia’s amusement shifted. She may have stopped commanding her thralls to attack for a moment to hear them speak, but now her patience ran out, and she lifted her hand to send out another gushing wave of zombie ants.

This one would kill everyone in the Divine Hall, so Zora didn’t wait for the wave to rush across. He stabbed the butt of his staff into the marble so hard the vibration jumped up his arm and into his teeth.

“Witness divination!”

The air ignited in front of all of them, and these flames wrapped the Divine Hall from floor to ceiling, a living wall of fire that cut the hall in half. The wave of zombie ants slammed into it like rain into a furnace, and the popping, hissing, and the crackle of countless bodies turning to ash mid-crawl made his stomach want to hurl, but… his fire wall was holding.

Just for a moment. There were so many zombie ants that their physical biomass was overwhelming his flames.

“Divination!”

Flames surged again, thickening, renewing itself, reinforcing the barrier. The zombie ant wave continued gushing. Continued hitting harder. His breaths tightened.

“Divination!”

Again. Again. Each repetition of the spell was a hammer-blow delivered by his own throat, and each reinforcement poured more heat into the hall until even the stone beneath his boots ran fever-hot, until the banners that still hung in the far reaches began to curl at their edges.

All the while, Baya looked up through it all, eyes unfocused from pain.

“I see,” he whispered. “Of course even the Divine Empress, manipulated as she may be, would notice the Divine Attendant’s outlandish moves to weaken humanity after the events of this past year… which is why the Divine Empress hasn’t been the Empress for the past year.”

Kita made a broken sound—half denial, half prayer. A tremor ran through the surviving soldiers despite the heat. Zora, too, was running out of breath continuing to fire ‘fire’ type spells at the unending wave.

“Now, the Attini Empire faces its most dangerous threat.” Baya forced his head towards Zora, as if looking at him could anchor him. “So I must ask you again, Thousand Tongue, in the name of all Noble-Blood, living and dead: will you defend the Attini Empire once more?”

Heat rolled against Zora’s face. The fire wall demanded more.

“... What do you think, Salaqa Lord?” he replied, gritting his teeth. “They say shared sorrow is half the sorrow. Decima may be dead, but… my job here isn’t.”

That was the cue for Enki to whip his arms back and open a dozen wormholes, punching them into reality. Every soldier, healthy or injured, hesitated to go through them, because leaving their lords behind went against every instinct a soldier had.

Zora understood that.

He also understood that Regalia wouldn’t allow their loyalty to become anything except a funeral.

“The wind will favour our second battle,” he said. “Back now, everyone.”

Wind answered, yanking hard at every soldier’s armor straps and sleeves and collars, yanking them back into the wormholes whether they wanted to or not. Most shouted in surprise. Some cursed at him. Some tried to brace and stab their blades into the ground to hold on, but of course they failed.

Kita fought it the hardest, jamming both her blades into the ground even as Machi, Ifas, Sora, and Enki dashed back through their wormholes.

“Father!” she cried. “This isn’t over! There is still so much we can do for—”

The wind tore her free anyway. It hauled her up and back like a leaf caught in storm-current, and even as she reached towards her father with a desperate arm, the wormhole swallowed her.

Her voice cut off mid-syllable.

Soon, only Zora remained in the hall—Zora and the fallen Regional Lords, half-rotten and still breathing, still trying to be the kind of men a nation could stand on.

Baya noticed Zora glancing back at them. Even half-dead, the Salaqa Lord still had the instincts of a man who’d spent his life reading rooms and practising his silver tongue, so he winced slightly before giving Zora a crooked, mischievous smile.

“What is it?” he asked.

“... Decima may not believe you are a good adult for your contribution to the empire’s suffering,” Zora said quietly, “but a music teacher once told me that even if you can never do a hundred point performance, you must always strive for a hundred percent performance. In my eyes, you are a good lord.”

For a second, Baya said nothing.

Then he scoffed, tilting his head back.

“Go,” he said. “And, please, look after my daughter for me.”

Zora nodded.

Then he unfolded his wings and let the winds drag him back into the final wormhole, leaving the Divine Hall behind.

The moment the Thousand Tongue vanished through the wormhole, the Divine Hall seemed to exhale.

The wall of flames went with him, naturally, but it left behind a heat-haze that clung to broken stone and scorched banners for a few seconds. For a breaths, Baya expected Regalia’s black tide to immediately roll over what remained of them—to finish the job without ceremony—but instead, the wave of zombie ants receded, and the Greater Ant God stood idly above the dais.

Courtesy, Baya thought, was a sharper blade than cruelty. It made the dying man grateful.

The Divine Hall was no longer recognisable. Zombie ants had chewed through the ornate marble pillars until the walls were simply ragged ribs. Sunlight poured in through wide, ugly holes in the ceiling. The ant-head throne—once a grotesque monument of conquest, perhaps—was now broken and half-eaten, its polished mandibles dulled by bite marks and ash.

Regalia stood on the remains of the throne with the posture of a queen who’d never known a day of defeat, and her chitin reflected the sunlight, making him almost want to wince and look away.

He did look away—lowering his gaze to his own body.

Half of him was already dead. The necrosis had crawled across his left side like spilled ink, eating warmth and leaving behind a heavy numbness that made his breaths feel borrowed. His armour clung strangely, one side tight, the other side slack where flesh had begun to give. Around him, the other Regional Lords looked much the same—men of banners and bloodlines reduced to lurching silhouettes, teeth clenched, eyes dim with pain.

But none of them spoke. They simply looked at one another, and Baya heard the old conversation passing between them without words.

We have done many terrible things, it said.

And we are here anyways.

Baya planted his sawtooth blade into the ground and forced his legs to obey. The movement sent a grinding ache through his ribs. He tasted iron. Nearby, another lord heaved himself upright, and another spat blood and laughed.

One by one, they lifted themselves and their weapons. Some unfurled their ant wings, while some shifted and locked their chitin plates under their corroded armour. Baya himself willed his Art to activate, summoning a small wave of army ants behind him that began to climb up all of them. Back in his day in the army, he’d healed many wounded men with his army ants. Treating necrosis completely was impossible, but he could at least make his army ants climb into torn seams, stitch the worst of open wounds, and swarm across half-severed flesh to knot them together from the inside.

This scaffolding sort of healing would keep them standing for a few more minutes, at least.

As they healed, Regalia watched them in silence for a beat, then spoke—boredly—as if she were commenting on a tedious play.

“You have all struggled on for far too long,” she said. “At some point, it becomes unbecoming.” Then her gaze drifted over the holes in the walls, the spilling sunlight, and the scattered corpses around the hall that’d been zombies only minutes ago. “Every Swarmsteel Front loses ground year by year, inch by inch. Humanity does not advance. My scouts tell me none of the six fronts are working together. The Attini Empire itself has Twelve Forward Armies formed specifically to fight and fend off humans from other fronts, yet even then—even within the empire—you are divided. Such a species cannot defeat a united enemy such as us, let alone under her command.”

Baya’s ears caught on one word.

United… under ‘her’ command?

Regalia said it so casually, but as far as Baya knew, the Six Greater Insect Gods were the strongest bugs there were in the Swarm. Did they, too, answer to a higher hand that had yet to even reveal itself?

In the end, we know nothing about the Swarm, huh?

Regalia lifted one hand, preparing to release her wave of zombie ants again.

“You should have given up long ago,” she said, almost kindly. “You should have stayed home. You should have stayed with your families. You should have all died doing what you enjoyed, instead of spending your last moments pretending this futile effort is noble.”

Baya frowned.

“... Do you know how the Attini Empire was formed, Regalia?” he asked.

Regalia paused. Her hand remained raised, but she didn’t unleash them as she inclined her head by the smallest margin—a queen granting a dying man his final indulgence.

He took that silence and filled it.

“Sixty-three years ago, before the Swarm ever fell onto this end of the continent, the south was not an empire,” he began. “It was a splintered thing. A thousand little lands, a thousand petty households, each clutching a mouthful of power and butchering their neighbours for another bite. We shelled our own fields. We burned our own rivers. We spread our own plagues. The soil was so choked with iron and ash that nothing would grow, and men starved with swords still in their hands.”

He gestured weakly at the ruined hall around them, stone chewed through by ants and sunlight spilling where a ceiling once stood.

“There was already war long before the Swarm,” he went on. “War and suffering were the only tongue this land has ever spoken fluently. When the Swarm came, it didn’t introduce us to death. It merely stopped us from choosing sides amongst ourselves, and when the Swarm began killing everyone equally, one girl picked up a saw.”

Regalia’s antennae twitched.

“She was a simple carpenter’s daughter,” he continued. “She had no title. No system. No blessing from the gods of the earth. She carried but one of her father’s woodcutting saws in her hands, and she killed a Mutant-Class bug with it. Back then, such a thing was unthinkable. Entire platoons of trained soldiers struggled to bring down Giant-Classes, and she butchered a Mutant-Class alone before anyone even knew how to quantify strength. Can you imagine that?”

“...”

“And from that day on, she walked this land from south to north, and then back again. She gathered the bravest lords from every brave household—and brave, I say. Not the cleanest, not the kindest, but the ones willing to stand and die where they were told. She bound them under one banner and marched an army back to the southernmost edge of the continent, straight into the Crawling Sea, and there, she planted her banner. She declared that the end of the continent would be the end of the Attini Empire’s borders, and she built the walls of the Divine Capital with earth and blood, chitin and stone.”

He tapped his blade against the stone beneath his feet.

“Since then, we have fed our land with the bodies of the Swarm. Your flesh sinking into the earth turned our soil fertile again. Your meat made our people strong enough that many no longer needed to eat as often, leaving food for those who could not fight. Your overwhelming bioarcanic auras swallowed and destroyed plagues that would have wiped us out before. You may not believe me, but I will say this plainly, Greater Insect God: this land is better now than it was back then.”

Then his grip tightened on the hilt as he raised it, pointing it at Regalia.

“So what if a few lords die here?” he said. “The Attini Empire was built on the corpses of men who stood where we stand now. Today, it is simply our turn to join the pile. Our bloodlines will continue. Our descendants will fight on. You are but another obstacle in a long, ugly history that has never been kind enough to give us peace easily, but rest assured that when you are defeated—and you will be defeated—the Attini Empire will be stronger than it ever has been.”

Regalia regarded him in silence.

Then the Greater Ant God inclined her head once again, this time a slow and almost courtly gesture.

“... The Thousand Tongue was correct. You are a good lord, full of ambition you know you can never achieve. I am much the same,” she said. “Perhaps I will keep you as a thrall, after all. Your body would serve quite well among my ranks.”

The stone beneath her dais split with a sound, and from the cracks, three humanoid bugs clawed up through rubble and blood-slick marble. Chitin spikes burst from their shoulders and spines, and multiple insectoid limbs were grafted onto their torsos, making them look like unholy amalgamations of everything the Swarm had to offer.

Baya’s jaw tightened.

He’d read the reports. He’d heard the rumours. The Thousand Tongue and the Worm Mage had encountered a ‘Grafting Bug Afflicted’ in the Grand Ayapacha Military Academy—man-made abominations born of desperation and cruelty. His investigators had sworn all of the Grafting Bug Afflicted were destroyed during the incident in the academy, but seeing three of them here, rising obediently at Regalia’s feet…

So Decima did manage to retrieve at least a few Grafting Bug Afflicted for Regalia’s control before the laboratory was destroyed.

The Thousand Tongue will have his hands full with these ones.

But while pain kept flaring through his side, he straightened anyway, resting his grip more firmly on his sawtooth blood.

He supposed that some part of him missed fighting with his own two hands.

As Regalia’s wave of black ants washed forward once more, the other lords shifted beside him, wings flexing, armour creaking as they all found their proud, noble stances again. Blood dripped to the floor in uneven rhythms, but none of them stepped back. Briefly, Baya’s thoughts flickered, unbidden to Kita’s face—too young, too fierce, too burdened already. He could only pray she never had to stand where he stood now, but…

If she had to, he knew she’d make the same decision as him.

My good daughter.

Hear me now.

“For the Attini Empire!” he roared.

He lifted his blade. Around him, the lords followed suit, raising steel and chitin and wings in one final, defiant line as they charged at the Greater Insect God.
