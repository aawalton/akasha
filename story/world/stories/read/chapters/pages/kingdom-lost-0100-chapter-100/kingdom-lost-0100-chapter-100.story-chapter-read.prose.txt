Chapter 100

The valley held the silence for a long time after Edrin walked out of it.

He did not look back as he crossed the snow. The bodies of the forward command lay in the disposition they had held when the pulse took them, the line of them still recognisable as a line, the cliffs above them unchanged. He carried the broken staff in his right hand with the cracked stone hanging dark at its head. His left hand he kept loose at his side because the trembling had not stopped and he did not want to watch it.

He reached the camp at the southern mouth of the valley a little before midday. The rear guard was beginning to stir. Two thousand men had stood at the supply train when the pulse came, and the pulse had reached them last and weakest, and they had gone down where they stood without dying. They were waking now in the order they had fallen, the men at the back of the line first, the men nearest the bowl last. Edrin walked through them as they sat up in the snow. None of them spoke to him. A few of them looked at him as he passed, and what was in their faces was not the look of soldiers looking at a mage of the empire. It was the look of men who had felt something travel through them and had not yet found the words for it, and would carry the weight of this experience with them for the rest of their lives.

He did not stop.

At the stockpile of supplies he found the quartermaster on his knees in the snow beside an overturned crate. The man's eyes were open and his hands were on his thighs and he was breathing the slow steady breaths of someone who had been told to count them. He looked up when Edrin's shadow crossed him.

"Mage," The quartermaster greeted.

"Stand," Edrin replied.

The quartermaster stood. He did it the way a man stood after a substantial injury, in stages.

"The general?"

"Alive," Edrin said. "Unconscious, but he will wake. Most of the forward line will not."

The quartermaster's eyes moved past Edrin's shoulder toward the middle of the valley. He did not say anything. There was nothing in the middle of the valley that needed saying.

"How many of the rear are standing?"

"Most, mage. We are still counting."

"Count faster. When the general wakes he will want the number."

"Yes, mage."

Edrin walked past him toward the cleared patch of ground at the back of the supply train where the mage's tent had been pitched on the first night and had stood through the days since. He went inside and closed the flap. He stood for a moment in the dim of the tent with the broken staff in his hand and the cracked stone above his head and let himself feel, for the first time since the pulse, the full weight of what had happened in the valley.

It was not a small weight he was carrying. He set the staff against the centre pole of the tent. He sat down on the camp stool he had been using as a writing surface for days and put his face in his hands and breathed through his fingers. The trembling came up his arms into his shoulders and he let it come because there was no one to see it. He stayed that way for the length of time it took the trembling to pass on its own, which was longer than he had expected.

When it passed he stood. He took from the inside pocket of his robe the small folded square of waxed cloth that held the portal stones. He selected one. The portal stones were not like the staff stone. They were small and clear and held only the working that had been put into them at the time of their making, and the working was simple and would not break. They opened no wider than a man crouched, and they opened quietly, and they did not trouble the horses or the air around them as the greater portals did. He set the stone on the floor of the tent in the cleared patch and stepped back from it and spoke the word over it that he had spoken a hundred times before, and the stone took the word, and the air above it parted without sound, a low and narrow opening no taller than his waist when he knelt to it.

The opening was the size of a doorway and the colour of the inside of the imperial palace, which was the destination he had set into the stone before the campaign began.

He stepped through.

The throne room was warm. That was the first thing he registered, after days in the valley, and the warmth was an offence. He had walked out of cold that did not warm at fires and into a room with braziers along its walls and underfloor heat in its tiles and the smell of citrus from the bowls on the side tables, and his body did not know what to do with the warmth, and he stood inside the closing portal behind him with his eyes shut against the heat for a count of three before he was able to open them and present himself.

The Empress Octavia was on the throne. She was alone except for the two guards at the far doors and the scribe at the small desk against the eastern wall, and she had been reading from a sheet of parchment when he came through, and she did not put the parchment down. She looked at him over the top of it for a long moment. She took in the broken staff in his hand. She took in the cracked stone at its head. She took in the snow that still clung to the hem of his robe and to the toes of his boots, melting now in slow drops onto the tile of her throne room.

"Edrin."

"Empress."

"Approach."

He walked the length of the throne room. He walked it the way he had walked the open ground in the valley, like a man who knew what he was going to do at the other end of the walk, and what he was going to do at the other end of this walk was lie to the Empress of the Clawborn Empire, and he had decided that in the snow and had not unmade the decision in the portal and was not going to unmake it now.

He stopped at the foot of the dais and inclined his head.

"Report."

"The working succeeded, Empress. The opening at the head of the valley is closed."

She set the parchment down.

"Closed entirely?"

"Reduced, Empress. To the dimension it held before the working began. Nothing further will pass through it."

"And the cost?"

"Significant."

She waited.

"The forward line is lost," he said. "Five hundred at the cliffs. The main body in the centre of the valley, the greater part of it. The rear at the southern mouth survived. The general is alive. Unconscious when I left him. He will wake."

"Numbers."

"Approximately four thousand dead, Empress. The count is not yet final."

She did not move. The parchment lay where she had set it. Her hands were composed on the arms of the throne and her face did the thing her face did when she was thinking, which was nothing at all. He had been her mage long enough to know the silences. This one was not the silence of shock. It was the silence of a woman doing arithmetic.

"And the working?" she said. "What did the opening yield to your stone before it closed?"

This was the question. He had walked the length of the throne room knowing the question would come and knowing the answer he had decided to give in the snow, and he gave it now in the voice he used for reports, even and uninflected.

"The opening resisted, Empress. Our efforts drove it back by a third and then it pulsed. The pulse was what took the army. The stone broke and the opening returned to its prior state, but it will hold."

"You saw nothing through it."

"I saw the inside of the opening, Empress. I saw the rotation at its edges. I saw the cold that pours from it. I did not see what lies on the other side."

She held his eyes.

He waited with a stoic expression on his face.

The room was very warm and the snow at the toes of his boots had finished melting and there was a small dark patch on the tile beneath him that the scribe would have to call a servant to clean after he had gone, and he kept his eyes on the Empress's eyes and did not let his own move.

"Very well," she said.

He finally breathed and he did not let her see him breathe.

"You will rest," she said. "Two days. Then you will return to me and we will discuss the next phase. The opening is closed but it is not sealed, and I will not have an unsealed opening at the head of a valley four hundred leagues from my northern border."

"Yes, Empress."

"One more thing, Edrin."

"Empress."

She picked the parchment back up. She did not look at it. She looked at him over the top of it the way she had looked at him when he came through the portal.

"There have been whispers of a new settlement to the south but before the divide. Whispers of walls, of soldiers in uniform. Of a woman who governs."

He waited.

"I had set the matter aside for the campaign," she said. "The campaign is concluded. The matter is now in consideration."

"Yes, Empress."

"Two days, Edrin. Then we discuss the opening. And after the opening, we discuss this new settlement on the rise."

She lifted the parchment back to her eyes.

He inclined his head and with a nod, he withdrew. He walked the length of the throne room with his back straight and the broken staff in his hand and did not let himself think, until he was through the great doors and into the corridor beyond them, about what he had just heard.

A woman who governs, then he thought, four hundred leagues from the valley, in the other direction.

He thought, the Empress had two questions for me today, and I lied about one of them, and she has saved the other for after I have rested.

He walked down the corridor toward the mage's quarters, and the broken staff made a small dry sound against the tile with every second step, and behind him in the throne room the Empress Octavia set the parchment down on the arm of the throne and looked at the dark patch of melted snow on the tile at the foot of the dais for a long time without moving.
