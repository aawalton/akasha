It was late by the time we finally returned to Hamel. Late enough that the entire village was asleep.  Lanterns still burned along the main path, though most had dimmed to low, guttering things that barely kept back the dark. There were no villagers anywhere I could see. I did not even see a stray animal.

My mana reserves were rather thin by now. Ash walked beside me. We had not spoken much since leaving the forest. It seemed that the Hero was thinking. About what, I could not say.

A thought occurred to me, one that should have occurred to me before with the passing of the day. “She will be asleep,” I said. Even if the woman was asleep, I would do what I came here to do.

Ash shook her head. "She won't be asleep. Don't worry about it." Ash sounded very certain. I wondered where she found her certainty from, though she would certainly know more about this than I.

The hut's door was not much of a door. It was nothing more than rough planks of wood set into a frame of mud. I knocked, and half feared even my knocking might tip the thing over.

Then we waited. The footsteps came two heartbeats later. They drew close to the door, and then it opened.

"Why are you here?" Shera asked as soon as the door opened. I knew her name now. Ash had told me on the way here, though I had not asked. This Shera swayed on her feet, and she seemed thinner than when I had seen her mere hours ago.

Hers was a strange question. "I have returned to do what I promised," I said. "Or have you forgotten in less than the span of a single day?"

The mother stared at me. After a long moment of silence, she stepped aside. We both looked at each other and stepped inside after her.

The hut's interior was more barren than I had expected, and I had expected no great luxury. This hut made Martha's look like my castle. The door opened into a single space with a hearth that had not been lit in some time, judging by the mound of ash inside. The mother moved toward the back of the hut, where a cloth hanging from a doorway had separated the space into something resembling rooms. “He’s through here,” she said, stepping inside first. Ash was already halfway to the cloth.

“Stop.”

The woman stopped and turned. Ash turned as well, and in her eyes was a question. I had thought about this. I had thought about this in great detail.

I raised my right hand. A wisp of black fire curled from my fingertips. It was small. The darkness of it caught the dim light and swallowed it whole. Swallowed everything. Shera’s eyes fixed on it. “This is the power I will use on your son,” I said. “It is a power that can hurt...that can destroy. In the wrong hands, it will unmake whatever it touches. This is not like the white flame.”

The fire curled and guttered between my fingers. The room was very still. “To heal the boy, I must use this flame to break what the Ink left in him. There is no other way, and I am confident in my ability to do what I have said.” I paused. The next words cost me something, though I could not have said what. “There is still a risk. It is small, but it is real, and it is not nothing.”

The mother’s face had not changed, though Ash’s certainly had. Shera watched the black flame for a time. It is unbecoming of a queen to send a retainer to a death they did not know was coming. That is why I told her. “The choice is yours to make. It is not mine.”

I let the Requiem die. My hand fell to my side. The silence that followed was the longest I had endured in Hamel. Shera did not look at Ash. She did not look anywhere but at me, and I watched the decision move through her like a thing alive. This asking was an uncomfortable thing, for it could fail where my demands never had. There was sense in my words, and the risk was truly small, and yet it was there. I would not hide from the truth. Shera hunched in on herself, and I thought she’d made her decision. I thought she was going to refuse.

Her arms had folded across her chest, and every small part of her trembled. It was not cold. I was asking much of her, and yet offering more still. I would not judge her for her refusal. Though once, I would have.

Then Shera’s shoulders straightened. There was no fire in her eyes and no battle cry on her lips. She simply stood a little taller than she had a moment before, and when she spoke, her voice did not waver. “Can this thing really fix my baby?”

“Yes.”

“Then there’s no choice to make.”

I held her gaze for a long moment. In it I saw a resolve I had not seen in some of my most capable lieutenants. Something different than the steel of my soldiers, and yet no less fine. This woman had no power at all, and yet, for this one moment, I considered her a great warrior. I inclined my head. I could respect resolve. Even I would not dare mock or tarnish such a thing. The mother turned and pulled back the cloth.

Ash fell into step beside me as we moved toward the doorway. “I didn’t think you would say that,” she said quietly. “Any of it.”

“Neither did I,” I muttered. The words had felt like handing someone a weapon and trusting them not to use it. A strange thing, but it worked.

We stepped through the cloth, and behind it was a space so small it barely had room for the short straw bed where the boy lay. There was a single wooden stool beside the bed. I watched the boy. His eyes were open, staring up at the ceiling. They did not truly see it. A small blanket covered him, though it was not cold. I had expected as much.

I had not expected Sara. The girl was curled on the floor next to the bed, asleep.

One of her hands rested near Marin's outstretched arm. In sleep, her face held none of the brightness I had come to know from her. She looked too young for a vigil she had clearly been keeping for far longer than tonight.

I looked at the girl for longer than I should have. “Ash. Move her aside...gently,” I whispered.

The Hero nodded and knelt. She carefully lifted Sara off the floor and carried her to the far corner. She set her down over a piece of cloth. The girl stirred once when Ash set her down, but she remained asleep.

I knelt beside the bed. I placed my left hand on the boy's arm. It was warm. I looked around the room. Ash stood by Sara. The mother stood next to the cloth, her arms wrapped around herself. There was no point in waiting.

“Let’s begin,” I said, to no one but myself.

The Requiem answered when I called it. I threaded a wisp of Ruin through the index finger of my right hand, thin as I could make it -thinner than I had managed with any creature in the forest.

The flame was barely visible. Nothing more than a tendril of darkness that I fed into the boy’s arm with all the care I could. Even now, I had to strain not to unmake the boy entirely.

I followed the Requiem with the Cradle. I pushed Bloom through the index finger of my left hand, letting it flow alongside the black, a sheath of warmth around a thread of cold. One to unmake, and the other to protect what lay behind.

Out of the corners of my eyes, I could see the room change around us. Some of the shadows lengthened, pulled close to Ruin. While Bloom cast a white light so harsh one had to squint to look upon it.

With my powers, I found the barrier in an instant. It was exactly as I remembered. A boundless wall with no chips and no imperfections at all. It was the System's decision on a child it had deemed unworthy. My Requiem pressed against that wall, and the barrier held. I pushed more mana, until the black flame spread across that formless surface. I was searching for a place for my power to find purchase. There was none to find. My jaw clenched.

Through the Requiem’s touch, I could feel what lay beyond the wall more clearly than I had the first time. The boy was there, distant and dim. Dimmer than he had been before. I could feel the shape of his mind, the texture of what he had been before the Ink had sealed him away.

He had been excited. That was the impression that came strongest, just as it had before.

I pushed. The Requiem strained against the barrier’s surface and the barrier strained back. My hand shook, and a pain was building behind my eyes -the old familiar pressure, though this time it came from this boy, rather than myself.

My mana was draining, and I could feel both the Cradle and the Requiem flicker, just for a moment. The thin reserves of this world’s power were simply not enough for what I was asking of them. I did not know how much longer I had left, only that it was not long.

I closed my eyes and felt for the boy again. He was fainter now than he had been moments ago. His life was drifting. Whatever spark of self remained behind that wall had been drifting for days, alone in the dark. I could feel much of him, and so I knew that there was no fear in him. Whatever fear there had been had all died in the first day, and it had left behind only exhaustion. A readiness to stop. Not to die -I did not think the boy understood death, not truly. He simply wanted an end to his prison, however that came about.

My hands were shaking, and there was a lump somewhere in my throat. So far, I had not so much as made a scratch in this barrier.

I had not yet failed, but I could see where this road led. Neither my Cradle nor Requiem were enough. My mana was guttering like a fire in a storm and I could feel the moment approaching where I would have to pull back or risk losing control entirely. If the black flame slipped even once….

The boy would be unmade. The way I had unmade so many things.

My arms burned from the effort. My vision was narrowing. The room had shrunk to the space between my hands and the boy’s small arm. There was only the child and the two marks on my skin that were not enough, that had never been enough, that-

There was rustling behind me. The sound was faint, only heard because the room was so quiet and still. And I knew where it came from. It was the sound of a child turning in her sleep. “You fool,” I hissed, and the words were barely above a breath. “If you make Sara cry, I will hound you to the ends of the earth.” There was nothing in him that could hear my words, and nothing in me that should have spoken them. I said them anyway.

And I tried again. I used more of my thinning mana until both of my marks flared along my arms. And through the black flame, I felt not just the surface of the barrier, but the Concept beneath it. The inside of the inside I had sought. The Concept itself was simple, only a single word. I knew what it was as soon as I had touched it.

Unworthy.

That was the judgement passed on a boy who had done nothing wrong at all. It carried the cold, crushing arrogance of the Gods. It pressed against my mind, demanding I yield to its design. I do not yield.

I had overturned decisions before. I had done these things with power that could reshape the world. I did not have that power now, but what I had was enough. There was only one judgement that mattered in this world, and that was my own.

The Requiem bit into the concept. Unworthy. There was resistance. Nothing else had given the Requiem resistance, but this Concept did. This single word had a weight to it that required effort even from the Requiem. Yet the black flame was eating it all the same. My Requiem bored into the barrier, carving out holes that the Cradle filled. Warmth where there had been cold. Beginning where there had been ending. There was a crack somewhere in the barrier.

The crack was small. Compared to that barrier, it was hardly anything at all. There was no sound, and yet my ears popped as the pressure in the room exploded. And through that tiniest of gaps, I felt the boy. I felt him clearly.

I poured everything I had into the crack. Both marks blazed. My arms were numb. My vision was a single bright point of white and black. Something was dribbling down my nose. Blood, I think. My core ached as I wrung the last of the mana from it. I did not care. The barrier split.

The crying woke Sara. She blinked lazily, and for the first few seconds, she did not know where she was. She wasn't in her own bed, that much she knew almost immediately. Instead, she was on the hard floor, her head over some kind of folded cloth that Sara was sure hadn't been there before. The room was dark, so dark she could barely see.

The sobbing was Shera’s, the kind woman who had taken care of her so very often. Sara sat up. She rubbed her eyes with the back of her hand and looked toward the bed. Sara saw two figures, both she knew well. She saw Shera and Marin.

She was bent over Marin, with her arms wrapped around the small boy. That wasn't a surprise. She had seen it often. More often, since that man had come to the village. Except Marin was hugging her back.

Marin had his hands wrapped around his own mother, and he was crying. Tears ran down eyes that were both open and saw everything. It took Sara a second to understand. He was awake.

Sara did not understand how. It didn't matter. Her own eyes were burning and she could barely see the two through the tears. She scrambled forward and threw her arms around them both. Marin was shaking. Marin was shaking, and Shera was shaking, and now Sara was shaking too.

“Marin,” Sara whispered her friend's name. “Marin, Marin, Marin.” She whispered it thrice more, because for the first time in days, he was awake to hear it. Through the blur of tears, Sara saw his arm. Where before there hadn't been anything, there was now a single yellow Spark.

Sara held on tighter. Somehow, she knew this wasn't a dream, even if it really felt like one.

The three of them stayed like that for a long time, until the sobbing softened into something quieter.

Lys!

Later, when she lifted her head and wiped her eyes, she looked around the room. There was nobody else there.

The night air was cool against my skin. I made it several steps out of the hut before my legs finally betrayed me. My knees buckled, then I was falling. An arm caught me before I hit the dirt. It was stronger than mine was now, and it held me upright easily. Far too easily.

“I…d-do not need a Hero’s help to walk,” I said. The words tumbled over each other. Even I could barely understand them.

“I’m offering the help anyway.” Ash’s voice was quiet. She moved my arm over her shoulder and kept her own arm around my waist. Then she walked. My feet dragged as she hauled me forward.

Neither of us spoke for a long time. Hamel was silent, save for the dragging of my own feet through the dirt. There was no light at all save for flickering lanterns, and I only saw their flicker reflected in the dirt. I could not raise my head.

When Ash finally spoke, her voice was very quiet. Even in the silence of Hamel, it did not carry far. "You lied to me, didn't you?" she half-said and half-asked. "You said this was all planned, in the forest. You said you had planned out the entire day around having just enough mana to heal him right now. I believed you then. I thought maybe it was possible, if it was you.” She dragged me for a few more steps. “But you didn’t have a plan at all, did you?”

It was a strange question to ask, when she seemed so sure of the answer. I did not answer her. I knew the path from that woman's hut to Martha's. It should not have been long, yet now it seemed to stretch for miles. Without the Hero's help, I would have fallen and not risen at all. We continued in silence. Ash did not ask the same question again. Eventually, the hut came into view. Warm light peeked through the open window. Were the two of them still awake?

“Why didn’t you stay?” Ash asked. Her voice was softer still now. “To see the boy wake up.” She paused. “To see Sara.”

The door was right there. “What right do I have,” I whispered, “to see something like that?”

I felt Ash’s grip tighten.
