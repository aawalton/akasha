“So tell me,” I said at last, my voice rougher than I intended. “What all of it… what was real?”

The words scraped their way out of my throat, heavy with everything I hadn’t said yet. I sighed and leaned back against the nearest shelf, cool stone biting through the thin fabric at my shoulders. The library around us felt impossibly vast, not merely large but layered, like a place that had learned how to stack infinities without collapsing. Books hovered in slow, deliberate orbits, some bound in leather, others stitched from bark, crystal, metal, or stranger things I didn’t have names for. Pages whispered to one another as they turned themselves, murmuring in languages I almost recognized. Not remembered. Recognized. Like a word you’ve never learned but have somehow always known.

My head still pounded, not with pain, but with the absence of weight. Like something immense had been lifted from my shoulders and I hadn’t yet relearned how to stand without it pressing me into shape. I felt hollowed out and overfilled at the same time, a contradiction that made a certain brutal sense.

I looked up at the woman standing in front of me.

I didn’t need to be told who she was.

Even stripped of the dream’s embellishments, even without the exaggerated regality Demeterra had wrapped her in like ceremonial silk, I felt her. The pull. The gravity. The heat beneath the scales. The familiarity that didn’t come from memory so much as resonance, like two notes struck in different octaves that still vibrated in sympathy.

Fallias.

She stood with her arms crossed, wings tucked tight against her back, her posture balanced in a way that spoke of centuries of learning where to place her weight. Her expression was careful. Not guarded. Measured. As if every word she chose would leave a mark, and she was deciding where the scars should go.

She wasn’t protecting herself from me.

She was protecting me from the truth.

“Us,” she said quietly. “Our feelings. Our desires. That was real.”

The words landed softly, but something in my chest loosened anyway. Not relief exactly. More like confirmation that one of the load-bearing beams hadn’t been imaginary.

“Cordelia,” she continued, and I felt the tightening before she even finished the sentence, “was not. Not as you knew her. She was a construct. A fragment of your ideals, your longings, your need for structure and guidance given a face you would trust. Alexandria was the same. Different shape. Same root.”

I swallowed, my throat suddenly dry. Cordelia’s calm authority. Alexandria’s sharp clarity. Two different masks over the same ache.

“So they were… parts of me,” I said.

“Yes,” Fallias replied. “And no. Demeterra doesn’t invent from nothing. She refracts. She takes what exists and bends it until it fits the narrative she needs you to inhabit.”

I stared at the drifting books, at the way their shadows didn’t quite line up with the light.

“Then… the power?” I asked. “The growth? The way everything kept stacking, like there was no ceiling?”

She shook her head slowly.

“No.”

It wasn’t cruel. It wasn’t dismissive. It was the tone you used when answering someone who asked whether a beautiful lie was true, knowing the beauty didn’t survive the truth but believing the truth mattered anyway.

“Skillcubes exist,” she said, choosing each word with surgical care. “But they’re shortcuts. Crutches. A bastardization of technique and growth, frozen into something transferable. They let people skip the part where failure teaches shape. They’re not how people become strong. Not truly.”

I let out a breath I hadn’t realized I was holding. It came out shaky, like air escaping a punctured lung.

“So I wasn’t… climbing,” I said. “I was being carried.”

“In part,” she said. “You were learning. But the pace was artificial. Forced. No soul can metabolize growth like that without consequences.”

“My Arte?” I asked quickly. “The books. The way I could… step into them. Live inside the stories.”

“That’s real,” she said immediately. No hesitation. No pause to consider whether it would hurt me. “Completely real. Your Arte is real. And so is your affinity for knowledge-space. That part of you is intrinsic.”

I felt something steady beneath my ribs. A small, stubborn pillar.

“But,” she continued, “everyone, Alex… everyone only has one mana type that is truly theirs. One that resonates with their soul without external scaffolding.”

“So the celestials,” I said slowly. “The mask. My Machina. Lumivis. All of that was…”

“False,” she said, gently but firmly.

The word landed like a stone dropped into still water, sending ripples through everything I thought I understood.

“When I say false,” she went on, stepping closer, lowering her voice, “I don’t mean meaningless. I mean constructed. Borrowed. Reflected. Demeterra builds her illusions out of truth fragments because full lies don’t hold. But they aren’t yours. They were costumes tailored to a role you were meant to play.”

My fingers curled reflexively, nails biting into my palm.

“And the people?” I asked. “The realms. My family. My mother.”

Fallias hesitated.

That pause cut deeper than any immediate denial could have.

“Alex,” she said softly, “when I tell you this, I need you to listen to the words, not the pain behind them.”

I nodded once, jaw clenched.

“The only people in the entirety of what you’ve been exploring who are real,” she said, “are me, Danatallion, his assistants, Gin… and that tall, irritatingly clinical man over there.”

She flicked her gaze toward the lanky figure hovering near a stack of floating ledgers, who was very obviously pretending not to listen while failing spectacularly.

“You knew him as Sadrin and Ratatosk,” she continued. “He is one person, not two. Different interfaces. Same being.”

My stomach flipped unpleasantly.

“And… my mother?” I asked, even though the answer had already started carving itself into me.

She shook her head. “She isn’t Juliet. She doesn’t have that Arte. That lineage was fabricated to give you a narrative you would accept. Something grand but familiar enough not to question.”

The silence that followed felt dense, like gravity had increased by a few crucial degrees.

“You,” Fallias said, her voice dropping lower, heavier, “are not from a normal bloodline.”

I looked at her, really looked, searching for any crack in her certainty.

“Alex,” she sighed, rubbing at her temple, “you are… unique.”

“How?” The word came out sharper than I intended, edged with something that might have been fear.

She met my gaze, eyes glowing faintly. Not with authority. Not with judgment. With worry.

“You are a chimera,” she said. “A successful one.”

I blinked. “That’s… not a metaphor, is it.”

“No,” she said. “It’s a designation.”

“The Chimera Project,” she continued, “was created by the Grandis. An attempt to bypass the natural limitations of singular mana resonance by engineering convergence at the soul level. Multiple aspects. Multiple compatibilities. One vessel.”

“And most people… didn’t survive,” I said.

“Most didn’t remain people,” she corrected quietly. “They destabilized. Fragmented. Collapsed under their own internal contradictions.”

“And me?”

“You didn’t,” she said simply. “You stabilized. You integrated.”

My mouth felt dry. “Wait. The Laws of Forces. The stuff Gin explained. That wasn’t just dream logic?”

“No,” she said, shaking her head. “Those are real. Absolute. Immutable. Demeterra didn’t invent them. She just hid them behind pageantry and myth.”

I laughed once, short and humorless. “So let me get this straight. I’m not the heir I thought I was. Not the prodigy. Not the infinite furnace.”

“No.”

“But I’m also not normal.”

She almost smiled at that. Almost. The corner of her mouth twitched, not in amusement, but in recognition, like someone spotting a familiar scar on a stranger.

“No,” she said again. “You’re worse.”

I snorted weakly. “That’s comforting.”

“You misunderstand,” Fallias continued, stepping closer, her presence warm in a way that felt earned rather than imposed. “Worse doesn’t mean broken. It means undeveloped. You, Alex, are in many ways a puppy.”

I bristled. “A what.”

“A puppy,” she repeated without apology. “You don’t have the bite yet. But you have the bark. The wit. The instinct to circle before you strike. And you’re still looking at me with that expression.”

“What expression?” I asked.

She lifted a clawed finger and gently tapped the space between my eyes. Not touching skin. Touching something beneath it.

“That nebula you call eyes,” she said. “The way you look at the world when you’re trying to convince yourself that you understand it.”

“I have… what?” I asked, blinking.

“Your truth’s image,” she replied. “It embedded itself into you the moment you became self-aware enough to lie to yourself properly. Most people never get that far. They live and die without ever forming a true internal image.”

She tilted her head, studying me like a puzzle she’d seen once before but in a different lifetime.

“I would wager,” she went on, “that you can already name your Truth. And your Lie.”

I exhaled slowly.

“Yeah,” I said.

The words came easily. Too easily. Like they’d been waiting behind my teeth for years.

“My Truth,” I said, “is that everything has a price.”

Fallias didn’t interrupt.

“My Lie,” I continued, “is that I am unafraid of that price.”

I paused, then added the last piece, feeling it settle into place with a quiet internal click, like a lock finally turning.

“And my Ideal,” I said, smiling despite myself, “is that I broker the price.”

There was no hesitation in me. No uncertainty. Just certainty. Solid and clean.

Fallias stared at me for a long moment. Then she nodded once.

“I didn’t expect you to claim the Ideal instantly,” she said. “Ideals don’t usually come with images. They don’t need anchors. They simply… are. People grow into them over decades, sometimes centuries.”

She folded her arms, wings shifting slightly behind her.

“So,” she asked, “what did you use as the anchor for your Lie?”

The question hit harder than I expected.

I looked down at my hand.

It wasn’t the hand I remembered.

It wasn’t human. Not entirely. A mosaic of golden scales and dense, dark fur covered it completely, seamless, intentional. No skin showed through. The joints moved smoothly, power coiled beneath the surface like a restrained animal. It felt right in a way my old hand never had.

I flexed my fingers slowly, watching the light catch along the scales.

“I didn’t choose something symbolic,” I said quietly. “I chose something honest.”

Fallias said nothing.

“A bronze mirror,” I finished.

Understanding flickered across her face, followed by something heavier. Respect, edged with concern.

“A mirror that doesn’t flatter,” she murmured. “That doesn’t distort. That only reflects what stands before it.”

I closed my hand into a fist.

“Because I’m not afraid of the price,” I said softly. “I’m afraid of lying about whether I can pay it.”

Fallias met my gaze again, the almost-smile finally fading into something real. “That,” she said, “is why you’re worse.” And for the first time, I understood that she meant dangerous.
