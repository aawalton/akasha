The abandoned mill creaked around them, a dying beast exhaling dust with each gust of wind that found its way through the collapsed walls. Moonlight cut jagged patterns across the rotting floorboards where Cade paced, his shadow elongating and contracting with each turn like a physical manifestation of his fraying patience.

“We’ve been here too long,” Elena said from her perch by the only intact window. Her nimble fingers checked her lockpicks for the fifth time in as many minutes. “Every second we wait is another second the Arbiters get closer.”

“And every second we wait is another second for Jer and Gav to return,” Cade countered, his voice rough with exhaustion. “We don’t move without them.”

A soft moan drew his attention to the far corner where Orro sat propped against crumbling stonework, his burned skin glistening with the salve Rayka had applied. The assassin’s breathing had improved, but his normally stoic expression was marred by lines of pain he couldn’t quite conceal.

“How is he?” Cade asked, joining Rayka at their friend’s side.

“Alive,” Orro answered before his sister could, the single word scraped raw from his smoke-damaged throat. “Which is more than that Arbiter can say.”

The stark reminder of the night’s casualties hung between them like a physical presence. Cade had seen enough bodies to recognize death when it came, and that Arbiter—drunk or not—had suffered an agonizing end.

“You’re lucky it wasn’t you,” Rayka said quietly, wringing excess water from a cloth before placing it gently across Orro’s forehead. “Running into a burning building... what were you thinking?”

“That a child was going to die if I didn’t,” Orro replied simply.

The matter-of-fact response silenced further criticism. They all knew Orro well enough to understand that beneath his ruthless exterior lay a rigid code—one that apparently included not standing by while children burned to death, regardless of the personal cost.

Cade moved back to the center of the dilapidated room, his core churning with the same restless energy that drove his pacing. “Jer and Gav should have been back by now. The outpost can’t be more than two miles north.”

“They’re probably just being cautious,” Nora reminded him from where she sat cross-legged near Evie and the sleeping Chubbs. “Taking different routes to avoid being tracked. It’s what you told them to do.”

“I know what I told them,” Cade snapped, more harshly than he’d intended. “Sorry,” he amended with a raised hand.

Nora raised an eyebrow but said nothing, allowing the tension to dissipate on its own rather than feeding it with a response. That was one of the many skills that made her such a formidable ally—knowing when words served no purpose.

Cade wished he could learn that lesson sometimes.

“We could just go to them,” Elena suggested, her tone carefully neutral despite the obvious impatience vibrating through her small frame. “Meet them halfway.”

“And if they’re taking a completely different route back? Or if they’ve been delayed? Or captured?” Cade shook his head. “No. We wait. Another hour, no more.”

“Evie’s lips twitched. “I wouldn’t mind meeting them halfway,” she murmured, her gaze fixed dreamily on nothing in particular.

“You wouldn’t mind seeing Gav, you mean,” Elena corrected with a sly grin. “Your crush is showing, siren.”

A soft blush colored Evie’s cheeks, visible even in the dim light. “I simply think Gavin is a sensible person with valuable insights,” she defended primly. “And exceptional bone structure.”

Cade and the others grinned warmly at Evie, who shrugged unapologetically. The brief moment of levity was like a single breath of clean air in a smoke-filled room—momentarily relieving but inadequate against the suffocating pressure of their circumstances.

Cade resumed his pacing, his mind replaying the events at the tavern with merciless clarity. The paid accuser pointing his finger. Rufus manipulating the flames from the shadows. The mother’s face transforming from tearful gratitude to vicious hatred in the space of a heartbeat.

“It shouldn’t matter what they think,” he muttered, not realizing he’d spoken aloud until Nora responded.

“Of course it matters,” she said simply. “Perception shapes reality far more than truth ever has.”

“Spoken like a true Fateweaver,” Orro commented dryly.

Nora didn’t deny it. “The Arbiters have their story, and in their version, we’re the villains. The evidence supports it: a destroyed city, a missing goddess, and the one blamed for it all conveniently claiming the title of ‘Destroyer’ while demonstrating destructive magic.”

“I had to get Orro out,” Cade defended, though the hollow feeling in his chest suggested he’d been asking himself the same question. “What would you have had me do? Let them execute him on the spot for saving a child?”

“No one’s saying that,” Rayka interjected, her gentle voice a balm against his raw nerves. “But you didn’t exactly help our situation by announcing yourself as the Destroyer.”

“So I should have let them believe a lie about me while they prepared to kill my friend for a different lie?” Cade laughed, the sound sharp and humorless. “At least this way I chose which lie would condemn me.”

The silence that followed would have crushed a god.

“We’ve all lost something,” Nora said finally. “Homes, reputations, freedom. But what they can never take—what no lie can erase—is who we truly are.” She met Cade’s gaze with unwavering certainty. “And you, Cade Stormhollow, are not the monster they’ve made you out to be.”

Before he could respond, a small sound drew everyone’s attention to the far corner where Chubbs had been sleeping. The toddler goddess sat upright on her makeshift bed of cloaks, her golden eyes wide open and luminous in the darkness.

“My thoughts are too loud,” she announced with the serious dignity only a three-year-old could muster. “Chubbs needs to help.”

“It’s alright, little one,” Evie soothed, moving to gather the child back into her arms. “We’re just talking. Go back to sleep.”

But Chubbs had already slid from her bed, her tiny feet padding across the floor with surprising purpose. She stopped in the center of the room, directly in the shaft of moonlight where Cade had been pacing moments before.

“But bad people are coming to hurt us like they did to Or-Or,” she stated with absolute certainty, her voice carrying an edge that didn’t belong to a three-year-old. “Need to be ready.”

The hairs on Cade’s arms rose. “Chubbs?”

She didn’t respond to her name. Instead, her small frame went unnaturally still, golden eyes dilating until they consumed the whites entirely. When she tilted her head, the movement was too fluid, too precise—like a predator tracking sound.

“She doesn’t look right,” Elena whispered, sliding off her perch and edging closer.

Chubbs dropped to her knees with the ceremonial precision of a priestess at prayer, tiny palms pressing flat against the dusty floorboards. The air around her rippled, distorting like heat waves off summer stone.

“We need doors that aren’t doors,” she said, but the voice that emerged wasn’t the high-pitched sing-song of a toddler. It resonated with harmonic undertones, a woman’s voice layered beneath a child’s, creating an unsettling echo. “And paths through walls that never end.”

A vibration trembled through the floor, like the first warning of an earthquake. Dust motes suspended in the moonlight began to dance with unnatural purpose, swirling into complex patterns that mimicked architectural schematics.

“What the hell—” Orro pushed himself upright, wincing through pain.

The transformation began at Chubbs’s fingertips—a ripple of change spreading outward like watercolor bleeding across parchment. The weathered floorboards liquefied momentarily before reconstituting as something richer, darker. Not wood anymore, not stone, not metal—something that shouldn’t exist at all.

Elena stumbled back as the transformation raced beneath her feet. “The floor is—it’s moving!”

“Don’t touch her,” Nora warned as Rayka instinctively moved toward the child. “That’s not just Chubbs anymore.”

Cade’s core stirred in recognition of power far beyond his own—ancient and raw, unfettered by the constraints that bound normal magic. The walls began to transform, molecules rearranging themselves under the command of forces that defied comprehension. Intricate geometric patterns carved themselves into stone, forming what looked like—

“Are those... gears?” Cade found himself momentarily distracted by the architectural complexity taking shape. Despite the danger, his inner architecture enthusiast couldn’t help but marvel. “Look at those counterweights! The tensile strength calculations alone would take a master engineer years to—”

“Cade!” Rayka’s sharp voice snapped him back to the urgency of the situation. “Focus!”

He approached Chubbs cautiously, circling to face her. The toddler’s eyes glowed with internal light, her small features composed in an expression of concentration no child should be capable of.

“Chubbs,” he said firmly, “what are you doing to the mill?”

Her head swiveled toward him with mechanical precision, but her hands never lifted from the floor. When she spoke, the dual-layered voice became more pronounced, the adult feminine tones momentarily overpowering the child’s.

“Sanctuary,” she replied, the word rolling off her tongue like honeyed wine. “Protection. Salvation.”

“How are you doing this?” Cade pressed, fighting the instinct to back away from the power radiating from her tiny form.

The smile that curved her lips belonged to someone far older, far wiser, and perhaps far more dangerous than the toddler they knew. Something ancient looked out through Chubbs’s eyes—something that recognized Cade and found him amusing.

“The Godsmith never reveals her secrets,” she replied, her voice entirely adult now, rich with power and the confidence of eons.

Then, as suddenly as it had emerged, the presence receded, leaving Chubbs blinking in confusion.

She swayed, small hands lifting from the floor. “My hands are... sleepy,” she mumbled, her voice childlike again, though the transformation of the mill continued unabated around them, as if the process, once initiated, required no further guidance.

Elena cautiously touched a section of wall that had transformed into a complex locking mechanism. “Is this... is this a trap trigger?”

“Don’t touch that!” Cade snapped, stepping closer to examine it. “It could be anything. It could be...” His words trailed off as understanding dawned. “It’s a pressure plate connected to a deadfall. But… that sort of engineering should be impossible.”

“Impossible how?” Nora asked, gathering the dazed Chubbs into her arms.

Cade ran his fingers along the intricate mechanism, his expression shifting between professional appreciation and growing unease. “These components shouldn’t work together. Look at these interconnections—they violate basic principles of mechanical engineering. The tensile load alone would snap these gears, except...” He pushed lightly, testing the mechanism. “Except they’re not just metal. They’re something else entirely.”

Rayka examined a section of wall that had split open to reveal what looked like a hidden passage. “Umm, guys?”

“What? What is it?” Cade asked with just a bit too much excitement.

Rayka gave him a flat stare, but eventually pointed with her thumb. “There are escape routes forming. Tunnels that weren’t here before.”

“How is she doing this?” Elena demanded, backing away from a section of floor that had begun to reconfigure itself into what appeared to be a concealed pit trap. “This isn’t just reshaping what’s here—she’s creating matter out of nothing!”

“Not nothing,” Nora said quietly, looking down at the child in her arms. “She’s tapping into the essence of what she truly is.”

Cade crouched beside what appeared to be a perfectly ordinary section of flooring, his fingers tracing a nearly invisible seam. “This is—” he pressed lightly, and the panel shifted with a whisper-soft click, revealing a drop beneath. “Oh, that’s clever. That’s incredibly clever.”

“What is it?” Elena asked, keeping a careful distance.

“Another deadfall trap with a selective trigger mechanism,” he replied, unable to hide the professional admiration in his voice. “See these tiny grooves? Weight distribution analysis. It can tell the difference between footfall patterns.” He couldn’t help grinning. “I’ve only ever seen theoretical designs for something like this in the Academy archives.”

“You’re enjoying this way too much,” Orro muttered, wincing as he leveraged himself upright using a newly formed wall support.

“Are you kidding? Look at this craftsmanship!” Cade ran his hands over the wall, which now featured a cascading series of interlocked mechanisms. “The double-action counter-rotation on these pivot points alone is revolutionary! The tensile load distribution across these joining struts is—it’s beautiful.” He caught Orro’s dark expression and cleared his throat. “I mean, terrifying. Completely terrifying.”

Elena cautiously nudged what appeared to be an ordinary stone with her toe. “So are we just... not going to talk about how a three-year-old just rewrote the laws of reality?”

“She’s not just a three-year-old,” Nora reminded them, cradling the now-drowsy Chubbs against her shoulder. The child’s eyes drooped, power apparently spent after her extraordinary display. “We’ve always known that.”

“There’s knowing and then there’s seeing,” Rayka said quietly, examining what appeared to be a newly formed weapon cache hidden behind a sliding panel. She withdrew a dagger that seemed to glow with its own inner light. “This metal—I’ve never seen anything like it.”

“Because it doesn’t exist in our world,” Cade replied, taking the dagger and testing its impossible balance. “Or didn’t, until about two minutes ago.”

Evie reached for Chubbs, taking her from Nora’s arms. “She heard us talking about needing somewhere safe,” the siren whispered, smoothing the child’s hair. “About waiting for Jer and Gav. I think she was just trying to help.”

“By casually violating every known law of physical creation,” Elena muttered, studying a series of mirrors that had appeared along the ceiling, apparently designed to redirect light into strategic locations. “No big deal.”

Cade moved through the space with the practiced eye of someone who had spent a lifetime analyzing security systems for weaknesses.

“It’s all interconnected,” he said, his voice hushed with reluctant admiration. “These aren’t just random traps—it’s a complete defensive network. Look—” he pointed to a subtle pattern etched into what had been ordinary stone. “This is a pressure sequencing system. Step on the wrong stones in the wrong order, and...”

He pressed his weight onto one particular stone, and immediately a section of ceiling above it hinged open, revealing a compartment that would have dumped something wholly unpleasant on anyone triggering it.

“Empty now, but you get the idea,” he murmured. “The entire mill is one massive security system.”

“With escape routes,” Rayka added, discovering a hidden passage behind what had appeared to be a solid wall. “This leads... somewhere.”

“Multiple exit points,” Cade confirmed, his thief’s instincts mapping the transformed space. “North, south, east, west—all disguised from the outside.” He shook his head in wonder. “She’s created the perfect fortress, hidden in plain sight.”

“Not her,” Orro corrected grimly. “The goddess within her.”

The words dropped into the room like stones into still water, ripples of unease spreading through their small company. Chubbs slumbered against Nora’s shoulder, looking for all the world like an ordinary, if exhausted, child.

“We’ve been treating this like a curiosity,” Nora said quietly. “A mystery to be solved. But what we just witnessed was a fragment of divine power—Creation herself stirring within that child’s form.”

“We need to tell her,” Nora said finally, her voice low but resolute. “She deserves to know who she is.”

Cade’s head snapped up. “Are you insane?”

“The power is awakening whether we acknowledge it or not,” she argued. “These episodes will only increase. She deserves to understand what’s happening to her.”

“If she remembers, truly remembers, we lose Chubbs,” Cade said, the words tasting like ash on his tongue. “She becomes something else—someone else. Creation in her full power and glory. And let’s not forget what that means for us.”

“My people remembered Creation,” Orro started, his expression taking on a thousand-yard stare. “I didn’t know that the Life we were dealing with was the same divinity, but I see it now. Creation, even before her millennia-long ruse, wasn’t some benevolent nurturing force,” Orro added, voice rough with pain and conviction. “She was calculating. Vindictive. There were stories… Stories of villages that displeased her, transformed into a forest of glass that shattered at the slightest touch, trapping the villagers in an eternal moment of agony.”

“Wonderful. Glad she’s just hanging out with us now,” Elena muttered.

“Those are just myths,” Nora countered, but with less certainty than before.

“Myths usually contain truth at their core,” Cade replied, watching the sleeping child. She was just Chubbs—their chaotic, loving, impossible toddler. But they had all just witnessed the ancient power stirring beneath that innocent exterior.

“Would that be so terrible now?” Nora challenged, though her voice had lost some of its conviction. “A fully awakened goddess might be exactly what we need. She could clear your name, explain what really happened at Elysia—”

“Or she could obliterate us for our role in her death,” Orro interrupted bluntly. “Gods aren’t known for their forgiveness, Nora. Especially not gods who were betrayed and murdered.”

“There’s too much we still need to know before we awaken a potentially vengeful deity,” Cade said grimly. He approached Nora and gently brushed a strand of hair from Chubbs’s sleeping face. “She’s not just a vessel for Creation’s return. She’s becoming her own person. A child with her own thoughts, feelings, and attachments. I’m not risking that.”

No one spoke. The mill that had been rebuilt around them was proof enough of what might happen if Creation was unleashed again upon the land.

“We don’t tell her,” Cade decided, his voice heavy. “Not yet. Not until we understand more about what’s happening to her, about who she was, about what she might become.”

“That’s not your choice to make,” Nora argued. “She has a right to her own identity.”

“Does she?” Cade met the ex-paladin’s gaze steadily. “Or does the goddess have that right? Because they’re not the same being, Nora. Not anymore.” His voice softened. “I won’t sacrifice a child for a deity’s resurrection—especially not one who might destroy us all in her first moment of clarity.”

The debate might have continued, but a subtle change in the atmosphere cut it short. The mill—or whatever it had become—seemed to vibrate with a new frequency, the mechanisms shifting in response to some external stimulus.

“What was that?” Rayka breathed.

“Someone’s coming,” Elena hissed from her position by the window. “Multiple someones, moving through the trees to the east.”

Cade was at her side in an instant, his silver-ranked senses picking up what ordinary hearing could not—the distinctive jingle of chainmail, the creak of leather strapping, the whispered commands of a disciplined unit.

“Shit. Arbiters,” he confirmed, counting at least eight distinct sets of movements. “They’re being cautious—spreading out to surround us.”

“How did they find us so quickly?” Rayka asked, already gathering her essential gear.

“They know these areas better than we do,” Orro answered, grimacing as he pushed himself fully upright. “And we weren’t exactly subtle in our escape.”

Cade’s mind raced through options, strategies, and escape routes. The newly transformed mill offered advantages they hadn’t had before, but it also presented unknowns—mechanisms and systems they hadn’t had time to fully understand or test.

“We need to split up,” he decided, the plan forming even as he spoke. “Draw them in different directions, rendezvous at the outpost.”

“And if Jer and Gav return here while we’re gone?” Elena demanded.

“They’re smart enough to avoid a place crawling with Arbiters,” Cade replied, though the thought of potentially stranding their companions twisted in his gut. “They’ll head for the outpost too—it’s our only viable destination.”

A metallic click from one of the mill’s newly formed mechanisms interrupted their planning. Something had activated in response to movement outside—one of Chubbs’s intricate defenses preparing to engage.

“I think we’re about to see what these traps can do,” Nora said, gathering the still-sleeping Chubbs into her arms.

As if in answer, a section of the floor near the main entrance suddenly collapsed inward, revealing a pit that hadn’t been there minutes before. From outside came a startled yell, quickly muffled—then the sound of booted feet scrambling backward.

“Clever girl,” Cade murmured, watching as other mechanisms throughout the mill began activating in sequence, responding to the Arbiters’ attempted approach from multiple angles.

The warning had been delivered—their hunters knew they’d lost the element of surprise. What followed would be a more direct assault.

“Go,” Cade ordered, already moving toward one of the hidden passages Chubbs had created. “Northeast exit for Nora and Evie with Chubbs. Rayka, take the west passage with Orro—he needs your support. Elena and I will create a diversion to the south, then circle back north toward the outpost.”

“And if these passages collapse while we’re inside them?” Rayka asked, eyeing the mysterious mechanisms with understandable suspicion.

“Then we die slightly sooner than if we stay here,” Orro responded dryly, already pushing away from the wall. “I’m not waiting around for those self-righteous bastards to finish what the fire started.”

Outside, the Arbiters had begun a more coordinated approach, their voices carrying clearly through the night air as they called positions to each other. Whatever caution they’d initially shown was rapidly evaporating in the face of the mill’s unexpected defenses.

“Find the Destroyer!” a commanding voice bellowed. “Honor demands justice for Elysia!”

“There’s your justice,” Cade muttered bitterly, gesturing for Elena to join him at the southern passage. “Punishment first, questions never.”

As they moved into position, ready to scatter like leaves in a storm, Cade cast one final glance at the sleeping Chubbs in Nora’s arms. The toddler goddess had transformed their refuge, created paths of escape when they needed them most—all while revealing a fragment of the divine power that slumbered within her.

The Godsmith.

A name that tasted of ancient magic and forgotten myth. It was just another piece of the puzzle that was Chubbs, and by extension, the mystery of what had really happened in Elysia.

He would solve this. He wouldn’t let another goddess catch him unawares again.

Outside, the Arbiters prepared their assault, unaware that their quarry was already slipping through fingers of stone and metal and impossible engineering. The first of them breached the mill’s entrance just as Cade and Elena disappeared into their designated passage, the sound of triggered traps and outraged shouts following them into the darkness.

Now, to go steal from their would-be murderers.
