“Why didn’t Ambrose say anything to me?” Nora demanded. “I could have tried healing him! I would have tried! You know that, right?” she asked, directing the last part at no one in particular.

Galenus, who had been quiet throughout my recounting of Ambrose’s passing, finally spoke. “He did not believe intervention was necessary.”

Nora’s head swiveled slowly toward the goat demon.

“He lived beyond his mortal years by a contract with our Mistress,” Galenus continued evenly, his gaze focused on one of the hovering maps. “Amity could not have restored structural integrity.” He paused, inclining his head. “At best, it would have accelerated the collapse of what was deliberately sustained.”

“You knew he was going to do this,” Nora snapped. “And you didn’t say anything to us!”

“Neither of us desired circumvention, Lady Nora,” Galenus replied. “Ambrose was explicit on that point, and I… find his choice of greatest benefit. With reallocation of the animus that held him to the physical plane, we no longer need to worry about expanding this space. His sacrifice should be not only valued, but optimized for greatest benefit.”

Nora’s face twitched for a moment before guilt surfaced. “Now I see how it feels when someone…” She nudged me. “Sorry.”

“Ah… Don’t worry about it.” I gave her a dismissive wave, unsure what specific it she meant. “You’re not the only one who does it.”

Galenus straightened and turned. “You wish to translocate to the Southern Jungles of Porta, is that correct?”

Nora sighed. “The Grandmaster Temple there, to be more specific.”

Galenus shook his head. “Its precise location is unknown. I presume it lies within the central southern region, where ambient animus density is insufficient to support stable transmission.” He paused, then cleared his throat. “Prudence suggests you bring five days’ worth of provisions and proceed under specific safety protocols.”

I blinked. “We have such a thing?”

“Well, I do,” Galenus replied, summoning a stack of parchment. “This particular algorithm accounts for many of Tetora’s anticipated behavioral variances,” he said, offering it to us. “Including anticipatory predator migration patterns and probable initial combat responses.”

Nora rubbed her temples but took the paper, knowing I would never read them. “Of course it does.”

“I also recommend minimizing detectable animus output upon arrival, as it may provoke hostility from other possible dangers in the area.”

“Noted,” I said. “We’ll try not to announce ourselves to the entire jungle.”

Nora frowned. “What are you going to say to him when you see him?”

“Oh, well, about that…”

Tetora was the only one who had wanted an explanation for why I’d made a deal with Olethros to leave Speranza. And instead of answering him… I deliberately insulted him, insinuating he had nothing to offer and should leave with the others.

I shrugged. “I’m sure I’ll come up with something good in the moment.”

Nora stared at me. “That’s your plan?”

“More or less.”

Olethros reformed beside me, hands clasped behind his head, his sideways glance saying everything he chose not to voice.

“May I come with you?”

“Um, well, first, thank you for asking,” I said, tugging awkwardly at my shoulder. “But you might actually be the only being in this entire world he’s more put out with than me.”

I mean, sure, the app insisted Tetora’s Faith and Trust in me were inexplicably high. It did not, however, possess a metric for his rage.

Olethros grinned. “All the more reason to clear the air quickly.”

His right eye opened just a fraction wider, a knowing green glint flashing through it. “And if I am to share my shenanigans,” he added, “then so should you. Don’t you think?”

You’ll stay out of it, right?

He gave an ambivalent shrug.

“Ugh, enough!” Nora snapped. “You’re only allowed to come along if you stop all the… whatever this is!”

“We’re not flirting!” I protested.

Oops.

Olethros tilted his head in defeat, his coloring dulling. “Truth spoken without tact often sounds like an even larger lie.”

The jungle was far from welcoming. Heat radiated from it all in a heavy, damp, and thick fashion. Insects far too large for my taste hummed and buzzed constantly while water dripped from everything the moment we looked away. Sure, the verdant overgrowth was bright and teeming with life, but most of that life was full of voracious little critters that wanted a piece of us.

Tripping over muddy roots one too many times reminded me I had been here before.

“I remember that Aleph just pushed through it all as quck as possible,” I murmured at one point, swatting at something buzzing near my ear. “But I don’t think Galenus was wrong to tell us we shouldn’t exactly announce ourselves, either.”

“Does anything look familiar?” Nora asked, wiping the sweat from her face.

“Yes, but not in a way that’s helpful,” I replied. “Tigers mark with scents that we can’t—”

I looked carefully at Olethros.

“No.”

“It was just a thought,” I muttered.

“I would not be able to discern meaning from the scents. I have not studied how to be a tiger.”

There went my one chance to see him with cat ears.

We pushed farther south on the second day after failing to sleep through a suffocatingly humid night. Nora, in particular, was deeply unsettled by the idea that the jungle required her to watch for snakes both above and below her at all times.

“Forest floor or canopy!” she complained. “They have to pick one!”

“Technically,” I said, “those are layers of a rainforest, not a jungle. And even then, snakes inhabit all four rainforest layers, so—”

She stopped dead. “Why do you know so much about this?”

Hah. Turnabout, for once.

“Well…” I hesitated. “I might have once thought the green-and-purple iridescence of the emerald tree boa was kind of charming to—” I cleared my throat. “Never mind.”

Later that afternoon, we found a lone wooden gate, covered in vines, standing atop a slightly raised stone platform.

“We’re close,” I said.

Nora peered into one of the grimy stone boxes flanking the gate. “What’s this for?”

“We’re supposed to leave our weapons there,” I replied. “Not that we’re going to this time.”

“Prudent,” she said slowly, arching a brow. “I suppose the custom was more about testing how dumb or overconfident someone was than about safety or honor.”

“Aleph was a weapon unto himself...” I added by way of explanation, once again feeling lost without him. “And so are you guys, even magically so.”

She straightened, brushing dirt from her hands. “Last time I’ll ask, any new thoughts on what you’re going to say to Tetora when you see him?”

“…I know what I’m doing. Trust me.”

Thankfully, she didn’t pull out her phone to show me exactly how much she didn’t.

It wasn’t long before the jungle went quiet. It didn’t happen all at once, but the steady decline of insects humming eventually gave way to the unmistakable feeling of being watched.

Olethros tilted his head abruptly, even as Nora froze, her eyes sharp but unable to find what she was looking for.

I didn’t see him either, but I didn’t have to.

“Prepare yourself!”

Something came spinning out of the shadows. I caught it, the length of wood slapping into my palm with a familiar weight. The bo staff wasn’t so much a kindness as it was an invitation to lay everything out in the small clearing I now stood in.

Iron Tiger Tetora burst from undergrowth an instant later, his fur standing in angry tufts as he held a bo staff of his own.

I swung my staff once, warning Nora and Olethros to stay out of our way.

“Leave. There’s nothing for you here.” Tetora circled me as he spoke, denying me the space to obey his order.

I tightened my grip as I tracked him. “I’m not leaving without you.”

His ears flattened. “Then you shouldn’t have come.”

He sprang forward, and I gave ground, retreating just far enough to keep my balance as the clearing pressed up against my back.

I knew this was how it was going to happen.

“You still owe me one hell of an explanation,” Tetora growled, striking without warning.

Both staves cracked as I caught the blow, my arms buckling from the impact.

“Don’t you mean you’ll never forgive me?” I shot back as I braced my stance and pushed him away.

“Stop talking,” he snapped. “I’m beyond angry with you.”

Called it.

He rushed me again, faster this time. I barely had time to reset myself before his staff clipped mine, twisting it as I skidded back.

“You didn’t think I was worth the truth!”

Another strike came fast and high. I barely got my staff up in time, the resounding smack jolting throughout my core.

Okay… even without taking a hit, this is starting to hurt.

“I didn’t have all the truth then!” I shouted. “It’s worth nothing if it’s incomplete!”

“And you brought him here!” Tetora roared, unleashing a flurry of strikes that drove me backward step by step. “Why?!”

I blocked, parried, blocked again, starting to fret that each blow was getting heavier than the last.

“So you’d get that whole truth you want, even if it’s ugly. But you won’t get it by just whacking away at me!”

That made him falter, which I mistook as an invitation to move to the verbal part of this exercise.

Then Tetora lunged, faster than anything I had ever seen.

I countered, but he stepped inside my guard. Instead of striking, he dropped the staff and snapped his arms around my shoulders, crushing me against his chest and knocking the air from my lungs.

“You’d better tell me everything, Rachel. Don’t leave a single damn thing out.”

I nodded, unable to speak—but I didn’t look away.
