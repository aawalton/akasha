“We are not chosen. We are tasked. The distinction is the only dignity the work permits.” - Codex of the Long Watch, Founding Tenet

1972 - Lake Geneva, Wisconsin

Gary rolled a d20 across the scarred wood of the table and it came up a natural one. He picked up the die, scowled at it, and threw it again as if the problem were the damn die's attitude.

Graph paper covered every flat surface in the stuffy room, and pencil stubs rolled against scattered dice. The basement smelled of cigarette smoke, stale beer, and of the desperation of men trying to invent something they couldn't quite name or understand.

“It’s too much math, Dave.” Gary said, jabbing a finger at the handwritten tables spread between them. “People want to play a heroic game, kill a dragon or two, get some epic loot, not solve equations. And why is the baseline for attributes a ten? Why not five? Why not twenty?”

A third man sat in the corner with his chair tilted back on two legs, and his face away from the harsh light overhead. He had the thick build of a manual labourer, but a stillness that didn’t match any physical profession. He had been listening to them for over three hours and had not spoken since dumping a list of neatly typed rules in front of them. Now, he leaned forward, the chair legs hitting the floor with a dull thud, and pushed a single handwritten sheet across the table.

“The numbers aren’t for the game, Gary,” he said.

Gary stopped fidgeting. Dave put his pencil down, looking up from his doodle.

“They are numbers for something much, much more important.”

Gary looked down at the sheet. It was another list filled with neat, precise handwriting. “So, why ten?”

“Because ten is the integer of an awakened human frame.” The Curator said and leaned into the light. His face was entirely forgettable, the kind you’d pass on any street in any century and walk past without a second glance. “Below ten is biological inefficiency and above ten is an upgrade. The number is not arbitrary. None of the numbers in front of you are arbitrary.”

Dave reached over and slid the sheet out from under Gary's hands. His pencil came back up, ticking down a column, rounding the figures as he went.

"These saving throws," Dave said, his eyes tracking the lines. "The progression curves, the hit dice. This is Risk Analysis. Who builds mortality tables for a game?" He glanced up at his own rounded numbers.

“Do not eyeball the integers,” the stranger said, ignoring the question.

His voice had gone cold, the tone of a man trying to calibrate a system that couldn't afford a single point of error.

“Precision is the only thing that separates survival from collapse. When the sky turns violet, the difference between nine and ten might be the difference between breathing and not.”

"And most of them will never learn the difference." The stranger said it in a tone that carried no cruelty. “The first day will be a measurement, and the ones left standing will not know why they were spared. Only that they were. The work you do is for the ones left standing."

"I'm sorry, sir, but you're— you're not making much sense. What sort of measurement?" Gary said.

"I wish I could tell you. The truth would put a fire under the both of you. But we all have our orders and truth without context never helps." The man said with a small smile.

Gary looked at Dave, who was staring down at the die. It had settled on a ten.

As the Curator stood, his shadow fell across the table, swallowing the graph paper and the scattered dice in darkness.

"Keep the attributes, call them what you want. Make it a game, something they play until the numbers become part of how they think. Spread the books. The more people who understand the mathematics of it, the more people will survive when the fence drops."

"What fence?" Gary asked.

The man straightened his jacket and walked towards the wooden stairs without answering. Gary had not expected an answer. The kind of men this stranger worked for did not give answers, only instructions. At the bottom step he paused, one hand resting on the rail, and looked back at the two of them.

"Neither of you will live to see it," he said. "You will both be a long time gone before it starts. Count that a mercy, gentlemen, and make the game good enough that the ones who do see it will already know the rules. Think of it as your gift to the world."

Then he climbed the stairs, and the basement door clicked shut behind him.

Gary and Dave sat in silence, staring at the sheet of numbers that would eventually become the first D&D Player's Handbook, the most widely distributed survival manual in human history.
