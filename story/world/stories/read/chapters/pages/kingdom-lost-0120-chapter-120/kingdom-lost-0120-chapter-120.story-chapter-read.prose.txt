Chapter 120

She walked back at a brisk pace and did not let herself grin.

The grin was there. She could feel it in the corners. But there were guards at the gate and villagers in the yard, and it was the kind of grin that would get read as something specific if it landed on the wrong face.

Level ten. Back home, level ten was where the map opened up. Alliance chat unlocked. Wonder buildings became placeable. It was the level where you stopped playing a tutorial and started playing the game. She remembered the sound the interface had made when the ten had landed the first time she had gotten there in Warfront: Kingdoms. A soft chime, twice, and then the map tiles pushing outward.

The HUD sat closed. She could feel it the way she used to feel a notification on a locked screen, a small square of attention waiting to be tapped. Not in the yard. Not with people watching. It was a wrapped thing. She would open it in her room.

The gate guards nodded. She nodded back.

Two steps inside the gate she stopped walking.

The tower had changed.

Four stories now. The stone thicker, darker than the stone she had climbed the ladder against on her second night here. Wider around the base, so much wider that the footprint had eaten a stretch of the yard she had used yesterday to cross to the forge. It stood a full head above the wall now, where a day ago it had barely cleared it. It changed the sight-lines. Anyone approaching from the north road would see the tower before they saw the wall. That was strategy she had not asked for and had just been given. A broad stone stair ran up to a double door at the top, and the stair was wider than the doors, wider than it needed to be. A heavy iron knocker hung at the center of each door.

She stood with her hand still half-raised from the nod.

The yard had stopped. The men on the wall had stopped. The girl pulling a bucket at the well had put the bucket down. Nikola and Landryn stood across the yard by the forge, both of them looking up, and then Nikola turned his head and looked at her, and one by one the others in the yard turned too, until she was the thing being watched instead of the tower.

Nikola took half a step toward her.

She moved.

She moved before he could close the distance, and she did it without looking at him. It was not a decision she made in words. She just wanted to see it alone. Whatever it was, she wanted to see it before anyone else.

She climbed the stone stair.

The doors opened under her hand. They were heavy, and they moved lighter than they looked, balanced on hinges she could not see.

Inside, a room she had never walked through. A foyer, high-ceilinged, with a stone bench along one wall and a rack for cloaks along the other, and a second set of double doors set into the far wall, closed. The air was cooler here. The floor was flagstone, laid tight. She noted the seams the way she noted everything, automatically, the strategist in her taking inventory before the rest of her caught up.

She pushed through the inner doors.

The great room.

The room she had slept in for the first months of this life. The room where she had cooked over her own hearth and eaten off her own plate and set the cube down on the floor when she had not yet known what it was. She recognized it and did not recognize it. It was twice the size. The hearth was three times what it had been, an open mouth of stone deep enough to walk into. It had no fire in it. Someone had stacked kindling and split logs in the grate, ready, but no one had lit it. Above it a mantle ran the width of a small table, timber a shade darker than the stone around it, carved from a single piece. And set into the stone above the mantle, taking up most of the wall from mantle to ceiling, the sigil of the tower. The cube.

Her eyes traveled up. Hearth. Mantle. Cube. Ceiling.

My room, she thought.

The stairs to the second floor were wider than they had been. She climbed them.

The second-floor landing opened directly onto the council room, and she stopped on the threshold and looked at it. The table was longer. It was a single piece, she could see that from the grain running the length of it without a seam. There were more chairs than there had been council members to sit in them. Windows had been set into the walls at intervals, and between the windows there were torches in iron brackets, unlit at this hour, waiting. The room was ready for a council that might need more members to fill those chairs.

She took the next flight two at a time.

The third floor was for Riley’s key people: Thorne, Valrik, Landryn and there were additional quarters waiting for new members to the future council. The door was open and inside she could see five other doors all closed. She did not stop.

Fourth floor. A single door.

She opened it.

The room was familiar, but different, it was bigger. Her desk was where she had left it, against the same wall it had been against this morning. The chest sat on the desk, the book beside the chest, everything she had put here over the past months still where she had put it. The room had been built up around her things without disturbing them. The window over the desk was a real window now, glass in it, and through the glass she could see the far edge of the wall and the treeline beyond, and the light in the room was the yellow late-afternoon light of the yard she had just walked out of.

Against the far wall, across a stretch of new floor, was a bed.

Not the narrow bed, it was a real bed. A frame she could see the joinery on. A mattress thick enough that the top of it came up past her hip. Folded wool blankets and pillows, more than one.

She crossed the room, she passed the desk without looking at it.

She stopped a pace short of the bed.

The stone floor of the first weeks. The carpet by the fire. The small hours she had spent shivering through. Waking up to a body that hurt before she had asked it to do anything.

She put one hand flat on the blanket. The mattress gave under her palm.

For a second she was somewhere else. A bed she used to have. The comforter she had picked out. A Sunday, sun through the blinds, the way you could lose an afternoon in it and not feel like you had lost anything.

"WARDEN?! ARE YOU UP THERE?!"

She closed her eyes.

She lifted her hand off the blanket, turned, and went to the stairs.

Thorne was in the foyer. Still wearing the same clothes he had left in, dust on his boots up to the shin. He had not stopped to wash. He had come through the gate, up the stair and through both doors, he had stopped and shouted for her.

"I'm here."

He nodded once.

"Report."

"Thirty on the ground," he said. "Twelve archers in the treeline. North and east, in the two stretches where the cover is thickest. Staggered. They have lanes."

He paused, then went on.

"Level Four regulars on the wall. Three-shift rotation. Same pattern both watches I sat."

She listened the whole way through and did not interrupt again after that.

When he was done she said, "Pull the wick pots from the kiln tonight. Not in the morning. Tonight."

He nodded.

"Brief Valrik, we ride at dawn."

He nodded again, and turned for the door.

She spoke at his back. "The archers. Twelve, you're sure."

He answered without turning. "Twelve."

The door closed behind him.

She stood in the foyer a moment. Then she climbed the stairs.

Second landing. Third landing. Fourth.

She stopped at the fourth-floor door.

She stood there.

She did not open it.

She turned and went back down to the war room, there was work to do.
