I ACCIDENTALLY BECAME A GUILD THERAPIST

Chapter 32 – Alondra, Session 3

ARC III: THE WORLD REMEMBERS

SYNOPSIS: Alondra Fayeblossom returns for session three. She said she would say it. She does. The armchair is very blue. Miles is in the corridor afterward and does not know why Livia looks at him the way she does.

The morning after the Verdant Expanse, Nyx’s flashlight was still off.

Livia noticed it at breakfast. He had the flashlight clipped to his belt as always — same position, same Hello Kitty face staring at nothing — but the light was off. He was eating in the specific silence of someone who had decided something and had not yet decided what to do with the decision.

She did not ask. She added it to the list of things that had their own time.

Alondra’s appointment was at ten.

[System Note: Current time: 09:41. Patient: Alondra Fayeblossom. Session 3. The system notes: she said she would say it. The system notes: the system believes her. The system notes: the system has been waiting for this session since session one. The system notes: some things take the time they take. The system notes: today is the time.]

Livia set up the planning room at 09:52.

Lamp on. The Goldmere dusk lamp, warm and non-intrusive. The diagnostic armchair in its position across from her chair. Notepad open to a fresh page: Alondra Fayeblossom. Session 3.

She looked at the blank page for a moment.

Then she wrote, underneath: She said she would say it.

Then she put the pen down and waited.

Alondra arrived exactly on time.

No binder today. No notes. No folder with dividers color-coded by framework. Just Alondra, in her practical green robes, the Saint of Bloom emblem at her collar, her hands empty at her sides.

She came in. She sat.

The armchair went deep blue immediately — deeper than session one, deeper than session two. The specific blue of something that had been waiting a long time and was now very close.

Livia noted it and said nothing.

They sat for a moment in silence. Not uncomfortable. The silence of two people who had arrived at a threshold and were taking a breath before crossing it.

“I rehearsed this,” Alondra said. “Three different versions. In the mirror. On the way here.”

“How did they go?”

“The first version was too clinical. The second was too apologetic. The third one I couldn’t finish.” She looked at the window. “I’m going to try without a version.”

“Okay,” Livia said.

Alondra breathed in.

“His name is Caelric,” she said. “He’s the guild master of Eclipse Covenant.”

Livia’s pen was still. She let her face do nothing.

I know that name.

I know that guild.

I stood three meters from him last night in the Verdant Expanse while he watched me with the specific attention of someone cataloguing variables.

I am not going to say any of that.

Not now. Not here. This is her session.

“We met at a server diplomatic event,” Alondra continued. “Six years ago. Before you arrived. Eclipse Covenant and Bloodbath and Beyond were… not hostile, exactly. Adjacent. Competing for the same tier of content. Miles managed most of the politics. I managed the rest.”

She paused. Her hands were in her lap, still.

“Caelric approached me at the event. Not as a guild master approaching a guild officer. As a person. He was…” she stopped. Started again. “He was very good at being a person when he wanted to be.”

Livia wrote: He was very good at being a person when he wanted to be. She wrote it exactly. Sometimes the patient gave you the precise sentence.

“It went on for months,” Alondra said. “We would meet at neutral zones. Talk. He listened in a way that felt real. He asked about things I’d mentioned in passing weeks before, which meant he’d remembered, which meant he’d been paying attention.” She paused. “I thought that was care. I know now it was something else. But at the time I thought it was care.”

“What did you think it was at the time?” Livia asked.

A long silence. The armchair held its deep blue.

“I thought he saw me,” Alondra said. “Not the healer. Not the Saint of Bloom Lv. 91. Me. The person who was tired of fixing things and wanted someone to ask how I was without needing the answer to be fine.”

Her voice was even. She had rehearsed it. But even through the rehearsal, the thing underneath was there.

“I believed him,” she said. “I want to be clear about that. I am not saying I was foolish. I am saying I believed him because he was very careful about giving me reasons to.”

Livia wrote: She believed him because he was very careful about giving her reasons to.

“He started asking about Miles,” Alondra said. “Not directly. Gradually. How does Miles make decisions under pressure. What are his habits when he’s moving between zones. How does he structure his schedule when there isn’t a raid on. I answered because…” she paused, and this pause was different from the others. Harder. “Because I thought I was helping. I thought if the two guilds understood each other better, if there was less friction, less competition that didn’t need to be competition…”

She stopped.

“What did you think you were building?” Livia asked.

“Something better,” Alondra said. “I thought I was building something better between two guilds that didn’t have to be enemies.” She looked at her hands. “I was very good at believing that.”

The armchair was a steady, deep blue. The lamp was warm. Outside the planning room, the guild hall was its usual morning self — distant voices, the sound of someone moving equipment, the specific ambient hum of a place that was alive.

None of it touched the room right now.

“He asked me once,” Alondra said, “where Miles went when he needed to be alone. Not in the guild hall. Outside it. In the server. Where he went when he didn’t want to be found.”

Her voice had changed slightly. The even quality was still there, but underneath it something that had been held for seven months was very close to the surface.

“And I told him,” she said.

Silence.

“I told him because I thought he was asking as someone who wanted to understand Miles. Who wanted to… reach out. Make peace.” She breathed out slowly. “I told him the zone. I told him when Miles was usually there. I told him Miles went alone.”

Livia wrote nothing. She did not write because some things needed the air they were said into to stay undisturbed.

“Three days later,” Alondra said, “Miles came back to the guild hall without one of his Aethelgard Contracts.”

She said it plainly. Like she had learned to say it plainly after seven months of practicing how to say it at all.

“He didn’t know it was me,” she said. “He didn’t know anyone had told them where he’d be. He thought it was a patrol. Eclipse Covenant players happen through zones. It was plausible.” A pause. “I made sure it was plausible. I never said anything to Caelric about being careful. I just… gave him the information and let him be careful himself.”

The armchair was the deepest blue Livia had seen it. Not distress in the crisis sense. The blue of something that had been buried very deep for a very long time and had finally broken the surface.

“I didn’t mean for it to happen,” Alondra said. “I want to say that and I also want to say that it doesn’t matter that I didn’t mean for it. It happened. Because of what I told him.”

She looked at Livia for the first time since she had started speaking.

“I left the guild three weeks later. I told Miles I needed a research period. I told him I had identified gaps in my practice. Both of those things were true.” She paused. “They were also both … much easier to say than this.”

[Therapist’s Insight: Patient: Alondra Fayeblossom. Session 3. Presenting issue: she said it was grief. She was not wrong. This is grief. The grief of someone who did something they cannot undo and has been carrying the exact weight of it for six years without putting it down once.]

[The system notes: she did not minimize it. She did not catastrophize it. She said it plainly. The system notes: that took six years. The system notes: that was the work.]

They sat with it for a moment.

Not long. Not the silence of avoidance. The silence of something that had been said and needed to exist in the air for a moment before anything else joined it.

“You’ve been carrying that,” Livia said, “alone, for six years.”

“Yes.”

“Caelric knows you know what he did.”

A pause. “No,” Alondra said. “I don’t think he does. I don’t think he considered that I would ever…” she stopped. “I don’t think he considered me past the point where I was useful.”

She said it without bitterness. She had already moved through the bitterness. What was left was something cleaner and harder.

“And Miles,” Livia said carefully. “He still doesn’t know it was you.”

“No.”

“Do you want him to?”

The longest pause yet.

“I don’t know,” Alondra said. “I know he deserves to. I know that’s not the same question as whether I can.” She looked at the window. “I’ve been asking myself for six years whether telling him would be for him or for me. Whether I want to say it because it’s the right thing or because I want to stop carrying it.”

“Can’t it be both?” Livia said.

Alondra looked at her.

“I’ve been telling myself it can’t,” she said. “Because if it can, then I have to actually decide.”

Livia wrote: If it can be both, then she has to actually decide. She has been using purity of motive as a reason not to decide. It is a very clean trap.

“You don’t have to decide today,” Livia said. “But I want to offer you something to think about.”

Alondra waited.

“You said you didn’t mean for it to happen. That’s true. You also said it doesn’t matter that you didn’t mean for it. That’s also true. Both of those things can be true at the same time. You were manipulated by someone who was very careful about how he manipulated you. That doesn’t make what happened not happen. But it changes the shape of what you’re responsible for.”

Alondra was very still.

“You’re not responsible for Caelric being who he is,” Livia said. “You’re responsible for what you did with what you knew. And what you knew, at the time you did it, was different from what you know now.”

“That sounds like an excuse,” Alondra said. Her voice was not defensive. She was genuinely testing it.

“It’s a distinction,” Livia said. “An excuse says: therefore I don’t have to do anything. A distinction says: now I know where the actual weight is. You can’t carry the weight accurately if you’ve got the wrong shape of it.”

Alondra looked at her hands for a long time.

“He took a Contract from Miles,” she said. “Aethelgard Contract. I don’t fully understand what they do. I know they matter. Really matter. I know Miles had two and now he has one, and the one he lost… he’s never mentioned it. Not once. In all the years since. I know there are three of them. And the last Aethelgard Contract are hidden somewhere.”

She said it like it was a weight she had been measuring.

“He carried it quietly,” Alondra said. “And I’ve been watching him carry it quietly, all this time, not knowing I was part of why he was carrying it.”

Livia wrote nothing.

Miles has one Contract left.

Eclipse Covenant has one.

One is hidden somewhere.

Caelric knows the value of what he took.

Miles has been carrying this quietly for how long.

And last night Caelric stood in the Verdant Expanse and was pleasant to me and got something out of it, and I still don’t know exactly what.

She kept all of that behind her face.

This was Alondra’s session. These were Alondra’s six years. The rest had its own time.

[Therapist’s Insight: Patient: Alondra Fayeblossom. The system notes: Livia knows things about Eclipse Covenant and about Caelric that Alondra does not know.]

[The system notes: Livia chose not to say them. The system notes: this was correct. The system notes: this was also hard. The system notes: the system noticed both things.]

The session ran long. Not because there was more to confess — there wasn’t. The rest of it was the slower work: sitting with what had been said, testing the weight of it, finding where it actually lived in her body and her history and her sense of herself.

Alondra cried once. Not dramatically. The specific, controlled tears of someone who had held something for too long and could feel it releasing. She wiped her face with the efficiency of a healer who had dealt with worse and kept going.

At some point she said: “I became a healer because I wanted to fix things. I thought if I could fix the physical, the rest would follow. I thought healing was transitive.”

“And now?”

“Now I think fixing and healing are different verbs,” she said. “And I’ve been a very good fixer for eight years.”

Livia wrote: Fixing and healing are different verbs. She underlined it. She was going to think about that one later.

At the end, Alondra sat for a moment with the particular stillness of someone who has set something down and is noticing how their hands feel without it.

“I don’t feel better,” she said.

“No,” Livia agreed.

“But I feel… located,” Alondra said. “Like I know where I am now. Before I said it, I wasn’t sure.”

“That’s usually what happens,” Livia said. “The feeling better is later. The knowing where you are is first.”

Alondra nodded. She stood. She gathered nothing because she had brought nothing.

At the door she stopped.

“I’m going to tell him,” she said. Not a question. Not a decision being made in the moment — a decision that had been made somewhere in the last fifty minutes, between the words, and was only now being confirmed aloud. “Not today. But I’m going to tell him.”

“When you’re ready,” Livia said.

“When I’m ready,” Alondra agreed.

She left.

[The system notes: session complete. The system notes: the armchair is still blue. The system notes: the system is aware the armchair should have returned to neutral by now. The system notes: the system notes this without explanation. The system notes: wait.]

Livia sat in the planning room for a while after she had gone.

The lamp was on. The Goldmere lamp, warm and non-intrusive. The notepad was open to three pages of session notes. The armchair was across from her, still blue — not deep, not distress-level, just the faint ambient blue of something recently present.

She looked at it.

Alondra had said: he took a Contract from Miles. And Miles has been carrying it quietly ever since.

She had heard Miles’s version of this — not spoken, never spoken, but present in every deflection, every controlled response, every moment he turned away from something she was getting close to. A raid. Allies. Betrayal. She had filed all of it under: when he’s ready.

She had not known the shape of it until now.

Now she did. And the shape was: Caelric. Eclipse Covenant. Cipher and Grimshot. A location whispered in good faith and used in bad. A Contract that Miles had never mentioned losing because Miles did not mention the things that cost him most.

I know something now that Miles doesn’t know I know.

Not because Alondra gave me permission to tell him. She didn’t.

Not because it’s mine to tell. It isn’t.

Because a session is a session. What’s said here stays here until the patient decides otherwise.

And Alondra said: not today. But I’m going to tell him.

So I wait.

She closed the notepad.

The armchair was still faintly blue.

She looked at it, then at the door, then back at the armchair.

Miles appeared in the doorway.

Not waiting. He had the specific posture of someone who happened to be passing the planning room and had stopped. He did this after every session with Alondra. She had noticed it the first time and filed it. She had noticed it again today and filed it again and now, standing on the other side of everything she had just heard, she understood the specific quality of what he was doing.

He was making sure she had left.

Not to confront her. Not even consciously. Just… checking. The way you check a room you used to be comfortable in and aren’t sure about anymore.

“Session run long?” he said.

“A bit,” she said.

He nodded. His eyes moved to the notepad, then away. He did not ask.

He was standing at the door. He had not come in.

The armchair was still faintly blue.

Miles was still in the doorway.

Livia looked at the armchair.

Then at Miles.

Then at the armchair.

He had not crossed the threshold.

He was not in the two-meter proximity range.

The armchair glows for what’s in the room.

Miles is at the door.

He hasn’t come in.

The armchair is still blue.

She looked at the armchair for one more second, making sure she was reading it correctly.

She was.

It’s not reading him.

It’s reading what he brought to the door with him.

Which means what he’s carrying is strong enough to register through a wall.

Miles said: “You okay?”

She looked at him. His expression was the usual one — controlled, attentive, the version that looked like easy composure and was something else entirely. He asked it lightly. He meant it completely.

“I’m okay,” she said. “Are you?”

A brief pause. The kind he took when he was deciding something.

“Yeah,” he said.

One word. She had learned its weight.

Yeah.

Not okay. Yeah.

Filed.

He pushed off the doorframe and continued down the corridor. She heard his footsteps — even, unhurried, the walk of someone who had somewhere to be.

The armchair faded to neutral gray.

Livia sat with the empty room for a long moment.

He’s carrying something that’s strong enough to register through a wall.

He doesn’t know I know what it is.

He doesn’t know Alondra is going to tell him.

He said yeah and walked away and his footsteps were even and I watched an armchair light up for a man standing in a doorway.

Progress: 22%.

The number still feels wrong.

I think it might always feel wrong until something in him decides it’s time.

All I can do is be here when it does.

She opened her notepad to a new page.

She wrote:

Fixing and healing are different verbs.

I’m going to think about that one for a while.

The same number as Yuki’s critical hit damage from the center cluster yesterday.

The same number appearing in the third different context in two days.

I don’t know what that means yet.

But I’m writing it down.
