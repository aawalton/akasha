The scout-ship broke through the haze, and Fangreach came up to meet them wrong.

Aiven saw it before Pelka said anything. The streets below, normally a slow churn of carts and stalls and beastfolk going about their business, had turned to chaos. People were running. Not toward something. Away. A loose, frantic current of bodies streaming down the main avenue, tails low, ears flat, mothers carrying cubs against their chests.

Then he saw the smoke.

It rose in a thick black column from the top floor of the Vulpine headquarters, the tall building bending the haze around it, glass glinting where glass should not have been visible at all.

"Oh no," Pelka breathed beside him, both hooves on the rail. "Oh no, oh no. That's the executive floor. Sir Aiven, that's her office!"

"I see it."

"The whole top is—is there even a—I can't see if the—" Pelka's voice climbed half an octave with every word.

Things were not looking good.

Aiven made the decision in the time it took to draw a breath.

"Land the ship properly," he said. "All of you, down at the dock, stay together. Pelka, the emergency door. Open it."

"The...emergency door? You want to jump?"

"It's faster. Open it."

Pelka stared at him for one frozen second. Then her training took over, the way it always did when someone gave her a clear task in the middle of a disaster, and she scrambled to the side hatch and hauled the lever down with both hooves.

The wind tore in.

"Be careful!" she shouted over it, fleece whipping flat against her. "Please, Sir Aiven, be careful."

Aiven nodded once.

He jumped.

The air took him hard, then he took the air. He reached for the wind the way he had learned to over the past week, gathering it beneath him, around him, shaping the fall into something closer to flight. It was not graceful. It would never be graceful. But it held, and the broken window of Cyria's meeting room swelled up in front of him, jagged glass framing the dark inside, and he angled toward it and let the airstream carry him through.

He landed in chaos.

The meeting room was wrecked. The long table he had sat at three days ago, eating tortoise buns and laying out his plan, was shoved halfway across the floor and tipped onto its side. Chairs lay scattered and broken. The windows along the far wall were blown out, every one of them, glass scattered across the carpet in a glittering spray. The wicker basket was crushed flat in a corner.

But there was no one there.

No Cyria. No staff. No enemy. No bodies, which was the thing his clerk-brain noticed first and held onto hardest. Empty.

Then he heard the explosion.

It came from outside and below, off to the east, a deep, concussive whump that he felt in his sternum before he heard it in his ears. He crossed to the blown-out window and looked.

An empty field, some distance from the building, out past the edge of the district where the streets thinned into open ground. A fresh scar of churned earth was still smoking. As he watched, another bloom of light flared across it.

His mind worked it through in the space of a heartbeat.

No bodies in the building. No fight in the building. The damage here was the start of something, not the whole of it. And the fight was now happening in an empty field a safe distance away from anything that mattered.

Cyria had pulled the enemy out. Whoever had come for her, she had not let them tear through her headquarters and her people. She had drawn them somewhere open, somewhere she could fight without burying the city under the rubble.

It was exactly what she would do.

Aiven did not waste another second.

He pushed off through the broken window, out into open air again, and reached for the wind, and flew toward the smoking clearing.

As he closed the distance, the field resolved beneath him.

There was a man standing in the middle of it.

He was big. Not bulky in the soft way, but built like something poured into a mold and left to set — broad through the shoulders, thick through the forearms. He wore armor, dark and segmented and clearly not ornamental, plated across the chest and shoulders and scuffed from use. His skin was a deep, warm brown. His hair was a startling red, cropped close at the sides and longer on top, pushed back from a heavy brow. And his eyes, even at this distance, even through the smoke, were a flat and steady green.

He reminded him of someone.

He stood in the churned earth with the patience of a man who had all day.

And around him, Cyria was a blur.

She dashed across the field, tail streaming, her movements quick and economical and absolutely furious. Light flared at her hands—high, clean bursts that could only be coming from artifacts. As Aiven watched, she swept one arm out and a translucent beast unfolded into being beside her. A great spectral cat, half again the size of a real one, shimmering at the edges, lunged at the armored man with claws bared.

He raised one forearm.

The beast broke against it like surf against a rock.

Cyria was already moving. Already summoning the next one. Already circling for a better angle, refusing to stop, refusing to give him a still target.

Aiven set his jaw and angled his flight down toward the field.

The summoned beasts came at the man in a pack, and Cyria came with them. She closed the distance behind the cover they gave her, fast and low, and went for the neck — the common weak point, the one that worked even on the durable ones. A few smaller beasts darted in first to pull his attention.

The man had seen it coming.

A pillar of flame erupted around him. The beasts faded mid-leap, unmade in the heat, and Cyria was already committed, already halfway through her charge with nothing left to hide behind.

The man dashed to meet her.

He caught her by the neck. He slammed her down. The ground cratered beneath the impact, dust and broken earth blowing outward in a ring.

Aiven was close now.

His heart stopped for a single beat.

The man lifted her. Cyria hung from his fist, tattered, her boots clear of the ground, and flickers of fire began to crawl up across his knuckles. The intent was plain. He was going to burn her where she dangled.

Aiven fired.

The mana blast streaked across the field. The man saw it, twisted, and it carved past him by inches — but Aiven had already followed it in, obsidian arm pulled back and humming, and he hit the ground at a sprint and drove the punch home.

CRACK.

It landed clean. It was not enough.

The man rocked, staggered, tumbled back a step — but his fingers stayed locked around Cyria's throat. She clawed at them, prying, twisting, her nails finding no purchase on the armored grip. Aiven could hear the sound she was making. The thin, scraping wheeze of someone whose lungs could not find the air.

He focused on the earth behind the man.

He asked it.

FWUMP.

A pillar erupted under the man's heels and threw him upward. Not high, just enough, just enough to break the angle of his arm and tear his grip loose. Cyria dropped. She hit the grass on her hands and knees and folded over, coughing, dragging air back into her in great ragged pulls.

Aiven put himself between her and where the man would land.

"Who is he?" he asked, not turning.

Cyria got one hand flat against the ground. Her voice came out wrecked, scraped raw. "Daran." A cough followed. "One of Lucien's."

Daran.

Aiven did not know if he could beat him.

But a cold, clear thought was already settling into place beneath the fear, the way they always did now. This man was Lucien's. This man came from the Fold. And the Fold was where Virelle was. Cyria may or may not have access to the Fold, but this man certainly did.

"We need to finish this quickly," Aiven said.

Cyria let out something that was half a laugh and half a cough. "That," she rasped, pushing herself upright, "is what I have been trying to do."

Aiven raised the obsidian arm. The cyan etchings flared along the knuckles.

"Then let's hope I change that. I cannot afford to waste a single second."
