Jill opened her eyes.

It was no use pretending anymore. She'd been conscious since the tunnel, maybe even earlier. Hard to say exactly when awareness had crept back in. First, she heard the sound of footsteps and breathing. Then the low rumble of voices she couldn't quite separate into words. Then sensation. The steady rhythm of someone walking, the warmth of arms beneath her knees and shoulders.

His heartbeat. That had been what woke her, really. Pressed against his chest, she could feel it thumping away beneath the thin cotton of his shirt. Steady and strong and alive. A sound she hadn't heard in six years. It was soothing to her ears.

She'd kept her eyes shut. Kept her breathing shallow. Let her body stay limp against him while her mind raced through every possible version of what came next.

What do you say to someone you tried to kill?

Sorry seemed inadequate. I had my reasons was true but insufficient. You're not really him was what she believed—what she needed to believe—but saying it out loud while he cradled her like something precious felt obscene.

So she'd taken the coward's way out. Eyes closed. Breathing even. Playing dead.

It was more convenient to pretend. Postpone the awkwardness. Let the silence fill the space where explanations should have been.

And—if she were being honest, which she tried very hard not to be—she enjoyed being carried.

It was pathetic. She knew that. The Moon Mother, feared leader of Vandiel's faithful, the woman who'd stared down demons and walked through plague fields without flinching, reduced to a swooning girl because a man picked her up. Not even a particularly strong man. She could feel his arms trembling with the effort. His grip readjusting every few minutes. That slight stumble on the uneven ground that he'd tried to cover by shifting her weight.

He was struggling. Clearly. And he hadn't put her down once.

That was the part that made it hard to keep pretending.

The old Clive would've set her down by now. The old Clive would've been practical about it, would've said something like you're heavier than you look with that stupid half-grin, and she would've hit his arm, and he would've deserved it. The old Clive rationed his effort. He conserved and optimized. That was the chemist in him.

This Clive just... held on.

She'd felt the moment he sat down against the wall. Felt how carefully he lowered her, his jacket beneath her head. She'd almost opened her eyes then. Almost. The tenderness of it sat like a hook behind her ribs, pulling at something she'd spent years burying.

Then he started talking to Maleus, and the hook twisted.

We were in love. Sworn to always be together.

Past tense. Present conviction. The contradiction of it hit her harder than Ignis’s flare breathe lance had. She'd listened to the entire exchange with her heart slamming so loud she was certain Azura could hear it.

But then Maleus started speaking. She didn’t like that. It wasn’t his story to tell. She couldn’t let him control the narrative.

So Jill stirred. She made a soft noise to attract their attention. Then she opened her eyes. The ceiling of the cave came into focus first. Then Clive's face, looking down at her. Closer than she expected.

She met his gaze. His expression cycled through surprise and relief.

She sat up. Slowly. Not because she needed to—the wounds were bad but not debilitating—but because moving too fast meant leaving the warmth of his jacket, and she wasn't quite ready for that.

"You’re awake. Are you alright?" Clive asked.

Jill brushed hair from her face, giving him a shy nod. "I’m fine."

“We’re halfway down the mountain, we’ll be safe soon.” Clive explained.

“That’s good.”

“Maleus told me you’ve been here for six years. “

Jill nodded again.

"Do you want to tell me what's going on?" Clive asked. His voice was careful. Gentle, even. The way he used to talk to her after they'd fought and he was testing whether the storm had passed. "If you're not ready, that's fine. I can wait."

“It’s fine.” There was no use delaying the inevitable any longer. She had to explain one way or the other and it was about time. She pulled her knees to her chest and set her chin on her knees as she gaze on the pool. It was easier to speak to water than to his face. “ I can talk. It started after you died.”

She felt him tense beside her. Good. Let him sit with that for a second.

"There was a fire. The apartment—something electrical." The words came out practiced. She'd told this story to herself so many times it had worn grooves in her mind. "By the time I got there, the building was already—" She stopped. Swallowed. "They said you didn't suffer. I don't know if that was true or if they just say that."

Clive was silent beside her. She could feel him wanting to speak, but he held it. He was always such a good listener.

"After that..." She pressed her fingernails into her shins through the fabric. "I wanted you back. That's it. That's the whole story. Everything else is just details."

"Jill—"

"I met a goddess." Her voice flattened. "She called herself Miracles."

Jill closed her eyes. The memory was still sharp in her mind. Not even the passing of six years had dulled it.

"It was a Tuesday. I remember because Tuesdays were the worst. Tuesdays were the day we used to get takeout and argue about what to watch. And now Tuesdays were just... Tuesdays. I went to your grave after work. I was there every day by then. Every single day. Rain, sun, didn't matter. The groundskeeper knew me by name. He'd started leaving a folding chair by the plot because he got tired of watching me sit in the mud. I'd talk to you. About my day. What I ate. What was on the news. Stupid, pointless things. I told you about the neighbor's cat getting stuck in a tree. I told you the pharmacy moved the toothpaste to a different aisle and it pissed me off. I told you I missed you. Every time. That was how I ended it. Like signing off on a letter."

The pool's surface rippled. A drop from somewhere above. Jill watched the rings expand and fade.

"That day I was telling you about—I don't even remember. Something from work, probably. And then there was someone sitting next to me. Cross-legged on the grass like she'd grown out of it. Barefoot. Young—she looked young. Like a teenager, almost. This small, pretty thing in a blue sundress, sitting in a cemetery."

Jill's jaw tightened.

"She was picking dandelions. Pulling them out of the ground one by one and lining them up on her knee like she was keeping score. She didn't look at me. Not at first. She just sat there, picking dandelions next to my dead boyfriend's headstone, humming to herself."

Clive said nothing. She could feel him holding still the way he did when he was afraid that moving would make her stop talking.

"I asked her who she was. She didn't answer. I asked if she worked there. Nothing. I told her to leave. She picked another dandelion." Jill's fingers curled. "Then she asked me if I wanted a miracle. She told me I was here too often. ‘What if you didn't have to visit anymore? What if he could just come home?’ "

The cave was silent. Even the water had stopped dripping.

"She showed me all kinds of stuff. Of where you were. Of magic that shouldn’t exist in our world. And I knew. I knew she wasn't human. I knew it was dangerous. I knew that whatever she was offering came with a price that no sane person would pay. I knew all of that." She opened her eyes and stared at the ceiling. "I said yes before she finished the sentence."

“What did she ask for?” Clive asked.

"She said all I had to do was—"

The words stalled.

"It's going to sound insane."

"Most of what's happened today sounds insane," Clive said.

Despite everything, a ghost of a laugh escaped her. "Fair point."

She took a deep breath. "She told me to jump off a building."

"What! You jumped off a building." Clive burst out, attracting the attention of the surrounding cultists who were trying their best not to stare at them. “That’s reckless.”

"The Harmon building. Fourteen floors. She said it had to be certain. No chance of waking up in a hospital. No room for a miracle of the wrong kind. Miracles only happen for those willing to sacrifice everything. That was her pitch."

"Her pitch?" Clive was still agitated, but strangely, it felt comforting to see him angry for her. "A goddess told you to kill yourself and you're calling it a pitch?"

"What would you call it?"

"I'd call it—" He stopped. The surrounding cultists were openly staring at them now. Clive seemed to realise it and calmed down.  "What if she was lying?" he said finally. "What if you'd just... died? For nothing?"

"Then I'd have been dead. And I wouldn't have cared, because you were already gone and the world without you in it wasn't—" She caught herself. That was too much.

She tried again, steadier. "I wasn't thinking about what-ifs, Clive. I was thinking about you. That's all I had left."

She took a breath.

"It worked, obviously. I woke up here. Same face, different body. Stronger. The marks were already on my skin." She touched her forearm where the crescent patterns traced silver lines beneath the dirt and dried blood. "And Miracles was in my head, telling me the work wasn't done."

"What work?"

"You." The word came out simpler than she intended. "She said you were coming, but the world wasn't ready yet. That I had to prepare the way. Missions. Tasks. People to save, alliances to build, fights to win. Six years of doing everything she asked, everything, no matter how—"

She turned her head against her knees and looked at him.

"And then came her final mission… She told me to kill you."

The words left her mouth and hung there, ugly and bare. She watched Clive's face for the flinch of betrayal that should have followed a confession like that.

It didn't come. His jaw tightened. His eyes shifted. That was all.

Of course. He'd already worked it out. She'd tried to put a blade through his chest hours ago. He'd had the entire walk down this mountain to do the math.

Somehow, his lack of surprise hurt worse than anger would have.

"She told me you were a fake. A copy. Something this world made that looked like you, sounded like you, but wasn't. A trick to keep me here. To keep me compliant. She said the real Clive was waiting somewhere else. A world where we could finally be together. And the only way to reach him was to destroy the fake."

She could hear how insane it sounded. Laid out like this, stripped of Miracles' velvet voice and the crushing weight of six years of loneliness, it was the logic of a madwoman. Kill the man you love to find the man you love. Believe the goddess who already convinced you to jump off a building. What's one more leap?

But six years was a long time. Long enough to erode certainty. Long enough for soon to curdle into never, and for desperate to rot into something that would believe anything if it meant the waiting could finally stop.

"And do you still think I'm a fake?" Clive asked.

Jill looked at him. The paint under his fingernails. The way he sat—shoulders forward, weight on his hands, leaning toward her even now. The small scar on his jaw from the time he'd tripped carrying groceries up the stairs and refused to admit it was because he'd been trying to read the back of a cereal box at the same time.

"I don't know," she whispered. And it was the most honest thing she'd said in six years.
