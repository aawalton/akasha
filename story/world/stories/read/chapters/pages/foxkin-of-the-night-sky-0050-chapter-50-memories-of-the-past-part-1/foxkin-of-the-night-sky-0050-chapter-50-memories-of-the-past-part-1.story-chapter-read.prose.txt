My posture is a few degrees too rigid.

I correct it before Mother notices. She notices things like that quickly. Two weeks was all it took for her to notice the change in my hair. Two weeks for the white at the roots to become impossible to dismiss as lighting.

Now we are sitting in a doctor’s office.

White walls. Framed certificates. A clock that ticks with soft, persistent authority.

The air smells faintly of disinfectant and something floral attempting, unsuccessfully, to soften it.

We are here to determine why it changed.

Questions come first.

Whether the color change is spreading. Whether there has been any increased shedding. Recent illnesses. Medications. Diet adjustments. Any family history of autoimmune disorders.

Mother answers before the final syllable of each question settles.

Father fills in dates with precise accuracy.

I sit.

Posture aligned.

Hands folded neatly in my lap.

Expression neutral, attentive.

The model daughter.

“Has anything changed recently in her routine?” the doctor asks. “School stress? Moving houses? Any significant life events?”

Mother smiles. It is the kind of smile designed to reassure investors.

“None.”

Mr. Dennison turns to me.

He has kind eyes. Observant, but careful not to appear intrusive.

“Does your scalp itch or hurt?”

I shake my head.

“No.”

“Have you noticed the hair changing yourself?”

“Yes.”

“When did you first see it?”

“Two weeks ago.”

My voice is even. Neither defensive nor distressed. Simply factual.

He nods and writes something down.

The questions continue.

Patterns. Sleep. Appetite. Fatigue. Headaches.

I answer when required.

Mother answers when possible.

Eventually, the conversation gives way to examination. The paper on the examination chair crinkles softly as I sit. Latex gloves snap into place.

He parts sections of my hair carefully, studying the roots.

The white is stark against the remaining black.

Not gray or faded.

Pure white.

He hums quietly, thoughtful.

No commentary yet. Just observation.

The clock continues its steady rhythm on the wall.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

I focus on keeping my shoulders relaxed.

Not slouched or stiff.

My posture is polished.

Perfectly so.

He checks my eyebrows next. Then my eyelashes.

He parts them gently with gloved fingers, studying the roots as if the absence of color might be hiding there as well.

“No change,” he murmurs. “Though in some cases, depending on the underlying condition, it can progress.”

Progress.

An interesting word.

He examines my nails afterward. Presses lightly against the beds. Studies the skin of my hands, my wrists.

“No pitting. No discoloration.”

He checks behind my ears. Along my hairline.

Nothing.

When he finishes, he removes his gloves and turns back toward Mother and Father.

“We’ll run a few labs, just to be thorough. This may simply be pigment loss in the hair follicles. Sometimes it’s isolated.”

Sometimes.

Mother nods once, as if she has already decided that will be the outcome.

Mr. Dennison draws blood. The needle slides in smoothly. I do not flinch.

He places a small bandage on the inside of my elbow.

We thank him and leave.

The lobby is quiet, empty apart from a potted plant that is trying very hard to look alive.

As we walk toward the exit, I glance down at the bandage.

A small square of white against pale skin.

I wonder if Mr. Calder will notice.

He usually notices everything.

The memory fractures.

—

I am sitting at Mr. Calder’s desk.

We are doing etiquette today.

“How to lie,” he says, hands folded neatly atop the desk. “How to manipulate. How to utilize others’ emotions to your advantage.”

His tone is clinical. Precise and measured.

There is no cruelty in it.

Only instruction.

It is simple, once the rules are understood.

Humans are simple.

Tone alters perception. Posture alters trust. Word choice directs outcome. Apologize without admitting fault, deflect without appearing evasive. Smile without warmth.

The correct combinations produce predictable results.

Nearly always.

It is not a matter of morality, it's a matter of execution.

We practice.

He presents scenarios. Social missteps, political traps, emotional bait.

I respond.

He adjusts once. Then not again.

The lesson ends sooner than expected.

Mr. Calder leans back slightly in his chair.

His composure fractures. Not dramatically, but enough.

His eyebrows draw together. His lips part as if he intends to speak, then close again.

Despite the content of his lessons, he is revealing too much. A flicker of surprise. A touch of disbelief.

Mother and Father instructed him to be harsh.

To correct every error, no matter how minor. To correct firmly.

Never physically. Words are sufficient.

This time, he finds none.

“I…” He hesitates. “I cannot find anything to correct.”

The words are almost muttered, as if he does not quite trust them.

He looks at my posture.

My hands. My expression.

Searching for a flaw.

There is none.

I do not make mistakes.

Not anymore.

—

“Her tests came back normal, which is good news.”

I blink once.

Normal.

Mr. Dennison folds his hands together on the desk, fingers interlaced, as though arranging the words before he releases them.

“What we’re seeing is most consistent with a condition in which the pigment-producing cells in the hair follicles stop functioning. It’s autoimmune in nature.”

He watches our reactions carefully.

“It’s not dangerous. It won’t affect her health, and it doesn’t damage the hair itself.”

Mother exhales softly.

Relief. Measured and controlled.

To anyone observing, it would read as maternal concern easing.

The distinction between that and financial reassurance is subtle.

Nearly invisible.

I am an investment.

One that Mother would prefer not to lose.

Mr. Dennison continues.

“Sometimes these conditions appear during periods of stress. Has anything significant changed in her life recently?”

The question is gentle.

Deliberate.

A probe disguised as curiosity.

Father answers without pause.

“No,” he says smoothly. “Her routine has been consistent. Tutors, lessons, structured extracurriculars. She thrives under it.”

His tone is warm. Reassuring. Polished.

It leaves no room for doubt.

Mr. Dennison’s gaze returns to me.

He holds it slightly longer this time.

He looks at my posture. At my stillness. The way my hands rest without fidgeting and the way my expression does not shift.

He tries again.

“Sometimes stress can affect the body in unusual ways.”

Father’s smile widens by a fraction.

“She’s an exceptional child,” he replies evenly. “Pressure builds character.”

The words land softly.

Final.

Mr. Dennison hesitates.

Just briefly.

His pen hovers above the paper before he lowers it.

“There’s no treatment needed unless it bothers her cosmetically.” A pause. “And even then, that would simply be optional.”

He signs the forms.

The sound of pen against paper is louder than it should be.

We stand. We thank him. We leave.

And we never returned to his office again.

The next day I am seated in a salon chair, spine straight, hands folded neatly in my lap.

A black cape is fastened around my shoulders.

The mirror in front of me reflects a small girl with white roots and a composure far older than her years.

The chemical scent is immediate.

Sharp. Acrid. Artificially floral in an attempt to disguise what it truly is.

My scalp tingles as the dye is brushed through my hair.

The stylist hesitates while working.

She speaks carefully.

“I do need to mention that these products can be very harsh on younger scalps. Especially if used frequently.”

Mother smiles at her reflection rather than at the woman herself.

“It’s only for a family gathering,” she says smoothly. “A one-time adjustment.”

It is not.

Imperfection is unacceptable.

But repeated visits to a salon invite observation. Observation invites speculation. Speculation invites rumor.

So the salon becomes unnecessary.

The maids are instructed.

They learn quickly.

Eventually, it becomes routine.

Every two weeks, I sit before my mirror in my room while my assigned maid prepares the dye in a small ceramic bowl.

Gloves. Brush. Clips. Measured efficiency.

The black is applied carefully, section by section.

The scent fills the room.

My scalp burns.

A slow, persistent sting that grows sharper as minutes pass.

I do not flinch. The pain is irrelevant. It serves no function.

The black that emerges is flawless. Too flawless.

Uniform from root to tip.

Deeper and more consistent than my natural hair ever was.

Mother selected the shade personally.

Dark enough to be striking.

Not so dark as to seem unnatural.

Attention-catching without appearing intentional.

Controlled, strategic, and perfect.

It does not look like my hair. It looks like an improvement.

My maid finishes the final rinse and dries it meticulously. She bows lightly once the styling is complete, gathers the bottles and brushes, and leaves without a sound.

The door clicks shut behind her.

The room is quiet again.

I remain seated for several seconds, hands folded neatly in my lap, posture unchanged.

The mirror reflects a small girl with flawless black hair and a perfectly neutral expression.

I reach up and remove the tie.

The hair falls over my shoulders in a smooth sheet, perfectly uniform and controlled. I lean forward slightly, parting it near the roots.

There.

A thin line of white already visible against my scalp where the dye did not quite reach.

It will be more obvious in a week. Two at most.

I smooth the section back into place. The burn along my scalp lingers, subtle now, a low hum rather than a sting.

I press two fingers lightly against the crown of my head.

Firm. Testing.

The sensation sharpens immediately.

Good.

It still hurts.

Which means it is working.

I release the pressure and let my hand fall back to my lap. In the mirror, my expression has not changed.

The black is immaculate.

Mother chose well.

I tilt my head slightly to the left, then to the right. Angles matter. Lighting matters. Perception matters.

From a distance, no one will question it.

Up close, no one will dare.

I practice a smile. Small and polite.

Not too bright, but also not too distant.

I hold it for five seconds.

Ten. Relax. Again.

This time with a fraction more warmth in the eyes. Not enough to invite familiarity. Just enough to appear genuine.

I let it fade.

The white at the roots will continue to grow.

The dye will continue to burn.

The ritual will continue.

That is acceptable.

Imperfections can be managed.

I gather my hair back into a ponytail and secure it neatly.

When I stand, my posture is correct.

Perfectly so.

I look at the mirror one last time.

The girl staring back is immaculate.

Her hair is not mine.

I smile anyway.
