[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 90 — 1:00 PM[Location]: West Campus · Yggdrasil Academy · Eight-Part Island · The Badlands

Five directory searches, four increasingly undignified letters, and one negotiation conducted through somebody else's coach had established a single fact about the Inner Sea in November: nobody left in it was willing to stand in for Clarissa Deiner. So they booked the Badlands wedge of the construct anyway, with nothing in it to shoot at.

Spent a week trying to rent a raid boss, Hathaway's developer brain observed, standing on plate rock scoured flat by a wind that had been blowing since the facility opened, and the patch notes read: empty field, one tape measure.

Victoria came across the rock with a crystal vial in each gloved hand and stopped a pace away. The wind moved her hair and did nothing else to her.

The vials held [Twin Solution], which was how Hathaway was going to borrow Victoria's eyes for the afternoon. The liquid had split itself down the middle of each vial, crimson on one side and blue on the other, and the two halves rotated slowly around a border neither of them had agreed to. One vial, one hour.

"You know the failure mode," Victoria said.

"Affinity decides how much mana stays in the link," Hathaway said. "Bad match, it leaks into the air until somebody's nose starts bleeding. Good match, you stop being two people until it wears off."

She and Surtrina had tested theirs at the bench in Roxanne's class, and the synchronization had come up flawless inside a second, which was an unarguable biochemical statement about two people who had never once been civil to each other. It held for ten seconds. Then the implication landed on both of them at the same time: Surtrina informed the ceiling that the sensation was disgusting, Hathaway agreed with her instantly and at length, and the coefficient fell off a cliff hard enough to give them both nosebleeds and ruin two perfectly good collars.

Victoria handed one vial over. "I do not expect to lose a single unit," she said, with no inflection whatsoever.

She just flexed at me with a leakage coefficient. Hathaway's spectator thread sat down with a bag of something. People write entire sonnets and get less mileage.

They drank, and it went through like credentials being accepted. Behind Hathaway's sternum a second heartbeat started up, cooler than her own and considerably better organized, and then a second mana reserve slid in over the top of hers the way deep water slides over hot sand, vast and motionless.

She opened her eyes, and the Badlands rendered twice. Once as a wedge of wind-scoured stone under an open sky, and once as current: the leyline gradient running downhill across the plate, a slow warm eddy still turning where Surtrina had been standing, a hairline fault in space opening and closing where Spectra was choosing not to exist at the moment. On a channel of its own, every mind on the wedge glowed at its own steady brightness.

Second monitor. Her developer brain caught the vertigo, throttled it, and started cataloguing. And the camera operator is a person who has already decided what's worth looking at.

"Your intake is faster than the literature allows for," Victoria said. "The routing is stable. Rely on it."

Hathaway rolled her shoulders, waited out the last of the nausea, and walked over to where the other three were waiting. She stepped into the middle of the circle, because that was where the job was now.

"Artillery first," she said, and turned her wrist over. The outermost ring of [Star Orbit] came off the bracer with a small clean chime and hung in the air over her palm, turning.

"This ring can leave my body. It carries my mana, and as long as the two of us stay in tune, the spell treats it as me." She opened her fingers and let it drift out over the plate. "So I don't fire the sun from my hand. I fire it from the ring. The sun closes a hundred and fifteen meters around wherever it was fired from, and I'm not standing there."

She pushed the ring further out, and it began to wander in a slow drift nobody had asked for. "The tune gets thinner with distance. Close in, it goes exactly where I put it. Far out, it goes roughly where I put it." She closed her hand and the ring came home. "So I fire from a hundred and fifty meters out, which leaves me a short walk from the edge of my own crater. That's the standard shot." She tapped the ring once. "There's one more setting. Empower pushes the radius out to two hundred and thirty meters, which is farther than the ring can go, so the sun closes over me too, and every bone in my body breaks at the same time. I've done that. It's one shot, and it's my call."

She thumped her chest with the flat of her hand. "Body: partial dragon. Four parts out of five: horns, wings, tail, heart. I'm leaving the brain on the shelf."

The brain is the good part. It runs two schools of magic at once. Her developer brain signed off on the cut anyway. It's also the part that finishes the shift, and a finished shift is thirty feet of dragon standing in an open field. The artillery wants to be small. Across two entire lives I have hated exactly one thing consistently, and it is a hitbox the size of a barn.

"I will hold human form." Spectra had arrived at the edge of the circle without using any of the ground in between. The new violin-staff rested on her shoulder, and her thumb moved along its bridge the way other people check a pulse. "The instrument requires fingers. Claws produce a sound I would have to apologize for."

Hathaway put her hands in her coat pockets, because otherwise they were going to do something with themselves. "I need three things," she said. "Nobody interrupts me while the matrix is open. My saves have to be high enough that a luck hex can't touch me. And I am not dropping our own artillery on our own people a second time. Who's got something for that?"

Surtrina rolled her shoulders, and the flame at the end of her tail came up a notch. "Top-tier [Avatar]," she said. "Ninety-five percent of anything hostile slides off you, hexes included. Anything trying to knock a cast out of your hands lands at half strength."

Hathaway's mouth got there before her brain did. "I thought Avatar was a melee buff."

"It is usually cast on oneself, for melee," Victoria said, "because it seals the caster's spellcasting for thirty seconds. Cast on someone else, the target receives the effect and the caster still pays the seal."

Hathaway turned and looked at Surtrina, who had somehow neglected to mention the invoice. Surtrina looked back with her chin up, daring anyone in the circle to make a thing of it. And Victoria read the price out loud in front of everybody on purpose, Hathaway's accountant thread noted, because she is not letting me spend a person without seeing the number.

"Your dragon shift is a spell, so you shift first and cast Avatar on me after," she said. "The seal takes your spell list. It leaves you the dragon body and every innate ability you were born with." She made herself finish it. "That's you below your best for thirty seconds, and I'm spending it anyway, because yours is the only floor in this cohort I can build a plan on."

"Blight, Flesh to Stone, and four more things you'd rather not stand near," Surtrina said, and her fangs came out. "Plus the tail. Thirty seconds as a flying siege engine. I'll survive it."

"Then Avatar goes out when I call it, and not before." Hathaway held her eye.

Surtrina's throat moved once, and she found a point past Hathaway's shoulder to look at until it was over. "Obviously," she said.

And neither of us is ever going to mention that, Hathaway's spectator thread noted, with enormous care, out loud, in writing, or on the far bank of the Styx.

She turned to Yenna. "Every scroll we own goes in your sleeves. All five inventories. You run the economy."

"Accepted," Yenna said, and drew a folded sheet out of her cuff and held it out. "Provisional manifest. It assumes you were going to say that."

Hathaway took it. Itemized, priced, sorted by cast time. She wrote this before lunch. Her accountant thread filed it next to a spreadsheet with thirty-one names on it. Somebody is going to have to tell her that committing all your labor to a decision nobody has made yet is not a personality. Somebody who is not me, because I'm going to use it.

That left the assignment she had been carrying around since Thursday, so she braced her feet and got on with it. "Victoria. You're taking Iridescent Dragon."

"I assumed as much," Victoria said, and adjusted a cuff.

"I'm not finished." Hathaway kept her voice completely level, and every thread she owned except the spectator evacuated the building. "You carry Spectra."

Victoria's face went blank, and Spectra's fingers stopped on the strings. Surtrina turned to look at Hathaway with the wide, reverent, slightly nauseated expression of a woman watching somebody walk onto a live minefield to retrieve a hat.

"The iridescent chassis puts up the strongest deflection field any of us has, by a wide margin." Hathaway pointed at Spectra without looking away from Victoria. "And it's immune to radiant damage and blindness, which matters because of me. The thing I fire is a sun. Every eye on this wedge shuts when it lands, mine included. Last Saturday I fired three, and I found out where the third one went when I opened my eyes and Spectra was a puddle. You're the only one who can look into the boundary while it closes and tell me whether she's still inside it.

"Spectra needs one minute. There is one platform on this wedge that can buy her that minute, and it's your back."

Victoria looked at Spectra, and Spectra looked at the ground, and neither of them appeared to be enjoying the afternoon.

Then Victoria took one measured breath, and her composure came back over her face like fresh lacquer, without a seam anywhere. "I understand," she said. "I accept."

And then, in exactly the same tone, to nobody in particular: "The chassis carries a passenger at combat maneuver without losing its turn rate. I tested it on Wednesday." A pause. "I was not calculating for her."

She tested it on Wednesday, for me. Hathaway's spectator thread put its drink down. And I just gave the seat to Spectra.

"I will not be riding," Spectra said. "The mist carries me." Her thumb moved along the bridge. "What it does not do is stop anything. Keep the field over me and put me where you need me."

Victoria inclined her head.

"Good," Hathaway said, and clapped her hands once. "Comms."

Shouting across a battlefield was slow, and everybody could hear it, so witches talked in three layers. A compression spell packed a sentence into one syllable and unpacked it directly into the listener's auditory cortex. Hand signs covered anything that had to cross distance in silence. Eye contact carried whatever needed encrypting, because a glance between two witches could pass a cipher key, a coordinate, and an order in the time it took to blink, whatever the romance serials claimed it carried.

"So we standardize," Hathaway said. "Somebody pick a system."

"Holheim encryption," Victoria said, and every thread Hathaway owned stopped what it was doing.

"Milan'thir," said Surtrina, and the temperature on the plate rock dropped about nine degrees.

"Holheim changes its key with every transmission," Victoria said. "Milan'thir has used one key per session for two hundred years, which means everyone has been reading it for two hundred years."

"It is the field standard of every army on this continent," Surtrina said, unfolding off her boulder to her full and considerable height, "except one, and that one would rather lose."

"In 1939 we marched into White City," Spectra said, to the strings.

"Majority," Yenna said. "Three of us are Milan'thir."

Four heads turned toward Hathaway, the third Milan'thir vote. I am a Ludwig of the White City, she thought, from somewhere inside a burning building, and my house keeps Ovelia's book because we knelt. And I would like it entered into the record that our first fight is not going to be about a cipher.

"Stop," Hathaway said, and cut her hand through the air. "Compression, encryption, and gestures are three separate layers. They can come from three different places." She held up three fingers and started taking them down. "Milan'thir compression, because the coordinate precision is genuinely better. Holheim cipher keys for the eye layer, because base twelve is a nightmare to crack mid-air and everybody here knows it. Gestures from neutral alchemical shorthand, because it's ugly and nobody here loves it." She looked around the circle. "Nobody yields. We write our own protocol, and it belongs to none of you."

"Define the variables," Surtrina said, and sat back down.

It took ninety minutes and produced five words.

"Radius," Hathaway said. "Followed by one number. When you hear it, that's where the boundary is closing. Don't be inside it.

"Hold. Absolute. If you hear it while you're casting, you dump the cast and eat the recoil, and you don't ask me anything about it until we're on the bench. Last week we died because people fired. Nobody has ever died from holding.

"Abort. Formation's dead, everybody scatters, we regroup on Yenna.

"Mark. Priority target, vector attached.

"Ignite. Avatar goes out on that word and no other way. It runs on a clock, and Deiner arrives when she arrives."

Silence is the default, she had written at the top of the sheet, in her own aggressively legible hand. Every transmission is an exception.

The finished codebook went round the circle a little after four: five words on the cover, and under each one a Milan'thir frame, a Holheim key, and a hand sign that belonged to neither. It came back to her with five names on it. There it is. Her accountant thread closed a file it had been carrying since Friday night. One page. All five.

They drilled it until the sun went down over the wedge, a fresh vial every hour, running the rotations dry, over and over, until the cultural friction wore off and the protocol became reflex.

Hathaway would catch Victoria's eye, and the iridescent chassis would already be leaning off the vector before a sound reached anyone. She would say one compressed syllable at conversational volume and Surtrina would drop an imaginary strike on a coordinate nobody had ever said to her in words, and on the next pass hear Hold and abort one she had already committed to, which cost her something visible every single time. Yenna had the right scroll open before anybody finished naming the problem. Spectra stayed under Victoria's field the entire time and played spatial coordinates directly into four skulls, which was the most efficient thing anybody did all afternoon.

On the fortieth run somebody was still four hundredths of a second early. The forty-first ran clean and silent from the first bell to the last.

They packed up in the long violet light, came out of the facility together, and split up at the intersection. Hathaway walked back to the Golden Bough with Victoria at her usual half-step and a second heartbeat still ticking quietly under her ribs, eleven minutes left on the fourth vial of the day.

Build's stable, her developer brain reported, filing the afternoon. Five modules, five different vendors, one undocumented protocol nobody outside the org can read. Ship it.

Four seconds later, the way it always did, her accountant thread arrived with the only line item that mattered. Every number she had ever been confident about had been her own. Today she had needed to be right about where four other people were standing.

Forty-one runs against an empty wedge said nothing about Clarissa Deiner. They said that every one of the four had been exactly where she put them.
