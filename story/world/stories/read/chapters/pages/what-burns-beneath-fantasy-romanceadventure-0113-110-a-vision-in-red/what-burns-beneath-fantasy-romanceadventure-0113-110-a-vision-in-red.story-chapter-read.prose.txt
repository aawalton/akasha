“They’re about, oh, this big, your graces. Very easy to keep, just gotta get them cooped up at night. A good hen’ll give an egg a day, an’ one carved bird’s enough to feed me an’ the missus for longer. That’s the lighter meat, there.”

I sat thunderstruck. My plate- and every other plate on the table- held generous portions of scrambled eggs, strips of crispy bacon, a wedge of chicken, a small wheatcake, diced beets, and bright red radishes.

Many of the Fae clearly had no idea what they were looking at. They regarded their meal as if it might bite them. Then, at Embriel’s encouragement, they slowly started eating.

This led to questions. Embriel had dipped his head graciously to the red-faced man- who stood with wringing hands and hopeful eyes- and asked him to explain it all.

So now we were learning about chickens.

“And are these birds dangerous?” A sharp-featured Fae from down the table asked. “Predatorial?”

I choked. Velidan looked at me, his face a mask of neutrality, and took a bite of chicken.

Theod, as the man had introduced himself, blinked. “Well, a rooster can be right vicious when something frets his flock. But our hens are as docile as can be.”

“What about reproduction?” Another voice chimed. “How difficult is it to maintain the numbers of this… flock?”

That incurred another blink. “Ah, well of course the rooster handles that. Twenty or thirty times a day, if we’d let him!” He chuckled. “If anything, keeping the numbers down is the difficult bit!”

A few murmurs echoed around the table.

Embriel looked positively wolfish. “Please, my friend, continue. Tell us about these vegetables.”

Theod went on and on, pausing to answer the occasional question. Some were practical, like ‘how long did this take to grow,’ and some bizarre, like ‘do radishes ever attack you.’

That one made me choke again.

Eventually Alora, who had listened with tented fingers and stormy eyes, spoke in a clear voice. “The details of your bounty are interesting, good farmer Theod. I have a different question, though. How did you meet lord Embriel, and by what terms is this meal offered?”

Theod and his wife exchanged glances. He cleared his throat. “Well, your grace, a few years back Marta and I fell in a terrible way. Our house is outside of Redshire. Lots of hills and fine farmland, an’ we’re blessed with our little parcel. One night, one of our neighbors went missing. Then she turned up in a field, clawed to pieces!”

Alora’s jaw tightened. “How tragic.”

Marta spoke in a tremulous tone. “It was, your grace. A bull went missing, next, and was found the same way. Then a stable hand from another farm, and one of the barmaids from an inn up the road. We were terrified! We thought we’d have to abandon our home-”

“Until the good lord Embriel appeared on our doorstep.” Theod lifted his chin as the figure slouched beside him smirked. “He said a wicked Fae- stars, no offense, your graces!”

“None taken,” Alora said as a few chuckles resounded around the room.

The farmer’s face turned beet-red. “Ah, yes, well, he said he’d come to protect us. He hunted the creature-”

“A rogue ferrin,” Embriel interjected gravely. “A terrible threat to the area.”

“It was! And of course we were ever so grateful.”

“And you promised him your service as thanks?” A thin, blonde Fae from down the table said dryly.

Embriel placed a hand over his heart. “You wound me, Sybion. I found humans in danger, so I ended the threat. No thanks were needed.”

“We offered him a meal much like this. To show how grateful we were.” Marta gave the Fae a shy smile.

He returned it tenfold. “And it was marvelous! You’re an excellent cook, my dear.”

She blushed. “He’s been very kind. He checks on us often and he brings coins every winter. And we have a daughter who’s always been so sickly; he brings tonics sometimes, and they help her much more than Redshire’s cleric.”

“The poor thing.” He rubbed her shoulder, the very picture of sympathy. “Theod, Marta, are you bound to me in any way? Held prisoner, threatened, pacted, sworn to do my bidding, any such nonsense?”

Both voiced fervent denials.

“Of course not. Thank you both for your time, and for the excellent meal. And,” he rose, placing a hand on each of their backs, “I’ll thank you again for your courage in allowing me to bring you here. I’m sure it’s been very overwhelming.”

Theod flapped his broad hands and gave a strained laugh. “I’ll be honest, I’m half-convinced the floor’s going to swallow me whole!”

“Not while I breathe,” Embriel chuckled. He nodded one of the gray-skinned creatures forwards. “See that my friends are compensated, brought to the lounge, and given anything they ask for. I’ll arrange for you both to be brought home this afternoon.”

The couple dipped awkward bows towards their audience. The stout little Fae led them out and silence descended on the room.

Alora finally shattered it. “How fortunate,” she ground out, sitting ramrod straight, “that you were there to protect them in such a time of need.”

Several Fae burst into laughter. A couple looked as displeased as Alora, and most- including Velidan- gave no indication of their thoughts.

Embriel spread both hands wide. “Consider it an experiment in altruism. I offered safety from a nuisance, a few paltry coins, and tonics I could brew with my eyes closed and one hand behind my back.” He sniggered. Then the veneer of humor faltered for a more serious tone. “Those are mortals with tangible needs, my friends, and their needs have been easily met. And in return, they happily shared what they had with me. We sit here bickering about food shortages, about poisoned soil and starving Fae in the Court’s outskirts. Yet this entire meal came from one plot of land worked by two mortals.”

“Food importation, then? Is that the topic at hand?” One of the red-haired women tapped her fork against her teeth. “Opening some kind of barter route?”

“No.” Embriel rose, bracing both palms on the table and voice dropping to a fervent pitch. “We’ve been struggling to rebuild our realm for twenty years. What have we got to show for it? A few utopias,” he glanced towards Velidan, “and a handful of sanctuaries scattered through a broken world. One where half the ground is still rotted and creatures are dying every day. I say it’s time to consider a new world.”

Momentary silence followed the statement before the cavern erupted in cries of outrage, mocking laughter, and slammed fists. But not from everyone; some of them looked intrigued, and a few looked excited.

Once some measure of order fell, Embriel raised one hand. “Hear me out. I don’t hope to sway many of you today, but I ask that you listen. We’ve always viewed humans as insects, but we should be looking at them as a resource. Not just for us, but for the lesser Fae.” He began to pace, voice rising above the snarls and mutterings. “Their livestock and crops are serviceable. Many of their settlements are suited to anything our size or smaller. They have smiths, builders, scribes, weavers, maids, farmers… I could go on for hours. They can couple with quite a few species, and some,” he nodded towards Shay and Sera, who sat wide-eyed, “are clearly thrilled by such pairings.”

“This is madness.” Alora was shaking her head. “Madness!”

I echoed it silently, sitting with white knuckles and a gaping jaw.

Embriel sank back down. “It’s reason, Alora. You all think I’ve been tearing my gruesome way into their realm for fun. But I’ve been educating myself. Planning. This can work.”

Saerith, still rigid, said tersely, “I’m not interested in a Court of human-made dwellings, half-breeds, and sunlight. Is anyone?”

“As if you and your little foul-blood live somewhere nicer now,” Saffine scoffed, rolling her eyes. “And have you ever heard of shade?”

Embriel cleared his throat. “Please, ladies, let me go on. Their land is fertile. At night, it’s safe. Thalessa’s bound to one place, and her saplings can be rooted up easily enough. There’s cropland, caves, mountains, oceans, wetlands; everything you could want. They have drinking water everywhere, when we have lesser Fae dying to protect theirs. Think of how many nymphs, dryads, and sprites long for a true forest! The mortal realm has plenty!”

Sybion was shaking his head, too. “Nevermind all that. It’s madness, as Alora says. Appeal be damned, the Day Fae won’t let an invasion- even a harmonious one- stand. Your grand new Court won’t thrive, it’ll burn. The logistics don’t matter.”

Alora slammed a hand down, rattling the entire table. “Logistics? How can you throw such a word out, when morality is the one you should voice? Mortals fear us, and rightfully so! They’d live in terror! You’d unleash thousands of lesser Fae on the human realm when one rogue mordelain could slaughter a village? When one hungry wraith could leave a dozen infants dead in their cribs? It’s beyond madness!” She shattered her glass against the table, and the room grew deathly silent as its sharp echo died out. “It’s murder! Genocide!”

“We’d protect them. We’d provide for them, as I’ve done with my little farmers. With more than them, too, all through the realm.” Embriel spread his hands palms-up, as if entreating her. “Some will resist, but in time they’ll see reason. Peacefully.”

She barked a laugh. “Reason and peace. Bold words when you’re soaked to the teeth in blood! How many humans are in your Pit right now? How many lesser Fae do you have chained away?”

He met her glare solemnly. “It’s not my Pit, Alora, it’s all of ours. And we wouldn’t need it if we had an actual way to feed the carnivores!”

“Enough!” She roared. “You paint a vision of utopia and rebirth. Your reality would be a realm of slaves, fodder, and broodmares!”

“No. We’re all more than capable of restraint, and we’ll keep the lesser Fae in check.”

She hissed at him.

“And while we speak of blood, let’s speak of the Day Court. The only real deterrent, here. They’re silent, my friends. They don’t care. The blood on my hands is ugly, but it had a purpose behind it.” The fervent light hit his eyes now, and he turned a fierce gleam towards each other Fae. “I killed humans to see if we could. I committed atrocities to see if I’d be struck down. Yet here I stand!”

“A butcher.” Alora spat it at him. “A disgrace to your own strength.”

“A man willing to commit necessary evils for the sake of hope. Dawn, Vel and I were just talking about runekeepers last night! Shall I tell the tale? Garruk found some a few years ago and…” he shook his head, the fervent gleam fading for shadows and sorrow. His voice grew thick. “I found out too late, of course. He’d scattered pieces of them in their temples, torn apart children at their feet. He’d roared at the skies through it all, daring someone to come try and save their beloved, blessed mortals. They could barely fight back, and they died slow. Begging for mercy while they tried in vain to call up magic. Dawn, it was awful, but it truly opened my eyes. The Day Court’s done caring, friends. They won’t get involved.”

Bile crawled its way up my throat. I watched the feigned remorse and wanted, perhaps more than I’d ever wanted anything in my life, to stand and scream that he was a liar. That he’d laughed about all this last night, that his talk of restraint and harmony were an act.

Clearly, Alora wasn’t fooled. “As if you didn’t enjoy every moment of such evil!”

“If it even happened,” Sybion snapped. “I’ll remind you all that the last time mortals were threatened was the defining moment of our lives. One where a Day Fae fell from the heavens to kill a monster that had this realm by the throat.” He glowered at Embriel. “The same will happen again, mark me, and I’ll call you a liar right now. If your creature truly ran wild and tortured runekeepers, he would’ve been struck down!”

“She’s a runekeeper.” Velidan’s voice was soft, but the gravity behind it silenced the room.

Every eye turned towards him. Then, with a weight that nearly crushed the air from my lungs, they all looked at me.
