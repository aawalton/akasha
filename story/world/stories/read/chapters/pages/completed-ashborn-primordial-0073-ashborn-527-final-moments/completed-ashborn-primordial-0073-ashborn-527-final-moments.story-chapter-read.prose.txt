Begin.

Ash prana surged through Demonic Overlord, and his awareness spread out, beyond his body through the pipes and the chains, controlling them as an extension of his own body.

Aspect of the Demon God and Yuma’s Embrace activated almost by habit at this point, and the Third Eye and Clarity fed him the immediate branching paths ahead.

He'd come to learn which paths to avoid with nothing but a quick glance. Which paths would end in death and which had the highest likelihood of success. It wasn’t anything conscious, and certainly not a skill he could put into words, but his tens of thousands of failures had, if nothing else, honed his instinct to a level beyond what was attainable for mere mortals.

The arts were ready a fraction of an instant before the prana of a realm crashed into him, drilling into every cell in his body, threatening to overwhelm his awareness.

Yet as incomprehensible as it was, Vir had long ago ceased fearing it. Now, it was simply normal. Just another part of this impossible process.

He let it cycle through himself and back into the machine, just below the threshold where his healing arts could no longer compensate. His body degraded even then, but he keenly understood exactly how much he could handle before the damage crippled him. Had grown so good at the task  that he now used his body’s degradation rate as a barometer of how successful this run would be.

Aindri's Jungal Beckons opened, and with it came the catalog of life in every realm.

The simplest organisms came first. Single-celled organisms lacking minds and hearts.

He copied them by the trillions and more. Quantities for which he had no numbers. Any fewer, and the rapidly growing levels of prana accumulated by the machine would overwhelm him.

He moved up through the chain. Small aquatic creatures then larger ones. The full spread of flora and fauna across three realms, each one requiring more prana than the last.

Then came the souls.

The rest—the mountains and the valleys, the rivers and the cities, even basic life—had come relatively easily for Vir. Though utterly impossible to know the how and why of everything, Vir was simply making a copy. As accurate a copy as he could manage.

With the aid of the machine and all the prana he could ever need, such things were, while not easy, at least learned through a great deal of effort.

Souls, however, were another thing entirely. For one, each soul transferred spiked the prana surging through his body, pushing him ever closer toward the threshold of what he could handle. The more complex the lifeform, the more prana and Crown Chakra focus their soul required to migrate.

One or two would have been easy. Even a thousand. But there were millions of humans and demons, to say nothing of higher-intelligence beasts, all of which demanded more and more prana.

Before long, he had to ease back on the rate of transference, just to save his body. The margin for error  was small and grew ever smaller as the machine’s accumulation rate accelerated.

The act that had once taken total concentration for just a single soul now happened nearly automatically as Vir learned to leverage the full capabilities of this wondrous machine. Thousands of souls were transferred in a near-instant, and Vir oversaw it all, though occasionally, a soul demanded a sliver more attention.

Most times, his conscious mind wasn’t even aware of the names of the souls he transferred. Only their presence. It was so very much akin to commanding an army. A general did not concern themselves with the individual arts used by their frontline combatants, nor even the movements of entire squads of troops.

They oversaw the flow of the battle. The broadest strokes, and diving too deep often cost them that perspective.

So it was here, but in this case, it was a loss of concentration, which in turn cost him the attempt. But it was fascinating when he transferred the souls of those he knew. Cirayus, Maiya… and of course, Siya.

Her case required a bit more effort. Hers wasn’t a simple transference, but a rehoming in an entirely new body. This, too, was included in the knowledge granted him via Jungal Beckons, though whether it would work as well as the god hoped, Vir could only guess.

The uncountable number of souls needing to be transferred shrank and shrank as Vir’s body crumbled.

But just when he was about to drop one of a thousand juggled balls, it stopped.

Suddenly, there were none left, and the new realm sat complete in his Crown Chakra mindscape.

A single merged reality lacking an Ashen Realm. Lacking the thousands of tears that scarred reality. A sun that rose and set, and clean air for every soul he'd copied, all of them exactly as they were, if not better.

None were more aware of their limits than him, yet even still, he tried to help where he could. Curing the incurable by delivering them healthy bodies. He couldn’t help them all, nor even a significant fraction, but he did everything within the power granted to him.

If only it goes this well…

Vir opened his eyes to find Maiya sitting on the bed with her legs folded under her, watching him. She shuffled up next to him, searching his eyes, and he leaned into her shoulder.

“You did it, didn't you?” she muttered in disbelief. “I’ve never seen you so at peace after one of your sessions.”

“Nothing gets past you,” he said with a chuckle. “It’s only in the mindscape, though. Reality is a different matter entirely. They'll interfere. They've already seen the outcome in some future timeline, and they've been laying the groundwork to prevent it for centuries.” He let out a slow breath. “I still can't quite believe Janak has no plan for that.”

“I don’t see how he could have a plan against an enemy that sees everything,” she said. “Not unless he can see as much as they do. I know how much you've looked up to him, but not even Janak has that ability.”

Vir went quiet for a moment, reveling in the freedom of thought that came with being out of the machine. Once, his mindscape served as a relaxing escape from reality. These days, it seemed like the situation had flipped.

“Ever since Brij, he was always something larger than life to me,” Vir admitted. “And then, when he started communicating through his projections, that impression only grew.” He shook his head. “Now he just looks like a broken old man.”

“I mean, he is,” Maiya said. “If you'd been through what he has for as long as he has, you'd be no different.”

Vir sighed. “I know. It's just...”

“Always tough when reality disappoints.”

“That too. It’s even harder to come to terms with when it means that sacrificing my life might be in vain. I mean, this is it, Mai. I have no other excuses to delay. No reason not to go to Janak and start the ritual right now.”

Maiya went still beside him.

“I know,” she said at last, and when she did, her shoulders were trembling. "You can’t know how hard it’s been not to break down and beg you to find some other way. But this machine… you… it’s something Janak planned for thousands of years. What could we possibly come up with now that’s better? I know all that. But… please. If there is no hope, if his plan fails… please come back to me?"

She turned to stare up into his eyes, though despite her quivering voice, there were no tears in her eyes. Vir suspected she’d already shed all her tears in private, when he was off practicing in his mindscape. “Successor or not, promise me that you’ll do everything you can to survive?”

“You know what that machine does to me, Mai,” Vir said softly, stroking her head. “I’m afraid there won’t be much of me left to save once it’s done.”

“That, I refuse to believe,” Maiya said defiantly. “And you’ll know well before the end whether their interference will cripple your attempt. Don’t sacrifice yourself for something  that is doomed to fail.”

“If it fails, that’s the end of our universe, Mai.”

“Perhaps,” she said. “And perhaps I’m a terrible person for saying this, but it doesn’t have to be the end of us. We’ll fight to the end, whatever that looks like, and then we find another way out. Another universe. Maybe the Imperium one where we got our weapons. We’ll ferry as many humans and demons to the other universe as we can. Then we start over. Think of a better way to survive. Even if it just means hiding from them. At least there’s still hope…”

Vir looked away, frowning as he considered the idea. Then he blew out a long breath. “It's a tempting dream, Mai, though I'm fairly certain the Vimana gates only worked because of Janak.”

“We'll figure that out. We'll have the time to." Her hand found his and squeezed. “So promise me you won't let your sacrifice be in vain. Because… I don't know if I can live on if it is, Vir. I mean that.”

He didn't answer, because he honestly didn’t have one.

When Vir had taken his crown so long ago, he’d stopped living for himself. So much so that the thought felt entirely alien to him. Wrong.

Could he live with himself if Janak’s plan failed?

He supposed that, despite his pessimism, he’d held out some hope. That his lifelong hero had some secret plan all along. That this was all just a ploy to outwit the Fateweavers.

Maybe that was why he was so willing to go along with this plan, despite Janak guaranteeing his death.

No, that wasn’t it either. Not all of it, anyway.

The mindscape had shown him something Janak had never mentioned.

His body lasted longer than it should have. Be it because of all his time training in the depths of the Ash—far more than any of his predecessors—or for some other reason, Aspect of the Demon God and Yuma’s Embrace both held him together past the point where Janak had said it would fail, and the margin, miniscule though it might be, was very much there. He didn't know what that meant. Likely nothing. Likely that perished a half-second later than Janak had predicted.

But it was something. Something that gave him hope. And if he sensed his body unraveling earlier? If he aborted the process before it consumed his body and soul?

How many times had he seen the Panav’s Ultimate Art bring back near-corpses on the very brink of death?

He looked at Maiya's hand over his. Felt how tightly she was holding on.

“You know I can't promise I’ll survive, Mai,” he said sadly. “But I can at least promise I won't throw my life away for nothing. If the process starts to fail, I'll do everything I can to come back to you.”

It wasn’t the promise she’d asked for, but it was all he could give.

She held his gaze for a long moment, searching for something, before nodding.

“That's enough,” she said with a small, sad smile.

He sat with her in silence for a time, but no silence could last forever.

“Say, Vir?” Maiya asked. “Do you remember the day we first met?”

Vir frowned in thought, struggling to remember. “It was at your father’s temple, wasn’t it? Rudvik brought me there for my first sermon.”

“It was,” she said, and you just couldn’t sit still. “You kept fidgeting until Rudvik got fed up and led you outside.”

“As I recall, it was your father who gave him the idea,” Vir said with a small chuckle. “I think he was more embarrassed than anyone. Got an earful that night. I certainly remember that.”

“Those were… good times,” Maiya said, before launching into the day she’d saved Vir from bullies, or that time the trading caravan had come from Saran.

Soon, they’d gone through a dozen memories, and all of them of Brij. Back when they wanted nothing more than to see the world and live grand lives. Or at least, Maiya had, and Vir thought he’d been influenced more than a little by her dreams of grandeur.

They chatted for hours, reveling in the nostalgia, until silence finally overtook them. But this was not the sad, melancholic silence of before. No, it was the comfortable silence that came when everything that had to be said had been. When there was nothing left to say.

Maiya leaned against Vir, her shoulder against his, neither wanting the moment to end. Wishing for some other answer.

But there were no answers out there. No miracles waiting to save them.

Only the heavy footsteps of an ancient god who looked more tired than either of them ever had.

“Come,” Janak said through the doorway. “It is time.”
