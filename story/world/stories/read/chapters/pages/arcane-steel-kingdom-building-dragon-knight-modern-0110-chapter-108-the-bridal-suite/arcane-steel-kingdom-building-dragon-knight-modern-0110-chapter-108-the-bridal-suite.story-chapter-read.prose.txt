The door to my old bedroom hissed shut with a finality that sounded less like a lock engaging and more like the gates of hell slamming behind a sinner. I stared at the wood, trying to process the sheer, terrifying efficiency of my mother’s logistical planning.

It had taken her exactly forty minutes to convert my austere, functional quarters into what could only be described as a romantic ambush.

The room smelled aggressively of rose petals. The soft, practical lighting I preferred had been replaced by floating, magitech candles that cast everything in a warm, flickering glow designed specifically to lower defenses and increase awkwardness. But the most devastating change was the furniture.

The couch was gone. The armchairs were gone. Even the small desk I used for late-night schematics had been banished to the void.

In their place, dominating the center of the room like a conqueror claiming territory, was the bed. It was huge. It was plush. It was draped in silks that probably cost more than a MECH chassis. And it was the only place to sit.

I turned to Nyxia. She stood near the door, her back stiff, her hands clasped tightly in front of her midnight-blue gown. Her face was a perfect mask of calm, but her eyes were darting around the room as if searching for a hidden exit or perhaps a large hole to fall into.

“Well,” I said, rubbing the back of my neck. “She works fast.”

Nyxia cleared her throat. “Your mother is… thorough.”

“She’s vindictive,” I corrected. “She took the couch, Nyxia. Who takes a couch? That was a structural element of the room.”

I sighed, walking over to the window. The simulated night sky of New Wighthelm glittered outside, a peaceful lie. “Look, I can sleep on the floor. The rug looks thick enough.”

I turned back to face her, expecting relief. Instead, Nyxia was looking at me with an expression I couldn't quite decipher. Her cheeks were faintly pink, and her crimson eyes held a strange, flickering light.

She took a small step forward. “My Lord… Alarion. That will not be necessary.”

I blinked. “It won’t?”

“We are married,” she said, her voice steady but soft. She gestured vaguely at the expanse of silk and pillows. “The bed is… substantial. And separate rooms are for the engaged. As your mother pointed out, we have passed that stage.”

She looked down, smoothing her skirt, then looked back up through her lashes. “I have no issue sharing it with you. I trust you.”

The words hung in the air. I trust you.

My brain, usually a supercomputer of tactical analysis, processed this information and came to the only logical conclusion available to a man who had spent the last four years talking exclusively to robots and dragons.

Ah. She is being pragmatic. Conserving resources. Maximizing rest efficiency. A sound tactical decision.

“Right,” I said, nodding firmly. “Good point. Sleep is critical for cognitive function. We have a war to run in the morning.”

I walked over to the bed, kicked off my boots, and sat on the edge. “I’ll take the left side. I tend to sleep like a rock, so I shouldn’t disturb your REM cycle.”

Nyxia stared at me. Her mouth opened slightly, then closed. A flash of something—frustration? Disbelief?—crossed her face, but she quickly masked it.

“Of course,” she said, her voice a little flatter than before. “Tactical efficiency.”

She moved to the other side of the bed. We lay down in the dark, separated by a vast, silk-covered demilitarized zone. I stared up at the ceiling, listening to her breathing even out.

Tes, set an alarm for 0500 hours.

[Alarm set. Good night, Master.]

I closed my eyes, content. Marriage was surprisingly manageable when you approached it with logic.

…

I woke up because my left arm had lost all feeling.

It was the specific, pins-and-needles deadness of circulation being cut off by a heavy weight. I blinked my eyes open, the morning light of the simulation filtering through the window.

I tried to move my arm. I couldn't.

I looked down.

Nyxia Black, the Ice Queen, was currently using my bicep as a pillow. She had migrated across the DMZ during the night, curling into my side. Her head rested on my shoulder, her dark hair spilling across my chest like ink. One of her hands was resting flat over my heart. She looked… peaceful. Softer than I had ever seen her.

Panic flared in my chest. If I move, she wakes up. If she wakes up, she kills me.

But the problem wasn't just my left arm. There was a weight on my right side, too.

I turned my head slowly.

Curled up against my ribs, drooling slightly on my shirt, was Lyra. She was fast asleep, her silver hair a messy halo, clutching her Reaper plushy.

And on my chest, a heavy, warm lump was rising and falling with a deep, cosmic purr. Kaelus.

What the hell?

I stared at the ceiling. My mother had banished the dragon. She had fortified the door. She had practically put up a "Do Not Disturb" sign in neon manuscript. And yet, here I was, buried under a pile of sleeping family members like a piece of furniture.

My mother thought she could contain the Master of Space. But she had forgotten that his jailor was also his accomplice.

Nyxia stirred. She murmured something soft and shifted, her hand tightening on my shirt. Her eyes fluttered open.

For a second, she looked confused. Then her gaze focused on my face, mere inches from hers. She realized where her head was. She realized where her hand was.

Her eyes went wide. The blush started at her neck and raced up to her hairline in record time. She scrambled backward, nearly falling off the bed.

“I—I didn’t—!” she stammered, pulling the sheet up to her chin. “I stay on my side! I always stay on my side!”

“It’s fine,” I whispered frantically, trying not to wake the others. “Strategic redeployment. It happens.”

Before she could respond, Lyra let out a loud snore and rolled over, flinging an arm across Nyxia’s legs.

Nyxia froze. She looked down at the little girl. She looked at the dragon on my chest.

“How?” she whispered. “The door was locked. Your mother put wards on it.”

Kaelus cracked one eye open. Wards are for people who can’t fold space, he projected smugly. Also, she was lonely.

Lyra woke up. She rubbed her eyes, looked at Nyxia, and beamed.

“Good morning, sister!” she chirped. Then her face grew serious. She put a finger to her lips. “Shhh. Don’t tell Mama. We did a jailbreak.”

She turned the full force of her puppy-dog eyes on Nyxia. Kaelus, sensing the play, added his own cosmic, starlit gaze to the mix.

It was a double-barrel blast of pure cuteness. Nyxia didn't stand a chance.

Her shoulders slumped. The tension drained out of her. A small, defeated smile touched her lips.

“Fine,” she sighed. “But next time… maybe give me a heads up? I’m not used to… crowded beds.”

Deal! Kaelus chirped.

The air shimmered. Space warped. And with a soft pop, the dragon and the girl vanished, teleporting back to their designated containment zone before the warden could catch them.

I sat up, rubbing my numb arm. Nyxia watched me, her face still pink but her eyes thoughtful.

“You have a strange family, Alarion,” she said softly.

“Yeah,” I said, swinging my legs out of bed. “I do.”

I looked at the clock. 0500.

“I have to go,” I said, the warmth of the morning fading as the cold reality of the day rushed back in. “The bread drop starts in six hours. I need to clear my head.”

…

I didn't go to the workshop. I needed air. Real air, or as close as the simulation could provide.

I walked out to the edge of the pocket dimension, where the rolling green hills met a sheer cliff that overlooked a sea of clouds. But I wasn't heading for the view. I was heading for the grove of ancient oaks that stood vigil on a nearby rise.

In the center of the grove stood a single, massive stone pillar. It wasn't ornate. It wasn't magical. It was a simple, brutal slab of granite, four meters tall, its surface covered in hundreds of names carved deep into the rock.

The Memorial.

This wasn't for the main branch of House Wight. Their names were recorded in the family crypts. This stone was for the others. The branch families. The cousins, the uncles, the distant kin who had guarded the borders, manned the watchtowers, and held the line when the world burned.

Ten branch families. Ten fortresses. All gone. Slaughtered to the last man, woman, and child by the Hegemony’s surprise attack.

I walked toward the pillar, intending to pay my respects before the chaos of the day began. But I wasn't alone.

A figure stood at the base of the stone, his hand resting on the rough surface. He wasn't wearing his ducal finery. He wore a simple, worn leather jacket and trousers, the clothes of a soldier off duty. His shoulders, usually so broad and unyielding, were slumped.

My father.

I stopped, not wanting to intrude. But he sensed me. He didn't turn around. He just raised a hand, beckoning me forward.

“Come here, son.”

I walked up beside him. He was touching a specific name, his fingers tracing the letters as if trying to feel the person they represented.

ORIAN WIGHT

I knew the name. My uncle. My father’s younger brother. But he was a ghost to me, a figure from stories I barely remembered.

“Do you remember him?” my father asked, his voice rough.

“No,” I admitted quietly. “I was too young.”

“Tes,” I thought to myself. “Show me.”

[Accessing memory archives. Date: 2003. Location: Wighthelm Nursery.]

A video file played in my mind. I saw my infant self, being held by a man who looked like a younger, rougher version of my father. He had a scar running down his cheek and a laugh that shook the room. He was holding me up, making baby noises, his eyes shining with warmth.

[Subject: Orian Wight. Duration of contact: 7 hours continuous holding. Subject refused to put the user down, citing ‘uncle bonding time.’]

I felt a pang in my chest. Seven hours. I had slept through it, treating him like furniture while he held me with a love I hadn't even registered.

“He was a good man,” my father said, his voice thick. “A Dragon Knight. He commanded the Iron-Pass Fort, the first line of defense against Cinderfall. When the attack came… he didn't run. He didn't call for aid. He held.”

My father’s hand clenched into a fist against the stone.

“He held for six hours against a force that outnumbered him fifty to one. He bought us time. He bought the kingdom time to wake up.”

A single tear tracked down my father’s cheek, getting lost in his beard.

“I received a letter from him,” he whispered. “Two days before the attack. His wife… she had just given birth. A daughter. They named her Ela, after your grandmother.”

He looked at me, his stormy grey eyes burning with a pain that time hadn't healed.

“They killed them all, Alarion. The soldiers. The servants. The mother is holding her newborn. They didn't leave a single heartbeat in that fort. It wasn't war. It was extermination.”

The rage in his voice was terrifying. It was the rage of a dragon, deep and hot and destructive.

He turned to me, placing a heavy hand on my shoulder. His grip was tight, almost painful.

“I tell you this not to fuel your hate,” he said, his voice intense. “But to warn you. Hate is easy. Rage is easy. It burns hot, and it gets things done. I look at you, son, and I see the fire in your eyes. I see the weapons you build. The way you erased those armies.”

He leaned closer.

“But if you walk the path of blind rage… if you let the vengeance consume you… You will lose what is left. You will become just another tyrant on a throne of ash. Orian didn't die so we could become monsters. He died to protect what we love.”

He released my shoulder, stepping back. He looked tired. So incredibly tired.

“Be a king, Alarion. Be a warlord if you must. But never forget that your first duty is not to punish the guilty. It is to protect the innocent. Don't let the fire burn away your heart.”

He turned and walked away, back toward the castle, leaving me alone with the stone and the names of the dead.

I stood there for a long time, the wind tugging at my uniform. I traced the name Orian with my own finger. I thought of a baby named Ela, who never saw the sun rise on her third day.

I felt the cold, hard resolve in my chest. The Icarus missiles. The MECHs. The fleet. They were weapons of vengeance, yes. But my father was right.

Today, we weren't dropping fire. We were dropping bread.

I turned away from the memorial, my stride lengthening as I headed back to the ship. My heart was hardened for the war ahead, but my eyes were clear. I would not be blind.

I would burn the world if I had to. But I would make sure something was left to grow from the ashes.

“Tes,” I said, my voice steady. “Status on the fleet.”

[Fleet is in position, Master. Drop zones acquired. The Operation is ready to commence.]

“Good,” I said. “Let’s go feed a kingdom.”
