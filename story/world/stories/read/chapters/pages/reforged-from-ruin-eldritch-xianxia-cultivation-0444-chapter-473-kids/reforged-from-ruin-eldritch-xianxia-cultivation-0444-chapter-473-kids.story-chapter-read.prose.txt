Mortal affairs are often considered the least of a cultivator’s worries, and that they should remain that way. Money, property, love, family, all of these things are ephemeral, transitory, and capable of transforming in an instant. A thousand changes might occur every other second, and to spend time keeping track of them all is seen as a tremendous waste.

This is, to me, tremendously stupid.

A mortal can cultivate a farm, a family, a fortune, in a scant few decades. Perhaps the simplistic, the untalented, or the unfocused might have to focus on one task to the exclusion of all others. Perhaps they might work on two, at most. A Dao, and one’s cultivation, though more often, just the latter. But oh, the effort that nearly every mortal in all of existence has performed for all of history, no, that is too much, that is too alien and useless a thing.

And yet, the hobbies some of the fools pursue! Games of chance, academic learnings, beast taming, pursuing a bloodline, exploring a few thousand miles of wilderness. Learning new weapons, new ways of killing, medicine, painting, cooking, sculpting, any and every other form of art. But somehow, these aren’t wastes! These are steps on the path of comprehension! Tools with which to explore ascension! Worthy pursuits for worthy immortals!

It’s like a bad joke. An excuse to pursue whatever superficial interests the weak might have in place of true passion, true growth, true and challenging Change. A family? Too much work. Mastering a thousand different paint-strokes to craft a thousand different paintings of colored water on canvases? Worth every possible effort. Worth a thousand years of practice! But to try and make a fortune off that work? Childish, stupid, too unpredictable to control!

Idiots and fools see the world in shades of mortality and immortality. Good and bad. As if yin and yang are only two shades, and not dappled with each other. I do my best not to suffer fools.

–Words of a traveling drunk, spoken during a sting of sobriety lasting six years, at the end of which several thousand well-known cultivators were found very thoroughly dead.

It feels like not enough time.

Two weeks from the start of the construction to its end. Two weeks since she first heard of their arrival, since she first broke the surface and began to dig and plan, two weeks of preparing food for her neighbors and accepting their grace and interacting with more people than she normally would choose to.

And yet, it feels like not enough time.

But it’s done.

She stares at the construction, feeling the heat of the setting sun paint her shoulders and soothe her scars.

It’s a three story building, at least on the surface. At its height, the building reaches the level of her cabin atop the hill, in spite of the fact that she’s built it halfway down the hill’s back. At that highest point, she’s made something like a courtyard, exposed to the sky above. She’s designed it to be something like a patio, with seating, some ferns arranged about its sides, but with a carefully crafted circle in the center, ripe for cultivation.

Beneath it, the second floor is where she’s made their living arrangements. Four separate rooms, each of them stylized to meet her understanding of her four guests. One of them, of course, more so than the others. She’s carved obsidian, made pale white walls, grown black flowers to mix with the reds and living greens, even broken her self-imposed rules to use her wider powers to craft soft down and pillows for him.

Beneath that, the first floor holds a simple living assortment. A kitchen, with space for tea and soup, rice and simple meats. A pantry, stocked with all that’s left of the supplies she’s hoarded or created. A seating arrangement, filled with a few windows that face out into the sky and the fields.

And the basement. Designed for stranger things, as will most likely be required by her disciples. She’s not sure what projects they’re working on exactly– even from a master, one deserves privacy, but she’s pretty damn certain that, seeing as they are her disciples, they’re going to need some kind of space to work that’s properly insulated from the world.

It’s not magical. Not yet. She can make it so, but… maybe it’ll be better to have them do it. Disciples should be given room to grow, and she is, frankly, overbearing enough as it is without giving herself new opportunities.

It doesn’t matter. It’s done.

A basement, a living and common area, bedrooms, and an upper floor open to the sky. Walls of clay, lain over stone and wood, all of it harvested from the very same land she’s spent the last few years living on, getting to know.

It’s ready.

She’s ready.

The last few hours have been a celebration.

It’s not every day that a new house gets built in West Laketown, and as far as she’s aware, they’ve never built a house like this. The town hall is only barely larger, and certainly not carved and sculpted out of a hillside. And yet, no one really seems bitter or uncertain. She’s worked beside them, after years among them, and they built this place together. She’s cooked for them and walked alongside them for every beam placed down and every chunk of earth dug up, and that matters enough that the worries she had about hurting them with this construction have dwindled.

It’s still a bit disrespectful, yeah. But it’s her kid coming. She’s made no secret of that, and she’s pretty sure that buys her some goodwill too.

People eat and drink and make merry. Fires are set up for roasting and to sit around, and Raika goes all out in making sure that every piece of food that is made on-site is as stunning as it can be, and that every bit of food brought in is swiftly shared to those who most want or need it.

It takes about six hours before she finally thinks it’s time.

So, she stands up and walks over to the thing she brought from across the world, out of a special reserve crafted from out of materials kept very carefully on the verge of transcending mortal realms. She stands, up and in front of the large tarp she’s covered it with.

“People of West Laketown!” she says, her voice easily carrying over the hillside to the hundred people all milling about.

“I am a good chef!”

There are some good-natured jeers and cheers to that.

“I also am a terrible chef!”

A bit less noise, then, mostly out of confusion. Some reassuring shouts, a few calls of condolences.

“But! Together, that makes me uniquely able to make good things terrible! And terrible things good! And there is nothing more good and more terrible than booze!”

This time, the cheer is thunderous, echoing over the hills.

“So here! To everyone who has helped me! For everyone who has been a part of building a home for my son! For everyone that has helped me, and who I have helped, let us celebrate in style!”

The tarp is torn away, flying high up into the night sky above, revealing a dozen barrels, each of them large enough to hold a body in them.

Even if she wanted to, there’s no stopping the rush that comes next.

If there’s one thing that fishermen and farmers know how to do, it’s drink and eat meat around the fire.

The party lasts well into the night. The drink is widely accepted and enjoyed, her own personal concoction of fermented fruit, yeast, and honey, made from her own creations. It’s unique in all the world, and while it’s not exactly strong enough to knock someone on their ass, it’s more than strong enough to encourage a good time.

Eventually, though, good times end. The peace that comes after, as people slowly trickle out of a celebration that lasts for hours and hours, is as loud as the singing and cheering ever was.

And as it ends, she sits, beneath the night sky, beneath stars that watch her at every possible waking moment. She sits, and she drinks from a cup full of a drink with no name, and stares at the building made with her own hand and the hands of those around her.

It’s pretty lovely.

La Gao stays back, after most of the others have left.

“Gonna be alright?” he asks.

“Yeah. Just thinking about tomorrow.”

“He’ll be happy to see you.”

“I know.”

He shrugs. “Ain’t never been a mom, but I’ve been a dad twice, and I’ve heard that there’s some overlap. Kids are tough. Kids are complicated. They’re scary.”

She turns to look at him, then. Listening.

“Could be the best kid in the world or the worst piece of shit to ever pop out of someone’s loins, it don’t matter. You love them anyways. Could grow up ten times taller than you, ten times stronger, and you still can’t think of anything but the fact that they might get hurt, and how it’s your responsibility to keep them safe. Could have a hundred of them, and every fucking one of them will matter just as much as the first. Kids are fucking terrifying. Nothing in the world scarier.”

She nods. Takes another sip, and turns to look at the home she’s built.

They sit in silence together for a while. He takes a sip from his own cup, much emptier than hers.

“I’ve seen some pretty scary shit,” Raika tells the fisherman. “Some really, really scary shit.”

“Scarier?”

She snorts.

He grunts in return, taking one last chug from his cup and then putting it to one side.

“Yeah.”

“Yeah.”

He slaps her on the shoulder, getting up, stretching with a groan, and letting out a big old sigh.

“Welp. About time I headed out. Early morning tomorrow. Gotta be up with the sun, or the fish’ll get wise to my tricks. Gotta catch them when they’re still groggy.”

She laughs at that. “Good talk, Gao.”

“Good talk.”

And then his steps are carrying him away, and the night is growing quiet.

She can hear them, all across the hills, all over the lake. The water carries the sounds of East and South Laketown to her just as clearly as West Laketown’s proximity does. The wilds sing of birds and rabbits and hounds and foxes, of snakes and deer and hunting cats and lumbering bears.

If she listens closely enough, opens up enough of her organs, flowers tendrils of sensoria out the sides of her skull, she can hear the moons shifting in the sky. She can hear that distant thing of shapes and eternity, floating above the world and beneath the stars, a cork on the threshold of the world.

If she really strains herself, she can hear the sound of the stars, and the eyes in them, blinking and crying and laughing. No words, but the sound of motion, slick and burning and alien in more ways than the obvious.

And then she closes herself back up, and takes another sip of her tasty little ale, and looks at the house she has built for her son and those who rely on her.

It’s done. And she’s ready.

She tells herself what she already knows.

Thousands of miles away, she opens a door in herself, and then another, and a third, and a fourth, right before the home she has made.

And she waits, as four very different people travel a very long way to her.

She timed it perfectly. The sun is just coming up over the horizon by the time they arrive. Even with her Daos of Space and Flesh, distance still matters, and even if it didn’t the Wall is still a bitch and a half to deal with, even with an anchor planted on each side of it. Beyond that, there’s the need for stealth. She’s not sure exactly how much the Emperor sees within the bounds of its domain, and maybe it sees everything, but there’s a difference between seeing and noticing. The arrays, Daos, esoteric techniques and conceptual powers she levies against its perception are the strongest she can generate, and they only get stronger the more she practices.

Not that she thinks that it even cares. If her theories about it are right, then there’s nothing she could do besides kill herself or explode that it would try to interfere with.

But that doesn’t mean she can afford to be careless, and that does mean that things take time.

She sits outside all night long, waiting.

Her skin is just starting to warm, the horizons of twisting snakes only just coming back together, when they arrive.

Out of the hillside, out of flesh and magic and stranger things, hidden in the skin of the world and just under it, a door is made.

Out of that door come four different people, timed to arrive at the same moment.

The oldest of them steps forward first, actively overtaking the rest of them to arrive an inch sooner than the rest. He towers over the next tallest of them by almost half a foot, his muscles well defined and showing through bright red and orange robes that are a bit too small for him. Black hair, bright red-gold eyes that match hers, and an arm wrapped tightly in a shell of bone that mirror an arm of flesh and bone almost perfectly, save for its color. In spite of the relative quiet, his presence rumbles in the world, an ever-increasing sound of force and light emanating from him, colored in by a bone-depth presence and hints of wavy dream-logic wafting off his head.

He bows, deeply and at the waist, still standing in front of the others. “Greetings, master.”

“Geng He. It’s good to see you. Please get out of the way of your fellow disciples.”

He steps aside, seeming completely unchastised, but willing to obey. The next one to step up is almost as eager as he was, though.

She smiles, her teeth broad and distinctly not-human. Each one is a fang, and her mouth opens wide enough to make her face distend awkwardly, like something pretending, poorly, at being a human being. The second-tallest of the bunch, she’s wearing thick furs, at least one of them still bloody, wrapped around dark-green skin freckled with tanned browns, with a bit too much skin showing to be proper, but enough covered to not be inappropriate.

“Hello master! I am here! It is so good to see you and I am so excited to see you and it’s nice to be here and I’m very hungry! That was a long long walk, are we–”

“Hello Chen Tai. There is food for you, after you let everyone else introduce themselves. Can you wait for a few moments?”

“Ok! I will, that’s fine, since there’s things to eat, and–”

“Thank you Chen Tai. Wei Zin. It’s good to see you.”

The slender, almost androgynous figure bows a bit more politely and a bit less dramatically than Geng He, the many-colored grasses his robes are woven from catching the light beautifully as he does. He’s still beautiful, almost strangely so, with dark skin and bright blue hair that’s grown more and more vine-like since she first met him.

“It is good to see you as well, master. It has been some time since we have last properly spent time together, and I admit that I have missed your instruction.”

Polite and political. A slightly annoying way to poke her with just how long she’s neglected her so-called student. She smiles, nodding her head.

“There’ll be time for that. This much, I promise you. For now, for all of you, I have nothing but time and focus.”

He bows again, more lightly, and steps aside, letting the final of the four disciples step forward.

She keeps her face calm, her eyes steady, her heartbeat quiet. She does not, in fact, leap up out of her seat and pick him up and twirl him around. She waits, so fucking patiently, as he steps forward and bows, as polite and restrained as ever.

He smells of whispers and smoke. He smells of the moment that one closes a door on a room that will never be entered again. He’s the soft breeze on lilies beside the headstone.

He smells like noodles and tea and Li Shu’s medicines and orchids.

He looks like her son.

Rai Jin steps out from the portal, letting it close behind him, and smiles at her.

“Hello, master.”

She lets her cane fall as she rises up off of the stone she was sitting on, takes two long steps forward, and wraps him up in a hug so tight that she can feel the air squeak out of his lungs, even with all his newfound strength.

And even with the fact that he’s grown nearly half his height again, that he’s got longer hair and brighter eyes and more muscle and more fat than when he was so skinny and so starved… even though he looks clean and his robes are so lovely and his presence in reality pulls meaning into his movements and being, telling the world of his worth, of his strength, even with all of that, he hugs her back immediately.

“Hello,” she whispers, as she holds him so so tight.
