The weight on Aiven’s chest shifted.

The blonde girl pushed herself up from where she had landed on him, her palms leaving the dirt as she rocked back onto her knees. Her hood lay crumpled around her shoulders, and the morning light caught the loose strands of honey-blonde hair that had fallen across her face.

Aiven sat up immediately.

His obsidian arm clicked against his ribs as he braced himself, and for a long moment, he did nothing but stare. The grey of the dome had faded. The pillar of white light was gone. The thunder in his chest had quieted into something soft and warm, like a heartbeat that had finally remembered its rhythm.

She was kneeling right there, in front of him.

His mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

“Lyra?” he whispered. And then, louder, the word cracking down the middle, “Lyra. Are you... are you really...”

He couldn’t finish.

“Is it really you?”

The girl smiled. It was small and tired, the kind of smile that had been waiting a long time to come back to a face. Her deep blue eyes glistened, and as she nodded, a single tear slipped loose and tracked a clean line down through the dust on her cheek.

“Yes,” she said softly. “It’s me.”

Aiven moved before he thought.

His real hand rose to her cheek, then his obsidian one followed, hesitant, the cold metal of the Armvil hovering just shy of her skin before he let it settle there too. He felt warmth under his palm. The damp of the tear. The faint tremble of a jaw working hard not to break.

She was real.

She was warm.

She was here.

He pulled her in.

His arms folded around her shoulders, the obsidian of the Armvil tucking awkwardly behind her back as he pressed his face into the crook of her neck. The scarf she wore smelled faintly of pine and ash, not the sea-salt he remembered, but it didn’t matter. Nothing else mattered.

“You’re okay,” he breathed. “Oh, gods, you’re okay. I thought... I thought I’d...”

His voice gave out.

Lyra’s arms came up around him, slower, careful. As if she were afraid he might shatter in her grasp. Her fingers curled into the fabric of his coat, and she held on the way someone holds on to a thing they had given up the right to want.

“I’m here,” she whispered into his shoulder. “I’m sorry it took so long.”

Virelle stood a few paces back, perfectly still.

Her hands, which had been wreathed in lethal lavender fire only seconds ago, hung limp at her sides. The flames had guttered out without her noticing. Her prismatic orb drifted by her shoulder, pulsing in a slow, uncertain rhythm, as if it too did not quite know what to do with the scene in front of it.

Her chest felt strange.

Not painful. Not exactly. It was a tight, hollow thing, like a cup that had been emptied without anyone telling it the drink was over. She placed a hand against her sternum, frowning down at her own fingers, as though the answer might be written somewhere on her own skin.

Normally, she would have already moved.

Normally, she would have torn through the air with a flare of indignant lavender, peeled this strange, sniveling girl off her Master, and dangled her by the back of her robe while suggesting fifteen creative ways a body could be reduced to constituent atoms. There would have been hissing about hussies. There would have been threats involving lemon juice and bone marrow. There would have been, at the very least, an arched brow and a sniff of theatrical disgust.

She did none of those things.

Because this girl was different.

She had felt it the moment the hooded figure had crashed through the dome. The hands that pressed against Aiven’s chest had carried a magic Virelle herself could not unweave. The lullaby at his ear had calmed a storm that even she had been failing to hold back. And the brown scarf at the girl’s shoulder was the same scarf that had been waving in the wreckage of her own broken memories, in the white light, in the marble that broke.

This girl was the one who had kept him alive in the thicket.

This girl was the one her Master cried for, in his sleep, when he thought no one could hear.

This girl was the reason her Master had ever walked into a dungeon at all.

Virelle’s throat closed around a sound she could not produce.

She lifted her gaze upward, away from the two of them, away from the way Aiven’s shoulders had finally relaxed for the first time in weeks. The morning sky was a soft, washed-out blue, threaded with the pale gold of new sunlight. A few lazy clouds drifted across it, indifferent, far above any of this.

She watched them for a long time.

Will I lose this, too? she thought, losing the only place I have ever been allowed to belong.

She did not have an answer.

She was not sure she wanted one.

The shimmering wall of the containment dome dropped with a soft hiss.

“S-Sir Aiven!”

Pelka came scrambling across the clearing, clipboard clutched against her chest, her heavy bangs flopping over her glasses with every panicked step. She skidded to a halt a few feet from the embracing pair, looked once at them, looked once at Virelle, looked once at the scorch marks where the Loom-Breaker had shattered, and then made a small, strangled noise that wasn’t quite a word.

She bowed at the waist.

“I am so terribly sorry to interrupt,” she said in a rapid, breathless rush, “but, ah, with respect, I, um. Who is this person, and, also, did she just reseal Sir Aiven’s mana?”

She straightened, then immediately bowed again.

“B-because the readings on my Aetheric meter just dropped from ‘active catastrophe’ to ‘ambient resting baseline’ in approximately two seconds, which is, ah, technically impossible, and I would very much like to know what just happened so that my report is not entirely composed of question marks.”

She paused.

“Please.”

Lyra let out a small, breathy laugh against Aiven’s shoulder. It was the same laugh he remembered from the back of her parents’ shop. She drew back just enough to look at him, brushing a strand of hair behind her ear, the tear-tracks still drying on her face.

“I suppose,” she said, soft and a little rueful, “I’ve got some explaining to do.”

The meeting room of the ship was small and round, ringed with brass-rimmed portholes that looked out onto the slow churn of clouds below.

A polished oak table sat in the center, its surface inlaid with a faint spiral of copper filigree that caught the lantern light. Pelka had set out four cups of something hot. Vane stood by the door, arms folded, his lion’s mane catching the orange glow of the wall sconces, silent as a pillar.

Aiven sat with his hands wrapped around his cup, not drinking. Across from him sat Lyra, hood pushed back fully now, the brown scarf folded neatly in her lap.

Virelle sat beside him.

She had not spoken since the dome.

Lyra drew in a breath.

“I don’t have a lot of time,” she said, looking around the table. Her voice had a careful quietness to it, the way someone speaks when they’ve rehearsed the same words many times. “So I’m going to try to be as clear as I can.”

Her eyes settled on Aiven.

“I know you have a hundred questions. I can see them all on your face. But please... let me say my piece first. It will save us all time.”

Aiven swallowed. He nodded.

He glanced sideways.

Virelle was sitting with both hands folded in her lap, her prismatic orb resting unusually still on the table beside her cup. She was not floating. She was not hovering. Her feet were flat on the floor, and her back was straight in a way that looked rehearsed rather than regal.

She was looking at the copper spiral on the table, not at Lyra, not at him.

It struck Aiven as wrong, somehow. The Virelle he knew would already have made at least three pointed remarks about the seating arrangement. She would have demanded to know why a strange woman was sitting across from her Master without her express written permission. She would have, at minimum, sniffed.

She had not sniffed.

He almost said something. He almost reached for her hand.

But she had a stillness about her that he didn’t want to crack open here, in front of strangers. Whatever it was, it was hers. She would tell him when she was ready. Or she wouldn’t, and he’d have to work for it. Either way, this wasn’t the moment.

He turned back to Lyra.

“First,” Lyra began, “the seal. The one I just placed on you again, a few minutes ago.”

She set her hands flat on the table.

“It isn’t a seal. Not really. I regulated it.”

Aiven blinked. “Regulated?”

“Your mana pool is... very large,” Lyra said, with the careful understatement of someone who could have used much harsher words. “Larger than a body your size is meant to hold. If you opened the floodgates all at once, it wouldn’t just hurt you. It would tear you apart from the inside, and probably take a few city blocks with you.”

She paused, choosing the next words.

“What I did was put a valve on it. A throttle. So that you could draw on your own power without it drowning you. The Loom-Breaker shattered the throttle, and that’s what was killing you back there. I just... put a new one in.”

Her eyes met his, steady and serious.

“You can use your mana, Aiven. You just have to learn to keep the valve in good repair, and to open it carefully. If you neglect it, or if you try to force too much through too fast, you’ll lose control again, and next time I might not be close enough to catch you.”

For a long moment, no one spoke.

Then, quietly, from beside him, Virelle opened her mouth.

“Then why,” she said, and her voice was not the bombastic silk it usually was; it was lower, flatter, almost careful, “why did you seal his powers in the first place?”

She did not look up from the copper spiral.

“If you could regulate the flow, then regulate it. Why hide it?”

Her fingers tightened in her lap.

“Why give him a wall, when you could have given him a door?”

Aiven turned toward her so fast his shoulder twinged.

“Virelle, wait,” he said. His voice came out unsteady. “What are you... what do you mean, the first time? This is... this is the first time I’m seeing Lyra. Since Hearthport. Since the...”

He couldn’t say the word.

His grey-brown eyes searched her profile.

“Why are you talking like the two of you have already met?”

Virelle’s mouth opened to answer.

It was Lyra who spoke first.

“Back in the thickets near Greenhollow,” she said gently, “when you lost your arm. Your power went out of control. The seal cracked, and your body started to burn from the inside.”

She held his gaze.

“I was the one who came. I put the regulator back in, and then I left before you woke up.”

The room seemed to tilt slightly.

Aiven’s memory snapped backward of its own accord. The clearing. The four-armed Kobold, its claws dripping. His own arm on the wet ground. The world greying at the edges. And then the impossible white light, the heat under his skin, the feeling of his veins boiling. He had been certain, in that moment, that he was going to die.

And then the heat had stopped.

Just stopped. Like a candle pinched between two fingers.

He had thought, in the days afterward, that it had been Virelle. He had even thanked her for it.

She had not corrected him.

Not once.

He turned back to her.

“Virelle.”

His voice was quiet now. Not angry. Just... tired in a way that ran very deep.

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

Virelle drew in a breath.

“I—”

“It was me,” Lyra said. “I asked her not to tell you.”

Aiven’s head turned, slow, as if it weighed too much.

Lyra met him without flinching, though her hands had begun to twist together in her lap.

“After the thicket, I asked her to keep it from you. Because I knew that if you found out I was alive, you would not stop. You would tear up every island in the sky looking for me. And at the time, where I was... where I had to be... was not somewhere you could afford to find me.”

She paused.

“I was with Lucien.”

“What.”

The word came out flat. Not a question, exactly. More like a placeholder for the avalanche of questions queuing up behind it.

Aiven set his cup down. His obsidian hand made a soft click against the wood. He pressed his other hand against his temple, the old, familiar gesture, the one he made whenever the world was producing more information than his clerk-brain knew how to file.

“With Lucien,” he repeated. “You were with Lucien. The man who, ah. Who told me three days ago that he’s going to remake the world. That Lucien.”

“Yes.”

“And you didn’t die at Hearthport.”

“No.”

“And you can put seals on people. Or regulators. Whatever they are.”

“Yes, though I may not be able to open the ones I have sealed in a certain way.”

He laughed once, short and humorless.

“Lyra, there are... there are so many things in this conversation that I don’t understand, I don’t even know which one to start with.”

Lyra reached across the table.

She did not take his hand. She just rested her own near it, palm up, an offering he could pick up if he needed.

“I know,” she said. “You want to know how I’m alive. You want to know what I have to do with Lucien. You want to know how I learned to weave a regulator on someone like you. You want to know why I let you think I was dead for so long.”

Her voice softened.

“I promise you, all of those answers exist. And in time, you will have every one of them. But it’s a long story, Aiven, and the parts that matter most aren’t mine alone to tell. Things will become clearer. They always do. But not now.”

She drew her hand back.

“What I came here to tell you is this.”

Her eyes hardened. Not unkindly. But the soft, tearful girl from the dome was, for a moment, harder to find inside the woman across the table.

“Lucien has come to you in person. He has made you an offer. You must refuse it.”

Aiven opened his mouth.

“I know,” Lyra said, before he could speak. “You were already going to. I can see it on you. But I need you to understand what refusing him means.”

She leaned forward.

“It means you fight him. Not someday. Soon. And to stand any chance against him at all, you will need every drop of the power you’ve been carrying. Which means you need the throttle, and you need to learn to open it properly, and you need to do both quickly.”

She drew in a breath.

“I tried to keep you out of this. I did not want you in this game, Aiven. I never wanted you in this game.”

Her voice cracked, just slightly.

“But Lucien came to you. He spoke your name. He showed you his face. The moment he did that, you stopped being a bystander. You became a piece on his board, whether you wanted to be or not. And there is no walking away from that. Not anymore.”

She looked at him, and the tear that gathered at the corner of her eye this time did not fall.

“My effort to keep you out of it... has failed.”

“I’m sorry.”

The lantern guttered once in its sconce.

Outside the porthole, very far below, a thin band of cloud drifted past the airship’s hull, soft and indifferent as breath.

Aiven did not say anything for a long time.

Beside him, Virelle had still not looked up from the copper spiral on the table.
