Chapter 69

“What the chamber tests, it tests by being itself. The instrument does not change its tuning for the candidate. The candidate changes, or the candidate does not leave.” -  Codex of the Long Watch, On the Threshold

The staircase from L2 cut into the limestone at a steeper angle than the others. The treads had been worn into shallow cups by feet that had stopped coming down them four thousand years ago. Arthur counted twenty-three before the light from the chamber above stopped reaching him.

Arthur led with his shield up out of habit. Steve was second, the rod across his chest. Sarah came third, Donna brought the rear. The hum that had been steady so far on level 2 resolved into discrete pulses as they reached the floor, the pulses even-spaced and slow.

The L3 chamber opened around them.

Arthur’s first read was that nothing in the room matched the floor plan above. The Hypogeum’s surface footprint was a fixed thing. He had walked the perimeter at the entrance, taken its measure in paces, and the L3 chamber underneath ought to have been a small enclosed room seven metres across at its widest. The room he was standing in was twenty-five at least.

“Bigger inside,” Steve whispered, “Same as the Printworks.”

“Internal volume exceeds external footprint by a factor of approximately four point four,” Archie said. “Standard architectural inversion.”

“Standard for who,” Donna said.

Archie’s geometry did not answer.

* * *

The chamber was roughly circular and the walls were polished smoother than any tool could shape limestone, smoother than water-cut river stone. They threw back the Ether-glow like wet metal. The polish was not even, though. Six broad sectors of the wall were finished to a higher tolerance than the rest, three on the left arc and three on the right, each sector about three metres wide. Inside each sector the limestone was cut into shallow vertical grooves, parallel and even, dozens of them per sector. The grooves caught the light differently from the unworked stone between them.

Steve was already at the nearest sector with his hand on the grooves.

“Wave-guides,” he said. “These are wave-guides. They’ve been cutting them for centuries. The grooves are exactly the depth and spacing the room is tuned for.” He stepped back and walked the chamber slowly, counting. “Six of them. Three on each side. The unworked stone between sectors is just structural.”

“So the room is the bell,” Arthur said.

“The room is the whole instrument.”

At the chamber’s centre sat a circular pit. Five metres across, brimming with water that had an eerie flatness to it Arthur didn’t like. It was a dark mirror that did not respond to their footfalls on the chamber floor. It felt older than anything else in the room.

"Reminds me of Crazywell Pool," Donna said, eyes on the water. "Same kind of watching back."

"Crazywell Pool?" Sarah was running her fingers along the cut grooves of the nearest wave-guide.

"Dartmoor, back home," Steve said. "Bog-pool the locals never went near after dark. Story goes, you stare into the water at midnight on Midsummer Eve and you see the face of the next person in the parish to die."

"It's strange how often water turns up in this kind of story. Loe Pool in Cornwall is supposed to take a life every seven years. Lake Baikal has water spirits in Buryat folklore, lords of the water under the ice. Lough Gur in Ireland is meant to be a doorway to the otherworld, an island that comes and goes on the surface. Every second English village seems to have a witches' pond." Steve looked at the still water. "Something about deep water people can't see through. You stare in and it stares back wearing your own face." He glanced sideways at Arthur. "Mind you, after the last few weeks, dear Nessie's looking more plausible by the day."

"Who's Nessie?"

"The Loch Ness monster," Arthur replied smiling.

"Oh, please." Sarah turned back to the wave-guide.

A limestone dais rose from the pit’s centre, half a metre above the water, ringed at its top by what might once have been a low parapet and was now a row of broken stumps.

The Holy of Holies aperture cut through the rear wall behind the dais. It was a bored shaft, a metre wide where it entered the chamber, narrowing into the stone and angled toward what should have been the winter solstice line. Steve's attention went to it before anyone else's did.

Near the apse, set into a small niche at chest-height, the Sleeping Lady was still where the priestess had left her. The figurine was the size of a palm, lying on her side with one arm folded under her head.

* * *

“Try the aperture,” Steve said. “Donna, you have the carrying voice. Stand at the rear-wall side and say something into it.”

“Say what.”

“Anything. A word. Try a low word.”

“This best not be one of your idiotic tricks”

Donna walked around the pit to the rear wall and put her face close to the bored shaft. She thought for a second.

“Hypogeum,” she said, low and flat.

The chamber answered, and the word came out of the aperture’s far side as a different voice. Deep, male, slow, projected across the chamber from a point near the dais. The four of them heard it as if a man had spoken aloud over Donna’s shoulder. The pitch was not Donna’s. The cadence was not Donna’s. The voice was an octave lower and carried the kind of authority Arthur had heard from priests at sermons.

Donna pulled her face back from the shaft and looked at Steve.

“Neat trick. Why though?” she said.

“Neat indeed,” Steve agreed. He had his ledger open, hand moving. “Every initiate the cult brought down here heard the same voice. The chamber is the voice. It doesn’t matter who spoke into the aperture. The geometry of the bore turns the speaker into one institutional male projection at a frequency the room amplifies. They built a god out of the wall.”

“They were experimenting with acoustics five thousand years ago,” Sarah said walking around the room

“They probably told the initiates this was their god speaking to them.” Steve replied “Five generations down, no one would remember the bore was a trick. The room had been speaking to the order for as long as the order had existed.”

“All that is well and good but isn't there supposed to be a node or a guardian in here?” Donna asked moving away from the wall

* * *

Sarah stepped over to the niche.

She looked at the Sleeping Lady for a long moment before she touched her. When her hand closed around the figurine, the warmth that went out of her was Mender perception, probing the limestone for whatever shape it might still hold.

"An unusual place for someone to sleep," Sarah said.

"She isn't sleeping. The going theory is that the figurine represents an incubation ritual," Steve answered. "Dream incubation. Pilgrims and priests would lie down in the oracle chamber to receive prophecies or healing during sleep."

Sarah turned the figurine over in her palm. "The painting on Level 2 showed a healer attending a woman with an elongated skull as she was dying. This figurine shows a woman receiving healing or visions." She set the Sleeping Lady gently back in her niche and chewed her lower lip. "The place seems to be a lot of things at once."

"One hundred and ten hertz is the chamber's resonance frequency, and one hundred and ten hertz demonstrably affects the part of the human brain that handles language and resistance. A chamber engineered to produce that frequency is also linked to visions. Welcome to the world of we-make-it-up-as-we-go-along archaeology." Steve shook his head. "Half of what we publish is reverse-engineered from what the ancients clearly already knew."

* * *

The party had probed every nook and cranny and finally came back to the pool at the center of the chamber.

“What do you think the pool is for?” Arthur asked, glancing at Steve “Ritual purification?”

“Before the system I would have said yes. Now I'm leaning towards hydromancy,” he said. “It’s older than writing. Every oracle in the ancient Mediterranean had a water apparatus. Dodona had a spring. Delphi had Castalia. Claros had a well the priest descended into. The chamber speaks, the water remembers the shape of the speech, and a trained reader can tell one shape from another.”

“And for those of us that didn’t attend nerd university,” Donna said.

“Standing waves. Vibration at one resonance makes a different surface pattern than vibration at another, and the patterns hold for a moment if the water is still enough. Most of the readings were probably theatre. Some of them might have been real.” Steve looked at the water without touching it. “This pit is the recording medium. The chamber speaks, the water remembers.”

* * *

The dais was ten metres from where Arthur was standing. He could not tell how deep the pit was without putting something into it.

“I need to know how deep this is,” he said.

“Don’t get in it,” Sarah said immediately. “I don’t know what that water has been doing for five thousand years but I don’t want it on us until we do.”

“I’m not getting in it. I just want to test the rim.”

He knelt at the pit’s edge. He brought the Ironhold across his body and lowered its lower rim toward the water. The shield met the surface and Arthur let it sink slowly, watching the water at the contact line for movement.

The water at the rim did not move. But the water at the centre of the pit did.

A ring formed on the surface around the dais, moving outward in a slow and even pattern, as if something underneath had pushed once against the water surface. Arthur pulled the shield back. The water at the rim still did not move.

* * *

Steve was on his feet.

“Arthur. Back from the pit.”

Arthur stood and stepped two paces away from the edge. The Ironhold came up across his body without him deciding to lift it. Donna’s hand was on her axe-haft. Sarah had moved to a position behind Arthur’s left shoulder. Steve had his rod out and was watching the dais.

A pillar of water rose from the pit between the party and the dais, two metres tall and smooth-sided, the surface tension holding it as though it had been poured into an invisible glass. It did not splash, did not fall back, did not behave like water at all. It simply stood.

Then the dais was not empty.

Arthur could not have said when the figure resolved. The dais had been bare in one heartbeat and not in the next. The Conductor stood on the worked stone at the centre of the pit, head bowed, arms at his sides. Its robe had turned to stone, and the body was bones which looked like shaped limestone. A calcified priest. It was about 5 feet tall with a face of limestone and bone.

The wave-guides along the walls began to glow. A low blue-violet luminescence in the cut grooves. The six sectors stood out in the polished limestone, three on each arc, the cuts catching light like the inside of an instrument. The geometry of the room as a weapon was finally legible.

Steve understood a moment later, “Those are amplifiers. The whole chamber is one.”

* * *

The guardian’s arms began to come up.

The motion was slow, the joints cracking reminding Arthur of old stone being forced. The gesture itself was small. A half-raised hand, fingers splayed and a turn of the wrist toward the east wall.

One sector lit brighter than the others.

The air in front of the east-side middle sector compressed into a band of distortion, low to the floor. Dust on the limestone lifted in a clean curving line as the wavefront began to form.

The party saw it a heartbeat before the wave moved forward.
