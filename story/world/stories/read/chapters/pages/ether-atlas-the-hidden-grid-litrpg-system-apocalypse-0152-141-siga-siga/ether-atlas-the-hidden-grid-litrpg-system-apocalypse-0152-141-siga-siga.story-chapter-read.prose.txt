Chapter 141

A careful captain does not ask whether the sea is dangerous on a given day. He asks what has been made ready for the journey. — Codex of the Long Watch, Watcher's Commentary

Spyros had the caïque rolled far enough onto her side for the repair to catch the morning light. He had the seam open to the wood by the time anyone else was properly awake, working a length of old rope apart with his fingers to see what was left in the fibres.

"She wants caulking fibre, pitch, proper fastenings and new rope," he said when Arthur came up to the boat.

"The old fastenings are moving around the repairs. See the heads?" Arthur said, pointing at three greened nail heads, not sitting flush against the boards. A fourth had opened a gap around it.

Spyros looked at the nails and then at Arthur.

"Next you can tell me the sea is wet."

"Fair enough, I won't get into your business," Arthur said, running his hand along the wood. "Fresh fibre, enough pitch to seal the full seam and new fastenings. The question is how do we get it?"

Michalis and Vangelis's caïque sat further up the shingle, its nets over the gunwale in a silver-grey heap, and Vangelis was up to his knees in the shallows scrubbing barnacles off the boat's hull with a flat stone.

Spyros exchanged a few words with them and turned to Arthur.

"Perdika has a boatwright and a good market. One hour, if the wind holds. They return to Perdika to sell the catch. There is a boatwright there who will have caulking fibre, pitch and fastenings. They carry two of you and bring the supplies back once they finish. We give them food in exchange," Spyros said, motioning at the second caïque.

"Right, well. So we need somebody who speaks the language and somebody who can tell a good fastening from a bad one," Donna said. "That rules me and Steve out on both counts."

"I resent that. My Greek has improved appreciably," Steve said, thumbing through his journal.

"Steve, you used malaka with a priest," said Sarah.

"That was a misunderstanding. And he laughed."

"Sarah goes obviously since she is the only one who speaks Greek that won't get us lynched and Arthur goes since he spent his adult life learning which nail fits which board," Donna said.

Arthur looked at her.

"You and Steve stay here?"

"I'm not leaving the field pack alone and unguarded, and somebody needs to help Spyros pull the bad fibre before you bring the new ones. Steve has thin fingers and an established relationship with manual labour."

"What in the world are you on about? I have no such relationship," Steve said.

"Well then, today is a good day to make a good first impression. And try not to lose a finger, as we will be a healer down for the morning."

"I'm assuming we are not giving our cores away," Arthur said.

"Don't be daft. That's what we have all the bronze you've been lugging around for," Donna said, crouching down in front of the field pack. She pulled a few pieces from the pack and went over to Spyros and showed him the metal.

After some inspection Spyros nodded and made the universal 'ok' gesture with his thumb and forefinger.

"There you go." Donna pulled a strip of bronze sheathing from the pack and handed it to Arthur, along with a few smaller plaques. "Spyros says the boatwright will trade for these and so should any food vendor. Get enough food for Michalis and Vangelis."

Michalis put a hand on the caïque's gunwale and said something while looking at them.

"He says the wind is kind now," Sarah translated.

"Off you go then," Donna said, not looking at either Arthur or Sarah. "Terrible business, splitting up the group like that."

Arthur caught her mouth twitching for a second before she turned back to the field pack and pointedly ignored both Arthur and Sarah, which was how Arthur knew the whole thing was her idea. Steve caught his eye and raised an eyebrow. Arthur shook his head once and Steve went back to asking Spyros about the keel.

Michalis's caïque smelled of fish and wet rope. Vangelis raised the patched sail and the cove slipped behind them. Vangelis sailed with his eyes half shut and one hand laid loosely on the tiller. Michalis would cheerfully name headlands every now and then.

Sarah sat beside Arthur on a coil of netting, their shoulders touching whenever the boat rolled.

"Donna was very pleased with that arrangement," she said.

"She was. I suspect she planned it."

Sarah smiled. "She did." She said nothing further.

Arthur watched Michalis take a fish from beneath the cloth and hold it up for Vangelis to judge. They argued about it for a minute, put it back and continued.

"They will be up by now. My mother will be in the kitchen, and Petros will be pretending he slept badly so someone makes him coffee," Sarah said as warmth rose in her face. "We should bring something back for them. Pistachios maybe. Petros will eat the entire bag and complain that we didn't get enough."

Arthur gave a smile and nodded.

Perdika appeared around the final shoulder of the island, white houses strung above a curved harbour with the green bulk of Moni island lying across the water. The old waterfront had changed its purpose and its clientele. Fishing boats filled the harbour where yachts had once tied up, and fishing nets hung from café railings. A woman had turned a shaded terrace into a vegetable garden. Nothing suggested abundance, but everything was being used.

Vangelis brought them in alongside a jetty with the confidence of a man who had done it a thousand times while exchanging a volley of Greek with a man mending nets that ended in both of them laughing.

Michalis took them to the boatwright while Vangelis set about unloading the catch. The boatwright had a yard at the end of the quay, and his name was Notis, though Michalis called him the Cypriot. He was short with heavy arms and shoulders, and rough hands that had worked metal and timber for longer than Arthur had been alive.

The boatwright listened to Sarah's list and brought out eight hand-cut copper fastenings, a sack of clean caulking fibre, two cakes of pine pitch and a small pot of darker resin. Arthur checked the fastenings one by one. They were roughly made but sound.

"And rope," Sarah reminded him in Greek.

The man's eyes went to the wrapped strip under Arthur's arm, and he added two coils of rope.

"He is asking what we have to trade," Sarah said.

Arthur unwrapped the bundle and set one of the bronze pieces on the bench.

Notis picked up the metal and turned it against the light. He then took a flat file off the bench and drew it once across a corner. Where the file bit, the metal shone bright and clean. There was no green residue and no pitting. It was brighter than any bronze that old should be.

Notis looked at the bright line for a moment, then put the file down and his face changed to that of a man discussing the weather.

"He says it is not bad," Sarah said, her voice not changing at all.

"Old bronze, from wreck or temple, who can say. Old bronze is old bronze," Notis said, trying his hand at English.

It was a good performance, one that would have worked on anybody who could not feel a pulse jump from across a room. Notis said something rapidly in Greek and shrugged.

"He says he would take it off our hands for the pitch and the fibre and find some use for it, since who else will want it."

Sarah named her price, and the man's eyebrows climbed up. He shook his head violently. Then the Greek got faster and Sarah stopped translating. Arthur knew her well enough by now to understand she was enjoying this.

An elderly man from the next yard came over to watch. He was joined by a woman carrying a basket. Within minutes the exchange had become the harbour's most sought after entertainment of the morning.

What followed was the most enjoyable ten minutes Arthur had spent in a while, and he understood almost none of it. The goings-on were obvious enough. Notis named a figure and looked mournful about it. Sarah in turn pretended to be appalled on behalf of the bronze. She said something that made Michalis, leaning on the mole with his arms folded, laugh out loud.

Notis put a hand on his heart and said something that sounded like swearing. Sarah pointed at the metal and waved her hand in dismissal. At one point Notis went to the yard door and pretended to close it only to come back and start the haggle again.

From what Arthur understood, the offer went up twice before Sarah was satisfied that the number was honest. She took the large piece of the metal and tapped it.

"We have an agreement. The tools and pitch for the large piece," she said, turning back to Arthur.

Then she took a smaller piece and kept it on top of the larger one and gave Notis a smile.

"You are leaving more. After all that?" Arthur asked.

"He had to admit what it was worth first." She switched back to Greek. The boatwright's expression changed as she spoke. "Generosity is choosing to leave value behind. Letting somebody cheat you is only poor bargaining."

"He needs a few hours," Sarah said. "The rest of the resin is in another yard, and the rope belongs to his brother. Michalis and Vangelis will need at least that long to sell the catch."

A woman named Kyra Froso ran a small grocery shop a few doors along the quay. She sold them bread, cheese, figs, two bags of pistachios and a stoppered flask of something dark red which she swore was better than wine. The Aegina pistachios were the best in the Saronic, the woman told Sarah, which Sarah already knew.

Sarah gave her three smaller pieces of the metal, which the woman took over to an older man for inspection and came back beaming. She tried to give two back, but Sarah held up her hand and motioned towards the jetty.

"I asked her to keep two against Michalis and Vangelis's purchases."

Arthur shrugged. "Saves us from carrying the groceries, I suppose."

Sarah motioned Michalis over and explained the arrangement she had struck, and the young man beamed and nodded.

"Michalis says there is an old olive grove up the hill. We can wait there and have lunch."

The boat materials still wanted cutting and fitting, and Notis had made it clear this would take the better part of the afternoon, so there was nothing for them but to wait. And waiting with a full basket and no urgent task was best done somewhere with shade. So they climbed towards the grove.

Arthur carried the basket without being asked. That was what happened to anything that needed carrying when he was nearby. Arguing about it had stopped being worth either of their breath.

The lane climbed quickly out of Perdika, past the low houses and gardens that had been stripped of anything ornamental and planted with the useful. They passed two small chapels above the terraces, their whitewash still bright against the dirt.

"Still sound?" Sarah asked, following Arthur's gaze.

"A few more centuries in it, I reckon."

"Michalis says nobody's farmed up here in years," Sarah said, picking her way over a fallen branch.

"Doesn't look it. The trees are still going strong."

"Olives don't need much looking after, once they're old enough."

The ground levelled out under a stand of trees old enough that their trunks had twisted themselves into various shapes. They ate sitting on a low stone wall that had marked somebody's field once, and for a while neither of them said anything, which had never been a problem for them.

Arthur held out the figs and Sarah shook her head. He set the figs down and picked up a stone instead. For a while he pretended it was a cricket ball, bouncing it up and down in his palm, then chucked it using a full range of motion and a little twirl of his finger at the end. Jim Laker would have been proud.

"Arthur."

"Hmmm?"

"Is there something you would like to say?"

"Not really, why do you ask?" Arthur said, averting his eyes.

"Because you are fidgeting like a sixteen-year-old on a first date."

They sat in silence for another few minutes, and Sarah made herself comfortable against his shoulder. Arthur sat stiff as a board.

"At Aphaia," he finally said. "After the fight, you said you were watching two people you…"

Sarah looked up. "Two people I care about being idiots."

"Hmmm."

She folded the empty cloth over the remaining bread. "Donna does not matter less to me. She is family now and so is Steve. All three of you are."

She took a breath before continuing.

"But watching you go out of my range is different. Watching you get hurt is different. It has been different for some time, and I think we both have worked hard at pretending otherwise."

Arthur rested his forearms on his knees. He had carried many versions of what he could say, each one sensible enough until he had to say it to her.

"Before we met, you had spent years going wherever you thought you were needed most. Then Malta put you in a room and took your choice away."

Sarah didn't respond, but her hand went still on the cloth.

"I didn't want you to look at me differently because we found you, or because we got you out of there, or because danger made us all hold tighter to the nearest person. I didn't want gratitude or relief to get mistaken for something else. Not after so many people had made choices for you."

"And is that the main reason?" she said.

"It is."

Sarah raised her eyebrow and stared straight into his eyes. Arthur broke first and looked down at his hands.

"The other reason is less storybook. I was frightened."

"Not of you, mind you," he added hastily. "Of wanting this when wanting anything for myself feels irresponsible, when we are actively courting death almost every day. And I'm a Candidate. I can't just drop it and go, 'Well, there lads, that's me done.' I can't walk away from whatever this is and whatever is coming just because I've found something I want to come home to."

Sarah's eyes brightened as a smile reached them.

"Then why don't you court me as well?"

Arthur laughed, and she squeezed his hands. "The problem with stepping in front of things is that eventually you do not step back. And if you care about the person standing behind you, they become the way someone gets to you."

"I already care what happens to you. If you die, keeping your distance or refusing to kiss me will not make my grief smaller. It will only mean we refused something we both wanted while you were alive."

They were sitting close now, very close.

"And nothing is safe any more, Arthur. My family is not safe because they are in Piraeus. Donna is not safe because she can cross a room before I can blink. You are not safe when you hold a wall up, and Steve is not safe when he throws fireballs. I know what the danger is, and we can choose to face it together."

Her thumb moved once across his knuckle.

"It is another reason to survive, another reason to return, another reason to get stronger. The world does not have to make sense for us to make sense."

"I want you," he said, the plain words tightening his throat. "Not only when one of us is bleeding, but I want the walk and the food and you telling me I'm checking the walls. I want an ordinary life with you, whatever ordinary means now."

Sarah's fingers closed around his.

"I want that too. I am not asking you to choose me instead of being a Candidate. I am asking you to choose me too."

He looked at her for a long moment, and then her free hand came up against his face and her mouth found his. The kiss was gentle at first, then much more certain. Arthur's hand settled at her waist. The tree at his back was rough through his shirt, and her fingers were warm against his cheek, and for several long moments it was just them and a cool breeze through the trees.

When they finally drew back, Sarah stayed close enough that her forehead rested against his.

"You took your time," she said.

"Siga siga," Arthur said, and they laughed together. It was as much a laugh at his Spyros imitation as it was a laugh of relief.

Arthur cupped her face and kissed her again. This time he took his time. Sarah's hand tightened in his shirt, and he felt her smile against his mouth.

"Does this count as courting?"

"It is a promising start," Sarah said.

They drank the last of the cordial and sat with their hands joined until the shade moved and told them it was time to go back.

Notis had every item assembled when they returned to Perdika, and Michalis and Vangelis had sold the last of the fish. The return journey carried four water jars, two coils of rope, the repair bundle and provisions.

Arthur and Sarah sat together on the netting, and her hand remained in his. Neither of them moved it when Vangelis glanced over and smiled to himself.

They found Donna, Steve and Spyros sitting on the beach when they reached the cove.

Donna looked at the supplies and said.

"Six hours to get a couple of buckets and ropes?"

"There was a queue," Arthur said, setting the repair sundries near Spyros.

"I'm sure there was," Donna replied.

Spyros checked the pitch, counted the fastenings and knocked one against the new plank.

"Kala. This is enough. We work now. By the next morning, she sails."
