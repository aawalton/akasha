The eyes of the baker girl had Craft coming back after a long day’s work. Any other place would sate his hunger, but the redness, the deadness—her eyes were a puzzle to him. He wanted to strike up a conversation and finally get to the bottom of it, but talking about someone’s odd body parts came off rude half the time.

He held out a dollar bill, resting his hands on the edge of the counter. “A croissant, please,” he said.

She wasn’t the friendly sort; didn’t even say, “Please come again.” She just did as she was told, slowly but confidently sliding over to the display case and retrieving the croissant with a pair of tongs.

Well, it entertained him. He’d made it a game to see if she’d ever deviate more than a quarter-inch from the last nine times she’d gotten him a croissant—and the position of the croissant, she’d always move it to an exact index in the display case before actually picking it up. There was that one time the case had run out of croissants, and she’d actually frozen for a good five seconds before remembering they still had more in reserve.

They’d even made eye contact that time. That was probably the most normal socialization he’d had in the past two weeks.

She deftly placed it in a paper bag and crimped the top edge, taping it shut along with the receipt. Craft reached out to take it—but, damn it, he really didn’t want to go back to the safehouse. All the guys there ever talked about was kill counts and hookers. Two topics; that’s it. Juggling between them was just… He was losing it.

The moment he opened his mouth, the shop’s door slammed open, unhinging the bell which clanged to the floor. “This is a hold up!” a man shouted, his face wrapped in a red bandana. His muscles bulged from his ripped shirt, and he waved around a Tokarev, of all things.

Craft wondered where the guy had gotten it—just a collector’s intrusive thought.

He pretended to cower while the rest of the patrons ducked under the tables or ran out of the store. It was a lot of movement for your average bandit to take in all at once, so it was tempting for said bandit to gloss over the tactical details. He was already the most powerful man in the room, anyway, and he was just there for one thing.

He threw down a sack on the counter. “Everything in the register! Now!” he shouted, pushing the Tokarev up against the baker girl’s face.

Yet, her eyes were still dead. She did comply, albeit slowly, keying the register and picking up cash in neat wads, tapping them on the counter before putting them in the sack, as if this was just a normal transaction.

It only took a second too long for the bandit to get antsy. “Faster!” he said. This time, he’d straightened his arm—and by that, Craft knew he’d already decided to pull the trigger.

He rushed the bandit at the same time that the gun fired. He disarmed him, kicked the back of his knee, and once he’d folded to a crouch, he cupped the man’s jaw and gave it a sharp pull.

Craft quickly pushed him down by the shoulders—hard enough to give anyone whiplash—and he took the Tokarev and slammed it to the floor, making sure to use enough force to shake up the gun’s shitty manufacturing tolerances just enough that it “accidentally” discharged, “incidentally” pointed right into the bandit’s neck.

“Holy shit, he slipped and killed himself!” he announced, making sure to back off and have a truly frightened look on his face. The remaining patrons looked up from covering their faces; some of them screamed, but others were relieved. At least one laughed. After all, it wasn’t every day that a bandit somehow managed to slip and kill himself. Some lanky guy in shorts and a tank top wouldn’t have been able to take down a buff man, so that was the natural conclusion—the perfect conclusion.

Yeah, he’d probably buy a newspaper tomorrow and brag about it to the guys at the safehouse.

Outside of funerals, no one was interested in staying in the same room as a dead body, and the patrons started filing out of the store. Craft observed each of them, making sure none of them shot a suspicious glance at him, and soon, he was trailing the last one out. Too bad about the baker girl, he thought. The paper bag she’d prepared for him was still on the counter, perhaps stained with a bit of gunpowder residue.

He paused and went back for it. He shouldn’t waste the croissant, at least.

The moment he turned around, however, he froze, downright shocked that the baker girl was still right behind the register. There was a hole clearly bored through her chest, and the cloth around the entry wound was clearly blackened. No matter how much he blinked, no matter how far his jaw had dropped, there was simply no blood to be seen.

With the ever-same dead eyes, she pushed the paper bag closer to him before awkwardly putting on a different apron, covering up any evidence of her dead-or-alive status.

“Please come again,” she said.

He had been considering killing her instead of leaving a witness in the scene, but her sudden niceness just had him saying, “Oh—er—sure,” and grabbing the bag from the counter, scratching his head as he went for the busted-down door.

He remembered he was hungry, and he popped the croissant from the bag. A spare wad of tissue paper went with it. “Same time tomorrow?” was written on it with thin ballpoint ink.
