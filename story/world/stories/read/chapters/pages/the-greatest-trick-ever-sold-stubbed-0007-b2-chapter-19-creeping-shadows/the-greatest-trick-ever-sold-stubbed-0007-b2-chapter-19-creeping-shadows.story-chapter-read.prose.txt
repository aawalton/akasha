CHAPTER NINETEEN

Creeping Shadows

There was more than one plume of thick smoke billowing past the canopy of trees. Yet despite the urgency fueling their steps, the party remained cautious in their approach. For as Bram claimed, and wisely too, “Only fools rush into danger.”

With guarded steps, they reached the edge of a fort clinging to the trees; wooden planks connected by thick rope spiraling up wide ancient oaks to reach hideouts beneath the treetops. Hanging bridges linked each platform of one tree to another, forming a network of hideouts above the ground. It was an ingenious display of craftsmanship mirroring the Thieves’ Road’s trap formations, though much of it was slowly being destroyed by little fires everywhere.

“Darn it, we’re too late,” Chris whispered.

Plumes of smoke clung to the flames burning the hideouts above. They were a stark reflection of the bodies lying underneath the carpet of fallen leaves.

Bram’s gaze fixed on every ashen face by his feet. He recognized them all, the men and women he’d spent a warm night of revelry with. These same people who’d been supplying his great undertaking with the sacrifices it required. They’d been so full of life when he’d met them, but those same eyes who’d looked at him without judgement were now lifeless.

“What in the heck happened here?” Chris murmured.

From the wounds inflicted on their bodies—arrows peppering one man’s corpse, a sword embedded in one woman’s gut—these were proof that the fire hadn’t killed these bandits. There had been a battle here, and the bandits lost.

Bram knelt on one knee to inspect a corpse.

“Still warm… It hasn’t been long since she died. An hour or two perhaps,” he reported.

“Much like the body we discovered inside the pit,” Rowan recalled.

“And now we know why.” Chris, who’d joined Bram in his inspection, shut the eyes that stared up into nothing. “He got caught in a trap while rushing to hightail it out of here.”

Bram couldn’t blame the dead pit thief for running away. The carnage on display was enough to make even him feel nauseous.

“There’s no one left,” Bridget whispered.

Bram could hear the quiver in her voice.

To feel compassion for these people she didn’t know…how unlike the people of Aarde she was. Aarders lacked such empathy, especially for dead commoners. This was one more reason the prince had such hope for these Earthers who’d arrived to change his world with their big ideas.

Bridget was wrong about one thing, though. The prince doubled checked, but not all the bandits he met were here among the dead.

His gaze turned upward.

There were also dead men on the platforms, though none of them looked large enough to fit the man he was looking for. At least not from where Bram could see.

“Lil’ Joss isn’t here.”

He believed it too.

Bram recalled with clarity the impressive mobility art that Lil’ Joss had shown him. A man with that sort of talent wouldn’t fall so easily. And if the brother was alive, it made sense that his sister would be safe too.

With hope filling his chest, Bram turned to Bridget and asked, “Can you still use your eyes to see what others can’t?”

“If I use all my magic up…” Bridget’s brow creased. “Maybe once more. Maybe.”

“Try, please, and look for any sign of survivors.”

“Okay, but I’ll need a minute… and it’d help if someone could deal with the fire.”

“Hajime and I can handle that,” Rowan promised.

“Eh,” a puzzled look flitted across Hajime’s face, “we can?”

Rowan gave him a pointed look.

“Y-Yes.” Hajime cleared his throat. Then, with more confidence, the kind born from one who’d completed his first job promotion, he said, “Yes. Yes, we can.”

Hajime placed his hands on his hips in a pose that was supposed to help one exude confidence. All it did was make Bridget and Rowan roll their eyes at him, though.

“Clench your teeth,” Rowan insisted.

As he watched her pull him up into the treetops, Bram wondered if Hajime had enough time to follow Rowan’s advice. For his friend’s sake, he hoped so. Bram knew firsthand how it felt to swallow bugs while flying in the air or bite his tongue from the sudden jolt of liftoff.

“I’ll get started then,” Bridget said.

It took her a bit to concentrate and draw her magic into her eyes, which was just enough time for Rowan and Hajime to stir up the air and deprive the scattered fires of their breath, though not all yielded to their magic. Still, with the distraction of the flames diminished, and her ‘Flametail Eyes’ awakened, Bridget set to the task Bram had given her. It didn’t take her long to call out.

“Have you found them?” Bram asked.

He followed her deeper into the fort grounds, where the signs of recent battle were more prevalent.

“Not yet, but I think”—she pointed to a banner whose pole spiked the ground—“we’ve found our culprits.”

The banner lightly fluttering in the wind was one Bram recognized; Three stocks of barley spread out like a pitchfork surrounded by a field of bright purple.

“It’s the crest of House Barton,” he said.

It made sense that the noble house who ruled Bravant Shire would want to clean out a nest of thieves, but this massacre was far from normal procedure. From the signs of upturned earth and flattened grass, it seemed the nearby dead got dragged toward House Barton’s banner to form a circle around it as if those who’d committed the massacre wanted to leave behind a mark of their hunt.

“This is the noble’s hunt,” he deduced..

Clearly, House Barton’s actions went beyond bringing a nest of thieves to justice. This was a hunt for men considered no different from beasts by those nobles who thought themselves cut from a better cloth.

“It’s horrible,” Bridget whispered.

Bram couldn’t disagree. The sight of this gruesome scene made him want to puke.

“House Barton…they’re part of Central Lotharin, ain’t they?” Chris asked.

Bram nodded. “But they wouldn’t have known about my dealings with these bandits.”

He lamented not having insisted that Lil’ Joss keep his token. It might have helped them avoid the slaughter.

Bram shook his head.

He couldn’t be certain his token would’ve stopped House Barton’s attack. It’s not like the nobles of Central Lotharin followed him willingly.

“Eorl Günter sent emissaries to Bastille not long ago to discuss an alliance between us,” Bram explained.

Chris nodded to the circle of dead surrounding House Barton’s banner. “Do we wanna be allied with folks who can do this?”

Bram couldn’t answer.

From what he knew of Bravant’s Eorl, Günter Barton was an honorable man, who, though he was a devout servant of Arawn’s Cloven Temple—having earned the title of Great Hunter of the wearg god’s Wild Hunt—wasn’t the type to rape priestesses or massacre weaker men like Arawn might have. The eorl had served with distinction in the War of Thirteen and was a former companion of Bram’s father, who’d also been the one to bring news of Ser Berenger’s death to the Sovereign.

“This might not be the eorl’s doing,” Bram wondered aloud, and then explained, “I’ve read in Ser Anthony’s reports that Eorl Günter has taken ill.”

“You mean ill like how the eorl of Lorraine was ill to avoid answering your calls?” Chris reminded Bram.

The prince almost laughed.

As Chris hinted, none of Central Lotharin’s lords had answered Bram’s summons when he’d first become governor. These days they sang a different tune, sending him representatives with expensive gifts and pleasant words. House Barton was no different.

“According to the report, whatever ails the eorl is genuine.” Bram’s gaze was fixed on the brightly colored purple banner. “Someone else might have ordered this.”

The prince had his guesses, but he didn’t speak them out loud. There was no point. The destruction of a thieves’ hideout hardly provided him with a justification for imprisoning nobles.

“Hey, Prince,” Bridget called, “I found tracks.”

Bram looked at what Bridget was inspecting on the ground. He wasn’t sure what she could see, but all he saw were dead leaves atop burned grass.

Bridget must be seeing more than he did, though, because she confidently led them past the scenes of death and carnage to the very edge of the grounds on the other side of the fort. There was an outhouse here that was relatively intact, which shouldn’t have been possible since nothing else around it survived the fires.

“Why would anyone wanna save the crapper?” Chris asked.

“Because it isn’t just an outhouse,” Bridget answered.

This was true.

Even Bram could see the faint remnant of magic coating the outhouse’s wall.

“Y’all sure about this?” the Chris asked.

“This is the place,” Bridget promised.

“Alrighty then.” Chris stepped forward. “Y’all step back. This thing’s likely booby-trapped.”

Cautiously, he opened the door, but no sharp objects flew at him.

“It’s—”

An explosion of stink wafted out of the opening, sending Chris doubling back.

“Sweet Christmas,” he coughed. “It smells like athlete’s foot had a baby with skunk spray.”

They waited for the stench to dissipate somewhat before looking in. The only problem was that there was nothing inside. This outhouse was a single, but not the kind with the ornate chamber pot and heated seats most nobles enjoyed. It was a cheap commoner derivative that lacked complicated indoor plumbing to ensure the stench was far from reaching one’s nose. It was just a hole cut into a wooden floor that led down into a typical latrine pit.

“There’s nothing here, Bridge,” Chris reiterated.

Bridget shoved Chris aside so she could inspect the inside of the outhouse herself. It didn’t take her long to say the one thing Bram hoped she wouldn’t say.

“Traces are feint, but they lead down.”

The prince grimaced.

There were just some things that a prince ought not to do, like wading in the shit of commoners. Fortunately for Bram, he had the perfect excuse to avoid toiling in the toilet.

“You’ll have to manage on your own, Bridget.” Bram patted Chris on the shoulder. “We’ll be busy dealing with them.”

Bridget’s eyes followed Bram’s gaze toward the fog that was slithering into the fort’s territory. With this thick fog came the soft whispers and shuffling of the shades creeping at its edge.

A puzzled look flashed on Bridget’s face. “Wasn’t the Thieves’ Road supposed to help us escape the fog?”

“Whatever enchantment kept the fog away isn’t functioning anymore. And there’s enough death around us to draw it here,” Bram deduced.

“I still think I’d rather fight whatever those are instead of wading through shit.”

“Oh, I doubt that very much. You haven’t seen how ugly the dead become after they’re touched by dark magic.”

Speaking of the dead, the first of the shades finally shuffled out of the fog.

It was a grotesque thing, twisted and mutilated, its limbs stretched beyond what should be possible.

“That thing doesn’t look human.”

“It’s not. Not anymore.”

The head was bald, with ears stretched out like pointy knives. Its pale face was more skull than man; browless eyes of the deepest black, slits for a nose, and a mouth set in an almost gleeful, childlike smile marred by dripping saliva.

“Dark powers took these dead and turned into a ruined form of man…”

A new tag flashed above the walking corpse’s head.

One of Chris’ bushy eyebrows hitched upward. “What the hell’s a…wiedergänger?”

“A very nasty, vengeful revenant.” Bram drew Dusk from its holster. “Beware its touch. Even a brush can leave you so cold you’ll freeze to death.”

The wiedergänger dropped to all fours like it had forgotten it was once human and was now a beast. Fog clung to it like a wispy cloak, though it barely kept the undead creature’s privates from showing.

“I can handle a little cold,” Chris insisted, his tone firm.

Bram glanced sideways.

“You want to try fighting that thing alone?” he guessed.

Chris nodded. “It’s about time I pulled my weight here, boss.”

Bram didn’t think this was the case. Chris had proven himself many times over. But if the Texan wanted to try a solo fight, the prince wouldn’t stop him.

“Go for the head. That’s the surest way to kill the undead.”

Bram felt nothing but pride as his soldier brandished his war axe against the undead creature glaring at him from the ground just beyond the encroaching fog.

“Y’all should be shaking in your boots.” Chris frowned. “I’ve got me a real sharp pigsticker aimed at you.”

Chris swung his war axe for added effect, but far from frightening the wiedergänger, the undead creature drew closer. It skidded forward on all four limbs like some overgrown insect, twitching grotesquely with each step. And it wasn’t long until grimy, bone-sharp fingers twitched toward Chris’ face.

“Guess you’re itching to dance too!”

As if accepting his invite, the wiedergänger leaped at Chris, but instead of cowering, the Texan yelled a bellow of challenge. He blocked the undead creature’s sharp-nailed strike with his round shield, which had the crest of the Gray Hounds painted on its front. Fortunately, its frame was made of cold iron, the kind that repelled dark things.

While he watched Chris’s fight, Bram also heard a distinct, “Fuck, it stinks in here!”

He couldn’t spare Bridget a glance, however, because Chris’ fight was becoming more entertaining.

The Texan pushed the wiedergänger back with his shield, creating enough space for him to twirl his war axe around so that its pommel was right-side up and ready to bash the undead creature’s head in.

“Pommel Strike,” Bram whispered.

As the prince expected, the Texan slammed the blunt end of his war axe down on the wiedergänger’s head. No spark of magic escaped the point of impact, but the damage was apparent.

The blow sent even the undead creature reeling back, allowing Chris to rush forward while twirling his war axe around a second time so that the blade was right side up now.

With a sideward swipe, the war axe’s blade sliced through the wiedergänger’s neck, nearly decapitating the undead creature. Nearly.

Undeterred by his failure, Chris rushed forward with his war axe raised and drove its blade deep into the wiedergänger’s chest.

“I told you to go for the head!”

“No backseat fighting!”

“Don’t let it touch you!”

Chris raised his shield up in time to avoid bony fingers from grasping his outstretched hand.

“I know!”

But he didn’t know. Not truly. Not until he saw for himself what a wiedergänger’s touch could do.

Frost clung to the front of his shield. Not yet enough to cover it entirely, but enough that he could feel the cold seeping into the handle.

“What the hell did you do to my shield?”

The wiedergänger’s bony fingers lashed out at him once more, but this time Chris avoided it rather than block the blow and risk losing his shield to the cold.

Two steps back—enough time to draw his sword from its sheath—and then a step forward so he could swing his blade down on the wiedergänger’s outstretched arm.

It didn’t take much to sever the arm too, which was the one good thing about fighting undead. They were brittle monsters.

However, quick as a hungry pit python, the wiedergänger’s other hand stretched forward, its fingers grasping Chris’ sword and causing frost to cling to its blade.

“Motherfucker!”

Chris countered with a kick to its ribs that sent the wiedergänger tumbling backward, though its bony fingers clung to his sword’s blade, making even its handle too cold to touch.

“You can keep the sword!”

As he let go of his sword, Chris leaped forward with only his shield in his hand.

“But I’ll take”—he hurled his shield forward in a sideward swipe—“your head!”

It was a move different from the ‘Shield Bash’ Bram expected, but one that used the shield’s edge to bite deep into the wound Chris had already made in the wiedergänger’s neck.

With strength worthy of his muscles, Chris forced his shield through flesh and bone, sending it from one end of the wiedergänger’s neck to the other and effectively severing the undead creature’s head.

“That was brilliant,” Bram whispered.

Having lost its head, the dark magic that animated the undead corpse was severed and the wiedergänger’s tumbled to the ground. As it crashed into the hard earth, its desiccated body shattered like hundreds of glass shards that vanished like sand carried away by an ill wind.

It was nearly double the experience Chris got for helping to defeat a flametail leopard.

“What’s this?”

The Texan leaned down to pick up a pinch of dust that was all that remained of his foe.

“Not a bad haul so far,” Chris said.

He reached out and picked up the war axe lying on the ground.

Thanks to being an enchanted weapon, Chris’ war axe survived its time embedded inside the Wiedergänger’s chest. It looked relatively unscathed except for the bit of frost still coating its blade. Its handle was cold to the touch, too.

Interestingly, Chris retrieved his war axe just in time, because two more wiedergänger skittered out of the fog.

He threw his war axe at the one to his left, its blade embedding deep into the undead creature’s skull.

The wiedergänger to his right sprang forward, taking a leap that sent it crashing into Chris’ shield just as he roared, “Shield Bash!” and slammed his shield against its desiccated body.

‘Wham!’

The round shield won that exchange, though a crack formed along its front, costing it some durability.

This proved a worthy sacrifice, though.

For with the wiedergänger stunned by Chris’ shield, Bram stepped in and sever its head from its neck with a single decisive blow of Dusk’s violet-orange blade.

“Sorry.” The prince fixed his stance. “I know you wanted to fight on your own.”

“Nah, I appreciate the assist.” As he waved off Bram’s concern, Chris pulled out the one-handed war hammer clipped to the back of his belt. “That was a nice clean hit, too.”

“You primed it for me.”

“I know you didn’t get ‘em a lot growing up, but you should learn to take a compliment, boss.”

“Very well… Thanks.”

While Bram pondered the lesson the much older Chris tried to impart, the wiedergänger with the war axe embedded on its head began rising to its feet. Even more concerning, from beyond its shoulders, they saw more shades beginning to escape the thick fog.

“It’s about to get gnarly,” Chris said.

Both warriors braced themselves as more wiedergängers appeared, surrounding them and the outhouse Bridget had vanished into. So, it was quite a relief to hear the war cries coming from inside the outhouse.

“Fight!” bellowed a familiar gruff voice.

A second later, Lil’ Joss jumped out of the outhouse’s opening. He glared, first at Bram and Chris, and having recognized them, turned his rage-filled eyes on the wiedergängers gathered around them.

“Don’t let them taint our brothers and sisters,” Lil’ Joss commanded. “Send the dead back to death!”

At his command, bandits filed out of the opening with weapons drawn.

From the savage look they gave the wiedergängers arrayed against them, Bram guessed that the bandits newly risen from safety now had murder in their hearts. So, following their lead, he also raised Dusk high, and with a grin, yelled, “Death!”

And then all hell broke loose.

Magic is an App Prologue:

I used to believe doing the right thing was a no-brainer, but I learned the hard way that I was mistaken. See, good or evil, right and wrong, such things only truly mattered to victims of hate and violence. Or, as was the case with me and my recent troubles, the rare fool too naïve to the ways of the world.

Evil only triumphs when good people do nothing.

My dad used to say this phrase to me a lot, like it was important that an eight-year-old kid from downtown L.A. knew them. I memorized them for him, though I didn’t understand what they meant. I was eight. At that age, video games, baseball, and anime were all I could relate to.

Dad didn’t mind, though. He just wanted me to know his favorite catchphrase for future reference, as if he knew he wouldn’t be around to remind me once I got older.

Spoiler alert—he wasn’t.

Dad was a police officer who believed in the badge and what it represented. It’s why, on a hot summer night, he died while upholding the ideals of his favorite catchphrase—and I had a front-row seat to his final heroic moment.

Like I said, it had been a scorching night on another 2017 heatwave, and I’d asked Dad if we could get ice cream on our way to picking Mom up from her new job at a talent agency in Hollywood. It’s why we took a detour into a nearby convenience store at the exact moment it was getting robbed.

I remembered how the air felt; stifling, heavy with tension, as if the world itself held its breath just to watch my family’s tragedy unfold. I remembered the robber’s hands trembling as he held the gun, his voice quivering in desperation.

“N-Nobody move or I’ll s-shoot the brat!”

I could never forget his cliched words, how he’d singled me out, the only child in a space full of adults. But mostly, I remembered the way Dad stepped between that gun and me. He didn’t even hesitate.

“There’s no need to hurt anyone.”

Dad spoke calmly, his voice a steady anchor in the chaos, trying to diffuse the situation like a police officer should. Too bad he was off duty. He didn’t have his gun to protect himself.

Then there was the deafening crack that shattered everything, and my life as I knew it was over...
