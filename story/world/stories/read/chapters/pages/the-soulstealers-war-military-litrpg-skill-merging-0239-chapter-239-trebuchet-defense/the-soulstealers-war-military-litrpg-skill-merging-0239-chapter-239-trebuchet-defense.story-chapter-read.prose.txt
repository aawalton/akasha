I fought until I was out of breath.

It wasn’t like training. Maybe I didn’t have riftrocks in my kit bag, and maybe the obstacle course was technically easier, but I was fighting for my life.

I stabbed orcs when they reached the top of the ladder. I leaned away from arrows, and I blocked swings with my shield.

I did my best to keep the other soldiers atop the tower alive. If one was in trouble, I lunged in to help them. If one was holding an orc back, or stuck in a stalemate or a bind, I bashed the orc with my spear or shield.

But I couldn’t save them all. Minute by minute, more orcs filled the top of the tower, forming a ring around the edge and pushing us in toward the trebuchet. I organized a defensive shield line.

“My lord!” a sergeant yelled. “There is a Hangman out there, isn’t there?”

“Yeah!” I replied. “We’ll deal with him!”

“You can?”

“We haven’t lost yet!” I skewered an orc through the eye of his rusted helmet. “Keep holding on! By sunrise, it will all be over!”

The sergeant ducked behind his shield to avoid a crushing axe blow, then slammed his sword into an orc’s chest. “We can’t hold the tower forever!”

I glanced back at the artillerists. They still had rubble and burning pots to fling, and the more damage we could do to the orcs, the better.

“Stick close to me! We can still make it through the night! We’ve seen worse before!” I paused. “The longer we have a deterrent for that battering ram, the better. If we give up this tower, they can approach.”

I couldn’t say if I’d truly seen worse when it came to orcs. But I was also stronger than before.

“Why would a Hangman stay back like this?” a different dupe asked.

“He wants us to use up our Presence,” I guessed. It was the only explanation that came to mind. “He knows there are strong Path-walkers protecting the city, and the only way he’ll beat us is if we’ve used up most of our Presence.”

That was the best explanation I could come up with at the moment. It set the other soldiers’ minds at ease, at least. They swung their swords and axes harder, they jabbed with spears, and they held the line.

I don’t know how much longer I fought for. In my mind, it was an eternity. My mind saw the piling up orc bodies and craved to use my Siphon, but I restricted myself for now. I couldn’t just take a break.

My fingers ached from holding the spear. Splinters stabbed through my gloves. I began breathing through my mouth, both to help save my enhanced senses from the stench of battle and carnage, and to get more air in.

My shield had taken a beating. There were a few holes in it, and the rim was cracked at the top. Three arrows stuck out of its face.

Oh, how I wished I had my better shield again.

The tower rumbled. More boulders pounded it. Orc catapults tried to take it down. Already, a tower to our right crumbled, and the catapult atop it collapsed with it. Another tower on the opposite side of the gate from me had fallen into orc hands, and they’d smashed the trebuchet on it with glee.

The tower that Lord Ceonwin had started defending was half intact. His efforts to protect it had caused a corner to fall away—either one of his Arts, or a shaman’s attack eating through the stone.

When it came to the tower I was protecting, we couldn’t hold it forever. I knew that. But we had to be able to keep it intact a little longer, right?

Before I could even jab my spear again, the ground shook. A swell of Presence flowed in front of me, and the tower’s floor ripped apart beside me.

First came an explosion of dust. Then, rising through the debris, a massive stack of fungal growths tore through the stone. Mushroom heads split apart like venus fly traps, grabbing bricks in their maw and crushing them to dust. Dark, slimy tentacles leeched out from beneath them, whipping onto the walls and grasping at the Dupes.

The side of the tower crumbled. I widened my stance, holding my ground against a plains troll that had scaled the ladder, but it didn’t help. The rest of the tower collapsed in a chain-reaction. The trebuchet we were protecting fell to the side, toppling over, and spilling the burning payload in its sling onto the city below. It shattered on our side of the wall. Flames spilled across the ground.

I fell through the rubble. Stones plummeted on me, and a landslide of rock and debris buried me. I choked on gravel, and tried to claw my way to the surface as I fell, but it wasn’t good enough.

I spent anywhere from ten seconds to a minute in a daze, buried beneath the debris, before finally, I clawed my way up to the surface. I shook myself off, then stared up at the fungal structure looming over us—which had blasted through the wall.

It was different from the regular growths that the Hangman had made. Its heads were just alive as its rot tendrils, and they whipped around, tearing the hole in the wall open wider with every second. Debris flew into the city, and civilians scattered, screaming.

Below my feet, the ground was soggy. The stench of rotten leaves and vomit leapt up from the ground, and the dirt turned into a black slurry that squelched under my boots, I jumped back, then swished my spear to knock away a tendril of rot.

That must have been what the Hangman was waiting for. It took time to use an Art like that, something that powerful. He must have been charging it up.

Worse, we’d expected them to go after the main gate. We didn’t have nearly as many soldiers here, ready to intercept any incoming orcs.

I jumped backward, leaning away from a thrashing mushroom head. Sharp teeth whipped past my nose, threatening to rip a hole in my throat, and a tendril caught my ankle. I guess it had settled, since it couldn’t reach my neck.

I slashed it off with my speartip, then rushed back as far as I could. A thin line of militiamen, Dupes, and mercenaries gathered behind me, ready to intercept the orcs. They pushed through the gap in the wall, weaving between the stalks of the mushrooms as they raced toward us.

With an accepting sigh, I raised my shield. The Hangman was out there, sure, and Lord Ceonwin and the others would need my help, but none of this meant anything if I let the city fall. It was my city, after all, and I’d vowed to do everything I could to protect it.

The orcs sprinted into our spears. “Push!” I yelled. “Don’t let them make a formation, and don’t get too close to the mushrooms!”

They were mine to deal with. While I stabbed an incoming orc in the chest, I whispered, “Sorry, Ceonwin.”

I couldn’t afford to save any more Presence. If I didn’t take out this plant formation, we were done for.

Targeting the two largest chunks of stone from the wall I could find, I created a gravity ‘tube’ around them—a double-columned well, both dragging toward a single point. I imagined that point to be exactly at the base of the mushrooms.

My stones flew, smashing together, while at the same time, cleaving through the stems of the structure. When the stones smashed together, they exploded into dust and a ring of rock shards.

I had envisioned a disc of stone erupting around the impact point, like a shockwave, and the result didn’t disappoint. Shrapnel exploded in a ring, cleaving all of the mushroom stems over. They decayed into black dust as soon as they were separated from the rotting ground.

On one hand, it made it easier for orcs to get through. On the other hand, it prevented the plants from ripping up any more of the wall, and it stopped the dark tendrils of rot from reaching out to us.

A cheer rose up from the defenders. More poured in behind us, and a lieutenant yelled, “Keep pushing! The plants are down!”

“Move to hold the chokepoint!” I added, pointing to the rubble ahead.

As a shield wall, we prowled forward, stabbing and slashing, until finally, we spanned the gap in the wall with our shields and spears.

Orcs poured toward the wound. We climbed atop the rubble, giving ourselves favourable high ground, and archers stood on the remains of the wall on either side, raining arrows down on the approaching army.

I spread my Focus, allowing myself to pay attention to more than just the battle ahead again.

With most of the trebuchets down—no, now all of them were down; the last tower had fallen now that Lord Ceonwin was gone—the battering ram approached the gate. I glanced up at the sky, trying to tell how long it had been. But it was hard to say. I still couldn’t recognize star formations here, not enough to say what time of the night it was or to navigate by.

Don’t think about the time, I told myself. Just keep surviving. There should still be enough soldiers by the gate.

This was going to be a costly battle. The most costly I’d ever fought.

Even worse was knowing that it was the most costly so far. I wouldn’t soon forget the stinging pain of splinters in the palms of my hands or the screaming of the Dupes who died beside me in numbers higher than I’d ever seen before. Worse, it was still pretty much my own voice, screaming for help or begging as orcs pulled them to the ground or bashed their skulls.

Finally, someone placed a hand on my shoulder. I barely registered it until a voice yelled, “Levi! We’ve got this! Go!”

I glanced back, to find the remains of Habit’s squad pushing to the front of the group. There were only two of them, now. I had no idea what had happened to Habit, and Squirrel, of course, was gone.

They had pledged to keep fighting, though, and we weren’t about to turn away those who wanted to help.

Now, with two fully trained sappers at the front of the line, I was far more confident leaving them.

I had a Hangman to take down.

Sweeping my spear ahead of myself to clear out a swath of orcs, I launched myself up to the remains of the tower, trying to get a view of the battlefield. If the Hangman was out there, I’d see him.
