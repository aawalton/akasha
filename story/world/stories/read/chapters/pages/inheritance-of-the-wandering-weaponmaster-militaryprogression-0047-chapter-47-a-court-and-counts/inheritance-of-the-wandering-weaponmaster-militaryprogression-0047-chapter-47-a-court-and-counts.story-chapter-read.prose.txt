Silence settled over the room, the Wainwrights both glaring at Georg and, though they only glanced at him, they didn’t look too charitably at Aaron either.

As the moment of silence passed, Winifred the maid’s voice seemed to begin quietly arguing on the other side of the door. Ezra wasn’t sure why, but Georg’s face lit up at the sound of a curmudgeonly person on the other side of the door, so he could figure that it was good news, if nothing else.

“Yes, I’m here, and if one of the Standage boys is asking for me,” the angry voice rose enough to be heard through the door, “then I’m going through that door. Now get out of the way, and we’ll all be happier.”

Winifred’s voice raised, panic overcoming her as the door started to open, but she also didn’t seem to be willing to stand in the way of the older lady who was speaking, so the door opened, and in walked a truly ancient woman leaning on a cane, wearing the vestments of the church.

Her robes, different from many of the other acolytes of different temples, were heavy and thick, a fur lining all over them to keep her warm. Her stole was the blue of Baltisse the Queen, and despite her age and obvious frailty, the old woman carried herself with the grace of a queen. Or so Ezra thought.

“As I’ve been announced, Acolyte Mabel si’Baltisse is entering, and you’ll give me a place to sit before I fall and break.”

“Thank you so much for coming with such short notice,” Georg said with a nod. He stepped forward, offering his elbow to the grandmotherly woman, Amy standing on the other side. Amy seemed to relax as she was able to step out of the escort role, instead going to stand near Ezra, similar to Petr and Troy. While Amy escaped her position, Mabel walked carefully and slowly, but her back was straight and her eyes a clear light gray. She looked into every person’s eyes, not hesitating to meet a single gaze as she made her way towards where Georg guided her. To Ezra’s surprise, Georg directed her past an extravagantly stuffed sofa to instead settle on a much simpler, lightly padded chair.

“You invited an Acolyte and won’t even allow her to sit on one of your best pieces of furniture?” Rufus demanded with a vehemence that made Ezra wonder if the man was actually a devout follower of the gods and Baltisse specifically.

“He invited me specifically because I am trusted by my goddess to make judgment,” Mabel said, her voice creaky, but insistent. “He didn’t invite me to show off the money of the family he left. He’s no fool, and an old woman like myself struggles to stand up from the stacks of mashed potatoes you people call sofas. I am exactly where I would like to be. Thank you, Georgie.”

While the lieutenant’s face shifted a little bit after that, he only bowed before her in acknowledgment of the nickname.

“So? Why am I here?” Mabel asked.

“The man to be sentenced by the Count today is Ezra, one of my soldiers,” Georg explained before anyone else could speak. “Disregarding other charges that have been admitted to and for which he’s been sentenced already, he’s being accused of attempted murder on Rebecca Wainwright, as well as men in her squad of guards.”

“And in cases such as these,” Mabel said, her face unmoving, “this is not a religious matter, but a legal one. The church has no bearing in this. Let the witnesses be spoken with and the truth discerned.”

“In this room are the two primary witnesses,” Aaron said, smoothly taking over for Georg. “Though Rebecca’s claims are supported by her soldiers and those in her squad, they are considered only auxiliary to her own. As a noble in good standing, her word is of greater trustworthiness than that of Ezra, a soldier who was recruited nearly two months ago to participate in the subjugation of the goblinoid army. However, Georg has come here in an attempt to find some measure of clemency for the sentencing.”

“And you hope for my word to reinforce the position of this Ezra?” Mabel asked, looking at Georg.

“Exactly.”

“It is entirely unnecessary and disrespectful for you to have called a member of the clergy here in a civil matter!” Rufus shouted. “There’s no precedent for having an unrelated Acolyte come in to serve as a character witness of sorts for somebody she doesn’t know.”

“Correct,” Mabel nodded.

“One of the key pieces of evidence, however,” Georg said, maintaining the attention and beginning on a path that Rebecca and Rufus both seemed to understand as he  explained, “is the level of injury Rebecca suffered at Ezra’s hands. After all, he is an Adept and can freely wield aura with attacks. Thus, judging by Rebecca’s state, the declaration and the foundation of the accusation is that Ezra’s attacks were in fact made with murderous intent.”

“And in a case wherein judgments must be made as to if an attack that was successfully made was truly potentially deadly,” Mabel continued, nodding once, “there is no better judge than one of the priesthood of the Queen, and so I have been called.”

“Again, exactly correct.”

As Georg nodded his head to the Acolyte, he raised his head with a sickeningly sweet smile towards Rebecca. Her own face went stony as the Acolyte looked at her.

“Then come here, girl,” Mabel said, gesturing towards Rebecca. “I’ll heal you, and when I do so, will be able to determine just how hurt you’ve been.”

“I’m afraid you won’t be able to heal her,” Rufus said with a grimace of what he seemed to think was regret but instead felt oily, “as she’s already been tended to by two lesser healings of the first circle.”

“Then you found an Acolyte who was willing to entirely exhaust themselves in pursuit of healing your daughter?” Mabel wondered, looking at the noble, whose face remained undisturbed. “How selfless of them. Now, I will not repeat myself. Come here, girl.”

Rebecca stood and walked towards Mabel, no longer with the faint, faked limp from before. As soon as the young woman was close to the Acolyte, her green eyes glinting, Mabel picked up her cane and laid it across her knees as she took Rebecca’s “good” hand in both of her own. Golden light emanated from the sitting woman as she stared directly into Rebecca’s face.

Only a few short seconds passed before the light faded, and Mabel’s eyebrows rose very slowly.

“Perhaps you were mistaken,” Mabel’s words came slowly and deliberately. “Because there is no evidence of a first or second circle spell having been cast on our dear, sweet Rebecca in the past day. I do see no small evidence of a creative application of makeup on her face, however.”

“Perhaps it was a third circle spell,” Rebecca started to say, but Mabel leveled a glare at the younger woman that gave her no room to wiggle. Instead of doubling down on her deceit, Rebecca went silent and hung her head before stepping back from the Acolyte.

“If you find a Priest, one who has been blessed by a great enough connection to their god to cast a third circle spell in my presence, one who is willing to stand before me and swear upon the god from whom they draw their power that they have cast a spell of the third circle or higher to heal you, I will change my verdict. After all, if a spell capable of regrowing a lost limb was cast on you, and with such acuity and specificity that I cannot find any remnant of this wound, then you have much to fear from this Ezra.”

Silence settled over the room as Mabel looked at Ezra.

She sat, staring at him for several seconds before she asked, “Can you use aura in the way that has been said today?”

“Yes, milady,” he answered. He had to fight to keep from jumping into an explanation of how he hadn’t attempted to kill anyone, and he managed to hold himself back.

“I’m no lady,” Mabel shook her head. “At this moment, I am Baltisse’s eyes. Now, on a target that the Count designates, would you show the kind of attack you would make if you were trying to kill something?”

“I could do that,” Ezra nodded. “I can’t really hit anything while I’m chained up, though.”

“That’s a fair point,” Mabel nodded. “How about we remove those manacles and let you try?”

“We can’t release the violent criminal and encourage him to use deadly force!” Rebecca said. “As a detective of the constabulary, I can’t allow that to occur and endanger everybody in here to the frenzied lashing out of an unhinged criminal. We don’t have anybody who themselves can use aura to stand up to him once he gets loose.”

“So, a soldier who, according to Lieutenant Georg,” Mabel asked turning away from Ezra to stare at the couch with the Wainwrights sitting on it, “has been training for less than two months is so dangerous that if we unchain him, he’s going to kill everybody in a room full of five members of the constabulary, four soldiers, an Acolyte of Baltisse, and two noblemen who have been trained in at least a passing familiarity with a blade?”

“No, but I wouldn’t put it past him to try to get revenge on the people who are bringing his crimes to light,” Rebecca replied.

“I wasn’t going to give him a deadly weapon, perhaps only a stick or a club. Then, if he turns away from the target that he is being permitted to use his aura on, he can be subjugated.” Mabel’s voice was painfully reasonable as she said it.

“He’s dangerous! You’re taking away the protections that we have to ensure that criminals don’t kill people!” Rebecca’s words were fiery and full of self-righteousness. “There’s a reason that the violent criminal’s hands are bound.”

“If he’s so dangerous that we can’t risk untying him for fear of him being able to take the briefest opportunity to kill somebody,” Mabel asked, “then how did you escape his murderous wrath without taking a serious wound? After all, I have decreed that, without the staking of a priest’s very soul, you have not been affected by a spell of the first or second circle.”

Rebecca’s jaw worked, her face going scarlet, but she held back from saying anything to the Acolyte when she challenged the detective’s words.

After about ten seconds of silence, Mabel spoke again.

“I repeat, if the accused soldier is so dangerous that unchaining him and maintaining him under the guard of another nine trained combatants is life-threateningly dangerous to all of us, how did you survive when fighting him while he was prepared in the streets with only five of your guardsmen? After all, there was not a single death when fighting him at the time, correct?”

“Acolyte Mabel,” Rufus said, his voice not quite angry, but certainly unhappy with her, “why have you taken the side of the criminal when the constabulary has already brought forward their explanations of his guilt? It would appear that you are less concerned with the law than you are with supporting the side that brought you here.”

“Do not presume to tell me what the intent of my judgment is,” Mabel snapped, her voice gaining a strength that the wavering woman had lacked until then. The dignity that she had carried became almost violent in its strength as she continued, “I have come as a representative of the Goddess Baltisse, the Queen. Her realm is that of judgment, and she has always been the perfect ruler. Every successful leader aspires to her example of justice and patience, strength and virtue. I strive to judge righteously, to judge with wisdom and understanding. I seek the truth, and my questions have been in pursuit of the truth. Is that a bother to you?”

Rufus stood, his long mustache quivering with barely repressed anger. “It would appear that the decision has been made. I demand that you, Count Aaron Standage, remember that on this day justice was circumvented.”

“I will remember your complaints,” the Count said with a nod as Mabel brought herself up to her feet and glared at the other nobleman.

“Don’t worry, I will ensure that representatives of each of the gods here in Villy are fully apprised of the situation wherein you continuously questioned, and then walked away from the judgment of an Acolyte of the Queen,” Mabel thundered. “Now go, take your deceitful words and forked tongue from my presence, and if you wish to worship, know that you are unwelcome in the chapel.”

Rufus’s face went white at that, but he didn’t say anything as he stood and stormed from the room. Rebecca’s response was much the same as she followed suit and stalked out of the room, her previous hesitation and demure bearing gone. Despite their attempts otherwise, Ezra’s faint connection with the wind brought their voices to his ears as they stalked away. He drew on his aura to make sure he could actually overhear the words.

“You said this was a sure thing! That the bastard would only sit there and take it!”

“Father, how was I supposed to know he had a connection with an Acolyte of Baltisse?” Rebecca whined. “Mabel retired years ago, and nobody’s seen her in the chapel for nearly a decade! I thought she retired to Brayton to be with family.”

“Then you need to show your face at the damn chapel and never make a mistake like this again,” Rufus hissed. “Now, shut up until we’re home. You never know who’s listening.”

Ezra allowed a smile to crack his face as he revelled in his victory. His internal gloating was interrupted by the Count speaking.

“They’ll actually be prepared for a fight next time,” Aaron said once the pair of nobles were well and truly gone. “I hope you’re prepared for them to keep hitting you like this, Georg.”

“They did this without prompting,” Georg replied. “I didn’t provoke them, they started it. It was an attack of opportunity, though, and I knew where the accusation would be weakest so I hit them there.”

“You’re resorting to using an old woman as a weapon now?” Mabel asked as she settled back onto her chair.

“You’re a flame that burns away deceit and lies,” Georg said, seeming to parrot something that he’d been told a lot. The old woman seemed to approve of the phrase, saying nothing but smiling widely in response.

“So I’m free to go?” Ezra asked, looking meaningfully at his manacles.

“No,” Aaron said, glaring at Ezra with what seemed to be a very real dislike. “You’ve been sentenced to one week in the stockade and to pay a fine of thirty large silver. That’s still the punishment you need to deal with.”

Georg looked at Ezra and nodded, saying, “I protected you from the worst of it, like I said. Now there’s the rest of the price to pay.”
