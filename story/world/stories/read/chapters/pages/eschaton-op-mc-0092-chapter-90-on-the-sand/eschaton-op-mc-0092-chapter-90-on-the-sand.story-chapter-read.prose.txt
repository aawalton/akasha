When the sun dips low and the evening falls, Thutmose packs away his wagon and leaves for an inn he had set up a reservation with in advance. With the Unveiling so close at hand, there’s few rooms left unoccupied in the entire city, despite its size. That might have been a problem if I actually needed to sleep. Instead I just start to wander around the city. Even at night it’s fairly lively. Much of the work required to keep a city of this size running is done after the sun has set, when it’s much cooler.

An army of street sweepers descend on the roads throughout Karnak. I watch as crews climb a hundred and fifty feet straight up to check the aqueducts for blockages or damage. Others use literal flying carpets as work platforms, hauling up equipment to conduct maintenance.

I wander throughout the fantastical and truly magical city of Karnak. There are even automatic street lights. Tiny crystals set in lamp posts flicker with a warm, fire-like light. They remind me of the crystals the Lumens had.

I make my way towards the river that bisects the city. I didn’t get a chance to see it during the day, as myself and Thutmose—I really do have to thank him at some point—were too busy with the tournament. I come to the river and walk down wide limestone steps to a well-lit river-side promenade. It’s a testament to the size of the city that they fit the entire 1,800 foot wide Nile River inside the walls and made it look small. The aqueducts start from here, and I can see dozens of sluices high overhead.

The river itself is astonishingly clean. It’s clearly done by magic, though I’m not sure how. Virtually everything dropped on the street on Earth will find its way to the local river eventually. Trash, toxic run off, even sewage from leaky pipes. I’m sure bodies of missing people wouldn’t be hard to find either if divers could see through the murk.

Here in Karnak though, this version of the Nile is sparkling clean. I can even see the riverbed without relying on non-visible spectrums or other sensor systems. I watch as fish dart between patches of river grass that gently sway in the current.

The magically pure river is connected to the aqueducts via tall tubes of individual floating rings. Each one is visibly glowing with a soft blue light, and all of them are some of the most magical objects I’ve ever seen. Every single individual ring is at least equivalent to one of the Hearts of Growth I found.

Water gently floats upwards through the glowing ring pumps. Tiny arcs of energy jump to the water from the rings every so often. I’m not sure if that’s a purification method or just extraneous energy discharge. Soft blue lighting races down the water columns that feed the aqueducts and spread out into the river. The Nile here is ethereal and magical. It glows in light of the moon and the magical pumps. I wonder if it’s by its own nature, or by yet another of the Great King’s works.

I hear multiple sets of footsteps above and behind me coming from the main road. From the clatter of metal I can tell there’s six individuals and five of them are wearing armor. The sixth sounds to be wearing long robes made of cloth. Linen most likely, given by the soft noises it makes.

A man with the head of a falcon descends the steps. He’s wearing extremely ornate robes dyed in hues of red and orange. Gold filigree is woven throughout, and he wears a medallion with the symbol of Amun-Ra—a golden disk meant to resemble the sun. He’s only a tad shorter than I am. It’s both odd and relaxing not looking down on people. I hate how much more comfortable I am here. This world fits me so much more than Earth does.

Only one of his guards, a well-toned and dark skinned woman with the head of a hyena, joins him. She has a small bronze shield buckled to one arm with a curved blade at her waist. Her fingers dance over the khopesh’s hilt nervously.

“I am Ramentauneb, royal messenger from the Great King Amun-Ra,” the falcon-headed man says. “Are you enjoying our great city, Visitor Machina?”

“I am. It is honestly amazing. I did not think such feats of construction could be done with stone.”

“Our magicks and magi are quite powerful. The most in this world and every other we have heard of from Visitors. Bar the Serpent Slayers, of course.”

“Certainly more advanced than what we have so far. Not to mention more powerful. Our world is new to magic, I admit.”

Ramentauneb nods before drawing himself up.

“We bring tidings from the Great King Amun-Ra,” he says far more formally. “We are aware you have decided to join the great games to set forth for the enjoyment of all.

“As a Visitor and a powerful individual, the Great King is willing to allow you to join the Finals Bracket that begins the following dawn. We all know that your strength guarantees you a place anyway. We would like to save our loyal subjects from unnecessary pain.”

“I wouldn’t have pushed harder than necessary,” I say defensively. “I’m not a monster.”

“Our experience with Visitors from your world is limited, and the only other example we have is of… let us say casual cruelty.”

The guard leaning against the flood wall behind us takes a step forward as I lose control of my Aura for just a moment.

“I am so fucking sick of these people,” I hiss. The royal messenger waves his guard back as he clears his throat.

“We are aware of your history. We pay close attention to Visitors.”

“One that’ll be short lived. Just tell me where he is.”

The royal messenger eyes me for a minute. His bird eyes narrow as he studies me.

“No,” he finally says. “You’ll meet him in the Colosseum. Until then you will not. The Great King has declared it to be so.”

“I could end it right now.”

“And how much of the city would you level in your haste?” The royal messenger scoffs. “We understand your nature. But the most dangerous man is not a tyrant hungry for conquest. He is a good man who believes he is fighting a righteous war. A man who truly believes that to save the world something must be done at all costs. A man who sees the world as good and evil, as sand and water. They stop at nothing. You will stop at nothing. You will meet your counterpart in the finals bracket. There is nothing more to be said.”

The royal messenger walks away, guard in tow. I fight to control my temper. It’s becoming harder and harder as time goes on.

Soon, I promise that ball of fury deep inside. Soon.

I meet Thutmose at his wagon before the dawn. He’s an early riser, though only slightly earlier than most. Given how brutally hot the desert is here, most of the daily outdoor tasks are done early in the morning or late in the evening. Unlike Earth cities, the limestone roads don’t hold heat nearly as well, so the nights are much cooler than I would expect them to be. Concrete is great at many things, and holding heat is unfortunately one of them.

I’ve been to Phoenix during the summer, and even at night it was still ninety degrees. That’s just not right.

I explain what Ramentauneb said to me last night. With our plans changed and us no longer returning to the Colosseum until tomorrow, I help Thutmose set up his shop.

“Ah, a shame. I was hoping to earn some money off you, yes. Even the open qualifier matches are open to betting. Ah, well. Instead I shall keep my stall open today. So all is well, yes.”

He pulls a board game out of a pack and brings it to the table. Like yesterday, he has a pitcher of lemon water, a popular non-alcoholic drink.

“Ah, let me teach you a game, yes? This is senet, have you heard of it?”

“Yes,” I say warily. I haven’t yet told him that his world is a copy of mine.

“Oh? Interesting! I thought you were one of the first visitors from your world to ours.”

“I am. It’s… a long story.”

“Ah! Good, very good. Tell it, and I will prepare the game.”

“This world… is a copy of mine.” I gesture to everything around us. “The architecture is similar, the names are similar. Hell, the Fracture I passed through to get here was in a place called the Temple of Karnak. Even you!

“Ancient peoples from thousands of years ago had legends and stories of people with animal heads. Birds and falcons and jackals and more. They worshipped them as incarnations of gods. The Kingdom lasted for thousands of years. It’s one of the oldest nations in recorded history, if not the oldest we have records of.”

Thutmose nods his head as he sets out pieces, seven to chess-like pawns to each of us. The board has thirty spaces, with six of the squares intricately decorated.

“I see, I see,” he says after a moment. “How do you know your world copied ours? Our world is just as old, maybe older. The Age of Apophis was long and filled with mystery. We have few records from that time. How do you know that you were the original? Or that we are the original?”

“I… don’t, I guess.”

“Ah, I understand very little of your world, yes. But you keep saying things are broken, and everything is changing. I think that your world was always broken, just a little. Maybe now it is fixed, yes? It is clear that you did not have a full understanding of creation. Now that everything is so different, you believe it is wrong, somehow. Not so!”

He pulls out a set of four sticks, one side painted white and the other side painted black.

“Perhaps our worlds copy each other, or another, or maybe none at all. Maybe we are cosmic brothers, born from the same spark of creation. Your world may be a late bloomer, as parents often say, hahaha!”

He shrugs.

“Well, with that out of the way, would you like to play a game of senet?”

“I don’t actually know the rules,” I admit. “No one really does, just modern reconstructions.”

“Ah, very good! I shall get to teach you then, my honor. Yes, so this is how you play…”

Thutmose teaches me how to play senet, and it’s actually pretty fun. The rules are a little different to what historians reconstructed from texts, and it plays not unlike Sorry. You have to get all your pieces to the end first, and you can sabotage your opponent in the process of doing so. Thutmose destroys me in the first game. There’s a surprising level of strategy to it. Throwing the sticks decides how far you can move, but there’s sometimes reasons to not move as far as you can.

There’s a few pauses every now and then when an interested customer shows up and gives Thutmose’s wagon a careful eye. Some of them come over to talk to me of all people. Visitors might be rare, but they’re not entirely mythical figures. They’re still worth talking to I suppose. Who wouldn’t want to learn about an entire new world?

I wonder how large all of creation is. We’re seemingly no longer constrained to our own universe, though there’s still a small part of me that’s honestly terrified this is just a Fracture. That everything I’ve seen is nothing but a story that will vanish into the aether as soon as I leave—assuming I kill the Great King.

I’m more scared about what happens if it’s real and the Unveiling is actually bringing Karnak City to us. What happens if tens of millions of people capable of slugging it out with a team of 3-Stars just appear on earth? Karnak would become an unstoppable superpower in an instant.

There is not a single nation on Earth that could ever hope to defeat them, and even if the world united we still might lose. I’m sure nuclear weapons would be deployed en-mass, and the Ascended would be hard pressed to fight on every front. Especially since I don’t know how strong this Great King is, and not every Ascended could be counted on to fight against Karnak.

Can I take the risk? As kind as Thutmose has been, I can’t count on every single one of his people to be just as kind. He said that those who live in the Burning Sands outside the walls are as prone to violence as humanity might be.

I’m just a college dropout. I never wanted to hold the fate of the world in the palm of my hand.

“Ah, well played,” Thutmose says as I pull my last piece off the board.

It was a well timed victory, another turn and I likely would have lost.

“I learned from the best.”

Thutmose laughs. “Ah, another game?”

I nod, and I start another match with my new friend.

Once the afternoon slowdown starts, I stand up with a sigh. Thutmose won most of the games, eight to four in his favor.

“Done?” He asks.

“Yeah, I think so. It was fun, but I think I need to prepare for when I win the tournament. I need to figure out how exactly I’m going to pay for those crystals. I’ll need as many as I can get. Hopefully all of them.”

Thutmose looks a little nervous.

“I… I may know someone who could help. I did not mention him before because we did not part well. He may not be happy to see me. We both said things we should have not. I regret my own words, but I cannot say the same for him.”

“I hate to ask for your help, especially after all you’ve done, Thutmose, but—” he holds up a hand to stop me.

“Ah, Machina, you have nothing to thank me for. You were a Visitor, to show hospitality to a guest is the right thing to do, yes. Now you are a friend. I know this is important, that more lives than there are stars in the sky hang in the balance. I could not call myself a good man, nor worthy of the Field of Reeds if I refuse to help. I may be a humble merchant, but it seems the Gods have called upon me to help, so I shall.”

He gives me a weak grin.

“And besides. It has been many years since we last spoke. Perhaps he may not be angry, yes? Maybe time has cooled such passions.”

I follow Thutmose towards the river, though this time we move into less fortunate areas far from the Colosseum and the Dedun Market. We hitch a ride on a magical tram. It looks very similar to the tram system in San Francisco. Wheels roll around in slight grooves in the road I mistook for drainage gullies, and a driver at the front guides the tram through the city. I assume it’s powered by magic, but I’m not sure where. There’s a hollow in the floor of the tram, so I assume the power source is in there.

I get several odd looks from the other passengers before they eventually turn back to their own business. Many of them are clutching satchels or bags filled with groceries or sheets of papyrus-based paper. A few people are reading what looks suspiciously like a newspaper.

Both myself and Thutmose hop on it at one of its stops.

“The Great King decreed these trams to be available for all. Karnak is the largest city in the entire Kingdom. The largest in all of Khetmenet, yes. So large a man could walk for days and not reach the other side.”

Thutmose spends the next several minutes giving me an impromptu lesson on the history of the tram system, though the word they use for it doesn’t really translate to ‘tram’. The exact word is ‘land-ferry’, with cargo variants referred to as ‘land-barges’.

A small child pokes my leg, and I look down.

“Where are you from?” she asks curiously. She has the head of an owl like her mother, and has a permanent curious look to her. Like she can’t quite get enough of the world.

Her mother gasps and admonishes her daughter.

“Amati! Don’t bother strangers!”

“I’m so sorry,” she says to me.

“It’s quite alright.”

I look down at the little girl, who barely comes up to my knee. If she were a human, I’d say she’d be six or seven years old. Eight on the outside.

“I’m from very far away. Very, far away.”

“Are you a Visitor?” She asks with gleaming eyes. “My Da said there were some in the city.”

“I am.”

She giggles and jumps in excitement. Other passengers look over and smile at her. Joy is often infectious.

“What’s it like there?”

“That’s a hard question to answer,” I say slowly. “Our world is many things. We have many peoples, many nations, and many cultures. Did you know we have seven thousand languages?”

She gasps. “Oh, that’s so many.”

“I even speak most of them. Not all of them were easy to find teachers for.”

In reality I was generally limited to the languages that had online digital support, which is actually surprisingly few. There are thousands of languages that have almost nothing online. Still, I think substituting teachers for online material is probably an acceptable omission of the truth. Trying to explain the internet is probably a journey too far.

“Would you like to see a few pictures of my world?”

She almost shoots through the roof with how excited she is. I pull out my phone. It’s still nearly fully charged, as I never use it for anything, but it’s only the digital screen I have.

I interface with it and start pulling memories from various places.

“Now this is a place called New York City, it’s one of the largest cities we have…”

By the time we have to get off for Thutmose’s associate, I had attracted quite a crowd. I do my best to make sure everything I show is as undamaged as possible, but there were many places I simply didn’t go until after they’d been ravaged by ruptures. One of the pictures that gets the most attention is actually a view of the Aurora Borealis from one of the times I’ve been to the North Pole.

In retrospect, it makes sense. Not a single one of these people has probably ever seen snow. Ice cubes for refrigeration or drinks maybe, but snow? Not a chance. Amati sobs when we have to leave, though Thutmose gives her mother a modern-looking business card. It has the location of his wagon on it. I guess another showing is in the future. I hope I have time.

We wander towards some rather shady looking dockyards, in more ways than one. Various people are loitering about, and not in the ‘I’m slacking on my job’ or ‘out for a smoke break’ kind of way. Rather, most of them appear to be trying to be subtle guards, though they certainly are failing at the subtlety aspect.

We arrive at a warehouse and the number of guards has increased. I notice they’re wielding small daggers and clubs. A pair by a door are openly wielding khopeshes, though these are shoddy and rusted.

One of them is a serpent-headed man, and when he spots Thutmose he glares.

“Thutty, what are you doing back here? The boss isn’t going to be happy to see you. You should leave while you still can,” he says.

Thutmose raises his hands.

“I am well aware,” he says without his characteristic cheer. “We parted ways with sand between us, yes. But I would not come back if I did not think it was worth it.”

“Who’s he?” He says, pointing at me.

“A friend. A Visitor friend.” I look the serpent guard in the eyes, and he shudders away after a brief moment.

“Alright,” he grumbles. “Your funeral.” He opens the door.

I follow Thutmose inside.
