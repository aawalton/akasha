Jane woke to the soft light of a Lee-day morning. The town was still quiet after yesterday’s full rest day. Her shop would only need to stay open for a few hours. Most of her regulars would come early, stocking up on their needs for the day. Then the shops would empty out as most people returned home to enjoy their friends and families.

She lay in bed for a few extra minutes, staring at the ceiling and letting her mind drift. The showcase was coming. The king was coming. Her menu was set, her recipes practiced, her techniques as sharp as she could make them in the time she had. There was nothing more to do on that front, really, except wait.

It made her restless.

Getting up, she went through her morning routine, moving a bit slower than usual in acknowledgment of the day's relaxed pace. It didn’t take her long to make a simplified version of her usual stock. When she opened, the customers came less in waves and more in one prolonged burst, just as she had expected. That was normal for a Lee-day. Getting bread would be the most intense thing these folks did all day, and they usually wanted it out of the way early.

Jane served them all, wrapping loaves and making change and answering questions. It was all easy, reflexive work by now.

"Got anything special today?" a middle-aged man asked.

"Just the usual. Lee-day, you know." Jane was in her Glenfall-voice, talking to her customers as if she was a local. By now, she kind of was.

"Right, right.” The man nodded with an understanding smile. “Give me two of those loaves, then.”

By midmorning, Jane’s shelves were nearly bare. A few stragglers wandered in, picking over what remained, but the real business of the day was done. She found herself standing behind her counter with nothing to do, which was exactly the kind of lazy restfulness a Lee-day was supposed to provide.

Today, though, it was exactly what Jane didn't want.

She tried to occupy herself with cleaning, but she’d had to keep her shop presentable during her days of practice, so the place was already mostly clean. She would have reorganized her storage shelves, but they were already organized.

Jane swept the floor, wiped down the counter, and dusted her shelves. None of it took more than a few minutes, and none of it quieted the buzzing excitement in her head.

The ovens sat cold and empty, waiting for her to fire them up again. She stared at them, hands on her hips, thinking about all the things she could be doing if she weren't trying to be sensible.

She could experiment with new fillings for the buns. There were combinations she hadn't tried yet, vegetables and meats and spices that might work better than what she had planned. She could tweak the herb mixture in her keln, push it in a different direction, and see if she could find something even more interesting than what she'd settled on. She could try a completely different approach to the burnt-top bread, or even shift to making pies and cakes, something she’d willfully left out of her royal menu.

All of these were bad ideas. She knew they were bad ideas. Changing her menu now, with only days to go, would mean starting over from scratch. Any changes she made might set her down a road to improvement, but that road would take days or even weeks to travel, much less time than she probably had. The new versions might be better eventually, but mostly she’d just be disrupting a good, stable stopping point.

Her hands itched to do something anyway.

Don’t be stupid, Jane. Just leave everything alone.

She found herself still staring at the ovens. She decided to say the words out loud, describing her situation to herself verbally to see if that defused her impulsiveness.

"I want to redo my menu for the king, but it's a bad idea."

"No room for improvement?"

Jane spun around. Allen was standing in the doorway, leaning against the frame and grinning. He seemed to know she had been talking to herself, rather than to him, but she decided to duck into the conversational cover he’d provided anyway.

"There's always room for improvement," she said. "There’s just no time to find it now. Not properly, anyway. If I start changing things, I'll just make everything worse."

"Then don't."

"I know. That's what I keep telling myself." She sighed. "I'm just fidgety. I feel like I should be doing something, but the preparation is done. There's nothing left to do except wait. I'm terrible at waiting."

Allen pushed off from the doorframe and walked over to her. He didn't say anything, just opened his arms, and Jane stepped into them gratefully. She pressed her face against his chest and breathed in the familiar metal, sawdust, and work-sweat smells of him. It was nice.

"I'm sorry," she said, her voice muffled by his shirt. "I've been so caught up in all of this that I haven't even asked how you're doing. How's your work going? What have you been making?"

"It's fine." Allen's voice rumbled through his chest, a pleasant vibration against her cheek. "Busy, like everyone else. The showcase has all the craftsmen scrambling to put their best foot forward."

Jane pulled back enough to look up at him. "What are you showing? I don't even know."

Allen shrugged, the motion easy and unconcerned. “A few things. The winch, mostly. The one I built to haul the barrels out of the lake, back when your dragon was causing trouble."

Jane remembered that winch. It had been an impressive piece of engineering, and Allen had built it in a matter of days, working from rough sketches and pure intuition. It had worked perfectly on the first try, too. She had been too distracted by dragon-related concerns at the time to appreciate it properly, but looking back on it, she felt it was exactly the kind of thing that should be shown off.

"That's a good choice," she said. "It's impressive. Even someone who doesn't know anything about the winch can see how well it works, and it’s got a whole story behind it."

"That's what I figured. Besides that, I'm working on a puzzle box. Something fancy, with hidden compartments and mechanisms that only open if you know the trick." He smiled. "And a couple of little tools. Intricate stuff, meant for fine work. That sort of thing."

Jane tried to picture those items in a royal showcase, laid out on a table next to the work of other craftsmen. The winch made sense. It was big and dramatic, the kind of thing that drew the eye and demanded attention. The puzzle box sounded interesting enough to be impressive in its own way, too. But the tools?

"Will the king understand the tools?" she asked. "I mean, they sound amazing, but does a king know what precision tools like those are even for?"

"I don't know," he said. "Maybe not. But my job is to show the best I have, and that's the best I have. If the king doesn't understand it, that's not really my problem. Right?”

Jane considered that. It was a very ‘Allen’ way of looking at things, practical and unbothered by concerns that couldn't be controlled. She envied him for that, sometimes. He had the ability to do his best work and then let go of the outcome, while her own mind never seemed to stop spinning.

She could hardly resent him for it, though. Not when the main way she observed the trait was his frequent use of it to steady her own more liquid emotions.

Like what was happening now.

"That's a good attitude," she said. "I should probably adopt it."

"You should. But you won't." He grinned at her. "You're going to worry about this until the showcase is over, and then you're going to worry about something else. That's just how you are."

"Am I really that consistent?"

Allen laughed, and apparently decided to not answer her question. He slid back from their hug and took Jane by the hand.

"Come on. Let's go for a walk. You need to get out of this shop before you talk yourself into something stupid."

The shop could close early on a Lee-day, and no one would think twice about it. The rest of the town was already winding down, and she had just as much right to relax as anyone else.

"All right," she agreed. "A walk sounds good. Let me just lock up."

She slipped on her good walking shoes and followed Allen out the door, taking a moment to flip the sign in the window to indicate she was now closed.

They walked without any particular destination in mind, just chatting and taking in the now-familiar sights of the city. A few shopkeepers were visible through their windows, tidying up or chatting with late customers, but the usual bustle of commerce had faded to something slower and more peaceful. The people on the streets walked with a kind of gentle laziness, heading somewhere but in no special hurry. Glenfall had plenty of practice with its own Lee-days, and it showed in the disposition of everyone Jane could see.

She found her eyes drawn to the docks as they passed. The spot looked so ordinary now, so peaceful, that it was hard to believe it had once been the site of such drama. Dragons and contamination and magical crises had all faded from the public memory, and from Jane’s, until these were just docks again: a place from which to launch pleasure-craft and fishing boats, and nothing more.

"Hungry?" Allen asked as they passed a small stall near the market square.

Jane realized that she was. She had been so focused on her baking that she hadn't eaten anything but a few bites of keln that morning. Her stomach chose that moment to remind her of the oversight with one of its famous audible growls.

"Starving, apparently."

The stall sold fried dough balls, cooked in hot oil until they were golden and crispy. They were a common street food in Glenfall, cheap and filling and perfect for eating while walking. Allen bought two paper bags of them, waited while the stall’s keeper dusted them well with cinnamon and sugar, and handed one bag to Jane while keeping the other for himself.

Munching on the sweet, perfectly fried treats, they continued their walk. The food was exactly what Jane needed to quiet her rumbling stomach and settle her restless mind. She fell into step beside Allen, chewing contentedly.

"These are good," she said, popping another dough ball into her mouth. "Maybe I should learn to make them. Add them to my menu."

Allen laughed. "See? You can't help yourself. Even on your day off, you're thinking about new recipes."

"It's not the same thing. This would be for later, after the showcase. A new project to work on." Jane grinned. "Besides, I bet you're already thinking about your next invention. You never really take a break from that, either.”

"Maybe." Allen's eyes had that faraway look they got when he was turning over a problem in his mind. "I've been thinking about water wheels, actually. There's a mill on the other side of town that's been having trouble with theirs. They tried to make it bigger, and it didn’t quite work. The mechanism keeps jamming."

"And you think you can fix it?"

"I think I can try. That's usually how the process starts."

The path they had taken wound around the edge of the lake, offering views of the water from different angles along the way. Jane watched the lake slowly fill with boats. Laughter floated across the waves as children played on the decks and reached down to catch the passing water in their hands.

"What do you think the king is really here for?" she asked eventually. "There've been all sorts of rumors."

“I wanted to talk about that.” Allen frowned slightly. “Have you heard the bit about him looking for a bride for his son?”

“I have.”

"And you think that's true?"

"I have no idea. It seems unlikely. Why would a prince look for a wife in Glenfall? There's no one here that important."

Allen was quiet for a moment. It was while Jane waited for him to speak that she realized what he was about to say.

Finally, he got the words out.

"You're here.”

Jane stopped walking. "Allen…”

"You're here. You're important.” He stopped too, turning to face her. “You're an archmage, Jane. One of the most powerful magic users in the kingdom. A hero. If the king is looking for someone impressive to marry his son off to, you'd be on the list."
