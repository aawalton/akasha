While Aodhán tested the limits of his power, a cloud of gloom hung over the Illusion Sect—deepest over a small garden of white roses tucked into an isolated corner.

Elder Malverin sat within that garden, dry-eyed and silent, gazing at the headstone of a woman whose true face she had never seen, yet had loved more than anything.

Lysandra Quinn.

Walker of the path of fractured perfection.

Year 2276.

A faint smile touched her lips at the sight of the name, and she raised a hand to caress the silver letters.

They were rough beneath her touch. Cold, too.

Her gaze drifted to the roses—grown from seeds Lysandra had once pressed into her palm as a gift of utmost perfection. She hadn’t seen it then, but now, looking down at their quiet precision—each petal pristine, each curve deliberate, as though shaped by an unseen hand—she had to admit they were indeed perfect. Pure. Just as Lysandra had fashioned herself. Just as—

“No,” Malverin whispered, cutting the thought short before it could swallow her whole.

It had been six months since Lysandra’s death. Six months since her life had been cut short by the transmigrants of the Storm Sect. Yet the grief remained—raw as an open wound, heavy as a lump lodged in her chest.

She hated it. Hated that she still came here every day, like a penitent bound to her guilt.

But she couldn’t stop.

Lysandra had meant more to her than she had ever spoken aloud—more than she had ever allowed herself to fully admit. Perhaps that was why the memory of their first meeting had never faded.

Even now, nearly a decade later, she could still remember the day Lysandra first arrived, escorted by merchants from a mundane city. She had been only fifteen at the time—so innocent and quiet, fragile in a way that felt almost unreal. Like a white rose untouched by the world.

But even then, at that young age, she had been hidden. Cloaked from head to toe in a veil of flawless illusions that concealed her trauma and scars beneath a face too perfect to question.

A face she had never let go.

Not once in six years had she dispelled it.

Her illusions had been perfect.

Until Aodhán Brystion tore them away to expose the truth beneath, cruelly advertising her imperfections to the world and forever desecrating her memory.

Malverin was not a fool. She understood war. Understood that death was inevitable. In Aodhán's place, she probably would have done the same thing.

Yet this was different.

It hurt far more than losing the war. More than losing the Illusion Sect's only transmigrant.

Because she had seen it coming.

She had seen it when Aspwick first proposed sending their transmigrants into battle. She had seen it when Lysandra chose Aodhán as her opponent.

She had seen the end from the very beginning. And despite how doubtful it was that she could have stopped it—knowing how obsessed Lysandra was—the possibility remained that she might have changed this fate, if only she had stood her ground.

If only she had refused to give in.

The possibility was small, yet it ate at her like a thorn that would not be pulled free.

Her vision blurred.

The tears came again—silent at first, falling onto the cold stone as they had every day for the past six months. Pain welled in her chest, fresh and raw, and despite her efforts to hold it back, she couldn’t.

The dam broke a moment later. A sob tore free from her throat—then another—until her entire body trembled beneath the weight of it.

"Why?" she choked, clutching a white rose in her fist. "Why?"

It was in that state that a messenger found her. His expression was sober as he paused at the edge of the garden, waiting until her sobs softened before speaking.

“Elder Malverin,” he said quietly. “There is someone here to see you.”

“I don’t want to see anyone.” She shook her head, wiping at her tears. “Not now. Not today.”

The messenger hesitated, then stepped a little closer, careful not to intrude.

“I’m sorry, Elder Malverin… but this is not someone I can turn away.”

She turned to him, eyes red and unfocused. "Who is it?"

"Your brother, ma'am."

"What?"

Shock lanced through her as sharp as lightning, and she lurched to her feet. "Are you certain?"

“Yes, ma’am.” He bowed his head. “He has just concluded an audience with the First Seat and requested to see you.”

Malverin stared at him, her heart pounding, before giving a slow, unsteady nod. “Where is he?”

“The West Dream Hall.”

She swallowed, forcing herself steady. “I’ll… I’ll come immediately.”

The messenger inclined his head and withdrew.

Left alone once more, Malverin released a long, shuddering breath.

Her brother, Severian, was a strict, overprotective man. Having raised both her and their younger sister, Emilia, after their parents’ passing, he had long since stepped into the role of father—and she had never quite stopped seeing him that way.

Even as children, it had been obvious that he would never awaken the illusion affinity. He was too practical for it. Too unwilling to surrender himself to falsehoods, no matter how beautiful.

Instead, he had awakened something far more dangerous.

The soul affinity.

An affinity so scarce there were only a handful of cultivators in the continent who bore it. But her brother was no mere cultivator. He was a High Elder—and for him to come all this way just to see her, it had to be important.

Inhaling deeply, she dusted the mud off her robes, wove an illusion of happiness over her face, and began walking to the Hall.

The West Dream Hall lay far from Lysandra's grave, and since illusion did not lend itself to flight or speed, it took her nearly half an hour to reach its luminous corridors.

She found him even before she laid eyes on him—the weight of his power so tangible it distorted the weave of illusions threaded through the entire hall.

Heart pounding, she turned the final bend, her robes sweeping illusory petals off the transparent floor.

And then she saw him.

He stood at the center of the hall, unmoving, as though the dream itself revolved around him rather than the other way around. Violet runes shimmered within his irises—deep, steady, inescapable—meeting her white-gold gaze without wavering.

Her breath hitched. Her body trembled.

It had been seven years since she’d last seen him. Seven years since she’d heard a word from him. Yet despite her desire to put up a brave front—to face him as an equal, as an elder who had grown in his absence—seeing him in the flesh shattered her resolve.

All at once, she was no longer the respected elder of the Illusion Sect, but a child carrying more than she could bear.

Her vision blurred again. Tears welled and spilled freely. But this time they were tears of joy. Tears of burdens lifted.

"Brother…" she choked, the word trembling as it left her lips.

She did not make it another step.

He moved first.

In an instant his arms were around her—firm, unyielding, impossibly steady. Warmth engulfed her, seeping into the hollow spaces she had tried so hard to ignore. His scent enveloped her, achingly familiar even after all these years.

Malverin broke.

A sob tore from her chest as she clutched at him, fingers tightening in his robes as though he might vanish if she let go. The years, the rank, the composure she had built in his absence—none of it mattered.

In his arms, she was no elder.

No cultivator.

Only a little sister—tired of being strong.

She wept without restraint, her body shaking as the grief she had held at bay finally found release.

Severian said nothing.

He offered no comfort. He asked no questions. He simply held her the same way he had done when they were younger. The same way he had done when they had lost their parents.

Malverin clung to that silence, to that steadiness, like a lifeline. For one fleeting moment, she did not want to let it go. Did not want to return to the weight waiting beyond his arms.

But after several long minutes, she forced herself to pull away.

To stand on her own again.

He let her do so without saying anything. His gaze lingered—concerned, searching—yet he remained silent, waiting for her to speak.

Malverin exhaled deeply before meeting his eyes once more.

"Hello, brother," she smiled. "It's been too long."

Severian's gaze softened. He nodded. "It has. It brings me comfort to see you, sister."

Malverin chuckled softly. “It does me, too.” Then her expression sobered. “I wish you’d come sooner.”

Something crossed Severian’s face, but he buried it beneath a smile. “I’m sorry. Things have been… hectic. This was the earliest I could make it.”

Malverin smiled. “I understand. I should have visited, too.”

“You should have,” Severian said with a chuckle. “You would see me more if you visited as often as Emilia. She and her daughter practically live in my sect now.”

“My High Elder won’t be too pleased about that,” Malverin said, amused. “And I imagine yours won’t be either.”

“We are a small sect, Verin. My High Elder jumps for joy at every addition, regardless of affinity.”

Malverin chuckled at the image before shifting her gaze to the transparent glass. “So,” she said quietly, “what brings you to the Illusion Sect?”

“You,” Severian answered without hesitation. “I heard about the war.” His gaze sobered. “I heard how it ended. I’ve been worried about you.”

“I’m fine,” Malverin lied. “I’m happy—”

“You’ve been crying,” Severian cut in gently. “Your soul is heavy—burdened with guilt and grief. You’re not fine.” He leaned forward, his brows furrowing with concern. “Is this about the war?”

Malverin shook her head. “Severian—”

“Is it about the girl, then?” he asked. “The transmigrant you lost?”

Malverin looked up, unable to hide any longer. Severian’s gaze softened further, and she closed her eyes, refusing to give in to her own weakness.

“I didn’t realize how much she meant to me,” she whispered, her voice cracking. “I thought I was only being overprotective because she was my only transmigrant. I thought—”

He placed his hands on her shoulders. “It’s all right, sister. It’s not your fault. The blame lies with those who killed her.”

“No.” Malverin shook her head hastily. “I can’t blame them. It was war. We lost.”

“It doesn’t matter,” Severian replied, his gaze hardening. “I’ll blame them for the both of us.”

Malverin frowned at him, but when he winked, a laugh slipped free despite herself.

“You’re more playful than you usually are.”

Severian shrugged lightly. “It’s been seven years, Verin. Maybe I’ve changed.”

“Maybe.” Malverin nodded, smiling. “The last time I saw you, you had just broken through the bottleneck at Tier 190. You must be nearing ascension now.”

Severian’s smile froze, and for a fleeting moment, the pressure in the room thickened before dissolving once more.

He smiled cheekily. “I'm a soul cultivator. I’m always nearing ascension."

Malverin laughed, the sound free and unburdened, as Severian repeated a joke he had been making since the day he awakened. The familiar humor, the nostalgia of it, eased the lump in her chest, and for the first time in a while, she felt a little lighter.

Severian’s smile widened as she laughed, joy plain on his face. “You’re too easy sister.”

“You have to let that joke go. It’s dead at this point.”

“And yet you laugh every time I make it. It seems very much alive to me.”

Malverin shook her head, studying him for a moment before sighing. "The deed is done, Severian. I’ll get over it. There's nothing left to change."

“That’s not true.” Severian’s frown returned. “I’m your big brother. There’s always something I can do—and I will do something.”

A faint chill crept into the air as he leaned closer. “The Storm Sect may have won the war, but I tell you, they're not as untouchable as they believe."

"Severian…" Malverin exhaled, wary now. "If you're thinking of starting another war, forget it. They’re too strong, and their allies are too numerous. You'll only waste lives."

"I agree." Severian nodded, his gaze unwavering. "But wars aren't the only way to bring people down." He studied her, his voice quiet but edged with steel. "They've hurt you sister. They have to pay for that.”

Malverin closed her eyes. The headstone flashed in her mind. Pain pricked her heart, and though she wanted to give in, she knew her brother too well.

“Is this for me, Sev? Or for you?” she opened her eyes. “You can’t fight all my battles.”

“No, but I can fight this one.” Severian whispered, his eyes darkening. “And what does it matter if it’s for me or you?”

He leaned forward. "Don't you want to see them fall, Verin? Don't you want justice for the girl you lost?"

Malverin looked away, and for a moment she said nothing. The headstone rose in her mind once more, followed by the roses— white, perfect, and forever untouched by the girl who had planted them.

Her hands curled into fists as anger sparked deep within her.

“No,” she finally replied, her lips quivering. "What I want is not justice. I want to put an end to the Storm Sect once and for all. I want to reduce them to rubble—to tear them down so completely they can never rise again." She glared at her brother. "You cannot give me that, brother. The deed is done. Better to let it go."

Severian stared at her for a long moment before nodding. "You're right. I cannot give it to you. I'm not a god."

Despite herself, disappointment flickered in Malverin's eyes. She nodded slowly. "I understand."

Severian studied her, weighing something, before finally speaking. “There is something I can give you, though. Something I believe only I may be able to do.”

Malverin glanced back at him, a fragile spark of hope igniting in her gaze. “What is it?”

Severian smiled. “I gained a special ability at Tier 190. It allows me to sense a looming ascension when I read the Origin Planes.”

Malverin’s eyes widened. “That’s… are you serious?”

Severian’s smile widened. “I read them six months ago.”

“That’s amazing,” Malverin said, smiling in congratulations. But then she frowned. “I don’t understand where you’re going with this.”

Severian leaned forward slightly, a small grin pulling at his lips. “Edrick Ghaltris of the Storm Sect ascended yesterday. I read the Origin Planes six months ago.” His grin widened. “I knew he would ascend. I also knew he was an inheritor…”

He trailed off, expecting Malverin to follow his train of thought.

She frowned. “Sev, what does Edrick’s ascension have to do with me?”

Severian sighed. “You’re not thinking, Verin. He’s an inheritor who ascended. He must have left a legacy.” His lips curled into a grin once more. “And whoever finds it becomes a double inheritor.”

This time, Malverin didn’t need help connecting the dots.

“You intend to find it first.”

“Indeed,” Severian said, leaning back with a smile. “I want the soul fragment Edrick Ghaltris must have left within it. I’ll give the seed to someone who shares our interests—someone who would go to the ends of the earth for us if we gave them a gift like this.”

Malverin stilled, a name already forming.

Olivia.

Olivia was Emilia’s daughter, twenty years of age and still unawakened. She was the only unawakened member of the family, since neither Malverin nor Severian had children of their own.

If they found that legacy and gave it to Olivia, they would not merely be helping their niece awaken. They would be planting a seed within the Storm Sect itself.

An ally bound by blood. An ally who was family.

Excitement stirred within her, rising in a slow, intoxicating swell. It was obvious now that Severian’s interest in the Storm Sect was not for her sake alone, but Malverin didn’t care.

She glanced up at him, her mind racing as she forced herself to ask the only question that mattered.

“How?”

Legacy seeds were notoriously difficult to find. In all her years, Malverin had never heard of anyone deliberately searching for one and succeeding. Those who found them usually did so by accident—by fate.

But Severian seemed utterly unconcerned.

He folded his arms, a faint smile playing on his lips. “Remember that special ability I told you about? Well, it turns out legacies containing soul fragments emit a certain… echo.” His smile widened. “It won’t be easy, but I’m confident I can find it before the Storm Sect does.”

Malverin stared at her brother in shock, unable to believe her ears. What he was proposing bordered on sacrilege—an act against fate itself.

And yet…

She could not deny the excitement rising within her. The relief. The sense that, at last, she would be doing something—anything—to answer what had been taken from her.

A smile touched her lips then, and she leaned forward.

“How can I help?” she asked softly. “And how soon can we begin?”

Severian’s smile deepened.

“Oh, little sister,” he said, “I’ve already begun.”

Above him, the looming pressure of the system tightened, and his smile wobbled—just for a moment—before he steadied it and clasped Malverin's palms in his, squeezing lightly.

"Let me avenge you, sister. Let me help you bring the Storm Sect to its knees."

Malverin looked at him for a long moment—her brother, her protector, still willing to move mountains for her after all these years. Then she nodded, her grip tightening around his.

"Then let's begin. Together."
