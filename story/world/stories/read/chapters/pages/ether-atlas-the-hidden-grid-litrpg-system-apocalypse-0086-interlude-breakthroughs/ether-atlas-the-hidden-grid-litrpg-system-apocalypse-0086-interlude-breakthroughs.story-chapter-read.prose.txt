Interlude: Breakthroughs

Venetian Lagoon in northern Italy

Seven hundred years ago the Venetian Republic gathered its glassmakers, rowed them across the lagoon to a low island called Murano, and told them not to come home. The reason was fire. Venice was a city of timber wedged into water, and a furnace that ran day and night was a disaster waiting to happen. So the masters were sent where they could blaze as hot as they pleased, and the Republic closed its hand around them. Take the art off the island, the Council ruled, and it would be treated as treason.

The glassmakers were imprisoned and flattered in the same breath. Their daughters could marry into noble houses. Their recipes for cristallo, glass so clear it might have been frozen air, were written in cipher and kept from every stranger who set foot on the quay. Venetian glass turned into the stuff of legends.

The fires of Murano have never gone cold. Through plague and occupation and the long centuries of tour boats, someone always kept a furnace breathing. They have not let it die now either.

Tullio fed the fornace another length of broken pew and watched the colour climb in the mouth of it, the deep marigold that told him the glass had come ready to take. For forty years his eyes had measured heat by its colour. The grid was dead and the lamps were dead and the vaporetti sat where they had stopped on the water, and still the one thing his hands knew how to do worked exactly as it always had.

“It is too thin at the shoulder,” he said.

Marta did not look up from the pipe. She had the canna, the long iron blowpipe, balanced across the arms of the scagno, the glassmaker’s bench, and she rolled it under her palm with the steadiness he had spent fifteen years putting into her wrist. At the end of the pipe a bulb of molten glass glowed and turned.

“It is meant to be thin, papà. It is a lamp, not a soup pot.”

“A lamp.” He said the word with no faith in it at all. “A lamp wants a flame, Marta. Or oil. Or that little wire the electric ones kept inside them. Va là. We have blown eleven beautiful empty jars.”

She rested the bulb back in the bocca, the furnace’s mouth, to keep it soft, and at last she looked at him. In her other hand sat the thing she had traded three mornings of bread for, down at the Faro landing where the patrols did their bartering. The stone that sat in her palm was no larger than a quail’s egg and emitted a soft blue glow.

“A man at the landing put one of these in a milk jar,” she said. “He told it to keep cold. His milk has not turned in a week, papà, not in this heat. Capì? If a stone can hold the cold inside a jar, it can hold light inside a globe. It only wants telling, and we are people who know how to make a globe.”

“The ones past the bridge are fools who will crack good glass.”

“Then we crack one jar. We have eleven.”

He wanted to argue and he wanted, very badly, to watch it happen. Those had stopped being separate feelings about his daughter a long time ago. She would take over his craft and be better than him. He was sure of it, and proud beyond words.

She carried the glass back to the bench and worked fast. A few turns of the borsella, the iron jacks, to draw the neck in. A breath down the pipe to round the belly. The bulb swelled into a globe the size of two fists, soft still, glowing its own dull orange from the heat alone.

“Now,” she said, and pressed the blue stone down into the molten crown of it.

The glass closed over the stone. For a moment nothing happened, only the blue sinking into the orange. Marta shut her eyes, and Tullio watched his daughter’s face go still. He steadied the canna for her without being asked, the two of them joined across a metre of iron and a globe of fire.

Then a blue light rose up through the orange. It came like a dawn, slow at first and then all at once, until the glow from the heat and the glow from the stone burned together. Eventually the glow from the stone won.

She rolled the globe along the steel marver, scored the neck, and cracked it free of the pipe. There on the bench, ticking faintly as it cooled, sat a sphere of clear Murano glass with a small blue star at its heart, throwing soft, even light into every corner of the dark fornace.

“Ostrega,” he said, very quietly.

Then he cleared his throat. “Ten more,” he said. “We have ten more of these.”

Marta wiped her face with the back of her glove and grinned at him, lit blue from below. “I thought you said we would only crack good glass.”

He had already turned back to the fornace, a man with work in front of him again. “Trade for every stone you can find. And go and ask who past the bridge has more of them. We will light the whole lagoon.”

Poweshiek County, Iowa

Before a prairie town had a church or a name the railroad would agree to, it had a fire. Somebody had to make iron behave. A wagon needed iron to bind its tyres, a field needed a plough that held its edge, a house its nails, and all of it came out of one hot building at the edge of the settlement. The blacksmith was the first necessary man. You could pray in a field. You could not plough one without him.

Then the factories caught up. Iron came by the railcar, stamped and cheap, and the smith went from necessary to quaint inside one lifetime, a curiosity people drove out to see at the county fair. The skill that had built the country became a hobby, then a thing you watched other people do on YouTube.

Cal Miller had over two hundred thousand of those watchers. His channel name was Tallgrass Forge: one man smelting iron from bog ore in a clay furnace he had built by hand, doing it the slow way because the slow way still meant something to him. People wrote from Norway and Japan to tell him he was an artisan.

Since the world went to shit the first thing Cal had learned, while hauling water and standing patrol nights with the others off the county road, was that almost nobody could make anything. People could find things and break things and run them dry. But no one had an answer to what happened if your hunting knife broke.

So he had come back to the barn to answer that question.

His setup was a brick trough with a cast-iron firepot at its heart and a hand blower off an estate sale up in Story County, and not one inch of it needed the grid. He could make fire out of wood and air and patience, and for six years he had.

What he could not do was make the new iron move. The billets had come from his own bloom, good iron that he had smelted last spring and stacked to age, and somewhere in the last few weeks they had changed.

They sat in the barn gloom with a colour iron had no business wearing, a faint blue down in the grain, like the light at the bottom of a propane flame. He had snapped a file clean on one. He buried a billet in the coals and cranked the blower until the fire roared white, and the metal came up dull and sullen. The fire that built the country was not hot enough for what the country was changing into.

Dell Hoffman had brought him a stone three days back, the morning after the patrol shot the thing down in the Vesely drainage ditch. He had set it on the bench like it embarrassed him.

“Pulled it out of the chest when we dressed it,” he said. “A few folks have started figuring out something to do with them. Lights, mostly. Eunice keeps her insulin cold with one.” He looked at the dark forge, then at Cal. “Figured if anybody’d find a use for the old kind of work, it’d be you.”

“I don’t know what it does, Dell.”

“Nobody knows the half of what any of it does.” Dell tugged his cap down and went back out to the cold. “You’ll figure it. You always could make the hard way look easy.”

Cal carried the stone in his coat for three days, turning it over at the patrol fire, a blue ember that never burned down. He had watched a kid two farms over push one into a dead flashlight and watched it stay lit. Press it in and want it to, the kid said. That was the whole of the instruction anybody had.

He was standing in front of the forge again watching a fire that would not bite. The thought came as the good ones always did, not in his mind but in his hands. His were moving before he knew fully what he intended to do. He was holding a piece of fire that would not go out, and his forge was a box built to hold fire. Here was a fire he would never have to coax again.

He raked the coals off the firepot with the side of his hand and barely felt the burn. He set the stone in the iron throat of the forge, where the blast came up through the tuyere, laid his palm over it, and thought about heat. He did not think it in words. He thought about the inside of a smelt at full roar, the way the air over the fire stands up and bends and swims. He held the one idea down hard and did not let it go.

The stone drank the idea and sank in. The firepot went from cold iron to dull red under his palm, and he pulled his hand away. The blue ember was an ember no longer. It was a sun the size of a walnut, the air above it standing and heat coming off it in waves.

Cal took up the tongs and buried a blue billet against the little sun. When he took it out it was glowing a clean deep orange he had chased all week. He set it on the anvil.

Under the hammer, the iron flexed. The bar spread like proper iron. It took the blow and answered with a ring, that high true note a smith heard in his chest, and a fan of orange scale flew off into the cold and died on the dirt floor. Weeks of scavenging and breaking and running things dry, and now, under his own hand, a thing was being made.

He had spent six years doing this and telling himself he was keeping the old ways alive, half suspecting he was only playing dress-up. But the modern world had gone away in a night, and he was not playing dress-up anymore. He was the smith at the edge of the settlement again, the first necessary man.

He set the bar aside to cool and stood looking at the rack of blue iron, more than he could quickly count, every piece now holding potential for the new world. Cal decided on spear points. Something with reach, for the guys in the patrol.

“Calvin,” he heard his mother call.

“Out here.”

She came in with a covered plate, the door of the barn open behind her on the cold, and stopped where she stood and looked at the small sun in the firepot.

“That’s not coal.”

“No, ma’am.”

“You eat before it goes cold.” She motioned at the plate. “And then you better make whatever it is they need.”

“Yes, ma’am,” he replied with a smile.

Nagano Prefecture, Japan

There is a moth that cannot fly. Five thousand years of human hands bred the flight out of it, and the fear, until what is left is a soft white creature that cannot feed itself and would not last a night in the world its ancestors came from.

In exchange for being kept alive it spins the strongest thread any animal has ever made, finer than hair and, weight for weight, the equal of steel. People wanted it so badly they built their houses around it, raised the roofs and gave the warm upper floor to the worms, and called them okaiko-sama, the honoured silkworms, because a family’s whole year hung on the little gods in the loft staying well.

It had made Japan the silk house of the world, and then the mills closed, beaten by a thread that came out of a factory and owed nothing to any animal. The lofts in Okaya emptied one by one, and the mulberry on the hillsides went to scrub. Toyo kept her worms anyway, the last in the valley to do it. She did it because her mother had, and her mother before that, and because a loft with nothing breathing in it felt empty to her.

She had hatched the spring worms in May out of stubbornness, and now in their last days before the spinning they ate without stopping, ten thousand of them working the mulberry at once. It was the sound that had filled the house her whole childhood, the sound of rain on a roof heard from inside.

On the third evening of the spinning Toyo took a cocoon down from the rack and held it to the lamp to judge the season. She rolled it between her fingers and tried to break a strand of the loose outer silk between her thumbs. It did not break.

She had broken a thousand thousand strands of floss in her life and knew the small giving snap of it. She tried again and the result was the same. She fetched her shears and cut the thread, and she could feel the resistance through the shears.

She sat with the cocoon in her palm for a long time, trying to think of a reason. The only one she could think of was that the worms had breathed the changed air for six weeks, and the changed air had gone into the thread.

She was still turning the cocoon in her hand when she heard Kenji on the road. She went to put water on for tea, still thinking of the silk that was not silk anymore.

He came smelling of the valley.

“Kā-san.”

“Sit. The water is just going.”

He sat at the low table and unbuckled something from underneath his jacket and laid it down between them. It was a leather vest, or what used to be one. It was much thicker now and had a faint blue glow to it.

“What is this?”

“Kā-san, we are finding stone in the creatures,” he said, pushing the vest towards her. “We can melt the stone into things to make new things. You only have to think what you want the stone to do.”

“Watanabe-san showed us how.” His eyes were wide in excitement. “A knife will not go through it, Kā-san. Iwao tried.”

“Bakabakashii.”

“No, I saw it. He tried to pierce it with the point. It skated off.”

He reached into his jacket pocket and took out a stone and laid it on the table beside the cocoon.

“For trade. Or for whatever you need.”

“Kenji.”

“Take it. I have mine.”

“Take it back down to Hana.”

“I have one for her too,” he said, looking at the cocoon for the first time.

“A good cocoon?”

“A strange cocoon,” she replied.

It was dark before she climbed back up to the loft, the stone and the cocoon in her hand. She thought of Kenji going back down to patrol the road with other young men, and of Hana, her son’s little girl, and of every child in the village below.

One stone was one vest. But the thread in the cocoon she had in her hands needed no stone in it. The thread was already strong, from nothing but the air. The worms had made the thread, ten thousand of them.

The racks ran the length of the loft, tiers of flat woven trays from the floor to the roof, the worms laid out on them in their thousands. Toyo knelt at the foot of the central post and pressed the blue stone into the old wood at the base of it all. She did not know the words other people used. She knew the loft, and she knew what she wanted. She wanted the air the worms breathed thick with the thing that had got into the thread, the loft pulling it down out of the whole sky and holding it close around the little gods until they were full of it.

It was three days before she was sure. The air up in the loft had weight now, heavier than the air in the house below. The worms in it ate without stopping and it did not sicken them. She climbed up and held the lamp to inspect the worms. Their pale bodies wore a faint sheen no worm had ever worn.

They spun out in the last week of the month, and the cocoons came off the trays heavier than any cocoon she had ever known and with the same sheen the worms had worn. Toyo reeled the first of them at her mother’s wheel, and the thread came off the colour of old ivory and would not break against anything she owned. She doubled it and trebled it and wove a hand’s width of plain cloth. When it came off the loom she laid it over the chopping block in the yard and took her son’s old hatchet to it with both hands.

The blade bit a few threads and stopped. Toyo picked it up and turned it in the light from the open door, a square of plain ivory silk a girl could have woven, that an axe could not cut. Up in the loft the sound of the worms went on and on.

She thought of her son and her daughter and her granddaughter, and for the first time since the sky changed she could see how she might keep them.
