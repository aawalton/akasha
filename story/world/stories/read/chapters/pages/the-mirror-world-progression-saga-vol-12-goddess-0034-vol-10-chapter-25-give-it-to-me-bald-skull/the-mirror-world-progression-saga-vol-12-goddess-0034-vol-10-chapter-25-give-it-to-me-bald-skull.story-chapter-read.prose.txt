Within his shadowy realm under the mountains of Baiz Risi, Crow set the new skull down by the others clustered here and there on the great platform. The Waiter, floating some distance away near the stone steps, turned its gaze somehow on both Crow and the skull.

No words were needed in this case.

The skull of the Consolidator began to slowly float upward. Faint whispers, rather than a cacophony of wails, consolidated from a mass seemingly of one man’s many thoughts, finally forming a very whispery, rich but piercing, echoing voice. Even the skull’s fully functioning mouth moved. “Ahhh,” he said, “it is done. I am grateful.”

Crow blinked. That was a new one on him. Usually, the faux spirits screamed and such, cast in confusion. “You are? To whom?”

“I foresaw my fate in death,” the Consolidator declared ominously, voice a sharp whip through the realm. “I regretted it, but faced it just as prescribed, the decision unavoidable. Such is the price to know, is it not? A curse you cannot escape. I am grateful that the final foul touch of my ill-used carcass, and thus my influence in the world, was put to rest, regardless that I have no direct memory or knowledge of this travesty, only visions.”

“I see. Is this why you come to me? Your wrong dealt is that you became a lich after your death, only known because you divined it?”

“Indeed. Ironic, isn’t it? But visited by a mutual enemy, I presume.”

“The Sage.”

The Consolidator barked a bitter, mocking laugh. “Is that his name, who came after and corrupted all I held dear? How… basic and underwhelming.”

“Sure. Does any of this have to do with the First Page, by the way?”

“It has everything to do with it. Through too much knowledge, through too much precious worth in our hands, we curse ourselves and do evil. Tell me, Agent of Death, if one does evil, have they not become it?”

Crow frowned. “I think those truly evil don’t think themselves so. To know right from wrong would seem to make it hard to do the worst of wrongs. Do you think yourself evil?”

“Yes. For I do know right from wrong, and have committed the worst of offenses: murder. I committed evil for the sake of an even more sacred goal than life — to me — and when I glimpsed the future, I saw that I was right, though for the wrong reasons. Right once, wrong twice, then delivered to an ironic end I would always refuse to turn from, not out of righteousness but for pride. For all of this, I am most certainly evil.”

“It seems to me to have been the greater good, if you’ve served Fate.”

“Words of excuse. They cannot absolve me of sin, nor can one solution absolve me of so much failure.”

“I’m not sure that’s correct, but I suppose that’s subjective philosophy for you, hmm? Moving on. So, again… this evil committed was for the First Page?”

“To protect its secret sequestration, yes. Three were needed, myself and two others. These I obtained service from in the act, then had killed, so I alone held the key or knowledge therein. Not even their bodies did I allow honor, their substance obliterated and cast as ashes to the sea. In secret sin, of course. Decoys still obtained honor, lesser men slain in proxy and their bodies preserved as of old, to give peace to their families and spirit-kin despite my crime. Interred as mine was a century later, though in truth rather than falseness. When I died, the secret died with me and passed on, and by the time this so-called sage came, there was nothing convenient left to retrieve.”

“If that’s the case, couldn’t you have let the other two die the same? He wouldn’t retrieve it from them, either.”

“I did it anyway.”

“Why, because you’re evil?” Crow asked mockingly.

“How do you know absolutely a secret is withheld? By absolutely holding it. There is no other way. A century I lived beyond, and they might’ve lived it too, and how likely would they have been so diligent as I under pressure? Or made backup plots and failsafes? I made many copies. I spread knowledge and power through the First Page, and men’s hearts and minds were filled with envy, striving, and greed for it, particularly in the ‘not knowing.’ Was it so powerful as they imagined, out of my hands?”

The skull left a long pause before continuing. “No. But they thought it so. They made it into more. Into an idol… One they had models of, enough to salivate over what glories the original might possess. Should they have obtained it, it would not exist in your day, Agent of Death. Like all other things from my time, it would be gone, and robbed of its final purpose.”

“Which is?”

“An instrument of the final end. Mercy against the corruption dealt. Mercy, like revenge against this tyrant that sullies the word of his title. Mercy against more, besides, though I didn’t understand it so much as know it in heart, through Fate; know that I was doomed to play my part. Trapped even before I saw the horrible reality I would fall into. Yes, I could have turned away. But only if I were not who I was, and thus, could never be. The paradox of free will, twisted by the curse of foresight.”

Crow understood the feeling. He was no different. Samantha and the others, together with him, marched on the deadly march to finish things. End the game, cease the games they were all weary of. Conspire, finally, for a win.

There was one primary question Crow had to ask. Much like the Consolidator, he was doomed to know it. “How do we obtain the First Page?”

The words of the Consolidator grew heavier and more foreboding, as if sinking deeper into reality. Now he was sounding closer to a floating lich skull. “Around the world, ten temples true. Eleven in all, one false, one new. Below lies enchanted, founding stone. Under years, layers; ancestral bone. But a chip, from each, is melted down. Forged in truth, a key is found. Undo a witch’s shadowed lock. So found, the [Words], in bedrock.”

Crow raised an eyebrow. “Can you only give me the rhyming, riddle version? Due to this curse, or some other bullshit like that?”

“No. I simply preferred it. In fact, I just made that up, in translation. Not bad, hmm?”

Crow glowered. Sighed. “Please give it to me straighter, skull.”

The Consolidator also sighed. “I see the youth haven’t changed so much since my day — no appreciation for poetic gravitas. Very well. I’m bound to answer you. I laboriously scouted out eleven desirable locales and made them even more desirable. Technically, there are twelve, including my own tomb’s catacombs, which is one of the strongest locations, but I deliberately avoided any clues whatsoever there, so it doesn’t factor. I installed enchanted bedrock that drew mana and pulled the world toward it. Mankind was destined to utilize it. Your civilization was likely built on it, and the one before, and certainly the one before that. The bedrock remains. It will not seem special except in the environment. The enchantment is mild, designed for function only over aeons.

“Nonetheless, take but a chip. One-tenth of a standard key’s mass, from each, and melt it down, to forge the key. Find any witch or wizard’s journal or spellbook: what they called a ‘shadow-maker’ in my day, and locked by long custom. Open the lock with the key, at which point it will turn to a page that will separate to become the clay tablet you seek.”

“Why is this so bloody elaborate?”

The Consolidator’s skull sneered derisively. “Why do anything artistic at all? Is that your question? Some of us are artists, and some of us, clearly not.”

Crow glowered fiercely at the skull, silently demanding more.

The Consolidator tsked. “It was necessary to hide the process by such arcane means, lest they be augured in some way. I could not simply place it somewhere, or inevitably it would be tracked and located — or eventually stumbled upon in the untold aeons between then and now. I made it exist both in Calrenazzod’s shadow, and nowhere at all, not even in the way of pocket dimensions. I materialized it as needed with my key, which I destroyed utterly many years before my death, lest I leave a convenient item for the world’s greedy treasure-seekers to find. The reforging process of the key was known by no one else.”

Crow considered it, and ultimately shrugged. “Paranoid, but I guess it makes sense. Where are these temples?”

“Oh, yes, here, let me give you a map of your world I know so well with everything marked with convenient exes.” The skull jiggled with sass.

“Sarcastic point taken. Can you provide any hints?”

“Most would be under catacombs. The enchantment is the most basic, and obscenely old. Older than any other magic. And in the way of my time’s proper construction — likely to be seen as too much trouble by you hasty ingrates — it utilizes a massive founding stone. This should be easy for your wizards to glean, as a whole.”

Wait. Could these locations be the same ones Samantha and Azure have been discovering already? Pulled to magnetically… historically? Oh. Oh, I think that is indeed the case. That seems to fit.

“What about this ‘ten out of eleven’ thing?” Crow asked. “One of the temples is false? Shouldn’t be combined as part of the key?”

“That’s correct.”

“How do we know which?”

A long pause. He seemed to be caught in consternation. “I don’t know. My understanding of this is hazy, but… I think I intentionally don’t know. As another failsafe… Hmm. There must be some way of discerning the right combination. Which to not draw from. But it seems left to the seeker and discerner.”

Crow sighed and rubbed his forehead. “Great. Just wonderful, skull.”

“Thank you for your kind words, descendant. If we don’t make our children jump through a few hoops in life, what good are we? This I ask you.”
