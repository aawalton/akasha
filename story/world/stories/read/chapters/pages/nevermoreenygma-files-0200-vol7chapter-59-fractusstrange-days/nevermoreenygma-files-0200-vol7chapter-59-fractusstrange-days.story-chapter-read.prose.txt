Chapter 59

Fractus/Strange Days

After gave first aid to the injured agent, they sought refuge in the mountains, to hide from the continuing rain of fragments falling from the sky. They drove until they could stop, and along the way they tried to communicate, but all satellite communications failed. Over the radio there was static, but they managed to transmit their coordinates in Morse code. After a few minutes of broken conversation on the other end of the line, they apparently gave up and sent a response in the form of a code, which seemed like the best option. Help was on the way.

In the meantime, the Mai and Noki placed Kazuo’s body in a body bag and had no choice but to wait. They watched the massive satellite-like object that had appeared, now moving westward. The rain of fragments had stopped, and the glow on the sea had disappeared. They didn’t know what to make of it.

The portal had been gradually fading away, closing like a wound in the fabric of reality. But it had left behind something that shouldn’t be in the planet’s space, and it didn’t seem like it was going to go away. The moonlight now bathed those fragments in its pale glow; as it struck the surface of the fragments in orbit, they occasionally emitted iridescent flashes, bathing the night sky in a beauty too strange to describe.

The wounded agent, Mai, and Noki simply watched the nocturnal spectacle as they stood near the body of their fallen comrade. Mai could not believe that in just a matter of minutes, things had unfolded so rapidly.

What was happening to the west, where the largest fragment was moving? Would it fall into the planet’s orbit? If that happened, they could only await their end. And she and the others waited for minutes. But that didn’t happen. Perhaps it wouldn’t happen quickly. Would it perhaps float like that forever in the sky, with a trail of smaller fragments entering the atmosphere?

Noki’s eyes were shining. She had been trying to contact her sister and her mother, but to no avail.

Rein. Leon. Nerea and Miria. Milena and Sari. What could they be doing?

Mai bit her lip, as she looked up at the sky with a lump in her throat and decided it was best to focus on what was at hand. Taking care of her wounded partner was the best thing she could do.

The ship that came for them, arrived an hour later and took them to the base, where chaos reigned. No one yet knew exactly what was happening, but it was clearly a global event.

The wounded agent was quickly taken to the medical wing. Although the wound had penetrated a lung, it wasn’t too serious given his regenerative abilities and the fact that it hadn’t touched his heart or spleen.

In a long hallway, Mai and Noki watched as another group carried Kazuo’s body away on a stretcher for an autopsy. Although he was a fey, he had left his body behind, which meant he didn’t have a shy organ.

He would surely be returned to Japan soon. It had been a short time, but it still really hurt to see a comrade leave, especially since death had reared its head at a moment when everyone was relaxing.

Mai clenched her fist, and Noki took her hand.

They said nothing as they made their way to the command room, and there was no need to, because Mai could sense that Noki was in a similar state of mind.

But at least the rudimentary communications were working, and they could now use the radio at many bases, just as the world was trying to broadcast what was happening. Apparently, those fragments were complicating matters, and radiation in the atmosphere was a global problem; tsunami warnings were being issued along various coastlines due to tidal shifts, and reports indicated that portals had opened in several locations.

Five hours later, Mai and Noki were on a new ship heading toward Siren Island, while the invasion had already begun.

And so the strange days had finally arrived.

***

Mai found herself in the main hall of the south base in Siren Island, staring at the television screens in front of her with a fixed, vacant gaze. The glow from the screens lit her face, but the images themselves were barely comprehensible, blurred by the chaos unfolding around her.

Over the past few days, rumors had grown louder and more confusing, while the world seemed to crumble into pieces. Satellites had fallen, one after another, and the information was a chaos. Reports trickled in, fragmented like broken glass of a new reality no one could not comprehend yet.

The screens flickered with images of destroyed cities, but the creatures responsible—initially amorphous shadows—were difficult to make out. What were they? Where did they come from? They came with the portal in space, and then appeared through the smaller ones that opened after? The questions just piled up, with no one able to resolve the mystery.

Mai couldn’t look away.

Evacuation groups had been wiped out in Siberia, while entire cities dissolved under the weight of creatures striking with overwhelming force. Europe, India, China, the United States, Canada, Brazil, Bolivia, Namibia—every corner of the world she had known was collapsing, with no one able to stop it.

The advance of those beings seemed unstoppable. And the reports, though slow, offered nothing but fragments, pieces of what had once been normal life.

Study groups of physicists and occult specialists tried to analyze impact sites, searching for patterns in the attacks. But the truth was that everything they knew about these beings was unreachable. The impact of the rocks that came from space had nothing to do with the invasion in the planet for start. They appeared from spherical anomalies that could vary in size and did not take long to close, while others could remain open long enough for dozens, or sometimes hundreds of thousands, to pass through.

The creatures were not limited to known matter. They moved between dimensions, as if existing in a parallel reality, not fully belonging to physical one, or at least what was understood by it. People vanished if touched by some of them, disappearing entirely.

Mai closed her eyes for a moment, trying to organize her thoughts, but the anguish remained.

Conventional weapons appeared useless. Another approach was necessary—something beyond the ordinary.

The Fractus.

That was what they were called.

It was unknown who first gave them the name, or where the word had come from, but in the days following the chaos, all reports, conversations, and media referred to them the same way.

Radio communications with specialists from around the world agreed that there was no reference to such creatures in the occult world, not to mention the different types. Some had argued that they closely resembled certain creatures from angular worlds rumored in certain obscure books known as Dimensional Shamblers or the Hounds of Tindalos, although the description were different, it was not posible that were the same creatures.

Some experts on grimoires and invocations claimed they were merely legends and did not actually exist. Others said that if they had existed, and if they were as described in the books, then it was highly unlikely that anyone could summon them. The price was always the practitioner’s life. And given the scale of what was happening, there was no known group capable of amassing enough power to carry out such a strange invocation.

The references were so obscure that it was too late, everyone called them Fractus.

Creatures from a different spatial dimension, manifesting in distorted forms within the three dimensions of the known world. At first, lack of information and general confusion reigned. But as studies progressed and reports accumulated, theories began to emerge.

Some of the most dangerous Fractus appeared fractal in nature, their forms disassembling and reconstructing endlessly, like infinite structures constantly changing. The notion of “fractal” became a familiar concept in the new war that was unfolding.

It was as if the very nature of these beings was a dimensional paradox. From nothing, like shadows cast by an incomprehensible reality, their bodies appeared, defying all logic.

The images arriving on the screens at Siren Island were fragmented and disconnected, showing a range of Fractus in diverse forms. Some had pure geometric structures, like patterns spinning and expanding. Others were more organic, with insectoid forms, and segmented bodies glowing with supernatural light and strange beauty. And some appeared monstrous, almost impossible to describe, with limbs bending at unimaginable angles, and faces that were not faces, but merely reflections of a chaos trying to copy the real world.

What caught the researchers’ attention was the Fractus’ peculiar way of moving, a motion that seemed to defy the physics of the third dimension. Like a tesseract, a four-dimensional object, they slid in impossible ways, flowing from one area to another without any transition. Some types of Fractus could mimic human or animal forms, while others dematerialized and reconfigured at will, making it impossible to predict their actions. Some of them, moving in groups, followed the lead of the first one to make the transition, forming larger groups. In the ravaged cities, there was no way to anticipate where they would appear or how they would move.

As the days passed and the world tried to adjust to the new reality, more reports began arriving at the island. Leaders from various organizations, both military and scientific, came to assess the situation.

The desperation of governments was palpable. Armies, even the most powerful, were calling up reservists and mobilizing resources at a frantic pace, knowing that what they faced was something beyond their anticipation. Around the globe, combat drones began deploying, attempting to confront the creatures on their own turf.

Meanwhile, the Aeons also started mobilizing. This was not just a physical battle, but a war on all fronts. Aeons spread across the world, using their abilities to study the creatures, trying to understand their patterns and discover how they could be confronted. It was not only about direct attacks, they needed to find the key to defeating entities that did not belong to their reality.

Siren Island became a neural hub where studies and strategies developed at full speed. Each day brought new information, new theories, but the reality remained immutable: the world faced a threat it did not understand and did not know how to defeat.

Reports began arriving faster, accompanied by more concrete data.

Most entomologists focused on studying the movement and behavioral patterns of the Fractus. What astonished them was how these beings behaved. For many researchers, some Fractus resembled insects, particularly those that moved in large colonies. Grouped Fractus formed complex patterns, almost as if they had a hierarchical structure, and their movement appeared coordinated, echoing patterns seen in certain organisms on the planet.

Yet, despite all the studies, the question remained: how could one confront such creatures? The first Fractus attacks had shown how unpredictable and dangerous they were. Some devoured everything in their path, leaving destruction behind. Others possessed an even more terrifying ability: they could make organic matter disappear at a touch, seemingly disintegrating anything they contacted. Some Fractus seemed to absorb liquids and feed on hydrogen, giving them the capacity to destroy entire ecosystems almost instantly. Others were more attracted to energy sources, and some were quite dangerous upon biological contact. As reports accumulated, confusion only deepened. No one knew what affected them or how to stop them.

However, some reports began shedding light on their behavior.

In the Sahara, a type of Fractus resembling isopods had been found, and the most surprising part came afterward. During a lightning storm, all these Fractus were virtually destroyed. The lightning appeared to devastate them, disintegrating them almost entirely. This phenomenon did not go unnoticed, and similar reports soon arrived from South America, the Amazon, Maracaibo, the Sahel, and northern Australia. In all these regions, Fractus were observed to be vulnerable to electrical phenomena, particularly thunderstorms. The same phenomenon was observed in parts of Iceland and the Pacific, where electrical discharges from active volcanoes had wiped out groups of fractus that had approached the volcanoes’ craters as if drawn by the heat.

Perhaps, electricity held the key. If that storm had managed to destroy those Fractus in the Sahara, could electricity be an effective form of defense? Could a strategy be developed that exploited this weakness?

Rein and some others thought the same. And the time showed that they were right about it even if was just an intuition at first.

As the news spread, the response was immediate. Within just a few days, the entire world began mobilizing. Electrical engineers, electricians, physicists, and anyone with high-energy science knowledge were called to collaborate in the research. The urgency of the situation was palpable. The entire planet was at stake, and everyone realized that there was a chance—however small—to use the power of electricity to confront the threat.

The battle against the Fractus had only just begun, and solutions would not be quick or simple.

***

The results were not long in coming. Within a few days, it became clear that electricity was effective against the Fractus. From the smallest to the largest, all were sensitive, though with varying intensity. Electric arcs, plasma weapons, railguns, and electroshock rifles became definitive options, though their effectiveness depended on the specific type of Fractus they faced. There was unexpected variability in their structures and abilities, making the battle far from easy.

Suddenly, the world seemed turned upside down. Overnight, all anyone heard about was electricity, as if it were the only hope to confront these beings from other dimensions.

Garage scientists, mostly self-proclaimed geniuses, began crafting improvised weapons they claimed could defeat the Fractus. However, these attempts generally ended in disaster. Homemade devices were unpredictable, and efforts to produce more effective electrical tools often proved more dangerous to their users than to the Fractus themselves.

Tasers and personal defense devices that people could kept at home began to disappear from store shelves and armories.

Panic spread rapidly when world governments tried to reassure the population, insisting that such devices would not be effective against the Fractus. But the authorities’ words did little to calm the growing hysteria in the streets. In the cities, people began buying anything capable of generating electricity, desperate to find a way to defend themselves. Even museums saw historic electrical artifacts vanish, treated almost as sacred relics for protection against the enemy. In some abandoned prisons, even electric chairs were reported missing.

A few smart alecks with the mindset of cult leaders lit light bulbs in honor of Tesla and Edison, as if they were the patron saints of a new religion.

It was chaos. Fear had gripped the population, and the streets were filled with terrified people who had no idea what to do or how to act against the unknown threat. Governments and militaries had to intervene to calm the crowds, while armies were deployed to control looting and begin evacuations from the hardest-hit areas.

It seemed that in recent years, humanity was finally emerging from its troubles and the civilization was waiting for signs from the universe. The sign had been the Fractus, who had truly caught the everyone with its pants down in the crapper.

Mai and the others in the island watched the news with a mixture of unease and bewilderment. She had never imagined she would see something so chaotic. As the world fall apart the weight of fear and uncertainty, the Fractus remained like an unreachable shadow, always one step ahead. Although electricity seemed to be the key to combating them, everything remained uncertain, and danger lurked constantly.

Rumors accumulated, and the news—distorted by communication breakdowns and widespread chaos—arrived in scattered fragments. The call from her acquaintance in Tokyo, asking her to come to Japan, lingered in her mind, but something held her back. The situation in Japan, all things considered, seemed more controlled than in other parts of the world. And this was not due to luck, but to policies implemented over many years.

Since the 1990s, Japan, along with China and the ex-South Korea, had established a refuge system for Fey, especially those hidden among humans. Over that time, human-Fey relations had slowly begun to change, and the integration of supernatural beings in these countries had been supported by years of secret negotiations and diplomatic agreements with governments. Thanks to this, there were roughly two million Fey, Aeons, and mages in contact with authorities, all ready to join the fight if needed.

Mai could not help feeling a mixture of discomfort and gratitude for that refuge now—a place where many creatures from her world could find safety—but the situation outside those boundaries was very different. Battlefronts in other parts of the world were plagued by chaos.

In Europe, armed forces struggled to halt the Fractus. In the Americas and Africa, cities were being destroyed by creatures that did not follow any known logic. Even the most advanced militaries were completely overwhelmed. It was not merely a matter of weaponry, it was something far deeper. The Fractus manifested in forms that even the greatest experts could not understand completely due the few analysis.

Mai also knew that Fey could not be expected to provide a quick solution. Integrating the feys with humans to face the new threat was not simple. Although there were isolated attempts to form mixed squads of humans and Fey, these battlefield experiments showed that integration did not work as well as hoped. Wishper had succeeded with years of training and patience, but this was different, the enemy had appeared suddenly and violently.

The abilities of the feys were not always compatible with human military tactics. There were too many factors to consider, too many risks. The way feys fought—their instincts, their strategies, even their sense of morality sometimes—clashed with human methods. It was something that required time, patience, and countless tests. Only after months of rigorous evaluation could feys and mages finally be integrated into the armies of humans and espers… if the trials proved successful.

But even that step came with a dilemma.

Revealing the existence of the feys to the world meant ending a secrecy preserved for centuries. It would be a drastic turn in history—a revelation that could unleash chaos far beyond what the fractus were already causing. Humanity was nowhere near ready to openly accept supernatural beings when in some parts even espers were treated harshly. What would happen if everything they had hidden for so long exploded into the world without restraint? The chances that governments would fail to handle the pressure were high, and everyone knew that the consequences could be unpredictable.

Despite everything, the thought of Tokyo pulled at her. Japan was one of the few places where the situation seemed more stable, at least for now. Fey assistance was already there—organized, coordinated, and cooperating with the government. Maybe going there was what she needed to understand the situation more clearly… but the doubt tightening her chest refused to let go. She would only go to Tokyo if the situation demanded it. And at that moment, she wasn’t sure the time had come.

Too much was at stake. And it wasn’t a decision she could take lightly.

The world stood at the edge of a global crisis, and the only certainty in all the chaos was that time was running out.

One of the greatest challenges lay in how integration would be handled. This was an issue raised by several nations and one the Aeons were forced to study in depth. While some feys would operate in specific, fixed areas, a broader system of integration had to be created. And the system proposed by the Aeons ran, in many ways, counter to the traditional functionality of an army.

All Aeon projections were built upon a single, immovable assumption: the war would end at some point. And when that happened, the structures created during the conflict could not be allowed to become the cause of the next one. Emotions, politics, and betrayals could persist even after the fighting stopped, but the true risk lay in accumulated probability if the conflict against this new enemy stretched on for years.

Among humans, forging bonds between combat units had long been considered part of tradition—the camaraderie born from sharing weapons and surviving together, and of course, fighting too. In the case of the feys, however, such bonds could present problems. For that reason, the Aeons decided that while some feys would indeed be assigned to border regions within the countries where they were already established, others would be better served by rotation.

When Rein told Mai and the others, it hardly needed saying, but Mai was almost certain that the Council intended to use the war to take the final step—even if that meant throwing the entire hidden world and humanity into a blender and seeing what came out of it.

Mai doubted it would be a delicious fruit smoothie.

But also had its logic. At first, it was preferable for them to be seen as allies rather than brothers-in-arms. That could come later, as the war endured. One reason for this approach was to prevent the formation of micro-cults around certain feys—or, conversely, the rise of discord within units where members remained stationed together for too long. A member who could be reassigned easily after a mission had a greater chance of success by planting seeds within teams that other feys, later reassigned, could build upon. It would not always work, but according to Aeon projections, the probability of success was higher.

Rotation also allowed for the study of how different human units reacted to the same fey, making it possible to develop an adaptation program that could be refined over time. After all, most of the feys who fought in the early stages of the war would be those with prior experience. The vast majority, however, had spent decades living as nomads or in hiding. It was unrealistic to assume that all of them would agree to join the war. For those who did, creating a training program and helping them fit in was part of the task.

There was also the black spot in the ass of distrust between nations. In certain countries, government-run refuge programs were perceived—and in some cases truly were—nests of spies. Deploying already trained feys to other nations and allowing them to remain there for extended periods was seen by some as planting a long-term intelligence asset capable of stealing secrets under the cover of war. A reassignment program aimed to reduce this suspicion, ensuring that a fey—like an esper or a mage—would not remain in a single unit long enough for others to believe they were an embedded spy.

Looking back at the history of the feys over the past two centuries, the Aeons also accounted for trafficking. Establishing a reassignment system reduced the risk of certain feys becoming high-value targets who could be captured in the midst of war. It was naïve to believe that the public revelation of the secret world would put an end to what had long been a lucrative business. The feys who rotated through units would effectively act as seeds that stationary feys—those assigned permanently—could later rely on. Their comrades would come to see them as just another member of the unit, and in the event of trouble, would protect them as they would any fellow soldier.

So yes—behaving well, earning trust, doing things properly, so others might benefit from it later became part of the work many feys were expected to do. The benefit flowed both ways: humans would grow accustomed to them, and feys who had never fought before would be less wary of joining.

Optimizing the war with an eye toward what would come after it ended.

Whenever that end might be.

Thanks to the defense and strategy departments, a rapid program was implemented to experiment with mixed squads and groups of human soldiers, espers, mages, and feys. Nevermore and other organizations around the world would be responsible for providing personnel for these tests.

When she was offered the opportunity, Mai just swallowed hard but nodded.

She would be part of the mixed test squads facing the new enemy.

She imagined herself as a headless chicken running around the world, jumping from spit on spit.
