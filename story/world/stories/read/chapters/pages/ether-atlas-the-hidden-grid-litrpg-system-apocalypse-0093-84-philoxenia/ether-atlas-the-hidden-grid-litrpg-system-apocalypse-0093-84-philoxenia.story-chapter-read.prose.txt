Chapter 84

“The ones who lasted longest were the ones with somewhere to walk back to, and we never found a way to put that on the chart.” — Codex of the Long Watch, Watcher’s Commentary

Her mother stood in the doorway with broken china at her feet, and did not bother to look down at it.

She had rehearsed this on the walk down from Koile. In the version in her head there had been words. Standing in the yard now, the evening going copper over the rooftops, she remembered none of them.

“Mamá,” she said.

It was the only word that worked. Eleni stepped over the china and pulled her in. A quiet sob escaped her as she said her daughter’s name over and over, like she was making sure this was not a dream. “Efharistó,” she whispered, looking up.

It was a while before the overwhelmed mother saw the three strangers standing at her gate, holding the posture of people trying to take up less room. The woman stood with her hands loose and visible. The large man was still, and smiling. The other one stood a little apart, studying the house.

“They’re with me,” Sarah said. “All three of them. They’re safe, Mamá. They got me home.”

Eleni pulled back to look at her, then looked past her at the gate. Sarah watched the café-owner arrive in her mother’s face, the part of her that had fed half of Piraeus and was already counting plates.

“Then why are they standing in the road like tax collectors?” Eleni said. “Ela. Inside, all of you. Po po po, look at you, look at all of you, you are skin and bones.”

Arthur had never been called skin and bones. After the System, he was in the best physical shape of his life. He shrugged and followed the others inside.

• •

Within a minute the hall was full of noise. A man came through from the back with engine grease worked into his knuckles and a toddler riding one forearm, took a single look at Sarah, and stopped dead.

“No,” he said. “No, no. Re, is this real?”

“It’s real,” Sarah said.

“It’s real, she says, like it’s nothing.” He handed the toddler to the woman behind him without looking, the easy handoff of a husband who had done it a thousand times, and then he had Sarah off her feet.

“Put me down, malaka, you’ll do your back,” Sarah said into his shoulder, and felt him laugh.

Yiayia came last, one hand on the wall and her eyes already working the room. She looked at Sarah for a long moment and said something too quiet and too quick for the others, a blessing with a hint of a complaint inside it, and Sarah went and put her forehead against her grandmother’s and stayed there.

When she straightened, her gaze went past Sarah and around the small crowd and stopped on Arthur, and stayed there.

Sarah had known that look her whole life. It was the look her grandmother gave a fever before she touched it, a reading taken from across a room. Yiayia had been taking those readings long before anyone had a word for what she was reading.

“This one,” Yiayia said, in English now, for the room. “Bring me this one. The tall one with the broken side. I want to look at him.”

They put Arthur in the chair by the window, which Sarah recognised as a declaration from her grandmother, because this chair was where her father had taken his coffee and no one had sat in it lightly since. Arthur had no way of knowing this and sat in it because a woman in her late seventies had pointed at it.

“Lift the shirt,” Sarah told him. Then she looked at her grandmother and continued, “Eighth rib, left side. I patched it on the trail and I want it closed properly before Mamá feeds us into the ground.”

He lifted the shirt to show the bruise that was angry red now. Yiayia came and stood at Sarah’s shoulder and watched her hands, and then put her own hand flat on Arthur’s back over the bad rib, without being asked. Sarah felt the clean fast knit of bone happening under her fingers. The old woman made a small satisfied sound, the sound of a person confirming something she had said for fifty years and been called superstitious for saying.

“You feel it,” Yiayia said to Sarah, in Greek.

“You were always right about the rivers in the body,” Sarah said.

Yiayia touched Sarah’s face gently and said nothing. Then she looked at Arthur and said, “This one has hands like your father.”

She patted Arthur’s shoulder twice, which Arthur received like a man being knighted by someone he was afraid of, and went to supervise a kitchen that did not need supervising.

Petros watched all of it from the doorway with his arms folded and an expression Sarah knew too well, the one he wore right before he made her life difficult.

“So,” he said. “A sister we have not seen for months, and now she walks in from the hills with three English people and a man who sits in Babá’s chair. There is a story here.”

“The story is after they eat,” Eleni called from the kitchen. “Petro, the table. We are not animals.”

Petros raised both hands in surrender to a higher power and went to pull the table out into the room.

“Mamá, we will take a bath before eating,” Sarah called out, and motioned the three to follow.

Sarah showed them the upstairs washroom, handed out towels that smelled of her mother’s soap, and left them to it.

By the time the three of them were clean and changed into the loose spare clothes Petros had dug out of a cupboard, a month of the dead world had gone down the drain with the grime of it, and they looked, for the first time since London, like people who lived somewhere.

• •

The table came out into the middle of the room and was then joined by another. The food began to arrive and did not stop.

There was moussaka with a deep brown top, a bowl of horta with a cut lemon beside it, fava under a coil of olive oil and psari plaki. Steve looked at the spread with the unguarded face of a man who had spent weeks eating tinned protein.

“Eat,” said Eleni, and it didn’t sound like an invitation.

Steve had a forkful in him before she got the word out.

Petros waited until everyone had food in front of them and Arthur had been handed a second helping he had not asked for, and then he leaned back in his chair.

“So. You two.” He pointed his fork between Sarah and Arthur. “How long?”

Sarah felt all eyes go to her. “Petro,” she said.

“It is a fair question. A man comes to my mother’s house, he sits in the chair, my sister has her hands on him before the soup is even out. Ela. How long?”

“I was healing him, vlaka!”

Arthur set down his fork and gave a vague answer. “We met in London. It is complicated.”

“It is complicated,” Petros repeated, delighted. “Look at him. Re, look at this one. He is making the duck.”

Arthur looked at the duck that was not on his plate.

“He means you are playing innocent,” Sarah said. “Kánei tin pápia. He is making the duck. It is what you call a man who sits there with a face like he has no idea what you are asking him.”

“I have no idea what he is asking me,” Arthur said.

“The duck,” Petros said, to the whole table, vindicated.

And the table laughed together. The children joined in because everyone else was laughing. Maria put her hand over Sarah’s and squeezed it gently. Even Donna was smirking.

It was into that, into the warmest moment Sarah had been inside of for a long, long time, that Archie chose to speak in their heads.

“The grandmother’s reading of the Matrix was accurate to within the margin I would expect of a trained operator,” Archie said. “I am noting it for later. Separately, your collective caloric intake this evening is the highest I have recorded for your group. I raise no objection. I am simply keeping a record.”

Sarah kept her eyes on her fish. Across the table Steve’s jaw stopped mid-chew and then started again, carefully, and Arthur developed a deep and sudden interest in the horta. Donna took it in stride, even keeping up a conversation with Maria.

Later, when the worst of the eating was done, Sarah looked down the length of the table and counted. Her mother. Her grandmother. Her brother and his wife and their two children with oil shining on their chins, and the three people who had freed her from a cage and made her their family. Nine, where a month ago she had let herself believe there might be none left at all.

And her father’s chair by the window stood empty, with no place set in front of it. Her mother caught her looking at the chair. For a moment neither of them said anything. Then Eleni reached across and put another piece of fish on Sarah’s plate, which was her mother’s way of saying things that can’t be voiced, and Sarah let her, which was Sarah’s way of saying them back.

• •

The sleeping arrangements were decided by Yiayia and were not open to appeal.

Whatever Sarah and Arthur were meant to be to each other for the benefit of strangers, they were not going to be it under her roof. The two men would have Petros’s old room. Sarah and Donna would have hers. Sarah opened her mouth to mention that they were all grown adults travelling as one unit, met her grandmother’s eye, and closed it again.

Donna had ended up in the kitchen drying plates, which was how Donna said thank you. Maria had stopped treating her like a loaded weapon somewhere around the second course, and the two of them worked now in the easy quiet of competent people with nothing they needed to say.

Arthur had not said much through any of it. He sat where he had been asked to sit, and let a family that was not his own close over him like warm water. When Sarah passed behind him she put her hand on his good shoulder for a second and felt how hard he was holding himself, a man braced against kindness because he had never been issued a defence for it.

• •

Once the children were in bed, the family sat around small glasses of raki, and Sarah’s mother asked, at last, how they had managed to get back.

Between them, Sarah and Steve gave her a toned-down version of what they had faced, and of how Sarah had been stuck in Malta before the party found her. There was no leaving the portals out of it, so they told her how they had crossed from Malta to Athens.

“Under the Akrópoli?” Petros set his glass down. “Through a gate in space.”

“I know how it sounds,” Arthur said. “But I expect you have seen a few things this past month that would have sounded just as mad to you in May.”

“Alíthia,” Petros agreed, nodding.

“You cannot tell any of this to anyone,” Sarah said. “For everyone else, I took my friends to Nafplio for a few days. We got stuck there when it happened, and we have only now made it back.”

“But why?” Petros asked.

“They have their reasons, Petro.” Sarah’s mother looked at Sarah as she said it. “Siga siga. We will have time enough to hear all of it.”

Then the talk turned, as it always did with her mother, to the accounting of the neighbourhood, who was still here and who was gone and who had turned out braver or worse than anyone would have guessed. Eleni went down the list over the coffee she had insisted on making, putting the briki three times to the flame.

“And old Markopoulos is still in his house, can you believe it?” Eleni said, pouring. “Still alive, still impossible. He used to come into the café and lecture me about chickpeas. Forty years a professor and he is going to teach me about revithá. Po po po.”

Across the table, Steve went still.

THE ETHER ATLAS — FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

Piraeus

ON THE OLDEST INSTITUTION

The Greeks kept a god at the door. Zeus Xenios watched over the guest, and the rule he enforced was older than the city: a stranger who knocked was owed water, a seat and a meal, and you did not ask his name or his business until he had eaten. The host who turned him away answered for it. We were taught to call this a moral code. I have come to think it was infrastructure.

Before there were states to vouch for a traveller, a man crossed a hundred miles of open country on the strength of a single promise: that the next door would open.

In the sixth book of the Iliad, Glaucus and Diomedes square up to kill each other, discover their grandfathers had been guest-friends, and trade armour instead, gold for bronze, in the middle of a war. Two strangers would break a token in half between them, a symbolon, a tessera hospitalis, and a lifetime later their grandchildren could press the halves together and be owed a bed.

A trust network with no centre, no archive, no enforcement but the god and the shame, and it moved people and cargo across a world that had nothing else holding it up.

I spent twenty years reading this as something that happened to other people, safely dead, behind glass. Tonight a door opened to four armed strangers and a meal was on the table before anyone asked our business.

I am revising the entry. Hospitality is not a courtesy. It is the first machine we ever built for surviving each other, and it is the first one back online.

✎ A guest is never free. Every gift is also a debt, and the same rite that fed us tonight is, somewhere across this city, teaching a stranger he now owes a stranger.
