“Colin Tremory.”

Eola was halfway to her feet before she realized what Instructor Tarik had said. It wasn’t her name; it was her friend’s. She sat back down, hitting the oak seat with enough force to clack her jaw shut. She hadn’t been picked.

A small voice in her head added a ‘yet’ to that, but the rest of her knew. Colin hadn’t said his score in Ideograms, just that he’d passed it. How highly had he scored? A five? Was Instructor Tarik only going for students who’d scored a five?

Colin shot her a confused look at stood up, and she forced her face into what she hoped looked like a smile. “Good job,” she said. He grinned back sheepishly and walked to the front as she watched, trying to choke down her envy.

She hadn’t been picked.

Colin accepted his piece of parchment and disappeared out the lecture hall’s lower door, and Eola sat, watching, as Maestro Yarrowbloom picked Irisbloom from the same group Eola had fought in. She barely saw the elf vanish through the same door Colin had taken. Then, a few professors later, the next round started, and Eola had to watch as Madame Reyanna selected a third noble child as an apprentice. At least it hadn’t been her.

She closed her eyes, squeezing against the incoming tears. Eola wasn’t very good at magic—she knew it, her parents had known it, and her uncle had definitely known it—but she’d done her best to hide that from as many professors as she could, and her scores hadn’t been awful. How could she not have been picked yet? It didn’t make any sen—

“Eola Lemiene.”

Her eyes snapped open.

Instructor Tarik’s intense eyes were locked on her. He reached up and touched his beard, waiting at the lectern with parchment in hand. Somehow, she’d missed most of the third round. She walked down, almost as dazed-looking as Colin had been, and accepted the note. Then, without saying a word, the professor sat down. Eola read his message.

Meet the others at my office. I have one more apprentice to collect. Welcome to your apprenticeship, Miss Lemiene. We are going to learn so much together if you’re willing to put in the work.

Master Tarik

That was it. She walked through the door and cut across the cold, snow-covered grounds, stockings squelching in her boots as freezing water got in them. Her hand tightened around the note, and she read it a second time. Then a third.

She had been picked.

And not only that. As snowflakes fell on the parchment, Eola stared at her name. The space in Instructor Tarik’s Alemic was perfectly sized for it. He hadn’t filled in her name after the second round; the professor had come into the choosing intending to select her.

And if he’d intended to select her, then he’d wanted Colin and Roth, too. Even more wildly, he’d known he’d pick her third. The professor had planned out his selections in advance, which meant one of two things; either every professor went into the selection knowing exactly where each student was going—an impossibility, given that they had less than two days to confer—or he was choosing based on different criteria.

Eola opened Instructor Tarik’s office door, expecting to see the neatly-stacked envelopes full of runes and piles of books sorted by subject she’d left four days ago.

Instead, it looked like a whirlwind spell had been filled inside.

Four dilapidated chairs sat in front of the plain desk, in a small, cleared space. Someone had strewn the towers of books that had recently occupied that space against the wall haphazardly. Eola had a pretty good idea who; Tarik had been busy with something, and hadn’t bothered straightening up. The black rabbit’s cage was open, and it stared at Eola with shockingly intelligent but anxiety-filled eyes. Its nose wriggled slightly.

“Are you coming in or standing there?” Roth asked. “Or maybe you’re here to tell us there’s been a mistake, and we’re being rejected.”

“No mistakes,” Eola said. She slipped through the door, letting it click shut behind her, and took one of the remaining seats. Her nose wrinkled. Something stank of fear, or maybe anxiety—and she was pretty sure it wasn’t the rabbit.

Neither Roth nor Colin met her eyes, and after a moment, she sighed. “Congratulations, Colin.”

“On what? Oh, being picked? I figured I’d get selected at some point,” he said, trying and failing to act nonchalant about it. Then he paused, looking around nervously. “You work in here sometimes, right? Is it usually this…?”

“Yes. Tarik is a lot like you.” Eola let that hang, and the room descended into an awkward sort of silence. Not that she minded. She fidgeted with the blue cat statuette in her robe pocket, rubbing the spot between Atta’s cold ears and closing her eyes. She was coming down from the nerves of the choosing, and Colin was, if not happy to be quiet, at least willing.

Roth’s fingers tapped on his pine chair, and after a moment, Eola’s eyes snapped open. The dark-haired, dark-eyed boy glared at her, daring her to say something, and after a few seconds to think about it, she decided to let it go.

She’d been chosen, and that was good enough for her.

By the time the door opened for the fourth time and Instructor Tarik came in, Eola was almost ready to draw her smallsword and attack Roth Gerr. His fingers hadn’t stopped drumming, and it was driving her mad. Even Colin looked irritated about it.

But one look at the red-haired girl following the professor, and Eola stopped caring about Roth at all. “Patrice!” Colin said as he saw her, too.

Patrice looked absolutely despondent, and Eola stood to put a hand on her shoulder, only for it to be shrugged off. “I can’t believe I wasn’t last…” the girl said.

“Were you hoping you wouldn’t get selected?” Eola asked. It was outrageous, but it was also exactly the sort of thing Patrice would be perversely proud of. After all, she firmly believed she was the worst mage in Varin’s Academy—maybe even in the history of the school.

“Sort of,” Patrice replied, grinning through her false tears. “Would have been an incredible story, anyway. The time I was such a bad student, I didn’t get an apprenticeship.”

Roth made a gagging sound, and Patrice shot him a look.

“Every student gets an apprenticeship, Miss Clerk. If you four will be quiet, I’d like to get started.” Instructor Tarik closed his eyes and steepled his fingers on the desk. “I’m going to ask for something most students aren’t required to divulge, for reasons that will become obvious by the end of this conversation. I need all of your attunements.”

“Really?” Roth asked. “You’re sure you’re not looking to back out on me?”

“Yes, I’m sure. And yes, I need them. Miss Lemiene, will you share yours?”

Eola hesitated for a moment, then nodded slowly. “Unattuned Mana.”

“Note that Miss Lemiene did not say she was unattuned,” Instructor Tarik said. “I have guesses about the other four of you. For instance, I believe Mr. Tremory is—“

“Order and Patterns,” Colin filled in.

“Excellent. However, my guesses for Mr. Gerr and Lady Clerk are significantly more shaky, so, if you’d be so kind…?”

Patrice stared at Roth, and after a moment, he cleared his throat and mumbled something into the floor, blushing crimson.

“I’m sorry, what was that?” the professor asked.

“Music. It’s music.”

“Excellent. Now, Miss Clerk—“

“No. Not right now. I’d be willing to tell you privately, but I…can’t tell my friends. It’d be…I don’t want them to think less of me,” Patrice said quietly. Eola stared at her. After the three attunements they’d just revealed, what could possibly be so bad that Patrice refused to talk about it?

“Very well,” Tarik said. “You’ll stay behind after we’re done here. I have a guess, and if it’s close, that’ll be good enough for my purposes. The reason I’ve selected the four of you has everything to do with those odd, difficult-to-use attunements you each have.

“You see, I’ve been teaching here for eight years—ever since I hit Archmage. Of those eight years, I’ve kept exhaustive records of the end-of-term examinations, and the attunements of every student who’s failed to advance to their second year, for five of them.”

Eola snorted, then flushed red. “Sorry, sir. I’ve seen your records.”

“No, point taken. My organizational scheme leaves…much to be desired, but I assure you that on this, I’ve gone against my nature.” The professor reached into a drawer and pulled out a notebook, bound in worn leather. He opened it, flipping through the pages one after another. “Here we are. What follows is mostly speculation, educated guesswork, and the results of incomplete research.

“Five years ago, there were exactly two students with attunements that, as I suspect Miss Lemiene’s or Miss Clerk’s are, were incompatible with Ordered Magic, and an additional student whose attunement was similar to Mr. Gerr’s—that is to say, an attunement with no spells in it, but that could be used with the core list of flexible First and Second Order spells. In other words, an attunement with a functional attunement modifier

“These attunements make sketching and filling First Order—or even Child’s Magic—ideograms impossible or extremely difficult. At the end of last year, I began asking the professors who’d been here the longest, and most of them remembered vanishingly few of these so-called broken attunements. My work in the library corroborated that. Most years had between zero and three, with a single standout year where seven students had odd attunements. However, a single year does not a pattern make, and that was nearly fifty years ago.”

Tarik stopped just long enough to look each of them in the eye. “In the last three years, that number has increased from between zero and three to six, eight, and nine students with abnormal attunements. A single year does not a pattern make, but three is enough to have concerns.”

“But why us?” Patrice asked.

“I’m getting there, I promise you.” Instructor Tarik rubbed his nose. “Where was I? Oh, yes. A brief tangent. You’re familiar with the very nature of Ordered Magic as a solved science, correct? There have been fewer than five new ideograms created in the last two hundred years, and all of them have been crafted entirely by accident—by battle mages in combat, in fact.”

“How in Y’aer’s name does that work?” Roth asked.

Eola cleared her throat, but before she could answer, Instructor Tarik held up a hand. “Not yet, Miss Lemiene. Mr. Gerr, that is one of the questions we will attempt to answer in this apprenticeship. My research, up until the beginning of this year has been mostly theoretical. However, I believe that Ordered Magic is not, in fact, a solved science, and that our lack of new spells is both a symptom if Ordered Magic’s incomplete nature and a sign of growing problems with magic itself.”

No one said anything. Roth glared, and Patrice might have been asleep. Eola glanced at Colin, and he looked just as confused as she felt.

Tarik sighed. “That sounded more dramatic in my head. Think of magic as a piece of bread left in the sun. It’s gone stale.”

“Gone stale, sir?” Eola asked. She tried to ignore her sudden hunger. “What do you mean?”

“I mean…” Instructor Tarik trailed off and stared at the assembled students. No, not at them. His gaze seemed to focus on a point slightly over Roth’s head. “To answer that, I need to get you up to speed on two topics. First, spellcrafting theory, and second, attunements and their relationship to Y’aer.

“So, first, spellcrafting theory. No one knows anything about how spells are made.”

“That’s impossible,” Colin said. “Eola—“

“Miss Lemiene—and by extension you and I, Mr. Tremory—is quite possibly the leading expert in the field. But that doesn’t matter. Spellcrafting theory isn’t studied. It’s a dead end. Every mage, at some point in their career, hits a point where they’re curious about it, though. They all make an effort to develop their own signature ideogram. And it usually ends in spell backlash as the magic fails. Everyone knows this, so no one seriously studies theory. Not for long.”

“Battle mages, though?” Roth asked. “What’s so special about ‘em?”

Tarik glanced at Roth, then went back to staring. “Until recently, I didn’t know. However, having seen Miss Lemiene work, I believe it has something to do with how they construct their spells for combat. The second half of the picture is something I’m more sure of, though. “What is an attunement?”

When none of the students answered, he pointed at Patrice, who’d been quiet. “Miss Clerk?”

“Well, they’re…” she paused to think.

“Take your time.”

Eola fidgeted in her chair as Patrice gathered her thoughts. Her finger touched the stone cat’s head again, just briefly. Colin’s ferret was draped around his neck, but Atta rarely left the room as anything but a statue.

Her? A leading expert in spellcrafting theory? That…was both incredibly flattering and also kind of pathetic. It said something about the state of magic that an eighteen-year-old girl knew more about how it was put together than anyone else. Then again, hadn’t she been researching exactly that for months? That had to count for something, right? She let herself smile slightly.

Tarik’s eyes caught hers, and a knowing look passed over his face.

“Attunements. They’re the type of magic your soul resonates with.” Patrice had figured out what she wanted to say. “You mentioned Y’aer, and how attunements relate to them. That depends on your denomination. The Cult Purists believe that Y’aer was nothing more than a person in the wrong place at the wrong time, and that their actions with the Chest were more accidental than anything else. If you’re a Purist, the source of attunements and magic remains the gods. Of course, you still love and hate Y’aer, but that goes without saying.”

“And what do you believe?” Instructor Tarik asked.

“Well, my parents were Purists, but they decided about eight years ago that Revisionism makes more sense politically. It’s not so much that Y’aer opened the Chest as that they, well, made it to begin with. Revisionists—at least the current bunch—believe that Y’aer created the Chest, filled it with the necessary components for magic—monsters, attunements, souls, and Mana—and then brought it to the world. The cultist who spoke at Y’aer’s Day last night was a Purist. You can always tell, because they talk about the Saint’s curiosity overwhelming her, instead of about how choice can be more dangerous than impulse.”

“And attunements?”

Patrice rolled her eyes. “Either way, they’re part of Y’aer’s Triad. Soul, Attunement, Mana. Without all three, magic doesn’t work. You need a soul to store Mana. You need Mana to empower spells. And you need an attunement to make the Mana work within different spells. That’s basic.”

“Correct,” Instructor Tarik said. “So, according to both branches of the Cult of Y’aer, the Saint provides attunements. Thank you.”

He stood and picked up a book, flipping through the pages. “Now, I’ve assembled the four of you for a specific reason—and no, it’s not because you’re the dregs of the school and none of you are in good shape to survive the end-of-term exam.”

“What?” Roth’s eyes narrowed into a fierce-looking glare, and his fists balled. Eola couldn’t blame him; hers had, too.

“All four of you—Mr. Gerr’s musical attunement, Miss Lemiene’s unattuned one, Mr. Tremory’s order, and Miss Clerk’s…special attunement…represent different potential approaches to understanding the nature of a part of Y’aer’s gift to us, and to magic in general.” The professor skimmed a page, then snapped the book shut, and Eola read the spine. The Attunement Compendium, Thirty-First Edition. What was he planning?

“I therefore propose a grand experiment, using you as both test subjects and research assistants. We will dig into your attunements and the very principles behind Ordered Magic, with the goal of discovering what every other mage has missed.”

~Lan Tarik~

Instructor Tarik stared at the empty chairs in his office as his fingers reached for a tiny glass bottle in his desk. They fumbled with it, and he stared at the mostly-empty, clear-green liquid inside, then returned it to its place, untouched.

There.

He’d shared his thoughts and gotten his apprentices to agree to his experiment. It was all in the open now.

His selections would signal to his colleagues exactly what he was thinking. Patrice Clerk, the lowest-scoring student in Varin’s Academy. Roth Gerr, the musician with the bizarre understanding of ideograms. Colin Tremory, the disorganized, plodding perfectionist. And Eola Lemiene, whose research had made this charade necessary—and also whose work had made it possible.

He’d spent the time he would have wasted marking ideograms on calling in favors. The Governing Houses weren’t among them, but a few lesser Houses stood to gain if his research bore fruit. He hadn’t bothered talking to the Cult of Y’aer, either.

But the other professors? The ones he could trust? He’d explained his true goal to them. Professors like Maestro Yarrowbloom, Lieutenant-Librarian Fringe, Instructor Clearance, and Lannith—he shivered slightly at the last name—either understood his need to dig into the mechanics of Ordered Magic, stood to benefit from his research into modifying marks, or simply wished to be informed and then left alone.

Then he’d used every bit of social standing he’d gained since taking the Ideograms job here at Varin’s to get the others to turn a blind eye. Madame Reyanna, Professor D’Leonide, and Headmaster Tomlish were all friends, but they were also believers, members of noble houses, or both. Still, they’d owed him, and that would buy him and his students some time.

“Nothing left but to stay the course, Garah,” he said quietly. The bottle of clear-green liquid sat in its place. The black rabbit stared at him—somewhat accusatorily, he thought. “I didn’t have a choice. My apprentices need to know the truth about what we’re doing, and it is tied together. Their attunements have to be a result of Ordered Magic. There’s no other explanation.

“And as for the ‘curriculum?’ I’m not sure there’s a better course of action than letting them loose under my watchful eye and seeing what happens.” He laughed quietly. “I know, I know, Garah. All this effort, and my apprenticeship turns out to be an independent study and homeroom? A cruel joke, but a necessary one. I intend to study just as much as they do. Maybe more.”

By the time he was finished, Tarik intended to have notebooks filled with his observations—perhaps enough to write a full treatise and change Ordered Magic. But the rules of magic wouldn’t reveal themselves. To see them, Tarik would need help.
