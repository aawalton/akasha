The door beneath the leviathan's tongue was already open.

Kage stepped through last, after the fragment had finished settling into his inventory and after he had given the gilded throat one final glance, and the gold around the frame's edge softened the way a candle flame softened just before someone pinched the wick. The platform behind him faded.

Then there was light.

Soft. Sourceless. Distributed and even, emanating from somewhere architecturally impossible.

He knew the floor before he looked at it.

Polished obsidian. Forty meters across. Roughly circular. A stage hung in absolute nothingness with the unhurried weight of a thought that had not decided what shape to take.

The lobby.

Kage closed his eyes for a half-second, opened them, and confirmed it. The same crumpled parchment moons turned in the void above. The same gilded picture frames coasted on their slow private orbits. The storm frame two hundred meters to the left was where the storm frame had always been. The burning city drifted past with the same unhurried weight.

The frame they had just stepped out of was not there.

He scanned for it. He gave it a full thirty seconds, which was a long time to give to something his eyes had already finished looking for. Among the dozens of frames visible in the sourceless light, none of them held the unfinished storm seascape. None of them held a leviathan, a reef, a wreck, or a tongue. The frame had either retracted into the void or had never been a real address in the first place.

Probably the second one.

"Okay," Jax said.

Jax was the first to speak. He usually was.

"Okay. Did anyone else just walk out of a fish's mouth and end up exactly where we started? Because I want to be clear that I did. So if I'm having a stroke, I would like everyone to confirm now."

"You're not having a stroke," Zara said.

"Fantastic."

"You're standing in the lobby."

"Also fantastic."

Lily exhaled. "It moved us back," she said. "Or it moved itself away from us."

"Both, maybe." Finn was already turning, neck craned, the hawk circling high above his shoulder in tight slow loops as though it, too, was looking for an exit it had misplaced. "I don't see the storm frame. I mean, I see a storm frame, but it's not the right storm frame."

"Frames cycle," Vami said. "They cycle," he continued. "We did the wing. The wing closes. The lobby reissues. Standard hub structure."

Kage said nothing. He was watching the marks.

His own first.

The archaic black script around his arm had retracted. Half the letters were back from where he had spent them on the storm draft and the leviathan and the edits in between. The orbiting characters drifted in a slow, even ring above his skin, replenishing in increments slow enough that he had to mentally compare them to where they had been ten minutes ago to confirm they were moving at all.

Then he looked at the others.

Vami's wrist glowed amber-blue, and the wet pigment of his mark hung a single thick droplet past his knuckle, suspended, refusing to fall. The droplet was pointing.

Past Kage's right shoulder. Toward something distant.

Ren's mark was glowing crimson and white. The droplet on his hand pointed in a different direction entirely. Up and to the left, toward a frame Kage had to actively turn his head to find, and even then could only confirm by the way Ren's eyes were already locked on it.

Zherdev's mark was glowing every color it could think of, because of course it was, and the droplet on Zherdev's massive gauntleted finger pointed straight down past the edge of the platform, into the void below, toward something coasting through the dark that Kage had to lean forward to spot.

Three different directions.

Huh.

"They split," Zara said. She had seen it the same instant he had. "Last room they all pointed the same way. Now they're triangulating."

"Five fragments total," Kage said. "We have one. That leaves four."

"Three marks pointing." Zara's brow drew together. "Four fragments. Math is short."

"Math is fine." He rolled his shoulder. "One per direction. We have three rare marks, not four. That means it should show after we complete another frame."

"Or it's in here," Lily said quietly, and her eyes moved up to the parchment moons turning slowly in the impossible sky.

Kage looked up too. He gave the thought the half-second of attention it deserved.

Maybe.

He did not say maybe. He said:

"We pick a direction. We clear the wing. We come back."

"Which direction?" Vami already knew which direction Vami wanted. The droplet on his finger had not stopped pointing.

Kage looked at where Ren's mark was pointing. Then where Zherdev's was pointing. Then back to Vami's.

Vami's was angled toward a frame that had drifted closer in the last minute, near enough now that Kage could see what was inside it without straining. The frame held a mountain. The mountain was open at the top in the way mountains were open at the top when they had decided to be a different kind of mountain. Slow orange light pulsed inside the rim. Smoke fed up from the cone and dissolved before it cleared the gilt edge of the frame.

A volcano.

"Closest," Kage said. "We do that one first."

Vami smiled, which was its own kind of warning sign.

Lily looked at Kage. She did not say are you sure. She had stopped saying are you sure somewhere between the reef and the leviathan, and Kage had quietly noticed.

"And how do we get there?" Jax said.

The answer arrived before Kage finished thinking it. The obsidian under their boots warmed, a single line of black script poured up out of the floor and unfurled across the void toward the volcano frame, and the frame slowed in its drift to meet them halfway.

"Same as last time," Zara muttered. "I hate that this place keeps doing what we ask before we ask."

They formed up. Anchors distributed evenly. They walked. The volcano frame came to meet them. Beneath it, in elegant, half-finished archaic script, two words floated and rearranged themselves once before settling:

A Draft of Fire and Earth.

The rim of gilt expanded until it stopped being a frame and started being a horizon, and they stepped through it onto warm rock.

What followed, Kage would later remember in pieces.

The wing was hot in the specific way that the dungeon understood heat. The sky inside the frame was rust and umber and a smear of low, unfinished black where the artist had failed to commit to a color. The mountain rose ahead of them on a half-sketched slope, switchbacks drawn in confident charcoal lines that ran for fifty meters and then trailed off into vague suggestion. Ash drifted sideways through the air on currents that did not exist. The Treads of the Ashen Dune negated the footing penalty without him having to think about it, which was as close as the boots ever came to applause.

The mobs here were different.

The reef had given them sea-scrawl. Glassfin and reefscript, things that moved like script trying to remember it had once been an ocean. The volcano gave them Cinder Stanzas. Tall, narrow figures of cooling rock with verses burned into their chests in glowing red script that pulsed in time with whatever rhythm the dungeon was breathing in. Their attacks came in declamatory lines, long and rolling, the kind of attack that announced itself two beats before it landed.

There were Magma Glosses. Smaller things. Fast. Liquid. They scuttled and pooled and the floor where they died stayed hot for longer than the mechanics deserved.

There were Earthen Footnotes. Squat, blocky, the asterisks of the volcano. They detonated. They did so with great enthusiasm.

Kage stopped narrating to himself somewhere in the second corridor. The party had figured each other out. Valdrias planted on the choke. Jax bracketed his off-side. Finn called weak points from elevation, hawk wheeling over the line, and the call always came a beat before anyone needed it. Lily and Julie operated as a single distributed system now. Lily caught the panic-damage. Julie caught the momentum. Vami moved through the formation in a refracting white blur, exactly fast enough to draw aggro and never quite fast enough to die.

Ren cut.

Just that. He cut.

Kage edited, mainly. Stepped in when necessary—so, not very often.

He used the mark sparingly, because the syllable debt from the leviathan fight had taught him exactly what kind of compensation the dungeon liked to charge. But the temptation was constant, because the dungeon was talking, the verses were always there, and twice in the first chamber Kage caught himself crossing out a line of incoming attack purely because it would have been faster than blocking it.

The first edit went well.

He removed a Cinder Stanza from a tunnel choke. The verse that produced it dimmed and dissolved, and the Stanza flickered out like an idea no one had finished. The dungeon compensated by spawning a pair of Magma Glosses where the Stanza had been standing, which was a downgrade by every measure Kage cared about.

[Edit Accepted. Syllable Debt: Minor.]

The second edit went badly.

He removed an Earthen Footnote about to detonate under Lily, and the dungeon, which had a sense of irony Kage was beginning to find personally insulting, replaced it with a single larger Footnote that detonated approximately where Lily would have been if she had not also taken his shouted directional cue and shifted three feet to the right.

[Edit Accepted. Syllable Debt: Standard. The stanza corrects.]

"Stop helping," Lily called over her shoulder, which she said with a laugh that was eighty percent genuine and twenty percent the kind of laugh a healer made when a tank had just nearly killed her.

"Noted," Kage said.

The third edit went well again. He crossed out a long incoming AoE line, and the dungeon replaced it with a long incoming AoE line that fired in a direction the party was already moving away from.

He stopped editing after that. The dungeon was not a tool. It was a partner who occasionally helped and occasionally did not, and the more verses he pulled out of his arm, the slower the orbit on his mark moved.

The party found rhythm.

That was the part Kage kept noticing. Somewhere between the second and third chamber, the ten of them stopped being ten people in a hallway and started being one organism in a hallway. Jax made a call without checking. Zherdev, of all people, planted his ridiculous greatsword in the right place at the right time and stopped a Magma Gloss from reaching Zara's flank, and Zara said "thank you" out loud, which Kage filed as a world event of similar magnitude to the Songstone announcement.

Julie found a use for her Sketch mark in here that no one had expected. She traced clean angular lines across heat-warped rock and the lines became cool. Stable footing in places where the floor had been threatening to give. She was also, Kage observed, cataloging Lily's tells without asking for them, anticipating Lily's missed intercepts a full beat before Lily missed them, and quietly catching the slack with a tether or a counter-pulse. Lily noticed. Lily said nothing.

Vami did Vami things. He hit Lux peak twice in the second corridor and drew an entire Cinder Stanza patrol onto himself, then Purged in the middle of them and reappeared behind the line with most of the patrol still looking at the place he had been. The white-leather aesthetic, Kage admitted, was at minimum a fifty percent advertising budget for his actual playstyle.

Finn was the surprise.

The hawk would call a weak point and Finn would call it a half-second later, and the call would come with a precise distance and a precise wind allowance that Kage had not heard Finn make before. The kid was reading the unfinished ash currents the way other rangers read wind, and the bow released at the exact moment the dungeon was looking the other way.

He grew up two zones ago, Kage thought. Tanglevine did that to him. He is doing the work.

They reached the volcano's rim somewhere in the long second hour.

The chamber was a caldera. Open at the top to the rust-and-umber sky. Slow, viscous magma circulated in the bowl below, and floating above it on three half-finished bridges of cooling basalt was the wing's anchor.

A massive figure of charred granite. A Cinder Stanza three times the size of any they had seen, the verses on its chest written so densely that the words ran together into a single continuous line of glowing red. Its weapon was a hammer of compressed magma the size of Valdrias.

[Magmascript Caesura - Lvl 26]

HP: 24,000 / 24,000

The fight was a fight.

Kage did not remember most of it later, because most of it ran on the same rhythm the rest of the wing had run on, and rhythms blurred when you trusted them. Valdrias planted. Jax bracketed. Finn called. Lily and Julie kept the line alive through three near-misses. Zara stripped resistances and slowed the boss's hammer arm twice, and when the slow landed at the right beat the hammer came down a half-second late and Vami was behind it before it cleared the floor.

The boss had a second phase. It dropped the hammer into the magma. It pulled the magma up the haft as a new weapon, a whip of glowing script that read like a sentence whose ending had not been written.

That was when Kage stopped being useful in the technical sense and started being useful in the directional one. He called four lines. He landed two physical verses, [Strike] into [Bind] on the boss's right arm, and the system registered both at Clean and gave him +4 Convergence and a momentary tightening of his own grip on Mumyo that he would have to think about later. The Caesura's right arm went stiff for two seconds. Vami used those two seconds the way Vami used everything.

By the time the boss was at ten percent, the party had stopped attacking and started watching.

Because the boss was already dead.

Ren was inside the boss's range.

He had been inside it for the last thirty seconds. He had not stepped outside it. He had stepped through it, three times, and each time the boss had moved to crush him, the boss had moved through a Ren-shaped space where Ren had been a blink earlier. The Magmascript Caesura's hammer-whip cracked through air and rock and air and air and air, and Ren walked the edge of the cracks, and the curved longsword in his right hand carved a thin precise line across the boss's chest in seven separate cuts that traced one continuous sentence across the densely written verses.

The sentence finished.

The verses on the boss's chest un-glowed. All at once. The red bled out of them as though Ren had cut the artery feeding the script.

The Magmascript Caesura took one more step. Then, because the verses that had been holding it up were no longer verses, it sat down. Then, because the verses that had been holding it together were no longer verses, it stopped being a thing.

[Magmascript Caesura defeated.]

[Editor's Key Fragment 2/5 acquired.]

[+18,000 EXP. Loot in progress.]

Silence.

Then Zherdev, because Zherdev was Zherdev:

"BRO."

"Yeah," Jax said.

"BRO."

"I heard you the first time."

Kage walked across the cooling basalt to where Ren was standing. Ren had not sheathed the sword yet. The blade was held low, point angled down at a precise twenty degrees, water-position, and the man's breathing had not changed from where it had been three minutes ago.

"That," Zherdev said, "was disgusting. In the best way. What just happened?"

Zara looked at the edge of the blade that was still coated in water, "What is your class."

Ren glanced at her.

He almost smiled. He almost did as his eyes drifted towards Kage for a fraction. The expression caught somewhere on the way up and decided to stay where it was.

"This wing was an easy match for it," he said, mild. "Fire wants to burn fast. Earth wants to hold still. The class. It rewards the third option."

"Which is?"

A pause.

"Flow," Ren said. "Anything I touch. It learns to be water. Briefly. The longer the engagement, the more of the fight wants to behave like water. Fire boils. Stone erodes. Things that wanted to be sharp become smooth. It's." He searched. He chose a word, then chose a different one. "It's an inclination. The class inclines the world. I just stay in it."

"That sounds horrifying," Vami said.

"It is conditional," Ren said. "It does not work everywhere. Like in Storm and Water, the inclination was already there. The class would have nothing to convert. It would be," and the not-quite-smile finally surfaced for a half-second, "redundant."

"In a wing called Fire and Earth," Vami said.

"It is not redundant," Ren said.

He did sheathe the sword then. The motion was clean and silent and cost no part of his posture

The class teaches the world to flow. Water-element conversion. That is the upper end of a Rare-class kit.

And he is already inside it.

Kage did not say any of that. He said:

"Loot. Then back."

The party looted. The Caesura dropped a tower-shield enhancement Valdrias did not need, a weapon Jax took, a small cache of Cinderscript Ink that climbed eagerly toward Kage's open palm before he had decided to extend it. He took it. He pocketed it. He logged it.

[Reagent Acquired: Cinderscript Ink. (Pigment-Adjacent.)]

The fragment, when it materialized, was warm.

He held it in his palm for two seconds. Then he put it with the first one. The first one cooled it.

The script bridge unfurled out of the caldera's lip. The party walked it back through three corridors that the dungeon had quietly cleared, and through the volcano frame's gilt edge, and onto a stage of polished obsidian under a sourceless light.

The lobby received them, as always.

The marks on the three rare carriers had already shifted.

Vami's still glowed. The droplet on his finger pointed in a new direction now, toward a frame Kage had to crane his neck to find above and behind them. Ren's pointed level, dead ahead, toward a frame so far away through the void that it read as a smear of green and gold. Zherdev's pointed down again.

"Three more," Lily said.

"Three more," Kage agreed.

He looked up at the parchment moons. They were turning. The shadows in the crumples held shadows. The shadows in those shadows held something the sourceless light had not yet decided to illuminate.
