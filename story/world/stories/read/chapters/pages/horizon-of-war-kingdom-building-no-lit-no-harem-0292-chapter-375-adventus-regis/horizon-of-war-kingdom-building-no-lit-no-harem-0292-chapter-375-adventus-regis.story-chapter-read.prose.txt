Chapter 375

Adventus Regis

Shogunate Force

Third Day of the Retreat

Under the summer sun, thousands of men and horses marched across the hard, dusty plains of Edessa toward Derenza City. Their bodies ached, and their limbs were heavy with exhaustion, but they had met no ambush during the night. That gave them a small sliver of hope that they had outrun whatever the enemy had planned for them.

Despite their good progress and the absence of any reported sighting of the Margrave’s army, the command staff was in no mood to celebrate. Unknown to all but his senior officers, there had been another reason Sir Harold chose to march through the night. He had wanted to get the army past the site of the ambush without the men realizing it, the same killing ground where their reinforcement had been destroyed at the start of the war.

The scouts had located the killing ground the previous day, and the commanders feared that seeing it in daylight would be too demoralizing for the troops. So the column had passed it during the night by taking a slight detour.

Separately, the commander had tasked his four bailiffs, who had previously held court in Sigisha and Ostaria, along with a column of three hundred riders, to inspect the site. They gathered what evidence they could of the Margrave’s betrayal while recovering valuable plate armor forged from Shogunate steel, along with other precious equipment that could not simply be abandoned.

Of the two thousand missing reinforcements, they counted more than nine hundred bodies. Most were clustered in a rough ring, suggesting that the men had formed a final defensive position and made their last stand there. The fields around them were littered with arrows, torn canvas, broken lances, and deep hoof marks left by repeated cavalry charges. Scores of dead destriers lay among the fallen, further confirming what had happened.

The ambush and the arrows alone had not been enough to break them. The Margrave had been forced to send in his elite knights to finish the job.

As for the rest, they had either fled in the rout or been taken prisoner.

Once the field had been searched and everything worth taking recovered, the riders gathered the bodies into piles, doused them with precious naphtha, and burned them. During the grim work, they recovered the column’s blood-soaked banner from beneath one of the fallen, where it had apparently been hidden from the Margrave’s men.

Unaware of this gruesome work, the common men-at-arms at the main column found the third day of the retreat easier than the days before. The Empire was far behind them, still held up by Ostaria’s walls. Meanwhile, the Margrave was nowhere to be seen, and their night march had likely taken his army by surprise.

But the night march had not come without a price. It had been hard on trained men and harder still on the untrained.

The last time the column had halted, just before sunrise, only about four thousand refugees remained. It was barely a third of those who had followed them from Ostaria. The rest had fallen behind during the night, scattered across the dark plains, given up on keeping pace with the column, or turned south toward another city under the Margrave’s rule.

Now, after hours on the march, everyone was looking forward to another halt before midday. Despite the fatigue and the heat, morale remained respectable.

At the vanguard, Sir Harold walked beside his horse, its reins held by his squire. He led by example. By now, most officers, aside from the lookouts and scouts, had also dismounted and were walking beside their mounts, keeping the animals fresh in case they were needed for action.

He had fashioned a piece of cloth into a crude covering for his head against the sun. He still wore his armor, though not the full harness, and had changed into the standard-issue marching boots, which were far more comfortable for the road. Most of the officers endured the march on foot just like the men-at-arms.

It was no grand gesture, but it mattered. The men saw their commanders bearing the same heat, dust, and exhaustion as the rest of them, and that alone helped keep morale from slipping.

They had been on the move since before midnight, marching through the darkness and halting only for a few hours before dawn, when many simply dropped where they stood and slept from weariness. Yet when morning came, they rose and resumed the march with scarcely a complaint.

A lesser brigade might already have begun to falter, but the Bone Breakers were different. Many among them were veterans, with enough seasoned men, including decorated knights, armigers, and squires, that the newer recruits followed their discipline without question. They were well fed, well trained, and already hardened by long marches.

Their boots, like the ones worn by the officers, were well made and issued in proper sizes, with padding where needed. Thick woolen socks or linen footwraps helped keep their feet dry and protected on the march.

All of it made them formidable over long distances, able to march for hours without blisters and foot pain wearing them down too badly.

Pride helped hold the discipline together. No one wanted to be the first to falter, to let down his unit, or to appear weak before his comrades.

Still, they were only human.

Karl and his squire rode up from the rear guard, their horses kicking up dust as they approached and drawing weary glances from the exhausted column. They dismounted near Sir Harold. Karl handed his reins to his squire, then walked toward the commander.

“Sir,” he greeted. “The rear cannot keep pace much longer. Many are still exhausted from the fighting before Ostaria.”

Sir Harold looked up at the sky. It was indeed nearing midday. He then turned back to the Armiger and gave a single nod. “Understood.” Turning to his vice, he said, “Prepare the column to rest.”

Sir Stefan, in turn, called the scouts over. As they approached, he asked, “What of the village ahead? Have your men checked it?”

“We have. It’s empty, and the wells have been ruined. We fear there may be traps left behind,” the scout leader reported.

Even after leaving the usual road and taking a detour, they had yet to find a single inhabited village. That alone had raised suspicions and made the officers increasingly cautious.

The second scout, much younger, muttered bluntly, “I don’t think it’s wise to risk whatever is waiting there just for some shade.”

None of the officers objected.

Karl, too, agreed, muttering, “The shade would be welcome, but if even a few men are wounded by traps, it will slow us down.”

Two of the officers nodded. They all knew the troops would likely search the village for food or anything useful. And with the men this exhausted, discipline would be harder to keep. If traps had been left behind, it was only a matter of time before someone was wounded.

Sir Stefan made his decision. “Send word for the army to rest in the fields. No tents. Use the canvas for shade. And forbid anyone from wandering off.”

The officers nodded, some more reluctantly than others, but they understood the need.

Turning to the scouts, the vice instructed, “I want the scouts spread out and more eyes watching our flanks.”

“At once,” the scout leader replied.

Then he recalled an earlier report from his men. “There are good grazing grounds ahead. The grass is still green there.”

“We’ll let the horsemen graze their mounts there, but rotate them in groups. We’ll still need riders covering the rear,” Sir Stefan decided.

Soon, nearly nine thousand men were resting in the open fields beside the road beneath the scorching sun. Most had only canvas stretched over their heads before lying down on the bare ground. A fortunate few found trees nearby and crowded beneath them for shade.

Around them lay little but dust and grass. Farms and villages could be seen in the distance, but word had already spread that the wells had been ruined and that traps or poison might be waiting for them.

So the men simply rested their weary feet and bodies. Everyone had been marching since the previous night and through most of the morning, with only a few hours of rest in between. Their exhaustion was crushing, but they knew it was better to pay in sweat than in blood.

...

While the men and refugees rested beneath the scorching sun, the command staff gathered between two parked carts laden with supplies, with a simple canvas stretched overhead for shade. They sat on patches of dry grass rather than directly on the hard, dusty ground. Around them, squires brought waterskins and silver goblets taken from the city.

Sir Harold had called Sir Stefan, Karl, and the scout leader to join him for a simple lunch. Hard biscuit had been broken up and softened in water into a coarse gruel, with crumbled goat cheese mixed through it. As officers, they also had a few olives and cuts of salted meat taken from Ostaria.

“Have the scouts reported from Derenza?” the commander asked as they ate. By now, they should have been nearing the city.

The question troubled the scout leader. “The others who ranged half a day ahead of us have returned. But the man I sent toward Derenza has not. He’s a good rider too, one of the tribesmen.”

Sir Stefan looked at him sharply. “Why was this not reported to me?”

“I thought he might have suffered some minor mishap and decided to give him more time. But now...” The scout leader stopped, then admitted, “The other scouts reported nothing unusual. I may have grown careless.”

Karl spoke up. “The night march exhausted all of us and threw off our sense of time. The scout may have lost his way, or his horse may have failed him.”

Sir Harold said nothing. He considered the worst that might have happened, and new plans were already forming in his mind as he continued eating.

Still, he clung to the hope that it was only an accident.

That hope lasted until two scouts came galloping back toward the resting column. They had spotted several horsemen shadowing the army from roughly half a day’s ride away, almost certainly riders belonging to the Margrave.

“How many of them?” Sir Stefan asked as the officers gathered around.

“Scattered. Perhaps two dozen. Most likely scouts, like us,” came the reply.

“Did they see you?” the scout leader asked.

“It was still dim, but they likely did. They gave no chase. We rode toward an empty village, then took the road for a while to make our trail harder to follow.”

The officers exchanged uneasy glances. Sir Harold remained outwardly unfazed, though inwardly he was troubled.

“Should we get the men moving and form up?” one of the officers asked.

“The threat is still far off. Forming up now will only slow us down,” Sir Stefan warned.

Sir Harold turned toward the open field where his men rested beneath a hard blue sky. The midday sun beat down without mercy, glaring off armor, carts, and the pale dust of the plains. Yet much of the brigade still slept through it, sprawled on the ground beneath scraps of canvas and cloth draped over their heads and bodies for shade.

The night march had wrung them dry. Forcing them back onto their feet now would only grind down what strength they had left.

Thus, he made his decision. “Give them another hour. Then we’ll leave the road and march across the plains, but not in formation. We still need speed.”

His officers nodded, mostly satisfied with the order.

But before the hour had passed, a commotion rose from the front of the column. A scout came galloping in, another man lashed to his back with leather straps. The wounded man hung limp against him, blood darkening his clothes.

At first, the patrol blocked his approach, prompting the scout to explain, “I found him alone on the plains. His horse was gone, and there were arrows in his back.”

When they reached the commander’s side, the guards lifted the wounded man down and laid him carefully on his side atop a canvas spread over the ground. The physicians crowded around him at once, doing what little they could.

The officers gathered and looked toward the scout leader. His face hardened the moment he recognized the wounded man.

“That’s him,” the scout leader said grimly. “He’s the tribesman I sent to Derenza.”

A stunned silence fell over the men gathered around them. Derenza was where they had hoped to find shelter and a defensible position. If the city was under siege, battle could be waiting for them only a few hours ahead.

“Did he say anything?” Sir Stefan asked the scout who had found him.

“He muttered—” The man faltered, then forced the words out. “He muttered that Derenza had fallen.”

The officers broke into anxious mutters. A few cursed under their breath, while others began speaking over one another in disbelief. None of them had expected the city to be taken. Whatever hope they had placed in reaching Derenza was snuffed out in an instant.

Amid the commotion, Karl asked, “Did you get a good look at the arrows?”

“I did. I cut the shafts short before bringing him back.” The scout glanced toward the wounded man. “They’re the kind used by the Margrave’s nomads.”

“We’re doomed...” Even the veteran of many wars snapped at the sudden disaster before them.

For the first time, Sir Stefan had nothing to say. He looked haggard and drawn.

Their commander, Sir Harold remained a bulwark of a man, yet even he seemed uncertain. The situation had become nearly impossible to salvage.

Their men were exhausted after two forced marches, and now there was no safe haven ahead. Corna was still six days’ march away, and the whole way there they would be hounded by the Margrave’s army.

The Margrave had truly driven them toward slaughter. He had read their intentions, anticipated their every move, and closed each path before they could reach it.

Now, if they headed west, the Margrave would destroy them as they passed Derenza and Sirmona. If they turned east, the Empire would find them.

It began to dawn on them that Sir Sterling’s sacrifice, and that of all the brave men who had remained in Ostaria, might come to nothing. The Shogunate force had been doomed from its first move.

Derenza’s fall had sealed their fate.

The southern summer wind swept dust over them. Above, their banners still snapped proudly in the wind, but their fate hung in the balance.

The command staff continued to deliberate, but no matter how many ideas were put forward, no solution could be found. Frustration set in. Here on the plains of Edessa, they had been thoroughly outmaneuvered.

...

Midday slowly passed while the column remained at rest. The sun burned overhead beneath a mostly cloudless sky. The Lowlandians were hardy and accustomed to such weather. The Midlandians took it harder. It was not merely the heat and sweat; the dry wind blowing in from the eastern sea left many of them miserable. Before long, their discomfort became a source of playful mockery among the men.

As the rest drew to an end, men began rising on trembling legs and weary bodies. Some rubbed at cramped calves and sore feet before searching for something to eat.

Soon, grim news of Derenza’s fall spread through the column.

The effect was immediate. Questions and curses passed from group to group. Some refused to believe it until they heard the same account from several others.

After leaving Ostaria and enduring forced marches, including through the night, they had all expected to find rest behind Derenza’s walls. More than hot food, the city should have offered the safety they had yearned for after days of marching through hostile territory. Now, that hope had been torn away from them.

Worse, everyone knew that getting past Sirmona would already mean a bloody fight. With Derenza fallen as well, their road to Corna had become far more dangerous.

Now, their night marches across the plains suddenly seemed almost wasted.

All that remained was six days of hard marching to Corna, undoubtedly longer if the Margrave blocked the roads. Water was already scarce and unreliable. Others spoke of turning back toward Ostaria. Neither choice sounded any better.

Slowly, the same exhausted, sullen look settled over everyone, many beginning to realize that they might have only days left to live.

Unnoticed by most, a group of patrolling riders was returning from the north.

There seemed to be nothing unusual about that. But the manner of their return caught a few eyes.

They were mounted, yet they walked beside their horses. And beside those horses walked several figures in cloaks.

One man rose to get a better look. Another followed, and soon more eyes turned toward the west.

Most did not know what to make of it. Many dismissed the sight after a glance, assuming the patrol had picked up stragglers, wayfarers, or merchants somewhere along the way.

But others continued to watch. It was strange enough that the riders had dismounted and chosen to walk beside the strangers.

The scout leader noticed it as well. He rode out with several men to meet the approaching group.

A short while later, they too dismounted and joined the group.

That drew more eyes.

Amid the drifting dust, one man-at-arms asked the lookout beside him, “Who are they?”

“Travelers?” his friend replied, peering through an Ekionian optic. Even he sounded unsure.

Low murmurs began to spread through the troubled column. Nothing alarming yet, only curiosity. But more and more men were turning their eyes toward the north.

The group moved on without challenge. Two more riders came out to meet them. After a brief exchange, both men dismounted and joined the procession.

More men began to stand. Still, there was no reason for alarm. No weapons had been drawn, and there was no sign of any threat.

As this group drew closer, a pattern became clear. The riders and scouts kept glancing toward one man in a traveling cloak walking behind the others. Yet he rode no horse, wore no splendid armor, and carried nothing that marked him as anyone important.

The wind strengthened, driving dust across the field beneath the shifting sky.

Soon, the newcomers reached the edge of the uneasy column, and a thousand wary eyes turned toward them.

One of the cloaked figures reached up and pulled back her hood, revealing the furred face and ears of a half-kin.

A gasp ran through the men.

“Ocelot!” someone shouted.

The half-kin turned toward the voice and gave a single nod.

Murmurs spread at once, the troubled mood giving way to growing curiosity. More and more men rose to their feet, craning for a better look as the group moved deeper among the thousands.

Surrounded by friendlies, another figure opened his cloak. Beneath it was a handsome young man, Elandian by the look of him, carrying a large leather bag over one shoulder.

No one recognized him. That only deepened the mystery.

Some men began crowding around the lookouts.

“Did you see an airship?” one of them demanded.

“I saw none.”

That only deepened the confusion.

Leading the group were five hooded figures who moved unopposed through the column. As they drew closer, some of the men recognized those in front as the Lord Shogun’s guards.

That alone drew gasps. Then someone looked past them.

The man walking behind the guards wore a dusty brigandine beneath his traveling cloak. His face was mostly hidden beneath a hood, and there was nothing splendid about him, nothing that should have drawn so many eyes. He said nothing as he walked. Even the thin, coarse beard drew only puzzled frowns.

Then they noticed its color.

A few men suddenly went still. One muttered under his breath, “Black... black...” as though trying to convince himself of what he was seeing.

Another raised a trembling hand and pointed. “That... that’s—”

Others followed his gaze. More men began rising from the ground. Those still lying beneath canvas pushed themselves upright, while even those caught in heated arguments over their fate fell silent and turned to look. Voices spread from one group to the next, first uncertain, then sharper, carrying through the column.

“Impossible!” someone blurted out. Yet even those who doubted it were already moving closer, their chests tight with anticipation. Something was happening, and the realization spread faster than any order.

Soon, even the officers, consumed by their own grim deliberations, began to notice the growing commotion. They turned and saw Ocelot, the young Elandian, and the rest of the hooded figures approaching through the column.

Karl and Sir Stefan readily stepped in front of Sir Harold.

Karl recognized Ocelot at once. She caught his eye and grinned. That was enough to tell him something was very wrong—or very right.

“What is this? I don’t recall inviting—” Sir Stefan began.

“Sir Stefan, you look as though you could use plenty of rest.”

The voice from the rear of the group stopped him cold. At that same moment, the two figures in front stepped aside.

The man in the dusty brigandine reached up and pulled back his hood.

A familiar face emerged beneath the hood, changed by a thin, coarse beard and unmistakable black hair.

Gasps came from the officers and the thousand men around them. The impossible stood before them.

For a moment, no one could speak. Just as the column had begun to believe there was no fate left for them but death, the man who had been gone for nearly two years had returned.

“Karl,” the figure continued, “good to see you.”

“My Lord!” Sir Harold bellowed before Karl could answer, the words breaking from him with years of grief. Still hardly believing his eyes, he pushed past Karl, whose eyes were already wet, and Sir Stefan beside him, then strode forward.

“Harold. By the Ancients, it’s good to see you well, old friend.”

The Lord had barely reached him before the knight commander seized him in a crushing embrace and lifted him from the ground.

That broke whatever restraint remained. The officers, old and young alike, shouted themselves hoarse, tears welling in many eyes. Others cheered hard. The cries spread through the column, and soon thousands more across the field would learn who had come to meet them.

For the moment, even the fall of Derenza was pushed aside. That was secondary. Their Lord had returned, and that was all that mattered to them.

On the plains of Edessa, the Lord of Korelia had taken command of his brigade once again.

Lansius

Sir Harold and the staff quickly brought him up to speed, though Lansius already knew much of it. Lord Avery’s aerial scouts had kept him informed of the general situation.

Beneath a field umbrella held by Ocelot, with a young physician still keeping an eye on him, Lansius studied a map pinned to the back of a guard’s pack. Both he and the guard were seated while the officers crowded close around them.

Several still could not quite master their tears. Lansius found their reaction almost excessive. To him, it felt as though he had merely fallen asleep beneath a curse and awakened again.

“My Lord, with Derenza fallen...” Sir Harold began, trying to return them to the matter at hand.

“This is war,” Lansius replied casually. “Whatever can go wrong, eventually will. We’ll mourn the dead and exact our revenge in time. Right now, survival comes first.”

Sir Stefan, kneeling with the others in a half-ring around Lansius, spoke next. “My Lord, we’ll be hard-pressed to get past both Derenza and Sirmona.”

Karl added, “If they took Derenza this quickly, then the Margrave must have committed a sizeable force.”

Lansius nodded and stroked his chin. He was still not used to the beard. During his long sleep, it had felt much longer, and now the real thing seemed almost disappointingly short. There was something oddly satisfying about running his fingers through it.

Finally, he spoke. “Derenza and Sirmona are not a problem.”

His answer made the officers exchange glances.

“But to reach Corna—” Sir Stefan began.

“That’s where you have it wrong.” Lansius grinned. “We don’t.”

Again, the officers looked at one another, murmuring among themselves.

Lansius gave a small shrug. “Why should we walk into the trap they prepared for us? We’ll make our own path.”

“But where?” Sir Harold asked. “Back to Ostaria?”

“No. I have another plan for Ostaria, but it isn’t for the Bone Breakers.” Lansius glanced at the sun, then pointed toward another direction across the plains. “We go that way.”

Everyone followed his finger and naturally saw nothing but open plains.

“South?” Karl blurted out.

Lansius nodded.

The officers immediately broke into argument. Some pointed out that south would take them farther from the safety of their allies. Others warned that it led deeper into hostile territory. They had only so much food and could not keep marching indefinitely. The Empire’s mounted irregulars would swing south and find them there. One concern followed another, and soon nearly every officer had something to say.

Against the barrage of warnings, Lansius laughed.

“You’ve all gone too soft," he remarked. "War is not something you can play safely. At its heart, it is deception upon deception. If the Margrave is clever, then we’ll have to outplay him.”

Turning to Sir Harold, he instructed, “Have the men march in loose order. I want speed. We’ll head south.”

“But can the men endure a long march?” Sir Stefan pressed. Beneath the summer sun, his face was haggard and slick with sweat, worn down by exhaustion and worry.

Lansius offered a thin smile. “In my birthplace, we have a record called the Anabasis.”

His words caught everyone’s attention.

“It tells of ten thousand mercenaries who marched deep into a foreign land to fight for a prince. They won their battle, but the prince was killed. Suddenly, they found themselves stranded far from home, surrounded by hostile lands and enemies who wanted them dead. And what do you think happened to them?”

Those listening exchanged glances, but none dared to answer.

Lansius continued. “Against the odds, they marched through hostile lands. It took them more than a year, across a vast distance, fighting battle after battle along the way. Of those ten thousand, seven thousand managed to make it home.”

He looked around at them. “Gentlemen, our march south will not take a year. It can be done.”

His words struck the officers and the thousands gathered around them.

“But will we find safety in the south?” the scout leader couldn't resist asking.

Lansius met the scout’s eyes. “Tell me, what lies in the far south?”

Everyone tried to decipher what the Lord was truly asking. They all had an answer, but none were certain whether it was the one he wanted.

Karl finally answered, “Salceslia?”

Lansius turned to him with a faint smile. “Correct.”

Sir Stefan was quick to point out, “But Salceslia is allied with the Margrave.”

“Neutral,” Lansius countered.

Murmurs immediately spread among the officers and the men gathered around them. Some knew it to be true, at least officially.

“That may be what they claim on paper, but in truth, they are allies of the Margrave,” Karl repeated, backing the vice’s point amid the growing din.

“That is also correct,” Lansius confirmed. “And this is where we use their own words against them. Since they never changed their official stance, then technically, they still stand with us against the Empire and the traitorous Margrave. They are vassals of the Imperium, are they not?”

The murmuring grew faster as the implication began to spread.

“We’ll use that pretext to call for their aid. And if they fail us, then they’re no better than enemies of the Imperium. And what do we do with traitors?”

The men stirred at once. Shouts and curses rose from the ranks, along with harsh calls for punishment, many of them answered by approving roars.

Lansius’ faint smile thinned. “With Edessa already turned traitor, we cannot afford another traitor on our flank. The Lord of Galdia is ready to march with the allied nomads. If I give the signal, we’ll strike Salceslia from two sides.”

That changed the mood again. The shouting gave way to hurried exchanges as men began to grasp more of Lansius’ design.

Sir Stefan frowned, already seeing holes in the plan. “But won’t the Empire and the Margrave simply chase us?”

“Of course.” Lansius gave a low chuckle. “I want them to.”

That answer only stirred the officers and men further.

“In fact,” Lansius added, “that would be a wonderful thing.”

With confusion still hanging over the men, Lansius showed no desire to explain further. He simply turned his back on them and started toward the vanguard, leaving the question hanging behind him.

The officers nearest him hurried after him, calling for an explanation.

“Please, My Lord, enlighten us,” one pleaded, catching at Lansius’ cloak, only to have his wrist seized by one of the guards.

Another called after him, “My Lord, we cannot advance with confidence if we do not know the plan.”

“Ocelot,” Lansius called.

The half-kin following close behind him, still holding his umbrella, answered at once, “My Lord.”

“Remind them of the military law concerning a commander’s plans,” Lansius instructed, knowing her to be a scholar before a soldier.

“My Lord.” The Umberlander turned toward the officers and the men around them. Her voice became rigid and formal. “By the military ordinances first laid down under the First Emperor and amended under the Third Emperor, the Ageless, the design of a campaign is held solely at the discretion of its commander and may be disclosed only to those he judges necessary. Any man who demands such knowledge without leave, or seeks to compel his commander to reveal it, may be detained and examined on suspicion of espionage.”

That brought the officers up short.

Lansius murmured to himself, “Let your plans be dark and impenetrable as night. And when you move, fall like a thunderbolt.”

Still burdened by doubt, Sir Stefan dared to ask, “My Lord, one question. What about food? We only have enough to reach Corna.”

“The Lord of Dawn will make the sky rain bread if he has to,” Lansius reassured him. Then he turned toward the thousands gathered around him. Faces young and old, exhausted, dust-streaked, and hollow-eyed, were all fixed on him.

“Gentlemen, put your hearts at ease. We are going to outplay the Margrave at his own game. This is but one small part of my plan. I’ll make certain that treacherous bastard and his allies pay for their treachery.”

A hard murmur rolled through the men around him. Faces hollow with exhaustion suddenly looked sharp and alive.

Then, walking past Sir Harold, Lansius said, “Get the signalman. From this moment on, we are no longer retreating.”

He paused. Their eyes met.

“We are advancing.” Lansius pulled his hood back over his head and kept walking.

Sir Harold straightened. The tall commander turned toward the waiting ranks and bellowed, “Sound the cornu! We’re advancing south!”

For a heartbeat, nearly ten thousand men stared back in disbelief. Then the cornu sounded, its deep call rolling across the field. The army erupted in a triumphant roar.

The Margrave had spent days driving them toward ruin.

The Lord Shogun had needed only moments to change the direction of the war.
