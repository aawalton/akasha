What’s wrong.

The question hung in the small cabin like a held note. It was not loud. Aiven had asked it gently. But it filled the space anyway, the way certain questions do, the way they take up more room than the words they are made of.

Virelle stood in front of him.

Her hands had moved without her permission. They were folded in front of her now, the fingers of one cupping the knuckles of the other, the way a person holds their own hand when there is no one else’s to hold. Her prismatic orb hovered low at her shoulder, dim, waiting.

What’s wrong.

She turned the question over in her mind and could not find a place to set it down.

She knew what the right answer was. The right answer was the one her Master needed. The right answer was the one that would let him train tomorrow and the day after, that would not crack the steady scaffolding he had built around himself this morning, that would not add one more weight to a back already carrying too many. The right answer was a small lie, neatly folded, held out at arm’s length.

She tried to say it.

“N-nothing is wrong, Master.”

The words came out of her mouth and she heard, immediately, how thin they were. They did not have any weight. They drifted in the air between them like cloth, and Aiven was already looking at her in the way he looked at a manifest where the columns did not add up.

She felt herself flush.

Aiven did not call her on it.

He simply patted the mattress beside him. Once, lightly, his palm flat on the blanket.

“Come sit,” he said.

Virelle hesitated.

It was the smallest of hesitations. Half a second. Long enough that, in a different mood, she would have hidden it under a sweep of her hair and a remark about how her Master was being awfully presumptuous, with a teasing remark that perhaps he was ready to be honest of his feelings towards her. The remark was already half-formed at the back of her throat.

She did not say it.

She drifted forward, slowly, and let her feet touch the floorboards. She sat. The mattress dipped. The frame creaked again, that same soft groan it had given when Aiven had sat down.

She kept her hands in her lap.

She left a hand’s width of space between them.

Aiven did not look at her right away.

He looked at his own hands instead, the obsidian one and the human one, both resting loose on his knees. He watched the way the lantern light caught the cyan etchings on the Armvil and slid off them again. He drew in a breath.

“I think,” he said carefully, “that I might have a gist of what is going on. I am not sure. I might be wrong. But I think I might know.”

He paused.

“What I want, though, is to actually know. From you. Not from me guessing. I would like for you to be honest with me, Virelle. If you can.”

Virelle’s mouth opened.

It closed again.

She tried to find the words, and the words were not there. It was a strange thing for her, this absence. Words had always been the easiest of her tools. She had a thousand of them, in a dozen registers, a phrase ready for every social situation she had ever been thrust into. She could insult the lineage of a stranger in seven different metaphors before her tea cooled. She could make a threat sound like a love letter and a love letter sound like a threat.

And now, asked a single quiet question by her Master, she could not seem to put one word in front of another.

Because the words she had were ugly.

The words she had were the ones that named the wickedness in her. The ones that made it real, that took it out of the dim corner of her chest where it lived and brought it into the lamplight where her Master could see it. The words for I was glad. The words for I did not want her back. The words for there is a thing inside me that is not what you want me to be.

She tried to choose a softer one.

She could not find one.

Her hands tightened in her lap.

Aiven watched her struggle.

He watched the small motions of her hands. He watched the way her shoulders had drawn in. He watched her open her mouth and close it again, twice, three times, like a person trying to lift something too heavy with the wrong grip.

Something in him gave way.

“I’m sorry,” he said quietly.

Virelle’s eyes lifted.

She looked at him with an expression he could not read. There was confusion in it. There was something else underneath the confusion that he thought might be hope, or might have been a kind of dread, or might simply have been a person bracing for a sentence that was going to land somewhere they had not yet figured out how to defend.

He met her eyes.

“I need to apologize to you,” he said. “Seeing Lyra again must have... it must have given you mixed feelings. I think I have been pretending I did not know that. And it was not fair of me.”

He drew in a breath and continued.

“Do you remember the morning on the Cinder-Fox. Before the Sunken Fane. The conversation we had, on the observation deck.”

Virelle’s breath caught, very faintly. She nodded.

“You were honest with me that morning. You told me what you felt, and you asked me what I felt, and what I told you was that I needed more time. That things were moving fast. That I was not ready to put a name on what was happening between us.”

He swallowed.

“And you said yes. You waited. You stayed. You teased me about it the way you tease about everything, but you waited.”

He looked down at his hands.

“And then Lyra appeared.”

The name landed in the cabin like a stone in still water.

“I had thought she was dead. For a month, I had been carrying her like a stone in my chest. And then she was alive, sitting across from me, with the same eyes and the same hair and the same voice, and...”

He made a small, helpless gesture with his obsidian hand.

“I have to be honest. Even after Lucien took her, my mind was full of her. Today, in the meeting room, when I was talking about training, half of what I was thinking about was her. She is, whether I want her to be or not, the reason I am doing all of this.”

Virelle’s gaze had dropped to her lap.

He could see only the top of her head now. The pale silver-lavender of her hair. The slight curl of it against her shoulder.

“So…I’m sorry, Virelle.”

His voice was very quiet now.

“I am sorry that you have had to sit with all of this. Not just today. Not just this morning. From the moment Lyra walked back into my life, you have been carrying something I never asked you what it cost. You have been waiting for me to sort out my feelings. And now, on top of all of that, you are also helping me train, helping me prepare for a fight, helping me get her back. And nobody asked you whether that was fair to you.”

He shook his head, slowly.

“I did not ask you whether it was fair to you. I just... assumed. I assumed you would be there. I assumed you would help. Because that is what you have always done. And I think I have been using that as an excuse not to look at what you might be feeling.”

He turned, just a little, on the bed. So that he was facing her more directly.

“I need you to be honest with me, Virelle. Not because I am ordering you to be. Because something has been wrong, and I have felt it. There has been a wall between us since Lyra showed up. A thin one. The kind I cannot quite see, but I keep walking into it. And I do not want it to be there.”

Virelle’s hands had begun to twist in the fabric of her skirt.

Her voice, when it came, was very small.

“What must I do, Master?”

She did not look up.

“What must I do so that you feel comfortable around me again? Tell me. I will do it. I will adjust. I will be whatever you need me to be. I am very good at adjusting. You have seen me adjust before. I can—”

“Virelle.”

He said her name very gently.

She stopped.

“It is not about what I want.”

He said it slowly. He wanted her to hear each word land.

“It is about what you want. I do not want you to adjust. I do not want you to be a different version of yourself for my comfort. If you are sitting here right now, conditioning yourself, holding things back, performing a quieter and more well-behaved version of you because you think that is what I need... that is not the relationship I want.”

He drew in a breath.

“I want you to be Virelle.”

He paused.

“Not a summon. Not a mage who works for her Master. Not a useful asset I bring along on missions. Just... you.”

Virelle did not lift her head.

But her shoulders had begun, very faintly, to shake.

Aiven hesitated.

He looked down at his own hands again. He felt, suddenly, the weight of his own hypocrisy pressing on his shoulders. The clerk in him filed it neatly. He picked it up and decided to say it out loud anyway.

“Although,” he said, with a small, tired half-smile, “I realize I might be a bit of a hypocrite, saying that.”

Virelle made a small sound. It was not quite a laugh.

“I am telling you to be yourself,” he said, “while in the same breath asking you to teach me how to use a power I do not understand, asking you to help me get ready for a fight I might not survive, and now asking you to tell me about your feelings on top of all of that. I am asking a lot of you. I see that. And I am sorry for that, too.”

He paused.

“Which is why I want to be clear. This is not an order, Virelle. None of this is. I am not your Master in this conversation. Or, well. I am, technically. But not in the way that means you have to do anything. You can do whatever you want. If you do not want to talk about your feelings tonight, then you do not have to talk about them tonight.”

He looked at her.

“I just wanted to put it out there. That whatever this thing is, that you have been carrying, you do not have to hold it back for my sake. If that is what you have been doing. I would rather know about it than not know. Even if it is hard for me to hear. I would rather hear it than feel a wall between us and not understand why.”

For a long time, Virelle did not move.

She kept her head bowed. Her hands kept their slow, twisting work in her skirt. The lantern guttered softly on the writing desk. Outside the porthole, somewhere in the deep blue, a night bird called once and went quiet.

Then, slowly, she lifted her face.

Her eyes were bright. Not with tears, exactly. With something harder to name. The lantern light caught in them, and for a moment they looked very young.

“Is it,” she said. Her voice was rough at the edges. She cleared her throat and tried again. “Is it... all right, if I am honest?”

“Yes,” Aiven said. “That is the point.”

She looked at him.

She looked at him for a long moment, the way someone looks at a door they are not sure they should open.

“Even if,” she said carefully, “the things I say are messy. And ugly. And not the things you want to hear?”

Aiven did not look away.

“Yes,” he said. “Even then.”

Virelle drew in a breath.

It was a careful breath. The kind a person takes before stepping off a ledge they have been standing on for too long.

“I am selfish,” she said.

Her voice was low. Steady. She was looking somewhere past Aiven’s shoulder, at a place on the cabin wall that did not have anything in particular on it.

“I have always been selfish, Master. From the moment I was summoned. From the moment I drew my first breath, I have cared for no one in the world except you.”

She paused.

“Not Rysa. Not Marnie. Not the villagers of Oakwood. Not the petrified rabbit at the inn. The only person I have ever cared about, truly, is you. The rest of the world is scenery.”

She gave a small, mirthless smile to the wall.

“I am aware that this is not a virtue. I am aware of it. I have been aware of it for some time. I have caused you problems, Master, more problems than I have solved. I have made enemies for you that you did not need. I have antagonized people you needed to negotiate with. I have spent more of your time managing my temper than I have spent being useful to you.”

She drew in a slow breath.

“And I am grateful, Master. For your patience. For the fact that you have not, in all this time, looked at me the way one looks at a very expensive piece of furniture that has begun to break the other furniture. I do not know how you have not. But you have not.”

Her hands tightened in her skirt.

“This, though.”

She closed her eyes.

“This is different.”

Aiven did not interrupt. He sat perfectly still on the bed beside her, his hands folded loosely in his lap, his attention given fully to her in the way only a clerk knew how to give attention. He was not waiting for his turn to speak. He was simply listening.

Virelle opened her eyes again.

“Since Lyra appeared,” she said, “since the two of you... hugged, in the clearing, after she came down from the sky, I have been feeling something. I do not know what to call it. I have tried. I have run my mind over it the way you run your fingers over a sealed envelope, trying to feel the shape of the thing inside, and I cannot find one word that fits. It is many things at once.”

She let out a small, unsteady breath.

“Some of it is jealousy. I am familiar with jealousy. I have felt it many times. When Rysa puts her hand on your shoulder. When the fox bows too close to your face. That feeling, I know. It is hot. It is bright. It is easy to swing around.”

She shook her head, slowly.

“This is more than that. There is jealousy in it, yes. But there is also... fear. The fear of losing you. Not in the way I have feared losing you to a monster. A different kind. A quieter kind. The kind that does not have a fight in it.”

Her voice caught, very slightly.

“And there is envy. I envy her, Master. I envy that she has known you longer than I have. I envy that she has memories of you that I will never have. I envy that you ached for her for an entire month, in a way I do not believe you would ache for me, if I were the one who had been taken.”

She paused again.

And then her voice grew very quiet.

“And there is something else. Underneath all of that. Something I have been trying very hard not to look at.”

She turned her head slightly. Not enough to look at him. Just enough that the lantern light caught the silver at her temple.

“There is a part of me that wants her to disappear.”

Aiven’s heart gave a small, sharp jump.

He did not let it show. He kept his hands folded. He kept his breathing even. But the jump was real, and Virelle had probably felt it anyway, because she made a small sound at the back of her throat that might have been a laugh and might have been the beginning of a sob.

“I know,” she said. “I know how it sounds. I know what kind of person it makes me. I have been telling myself it is not really there. That it is some passing static in my head, that it will dissolve once the morning comes, that it is not really mine. But it is mine. It is mine and I cannot get rid of it.”

She pressed her hands flat against her thighs, holding herself steady.

“I thought the selfishness I had before was bad. The little selfishness. The one that wanted to be the only summon you carried, the only mage at your side, the only one who got to call you Master. I thought that was the bottom of it.”

She drew in a ragged breath.

“This is worse. This is something else entirely.”

Her voice dropped further.

“Because I know Lyra is important to you, Master. I have seen the way your face looked when she was sitting across from you at that table. I have heard the way you said her name. I know you want to be by her side. I know you would burn the whole sky down to get her back. I know all of this. And I want her to stay away from you anyway. I want her to stay wherever Lucien has taken her, far from you, far from this ship, far from the conversation we will have tomorrow morning, far from the life we have been building. I want that, knowing how much it would devastate you. I want it anyway.”

She closed her eyes again.

“When Lucien took her in the clearing,” she said, very softly, “I was glad.”

The words landed in the cabin like a stone in a deep well.

“I did not look glad. I did not let my face be glad. But inside, where you could not see, I was glad. Because the seat beside you was mine again. Because the woman who had been holding your hand at the table was gone. Because I did not have to share the room with her anymore. I told myself, in that moment, that I was simply relieved that the fight had not happened, that you were safe, that all was well. But that was not the whole truth. The whole truth was uglier than that.”

She opened her eyes. They were very bright now.

“When I grabbed your wrist. When you were about to attack him, and I stopped you. I told you, with a shake of my head, that it was unwise. That Lyra had told you not to fight. That you could not control your power yet, and that to attack now would risk everything.”

Her throat worked.

“You might think I did it for those reasons. You probably do think that. They are good reasons. They are true reasons. But Master, they were not the only reasons.”

She did look at him now. Just for a moment.

“Deep down,” she said, “I did it so Lucien would take her away. Quickly. A fight would have delayed that. And worse than that. If you had fought and won, then Lyra would have stayed. With us. On this ship. In our lives. And I did not want that. I did not want that more than I wanted you to be safe. I did not want that more than I wanted you to be happy.”

She looked back at her hands.

“So when I stopped you. I was not just stopping you from fighting. I was helping him take her away. I was complicit. I did the thing that helped my wickedness, and I dressed it up as wisdom, and I gave it to you with a small headshake, and you believed me. Because you trust me. Because you have always trusted me.”

Her voice was thinner now.

“That is why I think I am selfish, Master. Not the small kind. The wicked kind. The kind that knows exactly what the other person wants, and works against it anyway. The kind that wears a smile of concern while quietly arranging the room so the other person cannot get what they want. The kind that calls itself wisdom when it is really only appetite.”

She took in a breath and it shuddered on the way down.

“I am that kind. I am that kind, Master. I have looked at it for the past two days, and I cannot pretend it is anything else.”

The first tear slid down her cheek.

She did not notice it at first. It went all the way to her jaw before she lifted a hand and brushed at it, the back of her wrist swiping clumsily across her own face. Another followed. Then another. They came faster than her hands could keep up with, and one fell into the silver fabric of her skirt, leaving a small dark spot in the constellation-cloth.

She rubbed her eyes.

"Three days ago," she said. Her voice cracked. "Three days ago, I had you comforting me when I was down, because the fox told me I was a dungeon core. I had only just stopped feeling like the floor was about to give way under me. I had only just started to feel like a person again. And now I am here, on this bed, telling you I have done a wicked thing, and crying about it like... like a child, while you sit there and listen to me, and the rest of you needs to be training tomorrow, and you have a fight in six days, and Lyra is still wherever Lucien has taken her, and you have so many things to think about, and I am taking up your evening with this. With me.”

She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

“I do not know what to do, Master.”

Her voice was wet now, and small.

“I am a burden. I am being a burden again. I have only just stopped being one and now I am being one again. I am sorry. I am sorry. I do not know what to do.”

Aiven did not move for a long moment.

The word landed on him the way certain words do. Not with a sound. With a quiet, low ringing, like a struck bell that someone else could not hear. Burden. She had said burden.

He had many things he wanted to say.

He kept all of them, for now, behind his teeth.

Because she was still crying. And what she needed first, before any of his words, was to know that the room had held what she had said. That the floor had not given out. That the man on the bed beside her was still there.

He reached, very slowly, for her hand.
