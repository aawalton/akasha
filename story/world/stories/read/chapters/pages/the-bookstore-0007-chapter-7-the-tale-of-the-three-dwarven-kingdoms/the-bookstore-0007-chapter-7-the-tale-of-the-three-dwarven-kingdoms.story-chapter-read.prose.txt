Rick turned toward the storeowner and found what felt like an entirely different woman from the one who had pinned him to the ground outside.

A moment later, his eyes caught the blue beast resting on the counter, and his heart lurched again.

How had he missed it before?

Even if it was only a familiar, a hunter should not fail to notice a magic beast at such a distance. He had already let himself get trapped once today. Realizing he had overlooked the creature too only made the humiliation hit deeper.

Anne noticed the shift in his face immediately.

After such naked curiosity, this sudden awkwardness could only mean there was something hiding beneath it. A wound, perhaps. A frustration. Some embarrassing problem. And what were problems, if not opportunities waiting for the right kind of attention?

At this point she still did not know what troubled him. So she did what she always did first.

She made it easier for him to speak.

“I just moved here,” she said, setting a cup before him. “Today is my first day of official business, so I do not know many people yet. My name is Anne. And you are?”

She spoke lightly and took a sip of her own tea, as though the conversation were nothing serious. The mention of not knowing anyone was deliberate. She had a suspicion the young man was not entirely comfortable with the town, and people in that situation often needed only a small invitation to start unburdening themselves.

Rick forced a smile and sat at the counter.

He discreetly smelled the tea first, then took a cautious sip before giving his name. The warmth steadied him, and when he noticed Anne’s eyes flick briefly toward the small game hanging over his shoulder, he felt compelled to explain.

“I’m a hunter,” he said. “I just came back from the forbidden forest, but...” He trailed off, embarrassed. “I was out there nearly a month and only caught enough to feed myself. And this little fox.” He gave a short laugh that died quickly. “But I’m not that bad. It’s just...” He looked away. “There aren’t many beasts on this side of the mountains, and the other side is too dangerous. That’s why we don’t get many adventurers. If only our town had turned out like Riverend...”

The words kept coming after that.

Perhaps because Anne did not interrupt. Perhaps because the tea softened something in him. Or perhaps because there was relief in speaking to someone who had no expectations of him yet.

“You’re probably thinking I should have gone there instead, right?” he said. “I did, actually. I argued with my family and left town. Said a lot of stupid things too. Riverend had everything we didn’t—more beasts, better materials, better chances. But it also drew too much greed. For people like me, it was a slaughterhouse.” His mouth tightened. “Fees for this, fees for that. Fees to enter the forest. Fees to hunt in good areas. Unless you had backing or rare talent, there was no way to grow stronger.”

Anne listened with patient attention, hiding her satisfaction behind another measured sip of tea.

The boy was opening up beautifully.

“Can you tell me more about your work?” she asked.

Rick blinked.

“Being a hunter?”

Anne nodded.

He hesitated, likely worried he would bore her, then answered anyway.

“It’s simple, I guess. Difficult, but simple. We hunt magic beasts. Kill or capture them.”

Anne waited. The silence stretched just enough that he understood she wanted more than that, so he continued.

“It’s not like being a warrior,” he said. “We do need to train, but fighting isn’t the center of it. A hunter survives by knowing what he’s dealing with. Every beast has habits, abilities, weak points... if you understand those, you can prepare traps, set ambushes, choose the right weapon, even decide whether a fight is worth it.” As he spoke, some life returned to his expression. “But hunters aren’t considered a main path. Not like warriors, mages, or healers. So most of us work alone, and that means there are places we just can’t go.”

By the end of it, his earlier discouragement had crept back in.

Anne leaned back slightly and let her voice take on a quieter certainty.

“I understand that better than you think. People always look down on those who seek power through something other than brute strength or combat magic. They treat knowledge as secondary. Decorative, even. But you don’t need them.”

Rick gave a weak smile. He appreciated the sentiment, but appreciation was not the same as belief. His own limits were too familiar to him to accept confidence from encouragement alone.

Anne saw it clearly. So she gave him what he actually needed.

“You do not lack ability,” she said. “You lack the right knowledge.”

There.

The line landed exactly as she wanted.

Rick watched her with sudden, open curiosity as Anne rose and disappeared into the room behind the counter. He did not know why those words affected him so strongly, only that they did. Something in them sounded less like comfort and more like diagnosis.

“What languages can you read?” Anne called from inside.

The question hit with almost physical force.

Rick winced.

He answered loudly enough that she would not have to ask again.

“I can recognize my name, numbers, and a few words in the common tongue, but...” He let the rest fall away in embarrassment.

“I see,” Anne said.

Even with all her training, she could not fully hide the disappointment in her tone.

The state of education had grown pitiful. A century ago, most common people would have learned to read at least one language properly and often picked up another through trade, worship, or work. But magitech had changed habits. When artifacts answered questions instantly and simplified whole tasks, people stopped valuing literacy unless their path demanded it. Now, most clung to one practical dialect and left letters to scholars, officials, and researchers.

When Anne returned, she carried two books.

Rick’s newly risen hope faltered the instant he saw them. He had not missed her disappointment, and now he assumed the worst. Books meant reading. And reading was just another failure.

But Anne placed the two volumes on the counter without looking defeated in the least.

“Let me see if I understood your problem correctly,” she said. “In a sense, it is not just your problem. It is Mountainend’s problem as well.”

Rick straightened despite himself.

“The main issue here is the lack of low- to medium-magic zones,” Anne continued. “Because the mountains disrupt the natural flow of energy, you do not get the usual gradual rise in magic density. The kind of progression areas that should exist between safe land and truly dangerous territory never formed properly on this side. So the town has almost no middle ground. You either hunt in scarce weak areas here, or you cross the mountains and face something much harsher on the other side.”

Rick nodded slowly.

None of that was new.

Everyone in the region knew it. High-tier adventurers could complain about the inconvenience and move on. For lower paths, though, the problem was far worse. Without a healthy spread of weaker zones, the whole local ecosystem of exploration failed to build properly. There were too few beginners, too few suppliers, too few growing fighters, and too few people strong enough to support those above them. Like a pyramid with its base carved away, the whole structure remained stunted.

Anne saw that he was following the logic but not yet seeing where she wanted him to look. So she smiled faintly.

“However,” she said, “I do not agree with the usual conclusion.”

That caught him.

“Have you ever heard the tale of the three dwarven kingdoms?”

Rick blinked and shook his head.

Anne rested one hand lightly atop the books and began.

“A dwarf emperor, old enough to distrust easy answers, once decided to test his three children before choosing an heir. He took them to newly conquered lands where three mountains stood near each other and told each of them to found a kingdom. The one who built the strongest realm over time would earn the right to succeed him.”

Rick leaned in without noticing he was doing it.

“The eldest chose the mountain where ore was easiest to extract,” Anne said. “And for a while, he looked brilliant. His kingdom grew quickly, wealth came fast, miners flocked to him, and everyone praised his judgment.”

She lifted one finger.

“The second chose the mountain with the greatest volume of ore. It was harder to work, but the resource seemed endless. Instead of controlling it tightly, he sold access and extraction rights to others. His kingdom became rich through trade and agreements. For a while, he looked wiser still.”

A second finger.

“The youngest was left with what seemed the worst choice. Her mountain had the highest quality ore, but it was buried deep, locked behind hard stone, broken veins, poor access, and all manner of complications. In the early years, her kingdom was the poorest of the three. Every spare coin went into research, tools, better methods, and people who could solve problems no simple miner could solve.”

Anne’s voice remained calm, but there was a steady heat beneath it now.

“For years, everyone thought she had already lost. The eldest had speed. The second had prosperity. She had expenses and delays.” A small smile touched her lips. “Then a century passed.”

Rick had forgotten his tea.

“The easiest mountain had been stripped carelessly and exhausted. The richest mountain had made many merchants prosperous, but much of its true value had been sold away to others. The difficult mountain, however...” Anne tapped lightly on the cover of one of the books. “It had forced the youngest kingdom to become smart and resourceful. They invented better tools and methods. Kept better records. Taught engineers. And had long-term plans. By the end of that century, her kingdom was the strongest by far.”

Rick stared at her. He had not expected a story, and had certainly not expected to care so much.

Yet as she spoke, he had found himself siding with the youngest dwarf almost immediately. The frustration, poor starting point, and the sense of having been left with the hardest problem. Then the turn, slow and satisfying, until what had seemed like a disadvantage became the foundation for something greater.

He could almost feel the victory of it.

And suddenly, Mountainend did not seem so bad.
