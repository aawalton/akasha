CHAPTER 8

“The node must be tested by approach, not by instruction. If the candidate is told where to go, the journey teaches nothing. If the candidate finds the way, the journey teaches everything.” - Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

The pull led them west.

Arthur didn’t try to explain the sensation to the others. It wasn’t a sound or a direction in any normal sense. It was a pressure behind his eyes that eased slightly when he turned one way and tightened when he turned another, like following a gradient rather than a signal.

Donna walked beside him, knife in hand, checking doorways and the gaps between parked cars. She hadn’t mentioned the construct since it had vanished and Arthur suspected she was hoping it would stay gone.

Steve was three paces behind, muttering to himself. Arthur caught fragments: “…western alignment… Cannon Street runs roughly along the old Roman road… if you follow the Walbrook basin…” His brain had started to map the direction against the city’s buried history, trying to match it to something in the archive of Roman London he carried around in his head.

The street was quieter than it had been near the pub. There were fewer survivors visible. The ones Arthur could see had retreated into doorways or behind shop windows, watching the violet sky through glass as though distance from it might provide safety. A dog sat in the middle of the road beside a body, whining softly.

Arthur clenched his jaw and kept walking. The pull sharpened. And then he almost walked past it.

The building at 111 Cannon Street was a nondescript office block of glass and stone. It was the kind of structure that existed in every financial district in every city, designed to be merely functional. It could have been the poster child for the function-over-form epidemic that was ruining architecture everywhere. Mounted into the wall beside the entrance and protected by a small glass enclosure, sat a rough block of pale limestone.

It was about the size of a football. The surface of the rock was scarred, chipped and visibly ancient. And it was displayed on the street like a museum piece that the museum had forgotten to collect.

Arthur stopped. The pressure behind his eyes had become a single, focused point, aimed directly at the stone.

Steve stopped too and Arthur saw the recognition hit his face. His eyes widened and he drew a breath.

“I know what that is,” Steve said with a start.

Donna looked at the chipped rock in its glass box. “I do too. It’s a rock.”

“It’s the London Stone.” Steve said.

“So it’s a rock with a name.” Donna said and turned to check the street behind them.

Steve stepped closer, his eyes running across the surface of the limestone. “This thing has been documented since at least the twelfth century. Some records go back further. Nobody really knows what it is or why it’s here. I’ve read four competing theories and none of them were satisfying.”

“Excellent,” Donna said. “A mystery rock. That’s exactly what we need right now.”

Steve ignored her. “Some historians think it was the central milestone of Roman London, the Umbilicus, the navel point where all roads were measured from. Others think it predates the Romans entirely.

"There’s a medieval legend that whoever controls the London Stone controls the city. Jack Cade struck it with his sword during a rebellion in 1450 and declared himself Lord of London.”

“So medieval revolutionaries staged a coup against a rock,” Donna said flatly, shifting slightly to the right.

“Against what the rock represented. Symbols like that don’t appear out of nowhere. They’re collective memories, stories that survive long after people forget why they started telling them in the first place. Maybe the peasants remembered something about this stone that history lost.” Steve said, staring at the stone.

“There’s something else.” Steve crouched, bringing his eyes level with the base of the limestone. “What you’re looking at is only a stump. This is the top of what was the original stone. In 1598 a man called John Stow wrote a survey of London and described this stone, and what he described is nothing like what’s in this box. A great stone, he said. Fixed in the ground very deep and fastened with bars of iron.”

“John Snow? You taking the piss?” Donna said.

“Stow, with a 't', John Stow.”

“Christopher Wren dug around here after the Great Fire. He came away convinced the foundation was far too large for a marker of this size, and that the visible stone block belonged to something considerably bigger underneath.”

Arthur crouched beside Steve and looked at the seam where the limestone met the modern brick, and his brain, which had no opinions about legends, began to object.

You did not sink a foundation for a signpost. Foundations cost money and labour and time, and every one he had ever surveyed had been sized to a specific load. Iron bars meant somebody had expected that force would be applied to this stone and wanted the stone to hold.

“They weren’t marking a spot. They were anchoring something.” He said.

Steve looked at him. Whatever he had been about to say next, he did not say.

“Stow didn’t know why it was there either,” he continued. “That’s the bit nobody quotes. He went through the records, looked for a reason and wrote that there was no memory of the cause, or the time, or anything else at all. This was four hundred years ago, and it was already so old that the city had forgotten what it was for.”

“And yet the city kept it,” Donna said.

“Kept it. Moved it, walled it up, put a grille over it, hauled it out of the road when it blocked the carts and then put it back.” Steve straightened. “And every generation till now has decided not to throw it away even though they can’t explain it.”

“Then why is it in a wall?” Donna said, her eyes quickly scanning the street and then coming back to the stone.

“You’ve just told me it’s the oldest unexplained object in this city and nobody can date it or account for it. And it’s bolted to the front of an office block on a road I’ve walked down a hundred times without noticing it.” She gestured at the frontage with the kitchen knife. “Why isn’t it in the British Museum, behind glass, with a queue?”

“It is a Heritage listing. It is the building’s obligation to display it at street level, on the original site of the stone.” Steve said it in the voice of a man reading out an answer he had never quite believed. “At least that is the reason on the paperwork.”

Arthur looked at him. “And the reason that isn’t on the paperwork?”

“I don’t know exactly but I'll tell you this, every May, two livery companies send their Masters to this spot. The Worshipful Company of Masons and the Worshipful Company of Spectacle Makers.”

He took a pause and let them absorb the names. “Both were chartered by a king. The Masons were granted arms in 1472 and their charter came from Charles the Second. The Spectacle Makers were chartered by Charles the First in 1629. They have coats of arms, a Master, wardens and a court.”

“And they do what here, exactly?” Donna asked.

“They come to Cannon Street, they look at the Stone and then they write a letter to the Lord Mayor. The wording they use is that they are verifying its continued existence.” Steve said.

“It’s ceremonial. The City is full of this.” Donna said unimpressed. “There are men whose entire job is to go up the Thames every summer and count swans.”

Steve was still looking at the stone, lost in thought. “I’ve brought students here. It’s a rock in a wall with a plaque that admits it doesn’t know what it is. The annual inspection was a charming bit of pageantry I have never given a second of thought to.”

He turned around, and the excitement had gone out of him.

“But an hour ago a floating geometry told us there was an infrastructure element six hundred metres west and that Arthur was required to interact with it. And Arthur has had a pull behind his eyes since the restaurant that has taken us to this exact rock.”

He paused as if trying to make up his mind whether to continue or not. “Somebody wrote a rule that this object gets looked at once a year by two chartered bodies, and that its continued existence gets confirmed in writing to the head of the City. That is custody under the name of curation.”

Nobody answered him. Arthur looked at the rock in its glass box and found he had no idea what to make of any of it.

“Which raises the question,” Steve said and looked at the two of them, “of who was worried.”

Donna stared at the stone for a long moment, and then at the empty street, and Arthur watched her decide to file it rather than fight it.

“The spectacle makers, why them? Masons I follow. It’s a rock.” Arthur asked.

“Ah.” Steve said, rubbing his eyes. “Because in 1671 they confiscated a batch of substandard eyeglasses and smashed them on this stone with a hammer. Two hundred and sixty-four pairs, by judgement of the Mayor’s court. That is genuinely why they still come.”

“So the ancient guardians of the Stone of London are the opticians.” Donna said.

“One of the two ancient guardians of the Stone of London is the opticians. Yes.”

“Well. Thank God someone’s watching it.”

Arthur stared at the unremarkable lump of limestone. The pull in his skull was undeniable. Every nerve behind his eyes said this mattered while his rational brain said it was a rock in a wall.

Without really meaning to, he lifted his hand and pressed his palm against the glass. The glass cracked.

It wasn’t from the pressure of his hand, he had barely put it there. It had cracked from the inside. The crack branched outward in a radial pattern and thin splinters fell across the base of the display.

And then, as if the world had decided there was yet more room for weird bollocks, the stone began to glow.

The glow was subtle at first. A faint blue luminescence bleeding through the cracks and weathered seams of the limestone, as though something beneath the surface had woken up and was shining outward through the only channels available.

What Arthur had assumed were centuries of erosion, the scratches, the chips, the accumulated damage of a thousand years of London weather, were something else entirely. They were fine channels, carved so shallow they'd blended with the stone's natural texture and were now traced with light.

“Those were always there, we just couldn’t see them.” Steve gasped.

The light within the channels intensified, spreading from groove to groove as each line linked with its neighbour. Within moments, the entire surface of the stone had resolved into a single geometric figure.

It was a circle with a point at its centre. The oldest symbol in mathematics. The sign for the sun in every ancient astronomical tradition Steve had ever studied.

Then for one second, less than a second, Arthur saw something layered over the street. Lines. Hundreds of them. Thin blue vectors stretching outward from the stone in every direction, through buildings, beneath roads, down into the bedrock. A network of connections radiating from this single point like a nervous system branching from a spine.

The vision vanished abruptly and Arthur staggered. Steve caught his shoulder.

“Did you see that?”

“See what?” Steve said, looking around.

Arthur blinked and saw that the street was ordinary again. Wet pavement, dead cars, violet sky. But the afterimage of those lines was still burned in his mind.

“A network,” he said, straightening himself. “Lines running through the city. Through the ground. There were hundreds of them.”

The light from the stone had brought people out. A woman stood at the window of the sandwich shop with her hand pressed against the glass, looking intently at the stone. Two men had stopped at the corner and were staring but were not moving towards them. Further back, in the doorway of the office block, a security guard held a torch that no longer worked and watched a rock glow in a wall instead.

Arthur realised then that the stone was not like the construct. The geometry had hung in a restaurant full of people and not one of them had seen it. The stone was shining out into the street, and the street could see it.

The construct chose that moment to reassemble itself. Thin blue lines folding out of nothing beside them, the familiar geometry rotating into coherence.

“Candidate resonance detected. The trial has commenced.”

“What trial?” Arthur asked under his breath.

“The trial designed for the candidate,” the geometry answered.

“That tells me nothing about what this trial is.” Arthur said, a note of frustration climbing into his voice.

“Pre-disclosure of trial parameters is excluded under evaluation protocol. The candidate's response to insufficient information is, itself, being evaluated.”

As the construct finished, the air above the stone shimmered and lines of pale blue text assembled themselves with the same cold clarity as the calibration notification.

Where the Umbilicus marks the Roman road’s birth,

The Sun-Thief’s shadow sinks deep in the earth.

Find the star-slain Beast by the Walbrook’s flow,

To ground the First Signal in the chamber below.

Donna’s hand tightened on the kitchen knife. She didn’t look at the words for more than a second before her gaze snapped back to the dark intersections and the abandoned storefronts of the city. “Does everyone see the floating poetry, or is that just me?”

The man from the corner had come closer. He was in his fifties, in a suit jacket with no tie, and he had crossed the road to see what the light was. He got within three metres of the stone, close enough to read the floating text and Arthur watched his lips move, stop and start again at the top.

“Sod that for a game of soldiers,” he said, throwing his arms up, then turned and walked away at speed. By the time he reached the far pavement he was running.

Steve stared after him. “He could see it. He could read it.”

“Everybody still standing can read it, that’s the point, innit? The quarter that dropped were the ones who couldn’t.” Donna said.

“He didn’t even finish reading it properly,” Steve said, wounded. “You would think by now people would be used to floating text.”

Steve leaned in, his glasses nearly touching the hovering text. His lips moved as he read it twice, three times, his brain attacking each line like a locked door.

“Well the evaluation, whatever that is, seems to cover solving riddles,” Arthur said, shifting the bin lid on his arm. “And for what it’s worth, if I’d put this in front of you on a Tuesday, you’d both have complained. Ancient riddle, gated entrance, artifact. It’s the laziest dungeon design there is. I’d have been told it was derivative.”

“You were told it was derivative,” Donna said, not looking away from the intersection. “By me. More than once.”

“I remember.”

“And now the universe has done it to us, with considerably better production values.” Steve said.

“Umbilicus is easy enough. The Umbilicus Urbis Romae. The central milestone, and as I said historians have argued for decades, that this stone was Londinium’s. If the milestone theory is right, we’re standing near Londinium’s starting point.

“And does Sun-Thief mean someone who stole the Sun or someone named Sun-Thief?” Arthur asked. “Unless it means the moon during a solar eclipse. Moon covering the sun,” he added after a second.

Steve’s eyes were unfocused. “Not the moon, I don’t think. Especially with the shadow sinking deep in the earth,” Steve said, shaking his head slowly.

He chewed his lips for a second longer and then his face lit up.

“Mithras. The Roman god. He was associated with the sun, or more accurately, with surpassing it. Some scholars interpret him as the deity who stole the sun’s power. The Sun-Thief.” He said.

“And the star-slain beast?” Donna asked, glancing back.

“The Tauroctony. It is the central image of every Mithraic temple. It shows Mithras killing a bull, and every animal in the scene, the dog, the snake, the scorpion, corresponds to a constellation. It’s a star map carved in stone.” Steve’s voice had picked up speed, the words coming faster as the connections multiplied.

“The star-slain beast is the bull, killed on a relief that is a star map. Hence star-slain beast.”

“Walbrook is the easiest thing in this riddle. That’s one of London’s lost rivers. It flows underground now, right through this part of the City. The London Mithraeum was built on its banks.”

“So we’re being told to find an underground Roman temple.” Donna said.

“Not just any Roman temple. The Temple of Mithras. It was excavated after the war, moved, then eventually returned to its original location. Now it is in a chamber beneath the Bloomberg building, about two hundred metres that way.” Steve turned and pointed down Cannon Street towards the dark silhouette of a modern building complex.

The construct’s geometry rotated, its internal lines aligning precisely with the direction Steve was pointing.

“Interpretation accuracy: eighty-four per cent.”

Steve’s head whipped towards it. “Eighty-four? What’s the other sixteen?”

“Irrelevant for current transit phase.”

Steve’s eyes twitched at the remark and Donna almost smiled.

Arthur was about to move towards Bloomberg when Donna spoke.

“Hold on.” She was looking at the slowly fading riddle text. “The Walbrook. The Roman milestone. You said this temple was built by the Romans two thousand years ago.”

“Roughly, yes.”

“And this riddle just appeared out of a rock that nobody understood, pointing us to a Roman temple, using Roman references, in a language we can read. Seems suspicious to me.”

Steve nodded slowly, sensing where she was going but not yet seeing the destination.

“Who wrote it?” Donna said. “The Romans didn’t have floating blue text. Whoever created this riddle knew about the Mithraeum, knew about the Walbrook, knew about the Umbilicus and embedded it inside a stone that’s been around since before the medieval period. Someone planned this a long time ago. And they planned it for right now.”

“That— That is an extremely good question.”

The construct said nothing. But Arthur thought, and he might have been imagining it, that several of its internal lines tightened, just slightly, in what could have been the geometric equivalent of satisfaction.

Behind them, the riddle text faded and the stone’s glow dimmed to a faint pulse, the circle-and-dot symbol still visible.

Arthur adjusted the bin lid on his arm, checked the kitchen knife in his belt, and started walking.

“The thing you need to understand about Mithras,” Steve said, catching him up, “is that we have no idea what any of it meant.”

“You just told us what it meant.”

“I told you what the pictures show. That isn’t the same thing.” He shifted the pipe to his other hand as he walked. “There are hundreds of these temples. Syria, North Africa, the Rhine, Hadrian’s Wall. About a thousand inscriptions. They had seven grades of initiation, and we know the names of all seven. Raven, Bride, Soldier, Lion, Persian, Sun-Runner, Father.”

“That to me sounds like quite a lot of idea.”

“It’s a catalogue. We’ve got the structure and none of the doctrine.” Steve’s voice had gone flat. “There isn’t one line of scripture that we can point to. We have found no prayer, or text, nothing written by anybody who was inside it. Everything we think we know we took off the walls, or out of the mouths of Christians who hated them. Three hundred years, temples from the Wall to Syria, and they left us bugger all.”

Donna had been listening with her eyes on the road ahead.

“Three hundred years and nobody wrote anything down, that can’t be just carelessness or oversight.”

Steve turned to look at her with the pipe still resting on his shoulder, and for once he did not fill the gap.

“You’ve got an organisation operating across an entire empire, in every province, for centuries, and it produced no documents. People write things down, Steve.”

She stepped over a fallen bollard without breaking stride. “If there’s nothing written, it’s probably because somebody made a rule about not writing things down and the others kept it for three hundred years.”

Steve walked several paces before he answered. When he did, he sounded like a man reading back his own notes and disliking the theory behind them.

“I’ve read six books on the origins of that cult. They disagree about everything. Where it came from, what the bull means, whether it was ever Persian at all. Not one of them put it like that.” He said.

“You were all reading the walls, not the system.”

Donna walked beside Arthur in silence, scanning the dark street ahead.

“You know, for a rock, that was surprisingly intelligent,” she said.

Two hundred metres northwest, in an underground Roman temple, something was waiting in a chamber that had been sealed since before the English language existed.
