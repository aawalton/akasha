-5 years later-

Borlor the Dixt looked out over the terraced balcony of Arcona’s Maran Monolith.

Beneath the great tower, the waves of the Northern seas lapped at the land once more, restored to a natural rhythm after five long, grueling years of restoration efforts. It had taken all the Hydromancers of the land to refill the sea-bed, and re-structure the decimated coral reefs and homes of sea-creatures that were vital to the fishing villages along Sentinel and the outer hamlets belonging to their Eastmarch cousins. It would still take time to restore their habitats to their natural states – a fact that Mallak and the Druids of the Aquatic Grove assured him would only occur after a few more decades and continuous monitoring.

But finally, one could look out from the rebuilt Ports of Argwyll and see the glory of its sea once more spread out from Lucent to the sun-drenched Gobrins, and to the gothic cathedrals of Camorran in the East.

Which reminded him – Cardinal Langley would be visiting for their 5th Annual Conclave on this day, along with his fellow human and hybrid emissaries from the East.

He hoped that, at the very least, they wouldn’t bring that giant – the one representing the Gobrin titans – with him today. The amount of time that had been irrevocably lost at the last Conclave trying to make space for the big guy didn’t bare thinking about…

He leaned across the balcony banister and smiled, thinking of what matters concerned him these days. He had no idea that he would become a Dixit concerned with matters of time, resource allocation, and management as he was right now. Indeed, before the Archon’s coming, he thought he’d simply meander away at his forge like his father and his father before him deep within the bowels of the earth.

“But that was then,” he said to himself.

He raised his hand and [Appraised] it.

Borlor the Dixit: Level (UNLEVELED)

Class: Statesman

Core Skills: Silvertongue, Local Lore, Consensus Appraisal, Strategic Omission, Uncanny Punctuality…

He stopped before looking at the rest of the list – where the new skills gave way to his old ones.

“…and this is now,” he said.

It had taken some getting used to. With the Law gone, all classes were suddenly flexible. Suddenly, all of Argwylian life was allowed to change – to make a choice. It happened sometimes when you least expected it – during moments of quiet contemplation, introspection, or even during heated arguments among friends. The notion that ‘I can be something else’ was suddenly no longer just a tangible dream…

Of course, that had led to its own problems. Law and order, for one thing, had to be reestablished and altered in accordance with Ethan’s alteration to the world. But he’d given them at least a decent head-start through the destruction of Sys. A destruction that, it seemed, had achieved a strange consequence…the emergence of the (Unleveled) status.

Virtually everyone had it now. Virtually everyone had been placed on an equal playing field in terms of their abilities, and in terms of what they viewed as ‘progress’. Before, such progress had meant little more than the attainment of martial prowess – often simply for the sake of achieving such prowess and little more. Now, with the myth of the ‘Level-Up’ out of the way, a real kind of reciprocity and mutuality and become real for the people of Argwyll. No more were they cowed by higher levels. No more could they be intimidated by stick alone, or by the indoctrination perpetuated by military organizations such as the Greycloaks, who often held the stick in their bloody hands.

Maintaining order now was a trickier task. But, luckily for men like Borlor, there were now plenty of great minds to work with. Plenty of minds that were focused on actually making the world a better place rather than carving out what they thought belonged to them. Plenty of people who weren’t afraid to try new ideas, to see how they could preserve a lasting peace, and to see that peace carried through.

Perhaps what the old prophetess Jun’Ei used to say was right after all: only after the greatest fires is the world free to breathe once more…Ethan’s fire had been great, indeed. And he’d put it out, himself, and given them the tools to build on top of what was left. Now, it was up to them.

Their road to this point hadn’t been easy. Indeed, in the aftermath of Sys’s destruction, anarchy had befallen the land for more than a few long months…but as he looked now over the newly restored seas, and smelled the fresh breeze of morning dew that drew across the land, Borlor allowed himself a few moments of peace in what was otherwise a much more busy life than what he was used to.

His eyes were then drawn to the city below, and the people – Hybrids and humans both – who walked together in the streets, strolling across the very same cobbled streets where the ashes of burned hybrids once lay.

He barely even heard Malak appear behind him. The old druid moved like roots through the land.

“The Conclave is ready,” he said. “Cardinal Langley has arrived and awaits you in the reception hall. The rest of the Council is currently…settling in.”

Borlor knew what that meant. ‘You should probably hurry along so that Cormyr and Fraxx don’t start lashing at each others’ throats again.’ Honestly, perhaps the hatred between cats and mice really was a genetic component to their beings as some Hybrid scholars suggested.

But the Dixit did not rush. Instead, he turned and eyed Malak as the latter came to stand beside him at the balcony edge.

“What do you see?” Borlor asked him. “Is this the world your druids always wished for?”

Malak followed his eyes down to the people, their streets, and the shining sapphire light of the sea.

“The cities of men have never been mine,” Malak said. “Even after I have been given the choice to accept them, I long for the forests and the seclusion of my Grove. One day, after true stability returns to this land, I shall return to the Albion and give myself back to the earth.”

“True stability will take a while,” Borlor commented. “It might take the rest of our lives, if we’re lucky.”

Malak bowed. “If that is what is necessary. I gave my life to the Archon’s cause long ago. That was by choice, and that choice has still remained unchanged.”

Both of them looked out across the city, past the market square with its brimming stalls and vendors playing wares, to the central plaza where Hybrids were once burned at the stake. Erected on that very spot was a statue dedicated to the Last Archon, Ethan Hawke, his single eye staring up at them just like he was still there with them. It was often said by the people of Arcona that if you stood beneath the statues gaze, you could feel the warmth and jovial laughter that the Archon used to bring to the world of the surface and what lay below.

“He never made it easy for us, did he?” Borlor asked with a little shrug.

Malak copied his gesture. “He paved the way. But it is we who must walk our own path.”

“You think we can make it?”

Malak seemed surprised that the normally reserved and confident Borlor was asking him if he had doubts of his own. Before answering, however, he and the Dixit were struck by the sudden sound of signing coming from the market below. The sound was accompanied by the pitter-patter of children’s footsteps – human and hybrid both – who ran to hear the afternoon songs of Praise-Singer Agna. Their parents came too, though with a little less enthusiasm. Some grumbled that they’d heard this one before.

But the children – they always sat rapt before her on the small steps leading up to what had once been the Palace of Lucent, a place once walled off to anyone who was not of the [Noble] Class. They sat or stood, shoulder to shoulder, fur and hair bristling against smooth skin, as her balads or elegies washed over them.

And unconsciously, Malak felt himself smile.

“Yes,” he whispered. “We can make it.”

Borlor shared the quiet, reserved joy at the sight below. He himself enjoyed watching the awe on the children’s faces. Sometimes, they joined in on the old woman’s singing without even knowing the words to her songs. She never looked happier.

“You were right back in Sanctum,” he said quietly. “When you said that, in the end, everything we do might not matter much for us. It’s for them, really. For those who will inherit this world after us, and walk the paths we lay for them, we have a duty – no, an obligation – to keep going. To finish what he started.”

He gingerly patted Malak on his frail shoulders, startling the man. The Druid was not exactly used to mortal contact.

“You always wished for forgiveness for the crimes you committed,” Borlor said. “And you wanted to get it from the Archon’s mouth, didn’t you? But in the end, the fact that you’re here with us – have remained here, with us – that’s enough. Even though he didn’t tell you so himself, I think he’d be proud of the man you became, Malak.”

Borlor couldn’t be sure, but he could detect a loosening of the old druid’s shoulders. Maybe it was the new powers of perception the [Statesman] Class conferred on him, but he felt that the man had wished to hear those words for a long, long time.

Borlor then motioned to the door at their backs.

“Shall we?” he asked. “Our cousins from the East are waiting.”

***

The Maran Monolith was generally filled with activity – and on the sacred days when the annual Conclave was being called, the palace was practically swamped.

Masons and Geomancers lined many of the halls, hanging from the bannisters or ceiling beams high above the decorated walkways and walls, each one ensuring the structural integrity of the great castle was sound – just in case of attack. Borlor had often spoke out against such precautions, but Cormyr and Fraxx were always there to shoot down his oppositions to these rigid safety measures. It was about the only thing the two of them could agree on.

That, and more wards against Petrification spells.

At the bottom of the grand staircase that led from the throne room to the Great hall where their meeting was to take place, the palace guards kept their vigil, stalwart and stoic against the throngs of people who had come from all over Argwyll to just catch a glimpse of their Councilors.

Borlor couldn’t resist giving them a wave as he and Malak made their way down the pearl-white steps.

“They flock to us, even though they know that they should love, and serve, no masters anymore,” Malak said. “Does this not cause you disquiet, Borlor?”

The Dixit blinked, lowering his hand at the thought. The Druid was right, of course – with hero worship often came Ego, and that was exactly what Ethan had warned them against cultivating.

Yet, there was someone in the crowd whom Borlor’s eyes latched on to before he gave his friend a reply – a small, blonde haired man, with two wide eyes and a smile that was more genuine than any the old Dixit had ever seen. This young man was shaking the hands of every person in the crowd, patting their backs and listening – just listening – to their words. No matter what they might have been.

“It seems we are not the only ones late to the proceedings,” Malak quietly observed.

Borlor smiled, waving to Cardinal Langley as the young man finally turned and allowed his rather beleaguered-looking delegation of guards to lead him through the palace door.

“Somehow, as long as we’ve got guys like him around, I think we’ll be ok,” Borlor whispered to to Malak.

The Druid merely shrugged, and ambled away after nodding to the approaching young man. “I shall see you inside.”

Borlor let him go, instead meeting Langley and accepting the handshake the young man offered.

“It is a pleasure to see you again, Lord Borlor,” the Cardinal beamed. “I have told my comrades to bring the pauldrons you were so taken with during our last meeting.”

“And I’ve told you that its just ‘Borlor’, son!” he chuckled back – though the armor pieces intrigued him. Even if he wasn’t just a [Blacksmith] anymore, some habits died hard…

“How was your journey?” he asked him, guiding him towards the Conclave chamber.

“Thankfully without incident. The roads out of Camoran are calmer than ever, thanks to our patrols, and the bandits that once plagued the Gobrins seem almost like something of the distant past. I dare say we can one day soon welcome the giants into the fold properly – as Lord Ethan no doubt would have wished.”

Borlor shivered a little at the mention of the giants.

One thing at a time, kid…

“And yet…one does wonder about what dangers may still lurk out there.”

Langley’s voice grew uncharacteristically grave – only for a few seconds – as he looked through the oval, stained-glass window that showed clear skies over Westerweald and beyond.

He was looking to the South. And Borlor knew what was occupying his mind.

“Has there been any word?” he asked the young Cardinal.

Langley’s smile wavered slightly.

“…nothing,” he said. “I fear that, even with our scouts combined efforts, we may never find him. Even Lord Ethan could not force an audience with him. All we have – after five years - are rumors. Just whispers…”

Borlor nodded. It seemed impossible that something as obviously imposing as a dragon could evade all the greatest military minds of Argwyll combined.

But after all this time, their search for the Architect had turned up nothing but dust.

“It may be safe to assume that he poses no threat to our world,” Langley said. “But when it comes to creatures like that…”

He turned to Borlor suddenly, cursing himself for his own poor choice of words.

“I am sorry,” he said with a slight bow. “That kind of attitude was the cause of so much animosity between our kind since the beginning of time. I think that sometimes my mind still strays to the old ways, old modes of thinking. I am trying to –“

Langley caught the apologetic smile Borlor was giving him. It was a smile that said ‘relax’ more than words ever could.

“I am no extremist,” he said. “I know the difference between a bigot and a man who has never once sought to harm my kind. And besides,” he added with a satisfied twitch of his whiskers. “We have a team on the job with an ace up their sleeve. Not even this Architect and whatever purpose he has shall be able to elude them.”

Langley’s face brightened at the insinuation.

“You mean that – the Guardians, and – and the Lady herself –“

Borlor laughed at the lad’s excitement.

If Tara was here, I’m sure she’d love to entertain a human fan…

“All in good time,” he told the Cardinal. “In fact, I will be discussing their last report in our meeting. Which, by the by, we are both quite late for…”

Langley nodded in agreement and turned back towards the doorway leading to the Grand Council chamber.

And yet, Borlor still lingered behind him in the hall.

Something – an errant thought – had grabbed him while he’d been speaking to the human priest. Something in the youth’s manner had always perturbed him, he’d never quite been able to pinpoint what it was, exactly. But after seeing the shame etched on his face at the banal comment he’d made, coupled with his proclivity for apologizing, Borlor decided that there was something he had to do. Something that was probably long overdue.

“Langley,” he said.

The boy turned, and was surprised to see Borlor’s huge hand extended towards him, palm open and inviting.

“I know the looks some still give you,” he said. “I know that there’s still some among us who can’t accept what Ethan started, and what we’re still trying to build. There’s some Hybrids out there who see you as an enemy even now, and there’s some humans who see you as a traitor to your kind.”

Langley stiffened, and his lip quivered slightly. But he said nothing. Nor did he extend his hand, yet.

“The truth is that there’s too many of us who still remember,” Borlor continued. “We remember – I remember – how this war started. What your kind did to ours, and all our retaliation efforts right up to the point when we were driven into the darkness of our Sanctum. Even though we’re trying - and we’re trying real, damn, hard – we can’t drag everyone into the light. Even I can’t forgive everything that happened. Not yet.”

He stepped forward as the sun dipped below the hills outside, and the shadow of both Hybrid and man were thrown across the palace floor.

“But what I can forgive, I do,” Borlor finished. “And at the very least, I can see past the sins of your forefathers to the man that’s standing before me, right now – just like Ethan did. I think I’ve been afraid to say this for five damn years, Langley, but – thank you. For guiding the East towards the new dawn.”

Though he felt his phrasing rather clunky (he’d never been one for flowery metaphors) Langley’s full-lipped smile reassured him like it always did.

The human stepped forward, took his bulky hand in his own, and shook.

“Come, brother,” he said. “The new world waits.”
