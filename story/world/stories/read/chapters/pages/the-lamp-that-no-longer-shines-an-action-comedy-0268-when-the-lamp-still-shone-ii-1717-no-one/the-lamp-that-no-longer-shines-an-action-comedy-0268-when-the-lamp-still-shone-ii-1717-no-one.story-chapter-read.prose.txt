Irene was an exceedingly common name. The Inner Sea was thick with shopgirls and baronesses and griffin drivers who carried it, and she had built the filter long before the seat: a flawless dam that took the static of a continent and washed it out before it reached her. It had never once been asked to hold against a targeted strike.

It had started years ago, on random afternoons, at quiet midnights, in the middle of duels. Just the name itself, turned over and over in a dozen lilting inflections by somebody amusing herself.

Irene had endured three weeks of it before she traced the resonance to a stretch of water off Ailuronia that only the most stubborn class of angler would ever have found. A hexagonal fishing pavilion wallowed over the inlet, towed at no particular speed by a pair of heavy, dozing fur seals. Under the canvas awning a charcoal brazier burned sweet and steady beside an iron grill, and the Aspect was kicked back in a low wicker chair with her boots up on the railing and a rod resting loose between two fingers, a Wondrous Item, as all of hers were.

"Have you no other occupation," Irene said, stepping onto the wet boards without a sound, "that you must incessantly say my name?"

Avenka rolled her head back over the arm of the chair and looked at her upside down. "Because cat thinks it is a very pretty sound!" She reached over with an empty hand, took a skewer off the coals, and blew on it twice. "Cat is not calling you! Cat is fishing! If you do not wish to hear it, put up a ward and block it out."

Irene looked at her for a moment, turned on her heel, and left. She never put up the ward.

The tournament had closed at two, and the last match on the floor had been hers. Golden Iris had come in led by two Grand Witches and gone out to a black horse in its first season. Irene had taken three of them onto herself and held, and every account of the match agreed on that, and every account of the match then arrived at the Tenth Seat.

The White City press had come to build her a reason. They laid the questions down gently, the way one lays planks over a hole: the interference in the arena, the scheduling, the long season. Heidi Lucent said that she had been outplayed, and said it again when they asked differently, and not one of them knew that she had taken the karmic load of a hundred worlds onto herself and paid for it with the win.

The foreign desks were not building anything. Irene took those, smiling, in four languages, and gave the win to the Greed Umbrella without a single qualifier attached to it, and said that the future belonged to them, and the sentence went out live across the Inner Sea.

At half past two the channel opened. Behind the voice there was wind, and the acoustics of a high civic terrace.

"Irene. Irene, will you come and find me?" A pause, and the small sound of a hand moving down a wing. "Come and see me."

Irene answered a question about whether the result constituted a generational turn.

"No," Avenka said. The lilt had gone out of it. "They are nowhere near. You know that."

One thread carried the tactical breakdown, another the languages, and a third held the smile at the exact aperture that would photograph as gracious rather than tolerant.

"They will ask if you are coming back next season," Avenka said. "Say yes. Then come and see me."

"We will be back next season," Irene said.

"See!"

A woman from the Marenia service asked whether the Fifth Seat wished to comment on the Tenth Seat's condition entering the final.

"Your hair has come loose on that side," Avenka said. "The little one on the left. It has been like that since you came out. Cat likes it."

Irene's mouth had opened. The thread on her mouth caught it at the top of the breath and put a full sentence into it about the standard of officiating, which was blameless, and which she praised. The scrum ran until twenty past three.

On the way back Heidi sat with her hands flat on her knees. "She spent a Wondrous Item," she said. "On a greeting." Outside, the sky-islands went by gold.

"She pays when she means to be there to watch it happen."

"Irene," said Avenka. The terrace wind had gone, and there was a street behind her now, and somebody being told to carry things. "Where are you? Cat has bought a scent." A rustle of paper. "Cat put it on my wrist and it does not smell nice at all." Quite so, Irene thought.

"You caught Cecilia at Babylon," Irene said. "That was yours, and you did it. What is between her and Evangeline has never had room in it for a third person. Standing near it does not make it yours."

Heidi looked out of the window and did not agree to any of it.

Allison was in the lobby. She was sitting in one of the low chairs by the water column with a magazine open on her knee, one ankle crossed over the other, entirely at leisure, and she had been there long enough that the staff had stopped noticing her. She closed the magazine and stood.

Irene turned her head a fraction toward the rest of the roster. They went on to the lifts without breaking stride, and the lobby was three people wide.

"I watched your match," Allison said warmly, to both of them. "You played beautifully."

Heidi inclined her head. "You are generous. May I ask what brings you here?"

Allison's face went quiet. "The Wellington sisters," she said. "I would ask that you do not involve yourself in what follows."

Heidi's brows came down. Everything easy left her posture at once. "Without a reasonable explanation," she said, "I am afraid I cannot comply."

"Irene," Avenka said. "Cat has broken two half-planes. An orchard, and a coastline with a very good sea in it. They have asked cat to stop touching things. Cat paid for both out of my own money, but cat has stopped anyway. Cat is being very good." A pause. "Why will you not come?"

A witch walking into one of the great furnishing houses for the first time tends to mistake it for a chapel: the hush, the height, and seven hundred metre-high windows drifting past in the air, each opening onto a half-plane with a finished room inside it and a few beautiful servitors standing about in the furniture. Avenka had gone along the aisle prodding the windows with the tip of her tail, two of the little estates had gone like spun glass, and Irene had bought the house on the spot, deed and land and every plane hanging in it, so the cat could take the lot apart at her leisure with nobody alive permitted to say no to her. Only once the title had cleared did Avenka huff, lose interest, and leave the displays alone.

She lets them bargain with her, and then she comes to me to report how obedient she was. As though she expects to be praised for it.

Allison sighed. "In the name of the Witch of Eternal Slumber," she said, "I give you my word that this matter will be resolved, and that it will be resolved well."

The lobby held very still, and Heidi Lucent stood with it for a long moment. Then she bowed, shallowly, and stepped back.

Now the only chair left at the table is mine.

"Thank you," Allison said, and the warmth came back into her face as though it had never gone anywhere. She said goodnight to both of them, then went out through the doors into the evening, and the attendant held one for her, and she thanked her too.

"Irene." Not sweetly. "Cat has been asking all afternoon."

Irene crossed the lobby and rode up alone and let herself into the suite, and the door sealed behind her with a sound like a page turning.

She poured a glass of dark wine and sat down in the high-backed leather chair by the window and held it perfectly still. Of course she was going to go.

A Wellington in extremity would sign anything, and there were two of them on the table tonight. Hold Cecilia and Evangeline and the house came along behind them, and Cecilia was the keystone of the Greed Umbrella, so taking the keystone took the rest with it.

All five of them already at Arch-Witch strength, Wei Changqing and Karula carrying the weight of future Grand Witches. And each of them out of a different district: Fusang, Holheim, Casendiara, Milan'thir, Marigold Bay. Five votes across five territories, moving as one body.

Heidi had gathered three and a fourth who kept her own counsel. Paddy had Adeline, and Adeline's disciple if one felt generous.

She had no use whatsoever for another trophy. Champions in their first season were another matter: the title would be priced into every one of those five names by morning and would take a decade off the climb, and she had given them the afternoon for exactly that reason.

A few minutes late would change nothing about the rescue and everything about the settlement, and nobody alive had the standing to audit her pace. Evangeline was too durable to end cleanly: she would go down to the underworld and spend decades putting herself back together, and Irene held a hundred administrative levers to keep her there. Cecilia would carry a felony, interrupted short of the unforgivable and permanent on the record, and the other four would stand in the file beside her as accomplices.

Cecilia would also be past mending, which made the remainder simple. A woman past mending signs what is put in front of her and can be instructed to go on living. The Umbrella would thank her on their knees for it. And afterwards there would be an instrument in a vault that could be signed over to any one of them on a morning of Irene's choosing, and five of the finest witches alive would spend the rest of their lives earning it: a bespoke pair of black gloves, fitted and delivered.

"Are you listening?" The edge had come off it again. "Come now and cat will not be cross. If you do not come soon it will be too late."

So you have put a clock on it. The words went through the ribs sideways anyway. You want me to keep them. Whole, and grateful, and out of my reach.

She crossed her legs, set the glass down on the side table, and turned her face to the window. Then show me what they are worth to you.

The phantom came and settled on the arm of the chair, tail moving. "Cat's calling you."

"For whom?" Irene said.

The phantom's tail went still. Her ears came down and her chin came up, and she took herself off the arm of the chair with tremendous dignity and was not there any more.

Irene did not turn her head, and the name did not come again. Five. Six. The sun went down behind the sky-islands. The suite was very large, and Irene sat in it with her face to the window and the wine going warm on the table beside her.

At four minutes past seven the tether opened. Avenka sent Mihaye Grün off on an errand, and set something heavy down, and then there was nothing behind the voice at all.

"Please, Irene."

Irene was already out of the chair. The crystal on the side table opened down the stem and let the wine out across the polished wood.

Acceleration and deceleration in one field, held in exact opposition, the contradiction pinned flat under a weight of mana the universe declined to argue with. Time ran in every direction at once. Past and present and the whole branching floor of what had not happened yet came up as translucent overlapping panes, and for something under an instant she was very nearly omniscient. She looked a little way into the future.

Through one pane, a street below the clock tower, Mihaye Grün standing in it with her hands full, and beside her a very tall woman who had been asking since half past two.

Through another, a grey void with no coordinates in the physical universe, five world champions bringing their matrices to peak, and a second-year with her fist closed around an enchanted coin and a distress call still milliseconds from sending.

In the first pane the very tall woman turned her head. The look crossed the distance and crossed the time and arrived on Irene, and she smiled, bright and entirely undefended.

Irene came off it the way a hand comes off a stove and went through the second pane. She came out of the wound with liquid gold bolted into its edges and caught the synchronized opening fire of the Greed Umbrella.

Nowhere near.

Then she turned her head and looked at the Celestial Witch. You. You are the reason she lowered her head to me.

The Ludwig girl's beacon reached her some while after she had come through. She turned and told the child that she trusted she had been well, and nobody in that room ever learned what had opened the sky over the White City.

Cecilia asked her for something that would keep her sister from ever leaving. Irene set the scroll on the coffee table, and Evangeline Wellington signed the servant's line, and the parchment took and sealed and held. Cecilia set the quill down and bowed to her, formally and very low, and offered the favor back at whatever price she cared to name, whenever she cared to name it.

Irene looked at the bent head of the second Wellington daughter. I have given up tonight. Someday I am going to call this in, and put the knife into this grateful child's hand, and stand her behind you, and every last thing you begged me to spare will be the instrument of it. Will you regret begging me, then?

Avenka came in eleven minutes later with Mihaye Grün in tow, and bent, and kissed the back of Cecilia Wellington's hand.

Cecilia's free hand went up to her own left cheek without being told to. The mark under it had lapsed years ago. The shape was intact and the warmth was still in it, down by a fraction every year, and nobody was putting any more in. Twenty-seven years old, and no use for it, and she had kept it.

I want that piece of skin off her face. And what is under it.

Then Avenka turned and asked the thing by the window how it felt to be caught, little bird. Little bird. Irene had taken the measure of that one on the day she met it and had never revised a word. The eyesight in this house had not improved since.

Then Avenka had Irene by the wrist, and cat wished to buy books, and Irene was coming. "Cat doesn't want to hear it."

Irene let herself be towed out through the door and across the city into a shop that smelled of dust and old glue, and stood in the aisle for an hour and a half holding a stack that kept getting heavier, while a very tall cat read titles out at speed and knocked three volumes off a high shelf and caught two of them. In the warm dark of the shop, with her wrist still holding the shape of the grip on it, she kept one thing from the evening:

She begged me. For them.

The blood in the courtroom came up under her jaw. She lifted her chin, the way one does, and the ring of dry marble held.

Emilia Lucas came, and set the gift down, and stood back from it. There was a procedure for this. Accept, praise, log, and never speak of it again. Irene had run it more than fourteen thousand times without a single deviation.

She opened the lid. It came up on its hinge at an angle somebody had worked out beforehand, and the gold caught the light before anything else in the room did. The chassis under it was black all the way through. The eyes were half shut. It looked at her with the flat boredom of something that had been lying there a while and would decide for itself how much longer it stayed, and the tail moved once.

Irene reached out, and the chassis phased sideways out of the world, and her fingers closed on cool air. One second later, from somewhere behind her left shoulder, came the sensation of being looked at.

Irene's hand stayed open over the empty velvet for a moment, then she closed it and put it back on her knee.

"Hathaway von Ludwig submitted this design," Irene said.

Emilia's breath caught, and her composure came apart all at once, the way glass does. "So it's true," she said. "You love Ovelia."

Irene did not answer. Her face was a still surface with nothing moving on it.

"Do you know," Emilia said, "how badly I wanted you to be refusing me because I wasn't good enough?"

Emilia looked at her. The tears came and she let them. "You are so good. You deserve to be loved by everyone in this world.

"And you knew. You knew how it goes, we have all of us watched how it goes. Ash. Famia. Akkukataya." Her hands had come up and she did not appear to know it. "Not one of them was ever answered. Not one."

Her voice broke. "Why did it have to be her?"

Irene's voice, when it came, was very quiet. "Thank you, Emilia. You are a friend better than I have any right to."

She looked at the greatest artificer of the age, standing in front of her and weeping. "I wish you happiness."

Emilia shook her head, and did not stop shaking it, and went.

Irene stood in the drowned room. She had not once looked away from Emilia's face. She never did, and it was the thing people remembered afterward about having been looked at by Irene Habsburg.

The phantom had been standing in that same place the entire time, her face laid over Emilia's, watching Irene through the tears of the woman who had just walked out.

The sorrow in the two faces was the same sorrow.
