I turned back toward Hyrdford. With the Hangman gone, the siege was as good as won, but that didn’t mean we could just rest. The longer we stood around doing nothing, the more of our own soldiers died.

Coughing and rubbing my neck, I stumbled back toward the battle. I couldn’t use any more Arts, but with two points of Presence left, I could still do some damage. I set off toward the orcs.

“Lord Levi,” Lord Ceonwin said, marching up behind me. “Wait.”

“If I can kill one orc, then that’s one less soldier who’s threatened.”

“I am aware. However, you have no Presence left—”

“I have two.”

“—and you will not survive walking into the back of the orc ranks forever. They will eventually kill even a Gold.”

I scowled. “So we do nothing?”

“No. Saeglaf?”

The Greencloak produced a horn from somewhere on his body, though I didn’t see exactly where it came from. It just…manifested. He lifted it to his lips and blew a loud blast.

From where I stood, up on the slope, I could see exactly what was happening within the wall. I could thank my enhanced eyesight for that. The hole that the Hangman had torn with his fungal attack still yawned wide open, but our soldiers held it, forming a line and beating back the attackers from atop a pile of rubble and bodies. Ladders lined the wall, and orcs climbed up, constantly assailing the defenders.

Still, we had the numbers to hold the line. If anyone fell, our soldiers rushed up to plug the gap. They dragged away the wounded and let the dead lie beneath their feet. The biggest weakness, now, was the wall itself and the damage the orcs had done to it. Though they had run out of rubble to fling with their catapults, they’d smashed away most of the wall’s merlons and battlements, making it harder for our soldiers to hide from ranged attacks.

And then finally, there was the gate. The orc battering ram stood in front of it, as if it were some victorious statue. It had bashed open the doors, and orcs were flooding in, only to meet the defenders in a pitched battle within the chokepoint.

At the sound of Saeglaf’s horn, however, our soldiers parted. The orcs flooded into the gap our soldiers purposely left, but a moment later, a stream of riders charged out, splitting the orc ranks. Our army pushed, giving themselves distance, then parted to let non-exhausted fighters charge through the gaps in their ranks, following the horses on foot. The riders plowed through the unexpecting orc ranks, aiming right toward us.

“They will bring our horses,” Lord Ceonwin told me. “You can join the fight from horseback.”

The riders charged through the orc lines, then approached us. Most of them turned, but their leader, one of the lieutenants I’d rode alongside before, said, “The Hangman is dead?” He rode alongside four other riderless horses, including Burr. They followed behind obediently.

“He is no more,” Lord Ceonwin replied. “How are our numbers?”

“We kept a force in reserve in case he smashed another hole in the wall, and we needed to repel them. However, that seems unnecessary, now.”

“Have them join the charge. We will route the orcs east,” Lord Ceonwin said.

“Understood, sir. I will tell them to move east at once.”

As they spoke, I climbed up onto the back of Burr. I’d gotten used to fighting while exhausted, and hey, two Presence was better than none. Most people didn’t even have a single point of it.

It was just comparatively low.

When I put my foot in Burr’s stirrups, Lord Ceonwin rode his horse up beside me and said, “Remember, Levi. You are more than just an infantryman now. Your life is worth infinitely more than theirs.”

I narrowed my eyes.

“I know you don’t like how it sounds. But we would not be here without you. They are following you, and if not you, then trying to follow through on the will of Lord Levi. You are more important than them. You cannot die yet.”

I shook my head. “I can’t let myself die, sure. But I’m not going to let any of them die alone while I cower in fear.”

I wasn’t going to pat myself on the back and ask why people followed me specifically. But I knew people would fight harder for me if I did everything in my power to keep them alive. I wouldn’t let it be a numbers game alone.

Before he could resist or say anything else, I spurred Burr. She reared up, then galloped to keep up with the rest of the charging riders. We collided with the rest of the orc army again, plunging back into them like a sewing needle and aiming for the hole in the wall. We could take some pressure off the defenders and push the orcs away.

As we galloped over the orcs from behind, I pushed Burr to the front of the group. An ogre turned, trying to turn to meet us, but I pulled my crossbow off my shoulder and loosed a resonance-Skill-enhanced arrow into its forehead.

It smashed through the beast’s skull, sending it staggering back. Another rider struck it with an axe in the knee, and more arrows peppered its flesh, until finally, it fell backward, crushing a swath of orcs beneath it.

When we reached the wall, the defenders made a channel for us to pass through. We turned around in the open space behind, circling around a house and preparing for another charge, but there was no need. The orcs were turning and fleeing.

Someone shouted, “The Black Captain is dead!”

“Yesman’s sappers killed him!”

“Make sure they flee east!” I yelled. “Don’t let the orcs run deeper into our lands!”

Without waiting for the rest of the riders, I turned away from the wall and circled around, riding west. If the orcs got past us, they would be in undefended countryside, free to wreak havoc on civilians and farms.

A few riders trailed behind me, moving to cut off the few orcs who had tried to flee west. I registered the tingle of the Black Captain’s controlling will fading. The orcs weren’t moving with any pattern anymore.

Once we were past the majority of the orcs who were fleeing west, I turned Burr around. Most of the orcs registered that we’d gotten in front of them and turned back, sprinting east. I picked off any who didn’t with my crossbow, then galloped back in the other direction, lowering my spear and aiming at the bulk of the fleeing orcs.

With my last Presence, I activated my spear’s resonance Skill and skewered through as many as I could, until finally, we were past the wall.

I slowed Burr down to a trot as the sky began turning orange. Clouds caught the sunrise, turning bright pink overhead, and a faint warmth washed over me.

I didn’t want to say Lord Ceonwin was right, but I couldn’t afford to keep pursuing the orcs.

Besides, there was too much cleanup that we had to do here. If nothing else, we’d have a lot of Presences to gather.

“I’m sorry,” I whispered to Yesman. I knelt beside him as I used my siphon to pull the Presence out of an orc. “If I could’ve done anything—”

“Levi, it’s not your fault that Liffen died. He was a good squadmate, and we’ll be down without him.”

I sighed. “I know.”

“We will be fine.”

“Yeah. But—”

“Levi. You are fighting a war. Do you expect no one to die?”

“He was your squadmate, and you—”

“This is how I cope. We can cry when we win.” Yesman glared at me. “Do I look happy about it?”

“I’m sorry,” I said again.

“We’re not going to betray you like Habit, if that’s what you were worried about. Something like Squirrel won’t happen again.”

I blew a long exhale. I wasn’t just trying to cover my own ass, was I?

“The sappers before us enjoyed relative peace,” Yesman continued. “Most of them never had to lose anyone from their squad. Few of them understood sacrifice. They were relatively happy and contented with their situation.”

“We won’t get that luxury, I know.”

“Those who make history don’t tend to be happy, Levi.”

“I know,” I stressed. He was starting to sound like Anchor.

But I’d promised I wouldn’t lose hope. Not now, either, especially after we’d routed the orcs.

Maybe it was selfish, but I was glad my squad wasn’t here. I hadn’t put them in any danger.

Turning away from Yesman, I kept working through the carnage, harvesting Presences and searching for wounded men.

~ ~ ~

At noon, I found Lord Ceonwin near the farthest east reaches of the battlefield. He stood over the corpse of a Black Captain.

It looked like a relatively unassuming orc. Judging by the body of a fell-fox beside him, he probably would’ve been mounted. Otherwise, he wore a tattered black cloak and a helmet made from a different fell-fox’s skull.

Lord Ceonwin kicked the orc over onto his back, then said, “Suppression collar.”

I grimaced, looking through the tatters in the orc’s cloak. Sure enough, a simple metal collar clasped around the orc’s neck.

“We think the Warlord was directly controlling them?” I asked. “It’s for certain?”

“Likely not personally. His Subtle Men are still active around here.”

I scowled. “So desperate to cling to power…this is the length he’d go to? Sacrifice half his country? I can’t believe it.”

“You can’t believe what? That a man would be greedy enough to sell his nation for his own gain?” Lord Ceonwin shook his head. “You should know by now what we’re dealing with. If it makes you angry, that’s a good thing. Hate can be a force for good if targeted properly.”

“O—of course, sir.”

I gulped. Hate? Did I hate the orcs? Or the Warlord? I didn’t want to think I did, but it was getting easier by the day.

But knowing what had happened to Lord Ceonwin’s son, I could see why he might have hated the Warlord.

I simply stared at the suppression collar, hoping an answer came to me, but nothing did.
