Seren had discovered that trust had an order.

This was not a thing she had wished to discover. Orders were for ledgers, room assignments, and the careful arrangement of problems so they could be handled. Unless Alric was involved, then they just compounded and went sideways.

Trust however should have been less tidy than that. It should not have had a first person, then a second person, then everyone else standing somewhere behind.

But it did. Alric had told Stromni first. Not Mara. Not Vale. Not Seren. Stromni.

Seren was far too sensible to be wounded because a man with too many secrets had chosen a dwarf with a hammer before choosing her. She also had Brannick, which meant any romantic interpretation of the matter would have been both wrong and deeply awkward. Brannick already occupied the portion of her life reserved for men who made her sigh, soften, worry, and take a near professional interest in annoying her.

This was about being trusted.

She had sat with Alric in his office while he told her impossible things. Another world. Death. A goddess of creation. Women treated as equal in law, at least in principle, though apparently his old world had been trying rather than succeeding, which he seemed to respect more than most people respected victory. He had spoken carefully, and badly in places, and honestly enough that she had believed him despite herself.

Then he had said only Stromni knew all of it. That had sat in Seren’s chest with quiet weight.

She understood why, or at least thought she did. She even knew the feeling was irrational, the dwarf was his best friend after all. Then a thought had struck her: Stromni was craft. Stromni was copper, heat, seams, hammers, measurements, and lengthy discussions about metal. Alric trusted craft. He trusted people who made things because making things gave his thoughts a target. Crafters did not ask him to be noble. They asked him whether something leaked, whether the malt had turned and where the hell a specific tool was.

Alric understood that language and Alric loved craft.

Seren remembered him trying to bake bread some weeks earlier. It had not gone well. She had made a joke that day and he had rebuked her, she understood why even. She had overstepped and pushed him too far she knew.

The bread had come out dense, pale in some places, too hard in others, and possessing the general spirit of a doorstop that had seen war. Alric had eaten part of it with the grim expression of a man refusing defeat.

The memory had returned to Seren with dangerous usefulness.

Alric loved craft, and he had failed at bread. Therefore, Seren decided, she would help. It was a kind thought.

It was also, as many kind thoughts around Alric tended to be, the first loose stone on a very slippery slope.

The headquarters kitchen had two ovens.

This had mattered very little to Seren until that morning. Before then, two ovens had simply meant that the old inn had once been large enough to feed more people than was sensible and had survived long enough for Alric to buy it, rename it, fill half the rooms with staff, barrels, paper, and problems, then act faintly surprised when the building began behaving like a headquarters.

Now, however, two ovens were an advantage. She could get them to different temperatures.

She was not entirely sure why this would matter yet, but it sounded like the sort of thing people said when craft was involved.

Seren stood in the kitchen with her sleeves tied back and considered the table in front of her.

There were bowls, several of them. Cloths, clean water, a knife, a scraping board. Three sacks of flour arranged in a neat row because if one was attempting to understand a man through bread, the bread should at least begin with discipline.

The first flour was pandemain, fine and pale and costly enough that the old cook who had helped her find it had spoken of it in a tone normally reserved for priests or dead relatives.

The second was a rye and wheat mix from Merchant’s Row. Sensible, respectable. Not poor flour but not noble flour. Bread that looked as though it had paid its rent on time.

The third was a mixture of barley, oats, and rye for something called carters’ bread. The man who sold it had said this with pride, as if carters were a recognised authority in the field of things that should remain inside a stomach during bad weather. Seren knew a few carters and the man was probably right.

She had no idea which one Alric would like. So she had gotten all three. This was either good preparation or the beginning of a flour problem. It has also sounded vaguely crafty at the time, in her mind at least.

Seren looked toward the yard through the open kitchen door. Brannick was training men in the courtyard. The morning had not warmed much, though dust still rose where boots dragged over hard-packed earth. Wooden practice weapons struck with hollow cracks. One of the newer guards stumbled back, recovered badly, and was struck again.

The yard was not ideal for training. Dust got everywhere. Men sweated, shouted, fell, and then brought parts of the courtyard into the building on their boots.

But she did not want them training near the stable area either. If you needed a guard, you did not need him in a few minutes.

Brannick caught her looking. He had a practice blade in one hand and the expression of a man who knew she was preparing something but had not yet been told whether he should be proud, concerned, or seek safely elsewhere.

Seren smiled at him, before she sent one of the maids to fetch Alric.

Alric arrived with faint scent of herbs and a look of confusion on his face.

He had clearly been interrupted in the middle of something. There was a greenish smudge on one sleeve, and the faint herbal smell clung to him. Sharp, leafy, and suspicious in the way of plants Alric had not yet decided whether to drink, distil or burn.

“Seren?” he asked, raising one brow. “Is something wrong?”

“No,” Seren said, it sounded more confident than she felt. “We are making bread.”

Alric stopped. His gaze moved across the table.

Bowls, flour, water, cloths, ovens, seren. Then his whole face changed.

It was not the polite smile he used when someone had done something thoughtful and he was trying to understand what shape gratitude should take. This was simple, immediate delight. He looked like a boy who had just been told stones could skip on water.

“We are?” he asked, his wide smile, still taking in the arrangement.

Seren felt something in her chest ease as confidence returned. This could work.

“Yes,” she said, much more firmly. “I remembered you tried before.”

Alric looked at the flours. “You got different kinds?” he asked looking at them in interest.

“I did.” she said, feeling perhaps a bit smug.

He moved closer, the happy expression only he could wear when he had found a newer problem. “What is that?”

“It’s Pandemain flour.” Seren said glancing at it.

Alric nodded slowly, clearly impressed. Then he looked at the second sack. “This one?”

“Rye and wheat, from Merchant’s Row.” Seren said, the smugness deepening.

“And this one?” He leaned over the third sack and frowned, but with interest.

“It makes carters bread I’m told,” Seren said, nodding at the happiness bad flour was bringing him.

Alric grinned, it was a a big grin, there was a predatory twinkle in his eye.

Seren felt briefly vindicated and then immediately concerned that she had understood him that well.

“That sounds excellent,” he said point to the carters flour. “Not soft, probably, but useful. Heavy, hmm... Might need more water.”

Seren straightened slightly. “I thought we could use the two ovens at different heats.”

Alric looked up from the flour.

“Eventually, yes, good thinking,” he said, glancing at the ovens but his attention returned to the flour, “First we make dough.”

Seren paused with one hand already half turned toward the hearth tools.

“No ovens yet. Later depending on how long they take, but bread needs time first. Mixing, kneading, resting, shaping, then baking.” He looked at the table again with visible pleasure. “This is going to be fun.”

Seren had not expected him to say fun.

She had expected perhaps gratitude. Perhaps surprise. Ideally some quiet recognition that she was attempting to meet him somewhere important. Instead he had found flour and become happy in the direct way men became happy when allowed to play with tools.

Still, it was a start.

Alric washed his hands, then reached for the pandemain first. He did not take over exactly. That was one of the things that made him dangerous. He included people in a way that felt like consultation right up until one realised one had been recruited.

“We should do many small loaves,” he said, nodding at the flour. “Same by weight would be ideal but we don’t have good scales in here, do we?”

“Uh, we do not.” Seren said, cursing inward slightly.

“Hmm... We’ll figure it out as we go.” He paused and smiled.

Seren watched as he measured flour roughly into the first bowl. His attention was sharp. He checked the texture between his fingers, rubbed it lightly, smelled it, then nodded as if the flour had answered a question.

“The fine flour should take water nicely,” he said, still studying it closely. “Rye is different. Less stretch. Oats make everything heavier. The carters’ loaf may be stubborn.”

“Flour can be stubborn?” Seren asked, one brow lifting.

“Flour can be many things,” Alric said simply, “Mostly depending on whether you are in a hurry.”

Seren accepted this as craft knowledge, though privately she felt flour was becoming too confident.

Alric looked over. “Do we have salt?”

Seren cursed inwardly again.

“Not too much. I’ll go get it.” she said trying not to show her irritation on her face.

It was not long after that they were both kneading. The dough she found was being difficult, but now with moisture.

The carters’ bread looked worse. The oats drank water as if they had been personally denied it for years. Alric laughed under his breath and added more.

“I suppose I should show you this.” Alric said glancing around.

He held out a hand to a nearby section of countertop. He closed his eyes.

She felt something shift, something moved, she could have sworn there was a flash. When he moved his hand away, a small white blob remained.

“You know the foamy casks? This is similar to that, but not the same. This is for bread.” He explained, pointing at the blob.

Seren stared, “A-and that, is your divinity?” she glanced at him wide eyed.

He checked around again to see no one was nearby, “This is the power I was gifted.” Then he poked it, “See? Touch it, it cant harm you.”

Seren leaned forward as the familiar scent of the brewery came to her. She reached forward and touched it. Nothing happened. She was almost disappointed.

“Bread is alive before it is bread, same as beer.” He said smiling, clearly enjoying himself. He gathered half and began to fold it into his dough.

Seren did the same while staring at the dough. Bread was alive before it was bread.

That sounded like the sort of sentence Alric could say casually while rearranging someone’s understanding of the world and then wondering why everyone looked at him.

The temple had acknowledged his power in a way. A goddess had given it to him. The brewery had proved it enough times for half the city to drink the evidence. Yet seeing it used, felt different. She almost wanted to pray right there.

Seren had wanted to meet him in craft. Instead, she had found the craft turning to gods. She began kneading with renewed determination.

That determination lasted almost a quarter bell before her arms began complaining.

Kneading was not graceful. That was the first insult. It looked as though it should be. Push, fold, turn, push again. A simple rhythm. A thing a capable person could master because capable people mastered things.

Instead, the dough stuck to her hand, then to the table, then to itself in ways that did not seem helpful. Her shoulders began to ache. Flour dusted her sleeves. The pandemain dough became smooth and soft under Alric’s hands. The rye-wheat improved after some effort. The carters’ dough behaved like a lump of road pretending to consider food as a future.

Alric was cheerful through all of it.

Outside, a wooden sword cracked against another. Someone grunted. Brannick said something low and corrective. Eventually, Alric covered the bowls with cloths.

“Now they rest.” Alric said, stepping back, he looked deeply satisfied.

Seren looked at the covered bowls. “They rest?” she asked, slightly suspicious.

“Yes. About a bell.” Alric said, not taking his eyes off them.

She looked at her hands. Then the bowls. After all that work, the bread was resting first. That seemed poorly arranged.

“I’ll come back,” Alric said, finally glancing at her. “I want to check something while the bread rises.”

“Of course,” Seren said, relieved for a break.

Alric still smiling faintly, and left the kitchen, but there was clearly a pep in his step. Seren watched him go.

The first part had gone well, she decided. Perhaps not as solemnly as she had imagined. There had been no quiet conversation about trust. No meaningful discussion over the flour. No sign that Alric had understood she had tried to reach toward the part of him that felt safest with crafters.

But he had been happy. That mattered, maybe it mattered more. Seren turned back to the covered bowls. The dough sat under the cloths, resting like nobility.

She narrowed her eyes at them before she began preparing the ovens. She lit the oven early and watched the bricks take the heat. The bread would go in after the fire came out, because Seren had thought about this for three breaths and already surpassed Alric’s attempts with ovens.

A bell was a long time when one had decided not to check the dough too often.

Seren checked it too often.

She told herself this was necessary observation. Alric liked observation, observation was part of trying. His old world had tried, and she was trying, therefore checking the dough every so often was not impatience. It was a method.

She checked initially like a first time parent would check on a sleeping newborn. Like what she imagined Mara to be one day here in the headquarters, at least the loaves could not wake up and cry... she hoped. By the ninth check she glared with suspicion.

By the time Alric returned, the ovens had been brought up carefully and then lowered because Seren trusted neither of them.

Alric lifted the cloth from the pandemain bowl and smiled.

“It rose.” he said, wearing a wide, satisfied smile.

The dough had become larger while doing nothing, which felt suspiciously close to management.

They shaped the loaves next. Alric showed her how to pull the dough into itself, how to tighten the top, how not to press all the life out of it. The pandemain loaf looked beautiful. The rye-wheat looked respectable. The carters’ bread looked like it was a descendant of cobblestones. They put the loaves into the ovens.

“Not too dark,” Alric said, stepping back slightly and folding his arms. “Light brown, mostly. We don’t want to bake them into shields.”

Seren nodded with the seriousness of a woman being entrusted with state correspondence.

Then the smell began.

At first it was only warm flour and oven heat. Pleasant enough. Then it deepened. Grain, sweetness, crust, something soft turning into food. It moved through the kitchen and out into the common room with no respect for privacy.

One of the maids passed the kitchen door, slowed, then continued walking with the stiff dignity of someone pretending she had not inhaled deliberately.

The smell continued.

A slow realization dawned upon Seren, that smell was more like a gossiping servant than loyal Retainer. She was being betrayed by her own creation, one that failed to inherit her discretion. She knew then, that she had underestimated the effect Alric had on all things new and novel.

She should have known this. Useful things spread, it was a theme with Alric at this point. Comfortable undergarments had proved that. Beer proved it daily. Cider had nearly turned the harvest festival into a civic uprising with music.

Bread had now joined the list. The loaves came out better than Seren had expected but Alric frowned at them.

The pandemain was the worst offender because it was beautiful. Pale golden crust, soft sides, and a clean shape that made ordinary bread look like it had not been raised without affection. The rye-wheat was darker and firmer, with a respectable smell. The carters’ bread was heavy. It looked like a loaf that could be used as ammunition on a siege engine.

Alric tapped the bottom of each loaf and listened. Seren did not know bread could be questioned in that way.

“It needs to cool a little,” he said, nodding as he listened carefully.

Seren nodded, not because she understood but because it felt like something crafters would do.

They waited less than they should have because Alric was not as patient as he claimed when bread was involved.

He cut into the pandemain first.

Steam rose from the slice. The inside was soft. Not soft in the way old bread became when soup had defeated it. Soft in the way cloth could be soft, or new wool, or a bed one did not deserve after a long day.

Seren took the piece he offered. She tasted it. Then she really understood.

Seren had eaten good food. Not regularly in her younger years, but she had worked in places where good food passed by close enough to be understood. Noble kitchens wasted enough education for a clever servant to become informed by accident. She knew the difference between common bread, good bread, festival bread, noble bread, and bread people lied about because the household paid too much for it.

This was different, she swallowed. Alric was watching her.

“Well?” he asked, concerned.

Seren looked at the slice in her hand. “This is very good.” she answered honestly, she held the bread wanting another bite.

Alric beamed.

That was the only word for it. Beamed, like a boy who had successfully tricked the world into giving him something sweet.

“We need cheese,” he said, his expression seemed nearly urgent.

Seren paused, she was about to bite again. “Cheese?”

“For sandwiches.” Alric said, his expression nearly solemn.

Seren looked toward the kitchen stores.

She had prepared everything she could think of for bread but she had not prepared sandwiches. The failure opened beneath her feet in a small but specific way.

“We have cheese,” she said, still holding the slice, reluctant to put it down. “Butter too. I am not sure what else. There may be cured meat, but I did not plan for...”

“Cheese is perfect.” Alric interupted quickly.

“Just cheese?” she asked, he expression shifting.

“Just cheese.” he nodded enthusiastically.

Seren was not certain whether this was mercy or madness.

Alric sliced the bread, buttered it, placed cheese between the pieces, and set the sandwich near the oven heat in a pot. He moved it, pressed lightly, turned it, then waited with the focused attention of a man handling something explosive.

It was bread and cheese. Then the cheese melted. The bread browned. The smell changed into something else.

Seren copied him with her own sandwich, carefully mimicking him like an apprentice stealing techniques from her Master. She did not ask questions because questions implied uncertainty, and hunger told her that questions would get in the way of keeping up. She watched the heat carefully, turned the bread too early, nearly lost the cheese flipping it back.

Alric cut his sandwich in half and lifted one piece carefully. Cheese stretched.

Seren stared. Alric took a bite and closed his eyes for a moment.

“Oh, that’s good.” he said, his eyes were still closed, his knees almost seemed to buckle.

Seren tasted hers. For several breaths, her eyes closed and she had no useful thoughts at all. When she opened her eyes, she noticed the first problem.

Alric had taken his sandwich into the courtyard. This lead to the second problem.

The first problem had been making the bread. The second was allowing the smell to meet guards.

The courtyard had settled into the middle portion of training, when men were warm enough to make mistakes with confidence and tired enough to resent correction. Brannick stood near the centre, watching two guards work through a shield movement. Dust marked the knees of one man’s trousers. Another had a red line across one forearm where a practice blade had taught him about timing.

Alric stood near the kitchen door, eating his toasted cheese sandwich and watching with open contentment.

Then Brannick noticed the sandwich. His eyes moved from Alric’s hand to the kitchen door. Then to Seren. Seren felt the future arrive.

“What is that?” Brannick asked, wide eyed.

“Bread and cheese, I can’t explain, you need to try,” Alric said with a sudden authority.

Brannick looked at Seren urgently.

“There is more.” Alric said gesturing with his head.

The yard changed.

Men did not need an excuse to take a break for something delicious, no one did. Brannick came in first because Brannick had earned the right to betray her directly.

Seren made another toasted cheese.

He watched the process with the careful attention of a man who knew this mattered to her, though perhaps not why. When she handed it to him, he took a bite too soon, burned his mouth slightly, and still looked impressed.

“That’s good,” he said after swallowing.

“It is only bread and cheese,” Seren said. She watched him closely, seeing if there was a barb there.

“How though?” He asked tilting his head at it.

The distinction warmed her more than it should have. Then the other guards arrived.

They came in the manner of men pretending they had reasons. One needed water. Another had been sent to check whether Brannick was returning. A third was apparently inspecting the doorway, which would have been more convincing if he had not stared at the pan the entire time.

Soon Seren was making more toasted cheese sandwiches.

Alric helped at first, then was removed from the process by the simple fact that he kept explaining options they did not have ingredients for. He mentioned onions, then mustard, then pickled things.

Seren pointed him back toward the yard with his second sandwich. He went willingly. The guards liked the sandwiches.

Of course they liked them. They were hot, filling, buttery, and full of melted cheese. One guard ate his in four bites and declared it excellent. Another burned his fingers and said it was worth it. A third asked if the darker bread was meant to be like that, then ate it before anyone answered.

They did not understand the bread. That became clear very quickly.

To them, it was a baked cheese thing. A very good baked cheese thing. A hot cheese thing. Possibly a better cheese thing than other cheese things, but still firmly in the world of cheese things.

One of them, holding half a sandwich made with the pandemain loaf, said, “Could use more cheese.”

Seren looked at him. He stopped chewing for half a breath. Brannick turned away. His shoulders moved once.

Seren's eyes narrowed at him. Brannick moved away before he could be accused of something.

“It could,” Alric said thoughtfully from nearby. “Actually, a stronger cheese would be good too. Or two cheeses.”

The guard looked relieved. Seren closed her eyes briefly.

They were not spiritually prepared for good bread. That was not their fault. Some men lived close to practical concerns. If food was hot, enough, and did not make them ill, gratitude followed. Texture was for people who had not recently been hit in the ribs with a wooden sword.

Brannick, at least, understood enough.

He ate slower than the others. He tried both the pandemain and the rye-wheat without needing to be told there was a difference. He looked at the bread, then at the table where the remaining loaves sat, then at Seren.

“You made this?” Brannick asked, he seemed impressed.

“With Mister Alric,” she said, she felt a flicker of pride and smiled.

Brannick nodded, and there was something warm in his expression that made the whole morning briefly worth the flour in her sleeve.

“You’ll have trouble keeping this quiet.” Brannick said glancing across the yard.

Seren stared at him. He took another bite and wisely offered no further explanation, retreating to the courtyard.

The kitchen had become a mess. The table was covered in crumbs, flour, a smear of butter, three knives, and the evidence of success. The ovens still held heat. The kitchen staff were pretending not to hover. The yard was full of men eating toasted cheese sandwiches between training bouts as if this had always been part of a disciplined guard routine.

Alric stood outside with Brannick, explaining something about browning without burning. He looked deeply content.

Seren should have felt victorious. Instead, she saw tomorrow.

That was the problem with success, especially when Alric was involved. Failure ended cleanly. Failure could be wrapped, apologised for, learned from, and placed in the part of memory reserved for things never to repeat without changes. Success stayed. Success made demands. Success looked around for a place to live.

One of the guards said, not loudly, not rudely, not even to her directly, “Will there be more tomorrow?”

Seren heard it anyway. Tomorrow.

The word settled into the kitchen like a new piece of furniture gifted by a doting parent that no one wanted.

She looked at the loaves. There was not as much left as there should have been. The pandemain was gone. The rye-wheat had suffered heavily. Even the carters’ bread, once toasted with enough butter and cheese, had been accepted by men who respected density that fought back.

If bread this good existed once, then ordinary bread would be compared to it. If toasted cheese sandwiches existed once, men would remember them the next time cold training ended and the kitchen smelled just ordinary.

If Alric looked that happy once, Seren would remember that too. She had not made bread. She had created a standard.
