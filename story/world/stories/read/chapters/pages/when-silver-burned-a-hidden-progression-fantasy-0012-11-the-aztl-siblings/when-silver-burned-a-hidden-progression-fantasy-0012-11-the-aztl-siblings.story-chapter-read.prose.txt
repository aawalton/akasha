They were standing in front of, according to Treyant, a safehouse right at the edge of the city. They could only see a harshly chipped wooden gate and the suggestion of a gray wall stretching upwards toward the sky, the rest of the building outside the bounds of their vision.

"We'll rest here for a spell," Treyant said. "Need to be fresh if we're to climb the walls."

"It’s getting late already?" asked one of the women in Jack's group, pulling on the reins of her blindfolded horse.

When Blindlight dominated the sky, it remained hanging there without pause, with no cloud able to obscure its ungodly radiance. Nights were exactly the same as days, and unless you had an extraordinarily developed sense of time, it was impossible to tell one from the other. Even your own body betrayed you as the headaches and bleary vision from the radiance’s exposure blurred together with natural tiredness until you couldn't tell which was which.

Treyant walked up to the gate with key in hand. "No more than five hours of sleep. Stay indoors, keep the shutters closed, and be ready when I return from preparing our passage."

Kalsin's eyes had to adjust to the dark inside the safehouse. When the last of them filed in and the gates closed behind, he could barely make out hanging armor racks and spiraling stairs. A spacious, out-of-use stockpile tower. The darkness was a refuge that soothed his overstrained senses.

A hand squeezed his arm and it took Kalsin a few moments of rapid blinking to notice Jack back at his side, towering over him with that familiar, protective presence.

As if obeying a silent agreement, each group dispersed to explore and search for an isolated spot they could call their own for the night. A few of them stumbled, still disoriented. Kalsin and Jack took the uppermost floor. Dust caked the low-grade spears lining the walls, and the cold, hard floor offered little comfort under their bedrolls. On a positive note, at least the ground was even, a luxuriant rarity for those who spent most of their days camping in the wilds.

Following a short meal, Jack stuffed her legs under the covers and stretched against the wall at her back. She began untying the knot holding her hair, then pinned Kalsin with an expectant gaze.

"Not our people," Kalsin said, keeping his voice at a comfortable murmur. "Runaway family with a debt they couldn't pay. Not a particularly believable story, at least no more than any other, but the story itself makes no difference. The important bit is that they weren't lying. Not about their past, and not when questioned about the azTéls."

Jack tilted her head sideways a fraction.

"Yes, I'm sure. As sure as I can be. The boy was easy to read. The girl less so, but you know I would've seen any deception thrown my way even from liars by trade."

Kalsin unclasped his sword sling and placed the scabbard next to his bedroll. His movements came sluggish as he laid down, both from a day speared by Blindlight's intensity and the mending of his arm. He checked the bandage. Still adequate. The neat surface wounds posed little trouble.

He felt Jack’s concerned gaze on him, lingering in the corner of his vision as if poised for an invitation to help. He pretended he didn’t see it.

"What about your end of the alley?" Kalsin asked, glancing up.

Jack slowly finished untying her hair, the long mane falling down one shaved side. She then shrugged and gave him a salute-like gesture.

"So it's not them either. They're soldiers?"

Another headshake. Jack thought for a moment, then put her hands together and bowed with a reverent expression, which proved an amusing performance considering she was a giant bundle laying with her back propped up.

"Ah," Kalsin said. "Never would've guessed they were the religious types. Followers of Hadúr, yeah?"

The god of conflict was a popular one among hunters and fighters, soldiers and mercenaries; those who risked their life on the daily. The deity wasn't heralded as an evil warmonger, however.

Hadúr encompassed the ideology of the necessary resolution between death and life, war and peace, how one cannot exist without the other. Some considered him a safe middleground between the law-abiding, all-knowing Tűzmother, she who created Warmlight to illuminate the necessary known, and the shrouded mystery of Vadász, bringing forth Blindlight to safeguard that which must never be revealed.

To the church, the gods were a means to coerce money from the common Scarrd, a fee that allowed them entry into Éden during the afterlife.

To Kalsin, the gods were just another set of variables by which people defined themselves.

"Could be a front. Are you sure their involvement is completely out of the frame of possibilities?"

Jack touched her mouth while arching her brows, then lightly brushed the scar on her jawline.

Kalsin stared, then tore his eyes away from the nasty sight. He cleared his throat before speaking. "That couldn't have been an easy topic to bring up." If someone asked him about his scar, Kalsin would've turned their nose into tissue paste. That, or flee and hide in a corner until he bottled it all back up.

With a not-a-big-deal wave of her arm, Jack mimicked a flapping mouth with her hand, grinning. They didn't mind her question, then. Opposite, they had been content to share.

"Huh. Never thought about it, but I suppose it makes sense. Belief in Hadúr could very well solidify the acceptance of duality between Clenn and Scarrd. Content with their role. Wouldn't take a cure if offered on a golden tray."

Jack brushed her hair in a deliberate way, making it spill forward and down one side of her head in a way that covered her scar. Face downturned and her dark eyes on him, she made a small beckoning gesture.

“I’m fine.”

Her eyes narrowed.

Kalsin let out a breath and scooted a bit closer with his bedroll, close enough for their sides to touch. Jack didn’t wait for him to offer—she immediately reached out and put his arm in her lap.

Kalsin scoffed. “Look, it’s all white and bloody immaculate. Satisfied?”

Jack ran a gentle hand across the cloth a few times.

Kalsin looked away. Though he didn’t retrieve his arm on instinct.

Her idle touches felt… soothing. Soothing enough to let exhaustion slowly take over as he laid there in the darkness. Somewhere far away, he was aware of Jack's watchful presence, and wondered at what point along the way her company had morphed into a rock solid shield he surely couldn't continue without.

Did she truly believe in their cause? He'd been clawing at the possibility of redemption for himself, for Cinder, but all they had seen so far was the coins looted off dead bodies in their paths. If this lead failed and the destination turned up empty, he'd have nothing. Nothing but wasted years for himself and, even worse, for the only people who cared enough to follow him.

He felt like he'd barely closed his eyes when Jack shook him awake. The tower was rousing, the sounds of creaking planks and thudding boots coming from below. Kalsin kept his eyes closed for a moment longer, gathering all the energy before having to leave the island of solitude once again.

With Jack’s large silhouette protectively looming behind him, he made his way down the steps and joined with the others at the entrance.

Treyant stood there, and with her signature brisk manner announced it was time to leave the city. The prospect seemed to bolster the spirit of many among their numbers. He caught a relieved smile shared between the two kids at the old man's side.

Kalsin turned to observe the couple, also aware of the cowled priests nearby. He needed an excuse to go along with one of those groups, and preferably arrange Jack to go with the other. For once, fortune seemed to smile upon him, because he was approached by the man and woman in hand.

Kalsin reflexively scanned for their scars again, and found his mood stained by irritation when he couldn't spot them.

"Excuse us. If we could borrow a minute of your time?" the man asked. His expression was hard to see in the dark, but his voice was friendly enough.

Kalsin extended a hand in greeting. The man matched his grip as the woman stared at him, then quickly looked away, unable to stomach the scar nestled between his ghostly bright irises.

"I'm Áron, and this is my fiancée, Erazza. We were wondering if you were willing to sell us your éj-lantern."

Over their shoulders, Kalsin could already see Jack orbit around the cowled figures like a bee detecting honey-potential. He tapped the glass hanging on his belt with a frown. An offer to buy his lantern? It was an odd request. One he wouldn't accept in a hundred Blindlights, but that was best left unsaid. "Why, if you don't mind me asking. Are you not satisfied with Darkfinder Treyant's service?"

"Oh no, we knew her from before. Friend of a friend," Erazza said. "She was the one to make the suggestion, actually. Our destination leads us away from her planned route, and we're in something of a hurry." She shifted the satchel on her back. "We can pay in hard coin."

"All lined up? Good, we're moving out," Treyant's announcement interrupted them. She pushed the gates open to let in a sense-incapacitating radiance. The éj-lanterns automatically gathered the incoming light and reflected it, creating gray bubbles of mobile refuge. Kalsin took his place at the end of the procession with Áron and Erazza in his circle. He didn't give a definitive answer to their proposal. Better to string them along.

They stepped out and continued their advance through the white expanse like razor in endless butter. The glare of Blindlight was harder on the senses this time, having ramped up after prior exposure. Though Kalsin kept his head down, he had to blink rapidly against the eye-strain and massage his temples. It hadn't been a minute since they left behind the safehouse, and he was already on edge.

"I never caught your name," Áron said. Kalsin noticed him observing the sword at the small of his back with a blank, unguarded expression.

A bout of impulsiveness told Kalsin that such a blank expression would be a perfect, clear canvas to stir some waves upon. Time was running low. So he decided to take the risk.

"Kalsin azTél," he said, making the split second decision. The effect was instantaneous.

A flicker of abject shock registered on Áron's face, eyes widening. It then shifted into terror as he realized they both knew he just gave himself away, then locked adamant with a stubborn token of resistance. Erazza, for her part, was much harder to read. If it wasn't for the subtle alteration of her stance giving her easier access to the hatchet holster at her side, Kalsin wouldn't have guessed she even heard him.

Kalsin shrugged apologetically without slowing his walk. "Fair play, you caught me in a lie. The real name of my family is Zares, but that's not a road I want to mud through anytime soon. Nobody wants to hear a sob story."

They didn't utter a word. Of course not. Those unaffiliated wouldn't have reacted to the name at all, carrying on with the pleasant discourse as usual. But Kalsin had caught them off guard, and now he knew the pair was involved with the azTéls, and they knew that he knew in turn. But they didn’t know what he knew. Which made him a threat.

Maybe that headache wasn't related to Blindlight after all.

"They're after you, the law," Kalsin said, voice turning serious. "The collector of Remallon, Quin, is leading the case. He received intel that heretics were about to flee the city in darkfinder company."

"And you know that how?" Erazza asked, having drifted closer to him with hand still hovering dangerously low.

In place of answering, Kalsin reached into his pocket and slowly, carefully extracted a crumpled piece of paper. "Stole it off his unconscious body."

Áron took it after a moment of hesitation. He glanced through it, then gave Erazza a light shake of his head.

"Not good enough," she said. "This city’s crawling with forgers and cheats by trade. And Bellé would never betray us."

"Perhaps. But would you state the same about everyone else here? Priest robes would be a prime disguise of choice for spies. A lone assistant making a shot at easy leechouse money. Warriors lying about their service to the divine."

Kalsin switched hands to hold his lantern, shaking out the limb. "You're skeptical; I understand that. Being hunted for what you believe does that to a person. But I don't think you're willing to murder someone who, maybe, just on the off chance, could very well be your ally."

"You're assuming a lot of things."

"I'm assuming I won't find deaf ears by saying you're in mortal danger. Somebody reported your route to the Civil House secret service. That's a fact. And while I'm not sure Quin managed to read the message before we took it off his person, it's better safe than dead."

"We?" Erazza asked, eyes snapping around. She stopped her foreboding advance, but she still looked ready for anything.

"Jack, my partner," Kalsin nodded in front of them, the shroud of his bodyguard's lantern just barely visible. "Or Jacqueline, but don't call her that. You don't want to get on her angry side. Plenty of bad memories in her past too, I reckon."

Erazza and Áron exchanged a look. "We're not interested in calling you anything. Either of you," she said with finality to her tone.

And that was that. They wouldn't budge, Kalsin could tell, clear as Warmlight. Not with conventional methods, at any rate.

The beginnings of a plan started taking root in his mind, coalescing with each step as they went through the light. Reckless, but provided with the right conditions, perhaps it could work.

Oh, well. The shitting lengths he was willing to go to prove a point.

Treyant's definition of city-edge was a generous one because it took them just about another hour until a seemingly boundless blockade of stone barred their way. The darkfinder raked her nails lightly across a chalk mark on the wall, then erased it with the sleeve of her coat. She turned left and continued leading them along the base.

They were clumped closer to one another now. When Jack signaled a question to Treyant, she reassured them in her flat, not-at-all reassuring tone. Guard patrols were present during Blindlight due to the high-profile marriage being held, she said, but their effectiveness was greatly reduced due to their shrunken field of vision. She'd timed and placed their route accordingly.

To Kalsin's relief, it wasn't a ladder or a secluded staircase that waited for them, but rope. The thick line was hanging from the side of the wall and disappeared into the light above. With all colors having been washed into a uniform grey, the rope and the wall blended together perfectly. Kalsin would've never spotted it without the darkfinder directing them.

"Now. Me, the old man, and my assistant here are going to leave on accord of some fabricated business through the main gates, horses and bahten in tow." Treyant nodded to her hooded accomplice, who had already begun herding the mounts. "While you lot—you’ll take the wall."

"Spare me the complaints," she said, deflecting the kids’ brewing retort before they could give it voice. "The fellas on gate duty know my face. We've dealt before, corrupt as can be. I can get away with bringing one extra, but the rest will have to go unseen. And so the climb for you it is."

"We're not splitting up," stated the girl, still half-carrying her father.

"Wings," Treyant said flatly. "Last I checked, neither horse nor bahten possessed the appendage to get over a thirty-feet-high city wall. Similarly, your old man doesn't seem capable of pulling his weight. The original plan was the climb for everyone, but animals, invalids, and another pair of éj-lanterns left this the most realistic alternative."

The girl moved a step toward his brother. "What? No chance. The deal we struck specifically stated the three of us remain together. That's what we paid for, darkfinder."

Treyant's face soured as if caught in the laborious process of chewing through lemon. Kalsin felt for her. When you've a plan drawn out before you, crafted and arranged by your own two hands, there isn't anything worse than others barging through the foundations you've laid for the future. But at the end of the day, personal businesses valued two things; money and reputation. Both were prone to dwindle with unsatisfied customers. That's why the best plans were the flexible ones.

"Fine. Your little family leaves through the gates," Treyant muttered, looking none too pleased. "But I'm charging you extra for guard-bribery."

Nobody dared disturb the darkfinder as she gave hushed directions, first to her assistant then to the family crowding her. When she was more or less—probably less—satisfied with the proceedings, she finally turned to the rest of them.

"Instructions." She gave the rope a few experimental pulls. "Lantern bearer goes first, respective hangers-on follow. Once you're up, pull on the rope to signal your group has made it and the next batch starts the ascent, but don't wait for them. There's another rope hanging from the outer side of the wall. Descend immediately. You'll see chalk markings lead farther away from the city walls. We'll meet there.

"If you people get antsy about heights, you're in a world of luck because you won't see fuck-all beyond a couple of feet below your noggin. See you on the other side."

With that positive note hanging in the air, the darkfinder left them in the gray.
