Ash swirled as I stepped forward, ankle-deep in a dust that felt too thick, too warm, like powdered bone stirred with grief. The sky overhead was the color of cooling blood, a deep, bruise-purple where the clouds broke, and the sun behind it looked swollen, pale, tired, appearing less like it was dying and more like it had simply given up. The Boon inside my ribs thrummed at a frequency just shy of pain. Hunger, always. But now there was something else beneath it: recognition. Every breath scraped down my throat like sandpaper.

The air here felt wrong, too heavy, too old. And it tasted like rusted iron, like marrow sucked from broken bones. Banners stood in rows ahead, stretching across the wastes like the ribs of a fallen giant, their black cloth displaying red spirals stitched with something that glistened, blood if I had to guess. They twisted in a breeze I couldn’t feel, snapping and shuddering as if they were in pain. I moved toward them, slow and watchful, every footstep sending a soft poof of ash up around my boots that didn’t settle but lingered, as if it were watching.

Then I saw them. Soldiers lined the trenches carved into the dead earth, hundreds of them, maybe more. Their armor was rusted to brownish gray, helmets bowed low, weapons sheathed but ready. Though silent and motionless, they were distinctly unfrozen. They were waiting. One by one, heads turned toward me as I passed, revealing hollow eyes and cracked lips, offering silent nods of acknowledgment, as if they knew me, as if they’d been waiting for me. The Boon pulsed again, harder this time; it liked this place too much.

The hunger in my belly twisted, and I didn’t know if it was mine or hers. One of them, a woman, broad-shouldered and missing a jaw, stepped out from the trench without a word. Her armor bore the same spiral sigil as the banners, and a ragged braid hung down the back of her helmet. She held out a canteen, rusted, dented, warm in her hands. I reached for it before I could stop myself. My fingers brushed hers, and she looked straight into me with milky, cracked eyes.

“For the Queen’s daughter,” she rasped.

I drank, the water warm, or rather, thick, like blood diluted in oil, slick and metallic. I gagged on the first swallow, but my throat took the rest without asking, my hands refusing to let go of the canteen. The woman watched me, unmoving, and then she bowed, her gaze directed to something behind me. I turned. The crowd had grown; dozens more soldiers now stood just outside the trenches. Their bodies were wreckage, scarred, bent, too many of them missing parts that should have killed them. One man had half a crow’s beak in place of a face. Another’s chest was a cracked birdcage, bones twisted around feathers. Their presence pressed on me, not like a mob, but like gravity. They knew me, or thought they did.

“I’m not your queen’s daughter,” I said. “I don’t even know who your queen is.”

Silence pressed in like a held breath. But I did know. Not fully, not yet—but the name was a thorn in my throat, and the Boon was humming like a tuning fork struck too hard.

[That’s the problem with old names,] Malice whispered. [They echo, whether you want them to or not.]

And then the wind shifted... A profound silence answered, but then the wind shifted, and every banner in the field snapped once, hard, in unison, a loud crack like thunder. The ash scattered, and the murmuring began, originating not from their mouths, but from the trenches, from the dirt itself. Thousands of whispers rose together, words in no language I recognized, but the Boon understood; she understood perfectly. She sang.

I dropped the canteen, clutching my chest as the hunger bloomed into something worse, something reverent, a worship mixed with starvation, and I dropped to my knees.

Visions bloomed across the cracked soil, distinct from illusions or memories. Reenactments. Crows swarmed a burning tower. A figure, cloaked in ash and feathers, tore their own heart out to feed a starving child. A god wrapped in silver chains was cast from the heavens as their wings molted in the shape of blades. And at the center of it all stood a throne made of bones and banners, its seat empty. The soldiers gathered closer.

One of them, a man missing half his skull, knelt and drew a sigil in the dust with a finger made of bird talon. Corviana. My mouth spoke the name before I could stop it; it didn’t feel like mine, it felt old, as if the name had been waiting for my tongue. I looked around. This wasn’t just a battlefield; it was a funeral, and I had stumbled into its heart, an intruder touched by an undeniable echo of its long-dead queen. Not a mask. Not a coincidence. Something deeper. Like a memory choosing a new mouth.

[Try not to make eye contact with destiny, dear. It gets clingy.]

The black feather settled into my palm... The black feather settled into my palm like a falling verdict, weightless and heavy all at once. It pulsed with warmth that was neither heat nor fire; it was hunger, my hunger. I didn’t mean to close my hand around it, but my fingers moved anyway. The moment I did, the world buckled. My knees hit the ground, but it was obsidian under me anymore, polished and cracked with age. A different sky arched above me, sickly violet and filled with what seemed at first to be birds, but were instead shadows wearing feathers. They wheeled in silence over a throne carved from bone and rotted banners.

I was different, taller, broader, clad in ragged silk stitched with runes I didn’t understand. Feathers clung to my shoulders. A face, unseen by me but intensely felt, settled over my own features. My mouth moved; my voice, when it came, was a declaration. I saw mortals at my feet, broken but not bowed. I felt love, a love that terrifies the other gods, a love that feeds instead of consumes. And then, gold, blinding gold. Aurentum stepped from the sky itself, eyes gleaming like molten betrayal. He raised a hand and the heavens answered, lightning dancing along a gilded blade. I raised my arms to stop him, to reason, to remind him that we had shared victories, but his smile was already cutting deeper than any weapon could.

He had allies; I recognized them all, every god that had ever shared a drink at my table. They stood at his back, pretending they were preserving balance, when all they wanted was the power I refused to hoard. My people screamed as the storm fell. Their wings burned. Their hunger began that day and never ended. I fell. No. The memory fell, and I fell with it.

I gasped and staggered back into myself, chest heaving. The feather in my palm had fused to my skin. I could feel it now, not just on the surface but inside, rooted. The Boon wasn’t just responding to this place; it belonged here, and some part of me, the part that had just fallen from grace, did too. The soldiers were on their knees again.

One by one, they looked up, no longer hollow-eyed ghosts but people, faces alive with purpose and fury. Behind them, a banner lifted into the air. It unfurled with a scream I felt in my teeth, revealing a black raven on a crimson field. The frontmost soldier touched his heart and whispered,

“For the Queen’s Daughter… the war begins anew.”

My mouth tasted like ash and iron. I didn’t correct him because, the worst part, I wasn’t sure he was wrong. The feather pulsed in my hand, its sensation worse than heat, as if it breathed. It felt like every beat of my heart triggered a faint, sickening twitch in the dark barbules, a reminder that this thing wasn’t truly dead, or perhaps it was, and I was what it needed to finish the process. I tried to let go, but my fingers remained locked.

A cold thread of amusement, undeniably Malice, unspooled in my head:

[Always drawn to the artifacts with the deepest shadows, aren’t we? Such an appetite for ruin.]

“Quiet,” I rasped, my voice sounding like old paper tearing.

The battlefield around me was still, frozen in that moment of final betrayal, yet the sky above had transformed. The clouds were now wings, hundreds, thousands, vast, dark pinions, unmoving but utterly intent, watching.

[You see that too, right?] Malice’s thought was sharper now, less mocking, more attentive. [Those aren’t clouds. Those are feathers.]

I shook my head, trying to clear it. This felt resonant, inevitable, far from any illusion, spell, or hunger-induced hallucination. The feather pulsed again, harder, and this time the ground responded, dust kicking up like a sudden exhalation from lungs long collapsed beneath my boots. I staggered as something behind my eyes flexed, tightened, then burned.

My vision tunneled, the edges blurring into that bruise-purple sky. I clenched my hand around the feather, my traitorous fingers finally, horribly, obeying a will that felt both mine and not, and pain lanced through my palm, less like a cut and more like an injection, as if liquid darkness was being forced straight into my nerves. I didn’t scream; I couldn’t, my throat had seized.

My knees buckled, hitting the ash-covered earth, and still I couldn’t release my grip. The feather wasn’t just in my hand; it was writing something into me, something deeper than words: ownership.

[Ah, yes,] Malice purred, a dark satisfaction in the thought. [The feast of memory. But be careful what you devour, little host. Some things have a tendency to bite back, or to merge entirely.]

I swallowed, the act raw and difficult. The hunger wasn’t gnawing at my stomach anymore; it was different now, a cold, dense coil settling within the flesh of my hand, spreading up my arm. I knew this story, though I felt I shouldn’t, but the taste of ash and iron on my tongue was a testament. My hand throbbed with something not alive, not dead, just waiting.

And I couldn’t let go. A final, shattering understanding bloomed, cold and vast as the bruise-purple sky. The face they saw, the echo I carried, the name that had waited for my tongue—it wasn't just a resemblance. Corviana was my dead mother. My mother was here in Penance, and somehow a god.
