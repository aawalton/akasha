The throwing knife split the air with a whisper, embedding itself dead center in the wooden target forty paces away. Five identical blades formed a perfect circle around it, each positioned with surgical precision. Orro exhaled slowly, his breath forming wispy clouds in the predawn chill. This was usually the part where his mind quieted—where the constant analysis of threats, angles, and vulnerabilities faded into the background.

Tonight, the silence wouldn’t come.

He retrieved the knives in one fluid motion, body moving through the space like liquid shadow. The training yard on top of Jameson’s estate was deserted at this hour—a blessing, given the foul mood that would probably get him locked up in Honor’s foulest dungeons if anyone tried to interrupt him right now. He’d abandoned sleep three hours ago, driven from his bed by dreams filled with toxic mist and a woman staked to the chair by his orange blade.

Orro launched into his Kata—the deadly dance his long-dead mentor had beaten into his muscles until they remembered what his mind might forget. Each movement flowed into the next: a backflip transitioning seamlessly into a spinning kick, landing in perfect silence before exploding into a series of strikes that would have severed arteries in a dozen different targets had they been real. His body twisted in impossible angles, knives appearing and disappearing from hidden sheaths as he carved death into the air itself.

Still, his thoughts remained a tangled mess.

The Blightmarshes. The Crown. Bernard’s crew. Arbiters. A hallucinogenic mist that would apparently melt his lungs.

And beneath it all, like a wound that refused to heal, the real culprit behind his insomnia: Rayka’s face. Not the perfectly framed face with tiny constellations of freckles. No, the bloodied visage of his best friend’s sister when he’d broken free of George’s enthrallment, the moment he’d revealed too much of what he’d kept buried for years. Even with the powerful enthrallment controlling his body and mind, he’d been aware enough to see the moment she’d accepted her death. An execution wrought by his hands, his blade.

He would never forgive himself for making her look at him like that.

The Grassblade assassin launched himself onto a wooden post barely wider than his foot. The small beam was anchored on the very edge of this open-aired rooftop. The drop to the ground would’ve killed anyone besides perhaps a god, but that didn’t matter to Orro. He’d long grown accustomed to such heights.

Balancing perfectly on his right leg, he lowered himself into a difficult squat as he blindfolded himself. Then, drawing four blades simultaneously, he began a complex series of flips between posts set at varying heights. With each landing, he released a knife. Four dull thuds in rapid succession announced their perfect placement in targets scattered throughout the yard.

Removing the blindfold, Orro scowled. Usually, this exercise centered him, but tonight his technique, while flawless, felt hollow.

“Damn it all to the four hells,” he muttered to himself, retrieving the blades once more.

Nothing was working.

He needed to be calm.

This time, he added complexity—small ceramic discs tossed into the air that needed to be shattered before they hit the ground. He moved like a demon given physical form, his body defying gravity as he twisted between strikes. Eleven discs exploded into dust.

The twelfth clattered to the ground intact.

Orro stopped, staring at the unbroken disc. He growled, low and menacing, at the persevering target, his twelfth and final knife sheathed several inches into the hardwood floor less than an inch from the disc.

So much for training to get calm.

“I’ve never actually seen a man try to murder the air before,” a voice observed quietly from the shadows. “The air appears to be losing.”

Something in his chest tightened, then released. He didn’t turn, not trusting what might show on his face. His unmasked face. He half-heartedly cursed his best friend under his breath for the hundredth time that day. If Orro ever returned to his homeland, he was not looking forward to explaining to his mother and father how their heirloom was destroyed.

The assassin knelt down and procured the persevering disc. The cold ceramic exterior fit perfectly inside of his gloved palm.

“You should be sleeping,” he said, voice carefully neutral as he collected the rest of his projectiles.

“Says the man performing aerial assassin ballet at four in the morning.” Rayka stepped into the dim light cast by the yard’s single lantern. She’d dressed for training—lightweight pants and a close-fitting top that wouldn’t restrict movement. Her hair was pulled back, revealing the clean lines of her face.

His chest tightened further.

Gods, she was perfect.

“This is how I sleep,” he replied, tossing a new ceramic disc lightly in his palm.

Rayka raised an eyebrow. “Badly?”

A corner of his mouth twitched. “Efficiently.”

For a moment, neither spoke. The silence stretched between them, filled with all sorts of unspoken words. Orro would’ve preferred to keep it that way. The kind they’d been avoiding since George, since the enthrallment, since his moment of weakness when he’d revealed the truth he’d guarded for years.

“You’re thinking too much,” Rayka finally said, moving to the weapons rack and selecting a slender practice sword. “I can see it in your shoulders.”

“My shoulders are that talkative, are they?” he countered, though the barb lacked its usual edge.

“Makes one of you,” Rayka shot back just as quickly.

Her nose scrunched when she saw him flinch ever so slightly. The blonde thief bit her lower lip in a rare sign of nerves. Orro just added it to the long list of impossibly perfect things she did.

She tapped the practice sword against her leg. “Want a partner? Since we’re both making such efficient use of sleep?”

Orro hesitated, then nodded once, replacing his knives with wooden training versions. No sense risking real blades—not with his concentration so fractured.

“First blood?” he asked, falling into his starting stance.

Rayka smiled, the expression transforming her face in ways that threatened his carefully maintained composure. “First pin. I’d like to reach the Blightmarshes with all my limbs attached, thank you very much.”

Then she attacked and thought became secondary to movement.

Rayka moved with an effortless fluidity that few outside their crew ever witnessed—her public persona deliberately understated to hide the lethal training she had insisted on. She feinted left, her practice sword whistling through the space where his head had been a fraction of a second earlier. Orro responded with calculated precision, each movement economical, nothing wasted.

Unlike his solitary drills, this dance had a partner—and it changed everything.

Their weapons clacked together in a rhythm that accelerated like a heartbeat. He read her intentions in the slight shift of her weight, the minute telegraphing in her eyes. She, in turn, anticipated his counters, twisting away from strikes that should have connected. They moved across the training yard like paired shadows, neither gaining a decisive advantage.

“You’re improving,” Orro observed, ducking beneath a swing that would have caught anyone else across the jugular.

Rayka spun, using momentum to power her next strike. “Someone has to keep up with you.”

He parried her blow, the impact traveling up his arm. “And you volunteered for this service?”

“It’s a sacrifice I’m willing to make.” A smile flickered across her face as she narrowly avoided his counter-attack. “You’re not too shabby yourself, Orr.”

“But?”

“But you’re fighting like you’re alone.” She slipped past his guard, tapping his ribs lightly with her practice blade. “Point.”

Something in her words struck deeper than the training sword. Anger disguised the grief he felt building in his chest.

Fighting like he was alone.

Isn’t that how he survived all these years? What had kept him alive through years of contracts, betrayals, and narrow escapes? His gods-damned exile?

They reset, circling each other with renewed focus. This time, Orro attacked first—a complex series of strikes that drove Rayka backward across the yard. She parried each blow, her movements adapting to his increased intensity. They were perfectly matched, her intuitive fluidity complementing his technical precision.

Sweat beaded on his forehead despite the cool air. He was pushing harder now, his movements sharper, faster. She matched him beat for beat, her breath coming in controlled bursts that matched his own. Orro felt a strange energy build between them with each strike, each breath that perpetuated this deadly duet.

“You’re still holding back,” she said during a momentary lull, their weapons crossed between them, faces inches apart. A single strand of her blonde hair fell from the tight bun holding it in place.

“So are you,” he countered.

The intensity in her eyes shifted, becoming something else entirely. “Am I?”

With a smooth upward push of her arms and an infinitesimal shift of her hips, she broke their stalemate. Her grin was wild and free.

How can you smile like that? Orro asked himself while he readied his sparring sword. After all that happened. All that you went through? How?

“I’m coming for you, Orro Yanson,” Rayka promised.

She lunged for him, using every one of her appendages in addition to her blade to strike at him.

Orro felt something inside him unravel—a tightly wound coil of restraint he’d maintained for years. Their next exchange transcended training. Each movement flowed into the next until it approached something akin to intimate. Sweat spilled down his neck and across his chest, and a grim part of him was satisfied to know the same was happening to her. Good. If he had to work in this sparring session, then so should he.

Her back pressed briefly against his chest as she spun away from a hold. His hand closed gently around her wrist before releasing it in the same movement. Their breathing synchronized.

In that brief moment, he wasn’t fighting alone anymore.

Their movements slowed, the combat evolving into something more deliberate. Each touch lingered a heartbeat longer than necessary. His palm brushed against her shoulder. Her fingers grazed his forearm. The practice weapons became secondary, almost forgotten as they moved together in the dim light. The city below them was a series of silhouettes that made the perfect backdrop to their near-silent dance.

Rayka’s eyes never left his, even as she ducked beneath his arm and came up behind him.

“Orro. I don’t blame you,” she said softly, breaking the silence. “I knew that wasn’t you. But hells, Orr, you sure know how to pick your moments, don’t you? Wanna say it again?” Rayka moved in closer, the space between barely enough to slip a dagger through. “That you love me?”

The words hung in the air between them. The first direct reference to what had happened with George—to the moment when he’d shattered an unbreakable magical compulsion through sheer force of will. For her. Because of her.

Orro’s rhythm faltered. In that moment of distraction, Rayka swept his legs from under him. He controlled his fall, but she followed him down, pinning him with her forearm across his chest, practice sword at his throat. Their faces were inches apart, breath mingling in the cool air.

“First pin,” she whispered.

Neither moved. The weight of her against him, the scent of her skin—familiar yet intoxicating—flooded his senses. His usual defenses felt paper-thin.

“You cheated,” he said finally, voice rough.

A smile played at the corner of her mouth. “I used what was available.”

“My moment of weakness?”

Her expression softened. “Is that what you think it was?”

Orro could have reversed their positions in an instant—they both knew it. Instead, he remained still, allowing her the victory, allowing himself this rare vulnerability.

“I said things,” he began, the words coming reluctantly. “In that moment, when—”

“When you saved me,” she finished. “When you broke free because of how you feel.”

His gaze dropped to her lips, then back to her eyes. “I—I hate words.”

“I’ve noticed.” Her smile widened, though something trembled beneath it. “Actions speak louder anyway.”

Her forearm eased its pressure against his chest, becoming a touch rather than a pin. His hand moved of its own accord, fingers brushing a strand of hair from her face. The entire world held its breath. Even the dawn seemed to withhold its first rays for Orro’s next move.

“Rayka, I—”

“Well, this is awkward,” Jer’s cheerful voice rang out across the training yard. “Should I come back after you two have finished... wrestling?”

Rayka closed her eyes briefly, frustration flashing across her features before she smoothly rolled away from Orro, rising to her feet in one fluid motion. “Your timing is impeccable as always, Jer.”

Orro stood more slowly, his expression returning to its customary inscrutability. But inside, something had shifted—a door opened that couldn’t be closed again.

Jer grinned, completely unrepentant as he balanced on the yard’s fence. “Just following orders. Cade says we need to move before sunrise. Apparently, we’re going to break even more laws than originally planned.” He stretched lazily while he balanced an edge dozens of stories in the air. “Your brother had my twin ‘borrowing’ some Lifekeeper uniforms to help us get past security. He’s been walking around the past hour practicing a persona he’s calling ‘Bazz the Insufferable.’ Gods help us all.”

“Thrilling,” Orro deadpanned, collecting his practice weapons.

“Don’t let me interrupt... whatever this was,” Jer continued, gesturing vaguely between them. “But if you’re not at the front gates in ten minutes, Cade threatened to let Bunny redecorate your packs.” He grimaced. “Last time he got into my things, I found half a wobblewump in my spare boots. I didn’t even know those furballs could come in halves.”

With that cheerful warning, he backflipped off the fence and vanished down the Clover’s balconied walls.

Left alone once more, the air between Rayka and Orro thickened into an unwieldy battleaxe of missed opportunities. She took a half-step toward him, then seemed to reconsider.

“We should...” she began.

“Yes,” he agreed, though neither had actually specified what “should” be done.

Rayka collected her practice sword, returning it to the rack with deliberate care. “Orro,” she said without turning, “whatever happens in the Blightmarshes—”

“I’ll be there,” he cut in, the simple promise containing multitudes.

She glanced over her shoulder, the look in her eyes making his chest tighten. “I know.” A pause, then softer: “That’s not what scares me about this job. Just… don’t die, okay?”

Before he could respond, she was gone, leaving him alone with the echo of her words and the phantom sensation of her weight against him.

Orro stared at the spot where she’d stood, then at the ceramic disc still clutched in his palm—the one failure among his perfect performance. With a sudden decision, he tossed it high into the air. His hand blurred, a knife appearing between his fingers.

The disc shattered into dust.

Sometimes things broke. Sometimes that was exactly what needed to happen.
