Too much struck Rick’s senses at once. After the silence of the outer cave and the terror of nearly drowning in the passage, the hidden chamber felt like another world. Light, scent, sound, shape—everything arrived together so suddenly that he stood still for several breaths, trying to force his mind into order.

The glow came first. At least two dozen crystals or stones shone from the walls in different colors, some pale and steady, others richer and deeper, all of them bright enough together to light the chamber in an eerie, uneven way. Once his eyes adjusted, Rick took in the rest.

The cave was larger than he had expected, and the ground ahead forked into two paths roughly ten meters away. The air still smelled of damp rock, but also of something sharper and more astringent beneath it, touched by a faint floral note that did not belong underground. From one of the forked paths came the unmistakable sound of falling water.

Rick tightened his grip on the spear, and took the stream path first. He moved carefully, following the sound and placing each step with far more caution than he was used to. It did not take long for the tunnel to end at a cliff.

A waterfall dropped through a hole in the ceiling and plunged into an underground river below. The trickle of water he had followed was only the smallest branch of it, spilling from the rock, striking a ledge, and then draining back into the narrow passage behind him. The wider cavern beneath the cliff stretched far beyond that.

Rick pressed himself back instinctively. Below the drop, a long, narrow valley had formed along the underground river, complete with vegetation and beasts.

He forgot to breathe and could only stare.

Then sense returned, and he retreated into the tunnel in case anything below had noticed movement above.

From his first glance, he judged the height at roughly five or six meters. Too high to jump without risk. Anyone wanting to descend properly would need to climb or use a rope. After a moment, Rick edged forward again and took a more careful look.

None of the beasts below appeared to have seen him. So he studied them. He could identify at least four kinds. Large tan felines with short fur moved along the riverbank with the ease of creatures that feared very little. Shorter dark brown wild dogs or wolves hid in several patches of greenery farther from the water. A heavily plated armadillo, far too large to be ordinary, emerged from a smaller tunnel opening. And in a few places along the walls, giant spiders clung with stillness so perfect they almost vanished unless he looked directly at them.

A whole ecosystem, Rick thought. Of the beasts below, he only truly recognized one. The armadillo was a yellow-hide, fully grown by the look of it. That made it at least bronze-tier. Since it did not seem to dominate the others, and nothing around it behaved like easy prey, the remaining beasts were likely bronze-tier as well, perhaps stronger.

He could only see perhaps three kilometers of the river valley before it bent behind stone and vanished from sight. Even so, Rick would have wagered that what lay before him was only a fraction of the greater cave system.

The thought made his pulse jump. Danger, most certainly. But also opportunity. Enough of both to change lives.

He withdrew from the cliff and returned to the fork. The second path went the opposite direction from the waterfall. Rick followed it with the same careful pace, reminding himself again and again that anything hidden in such a place could kill him before he even understood what it was. Moving alone had one great advantage—he could keep almost silent, which reduced the chance of being ambushed.

The path sloped downward for roughly a kilometer. Then it narrowed. At first Rick only had to bend. Then he had to squat. He decided he would go no farther once the tunnel became too small to move through safely while crouched. That had been a sensible decision when he made it.

Unfortunately, by the time he reached that point, he could already see the end of the tunnel a short distance ahead. That made sense much harder to obey.

So he lowered himself and crawled forward with the spear ready, moving only a little farther until he reached the opening. Then he froze.

The second tunnel also ended on a cliff, but what lay below was nothing like the river valley.

A giant hole opened beneath him, riddled with hundreds of smaller tunnels. The place swarmed with rat-like creatures moving in every direction. The sight would have unsettled anyone. To Rick, who had spent enough time hunting to understand what nests meant, it was immediately horrifying.

Yet what truly shocked him was what they were doing. Some carried objects into the tunnels. Others gathered water from a pool below. One passage glowed with fire, and at first Rick thought it must be some trick of the crystals or fungus or reflected light. Then he realized the truth.

They were cooking. The idea was so absurd that for a moment it nearly pushed fear aside.

But the moment did not last. One of the creatures screamed. It had seen him.

The sound snapped the whole nest into motion. The thing pointed up at him and made a series of rapid clacking noises. That meant something to the others, because every head turned his way.

Rick did not think further. He crawled backward as fast as he could, cursing his luck and his own stupidity with equal intensity. His heart hammered so hard he could barely hear, yet even through it came the sound of the nest erupting behind him.

He reached the broader stretch without injury, but the pursuit was closer than he liked.

Then the running skill saved him. The moment he gained enough space to rise, his body shifted into what the book had taught it. Breathing, stride, balance, conservation—everything aligned. He moved at nearly twice the speed he once could have managed in such ground.

It was still not enough. The shrieks behind him grew louder, not softer. More voices joined. Stones and sharpened wooden sticks began to rain through the tunnel. Most missed or struck only glancing blows, but a few hit hard enough to bruise and cut. The weapons carried no more force than a child’s arm might have managed, yet there were enough of them to matter.

The fork appeared ahead. Rick saw both options approaching. The water passage, with its narrow squeeze and near-drowning certainty. The river valley, with stronger beasts and room to be surrounded.

He barely had time to choose. So he chose the one he already understood.

Rick sprinted and threw himself into the water passage without sparing a thought for his skin, bones, or dignity. The dive bought him a few precious meters and, just as importantly, stopped the hail of missiles. The price came immediately. His body slammed hard against rock and water. Fresh tears opened along already damaged skin. But he did not stop. He kicked and shoved with everything he had.

Miraculously, he kept hold of the spear.

His legs and ankles felt scratching and grasping behind him—small hands, claws, something trying to seize him before he disappeared fully into the constriction. Rick kicked backward as hard as he could, partly to break free and partly because the resistance somehow helped propel him forward faster. Then his body slipped fully into the narrow section, and the attacks ceased.

Either the creatures hated the water or they were unwilling to follow him into it.

Rick did not care which. His next problem was air.

The struggle lasted only a few moments, though it felt longer. This time fear worked in his favor. The desperate dive and the pursuit behind him had driven him forward faster than before. The wider section came sooner than expected, and when Rick finally broke the surface and staggered upright, he was too wounded and exhausted even to laugh.

He leaned against the rock, breathing hard.

Then, despite the cuts, the bruises, the aching cold in his bones, and the simple shock of what he had just survived, a smile forced its way onto his face.

He was alive. More than that, he had succeeded. He had found a way through the mountain.

The passage would need planning, and perhaps help. The place beyond was far too large and dangerous to enter carelessly. But now he knew it existed, and that alone was worth the pain.

Far from the forbidden forest, a flower-carved carriage rolled toward Mountainend.

Inside sat a mother, a daughter, and a third passenger who would bring more than conversation to the town. Winter stood just around the corner, but cold would not be the only thing reaching The Bookstore soon.
