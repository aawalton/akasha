“I don’t like it,” Esme said. “You can’t even see the ears due to your hair.”

I stood in front of a full-length mirror in our bedroom, turning my head from side to side as I worked on my disguise. Esme lay on her side, a thin blanket concealing, yet clearly showing off, her curves. Her chin was propped on one hand as she glared at me, preening in front of the mirror. Lust was sated, for now, and Vanity was once again reigning supreme in my mind.

“Mordechai had some special scissors on order, but with my excommunication from the empire, some assholes in the city have cut ties.” I swept my unruly hair back behind my newly pointy ears and nodded.

My face was slightly different. All the elves I’d met, which admittedly came to a grand total of two, had long, thin faces. Perfect for sneering. So I’d moved my chin down a bit, made my nose slightly more beak-like, and brought up the cheekbones slightly.

Shuffling my facial features around like I was a Mr Potato Head, or more accurately, like my body was made of playdough, had been an odd experience. I’d used the shapeshifting thing to become an orlic, a dwarf, and… my old self. Sure, I’d enhanced a few things, mostly downstairs, and smoothed out the rough edges of my face from Earth, but beyond that, I hadn’t screwed around with my appearance.

“You’re not you anymore,” Esme said. “I don’t know, it’s kind of frightening to see you go from ‘Bob’, the man I love, to something else.”

“I’m not a mammal. I think I’ve been missing a trick, though,” I replied absently, adjusting the arch of my cheeks to make them as elf-y as possible.

“I can think of several things you miss,” she giggled.

I scowled, then shifted to a grin. “Ok, maybe I am a man. Do you think I’m doing the right thing?”

“It’s not like you to second-guess yourself, love. You need to stop this Changer, whatever the hell it is.”

“Apparently.” My nose shifted, the tip shortening a little. “No idea who the bastard is. Something like Dave, maybe?”

She shifted, lying back and putting her hands behind her head, completely derailing my chain of thought. “Would the WOO be playing both ends of the game? That doesn’t make sense. Why send Kat to help you and send the thing you need to stop?”

“Gods are di—very nice people.” I hadn’t been talking about IMPS, but I got the feeling that shit-talking the gods was only slightly less stupid than slagging off the power that controlled the multiverse.

“Do you want to see it?” she asked suddenly.

“When have I ever said no to that?”

“Don’t answer a question with a question! And I wasn’t talking about my butt. The dress.”

“Sure.”

She flew across the room, giving me a flash of flesh anyway, and disappeared into her wardrobe. I turned back to the mirror as she rummaged around.

My eyes always ended up slightly purple. The elves I’d met had green or blue ones. I could force the change, but they always faded back to a purpley-blue whenever I stopped focusing on them. All the other changes seemed to set and stay once I was happy with them, and my eyes were starting to frustrate me.

“What do you think?”

I turned to look, and my jaw dropped. Shimmering silver silk clung to her frame, flowing down into a short train that flicked out when she spun. It was backless, and her body had complete contempt for gravity, judging by her bust. Lust perked up and started fighting Vanity for domination of my mind.

“Well?” She crossed her arms and tapped a bare foot impatiently.

“It’s lovely.”

“And this is the only time you’ll see it, I’m afraid. It was reserved for the premiere.”

“We can still do a premiere. The Mill isn’t as big as Ankmapak by a long shot, but it’s plenty big enough.” I scratched my hairless chin. Elves were all stubble-free.

“I think you should. And not just so I can wear this in public. We need to be seen as a centre of culture and technology. Technology that isn’t just mutilated monsters crammed into Tim’s machinery.” She shuddered, which set the dress to shimmering due to how it caught the light as it moved.

“We need the bunnyborgs, love. We’ve talked about this.”

“Doesn’t mean I have to like it.”

Someone knocked on the door three times, a staccato rhythm with a slight break before the third knock.

“What is, Bottleburner?” I called.

“You’ve got a visitor, sah.”

“Who is it?”

“Mr Philpott, sah.”

Esme grabbed a robe on her way back into her dressing room, shooting me a smile as she went.

“Send him in. And keep One-Hand outside. No ninjas in the bedroom!”

“He knows, sah.” The door opened, and the soldier stood to one side, giving the merchant-turned-priest a sidelong look as he swept in. “It might be for the best, though, sah.”

“He’ll be fine with me. We’re old friends. I was his first major trading partner.” Philpott was sensibly dressed this time; no kimono-flashing would be possible in what looked remarkably like business wear from back on Earth.

“What’s up, Philpott?” The door swung shut, Bottleburner still scowling at the shapeshifted insect.

“I heard Esme’s voice?” he asked, looking around for her. “Nice room. Hey, nice face, that’s certainly different.”

“I’m just changing. I’ll be out in a minute,” she called.

He walked over to a sofa set against the wall beneath a tapestry that showed a dragon, not me, burning down a village. “The hanging is a bit on the nose?”

“I don’t get many visitors in my private rooms. And Esme likes it.”

He glanced at the other pictures, which were uniformly of rural scenes, meadows in spring, common people at work in fields with horse-drawn ploughs moving in lanes, and one that was a portrait of Morris, resplendent and looking smug, that hung above her dressing table.

“Yeah, I can see the theme. Denarios has an offer.”

“He can pop by whenever he wants. Why is a god sending you? The basta—gods aren’t shy about popping by to interrupt whatever I’m doing.”

“He has…competition. You’ve heard of the House of Lucratis?”

“Erm…” I scratched my head.

“That was Angtirm’s outfit. You kicked them out after the siege,” Esme called. “Remember, he stole your restaurant idea?”

“Oh, that bastard.” Wrath resumed his habitual place in my mind whenever thieves were mentioned.

“I, well, Denarios, have interests in the city that are being adversely affected by those bastards. We—he is looking for an influx of capital to stabilise things.” He produced a bunch of grapes from somewhere and offered me one. I shook my head, and he plucked one to pop in his mouth and chew.

“Nyal? What's the deal with this?”

“You shi—yes, I’ll tell him the rest! I just need to build up to it!”

A rip in reality opened to the side of his head, and an eyeball on a stalk, surrounded by writhing tentacles that thankfully blocked most of the mind-bending hellscape behind it, protruded into the mortal plane.

“He isn’t acting against your interests, Bob.” Her voice was, as ever, like nails down a chalkboard in a room full of donkeys being chainsawed in half.

“But there’s something more to it?”

“She’s never tried to rip me off, and has repeatedly forced you to tell the truth.”

“Well, other than that?”

“Is that not enough? Philly, you’ll be ironing in your dreams if you screw this up,” Nyal said sweetly. “It plays into your own interests as well, Bob.”
