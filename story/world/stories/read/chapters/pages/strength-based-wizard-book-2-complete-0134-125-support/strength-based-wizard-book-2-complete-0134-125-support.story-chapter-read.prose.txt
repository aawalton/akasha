Chapter 125

Support

I’m in Labonte’s office at Harvest HQ. The short, pink-skinned bald man who is now technically my boss sits across from me behind his glossy desk, fingers steepled beneath his chin. His expression is calm, though his eyes are bright and he’s clearly thinking of what to say as soon as I’m done speaking. Jerome sits in an armchair off to the side, one ankle crossed over his knee, jacket unbuttoned, posture immaculate. The Vice Captain and General Counsel of Harvest Guild listens quietly, his face giving away nothing.

I finish explaining my decision to opt-in to the next Phase of the God Game.

It takes a while. I start way, way back. Long before the System arrived, but end in the same place. With my Dad. I don’t cry this time, which feels like progress until I realize I’m gripping the arms of my chair hard enough that the wood is creaking.

Labonte doesn’t interrupt.

When I’m done, the room practically hums with silence. I think it’s just the blood pulsating in my eardrums.

Then Labonte exhales through his nose.

“Joe,” he says, “you put Harvest Guild on the map. And I mean on the map!”

I brace for the arguments against opting in.

“We’re big news now after these last two stunts you pulled off,” he continues. “Public Square and Lake Erie? Back-to-back? You alone generated more revenue for the Guild than our best-case scenario plans had us netting in the first five years! As it currently stands, with your stake you could retire today and be a happy man.”

“Yeah,” I say. “I know you’d rather have me opting out and focusing on the Guild. I’m sure not opting out may even be a breach of my contract in some way. But…”

I lean forward, ready to launch into the argument I rehearsed on the elevator ride up. It’s a good argument. Mostly. It centers on me still being a public-facing figure for Harvest while remaining in the Game, and the fact that I’ve already made my contract a massive windfall for him and his Guild, and the very reasonable point that a Phase Two participant backed by Harvest Guild could bring even more attention, influence, and money. Okay, it’s not a perfect argument, now that I think about it.

“I can still—”

“Eh eh eh!”

Labonte lifts one small pink hand and waggles a finger. The sound slices through my prepared speech like a cartoon guillotine.

“First thing first, kiddo,” he says. He glances at Jerome. “You’re going to want to cover your ears.”

Jerome’s lips quirk into a half-smile.

Labonte continues: “Fuck what the contract may or may not say! Let’s put all the business to the side for a second.”

I blink. This is not where I had expected the conversation to go. I’m not prepared for what comes next.

He leans forward, steepled fingers lowering to the desk. “Are you doing alright?”

The question lands wrong.

I stare at him. I feel numb. Sad. Still so, so sad. And scared. But I needed to numb those sensations to do what I needed to.

“Seriously, Joe. If you need time with your family,” Labonte says, voice softer now, “you should be taking it. You’re part of the Harvest Family now. While there are times to be transactional, now is not one of those times.”

I do not know what my face is doing, but it’s probably stupid.

“All expenses for your dad’s care will be footed by the Guild,” he continues. “And I’ll personally ensure he’s getting the best care. Specialists. Magical consultants. Out-of-state. Out-of-country, if needed. Whatever gives him the strongest chance and keeps him most comfortable in the worst case scenarios.”

My throat closes. Labonte’s expression doesn’t shift into his usual gambler’s grin. He doesn’t wink. He doesn’t turn it into a pitch for my obedience to the Guild.

“Whether that changes your decision or not,” he says, “it’s the least we can do.”

I don’t know how to answer. I came ready for a battle. I did not come prepared for kindness.

“I…” I stammer. “Er… Thank you. Seriously. That’s—I don’t know what to say.”

“Say you’ll take it,” Labonte says. “That you’ll accept our assistance.”

“I’ll take it,” I say quickly. “Obviously. For him. Thank you.”

Jerome’s gaze flicks toward me, then back to Labonte.

“But,” I add, because the word is sitting there like a nail in my mouth, “it doesn’t change my decision.”

Labonte’s brows rise.

“There’s no guarantee any treatment works,” I say. “Even with magic. We’ve already tried. The doctors don’t know what to do with it. It has something to do with the Gates.”

My hands curl into fists in my lap.

“The Game… What’s waiting at the end of it… might be the only thing big enough.”

Labonte studies me. Then he sighs, and for once it doesn’t sound theatrical. Something deep within him has truly resigned itself to my decision.

“Joe,” he says, “your decision doesn’t have any guarantees either.”

“I know.”

“No,” he says. “You know it like a man reading fine print. Not like a man who has internalized the possibility that fine print may actually play out. Take it from someone who’s been alive long enough to see that fine print carried to its logical conclusion.”

I look away.

He keeps going.

“You opt in, and you may die. Culled. Gone. And for what? A chance at a prize guarded by gods, monsters, and every ambitious maniac on the planet who is opting in for their own reasons.”

“If it could save my dad, I’m willing to take the risk.”

“It could save him. It could,” Labonte says. “It could also not. Or you could never get near the end of this mysterious Game. Or someone else wins. Or you win and learn that what you are fighting for isn’t what it’s cracked up to be. Or you die in the first Gate chasing a miracle your father never asked you to chase.”

The words hit like clean punches. I hate that he’s not wrong.

“You should focus on the time you do have with your dad,” he says. He swivels in his chair, looking out the floor-to-ceiling window behind him. “Do you know how I got to where I am today? A lot of sacrifice. The biggest of which was time. Time with loved ones. Time I could have been with my family. And now I’m alone. You should weigh your options, kid.”

The room goes sharp around me.

“I am,” I say.

Labonte tilts his head.

“I am,” I repeat, louder. “That’s what this is.”

“No,” he says gently. “I don’t think so… But I don’t think what I say matters much to you anyway.”

I open my mouth. No words come. So I close it.

The silence stretches.

I can feel my pulse in my neck. I can hear the faint hum of the wall display. I can see Jerome sitting there, still quiet, still unreadable.

“I’m not saying this because I don’t want you in the Game,” Labonte says. “Business-wise, a Harvest champion in Phase Two would set our Guild up for generations.”

“There it is,” I mutter.

He spreads his hands. “I contain multitudes. I would be lying if either decision you made couldn’t be taken as a net positive, when viewed in the right light.”

“You contain balance sheets,” I mutter.

“Also true.” His expression sobers again. “But like I said, there are more important things than business. One being family. I am saying all this because you are not thinking like a Guild asset right now. You are thinking like a son.”

“Good,” I say.

“Yes,” Labonte says. “Good. But sons make very bad risk calculations.”

I know he’s right. But my mind is made up, and I don’t care.  I rub my face.

“I can’t just do nothing.”

“No one is asking you to do nothing.”

“It feels like it.”

“Feelings are terrible analysts,” Labonte says.

I almost laugh. Almost.

Jerome shifts in his chair. It’s small. And if it wasn’t for my [Perception], I wouldn’t notice. But I do. And so does Labonte.

“Jerome?” Labonte asks. “You’ve got anything to add to this matter?”

Jerome uncrosses his ankle and leans forward, resting his elbows lightly on the arms of the chair.

“I support Joseph’s decision,” he says.

Labonte’s eyebrows rise to quickly, they practically crawl off his head.

I turn toward Jerome.

He looks at me, then back to Labonte.

“Not because the risk is small,” Jerome continues. “It is not. And not because I believe grief improves judgment. It does not.”

Jerome locks eyes with me. There’s something knowing in them. Something that understands me and what I’m going through.

“Phase Two will shape the global power structure regardless of whether Joseph participates. Many of the world’s most prominent Guilds are maintaining at least one dedicated team inside the Game. Pegasus, at minimum, is doing so in the United States.”

“Hm…” Labonte says. “That’s right.”

Jerome continues, voice level.

“If Harvest Guild opts entirely out of Phase Two, we become second tier to these other Guilds that can afford to take these risks and reap the rewards. Joseph is already our strongest public-facing asset and, more importantly, our strongest combatant. If he opts in without Guild support, we lose strategic influence and increase his mortality risk. If we support him fully, it’s a calculated risk.”

Despite what I had initially sensed from Jerome, his argument slams into me with the cold steel of logic. His support is more of a warm hug made of case law and political logistics.

I’ll take it, I guess.

Labonte drums his fingers once against the desk.

Jerome adds, “He will require a party. If he is going to do this, the safest version is one where he does it with structure.”

Labonte looks at me.

I hold his gaze.

My stomach twists.

Labonte sighs.

“Fine,” he says.

I blink. “Fine?”

“Don’t make me repeat myself. It diminishes the moment.” He points at me. “You have the Guild’s support in your continued participation in the Game. If you’ll compete in Phase 2, you won’t be doing it alone kid.”

Something in my chest loosens and tightens at the same time.

“Thank you,” I say.

Labonte waves it away like gratitude is a small bug.

“Jerome and I have both already opted out,” he says. “My talents are better applied elsewhere, and I wouldn’t let Jerome throw his life away even if he wanted to.”

Jerome inclines his head. “Unfortunately, that is true. Unlike the fight in Public Square, I won’t be joining you in this battle.”

Labonte turns back towards the window of his office.

“But you will have the ability to recruit whoever you want to join the Guild and form a party for the next Phase of the Game.”

For the first time since I sat down, I feel the edge of a real smile.

Labonte spins his chair around and leans back, eyes brightening again, the businessman returning now that the human moment has been properly filed and stamped.

“You’ll need a team,” he says. “A real one. Balanced. Loyal. Marketable, preferably.”

“Marketable?”

“Joe, like it or not the entire world will be watching your every move. We either control the narrative around your participation, or the narrative grows teeth and starts charging appearance fees. Appearance fees that take their balance in flesh. You follow?”

“Actually… no. I don’t.”

Jerome stands, smoothing his jacket. “We’ll need to move quickly. We can help recruit a few candidates, including within our existing ranks,” he says. “But Joseph should have final approval. Party trust will matter more than raw numbers.”

Labonte nods reluctantly. “Agreed.”

I look between them. Both opted out. Both are staying behind. Any yet both are still helping me step through this next Phase.

I’m opting into the next Phase of this Game, I think, affirming my decision. With the support of the Harvest Guild, success actually feels possible too.
