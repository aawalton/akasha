Ferris tentatively began reading the introduction to familiar bonds.

Usually, a core was taken from the corpse of a monster and embedded in a weapon or tool. When bonded, this became a unique and powerful item that would grow in power as a cultivator advanced.

However, the bond could technically be performed on any core, whether magically forged inside an item or not. Even a raw core provided random abilities, just without the focus it would have if part of something like a sword.

Then, what would happen if someone bonded a core still inside a living monster?

It makes a familiar, Ferris read, bonding the monster to you.

Monsters develop powerful abilities naturally, and bonding a core inside a living monster had amazing benefits. The monster would be able to fight alongside the delver, growing stronger with them. Familiars often provide abilities distinct to their species. Even better, they usually already had several innate abilities when they were bonded.

Having a familiar also helped increase the size of a delving team. Ferris still hadn’t learned how rift magic worked, but he knew it typically limited group sizes. A standard portal would close after five people entered the rift. This was why the trip escorting him from Bluereef had been so dangerous; Ferris took up a slot, then had to be guarded.

However, rift magic was said to be much more complicated than a simple ‘maximum number of people’ rule. Familiars were an example, as apparently, they didn’t count. The portal considered the cultivator and familiar a single person.

Numbers mattered heavily in a fight. It was often much better to have a friend to distract the enemy, rather than just a more powerful weapon.

For all their advantages, familiar bonds were not nearly as common as weapon bonds. Many abilities required physical contact with the weapon—or the familiar—to work. If the familiar was off fighting a monster, the delver could not activate the ability.

Even worse, all aspected abilities that relied on the core’s type required contact with the core itself. Forged items had special channels connecting to the embedded core, circumventing this restriction, but monsters had no such thing. Almost all monsters had their core inside their body, making it impossible to touch. This could be overcome at higher tiers or with special techniques, but this path was harder. It would be like bonding a water core, but then not be able to use any water aspected abilities.

Additionally, weapons forged by a skilled crafter were designed with several initial advantages built in. This included things like magical reinforcement, channels to flow energy through, and special techniques to speed up cultivation.

A weapon could be enchanted with a single ability before it was bonded. More than anything, this provided a solid head start to weapon bonds. Strategies on how best to synergize that starting ability were often the start of every martial tradition. Of course, you also needed to have an enchanter that could actually make the specific ability you wanted, which was even harder than developing it for yourself.

Bluereef’s spears were all enchanted with the Water Jet ability, which let their wielder swim faster by pushing water out the back of the spear. By itself, it may not be flashy, but it allowed a group of delvers to all share the same mobility and perform hit and run tactics.

As he read, Ferris felt captivated by the book. Secrets he had always wondered about, but no one would ever discuss were laid bare to him.

When Ferris meditated, he felt the presence of his soul to enter his soulspace. Monsters had something similar, but the book argued it wasn’t a ‘soul.’ The book called it a ‘spiritspace,’ which was similar to a cultivator’s soulspace, but missing some vital piece.

This spiritspace of a monster also developed as they grew stronger, but not with the focused construction of a crafter. Newborn monsters usually had a couple natural abilities, plus something the book called ‘spirit animals.’ It didn’t give any details on what they were, citing they were beyond the scope of the book, but placed great emphasis on them. Despite those advantages, the author argued that they were weaker than a single planned out ability of a weapon bond.

After the monster was bonded, their spiritspace gained whatever it was missing, and became a true soulspace. The book didn’t say how or why this happened, but it had at least one major consequence: the familiar gained sentience.

The clawling monsters Ferris had seen on his way to Scalefort had been dumb beasts, but many creatures in the rifts were smart. However, that intelligence was said to be… limited in strange ways.

A tribal monster may work together with its brethren, building sophisticated tools and traps. Ferris had even heard stories about monsters that could talk.

Yet, everyone insisted that monsters weren’t truly sentient. The smarter ones were simply acting out patterns for reasons no one knew. Many theories were given, but none had proof. At least, none that Ferris had heard. Perhaps a large clan like Hollowfield knew more.

Reading the book on familiars, Ferris doubted it. At least whomever had written it hadn’t known how a monster could talk and hunt in packs, but also lack any spark of personality.

The familiar, on the other hand, would be different. It happened slowly but inevitably, like a child learning how the world worked. They would one day be as real a person as Ferris himself.

This, more than anything else, might be why familiar bonds were less popular. The cultivator would then be mystically bonded to another mind, without knowing them beforehand. This was unsettling by itself, but it got worse. Progression in cultivation was deeply tied to personal insight and beliefs. Cultivators could—and often did—hit bottlenecks that might never be overcome. Bonding to another mind made everything more complicated.

Bonding a core, either in a weapon or familiar, brought many benefits. However, one disadvantage they all shared was that the cultivator was deeply tied to the core. If the core was destroyed, the cultivator would likely die. This made familiars an additional liability…

Weapons were forged and enchanted to be magically reinforced, which grew stronger with the wielder. If an enemy was powerful enough to break the weapon, they could usually just as easily kill the cultivator. Even if a weapon broke, it could be repaired if the core was intact. Living creatures couldn’t be ‘repaired’ as easily. If the familiar died there is no bringing them back. The cultivator might survive the death of a familiar if the core wasn’t broken, but they would suffer tremendous backlash and lose any abilities or techniques tied to the familiar.

Weapon-smiths went to great lengths to protect cores deeply within strong metals. Instead, a familiar was comparatively vulnerable, with standard weak points like the brain and heart. Combined with a typical tactic of sending the familiar out front, many had died from an unlucky hit.

Strangely, the book claimed cultivators would often bond familiars with the intent to hide behind them in battle, but then struggled to send their partner into harm’s way. It was apparently common enough there were suggestions on how to overcome it. Ferris didn’t see why, but the book made a big deal out of the problem.

Although less common, familiar bonds weren’t unheard of. It could be a desirable choice for someone without a martial tradition to follow, as the monster would give an instinctual path. This led to a stigma against cultivators with familiars, claiming they were too lazy to learn to fight with a weapon.

Finishing the introduction to familiars, Ferris wasn’t sure how to proceed. Instantly, he could see why Elder Harper would suggest a familiar bond. The fire healing ability, Revivifying Flame, was said to be reverse engineered from a phoenix. The book had even had a section explaining that abilities could be shared over the bond, although once again it was sparse on details. Her research project would likely seek to do the same for whatever monster he bonded.

He wasn’t sure how to write a report on familiars without knowing more. Walking up to Elder Harper, he handed her what he could. “I don’t believe the prosthetic bond is for me. My first choice is still the plant core.”

She nodded. “I expected as much, the only other option that remains is the familiar bond. I must warn you; Clan Hollowfield has a couple retainers with familiar bonds, but we don’t have much knowledge to help start you on the path. Most with a familiar bond have a family tradition to follow, or they seek help from a mentor.”

Ferris wasn’t sure about it. The natural abilities of a familiar would help compensate for not having a martial tradition, but the drawbacks…

“We have several journals detailing potential bonds,” Elder Harper continued, “are you willing to consider it?”

Was he? It would be like having another person in my head, for life, he thought. Ferris stood quietly for longer than was probably polite, but Elder Harper simply watched him.

“Yes,” he said. “I am willing to consider it.” He didn’t know if he would accept a familiar bond, but he had never even thought it was an option. As long as it helped him delve and return home, he didn’t mind the idea of a companion. The plant core was still more appealing, but he wanted to explore all options first. Especially if they didn’t make him wait years to delve.

Accepting his answer like she had known all along, she started handing him book after book. He ended up with six new volumes to study.

It ended up taking him three more days to study the options. Elder Harper no longer met him in the study room; apparently her research was finished. He was moved to a smaller room, but the attendants had the books ready for him each day. Though he was loath to admit it, he was a bit thankful this room was on the first floor. He fell into an easy pattern of research in the day, dinner with the residents of Lily House, and then meditation each night.

The ‘books’ on familiar bonds were more observation journals than anything else. Each one was a record of a single cultivator with a familiar, and their fighting style. Strangely, none were authored by the cultivators themselves. Instead, they were all written by someone else. The observers—or as Ferris slowly realized, spies—may or may not have even been from Hollowfield, as the clan may have acquired the journals from somewhere else. They were much less detailed about how abilities worked inside the soul—that was personal information not casually shared—but they did give logs of adventures and descriptions of abilities used to delve.

They were all fascinating. Half of the options Ferris ruled out based on their restricted ability to fight underwater: a worm with an earth core that could tunnel underground, a spider with a poison core, and a salamander with a fire core. Two options had water cores: a giant starfish that would grab and constrict enemies and a tiny creature called an axolotl that had ranged magical attacks. Ferris was particularly interested in the axolotl as most tier one monsters could not use complicated magic—usually they only enhanced a natural attack like claws or bites.

The most interesting option was one that Ferris initially scoffed at: a slime. Rifts commonly spawned slimes, but they were usually viewed as hazardous obstacles rather than opponents. Evolved versions could be dangerous, but despite their powerful acid, low tier slimes were looked down on for one reason: they were dumb. Most monsters had a vicious instinct to attack or defend themselves, but slimes would blindly roll around until killed—sometimes even ignoring delvers as they walked by.

The slime journal detailed a woman named Janice Landsworn. Along with an acid core, slimes had a natural ability to magically melt anything they could consume, and she had taken full advantage of this. She had found an ability to make her slime—regrettably named Gumly—much larger. Gumly would then roll around the battlefield until they swallowed an enemy, using powerful acid to digest it whole.

Whoever had written the journal must have known Janice well, as the abilities Gumly used were well recorded, along with Janice’s desires and plans. There was no mention of the author’s name… but it showed why many cultivators were so secretive. The author must have delved many rifts alongside Janice to know so much about her and Gumly. Likely they were friends, spending long hours discussing personal matters… only for the author to betray that trust by recording it all. Ferris wouldn’t want to put his name on it either. Despite the bitter feelings each new private detail invoked, he was irresistibly drawn to read deeper.

Slime bodies, as the journal emphasized, were very hard to hurt—overcoming a common weakness of familiars—since they had no vital organs beyond the core. Normal attacks would just slide through the slime’s gelatinous body, doing no damage. Magical attacks would hurt it, but the easiest way to kill a slime was to shatter or remove the core. Able to focus on a singular weakness, Janice spent a large amount of effort looking for abilities to protect the core, with moderate success. Thus, she had a very large, resilient monster that could attack while she stayed behind her teammates.

Even better, Janice was able to partially get around the classic familiar problem using aspected abilities. Usually, these abilities require touching the core, which is normally impossible for a core inside a familiar’s body. After she learned Acid Resistance, Janice simply stuck her hand inside Gumly and touched the core directly.

Unfortunately, Janice never made it past the first tier. She eventually died attempting to breakthrough to the second tier. It wasn’t uncommon to have deadly problems while cultivating, and advancing to a higher tier was the most dangerous time. Qi deviation, bottlenecks, backlashes, and more could all manifest as lethal problems in the soul.

None of the six journals had information on tier two. If the cultivator advanced, the book would just end at that point. One journal—the spider—had a similarly dark ending to Janice’s: simply a footnote at the end mentioning that the delver had died when the spider familiar was crushed.

Each journal included a description of a healing ability. Monsters were usually born with several ‘natural abilities,’ which Ferris knew were somehow different than the abilities offered by a core bond to a cultivator. For example, the phoenix monster probably had a natural ability to heal. Someone had used this to learn Revivifying Flame.

All six of the cultivators detailed in the journals had been observed with a healing ability which appeared to mimic their familiar. This was the part where things could get risky for Ferris, as he was reading secondhand accounts. None of the Adepts had directly said how they got their ability.

Monsters could also have different natural abilities, even within the same species. Just because most of a specific axolotl monster from a particular rift could heal, it didn’t guarantee the one Ferris bonded would have the ability. He wouldn’t be able to tell for sure without committing to the bond.

Although, that would likely be a temporary setback. The original book on familiars claimed that monsters could learn more natural abilities as they grew stronger. If Ferris’ familiar didn’t start with the ability, he could help them learn it.

The only real trap would be if the healing ability the delver from the journal had displayed wasn’t from their familiar at all. That was less likely, as each of the six types of monsters had records of healing without a familiar bond.

Interesting, all six were local to the rifts around Scalefort. Ferris wasn’t too surprised, as monsters were typically very specific to a location. Well, apparently basic slimes were the same everywhere, but the other five monsters each had details on how where they were found.

There was a section in each journal describing how the healing worked. While most of the monsters had what appeared to be enhanced natural healing, the slime’s ability reminded Ferris more of Revivifying Flame with its nature. When a slime’s body was cut, it would pull itself back together over about ten seconds.

The ability Janice Landsworn had gotten, which she called Acid Reconstruction, made her flesh act the same way; although much slower. Her wounds didn’t scab over and heal, but more… melt and reform. She had been observed losing a hand, which had taken a week to heal. She never explained how it worked, what requirements it had, or how long it took for her to develop it.

Elder Harper had left a separate note generic to all familiar bonds. The practice of deciphering a natural monster’s ability, and then creating a manual any cultivator could learn, was described as a common undertaking. It was not a quick process, and on average could take at least a year.

What really began drawing Ferris to the slime was not the healing ability. Nor was it the slime’s ability to fight in water: it could, but other options were comparable. No, what really caught his eye was a natural ability that Janice claimed all slimes were born with, called Adaptation.

She boasted it was passive ability that simply made it easier for the slime to gain abilities beneficial to its circumstances. Janice claimed it was the reason so many variations of slimes exist. Slimes that live around volcanoes would soon shoot fireballs. Slimes living underground could quickly adapt a rocky exterior. This inherent ability was not as flashy as some of the others—like the axolotl’s magic—but it did something vital for Ferris. With this ability, he thought, I could not only regrow my leg, but use it to achieve my other goals as well. Adaptation could, given enough time and effort, unlock almost any style. He was sure there must be some limits to how it worked, but Janice had been able to plan and then acquire several abilities like Acid Reconstruction—without needing a martial tradition. The ability would help mitigate the disadvantages of forging his own path.

Whichever option he picked, the familiar or the plant aspected core, he wouldn’t have a group of other cultivators helping to find synergies and new abilities to share. He could easily get stuck, unable to develop the types of abilities he needed.

Ferris did not feel drawn to Janice’s style of fighting—sending a huge slime out to roll over enemies—but that was the beauty of Adaptation.

Janice hadn’t shared everything, but she had mentioned Adaptation confidently while planning out her growth with her team. She'd wanted abilities to grow Gumly larger and protect the core, and then found them through Adaptation. I could develop my own style, he thought, getting excited, and be confident that I can find abilities to make it work. The plant core may regrow my leg, but I also need to think about what happens after.

He finished writing the reports on each of the six options for familiar bonds, but Ferris had already decided. The slime familiar bond was a strange choice, but it felt right to him, and that was important in cultivation. Beyond getting his leg back, the possibilities of Adaptation spoke to him.

Sharing his mind with a familiar made him a bit nervous, but it could also be appealing in its own way. He would have time to watch them grow and learn, evolving into a companion he could rely on.

It had been painful, leaving his family behind.

Wouldn’t it be kind of nice to have someone that stayed with him?
