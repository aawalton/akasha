Eola hated Mana Studies, and she especially hated it the next morning.

It had been weeks since Eola had rejoined the class, and Madame Reyanna still hadn’t forgiven her for leaving it the first time. “Today, we will be transitioning to a new subject. At the behest of one of my favorite fellow professors, we’ll be moving on from growing your souls’ capacity and focusing on streamlining the soul’s primary purpose in spellcasting. Who knows what that is?”

No one said anything. Eola kept her head down, just in case that might help.

“Miss Lemiene?”

“Y’aer's curse,” Eola mouthed silently. “The soul’s mostly used as a source of Mana—”

“Half correct. No points for being half correct, though. Not in my class.” Madame Reyanna cut her off, then started writing on the blackboard. “Mana is stored inside the soul, as Miss Lemiene so brilliantly stated. However, its main purpose within Ordered Magic is to store attuned Mana, then deploy it along Mana lines within the caster’s body and into a finished ideogram. What is a Mana line? Anyone?”

This time, Eola raised her hand. But instead of calling on her, the professor looked around, then pointed. “Miss Andresse?”

Catrine smiled smugly from across the room. “Mana lines are metaphysical connections between our souls and fingers, casting implements, and, ultimately, runes. They’re how Mana travels from soul to spell.”

“They’re only theoretical,” Eola said quietly, hand still raised.

“Well done, Miss Andrese. Miss Lemiene, wait your turn when called on.”

The blackboard filled in slowly as Madame Reyanna sketched a drawing of a person, labeling the critical body parts. “The soul, according to most reputable sources, rests just above a person’s stomach. It’s an organ just like any other, and—“

Eola had heard enough. She lowered her hand, tucked her head into her arms, and closed her eyes, trying her best to ignore the crimson-skinned professor. Her mom and dad had told her all about souls as a child. Then, after hers turned out to be attuned the wrong way, she’d done her own research on top of that. Madame Reyanna’s information wasn’t wrong, per se. It was just out of date.

Souls weren’t physical organs; they were metaphysical ones. No one had ever found an active Mana line in a body that wasn’t casting. Those lines only existed during casting, and even then, most mages only manifested them during their formative years. They were training wheels for a roller, and Eola’s parents had been convinced that they only made it harder to learn proper magic. Eola knew about them, obviously. They were just unnecessary, worthless, and a waste of time.

“All mages.” Madame Reyanna went on, “Have Mana lines. They’re essential to the casting process. Today, you and a partner will be identifying those Mana lines, then locating each other’s souls. Miss Andrese, Miss Lemiene, since you two were kind enough to contribute to my lecture, you will be my demonstration pair.”

Eola glared at Catrine. The noble girl smirked as she stood, robes swishing, and walked to the edge of the demonstration circle. Eola moved a little slower—she was sore from the fight against the feral book—and stood a healthy distance away.

Madame Reyanna nodded haughtily. “Miss Andrese will be the identifier. Miss Lemiene, please cast an ongoing spell of your choice on my command. Miss Andrese, you will cast the following rune. Unfortunately, Miss Lemiene won’t need to reciprocate—one demonstration should be enough, and she’s not to take part in any practical spell lessons, anyway.”

The professor continued as Eola turned red, and Catrine leaned over. “What happened to your face, toddler? Did you lose a fight with a cat?”

Eola narrowed her eyes. “No. I was in the library—“

“Those are paper cuts? You lost a fight with a book? That’s even more embarrassing!” Catrine grinned.

“Alright, enough, Miss Lemiene. Quit antagonizing your classmate and stand in the demonstration circle,” Madame Reyanna snapped.

Eola almost said something. It wouldn’t matter, though. Both Catrine and the professor had it out for her. This would only end one way.

She started casting the same anvil she’d been training her Mana with—Heavy Hold—and offered a tiny grin to Catrine. “Okay, I’m ready. Go ahead, Miss Andrese.”

“Lady, please, toddler. And I will.” Catrine’s wand whipped out of her robe with a flourish, and Catrine slowly drew a copy of Madame Reyanna’s sketch in the air. Eola watched her work, controlling her breathing as the Heavy Hold spell slowly drained away at her soul. It wasn’t hard. She barely had to work at it, even when Catrine botched the drawing and had to start over.

She finally finished the eye-shaped rune, added her attunement modifier, and poured Mana into it. The spell—Inner Vision—cast.

“Is that it?” Eola asked from inside the demonstration circle.

“Nothing’s happening, professor,” Catrine said quietly. “I can’t see anything.”

“That shouldn’t be possible.” The professor drew the same symbol, then stared at Eola, eyes narrowing. “What did you do, Miss Lemiene?” she hissed.

Eola shrugged. “I told you, Mana lines are theoretical. They don’t exist unless the caster needs them to. It’s sort of like souls. They’re metaphysical, just like Catrine—“

“Stop. Talking.” Madame Reyanna’s eyes had reached full glare. Her face was a mix of her own red and an angry purple. “Every child learns about Mana lines. They have to, to make Child’s Magic work for them.”

“My parents didn’t teach me that.”

“Miss Lemiene, leave my classroom right now!” the professor shouted, voice full of fury.

Eola turned and fled to her seat, gathering up her books and trying to keep a small smile off her face as she walked out the door. It didn’t matter if Eola stuck around for the rest of class; she hadn’t learned Mana lines as a child, and without an attunement, she couldn’t cast the spell to detect them in others.

If they even existed.

But she did have a new tool in her kit now. She’d copied the Inner Vision spell into her book before being called up, and once she figured out how to cast something like it, she’d be able to put it to use.

It was a messy spell, though; it’d be a while before she figured it out.

Eola Lemiene wasn’t very good at magic.

She was in the process of changing that, though—and the book cracked open on her desk was her next step. According to Colin, who was currently sitting on the edge of her desk with his back twisted awkwardly so he could read Theory of Magick: A Guide to Ordered Spellcraft over her shoulder, there was something missing in her understanding of Ordered Magic. That something was inside this book, or the next one.

And if Colin would let her focus, she’d be able to find it more quickly.

“What does Madame Reyanna have against you, anyway?” he asked from his perch on her desk.

"Uh,” Eola said quietly. The correct answer was that Instructor Tarik had pulled strings and forced the gnome woman to let Eola back into her class, and Reyanna seemed like the kind of person who ruled her little corner of the school with absolute authority. But that answer wouldn’t cut it for Colin. It’d only keep him asking questions. She needed him to stop doing that. So, instead of trying to answer, she tried to ignore him.

That didn’t help, either. He just had so many questions, and so many of them were more interesting than Theory of Magick. Just his presence in the room was distracting.

“Why are you always in my room, Colin?” she asked after rereading the same passage four times. “Actually, why don’t we ever do study sessions in your room? I know why we don’t use the common area, but you’ve got more space than I do.”

“Because, uh…” Colin tugged at his robes, which weren’t quite aligned right on his shoulders. “Okay, my attunement’s order, right? I’m…uh…not a very orderly person outside of studying magic and swordplay.”

Eola stared down her nose at him. Her somewhat-healed cuts itched, and she forced herself not to scratch them.

“What?”

“Ah, it’s a pigsty in there. That makes sense.”

Colin's cheeks pinked a little. “It’s not that bad!”

“Sure. We’ll use my room. Just…go get your desk chair or something. You’re making my back hurt.” Eola shifted her gaze down to the book as Colin slid off the desk and retreated for the door. The pages were brittle and yellowed, and she barely touched them except to turn the page carefully when she finally finished it. The ancient book was all about how Mana flowed through spells, but it didn’t cover anything new. Every bit of Ordered Magic in Eola’s spellbook already operated with the same principles.

She turned to the next page. It featured a stylized diagram of a rune—specifically, the one for Lighting Wing. She recognized the Bright Ball symbol in the middle, but couldn’t pick out the modifying marks. They had to be ones she’d never seen before, or they were buried so deep she couldn’t see them. The diagram felt…incomplete.

Spellcrafting in Ordered Magick is not to be undertaken lightly, Eola read. There are dozens of pitfalls that an aspiring spellcrafter may fall into, and Ordered Magick becomes more and more set in stone with each discovery. However, to those looking to experiment in magick, we mages of Turquen School offer these thoughts.

“Whatcha got?” Colin asked as he pulled up his chair next to her.

“Not much. A warning from a century ago that spellcrafting is a waste of time.” Eola shrugged.

The practice of spellcrafting is not a science. It is chaos incarnate. The Turquen School believes it to be one of the most dangerous professions in all of magick. However, even in the chaos of spellcrafting, it behooves a mage—be he rank amateur or seasoned archmage—to set some ground rules.

First, stay within your attunement’s bounds.

Second, seek connection and flow.

Third, find the places where Mana sticks and smooth them.

Fourth, experiment not outside of a cell or circle, for the sake of others.

Even then, the odds of a successful, replicable piece of new Ordered Magic are so low as to be, for all practical purposes, zero.

Eola went to flip the page, but Colin put a finger on the third rule. His eyes narrowed, and his lips moved as he turned it over in his head. Eola scratched at a half-closed paper cut and waited. “You can move on. I had something, but it’s gone,” he said after a minute.

“Actually, why don’t we check one of your ritual books for a while?” Eola asked. The constant interruptions were driving her mad. She couldn’t ‘find the places where Mana sticks,’ but she could find the places where Colin was sticking and smooth them.

“I’d rather work on that on my own,” he said. “Your problem’s much more interesting anyway.”

“Suit yourself. I’m going to tinker with my fifth spell.” She over, chalk in hand, and got to work, drawing and re-drawing the symbols in different orders as Colin took over half of her desk.

The book was right about one thing, though. Two, in fact. The second and third rules made total sense to her, and they explained exactly the problem she was currently having. She had her five spells to become a Journeymage. She just couldn’t cast them all. The fact that only Instructor Tarik knew she could cast any of them wasn’t a problem so much as the lack of Mana was.

Seeking connection and flow, and smoothing the sticky place, worked.

In theory.

No matter what she did, though, she couldn’t figure it out. There was no connection or flow to what she was doing. There were only places where her Mana stuck. And while Eola’s soul was significantly larger than, say, Catrine’s, that didn’t matter if her Mana stuck this badly. She kept working, though—and eventually, when she got sick of sketching her arrays, she grabbed the other book and got to work on translating the Old Alemic.

Components was significantly more informative and less full of warnings that understanding magical theory was pointless. It should have been helpful. Instead, all of her energy went into translating the symbols into something that made sense, and she had nothing left to understand the concepts.

It was late—well past nightfall—when Colin finally yawned. “Eola, I need to mull this over for a while. But first, I need to see what your magic looks like in action.”

“Didn’t you see me cast Chromite Cloth?” Eola asked, snapping Components closed and dropping it onto the blanket next to her. It bounced slightly.

“I was working on picking my own spell, and we were about to fight a feral book. I wasn’t paying attention to you.” Colin shrugged. “I could tell there was a hole in what you were doing, but I couldn’t tell what it was. I want to watch you cast a First Order spell.”

Eola thought about it for a bit. Her stomach grumbled, and she glanced up at the loft, where Atta was perched, staring at her. The cat’s head shook almost imperceptibly. Its yellow eyes bored into hers. Then she tore her eyes away from her familiar and reached for her cloak. “Okay. But we need to find an inn afterward. I’m starving, and you’ll owe me.”

Colin laughed. Then he locked eyes with Eola, and the laughter stopped. “Wait, you’re serious? You’re going to eat something good?”

“Absolutely.”

Twenty minutes later, a very cold Eola and shivering Colin stood in an empty demonstration hall, the big stone space lit only by a pair of Bright Balls hovering overhead. Eola squared up in the demonstration circle, wand in her drawing hand, and stared at her friend as he set up a notebook and pen on the lectern where Instructor Tarik usually lectured. “Are you ready?”

“Yes. Start drawing.”

Eola’s soul had mostly recovered from their battle with the feral book, and she elected to work through the same spell. She carefully sketched the Hasty and Delayed marks, putting them next to each other, then clustered Personal and Echoing around them. Her Mana filled each mark as she drew them, then filled the Safe Shield rune. “You get that so far?” she asked breathlessly.

“I think so.” Colin’s pen hadn’t moved. He stared, a little slack-jawed.

Eola didn’t waste any time. She drew the slash through the five symbols, and the Chromite Cloth spell cast. She poked herself in the stomach with her wand, and the delayed barrier activated. “That’s it,” she said. Then she breathed a shuddering breath.

“Perfect,” Colin said, and for the next five minutes, he scribbled in his notebook.

Eola was exhausted, hungry, and very, very grumpy, but as he kept working, her curiosity grew, and she peeked around the thin boy’s arm to see what he was writing. To her surprise, it was the spell-chain she’d drawn, repeated over and over with slight changes in its relative position. “I’ve been trying that, but it doesn’t change how the marks and runes interact. And if I put them too close, the spell stops working altogether. It demands a cloth or metal attunement, and I don’t have that. Patrice might, but I haven’t seen her since…” Eola trailed off.

“Got it. Okay, uh, I’m going to do some reading and see what I can come up with now that I’ve seen the whole process,” Colin said. “I’ll see you later. Oh, and I’m taking Theory of Magick.”

“Wait! What about dinner?” Eola asked, but Colin disappeared from the classroom. When she finally got back to her room, the book in question was gone, and Colin’s door was locked by a spell she couldn’t get past. She knocked, but he didn’t respond, and after a minute of waiting, she walked back to her door, changed into her nightgown, and collapsed into her bed in defeat.

Atta jumped up next to her head, golden eyes staring into hers. “Silly girl. You shouldn’t have told him.”

“What else could I do? He might be able to help me figure this out. I can cast spells—just not efficiently. If he can find a way to fix that, I can make Journeymage.” Eola sighed and scratched Atta’s head behind the ears. “And if I can make Journeymage…”

The cat purred and leaned into her fingers. “I’d love to see you make Journeymage. Even so, it’s a risk. That professor of yours already knows.”

“Who? Instructor Tarik? Of course he does. He had to know so he could—“

“No. The red gnome.” Atta’s eyes narrowed and got more intense, and her claws punched through Eola’s pajamas and into her thigh. “She doesn’t know everything, but she’s putting it together. It’s only a matter of time.”

“How do you know that?” Eola sat up, then hissed in pain as Atta’s claws ripped out of her leg. “And knock that off!”

“Because I’ve seen her journals.”

“You’ve…seen her journals?” Eola stared, open-mouthed, then groaned. “Atta, please tell me you’re not wandering the school?”

“I’m wandering the school. I do it while everyone’s asleep, so it’s not a problem. I’ve been doing it since—“ A hacking fit overtook the cat, and when she recovered, she changed the subject. “For now, don’t worry about it, and focus on solving that ideogram the professor gave you.”

“What about my efficiency, and Journeymage?” Eola asked. Her familiar’s inability to tell her certain things was…inconvenient. Then again, Atta could talk, and that gave her a leg up on Colin’s familiar. Nera, to the best of her knowledge, was just a little smarter than the average weasel.

The cat rolled her eyes, almost human-like. “That will come in time. Ideogram first, Journeymage later.”

“And a trip to the tavern before that,” Eola said. Colin owed her, after all.

Hopefully, he’d remember tomorrow.
