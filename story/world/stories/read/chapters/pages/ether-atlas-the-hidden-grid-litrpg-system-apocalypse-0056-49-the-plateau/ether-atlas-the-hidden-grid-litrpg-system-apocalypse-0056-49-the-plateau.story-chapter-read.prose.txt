Chapter 49

“The temples on this island are older than the pyramids. They were built by people who had no metal, no wheel, and no word for what they were building. Then those people disappeared. I keep thinking about that word - disappeared. Not died. Disappeared. There’s a difference, and the difference matters.” - The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr, Malta

The goat track was barely a track at all, a pale scar in the limestone where feet and hooves had worn the soft rock down to a shallow channel. Arthur climbed it the way he would climb the stairwell of a damaged building. Weight on the balls of his feet, one hand free, testing each foothold before committing. The cliff face was warm to the touch. Heat radiated from the stone in waves that smelled of thyme and chalk.

Donna was fifteen metres above him, moving fast, stopping at each switchback to scan the plateau rim before waving them forward. She hadn’t spoken since the figures disappeared behind the ridge.

Steve was between them, breathing harder than either, the pack full of stolen books pulling him slightly backward on the steeper sections. He’d stopped twice to examine grooves in the rock face. Parallel channels cut into the limestone beside the track, too regular to be natural erosion. Arthur had pulled him onward both times.

They crested the ridge into sunlight and wind, and Arthur saw the temple.

It sat on the plateau like something the island had grown rather than something people had put there. Golden limestone, weathered and pitted, the blocks fitted without mortar in configurations that his mind ran through before he’d finished his first step in the open.

Trilithon entrances, two uprights with a lintel set across, the weight distribution clean and deliberate. Corbelled walls that curved inward with a precision he’d seen in Roman aqueducts but never in anything Neolithic. And in the outer wall, sat a single megalith larger than anything else on the site. A stone that dwarfed the blocks around it.

Arthur stopped in his tracks.

The stone was at least five metres long and a metre and a half wide. Globigerina limestone ran roughly 1,800 kilograms per cubic metre. That block weighed in the range of fifty to sixty tonnes. Someone had quarried it and lifted it into a wall five and a half thousand years ago, with rope and ramps and human hands.

“That’s not possible,” he said. Then caught himself. “That’s extremely difficult.”

“Fifty-seven tonnes,” Steve said from behind him. His voice had the reverent quality it got when his pattern-recognition brain encountered something it couldn’t immediately explain. “Heaviest megalith on Malta. The accepted theory is coordinated labour and wooden rollers on prepared ramps, which is the archaeological equivalent of saying ‘we don’t know but we’re not comfortable admitting it.’”

Archie’s facets brightened. The rotation shifted into the configuration Arthur had started associating with incoming commentary. A fractional increase in speed, the geometry opening slightly as if making room for words.

Then it stilled and nothing came.

Steve prodded, “Archie.”

“Yes.”

“You were going to say something.”

“I have information relevant to a substantial proportion of your observations.” Archie’s rotation returned to standard. “That statement is general and accurate.”

“That is also not an answer.”

“No,” Archie agreed. “It isn’t.”

The Ether pressure was strong here. The node pull was dense and directional, concentrated somewhere inside the temple complex.

When Arthur turned from the temple he found Steve crouched low, his attention caught by something on the ground. Parallel grooves in the exposed limestone, running roughly north-south across the plateau surface. Shallow, V-shaped in profile, spaced about 140 centimetres apart. They crossed the path and continued toward the temple, weathered smooth by centuries of rain.

“Cart ruts,” he said, half to himself.

“Cart ruts?” Donna asked, looking behind.

"That's the name, but nobody actually knows what made them. They're all over this island. The most concentrated site is at Misraħ Għar il-Kbir, up in Siġġiewi. David Trump, the archaeologist who first surveyed it properly, called it Clapham Junction because the tracks cross and recross like the rail lines feeding the station back home.”

Steve took a breath, trying hard to rein in his excitement. “Parallel grooves carved into the bedrock. Some go off cliff edges. Some continue underwater offshore. The spacing doesn't match any known vehicle." He ran his finger along the groove. "I'll come back to these."

As they moved towards the temple, the fighters made themselves known, emerging from behind the outer temple wall.

There were six of them moving in a loose arc. They approached from the direction of the sun so the party had to squint to count them. They had the gait and confidence of professionals. They carried machetes, steel bars, one with what looked like a modified boat hook, and a hammer with a head that had faint lines. Ether-transmuted.

Donna’s hand went to her axe and Arthur put his hand on her forearm.

The lead fighter stopped five metres from them. He was in his mid-thirties, dark-skinned, a scar running from his left ear to his jaw. He looked at Arthur’s shield first, then at Steve’s focus rod and finally at Donna’s axe. His eyes lingered on the axe, on the dark metal, the ridge along the spine, the Ether luminescence at the edge.

“Weapons,” he said. His English carried a Maltese accent: the consonants clipped, the vowels rounded. “From where?”

“London,” Arthur said. “Via Marseille.”

The man cut his eyes to a woman on his left. She was shorter, stockier, and her right hand was wrong. The knuckles were too large. The skin across the back of the hand had a ridge of dense tissue that caught the light differently from the skin around it. Her fingers closed and opened with a mechanical quality that didn’t match the rest of her body.

Arthur read the limb as best he could. The tissue structure had been rewritten in a similar way as binding an artifact. The hand looked stronger and denser than its owner’s anatomy had designed it to be. The joint alignment paid the price.

Steve was staring at the hand. Arthur could see the pattern-recognition firing behind his eyes. Localised mutation, focused on a single limb.

Donna was watching the woman’s face and shoulders. She was focused on threat assessment, not biology.

“Mela,” the lead fighter said to the woman. “Tell Marco. Three, armed, from the coast. Bound weapons.” He turned back to Arthur. “You come with us, my friend. Captain will want to see you and know how did you manage to travel.”

Steve fell in beside Arthur as the escort formed around them, keeping enough distance to have time to retaliate if the party initiated combat.

“The woman’s hand. Did you see it?” Arthur asked in a low voice.

“I saw it,” Steve answered.

“The creatures in the Marseille harbour were mutated across their whole bodies. Ambient Ether saturation changes the whole system. That hand was localised. It was only on one limb. The rest of her was normal.”

“Meaning?”

“Ambient mutation doesn’t pick one limb. Something else is happening here.”

Donna whispered from ahead, “Something they’re doing to themselves.”

Steve glanced at Archie. “Is it stable?”

“The initial results are reliable. Tissue stress at modification boundaries accumulates over time. Most practitioners treat the early warning symptoms as an acceptable cost.” Archie’s rotation settled back to standard. “They rarely revise that assessment.”

Arthur had kept moving his mouth while Archie was talking, hopefully with the language barrier, the conversation would go unnoticed.

The fighters maintained their arc formation around the party. Three ahead, three behind. Donna catalogued the geometry without comment. She’d been escorted by professionals before. In her previous career, the polite version of this was called “client management.”

They passed through the temple site without stopping. Steve’s head turned as they walked. The trilithon entrance, the corbelled apse walls, the porthole doorways cut through single megaliths.

In a side niche Steve caught a glimpse of stone shapes, round bodies and heavy hips. The fat figurines of Hagar Qim, what little of them survived. He counted three. Arthur could see him filing every detail, his fingers twitching toward the guidebook. He made a sound that hadn’t decided whether it was a gasp or a complaint.

“I’m fine. I just.” He slowed. “Those corbelled walls, the curvature. The precision of it.”

“Keep moving,” Donna said.

“Moving.” Steve moved. His head continued to operate independently.

Beyond the temple, the plateau opened into a broader scrubland dotted with low stone walls and stands of prickly pear. A camp had been established in the lee of a watchtower.  A squat, round stone structure that Arthur put at seventeenth century, its walls supplemented with scavenged corrugated iron and timber. There were tents, cook fires and a perimeter of sharpened stakes that would slow creatures but not stop anything serious.

“The tower’s original construction is structurally adequate,” Archie said, his geometry orienting toward the watchtower. “The subsequent modifications are not. The stake perimeter would delay a standard Sanctum Zone creature for approximately forty seconds.”

The camp held thirty, maybe forty people. Civilians mixed with fighters. A woman scrubbing laundry against a stone trough. Two men were butchering something that had too many legs. There were also three children, quietly sitting in the shade of the watchtower. Children learning that noise in this world had a cost.

A man rose from behind a folding table as they approached. He was in his late forties, with the military bearing of someone who had kept the posture even after the uniform was down to a faded green t-shirt and cargo trousers. He had the shoulders of someone who’d carried a pack for decades, and the eyes of someone who’d stopped being surprised by anything in this new reality.

“Issa,” he said to the lead fighter, who gave a rapid report in Maltese that Arthur couldn’t follow. The captain listened, his eyes moving from Arthur to the shield to Steve’s rod and back.

“Captain Joseph Azzopardi,” he said when the fighter finished. He didn’t extend a hand. “First Regiment, AFM. Or what’s left of it.” His English was clear and direct. “From London, mela? You walked?”

“We transited,” Arthur said.

The captain’s expression didn’t change. “I don’t know what that means.”

“We’re not entirely sure either,” Steve said. “The short version is that we’re following a network of sites, the temples, the monuments, and the network has a transport layer built underneath it. We arrived through a chamber in the cliff.”

Azzopardi studied them for a long moment. Then he sat back down behind his folding table, which Arthur now saw was covered in hand-drawn maps, tallies, and what looked like a duty roster maintained in pencil on cardboard.

“So this transport…this transport layer brought you from London to Malta?” he asked, clearly not believing.

“We know how this sounds. You can check the chamber in the cliff yourself,” Arthur added hastily. “We just want access to the temple and a few other sites and we will be gone before you know it.”

“You came out of the cliff,” he said.

“Yes.”

“There’s a cave system in those cliffs. My men have mapped some of it. They didn’t find any chamber.”

“It wouldn’t have been visible until we arrived,” Arthur said.

Azzopardi absorbed this. He didn’t argue any further. Either two weeks of violet sky and mutated wildlife had adjusted his threshold for the impossible or he was keeping his cards close to his chest.

Then there was a shriek from the eastern perimeter.

The party was moving before the sound had finished.

There were two of them. Goat-derived. The body shape was caprine but the legs were too long and the horns had thickened into ridged plates that covered the skull. Each one was the size of a very large dog.

They came through a gap in the stake perimeter at a dead run.

Arthur took the first on the shield. The impact drove him back half a step. At Level 10, that meant the creature was hitting harder than anything in the Marseille harbour. He deflected the horned skull sideways, and Donna was there. The axe took the creature behind the head plate, driving through the ridge into the softer tissue beneath as Arthur’s hammer caught the side of its skull. It dropped.

Steve’s concussive blast caught the second mid-leap. The creature slammed sideways into a stake and didn’t get up.

Three seconds. Two kills.

Just over two hundred Ether between them. In Marseille, a harbour creature had been worth twenty-five. The Sanctum Zone density here was in a different category entirely. The level notification sat in his peripheral vision. Arthur filed it. Six attribute points. He’d deal with it when there was time.

As he turned from the goats he found Azzopardi’s fighters staring at them, some with mouths open. At the speed, the coordination, the casual efficiency of three people who’d fought together long enough that the choreography was unmistakable.

Azzopardi was standing behind his table. His eyes moving from the dead creature to the party. He glanced briefly at the perimeter before asking in a quiet voice.

“What level are you?”

“Ten,” Arthur said.

There was a cough and then the camp went still. The lead fighter who’d escorted them whispered something to the woman with the mutated hand. She whispered back.

“Mela,” Azzopardi said sitting down. He picked up his pencil, looked at it then put it down again. “Level ten. From London. Through the cliff.” He rubbed his jaw. “You’ll want to speak with the Baron, my friend.”

“Who’s the Baron?” Donna asked.

Azzopardi’s expression answered before his mouth did. It was a sour look. The two men were not friends but not enemies either.

“The man who runs everything south of the ridge,” he said. “Everything except this camp.”

“His patrols will have seen you on the track already, ta. If I don’t send you, he’ll come asking why I didn’t. And three people at Level 10…” He looked at the two dead creatures cooling in the dust. “That’s something he’ll want to know about before he hears it from someone else.”

“Sounds like you’re managing him,” Donna said.

“I’m managing a situation, my friend. There’s a difference.”

Steve was watching one of the fighters near the perimeter. “The ones who brought us in. Some of them have physical changes. What is causing that?”

Azzopardi was quiet for a moment. “The cores,” he said. “The stones that come from the creatures when you kill them. The Baron’s people push them inside.”

“Inside their weapons?”

“Inside themselves.” He said it flatly. The voice of a man who had watched it happen and decided he didn’t approve but couldn’t afford to say so. “They cut flesh, they push the stone in, then the flesh closes. The body changes. Stronger, they say. Faster.”

He rubbed his jaw. “Some of my people wanted to be stronger too. But I don’t allow it anymore. Not in this camp, my friend, not anymore.”

Steve frowned. “Why not? If people survive the procedure and recover from it stronger?”

“It is bad, my friend, bad when you don’t have the Baron’s doctor,” Azzopardi replied.

Azzopardi said it carefully, neither approval nor condemnation, just acknowledgement of a fact he had made his peace with.

“Baron’s doctor?” Donna asked.

“My friend, on this island there is one doctor. She is his.”

Steve was quiet for a long moment. “They’re absorbing cores directly into their tissue leading to mutations.”

“I don’t know your words for it. I know what it looks like.”

“What does it look like?”

“It looks like a man trading his hand for something that isn’t a hand anymore.”

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Donna said, still looking toward the ridge: “Tell me about the Baron.”

THE OTHER METHOD

They are absorbing cores directly into their bodies. Localised mutation in a single limb Stronger, denser, structurally rewritten. This is not what we do. We bind cores to equipment. Vessel, core, intent. The system rewards that with clean integration. What they are doing bypasses the vessel entirely. The body becomes the vessel. It works because we are not creatures. They take ambient Ether through biology that has no interface for it; the energy rewrites them outside-in, chaotic and total.

We have something they don’t. Whatever the system put inside us at Day Zero negotiates with the core. Keeps the integration to the limb that intent points at. The cost is the long-term cost. Two biological instructions in one body, arguing quietly for days, months, years? And that is only the body. The cores come from predators in a Sanctum Zone. The pattern brings more than muscle and bone density. If our equipment gets properties with core binding, will the properties manifest in humans as instinct? Something else? What happens if someone absorbs two cores or three? When do they stop being human? Will they have competing instincts inside them. This doctor might be able to help answer some of these question if she is treating people with cores in them. I hope we get to meet this doctor.
