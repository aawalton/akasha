We were still in the Dream even after the TV turned off, only the broken, woven stars lit the area as bright as the sun usually would.  I was feeling anxious about being here.  Ananke had said she had been planning on torturing me the next time I came to the Dream.  In the same breath, she had also said that she was planning on letting me struggle until I came crawling to her for help.  That’s the thing, it’s incredibly difficult to take stock in normal people’s words.  Things change, whether that be the situation, morals, or perceptions; it's virtually impossible to last.  That went double for what was essentially an eldritch god.  Who knew how her twisted view of reality and mind worked or how fast it changed … and if what Rowan said was true, then while I was in the Dream, the “situation” and the creature “perceptions” were one in the same.  I shivered, thinking about how easily she had stolen my mouth and nose before.  In short, I didn’t trust her to keep her word.

“Let’s get out of here.  You’re tuned to my world now, so if you die or fall asleep, you can get back on your own,” I said, glancing around to ensure nothing would come this way.

Maerin raised a brow at that, “Why would we go back?”

“The Dream and I aren’t exactly getting along right now.” I offered.  “If we stay here too long I’m pretty sure she’ll come squash me.”

That did seem to give Maerin pause, but she just as quickly shook her head.  “If what your brother said is true, I doubt it.  She would have already done something if she had any level of omniscience.

“Sure, but situations change.  I don’t want to piss her off and risk anything,” I pressed.

“We need to practice these abilities.  Unless you want a repeat of the spiders,” Maerin said.

I hesitated, looking around the Dream, but relented.  She was right.  Plus, I was going to have to come back to the Dream at some point.  Hell, I was going to in that night.  The loss of control, of being transported directly there without mentally preparing for it, had thrown me off. “Sure.”

I went over what we knew.  Which ended up being a short list:

After a brief discussion, we began practicing, sitting a ways across from each other.  We had already done something like this before, with the alcohol, so I didn’t imagine it’d be that difficult to replicate.  I was dead wrong.

I tried several things, tried to move a pebble with my mind, tried to create water, to create a sound from a ways off, to redirect a thrown object.  Really, the only thing it seemed like I could do was make myself drunk or not for a single Presence.  I thought that maybe it had to do with the change just affecting me, so I tried to make myself an inch taller to no effect.  I threw my hands up and closed my eyes, trying to think through why some things worked, but others didn’t.

I hardly had my eyes closed for a second before Maerin let out a distressed shout.  My eyes shot open, and Maerin instantly fell five feet out of the air and onto the ground.  My eyes shot up and around, looking for any assailants, but I found none.

“What was that?” I shouted.

“Loud,” Maerin muttered, and I scowled at her.  She rubbed her knee and stood up.  “I don’t know, one moment I was floating, the next I was falling.”

“On purpose?” I clarified.

“No, I didn’t fall on purpose.  The floating on the other hand was, but just for a second,” she said.

How did that work?  I considered the possibilities for a moment, and one came to my mind.  It felt silly, but we were beyond the normal workings of the universe now.  I closed my eyes, “Try doing what you were doing before.”

Silence.

Ten seconds passed, still nothing.

“Maerin?” I asked.

“I’m working on it.  Nothings happening,” she responded.

I frowned with measurable disappointment.  I really thought not influencing anything with my beliefs would work, even passively, but I wanted to make sure before moving on.  “Try something else, not floating.”

A second later, Maerin spoke, and I could hear the smile in her voice, “It’s working.”

Now to confirm.  Still with closed eyes, I asked, “What are you doing?”

“I’m moving a pebble with my mind!” She said, more than a little excitement worming its way in.

As soon as she said that, I heard the clatter of a pebble against the ground.  I smiled.  Maerin grumbled, confused.  I heard her footsteps approaching me, so I finally opened my eyes.

“What did you do?” she asked.

“I thought that there was no way you could do that without paying Presence.  I had already tried,” I said.

“But I did pay Presence,” she scowled at me, and then it was my turn to frown.

“How much did you pay for it?” I’d feel a little guilty, and more than a little confused if she paid ten, or God forbid, twenty Presence to temporarily float a fucking rock.

“One,” she said, relief and confusion warded.

“Of course that wouldn’t work!  You think having magical powers is equivalent to being sober on command!” I questioned.

“Yes,” she started, “you forget my world has magic inherently.  Yours doesn’t.  And what’s more.  It did work, so again, what did you do?”

I held up my hands in mock surrender.  “Nothing.” Maerin narrowed her eyes, so I explained a bit more.  “Really, I didn’t do anything.  I think you telling me about what you were doing caused the rock to fall.”

Maerin nodded along, seemingly having come to a similar conclusion as me, “because you didn’t believe I could do it without Presence, sure, but unless your subconcious effects these things then it wouldn’t matter because I payed for it.” She breathed out a sigh, “and now that I saw it out loud subconscious belief definitely plays a role doesn’t it?”

“It’s likely,” I confirm.

I saw her eyes focus on the pebble again. She frowned, but soon enough, the pebble started floating again.  She cocked an eyebrow at me.  “Really?  Eight Presence?”

“Nah, I thought ten seemed more appropriate,” I said.

She stared at me as if my brain was leaking out the side of my ears, “Ten?  To lift a pebble I could handle with a pinky?”

“It’s telekinesis.  Ten is cheap.”

She stopped arguing, instead just considering me for a moment.  Then my feet were off the ground, and I found myself floating in the air.  “That confirms it,” Maerin said as I tried my best not to flail.  “Perception certainly does effect this.  Let me take a guess.  When you think of telekinesis it’s a general sort of power.  It’s not so much that you cast it on something, you just cast it then use it?”

“Yeah, like a super power,” I confirmed.

“I don’t know what that is,” Maerin said.

“Right.  Different worlds,” I sighed in turn.

“The telekinesis I know works the opposite then, and the amount of power you use is AHHH—“ Maerin let out a shrill yell of surprise as I spent ten Presence to pull her into the air with my own telekinesis.  I burst out laughing.

“What the hell was that for?” She yelled at me.

“I just thought it was fair,” I regretted the words instantly as gravity dropped me the three feet to the ground, where I landed in a pile.  I did the same to Maerin, but instead of falling, she gently floated down to the ground.  “Like I was saying.  I think that your belief is influencing what I can do, how, and for what cost, but only so far as we know what the other is doing.  Also, not all things cost Presence.  For example,”

BOOM

A soft explosion went off overhead, and I flinched.  I looked up to see the trailing, fading light of a fire.  She cheered and pumped her fist at that, “HAH!  It worked?” I didn’t verbally ask, just raised a brow, and she got the message.

“It’s my bad a bit, but everything that’s worked so far has had Presence spent on it, but your brother didn’t say anything about that, so I thought it’d be possible to do it without Presence, but I also told you that things don’t have to cost anything right before I tried to make a firework.  I was trying to prime you so that your belief that these things need Presence wouldn’t conflict with my attempt.”

I nodded.  It was kind of like anchoring.  Anchoring’s a psychological trick where you anchor someone at a give number or thought.  The classic is asking someone, “Do you think Napeloen died before or after turning 72 years old?” Then you ask at what age they thought he died.  Unless people know specifically what age Napelon died at, they’ll say an age around 70.  In reality, he died at 51.  You anchor someone in a premise, and they naturally assume that the anchor is true or otherwise has some merit.

I thought uneasily about these new powers.  I liked rules. I liked guidelines and things set in stone.  I also liked breaking those rules, but only because they gave me an advantage over those who played by them.  Here … these abilities were completely different.  There were no rules.  Not really.  Ananke enforced rules and how things should or could be, but only because she wanted to.  If she desired, the rules could change at her whim.  We were ships unmored.  All I could hope for was that not many people knew about this secret of the Dream.  I sure as hell wasn’t supposed to know it at this stage.

My head began to ache.  I thought about my head, just not hurting.  Nothing happened.  I paid one Presence instead, and the headache went away.  It shouldn’t have cost anything.  Maerin had made a firework for nothing.

“It has to cost something,” I muttered to myself.  That was the crux of the issue.  Deep down, I didn’t believe that any of this could be done without paying in some manner.  Maerin’s sudden declaration had caught me off guard, and while I believed that it may not cost Presence, I was sure it had to cost something else in some way I just wasn’t seeing.  Nothing came for free.

It was like thinking myself invincible.  How would I make myself invincible for free if I didn’t truly believe I was invincible.  If I paid for it with Presence, I could see that doing the heavy lifting, or even if I sacrificed someone for it.  There was a trade, a transaction, or task that could empower or otherwise bring the thought into being, but for free?  I couldn’t imagine it.

I swallowed hard at those last thoughts.

“This really is dangerous, and I agree.  We need more practice.  If we run into another Visitor who knows this as we are now … I’m pretty sure with more Presence we’d get the floor wiped with us.”

“I’m not so sure,” Maerin said.

“Why,” I asked, a little tersely.

“Well if our beliefs can interfere with each other I’m sure they can assist with each other too.  That’s what your brother said makes things reality right?  Collective belief.  So if we pay let’s say ten Presence each we should be able to pool our Presence together for something.  That’s 10,000 total.”

On principle, I agreed, but shook my head in this case, “There’s no one around here except each other.  I think you’re right, but I also think right here, pooling our belief together will be just as effective as one of us leaving the area, so we don’t conflict.

Maerin nodded, not fighting me on that.  “Fair point, but also what we can effect is different.  I want to try something.”

“Yeah?” I questioned, not willing to agree until I heard her idea.

“You said the only way to go back to your world is to sleep or die right?”

I nodded.

“Well I say that’s not necessarily true.  I want to try to convince you that we can return to your world with these abilities.  For free.  Safely.” She looked excited, earnest, too.

I veered off topic, my curiosity burning too much.  “Were you a mage before?  You seem to like all this stuff.”

“Yeah.  I wasn’t allowed to leave the castle before.  Magic was one of the only things I could occupy myself with.  It’s like an infinite puzzle.”

I stared at her for a long moment before nodding, “Alright Maerin, convince away.”
