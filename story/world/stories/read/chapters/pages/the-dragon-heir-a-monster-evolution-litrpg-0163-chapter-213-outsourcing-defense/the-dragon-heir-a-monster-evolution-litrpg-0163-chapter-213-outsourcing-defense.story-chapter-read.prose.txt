The moment came sooner than I had estimated, which was mildly annoying.

The bow had been doing excellent work. Dozens of Kovrath down in under a minute. That kind of result surely made the convoluted construction effort feel justified. The shots had found a rhythm, the four sense fronts were parsing transitions faster than individual thought, the whole system was running with a smooth efficiency. It had almost stopped requiring conscious management and started running on its own momentum.

Then the rate of materializations dropped to almost nothing.

Not even a full minute.

I had known they were adaptive. I had accounted for adaptive. What I had not fully accounted for was this adaptive. But on further thought this kind of collective responsiveness suggested they were sharing information in real time across the entire city somehow. Also acting on it faster than what most coordinated military units should manage with direct communication.

The moment enough of them had died in the same way, the rest simply stopped giving me the opening to repeat it.

Irritatingly clever.

I kept firing at the ones still phasing through, the ones who hadn't received the information yet or had decided to test the intelligence themselves. The count dropped with each passing second. Phase one had run its course.

I had already been designing the replacement for this bow while it was still running.

The bow model had been improvised under pressure and it showed in the mana expenditure and overall efficiency.

Within the first minute of operating it I had identified the structural inefficiencies and run the secondary calculations almost automatically, the way my mind latched onto a clear problem now and simply refused to let go of it until it had produced something better. The new model was three times faster to cast. Significantly more efficient draw weight. I wanted to talk to Curious about the string architecture specifically, she would have thoughts, she always had thoughts, the problem was getting her to deliver them in under a few hours.

Later.

I felt the spatial flicker inside the barrier stack before anything visible happened.

I smiled. Right. Phase two time.

The barriers I had constructed were dark mana and lightning. Deliberately. Light mana would have interfered with the Kovrath phasing in through them, which, first, I didn’t have and second, interference wasn't what I wanted— I just wanted entry to feel possible. To feel like something a confident attacker could exploit. Quantum mana would have created unpredictable interactions with their transition and unpredictable was the one thing I couldn't afford right now because the entire point was controlling what they believed about me, and you couldn't control the narrative if the evidence kept doing unexpected things.

The barriers looked like protection but they were set dressing.

It had worked.

I tracked the flicker, identified the quadrant, started turning the bow toward it, and the Kovrath was already gone before the draw was halfway complete. Back into stealth, smoothly, and what came toward me in its place was a dagger moving at a speed that had no business existing in the material world, covering the distance between where it had been thrown and my torso in the time it took me to register that the throw had happened.

But even before the dagger closed the distance a shield of frost enveloped it and a frost-laden vine snatched it mid-flight, killing its momentum completely before crushing it into nothing.

Bingo.

I looked toward Alexei.

He stood just outside the barrier stack on a neighboring rooftop, the fog leviathan's miniature form circling him in slow loops, trailing cold mist across the stone beneath it.

He had his eyes on the barriers and not on me, already tracking the next disturbance, the Key responding to his attention with a subtle shift in the mist's density around the points he was watching.

I had not signaled him. Had not communicated anything about the façade through word or gesture or anything the Observer's Mark could have carried even if I'd tried. He had simply watched what I was building, read the shape of it, understood both what I was displaying and what I was taking deliberate care not to display, and arrived at the correct interpretation without any assistance from me. Then moved on it without waiting for confirmation that he was right.

That was not a small thing.

I returned to the bow and kept shooting, redistributing my attention now that I didn't need to manage the defensive performance simultaneously. With Alexei holding the line I could stop performing the part of something that needed to keep moving to survive contact and simply let him cover what the barriers were pretending to cover.

It would have been workable without him. Manageable, just like juggling too many things at once is technically manageable. This was considerably cleaner than manageable.

I rarely factored other people into initial plans unless I understood them the way I understood my clones. The probability I had assigned Alexei had been reasonable given what I knew. Watching him exceed it was, if nothing else, interesting.

He had been doing this for a decade and every decision reflected that accumulated experience. Nothing wasted. He knew which Kovrath attacks were committed strikes and which were engineered to pull a defensive response and drain mana on a feint. He knew where to place the vines for maximum coverage without overextending, where the frost was worth the expenditure and where burning it would cost more than absorbing the hit.

Watching a genuine expert operate when you had enough context to understand what each decision actually cost was its own category of interesting… separate from whether the situation itself was enjoyable.

The Kovrath pressing through the barrier gaps were not casual about it. Each wave came in slightly more coordinated than the one before, probing, testing the shape of the defense, looking for the inconsistency that would tell them where the real ceiling was. They were adapting to Alexei the same way they had adapted to the bow, reading him in real time and adjusting the pressure accordingly.

The numbers around us were not thinning the way the numbers elsewhere in the city were thinning.

And the math on one man's mana reserves against an indefinite sustained assault was not looking good. Alexei was working through mana potions at a rate that showed how much he intended to remain standing regardless of what it cost him later. The fog key was pulling mana out of him with the appetite of something that did not particularly care about the condition of the vessel it was drawing from.

That many potions in that short a span, for a mortal with spiritual mana veins, was not something the body simply recovered from. The veins would pay for it. Possibly severely.

I would have said something about it, would have pulled him back before the damage compounded into something permanent, the way I would have stopped anyone from burning their own foundation down for the sake of a single engagement.

But I knew what was happening below. And I knew why he wasn't stopping.

Then the city started paying attention.

It happened the way these things happen in the middle of a sustained engagement—through the gradual recalibration of people who had been fighting long enough to notice when the texture of the battle changed. A Rakaris warrior three streets over broke off her engagement, looked up at the building, made a decision in the space of two seconds, and repositioned. Two Faerins holding a chokepoint near the eastern stretch caught one of my arrows erasing a Kovrath that had been making their lives genuinely difficult, exchanged a look, and started moving toward the building's base. An Urgoth with a shield the size of a door simply stopped when the attacks on him ceased, turned, looked up, and walked in our direction with an unhurried pace.

It spread outward from there.

Fighters peeling off from engagements that had stabilized or that others could cover, redirecting toward the point in the city where the Kovrath concentration had become the densest and the most interesting.

The consolidation was loose as nobody had ordered it and nobody had coordinated it, but experienced fighters become coherent without instruction the way water finds level without being told to.

The Faerins took the gaps with their range weapons, bows and staffs covering the approaches the barriers couldn't. The Rakaris and Waryns handled mobile response, fast and aggressively, filling the spaces between. The Urgoths planted themselves between the building's base and anything that wanted to reach it, shield magic up. I finally had a few barriers of light mana around me.

Thus the pressure on Alexei redistributed.

Which left me as the focal point in the clearest possible sense. The attention coming up from below was not subtle either. Some of it carried suspicion, well, I couldn’t just expect these people to accept unknown variables at face value regardless of what those variables appeared to be doing on their behalf. Some of it also carried something closer to gratitude.

I kept shooting and let them look.

Markus arrived not long after, moving through the forming consolidation. His first response was the same as everyone else's— look up, assess, make a decision about what he was looking at. His second response was more interesting. He found Alexei, registered the mana potions and the state of his veins and the fog artefact pulling at his reserves, and something moved through his expression that he didn't entirely suppress before it was gone. I detected the subtle emotional change through observer’s mark I left on him.

The shadow that usually accompanied him was absent. I noted that and filed it.

He was suspicious. Had been suspicious since he'd detected the Key of Fog on Alexei without having been told it existed. That was a reasonable suspicion held by a leader with good instincts.

But he was also watching Alexei burn himself out holding a defensive line in the middle of a city-wide assault, spending mana he would pay for later in ways that would not be comfortable, doing it without complaint and without pulling back, doing it for the people in the streets below who were still breathing because of the line he was holding.

Suspicion had a harder time maintaining its footing against that kind of evidence.

It wouldn't dissolve entirely. I could already tell Markus wouldn’t let suspicion dissolve entirely on the basis of a single engagement. But it would soften. Would shift from active doubt into something more provisional, a wait and see rather than a what is he hiding.

That was enough of a foundation for the discussion with him that came next.

Including the conversation about me.

This version of me, specifically, standing on a rooftop with a building-sized bow and entirely too many visible barriers, playing a role I had constructed twenty minutes ago.

I allowed myself a small measure of satisfaction about that and then returned my attention to the next transition seam flickering at the edge of Curious's sense feed. Shot another sneak.

The decision to keep this identity separate from the Priestess persona settled itself cleanly enough. The two served different purposes and different audiences, and mixing them would collapse the utility of both. That was a problem for later construction. For now the separation held and I intended to keep it that way.

What had not left my attention for a single moment since it appeared was the figure on the eastern wall.

The Seventh Elder had stepped into visibility when the Kovrath death count crossed whatever threshold he had been using as his signal to pay closer attention. Not hiding anymore. Not using the perfect stealth that every other Kovrath in the city defaulted to as naturally as breathing. Simply standing on the wall where anyone with functioning eyes could find him, watching the battle.

He was large. Considerably larger than the others, the draconic elements pronounced further  — wings with actual span to them, claws that were massive, heavy horns, a tail moving with alertness. His scales ran silver with a mirror quality to them that made focusing on any specific detail frustrating. His eyes carried the same property. Slitted and reflective.

I had sent an arrow toward him when I first detected him. More probe than genuine attempt but It had stopped existing somewhere in the air in between.

Annoying.

He hadn't moved since. Hadn't responded to the probe, hadn't escalated, hadn't pulled his forces back or pushed them harder. He was simply there, processing, running whatever assessment someone like him would do. He was the one leading this charge after all. He had seen an unexpected variable appeared mid-operation and started demonstrating capabilities that didn't fit the established picture of what this city contained.

The Kovrath dying in numbers was clearly not the part that concerned him. I couldn’t pry much either from distance.

I kept the bow working and let him watch.

There was going to be a collision. That was already determined. The only open question was the timeline and what he was actually capable of when he decided the assessment was finished. I was not particularly concerned about the outcome. Overconfidence, however, was the one luxury I had never been able to afford, and I did not intend to start now.

He was a variable for me the same way I was a variable for him.

We were both still gathering data.

The battle around us had reached something approaching controlled. The consolidation of fighters below had redistributed the pressure enough that the engagements were no longer escalating, the Kovrath probes coming through the barrier gaps had thinned to occasional tests rather than sustained waves, and the ones still attempting to reach me were doing so with decreasing confidence and decreasing success.

Alexei had moved inside the barrier stack at some point during the redistribution, standing on an ice platform a few meters to my left with the fog leviathan coiled around him in slow protective loops.

He was steadier than he had been. Still burning through reserves I would rather he wasn't burning through, but steadier.

A projectile came from the north, fast. A Faerin mage deflected it with a wave before it reached the barriers, Alexei's own response already forming before the deflection connected.

He lowered his hand. Looked at the frost dissipating. Exhaled once.

Good enough moment as any.

"You look better," I said.

A small pause. Had to maintain the character. Couldn't go straight to the point. "The Key suits you. You're getting proficient with it faster than I would have expected."

He made a sound that was somewhere between acknowledgment and exhaustion. Up close the pallor of his face was worse than it had looked from a distance. The mana poisoning was already setting in at the edges. The hangover from that many potions in that short a span was going to be its own ordeal, separate from whatever the veins themselves had absorbed.

"Not as much," he heaved, "as I'd like."

Which meant the mana expenditure per use was still higher than it should be. Not something easily optimized while actively fighting for one's life, but the gap between theoretical efficiency and practical application was clearly bothering him.

"Sufficient is its own standard," I said. "And sufficient was more than enough. This whole performance would have been considerably more complicated if you hadn't read it correctly and moved when you did." I glanced at him then. "Most people wouldn't have."

He looked at my face and I held his gaze for a moment and winked.

The pallor on his face immediately caught a crimson flush. The grey-white complexion that mana poisoning produced was supposed to trend toward coldness and a creeping chill in the extremities and a certain flatness to the skin tone. What was happening to his face was the opposite of that.

I filed that away as something to diagnose at a less inconvenient time.

"The situation here is more or less contained," I said. "But there is someone who has been watching from a distance since before the consolidation formed. And he has not moved."

Alexei followed my line of sight without needing to be told where to look. The mirror-scaled figure on the far wall was not making any effort to be inconspicuous, which in its own way was the most conspicuous thing about him.

The recognition crossed Alexei's face.

"The Seventh Elder." His voice had flattened. "Of course it had to be him. Of all the raids, on all the days—" He exhaled. "Our luck has been consistent, at least. Consistently terrible."

"You say that with a particular… experience," I said. "Is there a specific reason he represents a worse outcome than the others?"

Alexei opened his mouth, closed it, looked at the Elder's form again grimly. "It might be too late to elaborate properly." A pause. "He doesn't lead raids. Usually. That's the first thing to understand. He sends others. The fact that he came personally means—"

He stopped. And I knew why.

The massive mirror-scaled form on the far wall began to compress slowly.

The wings folded and diminished. The horns receded. The mirror sheen of the scales tightened and swirled as the silhouette shed its mass smoothly, until what stood on the eastern wall was not a creature of pronounced draconic enormity but a figure that stood approximately as tall as my current elven form.

Long ears. Golden hair. A face arranged in an expression of settled amusement. Mirror-bright armour encasing a frame that was, in every external particular I could quickly assess, an almost precise reflection of what I currently looked like.

Save the eyes. His eyes had kept the mirror property.

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

I understood immediately why Alexei had run out of time to elaborate.
