After enduring the punishing conditions of the Dragonspine mountains for long weeks, we seemed to fly through the gently rolling hills of the Riverlands.

Given the way Vera and Jorge had talked of The Verdant Land, I had expected green pastures split by hedgerows and stone walls, pleasant copses of trees scattered about to house the giant flocks of starlings that would fill the sky with their murmerations at dawn and dusk. But instead, my eyes were met by a hundred shades of brown.

The Riverlands was a large kingdom that spanned from the base of the Dragonspines all the way to the Leviathan Coast and butted up against the Sunset Kingdoms far to the West. It was a rich and fertile country, courtesy of the mana-rich meltwater that spilled from the high mountain glaciers each spring, but this extraordinary fecundity was the very thing to blame for the sad brown mess that I now saw.

The uplands that we travelled through experienced a double harvest—once in late summer and again in early spring. Swedes, turnips, carrots and other root-bound vegetables were sown during late summer and laid dormant throughout the mellow winter months before bursting to life as the days lengthened and the sun returned. It went against everything I knew about farming, but given that I didn’t know much to begin with, perhaps that was no surprise.

In any case, fields of brown mud spread below me, punctuated with small green shoots and fronds from the vegetables crawling their way to the wan winter sunlight, and from my position far above it seemed as if the earth had been split into straight lines by some god-like being.

My perception was enhanced further than it had ever been before, similar to the rest of my attributes, and I felt like the entire world opened before me. I had steadily increased my perception with the bounty of my levels as we travelled, but the jagged topography of the Dragonspine Mountains prevented me from truly seeing the magnitude of the increase until now.

The winding rivers that gave the land its name spread outwards over the segmented fields below and reminded me of veins threading their way across an impossibly vast compound eye. It glared unblinking up at the heavens above, and I wondered what that view would be like. Would the stars more resemble the constellations of my skills if an eye of such scale could make out the subtle connections in the cosmos above? Or was it all just an empty void, occasionally broken by lonely twinkling lights cursed to hover in silence?

I marvelled at the view as I pondered a few more moments, before speeding up to catch the others as they descended down easy switchbacks ahead of me. It was trivial to do so, my body responding fluidly to my commands. We had spent near enough a month traversing the mountains, and I had grown substantially in power. 12 levels in the 2nd tier gave me more attributes than the entirety of the 1st tier, and while attributes weren’t everything, I knew I was a significantly more dangerous fighter now.

My skills were lagging behind a little, though that was the way of things in the 2nd tier, even for a combat-classer like me. I didn’t get the opportunity to experiment much with my more situational skills, like End of the Hunt and A Frozen Pyrre, but Break-Step at least saw a massive growth from my frequent use of it.

I had thrown myself around the mountains, attempting to perfect the ‘Ram’s Descent’, as I had taken to calling the technique of leaping from a great height and slowing my momentum not far from the ground with judicious use of Break-Step’s momentum-altering abilities so that I could slam into the earth with only bone-jarring force, and not splatter myself across the mountainside. It would make ‘The Drop Bear’ extraordinarily effective as an ambush tactic if I could fall from any height, after all. Vera just called it falling, but I liked to add a little pizazz to my arsenal.

Stride the Edge saw a similar level of growth from Nathlan and I prancing and playing at the roof of the world, and while we didn’t have much time for sparring, what with the hostile weather, I did see rapid and surprising growth in Vanguard of the Lost, as well. That was a whole separate issue that I’d spent much time discussing with Jorge, and who’s implications were so vast that I couldn’t waste time thinking about right now. I’d get to it in time.

Jorge and Vera hadn’t levelled as far as I knew, but that was to be expected, and it wasn’t like they needed to. I still didn’t know exactly how powerful either of them were, but ‘enough’ seemed to be the most appropriate answer to that question so far.

Nathlan had earned himself plenty of new strength over the course of our journey, now sitting at a similar level in the 2nd tier as I. His was the greatest increase in power amongst our group and he now matched me for attributes. When taken with his lifetime of swordsmanship, I routinely found myself beaten in our regular sparring. He still lacked the instinct for using his newfound physicality that I seemed to have, but his greater skill was more than enough to overcome that small distance between us.

It wasn’t an entirely one-sided event when we fought though, and I could sometimes edge my way to a clean victory or pyrrhic defeat. Privately I was beginning to think I had a knack for fighting; the mentality of it—that desperate struggle—seemed suited to the way my mind worked, and I had little trouble bending with the flow of battle where Nathlan himself appeared to sometimes flounder.

Sadrianna had gained a few levels as well, though not as many as us. That was both a function of her higher level to begin with, and therefore the greater experience requirements, but also due to her hanging back and letting Nathlan and myself engage in most of the battles that we managed to hunt out. Not to mention her support class—only diligent study, training and time spent using her skills would provide the level ups that we so quickly harvested with our weapons.

She was still beyond either of us though. Her greater level and experience proving more than a match for anything we could bring to bear. Without skills, we could just about beat her when working together, but who knew what would happen when she used that Razor-Beak skill of hers to cut through our defences, not to mention the devastation of her empowered Glacial Carcass skill. I suspected it would result in an overwhelming loss if we tried, but for now we had satisfied ourselves with pure weapons-work.

Jacyntha had gained no levels herself, being already at the peak of the 1st tier, but she was working with both Jorge and Vera to reshape her class into something that could work with the ritual literally written across her body, rather than be entirely supplanted by it. A lot of that work was mental rather than physical, but even Nathlan had to admit to being impressed with her progress.

Our first experience of the hulking barbarian had been of a contemptuous bully, but it had become clear from the moment she shared some of her story that she was more a scared child lashing out at the world than a person who thought of themselves as superior. ‘Child’ was perhaps an unfair way to describe a woman in her thirties, but she had showed a level of vulnerability in front of practical strangers that seemed almost child-like in its naiveté.

It was a considered approach, I knew; everything she had known had brought her ruin, so she’d thrown out everything she knew and relied on others who had had more success to help formulate her new world. Everyone needed a scaffold, after all; an ideology, a way to slot the confusing mire of the world’s constant surprises into a coherent picture. Some found solace in religion, others in smug superiority, fear of the ‘other’, anger at an uncaring world or love and profound gratitude for all things within it.

I knew that Jacyntha had once chosen anger, and now she seemed to be sticking to Vera to learn how to overcome that vice. They shared a history with that mercurial emotion, and it was clear for all to see the progress. Each time she disappeared with Vera for a day or two, she’d return more balanced. She started to open up around the campfire—mostly listening, true, but sometimes sharing a laugh or a short story.

Her apology to Nathlan had been repeated, more sincerely, and she’d left him with an olive branch. ‘I was wrong’ seemed a simple thing to say, but it was true, and it mattered. As someone who had rebuilt their life from the shattered pieces of the past, I could relate to her ‘cards on the table’ approach, and her bluntness was refreshing.

It seemed everyone in her past life—including herself—had been working with ulterior motives, hiding pieces of themselves from view. She had evidently decided that to carry forward similarly would be a fool’s game, and so had adopted an approach of radical honesty. She said what she meant, and nothing more. It took some getting used to, but we quickly learned.

Surprisingly, Nathlan seemed to find it the easiest to deal with. Given his past trauma and his class’s emphasis on truth, it should have been of no surprise, but I still found myself taken aback to see how he had warmed to her, and finding them sharing words and smiles around the campfire at dawn or dusk had been almost shocking. We were all hard people in one way or another though, so what was a little light maiming between friends?

Leaving the dramatic view of the Riverlands, and my contemplation along with it, I hurried after my companions. The land rushed past beneath my feet, potholes dodged and corners skidded around. At one point I leapt entirely from one switchbacking turn down to the next, clearing a hundred feet or more with a single bound, aided by the steep incline and a prodigious increase in strength gained over the last month. Break-Step saved me from broken legs, and I continued my headlong descent until I met the others on the path further down, rivers twinkling in the distance.

All in all, it was not the same group that left the Dragonspines as had entered it. We had been a party, riven by fractures and bound together by circumstance on the edge cases. The core of the group was strong, but that strength was mostly backed by Vera and Jorge.

The group that left the snowbound peaks and reached the flatlands below was a warband in truth. Perhaps not in number, but in power we rivalled many a mercenary outfit. Even if one were to ignore Jorge and Vera—a dangerous proposition in a fight, mind—Nathlan, myself, and Sadrianna were lethal combatants by now. Even Jacyntha should not be underestimated, and I imagined that if Jorge was able to push her at least partly into the 2nd tier, she’d be a terror on the battlefield with her aggression and raw physicality.

We were also a band in truth. Jacyntha still hovered on the outside, but all of us had warmed up to her at least partially, and we’d strengthened the growing bonds between the rest of us in earnest. Sadrianna felt like a true friend now, and we’d spent many an evening talking late into the night about our goals and shared purposes—her interest in my history and rebuilding of identity in the face of amnesia was obviously spurred on by the desire to rebuild an identity other than fighting for the clans’ old warriors, and it led to interesting and productive conversations.

The flatlands below made our purpose become clearer. A new sense of urgency, one that we had somewhat lost in our trek through the high mountains, began to appear in our steps once more, and the air felt like it was charged with thunder. We stayed the night in an abandoned bothy, and the next day saw us trekking past muddy fields, waving at the occasional farmer and drawing curious looks from each, until we emerged onto the bank of a great river.

It was one of the many that we had seen fleeing the freezing mountains and rushing towards the sea uncounted miles away. A good hundred feet wide at the least, it cut through the earth, wending this way and that. With the sun shining off its reflective surface, it resembled a great snake slithering through the countryside.

We followed it for a time, eventually emerging onto a well-travelled road; gravel surfaced, the raised sides paved with heavy river stones. Now and then, horse-drawn carriages trundled along—like the palanquins of the Copper Canyons but drawn by shaggy-maned draft horses rather than people—and in a new experience for me, I saw soldiers for the first time.

Not city guards, not criminal enforcers, not mercenaries or bodyguards to wealthy nobles. Actual soldiers in matching uniforms, armour glinting and polished, tramping across the road six abreast in a chain many hundreds long.

I was shocked. I’d been told, long ago, that large standing amies didn’t exist any longer on Tsanderos on account of the system and its unequal hierarchy of power. When a single powerhouse could slay an army, of what use was the army?

Jorge had consoled me though, explaining that while large armies didn’t exist in the same way as they had in antiquity, small ones were still in use. This was a fertile land, and the Riverlands as a kingdom was heavily populated, though power was divested to many feudal lords rather than a central administration, and so larger than average armies were able to be sourced, fielded and equipped.

They would collapse quickly in the face of the mountain barbarian’s upper echelon of warriors, and the same was true of other powers like that of the Leviathan Coast, but the Riverlands had their own powerful classers to safeguard the sovereignty of the area. These soldiers were for internal control.

Being an agricultural exporter to a wealthy and powerful neighbour like the Leviathan Coast—one with access to ocean trade, no matter how diminished—brought with it wealth and prestige, but that all hinged on the cheap and accessible transport that was provided by the flatlands and, more importantly, the rivers that wended across them.

The chief danger to the Riverlands was banditry, and thus a standing army of 1st and 2nd tier soldiers was required. Farming was hard work, after all, and many a young man or woman could be tempted to take up a blade and ply the busy trade routes rather than break their backs in the fields. Especially so considering the bountiful prey and mild weather making for an easy life out in the forests far from settlement and order.

It was the regular presence of these small travelling armies that allowed such busy roads to flourish, traders of all kinds travelling by foot, horse, carriage, and boat along the arteries that the rivers marked out.

We were soon to find out how necessary such soldiers were, but not before our little band came across a thriving town.
