I jerked awake with a soundless scream locked in my throat.

My body lurched upright, drenched in cold sweat, damp hair plastered to my face in a wild tangle. My chest heaved as if I had only just broken the surface after nearly drowning. My breathing was erratic, uncontrolled. The walls felt as though they were pressing inward from all sides, and the blanket draped loosely over my legs might as well have been a constricting snake, coiling tighter with every breath.

The world swayed. Shadows bent and twisted, crawling along the edges of my vision. Pale moonlight spilled through the array of windows, cutting across my shaking hands, and for a heartbeat my mind simply stopped.

I was trembling.

Me.

Why?

I had killed him once. I could do it again.

The reassurance rang hollow. Pointless.

My heart slammed against my ribs, too fast and far too loud. I pressed a palm to my chest, as if I could command it into obedience through sheer force of will. The tremors only worsened. My breaths came sharp and shallow, no longer breaths at all. Though no hands gripped my throat, I choked as if there were. The air felt thick, heavy, refusing to enter my lungs. Every gasp burned, like dragging shards of glass down my throat.

That dream. That vision. Those words.

He clung to my mind, to my skin, like tar. It had not been fake. I knew that with a certainty that made my stomach twist. I fought to calm myself, dragging every ounce of discipline I possessed to the surface, but my body refused to listen.

It moved without me.

I gripped the blanket with one hand, half to anchor myself, half to tear away the suffocating illusion of restraint. My other hand clawed at my throat, raking uselessly at an invisible grip. There was nothing there. Nothing to tear away.

My scream remained trapped. Silent as the night around me, broken only by my ragged breathing.

I kicked out hard, desperate, finally breaking free of whatever paralysis had seized my limbs, and tried to stand from the bed. My legs betrayed me immediately. I crashed to the cold wooden floor in a clatter of numb limbs and rattling breath.

The cold panic washing through me only intensified when I realized I could not access my cores at all.

They were frozen. Silent. Locked away somewhere deep inside me, mirroring my previously unresponsive limbs with cruel precision. No matter how hard I reached for the cores, there was nothing. No hum of power. No familiar warmth. Just emptiness.

That was when a hard voice pierced through the fog of chaos clouding my mind.

I recognized it immediately. I had, after all, just heard it.

My father’s voice.

The cause of my panic. And, twisted as it was, the solution to it.

I knew it was only a memory. I knew that logically, with the detached confidence of someone who had lived long enough to understand the difference between reality and recollection. And yet the voice was so clear, so sharp in my ears, that he might as well have still been standing directly in front of me, looming over my heaving form.

“Pain controls the mind of all,” he said, his voice gruff and husky, exactly as it had been in my sleep vision. “There are those who believe themselves above pain, who believe pain is nothing more than a trick of the mind that can be ignored.” A faint sneer colored the words, even in memory. “They are fools. Pain is the master of our souls. It is the answer to all problems. Remember this, Lilith. Pain is control. If you wish to control something, simply inflict pain in the right way.”

Pain.

I could do pain.

I balled my hand into a fist, naked of any core energy, and slammed it into the hardwood floor.

Again.

And again.

Ten times. Twenty. Fifty. A hundred. A thousand.

I lost count as crushed skin and knuckle met unyielding wood in a brutal, rhythmic impact. Pain exploded through my hand, sharp and immediate, radiating up my arm. Unwanted tears tore free from my eyes, tracing traitorous paths down my face before dripping from my chin to splash into the growing pool of blood and torn flesh beneath my fist.

Without any core energy reinforcing my body, I was left with only the innate strength of my altered physique and the wild surge of adrenaline flooding my veins. It was enough to keep my bones from shattering under the repeated blows, but it did nothing to stop the bleeding. Nor did it spare the skin of my knuckles, which peeled away and caught on splintered wood as I continued to pound, carving a widening crack into the floorboards.

The pain was raw. Honest. Overwhelming.

And it worked.

I did not stop until the roaring in my ears slowed, until the thunderous pounding inside my skull dulled enough that my thoughts could finally rise to the surface without being drowned. Once that happened, it did not take long for my cores to respond. They stirred, then hummed back to life, flooding my body with familiar energy as if nothing had ever been wrong.

As if I had not just reduced myself back to that little girl, trapped in a life dominated by another.

I leaned back against the side of the bed, my head knocking dully against the upper edge of the mattress, and muttered in a low, hollow voice, “Fuck.”

Shame followed immediately. Heat flushed my face until it burned, settling deep in my chest like a lava.

By the Gods, I was glad no one had been in the room with me.

“Two decades without a panic attack,” I hissed, lifting my hands to inspect them even as the torn skin began knitting itself back together, flesh smoothing over bone as though the damage had never occurred.

Or had it been more than two decades?

Time had blurred after I killed my father, Niro. Days, years, decades had all run together after that point, their edges made blury by blood and survival. It was entirely possible it had been longer than two decades since the last one. After a while, I had simply stopped counting. With my cultivation so advanced, my appearance had never changed. Perhaps it may have have, given centuries, but I had not lived long enough for that to happen.

Then, as if a switch had been flipped inside my mind, the shame and lingering panic burned away.

What replaced it was fury.

Rage.

He was alive.

Fine.

I just needed to kill him.

I had done it once. I would do it again.

In the meantime, I needed to put him out of my mind. His continued living did little to hinder my plans. It hardly impacted them at all.

And if what my replacement claimed was true, if she truly was the imperial princess of Pandoria, then Niro needed to die regardless.

This merely added to my reasons.

Having no hope of returning to sleep, I dressed quickly and made my way down toward the Spire kitchens in search of food.

The corridors were cool at that early hour, dawn light only just beginning to soften the air. My steps echoed faintly, as if announcing my presence to the sleeping stone, soft leather boots tapping against polished marble. Somewhere high above, a narrow window admitted the first thin ray of dawn, pale and fragile, not yet strong enough to be called morning.

The farther I descended toward the lower levels, the more the air began to change. Cold stone gave way to a creeping warmth that seemed to, somehow, quiet the torret of fury flooding me. The lingering scent of wax and incense faded, were replaced by smoke, damp grain, and the honest bite of wood ash. I heard the kitchens before I saw them, the low murmur of few early dawn staff already at work drifting through the corridors.

I pushed through the door and was greeted by firelight.

The hearth was only half awake, its flame painting the walls in soft amber. A thin veil of smoke drifted upward, clinging briefly to the rafters before slipping away through the hood overhead.

Only a few of the kitchen staff were awake, as was usual for the kitchens at this hour. Old Marwen hunched over a basin near the wall, washing trenchers with hands red from cold and age. He glanced up when I entered, offered a simple nod instead of a bow, then returned to his work without hesitation.

Fortunately, the man cared about nothing even approaching as much as he cared about preparing food, and I doubted he paid more than passing notice to my somewhat disheveled appearance. The older man was just as likely to follow decorum and bow as he was to stop preparing food. Which was nonexististent. I never pressed old Marwen for formalities. He barely spoke, was never rude, and made great food.

I consciously attempted to flatten my messy hair for a moment before deciding I simply did not care enough to continue.

There were a few others besides Marwen, but my gaze drifted through the room, searching for my occasional early dawn compatriot.

The first to spot me as I made my way down the steps, doing my best not to betray the splitting migraine pressing behind my eyes, was a young boy whose gaze went immediately wide.

His mouth opened and closed a few times before he dropped into a low, if clumsy, bow.

Whatever he might have done next, I never learned. An elderly woman gently nudged him aside, offering a practiced courtesy despite her homely appearance and stained apron.

There she was.

“My lady,” Bonney said, halfheartedly shooing the boy away. “Another early rise?”

I nodded, managing a tight smile. “Indeed it is, Bonney.”

“Would you like your usual?” she asked, of course referring to the flower petal sweets I had discovered during my time in Sealrite.

“I would.” It was not that I had grown particularly fond of the old woman, but she was companionable in the quiet hours before the rest of the Spire truly woke. And those mornings had been many. They had always been many for me. Sleep was a luxury my mind rarely afforded me, especially as my power grew and sleep became ever less of a necessity for my body to function. It seemed in this world I would need to locate proper sleeping herbs as well. “Some toast as well. And jam.”

Bonney raised an eyebrow, her kind eyes glinting with rare humor. “Commoner food today, Lady?”

I shrugged. “Food is food.”

The old kitchen aide tutted softly but ducked into the pantry, rummaging around for a few moments before emerging with the last half of a loaf.

“’Tis a day old,” she muttered, drawing a bulky knife from a drawer and slicing two thin pieces from it. Jam was slathered onto both, and she placed the plate on the counter I leaned against.

She disappeared back into the pantry, muttering softly about where she had left the leaf treats. I smiled faintly and bit into the cold toast, feeling my nerves slowly unwind in the quiet peace of the kitchen. The gentle warmth from the single lit oven at the far end of the room kept the damp chill at bay, carrying with it a surprisingly inviting scent.

I gestured toward the oven I had not initially noticed in use. “Bit early to be preparing warm food,” I observed.

Bonney did not answer at first, her head buried in kitchen stock. When she finally straightened, she grinned and handed me a small bag of sweets.

“I cannot believe you tracked these down,” I muttered, taking one. “I could never figure out where to find them.”

“Your ladyship is fortunate there was a local seller of the petal pastries,” Bonney said, referring to the sweets by the name I'd given them. “Otherwise I might not have managed.” She dusted her hands with flour and began to work a few handfuls of dough, molding it, or rather pounding it, into shape. “I was tasked with preparing the meal for our ambassador guests.”

I raised an eyebrow, taking another bite of toast. “The Tenebris ambassador is still here?”

Bonney shook her head. “No, they left months ago.” She eyed me over the dough, now beaten into a more submissive state. “You were not told?”

“I only just returned.”

“Ah, of course.” She stepped away to check whatever rested in the oven before continuing. “We have Holy Kingdom guests arriving tonight.”

I frowned, swallowing the last of my toast. “Didn’t I ban them?”

Bonney could only shrug. Even if I had issued such an order, the kitchen staff would not have been informed. There would have been little purpose in it.

I grunted. “They have quite the strong nerve, returning after conspiring with Cael to set off a mana bomb in Sealrite. Nearly killed me.”

“Perhaps they are here to mediate,” she offered gently. “I am surprised you were not told upon arrival.”

Instead of responding, I placed another of the leaf treats into my mouth. The familiar velvet texture and layered burst of flavor filled my senses. I could hardly explain to a Spire kitchen aide that no one had time to brief me because I had spent my few waking hours the previous day examining the eyes of dead Elyndor soldiers before collapsing from exhaustion.

After a moment, I answered, “I will be meeting with the others later to discuss what has happened in my absence. I imagine the Holy Kingdom’s ambassadors will be a significant part of that discussion.”

I closed my eyes briefly, focusing on the cascade of flavors from another petal pastry. Then I shook the still more than half full bag. “What are these called again? You never did tell me.”

“Your ladyship never asked,” Bonney replied with an easy shrug. “If you say they are called Petal Pastries, then that is what they are called.”

I rolled my eyes at her sarcasm and false piety. “What do others call them?”

“There are many names,” she said. “In the southern Pularean regions, they are often called Fae Ears or Elf Ears, on account of their shape. In more... civilized parts of the continent, they are called Lotus Cakes.”

“Are they truly from a lotus flower?” I asked, eating another. “Or are they simply a type of pastries?”

Bonney smiled, though her eyes never left the dough as she began cutting it into neat shapes. “They are made from a special type of lotus flower, though they are heavily mixed with pastry dough.”

“Huh,” I murmured.

I reached into the bag of Lotus Cakes, withdrawing a small handful and immedietely eating them with a not-so-queenly hunger.

Bonney sighed. “I will place a few more orders.”
