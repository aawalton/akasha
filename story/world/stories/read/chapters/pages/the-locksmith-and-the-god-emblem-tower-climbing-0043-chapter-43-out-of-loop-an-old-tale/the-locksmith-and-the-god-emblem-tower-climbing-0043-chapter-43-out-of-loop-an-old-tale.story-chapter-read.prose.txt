Lost in his notes, a man frantically wrote in an old notebook. Pages of esoteric information filled the book with content that, according to the man, would change the world.

To the writer's left, a telescope celebrated the stars through a circular window. As if searching for inspiration for his scribbles, he occasionally caught himself observing them. Was it wise to do so after what he had seen? No. Even so, the man couldn't contain himself. The revelation, however frightening, gave him hope like a castaway seeing a boat on the horizon.

"Together and separate. A Circle, a Square, and a Triangle. Together and separate." He spoke to himself in an attempt to rationalize the Shape. He then shook off the thought and continued writing in his notebook.

"Uncle!" A female voice once again distracted the writer. "We're short on pork for the stew..." Would you mind going to buy more? The young woman almost said, but held back and left it at that.

Nevertheless, the phrase seemed to have spoken itself, judging by the man's reaction, who then stood up with a deep sigh. Around him, besides the telescope observing the bright points in the sky, a shelf full of books was covered with flyers for events in the nearest city. From circus elements to artistic representations of old stories, one of them seemed marked in the middle.

He then bumped into something that was on the shelf. He quickly picked up the item and put it back in its place; it was a photo of a child and the same man. Almost... He thought and turned to leave.

Reaching the kitchen, only a few steps away, a young woman with a homely appearance seemed to be enjoying chopping vegetables. "Oh, uncle, it's... So, you don't need to go there again. I know you don't really like going out..."

Blanche, the writer's niece, tapped her feet and scratched her arms at the mention of her previous call. She had arrived a few days ago and would only stay until next Saturday. Her shoulder-length brown hair was gently brushed aside as she avoided the man's gaze. In front of her, like a war hero, a wooden board displayed its scars as it bore the weight of vegetables and a tiny piece of pork.

"No problem. I need to buy more ink, too..." the man said wearily. He then picked up a bottle from behind him and placed it on the counter. "Put more of this; he loves it."

"B-But I saw that these items cause leprosy, uncle..." she said, her voice trembling. "It's true! I overheard Barry and that chubby merchant talking about it!"

"Don't jump to conclusions, Blanche." He smiled at her as he bit into a piece of bread from the shelf beside him. "Those men sell lies as much as a poet sells words. In both cases, it's best not to listen to them."

"To the poets or the merchants?" Blanche asked, confused.

"...Both." And he went down the stairs again, leaving the house.

Seen from afar, the house in question even looked like a front for a clandestine shop with its... different appearance. Unlike the more modest houses of the villagers, this one was tall and robust; quality wood. Its well-placed, circular windows let light into every corner of the room, and at night, the breeze kept the whole house cool. Despite Blanche's presence, however, the house had been built for only two people. Perhaps that was the reason for the envy the inhabitants felt when they passed by. He doesn't deserve all this. These were the thoughts of the scrupulous, and if they knew what happened behind those walls, they would judge him even more harshly.

Then he arrived at the shopping center. As if he were royalty, people made room for the writer and his unshaven beard. Some looked disgusted, while others slightly turned their heads in curiosity. In any case, they were all ignored by the man.

He went to the last butcher shop on the long list of surly vendors. Leaning his head back on the chair, the vendor seemed to drool on the counter.

"Do you have any leftover pork?" The man said impatiently, hoping to wake the sleepy butcher. "I need whatever 36 Chimes can afford."

"A-Ah yes, of course, well..." The man jumped up with a disconcerted smile on his face. "Which cut do you want? The same as last time?"

"...That's fine." He said, looking around, then meeting the scrutinizing gaze of one of the officers. Frowning, he approached the stand. "Quick. I don't have time."

"R-Right, this one will do...?" And before the writer could answer, he made three cuts and handed them to him in a sealed bag.

In short, the man turned and continued walking down the busy avenue. As if wanting to hide from the world, he kept his head down, glancing back from time to time, realizing he had lost the guard. He wasn't sure why he was being sought, but he knew nothing good could come from an interaction with the officer. Complaints had been made against him. Mere warnings from concerned citizens. It was the letter he had received last month. In any case, he had fled from another encounter.

He then turned into a disgusting alley and, trying to ignore the smell, continued down an empty street.

"Trent's Treats" A shop hid in the shadows of the late afternoon. The writer then went inside. At the counter, a man read a newspaper unassumingly. Noticing a new customer, however, he turned and gave a warm smile.

"Cardley!" He greeted him with open arms. "It's been a while since I've seen you, lad. Are you here to buy more paper?"

"Why else would I come here? It's only been a week..." He replied, grinning.

"Maybe to see an old friend? You're my only customer, you know? These brutes can't even read..." He said as he turned and placed a box on the counter. "I thought you were finished already. How many months have you been writing? This story will have an anniversary soon."

"It's not a story, Trent," the writer said, now more serious. "But yes, it's been quite a while... With everything that's been happening... My niece came to visit me this week, just when I wanted to speed up the process."

"...Have you been having nightmares again?" Trent asked, raising an eyebrow.

The writer then scratched his head and refused to answer. He had an embarrassed look on his face.

"And that other project?" Trent asked. "Are you sure about that? The people here already hate you enough to report you. If you build a castle right before the village's edge, they'll definitely complain."

"I don't care," he replied curtly. "Besides, it's not a castle. The lenses I have aren't big enough. I need to do this, no matter what."

"And that's the sentence that scares me," The salesman said. "I'm not worried about you, Cardley, but about Raviel. What will become of the little one when you come back with another black eye? Maybe this time it won't be a punch, but a stab in the stomach."

"I..." Cardley didn't answer. Thoughtfully, the man looked out the window, the twilight giving even more texture to the white pages in the box on the counter. He ignored Trent's comment and began searching for coins in his right pocket.

The salesman then sighed deeply and gave a slight smile. "No need, lad. Think of it as an... investment. I want 10% of all the Chimes that your book sales make."

"...You know it won't sell that much, don't you?" Cardley said.

"Yes, but it'll certainly be worth more than this ream."

"I can't tell if you're a great investor or not..." Cardley chuckled as he put the box in one arm and the bag in the other.

"Just don't do anything you'll regret, okay?" He said before the man left the store.

I already did. He added inwardly as he simply nodded to Trent and walked through the exit.

Taking the same route back, now at night, he quickened his pace to get home. He didn't like leaving Raviel alone, even though Blanche was there to look after him. Crossing an alley to cut through, he then saw, out of the corner of his eye, a shadow move.

Thump! Cardley received a punch to the stomach hard enough to make him fall to the ground, dropping the box. The papers, now mostly soiled, were scattered across the ground wet from yesterday's rain. Less than half remained in the box, which, Cardley thought hopefully, was enough.

"...Grab the bag!" A low voice could be heard. When he tried to get up, he received another kick to the face and fell, grunting.

"That was the first warning. On the second, you won't be coming back." Then he heard quick footsteps.

Lying on the ground with a throbbing jaw and a bleeding nose, the man suppressed a sudden urge to cry. He knelt down and quickly gathered the leaves that were still usable. Wiping the tears from his face, he shouted furiously at the sky as he stood up with the now half-empty box.

He arrived home without further trouble. Opening the door, he saw the loving, bright expression of his niece draining from her face when she saw Cardley.

"Uncle!" She put down the knife and carefully checked on the man. "Are you alright? What happened? Was it them again? I swear that—"

"Don't swear anything you can't keep, Blanche." The man said with a weak smile. "I'm sorry, I couldn't bring the pork... My hand slipped. I simply tripped."

"Why do you still defend those worthless people!?" The niece said indignantly. She sighed and then hugged him tightly. "It's alright, uncle. You know we love you, don't you? We can just eat the vegetables and that piece of pork. You can share it, I'll eat what's left."

"No need. Leave the piece for Raviel." He said, heading to his room as if nothing had happened. "And tell him I went to bed early; he knows not to bother me while I'm sleeping."

Before hearing her reply, he went upstairs and closed the door. Looking briefly through the telescope, he got into bed and put both hands on his forehead. Without saying anything, after a few minutes, he got up and began to take the papers out of the box. He closed the notebook and set it aside.

On the cover of the book, the title "The Emblem Theory" was at the top.

The author's name? Cardley Logos.
