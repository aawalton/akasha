Loki came out of the Bifrost like something it had spat up. She hit the ice face-first, or would have, if she hadn't caught herself an inch short with a flick of her fingers. Another wave of her hand and she was upright again, glaring at the sky with her middle finger raised.

"Heimdall, you bloody arse," she told the empty air. "Learn to aim, for Odin's sake. I'm the future queen of the nine damn realms."

Nothing answered. The wind howled in her face instead, cold enough to make even a goddess's hair stand on end. She'd forgotten this place, but it was coming back to her now: the taste of the air, heavy with ozone, blood, and lost prayers. She remembered it far too well. Far to the east, a settlement smoked against the glacier wall.

"I'll find you, engineer," she muttered, and started walking.

By the time she reached the outer stones, the town was already awake. Giants moved between longhouses cut from ice-hewn rock, their voices deep and unhurried. In the middle of the square stood a shrine, and the sight of it turned her stomach: a statue of Thor with his hammer raised, frozen meat and dull crystals piled at his feet. Children had left little carved wolves. Adults pressed their foreheads to the stone and whispered their prayers.

Loki smiled, but only thinly.

"Of course," she said under her breath. "They love this one too. And for what? The man cares about eating and drinking, and that's about it."

She slipped into the lee of a wall and let her body change. Height first, two and a half meters of it, then the skin, blue as deep glacier ice, then the braids, combed through with bone. She caught her reflection in the ice and was pleased. It was a first-rate disguise. She walked into the square like she'd lived there all her life, and nobody spared her a glance.

A giantess was just coming out of the junkyard. Loki stopped her and asked, in the local tongue, whether there were any rumors going around. As she'd suspected, the engineer was already famous.

The story went like this: a strange little one had been spotted two valleys over. He'd made the gods' temple his home, and a woman of native blood had been seen talking to him.

That was him. That was who she was after.

"Found you, engineer," she said, her eyes bright.

Barry sat in the chief's seat as if the chief owed him money. In a way, he did, just not money. The village was eating well now. One of Barry's devices was already cloning their enormous cattle. He hadn't asked for much, only room to work, and they'd given it to him, one miracle at a time.

He was staying in the big hall at the center of town, three stories of stacked stone and ice, with the wind still pressing hard against the walls. The chief was happy. The people weren't.

Outside, Bela's voice carried across the square, amplified by the bowl of the glacier. "He is a gift," she told them. "Sent when the gods finally remembered we still breathe."

A big male near the front spat. "He's a human, not a gift. We should cook him properly and eat him." He stormed into the hall and pressed his palms against the crystal.

"This is desecration," someone else said. "He wrecked our temple. We'll all be cursed!"

Barry heard every word. Part of him wanted to tell them that Odin didn't care, Thor didn't care, nobody did, and that they should look after themselves. But that would have been stupid, and a waste of energy.

He knew where his attention belonged. The translation goggles laid text across his vision, still linked to the statue he'd taken from the cold, windy temple and set up in this much warmer room.

He'd already mapped the prayer node. For centuries the petitions had piled up like code, and they showed everything: hunger, cold, babies born wrong, raids that never stopped.

To finish this mission quickly he needed data, and all of it was in those prayers. What he hadn't expected was how they'd look together. Stacked up in his backlog, they stopped looking like prayers at all. They looked like a scream with no mouth.

"Fucking hell."

Outside, Bela was still going. "He gave you food when we were starving. You will all learn his worth in time."

The door swung open and she came in, shutting it with a thud that shook ice crystals loose from the ceiling. She studied Barry for a moment.

"I didn't know," she said quietly, "that my species could be this stupid."

"Finally," Barry said, without looking up. "Welcome to the club. Every species gets a participation trophy for stupidity. Yours just happens to be the one without insulation."

Bela's mouth twitched, a smile gone almost before it arrived.

"Like I told you, I'm a human, an outsider who desecrated their temple. Of course they're angry. Never mind the infinite free food."

Bela sighed and looked out the window at her people. He was right. They wouldn't follow a human. Not yet.

"Let's stabilize the town first," she said. "Food is solved, but there's sickness in the houses."

"I'll get to it."

Barry leaned back, the adaptive jacket settling around him.

"What are you doing?" she asked, watching him wave at the air as if he were shifting invisible objects around.

"Local scope. I'm turning everything into numbers, every prayer, every problem, so it's in a language I actually speak. I just need a little time."

Bela stepped closer, amused, drawn in by the goggles. "Can I see? The view of a god?"

"Not a god's view, no. It's science." He paused and looked at her. "Actually, I think your brain's developed enough by now. Try it." He handed her the goggles.

She smiled, a real one, and Barry wondered how long it would last.

"I can see it," she breathed. "Everything." The prayers, their meanings, the projections of what came next all scrolled across her eyes.

Five minutes. Ten. The smile faded slowly, just as he'd figured it would, and he began to think he shouldn't have handed them over at all.

"How long have we been dying like this?" she asked. "How many times have we made the same mistakes?"

Twenty minutes. Thirty.

At last she lifted the goggles off, her eyes red and wet. Barry moved in and took them back fast, before she could lose it and fling them across the room.

"You were right," she said. "We're going to go extinct."

Barry offered no comfort. He rarely did. He turned a dial on the goggles' filter as the crystal's light steadied.

"Your job was to talk to the other giants and find me somewhere safe," he said. "That's done. You can go back now." He pulled out the same gun, a different vial loaded in it.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean I'll turn you back into what you were. Idiot and naive, like the rest of them." He raised the gun. "You don't have to suffer through this."

"No. No, no, no." She dropped to the floor and clutched his boot, her forehead pressed against it. "Please don't. I'm the only one who can see it. Who understands. I can't leave my people like this."

Barry pulled his foot free and sighed, like a man who'd known exactly how this would go.

"Please," she said. "I'm begging you. I'll stop acting like a defective."

"You asked to be smarter, remember? Smarter comes with a heavy bill. And you're not defective. You're the only one on this ice ball who can see the shape of the cage. Use it. Don't just sit there."

Bela nodded and wiped her tears away.

"And you?" she asked. "When you look at us, what do you see?"

Barry's mouth flattened into a line. "A backlog. A very noisy one."

She looked at him, then nodded, accepting the answer for what it was.

They went back to work without another word.
