The small town of Mountainend was often ignored by adventurers, mercenaries, merchants, and every other sort of person chasing wealth or advancement.

Though it stood on the border of the rich forbidden forest like several other towns, it lacked the proper structure of an adventuring hub. The problem was not a lack of ambition, nor even a lack of vision from its leadership. It was, simply and painfully, a matter of bad position. Still, Mayor Ehran remained convinced that one day Mountainend would prosper.

To push that future closer, he had established a policy meant to attract and keep adventurers in town. Anyone carrying an official adventurer card from a recognized guild could claim a refund at town hall for lodging and meal expenses, so long as the amount stayed within reason.

Unfortunately, the policy had done little.

At best, it attracted those too poor to survive in stronger adventuring towns. That was precisely the situation of the party known as the golden trio.

A year earlier, the three young adventurers had traveled north from the south toward Riverend Town, full of the same dreams shared by countless others across the continent. Wealth, power, advancement, and a life greater than the one they had left behind.

The story was always the same.

Young people driven by heroic fantasies or hunger for strength would abandon home and head toward the borderlands. A few fulfilled those dreams. Most were ground down by the adventuring system, becoming one more rung in the ladder others climbed. They fought endlessly to rise above those at their own level, and even that was better than the fate of some. Others never found a place in the system at all, whether because they were not suited for it or because they refused to bend in the needed ways.

The golden trio were dreamers. Ambitious, yes, but not ruthless. They did not want to prey on others just to advance a little faster. They refused the underhanded shortcuts that Riverend’s low-tier adventuring scene had come to tolerate, and so they never truly fit there.

By chance, while searching for outside-guild opportunities, they had met a friendly half-beast mercenary who offered them a piece of advice.

Try Mountainend. Look for the bookstore.

The suggestion sounded unreliable, especially considering Mountainend’s reputation, but by then their choices were no longer plentiful. It was either take the chance or continue starving slowly in Riverend.

Of the three, Cyrus was by far the most excited about the possibility of free food and lodging.

So when they arrived in Mountainend, instead of looking for the mysterious bookstore, he dragged the other two straight to an inn. Even prepared for disappointment, the town still shocked them. How could a borderland town be this quiet? This empty? Could it not even hold enough adventurers to create noise?

Still, the food was decent and the beds comfortable, which was enough to feel almost luxurious after their recent months. A full day passed before the three finally gathered the courage to seek out the bookstore. They had grown so used to poor treatment and small humiliations that basic comfort alone had made them hesitate. Good treatment always came with some hidden price. That was the kind of lesson Riverend taught well.

“Hey, Hakon,” Iris said as they walked out of town, “what do you think this bookstore actually is? Some kind of magic emporium? Or maybe a guild with a fancy name?”

She was mostly trying to stop Cyrus’ endless praise for the inn’s food.

The foxkin walked behind them with the careful restraint typical of his kind, quietly observing the pair before answering. He had often wondered how two humans with such similar features could be so different in almost every other way. Cyrus was only a little taller than Iris, and the two shared blond hair, hazel eyes, and light-brown skin, though his was somewhat darker. But resemblance ended there.

Iris was clever and kind, yet prone to childish bursts of emotion. Cyrus was open-hearted, lazy, and honest enough to seem foolish when he forgot to think first.

“From what the mercenary said,” Hakon answered cautiously, “it should be something like a store that sells magic scrolls, but... not magitech.”

Like most foxkin, he disliked guessing.

The three continued trading opinions as they walked toward the forest road. The landscape followed the usual pattern of the region—dusty ground, pale plants, a tired sky, and the distant promise of danger. It did not take long before a large roadside building came into view.

None of them held especially high expectations.

Still, hope remained. That stubborn, foolish thing that refused to die even after Riverend had done its best to strangle it.

Perhaps the mercenary had been right. Perhaps this place might actually change something.

Anne had been busy restoring and cataloging the books Ceres brought when one of the most pleasant things possible happened.

Three brand-new potential customers walked in.

She took a quiet breath, smoothed her expression, and greeted them before excitement could rise too visibly.

The visitors moved slowly, looking around with open curiosity, which gave Anne time to study them properly. The first was a bulky young man who walked half a step ahead of the others. Warrior, almost certainly. Behind him came a petite girl in a pale red robe, carrying herself with the tense confidence of someone who needed people to know she was not to be dismissed. A mage, almost certainly a primancer. Behind both, and slightly apart, a foxkin moved with measured caution, his golden eyes scanning the bookstore while his red fur gave his restrained demeanor a strangely wild contrast.

An interesting group, for sure.

Foxkin rarely trusted humans enough to join their parties, and they were known for being sharp-minded, careful, and occasionally dangerous. Anne made a mental note to move more slowly with that one. Still, her early conclusions did nothing to weaken her confidence. If anything, they made the prospect of turning them into customers even more promising.

With her usual calm smile, she served three cups of tea and invited them to sit. The ritual always felt a little awkward with new guests, but the combination of warm drink, measured courtesy, and her own unhurried manner tended to smooth the roughest first encounters.

She waited until they had each taken a sip.

Then she asked, “Are you going to the forbidden forest?”

The question sounded ordinary, but it could reveal a great deal. So Anne watched them closely.

“No, we—” Cyrus began instinctively.

“Thank you for the tea,” Iris interrupted before he could finish. “Not today. But soon.”

The warrior looked down, embarrassed, and let the interruption stand. Anne did not mind. The rudeness itself was less important than what it revealed. The interruption, the quick embarrassment, the tension beneath the answer... yes, this party was struggling. They had not come here because things were going well. They had come for help.

And help, naturally, began with understanding the problem.

“I see,” Anne said easily. “I’m not an adventurer myself, but I have always found the profession fascinating. It is one of the pillars of our society. Trade, development, security—so much depends on it. But adventuring has changed a great deal recently, and it will continue changing. The guild-centered model from the older cities is no longer enough now that the forbidden forest is opening again.”

She saw immediately that she had their attention, even the foxkin’s, so she continued.

“Ten years is not long enough for the borderlands to find its pace. Every border town is still trying to create its own system of exploration, and none of them are finished. Places differ. People too. Sometimes success is not about forcing your way through one path, but finding the right place at the right time.”

The words were vaguer than Anne would have liked. Inwardly, she found them mediocre. Still, nothing better came to her in that moment, and when she saw the party’s expression shift, she knew the gamble had worked.

The idea had reached them.

“Riverend is not a good place,” Iris said after a hesitation that made the confession feel heavier. Admitting failure, even to a stranger, was difficult. It meant allowing it to become real. She drank more tea before continuing. “We worked there for a year, and none of us advanced to bronze tier. Everything is expensive. Even the better routes cost money. The low-tier guild jobs barely pay in copper, and the other low-tier parties...” She stopped for a second, anger tightening her voice. “They’ve started using robbery and similar trash to advance. We won’t do that.”

Emotion stormed beneath her words, though she held it back with visible effort.

“Well,” Anne said gently, smiling in a way that was reassuring without becoming soft, “it is better to remain firm in your convictions. Your chance will come. I’m sure of it.”

And with that, she prepared to work her craft.
