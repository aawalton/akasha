Space undulated above Aodhán as he and Nymera walked into the mansion, following behind the man who had served as both mentor and father to him for over ten years.

Aodhán still couldn’t believe his eyes. Couldn’t believe that this was real.

Elder Gyatso had been the one to accept him into the monastery, taking him off the hands of the travelers who had found him roaming the forest. He had been the one to appoint him North-Wing coordinator. He’d been at every birthday, and had been the one to teach him his first prayer.

In many ways, he was the father Aodhán had never had. A monk that made the whole charade worth it.

He should be back on Earth, reciting a prayer or teaching a sermon. Yet here he was, an entire universe away from home—like Aodhán himself—sitting cross-legged just as he’d always done back in the monastery.

Aodhán instinctively mirrored the motion, legs folding in remembrance of something he’d done a thousand times before. Meredith handed him and Nymera cold jugs of water, and after taking a sip, Aodhán asked,

“You have to tell me everything that happened after my disappearance. I know that must have been a trying time for the monastery, but how… how is any of this possible?”

Elder Gyatso smiled. “Well, it’s all thanks to you. And yes, your disappearance was a trying time indeed. We thought perhaps you’d been preyed upon by wolves.” His smile widened slightly as he shook his head. “It’s nice to know that we were wrong.”

Aodhán smiled back, and Elder Gyatso continued. “After your disappearance—which you’ll still have to tell me how—I and several other monks went out en masse to search for you. We searched the mountains, the flower fields, the forest—we searched everywhere we thought you could have gone, but didn’t find anything. Elder Donovan blamed himself for giving you that photography assignment.” His smile dimmed. “He blamed himself for giving you permission to leave the monastery on your own. And then he blamed me for approving it.”

Aodhán felt guilt rise within him, and in a small voice, he whispered, “I’m sorry. It was never my intention not to make it back.”

Elder Gyatso chuckled softly. “Of course. But we didn’t know that. After weeks of searching without finding anything, we thought perhaps you’d run. If you’d been preyed upon, we would have found something—scraps of clothing, blood, bone—anything. It made sense that you’d run, knowing how eccentric you were.”

“Hey! I wasn’t eccentric.” Aodhán’s expression brightened as he defended himself, glaring at Nymera, who turned sharply to smile at him.

“I wasn’t eccentric,” he repeated, feeling even more self-conscious as Nymera burst into laughter. Meredith soon joined her, and even Elder Gyatso chuckled.

“You were very different, Aodhán,” he said, a wide smile on his face. “Strange, even, right from the very first day I saw you.” He shook his head in amusement. “You probably can’t even remember it, but you scared the living daylight out of your friends in those early years. You were the child who survived three days in a forest alone without anyone. You were the child who watched his house and parents burn in a fire. The last thing you were was normal.”

Those words sparked a deep sadness within Aodhán, and unease settled in the air. Memories resurfaced, and this time, Aodhán didn’t push them aside. He let himself relive them. He let himself feel the emotions. And then he made light of them.

“Okay, fine. I was eccentric. What next?”

Elder Gyatso smiled. “We returned to the monastery. We were sad, but after two weeks of searching, there was nothing else we could do. Only I… I couldn’t rest. I couldn’t simply give up like the other elders. You were my favorite, after all.” He smiled sadly. “I was desperate to find something. To find anything. And that was when it occurred to me that there was one place we hadn’t quite checked. Your home.”

Aodhán froze, a frown marring his features. “My home?”

“Indeed,” Elder Gyatso nodded. “It was a long shot, but until I checked it out, I knew I couldn’t rest. So the next day, I left for your home.”

The emotions were stronger now—long-buried feelings welling up within his chest like a rising tide.

Aodhán pushed through it, his voice barely a whisper. “I haven’t been since… since I left.”

His chest felt heavy, his breath turned ragged, but just when he thought he might have to force the emotions back down or activate Axis of Ruin, a hand clasped his.

Soft. Warm. Reassuring.

Aodhán glanced at Nymera’s hand, feeling a shudder race down his spine even as his emotions began to steady. A part of him wanted to pull away, to put up a brave front—and perhaps if Nymera had been someone else, or if Elder Gyatso had been someone else, he would have.

Instead, he let her hold him and forced himself to ask, “What does it look like now?”

“Charred,” Elder Gyatso replied quietly. “Charred and overgrown. The fire hadn’t spared much, and what little remained had been overtaken by the forest. It was abandoned, and the moment I set my eyes on it, I just knew you hadn’t been there either. Everything seemed… static. As if, aside from the crawling vines that covered every inch of the building, the entire place had been left untouched by time.”

He shook his head, his gaze turning contemplative. “I had the distinct sense that no one had been there since you left. Yet I had to see for myself—to be certain you weren’t in there. I would never have forgiven myself if I left and you were inside. So I cut my way through the crawling vines at the entrance and stepped into the building.”

“I wasn’t there,” Aodhán stated, and Elder Gyatso nodded.

“No, you weren’t. There was nothing living in the building. Not even animals—save for one thing: a floating, pulsing core at the center of your charred living room.”

“A legacy,” Nymera whispered.

Elder Gyatso nodded. “Indeed. It called to me, and out of curiosity, I touched it.”

“And that’s how you became a transmigrant.” Meredith smiled, nudging Elder Gyatso playfully on the shoulder. “It doesn’t make sense that you’ve been keeping it secret for so long.”

Nymera chuckled. “It’s a beautiful transmigration story, Elder Gyatso. If only all our stories were so beautiful.”

“Oh, what a wonderful dream,” Meredith groaned, and they all laughed.

They all laughed—save for Aodhán, who had gone completely still, his mind looping around a single piece of information.

A legacy in his parents’ living room.

How was that possible?

Legacies could only be left behind by Ascendants. Which meant, somehow, someway, an Ascendant had visited his house in the years after the fire. Or perhaps—even worse—on the night of the fire.

That made two. Two Ascendants present that night. Two Ascendants leaving legacies behind. Was that a coincidence? What did it mean?

Elder Zarxan had suggested that Az’marthon was undoubtedly there on the night of the fire and must have been responsible for his safe passage through the forest. But if that was the case, then who was this second Ascendant?

“Aodhán?” Nymera’s voice cut into his thoughts, her expression worried.

“Are you alright?” she asked, but Aodhán was barely listening. Feeling a knot of dread begin to pool in his gut, he looked up at Elder Gyatso and asked,

“Did… did your patron leave behind a mental fragment?”

Elder Gyatso frowned. “No. I wasn’t quite so lucky. She did reveal herself to me once, though.”

“She?” Aodhán asked, feeling his heart thunder in his ears.

Nymera’s grip on his hand tightened. Her gaze sharpened. “What is it, Aodhán?”

But Elder Gyatso was oblivious to his growing panic, eyes closed and smile wide as he said, “Indeed. She looked quite ordinary, but her power…” He shook his head. “Outstanding. Would you like to see her?”

“Yes,” Aodhán responded immediately. “Can—can you do that?”

Elder Gyatso chuckled, and an instant later, what seemed to be a holographic screen appeared—seemingly projected from his mind—displaying the face of a woman Aodhán would have recognized anywhere.

Almost instantly, all his memories of her sharpened. Her usually blurred face gained detail. Definition.

And with a shuddering breath, Aodhán realized who he was looking at.

His mother.

His own mother.

His memories fractured in that instant, and Aodhán realized his past was far more complex than anything he could have imagined. One thing, however, was clear.

He was the son of an Ascendant.

"Aodhán?" Gyatso’s voice cut through his spiraling thoughts, concerned now. "You've gone pale. Do you… do you know her?"

Aodhán’s throat closed. He couldn’t speak. He couldn’t think.

Nymera’s hand tightened around his once more, grounding him—but it wasn’t enough.

"That's my mother," he finally whispered, voice breaking. "That's my mother."

Far away from the sect, while Aodhán dealt with the existential crisis that was his past, the alliance of enemy sects had called another meeting. Only this time, they had a new member.

Elder Helion Varkas of the Solar Sect.

With the Storm Sect’s alliance expanding, they’d been forced to do the same, and though the Solar Sect wasn’t quite as powerful as the Light Sect—their first choice—they were still formidable.

They stood around a wooden table now—all six of them—reviewing everything of importance that had happened since the war began. Silence reigned within the hall as scenes flashed across gigantic screens, the only sound the faint whoosh of static as the holographic projections glitched—until finally, they reached the icon activation and the catastrophe that had followed.

“By the burning dawn!” Elder Varkas whispered under his breath, his eyes wide as he took in the devastation. “This…” he began after the dust cleared, but words failed him. In the end, he simply shook his head. “Tell me we did great damage with this. Surely, an explosion like that must have had some serious effect.”

Disappointed sighs echoed through the hall in response, and Elder Aspwick leaned back slightly, exhaling something weary and heavy. “The severity of the damage caused matters little if it is misplaced. Our main targets escaped.”

Varkas raised an eyebrow in shock, clearly taken aback. “That’s… impressive.” He let out a small breath. “I take it that’s the doing of the Space Sect?”

Elder Clearfall nodded, then glanced at Elder Mournwind. “What’s our current status? Has anything changed with the Storm Origin Plane in the last three days?”

“Nothing,” Elder Mournwind said, shaking his head slowly. “The Origin Plane has been… still.” His brows furrowed. “Nevertheless, we cannot rest yet. The Storm Plane is still over a quarter larger than it was two months ago. An expansion that large means improved strength among their cultivators. More awakenings. More ascensions—which in turn lead to more legacies.”

“And more legacies mean more double inheritors and transmigrants,” Elder Malverin finished softly, her voice almost lost in the quiet. She shook her head. “Elder Aspwick is right. We cannot rest yet. Balance is still very far from being restored.”

A heavy silence descended on the room. Then Elder Clearfall spoke. “What shall we do then? After our attack on the viewing deck, I doubt the Storm Sect will be letting their transmigrants back onto the battlefield.”

Elder Aspwick nodded slowly. “They’ll be protected. Shielded behind the sect walls.”

“We’ll have to lure them out,” Elder Malverin sighed, her words reluctant. “There’s no point to the war if they are not in it. It doesn’t matter how many Mythics we kill. As long as they are alive, their Origin Plane will not shrink.”

Another beat of silence passed, and Varkas grimaced. “How do we intend to do that?”

The elders glanced at each other, their silence heavy, their gazes weighted. Seconds passed. Then more. The air itself seemed to thicken as the silence stretched.

And then, after what felt like hours of quiet contemplation, Elder Aspwick spoke.

“I hate the idea, but I know we are all thinking it.”

“It’s a bad idea,” Malverin refuted immediately, her expression resolute. “There’s a reason none of us are saying it, Aspwick.”

Elder Mournwind sighed. “It’s the only way we can lure them out into the battlefield.”

“No,” Elder Sang-Hoon shook his head, grief flashing across his eyes. “We’ve tried it before. It did not work. I’m not ready to lose another transmigrant.”

“Nor I—my only transmigrant,” Malverin nodded in agreement. “We’ve tried this plan before. I did not… it didn’t work in our favor. Perhaps you have forgotten, Aspwick—”

“I did not forget.” Aspwick shook his head. “The memory is as fresh in my mind as warm blood. I did not forget. But if you have a better idea, I would very much like to hear it.”

For nearly a minute, no one spoke. Then Varkas broke the silence. “What if we target the families of their double inheritors to force them to come out?” He glanced at the screen as Darien’s image appeared and smiled faintly. “If I’m not mistaken, I believe he has a father and a sibling in the Sect of Entropy. If we threaten to kill them, we can force—”

“Absolutely not,” Malverin cut him off, disbelief coloring her features. “We are trying to restore balance here, not become monsters.”

“Aren’t we?” Varkas challenged, his tone calm but pointed. “Because from where I stand, you’ve already killed thousands.” He spread his hands slightly. “What’s a kidnapping or two if it ends the war?”

Silence answered him.

A long, uncomfortable silence.

And then Aspwick spoke.

“Mordred Velkaris.” He said it quietly. “I believe that’s the name of his father.”

Malverin turned toward him slowly, disbelief flashing openly across her face now. For a moment, it seemed she might speak again—might argue, might refuse.

But the room remained silent.

One by one, the other elders looked away.

Seconds passed.

No one objected.

Finally, Elder Clearfall released a long, weary sigh.

“It is settled, then.” He folded his hands behind his back, staring at the image on the screen as though trying to convince himself of the necessity. “Let’s see if we can get in touch with his father.”

Another pause followed before he added quietly,

“We have scarred the earth in the name of balance. We are already monsters.”
