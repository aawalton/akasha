The world smelled like rain-wet stone and the faintest hint of moss.

Elynara blinked against the soft daylight spilling past her shutters. Her back ached and legs refused to move. Her body refused the idea of morning on principle alone, and every muscle filed a complaint. Even her fingers were quietly sulking.

“Stars above,” she muttered. “I didn’t sign up to be ninety.”

Starpuff pulsed smugly against her collarbone, wedged perfectly like a personal neck warmer who absolutely knew it was the favorite child. It didn’t move when she poked it. Or when she shifted. It just glowed brighter, like it knew she couldn’t do anything without disturbing it.

“Get off,” she whispered.

It didn’t.

Well. Fine then.

She stayed still a moment longer, soaking in the quiet. The rain had finally stopped – she could hear the world breathing again outside. No more heavy drumming against the roof, just that fresh after-rain stillness. The kind where even birds seemed to take a moment before chirping again.

Eventually, with the grace of a tree learning how to walk, she rolled upright.

Her joints cracked like old floorboards. Her shoulders protested. Her legs muttered rude things under their breath. But she was up. Mostly. Barely.

And that was when it hit her.

Right. The Plan.

She paused halfway through a stretch, blinking at nothing.

The Plan.

The definitely real, extremely coherent, not-at-all-vague Plan.

“…Hells.”

The Plan, as it stood, was more of a Feeling. An Urge. A capital-I Idea. Somewhere between “fix the world” and “do something meaningful before breakfast.” She scratched her head, ruffling her hair into more of a nest than it already was.

Okay. Deep breath.

She sat cross-legged on her bed, staff resting across her knees, trying to find the beginning. Not the part where she wanted to help people. That one was easy. But the actual first step? Slightly less obvious.

Well, it seemed obvious, yesterday.

Just teach people magic, Elynara, it will all work out somehow! Yes, maybe, kind of? It would be a start, but what then? It might have been the core idea, but the reality of there being very little mana around stayed.

So what could she really do? What did she actually have to work with?

She had mana. And some training, largely self-taught. Some notes she took. And… probably more raw experience than anyone else in town at this point. Which was both exciting and terrifying. What if that really was true?

But the more she thought about it, the more that spark grew inside her.

If she could show someone how to shape mana—just the tiniest bit—that would mean everything. It would mean it was possible. That this world didn’t have to keep crawling toward entropy. That the decline could be slowed. Reversed, even.

And yet…

Mana was scarce. Her own passive regen seemed to outpace everyone else's by quite a lot. Without it, without her Celestia-given bonuses, would she even be a mage? What about the average person? They couldn’t train if they barely had the energy to start.

Unless…

Unless she gave them the spark.

Mana-infused objects. She knew of mana batteries, monster cores, and even just spirit offerings being able to hold mana. Maybe she could supply them enough mana to practice shaping that way? Carvings worked for spirits, but leaked mana constantly. Still… It would be something to get past that first hurdle, even if inefficient.

She didn’t need to teach mastery. Heck, she wasn’t there yet herself! But just one person at first. One spark.

The thought pulsed inside her chest like a heartbeat. Warm and bright.

Yes, her plan was set. Or an inkling of one, at least. And now…

She closed her eyes and breathed in slowly.

It was time to train.

Her morning cycles had become routine now. Inhale, gather. Hold. Feel the stretch. Release. Inhale again. The rhythm calmed her, centered her. And it worked. Her core reached that familiar near-cap soon enough.

Ninety-four.

She smiled.

Then, casually, she lifted her fingers and called a spark to life. A single flicker danced above her palm—shaped into a flickering butterfly, just to see if she still could do it. Magic really was fascinating… just a little bit of work and she could have her own light show anytime she wanted.

Of course, Starpuff exploded off her shoulder like it had been waiting exactly for that.

The little puffball spun in a corkscrew, dove after the flame, nearly clipped the edge of her blanket, and bounced off her knee before correcting midair with a chirp that could only be described as smug.

“Show-off,” she murmured, and made a second butterfly.

The two danced through the room, looping slowly, glowing softly as the morning light brightened outside the window. Starpuff chased them with wild abandon, wings flickering through excited color bursts.

And as she watched the chaos unfold, a grin tugged at the corners of her mouth.

Yes.

This could work.

She didn’t know exactly how yet. The Plan had a lot of gaps. A lot of maybes. But it wasn’t a terrible one, at least. It could work.

However far the goal would be, a single step forward was still just that important.

All she had to do was to start.

Starpuff chirped in agreement, or whatever passed for one. And as soon as signs of life outside hit her, she was up and about, readying for the day.

The scent of honeyed bread met her the moment she stepped into the hallway.

Warm. Fresh. Slightly too good for how early it still was.

Her legs, despite having spent the last hour arguing with her spine about whether movement was even legal today, suddenly remembered how to walk again. Possibly even skip. Maybe. If bribed with enough butter.

Notebook tucked tight under one arm, she shuffled into the dining hall with purpose.

Father Alaric was already there, sleeves rolled and setting a small basket of baked goods near the window. Calm as ever, like morning light had decided to manifest as a person. Mina sat nearby, shoulders still hunched in sleepy comfort, while Pufflet had claimed her foot like a prize and blinked at Elynara as if deciding whether she was food or family today.

“Good morning, Elynara,” Alaric said without looking up. “You look like someone who’s been struck by inspiration.”

She grinned, sliding into the seat beside Mina.

“Possibly dangerous inspiration. Brilliant, even. Or incredibly foolish. I haven’t decided.”

That made Mina’s head lift immediately, eyes narrowing in suspicion—or curiosity, it was always hard to tell before breakfast.

Elynara reached for her bowl, took one bite, and promptly abandoned all pretense of patience.

“Father—I want to teach Mina mana manipulation.”

Spoon. Pause.

Even Pufflet blinked.

Mina, for her part, simply froze. Her hands still curled around her bowl like it might offer support. Or deflection.

Elynara plowed forward, notebook already open in front of her. “I’ve been thinking. A lot. If I could find a way to help people shape mana—even just a little—it wouldn’t matter how slow their recovery is. I could charge something, right? Tools. Trinkets. Tiny sparks. Give them just enough to work with.”

She glanced at Mina.

“And I think she could do it. Really do it. She’s already good with animals and patient with children. And focused. And honestly probably more patient than me.”

Mina opened her mouth. Closed it. Then gave a tiny nod of agreement, like even she didn’t know what she was agreeing to yet.

Father Alaric exhaled in that slow, thoughtful way of his, as if quietly checking the wind before setting a boat loose. His brow lifted just a little, the corner of his mouth edging toward a smile.

“So you’re asking to be a teacher now?”

“I’m asking to try,” she said, quick. Honest. “If she wants to.”

Mina sat up straighter, her voice soft but steady.

“I… I mean, yes? I think I do?”

Elynara’s grin threatened to split her face.

Alaric sipped his tea, gaze calm but not distant. Then, finally, with the weight of someone laying down the first stone on a path yet built, he said:

“Very well. As long as no one catches on fire.”

“No promises,” she blurted, then immediately cringed. “Um, okay, that came out wrong, sorry. Safe and slow. I swear.”

That earned a full chuckle from him, eyes crinkling.

“I expect updates,” he said, standing to fetch another pot of tea. “And for the church to remain in one piece.”

“We’ll be very responsible,” Elynara said with a very responsible tone. That Starpuff immediately undermined by letting out a victorious squeal and knocking into a cup somewhere behind them.

Mina giggled.

Then paused. Slowly blinked.

“Wait. I’m really doing this?”

“You are,” Elynara replied, already flipping pages in her notebook.

“And you’re really going to teach me.”

“I sure am.”

Mina stared.

Elynara scribbled something in the margins. Probably unrelated. Probably important anyway.

And then, slowly, they both smiled.

One of those quiet, slightly nervous, can’t-believe-it’s-happening sort of smiles.

Behind them, Starpuff spun in celebratory loops. Then got tangled in a curtain.

The day had officially begun.

The little corner behind the storeroom didn’t look like much.

A few crates, an overturned bucket, and a strip of stone warmed by morning sun. The air still smelled faintly of herbs from the church kitchen windows. Not far, but far enough to feel like their own space. A classroom in miniature.

Elynara sat with her legs crossed, staff resting across her knees, and notebook open beside her. She watched Mina settle nearby with nervous determination, hands clasped in her lap like she wasn’t sure whether to pray or prepare.

“Alright,” Elynara said, brushing away a few lingering crumbs from breakfast. “First step: just feel it. Don’t try to shape anything. Don’t pull or push. Just… reach. Like you’re sitting by a fire you can’t see, and trying to feel the warmth through your hands.”

Mina nodded slowly, already furrowing her brow in concentration. Elynara lifted her own hand, palm open and steady, and let her mana gather there.

She did so without any shaping. Just a soft, pulsing shimmer, low and steady. A quiet rhythm her core knew well by now. Like breath.

“Now bring your hand close,” she said. “Not touching. Just… hover it here.”

Mina moved carefully, her fingers shaking slightly as they hovered above Elynara’s. She bit her lip but didn’t pull back. Good sign.

“Don’t focus too hard,” Elynara added gently. “Your skin knows what to do. You’re not hunting it. Just listening.”

The courtyard breeze brushed past them, lifting the edge of her cloak. Somewhere above, a dove cooed lazily from the roof tiles. Starpuff floated into view a few moments later, settling on a crate like a smug supervisor overseeing the lesson.

They sat in stillness for a while. One minute. Then two.

Mina’s brow furrowed deeper. Her mouth pulled into a soft frown.

“…Nothing,” she said quietly, pulling her hand back. “I don’t think I’m doing it right.”

“That’s okay,” Elynara said, quick to respond. “Really. It took me a while too. Some people need hours. Or days. Or weeks. But once you feel it, that’s it. You’ll never forget how.”

Mina looked down, shoulders curled slightly inward, uncertain.

Elynara smiled and bumped her shoulder lightly. “And I’m not letting you give up anyway. I’ve got an entire day of ridiculous teaching plans. We’re in this now.”

That earned her a tiny laugh.

She stretched her arms above her head, spine cracking in protest.

“Alright. Lesson two, incoming. It’s time for dangerous experiments with possibly flammable wood.”

Mina blinked.

“What?”

“Don’t worry. That’s a joke. Mostly.”

From her satchel, Elynara pulled out the piece of wood she’d picked up yesterday—dense, barkless, and smooth. She didn’t know the name, but it felt like the right kind of thing to test with. Worked for spirit offerings, at least!

She held it in one hand, staff in the other.

“Okay. The theory is… if mana can be stored inside things and tools, then even simple things should be able to hold it too. At least a little. So I’m going to try and make this stick into a very basic mana battery.”

Mina nodded slowly.

“What happens if it breaks?”

Elynara considered.

“A broken stick just means two sticks.”

Mina giggled at that.

And then, after taking a deep breath, Elynara focused—trying to pour and compress it directly into the piece. She sent a pulse out.

And the stick cracked straight through.

“…Oops.”

A sharp split ran from one end to the other. The halves fell apart in her hands with a sad little thunk.

Starpuff squeaked, flickering green with what was definitely smug amusement.

Elynara glared at it.

“You’re not helping.”

Mina looked like she was trying very hard not to laugh.

“Take two,” Elynara said, grabbing another piece from the pouch. “This time, gently.”

The second attempt went better. She pressed mana into the wood a little slower, a little less. And stopped once it felt like pouring more in started meeting resistance.

It held this time, and seemed to pulse faintly with mana.

Turning to Mina, she offered both of the attempts to the waiting girl, who accepted them like someone had just handed her a sleeping bird and told her not to sneeze.

“They’ve got some mana in them,” Elynara said. “Try to feel it. Carry them around a bit if you want. Keep the whole one close, it should help.”

Mina looked down at the pieces, cradled in her palms.

“Will that really work?”

“It will,” Elynara said. “Eventually. I might need to recharge them later as they’re losing mana constantly. But if you manage to notice that... you’ll be on a good track already!”

A moment passed.

“Also don’t chew on them.”

“I wasn’t going to—!”

“Good instincts!”

That earned her an embarrassed nudge to the shoulder.

And that was that. First test done. First steps taken.

Elynara leaned back against the crate, exhaling slowly.

The Plan—whatever shape it would take—had officially started.

Now she simply had to keep that spark alive.

But for now, it was guild time!

After wishing Mina best of luck, Elynara walked out of the church, breathing in the fresh morning air. It smelled so clean - and humid - the rain washing everything off.

The clouds hadn’t even fully scattered yet, but the light was here anyway. Soft gold bleeding between them, as if the sky was still deciding how much effort to put into the day.

She liked this kind of weather.

It was just cool enough to wake you up without complaint.

Her boots splashed once before she adjusted her path along the drier edge of the cobbles.

Starpuff drifted ahead at head height, twirling slow lazy loops like it was pretending to scout. She giggled and let herself walk and stretch and breathe a little deeper.

Her thoughts didn’t feel quite as tangled today.

The Plan helped.

Even if it was still barely formed. Even if it was still more hope than structure. It helped.

She reached the halfway mark to the guild when something flickered faintly at the edge of her senses. Just a quiet ripple of something.

Mana. Faint. Ambient.

The breeze tugged at her scarf as she stopped to tap her staff once against the edge of a worn stone. Her senses stretched, held, and retracted again. Just enough to confirm what she already knew.

Her range had grown.

“Huh.”

She smiled to herself, adjusted her grip on the staff, and kept walking.

The guildhall was the same as she remembered. Still a little creaky at the door. Still smelling of parchment, adventurers, and worn leather. Quieter today, though. It seemed like post-storm days always brought a sort of slow start. People lingered at home, while traders checked their stock.

Elynara entered confidently out of habit.

And then immediately spotted Marcus standing far too close to the quest board for comfort.

She froze behind a decorative pillar, watching him lazily twirl a wooden training baton in one hand while reading through slips like they were snacks.

She was not getting roped into another footwork test today.

Her legs remembered.

“Very subtle,” Alicia called from behind the desk without looking up.

Elynara slunk into view, sighing.

“Just pretend I am the wind today.”

“You’re a breeze with poor timing,” Alicia said, already sliding two slips across the desk. “In-town errands. Light. No surprises.”

Elynara gave her the most grateful look she could manage.

“You’re my hero.”

“Better,” Alicia said, tapping her pen once against the counter. “Finish those and come back. I’ve got something for you.”

That tone always meant important. Elynara nodded and tucked the requests into her satchel, already skimming the descriptions as she walked.

A parcel to the leatherworker. A plant bundle to the apothecary. Help them both around. Simple. Easy.

Perfect.

The streets welcomed her with familiar faces and lazy waves. The baker’s boy nearly tripped over a cart trying to greet her. A curtain twitched near the herb stall. The air buzzed quietly with life returning to its routines.

And after the downpour of the past few days gone, she felt unburdened, and moved with fresh purpose.

Not even two hours have passed before she finished both of the errands, only really taking so long because the apothecary was a chatty grandma, and interesting to listen to. Elynara was happy to learn more about the herbs too. Might help her in future quests!

And she even got some unexpected sweets out of it!

She popped one in her mouth.

Mmh, sweet and herbal. Not minty though.

Snacking on her sweet prize, her slightly damp boots took her back to the guild. Her hands warm from the satchel straps.

When she returned, Alicia was waiting.

The desk was quiet now, and the space behind it felt more private somehow. A thin folder already rested beside her elbow. She tapped it once.

“Your name came up.”

Elynara blinked.

“Mine?”

Alicia nodded.

“Yes, a request from the farms. From the same village you helped before. They asked for you directly.”

She slid the request slip forward. Elynara reached for it slowly.

“They mentioned the spirits. The advice. Apparently, you left an impression.”

She read the slip.

Then reread it.

Halfway through the second pass, her frown curled into a smile.

Not a big one.

But a quiet, building thing.

“I have a new Plan.”

Alicia raised an eyebrow.

“That’s never a reassuring sentence.”

Elynara didn’t answer.

Not really.

Her thoughts were already halfway down the road—past the old shrines, past the forest trails. What if she could reach more people? What if the teachings worked? If even one or two of them could learn to shape mana, even just sense it…

What if they could spread that knowledge?

What if she asked Father Alaric?

For more than just permission to teach Mina.

What if she asked for trust?

Just enough to do something bigger.

She tucked the slip into her notebook.

“Thank you,” she said.

Alicia still looked skeptical.

“For the record, I don’t want to clean up any exploded barns.”

Elynara grinned.

“You won’t have to.”

And then she turned and stepped back into the brightening day.

The Plan was getting bigger.

The sun had crept high enough to warm the flagstones by the time Elynara returned to the church, its light spilling through high windows like it belonged there. Like a soft blanket across the hallway that made the day feel a little more forgiving than usual.

She stopped briefly at the doorway to adjust the strap on her satchel, then stepped inside.

One more thing today.

One more little ask, for the sake of her definitely-not-half-baked magical plan.

The first step: locate Mina.

The secondary step: prevent Starpuff from causing minor property damage.

Too late.

Starpuff, clearly inspired by sunshine and the delusion of helpfulness, had already zipped ahead. Within thirty seconds it had startled the laundry cat into a yowling dash, who then knocked over an empty bucket, right in the path of a passing nun carrying a cloth-wrapped bundle. She stopped, surprised, and Starpuff hovered directly in front her, like a particularly smug moth. The poor woman nearly flailed the bundle into the ceiling.

Elynara swooped in just in time to catch it, smiled with all the sheepishness she could muster, and then swatted the puff away with a pointed whisper:

“No mana snacks if you keep this up.”

Starpuff did a slow, lazy loop of protest. She ignored it.

After helping rewrap the linens and stack them into a neat pile—mostly to soothe her guilt—she started scanning the corridor corners.

Still no Mina.

She found her eventually in the side hall near the back staircase, arms crossed, head down, staring at the floor like it had personally wronged her.

Elynara didn’t slow down.

“Hey,” she said, already grabbing her by the arm. “You’re helping me with water buckets today.”

Mina blinked.

“What—why?”

“Because I need your biceps. And also your eyes. And also because I need someone to complain at while I do it.”

“I—what?”

“Come on. Pufflet’s coming too. It’s a team effort now.”

To Mina’s credit, she only grumbled once before following.

They worked in the courtyard, refilling jugs and hauling buckets from the pump to the side basin. It wasn’t hard work, not really—but it gave their hands something to do. And more importantly, gave Elynara room to talk.

So she did. About the morning, about weather, about a merchant who tried to upsell her on ‘blessed soap flakes’ that looked suspiciously like shaved chalk.

Mina listened. Huffed. Then snorted.

Her scowl faded after the second bucket. By the third, her shoulders were starting to relax.

Pufflet trundled after them, making snort-sneeze noises every few minutes like it was sneezing out its soul. Starpuff, naturally, decided it was time to race the creature from bucket to bucket, puffing out its glow dramatically and doing celebratory spirals every time it arrived “first.”

Not that Pufflet even knew it was in a race, but it did chase after the weird invisible pokes and bonks regardless!

Seeing the confusion on Mina’s face, Elynara explained what was going on. The shy-serious girl took that ‘race’ surprisingly seriously.

“I think it’s cheating.”

“Absolutely,” Elynara agreed. “It has no legs. Which somehow makes it faster.”

Eventually, when the last of the buckets were stacked and the tools rinsed, Elynara slipped a hand into Mina’s satchel and pulled out the two wooden pieces she’d given her before. Mina blinked, clearly not expecting her to remember.

Elynara just smiled. Held them up to the light.

The broken one felt light in her hand now. Empty of mana. All the warmth from earlier gone. But the other?

It was still humming just slightly with what she had left inside it. It was faint, but there.

“Hah,” she murmured. “Look at that. Still kicking.”

She recharged it before giving the whole one back to Mina.

“This one—” she tapped the cracked half, “—I went too hard. Tried to flood it with mana. And the material clearly couldn’t hold it. I hoped it would, even in pieces. But no such luck.”

Mina winced.

“But this one,” she said, holding up the intact piece, “is still holding strong. I recharged it for you.”

Mina reached for the piece, fingers gentler now, and ran her thumb along its side, still trying to sense the mana.

Elynara gave her a quiet grin.

“Lesson of the day?” she said. “Mana doesn’t like being bullied. It’s dramatic like that.”

That earned her a small, quiet giggle.

The best kind.

And when Mina tucked the piece back into her pocket, she did it carefully.

It was just edging toward afternoon by the time they found Father Alaric.

The back hallway of the church was quiet, warmed by slanting light and faint smells of old parchment and cedar polish. A stack of cleaned candle tins sat near the corner, and Alaric stood beside them, robes faintly dusted from whatever he'd been rearranging in the outer alcoves. A narrow ledger was tucked beneath his arm.

He turned at the sound of footsteps.

His gaze swept over both of them—Elynara, clearly trying not to fidget, and Mina, trying not to hide behind her.

One eyebrow rose.

Elynara ignored it with the determination of someone who absolutely thought through her plan.

“Father Alaric,” she began, just a bit too brightly. “I have a request. A small one. Ish.”

The eyebrow didn’t move.

“I want to take Mina with me. On a short trip. Back to the farms near where I worked before. A few days, at most. They have asked for help again, and I would like her to come. There’s nothing dangerous, I promise. Just work. And learning. I want to show her more. Teach her more.”

She said it quickly, words tumbling forward like she was trying to outrun doubt. But when she finished, she stood tall—hands laced behind her back, shoulders steady even if her fingers twitched.

Mina shifted beside her.

“I... I want to go,” she said softly. “If it’s alright.”

Alaric looked at her first.

His expression softened.

“You want to bring Pufflet too, I presume?”

“Yes, please.”

That almost-smile twitched at the corner of his mouth.

Then he turned back to Elynara, and the full weight of priestly seriousness settled behind his next words.

“And you will keep her safe.”

“I will,” Elynara said at once. “She’ll be safe. She’ll be warm. She’ll be fed. I’ll make sure of it. Not a single toe out of place.”

“Not even one?”

“Well, maybe the pinky toe—”

He sighed. Closed his eyes.

Then reached out, took both of their hands into his, and lowered his voice.

“May Celestia guide your path. And may your feet walk safe roads.”

His hands were warm. The words of the blessing wrapped around them like quiet sunlight—steady, certain, and grounding.

When he let go, neither of them spoke for a moment.

Then—

“I’ll pack snacks,” Elynara said, already grinning.

“I’ll bring extra socks,” Mina added, blinking like she hadn’t expected that to work.

They left with linked fingers and light steps, trailed faintly by Starpuff floating behind them in triumphant loops.

That night, under quilt and soft lantern glow, with Starpuff curled stubbornly against her cheek, Elynara lay wide awake and smiling.

Tomorrow, the world would get just a little bigger.

And this time, she wouldn’t be walking it alone.
