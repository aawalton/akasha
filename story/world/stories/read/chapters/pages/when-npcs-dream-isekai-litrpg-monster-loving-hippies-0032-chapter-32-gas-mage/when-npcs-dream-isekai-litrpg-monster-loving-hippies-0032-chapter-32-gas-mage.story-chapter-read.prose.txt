“I should go speak with Arjun,” I said. “He probably won’t attack me. We should hear him out.”

“Fine,” said Juliette. “But keep your distance, and be ready to bolt if he tries something.”

“We should also talk about what to tell him first,” Hyun-woo added.

I turned and shouted toward Arjun, “I’ll come! Just give us a few minutes!”

“Take your time,” he shouted back.

“Okay,” Hyun-woo began in a low voice. “First, what they already know?”

“They know we’re manipulative bastards,” I said. “No point pretending innocence. They know we lied, tricked them, and stole their gold. I don’t know how much they know about Miguel, Santiago, and Charlotte though.”

“They can probably guess about Miguel,” Juliette said. “He wanted to contact Arjun’s group, then vanished the same night we betrayed them.”

“Yeah,” I agreed. “They won’t believe he joined our side. They’ll assume we killed him.”

“I see no reason to lie about Santiago and Charlotte,” said Hyun-woo. “Farhad’s group may already suspect they joined us. Maybe it’ll weaken some people’s loyalty. There have to be others who don’t agree with Farhad’s way of doing things.”

“He mentioned a warning,” Juliette said. “Maybe they’ll offer us a chance to run before the attack?”

“How kind of them,” I muttered. “Okay, I’ll mostly tell the truth—except that we’ll eagerly take the chance to flee, because we’re weaklings with no real way to fight back.”

Hyun-woo and Juliette nodded. I walked casually toward Arjun.

I knew he was level two. He could attack me, kidnap me—probably before I’d have time to react. But I didn’t think he was the kind to pull a nasty trick like that. Nasty tricks were our thing.

Besides, it was a risk we needed me to take. This was important. My acting skills could make a difference in the battle to come. I wasn’t going to feign innocence, but I did need to fake weakness.

I came to a small clearing where Arjun sat on a low rock. I stopped about four yards away and sat down across from him.

“So here I am,” I said. “What does Farhad want to tell us?”

“First, Farhad has questions,” Arjun said. “Number one: what happened to Miguel, Charlotte, and Santiago? Did you kill them?”

“We killed Miguel,” I said, keeping my tone steady. “He tried to contact you using that magical device. I tried to stop him, tried to take it away. He punched me, and I panicked. I pulled a knife. He attacked again, and I… I stabbed him.”

I looked him in the eye.

“I didn’t mean for it to end that way. It’s probably not worth much, but I am sorry.”

A long lie—except the last part. That was true. I often thought about Miguel’s death, and yeah… I did feel terrible about it.

“Charlotte and Santiago?” he asked. “You killed them too?”

“What? No,” I said. “They’re here, in Kamatouu. They asked to join us, and we agreed.”

“You expect me to—” he started, but I cut him off.

“You don’t have to believe me. They said they didn’t like how Farhad does things. Said his war against the system would doom you all. They also said some of your people are just thugs—and they didn’t want to be part of that. Think about it. Does that fit with what you know of them?”

Arjun was taken aback. He looked thoughtful.

“…Maybe,” he said. “Maybe you’re telling the truth.”

“Something else?” I prompted.

“Yes,” Arjun said with a pained look. “Farhad wants to know… why you did it. Why you betrayed him. He really thought you were good people. He just wants to understand. Honestly… I want to know too.”

“It’s because we’re hippies.” I said simply.

“…Excuse me?”

“It’s simple,” I said. “We love the forest. We love the creatures. We like the NPCs. We like working with them. We like the town. We even like the goblins. You people want to go to attack all of that. Conquer towns, kill NPCs, enslave them, kill sentient creatures just for XP.”

“They’re monsters,” Arjun said, defensive. “You’re supposed to kill them for XP. And NPCs—they’re not real people.”

“You’re wrong!” I snapped, my voice rising. “NPCs are people. They have feelings. Goblins too. The truth is, behind your friendly speeches, you’re just a bunch of thugs and killers.”

“You… you could have talked to us,” he said, visibly shaken. “Explained this. We would’ve listened. We’re not bad people.”

“Maybe you would’ve listened. But that’s not even the worst of it,” I said. “The bird—the stupid talking cursing bird? She’s a player. A real person like you and me. Just from another world... You would’ve butchered her for XP.”

“That’s a lie!” he shouted. “I don’t believe—"

“Believe what you want!” I shouted back. I was angry—too angry. I was supposed to be faking weakness, not yelling like this. Damn it. But these bastards always pissed me off. I wanted to say this to their faces.

“Farhad wants to do things better,” Arjun said, voice colder now. “I’ll pass along what you said. About the bird, the goblins, and the NPCs. But at least we didn’t murder our host. That’s the greatest sin of all.”

“I never said we were saints,” I snapped. “I just answered your fucking questions. Now get to the point already. What does Farhad want?”

He stared at me—cold, sharp. He understood me. He hated me. That was fine. This was business.

“Farhad is a good man,” Arjun said quietly. “Even now, after everything, he doesn’t want unnecessary bloodshed. We will attack Kamatouu. And we’ll come with a great force. Any player who stays indoors during the battle, we won’t consider an enemy. If anyone fights with the town guards, they’ll be killed. No mercy.”

He paused, then continued.

“After we take the city, Farhad will offer the remaining players a chance to join him. Except you—you’ll face judgment for the murder if Miguel. If you stay.”

“If I stay?” I asked, curious.

“Farhad, in a show of generosity—against my advice—wants to give you this.”

He took a folded paper from his pocket and handed it to me. I opened it.

It was a map. A real map. Of the area around Kamatouu. Maps were rare. This thing was valuable.

“There’s a small town in a neighboring kingdom,” Arjun said. “Five or six days west. Farhad is giving anyone who wants it a chance to leave. It’s a long journey through the forest, but you’re such a forest genius—you’ll be fine.”

“Thanks,” I said. It was generous of him.

“One last question,” Arjun said. “What do you plan to do?”

“It’s not that complicated,” I shrugged. “Aside from Charlotte, we’re all level one. We can’t fight you. We’ve got nothing. I’ll talk to the others. Some might stay. Some might go to that far-off village. How much time do we have?”

“I’m not telling you that,” Arjun replied flatly.

It was worth trying.

“I think we’re done here,” he said. “I truly hope I never see you again.”

“Can I ask one thing?” I said.

He turned to look at me.

“When you scouted near Kamatouu… did the guards attack you? Did the system consider you criminals in this town?”

“Yes, they did,” Arjun said. “But that will change—once we’re the rulers.”

And with that, he left.

Good. If the guards attacked them here, that meant criminal records were shared between all towns in the kingdom. Which meant that Charlotte and Santiago weren’t considered criminals not because they were far away—but because the system recognized them as part of our party.

They’d be free in any city.

I returned to Juliette and Hyun-woo and filled them in—at least the parts they didn’t already hear, since we’d been shouting most of the conversation.

“So,” I concluded, “bit of good news in all that: I don’t think they expect us to put up any resistance.”

“Yeah,” said Juliette. “And we also got a cool map.”

“One more thing,” Hyun-woo added. “Duchess heard Arjun call you from really far away. Looks like she actually does have super hearing, like she says. We could use that.”

“About time that useless bird did something useful,” I muttered.

“We need to decide on scouting,” Hyun-woo said. “We need to know about Farhad’s assault at least half a day in advance. Enough time to organize the goblins and guards.”

“The goblins will patrol regularly,” I said. “They’re good at it. We’ll tell them to increase their routes.”

“Still better if we patrol ourselves too,” said Juliette. “And take advantage of Big Bird hearing.”

“Good idea,” I said. We won’t leave anything to chance. We won’t be caught off guard.

We returned to the tavern and filled in the group. Then I had another ale. I deserved it. Besides, Santiago was already on his third. Maybe I was building up a little tolerance by now.

Spoiler: I wasn’t.

I woke up the next morning wishing Vel’nari to stab me. How could I forget how horrible this feels?

I solemnly swore to never drink alcohol ever again.

I got up, washed my face and drank about a gallon of water. I had to be on my feet. Today was a super busy day.

First, the market: I bought twenty-two bronze spears and twelve steel knives—more than I’d promised the chief. Then I headed to the forester’s cabin to meet Vel’nari. Lori was out gathering herbs (please don’t fall into a pit again).

Goblins didn’t really write.

I hadn’t actually kissed any other goblin. Or, truthfully, any other… organism.

She pulled away. I was enchanted.

And just like that—she was gone.

Now then—In my busy day, after getting thoroughly confused by a goblin, my next task was…

Gas magic experiments.

Elrik and Lori kept a big hunting shed stacked with bows, arrows, and snares—perfect for experiments. I’d bought tallow from the chandlers, melted it, and soaked a strip of wool cloth in it. When lit, it made a thick, black, nasty smoke. I also had a torch burning in a wall mount.

I pulled out my gas magic book and my super-expensive Charm of the Binding Sigil for stabilizing magic circles.

The plan: create a containment circle for gas. The evaluation criteria were:

Any deviation from the perfect magic circle would reduce one or more of these values.

If I got this right, choosing a specific gas would make the spell at least twice as powerful and much easier to cast. But for practice, I needed visible gas—so smoke as general gas was my best option.

I began with dry runs. No gas. Just trying to stabilize the sigil in the air.

I muttered weird syllables, studied the diagrams, traced the shapes with my hand—again and again.

And… it sort of worked.

The amulet was the real deal.

It felt like the difference between carrying water in my hands versus using a bucket. Suddenly, the magic had shape. Bad shape—unstable, flickering, warped—but still, it existed.

It was exhausting. Like I’d sprinted half a mile—which, for me, was a lot. Each casting drained me. After every attempt, I needed to rest for 10 minutes.

Finally I had something stable enough that, for maybe ten seconds, the geometry stopped flickering and held shape. Good enough to try the real thing.

I tore a little strip from the tallow-soaked cloth, set it on a metal plate, and lit it. Ugly, oily smoke billowed up. I configured the circle for a tight four-inch influence sphere, close to me, stripped down to just the anti-mixing components—without the propulsion nodes I’d need later for movement.

I cast.

The cloth burned out in seconds, but a four-inch globe of smoke hung there—contained. Some leakage, a bit of air intrusion; after about five seconds the opacity looked halved. Then my circle unraveled and I dropped to a knee, utterly exhausted.

“YESSSS!” I shouted to no one. “IT FUCKING WORKED!”

I coughed. The shed was full of stinky smoke. But I didn’t care.

I was tired. I was wheezing. My arms trembled.

But I was so damn happy.

It actually worked. For the first time ever I actually did real magic.
