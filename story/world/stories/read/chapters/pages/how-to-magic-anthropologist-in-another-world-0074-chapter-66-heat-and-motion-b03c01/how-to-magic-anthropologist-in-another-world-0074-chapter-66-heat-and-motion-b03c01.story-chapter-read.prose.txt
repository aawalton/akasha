Dead zones: defined as untamable country-sized stretches of land.

Only a couple of dozen places in the Seven Realms truly deserved the name. They were places that promised far more death than reward.

When I first arrived, I imagined it would feel mysterious. Ancient ruins, forgotten magic, and lost civilizations waiting to be rediscovered.

After a few days of walking through one with a freelancer team, the reality was much less romantic.

Most ruins contained nothing but dust, bones, and the occasional group of undead that needed to be put down.

Maybe if we traveled further west, deeper toward the center instead of just combing the edge, things would be different.

But no, that would be too reckless, and wanting to confirm whether there was truly a gate to another world at the center of it all was not reason enough to risk everyone’s lives on an ill-conceived expedition.

We were traveling back to our sanctified camp after exploring another ruined village. This one had been overrun by skeletons rather than ghosts. Other than being better coordinated than the average undead, they did not pose much of a challenge.

The ruins themselves were a disappointment. Most of the buildings had already collapsed, leaving little more than broken walls and empty foundations. Whatever the villagers had owned was either long gone or rotted beyond recognition. We searched the place thoroughly anyway, turning over debris and checking the remains of old cellars, but we did not find anything of real value.

Still, the trip was not a complete dud. Skeletons might not carry coin purses, but monster corpses still had value. The average monster core alone was worth a couple of bronze coins, and a few of the leading abominations had fist-sized cores worth a lot more. We also loaded up on scrap metal from undead weapons and armor. With the price of iron scrap sitting at five bronze per kilogram, it was always worth taking as long as we still had space in our bags of holding.

Ja’a also spotted some worthwhile bone fragments, pieces that still carried soul remnants even after the creatures had been killed. Her soulseer ability let her see them while the rest of us could not.

Well, mostly the rest of us.

It did not take Calr long to start noticing them too, learning quickly from clues only he could understand.

Even so, I had hoped for more. Ruined villages always carried the promise of hidden treasure, lost relics, or at least something interesting to study. As an anthropologist, I would have been happy even with pieces of broken pottery or maybe spoons, something to help me understand how the people here used to live before the dead zone. But this place had offered nothing except dusty bones and ruined concrete walls.

We were still a few days away from the end of Raik’s challenge, so instead of returning to Hano immediately, we decided to remain in the dead zone a little longer. A few more ruins, a few more monster hunts, and perhaps our luck would improve.

Despite the cult attack, Raik did not seem overly concerned.

He believed that, in a fair fight, they were no match for us. The ambush had only worked because they caught us off guard. With Calr being resistant to dreamer magic, that advantage was mostly gone now.

“If they try the same trick again, it will not work,” Raik had said earlier that morning while we prepared for the expedition.

He might have been right. Still, the attack had left all of us a little more cautious than before.

As we walked through the gray wasteland between ruins, both Calr and I kept glancing toward the sky.

Every so often, one of us would pull out a spyglass and scan the horizon.

I had given him the older model and kept the fancy one Nina made for me. It was better by leaps and bounds, and I took every excuse to use it. That was the only reason. It was not like I was worried about getting caught in another ambush.

Ja’a noticed the first time I did it.

“You know that winged woman is not going to be hovering up there all day waiting for you to spot her,” she said.

“I know,” I replied. “I am just putting this tool to use.”

Calr was even worse.

At several points, he would stop walking entirely, raise his spyglass, and slowly scan the sky in a full circle.

If they were watching us again, we would be sure to catch them.

The landscape around us stretched into open meadows, giving way to hilly terrain. The dead zone was not fully dead, but the fauna and flora were not thriving in diversity either. Birds were as rare as fruits and berries, and most animals were small enough to live in burrows, nothing larger than rodents and ferrets.

The only real landmarks were the lake, a few hills, and the ruined villages.

Every few hours, we would stumble across half a ruined wall or the remnants of a road. More than two thousand years of neglect and roaming undead did not leave much behind to preserve.

This village had it particularly bad. The moss-covered stones and the way tree roots had crept through cracked foundations suggested nature would have claimed more if it had been thriving. But even nature seemed shy around here.

Katar walked at the front of the group, lazily swinging his sword over one shoulder.

“If the next ruin is another skeleton nest with no real challenge, I am going to lose it,” he grumbled.

“Who cares about the challenge? I want loot,” Ja’a corrected him. “I expected treasure in here.”

Kan snorted quietly.

“You have an ancient machine stored in your bag of holding. Do not be too greedy.”

“That was three days ago. I want treasure today,” Ja’a pouted.

Shingo walked near the middle of the group, carrying large sacks filled with broken swords collected from skeletons in the last village. I had offered to carry them in my bag of holding, but Katar and Raik insisted he carry the extra weight for his kindred training.

Raik walked beside him, scanning the horizon with the calm focus of someone who had spent most of his childhood preparing for this life. Most stories portray noble-born people as spoiled and useless, but Raik was not like that. I guess that is the result of someone whose systemic privilege was not wasted on him.

His clan would have given him the best education one could receive in this world. Traveling with him, I honestly could not feel his lack of experience. The boy was just nineteen, and this was his first time leading a full expedition. Usually, he only traveled with Katar and Ja’a.

I walked next to Vena. The girl had relaxed more and started integrating with the team, especially with Kan. Vena had even started opening up, telling stories about her late mother, who had been a paladin.

Kan, as well, was not as guarded as before. She openly flirted with Raik and joked with Calr and Ja’a.

Katar was the one I still did not quite know how to deal with. Honestly, he was helpful when it came to training and fighting. He mostly joked with Ja’a, the two of them constantly putting each other down in the way only childhood friends could manage without getting offended. With the rest of us, he was blunt and to the point. He was not exactly an agreeable person, but he was respectful enough, listened to Raik’s orders, and never caused problems.

Still, I wondered what it would take for him to open up to the rest of the team the same way he did with Raik and Ja’a. I think if Yon were here, he would act as more of a bridge between Katar and the rest of the group. Sadly, Yon’s presence would invalidate the challenge. Raik could not lead a team with a sergeant in it if he wanted his own challenge to count toward becoming a recognized sergeant.

I wondered what Yon was doing now. I had left him in a rough state after Vals's death. I should probably check on him the next time I teleported back to Hano.

Thinking about friends who were not here also led me to Nakera and Kuru, who had probably already made it to High Rock by now. I hoped they were staying safe out there.

My train of thought was interrupted by a feminine figure walking calmly through the fog, heading in the opposite direction.

I blinked.

The rest of the team reacted instantly, shifting into position as if preparing for a fight.

“Is that the banshee?” I asked out loud.

The woman wore a flowing white dress with a thin veil covering her face. As she moved closer, I could hear her humming a soft, mournful tune to herself. There was no hostility in her posture. If anything, she looked peaceful, like a traveler crossing paths with us on a quiet road.

“She looks pristine again,” Vena said quietly. “She is probably going to clear resentment from somewhere else.”

I frowned slightly.

“How smart is she?” I asked. “Could we talk to her?”

“There are records of them speaking,” Calr said. “But most of the time, they are reflexive creatures. They do not act. They react.”

As the banshee drifted closer, the air around us seemed to grow colder, the fog thickening slightly as if drawn to her presence.

Then her gaze landed on us.

For a brief moment, everything felt still.

I hesitated, then raised my hand and gave her a small wave.

The banshee paused.

Then, to my surprise, she smiled beneath the veil and gave a gentle wave back before continuing on her way, humming softly as if we were nothing more than a passing curiosity.

“Well, that was something,” Raik said with a quiet exhale, the tension in his shoulders easing now that a fight was no longer inevitable. “All right, everyone, back to camp.”

No one argued.

We slowly relaxed, though more than one of us glanced back at the fading figure in the fog.

Our last encounter with the creature had left us with bleeding ears. Somehow, this was even more unsettling.

Back at camp, I took a bath to clean off the bone dust and travel grime. It was still a couple of hours before dusk, and everyone was doing their own thing.

Katar was maintaining the silver blade I had gotten him, carefully polishing the edge with slow, practiced movements. Ja’a had insisted it was her turn to cook and was currently experimenting with the camp cauldron. Calr sat beside her, trying to mediate the culinary disaster slowly forming in front of him.

Shingo was napping. I think the ability to sleep whenever he can is part of his kindred adaptation. Being a giant probably burns a lot of energy, and he always seemed able to fall asleep the moment he closed his eyes.

Vena was walking the perimeter, checking the sanctified fences that encircled the camp. The stakes and blessed cords marked the boundary that kept wandering undead from drifting too close during the night.

Raik and Kan were sitting off to the side, probably hoping for a little time alone.

Raik lit a small fire using a couple of wooden sticks and his heat powers. Once the flames caught, he casually held his hand inside them, moving it through the fire as if it were nothing more than warm air.

To my surprise, Kan tried the same thing.

She slowly pushed her hand into the flame and kept it there for about two seconds. Her face twisted in pain before she abruptly pulled it back out and hurried over to Vena for healing.

They were not doing couple stuff.

They were doing magic stuff.

Which meant I had to get in on that.

I made a beeline toward Raik and sat down facing him, intentionally leaving Kan’s spot open so she could return once her hand stopped hurting.

“So what were you doing?” I asked.

“Helping Kan train her resistance,” Raik replied.

I narrowed my eyes at him.

“Was that really the best idea? Burning her like that?”

“Well, with Vena here, it is a rare opportunity to work on it,” he explained.

“I do not think it is sustainable,” I said. “There is a limit to Vena’s healing, after all.”

“Yeah,” he nodded as Kan and Vena joined us. “Kindred usually train their fire resistance by bathing in hot water. It is safer and more controlled. But Kan has already reached the limit of that.”

I blinked.

Kan could handle boiling water.

Right, I remembered that in the freelancer guild baths, there was a room with boiling water for training. There was also a room for acid.

“Can’t you just make the water hotter with your powers?” Kan asked as she moved closer to Raik, while Vena settled beside me.

“Water can only get so hot,” Raik said. “After a certain point, it stops getting hotter and starts turning into steam.”

“I am surprised you know that,” I said.

“Well, it is not widely known, but my innate power is heat, after all,” he replied.

I nodded.

“What Raik says is true,” I added. “Under normal circumstances, water temperature is trapped between freezing and boiling.”

“Let’s put numbers on it. Zero is the lowest temperature water can have and still remain liquid. Go lower, and it turns into ice. One hundred is the highest temperature it can reach. Go higher, and it turns into steam.”

Kan listened closely.

“So if you can handle water at one hundred degrees, that is already impressive,” I continued. “But a campfire is much hotter than that, at least several times hotter. Around six hundred degrees. It is not really comparable.”

Raik nodded.

“I could heat the air instead,” he suggested. “But out in the open, I would burn through my mana too quickly. I could do it in Hano if we rent a sauna or something similar.”

“Or we could heat up oil,” I suggested. “It can get much hotter than water without boiling. That would be more manageable.”

“Oil, huh,” Raik said, nodding slowly. “That is actually a good idea.”

“Could you teleport tomorrow and buy me a barrel of oil?” Kan asked, looking at me with clear enthusiasm.

I raised an eyebrow.

“I could do that,” I said. “But is there not an easier solution?”

“Like what?” Raik asked.

I looked at the small fire in front of him.

“Could you not make the fire colder?”

Raik frowned.

“My powers are heat, not cold,” he said.

“Cold does not really exist as a thing,” I replied. “It is just the absence of heat.”

Raik hesitated.

“When NoName stole my power, she used cold, too,” he said slowly.

“Right, I remember that,” Kan nodded. “She used it to shatter my chains. It was a struggle to fix them.”

“Does that mean you can use cold as well as heat?” Vena asked.

“I do not know,” he said. “Could it be possible that I just never realized it? Someone who had my power for less than an hour managed to do it.”

He sounded more dejected than curious.

“You are an Agame,” I said. “Your whole family specializes in fire. Of course, heat would come more naturally to you than cold.”

“Your fighting style is also similar to your family members, just with heat instead of fire,” Kan added.

I paused for a moment, thinking it through. Maybe it was some kind of mental block, something caused by not inheriting his family’s exact expression of power. He had learned to use heat to mimic fire, and now he was stuck in that pattern.

Raik stared into the flames, his hand hovering inside the bonfire. For a moment, he did not say anything. He frowned in concentration, his eyes fixed on the shifting light as if trying to force it to change.

Nothing happened. The flames flickered as they always did, unaffected by his effort.

He pulled his hand out slowly.

“It did not work,” he said with a frustrated sigh.

“Maybe you rely too much on instinct and not enough on visualization,” I said. “When I use my lightning soulbook, it helps to imagine the positive and negative charges moving through the air. After I got my perception soulbook, that kind of visualization became much clearer, and my efficiency improved a lot.”

Raik looked at me, thoughtful.

“But how do I do that?” he asked.

“Start by explaining how you usually use your power,” I said. “We will figure it out from there.”

He nodded slowly.

“As a divergent kid, I had to find my own way with my powers,” he said. “So there could definitely be something I am missing.”

He lifted both hands and held them facing each other vertically.

“To use my power, I learned that I needed to externalize my mana into the area I want to heat,” he explained. “Then, with focus and intent...”

The air between his hands began to shimmer.

I could feel the heat building, like leaning too close to a bonfire. It was not visible in the same way as flames, but the distortion in the air made it obvious that something was happening.

“...I increase the temperature directly,” he finished.

I nodded, watching carefully.

“How is that different from what your family does?” I asked.

“Most Agames do not heat their mana like I do,” he said.

He opened one palm. This time, there was no shimmer, nor heat.

“They pour mana out as fuel,” he continued. “And then they ignite it.”

He snapped his fingers.

There was a brief flash, followed by a small burst of flame, like a stove being lit.

“But you just did it,” I said, frowning slightly.

“That is my fireball soulbook,” Raik replied. “Not my innate power.”

Vena tilted her head.

“If you can mimic your family’s power that easily with a soulbook, what is the problem?” she asked.

Raik chuckled, though there was no real humor in it.

“If only it were that easy,” he said.

He looked back at the fire, his expression tightening.

“I can create fireballs,” he continued. “But that is it. I cannot create jets of flame from my feet. I cannot form wings to fly. I cannot shape fire into anything other than a sphere.”

He paused, clearly annoyed now.

“I cannot make a solid sword of flame. I cannot control flames that are not mine. And I definitely cannot manipulate fire the way my family does.”

His voice carried a hint of frustration by the end.

“I am stuck imitating something that should have come naturally to me,” he admitted quietly.

I decided to interrupt him before his mood dropped any further.

“When you externalize mana and heat it,” I said, “have you tried imagining the opposite? Not just cold as a feeling, but something more specific. Think of stillness instead of movement. Think of ice instead of fire. Think of blue instead of red.”

Raik shook his head.

“I already tried the ice and blue part,” he said. “That is the obvious approach. It did not work. But why stillness?”

I leaned forward slightly, thinking about how to explain it without turning this into a lecture.

“Because heat is energy, and movement is energy,” I said. “Heat is also movement. Not visible movement, mind you, but movement all the same.”

He frowned.

I rubbed my hands together quickly.

“Everything is made of tiny things,” I continued. “You cannot see them, but they are always there. When something is hot, those particles move faster. When something is cold, they slow down.”

I kept rubbing my hands until the friction built up warmth, then held them out slightly.

“Feel that?” I asked.

Kan leaned in, her cheek almost touching my hands, and nodded.

“That is heat created from motion,” I said. “Friction. I forced movement, and that movement turned into heat.”

Raik’s eyes sharpened slightly.

“And when things get very cold,” I went on, “those particles barely move at all. They become still. That is why ice feels solid and calm, while steam feels wild and chaotic. One is slow. The other is fast.”

Vena tilted her head.

“Is this knowledge from your world?” she asked.

I nodded.

“We do not use magic much, so we had to understand how things work without it.”

Raik looked back at the fire, thoughtful.

“So instead of imagining cold as a thing,” he said slowly, “I should imagine removing movement.”

“Exactly,” I said. “You are not creating cold. You are lowering the heat.”

He raised his hand again, hovering it above the flames. His expression was different, more focused, and less frustrated.

He closed his eyes briefly.

I wished I could see his mana, but even with my perception soulbook, I could only see the electric charge of things.

A few seconds passed.

Nothing happened. The flames burned as they always did.

Raik exhaled and opened his eyes.

“It is not working,” he said.

“That is fine,” I replied. “You are trying to change how you think about your power. That does not happen instantly.”

He nodded, though he still looked slightly disappointed.

Then he paused.

“Wait,” he said.

He raised his hand again.

“This time, I will try the opposite,” he said. “Instead of focusing on stillness...”

The shimmer of heat returned, stronger than before.

“I will focus on movement,” he added.

The air around his hand distorted more intensely, heat building faster than before. The flames reacted, stretching slightly toward his hand as if drawn to it.

Raik’s eyes widened a little.

“That felt easier,” he said.

Kan leaned forward, watching closely.

“Did your control improve?” she asked.

Raik lowered his hand slowly and nodded.

“To think I was trying to make cold,” he said. “But instead, I made my heat power stronger.”

I smiled.

“That still means you learned something,” I said. “Your visualization worked, just not in the direction you expected.”

He let out a small breath, the tension easing from his shoulders.

“My efficiency increased,” he admitted. “I used less mana for a stronger effect.”

“That sounds like progress to me,” I said.

Raik looked back at the fire, a faint smile forming.

“Yeah,” he said. “It does.”

Kan crossed her arms, clearly satisfied.

“Good,” she said. “Because I am not waiting weeks for you to figure this out. You have to do it as soon as possible.”

Raik laughed quietly.

“Of course you do.”

Without hesitation, Kan stuck her hand into the fire once more, to everyone’s surprise.

I could hear her counting through gritted teeth.

“One... three... five...”

Then she removed her hand abruptly.

Vena was on her a second later.

“Stop that. Any longer than that, and there might be scarring I cannot fix. You need to give your body time to heal.”

Kan pouted as if she wanted to stick her hand in a third time.

I leaned back slightly, impressed by her resilience.

The task had failed successfully. Raik was stronger, even if his power did not diversify the way we had hoped.

Still, progress was progress.
