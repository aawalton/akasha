Rick jumped down from the tree and landed lightly, already turning toward his next destination. It had become routine by then. Every few kilometers, he would stop, climb a tree, search the surrounding area for threats, then consult or update the Mapping Book. The habit cost time, but not nearly as much time as a mistake would. He was already at the foot of the mountains, moving north while searching for an easier way through them.

The new armor helped. Not just because it protected him better, but also because it moved well with his body. For a hunter, that mattered more than pure defense. Warriors could burden themselves with rigid metal and call it security. A hunter who tried the same would only become slower, louder, and easier to kill.

So hunters wore leather or other softer materials whenever they could afford them. Rick’s choice had been a low-quality bronze-tier wood-essence fiber set. It had cost him seven silver, which still made part of him wince when he remembered it, but the expense had been justified the first day he put it on. It was flexible, light enough not to ruin his speed, and far sturdier than his old clothes.

In truth, Rick knew very little about armor beyond the obvious. He could say with confidence that this was much better than what he had before. Anything more specific depended on the merchant’s honesty, the trio’s judgment, and luck. Unscrupulous sellers often passed off inferior goods as something better, which was why people preferred either prestigious stores or trusted sellers whenever they had the choice.

All magical materials and crafted goods followed the same broad logic. The essence within them determined the tier—copper, bronze, silver, gold, or diamond. The craft itself also had its own rating: low, average, or high quality. With so many combinations, proper judgment required either long experience or the right skill. Rick had neither, which meant he could only wear the armor, appreciate that it felt good, and hope no one had cheated him.

For his first real search, he was reexamining the thirty-kilometer stretch he had already mapped along the mountains. But this time he was not merely passing through it. He was studying it with full attention, checking every slope and gap that might conceal a climbable route. Whenever a section looked promising, he left the easier ground and hiked partway up the mountain to see it properly.

The work was painstaking. Ground he could once cover in a day of running now took the better part of a week to search with the care it deserved. By the end of it, he had marked four places worth revisiting. Three were smaller mountains with less brutal approaches. The fourth was simply a section with a wider, gentler incline.

Unfortunately, none of them looked truly good. Still, they were possibilities. When he finished the mapped stretch, Rick hesitated. He could return and begin checking the marked points in more detail, or he could press a little farther north and see whether the land improved. Going too far defeated part of the point of having a settlement nearby in the first place. But another twenty kilometers still felt acceptable. If he found nothing, he could always retreat later with more knowledge than before.

So he chose north. From there on, however, the ground was unfamiliar, and unfamiliar ground demanded slower steps.

A day later, he found a stream. It ran clear and narrow, winding down the slope in a thin line that probably fed into the river somewhere nearer the Wall. Rick followed it uphill. Water often led to useful things—shelter, landmarks, game, or trouble. Sometimes all four.

The stream’s source turned out not to be a spring but a small cave. Rick stopped outside the entrance and listened.

Caves demanded caution. Empty caves were useful, but if occupied, they could be deadly. With winter approaching, any enclosed place was even more likely to attract beasts looking for shelter. Still, he could not simply ignore it. A cave this close to the mountain might help later, even if only as a point on the map.

So he advanced carefully, spear in hand. The new weapon was a low-bronze spear, far better than the one he had nearly lost his life with during the boar hunt. He intended to keep this one.

Inside, the cave was damp and cooler than the air outside. The sound of flowing water echoed softly off the stone. Rick listened again, straining for any scrape, breathing, or shift in the dark. Nothing answered him.

The cave proved shallower than he had expected, but the source of the stream was still not visible. Instead, the water flowed out of a low rock passage near the back wall, small enough that it might once have been dismissed as a crack if not for the steady current running through it.

Rick crouched and frowned at it. He wanted to inspect it more closely, but a hole in a cave wall was exactly the sort of place where something unpleasant might be sleeping. He did not much enjoy the idea of putting his face near it in the dark.

So he left, gathered a torch, and returned ten minutes later. With proper light, he checked the whole cave again. The walls, the floor, the ceiling cracks, the entrance, the back. It was oddly empty. After enough cautious glances that even he began to feel a little ridiculous, Rick finally lowered himself and brought the torch near the rock passage.

He saw water, stone, and not much else.

Then he drew the torch back, preparing to stand, and caught sight of something that had not been visible before.

A glow.

Faint, but unmistakable.

Rick froze and leaned closer again. There was more than one source. Several, in fact. They shone from deeper inside the passage where, by the look of it, the narrow crack opened into a broader space beyond.

His heart quickened. What was it? And more importantly, how was he supposed to check it?

The passage did not look deep. Nor did the stone look especially strong. With a hammer, time, and enough care not to collapse the cave on himself, he might break through the constriction properly. But that would mean leaving now, fetching tools, returning, and spending hours on a path that might lead nowhere. For all he knew, the glow could mark little more than a dead pocket in the rock.

There was another option. Rick tried very hard not to think about it.

The passage was just large enough for him to force his way through. That fact was deeply unfortunate. It was also very stupid. The kind of stupid people regretted briefly and then permanently. Half-submerged in a stream, with barely enough room to wriggle, unable to turn or fight properly, he would be at the mercy of anything that went wrong. A beast in the water. A shift in the stone, sudden narrowing, or panic alone might be enough to kill him.

Rick stared at the opening. Then he thought of the bookstore, and everything it had helped him conquer. Perhaps, this was how he could star paying Anne back.

After a long breath, Rick lowered himself to the ground, held the spear out ahead of him, and began creeping into the passage.

The water was freezing. It soaked his chest, arms, then climbed toward his neck. The rock overhead scraped his scalp and shoulders. His elbows and forearms dragged over rough stone. Every push forward cost effort and sent cold through his body in sharp little waves.

Still, he kept going.

Then the passage narrowed.

Rick stopped. His head had to lower. The water rose closer. The rock pressed harder over his shoulders. From there on, his arms became nearly useless. He could no longer pull himself properly. He had to push with his feet while keeping the spear stretched ahead and his face turned just enough not to choke.

He had made a terrible mistake. The realisation arrived clearly and without argument.

Retreat would be hard. Perhaps impossible. If the passage narrowed much more, he would drown here like an idiot trapped halfway inside a mountain over some dim light he had not even identified.

The possibility was so terrifying that for a few breaths it drove every other thought from his mind, including beasts. Fortunately, none appeared.

Ahead, he could see the widening section now. It was close. Just a few meters. The passage also seemed to constrict a little more before it opened. He had no choice left except forward, and that, strangely, steadied him. A man with no options could at least stop wasting strength on choosing.

Rick filled his lungs as much as he could.Then he pushed. The water closed over his face. He drove himself forward with everything he had, no longer caring about the fresh cuts scraping open along his skin. He moved faster now—much faster—but every second robbed him of air and calm. His shoulders ground against the stone. His chest felt ready to burst. Desperation became the whole world.

Then, just as true panic began to take him, the pressure eased. The stone around him loosened. His shoulders slipped free into wider water. Rick lurched ahead, found space beneath him, and came up coughing hard enough to double over.

He stood there for nearly a full minute, one hand braced against rock, trying to convince his lungs that they were no longer dying. Only when the worst of the panic had passed did he finally lift his head and look around.

Then he forgot to breathe for an entirely different reason.

The passage had opened into a hidden chamber far wider than the cave outside had suggested. Water ran through it in a clear black ribbon, widening here and there into still pools. The glow came from all around him—not from one source, but many. Pale lights shone from the rock itself and from clusters deeper in the chamber, some high along the walls, others lower where the water bent around stone. They reflected and multiplied across the damp surfaces until the whole place seemed filled with quiet, submerged stars.

Rick stood in the middle of it, soaked, scraped, half freezing, and completely speechless.He did not even know how to begin describing what he had found.
