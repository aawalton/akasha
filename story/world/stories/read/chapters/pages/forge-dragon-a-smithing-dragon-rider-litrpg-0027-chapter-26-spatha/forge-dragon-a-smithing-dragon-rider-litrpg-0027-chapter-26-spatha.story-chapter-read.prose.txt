My journeyman trial in Master Dimel’s own forge.

This was an honor beyond anything the man had ever given him. Caleb didn’t know what to say, so he simply thanked his master.

He moved past the apprentices’ forges and to the one reserved for his master. This one was larger, with room for multiple strikers to work in tandem with the master smith. Caleb looked around, marveling that he had been allowed to work here. He quested out with his senses and tasted the aspect of the steel around him. Every tool, from the anvil to the hammers, was made from the same meta steel that he held. The exact same batch, in fact. While he marvelled at it all, a part of him felt disappointed.

This was his dream, to be working in this forge on his own project, but the place suddenly felt so small, the dream too humble. He could sense flaws deep in the heart of every piece of metal around him. None that mattered or compromised the tools, but still they were there and he could sense them. Similarly, only a few of the pieces maintained their aspects, and the best among them had a minor aspect while the rest were dissonant or poor. He had been given eyes to peer deep and far, only to learn the mountaintop he’d previously sought was just a foothill.

Still, he didn’t let the realization keep him down. He got to work heating the forge even as he planned what to make.

Once the forge was hot, he threw the billet into the coals, using his Imperium to shift it before he realized what he’d done. He turned quickly to see if anyone noticed, only to find his master watching, shaking his head in disapproval.

Caleb could almost hear him saying, “Place the steel precisely in the spot you intend it.” He’d endured countless lectures on how to achieve the correct heat in the forge, and a large part of that was ensuring the metal was where you had optimized the heat.

Now, Caleb had placed the metal exactly where he wanted it—far more accurately than he could have with tongs. He just couldn’t explain that to his master without revealing he was a rare Dragon Rider, and they’d already agreed not to do that.

In the end, he decided not to address it, giving a sheepish look to his master and grabbing the tongs to adjust the metal—well, pretend to adjust the metal. It had already been in the perfect position. When his tongs touched the steel, he could tell that the forge was a little cooler than he’d like, and once again he turned to magic for a problem that had mechanical solutions.

He called a gust into the tuyere and then abruptly halted it as he realized what he was doing. Instead he bent over and opened it manually, then move to work the bellows.

This is going to get old very fast, he thought as he waited for his steel to heat.

Several hours later, Caleb was completely covered in sweat and filth and holding a nearly forged blade. Nearly. If he’d been able to work in private, he’d have had a completely finished blade hours ago, but instead he’d been forced to use mundane means where the more magical ones would be noticed.

That didn’t mean he didn’t use magic, he just hid it.

The metal he’d been given had a flaw deep within. He’d sensed this immediately upon touching it, but for show he’d begun the process of folding the steel. To no one’s surprise—his master included—the flaw in the billet faded as he continued to fold and forge weld it together, over and over until the flaw disappeared. He had suspected his master had been aware of the flaw, and the smile on his face when he saw Caleb hammering it out proved that.

It was a test, he decided.

So on and on he went. When his Magical Perception detected a flaw, he found reason to discover it through mundane means. He made a big show of using a loadstone to test the properties of the metal as he used to do, and shaded his eyes from the light to better get a sense for the metal’s glow. All for show.

Without his new Vortex technique keeping the corrosive part of the air away from his work, he was forced to regularly descale his metal and add flux as he drew it out, cut it in half, stacked it on itself and welded the steel back together.

On and on he worked, using his new ingot-enhanced strength and constitution along with his Imperium to work the steel longer and harder than he’d have been able to a week before. He gathered a crowd as the other apprentices stopped all pretense of work to watch him go. When the heat grew stifling, a breeze took hold of the forge, blowing cool, refreshing air over Caleb. He allowed himself the indulgence just long enough before someone might think the constant breeze suspicious.

He worked long past the time the other apprentices maintained interest, and even past his master leaving for dinner. When Dimel came back, he told Caleb to stop.

“It’s too dark. And you must be exhausted. Go home, rest.”

Caleb knew there was no arguing with that command after his years of working under the always-safety-conscious master. So he packed up his tools, placed them at his normal workstation, and went home for the night. He still had energy to spare, but he thought it best not to try his master’s patience on this day.

He went straight home, washed up and went immediately to sleep, ready to go again at first light.

Master Dimel beat Caleb in despite the early hour and sat to watch once more as Caleb worked, just the two of them in the forge. The billet had been folded eight times, making for 256 layers in total.

The day before had been spent folding the metal over and over, until finally it was pure. He’d not leveled a single skill the day before, and as such he never became more aware of any deeper levels of impurities, though he saw why his master had never used the piece of steel before. The outside appeared fine, but the inside was a mess of impurities. Either someone had ripped off his master, or more likely the old man had bought the billet to test an overconfident student.

Despite the effort it took, Dimel gave no hint of his mindset as he watched Caleb work.

Finally happy with the steel, he got to work forging it. Through the folding, Caleb had held his mana pool tight to himself, holding the piece of steel outside it and forcing the mana out of the metal with each strike. As such, along with being free of mundane impurities, it was also free of natural mana—at least as far as Caleb could perceive. He suspected that the quality of the aspect was directly related to the amount of mana left unpurged. When crafted, if this billet was a minor aspect, it would mean Caleb had a long way to go with using his Magical Perception to craft higher-quality gear.

He heated the forge and, when ready, placed his billet inside using tongs, tweaking the position slightly with his Imperium when he felt the need. And so he worked again through the day, drawing the billet out into a blade. It pained him to not place a strength enchantment within the blade, but to do so now would advertise his ability to the world. His journeyman piece would be a talk topic in the small village for weeks. If he so much as gave it a point of strength, he had no doubt the whole mountain would know by the week’s end.

Despite the limited use of magic, it was the best thing he’d ever made. It was a spatha, a simple straight sword that tapered to a triangle tip. This one was eighty centimeters long and the largest blade Caleb had ever made. To reduce the weight of such a long blade, Caleb had forged in a double fuller and then drawn the edge out.

Next was the heat treat. The point where a piece of steel became a blade. He was not used to heat treating steel other than the cold-aspected material of the mountain, and this was likely another test. Every steel required different temperatures for quenching, and benefited from different oil compositions. While his master had provided Caleb with the proper oil—something he trusted not to be a trick—it would be up to Caleb to do the rest. Any errors could see his blade warping even if it was free of flaws. The flaws would cause cracks, but Caleb had no fear of that.

He placed the blade into the forge, holding it with his tongs as he stared into the flames. Then he let his eyes unfocus as he instead perceived the blade with his affinity. He knew the cold-aspected steel took longer to heat than most, and had a lower overall quenching temperature. Caleb watched the grains within the steel weaken, and as soon as he saw them lose their structure, he pulled the blade out, giving it only a cursory inspection for appearance’s sake before plunging it into the oil bath. The oil burst into flames as the glowing blade entered it, but Caleb paid it no mind.

Instead he held the blade fast with his Imperium, willing it to hold its shape. As fast as he’d moved, he’d been too slow and the tip of the blade cooled before the base. But the force of the warp was nothing compared to Caleb’s willpower. With 20 in the stat, Caleb could snap a blade over his knee—though with a constitution of 8, he’d badly cut his knee. Holding the blade still to prevent a warp was nothing. The challenge was doing so while still allowing the grains within to form a crystalline structure.

This was where his acuity came in. He carefully divided his control so he only held the outermost layer of the steel in place, allowing the structure within to move. All this happened in a mere instant, and Caleb expelled more mana in that instant than he had in the forging days leading up to it.

But it was worth it. He pulled the blade out of the oil—realizing only then that the ground was on fire from the splashed oil—and marveled at what he’d made.

Gasps broke out from the gathered crowd of apprentices as they too gawked at the straight blade.

He didn’t stop his work, however, and moved over to the grindstone to shape the edge.

The forge grew silent when Caleb removed the blade from the grindstone. He’d hardly needed to touch the edge, so sharp had his taper been, and the apprentices whispered amongst themselves at the short time he spent grinding. Next he moved to the whetstone to finish the edge, using his affinity for the steel to sense the edge and grind away even the smallest burr. Lastly he took it to the leather strop, and stared amazed for the first time as he detected on a microscopic level the leather aligning it to a razor’s edge.

And then he was done.

He focused on the newly crafted weapon and willed the system to recognize it.
