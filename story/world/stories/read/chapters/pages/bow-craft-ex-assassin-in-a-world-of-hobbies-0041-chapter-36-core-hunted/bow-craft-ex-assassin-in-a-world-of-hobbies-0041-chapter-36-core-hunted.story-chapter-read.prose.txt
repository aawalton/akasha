The core reaches the aphelion of its orbit and begins its daily migration to the south. It bobs along at a steady pace, taking care not to severely damage its own habitat.

This one, however, is clumsy. Its transparent shell, composed of two interleaved event horizons that warp the space around it, touches an unsuspecting branch. It is mercilessly and soundlessly consumed—zapped into the core’s inner workings.

The core stops and profusely apologizes to the branch’s owner. After some negotiation, the core decides to redo its route. The air around it swirls, and it, along with the damage it had caused, blinks out of existence.

A while later, it flies in from whence it had been just a moment ago. This time, it swerves below the branch it had previously destroyed. It also takes this opportunity to fix some minor damage caused by the mischief of Medusa fairies. A sharp green light emits from within its body, and the ground the light graces, previously a jagged rag of cement, reverts to a crunchy mat of shredded leaves and clay.

One would think, with its all-powerful defense and magic, that the core would sit on the forest’s ecological apex. However, as nature has proven time and time again, it only takes two words to unseat a king: “invasive species.”

***

The fluffy feeling in Craft’s chest was spelled “kindness” but pronounced “death.”

It hurt his feet to walk on the gravel of the dried-up stream, and so he’d kept to the side where the ground was easygoing and he could conserve his energy. He and Lei-rei were off looking for the core, following hints of its flight path.

It was going to take a while, thus he had no bloody clue why she had stayed in the stream, hopping from stone to stone with the excitement of a kid but the straight face of an assassin. It amused him, but he couldn’t stay amused for much longer.

Watching her, he should be trusting her, yet he saw only a god of the underworld to whom he owed his soul. I’ve seen five-percent of your inner world, the figurative god said, hence I reserve the right to reap the other ninety-five.

This fluffy feeling in his chest was wrong. There were various kinds of nerve agents, spells, and cult indoctrination techniques that were said to induce this very feeling. Of course, this couldn’t be any of the above, so what else could this be? What was this? Was it going to bite him in the ass one day? Was it going to turn him into a useless man in the end?

He was tired of confronting the unknown.

Icy fingers wrapped over his shoulder. He yelped and jumped away, leaving an equally stunned Lei-rei standing there with her hand left outstretched and eyes kept wide.

It took a moment for him to process this; he opened his mouth, but no sound came out, and when Lei-rei—still with a straight face—put her hand down and lowered her head by a degree, turning away soon after. He tried not to let such a small gesture get to him, but it did, and he found himself struggling to keep his head upright as well.

Lei-rei spoke up, “I’d rather not go around this.” He thought she was going to say something about him, but when he looked up, he found her taking off her gloves.

Well, I deserve it, he thought, but he finally noticed she hadn’t been looking at him. He turned to where she faced, and it was a cliff face. Oh, okay.

Ten meters up—not the highest cliff, but it was a cliff. There was even a carved, vertical patch where there must have been a waterfall at some point, though that spelled “soft rock” for him. Not the most climbable rock, but not like the cliff was made of anything else, either.

Moments later, they found themselves shimmying their fingers across inch-wide ledges. It was a rote skill, and he preoccupied himself trying to figure out what was going through Lei-rei’s mind. Really, what should he do with this awkward feeling? She had something on him, but he had nothing on her. She could pretty much blackmail him into doing anything, but she’d have acted on it by now if that was the case.

But he was too listless—too careless. He forgot to check the next handhold, and he’d already committed to it.

The stone came loose as soon as he grabbed hold of it. His left foot was dangling; his left hand had barely left its handhold; only his right foot was still planted on a ledge. He lurched, and his heart spiked. He scampered with both hands and his left foot to find purchase in the rock face, but he found none.

As he fell backwards, the first hand he saw was Lei-rei’s. He grabbed it without even thinking. Warmth suffused between their rough, sand-encrusted hands. He could feel the adrenaline ebbing, and with each passing second, a desire to keep holding on grew within him.

All that in just a fraction of a second.

But what would have happened if he’d dragged her down as well? They reached the top not soon after, and he wasn’t in a celebratory mood. They might’ve both fallen because of him. What else could he feel but shame for that?

***

They hadn’t needed to rest, and besides, the evidence of the core’s flight was more evident now: branches cut without splinters, smooth gouges in the earth, but strangest to him was an ever-deepening divide.

In the ground was a foot-wide scar. As he walked alongside it, it seemed shallow at first, but as he stalked the forest, he’d peer into the scar every-now-and-then and note how it was becoming more like a trench, then an unpiercable abyss.

It was a foot-wide scar, easily jumped, but with one misstep, it would swallow anyone whole. Averse to such risks, Craft soon realized he had to choose which side of the scar he would stand on. He could stay alongside Lei-rei, where he could have a chat to pass the time stalking the forest, but with every inch closer to her, a repulsive force skyrocketed. It confused him to feel that way, but no matter how much he thought, “She’s my friend” or “She’s not a threat,” it didn’t make her less of a threat. It was pure psychology, he knew, but it was still a wall that would exhaust him to overcome, and no matter his accumulated wisdom, nothing belayed the fear of not knowing what was on the other side.

He jumped, and now there was a foot-wide, mile-deep divide between him and Lei-rei. He didn’t want to note how she had moved away from the scar as well—farther from him, and unconsciously, he distanced himself as well.

The closer he was to her, the more it reminded him of how poisonous he, himself, was. The farther he went, the more his poison was realized. Damn if he did, damn if he didn’t—damn this trap and everything that went with it.

His thoughts stopped when Lei-rei did, and he stopped too when he found another scar in the earth, slicing across the one they’d been following.

Two scars. The area had been divided into a quadrant. “Why’s there two of ’em?” he blurted aloud.

“Is something wrong?” Lei-rei asked. She’d kept her voice low—unenergetic. He thought it was because of him.

“It could just be a looping flight pattern,” he replied, “or a second core.”

Lei-rei sighed through her nose. “I don’t know what to do with that information.” She crossed her arms and turned away, surveying the forest behind her, then towards Craft, surveying the forest behind him.

He’d done something wrong, didn’t he? But it wasn’t just that. Bouncing between anxiety and apologeticness, he was annoyed at himself already.

“Let me try something,” he said. Lei-rei just nodded.

He turned and followed the new scar, going farther away from Lei-rei. Each minute leading him into denser parts of the forest. The forest’s colors closed in on him; grayscale turned into smog, and smog turned into darkness, as if the forest itself rejected his advance, but he was more than familiar with this sort of trick, and there were things he was more afraid of than weak visual impairment.

The darkness blew away soon enough. There were less trees in the way now, and he was relieved to have decent sightlines again. Ahead of him, Lei-rei stood with crossed arms, still looking in the direction in which he’d disappeared.

She turned around with a hint of surprise as soon as he’d crunched a leaf with his next step. “How?…”

Craft stopped an arm’s length away. “There’s two cores after all.”

She paused and considered it for a moment, but slowly shook her head. “Implying?”

“We’re in the inner loop.”

There was a certain hierarchy inherent and necessary to keep a Matrioshka space from disassembling its occupants. Reality, in 3-D, would first be forcibly isolated and compressed into a quasi-stable 3.1-D “island” layer, acting as a safety shell in case the higher-dimension layers decided to spontaneously form a gestalt consciousness. Only then could the next layer, a 3.2-D “Mobius” layer, be compounded, which in turn kept the next 3.3-D “Escher” layer’s staircases from balistically piercing through dimensional walls as they often did.

“There’s layers?” Lei-rei said with a hint of gruff annoyance. “Can’t we just shoot them both down?”

“Even if we could, we can’t. The outer loop’s core isn’t actually here.”

Intersecting core paths didn’t mean they were in the same layer. They were like leftover construction scars from when other cores laid the foundations for the new core to take over. Because of this, only inner layers could have multiple core paths.

“I’ll just believe you,” Lei-rei said.

He tilted his head. “You’re not going to ask how I know?”

“It seems academic.”

“You’re not wrong. Anyway, we were going to destroy the inner core to start with. We can deal with the outer one later.”

“Alright.” Lei-rei didn’t look his way, creases showing on her forehead. “The bow, then.”

Just three words. The directness of it, and the way she wouldn’t look at him, cemented the thought that he’d done something wrong. If he could turn back time, he would, but all he could do now was be desperate to show her that he hadn’t meant any of it.

He opened up his inventory and popped out Terminal Velocity. Lei-rei tried to receive it with both hands, and rest it over her shoulder, but it was too unwieldy to maneuver, and it began to see-saw, tilting backwards. She began to tilt and shuffle backwards too, overcompensating in a way that would ultimately lead to her stumbling into the abyss.

Craft caught the rising limb and gently pulled it back down, and Lei-rei’s back stopped against his chest. She turned her head to look at him, mildly surprised, and her gaze traveled to his hand, her own hands, then down to the ground, readjusting the bow to sit more stably on her shoulder, and he pushed the bow along, helping her do so. Hopefully, that was a meaningful enough sign.

“Thanks,” she said.

It probably was.

She continued quietly, “Are you angry?”

That caught him off guard, and the quietness of her voice disarmed him. “I’m—I’m not.” Was that what she’d been thinking all along?

He caught her glancing at him. “I see,” she muttered. She looked at him again, this time confronting his gaze with her own. “Did I do something you didn’t want me to?”

His mind went blank, and he weakly shook his head. Why are you saying that?… Well, with how he’d been acting, it was obvious why.

“It’s not your fault. I”—he went through permutations of words; fields of sentences; an archive of literary examples of pain, but the best words were simply—“I was just afraid.”

She looked at him, and her furrowed brows slowly returned to baseline. Perhaps she’d found a thought or something else to comfort herself. “I see.” She turned away. “I’m not much better.”

Relief filled his lungs where air escaped it. The chasm was never there; the defeat was never there; best of all, neither of them pretended, not then, and not now.

Things had moved so fast, and all that was left was an itch on his cheek. “I should look for the core.”

As soon as he turned around, Lei-rei called out, “Craft.” He spun around just as quickly. She’d curled her hand into a “6,” lining up the hole with her eye, putting him in her sights. “Don’t forget,” she continued.

He gave a small smile. “I haven’t.”

She lowered her hand. “Alright. Go. Be careful.”

Her blunt tongue confused him a little, but he wouldn’t chide her for just being herself. He could see, now, why she’d said she preferred it that way.

As he turned around, he caught sight of how she’d bowed her head and clutched her chest, sighing deeply with a small but unmistakeable smile. He didn’t dare turn around again and confirm it, just letting the image linger as he ventured deeper into the forest.

He let his mind wander, and the more it did, it pieced the evidence together: she’d kept her distance simply because he did so as well, and the kind of fear they both held, when reflected at each other, did nothing but strengthen it. It was the terror of fragile affection, that at any moment, everything that had been built so far could be lost with just one misstep.

He supposed that was just the nature of trust. Just like this forest, it was hard to see more than ten feet ahead of him. Things like randomly encountering the core, burrowed beside a brackish pond where a black unicorn slurped water, wouldn’t be surprising.

Some things changed quickly, and other things, slowly. It was a stupid idea to try and predict what would happen next.

Oh, it actually happened, by the way.

He rolled aside as a bolt of lightning struck the ground where he once stood. The core rose from its nest and the unicorn panicked, running as another bolt of lightning hit the tree beside Craft.

He could feel his eyes twitching and his feet itching to run, but rationally, he knew he had a one-shot thunder of wonder watching over him. With shaky hands, he and gestured “6”es as best he could, lining them up like a scope in front of his eye so Lei-rei could see what she needed to see through his eyes. Centering the core in his finger-sights, this is it, he thought, and there was the sound of a harp string being struck by a wrench.

A note of E4 hung in the air with secondary dissonant tones—a sound which caused the core to seize, and by which Craft knew Lei-rei had loosed Terminal Velocity, even if he’d never heard it before. There was the sound of rolling thunder, dissipating, and as the core calmed from what it maybe thought was false alarm, Craft closed his eyes.

There was nothing happening in one frame, and then there was a phoenix dive-bombing the core the next. He had never before seen an arrow with fiery wings, and its tip glowed with the harshness of an arc welder’s spark.

Just this once, he was content to watch life in slow-motion. It had turned out he wasn’t the only one afraid, and twisted as it might be, he was glad they could both feel the same way.
