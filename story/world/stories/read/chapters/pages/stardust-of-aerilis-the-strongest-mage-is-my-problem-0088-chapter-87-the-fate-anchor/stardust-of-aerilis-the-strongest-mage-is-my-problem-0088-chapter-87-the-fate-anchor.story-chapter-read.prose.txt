The Cinder-Fox needed one more day before arriving at Dryadalis.

Aiven sat on the edge of the bunk in his cabin. The room was the same one he had woken up in yesterday morning and the morning before that. The world outside the porthole became a single wide blur of pale grey and pale gold, indistinguishable from itself.

His mind, in those two days, had narrowed to a single point. Stronger. Faster. Back to her.

He had turned it over and over. He had walked through his existing arsenal, every spell, every reflex, every trick Virelle had drilled into him during their week on the islet, and had found, each time, the same conclusion. It was not enough. It would not be enough for a long time yet, if he kept growing at the pace he had been growing. He needed acceleration. He needed a teacher who could compress into a month what should take a year.

Cyria had said the elves could do it. If they decided to. He would give them reasons.

Then he heard a soft knock at the door.

"Come in."

Lyra pushed the door open with her hip. She was carrying the small tray again, a covered bowl, a mug, a folded napkin. She had brought him meals three or four times a day since the departure from Fangreach, and the small routine of it had become the marker by which he was measuring the hours.

She set the tray on the desk.

"Warm today. The cook is trying something with the fish."

"Lyra."

She looked up.

He had rehearsed how to say it in his head. "You don't need to keep doing this. Bringing me the food. I can walk down by myself. I mean, I'm not disabled."

Lyra tilted her head.

She considered him for a moment. Then she folded the napkin more precisely along its crease and looked at him with an expression he did not immediately have a name for.

"So I'm only allowed to help you when you're disabled?"

Aiven opened his mouth and closed it immediately. "That's not — I didn't mean —"

"Because that's an interesting rule." Lyra took a small breath. "You'd have had a very short list of people helping you your whole life, under that rule.”

"Lyra."

"I know." A small, tired smile. "I'm teasing you. Mostly."

She pulled the chair by the desk close to him and sat down. She turned herself slightly to face him.

"I'm doing this because I want to. That's the whole reason. Nobody put me on soup duty." Her hands folded in her lap. "Unless I'm being a bother…Am I being a bother?"

"No." His voice came out lower than he had meant. He cleared his throat and tried again. "You could never be a bother. Ever."

"Then good."

She didn't say anything for a moment. The airship swayed gently beneath them. The mana-lamp in the recessed mount pulsed warm.

"I'm here," she said eventually, "because I want to spend time with you. Not because you're hurt. Not because you need it. Because I do. Because being in the same room as you, doing nothing in particular, is —" she searched for the word — "a comfort. I want you to know you're not alone in this."

He nodded. He did not trust his voice.

"And." She paused. Her hands twisted slightly in her lap. "I keep thinking. About how we haven't had a proper moment together, you and I. Not since the clearing. Every time we're in the same room something is on fire or someone is bleeding or we've just come out of something none of us know how to talk about yet. It's a shame. I want us to have…some of the time back. Some of it that isn't weighed down."

Aiven opened his mouth.

He wanted to say something. He was not sure what. Something about wanting the same. Something about not having known how to reach for it. Something about how looking at her, quietly, in a warm cabin with a bowl of fish stew steaming on the desk between them, was the first thing in a while that felt like the shape of a life he might one day be allowed to live.

Lyra spoke first.

"I know." Softly. "You don't have to say it. I've been thinking the same thing, and that's…that's part of why I've been here. Things have been happening so fast. I used to think we had all the time in the world. I spent so much of it on routines. Getting the shop set up in the mornings. Going home. Sleeping. Doing it again. And I always thought of later. Later I'll say the things. I think you thought the same. I think most people do."

Aiven nodded, small.

"And then it all came apart," Lyra said. "In one afternoon. Everything came apart. And now…now I feel like every single day, one of us might not come back from something. I don't want to waste it. There are things I want to say to you. I know most of them will have to wait until this is done. But I wanted you to know I have them. That I'm not pretending I don't."

Aiven looked at her.

He looked at her for a long moment. "I can hear them now," he said.

Lyra blinked. "What?"

"The things you want to say. Or…some of them. If you want." He drew in a breath. "There's something I've been meaning to ask you anyway. Since the clearing. I've been trying not to press."

She stilled slightly. "Okay."

"How—" His voice was steady. "How did you end up on Lucien’s side. How did you get the…the sealing. The magic. You never had such magic."

Lyra looked down at her hands. She did not answer right away.

"Hearthport," she said, finally, "isn't gone."

Aiven froze. "What?"

"Hearthport wasn't destroyed. Not the way you were told. Not the way anyone was told." She lifted her eyes to his, and there was something in them that was almost apologetic. "It's being held. In a pocket dimension. Everyone in it. The whole island. Every soul. Frozen. Nothing has moved since the day of the Catastrophe."

Aiven could not, at first, arrange the words into meaning.

"That's—" His mouth was dry. "That's not possible. An entire island. Under hostage. The whole island—"

"I know how it sounds. I still don't fully understand the magic of it, but it's there. It exists. He showed me. He showed me it more than once."

"Lucien."

"Yes."

Aiven's hands closed on the edge of the bunk. "Why?"

Lyra's small mouth worked for a moment. "Because he needed me to do things. And he needed leverage. He had spent a long time, I think, searching for someone. Someone whose mana-signature was compatible with a very specific artifact. An unsealing spell. It has to be worn by a particular kind of person or it kills them, and the kind is rare. Very rare. He found me. I don't know how, but I was the one it fit. And once he had me, he needed to make sure I would do what he asked."

She lifted her hand slightly and let it drop. "So he took the island."

Aiven could not speak.

"He gave me the artifact. It lets me open and seal things. Seals that were made centuries ago and never meant to be touched again. He has been sending me to those, one after another, and I have been opening them for him, and he has been taking what is inside. That is what I have been for.”

Aiven felt something at the base of his skull go very cold.

"How is Hearthport now?"

"Still there."

"If I took you away, then he should be able to use that leverage to make you come back to him. Which means…he purposefully let you go?”

Lyra nodded.

“So…he has told you to do something to me?”

Lyra's hands trembled in her lap. She was very quiet for a long moment.

"Yes," she said.

Aiven's whole body went still.

He had thought, in some part of himself, that he had already had the worst thought he was going to have on this ship. Something rearranged in his chest, quickly and coldly.

"Lyra." He did not raise his voice.

"Are you here to make sure I can't defeat him?"

Her eyes came up.

"Are you here to kill me?"

"No." The word came out immediately. She almost lifted from the chair with it. She reached one hand out toward him and stopped herself halfway.

"No. Not—not the way you mean. Not to hurt you. Aiven, I would never—I could never—"

"Tell me."

His voice was still low. Still steady. It did not sound to him like his own voice.

"Tell me what he sent you for.”

Lyra held there.

She sat back down. She took a breath that shook slightly on the way in.

"There's an artifact," she said. "In my quarters. He gave it to me before the escape. It's called the Fate Anchor."

Aiven's stomach dropped an inch.

He had heard the name before. Cyria had mentioned it once.

"What does it do?"

"It ties two lives together. It anchors one fate to another. When one of the two people it binds dies, the other dies too. Instantly. Wherever they are. However healthy they are. The bond doesn't care."

Aiven's mouth was dry. "He wanted you to use it on me?"

"Yes."

"Have you?"

"No."

"Are you going to?”

"No."

Her voice cracked on the second no. It was the crack of someone who had been holding a small heavy stone for a very long time and had just been asked to set it down.

"That's why I'm telling you," she said. "I would never have said any of this if I still meant to. I made my decision when we returned from the Fold. I…don't care what he does to me for it. I'm not doing it."

"Who?" Aiven's voice was not steady anymore. "Who were you going to tie me to?"

Lyra looked at him. "Myself."

His eyes widened. She held his gaze.

"The unsealing spell." She lifted one hand and gestured vaguely at her own chest, at her ribcage, at the whole vague geography of what a body was. "It costs me. Every seal I break puts strain on me. I don't feel it, most days. Sometimes I get tired. Sometimes I get short of breath. But Lucien has been clear with me. My body will collapse from it. Sometime in the next month, perhaps. He was very calm when he told me. He said it as if he were mentioning the weather."

Aiven's hands closed on the sheet.

"Why?" He barely got the word out. "Why didn't you tell me? Why didn't you say it the moment we were on the grass, why didn't you—"

"Because you had just carried me out of the Fold and lost her doing it." Her voice was very quiet. "Because you were on your knees in the sunlight and you had lost the one person who—who mattered to you. I wasn't going to—I couldn't put another weight on you. Not that day. I told myself I would wait. I told myself I would find a moment. And every day I was in your room and you couldn't stand up and I thought I could not burden you with this meaningless—"

"Lyra." Aiven held her shoulders. "You are not meaningless to me.”

The silence between them was the silence of two people who were now, both of them, understanding something they had been circling for days.

Aiven found his voice. "We go to Cyria." Firm. "The moment we land. We ask if she can pull the spell out of you. Break the — the whatever it is, the compatibility. Someone in the Vulpine network must—"

Lyra was shaking her head. "The only artifact that can lift it," she said, "is with Lucien."

The words landed. He looked at Lyra.

He looked at the small tired shape of her, the blonde hair, the pale exhausted line of her mouth. He looked at all of it. He thought about the little girl who had run beside him with a wooden sword through a field outside Hearthport when they had both been nine, the one who had been his refuge from the boring routine of being a clerk when they were working as adults.

Aiven exhaled once, long and slow.

"Then I'll go to him." His voice was very quiet.

"I'll get stronger. However fast I need to. Whatever the elves ask of me. Whatever it takes. And then I'll go to him to bring Virelle back, get that artifact that can free you, and return Hearthport. I'll take it all from him."

Lyra's eyes were wet. She did not say anything for a long moment, then she reached across the small space between them and put her hand, gently, over his where it rested on the sheet.

She did not squeeze. She just laid her hand over his, and left it there, while the airship was getting closer the green island that carried the hope of freeing everyone dear to him.
