Maiya found Cirayus sitting among a group of Asura.

They’d huddled around a fire at the back of their camp, far from the human soldiers who were only too happy to give the demons a wide berth. Maiya could imagine the distrust they must have had, harboring demons in their own camp. Exceedingly powerful demons at that.

She could also imagine the distrust they had of her, consorting with ‘the enemy’.

Even cross-legged, the half-Bairan stood taller than Maiya, his signature blade propped up nearby against a tree trunk. So heavy that no human in the realm could hope to lift it.

The terrifying Ravager, whom every Asura deferred to.

Never in a million years could she have guessed that such a being would come closest to taking the place of her dearly departed father.

“They said no,” he said, poking the fire with a stick.

“They did,” Maiya sighed. “I almost had them, but Tia clinched it in the end.” She sat down on a crate across from him, the other Asuras rising to give them privacy, nodding with respect to her as they did. “So I gave them the Vimana to think about, and then I left.”

“Lass, you should have led with that,” Cirayus snorted. “Now hear me once more. Give me the Asura tonight. You wish to minimize the blood spilled? This is the way.”

“Cirayus…”

“There’s not even a wall between us, lass.  They think us on their side! Two thousand of our Asura against fifty thousand in the dead of night, by surprise? That’s not a battle. That’s a culling, and we’ll have halved their number without a single Asura dead. The demon clans won’t have to lift a finger, and come morning, the humans’ morale will be so shattered, they wouldn’t even know where to pick up the pieces. I’ll even take care of that thorn in your side.”

“Tia?” Maiya asked, nearly considering the idea before scolding herself for even having the thought.

“Aye, the prickly princess has proven a most formidable thorn.”

“I thought we’d make an incredible team,” Maiya sighed again, poking the fire with a stick of her own. “The ultimate pair in lieu of Vir. I thought she’d turn us away from war, not towards it!”

“Everyone seeks to control the flow of events, lass, but I think we both know there is only one power capable of that particular feat.”

“Indeed,” Maiya muttered darkly. She knew all too well, didn’t she?

“Mark my words. We do this, and they flee with their tails tucked. This war ends in a single day, with a minimum of blood shed.”

“And what of the day after, Cirayus?” she asked, fighting to keep her voice level. “What comes when I’ve betrayed humanity’s trust in me? When demonkind has proven to be the backstabbing, untrustworthy creatures humans are so desperate to believe they are?”

Cirayus stayed silent, and for an instant, Maiya felt guilty at going off on the old demon. He truly was trying to save as many lives as possible, in the only ways he knew.

“Twenty thousand troops dead in a night by the Akh Nara’s wife,” she continued in a calmer tone. “Humanity’s so-called Saint. Every fear the humans ever had of the Pagan Order proved true in a single morning. And you know as well as I that the combined demon army would never stop at that. They’d surge forward, claiming this fortress, and then take the fight to the humans. To say nothing of how it would affect me personally. Not only would I be a traitor, Tara would never speak to me again, and Vir comes home to two races who hate each other and demons rampaging wild. An utter betrayal of all the power he left me.”

Cirayus was quiet a long moment at that. “You say all that, and yet, this is still not the real reason, is it?”

“No,” Maiya sighed. “It’s not.” What use was denying it?

“I’ve started to lose track of where my choices end and theirs begin. It’s nothing I feel, of course,” Maiya quickly added when Cirayus scowled. “They don’t work that crudely. Just that my plans come to nothing more and more these days. The harder I push for peace, the further it slips from my grasp. I can’t even tell whether striking plays to their ends or not, and no matter what I think, it doesn’t matter, does it? I’ll end up doing their bidding regardless.”

“Either way, it seems you’ve talked yourself into doing nothing,” Cirayus replied. “A Warrior’s might and an army’s will do nothing if they are not called upon to fight.”

“I know,” Maiya said, failing to meet his gaze.

“It’s naive. Naive and doomed, and I’ll tell you so again tomorrow when your threat fails and they call your bluff for what it is.” Cirayus stood up and walked over. “And through it all, I’ll stand beside you and put myself between you and whatever comes. I promised the Akh Nara I’d keep you whole.”

“Whole?” Maiya chuckled. “Don’t you mean safe?”

Cirayus bellowed a laugh, placing two of his great hands upon her shoulders. “Come now, we both know how you fight. There’s no point keeping you safe. It’s your opponents I’m more worried about.”

Grinning, Maiya reached up and put her hand on his, squeezing gently.

“Thank you, Cirayus,” she said. “With what’s to come, I may very well need you and Sikandar by the end of tomorrow.”

Having inspected the humans’ fortifications from the ridge that morning, Roshan understood precisely why the humans had chosen it.

Eighty paces of stone across the only valley wide enough to march an army through, the soaring peaks sealing both flanks better than any wall ever could. Against any demon army of his father’s day, it would have held indefinitely. But warfare had changed much since then. No longer did demonic clans trade arts and Chakras in open fields of battle. No longer did they slaughter their own.

Now, they fought another threat entirely. One far weaker than even the weakest of demons, and yet, Roshan didn’t dare underestimate them.

He had seen firsthand what Lady Maiya could do, and immortal or not, he would be a fool to underestimate any race capable of even a fraction of that terrifying potential.

The Rajas had all congregated in Jagath’s pavilion—a vast and impressive tent that had taken thirty Aindri to erect. Its interior was no less impressive, with silken rugs and gold-lined couches on proud display, the tent tall enough to fit even Roshan comfortably.

Jagath of the Aindri stood at the center, wining and dining the other commanders, as though this were some peacetime feast and not a forward command tent on the eve of their greatest battle.

Roshan wasn’t in any position to judge, however. It was Raja Jagath who had borne most of the effort of organizing the clans for this offensive, and moreover, it was he who had covered the not insignificant expense of mobilizing and feeding such a vast army.

An army the likes of which demonkind had not seen in generations.

So as much as Roshan wished for the Samsara to command the vanguard, it was only fitting the Raja took the honor for himself. Roshan had no doubt his mounted Warriors gloriously leading the charge would rally the troops like nothing else could.

Nothing, perhaps, Roshan thought with a wry smile, other than watching as dead Warriors rose to fight again.

Across the tent at the table of refreshments, he found Rajni Tara Panav in her half-naga form gorging herself, partaking in none of the politics and making no secret of the fact that she wished she weren’t here. Roshan could respect her candor, if not her stance on the matter.

Demonkind had been wronged. To not seek retribution was an insult to all their honor. Even hers.

Despite her grumblings, however, she’d pledged the entire Naga healer corps to this battle, and Roshan had no doubt they would save many lives this night.

As for the Chitran, they had sent a war-leader in lieu of the Raja the humans killed. He spoke little, but he didn’t need to. None here wished for this battle more than they, after all.

“My fellow Rajas, the plan is a simple one, as most great plans tend to be,” Jagath said, calling the strategy meeting to order. The lack of strategy tables and the copious drink in everyone’s hands made it feel like anything but.

“Yes, we may be fighting a new foe, but make no mistake, we do not fight blind. Even those of us not blessed with the Third Eye.”

A few chuckles went around the tent but Roshan was not among them. He found no levity or joy here. Only duty.

“It seems they cannot resist using their great magic, so they will spend it the moment our vanguard enters the maw. We shall let them. My riders will gallop through it, for we are demons and such magic has never bothered us before. After that, it will be flesh against flesh, where no human has ever been our equal.”

“And the wall?” the Chitran commander asked.

“That particular honor goes to the Samsara,” Jagath said, raising his glass to Roshan. “Their hundred and eight will siege the face, into the very thick of it. I can almost picture the terror on the humans’ faces as they watch our blessed immortals fall only to rise again and again, the enemy’s morale cracking and splintering along the way. I think it will not take overly long. They are a weak people, after all. In body and spirit both.”

Another round of laughter and claps of approval, and again, Roshan did not partake, fully aware of how much his behavior offput those around him.

It hardly mattered. Demons were no longer his kin. Only his Samsara bore that title now. Them, and their one true god.

“Then when the wall is taken and the Gates thrown open, we ride through,” Jagath declared before turning to a corner where Raja Sagun’Ra was perched atop a table. “And our Iksana friends are already past it. Where there are shadows there are Iksana, and our ghael friends have already started carrying Pagan Order infiltrators with them. Fewer of their mejai will be standing to cast than they have counted on, I think.”

Roshan knew the truth of that. One did not see a ghael coming until they were already behind you. They had always been the masters of infiltration and always would be.

It seemed very much to Roshan that the humans had built their wall to fend against cavalry and giants, and had entirely forgotten to guard the dark places.

Yes, the plan was sound. He’d studied it a dozen times and found no flaw. The Chitran would move as one mind beneath Demonic Overlord, tighter in formation than the most elite ranks of men.

All while the Panav lifted the fallen back into the line. And they, the Samsara, would do what the others could not.

The Akh Nara might have been missing from this field of battle, but it was his power that would climb that wall. For the Samsara were his arms and his legs. His hands, his feet, and his eyes, and they would not dishonor him this night.

“It sounds so clean when you put it like that,” Tara said, speaking for the first time in this meeting. “The Samsara might rise from the dead, but what of the rest of us? The larger the army, the higher the death toll. Make no mistake, there will be an ocean of blood spilled by the time this night is over. All for a fortress we could starve out instead. And have you forgotten about Maiya? About that Vimana she commands, floating above the battlefield?”

Jagath scoffed. “A bluff, and nothing more. You will see it clearly in a few hours’ time. As for starving them, it is a sound strategy… were this any other time. Now, however, a protracted siege will only hand the humans an entire winter to raise every able-bodied man they can muster. I’m sure I need not remind anyone here that our foes outnumber us ten to one.

“No, we break their wall now, and their backs with it. And then, with their morale in pieces, the human lands will be open for the taking.”

His gaze went around the tent, meeting every eye. “Matiman has been dead for four months. Murdered in his own square in broad daylight, may his soul rest with the gods. We answer that atrocity here, and in every town we burn after our glorious victory this night!”

The room went up in cheers, and this time, Roshan did join in.

He had brought his Samsara here for nothing more and nothing less, and when Tara gave him a disappointed gaze from across the tent, he returned it with a solemn one.

“One last thing,” Jagath said. “Your Chakras. Tell your people that all who have opened their Warrior Chakras are instructed to use it without mercy. The humans have no shield for it, and it is the surest weapon we hold.”

Even in war, there had always been an unspoken rule to use Chakras only sparingly and strategically. Where they could be deployed for the most gain.

A code of honor, of a sort, among demons.

Yet as all here assembled knew, their enemy this time was no demon. They had struck first. No quarter would be given.

Jagath stood up, beaming with pride as he swept his arms wide. “Then, my brothers and sisters, we march at dusk, and may the Akh Nara be with us all.”
