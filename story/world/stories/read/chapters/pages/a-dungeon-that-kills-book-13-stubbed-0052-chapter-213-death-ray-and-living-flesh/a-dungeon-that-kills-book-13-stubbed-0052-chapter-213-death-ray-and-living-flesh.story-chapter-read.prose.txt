The Apostle took a step forward. “You’re an attentive host,” he said, that same thin smile stitched to his face like it had been sewn there and forgotten. “I must say, I expected we’d exchange a few pleasantries before we got to the fighting. But I suppose men who have spent a few thousand years in some forgotten tomb rarely make for lively company.”

Khenemhotep did not deign to answer. He stood erect before his golden throne, his desiccated, bandage-wrapped form looming over his foe. He lifted one withered hand, and from behind the walls came the answer to his summons.

Skeletal warriors emerged first, clad in bronze armor that gleamed dully under the torchlight, their sickle-shaped swords raised high and ready, their empty eye sockets, black and bottomless and betraying no emotion, all fixed unerringly upon the enemy who had dared to intrude.

Behind the throne, eight undead mages stepped out of the stone, tattered robes hanging loosely from their bodies. They arranged themselves into a neat line just behind their master.

The warriors, now fanning out to encircle the Apostle from every side, surged forward all at once in a rattling, clattering charge. However... well, even though these guys carried fancier gear than their cousins out in the desert, bone was still bone. If that man could easily dispatch a towering tomb guard with a single punch, then this lot was never going to be more than an irritation. And sure enough, a casual backhand, almost bored, and the nearest skeleton went spinning into the wall in a hail of splinters. The Apostle didn’t even bother watching it land.

But of course, Khenemhotep never intended to face down this formidable enemy with nothing but a handful of shambling corpses. From the twin orbs of green light that burned within his eye sockets, two searing torrents of that same energy erupted, lancing across the chamber and forcing the Apostle to hurl himself to the side to evade them. The other mages joined the assault as well, though this time they refrained from unleashing the usual barrage of rocks that they might otherwise have favored, since there were a lot of skeleton warriors in the way. Instead, their attacks came from above and below. Great spears of hardened sand tore free from the ceiling in sudden, violent bursts, each one aimed with lethal precision at impaling the Apostle where he stood, while scattered patches of the floor under his feet shifted and liquefied into pits of quicksand, waiting for a wrong step to drag him down and swallow him whole.

The gray-haired man danced around the relentless onslaught, but even he couldn’t dodge everything. Now and then one of those green beams found him, and when it connected, the results were spectacularly horrific. Flesh withered, blackened, and crumbled to dust in the space of a heartbeat, as though decades upon decades of decay had been compressed into a single merciless second. All that muscle, strong enough to break steel and crush stone, proved utterly powerless against that green light. When the light came, the flesh simply gave up and died.

If Viktor had to guess, the magic Khenemhotep used here worked by canceling out life itself at its root. Made sense, really. Death magic, it seemed, was not limited to what could be done with someone’s soul after they died. It was just as capable of making living things, well, un-live.

And that explained why the Apostle regarded the ancient priest as the single greatest threat in this dungeon. After all, his power was based on the manipulation of living flesh—dead flesh too, granted, but that was because meat was meat, and people had been consuming the flesh of dead creatures to build their own living muscle all the time—while Khenemhotep’s magic completely neutralized the vital force driving the tissues in his body.

Nevertheless, taking a couple of good hits, however dramatic they might look in the moment, was not enough to put down a man like this. Those beams punched holes clean through him, true enough, but the moment the light passed and moved on, flesh came crawling in from the edges to fill the gap, repairing it anew.

Looks like it doesn’t do much unless it manages to strike a vital spot, Viktor thought.

That man had once mentioned his brain specifically, almost as if daring his opponent to go looking for it. If those words could be taken at anything resembling face value, then a hit there might do some real damage, or even kill him outright. The trouble, of course, was that nothing guaranteed the thing was housed inside his skull like a civilized organ. With a man like that, it could be lodged anywhere from his elbow to his ankle.

Thus, what was unfolding in the Chamber of the Dead amounted to a grinding battle of attrition. Every beam that found its mark stripped away a little more of the Apostle. It was not much, but it accumulated over time. Viktor doubted the man kept an infinite supply of meat inside himself, so sooner or later, that reserve would run dry.

His opponents, on the other hand, were not going anywhere anytime soon. A battle of attrition was a battle the dead would never lose. Every skeleton the Apostle smashed into fragments quickly reassembled itself and rejoined the fight soon enough. And even if one of them wandered into the path of Khenemhotep’s green beams by accident, it made no difference whatsoever. These things were simply incapable of getting any deader than they already were.

Viktor doubted the Apostle didn’t understand that. So he believed the man had merely been playing around. After all, there was no reason for him to trade blows with these walking bones when he could just use his superhuman strength to smash straight through them and close the distance to Khenemhotep in a matter of seconds.

Though it looked like the gray-haired man had finally decided a change of approach was in order. Suddenly, he broke into a sprint straight toward the ancient priest, shoving skeletons aside like a man wading through tall grass. Patches of quicksand yawned open in his path, but he leapt clean over them without breaking stride. Barriers of solid stone rose up to block his way, but he drove his bare fists straight through, shattering each and every one of them in a violent explosion of dust and flying debris. Beams of green light lanced toward him, but he flowed around them with an almost liquid grace. And soon enough, he stood directly before Khenemhotep.

The priest didn’t flinch. He merely closed his left hand over the staff his right already gripped—a staff he had always carried but never actually put to use until this very moment. The orb crowning its tip flared to life with brilliant green light, and in a heartbeat, that light came pouring forth and condensed itself into the shape of a blade. Khenemhotep swung, and the blade of green passed clean through the Apostle from hip to hip.

The result was immediate and catastrophic. In the blink of an eye, the man’s stomach was vaporized, and his two severed legs dropped to the ground with a thud. As the upper torso was still suspended in midair, two beams of green light erupted from the priest’s eye sockets, aimed squarely at the head.

The Apostle snapped it backward to dodge the beams. At the same time, he flung his arm out behind him, the limb stretching as though it was elastic, until his hand closed around the shattered remnant of one of the stone barriers he had destroyed earlier. He pulled himself back, hauling his ruined body out of harm’s reach and putting distance between himself and the ancient priest once more.

“Scary, scary,” he said with a laugh that held far too much amusement for a man who had just misplaced half his body. Flesh was already pouring down his ravaged torso, flowing and knitting itself together to repair everything from his abdomen. “I still can’t quite believe you’re wielding that power of yours so casually. After all, it’s a spell created by Nakhran himself.”

What?

“By my own tongue have I bound myself by an oath: this accursed might shall never again be loosed upon the living,” Khenemhotep said in his usual raspy voice. But he did not pause the assault to indulge in conversation. Even as he spoke, the undead under his command surged forward once more, attacking without so much as a hint of hesitation. “But you, I count not among the living.”

“Hypocrite. Just like your god.” The Apostle grinned as he turned to deal with the skeletons around him, his lower body continuing to reform all the while.

The heavy clang of metallic footsteps heralded the arrival of the Templars, soldiers in gleaming silver armor armed with sword and shield and long halberds, pouring into the chamber through the entrance connected to the Great Hallway. More of them came from the door behind the golden throne, climbing up here from the third floor to assist the ancient priest.

The situation seems to be under control, Viktor allowed himself to think. If the Apostle tried to close the distance and attack Khenemhotep directly again, he would be cut down by the staff. But if he chose to remain where he stood, he would be fighting a war of attrition he had no real hope of winning. So far, so good.

The battle raged on. The gray-haired man seemed to take the fight more seriously now, as he had stopped using his bare fists. A sword of white bone had appeared in his right hand, while a shield of dark chitin had sprouted on his left to match it. Both had been produced from his own body, of course. Viktor found himself wondering whether that shield had been made entirely from the swarm of insects the man had eaten earlier, or if the guy had gorged himself on some similar feast before he even set foot inside the dungeon.

One more thing had changed as well. It was that the Apostle had stopped moving around too much. Before, he had been a blur of constant motion, leaping and darting and never staying still for more than an instant. Now, however, he kept his legs planted against the stone floor, shifting his stance only in small, careful steps.

Did the effort of conjuring those weapons slow him down somehow, or was there some other game being played?

As expected, parries and blocks worked well enough against ordinary blows, not so much against deadly beams of green light. They passed through the sword and the shield like they weren’t even there, continuing on to punch hole after hole into the flesh behind them.

Viktor frowned. This is very odd. Fighting like this was obviously putting the gray-haired man at a disadvantage. What is he plotting?

He swept his vision across the whole of the chamber, searching for anything that seemed even slightly out of place. Anything hidden beneath the chaos, amidst the green beams carving through the air, the silvery blades flashing under torchlight, the shattered stone and bone fragments and broken metal spinning through the haze of battle.

And then, he saw it. Movement.

A snake?

It was thin and glistening, slithering low across the floor, starting from the very spot where the gray-haired man had planted his foot and remained anchored.

And it was creeping toward his old leg, one of the two ruined pieces still lying on the ground near the ancient priest, the leftover wreckage from when he had been bisected earlier.

The instant the tendril of flesh made contact with the severed appendage, the entire mass bloomed, unfurling into a blanket large enough to engulf a full-grown man. It kept spreading, fast, poised to swallow Khenemhotep whole while his attention was fixed on his opponent’s main body across the chamber.

“Celeste, tele—”

“Teleportation is forbidden in this room,” said the Apostle in an eerie voice, a tone completely different from anything he had used before.

What!?

And then, before Viktor could do a damn thing about it, the sheet of meat folded itself around Khenemhotep and consumed him entirely.
