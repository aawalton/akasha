Classes resumed with alarming normality. Of course, Brin’s fight with Vitor was the only thing anyone could talk about, but they talked about it as just the latest topic of gossip, not as a world-shattering revelation. A skinless red man leaping out of Vitor to defend him after Brin took the duel too far was surprising, of course, but not terribly so, compared to some of the past Midsummer Balls.

A few ladies came by to tell Brin they were upset with him, not for any sane reason, but because Brin had timed his disruption far too early in the evening and they hadn’t gotten to the part where they were all supposed to make flower crowns, something they’d been looking forward to.

But those were only a few, and if anything, Brin was more popular than ever. Sancha and Ares had to work overtime to fend away his admirers and would-be dueling partners.

He sought out Vitor between classes, and finally cornered him in a hall. There were the same two high-level [Water Mages] with him, and Brin was starting to think they weren’t actually Vitor’s friends.

“Back off, Lord Mistaken. Vitor has no business with you,” said one, while the other said to Vitor, “Not a word. Don’t speak a single word to that man.”

“But I have business with him,” said Brin, and tossed a black sack full of coins in Vitor’s direction.

Vitor must’ve feared it was some sort of attack, because he was able to catch it on the first try. Brin really wasn’t here to mine information, but he’d just gotten some anyway. Apparently, the activation on his curse had a whole lot more to do with Vitor’s perception of “in combat” rather than the reality.

Ten golden capitals didn’t look like much if you only saw the sack; it wasn’t until Vitor opened the drawstring and peered inside that his eyes widened at the glint of gold.

“I can’t accept this. You won the duel,” said Vitor.

“It wasn’t a clean win. I escalated way past anything we talked about. And we agreed to three wounds, but I kept going even after that,” said Brin.

“That makes it a draw at most,” said Vitor.

“Please just take the money. I would give you an apology instead, but what would I say? We’re on opposite sides of this, and sometimes we’re asked to do things. I’m sure you get it. Duke Cobol–”

Vitor stepped forward, squaring up. “Duke Cobol is a great man.”

“Not another word about the Duke,” said one of Vitor’s tenders. “We’re done here.”

The two pulled Vitor away again, leaving Brin alone in the hallway.

He stewed over the conversation for the next two classes, though it took him a while to even really understand why he was so upset about this. The long and tedious repetition of the calligraphy class kept his mind free to dwell on it, and he kept turning it over and over.

It was the naivete that bothered him the most. It was how everyone talked about a possible rebellion in the kingdom like it was just a game, just another political event that could happen like the replacement of a minister or a change in tax policy. This was a war they were talking about. And the two of his classmates who should know better, Bia and Vitor, didn’t seem to have a problem with it at all.

His next class was Philosophy, where long-bearded Brahm asked few questions and instead took the chance to drone on about the way that the concept of the common good evolved in the post-Ithmallian era. Bia went out of her way to sit next to him, and then followed him out of the room when it was finished.

As much as he’d primed himself to be cold with her, all of that fled the moment an attractive woman in his strike zone was smiling at him. And she hadn’t joined the duel against him, had she? That had to mean something.

“You really need to lean closer during class so that you can listen to my witty commentary. I had some zingers,” said Bia.

“You could tell them to me now,” said Brin.

“Nah, it wouldn’t be the same. It’s got to be live or nothing,” said Bia.

“We still have Enchanting together,” Brin suggested.

“You don’t think Baltezar is low-hanging fruit?”

“Because he’s short? Is that a short joke?” asked Brin.

“What? No!” said Bia.

“Because that’s beneath you, Bia.”

“I swear on Anshar I didn’t even mean it that way,” she protested.

“It’s so far beneath you it’s practically on Baltezar’s eye level,” said Brin.

“Ok. Alright,” said Bia, starting to understand he was teasing her.

“First she says commoners are our natural slaves. Now she’s making fun of short people…” He meant to say it lightly, but she must have heard something in his voice that he hadn’t intended because she suddenly sobered up.

They walked in silence for a moment. He was suddenly extremely aware of all the others walking nearby. Sallvador and Rafael. Bia and Ares. A few other ladies, a few other gentlemen, all of them listening close and casting each-other knowing looks.

Bia suddenly pushed Brin through an open doorway, shutting the door behind them. It was an empty classroom. There was a circle of chairs around a recessed floor, but no teaching materials or decorations on the walls.

“I think I put my foot in my mouth back at the ball the other day,” said Bia.

“You said what you really think,” said Brin.

“No, I don’t think I did. Look, I… I understand if you see nobles as these big hands that take and take and give nothing back, but can you at least believe that there are some of us who don’t think that’s right? We’re supposed to serve our people, not the other way around. In Guven, we really mean that. We don’t live in a house taller than our neighbors, or speak differently, wear clothing they couldn’t, or eat any food that isn’t commonly found in the market that the average commoner couldn’t afford. What we do is protect them, guide them, and make decisions based on how it will benefit Guven for the next hundred years rather than what’s easiest right now.”

Brin’s teenage body was fixated on the fact that he was alone with a girl who was standing very close, but he had the presence of mind to play it cool. He stepped back and folded his arms. “You had that little speech ready to go.”

“I’m paraphrasing something my dad says a lot. My point is, we take our responsibility to the land seriously. Even if that doesn’t make us popular in the short term, because that’s what it means to be a noble,” said Bia.

“That’s all well and good until you remember that Cobol’s tortured many of his own people into becoming monsters in a bid to make himself powerful enough to take the throne.” Brin could tell Bia wanted to interject from the way she bit her lip, but she couldn’t be goaded into revealing more of Cobol’s plans.

When she didn’t speak, he continued. “I mean, sure, we all know [King] Lancarote is a dick–”

Bia choked. “You… you can’t say that!”

“--but I’ve hardly seen him do anything worth rebelling over. We’re talking about thousands of lives here. And that’s not even to mention your attitude about the Oath slaves.”

“They are not slaves, Brin. Let’s make that clear. Oaths have limits and these people have rights, and you’re insulting them by calling them slaves, not me,” said Bia.

Brin flinched, but relented. “Fine. I’ll give you that much.”

“And these decisions always sound so easy until you’re the one who is the one who has to make a choice. You’ll get your chance to put your money where your mouth is soon enough.”

“I already know what I’ll say,” said Brin.

“No, I mean, for real. I’ve heard something. I’m telling you this as an olive branch, so that you’ll have time to think about what you want to do.”

“Are you saying… Is it Ares?” Brin guessed. He felt sick at the thought of it. Being that much richer than Ares was already a wedge.

“No. You’ll see,” said Bia. She reached forward and Brin felt his heart rate spike, but she was just grabbing the doorknob. He opened it to reveal a group of girls leaning their ears against the door–they’d heard nothing; Brin had warded it for sound the second they’d walked through.

The crowd at the door scuttled to step back as Bia marched through, walking away without another word.

Brin didn’t have to wait long to find out what she was talking about. Tonin informed him at the end of classes that day that he’d finally worked out a time and day to start lessons with Martin, the Dean of the Mind Track. According to Tonin, who’d been working with Martin’s assistants, Martin always underestimated how much time his sessions would take and so they were constantly forced to reshuffle appointments.

Brin’s angst about Vitor and Bia was forgotten in favor of the dread of meeting Martin again. He knew he needed to do it. [Terrifying] was a liability unless he could bring it under control. And he knew he didn’t have much to fear, because Brin’s mental resistances were strong enough that he was sure he’d be able to notice any tampering, even from someone as strong as Martin. But [Mind Mages] creeped him out; that was all there was to it.

Brin held out hope until the very last moment that Martin would overbook again and they’d need to reschedule, but when the appointment came due and Brin entered the Mind homeroom, he found Martin there waiting for him.

Just like last time, he found that he couldn’t really focus on the details of the room or even count how many others might be there as well, except for one girl sitting on a hard-backed chair next to Martin’s lounger. It was Monica, the girl who’d tried to mess with his mind the last time he’d been here.

Martin waved lazily, “Have a seat, if you’d like.”

Brin noticed another chair facing the other two, and sat down.

When no one spoke first, Martin nodded to Monica.

Staring at the ground, she said, “Lord Mistaken, please allow me to apologize for my indefensible conduct when we last met. My use of mental magic was akin to a grievous assault upon your person. I humbly apologize and beg for your forgiveness.”

“Did you put her up to it? I thought you might have sent her after me to prove a point,” Brin activated his two spells that measured biometrics to see if he could suss out lies.

“I did not. I wouldn’t dare,” said Martin.

The spells reported that his pulse was even, that his skin temperature did not fluctuate, and that his eye-twitch speed was completely average. The spell measuring his pulse, though, pulsed a bit of red at the back of Brin’s eye where no one could see it, a common way for the spells in his Glassmind to alert the user that the spell had failed.

He cast “The Unerring Lie Detector”, even though it was overdone and he didn’t completely trust it.

“I did, however, prompt her to make this apology,” said Martin.

This spell was complex enough to interface directly with his internal message log, and it left him a note.

The Unerring Lie Detector: Be warned. The target’s body maintains an inhuman level of regularity.

It appeared that Martin wasn’t susceptible to lie-detectors. Still, he doubted Martin was lying.

Monica still had her head bowed. “It shames me that I required Martin’s counsel in order to understand the grave nature of my offense.” She grit her teeth and then continued. “In recompense, I will grant you my fealty by way of an Oath on my Class and levels.”

Brin’s lie-detector spells told him that her heart rate and temperature were on the other end of the spectrum from Martin’s; she was practically having a nervous breakdown, though she kept her body still.

“No thank you,” said Brin. “Consider it forgotten.”

Bia was wrong. That hadn’t been hard at all.

Rather than look relieved, Monica grimaced as if she’d been stabbed. “Please. Please accept my Oath. I’ll do anything. I’ll clean your clothes. I’ll cook your meals. I’ll… I’ll…”

“I have servants for that. I can pay them with money. No Oaths required,” said Brin.

“But how much can you really trust them without an Oath?”

“Do you really want to swear to me?” Brin asked.

“Of course! Of course I do! That’s why I’ve asked,” said Monica. He didn’t need his lie-detector spell to know this was the last thing she wanted. The frantic desperation in her eyes was enough. “Brin isu Yambul, I swear to render you all good fealty, to obey your commands and–”

“I swear I’ll order you to reset your levels. First thing. I don’t want your Oath,” said Brin.

“You don’t have to decide right now. Just think about it,” she said, clasping her hands together to keep them from shaking.

“I already know my answer,” said Brin.

“Just think about it. That’s all I ask. Please, just consider it.”

“I’ll think about it, but–”

“Thank you. Solia bless you, Lord Mistaken. You won’t regret this,” said Monica. She was already walking to the door, rushing to get out of the room before he could tell her no again.

Brin watched the closed door, because it gave him an excuse to keep his eyes away from Martin. He could feel the man’s pressure on his mind that made him unable to see anyone or anything else in this room. He was fairly sure he could push it away if he tried, but he didn’t want to. If this is the amount of trouble he got for exposing Monica he didn’t want to see who else was in this room.

“You made the correct decision,” Martin said blandly.

“I know,” said Brin.

“Mentalists require two virtues above all: discretion and self-control. Her first meeting with you proved that she has not developed either. She would make a terrible servant,” said Martin.

Brin scowled at him. “And because Oaths of servitude are wrong.”

“Interesting. How so?” asked Martin.

Brin narrowed his eyes. “What’s your game here?”

Martin gave the barest hint of a shrug, not changing his empty face in the slightest. “I suppose I thought I might convince you to spare her life.”

“I wasn’t going to do anything about her attempted mental attack. I’d honestly forgotten about it.”

“She did not. She was not discreet,” said Martin.

“But what makes you think I’d kill her for it?”

“Why not?”

“Because murder is wrong,” Brin said, exasperated.

“You would let her go free?” asked Martin.

Brin shook his head, because they were going around in circles. He would say yes, of course he would, but then Martin could point out that she was a [Mage] with Mind powers and no self-control. In the Tower she was only a danger to herself, but out among the commoners she could end up hurting a lot of people.

He’d have to keep an eye on her. But then, it would be easier to keep an eye on her if she were sworn to obey him, wouldn’t it? The easiest thing would be if he told Monica to swear to someone else. But that wouldn’t be saving her from a System Oath, it would just be passing the buck to someone else. Someone who was very likely not as scrupulous as him. There were a lot of nobles in this Tower who’d be delighted to have someone like Monica on their staff for all the wrong reasons.

He could ask Lumina and Hogg for advice, but neither of them were against the system. They’d happily take this decision out of his hands, probably by having her swear to one of them.

He could’ve done what he’d threatened to do and just waited for her to finish the Oath and then forcibly reset her levels. But doing that would be making an enemy for life. What was to stop her from coming back ten years later as a [Witch]? He might as well kill her if he reset her Class. And now he was right back to the start again.

His last couple weeks of Philosphy classes gave him several ways to attack this problem, but that didn't actually help him figure out what was right. It was the moral clarity he'd discovered in the monastery that did that.

Letting her die woud be wrong. Letting her swear an Oath would be wrong. He'd need to solve this without doing either of those things.

“It’s a tricky problem,” Brin admitted.

“It is. And not completely the reason I asked you to come here today,” said Martin.

“You promised me you’d help with [Terrifying],” said Brin.

“I did, and I am. Just now, you experienced a number of negative emotions. I observed you during this conversation, and I believe there were moments when you were quite frightening. At other times, not at all. I don’t think it’s too soon to say that [Terrifying] rises and falls with your emotional state.”

This entire conversation had been a set-up to make Brin angry. He stood up. He’d let Martin’s mild manners and flat affect fool him; this man was just as crazy as everyone else in this Tower.

Martin remained unaffected. “I truly do hope you’ll find a way to save her. I wasn’t lying about that. I will not lie to you, not at any time. That is my promise. For this session, I had planned to ask you to do a bit of daydreaming and imagine scenarios that would upset you, because I needed to monitor you in an emotional state. Your reaction to Monica’s proposal satisfied that requirement in a way I honestly did not expect. Do you believe your views on System Oaths are influenced by your Arcaenean upbringing? Would the Oath have been more palatable had it been offered by a man?”

“Let’s get back to [Terrifying], if you don’t mind,” said Brin.

Martin didn’t blink. “Of course.”

“If it flares with my emotions, then that sounds like I won’t be able to control it. Not directly at least,” said Brin.

“Not necessarily. A toddler clenches his fists when he is angry. He screams and cries as well, and he can’t control any of these things. I say this by way of metaphor, and not as any insinuation on your character. Because the same toddler can learn to clench his fists when he is not angry. He might scream and yell as well. And with time, practice, and maturity, eventually he will learn to remain calm when he is upset.”

“That’s exactly what I need,” said Brin.

“Then let us begin.”
