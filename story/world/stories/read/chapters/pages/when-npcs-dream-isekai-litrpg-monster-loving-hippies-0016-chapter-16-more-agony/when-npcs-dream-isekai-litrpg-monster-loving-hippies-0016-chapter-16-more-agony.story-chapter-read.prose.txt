Sometimes crying helps. Sometimes it feels like you're washing the pain away.This wasn’t one of those times.

I felt like part of me had died. I was in a fog of darkness, barely present. I felt disconnected from reality—if I could even call this shitshow of a fantasy world “reality.”

Me and Juliette just sat there beside Bob’s body. Which was, come to think of it, actually dangerous. It wasn’t smart at all to linger in the middle of a battlefield with enemy patrols possibly nearby.

Then I heard a sound—a familiar screech, the high-pitched call of a scorpion suricata.

I turned my head. An adult one stood about ten feet away, moving slowly toward us. Through my teary eyes, I couldn’t even tell at first whether it was friend or foe. To be honest, I didn’t really care.

But as she got closer, we recognized her.

It was Jane—Bob’s mate. His wife.We’d met her once before. She wasn’t usually in the cave; her job was hunting with a care bear squad. Bob had talked about her a lot. He adored her.

We both reached out and placed our hands on her back.

She began to communicate.

She showed us a clear mental image: she had been waiting by Bob’s body for some time, under orders from the tribe leader—Alice. Her job was to meet us after the battle and tell us what had happened.

First, she showed us the children - safe. The majority of the tribe had escaped. They’d found a new location, not far from here, with shelter. The place wasn’t ideal—less food, no water source nearby, but it would do. It was far from any rival tribes and It was safe. She also showed us a mental map of how to get there. It was simple enough. We’d be able to find it.

“Good…” I managed to say. “The kids… the leader… they’re alive.”

“I’m so sorry,” Juliette whispered. “If we’d been faster—if we’d brought the spears just a few days ago…”

Jane probably didn’t understand all the words, but she understood enough.She responded with another memory: two of our armadillo care bears, holding the line against one of the terrifying cow-deer beasts, using spears—our spears. They held them effectively.

And because of that, the children escaped.

We both broke down crying again. But this cry was different. This was a better cry.Bob would be satisfied to know his children had survived.We held onto that thought.

After a few minutes, we quieted. Our breathing steadied. Jane had more to show us.

She told us to hide the supplies we brought, mostly spears, somewhere nearby. The tribe would retrieve it later and bring it to the new hideout.

And… she showed us an image of herself stinging us. Not as an order or demand. Just… as an offer.

“Thank you,” I said. “But not now.”

It didn’t feel right. Not so soon after Bob’s death. Not to be stung by someone else.

I wanted to bury Bob. But I remembered—it wasn’t the scorpion suricata way. The dead held no meaning. Not even the very sick or dying. They simply moved on.

So, we hid the supplies in the designated spot, covered them with foliage.Just as we were preparing to leave, Jane gave a low screech.Enemies.

I turned sharply.There they were—one white-striped surikata with two care bear soldiers. They were closer than I expected. We all froze. They were just as surprised to see us as we were to see them.

I could have run. We all could have. Jane was with us, and the hideout was already lost. There was nothing here to protect.

But they killed Bob.They fucking killed Bob!

So I did something that was really the opposite of... me—I took three quick steps and rammed my spear into the throat of the closest carebear. I put all my weight into the thrust. The spear pierced deep, and blood sprayed across my arm. He flailed violently, but I didn’t let go. I pushed harder.

There was another spurt of blood. He fell, twitched, then stilled. And then—just like we’d seen before—he vanished into thin air.

I felt it.

A pulse. Like Ramon described. That strange, subtle something in my body, filling a container I hadn’t known existed. It was EXP.

I didn’t care.

The second care bear was coming toward us, making a bark-like sound. Was that supposed to pass as a roar? I held my blood-dripping spear right in front of him. Then Juliette stepped up beside me, her own spear in hand. I think he knew he was outclassed and would retreat—unless he needed to protect his scorpion suricata master. He barked again and lunged forward, basically skewering himself on both mine and Juliette’s spears.

We both struck, hard. Spears driving deep into his chest and side. Over and over we jabbed, pushed, pulled. Blood sprayed. He staggered, collapsed, vanished. More weird EXP feeling.

I looked up.

The enemy scorpion suricata turned to flee—but Jane leapt in front of him, blocking his escape.

Now all three of us surrounded him. He screeched, and his fur began to vibrate. He wanted to talk.

Juliette stepped forward and stabbed him through the chest with one solid thrust.

No talking. He died. Vanished.

Jane made a high-pitched screech—her version of a victory shout—and climbed up onto my shoulder.

I placed my hand on her back.

She showed me what we’d just done—the enemies we killed. One of them, she revealed, was someone important in the white-stripe tribe. With his death—and the heavy losses they’d taken—their power had diminished.

It was supposed to make us feel good. It didn’t.

She then asked, visually, if we needed help returning to the town.

“No,” I said. “We’ll manage. Thank you. Goodbye.”

There was nothing else to say. I didn’t know how she felt about Bob’s death.Maybe she felt the same as us.Maybe worse.

We turned and left.

We walked back in silence.

My mind was blank. Grief and sorrow pressed down on me like a crushing weight.

"Hey. What are you doing?" Juliette suddenly stopped me, grabbing my shoulder. “This is horned hippo territory!”

Shit. I wasn’t paying attention. I could’ve gotten us both killed.

“Better if you lead,” I said. She nodded.

In the forest, losing focus could means death.

The problem was—I didn’t care. Not much anyway. I just felt empty.

"I..." Juliette started slowly, “I’ve never felt like this before. This… emptiness. But I had a dog once, Croquette. Part of the family. Me and my brother grew up with him. When I was twelve, a car ran him over. I was devastated.”

She paused.

“This is worse. But… what I’m trying to say is, it did get better. Eventually. And I think… I think this will too.”

I looked at her.

She was hurting too. Just like me. But she still tried to comfort me. Juliette was amazing like that.

And it helped. A little.

“Thank you,” I whispered. “I believe you. We’ll get better.”

But before it got better… it got worse.

We returned to the mansion, to Juliette’s room. We laid down—me on the couch, her on the bed.

Then we cried.

I’m not even sure who started first—probably me.

We slept a lot. Barely moved. Barely spoke. I think it was midday the next day when I woke up. Didn’t want to. Just did.

We didn’t go to work. Didn’t eat. Didn’t speak.

Just… existed. In that haze of grief and emptiness.

It was like every part of me had slowed down.Like moving through syrup. Like sound was far away.Like I was underwater, but there was no light above.

I think Juliette was the same.

A day passed. Or two. Maybe three. I lost count.

Neither of us ate all that time. I didn’t feel hungry.But I knew it wasn’t okay.

“I… think we need to eat something,” I finally said. “It’s… it’s not healthy.”

Juliette looked over, face pale and sunken. “You’re probably right.”

She glanced around the room. “We don’t have anything here. We brought everything to the tribe.”

The mention of the tribe hit like a blade.

“We have money,” I said. “I’ll go to the market. Just get some bread.”

“That’s good,” she said softly. “Good idea.”

Getting up? Much harder than it sounded.

But I forced myself. For Juliette. She needed to eat. I stumbled like a zombie through the mansion hall, out the door, into the light.

The sun was way too bright.

I walked the town, clutching a few silver coins, heading toward the market. The noise, the crowds, the brightness—it was too much.

The world spun.

I fainted.

When I woke up, I was lying on a sofa in a dimly lit room.The smell of warm pastries filled the air. For the first time in days, I felt hungry.

Two figures were nearby. I blinked. They came into focus.

One was the Korean guy—Hyun-woo.

The other was new. A brown-skinned girl. Very young.

“You were right,” said the girl with a smile. “He’s not dead. Just really stinky.”
