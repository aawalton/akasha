Orban stood near the front of the ship, leaning against the bulwark as he stared at the world below. Veronica stood to his right, following his gaze.

Forests swept past beneath them like waves of green, broken only by thin dirt roads and patches of open land. Birds drifted along the air currents, rising and falling beneath Mirabelle as clouds passed lazily overhead.

Even now, the old dwarf still looked astonished. “You’re tellin’ me this ship is actually yours?” Orban asked at last. “Not some borrowed vessel?”

Veronica nodded. “Yes. It’s mine. Twenty million vix, just like I told you.”

Orban hummed low in his throat, still processing the thought. After a moment, he turned fully toward her. “So yer tellin’ me an archmage bought this for ya? That you’ve got some kind of connection with the Mage Association?”

“I do,” Veronica said. “Archmage Ballamus owed me a rather significant favor.” She glanced toward where Theo had disappeared below deck. “If I remember correctly, you and your apprentice left the auction before the final lots.”

Orban gave a short nod. “Aye. That’s right. After ya snatched the starmetal right from under my nose, I’d had enough. Didn’t think the rest was worth stayin’ for.”

“Well, that’s a shame,” Veronica said. “Quite a lot happened after you walked out.” She swept one hand toward the ship around them. “Including this.”

Orban crossed his arms, studying Veronica with a hard, unreadable expression. “Alright,” he said at last. “You’ve convinced me you’re no ordinary lass. Nobody buys a ship like this. Hell, even most nobles would struggle ta justify spendin’ twenty million vix on an airship.”

His eyes narrowed. “So tell me. What exactly do ya want from me?”

“Well, I’m glad you asked.” Veronica allowed herself a small smile. “I’m looking to have a staff forged. Given your history with starmetal, I trust you know its properties?”

“Aye. Power of the stars and light,” Orban said with a slow nod. “Good for mages attuned ta light and martial artists who draw on constellations. Not many can use it, but for the ones who can, there ain’t much better.”

“I’m assuming that means you understand why I wanted it.”

“You’re a light mage, then?”

“Precisely,” Veronica replied. “The moment I saw the starmetal at the auction, I knew I wanted it. A staff forged from it would serve me for years.”

“So you want me ta make ya one?”

“That’s right,” Veronica said. “And my offer still stands.”

She clasped her hands behind her back. “I’d like to bring you on under my merchant company, Zenith. It’s based in Ronswick. We’re small now, but growing quickly. My partner’s name is Galland. I say this with the utmost respect: he is a brilliant man. Not only that, but we also have direct ties to the Mage Association. The only merchant guild in the kingdom to do so. You’d have access to some of the greatest mages in Vitian, work when you wanted it, and no chains to hold you down. Just an occasional commission when Zenith needs your hands. I’ll even provide a forge and home for you when I can.”

Orban went quiet. His brows lowered as he thought it over, and for once, he didn’t spit out an immediate insult.

Eventually, he exhaled and shook his head. “It’s… a temptin’ offer. Hard ta turn down. ’Specially if I really keep mah freedom.” He paused, his expression hardening again. “But I can’t make that staff for ya.”

Veronica frowned. “Why not?” She studied him for a moment before continuing. “Is it the payment? I know I can’t hand you twenty million vix today, but I can promise you’ll receive it. If you’re willing to wait a year, then I can even get the Mage Association to sign a—”

“It ain’t about the vix,” Orban said, cutting her off with a grunt. “Hell, after hearin’ you talk, I’d probably wait the year as long as ya cover my living expenses.”

“Then what’s the problem?”

“The forge.”

Veronica’s brow furrowed deeper. “The forge?”

Orban nodded once. “I don’t have one capable of refining starmetal. Nothin’ here can work the metal precisely enough. I’d ruin it before we even got close.”

Veronica stared at him for a long moment. “What are you talking about? If you couldn’t forge starmetal here, then why were you trying so hard to buy it at the auction?”

“Because there’s a difference between forgin’ starmetal for a blade and forgin’ starmetal for a staff.” Orban reached down and picked up a small pebble on the deck that he had tracked in with his boots. He rolled it between his fingers. “Blades, armor, shields—those are simpler. Shapes. Lines. Curves. Any magic can be layered on after. ‘Chanted on top if the metal is refined well enough. More of an enchanter’s field’a’work.”

He flicked the pebble over the railing and watched it fall, vanishing into the open air.

“A staff’s different. The crystal has ta sit in the metal from the start. The whole thing needs ta be forged in one heat. Shaft, channels, even the mana flow—all of it has ta harmonize together before the metal settles.”

Veronica tilted her head slightly. “Couldn’t you make the body of the staff first and add the crystal later?”

“For a wand? Maybe. For a cheap staff? Sure, if ya don’t care about quality.” Orban scoffed. “But for starmetal? No. Add the crystal after, and ya waste half the point of usin’ the ore in the first place. The metal won’t bind right. The channels won’t sing their song. Ya might get a pretty stick worth more than a house, but it won’t be what it could’ve been. Though it’ll come in handy as a club if the need rises.”

Veronica remained silent.

“For that,” Orban continued, “I need a forge hot enough ta melt it clean through. Extreme heat. Stable enough pressure. Some binding arrays strong enough ta hold the focus in place while the metal forms around it.” His mouth twisted. “Nothin’ in Maftem comes close. Not for a staff. Something like that needs a proper, top-grade forge.”

“Then why bid on the starmetal at all?” Veronica asked. “If you can’t do anything with it, why even come to auction?”

“Because for blades and plate, I still could’ve done it. Wasn’t ‘specting a mage to actually want a staff. Those wit aura could use some knuckle gauntlets or a dagger. But a staff? Never crossed mah mind. To make that…” He shook his head. “It ain’t possible here.”

“Then where? There has to be a forge that can do it then, right?”

For a brief moment, something darker crossed his face. “The forges that can manage it are back in the Dwarven Kingdom,” he said. “And that’s… not an option for me anymore.”

Veronica noticed the way his jaw tightened. The way his eyes shifted back toward the clouds, as if he had said more than he meant to.

She let the silence sit and didn’t press.

“So, just to be clear,” she said after a moment. “If you had the right forge, you could do it?”

Orban gave her a sidelong look. “Aye. With the right forge, sure. Needs to be top grade that’ll compare to dwarven forges. But here? No chance. Unless you’re willin’ ta throw five million vix to rent a place in the capital. They probably got one there.”

“Annesheim is too far. Even for Mirabelle, it’ll take weeks if not months,” Veronica murmured, half to herself. Then she looked back at him. “But maybe… not impossible.”

Orban’s eyes narrowed. “What do ya mean?”

“There’s a place. Close, but far.”

He stared at her. Then he let out a short grunt. “Yer one of those bastards, huh? Riddles and cryptic talk. None’a that answers mah question.”

“I’ll explain later,” Veronica said. “I need to talk it through with the others first. It does involve something important to us.”

Orban hummed, looking at her with suspicion.

From below deck, the scent of toasted bread and eggs drifted upward, with something sweet and fruity beneath it. Both of them glanced toward the staircase leading to the lower deck.

Veronica smiled lightly. “How about lunch? My friend is trying to get better at cooking. And it seems like your apprentice wanted to help, too.”

Orban snorted, though the hard lines of his face eased just a touch. “That kid’s a better cook than he is a smith. I’ll need ta pummel that brat and set his head straight.” His shoulders relaxed. “Fine. Lunch first.”

He pushed himself off the railing and walked toward the hatch.

“Then we’ll get to your riddles.”
