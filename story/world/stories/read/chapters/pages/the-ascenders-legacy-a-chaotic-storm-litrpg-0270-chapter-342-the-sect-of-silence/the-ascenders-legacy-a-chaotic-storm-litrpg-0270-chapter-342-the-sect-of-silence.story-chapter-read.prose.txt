Elder Gumi walked down the corridor of the elder hall with Elder Kobi and Sedgwick trailing behind her, their expressions grave as they processed the latest news from the war front.

It was only the first day of battle, and they had already lost a Calamity. Not to mention the thousands upon thousands of Mythics whose lives had also been lost.

It was an unprecedented amount of devastation.

But it was one Elder Gumi had seen coming, despite hoping against it.

This war wasn’t just any balance war. It wasn’t a war to push back an already expanded Origin Plane, but one to push back an Origin Plane which, even with all the devastation and death, was still expanding, albeit significantly slowed.

The reason behind that expansion was simple.

In the last twenty-four hours alone, over three hundred new familiars had been birthed, with more still to come. Aodhán wasn’t the one driving the plane’s expansion any longer. Now, it was the Interface, and it was promising to be even more drastic than the addition of another transmigrant.

A smile touched her lips at that, but it was almost immediately wiped off when she remembered the shudder she’d felt through the Origin Plane at Shen Gu’s death.

He had been a dear, dear friend. Perhaps they could have been more had she allowed it. But now, that card was no longer on the table. He was gone.

One moment, he had been there. And then… he wasn’t.

A grimace touched her lips—the only manifestation of grief she allowed herself—and she sighed, banishing his memory from her mind with a deep inhale.

No point carrying the dead in her mind now.

Behind her, elder Kobi and elder Sedgwick regarded her worriedly, aware of the relationship between her and Shen Gu.

They knew their master to be a hard woman and didn’t expect her to cry or crack or anything of that sort. But they certainly expected more than a cold shift of attention.

After a while, elder Kobi spoke. “We’re sorry about your loss, Elder Gumi. Calamity Shen Gu was a powerful man.”

“And caring one.” Sedgwick added somberly. “He cared for you.”

Elder Gumi glanced at him for a moment, then nodded. “Yes, he certainly was caring. Not quite smart enough to truly understand the intricacy of runes, but he was caring.” She glanced at Kobi. “And powerful.”

Her gaze strayed for a moment, sorrow clouding them for a heartbeat before vanishing—there one moment and gone the next.

She shook her head and sighed again. “You shouldn’t worry. Both of you. I’m sure the other Calamities will avenge me thoroughly.”

Elder Kobi shared a resigned glance with Elder Sedgwick, who simply sighed and shifted the topic to something else as he suspected Elder Gumi would prefer.

Carefully, he lifted a bag of interfaces in the air and asked. “Do the High Elders really intend to go through with this plan of deception?”

Elder Gumi glanced at the bag and shrugged. “It’s a brilliant plan, if potentially… disastrous.”

“Not just potentially, Elder Gumi." Elder Sedgwick frowned. “It is a disastrous plan. When our enemies find out that the interfaces we gave them are not usable, they will not appreciate being made fools of.”

“Of course." Elder Gumi nodded. “But at least three weeks would have passed before they come to such a conclusion, which would give us more time to shore up our power, create more interfaces, and gain more familiars.”

“But—” Elder Sedgwick began, but a stern expression from Elder Gumi cut him off.

“I understand what you’re trying to say, Sedgwick. Pushing a problem to the future is not always a solution.”

"Exactly." Sedgwick nodded.

“And I’m not a High Elder.” Elder Gumi stated firmly, facing forward. “Mine isn’t to advise the First Seat but to craft the items he requests. I am an architect.”

Her words caused an awkward silence to descend—a silence that caused elder Gumi’s jaw to twitch in annoyance.

“I am not a High Elder.” She repeated, her words tinged with irritation this time. “I am an architect. Architects do not do politics.”

Elder Kobi and Elder Sedgwick exchanged glances once more, and after a heartbeat of hesitation, Elder Kobi nodded. “Of course, Elder Gumi.”

Elder Gumi nodded, and silently, they continued their walk toward the palace chamber where the High Elders and their underlings sat to watch the war.

They reached the chamber a few minutes later, bowing as they walked in after Elder Gumi to find an array of holographic screens showing different views of the ongoing war.

A few Calamities and Mythics sat with them in the palace hall, but despite their numbers, the chamber was nearly as silent as a graveyard, each person watching the screen with rapt attention.

A few of them turned to glance at them as they walked in, but when their gazes landed on elder Gumi, nearly all of them stood up respectfully, nodding in greeting.

Elder Gumi, of course, bowed as if she were a common Mythic, and turned her gaze to the High Elders. “I have completed the task you gave me, First Seat. I have created the false interfaces."

For a moment, a pained silence stretched within the hall, and then the First Seat spoke. “I’m sorry for your loss, Gumi. We are all sorry. We know you and Shen Gu were… close.”

Elder Gumi raised her gaze and nodded. “Thank you, First Seat. Casualties are a part of war. It’s a shame it had to be him, but then again, it had to be someone.”

The tense silence stretched tighter.

The First Seat flexed his finger in resignation and sighed. “Of course.” He glanced at the bag of interfaces in Elder Sedgwick’s hand and frowned. “I fear, however, that I’ve changed my mind about using the false interfaces. Your apprentice here had some very solid arguments.”

Elder Gumi stiffened, and Sedgwick jerked his gaze up in shock, earning himself a smile from the First Seat himself. His face reddened. “I… I forgot just how good the hearing of calamities are.”

The First Seat smiled wider. “Understandable. I and the other High Elders had contemplated the matter already. Your arguments sealed the decision for us.”

Sedgwick nearly floated to the sky with pride. “I… I am glad, First Seat, that my advice was beneficial to you.”

The First Seat nodded, then glanced back at Elder Gumi. “If we’re going to try and earn allies, then it's better to make permanent allies.”

“Permanent allies are a risk, First Seat.” Elder Gumi pointed out carefully. “If we give them functioning interfaces, who’s to say they won’t use the power they gain against us in the future?”

The First Seat shook his head, having already considered that angle before. “That’s just a risk I fear we’ll have to take.” He glanced at the screen, and his gaze hardened. “Let’s start with the Sect of Silence. They haven’t joined the war against us yet. Let us hope this will keep them permanently out of it.”

Elder Gumi glanced at the screen and slowly nodded. “The war was too volatile already. The last thing they needed was the assassins of silence joining in. Hopefully, this wasn’t a grave mistake.”

Two hours later, elder Kivasmus of the Storm Sect arrived at the outskirts of the Sect of Silence, bearing with him the Interface elder Gumi had crafted.

Having served as an underling to the First Seat for nearly a century now, Kivasmus was no stranger to war or politics. He had served as an agent in the last two Balance Wars the Storm Sect had been involved in, bargaining the death of a transmigrant against the Void Sect and later, the Sect of Order—just as the sects of Serenity and Earth were doing this time.

He knew just how they were feeling—the desperation, the fear of extinction. The Storm Sect had once filled their shoes, after all.

But Kivasmus was a pragmatic and logical man. He knew that predation was the way of the world, and he was perfectly fine with it.

What he wasn't fine with, though, was the unsettling silence that currently cloaked him in a suffocating embrace, twisting around his neck as if the very act of breathing went against its laws.

The sect itself was small, barely a dozen acres of land surrounded by tall, pale mountains whose stones were worn smooth, as if sound itself had eroded them over countless ages. But the Sect of Silence had never been known for their numbers—numbers that had nearly been cut in half after barely winning the war against the Sect of Music several decades ago.

They barely numbered in the tens of thousands now, but their diminished numbers had little effect on their deadliness and effectiveness in small and large-scale battles.

Silence, after all, wasn't just the absence of sound. It was the absence of all.

And as Kivasmus walked toward the sect, he could feel that suffocating absence as keenly as his own heartbeat.

For an elder cultivating the path of Raging Tempest, the complete absence of sound was beyond unsettling. His core ached for the rumble of thunder and the sizzle of lightning. But the silence pressed down on him like a wet blanket, muffling even the storm within his spirit.

Instinctively, he slowed, his aura pressing ahead of him to scout for danger. He found none—yet he could feel the eyes on him, watching, following, observing.

He wasn't alone. That much Kivasmus knew, but he also wouldn't be cowed into introducing himself to hidden sentries. He was an agent of the Storm Sect. Killing him before he could state his business to the First Seat would mean inviting a blood feud between their sects.

And for all their power and deadliness, a battle against the Storm Sect was not something they wanted.

He reached the sect gates a moment later—a massive structure of architectural mastery carved from black crystal and veined densely with silence runes. There were no sentries beside it. No guards to announce his presence. No weary-looking outer disciples to jump in fear and reverence.

Just absolute, deliberate silence—emphasis on deliberate.

Slowly, hesitantly, he stepped through the gate, and when, still, no one revealed himself, Kivasmus decided to force their hand.

Inhaling deeply, he spoke. "My name is Ravesh Kivasmus, Agent of the Sect of Storm. Take me to the First Seat."

The words barely lingered an instant after exiting his lips before the oppressive silence in the air swallowed them whole—snuffing them out immediately as if their mere existence was a crime. But they’d been spoken to already, and Kivasmus knew he’d been heard.

Still, for nearly two minutes, the silence hung heavy in the air—and then an androgynous figure stepped out from the wall opposite him. Not appearing so much as correcting his awareness, the silence is rearranging itself around the figure rather than breaking for it.

The figure stared at him in silence for a moment longer, their hair as white as frost and eyes dark and bottomless. A thin line of severe displeasure marked their expression, and finally, after what seemed to be ages, Kivasmus heard a reply in his mind—the voice cold and undeniably male.

"Storm Elder. You are loud."

Kivasmus straightened, not much appreciating the theatrics he'd seen so far.

"Apologies, Elder of Silence," he spoke again, bringing the tone of his voice to the absolute minimum lest he offend them further in their domain. "But I've come to speak of balance. I need to see your First Seat."

The elder stared at him for a moment longer, then tilted his head, gesturing for Kivasmus to follow.

Taking in another deep breath, Kivasmus did just as the elder commanded, trying his best to be as quiet as possible. For the next few minutes, Kivasmus heard and saw no one—even the natural sounds of the earth were muted here.

The entire Sect looked desolate, completely and totally bereft of life, but as they journeyed deeper, Kivasmus began to notice subtle signs of life and movement.

A swaying door. Ripples across a pond that suggested that people were swimming in it. Several carts of fresh fruits, food, and magical items. Their merchants were nowhere to be seen, but the basket of fruit hanging in the air suggested that there was definitely a customer—albeit one with poor control over their affinity.

Minutes passed in that manner, buildings blending in his mind until finally, they reached the grand chamber of silence—a massive coliseum-like building crafted from black crystals and lined with veins of silver.

The building stood about seven stories tall, with unbarricaded openings lining its walls in their hundreds. An oppressive aura of creeping dread radiated suffocatingly from it, and standing before the entrance, Kivasmus finally saw someone other than the androgynous fellow—a tall sentry with the same signature looks—only this time, it was female, her frost-white hair flowing past her waist and eyes like abyssal pits.

The androgenous fellow walked past the sentry without a word, and though no lips moved, Kivasmus sensed that some sort of communication had occurred between them. He locked gazes with the sentry as he passed by a second later—his gaze searching hers for a clue as to what she was thinking.

But as expected, he found none.

Sighing, he walked into the chamber and stiffened as his footstep echoed within the hall like a thunderclap. The sound did not reverberate so much as accuse him—an intrusion made manifest, a declaration that he did not belong.

The echo, however, barely lasted an instant before it too was consumed by the silence—severed so abruptly, the sudden silence left his senses disoriented, as though the world had skipped a breath.

Kivasmus slowed, feeling the eyes of hundreds bearing down on him with the weight of their displeasure. The sense of creeping dread deepened, and when he glanced back at the androgynous fellow, he found them smiling at him, or perhaps smiling at something he couldn’t hear.

Kivasmus grimaced and straightened, his voice rumbling with distant thunder. “Am I to believe I am before the First Seat of the Sect of Silence?”

The smile on the fellow’s face vanished, replaced by severe displeasure, and with a hiss, they finally spoke. “You are to believe nothing.”

Their words were soft, barely above a breath. Yet they landed with a crushing finality that left the air feeling heavier than before. More suffocating.

“You stand before the presence of the First Seat,” the androgynous fellow continued, each word measured with surgical precision. “Whether you are before us is another matter entirely.”

Kivasmus scowled, hating the theatrics. It would be so much simpler if the First Seat simply revealed themselves so he could make his offer and leave this oppressive sect behind.

Still, he was an agent, trained extensively in negotiation and restraint. The last thing he would do was antagonize his recipients before stating his purpose.

Inhaling deeply, Kivasmus steadied himself and spoke again, his voice carefully reined in.“I apologize for any displeasure I may have caused. Allow me to reintroduce myself. I am Elder Kivasmus of the Storm Sect. I have come at the request of my First Seat to speak with whoever listens here.”

He glanced at the androgynous fellow, at the sentry behind them, and then at the seemingly empty spaces between—where another stretch of silence pulled taut, like a held breath.

And then, after what felt like ages, the fellow inclined their head by a fraction.“You have already been heard.”

The words sent a faint tingle of discomfort down Kivasmius’s spine.

“You have walked our streets loudly,” they continued. “Your thoughts announce themselves. Your power resists quiet. Even now, your storm begs to be acknowledged.”

Kivasmus frowned. “And yet you permitted me entry.”

“Yes,” the fellow replied. “Because silence is not the absence of noise.”

They turned fully to face him, eyes pale and depthless. “It is the absence of permission. And you, messenger, have not yet been denied.”

A smile touched their lips then, the expression not at all friendly. “State the reason for your presence.”

Despite the unease curling in his gut, or perhaps because of it, Kivasmus wasted no time.

He reached into his spatial storage and withdrew an ornate box, opening it to reveal a pendant of depthless black traced with dull silver accents. It was one of the few true pendants Elder Gumi had crafted, and even from arm’s length away, Kivasmus could feel the absence radiating from it.

He extended the pendant toward the androgynous fellow and spoke plainly. “The Storm Sect intends to strike a bargain against Balance.”

Their eyes flicked to the pendant.

“A bribe,” Kivasmus added evenly, meeting their gaze, “to keep you out of the war. Permanently.”

The words echoed through the chamber like a dropped weight, and for the first time since Kivasmus had entered the Sect of Silence, he heard sound.

Not footsteps. Not voices.

Consideration.

The fellow stared at the pendant for a long moment before speaking. “We have no plan to enter the war.”

Kivasmus nodded. “The Storm Sect would like to keep things that way. Hence, our gift.”

Another faint disturbance rippled through the chamber, and Kivasmus allowed himself a small smile as he saw the fellow shift—listening. After a moment, they looked back at him.

“What is this item you have brought?”

Kivasmus raised the pendant higher, and though he could see no one else, he raised his gaze to the walls where he knew the elders of silence were seated.

“This is a chaos interface—or filter, as its creator mundanely calls it. For as long as history remembers, Mythics and Calamities have gained familiars through chance—by stumbling upon moments of clarity within the chaos of the Origin Plane. He turned slightly, addressing the chamber itself. “This pendant filters that chaos. It manufactures the moment of clarity we all seek.”

His smile faded as he lowered his arm. “It is one of the reasons the Storm Origin Plane has been expanding so rapidly. Our Mythics and Calamities are acquiring familiars at an unprecedented rate.” He met the androgynous fellow’s gaze and said quietly. “I understand how hard this must be for you to believe, but I am prepared to swear an oath upon it.”

For the first time, the fellow’s composure cracked—just slightly. Their eyes widened, and after a moment of terse silence, they spoke.

“Do it, then. Swear the oath, messenger.”

Kivasmus sighed but nodded. The swearing of oaths was one of the most hated parts of his job as an agent, but it was also one of the most important. And after so many years as an agent and so many oaths, what was one more chain around his core?

Inhaling deeply, he reached inward, past aura and intent, to his core itself—the raging heart of the storm that defined him—and spoke.

“I, Ravesh Kivasmus, Elder of the Storm Sect, swear upon my core that the pendant I have presented is genuine in function and intent. That it filters the chaos of the Origin Plane as described. That no deceit, suppression, or hidden restriction has been placed upon it by my sect, my First Seat, or me."

Chains formed around his core instantly, slithering around the hundreds of the other chains that bogged down his spirit with their karmic repercussions. Had he been lying, the chains would have tightened around his core to shatter it in an instant.

When that didn’t happen, Kivasmus finally let himself relax, the instinctive fear of oath-swearing draining from his muscles. He glanced at the androgynous fellow and smiled. “There, sworn. The storm sect means you no harm.”

A deep silence stretched between them for a moment, and then something shifted.

Not sound. Not movement.

But approval.

The androgynous fellow smiled and stretched their hand to the pendant. “Your oath is much appreciated, elder Kivasmus. The silence finds no fracture in your claim.”

Kivasmus nodded, his hold on the pendant still tight. “Then I shall ask for something in return.”

The chamber tightened again, attentive now.

“A reciprocal oath,” Kivasmus explained. “That the Sect of Silence sign an alliance pact with the Storm Sect. We do not have to be friends, but if we hand this pendant to you, we most certainly cannot afford to be enemies. The Sect of Silence shall not take hostile action against the Storm Sect so long as we do not act against you first. That is the pact.”

Once more, Kivasmus felt a ripple pass through the unseen assembly. Even the sentry behind him had turned to glance at him in shock. But Kivasmus kept his gaze on the androgynous fellow, whose expression had become completely unreadable.

Silence stretched between them—a conversation hidden from his senses—consideration threading through the silence like a fine wire.

And then the fellow spoke. “How long does it take the pendant to function?”

Kivasmus shook his head. “There’s no definite time line. It depends on the one who bonds it and how quickly they get invited into the Origin Plane. Three days to a week is a good timeline though.”

The fellow nodded, then turned slightly, their eyes unfocusing as though listening to voices that did not exist. Time stretched. The silence thickened, folded in on itself, and weighed every implication.

Finally, they looked back at him, their decision made.

“We shall make a temporary pact of alliance hinging on the working of the pendant. If it works, then the pact becomes permanent. If it doesn’t, then you will consider the oath void.”

Kivasmus stared at the man for a moment before nodding. “The Storm Sect accepts.”

He placed the pendant back in its box and handed it over to the androgynous man, feeling a weight lift off his shoulders immediately.

A temporary alliance was a better outcome than he had expected, and if, or rather when, the pendant worked—then they would have gained themselves a permanent ally in his war, increasing their chances of survival drastically.

Keeping the sect of Silence permanently out of this war was a victory all on its own. Now, they just had to find a way to remove the blood sect too, and then the war was as much as won.

Unfortunately, Kivasmus didn’t think a hundred pendants would be enough to dissuade the bloodthirsty, ritual-loving disciples of the Blood Sect.
