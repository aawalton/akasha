Anne rested her cheek against one hand and watched Spring clean the tables.

She had been doing so for more than an hour, and if the time counted as productive, it was only because she had learned a little more about Spring’s habits. The real reason for her attention remained the same one that had occupied most of her thoughts since the transformation.

She still did not understand it. Not truly.

She knew the sequence of events, the materials involved, the spirit at the core of the original design, the role of woodmancy in the body, and the effect of the life-blessed enthon. Yet knowing the steps and understanding the result were not the same thing. Somewhere between those things, a servant construct had crossed a line and become something alive.

If Anne could properly map that path, she would eventually write a book on it. The thought alone stirred her ambition. Such a work would almost certainly deepen her craft and earn her more grimoire pages. The homunculus design had been a good beginning—wood, spirit, structure, the binding to a soul-tree source—but it did not explain this outcome. Looking back, Anne had to admit the first change in Spring had already been unusual. She had simply failed to recognize how unusual.

No, the real key had to be the blessing. The three unknown symbols inside the enthon still stood at the center of the mystery. Her craft had let her sense their wide effect, but that was far from true comprehension. Worse, although the memory of them remained fresh in her mind, Anne could not reproduce them. That would require understanding deep enough to make the shapes meaningful, and she was not there yet.

Spring moved to the next table with dutiful care.

Anne watched the former homunculus’s hands, the way she handled cloth and dust, the small pauses in her motion that suggested thought. Even now, the sight remained faintly unreal.

“Tell me, Spring,” Anne said at last, more to amuse herself than from any serious expectation, “are you treefolk?”

Spring paused.

“Tree... folk?” she said.

The voice was not truly a voice, but a shaped current of wind. Anne had only recently begun hearing those halting sounds from her. Spring had decided she wished to be addressed as “she,” and along with that decision had come a determined effort to speak. Anne had tried to find records of how treefolk communicated, but the literature on them was scarce to the point of insult.

“Yes,” Anne said. “I think you might be. At least, you look like them.”

Spring tilted her head slightly.

“Spring... treefolk,” she repeated, sounding almost contemplative.

Anne smiled faintly and let the matter rest. She could hear voices outside, growing louder as they approached. Customers. She thanked Spring for cleaning and asked her to go inside before they entered.

A moment later, the bookstore door opened and five people came in together.

The golden trio and Rick looked much improved after a night of rest and proper washing. Cyrus’s wound had been cleaned, leaving him looking foolish rather than injured, which suited him. Rick, for his part, carried himself with more confidence than he had a week earlier.

But none of them held Anne’s attention for long. That privilege was reserved for the fifth customer.

A tall, beautiful elf woman stood among them, silver-haired and striking in the way only some elves managed without seeming to try. The resemblance to Ailen was obvious. A daughter, Anne concluded. The absence of jewelry or richer adornment distinguished her from her mother, but the simplicity of her light green tunic only made its quality clearer. Another piece of Ailen’s story had just walked into her bookstore.

Then Anne saw the girl’s eyes.

Dark green. Deeply so.

Anne’s attention intensified. That was no ordinary blessing.

She welcomed them all in, invited them to sit, and served tea. The introductions were brief, though by the time Asha spoke, it was already clear to Anne that the young elf had not come only out of politeness or curiosity.

“Miss Anne,” Asha said, “who left the room before we came in? I felt...” She hesitated, then gave a faint, self-conscious smile. “I’m not sure how to explain it.”

“Oh,” Anne said, understanding at once. “That is not surprising. Your natural anthomancy is strong enough that you would feel Spring quite clearly. If you wish, I can introduce you properly later.”

Instead of being pleased, Asha looked disappointed.

Anne noticed it immediately.

So the problem lay there. The girl had come seeking a new direction, and the first thing Anne had done was point toward the power she already carried. Before Anne could guide the conversation more carefully, Iris threw herself into it with the subtlety of a dropped pan.

“She’s our candidate for beastmancy,” Iris announced. “Her soul is much stronger than ours, and she’s definitely stubborn.”

The mage sounded entirely sincere and only somewhat nosy, which for Iris counted as tact.

Asha turned toward Anne again, this time with renewed focus.

Anne was now quite sure she understood.

“Oh,” she said. “I see.”

Then she asked an important question.

“And would you like to learn beastmancy, Miss Asha?”

The elf looked surprised, but this time pleasantly so.

“I would,” she said quickly. Then uncertainty returned to her expression. “But... what about my gift?”

There it was. The real knot.

Asha wanted a path that was hers. Everyone else, it seemed, wanted her to walk the path already laid.

Anne found that irritating on principle.

“What about it?” she asked. “A gift is something to use, not a chain to drag behind you. If you wish to learn beastmancy, then learn beastmancy. Your anthomancy already comes to you instinctively.”

Anne leaned in slightly and lowered her voice just enough to make the next words feel personal.

“Between us, a girl can use a few secret weapons.”

Asha’s mouth curved.

“Yes,” she said. “She can.”

That earned from Anne not only satisfaction, but something closer to kinship. The girl had understood the point, and perhaps a little more besides.

“Well, then,” Anne said briskly, rising from her seat, “everything worthwhile begins with a good book. Let me fetch yours.”

She disappeared into the back room before anyone could ask foolish questions.

Anne did have a proper beastmancy training book. That was not the problem. The problem was that using it first would be the same as forcing a delicate mechanism with brute strength. Beastmancy rested on foundation, perhaps even more than most crafts. Without the right cornerstone, the training itself would become clumsy and dangerous.

So her list for Asha began elsewhere. The first book would be about the soul, the second on binding techniques, and the third on beasts themselves. Only then would the training text truly belong in her hands.

Anne was not fully satisfied with the sequence, but it was the best she could currently offer. What she lacked was a strong beastmancy compendium for discussion and comparison, along with a proper biography or memoir from an experienced tamer. Those would have tied the foundation together far better. Still, Asha would need time to work through the first four books. Perhaps by then Ceres might have found something useful.

Anne allowed herself another moment of satisfaction as she selected the first volume.

She liked all her customers, but Asha was different from the others in one critical way. Rick had come to her already struggling along a path. Cyrus, Iris, and Hakon too. Asha, however, stood at the very beginning. Anne would be able to guide her from the start and build the foundation correctly.

It would earn Anne pages, certainly. But more importantly, it would prove something about her craft. A bookmancer should not merely help wandering paths recover. She should also be able to start one well. There was also the matter of future study.

Asha’s third book would be the same beastiarium Rick still used. Most study was solitary work, and rightly so. Yet some books benefited from shared effort—comparison, debate, and different angles of thought. Group study might become useful later.

When she returned, she carried a purple-covered book that made her customers straighten with varying degrees of expectation and confusion.

“This,” she said, placing it carefully before Asha, “is Magic of the Soul.”

The elf took it with both hands.

“You should begin here,” Anne continued. “If you are to learn beastmancy, then you must first understand the soul—what it is, how magic can reach it, and why contact between living wills is never as simple as people pretend.”

Asha’s fingers tightened slightly on the cover.

“The book must remain here,” Anne said. “Its reading fee is higher than my usual books—eight silver per day. That is not cheap, but both the knowledge in it and the magic on it justify the price. I can also have a copy made for one gold. The copy would not be nearly as useful as the original, but if you can spare the money, it would still serve well as reference.”

Then Anne added, almost as an afterthought, “It is written in Crohnish. I trust that will not be a problem.”

It clearly was not. Asha had barely looked away from the book since it touched her hands.

“That is perfect,” she said. “Please make me a copy as well, Miss Anne. And... if it’s allowed, I would like to begin studying right away.”

That answer delighted Anne.

“Good,” she said. “Then you may also help yourself to that soft blue volume on the side shelf.”

Asha looked up.

Anne was already retrieving it.

“It is a Minding Book,” she explained, handing it over. “Write your notes in it. It will help you keep your thoughts in order and connect them more clearly later.”

Asha accepted the second book more carefully than the first, as if the gesture itself had taken her slightly by surprise.

“Do not worry about the price,” Anne added. “Consider it a welcoming gift.”

That brought the right kind of brightness to the elf’s expression—gratitude, eagerness, and the first real trace of belonging.

Anne sat back and looked at the customers gathered in her bookstore. Rick and the trio had brought opportunity. Asha had brought direction. Spring was learning to speak. Her shelves were fuller than they had been. Her circle was stronger. And now another path had begun beneath her roof.

The quiet days, Anne thought, were almost certainly over.
