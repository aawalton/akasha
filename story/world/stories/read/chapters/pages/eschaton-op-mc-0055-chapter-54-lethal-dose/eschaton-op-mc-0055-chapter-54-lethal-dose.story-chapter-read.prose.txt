The upside to a radioactive Fracture in a nuclear power plant is all the equipment to deal with contamination is already there. Major Zhao walks me through the plant after quickly suiting up in yellow hazmat gear. The Fracture itself is located in Unit 1’s building, right next to the reactor itself.

Fully suited soldiers crowd the hallways, though they make way for the two of us. Not a single millimeter of skin is visible on any of them. Their yellow rubber hazmat suits crinkle and squeak under their military gear. Each soldier gives the Major a crisp salute.

The reactor building itself is a cavernous single room. Huge cranes and catwalks crisscross over a pair of deep pools in the center, each connected to each other via a small channel.

The Fracture is obvious, humming with beautiful menace next to the pool where the reactor lies. One pool contains the extra fuel rods, either awaiting their turn in the reactor or cooling down to be taken away for recycling. The second pool contains the reactor itself, sunken into the floor. I step up to the edge of the reactor pool, and look down. To my more mundane vision, it looks cold and dead. To my more esoteric capabilities, the reactor is still alive, though brought to heel.

As much as a reactor can be, at any rate.

“The reactor is currently idling. It’s still hot, though. Pulling all the fuel rods out to try to quench it entirely would be a last ditch effort,” the Major says. “With you here, hopefully we won’t be forced to such drastic measures.”

I turn away from the pool and back to the Fracture. A single thin wire goes into it, vanishing into the pocket dimension. The Major notices me looking at it.

“We tried an analog drone controlled from outside. It failed, and we aren’t exactly sure why. It simply doesn’t work.”

He then points out piles of equipment placed nearby. Tents, showers, hoses, and huge drums of chemicals are surrounded by hazmat suited soldiers quickly assembling them.

“We’re working on setting up a decontamination shower for you once you exit. Given the drones that we managed to recover were coated in radioactive dust, it’ll be necessary. Keep in mind we can only handle so much. If you’re too radioactive…” He trails off. “You’ll have to figure out a way to reduce it. If you can signal us should that happen, we’ll work out some way to help.”

Here goes nothing.

I step into the Fracture, and the world shifts. A sense of falling into something washes over me, and I find myself in a dusty, crumbling hell. The real Daya Nuclear Power Plant was noisy, busy, and most of all: clean.

This Fracture is the opposite. The concrete walls are crumbling, and in some places corroded and decaying rebar is exposed to the dusty air. The radiation is immense, beyond that which was estimated. It’s closer to 15 Sieverts than the estimated 10, though it doesn’t matter much to me, thankfully. The real issue is the dust clouding the air. All of it is radioactive, seeming to be a mix of powered concrete and radioactive isotopes commonly found in fuel rods.

While I didn’t know much about reactors upon arrival at the plant, the short walk from the command tent to the Fracture itself gave me plenty of time to do some cursory research. I’m not an expert by any measure, but I think it should be enough to give me an idea of the things I might find. Given by the dust, whatever happened here was not good. A core meltdown is the bare minimum, and maybe even a core explosion is possible.

The only sound in the hallway is that of dripping water. Everything is still and quiet, even the air is dead. I break the silence, marching forward into the hideously lethal pocket dimension. I pull the drone blocking the doorway out of the way and the double doors collapse with a sigh, clattering to the ground and shattering like glass. It had been so badly corroded that it was more rust than anything else. How it stood up to the drone without falling apart is a mystery that will never be solved.

The fuel storage pool is massive, at least twice the size of the one in the real plant, and that one can hold over two thousand fuel rod assemblies, and each one of those can hold as many as two hundred individual fuel rods. A nuclear fuel rod is actually much smaller than most people think. Rather than thick cylinders, a nuclear reactor uses tiny rods, each generally less than half an inch in diameter, packed into assemblies or bundles of up to two hundred of the tiny fuel rods. Daya’s reactors have a hundred and fifty such assemblies.

What I see after looking over the edge of the pool is another piece of evidence that the Fractures are less than accurate about the things they copy. Rather than orderly rows of square assemblies, the fuel storage pool is full of a jumbled mess of huge cylindrical rods, dumped haphazardly into the deep pool of water. A deep green glow emanates from all of them, and even bubbles from a few as they fission outside of the reactor. The bottom of the pool is cracked and shifted. The entire wall is shattered and off-set, like a great knife had cut the pool in half and moved one half to the side.

Water leaks out the cracks, small vortices pulling down bits of broken rod and green glowing swirls in the water. Two huge pipes on either end of the pool pour more water to replace what is lost. They’re managing to keep up, at least for now. The water level remains stable.

I sigh and step off the crumbling edge, and chunks of concrete and rusted metal follow me in. I sink to the bottom, nearly a hundred feet deep. The pile of nuclear fuel rods shift under my weight as I touch down, but only barely. Uranium is extremely dense, and the rods are as tall as I am and four inches in diameter. Each one must weigh almost half a ton. Given the size of the pool and the number of rods, there must be at least a hundred and fifty tons of magical uranium—Saturnium?—here.

Can I even store that much stuff?

I try to grab a rod and store it away, but my metaphysical grip is too weak, and whatever ability of mine that lets me store things away somewhere fails. It must be too large, in some way. Too heavy, too tall, it’s impossible to tell.

Yet another avenue of training and experimentation.

My blade forms in my hand, and I carefully carve apart the rods into smaller pieces. I need to be careful not to chip the rod. The water was cloudy with dust before I jumped into it, and if I become coated in too much radioactive material, it’ll make exiting the Fracture a difficult process. Assuming I could exit at all.

The work is long and tedious, not to mention dangerous. Large amounts of poorly stored fissile material is inherently dangerous. Combined with the heavily damaged and unstable pool, it leads to a tense yet tedious few hours. I carefully carve the huge rods into more manageable chunks, both for myself and whoever will be tasked with processing all this material later on. I leave the small chunks of the rod that are bubbling as they undergo fission in the storage pool, and the bottom of the pool is littered with scraps of saturnium, though I take as much as I can.

Climbing out of the pool requires more effort than I expected, but in the end doesn’t take long. I leave huge gashes and gouges in the pool’s wall, though. Even more work for the water pumps to contend with.

I probably spent too long in the pool, though. The alpha radiation from lethal levels of Uranium-235—Well, Saturnium-235—crackles along the back of my metaphorical teeth. It tastes sour and sweet, though the crackling is the primary sensation. It reminds me of pop rock candy, and sticky taffy candy that fills the whole mouth. A flavor that stays behind no matter how much water I drink.

I march deeper into the dead complex, my sword resting comfortably on my shoulder.

I wonder what horrors lie within?

I wander through the labyrinthian hallways, my footsteps echoing throughout the empty corridors. I keep having to backtrack, as many of the hallways have collapsed.

Water endlessly drips down from above, and all of it is slightly contaminated. Not enough to compare the ambient radiation all around. If this were the limit of the contamination, I imagine even a Typical would be fine, assuming they had the proper equipment. Unfortunately, that is not the case.

Finally, after hours of searching, I come across a pair of double metal doors, similar to the ones that lead to and from the fuel storage room. The doors collapse at my touch in a similar manner. There’s a sign next to the door, but it’s been scorched to the point of illegibility.

Pushing past the doorway, I find myself in a massive, cavernous room, easily on par with super stadiums. The entry door is in the middle of a wall, and on either side of me is a row of turbines chained end to end. Each row is made up of four mountainous turbines, with one high pressure steam turbine followed by three low pressure turbines. Both rows are absolutely destroyed.

The high pressure steam turbine on my right is gone. Only a massive, forty foot deep crater remains. The adjacent low pressure turbine was obliterated as well, and only a twisted ball of scrap remains. What’s left of it has been fused with the half-destroyed second low pressure turbine.

The second low pressure turbine is a mangled mess, like a skyscraper sized dog mauled it. The turbine itself is partially exposed, the outer casing having been blown apart. The blades inside were not just snapped, but pulverized.

The final right side low pressure turbine was cored out. The far end, where the generator coils were, is a mutilated scrapheap. All the shattered internals and the shockwave of the exploding high pressure turbine ripped out the guts of the final turbine and slammed them into the massive copper coils.

The left side turbines are only in a marginally better state. The high pressure turbine, while mostly intact, has a huge rent in the outer casing. Like a great god came down to see if it was made of cake and sliced it in half. Looking up, I find the enormous turbine blades, each one the size of an airliner wing, impaled in the concrete walls and ceiling. The blades seem to be made of a red-tinted metal, though it’s difficult to tell underneath the layer of dust and grime.

While the high pressure turbine’s blade failure didn’t directly affect the following turbines, what caused it did. The massive concrete foundation the turbines lie half buried in is buckled and cracked. Massive steel brackets, each one the size of a tank and meant to keep the turbine in place, are bent and broken. The shaft between the second and third low pressure turbines buckled upward, and the foundation is cracked and shattered. Even the turbines themselves buckled, and they jut upwards too. It reminds me of a broken spine.

I drop down into the left high pressure turbine through the gash, more than large enough for me to fit into. I land with a crunch and the scream of yielding metal. The only remains of the internals are piles of scrap metal. All of it is made of more red tinted metal. Even after examining it with a careful sensor suite, I’m still not sure what it is. Nothing mundane for sure, but that’s a given inside a Fracture. I don’t have the equipment for serious, in-depth studies. I store away the largest chunks, which were probably the main driveshaft at some point.

From where the super-heated high pressure steam would come from is instead filled up with a distant howling. The radiation levels spike, as the air is filled with heavy isotopes and toxic compounds. I recognize the mixture from my quick research before entering.

Corium. What remains after a reactor core completely melts down into slag.

This pipe must lead to the core or near it. I march down it.

Probably something more interesting than just crumbling hallways that way.
