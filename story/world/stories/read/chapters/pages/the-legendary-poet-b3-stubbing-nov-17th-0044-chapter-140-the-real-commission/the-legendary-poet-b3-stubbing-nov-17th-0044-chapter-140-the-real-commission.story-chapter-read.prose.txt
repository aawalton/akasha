What came out was the Operator.

"The commission is binary." His voice landed flat and even, and some back room of his brain filed a note of relief at the sound. Familiar ground. Old playbook. "Tomorrow at four, a panel of Guild masters hears my verse and votes. Accepted or rejected. Two outcomes, one coin. When you hired me, you said that if I performed outside your expectations, you would consider the Token. Consider what, exactly? I work badly against undefined variables, Lady Vane. Before I spend the rest of tonight writing, I'd like to know what I'm actually being asked to produce."

There it is, he thought, listening to himself. Twenty-two minutes of watching her bleed light onto a canvas, and the first thing out of my mouth is a contract clause. The question was real, though. It had been sitting in his planning folder since the study, unresolved, and he'd learned long ago that when he had no idea what to say, the truth made an acceptable stall.

Lady Vane studied him for a moment. Then she laughed.

Short, dry as a ledger page turning, and gone before it finished, but a laugh, and he logged it with the mild alarm of watching a statue blink.

"A Poet who requires defined variables." She moved past him, unhurried, along the ring of immaculate landscapes, and her reflection slid across the glass ribs overhead. "I had assumed the profession ran on the opposite. Very well. Exceeding my expectations means the outcome serves House Vane regardless of the panel's verdict. I'd assumed that much was self-evident."

Kage waited two seconds for the rest of it. The rest of it declined to arrive.

And there's the technique. She had answered him. Technically, formally, in a complete sentence with a subject and a predicate, she had answered him, and the answer contained precisely zero new information. Regardless of the panel's verdict. The same seam she'd shown him in the study. You assume I am measuring the outcome. A patron who commissions a debut and then, twice now, goes out of her way to file the debut's actual result under irrelevant.

The transactional approach had maybe one exchange of usefulness left in it, and he could feel his patience with the whole script dying quietly, and he knew exactly what had killed it. She had. Twenty-two minutes of her at that easel, honest down to the wrist, had made the polite fencing feel like a waste of both their time.

Fine. New approach. Make the math show itself.

"Let's make it simple, then." He turned so the easel sat in his peripheral vision along with her hands. "Tomorrow, Lyra is accepted. Best debut the Guild has seen in a decade. The doors open, the patronages line up, her price skyrockets by a week." He kept his tone at inventory level, her own register, borrowed without permission. "What does House Vane gain?"

She had stopped by a small canvas of a frozen harbor, and she answered without turning, fluent.

"Prestige of discovery. The Guild has spent thirty years insisting Greywater produces nothing worth framing; the House that proves them wrong in their own salon sets the market for everything that ward produces afterward. Every canvas she signs raises the value of the pigment trade, and I am the pigment trade." A gloved hand made a small, economical gesture at the ring of paintings, the dome, the estate beyond it. "Leverage, Poet. Compounding leverage. The Guild becomes a debtor the moment it applauds."

Clean. Immediate. Pure her, every noun a balance-sheet entry. Exactly the answer a genuine patron gives.

"And tomorrow she's rejected," Kage said. "Publicly. The panel laughs her out of the room, the verse fails, the debut becomes an anecdote rival houses tell at dinners for the next ten years. Her career in this city ends before lunch." He let that hang half a second longer than the first question had. "What does House Vane lose?"

The gloved thumb moved. Press. Drag. Once, against the seam of the opposite glove, on the word lose, and her answer came a beat slower than the first one.

"Very little. Houses of standing absorb failed investments hourly. It will be read as charity, or as a rare lapse of my eye, and either reading costs me less than this conversation is currently costing me." A pause, unlike her. "She will not go hungry. There are arrangements."

Kage held very still as alarms rang through his head.

Run it back. Question one, the gain: House, market, leverage, Guild, trade. All of them wearing the Vane crest. Question two, the loss: two sentences about the House, dismissive, almost bored, and then the final line arriving unbidden, and the final line had a completely different subject. She will not go hungry. Off-topic by every rule she claimed to keep. He had asked what the House would lose, and the House's answer had ended on the welfare of a slum painter.

There are arrangements. For an artist she had allegedly never met. Reviewed through intermediaries. Arrangements, standing ones, the word choice of infrastructure rather than impulse, and the thumb still moving against the glove seam, press and drag, scrubbing at paint that had been gone—from what the Echo had shown him—for dozens of years.

His mind started running faster.

Why would a patron price in failure? No, worse than price it in. Prefer it. Because that was the shape hiding under both answers: the gain scenario recited like a cover story, the loss scenario padded with a safety net built years in advance. You install a net under an outcome you expect. Maybe an outcome you want.

But then why commission the debut at all? Why hire a poet, why the deadline, why force them to open the gates, why any of the machinery of success if failure was the funded position? Unless… the debut was never the product. Unless acceptance and rejection were both packaging, and the actual commission was something that survived either verdict, something the panel's vote couldn't touch, and his thoughts were sprinting now through every inconsistency he'd held since the study.

The corridor of caged paintings, dozens of them, expensive enchantments locked in three-second loops, motion imprisoned in frames. The single worn drawer handle in a surgically perfect room, dark residue in the joint, charcoal-colored. Three unissued Patron's Tokens. It must be honest, said with the effort of a woman lifting something heavy. Do not make her sound like a beggar. The intermediaries claim, followed by a description of Lyra's work specific as a diagnosis. The eyes, frozen in this room and molten in a Greywater studio. The echo of a laughing girl with blue paint on her jaw, years cooled. Lyra's grey woman, faceless after forty sieges, white gloves rendered like evidence, and a thumb, two thumbs, one bare and paint-crusted, one white-gloved and spotless, making the same motion at the same rhythm in two rooms that were never supposed to know about each other.

The conclusion stood at the end of that corridor of facts and waited for him.

He looked away from it deliberately. Not yet. Pull gently. You yank this and it tears through.

His eyes, moving, landed on the easel.

The mountains sat in their column of light, half-finished and already breathing, morning haze built out of glazes thin as held breath, and the question rose in him with no strategy attached to it at all.

"You painted," he said, "and corrected nothing. I watched all the way through. Every stroke landed once and stayed. So explain something to me, as craft, because I'm apparently writing about a painter and I'd like to understand what I'm describing." He nodded at the canvas without taking his eyes off her. "When a painter reworks a single passage forty times. Scrapes it down to the weave, rebuilds it, scrapes it again, and it still refuses to resolve. What is that? Failure of skill? Something else?"

The dome went quiet in a new way.

Lady Vane stood beside the frozen harbor with her hands folded, and for a moment the stillness she wore stopped looking like control. When she answered, her voice had dropped its clerk's efficiency; the words came slower.

"Skill resolves anything, given enough cowardice." She was looking at the mountains, past him. "A painter of adequate skill can finish any passage in an afternoon. You invent what you cannot see. You borrow a nose from one memory and a jaw from another, you flatter the gaps, and the panel calls it complete because they cannot tell arrival from surrender. Most do it by their third attempt. It is—it is the sensible economy… if you want to be successful. Successful does not mean brilliant." The thumb pressed against the glove seam and dragged, and stayed there. "Forty attempts means the subject is still alive to her. It means she is refusing to let the image become anything less than what it demands, and she would rather hang her defeat in public than frame a lie. That is the only kind of failure worth..."

She stopped. One breath.

"It is rare," she finished. "Whatever you saw, it will read poorly to the Guild. They prefer their surrender neat."

She.

Kage kept his face still while the word rang. He had asked about a painter. An abstraction, carefully offered. She had reached for her. Twice. Alive to her. She is refusing.

Easy enough to explain. They both knew whose work had brought him here. Forty attempts was hardly camouflage. From anyone else, he would have let the pronoun pass.

But she hadn't stopped at explaining the difficulty. Somewhere in the answer, she had begun defending the painter.

She would rather hang her defeat in public than frame a lie.

He had asked whether forty attempts meant a failure of skill. Vane had answered as though someone had proposed taking the brush away. The words had come slower; one sentence had broken off before she could finish it. That is the only kind of failure worth—

Worth what?

She had recovered before he could hear the rest. It is rare. A tidy assessment. Something a patron could say about an investment.

The pronoun might have been nothing. The effort of returning to that voice was harder to dismiss.

Something moved in his chest. It was heavy and it ached slightly and it belonged to somebody else, the feeling of watching a person carry something enormous up a staircase alone, for years, and pretending you couldn't see the staircase. He gave up on filing it.

And in the quiet after her voice, the commission rewrote itself in his head. The real commission.

She wanted Lyra accepted. But she had asked for honesty regardless.

He turned the contract over and read it again with new eyes. Compose an introductory verse. Deadline, fee, venue. Then the actual instructions, the ones delivered in that study with a dropped frame between them: It must be honest. Every poet this woman could hire—and he was sure she could hire the best ones—would walk into that salon and flatter, sand the Greywater off the girl, present her neat, because neat was what the Guild bought and poets for hire sold what the buyer wanted, and that, precisely that, was the expectation he was being paid to perform outside of.

The panel's verdict was packaging. Accepted, rejected, the coin could land either side and the real commission would sit there untouched, because the real commission was this: stand in front of the people who own the word beautiful in this city, and tell the truth about Lyra out loud, once, in public, where it could never be scraped down and repainted. Honesty, regardless of outcome. That was the product. That had always been the product.

The system offered him nothing. No chime, no updated objective, no golden text. The quest log still read compose a formal introductory verse, and the quest log, he now understood, was the packaging too.

I know what to write.

He didn't know the words yet, but now, at last, he knew their box.

"I've taken enough of your day," he said.

"You have taken a quantity of it, certainly." Lady Vane had already completed her reassembly; the weight was gone from her stillness, the clerk restored, and she moved back toward the easel with her spine at its exact angle, as though the last ten minutes had been an accounting error the room agreed to forget. "You came for your undefined variable, Poet. Whether you found it is your own affair. The commission is due at four, and I do not extend deadlines for men who stand in doorways." She took up the palette; the brush rose; the thumb of her glove pressed once more against the seam, dragged, settled. "Aldric will show you out."

He inclined his head to her back and left her there, centered in her ring of flawless mountains, painting distances.

The corridor unwound the estate in reverse, brass giving way to silver, bare stone refilling with paintings, and by the time he passed the caged bird crossing its identical three seconds of sky, the noise in his head had gone strangely clean. Less than twenty hours. One poem to write for a Guild that wanted lies, a painter who wanted freedom, and a client who wanted one honest sentence said where she could never say it herself.

"Cheapest commission in the city," he told the corridor of cages, "and it costs the most."

Aldric was waiting at the entrance hall with the doors already open, the day laid out on the marble like a delivered parcel. The old steward gave him that manifest-checking look one last time, and Kage walked out through the raked gravel and the stamped hedges with the shape of the first line already turning somewhere below thought, dark and unwritten, the ink still waiting in the bottle.
