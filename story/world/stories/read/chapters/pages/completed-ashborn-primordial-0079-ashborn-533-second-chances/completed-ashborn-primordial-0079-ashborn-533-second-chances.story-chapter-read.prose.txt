The prana was all wrong.

That was Tia’s first coherent thought, standing in the middle of the shin-high grass. It was far too sparse, for one.

The ground was brown and the grass wasn’t tainted gray as it ought to have been.

The changes didn't end there. The sky was a rich blue, which should have been outright impossible.

She’d almost forgotten the feeling of the sun on her face and the gentle breeze through her hair. No sun in the Ash—not really. And the wind always brought with it flakes of soot, which was why they all wore masks.

Ten years, at the very least.

Over a decade of breathing that infernal air. Of fighting day after day after day, having long ago forgone any hope of ever returning.

Tia had made her peace with that. She considered it penance for her many sins. She just wished her retainers hadn’t been dragged into her stupid recklessness.

Haymi, for her part, had gone completely still and Vason being Vason, had his sword drawn, scanning the horizon for any sign of threat.

“It… can’t be,” he said upon realizing no Ash Beasts were about to devour them. “Can it?”

Neither of the two women answered.

Vason reached up and ripped off his helm. He stood there a moment, staring at the landscape ahead of them with a thousand-yard stare.

“It can’t be,” he repeated. “That’s… Raaka, right? But how?”

Tia stared at the distant city’s wall. Walls she’d never forget, not even after a decade. How could she? She’d grown up seeing them every day from the palace windows and the fields beyond.

“Did we fall through an Ash Tear somehow?” she asked, not really believing her own words.

And yet, she dared not believe. She dared not hope, only to have it stolen from her as it had been every time they thought they had stumbled upon a Gate, only for it to lead deeper into the Ash.

Haymi shook her head before stating the obvious. “We would not be standing here in one piece, had we done so.”

“Then, what?” Tia snapped, her golden hair flying as she turned to face the mejai. “What in the realms just happened? Because between the prana density and that sun, it’s as plain as day we are no longer in the Ash!”

Vason said nothing for a moment. “Er, you two?”

All three knew each other like the backs of their own hands, and so when Vason spoke, both turned, sensing something in his voice.

“Is it just me,” he said, “or are those skyships flying awfully close to Vimana Matali?”

Tia looked up.

Vimana Matali hung above the city, its vast shadow falling over Raaka’s palace as it had for centuries.

Except it wasn’t.

Tia blinked, thinking it must be her eyes playing tricks on her.

But no. The Vimana was lower. Unmistakably, catastrophically lower, its lowest spire tip a mere hundred paces above the palace’s roof.

“They’re not close,” she muttered, half horrified, half awed. “The Vimana is just low. Far too low.”

Haymi grasped her arm. “Those ships need to flee. They’re done for if they don’t!”

“I am sure their captains are well aware of the danger,” Tia said, though whether to her retainers or to herself, she wasn’t quite sure. “Let’s pray they get away in time.”

Two of the three fled, beelining to safety, but the last…

The three of them stood in the grass and watched on in horror as the largest skyship adjusted its heading, moving directly towards Vimana.

Tia gasped as she awaited the flash of energy that would end the vessel and everyone aboard it.

“What are they doing!?” Haymi cried.

Tia muttered a prayer to every god she knew.

The gods, it seemed, answered, for the devastating lance of pure prana never came.

Instead, the unthinkable happened.

The Vimana’s hull split slowly open, revealing an enormous bay door of what must have been incomprehensible size… and the skyship flew into the gap, disappearing inside, the doors closed behind it.

The three of them simply stood there in shocked silence, the strangeness of their new situation temporarily forgotten.

Vason was the first to bring them back to reality. “What will we do now?”

Tia broke her eyes away from the mysterious Vimana, pondering his question.

Twelve years she couldn’t take back, of fleeing from her duties and her people.

Her lifelong friends and retainers had followed her in knowing they wouldn’t come back, and yet, somehow they were standing in a field outside Raaka under a bright blue sky.

Tia—no, Princess Tiyana Matali—was many things, but stupid was not one of them. Not anymore.

This was, without a doubt, a second chance, and gods knew Tiyana was not the person she once was.

Twelve years of Ash would change anyone, and it certainly had her.

How many times had she regretted her folly? How many times had she wished for exactly this chance?

Tia drew in a long breath, closing her eyes to savor the fresh, clean air.

The world had changed while they were gone, surely. She didn’t know in what way or by how much, but she knew what she had to do first.

“I’m going to do what I should have done a decade and a half ago,” she said, opening her clear blue eyes. “I’m going to see my brother. And then I’m going to right the wrongs I have wrought upon this realm.”

General Devan Gurveer had been advised by the good captain to turn away. Twice, in fact, in increasingly worried and frantic tones.

When he hadn’t, the captain had ordered his helmsman to divert, but Devan had belayed it, and now the Vimana filled his vision. So close now that he could see the texture of the plates on its hull. Something, he was quite sure, no other humans had seen.

Well, seen and lived to tell the tale, anyway.

The skyship’s crew had gone so quiet he could practically hear the prayers they muttered in their heads.

Or the curses they fling my way… he thought wryly.

Even he was surprised by his command.

You know why, a voice said somewhere in the back of his mind.

Indeed he did. Sure, he might’ve passed it off as simply freezing, or perhaps that his instinct honed through long years of command had suddenly and inexplicably failed him in an unprecedented moment of panic.

But the truth was both simpler and far less flattering. As the reigning General presiding over Matali’s safety for the past decade, he’d had enough.

Had seen enough.

Of Ash Tears splitting open over Matali’s poverty-stricken cities. Of people dying by the masses to the random Tears, unable to lift a finger to help them. Of giving eulogies every single day.

It was more than any man should bear, and Devan had borne more than his fair share. Everyone he’d started his career alongside was now nothing more than ashes in an urn or spread across some shore somewhere.

And yet Devan was too much a coward to take his own life. With the Vimana descending, all he had to do was stand by and watch.

No action required. Simply stay the course and let the gods do the rest. Yes, he bore guilt over carrying the others to their deaths, but realistically, what difference did it make if they died here or to an Ash Tear tomorrow? They were all dead sooner or later anyway, and a quick death by the hand of God would be far swifter and more merciful than the fate his wife and son had endured at the hands of those vile demons.

Devan closed his eyes. He resolved himself to death.

And then the hull opened.

Enormous bay doors parted slowly ahead of them, blinding golden light spilling out from within, as though beckoning them to enter.

“How,” the Captain breathed. “How did you know, sir!?”

Devan hadn’t, of course. He hadn’t a clue. But he betrayed none of that to the captain, too transfixed on the spectacle before him.

“Take us in,” he muttered.

“Aye, sir!”

Devan saw the open doors. Saw the light inside.

The crew burst into action with renewed life and vigor, mistaking Devan’s silence for supreme confidence.

Wind mejai manipulated the orb arrays, and the ship surged forward, passing through the colossal doors into a hangar on a scale that made even someone as jaded as Devan’s grow excited.

The ceiling was easily high enough to stack three skyships of the line and still have room to spare, and the walls were lined with strange mechanisms and inscriptions he couldn’t begin to intuit.

They set down at the far end, and the crew thew down the ladder, securing the immediate area. It was empty, and so Devan debarked, leaving the captain and a few others onboard.

Through vast corridors they walked—halls that had seen no human enter in an age—in a daze that felt like a dream.

It had to be a dream, Devan felt, for such a miracle could not be possible. Not for a soul as wretched as his.

Could it?

They emerged into a grand central chamber, in which several lifts sat idle, just awaiting their use.

With so much to explore, Devan wondered whether they should embark them. Surely, the proper course of action would be to return to Raaka’s castle and inform the King of this wondrous discovery…

But Devan was done being a pawn, albeit a well-decorated and highly respected pawn.

No, this was his discovery to make, and so they rode the lift.

All the way up its gilded walls made of metals so durable he could only dream of them, to a room.

Not just a room, he realized A command center.

Sitting at its highest point was a circular space, with projections that displayed the outside. What should have been the Boundary of that infernal Ashen Realm instead showed nothing but gently rolling hills to the horizon.

Devan stared at the central display, upon which a great map showed a single continuous landmass.

No Ash boundary to be seen, no desolate land in which all humans perished.

And then, further to the east, was geography he recognized immediately.

Not five years ago, the demon realm was little more than myth. Seemingly overnight, their vilest leader tunneled his way to the human realm, polluting it forever.

And so, their evil had bled into the realm. Demons came by the thousands, and their culture and their geography became known to the world. He’d seen the maps. Everyone had, even those with no particular interest in the subject.

Devan had more than a passing interest.

And now, only land lay between the human territories and the demonic lands to the east. An enormous expanse of land, to be sure, but certainly this Vimana was capable of the journey?

He didn’t need the map to confirm it. He’d seen it with his own eyes as they flew in. The Ash was miraculously missing, the land beneath it ordinary and unremarkable in every direction.

“We believe we can control the Vimana from here, sir,” a soldier reported. “The controls are strange, but not entirely foreign. With time, we should be able to understand the other systems as well. This is a great day for Matali!”

He didn’t reply immediately, instead staring at the map, his eyes fixated on the east.

He thought about his ledger. He thought about the great weapons the Vimana boasted.

Weapons capable of reducing a skyship to vapor before its crew even knew they were under attack. And while Vimana were great machinations of the gods built for a higher purpose, he knew at least a part of what Vimana were.

The answer came without any hesitation.

He looked at the soldier and smiled a hollow smile.

“East,” he said.

General Devan Gurveer had been given a second chance, and he would not waste it.

He would right the wrongs the realm had wrought upon him.
