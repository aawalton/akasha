I stay silent for several minutes after the revelation that this Fracture has existed longer than it has in our world. According to Thutmose, a band of Visitors—his name for people who come through Fractures—came through and slayed a great monster that had plagued the land for centuries.

There are other worlds like ours.

That’s what I take away. There are other worlds with Fractures—Thresholds, Gateways, who knows what they call them. I feel bad for Dr. Patricks. Every time he thinks he has a handle on these things, they throw us a four dimensional curve ball. This all assumes that Thutmose is telling the objective truth. I doubt the man himself is lying, but who’s to say that his memories haven’t been fabricated? Most dreams seem real at the time.

I listen to Thutmose continuing to chatter about his home, a port city by the name of Thonis, the weather, the prices, the history of Karnak and his world, which they call Khetmenet. The nation is simply called The Kingdom of Amun-Ra. It’s probably not a coincidence that’s the same name of the Great King.

We pass by streets only slightly smaller than the massive main road we’re on. It’s as wide as a six-lane highway, and tall limestone buildings line both sides. There are tens of thousands of people moving in both directions. Some are individuals with handcarts, others are convoys with train-sized carts like Thutmose.

Karnak City is a metropolis larger than perhaps any city on Earth. At first glance it looks like an ancient city, but it quickly becomes apparent that it’s far more advanced than it appears. Using seismic mapping from the thousands of footsteps, I can tell there’s a complex and extremely advanced sewer system. Every so often I notice a street sweeper sweeping animal waste, sand, and trash into storm-drain like gaps in the gutters.

Aqueducts high above the fifteen story tall buildings weave throughout the city in a regular-grid like pattern. Whoever designed it knew what they were doing. Every building has a regular water supply. Wherever it comes from, I don’t know. The closest mountain is across the Nile from Luxor—though if that’s true here, I don’t know. Either way, that mountain would not be a source of water.

Thutmose notices my gaze upon the aqueducts, and squawks appreciatively.

“Ah yes, the aqueduct system! You noticed, yes. It was a project, built by the Great King many centuries ago! There is a reason why he is the Great King, you see. It brings enough water for everyone, as decreed by him! No man, woman, or child under his rule goes without water!”

“This place is truly amazing.”

Thutmose beams. “Just wait until we become a bridge! Then all your people will be able to visit here, yes! Ah, a new golden age is about to dawn, for both our peoples!”

“How much farther to the market?” I ask, changing the subject.

Thutmose points out a pair of massive obelisks just ahead. Each one is at least two hundred feet tall, and plated in gold. I can see cartouches made of turquoise, lapis lazuli, and probably powdered ruby inlaid on all sides of the massive structures.

“There,” he says, pointing with a tanned hand. “That is the Market of Dedun! It is not the largest market in all of Karnak,” he says. “But it is one of the best. The Royal Market is reserved for only the wealthiest of merchant caravans. Other Kingdoms send their envoys there. It is not for the likes of me, the humble merchant I am.”

We pass through the two massive obelisks, and the streets widens out into a wide square more than two miles across. Thousands of merchants are in the process of setting up or are already hocking their wares to people passing by.

Even only half filled at best, the Market of Dedun is still a crowded and bustling bazaar. The rich scents of spices and oils and wines fill the area. The tinkling of coins put into pouches and the faint rustling of fabric. Both from bolts and the packed marketplace with thousands of people pushing past each other.

The market is covered by thousands of sheets of canvas or cloth. All of it is brightly colored, woven with different colored thread and dusted with something that even glimmers in the sun's brutal glare. A central tower rises high above even the buildings that surround the market. It looks like a bell tower without the bell. Holes are drilled into the tower of limestone all along the face, and I can see flags and cloth covers snap and dance in the breeze. It’s clearly been built to pull in the breeze over the city and redirect it to surface level.

Music can barely be heard over the thousands of bustling people as more than just traders hock their wares or their skills. Passersby sometimes toss coins at them. Some merchants are even negotiating with them, and once some deal is made, the musicians set up near their now employer's stall. It's a cacophony of life and trade and art as well. A city is alive, and everyone within five miles of here knows it well.

I pull Thutmose’s cart into an empty spot evidently reserved for him. He signs a few papers an ibis-headed woman shows him, and he hands them back. I catch a few glimpses of it. All of it is in Ancient Egyptian. I have a basic understanding of it, mostly done via cursory research during my binge of learning every language. Most of the context is lost on me, and whatever is written on the paper is almost entirely illegible to me. It must be some legal document I have little basis to start with.

I start up a translation thread in the background. Maybe eventually I’ll be able to come up with something.

A young boy comes up to Thutmose, and they have a conversation about stabling his bull. Thutmose hands over a few gold coins, and the young boy leads the animal off.

“Now,” Thutmose says, patting at his vest. “Thank you for assisting me, and let it never be said that Thutmose turns away from his debts!”

He hands me a few gold coins, more than he gave the boy who walked off. He gives me a wink.

“For later! After the Unveiling. We will be great friends?”

I nod. “Yes, great friends.” I close my fist around the coins and they vanish away.

“Good, good. Now, what is it you seek?”

“Before I tell you, I have to tell you something else first.”

He nods, and I continue.

“My world is dying. It is on the brink of falling apart. These Fractures—Thresholds, whatever you want to call them, are tearing it apart. Terrible monsters pour from them if we don’t go in and destroy them first. There are more than we can stop, and they are overrunning everything except our largest cities.”

Thutmose grows visibly distraught.

“Surely it is not so bad. Do you not receive Visitors to help you?”

“No. It is just us. Only some of us have gained the magical power and strength to fight back. Only a fraction of our population is capable of going into the Fractures to stop the monsters from coming out.”

It takes me nearly half an hour, but I explain the state of the world to him. I tell him of a world on fire, of fighting things we thought were relegated to fiction. Of a world on the brink of starvation, of a last ditch effort to stave off a second apocalypse and praying for just one last piece to hopefully save us all.

“And that is why I need something like a sun crystal. It doesn’t need to be exactly that, but something we can build more of that can act like it.”

Thutmose strokes his break thoughtfully.

“Hm, yes. Arcane crystals such as that will not be in this market or any of the other Outer Markets. Only the Royal Market would have anything like that, yes.”

“Good, I’ll go there,” I say, turning away. He grabs at my arm with a squawk.

“Ah, my friend, it is not so easy. The Royal Market is for royalty, yes. The Normarchs of the provinces, noble families, you understand, yes?”

“Even as a Visitor I wouldn’t be allowed entry?”

He shakes his head. “No, access to the Royal Market must earned, yes? Simply being a Visitor, as prestigious as that may be, is not enough.”

I draw myself up.

“I could get in,” I say darkly, and Thutmose jerks back.

“We are desperate, Thutmose. Billions of lives are depending on me. When faced with such carnage, what else am I supposed to do?”

“Now, slow, friend, slow. I understand you are strong, I can feel it in your soul, yes. Stronger than any I have met, which is many. But you should know that while many laws of this land are protected by the guard, the Royal Market is one of the few exceptions: The Great King himself protects it. Great magicks worked over decades by the grandest of his magi encircle it, and he himself will step down from his throne to challenge those who attempt to shatter such a peace.”

He studies me with a merchant’s appraising eye.

“For one of your strength, he may choose to step off his throne no matter what you challenge, Royal Market or not. But all is not lost. The Royal Market is for important people yes, but that right can be earned. You see, for such a great event as the Unveiling, the Great King has commanded great games to commence, for the enjoyment of all. Peoples from across the sea, from beyond the River’s Heart, and even across the Burning Sands have come for it.

“Anyone may join these games; there are many. Some of strength, some of wit. Others of charisma and humor. You are lucky, in two days time a tournament of strength of arms is to take place.”

I sigh, dragging a gauntlet over my faceplate.

“There’s more, too. The earlier Visitor you mentioned? I need to find him.”

“Ah, a friend of yours?” Thutmose asks eagerly.

“The opposite,” I mutter. “I’m probably going to have to kill him.”

“My friend, please keep your voice down,” Thutmose says, waving me down and looking around nervously. “Violence of any kind is not allowed in Karnak. The games precluded, of course, but they are not lethal, yes. You seem like such a genial fellow, why such rage? I can feel it yes, like blood and sun and sand.”

“He’s responsible for the deaths of millions.” I growl. “I don’t know which one of the three it is, but it doesn’t matter. All of them are some of the worst monsters my world has ever seen.”

Thutmose clacks his beak.

“Your world is a terrible place,” he says.

I shrug. “I do my best to make it a better one. People like him—whoever he is—do their best to make it worse.”

“If your enemy is here, perhaps he too is looking for the crystal? He may enter to earn the right for it as well. There you could teach him a lesson, perhaps?”

On second thought, I may actually know who has entered. Berhane would never allow anyone but his Vassals to enter a Fracture, not with the kind of wealth that’s in them. Out of the three Ascended running the Empire, both Berhane and his General would never follow the rules of this city. They’d take what they want or die trying. The fact this city isn’t on fire or in the midst of a bloody struggle is pretty telling who it is.

Berhane’s other Vassal besides his general is only known by the name of Vizier. He’s Berhane’s governor and right-hand man. Supposedly he’s the reason that the Empire is as functional as it is. Whatever his powerset is, it clearly has something to do with governance. That, or he’s simply an exceptionally skilled bureaucrat.

I explain that to Thutmose and he clacks his beak again, clearly a nervous tick of some kind.

“Ah, friend, this ‘Empire’ truly is a terrible thing. You say that Karnak shall be in the middle of their territory? Perhaps peaceful trade is not in our future.” He sounds sad. I can’t blame him.

“I’ll do my best to make sure they fall,” I say, fist clenching. “No matter what it takes.”

Thutmose decides to guide me to where I can actually sign up for the tournament. He mentioned to a wandering pair of jackal guards that his wagon would be unattended for a while, and apparently that was enough. When I asked if he was worried about thieves, he brushed it off, saying the guards and the magicks around the Market would protect it.

I follow him as he shows me the way to the Colosseum of Horus, the largest one in the entire city. Once we approach it, I’m once again left in awe of the scale of Karnak. Its size dwarfs everything on Earth, and is built almost entirely out of stone and granite. There’s virtually no wood used. The outdoor furniture, doors, and window shutters I’ve seen use weaved papyrus. I have yet to see wood used for anything other than carts, and lots of the smaller ones use bundles of papyrus as well.

I can’t see the interior from here, but the exterior is absolutely gigantic. It’s at least four hundred feet tall, and probably a mile in diameter given from the curvature. Assuming it’s circular, of course.

“Impressive, is it not?” Thutmose beams. “The Great King had it built many years ago. He likes to build things, which we all very much enjoy. Who doesn’t prefer creation to destruction?”

“You’d be surprised.”

Thutmose gives me a sad look.

“Ah, yes. I am sorry, my friend. I did not mean to insult your home.”

“It’s not all bad, but there are terrible people who see the world as theirs. If they can’t have it, then no one can.”

With that said, I follow Thutmose inside the Royal Colosseum itself. We have to push our way through the continually crowded city. Most people get out of my way. I clearly don’t fit in, and I hear whispers about another Visitor.

Eventually we reach an office with almost a dozen lines of various animal-headed people. Some of them are clearly not of Thutmose’s people. One is a minotaur even larger than I am wearing a laurel crown and a Greek toga. There’s a snake person of indeterminate gender with a vest full of pockets, much like the one Thutmose wears over his own tunic.

Here I receive fewer glances, as I fit in in how I don’t. This is where people are signing up for the royal games to get the chance to earn money, glory, and maybe for fun. One of the entrants is a lion-headed father surrounded by his children. Him and his four children are laughing as he tells jokes. His wife giggles as well.

“There are all kinds of games and competitions,” Thutmose says. “Everything and anything, from art to strength. Even games for merchants like me! Number challenges, yes. I will not compete. I have my wagon to watch over, yes. There is much coin to be made!”

Soon I’m up next, the bureaucrats and scribes who run this office are clearly efficient. Thutmose steps up for me, as he’s both far more familiar with the legal system of his own world, as well as capable of reading the dense legal documents that come with signing up for a tournament like this.

I tap him on the shoulder as he discusses which games I’ll be joining in on.

“Thutmose, sign me up for those number games, if they’ll help earn a ticket to the Royal Market.”

Thutmose nods and turns back to the scribe. They pass documents for a few minutes before I’m handed something to sign. I take the quill and stop right before it touches the papyrus.

“Thutmose, should I try to translate my name to your language, or sign it how I would in my own?”

“In your own. If you translate it, it’s not your name, yes?”

I sign it and hand it back. Thutmose looks at my printer-perfect handwriting with a raised eyebrow, and the scribe does as well.

“You could earn a living as a scribe here, yes,” Thutmose says to me. “The lines are perfect. Few scribes could do better, yes.”

“Well, what’s next?” I ask.

Thutmose points to a line of brightly painted sandstone on the floor.

“Follow that line. The big matches are in two days, yes, but they run competitions at all times! There are too many applicants for one big bracket for them all. So they run games constantly. Small ones. Win enough times, do well enough, and you shall join the larger brackets! Only a handful of people will be allowed to compete, yes.”

As I follow the blue line, stylized as a river, he calls out to me one last time.

“Do well! I will place money on you. Win!”
