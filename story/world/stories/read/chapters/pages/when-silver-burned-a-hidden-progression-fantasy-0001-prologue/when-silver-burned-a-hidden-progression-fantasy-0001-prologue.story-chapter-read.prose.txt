Kalsin leaned on the crooked railing of the fighting pit, one boot balanced on the splintering edge. He watched the chaos with feigned disinterest, chewing on a stalk of dryroot and counting the idiots still standing.

Seven left.

The grown-ups muttered as one particularly large brute caught a tall girl across the jaw with a heavy punch that could’ve knocked the teeth from a granite statue. She spun, went down hard, dust coughing up from the arena floor.

“Gone,” someone muttered beside Kalsin.

But the girl wasn’t.

She rolled once, blood spraying from her nose, then surged—not rose, not climbed, but surged—from the ground like a thrown spear. Her arm blurred as she tackled the brute who’d dropped her, knocking him flat with a thunderous crash. The few other ongoing bouts in the fighting pit paused. But she didn’t, not for a second.

Kalsin's foot slipped from the railing for a moment, forgetting that he was supposed to be confident. But it was hard to look confident when, just a couple of feet away, a girl not much older than him was beating the shit out of a man twice her age.

The girl then stood—tall, broad-shouldered, and clearly someone who’d still have to change the size of her shoes—and swayed a little. Her face was soaked in blood that wasn’t all hers. Her eyes scanned the yard like a wolf who hadn’t eaten in weeks.

“The daughter, mh?” Kalsin asked the man beside him.

“The daughter,” he replied.

Kalsin made a show of humming. A nice, thoughtful hum, one that was supposed to be full of wisdom rather than the insecure attempts of a stray kid barely holding on to sanity.

The girl turned, sweeping the edge of the pit with her eyes. She was about to pass him over, but then sharply looked back, right at Kalsin. She tilted her head.

She didn’t look away.

Shit. Kalsin bounced his leg and tried a disinterested look to the side. Those damned silver eyes of his. And his scar wasn’t exactly a pleasant sight, either.

A new fighter charged the girl, hollering. She didn’t dodge. She slapped the training sword from his hand, ducked her shoulder into his gut, twisted him midair, and used his own momentum to slam him down spine-first onto the edge of the pit’s inner fence. The wood cracked, and Kalsin hoped the same wasn’t true for the man’s back.

A few of the grown-ups pushed away from the guardhouse to make their way toward the fighting pit. They didn’t look happy—but then again, they probably shouldn’t. Kalsin would have wept with shame if a lone teenage girl was trashing his company of acclaimed bodyguards-for-hire.

“Now, I know Samuel did say you could have your pick,” the man told Kalsin. “But that one is—”

Kalsin straightened, spat the root to the ground, and vaulted over the railing.

Naturally, he was cursing up a storm inside. His gut stirred with nerves, and he once again questioned if he should be here at all. The last words of his father rang in his ears, the coins falling on the planks as he turned away from his son.

He’d been a failure his entire life. He didn’t want to be a failure any longer.

The distant grown-ups noticed. One of them started to jog toward the fighting pit, his armor rattling and halberd awkwardly wobbling on his shoulder.

“‘Ey, kid! That’s company property!” the man called to him over the railing.

Kalsin ignored the grown-ups and gave a casual wave toward the girl. His boots snagged once on the bloodied man groaning on the ground as he approached her. “You broke his jaw.” Kalsin pointed at the fella. “That’s not a nice thing to do.”

Damn it. This charisma thing was still a new concept for him. Though being homeless was a good motivator to learn.

The girl didn’t answer. She squinted her eyes at him, looked him over once, then did a little hand gesture, palm open and moving to the side.

Kalsin looked down at the incapacitated man, then back up at the girl. “Um… sure,” he said, stepping away toward the left. When the girl did the motion again—now wearing an almost pleasant smile—he stepped one more time, clearing enough ground to stand in the middle of the fighting pit.

Clearing enough ground for the girl to sprint at him.

She moved fast, even without her surge. He slipped under her fist flying at his chest, caught her wrist, twisted—and caught a slippery elbow to the ribs that rattled his teeth. He spun away, wheezing, and laughing despite the pain.

Would ya look at that. She didn’t go straight for his face. This gal was already the exception.

Kalsin circled wide, ignoring the fire creeping up his ribs. He raised his fists—not like a fighter, not properly. He didn’t have the training. Cinder had only focused on teaching him swordsmanship. But he’d seen enough beatings to fake it. Taken some during the last few months, too.

The girl’s feet padded a half-step in time with his. Calm. Patient. Her nose still bled down her lip, but it didn’t seem to bother her. If anything, it looked like she’d forgotten about it completely. From time to time, her eyes still snagged on his silver pair. He was trying to do the opposite, to avoid looking at the black scar sitting on her jawline.

She feinted with her right shoulder, and Kalsin went to block. At least that was the plan until her boot swept his leg out from under him.

He hit the ground hard enough to knock the breath from his lungs, rolled quick to avoid a boot to the side, then came up with dirt in his mouth and maybe a little less pride intact than before. He coughed, spit, and gave her a mock-scandalized look.

“Bit much, don’t you think?” he wheezed. “I just wanted to have a chat.”

She just tilted her head, wiped her bloody lip with the back of her hand, and gestured again—easy enough to understand, this time. Beckoning. Come on.

Kalsin muttered, “If you say so.” Then he charged.

It wasn’t clean. It wasn’t elegant. But he managed to catch her arm mid-swing, hook her ankle with his boot, and clean sweep her off her feet. She went down hard.

“Heh,” Kalsin grinned. “You’re not a mountain after a— Aaah!”

Her hand wrapped around his shin and with a single surge-powered yank, she crushed him level with the ground. Another dosage of dirt in his mouth. And he didn’t even have time to complain before he felt a light jab into his side.

“So what’s your story?” he asked, shifting sideways to avoid a grapple that stirred up dust next to his face.

She deflected the punch that came with his question with her forearm, did a little hand gesture that Kalsin had no cocking idea what it could’ve meant, then elbowed him in the ear.

“For me, well, I was a cobble-shagging gutterling for the past while. And— Ow.” He drew up a knee to avoid some dull fingernails. “I hope my parents die in a fucking river, my lovely sister won’t leave me the hell alone, and my last few coins are bogged down on trying to recruit a bodyguard. The name’s Kalsin.”

She gave a low snort, then immediately regretted it as dirt similarly found its way to her airways, too.

She rolled over and was about to deliver a particularly painful-looking smack to the stomach when armored hands grabbed them both from above. The adults, doing a piss-poor job of the profession they were supposed to employ, dragged the two kids to their feet.

Kalsin kicked a few times as a guard hoisted him up, trying to take a peek between the armored bodies at the girl who had been the only fighter in this circus worth a damn.

Between scolding men and rattling plates, he could see the girl doing the same, her previously vacant look almost physically reaching toward him with interest.

One of the soldiers was louder than the rest, berating her. “What the hell were you thinking, beating the piss out of potential clients. Eh? Eh? If your father saw just how—”

Kalsin let the men drag him away, retreating into himself after the performance. The quiet, lonely boy missed the times when he could afford to be alone. But life was never that simple. You either learned to adapt, or you were crushed under the wheel rolled by those who did.

Thus, the first step. Getting a bodyguard for the next few days, eh? Just as an experiment. Just to see where the dice fall.

This gal seemed like a decent sort. Maybe they could be friends, even.

He wondered how it would all work out, in the end.
