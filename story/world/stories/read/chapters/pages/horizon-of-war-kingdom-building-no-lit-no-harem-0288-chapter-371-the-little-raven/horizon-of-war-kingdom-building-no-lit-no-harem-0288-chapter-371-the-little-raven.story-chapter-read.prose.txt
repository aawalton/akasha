Chapter 371

The Little Raven

Skodra

Renting a private room at an established inn came with luxuries of its own. Sagarius leaned back against the wall of the wooden tub, cushioned by several folded layers of thick linen. Warm water lapped against her shoulders, carrying away the dust and weariness of the road. Steam hung lightly in the room, touched by the faint floral fragrance of the soap.

It had been weeks since she had enjoyed a proper bath. She had washed when convenient along the journey, but there was only so much that could be done with a basin, a damp cloth, and cold water.

Her kind did not sweat as heavily as humans, nor did their skin produce as much oil, but weeks of travel had still left her feeling grimy and unkempt.

She worked a coarse linen cloth across her skin with scented olive-oil soap purchased earlier in the city. Slowly, the road dust came away. Sagarius closed her eyes and allowed herself to enjoy the warmth and the fragrance in the air.

The loss of her left hand still troubled her, though she had gradually learned to make use of the healing stump. Washing her hair, however, remained another matter. For that, she had called for one of the inn’s maids, who now arrived carrying a basin, a pitcher of warm water, several linen towels, a wide-toothed wooden comb, and a mild herbal wash scented with rosemary.

It was hardly an extravagance. Travelers commonly called upon the inn’s maids for such assistance.

The bath and the maid’s assistance were modest expenses compared with the private room itself.

Sagarius had always kept several gold coins in her purse and spent them carefully. More remained in the newly discovered vault at Vindebona. Bald Eagle had also provided her with an allowance for the journey.

Yesterday, after learning about the Lord, the lich, and Lans’ likely identity, Sagarius and Marc had returned to the clerk. They had asked him to arrange a meeting with the Bank so they could discuss travelling by airship.

While air travel existed, it was still in its infancy. There were few airships, little room aboard them, no dependable schedule, and frequent delays caused by unforeseen repairs. Travelers sometimes waited for days before a place became available.

Yet few complained.

An airship was more than a means of travel. It was a mark of prestige, reserved for important persons summoned by the Shogunate or wealthy clients of the Bank.

Still, Sagarius was no novice, and she would not deal with an unfamiliar organization before learning enough about it. In the past, she had spent months studying a guild before she understood its inner workings and uncovered its secrets.

Such patience was impossible now. Lans’ body might wither if she delayed too long. Even so, she needed at least a day to learn what sort of people she would be dealing with.

For that reason, she had sent Marc and Marik out to make inquiries. With luck, they would return with something useful about the Bank.

Marc

Despite Sagarius’ orders, Marik remained at the inn as arranged, feigning a stomachache so he could stay behind and guard Lady Sagaria without drawing suspicion. That left Marc to visit the market with the coachman.

They found the place crowded with peddlers, merchants, and townsfolk bargaining over the morning’s goods. That the market remained so busy well past dawn spoke of a city rich in commerce.

Carts came and went through the gates, while wagons laden with sacks, timber, pottery, and casks crowded the main road leading to the market square. Drivers shouted at one another whenever the traffic slowed, and porters squeezed between the wheels with bundles balanced across their shoulders.

The smell of mules and manure hung heavily in the air, though stone-lined gutters carried much of the filth away. Compared with other cities Marc had visited, the streets were remarkably clean, and the stench never became unbearable.

The market alone was enough to make him marvel at Skodra. From the stories he had heard, much of the city had nearly burned to the ground during the upheaval, only to suffer repeated raids in the years that followed. Yet there was little sign of that ruin now. Goods filled the stalls, merchants called out from beneath striped awnings, and porters hurried through the crowd with crates, baskets, and wrapped parcels brought in from beyond the city.

Laughter rose above the din. Buyers haggled loudly over prices, sheep bleated from their pens, and peddlers shouted out their wares. Even a pair of minstrels had claimed a place near the square, playing for whatever small coins the passing crowd might spare.

Marc’s purpose there was not merely to gather information. He had saved some coin from his service in Vindebona and had wondered whether he might try his hand at trade. They were already travelling east with carts, and it seemed wasteful to make such a long journey without carrying goods that might earn them a profit along the way.

The idea had originally come from the coachman. If several of them pooled their money, he argued, they could purchase in greater quantity, bargain for a better price, and divide the profit afterward. Sagarius had entrusted Marc with a sum early in their journey. Knowing her, she had probably forgotten about it by now. Marik had also contributed some of his own savings.

Now, Marc and the coachman moved from shop to shop in search of goods worth buying.

Yet as they wandered through the busy streets overflowing with merchandise, surrounded by shouting sellers and buyers who seemed to know the value of everything at a glance, the reality slowly dawned on him.

It was far easier to speak of becoming a trader than to become one.

He had no experience.

Most merchants learned beside their parents from an early age. They learned what to buy, where to find it cheaply, and how to judge whether the goods were of sound quality. Meanwhile, Marc knew none of it.

He felt less like a merchant than a sheep being led through the market to be sold.

The last few years among soldiers had given him confidence. He had survived hardship and learned that he was capable of more than being a farmer or an ordinary footman. But here, among merchants, that confidence began to crumble.

His stomach tightened as he watched the coachman awkwardly haggle over a tray of sealed jars. Suddenly, the whole venture no longer seemed clever. It felt like a wager made by men who did not understand the game.

His instincts warned him that someone would cheat them.

A nagging thought settled in his mind. They were going to lose the money.

In Bellandia, people often said that merchants grew rich by taking other people’s coin. Standing there now, Marc wondered whether they had been right.

Only then did the whole idea begin to feel reckless, irresponsible, and poorly considered.

Korelia, Great Keep

Since the previous night, Lansius had allowed the physicians to treat him while he pored over scrolls, trying to catch up on everything he had missed. His memory remained a mess. It felt as though years separated him from the life he remembered. He could recall Bellandia, his family, the long journey from Toruna to Korelia, and all the wars that followed, yet those memories seemed to belong to another life.

He had been trapped in the Corridor of Mind for so long that this world, the real one, no longer felt real.

So unreal that he had asked everyone to keep his awakening secret, lest he become mired in social obligations when he could barely remember who everyone was.

Even now, his memories of the beach beside the red ocean remained painfully vivid. He had spent what felt like an eternity there, spearfishing, watching the sun sink, and getting drunk on palm wine he made from fermented coconut sap. Most batches had failed, and many had nearly killed him.

Those endless days still felt more real than the study around him.

Seated in the unfamiliar chamber, Lansius carelessly tossed another scroll onto the stacks of letters and let out a long sigh. He had been reading since the previous evening without a moment’s sleep, and strangely, he didn’t feel that he needed any.

More strangely still, he had stopped coughing blood. He had even eaten a full meal at midnight, heartily enough to delight the cooks and servants, who gathered at the hall's doorway and watched him as though he were some exotic animal.

His personal squire had kept pace with him, remaining on his feet for nearly the entire night without rest. Granted, others had come and gone from the chamber, including signalmen, scribes, the hunter attaché, guards, nomads, and everyone else Lansius had summoned, so he hadn’t paid the squire much attention.

Only now, with the chamber quiet and the two of them alone, did Lansius realize that the squire hadn’t rested at all.

“Your name is Margo, correct?” he asked.

“My Lord.” Margo immediately straightened.

“I seem to recall that we had an arrangement. You were permitted to sleep whenever I worked late. Have you forgotten?”

“That is correct,” Margo replied with a relieved smile. “Pardon my selfishness, but I feared that if I closed my eyes, I might wake to find you back in the White Chamber.”

Lansius laughed aloud. He no longer cared much about formal manners, and if anyone complained, he could always blame it on his illness. “No, I’m definitely back. And just in time to find that everything has gone utterly mad.” He exhaled sharply. “What is wrong with this Margrave? I’m going to chop this bastard's legs into pieces and feed them to the ducks.”

He turned back to Margo. “We still have those enormous, menacing great ducks, correct? Or am I misremembering things?”

Margo couldn’t suppress a smile. “We still have the war ducks, My Lord. They’ve been breeding successfully here as well.”

“That is wonderful news.” Lansius glanced toward the clock he remembered being brought from Canardia, one of the valuables confiscated from the Houses that had rebelled against him. Its hands showed ten o’clock.

He sat relaxed in his padded seat, took a drink from his goblet, and asked, “Still no word from Korimor?”

Margo hurried to the door, opened it a crack, and spoke quietly to the guard outside. There was no real need to ask. Any message from Korimor would be treated as a priority, and the guard would knock the moment one arrived. Still, with the crisis in Edessa, Margo checked anyway.

Soon, the squire returned to Lansius and reported, “We’ve yet to receive an answer from the Lady.”

Lansius nodded.

The squire then resumed his place beside the guest chair, standing at attention as before.

“Also,” Lansius added, “gratitude for everything you’ve done while I was asleep.”

“My Lord.” Margo inclined his head, accepting the praise with quiet respect.

As Lansius’ personal squire, Margo had remained responsible for him even while he slept. He arranged the infirmarians’ visits and daily care, made certain the House instructions were followed, and also oversaw the security. Whenever necessary, he also remained with the guards assigned to the White Chamber.

Fortunately, or perhaps unfortunately, he hadn’t been there when Lansius awakened.

“Using a straw mattress was a good decision,” Lansius remarked. It was dry, airy, cheap, and easily replaced, making it far more practical for someone bedridden for months. “Who suggested it, the physician?”

“Yes, My Lord,” he confirmed, then went into detail. “We used the spring mattress at first, but it became damp and difficult to clean. Medicinal oil, crushed herbs, and other residue had soaked into it, and the middle had begun to sag.”

“Obviously. I lay on it for months,” Lansius replied. Copper-alloy springs were never going to hold their shape as well as modern steel ones.

Margo added, “Lady Valerie also agreed with the straw mattress as well.”

“Valerie?” Lansius hadn’t heard that name in a long time.

“Lady Valerie visited in the spring of last year. Together with Lord Avery and the Umberlander shaman, she tried to awaken you, though without success.”

Lansius stroked his beard that actually felt thinner than he remembered.

“I do recall hearing voices,” Lansius said. “I think Avery was among them, along with several others. But I don’t remember Valerie.”

“She didn’t stay for long, only half a season before returning to Toruna. I believe that without you here, she never felt at home.” A trace of pain entered Margo’s voice.

Lansius remembered the young squire’s long-standing infatuation with Valerie, which had begun when they first met in Korelia years ago. It was a hopeless affection. Valerie was considerably older and fiercely protective of her freedom. She was also wary of relationships, especially when pregnancy and childbirth could prove fatal to someone whose body differed so greatly from the women of this world.

Unlike Lansius, she had arrived in this world as if stepping through a doorway. In her case, a mysterious portal.

The thought drew Lansius’ gaze toward the unfamiliar ceiling.

Morning sunlight streamed through the narrow windows and ventilation holes.

Why had he suddenly returned to this world?

He remembered nothing unusual. He had simply awakened in the White Chamber without warning.

Dazed, groggy, and unfamiliar with his surroundings, he had mistaken the guards for liches or wraiths lying in ambush and nearly killed them before realizing they were human. Fortunately, no one had died, though three would require considerable bed rest.

As he sank deeper into the padded seat, his hand unconsciously found the two gemstones of strength. One was his. The other was smaller and unfamiliar. Ingrid had told him it was among the spoils taken from Clavieras in the Five Peaks.

His eyes suddenly widened. He carefully touched the skin around the eyes, then passed a hand before his face to test his sight. The redness that had long clouded his left eye was gone.

His vision had returned to normal.

More than that, he had never felt this fit before. He curled his hand into a fist and clenched it as hard as he could. The power flowing from the gemstones responded almost instinctively.

Somehow, the long sleep had left him without any noticeable muscle wasting, which was a blessing in itself. He had no idea whether the gemstones were responsible or whether something else had preserved his body.

Thinking it was fruitless to keep dwelling on the matter, Lansius once again turned toward the map displayed upright according to his preference and studied it.

His gaze easily followed the route from Korelia to White Lake, then onward to Edessa.

A thought that had first taken shape the previous night returned with greater force, burning ever brighter in his mind.

Then another followed.

“Avery,” he muttered as he studied the map. “There’s no way you don’t have contingencies...”

Lansius knew he needed to meet the man. Lord Avery knew the region better than most, having turned it into his personal battleground over the past year.

Slowly, Lansius gathered his thoughts. He had almost no plan and no time to assemble an army. For Edessa, he would have to scrape together whatever forces were still within reach.

At least the Shogunate is safe.

His strategy had bought Midlandia, Lowlandia, and Nicopola nearly two years of peace in which to recover. More than that, the Houses had followed his policy of suspending all agricultural taxes. The relief had brought about a rural boom. Farmers reclaimed neglected fields, expanded their planting, and kept more grain for the following season. Harvest after harvest, the price of grain fell, even as the army needed vast quantities to supply the Edessa campaign.

When the war failed to bring the shortages everyone had feared, merchants stopped hoarding, and prices fell even further, settling at their lowest level in years.

And unexpectedly, the army’s purchases kept the surplus from becoming worthless.

That abundance had inspired confidence in the Shogunate. Despite the war, and despite him, the Lord Shogun, lying ill, food remained plentiful and prices stable. To the common folk, there could be no clearer proof that the realm stood firm.

With no taxes on the harvest, silver from grain purchases flowed steadily into the villages. Households that once spent only on seed and survival began purchasing better clothes, medicines, new tools, and wedding feasts. Some sent their children to stay with relatives in the cities, where they could receive an education or attend the growing number of schools open to commoners.

Craftsmen everywhere found more customers. Markets grew busier, inns filled more often, and reports spoke of livelier streets and increasing numbers of merchants and travelers.

The wider recovery had begun from the ground up.

Lansius’ other policy had also begun to change rural society. Many common-born men in his service had risen to become lieutenants, adjutants, and captains, yet military rank alone did not grant them a place among the lower nobility, especially once they retired through injury or by choice.

While Lansius, as the Lord Shogun, could bestow such ranks himself, doing so too freely would only breed envy. Nor did every officer desire a title. Some preferred to return home with silver, livestock, or a mule and cart to secure their family’s future.

Fortunately, the suspension of agricultural taxes had another side to it. A separate policy allowed anyone to purchase a recognized rank by delivering the required number of cartloads of grain to a designated Shogunate granary. This created a direct path for social advancement. Commoners seeking greater standing could purchase the rank of elder, page, esquire, or cavalryman.

Those able to provide far greater quantities of grain, while also owning horses and having trained in mounted combat, could purchase the rank of Armiger, with the chance to be knighted after proving themselves in a tournament, mock battle, or military campaign.

It was an arrangement that benefited everyone. The officers chose the reward they wanted, the House avoided accusations of favoring one man over another, and the grain paid for the ranks filled the Shogunate’s granaries despite the suspension of agricultural taxes.

More importantly, payment was only considered complete once the grain reached its appointed destination. The buyers therefore had to arrange and pay for the transport themselves. During the previous year, the designated granaries had stood near the Edessa border, where the army needed the grain most.

The policy mobilized merchants as well as soldiers. Wealthy traders and landlords who desired greater standing, along with the privileges attached to it, purchased grain from the western regions and hired carts, guards, and draft animals to carry it eastward. What might otherwise have required a vast effort from the House was instead handled by men eager to raise their standing in society.

The lure of rank and prestige proved so powerful that the grain delivered through the scheme covered nearly the entire cost of feeding the Shogunate’s forces during the Edessa campaign.

But these were not empty titles. Their households were exempt from future military levies, and their sons could inherit the title at one grade lower. Every harvest, they received a gift from the House according to their standing. For some, it might be wine, bolts of the finest linen, fur-lined clothing for winter, or well-made wooden furniture. When their children married, the House would send a city official bearing gifts, along with personal congratulations signed by the Chamberlain and bearing the official seal of Castle Korelia.

For people who lived far from castles and courts, such recognition carried enormous weight. The gifts made his standing visible as well, leaving little room for doubt that he had risen in status and now held a place neither his neighbors nor the local elders could easily dismiss.

Those of higher rank received even greater recognition. Valiant captains who had purchased the title of Armiger or Knight would receive yearly gifts that might include silks, worked silver, the finest fur coats, decorated helmets, a finely made saddle, or even a prized foal from a proven bloodline, depending on their needs and at the House’s discretion.

The House made no distinction between ranks purchased through this policy and those bestowed directly. Once granted, the title carried all the rights, honors, and obligations that came with it.

To the public, the policy was about rewarding service and giving everyone an equal chance to rise. Within the House, however, it served another purpose. Each newly elevated family became a staunch supporter of the Shogunate, bound to it by gratitude, privilege, and a shared interest in preserving the order that had raised them.

As their wealth and influence grew, so too did the House’s roots spread deeper into the towns and villages. In time, this rising class of veterans, officers, and newly titled families would provide a counterweight to the old landed nobility.

By the time Lansius finished the report, he was smiling. His policy had worked.

The policy was not without mishaps, and complaints were plentiful. The land officials warned that it would break the province. Without the grain tax, they feared that castles, fortresses, and poorer districts dependent on the House would struggle to secure enough food. In several places, that was exactly what happened. Some garrison commanders were forced to purchase grain at steeper prices simply to feed their men.

There were abuses as well. Several people attempted to deliver grain of poor quality by mixing old stock with the new harvest. Much of it was fit only for mules and horses, yet they claimed that it had spoiled along the road and that the fault was not theirs. They were fined accordingly, both as punishment and to ensure fairness toward those who had delivered grain of proper quality.

Many also voiced concern that allowing anyone to purchase rank would undermine the old order. Established families complained that men without clear lineage could now stand beside them and claim the same courtesies. To them, every newly raised elder, esquire, or Armiger diminished the distinction they had guarded for generations.

And indeed, not every new titleholder proved worthy of the rank. Some became arrogant, mistaking recognition for authority. Disputes arose over seating at local banquets, the right to bear arms in public, and countless other petty matters of precedence.

Moreover, rumors were rampant that brigands had used the policy to purchase rank and escape justice. Those claims remained unsubstantiated. However, several wealthy families did purchase ranks for themselves in hopes that their new standing would help shield sons implicated in crimes from the bailiffs. It did not work. Their efforts only brought greater scrutiny from the House.

Fortunately, the Midlandian scholars stood firmly behind the policy and defended it vehemently.

For once, Lansius found himself on their good side, and he had to admit that it was pleasant not to have his House on the receiving end of their arguments.

Quietly, he offered a solemn prayer of gratitude to Emperor Wen of Han, whose wisdom and statecraft had endured across the ages.

Afterward, he put the report onto the pile, glanced at the clock once more, and drew a long breath.

Beyond the Shogunate, its armies, and all its burdens, what mattered most was that his family was safe. Currently, Audrey was making a brief visit to Korimor, where she was overseeing her city and the new wing of the military academy. The Shogunate still need more officers, and from the reports, the effort seemed to be progressing well.

Korimor would serve as a proving ground for the new officers. Every rider sent there would have to endure the long journey across the Great Plains. Even those who travelled by carriage would learn to respect the nomads and gain some understanding of their way of life.

The city was an ideal place for officers to learn without too many distractions. It lay close enough to Midlandia and Korelia to remain useful, yet far from the main trade routes.

Meanwhile, Mother was with Tanya, who had taken up the governorship of Canardia. She would need all the support she could get.

Lady Daniella and Francisca were there as well. The half-kin had been injured in the fight against the lich and had needed nearly a year to recover, but her strength and judgment would now be invaluable to Tanya.

There was also the matter of the assassin who had long haunted Canardia and had now been captured by the magic-sensitive half-kin Lord Beatrix had lent them. Given the sensitivity of the situation, Lansius would need to deal with it in person. Until then, the prisoner’s identity and capture had to remain tightly concealed. If word leaked out, it might jeopardize his relationship with the Guild.

Still, he trusted Tanya to manage the situation.

At the very least, he had reason to believe that the assassins wouldn’t try to harm her.

...

Another hour passed before a light knock came at the door. Margo went over at once and spoke quietly with the guards outside. The exchange lasted longer than usual, with Margo asking several questions.

Lansius thought little of it, assuming it was probably lunch. He had eaten only a light breakfast of bread, olives, and cheese, sent to his desk along with medicinal concoction.

At last, Margo stepped aside and allowed the door to be opened for the visitors.

Seeing that Lansius was unoccupied, he announced, “My Lord, the Chamberlain of Korimor and the maids.”

Ingrid entered first, followed by twelve maids dressed in black gowns with crisp white collars, white scarves, and long white aprons. The uniforms were the culmination of Valerie’s influence on his House since their first year in Korelia. The Frenchwoman had always adored Victorian maids.

“My Lord, may I present the maids who will attend to you,” Ingrid said, standing among women half her age with undiminished charm and confidence.

The twelve women arranged themselves in a neat line and curtsied as one.

It was an ordinary formality, much like the changing of the guard, though it only took place whenever Lansius moved to a different castle or residence. He merely needed to acknowledge them and offer a few words of thanks for their future service.

Yet before he could speak, his eyes widened.

One of the twelve was not what she appeared to be.

Before he fully realized what he was doing, Lansius had risen from his seat and crossed the chamber toward her. His gaze remained fixed on her beautiful, sombre face, her shoulder-length brown hair, and those peculiarly intense brown eyes.

The maid kept her head lowered, her expression unreadable.

As he drew near, the maids curtsied once more.

Lansius stopped before her, frowning, his body tense. He cast a sharp glance at Ingrid, who, as expected, politely looked away.

So that was what this was about.

Without warning, he bent down, slipped one arm behind the maid’s back, and reached the other behind her knees, intending to sweep her into his arms. Yet one of her feet slid back almost imperceptibly, widening her stance as she lowered her weight. She held her ground effortlessly, all without breaking her expression.

“What is this?” Lansius asked as their eyes met. “Since when do maids stand like an Elandian swordmaster?”

At last, her expression cracked. A pained smile appeared on her lips as her eyes began to glisten.

Lansius tried again, this time with a little more force, though still careful not to hurt her. She finally released her stance and allowed him to lift her into his arms.

A wave of gasps and excited whispers swept through the chamber.

“What kind of joke is this?” Lansius demanded, holding her tightly against his chest. “Whose idea was it? I may have slept for years, but I would never forget you.”

Several maids clasped their hands over their mouths, while others exchanged delighted looks, barely restraining themselves from squealing aloud.

The maid in his arms wore heavy makeup, yet even that had failed to hide her from him. “We thought you might still be under some spell,” she explained. “I needed to be certain.”

Lansius merely snorted and carried her out of the study. The people gathered outside drew back in surprise. Only then did they recognize the woman in his arms, and their confusion gave way to delighted murmurs.

At long last, the Lord and Lady were reunited.

Lansius quickened his pace, just short of breaking into a run.

“W-where are you taking me?” Audrey asked, her maid uniform fluttering.

“Where else?” he replied, giving her backside an unabashed squeeze. The familiar softness beneath his hand made him feel more alive than he had in years.

Audrey’s eyes widened as she realized that this truly was her husband. “But it’s noon!”

“It’s been years," he countered.

“B-but you were asleep.”

Lansius only laughed.

“Hold on.” Audrey suddenly twisted in his arms, trying to make him slow.

Lansius tightened his hold on her, but then noticed an elderly nursemaid waiting farther down the corridor.

He slowed down. Something small rested in the cradle of the woman’s arms, wrapped in fine cloth. Some of his finest guards stood around her, and one of them was already wiping tears from his face.

“Is that...?” Lansius asked, his eyes widening.

Audrey pouted. “Well, I can hardly complain. I did tell you to give me another.”

“T-that’s...” The words failed him.

She leaned close to his ear and whispered, “I gave you another. This time, a daughter.”

Lansius swallowed. A storm of emotion rose in his chest. Still carrying Audrey in his arms, he walked toward the nursemaid and looked down at the sleeping child. The tiny hands. The tiny face. Then he noticed her hair.

Black.

A gasp escaped him.

Everyone around them was smiling or weeping openly. Ingrid, Margo, and the maids who had hurried after them now crowded the corridor, several with tears running down their cheeks as they watched with barely contained joy.

The House of Blue and Bronze had grown. And now, after a year and a half of sleeping sickness, Lansius had finally returned to them. At last, the House felt whole again.

Audrey leaned close and whispered, “Remember your promise. I want another airship.”

It was such a shameless blow that Lansius couldn’t help but chuckle. The sound stirred the infant in the nursemaid’s arms. Her tiny face shifted beneath the fine cloth as she heard her father’s voice for the first time.

Among the guards watching the reunion with open delight, the airship crew stood waiting, weary from the overnight flight but also caught up in the joy of the moment.

With the airship, Edessa was within reach.
