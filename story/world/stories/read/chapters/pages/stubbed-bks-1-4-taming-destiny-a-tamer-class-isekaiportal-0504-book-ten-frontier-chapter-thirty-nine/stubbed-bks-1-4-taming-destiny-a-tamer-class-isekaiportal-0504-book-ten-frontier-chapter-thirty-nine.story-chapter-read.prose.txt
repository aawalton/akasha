After the interruption from Nessie, I spend more time experimenting with Bond Teleport. By the time I’m satisfied with how the new version works, I’ve learned some important things.

The first is that the ease increases with how much the being trusts me. Teleporting with Fenrir in my spatial tattoo was cheaper than transporting Sirocco, for example, though their average levels of power are roughly similar.

The second is that distance isn’t as much of a factor as I originally thought. It is a factor – the teleportation grows in expense as the start and end point move further apart – but the increase in expense isn’t as quick as I was expecting. I have to guess it’s something to do with the streamlining I did in Bond Teleport’s matrix. It makes me wonder whether there’s more streamlining I could do to improve it further.

Maybe when I know how Bond Teleport works better in its current form, I decide. I need to use it a fair bit more before I’m likely to make meaningful – and effective – changes there.

Fenrir returns to the settlement with me, while Sirocco goes off into the forest, Fire Sirocco flapping alongside her. The two seem pretty inseparable right now – hopefully Sirocco isn’t too disappointed when the fire version loses mana and disappears at some point.

“Would you like me to make you Earth Fenrir again?” I ask the lizog. He responds with measured enthusiasm, so I concentrate on forming a new companion for him.

When Earth Fenrir appears, he immediately goes to greet Fenrir. Curious. Does it remember the last time it was around? Or is this because part of what I put into it included instructions of following Fenrir’s orders?

Something else I might want to test.

For now, though, the sun is heading rapidly towards the horizon, and I’m getting rather hungry, having missed lunch – again.

Standing on the sloped road leading up to the settlement, I take a moment to survey my domain in the soft evening light, the two Fenrirs keeping me silent company.

The forest looks so calm right now. It hides its dangers well – just as the sea, with its gentle waves, conceals the multitude of creatures lurking beneath the surface. My eyes drift toward the mountain that stands out from the rest of the range. That must be the one Rockfell wants to claim – the one rich in ores.

I feel an immediate pull to go and explore it, but I quell the urge. We've only just started to find some sort of even keel after all the challenges of our arrival. It's not yet time to set off into the mountains.

And yet I find myself longing to. It's been too long since I struck out into the wilderness with nothing but my companions, and strangely enough, I miss it.

Telling myself that I will save it for another day, I continue up the slope. Heading into the settlement proper, I take in the atmosphere, enjoying the relaxation I see in people’s faces. With beasts killed or negotiated with, parasitic influence healed, and food now in ready supply, the general feeling of the settlement is one of hope.

We really ought to come up with a name for this place. I consider making the decision myself, then decide against it – I don’t have nearly enough faith in my naming skills to insist on choosing for everyone else. I'll ask Loren to organise a poll instead. I decide. Get suggestions from the people, let them vote on it. Everyone here belongs to this settlement; they ought to have some say in what it's called.

With the food situation on the verge of being resolved, I suspect the other lords will soon start making noises about establishing their own settlements. We should begin sending scouts specifically to identify suitable locations – places near good resources, and defensible ones too, I think, with the other competitors in mind.

Although my stomach is calling me toward supper, I don’t make haste towards my house. Instead, I scratch Fenrir behind the ears and then send him on without me.

Sure? he checks.

Yeah. I want to see more of our town, I tell him decisively.

He eyes me for a moment, then sends a sense of nonchalance across our Bond – and an admonition to keep watching out for danger.

I hope that I won’t encounter any of that in my own settlement, but I promise him that I will. Then he and Earth Fenrir head off, looking a bit like Moses as their presence parts the crowd on the main street.

Taking advantage of his distraction, I trigger both Stealth and Fade, then step over to where a building’s overhang casts a light shadow. Donning a nondescript brown robe over my distinctive armour, I release my Skills, then step into the movement of the crowds, matching their pace and following their general direction.

I find myself entering District Three’s marketplace and surreptitiously conjure a small bench at the edge of it. There, I sit for a while, watching the world go by.

I see workers returning from their shifts, covered in dust and dirt and wood shavings. Some visit the settlement-sponsored stall that offers a single bowl of basic stew for free to anyone who wishes to avail themselves of it, but I’m interested to see that not everyone does.

Some of the workers head straight for their houses – these are invariably ones with lit windows, and a family inside. Many already have food on the table, and the scent of the smoke drifting out of the chimneys indicates that the food was cooked at home.

Others stop at a market stall for food first – but not necessarily the one offering free stew. Instead, it seems like several enterprising merchants have set up rival food offerings. With grain available as well as meat and fish, the range of offerings is much wider and some of the smells are delicious enough to tempt even me.

In fact, I decide as my stomach gurgles, why don’t I investigate? It’s important information on how the settlement is doing, I justify to myself. I know that food has probably been made for me back at the manor, but with enchanted cupboards in the kitchen, I know that it won’t go to waste even if I eat here.

Decided, I head for one of the nearest stalls, the scents drifting off it irresistible. I do let the others know not to expect me – River promises to inform the kitchens. She’s more than a little curious about what I’m doing instead, so I promise I’ll tell her more when we see each other later.

The queue moves quickly and soon I’m facing the stall-keeper. She has a face that’s vaguely familiar – someone I’ve seen in passing rather than someone I’ve ever spoken to. Fortunately, though her eyes narrow briefly in the same kind of vague recognition, she doesn’t immediately identify me.

“What would you like?” she asks, polite enough, but with a note of impatience as I browse the choices. I don’t recognise any of them.

“Can you walk me through the options?”

She blinks, slightly taken aback – though by what, I’m not sure. Still she gets over it quickly enough and talks about the five different dishes on offer.

I’m impressed – though the main ingredients of each dish seem to be the same, natural since we still have limited variety, the way they’re prepared is different.

“Where did you even get all of your spices?” I ask her with appreciation.

“I brought them from Moriax, didn’t I?” she returns with that impatience clearer to hear.

“Do you have any seeds to grow them here?”

She stares at me as if I’m stupid.

“I’m not a farmer, am I? Now, you gonna eat or what?”

“Of course. I’ll take the fish and mulspa stew, please.”

She takes one of the thick wraps – something between pita bread and naan bread – and dumps a large spoonful of a fragrant orange stew into its centre, skillfully twisting the wrap around so not a drop escapes.

“That’ll be ten copper.”

My eyebrows shoot up a little. That’s pretty expensive for a food-stall in a marketplace – on Moriax, people would expect to pay three or four coppers for a meal like this. Ten coppers and more for a meal tends to be reserved for an inn – even a tavern would charge less.

But I suppose it’s reasonable – the spices she’s using are in limited supply since they came from Moriax. Besides, it’s a choice to come here at all – people who don’t want to spend their money could go for the basic stew and pay nothing.

On another note, it seems that either Loran and Sarran have moved quickly, or the people have decided for themselves what the currency will be.

Reaching into my Inventory, I realise that I only have seven coppers. Instead, I pull out a silver with an apologetic smile.

She takes the coin with another blink, then fumbles through her pouch.

“You’re gonna clean me out of change,” she grumbles. “What’m I gonna do with a silver eh?”

“Tell you what, you can keep the change,” I offer. She stills and looks at me with a suspicious gaze.

“What’s the catch?”

“Just answer a couple of questions for me?”

She leans sideways and looks at the queue of tired and hungry people behind me. Already, I hear several grumbles about what’s taking so long. Her expression tightens as she sees two people leave the queue.

“You’ve already cost me twenty coppers,” she complains.

“And you have ninety extra coppers there and the food of nine people who didn’t take it,” I point out. “But if you don’t want to take the time, you can just give me seventy coppers in change.” Being mean about money that doesn’t matter much to me but does to her isn’t something I want to do.

The woman’s face softens again as she considers my offer.

“Alright. Three questions, then you’re done,” she bargains.

“Fine.” I can probably learn everything I wanted to in that time. “Where are you getting the food from?”

The woman shrugs.

“From the manor kitchens. They’re selling surplus. I wanted to get it from the hunters and fishermen direct, but they refused.” She doesn’t sound happy about that.

“And what are you going to do when your spices run out?”

“Figure something else out,” she tells me immediately, the glint in her eyes turning a touch hostile. Perhaps she doesn’t like the reminder. I’m not quite sure why, though – surely that would be a pretty good marketing tactic. Things that seem scarce are often perceived as more valuable. “One more question, then clear the way for my other customers.”

“If you could choose what the farmers focus on growing, what would it be?”

The hostile glint softens again, turning into thoughtfulness.

“Grain for sure – that’s the base. Maybe malfa root – that has a good flavour and thickens up a soup. Green lith leaves are good too – they add a nice freshness and crunch to lots of dishes. And for seasoning, we could do with more salt – and peppercorns. Those’re the basics – got a whole list of spices if you want one.”

“Maybe leave one at the kitchens the next time you visit,” I suggest. “We’ll see what we can do.”

She eyes me suspiciously and I think that she’s probably putting the pieces together. Before she can arrive at a conclusion that I’d rather she doesn’t, I smile at her, thank her for her time, and then move away. She gazes at me for a long moment, then shakes herself and turns to the next customer who’s impatiently pointing to his desired meal.

Moving back over to the bench with my meal in my hand, I realise that two other people have taken my space. Thinking about it, there really aren’t any places to sit, unless someone wants to find a space on the floor and hope they won’t accidentally be trampled.

That’s easy enough to solve. Trying to move as subtly as I can, I press mana into the earth below my feet and grow benches at various places around the square.

Of course, benches suddenly growing like plants filmed with a time-lapse camera are bound to attract attention regardless of how quietly I do it, and shouts of surprise ring out around the square. But Moriaxar people are used to magic – after a brief period where they look around for the Earth Mage, and I pretend to do the same, they just shrug and accept the new amenities.

Soon, the benches are all claimed – I even have a companion next to me as I enjoy my meal.

The stew is so much better than the fare that we had to survive on in the tail end of the manastorm, but it’s definitely not as good as some of the food that came out of Nicholas’ kitchen back on Moriax. That’s to be expected, of course – a street cook and a lord’s chef are bound to be worlds apart in quality.

Still, it’s enjoyable – reminiscent of a tikka masala back on Earth, but with some oddly sour notes. I don’t know if that’s normal, or a remnant of the method we’ve been using to make the local products safe to eat. And the fish is different, of course. The bread is dense, holding the stew perfectly without leaking, but the parts that don’t get soaked in the juices are a bit hard to chew. I learn that I’m eating it incorrectly when I see others breaking off the dry top sections and dipping them in the stew in the middle. Changing my method makes the bread much more edible.

I reflect on what the food-seller told me and decide I’d better check in with Loran on decisions made about the food situation – and whether tax has started being implemented on it and similar sales. But before that, I need to decide how to approach it all for the city. Having all the food come to the kitchens and then the extras be made available to various merchants is one way of doing it, but is it the best way?

As far as I know, the usual way of going about it is that each person or organisation makes an agreement with different food suppliers. Bigger organisations ordering lots of products can often get them more cheaply than those only ordering a few, and speciality or high quality items go for a higher price than common and low quality ones. But that risks start-ups finding it impossible to get contracts. Is that the way I want to take my city?

I’m distracted from my thoughts as the worker next to me on the bench – from the tannery, if my sense of smell is anything to go by – pulls out a few carven figures from his pocket along with a chalk.

Kneeling down on the floor in front of the bench, he draws a board with nine by nine squares, and places the pieces in the four corners. Two of the corners have human figures; two have beasts.

As if this is a nightly occurrence, several people – including some children – gather around, each jockeying to take one of the roles. The man, Ker Nulian from their cries, calms things down and soon chooses four people – two adults, two children – to take the different roles.

Watching them play, I realise it's a turn-by-turn strategy game – everyone wants control of the central square. Beasts can team up together and win for the beasts; humans can team up together and win for the humans. However, they don’t win as many points that way as if only one player wins control of the square and eliminates all of their opponents.

I find myself enraptured, and keen to learn myself. And along with it, I’ve got a new idea – and one I need to discuss with Loran and Sarran as soon as possible.
