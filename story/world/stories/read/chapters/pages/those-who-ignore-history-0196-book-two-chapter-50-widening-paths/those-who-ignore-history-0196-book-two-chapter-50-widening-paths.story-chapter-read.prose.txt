We were in a forest—that much was clear.

Yet before me laid a woman in a beautiful dress, pouring me a cup of tea for once.

“Cordelia. Hello.”

Her voice was porcelain. Not fragile—refined. Controlled. Every syllable balanced as though weighed on scales far too precise for mortal throats. Her face mirrored it: flawless symmetry, smooth as polished marble, untouched by age or strain. Even the way she moved, lifting the teapot and tilting it just enough, felt intentional beyond the needs of etiquette.

Her entire body was carved perfection.

She sat beneath a gazebo grown entirely from flowers—lilies of every size and color woven together into living arches. White dominated, but streaks of gold, violet, and deep crimson threaded through like veins in marble. Light filtered down through their petals in soft, shifting patterns. The forest beyond the gazebo felt distant, muted, like a painting hung too far away to touch.

The tea steamed.

The scent was unfamiliar. Not unpleasant. Just foreign.

“I don’t recall accepting an invitation,” I said calmly, not touching the cup.

“You rarely do,” she replied gently, setting the teapot aside. “Yet you always arrive.”

I studied her for several seconds. I could feel it immediately—this was not an illusion. Nor a projection. Nor a spirit construct posing through borrowed matter. The mana density around her was…balanced. Not compressed like a weapon. Not diffused like a dream.

Anchored.

“You’re not a dominus,” I said.

She smiled. “No. I am not.”

“Nor are you a herald,” I continued. “You lack the tethering marks. I don’t even sense a governing contract.”

“Correct again,” she said pleasantly. “You always were observant.”

That earned a faint narrowing of my eyes. “You speak as though you know me.”

She lifted her teacup and took a small sip. “I know of you. As one might know a river by its erosion patterns. Or a star by the shadow it casts when it dies.”

I ignored the metaphor. “Then let’s skip the ceremony. What are you?”

She set the cup down slowly.

“I am a witness.”

The word settled into the air with quiet gravity.

“To what?” I asked.

“To thresholds,” she said. “To the moment before something becomes inevitable.”

I exhaled through my nose. “That’s vague to the point of uselessness.”

“Only to those who believe inevitability arrives loudly.”

I finally reached for the cup.

The porcelain was warm in my hands. The tea tasted faintly of honey and ash.

“…You’re not hostile,” I said.

“No.”

“But you’re also not neutral.”

She inclined her head a fraction. “No.”

Silence stretched between us, filled only by the low hum of the forest and the shifting glow of the lilies.

“You separated me from my companions,” I said at last.

“I did.”

“That alone is grounds for retaliation.”

“Yes,” she agreed. “But you haven’t acted.”

I watched the steam coil upward. “Because if I had, this space would already be collapsing.”

Her smile returned—smaller this time. “You always did leap to the architecture of things.”

“I have to,” I replied. “Reality rarely survives people who don’t.”

She regarded me quietly for several seconds. Then she said, “Your group carries an offering.”

“Honey,” I answered.

“Growth,” she corrected.

A pause. Then I inclined my head. “Fair.”

“You believe it is a trade good,” she continued. “A bridge between realms. A tool of diplomacy.”

“It is all of those.”

“And also a catalyst,” she said.

I felt the faintest tightening at the edges of my senses. “For what?”

“For pressure.”

I didn’t respond immediately.

She went on, undisturbed. “Every realm sits at its own equilibrium. Mana density. Life tolerances. Dominance hierarchies. You introduce accelerated meridian growth into a closed system, you do not simply ‘improve’ it. You tilt it.”

“Into stability,” I countered.

“Or into collapse.”

I finally met her gaze fully. Her eyes were not unnatural. No glow. No void. No sigils. Just eyes.

Too normal.

“You speak as though the outcome is already chosen,” I said.

“No,” she replied softly. “I speak as one who has watched this outcome many times.”

“That implies precedent,” I said. “Yet your dominus is absent.”

“Absence does not halt momentum,” she answered. “It only removes the hand from the brake.”

We fell quiet again.

After a time, I asked, “Why show yourself to me at all?”

“Because you are the only one among them who would argue rather than react.”

I gave a thin smile. “An appraisal, not a compliment.”

“Those are not mutually exclusive.”

I leaned back slightly in my chair, gaze drifting toward the lilies overhead. “You fear disruption.”

“I fear blind disruption,” she corrected.

“And you believe our presence constitutes that.”

“Yes.”

I took another sip of tea. “Then why allow the gate to open in the first place?”

Her eyes shifted—not away from me, but inward, as if consulting something far older than memory.

“Because the gate is not mine to close.”

That was…interesting.

“Then you are not a ruler,” I said. “Nor a servant. You observe, but you do not govern.”

“Correct.”

“Which means you lack authority.”

“Also correct.”

“Then why warn me at all?”

Her fingers folded together in her lap. “Because awareness alters probability.”

I exhaled slowly. “Only slightly.”

“Sometimes,” she agreed. “Slightly is enough.”

We sat in silence again.

Finally, I asked the question that had been coiling in the back of my mind since the moment I arrived.

“What happens to this realm if we succeed?”

She did not answer immediately.

When she did, her voice was lower.

“It becomes louder.”

“Louder how?”

“More alive,” she said. “More fertile. More capable of change.”

“That doesn’t sound like a warning.”

“It is,” she replied. “All great changes sound like blessings at first.”

“And if we fail?”

She met my eyes.

“Then this forest remains beautiful,” she said. “And alone.”

The word alone echoed in the gazebo like a dropped cup that never quite shattered.

I set my tea down carefully. “You’re asking me to sabotage my own mission.”

“No,” she said. “I am asking you to be precise with it.”

I considered that.

“You separated me,” I said slowly, “to tell me something you could have risked telling my leader.”

“Yes.”

“Why me instead of Alexander?”

A faint sadness touched her expression. “Because he carries too many futures at once. You are the only one among them who can afford to choose one and discard the rest without breaking.”

That…was uncomfortably accurate.

I stood.

The gazebo did not resist me. The forest did not stir. The woman remained seated behind the tea.

“When next we meet,” I said, “it will likely be as adversaries.”

She lifted her teacup once more. “Or as collaborators who never admit it.”

I turned away, already feeling the space thinning, reality knitting itself back into weight and direction.

“Cordelia,” she called softly.

I paused.

“Whatever you decide,” she said, “do not mistake inevitability for intention.”

Then the lilies dimmed.

The tea cooled.

And the forest took me back.

***

The visions peeled away from me in shivering strips of color, dissolving like wet paint under rainfall. When the last ribbon of someone else’s memory slipped free, I was left again in the clearing with Sadrin. The blue-lit forest hummed like a sleeping beast, but nothing was louder than the pounding of my heart. My lungs felt squeezed, as if every memory I’d just witnessed had been poured into me like molten gold, heavy and scalding.

I swallowed hard.

“Why… why did you show me all of that, Sadrin?”

He leaned back against a root that had curled into a throne for him, grapes still in his hand, posture as languid as a cat sunning itself on a temple step. But his eyes, pale and distant as winter dawn, didn’t so much look at me as weigh me. Every blink felt like a judgment passed in silence.

“You desired the answer,” Sadrin said, almost gently. “You wished to know. You beckoned me to bequeath you the information.”

I clenched my hands. “Don’t twist this. I wanted my team safe. Not… violated.”

“A strong word,” he murmured, tilting his head. “Yet also not incorrect.”

That set my teeth on edge. “Those were their private fears. Their fights. Their… selves. And you just—”

“Revealed, not altered,” Sadrin cut in, still too calm. “I have no need to invent their truths. They walk with them always.”

I wanted to shout at him. Instead, I forced myself to breathe, remembering Cordelia’s endless mantra of clarity before reaction. So I steadied myself.

“You didn’t have permission.”

He gave the faintest shrug. “Consent is a concept with many definitions among dominions. In this one, what is witnessed by the nexus spirit is recorded. What is recorded may be shared. You simply requested to see the others.”

“I didn’t ask for that.”

“You asked what they faced.” His voice was silk pulling tight. “You asked where they were. You asked if they lived. And I answered you with perfect accuracy.”

His logic was a trap with polished walls. Difficult to argue against without falling into the pit he’d shaped.

I dragged a hand through my hair. “Still feels wrong.”

“That is because you care for them.”

The way he said it… made me go still.

“You carry the fear of losing yourself,” Sadrin continued. “So you look to them, and seeing their struggles only magnifies the reflection.”

My voice tightened. “Explain that.”

“You fear power,” he said. “Not because it is dangerous, but because you suspect you may enjoy it.” His gaze sliced through me. “You fear gluttony of strength, not its burden.”

There it was. Dragged into light with no ceremony, no kindness. Just truth, sharp and unavoidable.

I hated how much it rang inside me.

“I’m nothing like the others who chase power for its own sake.”

“Perhaps not,” Sadrin allowed. “But you are of Hydra blood now. You are a creature of multiplicity. Hunger is written into every fiber of you. The desire to grow, to stretch, to consume challenge until there remains none capable of matching you.”

The worst part was that a small, traitorous part of me thrummed at that. A whisper in the marrow. Something old. Something curious.

I shoved it down.

“And that’s why you showed me their visions?” I asked.

“No.” He shook his head, then leaned forward. “I showed you so you might understand what unites you all.”

I blinked. “Which is…?”

“Each of them stands at a precipice,” Sadrin said. “Cordelia with her fractured certainty. V with his protective instinct sharpened into guilt. Ten with her haunted strength, still shackled by the echoes of her mark. Fallias with her longing for connection, terrified it may vanish. Fractal with loyalty burning bright enough to become reckless.”

As he spoke their names, each memory flickered behind my eyes again — Cordelia debating peace with a figure carved of lilies, Fractal bleeding illusions into light as wooden beasts shattered around her, Fallias whispering poems to an empty forest, V hiding fear behind bravado and traps, Ten’s electrified fury reborn through the scars of her past.

“You saw not their weaknesses,” Sadrin said, The parts of themselves they cannot outrun.”

I frowned. “And what does that have to do with me?”

“You asked why fate spirals around your steps,” Sadrin said, standing. His robes fluttered like frost-lit banners. “It is because you attract those who tremble on the edge of transformation. Those who crave change but fear its cost. You rally them, even unknowingly.”

I stared at him. “I’m not a leader.”

“You are,” he said. “Though you reject the crown, you still create a court.”

“That’s dramatized.”

“Is it?” He raised an eyebrow. “You stand with a scholar of memory, a dragon-blooded warrior, a storm-marked survivor, a shadow-born infiltrator gifted in bismuth and deception, and a weaver of fate’s quieter threads. And they chose you.”

I felt a strange ache climb into my chest. Something warm and terrifying.

“I didn’t ask them to.”

“That is why they trust you,” Sadrin said in a tone that almost sounded approving. “Weak men demand loyalty. Strong ones inspire it.”

I looked away. His words scraped at something raw inside me.

“So all of this,” I said quietly, “was some kind of test?”

“Not a test.” He took another grape, crushing it between long fingers. The juice trickled down like a droplet of blood. “A proof.”

My breath hitched. “Of what?”

“That you remain yourself,” Sadrin said. “Even as your reflection changes. Even as your status shatters and reforms. Even as your blood wakes to older hungers.”

I blinked. “What do you mean remains myself? I haven’t changed.”

Sadrin gave me a look that said I’d just proclaimed water wasn’t wet.

“You are Hydra now,” he said. “Your soul strains and expands. Your mind houses echoes of your other selves. Your status fractures because it cannot categorize you. And yet—”

He gestured at me with a slow sweep.

“—you still worry for your companions instead of your own evolution. That is why I showed you their struggles. To remind you that you are anchored not by fear, but by connection.”

I froze.

“That is why you do not lose yourself,” Sadrin murmured.

My throat tightened.

Because the truth was: sometimes I did imagine losing myself. Power swallowing personality. Strength dissolving identity. Becoming something monstrous with too many heads and not enough conscience.

But then I thought of Fallias sleeping on my arm, Fractal humming as she polished her feathers, V threatening to salt-bomb anyone who hurt Ten, Cordelia brewing tea with maddening serenity, Sven fussing over paperwork, Wallace barking orders like an overprotective uncle.

They were my tether.

Sadrin stepped closer, almost within arm’s reach.

“You see now why the nexus allowed you entry,” he said. “Why it separated you. Why it showed you their truths.”

“So I’d understand them better,” I said.

“So you’d understand yourself better,” Sadrin corrected.

I looked down at my hands. “What now?”

“Now,” he said, spreading his arms as the air thickened with luminous motes, “you reunite with your companions. And you walk forward.”

The forest shifted. I felt the world tilt, the clearing dissolving beneath my feet as though someone had folded reality like parchment.

But just before the light swallowed me again, I heard Sadrin whisper:

“You fear gluttony of power. Good. Appetite needs awareness. Without it, you would devour the world.”

And then I fell through the blue.

My team’s voices rushed toward me like returning gravity.

And for the first time since waking from the dreamworld, I felt something close to certainty.

Not comfort.

Not peace.

But certainty.

I would not lose myself.

Not while they were with me.

Not while I chose them.

Not while I chose to stay me.

And if Sadrin was right… the path ahead was only beginning to widen.
