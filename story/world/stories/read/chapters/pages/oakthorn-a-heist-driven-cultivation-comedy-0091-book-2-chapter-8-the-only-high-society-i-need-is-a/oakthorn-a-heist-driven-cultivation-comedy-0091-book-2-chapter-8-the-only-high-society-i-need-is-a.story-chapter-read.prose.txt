“—and then the Arbiter actually bowed,” Cade concluded, savoring each word like fine wine as they crouched in the shadows across from The Bent Shieldmaiden tavern. “Bowed! To me! The literal Destroyer they’re hunting! I almost lost character right there.”

The fading light cast long shadows across the packed dirt road leading to the unimpressive tavern. It was a squat building of weathered stone and ancient timber that had clearly seen better centuries. Smoke billowed from a crooked chimney, carrying the tantalizing scent of roasted meat and fermented regrets.

“Yes, Cade, we were all there,” Nora sighed, adjusting her borrowed dress with poorly concealed discomfort. “For all five tellings of this story.”

“Six,” Elena corrected, her nimble fingers braiding Evie’s hair into the simple style of a frontier wife. “He told it twice while we were changing disguises.”

“The best stories deserve repetition,” Cade replied, unrepentant.

The young thief peeked around the corner of the abandoned stable where they’d taken refuge, studying the steady stream of black-armored figures entering the tavern. “Look at them. Twenty heavily armed Arbiters, sworn to uphold Honor’s divine justice, masters of combat and righteous judgment—”

“Each one capable of killing us in fourteen different ways,” Orro interjected flatly.

“—and not one of them from Honor’s Light realized they saluted the very man blamed for Elysia’s annihilation,” Cade finished, eyes gleaming with mischief. “It’s beautiful. Poetic, even.”

“Your ego is showing,” Rayka remarked dryly, but the corner of her mouth twitched upward despite her best efforts.

“Can you blame me? That was masterful improvisation.” Cade turned to face his crew, spreading his arms wide. “Brother Bazz, legendary hunter of the Destroyer, tracking his nemesis across Hallec’s Crossing! I’m just glad they didn’t know that Bazz fell to Stephen’s trickery.” The thief shuddered at the memory.

“The same Stephen who’s a lich. The same one—according to what we’ve heard on the run—now rules unopposed over the charred ruins of Elysia. Because you did, in fact, help destroy an entire city when you freed a long-forgotten goddess who then killed another goddess before our eyes,” Gavin concluded with pursed lips. “Your life is exhausting just to witness.”

Cade bowed with theatrical flair. “Thank you for your attendance.” He straightened, his expression shifting from playful to calculating in an instant. “Now, for our next performance. You all saw the same patrols I did coming in here. The Bent Shieldmaiden has got to be the favorite watering hole for our dear Arbiters. This is our best chance to gather intelligence about the Arbiter outpost just up the road and those fancy breathing masks we need.” He glanced at his disguised crew with critical assessment. “Remember your roles. We can’t all go in together—too conspicuous.”

“I still don’t understand why I have to be a farmer,” Jer grumbled, tugging at the rough homespun that had replaced their stolen Lifekeeper finery. “I’d make a much better wandering bard. I know at least three tavern songs.”

“All three of which end with someone getting stabbed, hanged, or both,” Elena pointed out. “No, your face is much more fit for a turnip-grower.”

“Hey!” Jer shouted in protest.

Elena stuck out her tongue like a true professional, but Bunny yanked on the hem of her disguise. She yelped and Jer used the distraction to tackle his twin to the ground. Their fight went ignored by the rest of the crew.

“Nora, Evie, and Chubbs first,” Cade directed, his eyes never stopping their constant scan of the street. “Family of three, passing through on their way to visit relatives in Westmire. Husband died last winter. Looking for safe passage advice.”

Nora nodded, slipping effortlessly into her role as she adjusted Chubbs on her hip. The toddler goddess was mercifully cooperating, her golden eyes temporarily dimmed to a more mundane hazel by one of Evie’s glamours.

“And remember,” Cade added, “your accent, Nora. Could you work on it? I hate to say it, but you sound like a paladin even if the sigil is gone from your chestplate.”

The former sect leader of the Fateweavers raised an eyebrow. “I’d hate to inflict that on the world.”

“Well, now’s your chance to inflict away,” Cade grinned. “The thicker, the better.”

Nora sighed, then transformed her usual precise diction into something entirely different. “Reckon them Arbiters won’t be mindin’ a poor widder woman and her young’uns askin’ a question or three ‘bout the road ahead.”

Cade’s jaw dropped. “That was... disturbingly good.”

“I grew up in Cleft Hill,” Nora said with a shrug, slipping back into her normal voice. “Spent fifteen years unlearning it after joining the Fateweavers. They’re not big on regional dialects in the order.”

“Tragic waste of a perfectly good accent,” Cade declared. “Wait for ten minutes after they enter, then Orro and I follow separately. Elena, Gavin, Jer—you’re our extraction team if things go sideways.”

“Define ‘sideways,’” Gavin requested.

“Screaming, explosions, spontaneous lizard transformations—the usual,” Cade replied with a dismissive wave. “Keep Bunny with you. Last thing we need is him deciding Arbiter helmets are nesting material.”

The small dragon, currently investigating a beetle with extreme prejudice, looked up at the sound of his name with an expression of pure innocence that fooled absolutely no one.

“Ten minutes,” Nora confirmed, taking Evie’s arm and guiding her toward the street. “Try not to get arrested before we even make it to the swamp of certain death.”

“No promises,” Cade called after them, watching as the three figures—Nora, Evie, and Chubbs—emerged from the shadows and crossed toward the tavern’s welcoming light.

They moved differently now, Nora’s usual warrior’s stride replaced by a weary mother’s gait, Evie’s ethereal float transformed into a nervous young woman’s tentative steps. Even their postures had changed—shoulders slightly hunched, heads lowered in the unconscious deference of those accustomed to being overlooked. They disappeared into the tavern without a single Arbiter giving them a second glance.

“Beautiful,” Cade murmured appreciatively. “Absolutely beautiful.”

“Which one?” Rayka asked with a wickedly knowing grin. Cade’s head whipped to the side, but Rayka only cackled harder. His sister handed him a battered hat that completed his disguise as a road-weary trader down on his luck. “Try not to enjoy yourself too much.”

“Me? Never.” He placed a hand over his heart in mock offense. “I am the very model of restraint and subtlety.”

The synchronized eye-rolling from his entire crew would have been hurtful if it weren’t so well-deserved.

The next ten minutes crawled by, each second stretching as Cade mentally rehearsed his approach. Not that he needed to—improvisation was his lifeblood—but preparation never hurt. Finally, he nodded to Orro, who had transformed himself into the visual approximation of a mercenary through the simple expedient of looking exactly like himself but scowling even more than usual.

“Showtime,” Cade announced, mussing his hair and adopting a slight stoop. “Remember, you’re mute. Not silent—mute. Big difference.”

“I know what mute means,” Orro replied flatly.

“Then prove it by not talking,” Cade grinned, slapping his companion on the shoulder before heading for the street. “Meet you inside. Try to look less like you’re planning everyone’s murder.”

“It was one time, Cade,” Orro muttered but obediently fell silent as they separated.

The Son of Ruin slipped into character like putting on a favorite coat, his entire bearing shifting as he shuffled toward the tavern’s entrance. He’d played this role before—the down-on-his-luck trader, harmless and forgettable, precisely the kind of person no one remembers seeing. By the time he pushed through the heavy wooden door, Cade Stormhollow had vanished completely, replaced by a nameless nobody worthy of neither attention nor concern.

The blast of noise, heat, and competing smells hit him like a physical force. The Bent Shieldmaiden’s main room was packed to capacity, nearly every table occupied by Arbiters in various states of armored dishevelment. Some still wore their distinctive black plate, while others had stripped down to the padded gambesons worn underneath. All retained their weapons, however—no Arbiter was ever truly off-duty.

Cade spotted Nora immediately, seated at a corner table with Evie and Chubbs. They’d already attracted the attention of a trio of older Arbiters, grizzled veterans by the look of their scarred armor and the deference shown by younger soldiers passing their table.

Perfect.

He shuffled toward the bar, careful to avoid direct eye contact with anyone while keeping everyone in his peripheral vision. A lifetime of practice made it second nature to catalog details—the tension patterns in the room, alliances and rivalries evident in seating arrangements, the subtle hierarchy revealed through body language.

Information was currency, and Cade was silently minting coins with every observation.

The barkeep, a mountain of a man with arms like tree trunks and a beard that could house small wildlife, acknowledged him with the barest nod. “What’ll it be?”

“Whatever’s cheapest,” Cade replied, his voice pitched higher than normal, the cadence altered to suggest a man perpetually on the verge of apology. “Been a hard road, sir. Very hard indeed.”

“Isn’t it always,” the barkeep grunted, sliding a mug of something that smelled vaguely alcoholic across the scarred wooden surface. “Three copper.”

Cade fumbled with a small pouch, making a show of counting out each coin with pathetic care. “Thank you kindly, sir. Much appreciated.”

But the barkeep had already moved on, attention captured by more profitable customers.

Too easy, Cade thought.

The not-so-poor merchant took his drink and found a wobbly stool in the corner, positioning himself at the perfect angle to observe both Nora’s table and the main entrance while appearing to stare dejectedly into his mug.

Oh gods, the young thief swore when he saw Orro stride into the tavern. This is even worse than Scorn’s Temple.

The assassin shoved his way into the packed inn. His transformation into a mute mercenary consisted primarily of looking even more intimidating than usual, but that wasn’t the worst part. In fact, that was forgivable even. But what drew Cade’s gaze was not.

Orro was wearing an eye patch.

The Grassblade exile stalked to the opposite end of the bar, ordered with a series of grunts and hand gestures, and then positioned himself with his back to the wall, glowering indiscriminately at the room.

Subtle, Cade thought with a bemused shake of his head. Very subtle.

He turned his attention to Nora’s table, where the former paladin was now engaged in animated conversation with the Arbiter veterans, her accent flowing thick as honey and twice as sweet. Evie played her part perfectly, gaze downcast except for occasional nervous glances at the armored figures surrounding them. Chubbs, in a miracle of miracles, was quietly coloring on a scrap of parchment, tongue caught between her teeth in concentration.

Time to listen.

Cade had perfected the art of eavesdropping decades ago, a skill that required not just good hearing but the ability to filter and prioritize information in real-time. He focused on Nora’s conversation, tuning out the surrounding tavern cacophony with practiced ease.

“—just don’t know if the northern route is safe for a woman and child,” Nora was saying, her transformed voice carrying the musical lilt of Cleft Hill’s distinctive cadence. “My sister’s expectin’ us in Westmire ‘fore the moon turns, but folks been talkin’ ‘bout monsters on the road.”

The oldest Arbiter, a white-haired veteran with a face like weathered granite, nodded sympathetically. “You’re right to be cautious, ma’am. The northern routes ain’t what they were. Too close to the quarantine zone.”

“Quarantine?” Evie pushed, her voice pitched to suggest innocent curiosity mingled with appropriate fear. “Is there sickness?”

The three Arbiters exchanged glances heavy with unspoken meaning.

“Not sickness, young lass,” the second Arbiter clarified, his voice lowered despite the tavern’s noise, and so Cade strained to hear his next words. “The Blightmarshes. Many years ago, Honor crusaded against the foul beasts and magicks that spread from there. Using his divine rights, he quarantined it off with his power. But…” He trailed off, glancing at his companions.

“Lately the boundary’s been weaker,” the third Arbiter finished, a stocky woman with a scar bisecting her left eyebrow. “More breaches. More... things... getting through. If Honor wasn’t so busy putting out fires from anarchists surrounding Elysia now that the goddess Life has abandoned her post, he would be able to restore the great array around the marshes. Alas, it is our privilege and duty to stave off those vile things until he returns.”

“What kind of things?” Nora pressed, her expression a masterpiece of motherly concern.

Another loaded glance between the Arbiters. The oldest leaned forward slightly.

“The kind that used to be human,” he said gravely. “The mist changes people. Animals too. Warps them into something else. Not undead—we know how to handle walking corpses. These are... different.”

“Abominations,” the female Arbiter added. “Honor save us all.”

“Honor protect us,” the other two echoed automatically, making the sign of the balanced scales across their chests.

Cade watched the exchange with keen interest, noting the subtle shifts in their expressions when discussing the mist. These weren’t just frightened civilians repeating rumors—these were hardened veterans genuinely disturbed by what they’d witnessed.

“Is there no safe passage at all?” Evie asked, her lower lip trembling perfectly on cue.

“The main road,” the female Arbiter assured her. “Heavy patrols, regular sweeps. You’ll be fine so long as you stay on the marked path and travel during daylight.”

“What about them there outposts?” Nora inquired. “Heard tell there’s Arbiter stations all along the boundary. Wouldn’t that be safer, travelin’ post to post?”

Cade silently applauded the perfectly natural way she’d introduced the subject.

“Outposts ain’t for civilians,” the oldest Arbiter said firmly. “They’re forward positions. Defense points.”

“Then masks do work,” Chubbs announced suddenly, looking up from her drawing.

All three Arbiters froze, staring at the toddler with expressions ranging from surprise to suspicion.

“What’s that, little one?” the female Arbiter asked carefully.

Chubbs blinked innocently. “The masks. That let you breathe in the icky place. They work good?”

Time seemed to suspend itself as Cade watched the moment unfold, ready to intervene if necessary. Nora’s hand drifted casually toward her concealed blade.

Then the oldest Arbiter laughed, a rusty sound like a door hinge long unopened. “Sharp little thing, aren’t you? Been listening to the grown-ups.”

“I’m very smart,” Chubbs agreed solemnly. “Mama says so.”

“She is that,” Nora confirmed, the tension in her shoulders easing slightly. “Too smart for her own good sometimes.”

Crisis averted. Barely.

Cade filed away the information—the masks existed, and these Arbiters knew about them.

“The breathing apparatus is standard issue for boundary patrol,” the female Arbiter explained, apparently deciding there was no harm in discussing common equipment with a three-year-old. “Enchanted filters that neutralize the mist’s effects.”

“When they work,” the second Arbiter muttered.

“They work just fine,” the oldest countered sharply. “So long as they’re maintained properly.”

“Tell that to Gibson’s squad,” the second replied with a grimace. “Three of ‘em never came back from that last patrol. The fourth...” He trailed off, glancing at Chubbs.

“Had to be put down,” the female Arbiter finished quietly.

“Honor’s mercy,” Nora murmured, making the sign of the scales with just a bit too much familiarity. Cade wondered what history her old order had with the Arbiters, if any.

“Honor’s justice,” the Arbiters corrected in unison, their expressions grave.

“The masks work, civilian,” the oldest stated firmly, clearly wanting to end this line of conversation. “But they’re for Arbiters only, and they stay at the outposts when not in use. Divine equipment for divine purpose. Honor’s will made manifest.”

Cade resisted the urge to roll his eyes.

Arbiters and their religious devotion to Honor were tedious at the best of times. Still, the information was valuable—the masks were kept at the outposts when not in use, confirming Astrid’s intelligence.

“Speaking of Honor’s will,” the female Arbiter said, clearly changing the subject, “did you hear about the Lifekeepers that came through this morning? A passing patrol said they spotted Bazz of all people, mucking about with commoners.”

Cade nearly choked on his drink.

“Bazz?” Evie gasped in faux surprise the Arbiters clearly missed. “Here?”

Somehow, the siren peered through the bustling crowd and made direct eye contact with Cade. The thief shrugged, which only made the oracle roll her eyes before returning to her ruse.

The second Arbiter nodded gravely. “Word is he’s hunting the Destroyer personally. Not surprised he’s been knocked down a peg or two after his city got razed by those foul magicks. A leader should always be willin’ to do what he asks his subordinates to. Just like our heroic patron, Honor.”

All three Arbiters made the sign of their god.

Cade was impressed by the choreography.

“What’s this Destroyer look like?” Nora asked with perfectly calibrated casual curiosity. “So we know to run if we see ‘im.”

“Nobody knows for certain,” the female Arbiter replied. “They say he changes his appearance, wears faces like masks. Could be anyone.”

“Could be right in this room,” the second Arbiter added ominously, his gaze sweeping across the tavern.

For one heart-stopping moment, his eyes lingered on Cade, who maintained his dejected slouch with practiced ease while mentally calculating the fastest exit route. Then the Arbiter’s attention moved on, and Cade allowed himself a small internal sigh of relief.

“But don’t you worry,” the oldest Arbiter assured Nora, patting her hand in a grandfatherly manner that made Cade bite back a smile. If only he knew he was comforting an ex-Fateweaver who could drop him with one blow. “Between good ol’ Bazz and our outpost patrols, that creature won’t get far.”

“That’s mighty reassurin’,” Nora replied, her accent thicker than ever. “Ain’t that right, Evie-girl?”

Evie nodded enthusiastically. “Oh yes! Very reassuring indeed! Honor protect us all!”

Perhaps a bit too enthusiastic. The female Arbiter’s eyes narrowed slightly, studying Evie with renewed interest.

“Speakin’ of faces, you seem to spark an old glowflake in my head,” she said slowly. “Have we met before?”

Cade tensed, his hand casually dropping to the concealed knife at his belt. Across the room, he noticed Orro shift his weight, ready to move. Cade’s core churned, and he grimaced. Something was wrong.

Evie blinked rapidly, her eyes widening with genuine alarm. “Me? Oh, I don’t think so. I’ve never been... I mean, I wouldn’t... I’m nobody important!”

The Arbiter’s frown deepened. “No, I’ve definitely seen you somewhere. Recently, too.”

Cade’s core spiked with power and sweat started to collect over his brow. His Destruction magic was… was… recoiling. There was another energy inside of him, lashing out at his core. He clenched his gut, tunic wrinkling as he doubled over as inconspicuously as possible.

“You alright there, lad?” an elderly farmer inquired with just a modicum of concern. He nursed his pint of ale with a reverence the liquid inside didn’t deserve.

“Oh, yeah. Just fine. The past few miles on the road are just catching up with me,” Cade hissed out, his breath coming faster and faster.

What in the hells is happening to me?

“Ya look like one of d’em draugrs, ya do,” another patron to Cade’s right commented with a raised eyebrow. “Yer not goin’ to turn, are ya?”

“What? No! That’s not even how—” Cade started before he shook his head, trying to focus on Nora and Evie. “I’m fine.” He gave the two concerned tavern-goers a weak smile.

“Did you attend the Tournament of Life, young lass?” the eldest Arbiter asked Evie.

Cade looked up, his free hand clenched tightly on the bar’s chipped corner. His magic churned in a whirlpool of chaotic energies. He felt like his insides were on fire.

The moment balanced on a knife’s edge. Cade prepared to create a diversion—something loud and chaotic that would give them cover to escape. But before he could move, Evie did something completely unexpected.

She giggled. Not her usual laugh, but a high-pitched, vapid titter that immediately transformed her entire demeanor.

“Oh, are you a patron of the arts?” she gushed, clasping her hands beneath her chin. “I performed with the Whispering Willows Traveling Players last spring! ‘The Merchant’s Folly’—I played the handmaiden in act three! Only had two lines, but Mother always said I stole the scene!”

The abrupt character shift was so jarring, so completely at odds with Evie’s usual ethereal presence, that even Cade was momentarily taken aback.

The Arbiter blinked, clearly thrown off balance. “I... don’t attend the theater.”

“Oh.” Evie’s enthusiastic smile dimmed slightly. “Well, perhaps we just have one of those faces! My aunt Mildred always said I looked like everyone and no one at the same time. Isn’t that the strangest thing to say? I never understood what she meant, but Aunt Mildred was touched by the moon spirits according to Mother, which is why we don’t let her cook with sharp implements anymore after the incident with the—”

“I was mistaken,” the Arbiter interrupted hastily, now looking desperate to end the conversation. “My apologies.”

“No need to apologize! Happens all the time!” Evie said with the brightest smile he’d ever seen on a mortal. “Just last week, a man in the market thought I was his cousin’s wife’s sister from Blackvale, and I’ve never even been to Blackvale, though I hear the cheese there is simply divine—”

“Perhaps we should be going,” Nora interrupted, a hint of genuine desperation in her tone as she placed a restraining hand on Evie’s arm. “It’s getting late, and little ones need their rest.”

The Arbiters nodded quickly, clearly eager to facilitate their departure. “Safe travels, ma’am,” the oldest said, rising slightly in a gesture of respect. “Remember what we said—stick to the main road. Daylight.”

“Thank you kindly for the advice,” Nora replied, gathering Chubbs and standing. “Honor protect you all.”

“Honor guide your path,” they responded automatically.

As Nora herded Evie toward the door, Chubbs offered a final observation that carried clearly through a momentary lull in the tavern’s noise.

“The funny man with the broken nose should stop staring at us,” she announced, pointing directly at a figure seated in the shadows near the back door.

Every head turned, including Cade’s. The man in question rose quickly, his cloak falling open enough to reveal a lean figure with wandering eyes and silver streaking his hair.

“Shit,” Cade muttered, dropping all pretense of his disguise as he stood. His core still writhed with unruly powers, but he’d managed to gain a semblance of control over his faculties.

The spy—Nash, if Cade recalled correctly—locked eyes with him for one frozen moment of mutual recognition. Then he bolted for the back door.

The game had just acquired a new piece, and Cade didn’t like it one bit.
