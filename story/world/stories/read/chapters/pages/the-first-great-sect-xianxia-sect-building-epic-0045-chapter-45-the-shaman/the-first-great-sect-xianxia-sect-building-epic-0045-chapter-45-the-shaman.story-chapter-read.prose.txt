Her head ached enough for her to doubt the sight before her.

Hua forced her eyes fully open, enduring the painful brightness of dappled sunlight and glimmering Qi in the air. Hua had forgotten to purge the alcohol in her blood, too distracted with music and laughter and a sense of comradery she had never known except with Qing—and that was deeper, much deeper than friendship, further than the breadth of any other love Hua held—and never known was possible to have with any other. So, she had enjoyed her wine and suffered a debilitating headache that had yet to fade.

The ache of it came full force now as though her head were a piece of metal in need of shaping by a hammer, and perhaps she needed a new shape for her head as a relic of an older age forced its way to the present. Walking toward them, unheeding of bristling Liao soldiers, was a shaman.

A Shaman. Standing before her instead of the Ten Kings of Hell. If there was a stereotype for shamans, it was fulfilled down to the smallest trinket metal anklet. Hua had seen paintings of shamans dressed like this. The fox fur formed a hat. Layered robes in stark black trailed behind the shaman. Rough material made up the thread, but it had been stitched with great care and, as Hua’s golden eyes took in more information, she could tell that what she had initially taken for black was a shade of purple that only looked that way under the sun.

Bandit armies and shamans, all within striking distance of the Liao stronghold. This was too much. Hua’s stomach rebelled at the idea. Roiling nausea forced her eyes shut. Had her leaning closer to her cousin who made a sound that might have been a warning, might have been admonishment. Hua just wanted to turn her head and hide her forehead in his chest.

Hua was too hungover for this.

“Shaman Yan,” greeted the lesioned mortal, his expression brightening. A plant in the undergrowth sought the sun with less enthusiasm than this mortal did the shaman.

The supposed shaman met the mortal halfway and embraced him. Well, he embraced her, engulfed her as she was both short and slight, her staff giving the impression of height and heft. They clung to each other in a desperate hold that spoke of uncertainty. Hua recalled the warmth of Weiji’s embrace when they saw each other in the clan compound after they had been separated again.

They pulled away and the shaman looked up at the mortal.

“Your wounds got worse,” she said with a rough voice, holding his cheek with kindness. She said a word, a phrase, that Hua could not understand. Something like Qi infused that touch. Hua forced herself to pay attention through her hangover. “Needing your forgiveness for the task I gave you. Wish it hadn’t come to it.”

“Nothing to forgive. You sent us at the perfect time. I should be asking your forgiveness, giving you my sorrys for doubting. We found help earlier than I ever imagined.”

He gestured to them, then. As though they were the supplicants who needed to be introduced to a lord. Weiji’s Qi crackled. The subtle hum of it was soothing. Hua forced herself to her full height before Weiji killed the shaman and stole any potential entertainment Hua in the future would have from her.

“Wasn’t your cult slaughtered to the man after the last purge?” Weiji asked, staring at the yellow streamers, the exact same shade as the Yellow Caps her father had faced in the last war. The war that made his legend and made great the name Liao.

The shaman stamped her staff. It was far taller than her. The shaft was connected to a ring of wrought iron which in turn was the first in a series of three. Each ring had a different streamer of cloth attached to it: white for mourning, black that might have been water or yin, and a yellow so bold it could only be suicidal.

But that was less important than the mortal. His wounds were not as prominently red and wet, dried up and gentled. As though they had healed a few days in the span of moments. Hua and Weiji shared a look. Not as powerful as Zhao’s healers could work but it spoke of old truths. In the histories they learnt, shamans could supposedly heal many at once. You had to be certain a Yellow Cap was dead, or he might be healed soon afterwards.

Hua frowned at the staff and the shaman who carried the teachings of a dead people. “Clearly not if she’s still alive. What possesses you to carry such an obvious sign of your treason?”

“Got a few things more important than a kingfisher’s view of treason,” the shaman said, still not looking at them. “You worth more than treason, old friend. Later, when we don’t got angry lightning on our heels, you can tell me all the things you seen. We will speak later,” she told the lesioned mortal. And then, with barely a glance spared in their direction, told them, “Come, let us speak quiet and discuss the pile of shit you’ve left in the west.”

“Weiji,” she warned before lightning could flash and strike the shaman down. Speaking hurt. Why did speaking hurt? “Captain Yao, I’ll leave the rest to you.”

They followed the shaman through the tent city. The mortals were in a bad state, but they had banded together. Worked to build and form community again. They managed to do, in a place so remote and under threat, what Hua’s home failed without the encouragement of her violence.

Was it the bandits and loss that made them work together? Did she need to sponsor bandits to menace her home?

Such thoughts she would entertain later, beneath the protective formations of her home, and not entering the tent a shaman enacted their strange evils. It reminded her of Old Ren’s pavilion. The same smell of pungent herbal remedies, sweat, sick and bad news. Terracotta red and living green were the colours she saw first. Plants growing in pots, mushrooms growing from logs in reds and wintery grey. Beneath the tall tent grew enough herbs and plants to poison an army. Or perhaps to heal one if one were foolish enough to believe in anything good coming from a shaman.

Under the protective canopy of her tent in a tent city, the shaman Yan removed her fur hat with flaps that obscured her features.

Wu Yan was not a tall woman. This, Hua had known. She had not expected such youth. The pale marks lining her face only accentuated that youth. Barely older than Weiji. She did not know what those marks represented. Illness, perhaps, or some strange religious marking that only a Shaman understood. With eyes and hair dark as midnight, and a deep tan, she looked the part of every villager of no important lineage. Beyond, of course, the black painting her lips and teeth and tongue, creating a cavern, pit, void.

“May this lord know what method of death you seek for your treason?”

“Wu Yan greets Liao,” said Shaman Yan rudely, ignoring entirely the question Hua asked. Mortals had a strange propensity for doing that. “Only took Liao a couple years of pillage and rape for Liao to visit their lands. Thanking you, Lord Liao, for finally heeding our call after years of silence. Truly, the Amber Sea ain’t never seen safer hands.”

“This lord acts where necessary,” Hua said, extending her arm and stopping Weiji before he ripped the shaman’s head off. A wave of nausea hit her. She hesitated, just keeping her gorge down. “And this lord does not come at the bidding of mortals without the strength to plead their case.”

Weiji pushed her arm down but kept a light grip on her bicep. Wu Yan glanced where they made contact and her lips curled in distaste.

“Too busy on noble nonsense,” Shaman Yan pronounced, the words blades that entirely missed Hua, but they struck Weiji, his grip tightening around her. “We moved our camp once a survivor from a village a couple day’s walk stumbled on us. A hunter and fur trader. He was starved, beaten, and nearly dead. He died soon after. If we hadn’t had people who had traded with them, we would never have known who he was.”

If there was a point to her monologue, Hua had missed it.

There was a dying mortal on the shaman’s examination bench who Hua assumed was the hunter. He was dying. That much could not be denied. A hunter with the same lesions as the earlier mortal who had found her. A sacrifice, Hua summarised. If it was a communicable disease, then sending out people with a low chance of survival, who might spread it—I need to oversee the camp!

“How quickly does this disease spread?” she asked.

Weiji was slower on the uptake. “Shit.”

Wu Yan gave them a look that spoke of her profound despair to have encountered nobility uneducated in her field who could and would determine the funding she would receive. It was an expression that morphed quickly into one of acceptance.

“Ain’t no disease, this,” Wu Yan explained slowly, in her rude syntax. Then, she spoke a shameless lie. “Demon’s curse that hit him.”

“Demons do not exist. They were all wiped out.”

“Not gonna argue with folks blinded by their own glory they can’t tell the sun from an asshole,” she told Weiji who was showing rage and personal affront befitting their family.

“Do you know who you speak to, Shaman? Do you have the vaguest idea who you insult? Before you stands Lord Liao, your liege, and you will show him respect.”

“I don’t care even a little bit. Got people to heal. You either help or you kill me now. Don’t be wasting my time. Don’t got enough of it.”

Hua felt like she would be losing if she did either.

“What reason do you have to believe we can help with a demon curse? You disdain us enough.”

“You have Qi,” she said, again, like they were fools.

“You want our divine lightning!”

Hua had to grab Weiji before he killed the Shaman. That was the problem with loyalty, even insults could be considered treason, and Weiji was loyal to Liao. He embodied their ancestral memory of a war long ended yet only a generation removed.

In truth, Hua was utterly bewildered to be holding someone else back from inflicting violence.

“I want your Qi, you utter floorboard of an ox!”

Hua let go of Weiji's wrist, struck dumb by the insult. It was so far beyond her limit of insults. Beyond Weiji’s for he was just as still and bewildered, mouth agape at being called a floorboard and an ox. Hua mouthed the insult, trying to add it to her repertoire, and failing completely.

“If there’s any space in your heads beyond thinking about fucking all the kingfishers you sew in your clothes, then listen to me. I got people to save, people to heal, and Qi is what I need. You and yours had best make yourselves useful or get the fuck out of my camp.”

“You are mad if you think we’ll give an enemy our Qi. For all we know, you were the one who cursed them.”

“That’s as accurate as thinking Liao ever stood sentinel.”

“You dare!” Weiji roared.

Hua’s eyes widened. “What did you say?”

“It’s what your people shouted as you slaughtered mine, isn’t it? Your ancestors put a lot of people they called enemy to the sword, people who ain’t done nothing worse than wanting to not be on their knees. If you actually care that much about shit like legacy and honour, then take that sword out your ass and raise it n defence of your people.”

“You would dare call me a coward,” Hua hissed, fury rising in her. The force of it made her head throb hasher. “Give me one reason I shouldn’t kill you for your impertinence.”

“I will give you many,” the Shaman countered, “if you are willing to follow. I’ll show you the ruin you left behind, false sentinel.”
