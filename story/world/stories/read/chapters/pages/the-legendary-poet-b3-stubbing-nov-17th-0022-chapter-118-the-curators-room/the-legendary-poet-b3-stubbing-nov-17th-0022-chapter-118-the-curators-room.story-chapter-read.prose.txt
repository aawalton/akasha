The silence held a beat longer than it should have.

Kage felt it the way he sometimes felt a missed cue in a dungeon. Just off. The fungi pulsed above them on their deliberately-arrhythmic schedule, the dark pool at the chamber's center kept whatever water it had decided to be, and the party was looking at him with the polite blank attention of people who had been doing something else two seconds ago and were now committed to pretending they had not.

Huh, he thought. He ignored it. He did not have the bandwidth for it right now.

"Lobby," he said. "Regroup. We figure out what the keys do somewhere with more floor."

The party moved. They moved with the slightly-too-deliberate motion of a group that had just remembered they were a group, and Kage watched Jax in particular adjust his shoulder rig with the kind of vigor reserved for a man who needed an excuse to look down.

Weird, he thought, and slotted that, and let it be weird.

The corridor up out of the hollow took less time than the corridor down had. The dungeon, having gotten its boss out of him, was no longer interested in stretching its own geometry against his patience. The Buried Refrain that had earlier been tunneling alongside them did not surface. The roots had stopped pulsing entirely. The dungeon was, by every available signal, satisfied.

Kage walked at the back. From the back he could see all of them, and from the back he was harder to look at directly.

Lily kept glancing over her shoulder. Not at him exactly. He noticed. He did not comment.

Ren walked at the front, beside Valdrias, who had become Ren's preferred walking companion for reasons Kage suspected had nothing to do with Valdrias and everything to do with the fact that Valdrias did not ask questions. Ren's hands were folded behind his back. His level icon now read 25.

Kage caught that and felt something he did not entirely like.

He's twenty-five, Kage thought. He's at the choice.

Ren had not opened the selection panel. He was walking, and thinking, and his face looked like the choice was hard and not something to be done hastily. Kage respected that. Kage also, privately, hoped Ren took his time about it.

The lobby opened around them the way it always did.

Obsidian platform. Void. Frames. Crumpled paper moons turning their slow rotations overhead, casting their long shaped shadows down across the floor.

"Okay," Jax said, mostly to break his own silence. "Okay. We have the thing. The five thing. The key."

"Fragments," Zara corrected. "Five fragments. Of one key. We don't have a key. We have a key in pieces. There is, presumably, a slot."

"There is presumably a slot, yes, thank you."

"You're welcome."

Julie had drifted to the edge of the platform. She stood with one hand resting on the geometric ink at her opposite wrist, and her head was tilted up at the moons in a way that suggested she was reading them, or trying to. Vami was beside her. Vami was not looking up. Vami was looking at the drifting frames, and his face had the expression it got when his brain was solving a problem one layer below where his voice operated.

"Each one was a thing," Vami said.

Lily turned. "Each frame?"

"Each frame. A draft of." Vami waved a gloved hand vaguely. "Storm and water. Fire and earth. Wind and glasses. Grass and sun. Root and hollow. Each one is a thing."

"They're elements," Zherdev offered. "Like. Pieces. Of a place."

"Pieces of a world," Julie said, without taking her eyes off the moons.

Kage felt his attention sharpen.

It was the Operator, recognizing the shape of a system he had been walking around inside of without seeing. Five drafts. Each a fundamental component. Storm. Earth. Wind. Grass. Roots. Not five dungeons, exactly; the naming was specific. Five drafts, the way an artist drafted parts of a single composition before assembling them.

A world, he thought. He looked up at the moons.

He had not, until this moment, really looked at the moons. They had been part of the décor. The crumpled paper moons of the unfinished archive, hanging in their void, doing their slow celestial rotations. He had cataloged them on entry as atmospheric and then ignored them, because they had not threatened him, and unthreatening things did not earn his attention.

He looked now.

The wrinkles in the paper, this close to the lobby, caught the sourceless light at angles, and at those angles, on the few facets that lay near enough to the platform to read, there was writing. Tiny. Tightly packed. The kind of script that filled a margin when the person doing the writing had more to say than the page would politely accept.

Huh, he thought, again, and this time the huh had teeth in it.

"Hey," Finn said. He had perched on the platform's edge, hawk back on his arm, eyes upward. "Are those. Are those covered in writing? Like, all the way?"

"All the way," Julie confirmed.

"Cool," Finn said, the way Finn said cool when something had just gotten worse.

"We've been doing this wrong," Zara was the one who said it. She had walked back from the edge and was standing near the center of the platform, arms crossed, mouth a tight line. The frost on her hems caught the false light and did the cold sparkle thing it did when Zara was annoyed by something her own brain was suggesting.

Kage turned his head toward her. "Explain."

"We followed the rare marks. The droplets pointed at five frames. We entered those five frames. Got the five fragments. Standard dungeon logic. The frames pointed at by the keys are the relevant frames. Everything else is decoration." She gestured at the void. The frames. The slow drift of unrelated paintings through the black. "There are. I have not counted. Many more frames than five out here. Significantly more."

"Sure."

"What if the other ones aren't decoration?"

Kage breathed in once, slowly. "So, what if we walk into one not pointed at by the keys, yes?"

"That's what I'm asking." Zara nodded.

"We can try, sure" Kage said. "After figuring out the fragments."

Kage looked up again, at the moons, at the writing crawling across their crumpled surfaces in a script too small for him to parse at this distance.

His hand drifted, of its own accord, to the inventory marker where the five fragments sat humming their quiet synchronized hum against each other.

"Stand back," he said. The party stood back as he pulled the fragments.

They came into his hand in five points of light, one after another, in the order he had acquired them. Storm. Fire. Wind. Grass. Root. Each one warmer than the last. Each one a small shaped piece of something that did not, in his palm, look like part of a key.

He held them out flat on his palm. He did not know what to do next.

He did not have to.

The first fragment lifted off his palm by itself.

It rose, slowly, to about eye height, and stopped. The second fragment lifted next. The third. The fourth. The fifth. They arranged themselves in the air in front of him with the small patient intelligence of pieces that knew, at last, where they were going. Each one slotted toward the others on edges that had been waiting for these specific edges.

Click.

The five fragments locked together into a single object the size of his palm. A circular wax seal, red shading into black where the script crawled across its surface, an emblem at its center. The seal hovered.

Then it rose.

It rose slowly at first, almost like a balloon rose when no one was holding the string. It cleared the party's heads. It cleared the platform. It climbed steadily into the void above the obsidian, growing as it climbed, the way an object did not grow but in this place apparently did, expanding outward as it ascended until it was no longer the size of his palm but the size of a shield, and then the size of a door, and then the size of a frame, and still rising.

"Oh," Lily said softly.

The seal rose past the lowest of the crumpled paper moons. The moon unfolded.

It was not a fast unfolding. The wrinkled paper began, at one corner, to smooth itself out, as if a slow careful hand were pressing the crumples open from the inside. The other corners followed. The folds released. Within a few seconds, the moon was no longer a crumpled ball. It was a vast flat sheet of paper, easily the size of a continent at the scale Kage's eye was now refusing to process, hanging in the void above them.

And on its surface, in script suddenly large enough to read, was a poem about thunder. Kage felt his breath catch.

He could read it. Not all of it. The text was archaic and dense and went on for what looked like several thousand stanzas, but the first few lines were clear, and they were about thunder. Specifically. About what thunder was, and what it did, and why a sky needed thunder to be a sky.

The next moon unfolded.

This one was about water. About the particular gravity-friendly behavior of water, and the specific patience with which water consented to be shaped, and the relationship between water and the surfaces it touched. The lines went on. The poem was enormous. It described, at length, the specific way water remembered the cup it had been in.

The next moon. Grass. Hundreds of stanzas, describing the specific obligation grass had to bend in wind, and the agreement grass had reached with the sun.

The next. Stone.

The next. Bone.

The moons opened in a slow cascade, one after the next, across the entire bowl of what Kage had been calling sky, and each one was a poem, and each poem was about a single thing, and the things were everything. Light. Shadow. Heat. Cold. Salt. Iron. The angle at which a leaf attached to a stem. The exact mathematics by which a wave decided where to crest. The flavor of rain when it had touched dust. The reason a fire wanted to be tall.

A world, Kage thought.

Somebody had written a world, line by line, poem by poem, draft by draft, and discarded it, and the discarded drafts were moons here, the way a writer crumpled paper and threw it across a room, except this writer's room was a void, and the crumpled paper was big enough to be celestial bodies.

Oh, he thought. Oh, well, that's what this place is.

The Operator, briefly, had nothing to say.

The seal kept rising.

It had grown, on its long ascent, to a size that defied the geometry of the void. It was, at the height it had now reached, the size of one of the unfolded moons. It hung above the lobby, vast and red, and the script across its face was, Kage realized, the same script that lived on the back of his own hand.

The seal moved.

It moved laterally, toward the nearest unfolded moon, and it set itself against the moon's surface, gently, and pressed.

When it lifted away, the poem was gone.

The text on the moon, the thousand stanzas of thunder, swirled. The letters detached themselves from the paper. They lifted off the surface in slow black streams, the way ink lifted when water touched the page, and they flowed inward through the void, toward the center of the sky above the platform.

The black ink converged. It did not splash. It did not pool. It arrived at a point in the air above the lobby and began, with the patience of something being drawn, to outline.

Kage saw a hinge. He saw an arch. He saw the long vertical line of a doorframe, and then the second long vertical line, and then the crossbeam, and the suggestion of an upper lintel.

The seal lifted off the first moon and moved to the second.

Water. Pressed. The stanzas swirled. Black ink flowed inward, joined the outline above the platform, filled in detail. The doorframe gained a panel. A line of script ran down its center.

The seal moved to the third moon. Grass. Pressed. The lines swirled, flowed, arrived, added themselves. The door gained a handle.

It went on.

The party stood, on the obsidian platform, with their heads tilted up, and watched the sky un-write itself one poem at a time. Each moon, after being stamped, hung silent and blank above them, its long-completed verses converted back into raw ink, the ink contributing itself to the door taking shape in the sky.

Kage counted, automatically, the way he counted everything.

There were a lot of moons.

He stopped counting at thirty. The cascade kept going. The moons opened and were stamped and emptied, opened and were stamped and emptied, and the door above the platform grew more detailed, more specific, more real, line by line, ink-stroke by ink-stroke, as the entire ceiling of the archive donated its written world to the construction of a single passage.

"This is," Zherdev said softly, "the sickest thing I have ever seen."

For once, no one disagreed with him.

Jax had his mouth slightly open. Zara was, for the first time since Kage had met her, looking completely mesmerized. Finn's hawk had landed on the platform and folded its wings and was watching the sky the way Finn was watching the sky. Lily had a hand pressed flat over her chest. Julie was tracking the ink flow with the focused attention of an artist watching another artist draw, and Vami was watching Julie watch.

Ren kept his eyes on the seal as it pressed the next moon, and the next, and the next.

The last moon went pale.

The seal hung in the void for a long moment after the final stamp. Then, having done its work, it shrank. It contracted back down through its growth, smoothly, retreating in size as it descended toward the platform, and by the time it reached his hand it was the size of his palm again, and red and black and warm.

He pocketed it.

Above them, the door was complete.

It hung in the sky, fully drawn, the size of an entrance, suspended in the air where once there had been crumpled moons and now there were only blank white sheets, drifting empty above the void.

A panel opened in Kage's vision.

[The Curator's Room has opened.]

He read it. He felt, for some reason, the small specific weight of the word Curator and the small specific weight of the word Room, and the part of his brain that had once admired sentences noted, against its will, that Room was a smaller word than Chamber or Sanctum or Vault, and the smallness of it was deliberate.

The rare marks on Vami, Ren, and Zherdev, almost in unison, repointed.

Three glowing droplets. One vector. Up. Toward the door.

"Up," Vami said, with a small dry smile that was working hard to be a smile and not something else. "We're going up."

Kage looked at the party, at the nine faces tilted toward the sky and the new door waiting in it, at Lily's open hand and Julie's quiet calculation and Jax's loud silence and Zara's frost-threaded hem catching the light from a sky that no longer had any text in it.

"Yeah," he said. "We're going up."

The door above them pulsed, once, slowly, like a thing taking its first breath after a long sleep.
