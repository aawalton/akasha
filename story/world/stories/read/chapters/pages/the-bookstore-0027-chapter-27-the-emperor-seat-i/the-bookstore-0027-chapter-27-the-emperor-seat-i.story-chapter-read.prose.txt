A flower-carved carriage raced east along the stone road.

If the clatter of iron-shod hooves was not enough to warn slower travelers aside, the gold inlay flashing in the sun usually was. Inside, Asha leaned against the open latch and watched the landscape change. It was far preferable to listening to her mother’s attempts at conversation.

She had traveled this road many times before. This was simply the first time she had noticed how well maintained it was.

The realization drew a small curl of scorn to her lips before she smothered it. Roads to the Capital were always maintained. There was nothing strange about that. Yet Asha could not help thinking that the path to the bookstore and the forest mattered more to Mountainend’s future than this polished route east ever would.

That, in her opinion, was where life was actually beginning.

Across from her, Ailen had been watching her daughter for most of the trip without appearing to do so. Asha was usually difficult, but she had never before refused to accompany her mother so coldly, nor passed days in such sustained silence. When Ailen first learned that Asha had been spending time at the bookstore, she had allowed herself a brief hope that the girl was finally coming to terms with her natural path. Instead, the opposite seemed to have happened. Asha had grown more stubborn, not less.

Still, Ailen was not foolish enough to demand answers from the storeowner. If Anne knew anything, it could wait. Asha always spoke eventually. She complained, argued, deflected, disappeared for a while, and then returned with the truth in some form or another. That had always been her way.

This time the summons had come too quickly to allow patience. Ailen had taken her daughter with almost no warning because blood ties mattered too much in places like the Seat. Certain people expected their presence. If they failed to appear, those same people might well come looking for them, and that would be worse.

So Ailen could only hope that Asha’s temper would cool before they entered the Clows’ reach.

Unfortunately, it did not.

A week passed on the road. They traveled day and night, stopping only once in the Province Capital so the drivers could properly rest. Asha remained largely silent through all of it, but that did not mean the journey had gone unused.

Quite the opposite. Outside the carriage, the lands changed from the harsher, more withered stretches near Mountainend into greener heights and, eventually, the richer lands that fed the Seat. The farther they traveled, the stronger Asha’s gift became.

Her mother, had she known, would likely have taken it as proof that nature was rewarding proper obedience. The thought almost made Asha laugh.

This had nothing to do with obedience. For the first time in years, perhaps ever, she allowed her anthomancy to spread freely through the land around her.

She had not spent the entire journey just ignoring her mother. She was learning to feel beyond herself, to loosen the hard shell of her own mind and let the living world brush against it without restraint. Mountainend had become too bare, broken, recently wounded by change for her gift to bloom there in full. And on earlier trips east, fear had always kept her power tight and hidden. She had never allowed herself to reach.

Now she did. The trees in the green mountains had felt sturdy and taut, their roots clutching rock and soil with stubborn strength against wind and erosion. The plains nearer the Seat felt different. Far reaching and fresh. Friendlier in one sense, yet hurried in another. The crops and managed growth there carried the pressure of care, use, harvest, and replacement. They did not struggle for survival the way mountain growth did, but neither would they live freely or long.

Asha absorbed all of it.

And when the giant stone walls of the Seat finally appeared, she reluctantly drew her inner reach back.

They had entered Clow territory now. Here, depth was dangerous.

The carriage stopped at the gate for inspection, though not for long. Their family name and standing bought them a swifter passage than most, which only annoyed Asha further. Once they rolled through, the Seat opened around them.

It was unsettling. Anyone who had seen a provincial capital might expect the greatest city in the nation to be larger, richer, louder, and dirtier. Instead, the first shock was its cleanliness. There were no slums pressing visibly against the outer districts, no beggars huddled in public sight, no overflowing filth or common disorder. Asha did not miss such things, of course. No sane person would. But their absence felt less like prosperity and more like a decree.

The Clows would not permit the lesser where they ruled directly. They would not permit disorder either, nor any visible reminder that ordinary people were messy creatures with needs that offended polished stone and noble sensibilities. It was their home, and everything in it reflected that truth. No one in their right mind challenged the Clows in the Seat. The city might as well have been called Clow’s Seat instead of Emperor Seat, because the Emperor was a Clow and always would be.

The carriage rolled deeper into the city. The districts passed in ordered succession, the buildings neat and proportioned in ways that pleased the eye too perfectly to feel natural. Even wealth here seemed regulated into acceptable shapes. Asha spotted knights in Clow colors and several other noble carriages marked with family insignia she recognized at once and some she did not care to.

Then the castle came into view. It stood beyond a great lake, separated from the city by water and bridge as though even the Seat itself were not permitted to touch it directly. Asha felt her breath shorten the moment she saw the walls.

Neuromantic symbols had been worked openly into the castle’s surface. They were not hidden. That was the point. The Clows wanted every visitor to see them, to feel that they had crossed into a place where the ruling family’s power defined the air itself. Whether the dread those symbols caused came from true magical pressure or only from generations of fear hardly mattered. The effect was the same.

As the carriage rolled onto the bridge, Asha forced her face still. She would not give them more than they were already owed.

When they arrived, servants helped mother and daughter down from the carriage. They were treated with perfect respect and no sign at all that such respect implied equality. That too was done on purpose. In the Seat, a visiting countess remained a visitor.

They were escorted to separate rooms on the second level to wash and prepare before the formal meeting.

Asha took her time with the bath.

She had no desire at all to dress herself up for the judgment or amusement of two groups she neither trusted nor cared for, but she was not foolish to risk offense carelessly. Attention in this place was rarely a gift. So she soaked just long enough to ease the stiffness of the road from her body, then rose before delay could be mistaken for insult.

As expected, her mother had already arranged everything else. A silver dress with blue lace waited for her in the hands of a servant, along with fine elven adornments selected too carefully to be accidental. Asha almost laughed when she saw the golden flower ear clip and the sapphire necklace.

So much for subtlety. Ailen was sending messages again. To the elven court, perhaps. To old allies. To old enemies. Possibly to both at once. The woman did enjoy speaking through clothes and jewels whenever direct words became dangerous.

Well, if the Clows had tolerated it before, they would tolerate it now. Or they would not. Either way, the choice had already been made.

When Asha stepped back into the corridor, she found her mother waiting outside. Ailen wore light blue and similar adornments, elegant as ever and far too composed for someone willingly walking into the Clows’ private theater. Mother and daughter exchanged a brief glance and the smallest of nods, but neither spoke. In the castle, words needed to be measured before they were breathed.

They followed their escort upward to the fourth floor. The corridors grew grander the higher they climbed, though not softer. Nothing in the castle was soft. The carved stone, the polished wood, the precise arrangements of servants and guards—everything announced control.

At last they reached the meeting hall. A beautifully carved arch framed the entrance. Asha and Ailen stopped before it and waited in silence while their presence was announced.

“The Countess Ailen Treefehl and Lady Asha Treefehl.”
