Far to the south. Beyond the active front.

The Ael'vari built their cities on the bones of the conquered.

A long-held tradition.

On their homeworld, when the Ael'vari took a rival's stronghold, they raised their own towers through the wreckage. Stone grown over stone. Root wound through foundation. The conquered towns became the skeleton, and the conqueror's work became the living flesh around it.

They had done it across a hundred worlds now.

The capital of the northern Ael'vari territories rose from the corpse of the largest human city they had taken in the first century after they started conquering. The old towers still stood — steel frames stripped of glass, concrete husks gutted of everything. Parking garages had become terraced gardens. Office buildings had become barracks.

The Ael'vari went beyond just occupying the ruins. They had grown through them.

Living stone climbed the old steel in spiraling columns, smooth as poured glass, veined with luminous mana channels that shifted color with the time of day. Bridges of shaped wood connected tower to tower at heights that would have killed anything that fell. The wood was not cut or joined. It had been persuaded. Grown from seed to span in a matter of weeks by shapers who could coax a sapling through a hundred years of growth between sunrise and noon.

The streets below were corridors of shaped earth, smooth and pale, channeled between root walls that rose to twice a soldier's height. Water moved through carved stone gutters in constant, silent flow. Mana-glow drifted in fist-sized spheres above every junction, their glow steady and cold and the color of old silver.

Every visiting officer from the southern theaters saw it immediately. The old civilization's towers still stood because the Ael'vari wanted them to stand. They were trophies.

At the city's center, where the old world had built a broad plaza of cracked concrete and dead fountains, the Ael'vari had raised the Palace of Conquest.

The military academies along the eastern ridge were taller. The shaping halls that trained new generations of direct-casters covered more ground.

The Palace of Conquest was the oldest.

Its walls were pale stone shaped without seams, rising in smooth curves to a roof that did not exist. The ceiling was open sky, framed by inward-leaning pillars carved with the campaign histories of every territory the Ael'vari had claimed since their arrival on this world. Rain did not enter. Wind did not disturb the interior. A ward as old as the building itself held the weather at bay above the pillar line, so that those inside stood beneath open sky without feeling its touch.

Inside, the floor descended in shallow tiers toward a central platform of dark polished stone.

Vaelith stood on the fourth tier and waited.

He wore campaign dress. A dark coat cut close to the body, fastened with dull silver clasps from throat to waist. Its cut identified him as an officer who had held field command.

He kept his hands behind his back.

Around him, the tiers held perhaps two hundred Ael'vari. Most wore formal court robes, pale fabric layered in the traditional style, sleeves long enough to brush the floor. Hair bound in silver, pale gold, white.

Vaelith's hair was dark. Almost black.

Among the Ael'vari, dark hair meant youth. It meant you had not yet lived long enough for the pigment to surrender to the centuries.

Vaelith was forty-three.

By the standards of his people, he was barely an adult.

He had also just destroyed a Vireth root-network that had resisted six previous commanders over the course of two hundred years.

The campaign had lasted eleven months. He had burned through three forward operating positions, lost four hundred soldiers, and driven a shaping assault so deep into the Vireth's root structure that his team had found the core mass, buried beneath forty meters of living wood and soil, its consciousness distributed across a network of roots that stretched from the mountains to the coast.

It had taken the kind of operational thinking that did not emerge from privilege or instruction.

The southern boundary of Ael'vari territory had moved eighty leagues inside Vireth territory.

No one under fifty had achieved that since the first attack on this planet.

The faces around him weren't proud of that.

Some of them looked envious. Others wore expressions of carefully maintained neutrality.

A pair of senior officers on the tier above him spoke quietly without looking in his direction. One of them wore the triple-chain insignia of a Vireth theater command. She had held that post for ninety years. Well-formatted reports. A spotless record of not losing ground.

Vaelith had made her irrelevant in less than a year.

A silver-haired attendant appeared at the base of the central platform.

"Commander Vaelith," the attendant called.

He descended.

The tiers watched.

His boots struck the polished stone with precise, measured impacts. He walked slowly. Haste was vulgarity, and vulgarity was the first thing his enemies would use against him.

He reached the platform.

Two Ael'vari waited there.

Both were old. Truly old. Their hair was so pale it was nearly translucent, falling past their shoulders in straight curtains. They wore robes of deep green embroidered at the hems with thread that shimmered like spun mana. Their faces were smooth. The Ael'vari did not wrinkle. Their eyes held centuries.

The taller one held a scroll bound with dark cord.

"Vaelith of the Southern Theater," the tall elder said. "For distinguished service in the Vireth campaign. For the destruction of one of the root-networks. For the expansion of Ael'vari territory across eighty leagues of contested ground."

He extended the scroll.

Vaelith lowered his head and received it.

The scroll weighed nothing. Every commander who had received this recognition in the past century had been elevated.

Vaelith straightened.

The shorter elder spoke.

"In recognition of your talents, and in light of current strategic requirements, you are hereby appointed Commander of the Northern Territories."

The elder's expression did not change. His tone held the same formal cadence that every appointment carried.

Northern Territories meant the human front.

"I accept with gratitude," he said.

Then he descended from the platform, walked up through the tiers, and felt every eye in the Palace of Conquest follow him.

They were rejoicing. Some of them openly.

Vaelith kept walking. The Northern Territories were a grave.

The human front had not moved in the last centuries. They were dying on a timetable the Ael'vari were content to keep.

Every commander assigned to the north stagnated. Their careers froze. They managed bombardment schedules that accomplished nothing. They watched humans build increasingly elaborate defenses that would never be tested, because no one in the Ael'vari command structure believed the humans were worth the cost of a serious assault.

The last northern commander had tried to change that.

He had designed a coordinated strike. It was supposed to accelerate the collapse. Push the timetable forward by a few centuries. Give the northern command something to report.

The operation had failed.

The northern commander had been transferred. Reassigned to an administrative post so far from any active theater that his career would quietly suffocate over the next century.

Now Vaelith had his position.

Seven days later, Vaelith stood at the edge of a forward observation platform and looked north.

The human front stretched below.

Impressive, in the way desperation sometimes is.

The humans had built defenses in layers. Trenches cut into the earth in zigzag patterns, reinforced with shaped stone and crude rune-work that flickered unevenly in the morning light. Behind the trenches stood bunkers, low ugly concrete, ward-symbols drawn by hands that knew the theory better than the craft.

Beyond the bunkers, the terrain had been seeded with runic mine fields. Vaelith could sense them from here, thousands of pressure-triggered formations under the topsoil, each one containing enough compressed mana to kill anything that stepped on it.

More trenches, more bunkers, then a second line on the old-world ridgelines and river crossings.

The humans had turned the entire landscape into a mechanism designed to slow, bleed, and exhaust any force that tried to cross it.

Vaelith studied it for several minutes.

Then he turned and looked at the elven positions.

A single trench line. Shallow. Unfinished in three places where the soil had eroded and no one had repaired it. A handful of observation posts spaced at wide intervals along the ridge.

Behind the trench, the elven territory.

The humans had fortified their side with everything they possessed. The Ael'vari had built a ditch. It was enough.

Vaelith descended from the observation platform. Three officers waited at its base, standing at attention in the formal posture reserved for a new commanding officer's inspection.

The tallest was a woman with silver hair bound at the nape. She introduced herself as the senior operations officer. Beside her stood two field captains, both wearing campaign coats that showed minimal wear.

Their coats were clean because they had nothing to campaign against.

"Commander," the operations officer said. "Welcome to the Northern Theater."

Vaelith nodded.

He walked the line.

The trench was adequate for observation. If the humans ever advanced, the forward positions would fold in minutes.

But the humans would never advance.

They could barely hold what they had.

Their magic was rigid and slow. They built their spells like they built their bunkers, layered and defensive.

Vaelith had studied the intelligence summaries during his journey north. Three centuries of reports. He had read them in four days.

"Commander, do you wish to review the bombardment schedule?"

Vaelith glanced at her.

"What is the current pattern?"

"Standard rotation. Four barrages per day, distributed across the forward trench sectors. We rotate the caster teams every three weeks."

"Casualties?"

"Minimal. The humans have reinforced their bunkers to absorb the standard yield. Our barrages damage surface structures and occasionally catch exposed personnel, but the kill rate has been declining for decades." She paused. "The previous commander attempted to increase frequency and yield in his first year."

Vaelith stopped walking.

He looked north across the scarred terrain.

"And the humans?" he asked. "What do they do?"

"They rebuild. They rotate their garrison. They maintain their wards." The operations officer's tone was factual.

"Have they attempted an offensive in the last century?"

"No, Commander."

One of the field captains spoke.

"Commander, shall we increase the bombardment schedule? It is customary for new commanders to demonstrate initiative in the first—"

"No."

The captain fell silent.

Vaelith looked at the human trenches again.

Thousands of lives sheltered behind those walls.

"Maintain the current rotation," Vaelith said. "There is no value in wasting mana against them."

He turned from the observation ridge and began walking south. "In a century, or two, or five, they won't matter."

The operations officer matched his stride.

"And if they attempt something unexpected?"

Vaelith paused.

"They are humans," he said. "What could they possibly attempt?"
