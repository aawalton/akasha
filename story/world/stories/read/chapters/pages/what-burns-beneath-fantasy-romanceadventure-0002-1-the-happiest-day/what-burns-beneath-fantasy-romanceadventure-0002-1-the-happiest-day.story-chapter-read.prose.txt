My twelfth birthday was the best day of my life.

It started normally enough. Dawn greeted me with the smells of burnt bacon and freshly made oatcakes. Durst was grimly at work in the kitchen, bushy black eyebrows furrowed as he measured out a spoonful of golden honey.

He wished me a happy birthday, smearing the portion of honey onto my pewter plate.

I smiled and thanked him, dipping one too-salty cake into the rare treat.

We ate in relative silence.

Relative silence was familiar. Our home was a small one-story affair nestled in the witchwood grove encircling Fellbrook. Townsfolk often bustled about in the distance, doubtlessly making all sorts of noise, but Durst and his stone walls were quiet.

My days were spent tending to the witchwood. I don’t think the guardian grove actually needed me to care for it; the trees never lost their leaves when the weather turned cold, and during storms they always remained undisturbed.

But I knew my efforts were appreciated, nonetheless. Night-black, velvet-soft leaves always brushed my hands in appreciation and graceful silver branches stretched up to offer shade while I weeded.

When the quiet became too heavy, I would sing or talk to the empty air. The trees didn’t seem to mind that, either. Oftentimes the leaves rubbed together, rustling out whispers in response to my chatter.

I could never quite make out what they were trying to say.

A knock at the door came shortly after breakfast. Opening it revealed a broad, smiling face framed by unruly hair the color of river sand.

“Mom baked you a cake!” Royce’s blue eyes sparkled.

Before I could respond he had grasped one of my hands, and I was being unceremoniously tugged from Durst’s tranquil home.

“And I got you a present,” he added, chest swelling with pride.

I grinned, allowing myself to be pulled along the narrow dirt path. Dangling black leaves hissed softly, but not unkindly, as we passed. A few reached out, brushing at my shoulders.

I tangled my fingers into the velvet. “You didn’t have to do that.”

Royce ducked under a low branch, which ignored him. This was typical; my beautiful silver trees ignored anyone and anything besides me. “‘Course I did, Brin! You’ll love it, too! There was a merchant in town a while back, don’t know if you saw him from your hole.” He gestured behind us with an exaggerated eye-roll.

“Hey!” I swatted his arm, which earned me a grin.

“He had lots of stuff! And I’ve been doing really good about saving my coppers, you know. And I got- well, you’ll see! You’ll love it.”

Walking from the grove to the town proper was always a bit of a surreal experience. It felt like stepping into a painting; my world of silver-grays and night-blacks and mud-browns was replaced with bright green grass, burnt oranges and reds from flowers dotted along the ground, and cheerful blues and yellows from shuttered windows and brightly painted signposts.

And the sounds. It was still early, but already there were townsfolk trotting up and down the cobblestone streets, calling out greetings to each other and stopping to chat. Birds twittered happily from their perches, chickens clucked and babbled, hunting hounds bayed their greetings, and I even heard the distant bray of a donkey.

It didn’t take long to reach Royce’s house. Its wooden door was propped open and the enticing smell of fresh bread wafted out onto the cobblestones.

Royce bounded in, then rounded with a dramatic sweep of his arm. A wooden board laden with still-steaming bread, bright red strawberries, and what appeared to be a small cake garnished with dawn-pink frosting lay on the Freth’s kitchen table.

“Happy birthday!”

I gawked, mouth stretching into a somewhat embarrassed smile. His mother must have gotten up well before dawn to make all this.

As if on cue, Marion Freth flurried into the kitchen. Her apron was spattered pink and she was wiping her hands with a rag. “Brin! Good morning, dear. And happy birthday!” She swept forwards, all dimples and curly brown hair, and wrapped me in a warm embrace.

“Thank you, Miss Freth. This is really… you didn’t have to do all this.” I hugged her somewhat awkwardly. Life with solemn Durst was generally devoid of warm embraces.

One plump hand swatted through the air. “Nonsense, sweetheart! It was my pleasure! Have a seat, and you, Royce, there we go. Have you already had breakfast, Brin?”

“Yes, ma’am.” I felt my cheeks grow warm, thinking back to my black bacon and salty cakes. Durst’s cooking was all I’d known for many years. I’d spent most of my childhood believing that food was purely for sustenance. I could still remember the first time Royce had invited me over for supper and I’d tasted a meal that… well, that Durst hadn’t prepared. It had been eye-opening, to say the least. “But, um, I’m definitely still hungry.”

His mother gave a sing-song laugh, reaching dimpled arms out to cut and serve us each a portion of the enticing spread.

The bread was warm and thick, and the sweet berries were still wet from morning dew. I saved my cake for last. It tasted like fresh strawberries and smelled like the honey I’d had earlier. As soon as I finished, Miss Freth plopped another slice onto my plate.

“Hey, I almost forgot your present!” Royce produced a small bundle of red cloth from one pocket. “Um… here.”

Miss Freth stood, scooping up the empty plates and moving towards their kitchen washbasin. Wood and copper clattered from behind as I gingerly unfolded my gift.

A piece of witchwood lay nestled in folds of lush scarlet. But, though I’d spent every day of my life looking at the sacred trees, this was unlike any I’d seen before.

The wood was ash-gray, polished smooth and cool as glass. It was bent into a graceful arch and fastened to itself by a small metal clasp. Along the wood were strands of split, night-black leaves that wound and twisted into feather-soft vines.

“It’s a bracelet.” Royce rubbed one hand back and forth through his unruly hair.

“Is it… dead?” I whispered, peering fearfully at the ornament. It was beautiful. Perhaps the prettiest piece of jewelry I’d ever seen, much less owned, but something about it made me very sad. The ashy wood, maudlin gray instead of proud silver, the broken leaves that didn’t reach out for me…

“What? No!” His voice rose an octave and his cheeks turned crimson.

Guilt knotted in my belly as my own face grew warm. Oh, well done, I chided myself silently. That probably wasn’t the response he expected.

I quickly cleared my throat. “It’s beautiful. I’m sorry, I just… I haven’t seen it, er, like this. The wood and the leaves, I mean. But it’s beautiful. Thank you, Royce.”

“You’re welcome,” he grumbled, examining the floorboards.

I wondered how much the bracelet had cost and how long he’d been waiting to surprise me with it. The knot in my belly tightened. “Will you help me put it on?”

Bright blue eyes flicked up, squinting a bit as I held out my wrist.

Behind us, his mom chuckled and drifted out of the kitchen.

“S-sure.” He took two quick steps forward and reached down, slowly affixing the clasp. The bracelet felt pleasantly cool against my skin.

“It’s really pretty.”

Royce swallowed. “You really like it?”

“Promise. It’s probably the nicest thing I own.”

Royce flushed beet-red as I gave him a hug. He mumbled something unintelligible.

“I love it,” I assured once more, pulling back with a wry grin. “Really. And this- all of this,” I gestured to the now-bare table and the dirty dishes, “was a wonderful surprise.”

He ducked his head. “Thought you’d like it.”

“I do. Best birthday ever.” I turned towards the wash basin, picking up the horsehair brush beside it.

Royce huffed, snatching the brush away from me. I tugged it back, and we devolved into several minutes of good-natured arguing about who would help his mother do the dishes. He won, because it was my birthday. Before long I was out the door with a full belly and a happy grin plastered across my face.

The quickest way home brought me through the town square. Townsfolk bustled by, chatting in cheerful tones.

I waved at the familiar faces before my gaze was drawn upwards. As always.

The morning air was warm with sunshine and the little fountain in the center of town sparkled merrily. But that wasn’t what drew my eye. It was the statue crafted with utmost care atop the fountain, that of a man raising a sword in triumph.

The hero. ‘Divine,’ people called him. ‘Sent from the fallen gods.’ A figure wrought of polished whitestone, save for a small, jagged piece of what looked like liquid fire embedded along the blade.

Durst had told me the story piece by piece, through fumbling lips and distant, grief-stricken eyes. When I was a baby, the mortal realm’s centuries of near-peace had been violently shattered. A terrible Fae appeared in the south; a monstrosity of darkness and dread whose very presence blotted out the sun. It swept through the southern continent, leaving death behind. People called it the Nightmare.

It was as though a door between the realms had been propped wide. Other Fae followed in the Nightmare’s wake; creatures wrought from humanities’ darkest dreams. Shadow-wrought wraiths and blood-soaked vampires, men with the faces of angels and the appetites of wolves, women whose teeth dripped venom. Monsters long-sealed within the Fae realm freed to roam, hunt, and feed as they pleased.

Every city in the realm had sent forces south; runekeepers and paladins and clerics and knights. But it wasn’t enough. The sun itself was choked away by darkness, mortal armies falling before the Fae.

All hope was lost.

And then the sky split open.

A shining warrior appeared in a flash of light.

He strode fearlessly into battle, wielding a sword of burning gold.

No evil could stand before him.

My gaze wandered to the ground nearby. Scorched earth. A gash along dulled cobblestones and once-green grass, charred black as pitch. A hideous wound carved into the very earth.

The place where the hero and the Nightmare had dealt each other fatal blows. And then, when the smoke cleared… they were both gone. Nothing remained save smoldering earth and shattered pieces of blazing steel.

The tale was one of victory… but also of sorrow. Durst’s wife had been killed by the monsters. And Royce’s father, and countless brave runekeepers and paladins and…

And my mother.

Durst said she’d been found nearby. Curled around me with a smile on her face. Sometimes on my journeys through town I would let my gaze roam across the ground, wondering with vague horror where she’d been.

I felt myself wondering now, and shivered.

Distant thunder broke my reverie. The townsfolk nearby glanced up, noted that the sun was still out in cheery force, and apparently decided that there was no rush to leave.

I, having a much farther journey home, disagreed.

The rain started just as I reached our cottage. It was the kind of warm summer downpour that turned the sky to steel and made everything smell like fresh, rich earth. I stayed outside with my beautiful trees, reveling in the scent and weeding contentedly as branches stretched out to give me shelter. Eventually, distant flickers of lightning and growling thunder sent me indoors with a resigned sigh.

Our kitchen was cast in shadow. I lit a candle for light, and my gaze fell on Durst’s bedroom door.

It was open.

He never left it open.

I didn’t see his boots or bow anywhere. No sign that he was home. I crept to the door, peeking into the gloom with wide eyes.

Empty.

Rain drummed against the roof. A flash of lightning illuminated a low bedframe with two squat nightstands on either side.

My curiosity swelled. I prodded the door open further and dared to step inside. There was a small table beside the door with a leather-bound book on top of it. On the room’s far side sat a wooden rocking chair propped beside a window. There was a wooden trunk next to it and a sturdy black armoire in one corner.

My gaze wandered to the bed. One side was rumpled, with a large depression in the mattress. The other side was smooth. Untouched.

The sight made me feel incredibly small, incredibly sad, and incredibly guilty. I stared at it for a moment longer, then quickly turned to leave and slammed straight into the table. The book fell with a heavy thud.

I rubbed my throbbing knee, then stooped to retrieve the fallen object. Lightning flickered, casting everything in vivid silver. Rain thrummed as my fingers brushed the spine.

It was bound in brown leather that smelled of earth and smoky wood. Gleaming golden thread embroidered the cover in waves and swirls.

I scooped the book up, curious but resolved not to pry further. A small piece of folded yellow parchment slipped out, fluttering to the ground. I knelt to retrieve it and my heart nearly stopped.

Scrawled along the parchment in tidy, unfamiliar handwriting were the words “To my beloved Brin, the light of my life.”
