Chapter 131

He mended three straps and ground his blade before he left, which cost him the better part of a morning. The man who passed through the same village at first light was faster by half a day and died at the first ford. — Codex of the Long Watch, Field Observation, 9th Century, the Peloponnese

The plate was full again before Arthur had finished what was on it.

He hadn’t seen Eleni do it. She moved around the table without appearing to cross it, and a second piece of fish arrived beside the first while his attention was on the bread. She might be running her own version of Threshold Drive for all he knew. He worked through the fish because it was in front of him and because sending it back was not something that happened in this house.

Across from him, Sarah was arguing with her grandmother about oregano in a mixture of Greek and English.

Maria’s youngest came round the table at knee height, delivered a wooden spoon into Arthur’s lap without explanation and left. Arthur put the spoon back on the table. Nobody remarked on it.

Donna came in from the yard with wet hair and took the chair beside Arthur. Eleni appeared next to her before she had finished sitting down and set a plate of bread, white cheese and olives in front of her. “Sit. Coffee is coming. You are too thin, all of you are too thin, but you especially.”

The knock came a while later, when all of them were on their second cup of coffee and Petros got up to see who it was.

Petros opened the door and a young man stood on the step with an envelope in his hand and a notebook under his arm. Once he had confirmed he was at the Vassilou house, he handed over the envelope and the book and left without lingering.

“From Antoniou,” Petros said, setting the envelope down over the notebook.

Arthur opened the envelope at the table. It contained a neatly folded single sheet of paper. The paper turned out to be Council letterhead with two stamped seals and Antoniou’s signature across the bottom. The text was in Greek, so Arthur passed it over to Sarah, who read it out loud.

“Transit authorisation for one vessel, harbour of Piraeus to the island of Aegina. Four passengers plus captain and any helpers. Valid for a single crossing, outbound and return, at the captain’s discretion.” She set the paper down. “Signed by Christos Antoniou. Stamped by harbour authority.”

The transit was conditional on Kastella clear, and Kastella was clear now. Arthur folded the paper and slipped it back into the envelope.

“This is rather thin,” Steve said, picking up his promised notebook. “I was hoping for something a lot more substantial from a Council member.”

“Not that I don’t feel for Steve, which I don’t. We should start planning for the trip,” Donna said.

Petros pushed off the doorframe. “Then I go to Spyros today, before Antoniou changes his mind or the weather does. I should be the first person he sees before he makes other plans.”

His eyes went round the table and stopped on Arthur with a dangerous gleam in them.

“You know who else made plans, once,” he said. “Stelios, Kosta’s boy from the corner. Before Sarah went off to save the world, this one is fourteen, following her home from the school like a dog. Flowers, poems, the whole business.”

He spread his hands wide. “And Stelios, poor Stelios, he ate the noodle-pie.”

Arthur looked at Sarah. They were all used to Petros’s idioms. When Petros says something weird in the middle of a normal sentence, assume it’s an idiom.

“He got turned down. It means he got turned down,” Sarah said with a sigh.

“The noodle-pie,” Petros agreed with a satisfied look.

Donna made a low sound into her coffee and fixed her own gaze on Arthur.

“Chylópita,” Steve said after flipping through his new and not-so-shiny notebook. “That’s chylós and pita, gruel-pie more than noodle. It was the cheap dish that you served when there was nothing better in the house, so feeding a suitor the chylópita told him he was not getting the royal treatment. The Italians have almost the same—”

“Re,” Eleni said and set more bread in front of him. “I know where my words come from. Eat.”

They went upstairs after the meal, into the room that had been Petros’s before he was married, and settled down to finish the allocation. All of them were too tired the night before, and the thought of a bed was more appealing than muscle tremors.

Arthur went first, as was the custom. The allocation rolled through him in the usual thirty-second wave, connective tissue adjusting, the heat in his shoulder briefly flaring and then settling into the new frame. He rolled his neck once when it passed and that was all.

“Strength, Constitution, Dexterity,” he said, and felt each point changing him from within. “I am still too slow when things come at me sideways. The Dexterity is for that.”

By the time they came back down the stairs, the light had turned amber. Petros was back at the table, boots off, a cup in front of him. He had walked to the next harbour and back, and he was in no hurry to give up what he had heard.

“Well?” Donna said.

“Well. Spyros will take you. Four stones, and the paper is good, all of it as I said it would be.” He held up a finger before anyone could be glad about it. “Tomorrow, no. The day after.”

“Why the day after?” Arthur asked.

“Because Aegina is not the near water. Aegina is across. Open water, no radio, nothing under you for twenty-seven kilometres but the wrong sort of company. He will not cross on a hull he has not had up on the slip first. He wants a day to work the seams.”

He gulped the rest of his coffee down. “And I want a day to go over his rigging. The last time he swore to me his rigging was fine, I found a shroud three good pulls from parting. One day. You can survive one day of my mother’s cooking, ne?”

Donna weighed it and let it go. A day was a day. And there was nothing she could do about it anyway.

“That is not altogether bad,” Arthur said. “Gives us time to bind the gear Maria has been working on. And gives Sarah time to pick the shoes that go with the new gear. That’s gonna take a day.”

“As you Brits are so fond of saying, ‘Sod Off’,” Sarah said.

The knock came after dark. Petros went to open the door, grumbling about a man’s right to digest in peace. The man on the step was Kostas Markopoulos, a wide smile on his face, having walked across half of Piraeus in the dark to knock on a door he could as easily have sent a boy to.

Sarah and Eleni were up and across the room. He had a glass of water and a cup of coffee before him before the pleasantries were done.

“You came to check my maps and then vanished. And now I hear from Eleni you have been the length of Attica and back making trouble,” Markopoulos said, looking at Steve.

“Tomorrow. My house, coffee. All of you. I get so few opportunities to host interesting people.”

Steve was saying yes with his whole face before the invitation was finished. “I would like nothing more.”

“Papadakis will be there as well, or I hope she is,” Markopoulos said. “All of you have met her, yes? Such a sharp scholar.”

“We would be glad to. All of us,” Donna said.

Markopoulos looked pleased, and a shade relieved, which was its own small piece of information.

He did not stay long. He kissed Sarah and Eleni on the cheek, set his hat on his head and went back out into the dark.

“Well. That is tomorrow sorted then,” Steve said, gathering up his notebook.
