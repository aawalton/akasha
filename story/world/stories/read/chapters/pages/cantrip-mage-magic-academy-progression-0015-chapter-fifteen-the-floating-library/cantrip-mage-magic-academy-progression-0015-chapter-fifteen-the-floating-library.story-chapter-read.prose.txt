Maestro Yarrowbloom stared at the test rune on his desk for a long time.

Eola fidgeted as the elf’s thick brows furrowed into a glare. She looked anywhere but at his face. The walls were covered in swords; they hung from racks or sat mounted like trophies. A small library of books occupied a single shelf. Eola glanced at it, then did a double-take. “You have Combat Tricks, by Tiphane?”

“Yes. Translated by myself, page by page.” Maestro Yarrowbloom went silent again.

The seconds ticked by on the clock in the corner, and Eola fidgeted. Patrice had waited for her outside of Instructor Tarik’s office, then followed her up the stairs to Maestro Yarrowbloom’s. She was sitting out in the hall, probably bored out of her mind, and the professor seemed like he was stalling. Eola’s eyes flicked to the door for a moment, and in that moment, the professor moved.

He rolled the scroll up and shoved it at Eola. “This spell is an affront to the Taron-Li, Miss Lemiene. Return it to Instructor Tarik, and don’t show me anything like it again.”

“But—“

The professor cut her off. “Every year since Varin’s hired him, Instructor Tarik sends a student to me with this spell. They ask the same questions, and I give the same answer. This is an affront to the sword dance. It’s an affront to spellswords. Your follow-up is going to be ‘But, Maestro, Instructor Tarik said that the Taron-Li is a pillar of combat, but it’s not the only one.’ He’s…correct. However, in the class I co-teach next term, you’ll learn how to kill a siege mage long before they get the chance to cast a spell like this.”

Eola didn’t say anything, and after a moment, the elf continued. “The Taron-Li isn’t for everyone. Many of your classmates lack the rhythm to follow the dance—or the discipline to control their own magic. It takes artistry to avoid the trap of overcasting and instead rely on the blade, and to know when to probe with a cantrip or try for a finishing blow with First or even Second Order magic. Keep that in mind when spells like this tempt you.”

“But siege mages, Maestro? What about them?”

“A menace and an affront, but a necessary part of most lords’ militaries. They’re not as glamorous or skilled as a good spellsword in action, but when protected well, their rituals are devastating. Just don’t expect one to do well in a duel.” The elf stood up. “Office hours are over. I’ll see you in Magical Dueling tomorrow.”

The rolled-up Steel Snarl rune weighed on Eola as she left the dueling professor’s office and stepped into the hall. She sighed. Patrice’s eyes were closed, and her mouth hung slightly open as she leaned against the wall. She’d done that outside of Instructor Tarike’s office, too. Eola went to wake her up, then stopped herself.

Maestro Yarrowbloom’s reaction to the Steel Snarl rune had been pure emotion—disgust, annoyance, and arrogance about the Taron-Li. And Eola enjoyed magical dueling. The rhythms of the sword dance had been drilled into her from the time she could swing a stick around. They reminded her of her mom. But there’d been a kernel of truth hidden in the emotion, and it hadn’t been annoyance at Instructor Tarik.

It had been fear.

Not terror. Not cowardice, either. A healthy respect, buried under ego and posturing. Instructor Tarik was right—the Taron-Li wasn’t the end-all/be-all of combat. Her own experiences matched that, too; the battle against the feral book hadn’t been her victory. It had been won by a ritualist-in-training, drawing a single spell and casting it over the course of the fight. But he’d been vulnerable the whole time he’d cast it. Without her, he wouldn’t have won, either. She made a promise to herself.

The next time she saw a group of third-year students heading into the library, she’d follow them through the first floor to see how they fought. Their group compositions mattered. How they worked together did, too.

Then she grabbed Patrice’s shoulder and shook it. “Hey, wake up.”

“Huh?” The redhead’s eyes flicked open, and she yawned right in Eola’s face. “You’re done already? Great. I’m ready for bed.”

“Suit yourself,” Eola said. “I’m going back to my room and getting ready for a library trip.”

“I’ll go with you,” Patrice said, smiling. “I’ve been waiting to get on the second floor again, and now I’ve—“

“I’m not going to the second floor.” Eola started walking as Patrice pushed herself off the wall and fell in behind her. “It’s time to look at the third floor and see what the less-visited shelves are hiding.”

“Sounds fu…” Patrice trailed off. She yawned again, pinched her arm right above the elbow, squeezing hard enough to yelp from it, and shook her head. Then she blinked and hurried after Eola. “Wait! The third floor!? How’d you get access to the third floor!?”

A half hour later, Eola regretted telling Patrice where she was going.

The three of them—Colin had been waiting by her door—were crammed in her room. Between Patrice, who wasn’t a small girl, and the other two, there was little enough space, but the presence of Colin’s weasel and Eola’s cat, who was stalking the long, yellow familiar like prey, made it feel even more claustrophobic.

The subject matter didn’t help anything, either.

“You can’t go to the third floor,” Colin hissed. His fists were balled, and he stood blocking the door. Nera, his weasel, stood between his feet. He bared his teeth and glared. “Haven’t you been listening to Instructor Clearance’s lectures? It’s dangerous there. Way more dangerous than the second floor.”

“I don’t care,” Eola said.

“What do you mean, you don’t care?”

“I mean, Colin, that I need to know more than the first and second floors can teach me, and that means pushing into the higher floors. Instructor Tarik gave me the key to the floor for a reason. He trusts me to handle it.” Eola carefully neglected to mention the professor’s warning to wait. That wasn’t relevant to her conversation with Colin and Patrice. Getting access to the upper floors’ secrets early could be the difference between saving her parents and failing to.

The tall girl raised a hand. Colin and Eola both glared at her, and she put it down. “Sorry. I’m with whatshisname. You seem pretty on top of things, Eola, but my brother says it’s a lot worse. Most of the second-years travel in packs when they go that far, and the whistles don’t work to get help. Plus, there’s the drop-down. Mess up on those stairs, and splat, you end up on the floor next to some poor visitor. I think you should wait, too.”

Eola shook her head and stepped onto the ladder leading to the loft. She grabbed her mother’s sword and breastplate, then pulled off her cloak and robe. “Help me buckle these straps, Patrice.”

“No.”

“Colin, help me. Please.”

“Fine.” Colin didn’t move. He stood by the door, arms crossed. “But only if you let me come with you.”

“That’s impossible, and you know it. You don’t have the key to the third floor.” Eola rolled her eyes and sighed. “I appreciate the offer, and if you could get past the gate, I’d let you in a heartbeat, but you can’t do it.”

“Actually, hear him out,” Patrice said.

“The whistles don’t work because it’s too far to call for help, but if we’re at the gate, we can help you with anything that follows you through. That way, when you get in trouble, we’re there for you,” Colin said.

“Now it’s ‘we?’” Eola huffed, then held up her arms. “Fine. Someone help me with these straps. It’s a lot easier with help.”

“Great! I’ll go get my gear.” As Patrice side-stepped around Colin, he sighed and carefully tightened Eola’s armor around her. The armor adjusted itself perfectly to fit around her shoulders, bust, and waist as he worked, reminding her once again of her mom’s hugs. When it finished, she sat down on the bed to wait for Colin and Patrice to get their own equipment.

And, from above, a pair of yellow eyes stared at her. They looked frustrated, or maybe worried, and Eola couldn’t tell if Atta’s annoyance was at her or the two students who insisted on escorting her through the library’s second floor.

By the time they approached the third floor’s gate, Eola had changed her mind. If she could have, she’d have taken Patrice and Colin with her to the third floor in a heartbeat. Especially Patrice.

The sleepy-looking girl hadn’t cast a single spell, but her gear was outstanding—and so was her desire to fight. Eola had misjudged her. She was big, sure, but it was all muscle and power. As they leapfrogged the last three rows, Eola tried to figure out where she’d heard of the Clerk family. It rang a distant bell, but that was it. Maybe trade? Actually, almost certainly trade. Greenarbor’s docks attracted all types—they’d almost certainly attract a noble family or two.

Then they stopped.

Patrice yawned and sat down on the floor next to the gate. She was still wearing her cloak, and she flipped it over her head. “Right. Colin, you’re on watch. If something comes after us, scream, and I’ll wake up.”

Colin stared at her, eyes widening. “You’re going to sleep?”

“Of course I am. It’s past my bedtime.”

Eola cleared her throat. “Before you go to sleep…if I need help, I’ll try to get back here. You’ll probably hear my whistle before I show up. Colin, get ready with a ritual. Patrice…do what you do, I guess?”

“Can do,” Patrice shifted under the cloak. Her long hammer’s handle popped out. It was black leather, with a filigreed pattern of leaves and vines crawling up its entire length until it disappeared under her blue cloak. “Night.”

“Don’t leave me here with her.” Colin looked horrified. Eola stared at him, and he pulled himself together enough to mutter, “Yeah, I’ll wake her up. We’ll be fine.”

“Thanks,” Eola said quietly. There wasn’t anything left to say, so she held up her badge to the gate. It glowed for a moment and cracked open, and Eola stepped into the dungeon’s third floor. The gate clicked shut behind her.

“You shouldn’t have dragged them into this,” Atta hissed as soon as they were alone. “Even better, you should have listened to your professor and waited. Just because he gave you a key didn’t mean you had to open the door right away! Don’t get me wrong, I want—”

Eola didn’t say anything to the cat, who was hacking and coughing on her shoulders. She was too busy staring at the library’s third floor.

Instructor Clearance had done a good job of describing the staircases and hanging shelves, the suspended rooms hovering magically in mid-air, and the variety of monsters that contrasted with the relatively tame second floor. She’d probably covered the monsters well, too. But the hamadryad had missed one point that, to Eola, was crucial.

The third floor was enormous.

She’d expected two or three staircases leading from the cleared and weeded, semicircular room beyond the gate, not twelve. But twelve there were. Each started at the base of a slender, graceful arch between shelves that had to be thirty feet tall—or taller. A trio of wheeled ladders, each for a different section of the gigantic shelf, sat on steel bars, their rollers oiled. When Eola touched the lowest one, it shifted slightly; clearly, the dungeon was maintaining itself. Either that or the librarians were taking care of it.

The books here were all spellbooks—specifically, Second Order spellbooks. Eola ignored them. She was a long push from casting a Second Order equivalent, and besides, she had plenty of them in her own book on her hip.

“Right. Which way do you think?” Eola asked. She had a sheet of papers with coordinates and room descriptions, but the third floor wasn’t a simple Row-and-Shelf system anymore. Instead, it was a short description of a floating room, and that was it. Worse, the coordinates would only be marginally helpful—they showed one place a specific room might be found, not all of them. She hadn’t realized that until she was already committed, but it was going to make finding anything much more difficult.

“I have no idea, girl.” Atta hopped down from Eola’s shoulders and licked her paw. “This floor is a maze, and you’re trying to navigate it all but blind.”

Eola shivered and drew her sword and wand. “Well then, I guess we’ll start with the far left and see where we end up. We can always back-track if we need to.”

“A truly stupid plan,” Atta said. "I’ll try to protect you, but don’t expect miracles. I’m no Y’aer.”

As she worked her way up the narrow, wooden set of curved stairs, Eola stared out at the expanse of shelves and rooms floating overhead, then glanced down. The moment she did, vertigo rocked her, and she leaned on the railing for support. A claw stabbed into her pants-leg, right at the stitching in the leather. “Don’t look down, and don’t trust the stairs!” her familiar hissed, an urgency in her voice.

Eola stopped and let go of the mahogany railing. She steadied herself and kept climbing. “What was that?”

“You either aren’t paying attention or your professor’s missing some vital information. That’s the barrier between the second and third floor. One-way only, very disorienting to look at, so don’t.”

The rest of the staircase went much more smoothly, and Eola stepped off of it and into a room that was shaped something like an E. It was almost completely surrounded by short, stocky bookshelves that went up to her waist, and every one of them was unweeded. There were no walls beyond those shelves. But on the far side, on one of the wings, a crumbling brick wall supported an arch—and a gate.

Eola’s finger ran across the books’ titles. Alchemical Solution-Making, A Thousand Herbs of the Southern Islands, and other tomes on potion-making. Some students did learn alchemy. Ordered Magic governed that field, as well as artifice. But there weren’t many. Potion-making and creating constructs were both slow, difficult work, and it was almost always easier to hire some poor sap to do it for you.

“This isn’t it,” she said after a minute. “Let’s go back.”

But when she turned around, the stairwell was gone. “Or not.”

Atta didn’t say anything. The blue cat’s yellow eyes peered deeper into the room, and her tail and back slowly puffed out. “We’re not alone.”

“No, you ent,” a cracking, dry voice said. It was muffled and quiet, but it sounded middle-aged and male.

Eola raised her wand and sketched a Chromite Cloth spell-chain. Her soul emptied like the bottle of shaken spirits it was, pouring almost half of her Mana into the spell. It activated, and Eola lowered the wand. Then the sword went up, but not into a full First Guard.

She had a bad feeling that she knew who the mysterious man was. “Well, who are you, then?” she asked.

“Bad idea,” Atta mewled by her feet.

A figure stepped around the corner. He was thin—almost skeletally so. At first glance, he had to be in his sixties or seventies, but as Eola looked more closely, she realized that couldn’t be the case. His skin wasn’t that old. It was cut up, sure. Dozens of long, thin scars covered every inch of exposed skin, and pages from shredded books patched the tattered robe he wore. He was incredibly pale. Almost translucent. Eola could see veins and arteries under his skin, almost like it wasn’t even there. But his face wasn’t the face of a man a day over thirty—thirty-five at the oldest.

He had a sword in his hand, too. Unlike hers, his was rusted and dull. Also unlike hers, his was in First Guard, and a half-formed spell hung in the air, finger held out to finish drawing it. No wand; he was a hand-caster.

Atta’s air stood even more on end. She hissed and spat between Eola’s feet, yowling in anger—or something else. And Eola realized exactly who, and what, this man was.

She’d found the Scholar's Cult…or, more accurately, the Scholar's Cult had found her.
