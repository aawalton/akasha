Chapter 142

A chair that stays empty long enough becomes its own sort of answer about the structure of power. — Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

The caïque was being put back into the sea on rollers cut from two lengths of weathered pine. Spyros had chosen the trees and Arthur did a passable impression of a chainsaw with his 37 Strength.

“Now,” Spyros said.

Arthur set his shoulder under the gunwale and pushed. The repaired hull slid over the pine, the pitch-black seams catching the early light, and then gathered enough weight to carry itself down the final slope. Cold water rushed round Arthur’s boots.

The boat floated.

“I think you wasted time cutting those trees. You could have simply lifted the boat on your shoulders and set it down into the water,” Steve said.

“Malaka,” Spyros said, shaking his head.

Spyros caught the bow rope before the morning swell could turn the boat sideways and stood looking along the hull. The new fastening heads sat cleanly against the planks and no water welled through the worked seam.

“She looks sound,” Arthur said only to see Spyros giving him the look.

Arthur held up both hands. “Not getting into your business.”

“Good.”

The loading went quickly, with water jars following the left over provision and the ropes. Steve settled the field pack in the place Spyros indicated and tied it off himself, after Spyros showed him three times the exact knot he was supposed to use.

Arthur stepped aboard, turned and held out his hand.

Sarah took it without hesitation. He drew her over the gunwale, and when the boat shifted under her feet his other hand settled at her waist. She smiled, gave him a quick kiss and moved towards the thwart.

Donna watched the scene with one eyebrow raised. Steve looked from Sarah to Arthur, gave a happy smile and climbed onboard.

“About time,” Donna said quietly.

Vangelis cast off the bow line, the sail rose, snapped once and filled.

Michalis and Vangelis remained in the shallows until the caïque had cleared the rocks. Sarah raised a hand and they waved back

“Good men, those two,” Spyros said. “For Aeginians.”

The sea was behaving well enough that Spyros let Steve take the tiller. Steve held the tiller in one hand and kept the other resting on the ledger in his lap, unwilling to surrender either.

"You are steering us to Crete," Donna said.

"I am steering us dead straight on the mark Spyros gave me."

Spyros looked along the bow, and adjusted Steve's grip on the tiller by two fingers' width without a word. Steve chose to interpret the action as a joint effort.

Arthur sat amidships because he was more experienced in the ways of the sea now even if it still made the occasional attempts to recover its earlier advantage. He watched the coastline solidify into rooftops and felt entirely at ease.

As Piraeus resolved itself, Spyros adjusted the tiller and the sail loosened. As the caïque came round towards the harbour mouth they saw a rope stretched between two mooring posts. It forced incoming boats into a single narrow lane beneath the wall.

Two soldiers stood at the opening with writing boards. A third watched from above. Each boat came alongside, gave a name and a berth, and was waved through.

Donna shifted her weight, studying the quay. “That wasn’t there before,” she said.

“No,” Spyros replied. He had stopped humming.

The caïque drifted up to the rope and one of the soldiers caught the bow line and looped it over a bollard while the other opened a register.

“Vessel,” he said in Greek.

Spyros gave the caïque’s name and his own. The soldier found the berth on an older list and then drew a line through something already written.

“Port of departure.”

“Aegina.”

The pencil stopped and the soldier looked into the boat for the first time. His gaze moved across the people, the shield, the gear and the weapons.

“Passengers. Names.”

Spyros reached inside his jacket and produced Antoniou’s transit authorisation.

The soldier took the paper, read it carefully then carried it to a corporal standing beneath the harbour wall. The corporal studied the paper for a long while and then came over.

“This was issued by the Civil Council,” he said in English.

“It was issued for the outward and return crossing,” Arthur said.

The corporal folded the paper and returned it to Spyros.

“Civil transit authorisations are suspended under the emergency order. All arrivals are entered in the military register.”

“Suspended by whom?” Sarah asked.

“The harbour operates under the garrison order now,” the corporal said, thumbing through the register.

“Your names,” he said.

They gave their names and Sarah gave her full name in Greek. The soldier glanced up at Vassilou.

“From Kastella?”

“Yes,” Sarah said.

The soldier nodded and made some notes.

“Are you making notes on every passenger?” Sarah prodded in Greek.

“Yes, everyone entering from outside the harbour district.”

He wrote something on a slip of paper and handed it over to a younger soldier who took it and hurried towards the Port Authority building.

“You may land,” the corporal said, as the first soldier released the rope.

Spyros brought the caïque through. His berth was still the same, between a patched fishing boat and a narrow motor launch whose dead engine had been stripped for metal. He stepped ashore and tied off the boat.

It was then he saw the two soldiers were waiting for him. One of them carried a chain while the other carried a sheaf of printed forms with blank spaces left to be filled in by hand.

The older soldier looked unhappy to be there. “Captain. Under the coastal transport order, all sail-capable vessels above six metres are subject to requisition for supply and military movement,” he said in Greek.

“This is a fishing boat,” Spyros said.

“The order says all sail-capable vessels. And it could be temporary; boats are being released for fishing but only after they are registered up the hill.”

“It is my boat.”

The soldier offered him a filled form as a reply which Spyros did not touch.

The younger man passed the chain through the bow ring and around the iron staple set into the quay, then closed a heavy padlock over the links. Spyros watched his boat become something he could no longer move.

The older soldier held out the paper again. “This is the receipt. It records the condition of the vessel and your claim for compensation if it is requisitioned.”

For several seconds Spyros made no move to take the paper. Then he took the page, folded it once and placed it inside his jacket beside Antoniou’s authorisation.

The soldier cleared his throat. “Personal effects may be removed. The vessel is to remain provisioned until further orders are received. Present yourself at the Acropolis stores office.”

Spyros gripped the gunwale and shifted his weight but before his body could move, Donna’s hand was on his shoulder.

“If we do this here, they will get the boat and you,” she said quietly.

Spyros stepped back aboard. He took his knife, his oilskin and a small wooden box from under the stern board. He paused with his hand on the tiller for a long moment then climbed onto the quay.

Arthur was looking at the chain. To him it seemed like a flimsy thing that he could break with one proper jerk. But that was not the same as having the right to break it while two ordinary men stood beside it with a stamped order and no desire to fight.

Donna saw the same thing. “Let’s unload and figure this out back home,” she said.

The harbour had not stopped working, which made naming the wrongness of it difficult to name at first.

Fish still changed hands beneath the awnings, a cooper hammered a new hoop onto a barrel and a line of women waited at the grain office with sacks folded under their arms. A group of men rolled a cart of charcoal towards the upper road, cursing one another at the corner when a wheel struck a rut.

But every road out of the harbour had soldiers at it.

At the first junction, a pair of men checked handcarts against a fresh roster nailed to a board. The paper bore a single stamp where the old notices below it had two smaller seals. At the second, three soldiers stood beside a table and copied the names of everyone moving inland.

The party joined the queue.

A dockworker ahead of them gave his name and destination. The soldier found him on the page, marked the time and let him pass. An old woman carrying a basket was waved through without any questions, though the corporal watched which road she took.

When Arthur reached the table, the clerk looked down at a loose sheet.

“Arthur Ashworth. Donna Callas. Steven Parr. Sarah Vassilou.”

He repeated the four names and Arthur saw him mark the names on the paper.

“Our names are already on the sheet,” Sarah said.

Steve looked back towards the harbour. A runner could have come out by a side entrance and taken the shorter route while they were unloading the caïque.

“Yes.” The man tapped the paper. “Arrived from Aegina.”

“What else does it say?” Sarah asked.

“Nothing relevant,” the man said, reflexively moving a hand to cover the lines.

“You have passage papers from Christos Antoniou,” he said, putting his hand forward.

Spyros came up, gave his name and thrust the passage paper towards the soldier. He passed the paper behind him and said something to Spyros.

“He says we would need new papers from the hill for general passage around the city,” Sarah said.

Donna had been watching the patrol at the next crossing. “How long have the checkpoints been taking names?” she asked, looking at Sarah who translated.

The corporal beside the table shifted his stance.

“Emergency movement order.”

“That also wasn’t what I asked,” said Sarah.

“We were told to record movement,” the soldier said. “You are free to continue.”

“They’re mapping the city's movements,” Donna said as they walked past the checkpoint.

“Or teaching it to report itself,” Steve said.

The patrol density across the visible harbour perimeter has doubled since your departure, Archie said. The checkpoint spacing is consistent with a controlled-access regime, not a temporary security elevation.

The roads above the harbour looked exactly as they had when they had left. Yet familiar corners had acquired unfamiliar men. A single fresh sheet was pinned to a noticeboard at the corner of two streets. Four lines were written on a tight, careful hand. Two in English and two in Greek.

The military authority assumes interim governance of the Saronic coast, effective immediately, until the security situation permits the restoration of civilian oversight.

"That's not a temporary measure," Steve said as everyone huddled around the notice. "Rome called it iustitium once. A recess from ordinary government, for the duration of the emergency. Some of those recesses ran twenty years."

The clause is structured to avoid a measurable condition, Archie said. It specifies no threshold for when the security situation permits restoration, and names no authority to decide when that condition has been met. It is, functionally, indefinite.

Sarah turned towards the house and quickened her pace.

Petros’s boatyard lay along the route towards the house, and they knew something was wrong by the absence of any sound.

When they reached the yard they found the outer gate was shut and a notice pasted over the latch. Sarah tore it free.

It was a requisition order for the yard’s stores, lifting gear and usable timber. Below the list, another hand had written that the principal operator was absent under essential-service authority.

“Principal operator. That would mean Petros,” Sarah said, and hurriedly went round to the smaller workshop door to find it locked as well. Beyond it, Maria’s treadle Singer was silent.

Arthur came to stand beside her. He did not say everything would be all right or that there might be an ordinary explanation, because there was no ordinary explanation left.

Sarah broke into a run.
