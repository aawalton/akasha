Lira did not go to bed that night. Instead, she went around to her bookmarks’ hiding spots and reactivated her mana obfuscation and mana stabilization runes, one after another, until the library was reasonably well covered against anyone sensing her casting. She only felt marginally safer, since the only source of her confidence that those enchantments were working was the man who had seen through her casting for months.

Her eyes landed on her journal, where the spiderweb rune was still burned into the page. She made sure to memorize it, then tore out the page and burned it completely, along with the line about Master Jorel Velora’s calligraphy book that was still at the bottom of her to-do list.

“Pika. Fila. Curad.”

Finally, she locked the door, cast her breathing spell, and sat down at her desk to think, since sleep was not going to happen anyway.

She could not decide whether researching karma was the best use of her time or whether she should be working out how to flee the capital without Emberborn noticing. The mage had known about her for a long while and had only chosen to show himself now, and she did not believe for a second that the Canary spell was the only reason. More likely than not, something big involving karma was happening in this kingdom, and Emberborn wanted her to do something about it, or at least wanted her looking in the right direction.

She hadn’t been arrested yet, and that meant her life was not in immediate danger, which she supposed was good news. However, it did not change the fact that her life was still in the mage’s hands, and Lira hated that more than she could say. She had chosen to live on the street rather than live in her uncle’s hands when she was twelve, and she sure as shit was not going to spend the rest of her life in Emberborn’s.

Still, she could not afford to ignore the man or the hint he had given her, which meant she needed a spell to see karma, and for that, she needed to find out what karma actually was.

Luckily for her, karma was an old philosophical and religious concept, and this library had no shortage of either kind of text. Unlike medicine, which had required the most contemporary writing she could find before she could crack it, a concept like karma benefited greatly from older perspectives, from people who had spent centuries arguing about it.

“Ladar. Linasara. Oshkan. Cognar. Turat.”

Hundreds of books pinged at the back of her mind, some of them on the shelves around her and a good number of them upstairs in the storage rooms. A few were so old that she could already tell they would need her Book Restoration spell before she could read a single page.

She could not absorb hundreds of books all at once, nor could she afford to drain herself restoring half of them. So she limited herself to twenty: ten of the most credible and relevant books she could find, written by people who had actually studied the concept and specialized in it, and ten more chosen from other fields where the concept of karma was discussed in an interesting manner. Lira found that those kinds of books offered a broader perspective, even if they only touched on karma in passing.

She sorted through the pings carefully, curating the knowledge.

“Linasara. Luneth. Velar. Moren. Kaldris.”

Only one of the twenty needed restoration, which she decided she could pay for without taxing her metabolism too much, since the book was probably as old as this kingdom. She opened it and began paging through it while magically absorbing a new book every thirty minutes.

“Pika. Linasara. Cognar. Coopar. Nagla.”

She spent the rest of the night studying, with the candle burning down and a second one taking its place. By dawn, the shape of the concept of karma had started to emerge.

The oldest text was a temple scripture from the Abadi era. Abadi was one of the languages her father had taught her alongside Magari, so she was able to read it. The book treated karma as a weight carried by the soul, every deed adding a grain to one pan of a scale that the gods would read when the soul left the body. Two others from different faiths agreed on the scale and disagreed on almost everything else, since one held that the weight decided where a soul went after death, while the other claimed it decided what the soul became when it returned to life. Lira had no trouble believing in the gods or the soul, since accounts of gods manifesting were too numerous to ignore, and she had felt her own soul while sensing her mana.

Rebirth was the part she could not settle for sure. The only evidence she could find was in the records of heroes and champions, where a few claimed to be old souls, with scraps of memory from a past life or an instinct for a weapon they had never held. However, she had never met anyone like that, and historical accounts were written by people who wanted the hero to be special. Whether her karmic scale existed and whether her deeds had ever moved it were questions no book could answer.

The next few books were more useful because they stopped talking about abstract concepts like the soul and started talking about people. A philosopher from a trading hub city wrote that a favor was never free, since the moment one person helped another, a weight settled on the receiver that remained until it was repaid or rescinded. He argued that this was karma, the true glue of every society, older than law and stronger than it. He described the karmic link as a ledger kept between two parties, each side marking what was given and what was owed, with each assigning a different value to the merchandise. That line brought back a memory of her father scribing ledgers, and she remembered the kinds of disagreements that happened, especially when barter was involved.

Another author, a skeptic, dismissed the soul entirely and argued that karma was only the memory people kept of each other, with no scale and no gods involved. Lira gave him credit for noticing that the debt worked whether or not any higher being was watching over it.

None of this debt talk surprised her. Pallan’s whole organization ran on it, with favors given and remembered, a loaf of bread today for a lookout tomorrow, and nobody in the syndicate ever calling it karma or pretending it was a mystical power. The palace was no different, if she was honest with herself, since every noble who smiled at another had an ulterior motive, and every servant who covered for someone expected help in return. Friendship was never unconditional. A friendship in which only one side gave tended to collapse because the giver began to keep count while the receiver forgot the receipt... the people who claimed otherwise were usually the ones collecting karmic debts without giving anything in return.

The last book she opened before dawn was a short, badly bound treatise on rulers. She almost set it aside until she reached a passage about how the cheapest leash was obligation, because a man who felt he owed you would argue your case to himself without being asked. The author called it the gentlest form of mind control, and the only one that left no mark for a mage to find.

By the time the sky outside the window had gone pale, she had stopped taking notes and started sorting what she had read into two piles.

The first was karma as weight, the account a single soul kept with whatever sat above the world, tied to the afterlife, religion, and rebirth. She could neither prove nor disprove it. She could not say whether her deeds had provoked a reaction from the universe, and she suspected no spell she made would be able to either.

The second was karma as a link between two people, the debt that grew whenever one helped or harmed another. That one she understood completely because she had been living inside its rules since she was twelve. It could be observed, tested, and used, which was exactly why it worried her.

Emberborn had hinted that the other Archmage was doing something with karma. If a link could be seen, it could probably be shaped, and her book divination spell had told her long ago that Harwin was interested in mind magic. Moral debt was the best lever there was for pushing a mind without touching it directly, and a man with a lot of coin and a talent for putting it in other people’s hands would have the best opportunities in the kingdom to apply it. If the other mage was Harwin, that was... But if it was Kelroth, then she was facing something much larger than she had imagined, and dealing with him would be harder than dealing with Kavard had ever been.

She closed the last book, looked at the stack on her desk, and stretched. Enough with the theory; it was time for spell building.

The first idea for a spell was simple and intuitive.

Pika. Kalma. Syneth.

Mind. Karma. Interpretation.

It was Karma Sight, like Mage Sight but with karma instead of magic.

She started from Mage Sight, which let her see the drifting currents of mana and identify disturbances. What she needed was the same lens pointed at something different. The difficulty was that she was not an expert when it came to karma. But, to be fair, she barely knew what mana was either when she first learned Mage Sight. Emberborn had given her children’s books about mana, and she had managed to create Mage Sight. Now he had given her only one word, and she needed to create Karma Sight. Luckily for her, the library had plenty of books to fill that gap.

“Pika. Selan. Tureth.”

As she entered the stillness of meditation, she thought about the two half-sorted concepts she had found in the books, holding both in her mind at once: karma as the scale that held the soul to account and karma as the ledger kept between two people. She thought of her father’s ledgers, with the debit column on one side and the credit column on the other, the favors that moved through Pallan’s tea houses, and the spiderweb rune burned into the page of her journal, with its threads crossing in every direction. For an hour, she meditated on the duality of debt and obligation, morality and sin, burden and blessing.

Pika. Kalma. Syneth.Mind. Karma. Interpretation.

Click.

With the spell imprinted, it was time for testing. Syneth needed a visual aspect to interpret, which meant Lira needed a mirror. She carried the candle and her mana obfuscation bookmark up the spiral stairs to one of the storage rooms. It held nothing but cracked chairs, a rolled carpet, and a bronze mirror leaning against the wall. She wiped the glass with her sleeve, making sure it was clean, as her honey-colored reflection looked back at her. She took a breath and cast.

“Pika. Kalma. Syneth.”

As soon as she finished speaking the magical words, threads burst out of her chest in every direction. There were hundreds of them, running through the walls, the floor, and the ceiling, and out beyond the tower. Some were as thin as hair, while others were as thick as rope. Each bore a different hue of green, red, blue, or every shade in between, so many that the storage room disappeared behind them. With an effort of will, she was able to dull them down for a few seconds, only for the second layer to hit her. Her reflection in the mirror had her hands dripping with blood, though when she looked at her real palms in the candlelight, they were clean. The vision was clinging to her reflection, not her body. In the mirror, black tears ran from her eyes and down her cheeks, thick as ink. Trying to wipe them away only smeared blood over the black streaks. Her reflection’s wrists and ankles were enclosed in iron shackles chained to a heavy ball on the floor, the kind dragged behind a prisoner, and the longer she looked at it, the more she felt its weight pulling her down. None of the books she had read described anything like it, and she could not tell whether she was seeing the accounting of the universe or her own conscience.

Her knees buckled, and she let go of the spell.

Everything vanished, and she dropped to her hands and knees on the dusty floor, her stomach still convulsing from the spell. Since the Book Restoration spell had left her starving hours ago and she had not eaten since, there was nothing to bring up, so she dry-heaved until her ribs ached and the room finally stopped tilting.
