The millstream announced itself by the low persistent sound of water moving over stone somewhere in the mist.

“We’re close,” Clive said. He followed his ears. The ground dipped, the grass grew longer and darker at the roots. Then the stream was there, narrow enough to step across, running clear over a flat bed of pale rock.

He stood at the edge of it for a moment. The water was the color of glass. He could see every stone on the bottom. Compared to the rest of Vandiel with its shadowy aesthetics, this almost seemed pure in comparison.

They followed it north.

A stone wall emerged from the mist, running along a low ridge parallel to the stream, old enough that the mortar had mostly given way to moss. And along its base, exactly where the merchant had said: roses. Red ones crowding dense at the center, a few white ones at either end where the wall curved away, all of them still holding their petals against the damp air.

More than that, though.

The ridge beyond the wall opened into a shallow basin, sheltered from the wind, and the basin was full of flowers. Many he didn’t recognize. Low clusters of something white and star-shaped pressed up between the grass. Taller stalks carried blooms the color of embers, deep orange shading to red at the center. A drift of blue-violet ran along the far edge. They covered the ground the way wildflowers did, spilling from one side of the basin to the other.

He stood at the top of the ridge and looked at all of it.

Beside him, Azura was quiet for a moment. "I didn't expect this. They’re beautiful."

"Yea."

She descended the slope ahead of him, moving through the flowers. She crouched and turned one of the blue-violet blooms in her fingers without picking it, studying the way the petals were arranged.

“What is this?” she asked, holding out the blue-violet flower.

Clive shook his head. “No idea. But its beautiful.”

Clive followed her down toward the wall where the roses grew thickest. He didn't take the first ones he saw. He moved along the base of the stones slowly, looking, not for what was most obvious but for what was right. Full petals but not overblown. Color that went deep at the center rather than sitting flat on the surface. He found what he wanted a few feet in, and cut the stems long with his dagger, laying them across his forearm as he worked.

The red against the blue of the millwort. He found himself thinking in color theory without meaning to — warm against cool, the kind of contrast that pulled the eye without fighting itself. It was the same principle he used when he painted. You didn't need everything to match. You needed things to answer each other.

He added the white roses for balance, a few more stems of millwort from the bundle he'd carried out from the village. Held it at arm's length and looked at it honestly. The bones were right but it felt closed-off somehow. It felt too manufactured. Like something arranged rather than gathered.

Perhaps adding more flowers might do.

He looked out at the field.

"Azura. Pick a few of those — whatever looks right to you. Don't think about it."

She moved through the drift without hesitating, pulling stems here and there with no system he could identify. She didn't deliberate. She just picked. When she brought them back he worked them into the arrangement one at a time, and the difference was immediate — the looseness of them broke up the formality of the roses, made the whole thing look less like a studied composition and more like something gathered by someone.

"You have a good eye," he said.

"I have excellent eyes," she said, not modestly. "What do you think?"

He looked at the bouquet. Not perfect. A little asymmetrical, heavier on the left. But the colors moved well from one to the next, and the unknown blue-violet blooms tied the millwort to the roses in a way he hadn't anticipated.

"I think it's a good start," he said.

Azura looked at him. "For the flowers, or for her?"

He didn't answer that. He wrapped the stems in the cloth from the millwort bundle and they made their way back.

By the time they returned, it was already evening.

At the inn, Clive smelled something savory that made his mouth water.

Azura glanced at the kitchen door, then at him. "She's inside."

He knew. He could feel himself arranging not only the bouquet, but also the words he didn't have yet, the version of this that didn't go wrong somehow. He'd been doing that the whole walk back. It hadn't helped.

He opened the door.

Jill was at the hearth with her back to them, managing two pans. A haunch of dark meat sat to one side of the fire, and on the other, a pan of fish, skin down, just beginning to crisp at the edges. She didn't look up when they came in.

"Wash your hands before you touch anything," she said. "Both of you."

Azura went directly to the basin without complaint. Clive stayed in the doorway.

"You didn't have to cook," he said.

"I know." She turned the fish over. "But Azura ate everything in the larder last night and someone had to go to the market."

"Thank you."

She made a sound that wasn't quite a response. He crossed the room and set the bouquet on the table beside her — not handing it to her directly, just placing it there, where she'd see it when she turned around. Then he went and washed his hands.

He heard the moment she noticed.

When he turned back she was holding the bouquet at her side, looking at it. The roses, the millwort, the blue-violet blooms from the basin that neither of them knew the name of. She turned one of the unknown flowers between her fingers the same way Azura had, studying the petals.

"Where did you find these?" she asked.

"East of the village. Past the millstream." He dried his hands. "Someone told me there were wild roses along an old stone wall. The rest was already growing there."

She was quiet for long enough that the fish started to matter again. She set the bouquet carefully against the wall and turned back to the fire, adjusting the pan by feel.

"They're called duskbells," she said. "The blue ones. They only grow in sheltered places. I didn't know there was a field of them out there. And I've been here longer than you."

He didn't say anything to that.

She plated the fish and set it on the table, then the boar for Azura, who had already taken her seat. Jill sat down across from Clive and looked at the bouquet once more, briefly, before picking up her fork.

"Thank you," she said.

"I’m glad you liked it," he said. "I didn’t know if you would like it. Had to guess."

She looked at him then. Something moved across her face that he couldn't fully read, the way her expressions had always run a little ahead of what he could follow.

"You guessed right," she said, and turned to her meal.

The rest of dinner went quietly. Azura ate with single-minded focus and excused herself early, disappearing upstairs. Clive cleared the plates. Jill put the leftover fish away and wiped down the hearth. When there was nothing left to do she said goodnight and they both returned to their rooms.

He sat on the edge of his bed in the dark without lighting the lamp. When he looked out the window, he realized it was that time of the month again.

He went outside.

The village square was empty at this hour. Jill was sitting on the bench beside the village fountain, the bouquet resting on the seat next to her, looking up.

He followed her gaze. The moon was high and close.

He crossed the square and sat down on her other side, leaving the bouquet between them.

"You still remember our promise," she said.

"I've never forgotten."

She was quiet for a moment. The fountain ran. Somewhere in the dark a shutter moved against its latch in the breeze.

"I used to wonder if you would," she said. "After everything. I thought maybe you'd — I don't know. Move on from it. File it away somewhere as something that didn't transfer."

"It transferred."

She pulled one of the duskbells from the bouquet and turned it in her fingers, not looking at it, her eyes still on the moon. "We were on your balcony," she said. "It was late. I'd had a glass of wine, you'd had too much coffee, and we were arguing about something stupid. I can't even remember what."

"The film," Clive said. "Whether the book was better."

She glanced at him then. A small, involuntary thing. "The book was better."

"It was."

The corner of her mouth moved. It wasn't quite a smile but it was adjacent to one. She looked back at the moon.

"And then we stopped arguing," she said, "because the moon came out from behind the clouds, and it was full, and you said —"

"No matter where we end up," Clive said, "we look at the same moon."

She exhaled slowly. "I used to do it every night, even after I came here." She turned the duskbell once more and set it back with the others. "Every night. Out of habit, or stubbornness, or —" She stopped. "I don't know what. Some part of me that wished you would come back."

“I’m here now, Jill.”

She looked at him for a moment before returning her gaze to the moon.

"You are," she said.

They sat with that for a while. The fountain ran. The moonlight laid itself across the cobblestones in a way that softened every edge.

"Can I ask you something?" she said eventually.

"Yes."

She took a moment, as if deciding how to frame it. "You paint fire. Ice. Lightning." She tilted her head slightly toward the sky. "Have you ever tried to paint that?"

He looked up. "The moon?"
