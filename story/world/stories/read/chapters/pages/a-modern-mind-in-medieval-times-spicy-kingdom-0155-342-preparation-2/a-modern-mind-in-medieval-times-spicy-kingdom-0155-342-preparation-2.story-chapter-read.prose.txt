On my way back to the castle, I went through the forest of tents that surrounded the city under construction. The cloying smell of mold and damp earth was everywhere, overpowering everything else. My boots squelched on the muddy ground. Even with trenches dug to keep the water away, months of rain had turned it soggy.

Every tent was dirty; gray or brown in color, with edges frayed. They were not going to last through the winter, nor would they be enough protection from the cold. There were fewer of them now than when I had first laid my eyes on them, but not by much. Only a few hundred people had a sturdy roof over their heads. No wonder the people weren’t happy about me prioritizing the immigrants. It was a sunny day, so people had placed their clothes on top of tents to dry. Large spots of mold were visible on them.

The men swept the covers off the three field cannons. Gleaming bronze caught the morning light.

“Cannon one, ready!” he shouted.

One of the men unscrewed the breech, while another brought a brass cartridge forward.

Another stepped back and blew on his slow match.

A fourth one took the long cleaning rod and pushed it through the now open tube, scrubbing the inside clean.

As he stepped aside, the soldier carrying the cartridge pushed it inside.

The one holding the breech screw put it back in, while the third man punched a hole into the powder bag with a long spike, through the aligned holes of the cannon and cartridge, then shoved a fuse through it.

The crew commander, holding the slow match, approached, made sure the cannon was pointing correctly and gave the match to his number two.

“Fire!”

They cleared the cannon’s behind and the Number Two lit the fuse.

Nothing happened for a few seconds.

Then, the air rocked with an explosion.

A cloud of gray smoke billowed outwards from the cannon, which had rolled back several feet.

Downrange, dirt exploded from the already pockmarked hillside.
