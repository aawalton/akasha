It had been one day.

It felt like considerably longer.

Aiven sat on the edge of the wide bed in the room and turned it all over in his head for what was probably the hundredth time since he had woken up. The recessed mana-strips overhead held their warm, false afternoon.

The thing he could not get past was the gap.

Virelle had told him to die. The fireball had bloomed in her palm at point-blank range. He had been on his back on the red carpet, burned through his coat in five places, his breath gone, his shields gone, his everything gone. And he had woken up here, bandaged, in a vast Vulpine guest room with the linens turned down for him.

He had not died.

Cyria, by Lyra's account, had also not been harmed. She had come back on her feet. She was, somewhere in this same building, already running half the Vulpine operation again from a relay.

It did not add up.

His brain ran the ledger again, the way it had been running it on and off since the first hour of consciousness.

Lucien wanted him dead. That was not a hypothesis. Lucien had said so directly. Killing him would have cost Lucien absolutely nothing. It would have solved Lucien's largest standing problem in the work of a single breath.

It had not happened.

Lucien had not even been in the hall. Sylphaine and Valerion had been waiting in the antechamber — and Valerion had pointed at the door himself, leading Aiven to Virelle.

The arrangement had been deliberate.

Aiven had been meant to walk through that door alone. He had been meant to find her on the red carpet. He had been meant to receive whatever was in that room, exactly the way it had been delivered, and he had been meant to come out alive.

A hope began to settle in his chest, and he held it carefully, because hope was the thing that made you misread evidence.

He turned it slowly.

What if the room had been hers?

What if Virelle had asked for it? What if she had made a deal that traded her cooperation for his life and Cyria's. What if the staging of the room was the shape of an arrangement she had bargained for.

Let me deal with him alone. Let him live.

It would explain the gap.

It would explain why the mana blasts had hit him like blunt poles instead of cutting clean through him.

It would explain why she had lectured him, mocked him, called him filthy, smiled at him, fired barrage after barrage of fire and lightning and wind. She had given him a fight he could lose without dying. She had given him insults, the cruelest she could find, the kind designed to drive a man out of a room and not back into it.

It was the part designed to make sure he never came back.

A small, careful sound escaped him. It was almost a laugh, and there was nothing of joy in it.

It was a message.

It was Virelle, in her own theatre, with her own staging, telling him: I have chosen. He lives. You live. Do not come for me again.

He sat with it for a long time.

Then he rejected it.

Not the reading. The reading was almost certainly correct. He rejected the conclusion. He rejected the part of the deal that asked him to leave her there. He rejected the version of his future in which Virelle floated at Lucien's shoulder for the rest of her existence as a glorious tool, doing the work she had been made for, dying a little more each century inside a costume she had not chosen.

She had bargained for his life. Fine. He would use it.

The trouble was the math.

The math had been bad in the red room. The math had been worse in the hall before the door. Cyria was alive because Lucien had let her be alive. He was alive because Virelle had spent everything she had on a deal to make him so. He had walked into a room with three of them in the building and would not have walked out without Virelle's intervention. That was the floor.

He could not climb to her from this floor.

He needed to be stronger. Quickly. By orders of magnitude. The clerk-brain wanted to set the impossible aside and call this fantasy, and the clerk-brain could be ignored for a little while longer.

He needed information. He needed a teacher. He needed to know what Virelle actually was, what the Starweave actually was. He needed the parts of her past she had not been able to tell him because she had not remembered them, and the parts she could not tell him now because she had chosen not to.

He needed Cyria.

He stood up.

His ribs protested. His back protested. The lightning burn under the bandage protested. He set his weight carefully, found his feet, and made it to the door without his knees giving out.

He opened it.

Lyra was standing on the other side.

She jumped back half a step, hand pressed flat against her chest, and the small startled sound she made was almost a laugh once she recovered from it.

"Aiven!" She steadied herself. "I was — I was about to knock. Are you…where are you going?"

"To find Cyria."

He had meant it to come out steady. It did not, quite. He cleared his throat.

"I need to ask her about a few things."

Lyra looked at him.

She really looked. Up and down, in the way of a person who had been doing the small careful work of caring for someone and was now assessing whether their patient ought to be on his feet at all.

"Are you sure you're feeling all right?"

"Mostly. Some…stings. When I move."

"Stings."

"It's manageable."

"Aiven." She let out a long breath. "No. Sit. You don't need to walk halfway across the building. I'll go find her. She's somewhere downstairs working a relay. Just…please." Her hand came up. "Stay. Take care of yourself a little. I'll bring her up."

He opened his mouth.

He was going to argue. He was going to say it was fine, that he could walk, that he had been through worse, and then he looked at her.

Her eyes were tired in a particular way; the tired of someone who had spent the last day-and-a-half watching a door and not sleeping behind it. Her shoulders were drawn high and tight. There was a thin tense line between her brows that he had never seen in the Lyra of his childhood.

He closed his mouth.

"All right."

"All right." She exhaled. Some of the tightness eased. "Back to bed. Come on. I'll help you."

She slid one arm under his shoulder, the way she had done yesterday, and walked him the few paces back across the room. The bed swallowed him again. She tugged the cover up.

"I'll be back in a few minutes."

She paused at the door.

"Don't get up."

"I won't."

She gave him one last assessing look, and then she was gone.

Cyria arrived a quarter of an hour later.

She came in already talking.

"I have a rough guess," she said, the moment the door slid shut behind her, "as to what you want to ask me."

She crossed to the chair by the bed and folded herself into it with the easy contained grace she always had. Her bandages were minimal — a wrap across her throat where Daran's hand had been, a strip across one forearm. Her hair was a little less perfect than usual. Her eyes were as sharp as ever.

Lyra slipped in behind her and took up a position by the door, arms folded. She did not leave. She did not ask whether she should. Aiven did not ask her to.

He pushed himself up against the pillows.

"I need to get her back, Cyria."

He had said it before he had quite arranged how to say it. It came out plain.

"I know I can't. Not now. Not the way I am. The fight in that room, she could have killed me ten times over. She held back. I don't know what kind of deal she made, but she made one. Lucien knows it. He let it happen. And I — I can't —" He swallowed. "I can't let it stand. I have to get stronger. Quickly. Faster than I've been growing."

Cyria's expression did not change.

She nodded once. She had already worked through this. He could see it in her face. She had been thinking ahead of him, possibly since the moment they had been spat back out onto the grass.

"There is no one in the Vulpine network," she said, "who can teach you what you need. We have specialists. We have artifact-makers, combat instructors, a handful of D-rank tutors. None of them are at the level you have already passed. Virelle was the only one good enough for the work."

"Then I need you to find someone who is."

Cyria's tail flicked once.

She did not answer immediately. She turned her face slightly toward the recessed mana-strips, in the small considering posture he had come to recognize as her thinking posture. The tail twitched again, slower.

A long pause.

Then she said, mildly:

"Tell me. Which race has the highest magical aptitude in this world?"

Aiven blinked.

The question caught him sideways. He worked through the obvious answers; humans, the various beastfolk lineages, even the vampires. None of them fit. Magical aptitude in the formal academy sense pointed in only one direction.

"Elves."

"Elves." Cyria's gaze came back to him. "Yes."

He sat very still.

"There is a continent," she said, "across the eastern sky, that is theirs. They do not particularly welcome visitors. They do not particularly acknowledge visitors. But the Vulpine has had threads of contact with them for a while, and I have one or two relationships I have not had cause to call upon in a long time."

She let the thought settle in the room.

"If you wish to be taught at the level you require, in the time you have, that is where we go. The masters there can do in a month what would take a year anywhere else. If they decide to."

"And if they don't decide to?"

"Then we shall convince them."

Cyria's expression did something small at the corners of her mouth. It was not quite a smile.

"And." Her voice lowered a touch. "I think it is also the place where you will begin to find answers about what your Virelle actually is. About the Starweave. About what Lucien meant, in any of his speeches, when he said the things he said to her."

Aiven held still.

The room held with him.

He had walked into this conversation knowing only that he needed to be stronger and that Cyria might know how. He was leaving it with a destination, a thread to follow on Virelle's origin, and the first real shape of a plan since he had come back without her.

It was not enough.

It was the first thing in two days that resembled enough.

"When can we go?"

"Tomorrow," Cyria said. "If you are feeling fine. The day after, if you are not. I will have the arrangements moving within the hour."

He nodded.

She rose.

At the door, she paused, and looked back at him.

"Aiven."

"Yes?"

"It is good to see you sitting up."

She let herself out.

Lyra hesitated for a moment at the door. She glanced between him and the closing door and the bed. Then, without saying anything, she crossed back to the chair Cyria had just vacated and sat in it.

She did not pick up the tray. She did not fuss. She simply sat, with her hands folded in her lap, and watched him think.

The mana-strips held their warm afternoon.

Outside, somewhere beyond the Vulpine walls, the sky over Fangreach was clear.

Aiven looked at the cover folded across his lap and worked it through, slowly, the shape of what came next.

He would need the help from the elves.
