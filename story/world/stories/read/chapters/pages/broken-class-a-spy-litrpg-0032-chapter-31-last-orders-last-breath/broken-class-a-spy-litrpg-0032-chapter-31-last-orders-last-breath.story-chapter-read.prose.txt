The night pressed in cold and heavy, settling into their clothes, their bones. Tannis lay still at their feet, her eyes still open.

A growl rumbled behind them.

Rowler’s head snapped toward the sound, then he turned away. “We move. Now.”

No one argued. There was no time for burial rites. No time for guilt. Just motion.

We ran tight and quiet, trying to keep formation, but Brigg’s limp threw the rhythm off. I kept adjusting my pace, gritting my teeth. He muttered an apology. I didn’t answer. No one did. Every snap of a branch felt too loud. The trees closed in, lined up like they were guiding us somewhere. And not anywhere good.

Then came the snap of twigs. Fast. Close.

Two creatures burst from the trees. Nasty ape-like monsters that stood just over six feet tall when upright. Their muscular frames were covered in coarse, slate-colored fur interwoven with jagged patches of compacted stone. Their disproportionately long arms ended in claws as sharp as obsidian blades, capable of tearing through bone and armor with terrifying ease. Stoneclaw Howlers.

Rowler’s eyes went wide. “If they howl, we’re done.”

He planted his feet and raised both hands. Ice flickered around his fingers, bright against the dark.

“Muzzle Frost!”

A flash of cold whipped outward. The air snapped, and frost laced across the howlers’ snouts. Their jaws locked shut mid-growl.

It worked, but Rowler collapsed to a knee, gasping. “Cover me!”

The howlers charged.

Kell darted forward, driving his blade toward the creature’s chest. But it twisted, taking the hit in the shoulder. It staggered, then rebounded fast, claws lashing out.

Another leapt onto Brigg. The big man caught it with his shield, but it slipped around the edge, claws raking across his side. He roared in pain and went down hard, blood already soaking through his tunic.

I sprinted in, cleaved into the monster’s side. It shrieked, staggered. I stabbed again, this time through the spine. It dropped.

By the time I turned, Kell was already down. The Howler stood over him, its claws slick with blood. His sword lay a few feet away, half-buried in the moss

Then it raised its head, sniffed the air, and turned toward Rowler.

He stumbled back, pale and barely upright. “Hark….help!”

I didn’t move.

He saw me watching.

“Hark!”

But I was already running.

Branches slapped at my face. Roots snatched at my boots. Behind me, the forest lit with snarls and metal and pain. I didn’t look back.

The shouting faded. So did the sound of blades.

Ten minutes later, I collapsed under a gnarled oak. My lungs burned. My legs shook. I tried to slow my breathing.

Then I heard it.

A shuffle behind me. A low, breathy snort.

I turned too late.

The beast was massive. Bear-like.

It swiped once. My leg split open.

I screamed and dragged myself backward, dirt and leaves grinding into the open wound on my leg. The thing didn’t rush. It followed at a slow, deliberate walk, like it knew I couldn’t escape.

I tried to rise. Another blow crashed into my back, hard enough to dent the armor. I hit the ground face-first, the world spinning. My fingers fumbled for the dagger at my belt.

It stepped on my wrist.

Bones crunched. I howled.

Then it leaned in, slow and silent. I saw its eyes: small, black, unblinking. I saw its teeth: thick, yellow, ridged for tearing.

Its jaws clamped down on my shoulder. The armor held for a moment, just long enough to give me hope. Then I heard the metal bend.

Agony flooded in. I felt the bite sink through, felt flesh tear and muscle snap. My body jerked, but the strength was gone.

I think I screamed. Or maybe I didn’t.

Its claws raked across my back, found the gap in the plating.

They dug in.

And then everything went still. Not around me, inside me. The pain was too much, too sharp. My body couldn’t hold it, so my mind let go.

It was like watching from a distance. The beast crouched over me, its breath steaming against my cheek. I knew I should be screaming. Should be kicking. Should be fighting. But all I could do was stare. The forest tilted sideways. My limbs weren’t mine anymore.

I heard the wet sounds: the tear of fabric, the snap of bone. I felt pressure, not pain. Just dull thuds and dragging heat, like someone else was being mauled a few feet away.

My head lolled to the side. Leaves blurred into darkness. I could still hear my heartbeat. Slower now. Fainter.

The monster tore something loose from my back. I didn’t even flinch. Not from courage. Just emptiness.

This isn’t happening to me, I thought. It was like watching someone else. The coward who ran, the fool who abandoned his squad. I didn’t know him. Didn’t want to. Because knowing him meant being him.

A crunch near my ribs. A flood of warmth across my stomach. My eyes fluttered. Still open.

Somewhere far off, a bird called. Or maybe it was just the wind.

The monster kept going. Methodical. Like it was unmaking me piece by piece.

And I just watched.

Waiting for the world to blink out.

Arthur stepped into the clearing without hurry.

The body lay twisted near the roots, spine torn open, one arm bent the wrong way.

He knelt beside it, pulled the short sword from the half-sheath, and held it for a moment in his hand. Still sharp. Still clean enough to use.

The Agency had trained him not to dwell. Not to feel. Eliminate, move on.

But this time, something settled in his chest. Not anger. Not grief.

Satisfaction.

These men had treated people like bait, like parts. Had laughed while others bled.

Now they were gone.

It wouldn’t change the world. Wouldn’t bring justice to the ones already lost.

But it was a start.

No mission. No handler in his ear. Just him. And the choice to do what felt right.

Arthur stood, tucked the blade away, and vanished into the trees.
