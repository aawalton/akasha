Ethan looked down at the unassuming little man sitting before him, contemplating him like a cat would an injured mouse.

Could be an illusion, he thought. But – no. Appraisal doesn’t lie…

His eyes bore into the soul of the feeble-looking God. And he saw what he needed to make him believe:

Creature Identified: KAEDMON (LVL: ???)

HP: INFINITE

WILL: INFINITE

WEAKNESSES: NULL

The old man’s eyes regarded him with joviality.

“Honestly,” he said. “You could have just asked me for my stats.”

Ethan, still standing, regarded him dispassionately.

“You think I would trust you?”

The old God seemed mightily entertained by the question.

“You think I attempt to deceive you? Me? Begging your pardon, but I find that those who distrust are often themselves untrustworthy individuals. After you blindsighted me with that rather brilliant display of bait and switch in Argwyll’s core, I knew that no illusions or tricks of mine would work with you any longer.”

He rocked back in his chair like a kid that was satisfied with its own joke. But Ethan wasn’t laughing with him.

“After everything that’s happened, and after seeing just how performative you can be, can you really fault me for thinking this is all an elaborate ruse? A way for you to appeal to my sense of fairness so that I let my guard down?”

“Point taken,” Kaedmon nodded. “I suppose it is just exciting for me to put my life in the hands of another, for once. You’ll have to forgive me, Ethan. I’m quite old. And possibly senile.”

Ethan scoffed. “Is that the excuse you’re going to make for everything you’ve done? That you’re mad?”

Kaedmon narrowed his impossibly blue eyes.

“I am vain enough that I would like to be remembered as a cognizant old man. One who showed his greatest foe some modicum of respect before he shuffled off his divine coil. To that end, if it at all matters to you, I would offer you an explanation of sorts. I think I owe you that much.”

“You owe all of Argwyll more than that,” Ethan quipped. Though he could feel the tension that had been building in him begin to relent. Each of his Hosts, in turn, were reacting to the energy of the God-Man. The maker. They each wanted to understand the meaning behind their existence and all the suffering they’d gone through.

But the oddly jovial eyes of this old man quietened Ethan.

“You…are not what I expected.”

Kaedmon smiled.

“You were probably expecting a lot of things, Ethan,” he said. “You were probably expecting some large, climactic battle on a cosmic scale. Something long, with extraneous movements, taunts, and agonizingly lengthy strategic special skill usage. Moves and counter-moves that could take up entire Acts of a play. And we could do that. We could dance around each other till the suns of each and every universe out there die away. But I rather think it would be all for naught. The outcome would be unavoidable: I would perish.”

From the stage came the sound of trumpets – like the signals before a great battle. It seemed that Kaedmon’s little ‘production’ was about to begin.

“Since that’s the case,” he continued, unperturbed. “I don’t see any reason for us to do battle. To be honest, I find it to be a rather droll experience – two men panting and sweating as they lay into each other. All that rather oddly masturbatory verbal back-and-forth that goes along with it. It is an inelegant state of affairs.”

The stage lights turned on – torch sconces that flared to life with dazzling blue, indigo, and purple flames. The light was blazing, all-consuming. It was all Ethan could do not to shield his eyes from the glare.

Kaedmon, however, simply sat back and sighed, contented.

“I would much rather that I was remembered as a deity who held a polite conversation with a fellow gentleman on the nature of his own mortality, and what that has made him realize, rather than be reduced to little more than a dismal screech flying into the dust of an uncaring universe. Conversely, you could simply do what you came here to do and ‘get it over with’, as it were. But I rather think you owe me, too, Mr Hawke. After all, I stalled this production for you for some time.”

Ethan couldn’t help but laugh. His ‘confrontation’ with the God was so ridiculous that it bordered on parody. But he understood what was happening here. He was being given a choice: strike Kaedmon down now and gain all his knowledge for himself or indulge the old man in telling his tale with his own voice.

Looking at the feeble, wrinkled being sitting in the theater chair before him, it was hard to believe that this was a being whose hands were bloodied with the deaths of hundreds of thousands. That this was the deity who had overseen centuries of genocide beggared only disbelief.

And yet, that might be exactly what he wants me to think…

He couldn’t shake that thought. Appearing as an old, frail human – and a lover of stories at that - might just be a way to tug at Ethan’s heart-strings enough for him to let his guard down. But then, what could the old man do? This was his realm, it was true, but Ethan held the cards. The Judgement ability would work on him. He could feel it, even though he hadn’t dealt the God a single blow. He could end it all right now. Thinking about it, that’s probably what Sys would tell him to do.

And just where the hell was Sys, anyway?

But, with a sigh, he sat down beside the aged God and leaned back in his appointed chair. Because when all was said and done, he did want to know. He wanted to hear from the divine horse’s mouth why all this chaos had been allowed to happen in his name.

“Talk,” he said. “But don’t try anything. You know that I can best you with a single skill if I have to.”

Kaedmon smiled. “I won’t be doing anything,” he said. “Except for enjoying the last play of my life. I do hope it’s a good one.”

“Didn’t you write it?”

“In a manner of speaking,” Kaedmon chuckled. “The premise blooms in my mind. I set down the first words. But the characters – they always change the story in such unpredictable ways. I can provide a direction. But they give it life.”

Before Ethan could ask the apparently senile God what the hell he meant by that, the show began.

The stage filled with life, and the great hanging emblems high above began to glow as images stuttered into being on their smooth surfaces. Images that were all too familiar to Ethan.

Each oval symbol showed the image of an Archon, and their Lightborn. Each one paired up, each one in chronological order.

The ‘play’ then began. Ethan marveled as a few stick-like spirits with blank white faces mimed the first Archon’s coming to Argwyll. One mime came out wearing a pair of leather-black wings, with a crude dragon mask on his featureless face. The background of the theater was painted jet-black, and a chill air was pumped into the room from a place unknown to Ethan.

“Your prop department needs some work,” he whispered to Keadmon. “But the special effects aren’t bad.”

Though crude, he couldn’t help but feel a sense of camaraderie as Archon Karfaang ‘flew’ around the stage, suspended on a set of two strings above a crowd of faceless mimes, each one clapping for him and screaming their joy to the winds.

Then the background of the scene changed. The dawn came with a burning painted sun. And out of this sun stepped a crudely designed Angel-mime.

Beneath her long, flowing robe, little mimes ran amok – each one bearing a blade and urging her forward with cries of war. And the scene changed from one of tranquility to one of conflict almost instantly.

“You knew that he lived in peace with the Tialax,” Ethan said. “So, why get Krea involved at all? Why begin the cycle?”

Kaedmon’s smile briefly dropped at the question. He mulled it over while they watched Krea and Karfanng flail at each other in mock-battle, little red-ribbons spurting from Karfanng’s side every now and then when Krea’s ‘sword’ swung toward the air before him.

“All things cast a shadow. And no man can ever escape his. Don’t you think, Ethan?”

The black dragon cried out in pain as Krea sank her blade into its head.

“You’re telling me you had no choice? That you’re bound by the cycle yourself?”

“The Cycle,” Kaedmon sighed. “A name we give to the indisputable structure of the universe. I created my Law to make sense out of it. To give the people what they want – what they always want: a semblance of order in the chaos of an uncaring universe.”

Ethan scoffed as he watched the scene change to one of tranquility in the wake of Karfanng’s death.

“Your ‘order’ includes the periodic genocide of an entire species. You call that justice?”

“I – I did not wish to consign the Hybrids to an infinite stream of death. But there must be an Enemy. That is what I have learned from all the eons I have spent alive. In order to preserve society, one people must be chosen. Only then can a thing endure.”

“Spoken like a true Despot,” Ethan said. “You’re justifying slaughter in the name of what you call social progress.”

Keadmon shook his head sadly.

“I told you I will not try to justify anything,” he said. “Only that I would offer an explanation. I don’t even promise you that it will be one you will accept. After all, you have already judged me, haven’t you?”

“Someone has to.”

“Someone already has,” Keadmon sighed again. And before Ethan could make another protest, he pointed towards the stage where a new character had suddenly appeared out of fake smoke and ash.

Slowly, Ethan realized who it was supposed to be. Through the crude costume of matted fur stitched together in what looked like a rushed First-Grader’s home-ec project, he could see the resemblance to Jun’Ei that the creator was getting at.

She bowed and then chuckled mischievously like a mustache twirling villain. At her feet lay the body of the fallen Archon, while the moon hung low behind her.

“For every light, there must be shadow,” Keadmon said. “For all order, there must be chaos. Or is it the other way around? It is difficult to tell when it comes to the Cycle.”

Ethan watched the Jun-mime touch the fallen dragon gingerly, then close her eyes and howl to the moon. A small wisp of smoke billowed from a trap-door and covered the downed Archon’s body. Ethan assumed that this was to symbolize the ‘resurrection’ ritual – Jun’Ei’s taking of one spirit from Earth and transporting it to Argwyll to assume the mantle of the new Archon.

“She was my shadow,” Keadmon said. “Unlike me, she was bound to the world. Unlike me, who longed for a constantly repeating past, she longed for an unburdened future. She brought the first Archon to this realm. I brought the Lightborn. And our grand game began.”

Ethan watched a mime with Kaedmon’s face plastered over its own featureless void appear beside Jun. The Lycae prophetess turned, and both then bowed to the other as though announcing that the prologue of the show was over. Now, the real show was about to begin.

And begin it did. The image of Karfanng and Krea at the top of the stage curtain dimmed and the image of Gelsaadra and Lightborn Casavir began to glow.

Ethan got the idea. This was a play in Five Acts, it seemed…

And that drew him right back to Keadmon sitting beside him.

“A Five Act structure,” Ethan murmured. “Don’t tell me that’s why…”

The God flashed him a dry, humorless smile while Gelsaadra began rallying her armies against a poorly painted desert backdrop.

“You wish to know: why five cycles? Well. It is her doing, Ethan. Not mine. She saw her death in the Fifth Cycle – your Cycle. She saw it and thus knew she had five chances. One every century. I must say, before your coming she was getting rather desperate. She knew her powers could never match my own. And yet – as the fourth Archon perished – we were both given the first surprise we had seen in 400 years.”

They watched the procession go on – from Archon to Lightborn, defeat and rebirth, one tragedy after another played out against a backdrop that became darker and darker until the background was painted with nothing but darkness. Center stage, a dozen mimes stood on each others’ shoulders wearing a Gyko costume, mimicking the movement of the great Demon Flower as she choked the world with her poison.

Then one mime jumped out from the side curtain wearing a ridiculous mop of grey-white hair. Two goggle eyes, each facing a different direction, jutted out of his formless face. He waved around a wooden sword limply, as though his body just wouldn’t let him act properly.

And Ethan felt eyes behind his own react – ever so slightly – to the sight.

“Artorious.”

Kaedmon nodded, shoulders sagging at the sight of his latest champion strutting towards Gyko and ‘cutting’ down her minions on stage.

“I didn’t see him coming. As far as I could tell, he was the same as any other Lightborn. I made him that way, anyway. And yet as time went on – there was something different. Something nobody else had.”

Ethan watched the bungling figure sway this way and that – like a confused drunkard, both eyes spinning madly around with his every step as he slowly made his way towards the towering plant-demon.

“Doubt,” Ethan said.

They watched the moment he sunk his blade into Gyko’s shuddering body. And they heard her laugh mockingly in his face.

For as lousy as the production quality was overall, the effect was felt. Ethan could feel the Arty behind his eyes quiver as he re-lived the moment of his failure.

“You’re saying you had nothing to do with it?”

“I’ve thought about that. It might have happened by accident but…I think I put too much of myself into him. Like painters laboring over a blank canvas for months on end, throwing their soul into producing something wondrous, who tend to end up producing little more than a reflection. And to them, their work will always be ugly as a result. Because we never truly see the good in ourselves.”

They watched as the grey smoke of Jun’Ei’s resurrection swept across the stage again, and the Arty-actor let out a cry of shame and sorrow all in one.

“You couldn’t have…removed his doubt?” Ethan asked. “I thought Gods were supposed to have full command over their subjects.”

It was Keadmon’s turn to scoff, this time.

“Of course not. I’m just the playwright, remember? I set down the words, form the premise, put things in motion. But characters don’t always follow the rules – even Archetypical ones.”

“Then they cease to be an Archetype,” Ethan said. “They become –“

“Real.”

Ethan glanced at the old God, who now seemed perfectly entranced. He could easily take him out now, were it not for the certainty that he had to see this play to its end, now. Despite how crummy the acting was, he got the feeling that Kaedmon wanted him to see the ending. Because it felt like he knew exactly how it would all go down.

He saw himself awaken – a crude little puppet-hat being controlled by some mime underneath the fake-rock that had been placed on stage. He watched as the puppeteer flopped him around, wearing different monsters as his Hosts, having his first confrontation with Artorious, being saved by Tara, Klax, and Fauna and discovering Sanctum…

And, bizarrely, he heard Kaedmon chuckling beside him.

“You were certainly the most entertaining of the lot of them,” he laughed. “More than a match for poor dear Artorious Pendragon.”

As the play dragged on, showing each of his own adventures through the Delves, Haylock’s dungeon, and his rampage through Westerweald, Ethan couldn’t help but turn his scorn against the one person he was sure deserved it.

“Don’t you have any regret?” he asked. “Does the death of your ‘characters’ mean anything to you?”

“Ethan,” he replied. “Regret is all I have.”

“Then why keep it all going?” Ethan suddenly shouted – incensed by the sorrow that was evident in the old God’s voice. “The Cycle could be stopped at any point – by you. You must know that Jun wouldn’t have stopped. After all, it was her people you were aggressing against in the first place. All of this could have been avoided if – you’d simply been able to let go.”

Kaedmon put a skinny finger to his choppy lips.

“Hold on,” he said. “We're getting to the most interesting part.”

Ethan turned his attention back to the stage and saw himself – as he was now – the dark angel that had conquered Argwyll. He stood triumphant against a sea-blue sky backdrop, the sounds of ocean waves being piped into the theater to simulate a sea setting.

Then, a trapdoor opened, and a tiny black rock emerged from beneath the stage.

“The Cradle…” Ethan murmured.

He watched the mime pantomime a victory dance over the sea-sky, with cheers and barks and hisses now accompanying the sound of ocean waves. Slowly, as the dance wore on, the lights in the theater dimmed, and a darkness that felt all too real crept in to the performance.

“Here it comes…” Keadmon whispered.

Ethan eyed him.

"What?"

"...The End."
