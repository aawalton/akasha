Chapter 53

Ritsuko Asami

The train dropped her off at Shinjuku Station. As the doors opened, humid summer air rushed in. She pulled off her headphones—and the music died.

Tokyo was still the concrete jungle she remembered, though now there were more green spaces.

Unlike her days in Wishper, she wasn’t carrying a mission briefcase—but she did have a hidden weapon in her boot, just in case. She had a backpack, a digital rental card, and a somewhat reckless idea: returning to the private investigation business.

Aika and a few old acquaintances had put up a bit of a fight, but she didn't change her mind. They might not be as serious as the dangers she had faced around the world, but anything involving the supernatural and the Dark Events was no laughing matter.

Her body might not bear a single scar from those cases, but those memories were certainly etched into her mind. The days of undercover work, bullets grazing her cheeks, or the days—sometimes weeks—of recovery after she’d been wounded and had nearly lost her life in the past.

That’s why she knew what she was facing, but she still thought that compared to her past life, it might not be so dangerous. Compared to those years with Miho and the cases she solved in that first decade, she now had years of experience. But more than anything, she wanted to help. That, at least, hadn’t faded, and the period of hibernation had caused her to wake up again, ready and in high spirits.

After the research she had done in previous days, she met with the person she was expecting and finally got down to business.

She had found a small apartment on a corner in Kabukicho—the kind of shady building that always seemed on the verge of demolition, but never actually was. The rent was reasonable—a relative term in Tokyo—and the landlord, a man who seemed not to want to know anything about his tenants’ lives, was surprisingly cooperative. Mai took it as a good sign… or a warning.

The apartment was on the fourth floor, with a direct view of a neon sign that flickered insistently all night. A former massage parlor occupied the floor below, and on the ground floor, a jazz bar, advertised by the neon sign, opened at dusk and closed after sunrise. Not exactly the type of environment one would associate with “professionalism,” but it had what she needed: discretion and quick access to the heart of the city. On the other hand, bars had always been a gathering place for the underworld. That was something she liked.

For the next two weeks, she set up the space. She brought one of the desks of Miho from her home and the old coffee maker that had probably survived two wars already, and an armchair she described as “comfortably suspicious.”

The sign on the door was discreet:

Consulting and Private Investigation Office — Asami Ritsuko

Nothing more. Nothing less.

Of course, she didn’t want to use her real name. In Tokyo, names carried weight, and Mai Izumi carried too many shadows. So she opted for a new identity, clean and unremarkable: Ritsuko Asami, red hair, reading glasses, and a slightly altered accent. The nanomaterial hair dye, applied instantly with a clip, worked so well that even under ultraviolet light the change looked natural. “Red suits me,” she told herself the first time she looked in the mirror with her new appearance.

The idea was keep a low profile, offer investigative services in cases related to the paranormal—Dark Events, unusual disappearances, lost objects that no one could find—and, in the process, maintain a network of contacts in the occult world who still trusted her. The Inoue Section had secured the necessary authorization for her to operate “officially,” meaning the police wouldn’t intervene as long as she stayed quiet. In theory. She had considered calling herself a private detective, but she doubted the police would welcome it warmly, even if the older officers knew who she was.

It wasn’t a new job for her after all. She had done exactly that: tracking rumors, following leads, helping the police, and charging for information no one else could get. But this time, she would do it on her own rules again. No bosses, no chains, no protocols.

Her first Monday of opening passed with the calm of a funeral. She commuted, arrived at the station, stopped by the Shinjuku police station to sow some goodwill as she had in the past—part of her techniques for making a good impression on those who didn’t yet know her—she handed them the business cards she had prepared and explained that she would be working independently of the Inoue Division, but that she would always be willing to lend a hand if they needed consulting services.

Then she went to the office. She turned on the coffee maker, checked the local ley-line network connection with an talisman Aika did—which, fortunately, remained stable—and updated the logs on her new computer.

By midday, she was already thinking of closing early for lunch when she heard three sharp knocks on the door.

She checked the security camera and was surprised.

When she opened the door, two familiar figures were smiling as if they were home.

“So this is how you greet your old friends?” said Miria, in her humanoid form, leaning a hand on the doorframe. She wore sunglasses, a dress that looked straight out of a cyberpunk fashion catalog, and the same carefree attitude as always.

“We come in peace,” added Nerea, with her poker-faced expression and slightly more discreet look: a long skirt and short-sleeved blouse, holding a bag of rolls as if it were a sacred offering. “And with a belated breakfast.”

Nerea had almost white gray hair and black eyes that accentuated her freckles and pale face. Her dark circles gave her a tired appearance, but that was just her normal look. Those dark circles under her eyes were the reason why some of her acquaintances called her Panda.

Mai hugged both. And Miria took the opportunity to do the same, while Nerea looked like she was about to suffer from heatstroke.

“It’s too hot to be all over each other—get away from me. Come back in December,” Nerea complained.

“Shut up and accept my octopus hug,” said Mai.

“Octopus? So you’ve finally turned into one of those hentai monsters. Don’t eat my clothes, little moth.”

Neither of them had changed a bit, Mai thought.

When she moved away, Nerea held out the bag of rolls again.

“It’s already noon…”

Nerea pouted. “I just woke up, bitch. Take it, or I’ll eat them myself.”

Mai raised an eyebrow but couldn’t help smiling. She just sighed and accepted one.

“How did you find me so fast?”

“Oh, please Mai-ichi.” Miria gently pushed Miria more inside. “You’re in Kabukicho, not a Tibetan monastery. You left a digital trail as discreet as a parade of monkeys with machine guns.”

Nerea raised a hand. “I just saw you coming here from the station a couple days ago.”

“You didn’t stop to say hello.”

“I was busy. I just watched you go.”

“When did you guys get here?”

“Just a little while ago,” said Miria. “Since you woke up, we stopped by to see what you were up to.

I got here three days ago, but I was hanging out with some people I ran into.”

“And you?” Mai asked, looking at Nerea.

“Just a few days ago. I saw you around town, that’s all. You were wearing the same outfit.”

“You’re the kind of pervert who can recognize someone by their hips. It’s the only way you’d detect me with this new look.”

“You have a big ass like always.”

“No need to remind me that. So you’re not denying you can recognize someone that way.”

“It’s a skill only wise perverts possess,” Miria nodded, smiling.

“I’m joking.” Nerea interjected, as she set the bags on the desk. “The Inoue Section told us you were ‘reinventing yourself.’ It sounded suspiciously boring to be true, so we came to check.”

“I doubt they just gave you the address that easily.”

“They didn’t give it to me. I had to hack it. They need to improve their system.”

“Please stop getting me into trouble. I’m going to have to apologize for you if they find out.”

It really was the same old Nerea and Miria. Nerea was always a bit impulsive, and on more than one occasion she’d gotten scolded for acting without listening. But she wasn’t a bad girl, she’d always been likable.

Obviously, the news that she was planning something must have reached them through Wishper too. She hadn’t imagined the two would be so close by.

Mai sighed and sank into her swivel chair, which let out a pitiful squeak. “Well, as you can see, here I am. Alive, functional, and officially back in business.”

Nerea glanced around the place. “Minimalist. I like it. Has that vibe of ‘if something explodes, at least there’s not much to clean.’”

Mai gave a nervous smile. “Please don’t make anything explode…”

Miria laughed as she opened one of the windows. “And with a view of the ugliest neon sign in the neighborhood. Perfect for meditating.”

From the street, a dark-haired man looked up—it was the bar owner. “Who said that?”

Miria slipped inside and discreetly closed the window. “Sooorry,” she muttered as she hid.

“Please don’t make the neighbors hate me.”

“The place isn’t bad,” Miria said, trying to change the subject.

“I rented it because it was available and nobody else wanted it,” Mai replied, shrugging. “I would’ve taken the first floor, but it’s noisy with the bar in the evening. Seemed like a good sign.”

“Do you already have clients?” Miria asked, curious.

“Not yet. But I’m sure someone with problems will show up at any moment. This is Tokyo, after all. If there’s no vengeful spirit or a family curse nearby, it’s only because the month just started.”

The three laughed, and for a moment, the office didn’t feel so empty for Mai as in the morning. There was something comforting in that unplanned visit, as if the world wasn’t so far from being put back together.

Nerea leaned against the window frame, watching the street below. “I can’t believe you’re back. I thought after Wishper you’d disappear for much longer.”

Mai took a sip of coffee and smiled. “That’s what I tried. But, you see, I’m not good at staying still. Not that I was putting a target on my head saying, ‘Here I am.’”

“No, definitely not,” said Miria. “You made a lot of enemies.”

“Look who’s talking,” Nerea retorted.

Mai raised an empty cup in an ironic gesture. “Here’s to that. To new beginnings… and old habits, that never die.”

The three clinked their paper cups as the jazz bar sign outside flickered to life under the afternoon light. Outside, Tokyo kept spinning relentlessly. Inside, a new story had just begun.

“By the way, do you need any help?” Miria asked.

Mai had thought life in Kabukichō would be quieter during the first days. She was wrong, of course. It only took a week for the first supernatural cases to start coming in more frequently, some sent by the Inoue Section, as if the neighborhood were a magnet for everything that lurked in the shadows.

An abandoned building with lights that turned on at midnight without any electricity, an empty clinic where breathing and heartbeat sounds echoed as if the place were alive, or that restaurant where the plates returned to the kitchen on their own. Small things at first, where she even had to ask Aika for help because they involved spirits. Then came the bigger cases.

Nerea proved to be an invaluable help. She had always been so in Wishper as the heavy hand, but her technological skills gave her a new utility. She could track forums, private databases, and encrypted networks that not even the police knew existed. In the office, while Mai reviewed reports and recordings, Nerea worked across three screens at once, sipping cold coffee and humming old video game tunes.

“This is an occult forum, but 80% are just people looking for dates, people who like these types of themes. Basically summon your own goth boyfriend or girlfriend,” she muttered one afternoon while searching for leads on a summoning case. “Though, see, the other 20% can actually summon things with these digitized old books, so I guess there’s balance. Although you'd have to be crazy to try to get a date with your own angel through the Abramelin ritual.”

Mai barely looked up from the file she was reading. “Perfect. Tell that 80% to stay busy with that. They’re saving us work. The last thing we need is an infestation case with a bunch of people summoning spirits into androids.”

Meanwhile, Miria had found her own place. The jazz bar owner offered her a job after she spent nearly every night there listening to the local musicians. She had a talent for the atmosphere: serving drinks with a smile that even the grumpiest customers couldn’t ignore.

“Apparently my presence boosts tips,” she commented one day, happy.

Mai raised an eyebrow. It was probably due to Miria’s aura. It was better if no one knew she actually had a part-equine body and giant wings.

The bar became the three of their meeting point. Sometimes, after solving a case or surviving a tense week, they would go there to disconnect. Mai usually ordered some beer or something strong. Nerea preferred cocktails with unpronounceable names, and Miria, of course, never paid. They talked about everything—what had happened during the week, their days in Wishper, places they’d been, old companions who were no longer around. And when the conversation got too serious, Miria or Nerea always found a way to break the tension with some nonsense.

“If I ever disappear because of a Dark Event, make sure to check my folders and history. I want the world to inherit my memes, not have them swallowed by a dimensional portal,” Nerea said one slightly drunk day.

“Your memes are terrible,” Mai said, taking a sip.

Nerea opened one eye. “If you disappear, I’ll make sure to upload your search history, Miss Who-Likes-Outdoor-Threesomes videos.”

Mai spat out her drink, spraying Miria. “Stop spying on what I do on my computer!”

“If you say it out loud, there’ll be no need to upload anything, because you’re broadcasting it yourself.” Miria said, taking a napkin to wipe her face.

“Better not talk, you’re worse than us,” Nerea added.

“You’ve been spying on me too?”

Though they had their own routines, the three maintained a constant closeness. Nerea and Miria rented a small room in Kabukichō, but they often ended up at Mai’s house in Saitama, especially on weekends. Sometimes they slept over, other times they just used the kitchen and left a mess behind.

Mai protested, but also felt good about her new life with them around. She knew that, after so much time in missions and loss, this chaotic normality was the closest thing to a family she had in years.

Many of her human friends were no longer around, other feys had moved away over time, and even Aya had died without her knowing, only learning after the cremation. It filled her with sadness, but at least her friends consoled her. Nobody truly knew about her relationship with Aya, and everyone assumed she was just another friend. Mai couldn’t explain why Aya had been so important in her life, but since then she would visit the family grave where her ashes were kept, just as she had done for years for her father.

In the following months, work became constant. What began as a small task ended up becoming a reference point for everything the police preferred not to touch and the Inoue Section couldn’t officially take on due to bureaucratic processes that slowed everything down.

Supernatural events didn’t care about such protocols and seemed to multiply like a silent infection if not investigated and stopped—some subtle, others large enough to require entire teams. After investigating everything Mai, for her part, simply logged each case in a metal folder with color-coded labels and moved on. Sometimes solving the case, other times leaving it open due to its association with Dark Events, which often left loose ends.

She hated cases with loose ends, it had happened too many times in her life that some detail she’d overlooked—left unresolved—would eventually come back to haunt her like a boomerang she’d thrown at her own neck. But there was no other choice when there were no more leads left, and even Nerea's predictive models and the AI systems in the Inoue Section seemed unable to find a solution either.

Like the one that began with a string of suicides among users of a new anonymous virtual reality software. Everything initially pointed to mental collapse induced by excessive immersion, until Nerea discovered that something in the servers didn’t match any human-written code. An entity had materialized within the network, using the players’ data to manipulate their emotions and break their minds. It was a conscious code that had managed to do something unthinkable: infect the psyche like a virus to drive them to commit suicide.

Nerea immersed herself in the virtual environment with a modified visor, while Mai confronted the affected bodies in the physical world, already under the entity’s control. It was like fighting shadows with flesh. When Nerea managed to isolate the digital core, Mai destroyed the remaining equipment and sealed the server. Three days later, the game vanished from the network without official explanation, disappearing as if it had never existed.

A consultation with the Aeons suggested that the entire game had generated itself, as if the code had been written by invisible hands—a Dark Event manifesting in the digital world.

Another case led them to an industrial area in northern Tokyo, which began recording disappearances and strange lights in abandoned factories. Mai discovered a clandestine laboratory assembling cybernetic bodies for the black market. The Dark Events had interfered with the control chips, awakening a rudimentary consciousness in the cyborgs.

Mai and the police infiltrated the complex, disabling defense systems and neutralizing the rogue units, some capable of manipulating electric fields. The case drew the attention of several autonomous artificial intelligences, who considered the cyborgs an “emerging new species,” albeit a rudimentary one, and offered digital assistance for their deactivation. It was her first collaborative work with them. Nobody spoke of that factory again, though some claimed to have seen lights moving beneath the industrial floors even days after the investigation. Aika and other monks and priests performed a purification ritual, revealing that the so-called emerging species could have been nothing more than spiritual remnants of souls gathered in the location under the ground where centuries ago there was a cemetery.

On another occasion near her home, several kilometers from Lake Naguri, rumors began circulating about a hidden shrine and lights floating over the water. Mai traveled with Miria to investigate. They discovered that an ancient tutelary deity had been disturbed by experiments conducted by a group of scientists, seeking to exploit the vital energy of the Dragon Lines.

The entity’s awakening disrupted the ecosystem: animals exhibited aggressive behavior, plants grew in impossible shapes. Mai and Miria called in local experts and were able to seal the shrine after a confrontation combining magic and diplomacy, because the creature was not entirely malevolent… it was simply furious at being awakened by the disturbance of the underground Ley Lines. The area was restricted, and the case was classified as a “mixed-origin event.”

Beneath Harajuku, an underground market traded in arcane artifacts, bodies of deceased espers, and items from Dark Events. Mai, with support from Nerea, Miria, Aika, several Aeons, and undercover Wishper agents, infiltrated under a false identity.

The goal was not only to dismantle the network but also to free the souls trapped in the machines. Aika performed the purification rituals while Mai confronted the market leader: an engineer who had learned to generate small-scale artificial Dark Events by combining pieces from others. When the operation ended, only empty corridors and smoke remained in the subterranean sections. The press never mentioned any Dark Events—only a pipe explosion that required an evacuation.

Although Dark Events were now known, the aim was to keep the risk of mass hysteria minimal by classifying the matter, not just in Japan but in all countries. Besides, the press couldn’t report anything about the feys, and especially not that espers, magicians, humans, and Aeons were resolving cases together.

There was also the case of a newly opened luxury hotel in central Tokyo. Disappearances and guests returning with lifeless eyes, saying that they spent days lost in the hotel. Mai discovered that the building sat atop an ancient sacrificial site, where a spirit had been sealed centuries ago in an unknown cave system.

The caves had long since disappeared, but the spirit in the place had managed to strengthen its presence there, and that may have triggered the Dark Event, which transformed the place into a labyrinth of corridors and rooms from which there was no escape. Perhaps the spirit wanted others to suffer what it had suffered, lost in the caves, by transforming the hotel into a similar labyrinth. You could even smell the dampness and feel the claustrophobia before entering the place. And while some managed to escape, they did so only on the brink of death.

With Nerea and the Aeons’ help, she located the exact point where the seal had broken during construction. The entity, capable of possessing minds, used the guests as shields, though they were still alive. Mai had to use her magical knowledge, channeling an improvised exorcism that lasted several hours until reinforcements arrived. At dawn, the hotel was closed “for maintenance.”

At the end of each case, Mai returned to her office, reviewed the reports, filed them, and poured herself a cup of tea, coffee or a beer. She had returned to routine, but not the kind she had imagined: a life among digital specters, souls born in the network, and sleeping gods. And somehow, she felt more alive than ever.

It certainly couldn't compare to the constant danger she'd faced during her years at Wishper, but it wasn't as if this new phase was easy either. Still, it made her feel useful, and even though she didn't say it, Nerea's dedication told her the same thing.

The real, digital, and hidden worlds were now intertwined in a strange symbiosis. Whether one called it the supernatural or Dark Events, it seemed to unify everything, further blurring lines that had once seemed impossible to merge.

In that way, almost ten years passed. Mai plunged into a long period of work.

The neon sign outside the building was replaced twice during that time. She started driving again—though she still preferred older cars over modern ones. A side effect of being over fifty, perhaps.

Nerea had a surprisingly long relationship, which she eventually ended—not because of the cheating itself, but because he hadn’t asked for permission first.

Miria, on the other hand, stayed at the bar. It had grown popular over the years, likely thanks to her. Maybe that was also why the rent in the building went up, despite its lingering decay. It took some time, but parts of it were eventually renovated.

The seasons came and went. Summers heavy with heat and noise, winters quieter but never truly still. Even Miho’s old coffee machine finally gave out.

But the work never stopped. It kept finding its way back to Mai—through her computer, her phone, and the door of her office.

During that time, she stayed in contact with her old friends, those who remained active in the hidden world and against Dark Events and conflicts that seemed never-ending. Some shared accounts of how the world was slowly adapting to changes, though many of these stories were tinged with hopelessness. Tensions were rising in various regions, while old issues of resources and political conflicts remained constant.

However, amidst this chaos, one piece of news stood out among the echoes of confusion: Wishper continued to operate, now with stronger support. The Empire, which had begun to extend its influence in various parts of the world, had reached out to the organization thanks to ZAIEN and R&R. A base had been established in Canadian territory, occupying a ten-kilometer area dedicated to those of the hidden world. This place was considered a safe haven, a new opportunity for those of her kind who found themselves persecuted by a changing world.

Mai was invited to join this new settlement, the fey community beginning to take shape in the cold north. Yet, although the proposal was tempting, Mai decided to stay in Japan, where she was working comfortably. Doing what she could. Even as the world continued to change and opportunities came and went, her heart remained steadfast in her sanctuary, even when offered the chance for a new beginning in a safer place.

It was during this period that she allowed herself some intimacy, something she had kept—or tried to keep—away from her mind. She still remembered her arrival, and the possibility that there had been someone in her past she had lost when she appeared in that alley in 2004.

After all, she was a woman with emotional and physical needs. She couldn’t live only on fantasies to distract herself and soothe those needs. Most feys could be quite open-minded in their relationships, but she had repressed those needs due to being exposed to so much death and darkness.

During her time in Wishper, she had a couple of short-lived crushes, but that was all. Now the situation hadn’t changed much, but at least going on dates, meeting people unrelated to the darkness of the hidden world, was also good. Just a few relationships that lasted a couple of years at most, but at least they allowed her a glimpse of the ordinary life that some couples experienced.

It was during one of those quieter stretches that something unexpected entered her life.

It was the autumn of 2057 when she met Takashi.

Mai had been living in Japan for nearly a decade. The daily routine in the city and observing the passing seasons at her home had given her a certain calm, as if the serene landscape also reflected her own nature compared to the ever-turbulent Tokyo: calm, yet constantly changing. Yet, that autumn, something was missing, something she could not define until she saw him for the first time.

Takashi was an older man, with graying hair and a sparse beard, who had moved into the house next to hers for a short time.

His presence was calm, but he carried a sadness that Mai soon perceived. Although he never appeared particularly sociable at first, Mai noticed something different in his gaze, as if every word he spoke had been weighed a thousand times, carrying something else. And, in some corner of her being, Mai recognized that burden. She had seen it many times in humans.

At first, their interactions were simple: a greeting at the entrance, a polite nod and smile. Takashi spent long days in his garden, or sitting on his terrace gazing at the horizon listening rock music, in perfect calm and peace. He had been a businessman, as she later learned, someone who had dedicated his life to work, lost his wife years earlier, and never had children. Life, it seemed, had passed him by leaving only a heap of memories and empty accomplishments, but he had enjoyed it—or at least tried to make the best of his time.

Cancer, with its relentless certainty, had arrived to remind him that his remaining time was limited. Just a few months, at most. And Takashi accepted it, like a weary man who had walked so far that he had no energy left to keep fighting, but he had rented that house to be alone and reflect. It was not just his body that was falling apart, but his soul, marked by years of sacrifices that ultimately brought him nothing but profound loneliness in the big city.

Mai, with her many years of existence, had witnessed hundreds of deaths. The deaths of humans, and of feys who faded like candles at the end of their time in the world. Some affected her more than others. She had seen children grow and age, lovers separated by the distance of years, creatures disappearing into the stillness of time.

But what she felt for Takashi was different.

It was a connection she had never experienced before. It wasn’t the fleeting attraction she usually felt for those she met briefly, like shooting stars that shone only to vanish quickly. It was something deeper, more human.

Perhaps the key lay in how he viewed the world. Takashi was not afraid of death. On the contrary, he seemed to accept his fate with a serenity that Mai envied. He did not flee the end, as others did. Yet it was in his farewell to life that Mai found something she hadn’t seen in a long time: even in that solitude, there existed the will to be remembered.

One Saturday, after a bit of intimacy, while she accompanied him sipping a drink and watching the sunset, he seemed far more melancholic.

“I think there’s nothing worse than being forgotten,” he said one day, as they watched the last autumn sunset, tree leaves illuminated by the golden light of the sun. “Death doesn’t scare me, Mai. What scares me is that in a couple of years, no one will remember my name. Maybe not even you.”

“But you didn’t want to stay in Tokyo.”

“I want someone to remember me, but I also don’t want to leave behind a memory of how cancer consumed me.”

Takashi’s words pierced her, somehow, as if he were speaking not just about himself, but about all of humanity. The people Mai had known over the years, who were no longer around. Their faces could blur over time, like a drop of ink in a sea of memories, yet memory could occasionally bring those images to the surface, even if only for a fleeting instant. There was no way for Mai to hold on to everything she had lived, to everything she had lost, but she could remember. Everything faded, and the only thing that remained was that memory, which, as a fey, she could carry even when the people of those memories no longer existed in the world.

Her fear of ghosts might have stemmed from this. Some ghosts were beings that sought to be remembered, others who had left no mark on anyone’s life and tried nonetheless, tearing their souls until their ego completely vanished from existence.

Takashi, unknowingly, was giving voice to something Mai had hidden within herself for so long: the uncertainty of what it truly meant to live when you couldn’t be part of that fleetingness, merely existing but not really living at all. Just as Miho had said.

The years had given her perspective, but sometimes it took a spark to bring those memories to the surface and reflect on them.

“I will remember you,” she finally said, though the words sounded emptier than what she truly felt. How could she explain to him what it meant to be long-lived, to witness countless lives vanish without leaving a trace? Mai had seen so many people leave, so many loved ones in her life who were already gone. Each had left a mark, but those marks disappeared over time once those people vanished, as if they had never existed. What remained of them? What would remain of Takashi?

No one knew the answer to that question, not even Mai. She understood the passage of time as something strange now that she had lived more than fifty years with the appearance of someone in their early twenties. Time stretched for her, like an eternity that seemed to stop only at certain moments, when confronted with beings that touched her heart in inexplicable ways. Like Takashi.

Their friendship grew slowly, with conversations about the past, the present, and the inevitable farewell. Takashi spoke of his life, his wife, his youth filled with ambitions and broken dreams. Mai listened attentively, aware that human stories were finite. Perhaps because each of those stories was a spark of life that, in some way, left its trace on the world.

Takashi told her that maybe she should think about starting a family in the future. After all, that was one of the things he regretted most. Focusing so much on work had robbed them of what could have been a small source of happiness in his life and his wife’s.

“I’ll think about it,” she replied.

The very idea of a family filled her with a sense of strangeness.

There were feys who did it. Even with humans, when there was a chance that one of them would die sooner or later.

She could do it too.

But the idea almost terrified her.

Yes, it was true. She could find a partner. She could become a mother.

But should she? She? With the life she’d had?

If she had children, what would she tell them about her past? Wasn’t there a chance that someone would come after her and her family? That possibility made her rule out any chance of it, even though she couldn’t tell Takashi.

Humanity seemed to be slowly coming to terms with the idea that longevity would be achieved in the future. And with that came another problem. A society where people don’t die can’t give birth to anything new, because the past would always be present. For something new to be born, the past must die. Although the understanding, knowledge, and wisdom of the past must be preserved so as not to repeat mistakes.

That was true for the feys as well.

They could start over once they reached Earth, but at the cost of a kind of rebirth that erased memories of the past. Even if that wasn’t entirely true for her, some memories had been erased.What had happened in her past? Had she had a family? The tests said she hadn’t been a mother, but she wasn’t a virgin either, and she’d had milk in her breasts. So she must have loved enough in her past to have formed that bond. But that had been erased when she’d appeared in 2004.

That feeling had only surfaced for a few moments when she met Milena and Sari, but perhaps it was just a passing infatuation because she truly loved them both.

The idea might seem lovely, almost as much as her ideals of wanting the world to be a better place.

But carrying it out and how it would be was another matter. Even so, she promised Takashi she would think about it.

Who knew. If she managed to live long enough, maybe she’d reconsider it in a century or more.

Especially if the ghosts of her doubts faded away completely.

Miho would surely have given her a gentle tap on the head at that moment.

Cancer progressed, and Takashi began to deteriorate. Mai noticed, but could do nothing to prevent it. She could only stay by his side and offer emotional support. She could not intervene in the course of nature, even if she wished she could.

The price of extending a life was too high in the hidden world. Perhaps that was why so few feys grew old. But as her friend faded, something inside her began to change. The man withdrawing from life reminded Mai, in some ways, of Miho’s way of thinking: despite the ephemeral nature of human existence, the true essence of life lay in the connections you made, in the memories you left behind.

And in a way, he felt that he had been happy to meet her, even if he regretted that it hadn’t been sooner.

When Takashi passed away in January, Mai felt a strange sensation. The sadness, though present, was not as bitter as that which she had experienced in other deaths. There was something in his passing that gave her peace. Perhaps because she understood, for the first time, that the legacy of a life is not measured by its duration, but by the mark it leaves on others—just as so many had left their mark on her and the marks that Mai herself left on the people she knew.

It could also have been because she hadn’t seen many peaceful goodbyes in her life.

Miho had been caught up in a violent upheaval that had torn them apart before either of them could say everything they wanted to say to each other. Or rather, what Mai would have liked to say to Miho before that day. And what she would have wanted to say to so many others who had left.

Thank you.

Thank you for letting me get to know you.

Thank you for letting me be a part of your life.

Thank you for being here.

The winter wind whispered through the trees of the forest, and Mai remained there, in her house, gazing at the horizon, but now she was alone again.

But Takashi's rock music could still be heard in the background.

She knew her existence maybe could be long in the future, but she understood that the true gift of life was not its length, but the connections built in the time given. Short of long. And in some way, Takashi had left her that lesson, even though his life had been brief in comparison to hers.

Mai would not forget Takashi. No matter how many years passed.

Mai smiled faintly as the music continued to play. And it really felt as if he were still there.
