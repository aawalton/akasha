It was easy to track down her cousin. He was a bright spark where the formations were bundled in her mind. The formations led her to one of the storage areas built onto a naturally flat piece of land that had been made flatter by the efforts of mortal labourers. One of the anchors was near here, the anchor that represented fire.

The formations could not perfectly tell her the precise location of someone, but she did not need that. Repetitive thudding provided that precision. It was coming from the smallest of the storehouses which had a comparative dearth of mortals. They were all very carefully working in the eastern storehouses, loading carts lashed to placid donkeys placated with their feed. All five traditionally farmed grains were being added: bags of processed wheat, bundles of rice, sacks of both varieties of millet—foxtail and proso or Ji and Shǔ— all joined by unprocessed hemp, and finally soybeans for animal feed. Bundles of cloth, stacks of blankets, and winter coats. A variety of tools for craftsmen to work on repairing and replacing the caravans. There were no weapons if only because the soldiers would load those on their own. And, truly, no one should give overworked servants weapons to inflict violence upon each other.

As the servants saw her, they stopped what they were doing to bow. Hua gestured them to return to their work, walking down the paths made from mismatched stone, constantly replaced as needed over the centuries.

The banging led her to a storeroom where her cousin was kicking a shelving unit. Hua watched as he kicked it again and again, pots and jars rattling, bags of grain threatening to tip over. There was barely any force to it as the shelf did not immediately collapse. Weiji was breathing hard when he stopped, face flushed.

“Who do they think I am?” he asked himself, running his fingers through his tied hair. “Oh, Weiji, can you organise this elder’s household and make sure this baggage train is adequate and look, here are all the horses you have to deal with! I can’t be in that many places at once, you god’s forsaken—”

Hua unfurled her presence, letting a trickle of Qi seep out past her skin. Weiji jumped instantly, whirling around with wide eyes and sparks crackling between his fingers. He only calmed a moment later when he got a good look at her. He clutched to his chest a sheaf of paper, likely the supplies he was organising for however many elders had saddled him with work. If he was cursing them, Hua was about to get cursed out as well for saddling him with greater responsibilities.

“Cousin Ji,” she greeted, offering him a small nod.

“I told you not to startle me for no reason. I’d like to make it to fifty, maybe grow older than my father. But I won’t do that if you keep giving me a heart attack every week!” He exhaled slowly, rubbing at his chest. Did… Did he truly get a heart attack from that? “Now, if you’re done terrifying me, I have things to do. For apparently half the elders who saddled me with the work for their baggage trains. So please, can you schedule your bullying later?”

“Sadly, I cannot reschedule my bullying. I’ve brought even more work for you.”

“I’m starting to regret not running away when the earth broke. The north would be so peaceful, savages included. Fine, I’ll handle whatever supplies you need. Now go away.”

“This is serious, Weiji, I have a difficult request to make of you,” she said.

His features became guarded which wasn’t entirely unfair. Hua had pushed him. One sentence too far. Mentioning the baker girl he fancied had twisted his face into a look of grief she related to intimately. He’d tried to hide it, but that too was familiar. She just had to look for the twitching muscles in his neck as he prevented his jaw from clenching, the tightly pursed lips to stop from frowning.

I don’t know how to apologise now, but one day, I hope you’ll forgive me.

“What is it?”

“As Lord, there will be things I can’t do. Times I’ll need to be in more than one place. Things I need to be able to entrust to someone else. I need a second in command and I’ve chosen you for that role, cousin.”

Liao Weiji gave her a look of true suffering. Such abject despair that it should be painted and commemorated for future generations to witness.

“Of course, you would make my worst fears a reality,” he muttered, and her heart clenched uncomfortably.

She hadn’t expected a rejection. That wasn’t a thing she’d even considered. Not really. Not from Weiji. For all she insulted him, he was as constant as the mountain they called home.

“Do you accept? If you say no, I’ll accept it,” she said hastily. “No tricks, no punishment, I’d accept—”

“Hua, please, don’t make this more awkward than it needs to be.” He raised his hand before she could speak. He stood there, just thinking. Flashes of emotion crossing his features faster than Hua could fully decipher. “Just give me a second to recall the words. I need to say them right, so they mean something. Otherwise, this will be worthless.”

“I trust you,” she said unthinkingly and found it to be true.

“I’ve given you no reason to, so don’t.”

Hua nodded, allowing him to believe what he would. As if she wouldn’t have forgiven whatever petty treasons he had committed. Family was the exception to every rule. More importantly, that grief he’d felt for his mortal couldn’t be faked and she trusted in that, if nothing else. Trusted that shared loss to bind them even if it went unspoken.

Finally, after a deep breath, he composed himself. Weiji set down his papers on the shelving unit. Then, he dropped to one knee on the dusty floor. He canted his neck up so that Hua could see his startlingly green eyes filled with a faith she did not know she could live up to.

“This Liao Weiji swears to serve Liao Weilong faithfully and know him as my Lord,” he began with a confidence and steadiness she had never seen in him until now. “From this day forth, your enemies shall be my enemies, and their blood will stain my blade. I will protect your interests and defend your house. This oath I swear to my last days. Should I outlive my Lord, I swear to avenge him. Should I fall before my revenge, I swear my future children to this oath. By the lightning in my veins and by my blood as the only son of Lady Sing, I do so vow.”

Her chest tightened uncomfortably. There was not a hint of sarcasm or mockery in his words. This was an oath of service. She could tell him the place he would die and Weiji would be honour bound to fall where she decided.

He would do it, she realised, the immensity of that realisation rocking her back.

“I accept your oath,” for it was needed. And then, she added, “So long as I live, my blade and my lightning will always defend you. You will always know a home beneath my roof.”

A smile flashed across his face for a moment. There and gone, faster than the lightning they both promised by.

“I know.” He rose and met her gaze, both confident and with a contradictory uncertainty. “And one day, I’ll learn to believe it. What would you ask of me, my lord Liao?”

“Let us go see the Magistrate. He’ll be happy to know about the change in plans.”

“I can promise you that he won’t. And no, you’re not going to see the Magistrate right this instant. You can wait a few hours for me to get everything sorted. I’ll be surprised if you’ve even told everyone in your convoy that they’re part of it.” Hua stayed silent. “Please tell me you personally went and verified who would be joining you.

“Grandmother said she would handle things,” she offered weakly.

“Liao Furen delegates everything and it just causes chaos. I didn’t even know you were leaving soon so I promise you, no one does. I can’t believe you’re doing this to me.”

“That’s why I have you now. To handle that.”

“I already hate this arrangement and we haven’t even made it an hour!”

“Imagine how bad it will be by tomorrow.”

The sound he made reminded her of a dying animal. She patted him on the shoulder and left him to organise things in her name.

“Announcing the honourable Lord Liao,” a servant declared as Hua returned to the Magistrate’s estate for the second time in as many days.

The smell of smoke had lessened somewhat. Less likely to slap you for the terrible crime of breathing. She dismounted the same horse from the first trip, patting its silken neck. It exhaled a gust of moist air over her hair in thanks. Hua liked horses. They were solid creatures, tempered very much to reflect the earth beneath them. It was easy to like them.

One last pat, a quick adjustment of her father’s mantle, and then she was heading forward.

Weiji walked to her left, one step behind, while Liu Xin was a few paces behind and to his left. She did not know or even care to understand the politics going on between those two. That they had reached this compromise was enough. She was excited for the eventual murder attempts, not this posturing.

Dressed now in the colours of a Liao retainer—a black outer travelling cloak of sturdy material with a lapis blue kingfisher on the back over navy mid layers—Liu Xin looked the full part of her attendant. Attached to the navy sash around his waist was the emblem of his prior affiliation, the Doubting Antiquity School. Hopefully, he still had some connections with them, assuming they hadn’t been slaughtered and then buried beneath rubble. It would make Liu Xin more useful to have spies working at Hua’s behest instead of just relying on her grandmother’s connections.

Weiji had found the time to dress ostentatiously in silks of powder blue, patterns of golden leaves falling like a child throwing autumn leaves at the sky. There was a degree of detail and refinement that a retainer’s clothes lacked, golden stitching on either border of the piping, ivory buttons instead of cheap copper, and layers of padding and support that kept the drape constant as he walked. His hair crown carried the same motif of autumn leaves, a wide golden maple leaf cut in half. He looked the part of an elder’s only grandson.

Hua felt mildly underdressed wearing the same robes she had the other day. But she was forced to steal from her brother’s supply and hope no one noticed the mismatch in size. When there were more than a few days of peace, she’d be able to commission a new set of robes. One of the never-ending laters plaguing her. But her father’s mantle would have to carry with it enough authority.

The Magistrate greeted them in the same receiving room though this time both desks were occupied when they arrived. Both scribes were frazzled men, with stacks of paperwork, ink on their robes, and exhaustion carving their faces as surely as wind eroded mountains.

“I did not expect to be graced by your presence again so soon, Lord Liao.”

“This will be a quick visit. How far along were you with the orders?”

“I had only just begun to write the seizure orders,” he said warily as a chilling breeze swept in from the inner courtyard.

“Excellent, you will not have wasted any ink then.”

The Magistrate sighed with the weariness of someone used to his seniors changing their demands on a whim. “I take it some changes are being made.”

“Yes. Fairly extensive changes.”

“And they are?”

“We won’t simply secure the food supplies within the region, we will acquire all gold and silver within Liao territory.”

“Excuse me? I think I misheard you,” he said faintly, swaying on his feet. There was blood dribbling down his lip. “Did you say all the gold and silver in Liao territory?”

The Magistrate would live a very short life if he coughed blood each time Hua spoke to him. Easy way to choke to death, that. She’d prefer he didn’t die. Useful officials who didn’t obstruct her were… well, Hua couldn’t definitively say they were rare since she avoided dealing with them, but she imagined they would be since people had proven continually disappointing these last few days.

“Yes.”

He reached out with grasping hands for his desk. One of his scribes jumped out of his seat and handed the Magistrate a cloth. The Magistrate glared at him because he clearly needed help to his seat and not a means to neaten himself. That irritation buoyed him enough to snatch the cloth, wipe his chin, and sit in one fluid set of movements.

Hua almost clapped. Almost. She could spare the Magistrate a little face.

“This order will get us killed,” he sighed from behind his desk. “Get me killed. I might be a Cultivator, but enough guardsmen can stab me dead. And the Dragon Throne will have me strung up and quartered for an order like this. It will be treason.”

“I doubt it.”

“I know the types of treason. You wish to use my seal, the seal that carries the authority of our Emperor, to commit twelve acts of treason and four counts of sedition. You wish to make a great dragon through this paper-thin lie.”

He used her name. Weilong. The Great Dragon.

“We won’t be lying. We have the mandate from the Dragon Throne to rule this land as our fiefdom. You’ll just be stretching our authority a bit.”

“That argument will work well when our manhood is cut off and our faces dunked into a vat of boiling oil.” Hua winced slightly. She was rather attached to all of her parts. “Not just every imperial resource, you want me to command all civilians, merchants, and businesses concerns to surrender their gold and silver effective immediately. I… you’re mad. Truly and utterly mad. I was warned that Liao was madder than Yu and Zhao combined but I did not believe it.”

“I go only as far as is necessary to prevent chaos. I’ve watched money burn in the streets and priests threaten grieving fathers for a piece of gold. Here, in this city, watched by my clan and your garrison. If they’re willing to do that here, we’ll have bandit armies roaming the countryside before the first snows.”

“Just take your blade and slit my throat now. Save us all the time.”

“No. This will work. I know it will and even if the world disagrees, I will force the world to change.”

“Do you know just how many mercenaries the mercantile consortium has?

“No, we won’t start with Liaojiangkou. The Liao Elders will make the journey to some of our outlying territories whilst the Lady Furen will handle acquiring the gold and silver at Seagate City. Alongside the honoured Magistrate, of course. Worry not, the Hummingbird Blade will protect you. There is no safer place.”

“And where will the Lord Liao go?”

“I will secure our resource base at Coaltown.”

Coaltown was the largest town in the western extents of Liao territory, where the refineries and storage facilities for the many mining operations in the region. It was a trip disconnected from the Liao River, but one well worth it for the vast quantities of precious minerals that could be plumped. Seagate City lay to the southeast and was the last city before the Liao River met the open ocean. Between them lay the extensive farming fields and bountiful lakes teeming with fish. Hua needed to direct them inwards, back to Liao control and in so doing apply political pressure against the mercantile guilds within Liaojiangkou to surrender their silver.

They would not be happy but happiness was not Hua’s concern. Stability was. She would always have needed to kill people one way or the other. Better they be less connected, less important, less likely to be able to mobilise a revolt.

“I don’t require you to leave immediately,” she told the Magistrate. “My party will depart immediately since the journey is quite far and the roads bad on a good day, but you can spend a few days to finish organising. I’ll need someone from your office to carry your seal.”

“My scribe Tang,” he decided instantly.

The scribe who had given him the cloth went dead-eyed at that pronouncement. Did Weiji have a twin she’d never heard about? That look of despair was an almost perfect replica.

“Your scribe Tang it will be.” She turned to face the mortal scribe whose name she had already forgotten. “You have two hours to meet us at the western gates. If you’re late, you can catch up on foot.”

The Magistrate shook his head but reached for his brush and the large paper taking up his desk. The scroll upon which he would write his decrees.

“I pray your sword is mighty indeed, Lord Liao. Our fates are tied together now. I will rise with you or die beside you.”

“Magistrate, even the gods will not stop me.”
