CHAPTER 88

The village moved the way it always moved when it knew something was coming.

Not faster. Not slower. Just differently, in the small ways that anyone who had lived here long enough would recognize and anyone passing through would miss entirely. The grain sacks were stacked closer to the central fire than usual. The children were inside. The woman who normally sang while she ground the morning meal's grain was grinding it in silence, her hands keeping the rhythm her voice had abandoned.

Tama stood near the well with a bucket she did not need and watched the tree line.

She had been watching it since first light. Not obviously. She had learned a long time ago not to do anything obviously when this day came around. Obvious things drew attention and attention from the wrong place had a cost she had already paid more than once.

The tribute was ready. She had counted it herself the night before and counted it again this morning. The wood, ore and wheat had been prepared for travel. Everything stacked neatly near the gate in plain sight because the worst thing you could do was make them look for it.

She set the bucket down and straightened her back.

They came from the south road, four of them, riding the same stocky horses with the same Clawborn marks on their chest plates. The beast at the front she knew, his face seared into her mind.

Korr-Va rode through the gate without stopping and pulled his horse up in the center of the village. His eyes moved across the space with the practiced efficiency of someone counting what was there and what might be absent.

Tama walked toward him.

Korr-Va grunted and beat his chest once. Maybe he felt words would be wasted.

His gaze moved to the stack near the gate. He studied it for a moment, then swung down from the saddle and moved towards it. One of the Clawborn followed. The others stayed mounted near the gate, watching intently while the wagon sat ready to receive.

Tama stood motionless, her breath held to where all she could feel was the faint thud of her heart.

***

Thorne saw the horses and wagon before he saw anything else.

He had been moving at the front of the column, through the trees, when the village came into view through a gap in the brush and the first thing he saw was Clawborn marks on chest plates and stocky horses standing inside the gate.

His fist went up.

The column stopped. No sound. The soldiers in their under tunics crouched without being told, the training holding even if the faces behind it were uncertain. Thorne stayed standing, just inside the tree line, close enough to see clearly and far enough back that the shadows held him.

He found Tama.

She was standing near the tribute stack, watching Korr-Va count. Her posture was straight and her hands were at her sides and her face was doing the thing he had seen other faces do in situations like this — not blank, not afraid, just carefully arranged into something that gave nothing away.

He watched her for a long moment.

Behind him one of the younger soldiers shifted his weight. A twig snapped under a boot heel, barely audible, but Thorne felt the movement before he heard it. He turned his head slightly without taking his eyes off the village.

The new soldier went still.

Thorne turned back.

Korr-Va had finished with the tribute. He straightened and said something to one of the Clawborn, who began loading the goods onto the wagon. Korr-Va himself turned and looked at the village properly for the first time since arriving. His gaze moved across the buildings, the fire, the grain stone, the people.

The people who were not working.

His expression changed — not dramatically, but just a tightening around the jaw, a shift in the quality of his attention that Tama would have read instantly because she had been reading it for years.

He turned toward her.

"No work!"

It was not a question. His voice carried across the village without effort — the flat declarative of a man who did not need volume to make himself understood.

Tama did not answer.

Something moved at the edges of the village. A woman lifted a water bucket and walked toward the well. A man picked up a hoe and turned toward the garden. An older boy appeared from behind one of the huts and began stacking firewood against the wall, his movements unhurried, as though this was simply the next thing on his list. One by one the villagers found tasks and went to them, the whole village reorienting around Korr-Va's displeasure without a single word from Tama.

Korr-Va looked at her.

She felt his stare, paused, then met his gaze and held it.

He looked away first. He walked back to his horse and swung up into the saddle and said something to the other Clawborn. Everything was loaded and the party took their leave.

Tama watched them go.

She watched them reach the road. She watched them turn south. She watched the space where they had been for a long moment after the sound of hooves had faded and the road was empty and the tree line was still.

Then her shoulders dropped. Once. A fraction of an inch, barely visible, less than a breath. Then she straightened and they were where they had always been and nobody who had not been watching for it would have seen it at all.

Thorne walked out of the tree line first.

The soldiers followed in a loose line, emerging from the brush and crossing toward the gate in their under tunics, the Clawborn gone, just men in plain clothes moving through morning light.

Tama turned when she heard them. She looked at Thorne and he looked at her and for a moment neither of them said anything. The village was still going through its motions around them, the work that had started for Korr-Va continuing now for its own reasons.

"You were in the trees," Tama said as her face relaxed.

"Yes," Thorne said.

She looked at him steadily. "How long?"

"Long enough."

She held his gaze for another moment, then said, "The grain will need to be restocked before the next collection. The new soldiers are a welcome sight."

Thorne nodded.

That was all she needed. She turned and called across the village, short and direct, and the Hoshin who were leaving began moving toward their homes to gather what they had packed and kept ready for exactly this moment.

They came out in small groups and carried little. A bundle of clothes, a tool, something small and personal held close. They were told to leave without looking like they were leaving. They moved through the village saying brief things to the people staying behind, touching hands, pressing foreheads together, the interactions taking less time than they needed. Time was a luxury.

Thorne's soldiers spread through the village, quiet and unhurried, taking positions that looked like rest and were not.

Tama moved among her people. She spoke to each family that was leaving, something short and direct, and to each family that was staying something different, something that made them stand a little straighter after she had gone. She did not cry or linger too long. She did the work of holding the village together in the same way she had been doing it for years, with a competence so complete that it barely showed.

When the last of the departing Hoshin had gathered at the gate, Tama stood back and counted them. Then she counted the ones remaining. The number was smaller today on both sides. That was how it was supposed to work. It still required counting.

Thorne came to stand beside her.

"Riley will send for the next group within the week," he said.

Tama looked at the faces of the people staying. Children who had come out of the houses now that the horses were gone. Adults returning to work that was real work now rather than performance.

"I know," she said.

The departing group moved through the gate and waited. Thorne joined them, then led them to the trees, and the group disappeared moments later. Tama stood at the gate and watched until the last of them were gone, then she turned back to the village.

She counted the faces she could see. Not necessary, more of a distraction than anything. She would count again at the evening fire to be sure. Counting had a price. She paused for a moment when the counting was done and let herself breathe. She had counted everything except her days left in this village. That number could not run to zero fast enough.

She picked up the bucket she had set down that morning and walked back to the well and went back to work.
