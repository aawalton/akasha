“This place certainly doesn’t seem like you,” I whisper as we pass by what’s obviously a smuggling operation.

It all appears to be legal goods. They’re probably just avoiding import taxes rather than selling anything truly terrible. It’s what most smuggling throughout history has been, actually.

Thutmose shrugs.

“We all have a history, yes. I have tried to leave this one behind. I am a humble, legitimate, law-abiding, tax-paying merchant. Now, yes. Then? Ehhhh…” He waggles a hand. “Less so, perhaps.”

This warehouse has a bronze scaffold inside, the single largest use of metal I’ve seen in all of Karnak so far. There’s even a primitive crane. Thick bronze gears are attached to the claw that lowers down and picks up large papyrus and bronze crates. The gears themselves are inscribed with blue glyphs that faintly glow in the dimly lit warehouse.

The warehouse is filled with workers. Dozens of people are moving boxes in and out while the hoist rumbles in from outside and gently places crates on wagons that quickly roll away. I catch a glance or two, but not for long. They’re all too busy to gawk at me and my clearly otherworldly form.

In the rear of the warehouse, on the opposite of the dock is a raised office, likely meant for the warehouse supervisor. It even has glass windows, though the glass is scratched and cloudy. Thutmose and I ascend a metal staircase, though it’s rather difficult for me. Despite being of similar height to everyone here, I’m far more bulky. It makes it a tight fit.

Once we get to the top, Thutmose takes a deep breath and knocks on the door.

“Come in!” comes the growled, muffled reply.

Thutmose opens the door nervously. Inside is a crocodile-headed man growling at a nervous looking subordinate.

“I wanted those crates off that barge by last night! Why are they—” He cuts himself off as he notices who exactly opened the door. His lips curl with fury and he shows off his mouth full of sharp teeth. He stomps forward.

“You rat,” he spits, raising a hand to hit Thutmose.

I step forward and catch the first.

“Don’t touch him.”

He tries to pull away, and fails to break my grip. After a moment I let go, and he takes an uncertain step back.

“Picked up some Visitor muscle, I see. What do you want, Thutty?”

“I have a deal for you, Osorkon, yes. One you will never see the likes of again.”

“I’m not sure why I should trust you after you ran out on me. You cost me a lot of money.” Osorkon snarls, but he doesn’t advance again.

“Because enough money can buy a lot of trust.” Thutmose says coldly. The affable merchant is gone, replaced by something else. “With your connections, we will make more money in a day than you have in your entire life.”

Osorkon snarls again. “I’ve heard a lot of offers like that in my time, Thutty. You’ll have to do better than words.”

Thutmose looks to me.

“Show him.”

I pull the Cat-1 Fractal Shard out of my storage space. Osorkon is left speechless for a moment before turning to his subordinate.

“Get out. Don’t say a word of this to anyone or not even the jackals will find you.”

He scrambles out, studiously ignoring us all.

“You found a God Heart, Thutty? I don’t trade in those. No one trades in those.”

“We both know you know people who would. I introduced you to half of them.”

The crocodile man growls.

“Something like this won’t be easy to move. Or quick, either. You’re looking at months before any of us sees a single coin.”

“We don’t need money. We’re willing to trade,” Thutmose says.

“What could possibly be worth a God-Heart?”

“Sun Crystals. As many as you can.”

Osorkon’s reptilian eyes flick to me.

“Why do you want them so badly, Visitor? I know Thutty here doesn’t want, need, or trade in things like sun crystals. Not anymore.”

“My world is dying. We need the sun crystals to survive.”

Thutmose winces, and Osorkon licks his lips.

“Oh?” He says with a smile. “Well, I’m not sure a single God Heart will be enough in that case.” He waggles the one I gave him. “I’ll consider this one a down payment. I’ll get you sun crystals, as many as you want. You just have to do one little job—”

“How many?” I interrupt.

“What?”

“How many Fractal Shards—God Hearts—do you want? I want quite literally every single Sun Crystal you can get your hands on. All of them.”

He snorts. “You have more than one God Heart? Hah! Lie to me again and I’ll throw you out myself.”

With a flash of light, more Fractal Shards appear in my hand. I drop them on the ground one at a time. They’re far more durable than their crystalline nature would imply. Each clink of the shards hitting the ground is punctuated by a clipped word.

“How. Many. God Hearts. Do. You. Want?”

The foot long shards of quartz like material glow and slowly oscillate up and down the electro-magnetic spectrum. All four are less than 1.1 Amp. As extremely weak Fractal Shards I never really felt the pressure to hand them off immediately. I really should clear out my storage space more often. Both Thutmose’s and Osorkon’s eyes go wide.

“You said you wanted all of the sun crystals, right?” Osorkon says weakly. “When?”

I mull it over. The more I get, the longer I can wait. It means we’ll have more seed crystals and the ability to both build hydroponic towers and grow more simultaneously.

“You have a week to get as many cases as you can. I will pay in God Hearts and if necessary, from the tournament winnings.”

“You sound so sure you’ll win. There’s another Visitor and I’ve heard he’s very powerful.”

I finally release just a bare hint of my Aura. Osorkon flinches, staggering backwards and putting a hand on the table at the back of the room to support himself. He starts to sweat, slitted eyes wide.

“Oh, I’ll win.”

“If you don’t mind, Osorkon, I’ll do the negotiating, yes?” Thutmose says with a smile. It’s not a kind one.

Osorkon hauls himself off the table and shakily nods.

“Yeah, alright.”

An hour and seven God Hearts later, Thutmose and I walk out of Osorkon’s warehouse. Whoever he is, he must be one of the premier smugglers of the Kingdom. Given what I’ve been told about just how expensive God Hearts are, I’m shocked that he knows people who trade in it.

I have to wonder if he truly is breaking the law. If the Great King wants to obtain something while keeping it quiet, having a smuggler on the books would be perfect for that. Not to mention you get to keep other, more illegal activities down. Like smugglers who deal in more than just tax evasion. Instead of trying to play whack-a-mole with them, just fund a predator in their midst.

Thutmose rubs his hands and huffs.

“Ah, stressful. It has been a long time since I have negotiated such a high stakes deal, yes.”

“I can’t thank you enough, Thutmose. You just saved billions of lives.”

He smiles.

“Ah, feels good, yes. Build a statue of me, yes? Make it big.”

“I know just the person. Don’t worry, it’ll be big. I’ll make sure of it.”

Thutmose is still getting a cut for his hard work. He deferred at first, but I insisted. I think he’ll be doing more to save Earth than I did here, though there is some work ahead of me. Despite the deal being done, I’ll only be able to get a hold of the sun crystals if I have access to the Royal Market.

That amount of merchandise can’t be smuggled away. Especially not given just how damn expensive the sun crystals are. Osorkon is merely arranging the payment through other means. I’ll need to go to the Royal Market to pick up the crystals from the traders there.

While the crystals are within reach, I still don’t have them in hand yet. It looks like the tournament is still on. I might even have time for the math bracket, but that depends on how long additional sun crystals take to get here. There are a few more cases in transit that haven’t yet arrived. They’ve been ordered to expedite the delivery, as they well should given how much I’m paying.

To call it a king’s ransom in Fractal Shards would be selling it short. It’s a fortune even by Earth’s standards. They might not be as expensive there as here in Karnak, but Cat-1 shards still go for a pretty penny. I probably forked over ten million dollars worth in Shards. Still, it’s an absolute steal when it comes to saving the world.

Including the ones not yet here, there’s a total of eight cases, or a hundred and sixty sun crystals. I hope that’ll be enough. I’m not sure if I’ll be able to come back. I still haven’t decided on whether I can take the risk that this is just a regular Fracture disguising itself as something else.

It’s hard to know what’s real these days. I’ve always believed in people though.

When we get back to Thutmose’s wagon, there’s a small crowd gathered. I notice Amati and her mother among them, and they both give us an excited wave. Thutmose, back to his normally cheerful self greets the crowd. There’s a multitude of children, and I sit down on one of the benches so they can see my relatively tiny phone.

I wish I could pull from my memories before I Ascended. I obviously still have them, but they’re just different from the ones I make now. Fuzzier and harder to grab a hold of. I’ve seen a lot more places intact as a human.

I show them the view from Mount Everest. The blizzard at the time would have made it difficult to see the Himalayas had I been limited to human eye-sight, but I’m not. I put together a fairly good image from the wide variety of sensor data.

“Who’s that?” Amati asks, pointing at Guan Yu. Her mother, and many of the other parents are talking with Thutmose, and he’s making brisk business with them.

“That’s General Guan Yu. He’s… a friend.” That might be a small lie. Or maybe not. Friends disagree at times, though it’s hard to classify our difference in opinion on the Empire as a “disagreement”.

“What’s he do?” The owl girl asks with eternally wide eyes.

“He… protects humanity. He leads soldiers into battle.”

Amati blinks. “Oh. Like the Great King! He leads soldiers into battle. Ma says he defends the Kingdom from evil.”

“I’m sure he does,” I say as I put my phone away.

“Aw, is that all?” Amati asks.

“I’m sorry, it is. I don’t have many pictures of our world before the Apotheosis. Before everything went wrong.”

“Apockytoes? What’s that?”

“A-poth-e-oh-sis,” I say, sounding the word out for her. “It’s what made me into what I look like now. I wasn’t always like this. It’s why I’m here actually. I’m looking for something to help my world.”

“Why? What’s wrong?”

“It’s a long story,” I say with a sigh. “And not one suited for you, I think.”

The children groan, having clearly been hoping for story time.

“Did you find it? What you need?”

“Yes, and to get it, I’ll be in the Strength of Arms finals bracket tomorrow. When I win, I’ll get access to the Royal Market, which is where what I need is.”

Amati and all the children gasp.

“We’ll cheer for you!” They stay, leaping to their feet, fists raised.

I laugh.

“Thank you.”

With my showing of images of Earth done, and the various adults and parents having finished their shopping, the crowd dissolves away until only myself and Thutmose remain.

Thutmose looks at his depleted wagon with joy. “Ah, I am doing quite well, yes. I did not expect to run through my stocks nearly this quickly.”

“Happy to help.”

He nods, his bird head dipping low.

“Yes, yes! Friends help each other, yes. Together we will do well. I shall close up early, and we shall play more senet! Tomorrow is the finals, and then we both win big, yes.”

The rest of the day passes amicably. A few other merchants stop by from time, mostly others set up near Thutmose. The traffic I drew to his shop often bled over to theirs. Our little corner of the crowded and busy bazaar is much quieter now that everyone here has sold out, at least for the day.

One of the other merchants leads in, a local spice merchant with the head of a crane.

“And your people fly? All over the world?”

I nod as I move one of my pieces along the border. I land on one of her squares and kick her pawn back a space.

She grumbles at my move.

“I was even learning to build them,” I continue. “Well, at least parts of them. Aircraft are very complicated machines with countless moving parts. As a mechanical engineer I would probably focus on the electro-mechanical control systems.”

She shakes her head. “I know little of what you speak. But it seems like you know it well.”

“I would have,” I sigh. Thutmose skips us both and it looks like he’s about to win. Again.

“I dropped out of school. I never finished my degree.”

“Ah, why?” Thutmose asks, clacking his beak.

“My parents died. An accident.” Both Thutmose and Tapera, the spice merchant, wince.

“Ah, my friend, I am sorry—” I hold a hand.

“I’ve heard that too many times. It’s fine, you didn’t know. It is what it is.”

The two of them nod, though still slightly embarrassed.

“Fun fact, the college I went to offered me an honorary degree. I turned it down,” I say, changing the topic.

“Why would they do that?” Tapera asks. She kicks one of Thutmose’s last pieces back and nods her head happily.

“I… am very famous on Earth. Maybe people consider me to be the strongest hero. They wanted to give me the degree in recognition of my service.”

“You are one of the strongest warriors of your world, yet you are here. Playing senet with me. And losing.” Tapera says flatly. I can’t tell if she’s unconvinced or believes me and is surprised someone held in high regard is here.

“Losing badly, I might add,” I say with a chuckle. She shakes her head.

“You do not seem like a famous person,” she says.

“What do you think famous people are like?”

She thinks for a moment, idly tapping one of the throwing sticks on the table.

“Larger than life. Oh, I do not know how to say it. Heroes are from stories and legends. They should seem story-like.”

I shrug. “If it makes you feel any better, I don’t feel particularly heroic most of the time.”

I throw my sticks and four black. I get to move five spaces, and one of my three pieces finally makes it off the end of the board.

“Why not?” Thutmose asks as he takes his turn.

“It’s complicated. But I’ll try to explain.” I pull out my phone—no service, shocker—and explain its functions. When I tell him what exactly it could do on Earth, both him and Tapera shake their heads in awe.

“Are you sure your people did not have magic? This sounds like magic to me.”

“It’s not.”

“Ah, what is the problem? This sounds like a good thing.”

“I am connected to the internet, this communication web. It’s how I’m so effective at fighting monsters and clearing Fractures. It also means I have to listen to millions of people every day screaming for help.”

Thutmose drops his piece with a bird-like squawk.

I put my head in my hands and lean on the table.

It’s been awhile since I’ve been able to lean on furniture. That’s a nice feeling.

“Everyday I listen to millions of people from all across the world begging for me to save them. To save them from the monsters pouring from Fractures. To save them from the monsters that were already here, and to save them from the monsters ultimate power often turns people into. And you know what Thutmose? This is better. I used to go out and search this web for every little scrap of terror and blood and death I could find in the desperate hope it might help me save just one more.

“Do you know what it’s like? To have the worst of the world constantly streamed into your head? The only time I ever get to simply stop is when I’m not on Earth. I live inside Fractures. I simply survive outside of them.”

Thutmose pats my shoulder.

“You could always return here?” he offers.

“My world needs me.”

“After you bring back the sun crystals the world will be saved. Surely you do not need to give more than you already have,” Tapera says softly.

The sun is setting on my second day here in Khetmenet, casting long shadows in our wake. A cool breeze blowing in from the desert covers a sigh. A tall tower set in the center of the market place was designed to capture it and funnel it down to street level. What the people of this world have done with mere stone, sweat, and magic is truly impressive. They’ve built things to outclass Earth’s greatest works and they did it without the heavy industry and tools we assumed was necessary for such things.

“I want to be a good person. What else am I supposed to do?”

“There must come a time when even you must stop, yes? There will always be one more crisis, one more job, one more critical task only you can complete,” Thutmose says gently. “I know this well. There will come a time when you must say no or you will lose yourself. You could stop, yes? You have said many things about your world. You break things by existing. Crowds become shops full of glass you must dance by lest you hurt them. You could not even go indoors for months. This cannot continue.”

I want to stay so badly.

Thread terminated. Thought discarded.

“I’m not done yet, Thutmose,” I say with cheer I don’t feel. “I have more work to do. Just a few more things left. Once the world is stable I can rest.”

He nods and pulls back. I wonder if he believes me. I’m not sure I do.
