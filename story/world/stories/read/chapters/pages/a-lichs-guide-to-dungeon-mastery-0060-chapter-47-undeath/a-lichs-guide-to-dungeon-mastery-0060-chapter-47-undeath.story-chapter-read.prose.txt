Unsurprisingly, the first rat on the chopping block was turned into an undead. It seemed highly confused, which I took as a good sign, and I made an effort to reach out to its mind, but that didn’t actually help much. The rat was confused, anxious, and hungry, but those were all sort of standard rat behaviors in my mind.

That said, undead didn’t tend to experience anxiety unless they were highly intelligent, so either this rat had somehow naturally become a very smart creature in the conversion or my experiment was a success. Hopefully the latter, though a way to create intelligent undead would also be a boon.

Now I just needed a more intelligent test subject. I could waste time slowly going up a ladder, or I could skip straight to human trials and bypass all that nonsense.

I knew which one I preferred, but that did mean I needed someone willing to be a human trial.

Or, I could use it on an unwilling subject that happened to deserve it. That was a slippery slope, of course, but if I could navigate it then I’d have a supply of test subjects to play with.

I scanned my town, which I only now realized I’d never actually given a name. To be fair, I’d never been overly invested in its development, and it was honestly a shock that the entire system hadn’t collapsed.

Most of that would have been Azrael’s work, coordinating the locals that I couldn’t be bothered with.

Figuring out who was in charge took only a few moments of observation– they were currently observing something that reminded me of a city council meeting.

Conveniently, there was a very obvious prison block beneath the building they were in, but I figured some more investigation to ensure I wouldn't be bungling things would be good.

Five women sat up on stands, each standing up in turn and saying their piece– a lot of nonsense that mostly related to the visitors that had started flowing in since my dealings with Queen Nadiya.

One member brought up the harvests, another the construction of buildings outside the city walls to house the visitors, and so on. The leader– a particularly tall woman whose skin was littered with scars– pitched in on certain issues, and asked for clarification in other areas. I watched for a few minutes before I got bored and portaled into the hallway just outside.

A few guards were standing outside the door to the conference room and they panicked when the portal opened and I dropped onto the floor in my skeletal magnificence, readying their weapons.

A single glance froze them solid, and they stepped aside.

I stepped up to the door and undid the lock with my magic, popping it open with a casual wave.

The entire conference room erupted into a flurry of motion as weapons were drawn and hackles were raised, only for a wall of silence to shutter over the room an instant later as they realized who I was.

These women had all been there when I’d led them to my tower. They knew what I was, and what I was capable of. Furthermore, Azrael had doubtlessly shared stories about me while they hunted and did whatever else it was that women and their undead friends did together these days.

So, in other words, they were either terrified of me, utterly unimpressed, or somewhere in between.

Probably that last one, actually, if gossip worked the way I thought it did.

I spread out my arms. “So, ladies, would you happen to know where an undead like me could find some criminals?”

They were silent for another half a minute before I let my arms drop and sighed. “Come on, work with me here. I know my Runic Orbs aren’t perfect– they only handle violent crimes and even then I’m sure someone’s found a way around them. So… bring out your soon-to-be-dead?”

They stared for a while longer, but eventually the leader– whose name I did not know– spoke up: “Lord Ambrose, forgive me, but what are your designs on these people? And also, where have you been for the last few months? And where did Lady Azrael go?”

I stopped for a second. Right. They hadn’t been made aware of Azrael’s departure in any way, had they?

“In reverse order,” I raised a finger. “I have no idea, east I guess?” I raised another finger. “I’ve been in my tower, like always.” One more phalange went up. “And I’m going to turn them into zombies. Any other questions?”

They looked at each other with bewilderment clear in their eyes. “I mean, why?” The agriculture lady asked suddenly.

I gave her a long look. “Are you seriously asking a lich why he wants to make zombies?”

She flushed with embarrassment and looked away.

“To be fair,” their leader interrupted, “You’ve never shown an interest in such behavior.”

I frowned skinlessly. “Of course I have. I built a tower! You can’t tell me liches don’t normally build towers.”

She raised her eyebrows at me. “Liches normally build fortresses to command legions of undead from. They act like warlords and gradually convert as many people as they can before some paladin or another smites them into dust.”

I shrugged. “Hey, maybe I’m just returning back to my roots?”

The entire room’s hackles raised, except for the woman I was speaking to, who seemed utterly unimpressed. “If you were doing that, you’d have started by killing all of us.”

“Fine, fine,” I grumped, “I’m not turning into a supervillain just yet. But still, I want to experiment on some people and I figure that nobody’s gonna volunteer just yet, so. Criminals. Where do you keep them?”

One of the woman shifted uncomfortably. “I mean, our current system is to keep them beneath the town hall, but we should–”

“You’re going to explain what’s going on before anything else,” the leader interrupted.

I sighed. “Okay, fine. But first, tell me your names. I’m starting to feel a little weird about it. Oh, and what’s the city called?”

“I am Mitref. I am temporarily in charge of the town while Azrael is gone. These are my councilors Shakla, Lishor, Tziya, and Migdali. We’ve been calling the city Omination.”

I nodded. As always, the people here have weird names. At least they didn’t decide to call this place Sanctuary, I would’ve had to force them to change it to something less cheesy. “Alright then. What did you want to know?”

“When will Lady Azrael be returning?” Mitref almost demanded.

“Dunno. She’s alive, but I’ve got no clue where she is other than that she’s outside my domain. At this point, I wouldn’t be shocked if she just marched all the way across the world and circled back, though.”

Lishor frowned. “What do you mean, circled back?”

“You know, walked all the way around the world and ended up on the other side? Wait… Is that not common knowledge here yet?”

Lishor seemed even more confused. “How would she end up on the other side? And what’s not common knowledge?”

“So, the world is a sphere. If you go too far in any one direction, you’ll end up on the other side,” I explained quickly.

It was clear from her face that the councilor of trade absolutely did not understand– or at least didn't believe– what I was saying, but the others still had things they wanted from me.

“Can we get some help with some animals and fields? We’ve been stuck eating the same food, and all our meat is imported,” Shakla asked hopefully.

I waved a hand dismissively. “Sure, but I might not get to it for a while.”

Shakla looked disappointed at that, as did the others, but I simply wasn't concerned with their needs considering what I had going on.

The other's concerns were similar in nature. They wanted help communicating with the outsiders, they wanted more space and buildings, yada yada.

I largely brushed them off. While their concerns did matter, they weren't at the top of my list. I'd get to them soon, but knew it might be a while.

Once they stopped yapping my ossicles off I popped downstairs to grab a few human test subjects.

Most of these people would have committed nonviolent crimes, or at least committed their individual evils outside of my Runic Orbs’ sight, but I wasn't too worried. They had all either walked into the domain of a bloodthirsty lich and ignored the rules, or received my generosity and committed crimes against the rest of the collective anyways.

I wasn't in a very charitable mood, besides.

With a few test subjects teleported up to my tower and bound in place, I got to work pasting in the rune I'd developed beneath them and powering it on.

The first thing I noticed was that the cost was far higher than I liked, and I realized quickly that this rune fundamentally would not work on a large scale, at least not without an adequate power source.

The second thing was that it was working… but too slowly. Parts of the first test subject– a confused and cowardly middle-aged man– were transforming into undead flesh, but it would take minutes at the least.

That doesn't sound so bad, until you realized that the living and dead flesh were at war with each other.

The rune was doing its best to keep the man intact, but it wasn't designed to prevent the sort of immune response that the magic was having. It was minor, but each part of his body was at war with each other, and his more delicate organs soon failed.

The man's body slumped over as his brain accumulated too much damage to function, having torn itself apart as the Life attempted to flee the Death, and the Death attempted to devour the Life.

“Well that's unfortunate.”

Was this an issue of throughput, or more runes? Should I develop a network that stabilized the subjects, or would it be better to alter the current rune so that I could slam more energy through it to make it faster?

My energy requirements were already going to be drastic, so I chose to first try the stabilization, hoping it would be more efficient.

I figured it could be an Ordinance rune for the stabilizing effect, though noted that if this worked I'd have to make a new rune from scratch to improve the efficiency.

I realized as I looked at the next test subject that I probably should have placed them so that they couldn't see one another. Her pleadings were far more fervent than the prior man's, considering that she'd just watched me turn a man into a sack of lifeless meat, seemingly without rhyme or reason.

I didn't feel much like caring, though, so I quickly pasted one rune and carved out the other by hand, then poured power into both.

As expected, the Ordinance rune was less efficient than a specialized version would have been, which was a big part of why I didn't typically use them anymore. It was just bad for my energy economy.

Still, the woman didn't face the same tearing phenomenon, at least not on the same scale, instead weeping openly as the warmth was torn from her flesh and death flooded in to replace it.

She tugged at her chains, begging to be let free… and I obliged.

I didn't bother telling her that the experiment had been a success, instead portalling her and the remaining prisoner back to their cells.

With the rune’s functionality proven, now I just needed to come up with a deployment method and a power source… and I had a plan for both.

A few days later, I looked out at the latest dimensional tear with a feeling halfway between satisfaction and wariness.

I'd collected my due from the Kerenth queenling in the form of a vast array of advanced energy types, and used those types to create portals. I'd then passed through each of those portals and set up an outpost– just an outpost, no need for total domination quite yet– and sent out orbs with a fraction of my abilities to harvest as much power as possible.

The energy crystals were piling up, and I had created a set of runes to convert them into power usable for my purposes, but I was getting awfully suspicious.

I'd entered twelve realms, and not a single defender had attacked me?

Something was going on, but I wasn't certain I had the time to figure out what it was.

My time was practically gone, and if I wanted to conquer Yalten before I was attacked by Esheth, I needed to move now.

I took amounts of energy from each portal and used it in the creation of a brand new model of Runic Orbs–these ones designed for conversion, not governance.

My deadline was slim, and my needs were great, but if everything went just right? The sun would soon set on a nation of death.
