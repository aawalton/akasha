After cleaning up the battlefield, we checked the inside of the city, making sure no other cultists had snuck inside. Once the coast was clear, Cuthward and the new portreeve met us at the gate.

I dismounted from Burr, then followed them up to the ramparts atop the gate. We looked down over their fields outside.

First, I gave the new portreeve a glance over. He wasn’t friendly, I knew that much for certain, but he was doing his best to look kind. His robes were a shade of pale yellow with gold hem, and he wore a brass circlet in his honey-coloured hair. He had a short beard, but he couldn’t have been any older than thirty years old. Everything about him was perfectly maintained in a way that made my stomach crawl.

“Appointed by the Warlord, huh?” I said, leaning on the wooden merlons atop the gate.

“Not by the Warlord, Sir Levi,” the portreeve said with a bow. “A lesser decree from the Witenagemot. It is far more efficient to say that the Warlord appointed me, and that is where my authority comes from, but I have never met him once in my life.”

“You come from up north, like Tunmod?”

“Academy trained, sir.” The portreeve narrowed his eyes. “I have heard what you did to Tunmod, sir. Such barbarism isn’t befitting of a thegn.”

I was punishing barbarism, I thought. But typical of them to get mad at the kid who defends himself from the bullies.

“I did what I had to. Are you here to punish me?” I cast him a knowing smile.

The portreeve said nothing. As expected. Even if he wanted to, he didn’t have enough centralized power, and he couldn’t remove a thegn who had rightfully won these lands in a trial-joust.

“Your defense of the city was equally barbaric,” the portreeve scolded.

“So I should have let it burn?”

“You executed prisoners.”

“The Greencloaks took away Hinbern’s two other men, and they’ll find a dungeon for them somewhere,” I said. I wasn’t going to go on some rant about justice, but I had done what I had to with Hinbern. Leaving him alive wouldn’t get us anywhere.

“You look like you’re enjoying this.” The portreeve threw his arms down in frustration. Cuthward stood behind him, merely grinning. After a drawn-out sigh, the portreeve added, “I know what you and your lackeys did after you slew Tunmod.”

I shrugged. “And there’s nothing you can do about it, not now that Lord Ceonwin is making this his temporary base of operations. You thought you were powerless before? Now, you’ll have an Ealdorman telling you what to do.” I pointed back toward the hall. “You’re dismissed.”

The portreeve slunk away like a kicked puppy, as if trying to garner more sympathy from me. I let him walk away.

“Well, then,” I said, turning to Cuthward.

“He will get over it,” Cuthward replied. “If you, however, have any other concerns…”

“What’s your official role, now?”

“Deputy Portreeve,” he replied. “And I’m still the master of my temple.”

“Excellent.”

“Indeed. It gives me plenty of time to restore the temple.” He paused. “You’ll be here for a while?”

“Only a day, or so. We’re heading west to Steelshore. But Lord Ceonwin and the rest of the castle staff will remain. You will have access to some of the best blacksmiths and accountants I know. Hopefully, they’ll all pull their own weight.”

“Once the snow melts, I’m sure we could use their help with tending to the fields and digging the wells.”

“Perfect,” I said. “You think you’ll have any time to help them get settled? Preferably, you could get the blacksmiths set up as soon as possible. They have a few in-progress pieces that they need to finish…” I wasn’t the only one who had armour in progress, waiting for us.

“Right away, sir.”

~ ~ ~

We settled Drywsone and his apprentices in an abandoned smithy near the shore of the river. As soon as we’d finished offloading all the goods, I presented him with the Keeper Topaz Dragon’s core.

He raised his eyebrows. “So you did retrieve it, after all?”

“And a little more crystal dust,” I said. “Lastly…” I motioned to Sage and Brawl. They each retrieved a handful of the dragon’s mouth-hairs from their bags. “...the plumes. With that, it should be everything.”

“I will begin immediately. Return tomorrow morning, and I will have a completed set for you and your squad.”

“We appreciate it,” I said.

Once it was ready, I could check in on all the Skills embedded in the armour, then merge them to bind it. As soon as it was finished, it would keep me busy on the rest of the journey to Steelshore. I’d use the travel time to complete a few more Merges.

~ ~ ~

Next, Cuthward brought me to the guild hall, where the Dun Altir Order’s alchemists-in-training had an office.

I knocked on the door, and it swung open. The office was clean inside, but it reeked of cheap candle lard. The window was still boarded up from the guild hall’s closure. (According to the alchemy book I’d read, that was because the minor mae energy in sunlight could mess with some finicky potions, and the alchemists didn’t need to cause a chemical leak with some unintended reaction in a half-finished potion.)

The office had four residents. All of them wore baggy brown robes that reminded me of English monks, but they had full heads of hair, and they wore their long hair in ponytails, separated from their scalps with a beaded aglet.

One of them looked to be about forty years old, and he wore a necklace with a titanium emblem hanging in front of his chest. The other boys were anywhere from sixteen to eighteen, and they wore necklaces with iron badges. That had to be their order’s way of telling their rank.

As I stepped into the office, all eyes turned toward me. I said, “Greetings. You’re the alchemists, I take it?”

“Sir Levi,” the oldest monk said, bowing toward me. “I am Adept Brother Trawald. I take it you are here to inspect our work?”

“I won’t be here long,” I said. “I’m tying up some loose ends before I depart, and checking in on our progress.”

“You’ll be pleased to know that the quality of your potion base is very high,” Trawald told me, clasping his hands together. “Surely, it has benefitted my apprentices in their education. They’ve been crafting the healing potions that you requested. However…”

“I’ll pay the extra little fee for quality, don’t worry,” I said.

“It is appreciated.” He lowered his voice. “Our Order is quite…stingy when it comes to giving out gifts.”

“Are all of you alchemists?”

“Most of us aren’t,” he replied. “Most of us are scholars, minding our own business in the mountains, collecting manuscripts and Art scrolls. We require raw materials, however, like your potion base, to make our potions.”

“Why do you guys need healing potions?”

“The cultists sometimes threaten our borders, and more and more monsters leak over from the Bane-lands as the Greencloaks are spread thinner and thinner,” he said. Then, he motioned across the room. “Lesser Brother Elgar has been brewing as many potions as he can for you.”

I raised my eyebrows, then followed Trawald between the tables until I reached the far corner, where a boy stirred a tiny pot over a candle. It bubbled, slowly steaming, as the thick green liquid gained a glossy sheen.

“Sir Levi,” the boy said. He stopped stirring for a second, then pulled open a drawer and retrieved four potions from the drawer. They were tiny vials, no larger than my pointer finger, and each of them had a cork stopper, holding in the neon green liquid. “These are for you.”

They gave off the same pressure as a Gold-tier enchanted weapon when I used my perception Skill.

“These are for you, sir.” The boy held them out to me and bowed. “Your lands’ resources and Brother Trawald’s assistance is the only reason they are so strong.”

“Thanks.” I took them, then added, “I’ll make sure you guys get your payment. If there’s no loose cash, we’ll take it off your next bill and won’t charge as much. Tell Hardric how much we owe you, and he’ll make you whole.”

After that, I left the room, then brought the potions with me to the great hall, where I found Lord Ceonwin setting up tables and maps. He and his men coordinated the next moves, assessed how much of an army we had left, and determined how capable we were of continuing to fight.

I approached the table, then inched in beside Abacus, who listed all the supplies we had on us for Lord Ceonwin while leaning on a crutch. Every word seemed to take extra effort. When there was a break in the conversation, I handed three of the potions to him. I said, “For your troubles.”

“Thank you, Sir Levi,” he replied. “I was beginning to wonder if you were going to ever get them to me.”

“Don’t worry,” I replied, “Like you said, I always keep my word. Now, I’ve gotta go introduce Cuthward to Lord Ceonwin. Use the potions well.”

“Indeed, I will. It is likely that I won’t see you until you return from Steelshore, Sir Levi, so I wish you the best of luck and a safe journey.”
