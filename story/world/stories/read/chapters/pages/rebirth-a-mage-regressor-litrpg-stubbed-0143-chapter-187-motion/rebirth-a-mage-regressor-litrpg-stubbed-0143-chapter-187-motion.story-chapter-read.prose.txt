The days went by one at a time. Seven, then six, then five, each one lighter than the last and heavier for it.

Adom had a watcher now, and he was learning what that meant.

Peregrine Hook went where he went and stood where he stood. Blond, unremarkable and quiet, hands folded, eyes moving over every room before her boots had finished crossing it.

She called him "my lord", ate twice a day and never otherwise: waybread at the second hour past noon for seven minutes, and again at seven in the evening for four, the same gray ration each time, broken the same way, to the minute.

She didn't sleep. Not the way people did. Once or twice a day she would set her back to a wall, shut her eyes, and be gone for ten minutes exactly, standing the whole time; then she'd open them and be good for another full day, as sharp as the moment she'd closed them. Adom thought this was the most ridiculous thing he had ever heard, and he had met Valiant.

So one afternoon he tested it. Walked up while she stood there with her eyes shut, and waved a hand slowly back and forth in front of her face.

Her eyes opened.

"My lord."

"...Sorry," he said. "Curiosity."

She didn't go back to sleep after that. She stood off the wall and stayed up the rest of the day, and Adom noticed she was a little slower to mark the room after that, a little less everywhere at once, as if the ten minutes she hadn't finished were owed and being collected all afternoon. So he felt a bit guilty.

But he also found it very interesting.

The rest of it was harder, and went on every waking minute.

Bennu felt it worst. The phoenix who could never normally be made to stop talking went quiet the moment Peregrine entered a room and kept his eyes off her, sitting through whole evenings without a word.

He knew what she was there to find, and his entire self was folded up behind a child's face precisely so she wouldn't find it. So he said nothing, which from Bennu was its own loud alarm. Ada kept asking why he was sulking, but nobody had an answer they could give her.

The family did what families do with a stranger who won't leave. Maria set a place for her at every meal and got clipped courtesy back. Arthur, who had been a soldier longer than Adom had been alive, knew another soldier when he saw one, and watched her with a flat, even attention she returned without complaint.

And once a day, Adom asked her to play tag.

She always accepted. Pride wouldn't let her do otherwise, not when she had yet to lay a hand on him. And because of it, Adom had some time everyday to go review the Castle White operation with Valiant, due for the seventh day.

The council was the strangest hour of all.

Ten magi at the round table, and behind every chair a Shadow against the wall, ten of them, motionless. Each magus had found a different way to carry it.

Beth knitted and offered hers tea. Nox treated his as an open insult and said so, twice. Kyrian had tried charm and been handed courtesy in return. Thorne behaved as though his did not exist. Draven already knew his Shadow's name and used it in every other sentence. Merlin was courteous to his, and nothing more; he gave it the same even politeness he gave everyone, and let it stop there. The other magi were all variations of these.

They did their business under the watchers, quieter than they used to, every word weighed twice before it was spent.

Then it was the seventh morning, and the house woke in the dark.

As tradition demanded, the whole family was at the table before the sun, no one allowed to hurry it. Eggs, bread and tea poured cup after cup from the patient kettle.

Ada's chair was empty. They had let her sleep, and it had not been an accident. Five years old and missing nothing, she'd have taken one look at the dark windows, as well as the low voices and asked it straight out: Is Adom going away again? And Peregrine would have filed the question away behind her eyes with all the others.

Adom had risen in the dark with a knot already low in his stomach, and nothing he did loosened it. Part of it was the day ahead, the other part… he wasn’t sure.

They ate mostly in silence. His mother and father both knew where he was going today, and neither of them said so.

That was the one thing about the morning that wasn't ordinary, the hour pulled forward into the dark. Everything else they kept exactly as it always was, and after breakfast, they said goodbye the way they would do on any ordinary day, and Adom left.

He had three days' leave starting today, the first he'd taken in longer than he could remember, and he'd told everyone he meant to spend the morning walking. So he walked, out past the gate and down toward the green sprawl of the park, in no hurry at all. Peregrine fell in behind him as usual.

The park was near empty this early, the paths wet, the light only starting to come grey through the trees. Adom let the quiet run a while. Then he looked back over his shoulder.

"You alright back there?"

"Yes, my lord."

He slowed until she came level, which she did, because hanging back would have said too much. "I wanted to say sorry again. For last night."

"It was nothing, my lord."

"It wasn't, though. I was restless. I've got a potion I'm working on, and I wanted to try a step on it before the idea got away from me. I hit a stage I wasn't expecting, and I didn't think the reaction would be that loud. I'd have warned you if I'd known." He pulled a face. "Sorry again."

"It was nothing, my lord."

"Oh come on, it cost you your sleep. A friend of mine talks about this. He calls it The Sleep."

Adom kept his eyes on the path. "You know how there's a sleep in you that needs sleeping? Like, a specific sleep. And every night you sleep, and you sleep fine, you sleep plenty, but you never sleep that sleep, the one that actually needs sleeping, so it just sits there. Unslept. And the more you sleep the wrong sleep the more the right one piles up, and you think, tonight, tonight I sleep it, and you don't."

He heard himself and nearly laughed; he sounded like Valiant, whiskers and all. "Drives you a little mad, right?"

"...Yes, my lord," Peregrine said. "I understand."

"Right?" He laughed for real then, and stretched, rolling the stiffness out of his shoulders and arms. "Anyway."

Adom turned and walked backward down the path to face her.

"Ready for tag?"

She didn't answer right away. "My lord, I don't think—"

"What's the score now?" He counted on his fingers, slow about it. "How many days have we done this? And how many times have you laid a hand on me?"

She said nothing.

"That's the one. None." He grinned. "Tell you what. You're a touch slow this morning, long night, my fault, so I'll take it easy. Give you a real shot at me. As slow as I can stand to go."

Her jaw set, just barely. He'd learned to catch it this week, the proud thing under all that flat quiet, there was a part of her that had hated losing every single day and would die before it said so out loud.

"As you wish, my lord."

So they played.

It was over almost before it began. Adom gave her the opening, let her commit to it, slid the wrong way under her reach, and was up a wall and gone into the streets in under five minutes. Quick, even for him. Behind him she'd be settling in to hunt him the way she always did, weaving the long spell that took her twenty minutes to run him down across the city. Thirty, on a bad morning.

Thirty, then. He'd seen to the morning himself.

The moment the rooftops closed behind him and Peregrine was a problem for her own spell to solve, he finally let go of the brake he'd ridden all week.

The streets smeared. A washing-line, a startled gull, the whole length of the river road, gone before any of it registered. He crossed Arkhos top to bottom in under ten minutes, came down off the last roof onto the boards of the east quay, and slowed to a walk with his pulse barely lifted.

The meeting point was the gutted customs house at the end of the pier, a long dead shed the harbor authority had given up on years ago. He came through the side door at two minutes to the hour. Everything here ran to the minute today, his end of it most of all.

They were already waiting. Valiant up on a crate so he could see over the table, Thormund filling a third of the room on his own, Maya and two others Adom knew by face from the cellar. And beside the wagon backed into the dark of the shed, the two changeling brothers: Keth-Sil and Seth-Horb.

One of them stepped out of the wagon's shadow, and Adom almost gasped.

It was himself. The white streak, the warmer blue of the eyes, the exact set of the shoulders, down to the two gloves and the travel coat, because all of it had been planned to the stitch.

He'd seen it half a dozen times this week, in stolen hours while Peregrine combed empty streets for him, and they'd drilled it until it was perfect. Knowing that did nothing for the lurch in his chest at meeting his own face across a room.

"Okay, all right, sorry, sorry." Valiant gave a small shudder, looked away, then made himself look back, polite about it. "Don't mind me. It's not you," he told Seth-Horb, "it's two of him in one room, my eyes keep trying to pick the real one and they can't, and it does a thing to the back of my brain I really do not — okay, you know what, that's probably going to give me a headache now, the eye-strain kind, I get those, I had one for three days last month and Maya said it was nothing but Maya's not the one who — anyway." He pulled himself back. "You're good. That's the problem. You're too good."

Seth-Horb inclined his head, taking it as the compliment it was.

"Valiant," Adom said. "We're on a clock."

"Boss is right, little boss," Thormund rumbled.

Valiant's head whipped around. "Okay. No. He's not the boss. Adom is a… he's a patron. He commissions work. I am the boss. This is my crew."

Nobody at the table said anything. They just looked at him.

"Why are you all looking at me like that?" He flapped a paw. "He pays for things, sure, but that's what a client does! Just because he paid for the shed, and the wagon, and your wages, and the safehouses, and he found the brothers, and he planned the whole thing, and he's the one we all sort of look to when — oh shit, he's the boss."

He stopped dead. The paw hung in the air where he'd left it.

"...When did that happen?" He turned to Thormund, genuinely stricken. "Was there a day? Did I miss a day?"

Adom was already moving past him. He stopped in front of Seth-Horb and held out a small clouded crystal on a cord.

"For her. Peregrine." He said. "You've got the look. The walk, the voice, all of it. What you can't copy is what I feel like underneath, and she can sense a mage's core from across a room. She'll be expecting to feel my core, even if a little."

He set the crystal in the changeling's palm and closed his fingers over it. "This carries the feel of mine. Place it against your chest, under the shirt, and it stays there. Don't take it off for anything."

Seth-Horb lifted the cord over his head and tucked the crystal away, and when he answered, even the voice was right.

"Understood."

"Time," Maya said from the doorway. "Everyone is here, including the professor already in his cabin. We move."

Adom nodded, but he didn't go yet. He looked at the man wearing his own face, and for a moment he set the clock down.

"Thank you," he said. "You didn't have to do this. Three days, maybe less, and I'll be back to take my face off your hands. But I know what I'm asking of you. So. Thank you."

Seth-Horb gave a small shake of the head, and it was Adom's head, Adom's wry tilt to it, strange to watch from the outside. "It's nothing. You hold up your end, our brother comes home. That's the whole of it for us." The mouth that was Adom's mouth smiled. "Three days as a magus. I'll try not to enjoy it too much."

Seth-Horb pulled the travel coat straight and walked out into the grey to go and be Adom, while his brother Keth-sil shouldered the bag by the wagon and came to stand at Adom's side.

"...I just don't know when it happened, is the thing," Valiant was still talking to himself. "Was it gradual? Was there a moment? I feel like there should have been paperwork."

"You shouldn't have called me that," Adom told Thormund.

"It slipped." Thormund rumbled it without a trace of regret. "Sorry, boss."

"...Right." Adom went and stood at the crate, bringing himself level with the mouse. "Valiant. Listen to me. You don't work for me. You never have. We're partners, you and I, and every piece of what happens over the next three days runs through your hands, not mine. You hold the reins."

Valiant looked at him. "...Really?"

"Really. But it only works if you believe it. So." Adom straightened up. "Whose operation is this?"

"It's, ah." Valiant cleared his throat. "It's... mine?"

"Try that without the question on the end."

"Right. Yes. Yeah." He got to his feet on the crate, drew himself up to his full and unimpressive height, and the sparks came crackling back along his whiskers. "Yes. I'm the boss."

"Boss says what?" Thormund rumbled.

Valiant's head snapped round, delighted. "Oh — that's me, that's mine—" He filled his small chest with air, and spoke. "All stations. Move. Let us begin Operation Castle White."

"Yes, boss," the room answered as one, and the dead old customs house came apart into purpose, crew breaking for the doors and the docks and the wagon, the whole patient week of it finally in motion.

By the time Adom reached the gangway there was a small white weight on his shoulder. Valiant had scrambled up to ride the last stretch, and now he leaned in close to his ear.

"Hey, how was that?" he whispered. "Was it good? Be honest. The chest thing, the voice; was that a good boss thing to say?"

"Yes, Valiant," Adom said. "That was good."
