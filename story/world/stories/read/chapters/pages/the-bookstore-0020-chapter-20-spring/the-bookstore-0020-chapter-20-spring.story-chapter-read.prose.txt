Anne watched her excited customers walk back toward town until the road swallowed them.

A quiet, unexpected sense of satisfaction settled in her chest. She had not predicted that helping customers connect to one another would feel this rewarding. Strictly speaking, it was not part of a bookmancer’s formal duties. Then again, the more Anne considered it, the less certain she became of that distinction. If her craft was meant to guide people toward the right knowledge, then helping them reach the right allies might not be so different.

It was, at the very least, adjacent to her work.

That thought pleased her enough to linger for several steps before she forced herself to let it go. The bookstore did not run on pleasant feelings alone, and her daily tasks were far from finished.

She returned to the archives. The moment she entered the storage section, she heard the disturbance. It came from the smaller scroll room—a muffled commotion of movement, light impacts, and what sounded suspiciously like Moruh trying to sound authoritative while being ignored.

Anne sighed and changed direction.

Inside, the scene was exactly as unpromising as the noise had suggested. Moruh stood beside one of the storage chests, hat tilted back in outrage, trying to supervise Spring’s cleaning with all the pomp of an offended administrator. Spring, in turn, was very clearly not submitting to that supervision with proper humility. The little wooden servant was moving around the room with quick, prickly energy, doing its work just well enough to remain technically blameless while also making a point of disobeying every part of Moruh’s tone it disliked.

The homunculus had been showing more personality lately, which Anne had expected. A summoned nature spirit bound into such a body was never going to remain a perfectly empty servant forever. Still, she had not anticipated bad temper.

With Anne, Spring was obedient, respectful, and eager to please. With Moruh, however, it had developed a near instinctive rebelliousness, as though it had decided that being ordered by the familiar was an insult to the dignity of all rooted things.

Usually Anne found it amusing. Today, less so. Playfulness was acceptable in many rooms, but not here, around her delicate scrolls.

She stepped into the doorway and said nothing at first. She only looked at them.

That was enough.

Moruh stiffened at once. Spring froze halfway through some suspicious motion beside a storage chest.

“Moruh,” Anne said at last, her voice calm and displeased in the most dangerous way, “do you think it is appropriate to quarrel near my scrolls?”

The question clearly required no real answer. Only submission.

“Apologies, mistress,” Moruh said immediately, lowering his head. “I was in the wrong. But this insubordinate—”

Anne shifted her gaze to him.

He stopped. A wise familiar knew when not to finish a sentence.

“Good,” Anne said. “Now tell me clearly what happened.”

Moruh recovered enough dignity to do so with impressive speed.

He had been overseeing Spring’s work in the storage rooms while handling some archiving of his own. During that time, he had noticed the servant trying to open the enthons chest.

Anne stepped back instinctively.

The reaction came before thought, a brief physical answer to the possibility of danger. Then reason returned, and with it her composure. Betrayal was impossible. The binding array anchoring Spring’s spirit to its wooden body and power source would shatter long before any true revolt against its summoning mistress could take shape. If Spring had tried to open the chest, then it had not done so out of disobedience or malice.

Anne looked at Spring more carefully. The little being looked back at her without guilt, which in itself said quite a lot.

“Well?” she asked.

Spring could not speak, and its attempts at human-like gestures were still unreliable. Yet it had been learning, and rapidly. More than that, it was willing to communicate with Anne in ways it refused to use with Moruh, who was still plainly regarded as some sort of rival authority rather than a superior.

After a short pause, Spring shuffled toward the chest and tapped the lock with one small wooden hand.

Once.

Twice.

Then it looked up at Anne expectantly.

The bookmancer almost laughed.

There was something absurdly endearing about a magical servant trying to imitate the behavior of a child asking for a locked sweet-box to be opened. She suppressed the impulse immediately. Laughing at earnest attempts to communicate was the sort of mistake that encouraged lifelong resentment.

So instead she knelt beside the chest and opened it. Inside lay neatly arranged scrolls and a few wrapped books, all stored with the care proper to old possessions of uncertain value. Spring did not paw about randomly. The moment the lid lifted, it reached carefully toward one specific scroll sealed with the mark of life.

Anne’s eyes widened. Understanding came all at once.

She placed a hand on Spring’s shoulder—not quite to restrain it, more to calm it and buy herself a moment to think.

The scroll was one of her enthons.

That was what the craft called them: sealed blessings or magical gifts preserved in scroll form, meant to be opened only once, at which point the stored effect would be released and the enthon itself would vanish forever. This particular one had passed down through her family line from a distant bookmancer ancestor who, according to tradition, had received it in exchange for a book. Whether the blessing had come directly from the Goddess of Life or merely from one of Her temples, Anne did not know. The seal only told the general direction, not the exact nature of the contained power.

That uncertainty was the problem. Once unsealed, the effect would occur. There would be no testing it halfway. No returning it to storage because the timing was poor.

Still, this enthon was valuable rather than irreplaceable. If it had belonged in the latter category, it would not be stored in so ordinary a chest behind so ordinary a lock. Anne could afford to use it.

That did not mean she would do so carelessly.

“Come,” she said, taking the scroll. “We are not opening this in here.”

She led both servants outside to the backyard.

The circle beneath the bookstore still extended its strength through the grounds, which made the yard the obvious place. Her books would be safe indoors, the open air would reduce the chance of collateral damage, and if the blessing proved unstable or dangerous, the domain would still support her spellwork.

Once they reached a good distance from the house, Anne raised her hands and cast.

“Restrigere, Spatium!”

Blue light flowed outward in clean lines and rose around them into a curved barrier. It formed a contained ring with the sealed enthon at its center. Even Anne felt a flicker of satisfaction at the result. Supported by the bookstore circle, the spell was far stronger and effective than when she had cast it around the rift. The barrier’s surface held with greater consistency, its glow steadier, its shape more exact.

For now, it existed simply to contain the blessing if it attempted to spread outward. If Anne sensed true danger, she could collapse the space tighter and isolate the scroll alone.

She turned to Moruh.

“You do the honors.”

The familiar straightened, pleased by the trust.

When duty required it, Moruh could draw upon Anne’s grimoire through their bond and cast from it, though his command remained far beneath hers. That was secondary here. An unsealing spell was simple work by comparison.

Moruh gathered the needed magic.

“Inverto Apio!”

The seal brightened into pale green light and then dissolved.

The scroll rose, unfurling in the air. Three luminous marks shone within it, each one formed of layered life-essence script so old and elegant that Anne felt a small thrill of scholarly envy even through her caution.

A heartbeat later, the marks came apart.

They dissolved into a swirling knot of green and turquoise essence.

Spring moved toward it in a heartbeat.

Anne did not stop it.

By then she already understood.

The essence spun once around the little servant’s outstretched arm, then poured into it. Spring began to glow. The wooden body trembled, not violently, but with the deep structural strain of something being remade from within.

It grew.

Not enormously, but enough that within moments it stood near Anne’s shoulders. The gaps between pieces of carved wood disappeared as vines thickened and spread over the body, weaving themselves into a flexible, fibrous skin. The old puppet-like look softened. What had once resembled a moving construct now seemed closer to something grown.

The head changed next. Features emerged with startling speed. Light blue eyes opened like clear spring water beneath fresh leaves. A small angular nose formed. Soft rounded ears of pale wood shaped themselves at the sides of the face. Even the mouth changed, becoming a greener line of living vine rather than a carved groove. The feet remained somewhat rough—bundled kindling gathered into stable shape—but the hands refined themselves into proper fibrous fingers.

Anne stared.

The answer rose in her mind with the force of memory.

Treefolk.

She had never seen one in person. Few people had. Such beings were said to come into existence only when an ancient tree developed true sentience and a soul, which made them almost absurdly rare—perhaps nearly as rare as a living bookmancer line. Yet the resemblance to old drawings and archived descriptions was undeniable.

Not fully grown, certainly. Younger and smaller. Something between a sapling form and a true treefolk body. But close enough.

More importantly, one conclusion was impossible to avoid.

“Wrong,” Anne whispered to herself, correcting the thought as it formed. “Not a homunculus.”

Spring was no longer that. The wooden servant standing before her had crossed some boundary Anne did not yet fully understand. It had become something alive in a way it had not been before. Blessed, transformed, and no longer reducible to craft terminology alone.

A living being.

Spring looked down at its new hands, then up at Anne.

For the first time since its creation, Anne felt no urge at all to think of it as an object, a project, or even a servant first.

Only as Spring.
