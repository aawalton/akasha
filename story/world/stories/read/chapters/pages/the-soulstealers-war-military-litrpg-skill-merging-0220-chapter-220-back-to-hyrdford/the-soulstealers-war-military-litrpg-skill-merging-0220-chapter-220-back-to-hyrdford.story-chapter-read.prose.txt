Our horses’ saddlebags were already prepared, and we could carry anything else in our kit bags. We spread the newly gathered crystals and dragon hairs out throughout our horses’ bags, then put the core in Burr’s saddlebag. Since she was the strongest, she could manage the extra weight.

As soon as our three horses were ready, we raced out the stable doors, then rode out the castle’s main gate. Our horse’s hooves kicked up a plume of frost and snow as we turned west.

Then we rode as hard as we could. Burr pulled ahead, and I kept trying to slow her down to match my squad’s pace. Their horses tired faster, but I wouldn’t stop until we’d put those orcs so far behind us we couldn’t see them.

We’d deal with the enemies from within before we dealt with the outsiders. I couldn’t do anything without consolidating the resources of Hinlaw’s domain. Once I was Ealdorman, we could help hold back this tide.

We rode toward the foothills. As we travelled, the rising sun melted the springtime snow and frost, making the ground sloppy. We stuck to the high hilltops as best we could. At least that way, if there was a threat, we’d see it coming. Besides, in the spring, the fangrass didn’t whip the winds into a frenzy. We could cross with ease.

It only took an hour before we couldn’t see the orcs anymore. I slowed down to a gentle trot.

All morning, we passed empty farms and ranches. Hopefully, Lord Ceonwin had evacuated them as he had passed.

At noon, we grouped up with the main column of retreating men. There were about two hundred civilians with them. Castle staff and others from the countryside who were evacuating. The sapper candidates flanked them on horseback, protecting them as they fled.

I rode to the front of the column, where Lord Ceonwin, the Drillmaster, and Sir Sidal rode on horseback.

“My lord,” I said, announcing my presence.

“Sir Levi.” He glanced over his shoulder at me. “Good to see you survived. Did you see the orcs?”

“Only from a distance,” I replied. “I didn’t stick around to test whether you were right or not about the Hangmen. I couldn’t see all of them, but there had to be two-thousand in view already. I bet they’ve already started spreading out, too.”

He leaned forward and patted his horse as it trotted, then gave another cautious glance over his shoulder. “How fast were they marching?”

“Seemed to be about a jogging speed, sir. But they’ll tire soon, won’t they?”

Lord Ceonwin shook his head. “Perhaps. And the castle will slow them. They will take it over and claim it as their own, if nothing else. Still, we should still increase our pace. Everyone here is healthy and can afford to move faster.”

“What happens when the orcs reach Hyrdford?” I asked. “It’s less defensible than Castle Urcia.”

“By then, Sir Levi, the Greencloaks will have contained the most potent of arcane threats, or at least scattered them. The orcs will spread, ravaging the countryside, but the hammer-blow will not fall the hardest on your city. From there, we can plan a counter-attack.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Now, you as well. Pick up the pace. If those orcs catch us in this state, we will regret it.”

~ ~ ~

At this pace, it took less than a week to reach Hyrdford. We didn’t stop nearly as long in the evenings (we only camped for about four hours every night), nor did we take breaks throughout the day. Burr handled it all with her head held high, and she didn’t show signs of slowing even once.

Fitting for a soon-to-be arcane beast, of course.

As we rode, the others took sporadic breaks by walking alongside their horses, but Burr didn’t have such limitations. She trotted through the snow-crusted grass with ease. Closer to the mountains, there was far more snow still on the ground, though it was wet. With each step, it crunched, and it made everything smell musty.

On the last day, as we approached Hyrdford’s valley, I patted Burr’s neck and whispered, “You’re coming along nicely.”

She neighed approvingly.

“Sir Levi!” Lord Ceonwin called from the front of the column. “We are almost there. Join us at the front of the column, if you will!”

I snapped Burr’s reins, and we galloped to the front of the cluster. We approached a ridge looking down into the river valley.

Smoke was rising from within the valley, and I couldn’t tell if it was chimney smoke or a fire. Knowing what I did, it was probably a fire.

I galloped ahead of Lord Ceonwin and the others at the head of the front, then stopped at the ridge overlooking the valley. From this vantage, I could see the entirety of the city and its walls.

The cultists were already here.

Tents surrounded the city, but they were placed sporadically, and at a safe distance, where arrows couldn’t hit them. There weren’t enough tents to host a massive army, but that didn’t matter. These tents were made of black fabric. Standards emblazoned with red teardrops fluttered in the breeze.

The smoke came from smouldering campfires, and from a few buildings near the edge of the city which had caught aflame.

Within the city, people shouted, splashing buckets of water from the stream on the flames. My guards manned the walls. Most were mortal villagers, but I picked out our squad from the 294th, holding a watchtower.

At first, it was difficult to spot the cultists. By my best estimate, there were only about a hundred of them, and they attacked at different points along the wall. They used mantlets to approach the wall. When they drew close, they launched arrows and threw spears at the defenders. Every so often, one’s tattoos would begin glowing, and all of the nearby defenders would scatter. The cultists couldn’t draw anything.

On average, they were Steel, with a few Titaniums, but that was where the Greencloaks came in. If the Titaniums tried to jump up to the wall, the Greencloaks dealt with them, striking them down with their Arts.

I could only see two Greencloaks—Saeglaf and his apprentice. Cuthward was right. The third had gone missing at some point, and I had to assume the worst.

“The cultists weren’t expecting to get this much of a fight, I’d say,” Sir Sidal said, trotting up beside me. “They can’t use their Arts very well without something to draw maegen from, and they’re forced to use traditional siege tactics.”

“Siege warfare isn’t my specialty,” the Drillmaster said. “But I know enough to say that these boys weren’t expecting much of a fight at all.”

“They’ve been probing our defenses for months…” I said. “They’re still doing some damage, and they’re picking off the defenders. They’re going to try to starve the city out. That must have been their plan. They saw no need to take it quickly.”

“Perhaps,” Lord Ceonwin said. “But look over there.” He pointed to the city gate. A troop of cultists, sheltered by mantlets, approached the gate. They carried a heap of corpses between them.

“What are they doing?” Sir Sidal breathed.

“You said they need to draw energy to use their Arts,” I said. “Well, looks like they finally picked off enough of my people from the walls to get those bodies. They’re going to draw energy from the bodies right next to the gate, then use that to empower their Arts and bust it down.” I pointed to the very rear of the cluster approaching the gate. “And I bet that guy’s gonna be doing the busting.”

A massive cultist, nearly twice as wide as the others and at least a few heads taller than them, approached. He wore their standard black robes, and his head was entirely bald, save for the etchings in his scalp. An arrow stuck out of his shoulder, but he didn’t seem to mind. The defenders weren’t focused on him.

In fact, they didn’t seem worried about the potential gate breachers at all. The bulk of my forces concentrated on the opposite side of the wall, fighting a different cluster of attackers.

Most of the second cluster were cultists, but at the center, a trio of men in yellow gambesons and chainmail hid behind mantlets, carrying Great Warbrands and round shields.

“Those must be the commanders,” I said. “Someone sent by Lord Hinlaw.” I couldn’t make out their faces, but they seemed to be Path-walkers as well. “They’re drawing the defenders’ attention away from the gate.”

Saeglaf was a wicca, and his apprentice was a druid. They didn’t have any ranged attacks, but whenever Hinlaw’s men used an Art, they countered it with one of their own. One of Hinlaw’s men ducked out from behind his mantlet and launched a bar of flame off his fist, but Saeglaf dispersed it with a palm strike, scattering sparks across the battlefield. If the defenders just left these guys to their own devices, they’d burn down the palisade wall without a second thought.

“This is a mess…” I whispered.

“I say it’s high time we helped them out,” Lord Ceonwin said, pulling on his helmet and drawing his Great Warbrand. Sir Sidal did the same, and the Drillmaster pulled an axe from his saddlebag and tightened his shield. “Sir Levi, you know this castle the best. I leave the plan in your hands.”

“Mine?” I said.

“For this hour, and this hour alone, I will make you the highest ranked candidate,” the Drillmaster said, approaching from behind and tapping my shoulder with his shield. “According to the tradition you set, the candidates will follow you.”

“Not the lieutenants?”

“As a thegn, you do technically outrank them,” Lord Ceonwin provided.

“Yes. Alright.” I cleared my throat, hoisted up my spear and donned my helmet, then said, “I’ll lead half the Dupes around the gate and stop them from breaching. Lord Ceonwin will go around the other way and deal with Hinlaw’s men. We’ll wrap around and meet in the middle, then search the interior of the city to clean up any threats. Brawl has a horn, and Sir Sidal has a horn. If either party is in trouble, signal, and we will race to your aid. If neither party needs a signal, we’ll take it as a good sign. It’ll mean that no one needed help, and Hinlaw couldn’t muster a very effective force.”

I listed off a group of squads, which included Yesman’s squad. Habit was still missing, but I brought his squad with me, regardless, as well as a few others. We broke off into two columns, and left a few squads behind to guard the civilians and castle staff who had no part in this.

I glanced at Brawl, then gave him a nod. He lifted his horn to his lips and blew a heavy blast on it.

It had been months since I’d heard the horn sound, and he’d grown far stronger. He could push more air through it. Echoing around the valley, the blast caused distant flocks of birds to rise from their trees, even miles away.

I pointed my spear and charged down the slope.
