Three of their company had died. One’s fate still hung in the balance. Of the departed, two Kalsin knew well.

“Diplomacy,” Brownweather said, holding the torch above the ditch filled with kindling and the unmoving body of the bookish woman who had been a quiet fixture of their numbers. “Was not a person to mince words. Not unless they were words to keep unruly action getting out of hand. A peacemaker, I thought she was. A peacemaker sent off to do the nasty business of war. Not many of those around, I reckon. And now… strike off one more from those dwindling numbers.” He stared down into the ditch before letting the torch drop into it. “See you later, Diplomacy. Eventually, eh? Eventually. One way or another.”

Kalsin stood among the crowd, watching the flames catch and the smoke slowly starting to billow. Three graves had been dug in a triangle, the company circled around their former comrades in the dead of night. Glowing fires of several farewells happening all across the outer line of defense could be seen in the distance.

Pike ignited one of the torches and handed it over to Brownweather, who stepped across the edges of the graves to stand above the second ditch. His boots kicked up the black dust of the plains.

“Crawl. A scrawny little fella who couldn’t find his feet when the rain got the mud going.” Brownweather absently rotated the torch in his hand, watching the burning tip. “Could’ve been a good soldier. Hm, coulda been. Young, impressionable—that’s where the strongest of our mates start from. But sometimes… sometimes the good ones get culled before experience could shape them into a man.” He let the torch drop. A whoosh as fire caught kindling. “What a waste. What a damn fucking waste.”

Kalsin didn’t need to turn his head to see Ringtail swipe an angry hand across her eyes. She didn’t know Crawl as well as he did, but he could easily guess the same questions that she was asking herself. Maybe their presence would have made a difference. Or maybe it would have killed off that Rolce fellow and a different son of a different mother of a different company.

There was no point speculating. No damn point of this whole bloody meatgrinder. A soldier asked no questions—empty, they just watched the fires burn. Waiting for their own turn in the ditch.

Across the rising fires, Littleguy was a ghost of his former self. Hasn’t spoken a word since. Not even when the lieutenant asked for his real name. János, was it? Crawl had mentioned it once. It seemed almost like yesterday.

Brownweather waited above the last ditch in silence for some time. A scent of smoke was starting to descend along the border zone.

“Shutter,” Brownweather said. “Odd bugger, but hardworking. Solid shield arm despite his size. I never told anyone the story of his name, and now I reckon he wouldn’t want it shared anyway. I guess that’ll be one more thing you’re taking to the grave with you, soldier.”

Kalsin remembered. How scared the boy was, of others seeing his otherness. All but a memory now, a ghost from the past.

“Life is a harsh teacher. I had to be, too.” Brownweather lowered the torch and gently dropped it below. “Too many of you burn, in the end.”

Sparks hissed, heat rising from the trio of graves. Brownweather stepped back slowly until he was standing among the former recruits, joining them in silent guard over their departing mates. A gust of wind stirred the flames even higher, giving life to the orange-and-red blaze until they stretched higher than the earthen walls beyond.

The flames were starting to make his eyes hurt. Kalsin kept on staring.

Fals had stepped forward at some point. She did not bring her lute tonight. She didn’t need anything but her voice to say goodbye.

Somewhere far away, he could feel Ringtail’s hand grip onto his arm, wrapping herself closer to his side. But most of the world was reduced to the guilt, the fire, and a lone voice singing above the roar of the flames.

What a sight this burial must’ve been to the distant observer—an endless row of lights stretching for miles upon miles. You could almost picture the common folk pointing skyward, marveling at it like a distant constellation, a flicker of joy lighting their hearts. Unaware that the same joy they felt was being extinguished in the hearts of those who kindled the flames.

Crawl, dead. Shutter, dead. Batshit, still barely holding on to life.

How many more would die the next time?

This place, this outer line of defense, was a butcher’s yard where the Clenn herded their lessers to die in their steads. The Clenn; they—who held the upper powers of the surge, who had both strength and wealth far above any Scarrd. The golden soldiers treated this cursed place like some twisted vacation resort only to return to their protected forts and honeyed lives.

The job of the strong was to protect the weak. Not to let them be slaughtered for the sake of their comforts like expendable rats.

Kalsin watched the flames, numb. Numb from the reality he suddenly woke up to. Numb, so he could escape the responsibility that sheer chance has put on his shoulders—a responsibility he did not want to accept.

The crowd was slowly starting to disperse into the night as flames died down to embers. Kalsin walked, too. It was dark after staring so long into the brilliance of the flames, but he could make out his hut on the edge of their lodges well enough. No stars reflected up in the night sky.

“...to be alone right now, Ghost.”

Kalsin was startled to find Ringtail walking with him. He stopped at the entrance of his place, looking down at the grip she had on his arm. Then back up at her.

There must’ve been something in his eyes. His silver orbs were disquieting at the best of times, after all.

Ringtail let go of his arm with a start. She opened her mouth to speak, then frowned instead. “I know it’s still raw, but—but we knew this would happen from the start. Maybe not this exactly, but, I mean… shit.”

“Go back to your hut, Ringtail.”

Kalsin didn’t need this right now. He didn’t know what he needed—but not this.

“No. Not until I see you lucid enough not to slip onto a sharp object by accident.”

He let out a sour laugh. “What, after all that time being a pain in the ass, you’re suddenly concerned about my wellbeing?”

Ringtail turned away. She only took a step away before she turned sharply back and thrust a finger in his chest. “I was never a pain— Like, I never wanted to, I was just… You’re just… Ugh!”

“Say it, then,” Kalsin said, grabbing her wrist to keep her finger from digging into him. “What is it that makes you just sooo insufferable toward a guy who just wants to be left alone?”

Ringtail pushed on his chest with her free arm. “Oh, so now you want to be left alone? Surely that Clenn girl you hugged after the battle was an accident, right?”

Kalsin kept his balance, but had to tug her closer to keep her wrist from sneaking out of his grasp. “What does that have to do with anything?”

“I dunno, you tell me! Like, here comes Ghost with his brooding dark gait and silver eyes, not interested in anyone, and a girl’s gotta go the extra mile to be even noticed, and then—”

“Your antics made it pretty damn hard not to notice you, when you keep acting like an annoying fucking brat at each turn!”

“Oh, yeah? Says the guy who runs away the moment I try to apologize!”

“Apologies don’t work when you don’t stop doing the thing you’re trying to apologize for!”

“Your shieldwork is laughable!”

“You kick like a moron!”

Ringtail grabbed his collar with both hands, pushed her chest into him, and pulled him down to smush her lips against his.

Kalsin’s body froze. Froze in a way that he hasn’t in a very, very long time.

He felt Ringtail’s lips move against his, the smaller woman’s desperate grip trembling against his collar to keep him at her level. He felt the warmth of her body pressing close, trying to push herself whole into him. He felt…

He felt… something.

Something that wasn’t emptiness. Something that wasn’t failure, or aimlessness, or desperation.

Kalsin staggered back half a step, Ringtail sticking on him like glue—and then his grip on her wrist tightened. He pulled her off of him with some effort, the maneuver making both of them spin, switching places.

Ringtail’s back hit the door with a breathless gasp, the fire in her eyes not sated in the slightest as she tried to push off toward him again. But she didn’t get the chance. The door rattled as Kalsin pinned her in place with his own body, leaned down, and kissed her twice as hard as she’d done just a second before.

Her hands slid up, clawing into his hair. His tongue pushed past her lips, tasting smoke and citrus and entering a battle that she simply refused to give up. She pulled away just enough to free their lips, and then back on the offensive she was, feverish to come out on top.

One of his hands trailed down to her hip, squeezing. She responded to his touch, and would have offered him more if the door behind her didn’t swing suddenly inward, making both of them stumble inside the dark hut.

They fell, then scrambled back up using each other’s bodies for purchase. Hands clutched desperately, seeking contact and the bed further inside. The door slammed shut from a wayward foot kicking into it.

There was a scraping of boots across the wooden floor, then a thud as they landed in one corner. A shirt slipped off in the dark, followed by another, and then they were back at breathing the same air. Teeth scraped lips, nipping and biting, and then they fell onto the bed in a tangle that wasn’t clear on who was above or below.

Whispers, as she was saying something. Other words followed, his voice, though he hardly remembered. Everything was a blur, vague outlines of vision and sounds and feelings that existed to fill the void with something good. With something past the pain of war and grime he had known his entire life.

Too much death. The funeral pyres burned along the outer line of defense to remind folk the people they had lost to senseless things and causes they didn’t believe in. Men and women walked away from their burning comrades and rested their heads on their pillows, telling themselves that maybe, some day, this hollowness would go away and become a distant memory.

Kalsin knew better.

But for just one single night—the boy the world had abandoned wanted to pretend that he was loved.
