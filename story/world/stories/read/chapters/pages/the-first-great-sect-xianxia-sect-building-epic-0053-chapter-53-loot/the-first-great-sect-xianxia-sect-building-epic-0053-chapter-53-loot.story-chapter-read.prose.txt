The losses on Hua’s side were negligible. Four deaths and eight casualties. One in four of her soldiers harmed. But against four times their number, she could live with that, and the eight were up and walking anyway. It was not the perfect victory Hua had desired. It far exceeded the total defeat everyone else expected.

The Imperial Captain Ciao was in the healer’s tent. He’d been the only casualty amongst that contingent. Hua remembered intimidating him in Liaojiangkou, a rather minor distinction as she assumed most people who encountered her had been victims of intimidation at one point or were targets for future intimidation. As for the mortals, Hua truly had no idea, and could not be bothered to find out.

Hua glared at Kang’s corpse, attempting to immolate it with the force of her glare.

You were disappointingly weak. Which means the Jolly Rangers were all weak. Ranger Xue, I will fulfil my obligation to you, and that will be the end of it between us.

Hua was very good at ignoring people who disappointed her. This would be no different. At least her soldiers hadn’t disappointed her. Captain Yao had ordered some of his men to go about fulfilling her desire for heads. A good man in a jovial mood now that he had advanced. Hua approved of how easily his swagger and cheer raised the spirits of his men.

The others, those uninjured, were mixed between those performing triage, those eating and drinking, and the rest working their way through the bandit camp. Searching for anything of interest. Hua would happily accept money, knowledge, and artefacts.

She would be forced to wait a full day for the bandit camp to be fully looted.

They first had to wait for the fires to die down. Liu Xin had done too efficient a job in spreading them and whatever records Kang kept were ashes. So, they were left with corpses to piece together information and a timeline barely coherent given by the refugees. Hua quickly realised she truly, truly, did not care and left others to manage it whilst she nursed her aching arm. The fight hadn’t been too difficult, but she had taken a full-strength blow from a Body Cultivator with her bad arm. The newly opened wounds and pained joints spoke to that foolishness. Punching his face in had likely cracked a few of her handbones.

She leaned against a tree, near where the horses had been tethered, and simply watched without thought as order was found in this field of slaughter. Vultures descended to feast on the fallen bandits. She’d even spotted a particularly brave leopard swipe a body, padding silently across pools of bloody and melted ice, avoiding light, always finding shadows in the plain. So audacious a move was it that Hua doubted it had truly occurred.

The corpses had largely been dragged, rolled, and shoved into a deep depression. Piled high with whatever wood and dry fuel had survived their camp and set ablaze without a word for them. Sizzling fat and flesh was preferable to the bloating corpses whose smell only deepened with the rising sun.

Well, if Hua did wish to speak to them, she had their heads to communicate with. A rather amusing situation that the vultures were using to stuff themselves with. A dozen eyes had been filched already, one-eyed gazes from beheaded housing tracking Hua, judging her. Hua felt it rather amusing that her mind so easily made stories for the indifferent dead.

“My lord, we’ve finished up over here.”

She nodded at the soldier who, for all he had killed perhaps a dozen bandits, still looked at her with squeamishness. She wore a brown dress of rusted blood and a crown of bone fragments and broken teeth. Hua had not been trying to bathe in the blood of her enemies. It happened anyway.

The bandit’s stolen wealth had been collected, placed in trunks and boxes, and left for Hua to peruse. There was privacy enough for Hua’s liking when she arrived with Weiji on her heels. He wasn’t covered in anywhere near the amount of gore as Hua. Maybe there was something to be said for breaking necks instead of stabbing through them?

Over the months and potential years Kang had run roughshod across the Western Reaches, he had accumulated a great quantity of silver. A dozen great chests and many smaller trunks of silver had been salvaged. They were good chests. Hardwood, wrapped in good leather, banded with brass bars. Good construction all around. Even bandits knew to keep their goods somewhere strong but also easily moved. Had a warning from Kang’s scouts gone out, she didn’t doubt these chests would have gone missing in the fighting.

“These bandits didn’t discriminate,” Weiji pointed out, lifting a variety of silver pieces in his hands.

Some glittered, some were dull, others sharp. Silver shaped into all sorts of shapes: flat coins and rotund taels, long necklaces and broad arrows stamped with characters; bullion thick and convenient sat beside raw chunks of ore; and here, the miscellaneous jacket buttons and hair pieces, bracelets and stolen heirlooms. Anything and everything that was considered silver, the bandits kept.

“Lots of foreign coinage. I don’t even recognise this one,” Weiji added, showing an unevenly shaped coin. One side had blocky, ugly, harsh characters trapped within a circle of leaves. The other the face of a man with strange hair and stranger features.

Hua placed the coin back amongst the others. “Neither do I. Some of these, though, I do. Eastern Sanctuary coinage here—not sure if this is current, but they overthrow their government every century so anything that’s the right weight is close enough to legal tender. I think some of these belong to the Jurchen tribes.” She paused. Weiji said nothing. “Not going to accuse them of a foreign scheme to destabilise us?”

He threw some silver at her. Hua caught the silver arrow and pocketed it without thought. Maybe a gift for the Ranger?

“One, Jurchens aren’t smart enough for a scheme this complex. Two, we live near two borders and within spitting distance of the coast is another landmass. We get enough foreign coinage every year. You should know this. For someone who wants to make a Counting House through the Clan, you certainly know very little about coinage. We exchange all the foreign coin we get paid in for standard Imperial coinage at the Counting Houses at a preferred rate for, well, not violently murdering any and all merchants who irritate us.”

“You think Grandmother is having fun murdering merchants at Seagate? They must have more entertaining Cultivators to kill than Kang was.”

“Sadly for Seagate City, while you are her foremost student, you are still a student, so I expect the sea to run red by the time Liao Furen returns.”

“Why is it that I never get any entertainment?”

“A killing field and the many depopulated villages we encountered weren’t entertainment enough? Remember, my lord, we haven’t even made it to Coaltown. There is still an opportunity for mortals to be foolish.”

“Are you managing me?”

“In the futile hope I can stop you stumbling into another nightmare scenario, yes. Plain and simple, I don’t trust our luck. Please tell me you know why I need you to move cautiously from here onwards?”

“Of course,” she said resentfully, hating the tone he took.

“Then tell me why,” he said steadily.

“Because we don’t know how many people are waiting to fuck us over in Coaltown,” she said rudely just for his scandalised squawk. Except, he simply watched her, unphased. Waiting for her answer. “It could be anything we find once we reach Coaltown. Can’t know if we’re going to be surrounded by an army immediately or if it’s all fragmentary alliances or if this was all just a set of coincidences that chose now to pile up.”

“You might manage to escape if the entire city turns on us, maybe the both of us, but the mission will be a failure, and the Elders will pick a new lord immediately.”

She glared at him. “I know the stakes. I will not see myself supplanted through sheer stupidity and arrogance. Even I know there is a time for games and a time to handle matters seriously.”

“And when it comes time to maintain your authority, you do not play those games. Accept the insults your vassals sling behind your back. Put up with their petty games for influence because it’s inevitable. But when it comes time to secure your position, you put away the weiqi board and you pick up your sword. You named yourself a dragon. Show the world your claws when you must.”

“You’re being paranoid.”

“Am I? We’re both covered in the blood of our enemies under circumstances that should never have occurred. Someone betrayed us long before we came here. In Coaltown, don’t let your need for amusement be the reason everything collapses.” He huffed, shrugged, moved on. “Do you plan on giving the mortals any silver?”

“Perhaps some of it. Enough to keep them in line. The rest will be in paper bills. Maybe we’ll get the shaman to bless the bills.”

“Money tainted by a shaman. Our forebearers are shuddering in their graves.”

“Pretty sure their spirits all got burnt up, so you won’t have to worry about that.”

One of the trunks had been marked for her interest. She opened it. Inside were trophies. They could only be trophies. Dinged, marked up, burnt and broken, they had been taken from enemies after battle.

In place of pride was a set of Medallions. They carried a symbol of a grinning mask, but in the swirling patterns that made up that mask, a phrase could be read: The Jolliest of Rangers.

These had belonged to the Jolly Rangers, which was apparently a shortening of their true name. Well, Hua had another gift for Xue Meili.

“My lord, might I offer some advice.”

Hua only realised she was smiling when he spoke and killed her fondness with the same aggression she had pulped a man’s skull.

“No.” She could guess what he was to say. “I’m returning what belongs to her, nothing more. Don’t bring it up again.”

“As your Deputy—”

“Weiji.”

“As your cousin—”

“Weiji,” she warned, flickering her Qi.

“Fine. You have my apologies. I won’t bring up your infatuation again.”

Hua took the medallions, placed them in a pouch, and from there in one of the pouches sewn into her robes. She closed the trunk of trophies, indifferent to whoever they belonged to, and ignored Weiji’s judgemental gaze.

“Was Captain Yao expected to advance?” she asked, ignoring his judgemental stare.

“Do you think I just keep that information on hand at all times?”

“You always seem to know everything going on in the clan.”

“Some prophecies are fulfilled by avoiding them. If you have information on hand, then you can shoo someone away faster. But then it gets out you know a lot, so more people keep coming, and before you know it, you’re at the centre of the web.” He sighed, deeply, a weariness found in his marrow. “Captain Yao wasn’t projected to advance to the second star in the next five years, possibly a decade. He was never considered incredibly talented by clan standards, but exceptional for the vassals. A good option to marry into the main clan.”

“He’s unmarried? I thought he was a man grown.”

“Hua—I mean, Weilong, he’s only a bit older than me. He does have a child, but we don’t talk about her.”

“Should I be more concerned about more bastards running around?”

“She was adopted by his uncle, so no. It was a minor scandal, admittedly, but a daughter can be married off and bear many children, so his lineage, and hopefully his talent, are secured. He can take a bit of time to find a different wife, and after this breakthrough, he’ll have his pick of the clan.”

“Is there anyone you suggest?”

“A month ago, I’d have suggested you marry him and save me the suffering. Now… I don’t know. Truly. There aren’t many good options that wouldn’t weaken you in some way. I’ll have to think it through a bit more.”

“You do that. I think it’s time we head back.”

“Skulls and all?” her cousin asked with a miserable sigh.

“Skulls and all,” she agreed cheerily.

***

The healing tent was a familiar destination, one Hua found herself more eager to visit than her own tent. And maybe if not for her cousin giving her an unimpressed look and pointing out she smelled like a battlefield—of which, Hua was learning, were shit, piss, fear, mud, and rot—she would have headed straight there. But Hua had some dignity and cleaned out the teeth tangled in her hair, washed away all the brown smudges from dried blood, and wore a clean set of robes.

She did keep the shoulder guards and the skirt of pleated armour around her waist. Hua thought they looked appropriate for a returning lord at war. Just some armour and her sword. Nothing extravagant.

Oh, and a head in a sack.

“Might this lord enter?” she called loudly, voice cracking, entering without waiting for a response.

“If you are looking for the Ranger, she was moved,” the Young Ren told her, dead-eyed and dead tired, sleeves tainted red.

Hua felt a hint of embarrassment and took in the rather injured soldiers. The casualties. Hua had forgotten about them. She could never, under any circumstances, let Weiji know about this blunder.

“This lord had hoped to see his soldiers well. They brought Liao a great deal of valour and should be honoured,” she lied and lied and lied like a lying liar. “A greater portion of silver will be rewarded for your bravery.”

“Just leave already,” Old Ren demanded, gesturing at the sack saturated red at the bottom. “And never bring a skull into my healing tent again. There are not enough silver taels in the world to make me want to have skulls enter my healing tent.”

She left in a rather dignified huff managing to comport herself with all the petulance of a lord scorned. Hua asked around casually. A hint of looking. A few pointed threats and the very pertinent example of a head in a skull to get the answer she desired.

“Might this lord enter, honourable Ranger?”

“The lord may,” came the raspy, weak answer. There was a lifetime of weariness to it that could not be healed in a few days. Hopefully, a skull would aid it along.

Ranger Xue Meili was much recovered in that way of Cultivators given resources and time. She was still sickly pale, still swaddled in bandages with droplets of red, and deliberately slow in her movements. No gut wound could be so easily ignored.

“A Ranger is told the kingfisher flew victorious. Kang has fallen and Lord Liao returns triumphant.”

“This Lord was uncertain if the Ranger desired all the heads brought at once, but he brought Kang’s as a preliminary gift,” Hua said confidently, arrogance to counter the Ranger’s grief. Barrelling forward ceaselessly was the only thing Hua knew to do.

She retrieved the head from the sack and placed it on a table. With the sack folded up beneath it so it wouldn’t drip and leave a red mess, of course. Hua wasn’t rude, just ruthless. The Ranger stared at the skull, not with fear, not with hate, but a bitterness that deepened.

“You truly did it.”

“The lord made a vow to you, Xue Meili,” she said, savouring the taste of the name on her tongue. Hua gave her a cocky grin, copying the memory of her brother, the true heir. “This lord would not be made a liar.”

“You did what we could not. What we vowed to do. Fulfilled my contract. Our contract.”

Hua was unsure what to do with that. Felt her confidence evaporating. It felt… unsuited for this moment, this time carrying grief and loss in the air.

“This lord comes bearing another offering.”

“One might think a lord is courting below his station,” she whispered, eyes still wide.

“A lord does as he pleases within his domain. But this is an apology.” She switched then to less formal language. “Liao was meant to hold sentinel over these lands. You and yours did what my clan did not. It should not have been necessary, not within the territories of Liao, and for that, you have my apology.” Hua bowed deeply, disregarding her dignity, and presented the Ranger with a leather pouch.

It jangled, the metal within knocking against each other. It was loosened already and the Ranger taking it opened it further, revealing the medallions within inside. Hands shaking, fingers missing, the last Jolly Ranger took the medallion her companions had once carried. She examined the first.

It was bigger than her palm.

Tears bloomed, but the Ranger valiantly controlled herself. Hua felt her heart sink.

“Why? Everything Shaman Yan has said of you made you out to be a bored leopard. Why go this far for another?”

She looked so young. Barely older than Hua. A woman grown, but not by much.

“Because you asked, Xue Meili,” she said, imparting an apology in her name. Trying to say I care and I know and I feel your same pain through her name alone, a name chosen aptly for there was beauty in resilience and grace in survival.

The Ranger surged forward. Hua startled, fear surging. And then, there was no pain.

Just a girl shaking in her arms.

Xue Meili wept in Hua’s arms.

They stayed like that for a time.

***

The Ranger fell asleep in Hua’s arms. She helped her to a more comfortable position, laid blankets over her, and once she was sure the Ranger would sleep well, Hua fled. Not for any reason she could name. But her throat was tight and her heart ached. The ghost she was ignoring felt more real and not in the way she sought.

Her swift feet and bubbling panic led her to the forests between the two camps. Brought her beneath tall trees that stretched and stretched and kept going. Hua focused on her breathing like any good Cultivator, forced her body to a state of calm, and shoved all those things she could not think about into a corner of her mind where they could wither and die like crops during a drought.

Aimlessly, she walked, and an aim she walked to. It took only minutes of walking in a haze to stumble upon a shaman plotting something secret and felt a surge of betrayal. Hot, furious betrayal, something she could latch on to. Not the cold, dead grief that led to her ghost.

“Can you track him down?” Wu Yan spoke softly. “Knew he’d be going dark for the winter but—who is there? Come out and stop with your sneaking.”

She had good senses, this shaman. Hua wasn’t keeping her presence suppressed, but it still should have been a quiet thing. Walking silently was instinct at this point, forcing the full weight of her presence needless. Wu Yan turned with a pained grimace.

“Liao,” she said her family name like a curse. “What you need from us? Privacy too much to give.”

“Who are you tracking and how will this cause me problems in the future?”

“Ain’t not everything your business.”

“Your foreign masters maybe.”

“Got a saying, don’t you. Something about the oriole. No wonder you be so paranoid. Ain’t a thing foreign in the west beyond you.”

“Then tell this Lord who you speak of,” she said furiously, lightning arcing between her fingers.

Wu Yan, for once, did not dissemble or insult Hua. Accurately read that there was something deeper and chose not to die for someone else’s problems.

“The smuggler we’ve been using. Told you about him. Kong the Sailor. Want to find him, I do. Got another job for him.”

“You have yet to explain why he’s important enough for you to send your beloved mortals to find him.”

“Got connections on connections he does. Tell him one thing, the entire West will know in weeks. Way for people to know they ain’t got to worry about no serpent biting them. Make people feel safe, know they don’t have to pack even the dust from their homes.”

“That’s worth sending your people out for?”

“They’re all my people, everyone, different village or no. All the same shit we live through, all the same struggle. Don’t get that, you, but it means everything to me, us.”

Her anger deflated. Hua felt rather small.

She looked at the mortal standing quietly, fiercely, beside Wu Yan and thought she… not recognised him, but remembered him in relation to another. The lesioned mortal who had died to Kang. He’d been greeted warmly by another mortal when they reached the convoy. Hua thought this was that same mortal.

“Go to my deputy and tell him I’m dispensing a stipend for your mission.”

Both shaman and mortal were shocked. Hua herself was shocked by her words, so informal and generous. Things a lord should not be.

“Not doing this for the goodness of your heart,” Shaman Yan said slowly.

“No, I want it known that Lord Liao Weilong struck down that bandit camp. I want everyone in the west to know that Liao has arrived and that this savagery will end only in death. Liao remembers her people and Liao will not forgive.”

“Don’t much like you but this, this I can do with.”

***

A day would pass for her men to receive further treatment, for supplies to be catalogued and for the bounty from the bandit camp to be dispensed.

On the second day, they buried those who had fallen. The Rangers, the villagers, her soldiers and the refugees who died in battle. A ceremony that Hua led, giving Qi to light the fire. She was not a priestess, but the ways of Spiritualists were known to her. Her eyes let her see what she did not remember, the path to guide the dead. Paired with a Shaman walking her Shaman Steps, the fire burned gold and green, dawn and spring, Lord Liao and Shaman Yan.

A dozen mortals left after the funeral with pouches full of bandit silver, on a mission for their beloved shaman. To find a smuggler. To spread the presence of Lord Weilong.

The third day would see dual convoys set off, becoming one great mass, a shambling and mismatched monster of logistics, horses, donkeys, refugees, Liao Cultivators, and Imperial soldiers. A senseless conglomerate, a collage without meaning beyond the simple unity of slaughtering bandits together.

It turned out that most of the refugees would join the convoy instead of returning to their homes and rebuilding. Hua was surprised. She had assumed the call of home too strong for most to ignore.

“Justice,” Ranger Xue Meili told her. She was stronger—partly from pills Hua had given her, partly from her own resilience, mostly the satisfaction of a dead serpent—and flitted between acting as the shaman’s bodyguard and spending time with Hua. “You rendered justice in blood. They want to see what justice you render unto those who let Kang run unopposed for so long.”

Hua would rather prefer the Ranger stay with her, ride at the head of the convoy beside her, instead of sometimes falling back to the Shaman who spent her time with her people. Even Weiji’s steadily deepening displeasure at Hua’s focus—not infatuation as he mockingly implied through his poetry—could not disabuse Hua of that desire.

“Is this display truly necessary?” Wu Yan asked days later, grimacing as she batted away a green fly, just one of the cloud buzzing around the skulls.

“This lord promised to mount the heads of Kang and his group. This lord will not break his promise. Besides, this display will engender cooperation with the Mayor and the Justice.”

“Don’t like it not one bit. Destroying your karma for no reason, this. Winning don’t need a trophy.”

“Winning always deserves a trophy. And if the Ten Kings of Hell complaint, your name shall be the one on my lips should I ever die.”

Shaman Yan opened her mouth to respond. A fly flew right into it. Hua choked out a laugh. That felt like a victory as well.

The flies were a surprise and the first indication that the unreasonably early winter would give way to an unseasonably warm fall. Day by day the temperature crept higher, even as the sun rose later and set earlier. Ice no longer concerned them as much as deepening bodies of standing water created as frost and ice melted. The bodies of water themselves were not an issue, but confused insects which hatched a season early, and by the end of the first week, they were assaulted by clouds of mosquitoes.

Much as she appreciated it, the travelling cloak proved too hot and she divested herself of it. Choosing instead to waste her Qi by creating a subtle buzzing aura that zapped any insects that came too close. Weiji tried and found himself quickly exhausted by the endeavour.

Only Hua managed and for days she lorded that over her cousin. Then she realised the mosquitoes couldn’t even bite through his steely flesh.

Finally, cresting over the horizon, was a sight Hua had begun to think a myth.

Coaltown.
