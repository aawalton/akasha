Fortunately, when I approach the well, the marketplace is all but deserted. With the storm preventing people from retrieving things from the outside to sell and increasing lethargy caused by limited food, most people prefer to stay at home.

There is a man drawing water from the well when I first arrive and another waiting. Stepping forwards, I put my hand on the bucket the man has just drawn up with listless movements, preventing him from lifting it.

It takes him a moment to realise someone is stopping him from taking his newly-retrieved water.

“Hey!” he protests, an angry red flushing his cheeks. “What’re ye doing?” He tugs at the bucket hard but fails to shift it an inch.

“I’m here to inspect the well,” I tell him calmly. “Please could you return the water to it.”

The man’s face sets into recalcitrant resistance. His lower jaw emerges and he shifts as if getting ready for a fight.

“My family needs that water there,” he replies, his tone almost daring me to argue with him.

“I’m sure they do, and I’ll be sure to fill the bucket. But not with this well’s water.”

That clearly wasn’t what he was expecting as the lines of his face soften slightly and his forehead creases between his brows. He looks me up and down for a moment and I see his eyes linger on the obvious quality of my robes – they might not have my family’s crest on, but they’re still well-made.

Then his eyes return to my face and I see the dawning realisation. He suddenly drops his hands from their ready position, and backs away from the well.

“O’–o’course my lord. Do what ye need to, an’ my apologies for how I behaved there.”

I hold my sigh back at the fear in his voice – this was what I was trying to avoid.

“No harm done,” I tell him reassuringly, tipping the contents of the bucket back into the well. Using water-magic, I produce some water from my fingertips, first rinsing out the bucket – just in case – and then filling it. “Here you go.”

The man takes it with a blink, clearly not having expected that.

“Thank ye…my lord,” he says finally, then, evidently deciding not to look a gift horse in the mouth, turns and scurries away, his bucket of water held tightly to his chest.

I turn to the woman waiting.

“Would you like me to fill your bucket too?”

“Aye my lord, iffen ye would,” she replies politely, setting it down on the side of the well near me. I start filling it too. “My lord?” she asks hesitantly. I raise an eyebrow at her to show I’m listening, even as water pours out from my fingertips. “Is there somethin’ wrong with the well?”

“I’m sure it’s fine,” I reassure her quickly – I don’t want to cause a panic for no reason. “I just want to inspect it – make sure everything is fine. And if something is wrong, there’s no point in you two walking away with contaminated water, is there?”

She shakes her head, her expression easing a little. When I hand her the bucket, she bobs a bow, then walks away slowly, the weight of the water clearly a challenge for her to bear.

Seeing as no one else has joined the queue in the meantime, I turn my attention to the well. Placing my hands on either side of its round body, I look down into the water below. A metal grate about a foot down prevents children from falling in, with a hole in the middle for the bucket to descend and rise. Technically a sufficiently determined child might still manage it, but the design has proved its worth – of all the issues besetting our colony, at least I haven't heard of any children being drowned in the wells.

The water's surface is several metres below and glints blackly as I tilt my head from side to side. I can't see anything, but I wasn't really expecting to. If I could see something with my unenhanced gaze, everyone else would be able to as well. Instead, I close my eyes and send my magic through the earth and down into the water.

I feel the hum of the enchantments lining the shaft of the well, preventing anything living from entering through the micro-passages in the surrounding rock, while gently attracting the groundwater itself. These wells will only run dry when the land around them is completely parched, yet no insect can enter. The enchanters have done good work. There are no cracks in the walls, nor notes of discordancy in the enchantments which might indicate a crack or sabotage.

As I test the magic within and feel the hum of the wards, I realise that no bacteria could enter either – they are still living, by the widest definition, and will not be permitted through. They remain outside the wards, along with any parasite that might otherwise have made its way into the water. It seems unlikely that the sickness originates from contamination entering through the ground.

I investigate the water itself next. It seems clear, normal – nothing draws my attention.

Yet as I pull my consciousness back up through the walls and return to myself, I find I'm frowning. I didn't notice anything. And yet something still feels wrong. I just can't put my finger on it.

I tap my finger on my thigh, considering the situation for a long moment. Then I nod decisively. I don’t have evidence other than my unease, and this being the only common point of the initial patients, but that’s the benefit – and greatest responsibility – of being the lord: I don’t actually need any to take action.

Placing my hand on the lip of the well, I activate the emergency sealing enchantment. Quickly, earth grows from around the well, forming a dome over the well. I turn around, seeing someone else approaching with a bucket, dismay on her face.

“I’m sorry, but this well is currently unavailable,” I tell her apologetically. “You’ll need to use another one for now.”

Feeling guilty about blocking off her water source, I use Water-Shaping again to summon water for her.

She thanks me gratefully, and I nod at her before quickly walking away, my mind buzzing with thoughts. Already, I can see people beginning to whisper about the well, worry and despair on their faces. No doubt they’re unhappy with needing to go to a well in a neighbouring district – to walk extra distance when they’re already weak from hunger.

Is it really the right thing to do – I don’t even know for sure something is wrong. Then again, what kind of leader would I be to allow my people to continue drinking water that I’m not certain is good for them?

I shake my head sharply. I can’t forever second-guess myself. Time will tell if the rate of new outbreaks of sickness slows. At least there hasn’t been any evidence so far that it’s contagious like a cold – one sick member of a family doesn’t necessarily mean that everyone else gets sick. It gives me hope that if we can find the vector by which people are being infected, we might be able to stop this thing in its tracks.

Though knowing paperwork waits for me in my office, I feel like there’s a place where I’ll be more useful. The healer compound it is.

"Lord Markus, it's late. Only the night Healers remain, and to my knowledge, you have not even had dinner. You also need to sleep."

I open my eyes and look up at Thessalanor, the only one who dares to interrupt me. With the cavern closed as it is, there is no natural light to give day and night true meaning, however we’ve been trying to mimic it with enchanted lights that brighten and dim with the rise and fall of the sun outside – I thought it would be better for everyone’s mental health like that. Right now, the room in which I’m sitting is enveloped in shadow, only a dim light mimicking the moons coming in the window.

I'm sitting by the bedside of a patient who is currently unconscious. She's one of those who seems to have been worst affected by the sickness: her temperature rose worryingly high and she lost consciousness. Even now it’s broken, she still hasn’t woken up.

I've been sitting here for who knows how long, trying to find the source of the sickness in her system, but still no luck. It's deeply strange, because this isn't the first time I've encountered illness. There was an outbreak of scale mites among the samurans, which I worked with Tarra and River to find and eliminate. There was the sexually transmitted parasite I encountered at the festival. There was even an outbreak of a virus that affected the samurans' lungs, causing severe coughing and damage to the internal membranes.

In all three cases, I was able to find the source of the illness with my Flesh Shaping and do something to help. Although I couldn’t overcome the virus directly, I discovered that I could sense it once it had propagated enough to start causing symptoms, or when the body began fighting back. I was then able to aid the body in its battle while protecting its vulnerable parts from further harm.

Here, though, I can't even sense a concentration of virus or bacteria cells that might be the source of the sickness. To all intents and purposes, it looks as though her body has simply started creating these symptoms for no particular reason – which goes against everything I know about illness. It's immensely frustrating, and I know I'm not the only one feeling that way. I can see it in the eyes of every healer I encounter. I can see it in the lines on Thessalanor's face now as I look up at him.

I look back down at the woman.

“Does she have enough time for me to have a peaceful night’s sleep?” I wonder out loud, bitterness in my voice.

“We have only lost a single person to this unknown illness, Markus,” Thessalanor replies, his voice sympathetic, but with a firmness to it that clearly comes from years of managing difficult patients. “Everyone else has recovered after three to five days. This is only the third day for her – she still has time. And you will do a better job if you are fed and rested.”

“Are you following your own advice?” I ask pointedly, looking at the grey tone to his face, the shadows under his eyes, the clear loss of weight on his form.

Thessalanor gives me a mirthless smile.

“We Healers are excellent at telling others what they should do to be healthy; we are less good at following our own advice.”

I chuckle humourlessly, accepting his point.

“Fine. I’ll sleep a few hours. Just enough to take the edge off,” I reply, giving in. “Only if you do the same.”

“I was already intending on doing so – I am doing my final rounds before the night. However, unlike you, I’ve already had dinner.”

I grimace at the thought.

Thessalanor seems to read my mind as he levels me with a chiding look.

“Lord Markus, you must eat.”

"I can bear starvation far better than any of the non-Classers," I point out logically. "Missing a few meals won't harm me."

Thessalanor can't argue with that – he knows the effects of a superhuman Constitution, and I've added points well beyond twenty in that stat. Still, he wags his finger at me.

"At least one meal a day, my lord, or I'll be forced to bar you from the healing room."

We both know I could technically force my way in – and we both know I won't. He is the Head Healer and has authority here that I won't overstep – the people must know that even I, the lord, have limits.

"Alright," I promise him. "Breakfast tomorrow, at the very least. If we can improve the food situation, I might even manage more than that."

He nods firmly.

"Good. And take your companion with you. She has also been overdoing it and needs food and rest."

"Which companion?" I ask, frowning.

"River. The alchemist." He gestures towards a side room in which a light is still glowing. "She's been in there since before dawn and is still there now, and that after only a few hours of sleep in one of the healing rooms last night. Take her with you, Lord Markus – she'll be no good if she's too exhausted to think straight."

My concern rises for River in a way it didn't for myself.

"I'll speak to her," I promise. I pat Thessalanor on the shoulder and walk past him to go and find the Pathwalker.

"River," I call gently from the doorway.

She's leaning over the table, the tip of her muzzle almost touching the desktop. I know she does this because it's easier for samurans to make out the details of text up close – not nearsightedness exactly, more that their eyes aren't adapted to reading, and they haven't grown accustomed to picking out fine detail against a flat background. It's something I haven't yet worked out how to help with, beyond ensuring the contrast between pale parchment and dark ink is as stark as possible, and that the lighting is good when they're trying to work.

I hadn't actually realised that Loren was teaching the samurans to read and write until I came across one of their little circles on the ship on the way here. The reading has been considerably more successful than the writing – samuran fingers aren't quite dexterous enough for the fine motor control required for neat writing, though the Pathwalkers have fared better than the Warriors, their bodies being slimmer and more nimble. Either way, they seem to have found some success, even if the characters they use tend to be considerably larger than those of the average literate human.

Right now, I'm not sure River is aware that her pen is touching the parchment and leaking ink in a spreading blot – and she's certainly not aware that I've spoken to her.

"River," I say again, a little louder.

Still no response. This time I reach out through the bond and touch her mentally. River. Are you ignoring me? I add, a little teasingly, though I suspect the concern underneath is tangible enough.

She jerks, twisting her head around and spotting me. I hold back a laugh – the tip of her muzzle is smeared with black ink.

"Markus. I didn't realise you were there," she says unnecessarily.

"I noticed," I reply pointedly. "It's late. Come on – Thessalanor has just chased me out, so let's go and get some rest. And something to eat, for you."

"I'm fine," she tells me, turning back to her work. "I feel like if I just keep going a little longer, I'll get it."

"Get what?"

"The way to eliminate the necessity for the substance to be acidic. I've been working on it for the last day and a half, and I feel like I'm almost there. There's just something I can't quite grasp."

I look at her with concern. She's pale, her scales a greyish green rather than their healthy vibrant colour, her eyes dull, her spikes flickering with tired emotion. It's taken me too long to see the strain she's put herself under, but now that I have, I won't ignore it.

"Maybe you need a break. Sometimes our best ideas come when we relax our minds and let them surface, rather than forcing them."

I'm aware of how hypocritical I'm being – and I recognise ruefully that Thessalanor probably directed me towards River partly so that I would see my own failings reflected in her, and perhaps do something about both. He's no fool, that Healer, and has had years of experience with people to understand how they work, humans or otherwise.

"Come on," I urge. "Thessalanor says you've barely slept in a day. And knowing you, I doubt you were sleeping much before that either. We miss you, River. Come spend a night with the pack, and get back to this in the morning when you're fresh."

I see her hesitate, sense how she longs to share a night with the pack the way we used to. Since we arrived she's been distant, consumed by work. Absent, though not intentionally.

But I also see her determination to see this through. That's something I love about her. Whenever people are suffering – samurans, humans, our packmates – she gives everything she has to help them. I wonder if that's why Tarra originally chose her as her assistant, back when she was still Unevolved.

But she also needs to learn to take care of herself. And, I acknowledge ruefully, so do I.

River seems to read my thoughts, or perhaps just knows me well enough to interpret the emotional overspill leaking through the bond. She cleans her pen, caps the inkwell, and rolls up both the parchment she was studying and the one she was writing on, tucking them into the drawer.

"Very well, Markus. I'll come," she tells me, with a quick flick upwards with her chin.

I smile at her, the expression small, but genuine.

"Everyone will be happy to see you," I tell her sincerely. "They’ve missed you. And so have I."
