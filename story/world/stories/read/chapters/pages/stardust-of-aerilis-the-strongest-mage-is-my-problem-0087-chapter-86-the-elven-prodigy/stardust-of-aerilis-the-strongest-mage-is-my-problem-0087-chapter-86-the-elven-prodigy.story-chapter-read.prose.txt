Dryadalis did not look like a kingdom.

It looked like a forest that had, at some ancient point, agreed to be lived in.

Great trees rose in slow spirals from the ground, their canopies interlocking overhead into a green ceiling that filtered the sun into a soft dappled gold. Rivers wound between their roots, catching the light, spilling in slow silver curtains down mossed cliffs into pools the colour of jade. Ferns grew waist-high along the paths. Vines had learned, over centuries, how to wrap themselves around the buildings without cracking them.

The buildings were few. Marble, pale and veined, and often small; a colonnade here, a domed pavilion there, a chamber set into the base of a great tree as if it had grown there rather than been built. The elves did not need much. What they needed, they made beautiful. What they did not need, they left to the trees.

It was its own world.

In one of the larger structures, a long chamber wound with ivy at its eaves, its columns rising to meet the branches above, three young elves walked briskly down a hall of pale wood and green light.

"Where is Meriel?"

The first one who spoke had long silver-blonde hair that fell nearly to her waist, tied at the crown with a thin green ribbon. Her voice was pitched low, the tone of someone who had decided to be worried about something and required the others to catch up.

"She should have returned a while ago. Think she has gone for the extra mile again?"

"Almost certainly."

The second walked at her shoulder. Her hair was cropped in a neat bob, dark at the roots and silvered at the tips, and her small pointed chin was set in the shape of someone who had said this exact thing before.

"Someone of her caliber should have been back long before now. The Elder truly must give her a harsher warning. She is capable, yes, that is not in dispute. But running off on her own every third assignment will get one of us in trouble one day. Or all of us."

The third, a shorter elf with a mass of pale curls pinned back with a wooden clasp, made a face.

"Imagine her wandering off far enough that more humans see her. Not the ones who know what she is. The ordinary ones. As weak as they are, they are despicable. She might be captured. She might be tortured. She might tell them where we are."

"They are not strong enough to attack us," the long-haired one said, dismissive. "That is not the concern. The concern is that they might see one of our spells and find a way to replicate it. With those little tools of theirs. Their mana-devices. They are primitive as of now, but who knows what they will crack in the next century."

"We are getting off topic," the bob-cut one said. "We should tell the Elder about this. Meriel cannot be permitted to roam so freely all the time."

They came to a set of tall doors at the end of the hall.

Two elven guards stood at either side, hands loose on the shafts of long thin spears. They were young, both of them, and they exchanged a small uncertain look as the three approached.

"The Elder is in council," the taller of the two said. "There is a report in progress."

"This is more important than the report."

The long-haired elf did not raise her voice. She simply used the tone the daughters of prominent houses were taught to use from a young age; the one that indicated that the person hearing it had already lost the negotiation and had not yet noticed.

The guard hesitated. He looked at his partner. His partner shrugged fractionally.

The doors opened. The chamber beyond was long and narrow. A white carpet ran the length of it, from the doors to the far end, where a modest throne of woven wood and living branches rose against the back wall. Sunlight filtered down through a small circular skylight far above and struck the throne in a soft diagonal shaft.

Elder Gorre sat on the throne. He was ancient, even for an elf. His hair was white and thin and fell past his shoulders. His hands rested on the head of a staff nearly as tall as he was, dark wood polished to a soft sheen, worked with faint green runes along its length.

His face did not move as the doors opened. Nothing on it moved. He simply, in the manner of very old people, redirected his attention without shifting his body.

"Elder Gorre," the long-haired one began, sweeping into a slight bow as she stepped forward, "we would like to—"

She stopped. The three of them stopped, together, in a small ragged line just inside the doors.

There was another elf in the chamber.

She stood on the white carpet halfway to the throne, one hip cocked slightly, as though she had been in the middle of delivering an unhurried report and was now, mildly annoyed, going to have to interrupt herself. She had long, flowing silver-white hair, threaded through with the faintest wash of soft pink and lavender at the ends, so subtle that it might have been a trick of the green light through the leaves overhead. She wore a fitted navy-and-ivory tunic gathered at the waist by a slim belt, a pleated knee-length skirt of the same navy, and calf-high boots of dark navy leather laced neatly to the top. There was a slim wand tucked at her hip that appeared to be part decoration and part warning.

She turned.

Her eyes were a bright, vivid emerald. Her mouth was small and pleasantly shaped, and the smile she offered the three new arrivals was gentle and confident and did not, in fact, contain any welcome at all.

"What are you girls doing here?"

Her voice was light, the syllables clipped with the barest edge of irritation.

The long-haired elf's shoulders squared.

"Meriel!" Her tone shifted into a bright synthetic warmth. "Since when did you return? We were just now on our way to see the Elder; we were just wondering if perhaps you had gone somewhere you shouldn't be. It is a dangerous world out there, you know how the other races view us elves —"

"I have been out there," Meriel said, examining a fingernail, "clearing anomalies you three are either too weak or too slow to reach in time. You are welcome, by the way."

"What—" The bob-cut one flushed. "We were worried for you! That is hardly the way to—"

"Please."

Meriel let the fingernail drop. Her expression did not change, but the friendliness went out of her voice in a single clean subtraction.

"Do not insult me, Alissa. The three of you are not worried for me. You are worried about me. There is a very clear difference. You have been running about the corridors these last two weeks whispering to anyone who will listen that I have been seen consorting with humans on my longer assignments." Her small emerald gaze passed over the three of them like a light held to a page. "A human man, was it? I forget which one you settled on. It was a different one last week."

The curly-haired elf opened her mouth.

"That is—"

"You do this," Meriel went on pleasantly, "because I am better than each of you, individually, at every single one of the arts we practice. And because it is easier to circulate the rumor that I am doing something distasteful in the outer world than it is to admit that I am simply the most capable member of our year, and that when the Elder needs a difficult anomaly cleared, I am the name he speaks. It is transparent, girls. It is transparent to everyone in this house except, apparently, yourselves."

She smiled at them.

It was a small polite smile. It was the same smile she had greeted them with. The only difference was that all three of them, now, appeared to be lightly struggling to remain upright.

"So," Meriel said, tilting her head, "since you have gone to such trouble to come and see the Elder, which of the rumors would you like to bring to his attention first? I recommend the human one, personally. It has the most narrative texture. But if you would like to run through the others—"

THOOM.

Elder Gorre had lifted his staff, once, and set it firmly back down against the pale wood of the dais.

The sound was not loud. It did not need to be. It filled the chamber the way an intake of breath fills a room when a very old person is about to speak.

He did not speak. His expression, which had not moved through any of it, still did not move.

The atmosphere in the room changed. All four elves turned to face the throne at once.

Meriel held for a moment. Her small emerald gaze rested on the Elder. The polite smile was still on her mouth, but it had thinned to a very fine line. Then she clicked her tongue.

She turned on her heel and walked out of the chamber. Fast. Her boots were nearly silent on the white carpet. She did not look back at the three others. She did not slow when she passed them. She did not slow when she reached the tall doors. She did not slow when she stepped through them into the green-lit corridor beyond.

The doors closed behind her.

The three other elves stood in a ragged line in the middle of the carpet, staring at the empty space she had left behind.

Elder Gorre did not speak.

He looked at them.

That was, at the moment, sufficient.

Meriel walked.

She walked through the long green corridor, through the leaf-shadowed atrium, down the wide mossed staircase that spilled into the outer glade, past two more guards who saw her expression and elected, wisely, not to salute. She walked out into the open air of Dryadalis and did not stop walking until she reached one of the smaller waterfalls at the edge of the western terrace, where the noise of the water made it easier to be alone.

She sat down on a mossed stone. She did not, at first, look upset. She looked bored. The wand at her hip caught the light as she folded her hands over her knees.

This, she reflected, had been her life for the past six weeks.

There were anomalies out in the wider world. That was the term the older mages used. She thought the term was underwhelming for what they actually were—deformed, warped creatures, appearing without warning across the whole of Aerilis, growing more frequent. Something was disturbing the deep weave of things. Something was pulling threads that had been laid down long ago, and where those threads pulled, the world puckered.

Aelira, the spirit of the deep forest, had begun bringing them reports. Locations. Sightings. And, more recently, the whereabouts of certain hooded figures who seemed to be present at a great many of the affected sites.

The elves had responded, as they always did, with slow ancient decorum: they had opened selected doors to selected mages and assigned selected duties. The most capable of the younger generation had been drawn into it. Meriel was, of course, among them.

She was, in fact, at the top of the list. Which was inconvenient for a number of the girls in her cohort. It was also convenient for her, because the anomalies took her outside.

Beyond the ferns and the marble and the endless green corridors and the endless slow committee sessions and the endless whispered rumors of the daughters of the great houses. Beyond the Kingdom that she had lived in for the entire span of her considerable early life. She could see, on assignment, the drift of the sky above ordinary human towns. She could taste, on assignment, the salt of a coast that was not their coast. She could stand at the edge of a broken forest and watch the last colours of an anomaly's death drift up into the wind and dissolve.

She could, on her way back from an assignment, take just a little longer to return.

The anomalies themselves rarely took her more than a few minutes. She had been unfairly gifted with mana precision at birth, and years of drilling had turned that gift into something close to inevitable. A distortion the Elder assigned three teams to had, on her rota, become a single-name errand: Meriel, if you please. The others were sent when she was not available. The Elder had not once, in six weeks, been forced to send them when she was.

So she had begun requesting more.

The Elder had lifted one white eyebrow, at that.

He had granted it.

She had, therefore, spent the past month-and-a-half seeing more of the world than any elven girl her age had seen in the last three hundred years combined, ostensibly in the service of protecting the kingdom, in practice because it was the only excuse she had ever been given to leave.

The world was plunging into chaos.

She was, for reasons she was not entirely comfortable examining, not particularly distraught about this.

Most of what she encountered out there was well within what she could handle. It was not arrogance, she felt, so much as accurate self-assessment: very few beings in the wider world could stand against her at full effort. And beyond the endless green corridors, there were sunsets over cities she had never seen. There were markets where humans shouted at each other over the price of fish. There were beastfolk children who chased each other along piers, whose ears twitched when they laughed. There were, occasionally, forests she had never smelled before, whose leaves fell in colours the kingdom had never produced.

Meriel wanted, badly, to see all of it before the world ended.

The mossed stone at the edge of the waterfall was as good a place as any to sit and let the noise of the water wash the chamber and the three others out of her head. Going back inside was not on the agenda. Not the throne room. Not any other building. Not for the next few hours.

She lifted her chin, closed her eyes, and let the cool spray settle on her cheeks.

Somewhere far away, on a continent she had never seen, a former clerk sat in an airship with his mind full of many things, but one thing stood out from the rest. He wanted to get stronger, and he would need a prodigy to teach him.

Meriel did not know it yet.
