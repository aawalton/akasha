Nineteen Thousand Years Ago

The Thunder King—no.

No.

The God of Thunder flew in the lightning-filled skies, high above the wreckage that had been Stormlord Nalthelas’s lightning laboratory. It had taken him a long, long time to reach the point where he could stand against someone like the Stormlord.

The Stormsteel Path had helped, of course. But in the end, a mere S-Ranked Paragon couldn’t hope to fight the founder of the Stormstrike Path—an SS-Plus-Ranked monster who, until his attempt had failed before it had even truly begun, had been on the precipice of another universe. The power gap between an S-Ranker and someone like Nalthelas was simply too wide. A full two ranks. It may as well have been the space between worlds.

As he’d grasped at whatever power he could, the God of Thunder had often thought he saw a woman. A white, ghostly old hag. She’d appear, then vanish. Appear, then vanish. At first, her presence had bothered him. Why couldn’t he tell how strong she was? But eventually, he’d gotten used to it.

And now, here he was. SS-Rank. His mentor-turned-mortal enemy dead, and all his works strewn across the forest below.

One lightning rod still reached for the sky.

That was all that remained of the Stormlord’s lightning lab.

And he was SS-Rank. He could take Stormlord Nalthelas’s place. His Path was superior to the Stormstrike Path. It’d be simple, and part of him knew that, unlike the elf mage, he was worthy. He was too big for this universe. He deserved more. No, not deserved. It didn’t matter what he thought he deserved, or what this universe was willing to give him. He was too big for it, and soon, he’d be too strong for it—and when that moment came, he’d force the door open as Nalthelas had failed to do, and he’d ascend.

He would ascend.

“Ah, there it is,” a voice so old it sounded more like a door said. “The moment it hits them is all the same.”

“What are you talking about?” the God of Thunder asked as he turned his massive yet sleek body to face the woman who dared intrude on his victory. “Why are you here?”

“Why, to see the rise of a new great power in our universe, and to offer you a warning. Do not seek ascension.”

“And you are? Why do you think you can tell me what to hunt for?”

The ancient, moonlight-colored woman raised a gnarled hand, and an aura slammed into his. As he tried to fight it, she smiled through cracked lips and missing teeth. “I’m the Crone, dragon. And you and I will get to know each other well over the next dozen or two millennia. I will be your friend, your enemy, your rival, and…your jailer.”

The portal world we arrived in felt…not sterile. Sterile implied hospitals, clean walls, and disinfectant. It was more…scorched by the sheer power around us.

Terrel and Sarah obviously felt it. Their faces were drawn and tight within moments of our arrival in a narrow, ceramic-walled room with open, glassless windows. Ellen and I noticed it, too. There was a great power here—something on par with the God of Thunder or the Placid King. Not with the Crone, but then again, nothing was.

“Be on your guard,” I said.

Terrel nodded, and the twinned axes came out. I walked to the nearest window and peered out. “Ellen, are you seeing this?”

She joined me, stared for a moment, then nodded. “Arboreal.”

That didn’t make any sense. This was a memory of the God of Thunder—or at least, Magda had told us it was one. And the energy coursing through the very air around us implied that something strong made its home here. But all I could see, covering the world far below us, was forest. Endless, unbroken forest. Here and there, an elven palace’s spire reached upward toward the sky, but none even came close to reaching the same heights we’d arrived at.

“Okay,” Ellen said. “We’re looking at an Arboreal world, which makes no sense. This is supposed to be more like the Ghostdream portal types, but it’s not behaving—“

Lightning hammered down all around our narrow room. The flash of red-orange light burned my eyes, and when I blinked, afterimages followed me. The thunder hit a moment later. It sounded like the massive, dry storms that ripped across the Sonoran Desert sometimes, but it went on and on for almost a full minute.

When it stopped, I readied the Storm’s Blade. “We need to keep moving. Who knows what we’re going to find here?”

“That eel probably does,” Ellen said. But she fell into place, near the back of the group where she could cover Sarah. A brief moment later, Terrel opened the door, and I stepped through it.

The signs of an Arboreal world were everywhere. The graceful arches. The curving walls. The narrow, railing-less bridge that led from our landing room to an enormous, ceramic-lined building with four needle-thin rods reaching for the heavens, one on each side. The building looked to be the size of the GC tower back in Phoenix, but made entirely from an off-white, vaguely shiny stone or clay. I tapped my foot on the ground; the armor clicked slightly, like I was standing on glass.

And now that we were outside, the stink of ozone was overwhelming.

I was on edge and ready to jump into a fight. This couldn’t be a Ghostdream portal. The Hall of Thunder, whatever it was, had to be real—and that meant this was a trap.

Then Sarah Cullman cleared her throat. “Kade, look out in the distance and tell me what you see.”

As I followed her pointing finger, the world slowly faded from the vivid greens and browns below and red-orange lightning around us until, maybe a mile away, it was almost sepia-toned and out of focus. It was like whatever sustained this portal world didn’t want to bother with anything that didn’t matter. And, in its own strange way, it reminded me of the Ghostdream.

“Sarah, thanks. Everyone, this is a Ghostdream, or near enough. I’m not expecting typical Arboreal enemies. Be ready for anything—but expect lightning, thunder, and wind. Let’s get moving.”

Then I stepped onto the narrow bridge between our entrance point and the gigantic structure, and started moving across.

The lightning storm overhead unleashed its power in pulses. The first one had hit while we were safely inside, but the next crashed down around us while we still had a quarter of the long, narrow bridge to go. And it didn’t come alone.

Stormlord’s Sentinel: S-Rank

Two of them dove down on us. Each looked familiar, but it took me a moment to place them. Winged serpents. But unlike Cheddar or Pepperoni, whose bodies were—at least mostly—flesh and blood, the Stormlord’s Sentinels seemed molded from black clouds and red-orange lightning.

Each was also twice the size of either of our summoned familiars.

The lightning surged around them as they dove. “Brace!” Terrel said. Then he turned, putting his entire body into the motion, and threw his axes. A band of Mana formed between them, and another connected to his still-moving hands; the two blades whipped through the air, spinning against each other.

When they hit the first Sentinel, they exploded with energy. Cloud and lightning crackled outward in every direction, forming a roughly circular ball of gray-black smoke around the monster. It screeched, but there wasn’t an ounce of pain in its voice.

The other Sentinel dodged the explosion and bored in on us.

Until it was ripped out of the air by Ellen’s Black Hole.

She slammed her spell into the winged serpent before I could even start setting up a Hand of the Thunder God; it jerked to a stop so sudden it hurt my neck. “Go!” she yelled.

We went. As lightning surged all around us and the first Sentinel pulled itself free of the two Mana-bound axes wrapped around its body, we broke into a sprint. S-Rank speed was amazing, and before the storm’s pulse could finish, we’d arrived at a massive, arched double-door. Trees were carved into the ceramic all the way up to twice my height, but the door kept on going after that, reaching for the sky—and for an engraved depiction of an elf in a robe, carrying a staff. Lightning poured from his weapon to rain down on the forest below.

I touched the thirty-foot-tall door, readying myself to push, but it swung on its hinges under the slightest weight. “Inside, now,” I said as the two Sentinels closed in. We ducked inside, and as we did, the door silently closed behind us. A portal metal bar slammed down onto it, easily twenty feet up. It wouldn’t be a problem to open—not for a bunch of S-Rankers—but the meaning was clear.

Wherever we were, and whatever we were doing, the portal world wasn’t behaving like a Ghostdream. It didn’t want us out or dead. It seemed like it wanted us inside.

And speaking of being inside…

The smell had changed. That was the first change from outside, where the acrid, sharp scent of ozone and lightning had filled the air. It was still here, but inside the massive, ceramic building, it was joined by the stink of death. Not just death, but slaughter.

The two Sentinels’ bodies crackled just outside of the massive clay door. They didn’t seem interested in entering the building we’d taken shelter in, though; just in waiting outside. I counted to twenty to be sure, then got my bearings.

A stairwell ran up and down, its railing-less steps sharp and angular in contrast with the typical Arboreal spirals and curves. It led to a network of clay paths between a framework of copper and portal metal; the entire structure seemed hollow except for the skeleton of metal. But, about halfway up the two-or three-hundred-foot-tall building was a door. Much like the one we’d come through, it—

My instincts screamed at me, and I parried the blow without even seeing it.

Nalthelas Cultist: A-Rank

Dozens. All A-Rank. Every shape and size, from orc to elf to hobgoblin and bugbear. Monsters from dozens of different portal worlds. Swords and maces cut at me, and I cut back. To my left, Terrel’s twin axes went to work, cleaving into the horde of red-orange-robed, masked monsters. Blood poured from wounds. The Storm’s Blade sang. Thunderlord stance. Laminar Blade. Slipstream. Twenty attacks as I moved through the horde with the zephyr at my back.

Ellen’s body stiffened. A spear had hammered into her side, leaving a…nothing. Nothing was left behind. No wound. Her skin went purple-black, and she started to cast as she shifted into Voidwalker. Abyssal Storm covered the room. It tore at the assembled monsters, opening the puncture wounds from Slipstream into wide holes that bled freely.

Our damage outpaced the A-Rank cultists’ healing, and it wasn’t close.

But there were so many of them.

We’d reached an impasse. Every vermillion-robed cultist we killed was simply replaced with another. After a minute, Ellen, Terrel, and I had filled the room with corpses. But that didn’t matter against an endless tide.

Then Sarah yelled something from the downward-leading staircase.

“What?” I shouted back.

“Get your asses over here! You’re gonna want to see this!” She sounded like she wasn’t even worried about the fight—and why would she be? None of the three of us had suffered more than a few scratches.

I nodded. “Terrel? Duck!”

Then I shifted to Cyclone Forms and fired off a quick Hand of the Thunder God combo. The beam sliced into the monster horde; Ellen followed it up with a Shadow Puppeteer that mirrored our enemies and turned the room into a mass of brawling, clawing combatants. And Terrel slammed through the few cultists who survived the Hand of the Thunder God.

We dashed through the gap and pulled up next to Sarah. Unlike us, she was hurt. Not badly, but badly enough that I could see bicep muscles. She shrugged off the wound, though. “Check this out.”

I followed her down the stairs and into a wide, expansive prison.

No, not a prison. A butcher’s abattoir.

So far, we’d been in the Hall of Thunder for maybe twenty minutes. Probably less. But every second mattered, and we hadn’t found a single possible clue that’d help us against the God of Thunder. This was supposed to be his memory, so where was he?

The abattoir we found ourselves in answered that question.

Ceramic walls, originally white, had been stained every shade of red, green, or blue imaginable—but all of it was definitely blood. The slaughterhouse floor was covered in more of the same. But while signs of violent death filled the room, only one monster appeared to be present.

The Thunder King: S-Rank

I stared at him, mouth dry. I’d seen that name before, of course. The Thunder King had been a low-ranking Paragon I’d killed during my attempt to push to C-Rank. He’d been a hobgoblin and a summoner—and, though we’d never figured out exactly how, he’d also been present at the trap portal I’d gotten the strange core from.

But this Thunder King reminded me less of a pathetic goblin and more of…no.

It wasn’t that he reminded me of the God of Thunder. He was the God of Thunder—but the God of Thunder as he was before. A column of lightning, fifteen feet tall, that bent over a table like an old, hunched man. His form was featureless, and unlike the swarm of cultists that Terrel was currently butchering on the stairs, he wore no clothes.

He seemed intent on the corpse of a monster. Whatever it had been, it had once been massive—a plate-backed, spike-tailed thing that looked like a stegosaurus if the dinosaurs were bright, neon green. Even in death, lightning cracked across the corpse’s plated spine.

But its chest had been removed. Its ribs were gone, and what was left behind had been cleared away. The stink of death only increased as I got closer, the Storm’s Blade ready. The Thunder King was the God of Thunder. If I struck a blow here, would it hurt him now?

Then he turned, and a tendril of lightning reached into the plated monster’s body. It came away with a crackling core the size of my fist, and for the first time, the Thunder King spoke. “Another core for you, Stormlord Nalthelas.”

And for the first time, I realized that we weren’t, in fact, alone with the Thunder King. Another person had joined us in the room without being noticed, but as he stood, his aura pushed out like a moving wall of steel.

Stormlord Nalthelas: SS-Plus-Rank
