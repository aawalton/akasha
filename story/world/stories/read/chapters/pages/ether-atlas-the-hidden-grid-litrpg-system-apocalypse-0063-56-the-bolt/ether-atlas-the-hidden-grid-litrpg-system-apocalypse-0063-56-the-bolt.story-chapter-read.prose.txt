Chapter 56

"The cage that works best is the one the occupant believes they chose. Remove the illusion of choice and the cage becomes visible. Visible cages require guards. Guards require resources. Resources create dependencies. Dependencies create vulnerabilities." - Codex of the Long Watch, Administrative Caution

Sarah arrived for the third session with bruises under her eyes and a tremor in her left hand.

She knelt beside Donna’s cot without speaking. The fighter at the tent flap was different today. Bigger and meaner. He stood closer than the previous escorts had, angled so he could see Sarah’s hands and Donna’s face simultaneously. Sarah kept her eyes on Donna’s shin and put her palms down and started working.

The violet glow was dimmer than yesterday. Sarah’s channelling was slower, the light tracing the fracture line with less certainty. She was running on expertise instead of Reserves.

“You treated them,” Donna said quietly. “The people from the market.”

Sarah’s hands didn’t stop. “I’m a doctor.”

“The Baron told you not to.”

Sarah said nothing, but Donna saw her jaw tighten.

“The man with the arm,” Donna said. “Did he make it?”

“The tourniquet your friend applied was competent. I cauterised the stump and managed the shock. He’ll live with one hand.” A pause. “The girl lost everything below the knee. I closed what was left and kept her out of sepsis. She’s fifteen.”

“And the Baron?”

Sarah’s eyes flicked to the new guard. “The Baron sent a new escort to show his disappointment.”

The guard heard it and didn’t react. Donna filed that.

“One more session,” Sarah said. “Tomorrow. Then you’re weight-bearing.”

She withdrew her hands, and the tremor was worse. She stood and walked to the tent flap. The new guard fell in behind her, closer than the previous escorts had been.

Donna watched them go, then turned to Arthur. “Shut the flap.”

Arthur closed the tent and Steve looked up from his guidebook.

“The doctor isn’t there by choice,” Donna said.

“We know that,” Arthur said.

“You know the outline. I’ve had three sessions with her. The first time, she looked at the guard for permission before she touched me. The second time, she couldn’t say what she wanted because he was standing close enough to hear her breathe. Today she arrived with a new guard because the Baron doesn’t want her treating the people he told her not to treat.” She looked at Arthur. “The leash is tightening.”

The tent was quiet for a long moment.

“I’m going into the compound tonight,” Donna said.

Arthur looked at her leg.

“It’ll hold for a walk. It won’t hold for a fight, but I don’t plan on fighting.”

“You’re going alone?”

“You’ll be a liability on this one, Arthur. Clandestine and shield-bearing tanks don’t mix.” She looked at him with a faint smile. “I need to see her room. I need to know how she’s held. And I need five minutes of conversation without a guard listening.”

“The compound has a perimeter,” Steve said. “Fighters on rotation. The Baron runs a tight operation.”

“The Baron runs a smuggler’s operation. Good at boats, good at supply chains, good at controlling a harbour. His compound security is converted fishing warehouse architecture with improvised reinforcement. I've broken into better with a credit card and a grudge.”

She ticked the access points off. “The eastern wall has a timber patch where the stonework failed. The drainage channel on the western side is wide enough for a person. And the shift change between the night watch and the early patrol has a fifteen-minute gap.”

“Fourteen,” Archie said. “The gap is fourteen minutes. The night watch has been consistently early by forty-five to ninety seconds for the past four rotations.”

Donna looked at him. “You’ve been monitoring their patrol schedule?”

“I monitor all observable patterns within my operational range. The Baron’s security rotation is not classified information. It is simply information nobody asked about until now.”

“Eleven minutes,” Donna said. “I’ll take fourteen.”

* * *

The compound was quiet at two in the morning.

Donna came from the western side, through the drainage channel Archie had confirmed was unmonitored. The channel ran beneath the perimeter wall through a limestone culvert that smelled of fish oil and seawater. She crawled through it on her elbows with her bad leg trailing, the pain a white line from shin to hip that she processed and filed and kept moving through.

The courtyard was dark. Two fires banked to embers and a fighter sleeping in a chair by the gate. The Baron’s quarters in the south building had its lights off.

Sarah’s room was on the second floor of the central building. Donna knew this because she’d counted windows during the party’s first visit, matched them against the building’s possible layout, and asked Azzopardi’s soldiers which windows stayed lit latest. The answer was always the same one.

She climbed the external staircase with her weight on her arms and her good leg. The corridor was quiet, and Donna's pre-apocalypse skillset made her uniquely suitable for this visit.

Sarah’s door was the third on the left with a bolt on the outside.

Donna stood in front of it for a moment. A simple iron bolt, the kind you’d find on a garden shed. It was functional, not decorative. The screws were fresh in the wood. Someone had installed this after the building became a compound. Someone had looked at this door and decided it needed to keep a person in.

She slid the bolt and opened the door.

Sarah was awake. Sitting on the edge of a narrow cot, dressed, hands in her lap. She’d heard the footsteps. Her body was coiled in the posture of someone who expected the door to open and never expected anything good to follow.

“Donna?” Her eyes grew wide in surprise.

“How did you get in?” she asked. Her voice was barely above a whisper.

“That’s not important.” Donna stepped inside and pushed the door nearly closed behind her. “What’s important is why your door is bolted from the outside.”

Sarah looked at the door. At the narrow gap between door and frame where the bolt-housing was visible from inside. She’d been looking at that housing for weeks. Knowing what it meant. Unable to reach it.

“He told me it was for my protection,” Sarah said. “The first week, I believed him.”

“Tell me.”

Sarah told her. The invitation that became a cage. The healing sessions that became shifts. The escape attempt, the goat track, Renzo sitting on a rock eating an orange. The disappointment that was worse than anger because it treated her compliance as something the Baron was entitled to.

Donna listened without interrupting, leaning against the wall with her arms crossed, letting Sarah talk until she ran out.

“As I see it,” Donna said, “you stay here and keep healing until the Baron decides you’ve earned enough trust to move freely. Which will be never, because a free healer is a healer who can leave.”

Sarah’s jaw tightened. “I’m aware of the situation.”

“Then you know the other option is someone opens that door from the outside. We can do that. But we can’t stay on Malta. We have somewhere to be, and once we’re gone, you’re back in this room.”

“So it doesn’t matter either way.”

“Yes, it does.” Donna paused. Sarah looked up. “It matters if you want to come with us.”

The room was quiet for a long beat. Somewhere below, there was a creak as the sleeping fighter shifted in his chair.

“You don’t know me,” Sarah said.

“I know you defied the Baron to treat civilians who were bleeding out. And I know that three weeks in this room haven’t beaten the doctor out of you, which tells me everything I need to know about who you are.”

Sarah looked at her hands. The tremor had stopped during the conversation but it came back now, a fine vibration in her fingers that she watched as though they belonged to someone else.

“I have patients here, Donna. People who depend on me.”

“What the Baron is holding you for can be taught. He is just ignorant of that. Steve and I can find people at the hospital camp with an inclination toward healing and show them the basics.” She scanned the corridor through the crack and continued, “Give it a week and the Baron has three healers instead of one. They might not be as good as you, but what you do for him can be replicated. What you are is yours, and he doesn’t get to keep it.”

Sarah was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Donna could hear the patrol’s boots on the track below the compound. The fourteen-minute window was shrinking.

“If I say yes,” Sarah said. “How?”

“I’ll work that out. You just decide.” Donna moved toward the door. “We’ll be at the compound tomorrow. The Baron invited us for dinner. If you’ve decided, find a way to tell me. A word. A signal. Something the guard won’t read.”

“And if I say no?”

Donna stopped at the door. She looked back at Sarah on the cot in the room with the bolt on the wrong side.

“Then I’ll slide this bolt closed, and you’ll hear me walk away. And tomorrow I’ll sit across from the Baron and eat his food and say nothing.” She held Sarah’s eyes. “But I don’t think you’ll say no.”

She left. The bolt slid home behind her. The iron was cold, and the sound was final.

* * *

She made it back through the drainage channel with two minutes to spare before the early patrol. Arthur was sitting against the watchtower wall, scanning the path, the hammer across his knees.

“Well?” he said.

“Her door bolts from the outside, Arthur. A garden shed bolt on a doctor’s bedroom door.”

Arthur was quiet for a moment. Then he set the hammer down.

“Tell me everything.”

Steve appeared from the watchtower shadow, guidebook closed, fully awake. He’d been waiting too.

Donna told them. The bolt. The escape attempt and Renzo on the goat track. The disappointment that functioned as punishment. The cage built from reasonable offers that closed one at a time until the door locked.

“She wants out,” Donna said. “She didn’t say it. She didn’t need to.”

“Can she fight?” Arthur asked.

“She’s a healer, Arthur.”

“That’s not what I asked. If she comes with us, she’s walking into what we walk into. What level is she?”

“I didn’t ask. Six, maybe seven. She’s been healing under the Baron for three weeks. The Baron apparently took her on hunting trips for passive Ether absorption. The system’s been counting that.”

Arthur rubbed his jaw. “We’re Level 11. The things ahead of us are scaled for Level 11 and climbing. A Level 6 healer with no combat experience—”

“We grind her up,” Steve said. “The Sanctum Zone here is dense. If she’s with us while we fight, her healing generates Ether. Active healing in combat, the system would count it. I’m sure of it. She will level fast.”

Arthur nodded slowly, doing the structural calculation. Adding a fourth member changed the load distribution. A healer meant sustained operations, fewer forced retreats, faster recovery between fights. The liability at Level 6 became an asset by Level 9 or 10. The question was surviving the gap.

“So we’re doing this,” he said. “How. Ideas?”

“As I see it, we have two options, both not so great,” Donna said.

“Option one, we pull her out of the compound tonight. But then what? We still have business on this island. We can’t transit until the nodes are done, and until then we’re on the Baron’s territory with his forty fighters looking for the people who stole his healer.”

“Option two,” she continued. “We offer the Baron something worth more than a healer. Except I’m not keen on handing over high-grade cores, and we already made that deal. Adding Sarah to the price changes the negotiation. He’ll see it as us raising the stakes after he extended hospitality.”

“And they have Sarah’s patients as leverage,” Arthur said. “The moment she’s missing, the Baron stops sending even bandages to treat the civilians. She’d come back herself before she let that happen.”

The options sat between them in the dark. Charm wouldn’t work. Negotiation was already committed. Stealth would work now but wouldn’t survive daylight.

Arthur looked for a long time at the hammer on his knees. Two approaches, two failures. In D&D terms, he knew what he would ask his players to roll.

“What’s left when you fail a charisma check and a stealth check?”

Steve looked at him.

“Intimidation check,” Arthur said.

“We walk into the Baron’s compound tomorrow,” Steve said slowly, “sit down at his table, eat his food, and then tell him we’re taking his healer.”

“We walk into the Baron’s compound tomorrow, sit down at his table, eat his food, and make him understand that the cost of saying no is higher than the cost of saying yes.” Arthur corrected.

“The historical success rate of intimidation-based negotiations with resource-controlling entities is approximately thirty-one percent,” Archie said from above. “The success rate improves significantly when the intimidating party can credibly demonstrate the capacity to make good on the implied threat.”

“Can we?” Steve asked.

Arthur picked up the hammer and then motioned at Steve’s focus. “We can demonstrate.”

“Then we need to give him something to say yes to,” Steve said. “Intimidation without an exit ramp is a siege. He needs to walk away feeling like he made a deal, not like he lost a fight.”

“I already told Sarah,” Donna said. “What the Baron is holding her for can be taught. Anyone with the right inclination and a few levels can learn basic healing. Steve and Sarah train a handful of people from the hospital camp or the civilians. The Baron loses one healer and gains three or four. The maths works for him.”

“And he lets us walk out with Sarah because the replacement deal is better than a fight with a Level 11 party,” Arthur said.

“That’s the shape of it.”

Steve tapped his pen against the guidebook. “Level 6 in a party scaled for Level 11. The survival curve on that is steep.”

“She doesn’t need to take a hit. She needs to stay behind the line and heal what gets through. That’s the role. We’ve been operating without one.” Arthur looked at the Ironhold. “I hold the front. Donna flanks. Steve casts. Sarah keeps us in the fight longer than we’d last without her.”

“And if we run into something that needs all of us and she’s not ready?”

“Then we keep her alive until she is. That’s what the shield is for.”

“Torek’s support team on Yueshen included a combat medic,” Archie said. The geometry had drifted to the tent’s entrance, angled toward the compound below the ridge. “She stabilised casualties during engagements and maintained operational endurance across extended campaigns. Torek’s party lasted longer than any other in my records.”

“What happened to her?” Steve asked.

“She was the last to die.” A pause. “I mention this as operational context. A healer changes the mathematics of attrition. The mathematics of attrition is the only mathematics that matters.”

Steve was quiet for a moment. He nodded once and smiled. “Tank, DPS, ranged caster, healer. If Sarah comes with us, the party is balanced. Properly balanced. For the first time.”

“I was wondering when you’d get to that,” Donna said with a smile.

Arthur looked at the compound below the ridge sitting in darkness against a darker sea.

“We tell the Baron we have business at the temples. Once we’re done, Steve and Sarah train anyone who’s capable. He gets his healers. We get Sarah. Everybody walks away.”

“And if he says no?” Steve asked.

“Then we find out what Level 11 looks like from his side of the table.”
