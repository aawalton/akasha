As we made our way through the city, I couldn’t help but look around. Parts of it felt similar enough for me to think that I had been here before, though I couldn’t say with absolute certainty. The people were definitely on edge. Looking at me, they were whispering how I had gotten here, whether I was sent by one noble faction or another.

The worm infestation had been an unexpected event, with many speculating it to have been deliberately caused. Given that I knew very little about the politics of this trial-world, I wasn’t in a position to say. I had no intention of doing so, either.

“How many temples do you have?” I asked casually as we walked.

“Temples?” The herald looked at me, even more confused. “Why would you want to go there?”

“To give my thanks to the deities that I arrived safely,” I replied. Nepotism at its best. The boy really was rather dumb. “I take it you sell sacrifices?”

The moment I asked the question, the herald and both guards slowed down. That had definitely touched a nerve.

Where the hell did you send me, Harlequin? This was way more complicated than a simple hunting trial. My guess was that he wanted to weed out a few more participants to prove the value of his group.

“You practice sacrificial prayers, right?” I clarified, giving them an excuse to think I remained in the dark.

“Oh, of course, of course.” The boy smiled, sweat covering his fat face. “It’s… unusual for travelers to do that.”

“Being a free lance, I have a lot to be thankful for.”

The city followed the standard, usual ring and quarter principle. Since it was bigger, the rings were four, divided into eighths instead of quarters. It was notable that forts could be seen among several sections. Thick pillars of smoke rose from the opposite side of the city.

“Are you preparing for war?” I asked.

“Why do you ask?” one of the guards interjected, preventing the herald from making another mess.

“All your blacksmiths are working rather intensely.” I nodded in the direction of the smoke pillars.

“Standard rearmament,” the guard replied. “Armor doesn’t last long here.”

“This far from the fields?” Now it was my turn to be surprised.

“The fields aren’t the only danger.”

The building I was taken to was a stone estate in the second ring of the city. If I had to guess, this was a sector reserved for minor nobles and other people of importance. It was evident that the noble wasn’t the big cheese. More likely, he was in charge of the city guard. That was good—it meant I wasn’t considered dangerous or valuable enough to be sent to the real snobs.

Guards filled the place, ranging from the common sword-wielders to such with more color in their uniforms. Without another word, I was taken to the second floor.

The closer we got to the door in the middle of the corridor, the more tense the herald got. Reaching the spot in question, he paused, brushed his hair back with his fingers, then knocked. A few seconds later, he opened the door and almost jumped to the side, as if fearing an arrow to come from within.

“Please,” he said, indicating that I was the only one to enter.

“Hold this for a while.” I handed my wolf skins to one of the guards, and then I did.

As I stepped into the large stone chamber, the door closed behind me. The place was rather nice, combining frontier stoicism with comfortable luxury. The walls were mostly bare, occasionally adorned with the heads of monsters, some of which I had seen in the past. Several warm carpets covered the floor, making the finely crafted furniture seem at home.

The lord in question was a thin man dressed in a combination of black and silver clothes. Initially, I had expected him to be sitting behind some desk of sorts. Instead, he was at the window, looking outside. Of course, that wasn’t the only thing I saw in him.

More than you know. “Five.”

I’d bet. I had heard rumors of what happened to participants who disappointed their patrons. There had been whispers that a few unlucky ones had been scattered in trial-worlds, left to fend for themselves until their deity took mercy on them and reclaimed them back again. Even at the time, I knew that to be a lie. A deity willing to condemn its follower to such a fate wouldn’t ever show mercy.

“So, what’s the trial?” he asked. “Is the city done?”

“I’m here to hunt,” I said.

There was no need to mention the trial of my previous run. From what I remembered, three quarters of the local inhabitants had ended up dead, including a lot of the nobles. It was impossible to guarantee that he was among them, but as the leader of the city guards, and with only five cards, it wasn’t a stretch to assume so.

“First to get a good creature gets sent back,” I added. “Plus, there’s the deity requests.”

“The deity requests,” he sighed.

“What’s going on?” The thought of helping out crossed my mind. It wasn’t my task, but getting in a few good words with the locals, and a former participant, could pay dividends later on.

“Sabotage and intrigue. Nothing that’s related to your trial. Although…”

I could see the wheels turning in his mind. Regardless of how long he had been trapped in here, he remained a participant, and as such, there were some things he couldn’t ignore.

“I’ll hire you as an outside mercenary,” he said in a firm tone. “Most of the locals are scared or incompetent. Those that aren’t are too valuable to be sent to their deaths. You, on the other hand… How many cards do you have?”

“Enough.” I definitely wasn’t going to admit having a Grand Arcana. People have killed for less, not to mention he might get the bright idea to sacrifice the card to get into the good graces of his patron deity.

“The creature that did this is an asperpine variant. No idea if it came by accident or someone put it here. Let’s just say that there are a few people that don’t want this place to exist and ten times more that think they should be the ones in charge of it.”

I nodded. The creature was more than familiar. The best way to describe it was as a cross between a pangolin, a porcupine, and a puffer fish. Spikes covered parts of its body, each of which could be sent flying like a crossbow bolt.

“The spikes are parasites. They don’t kill entirely, just take control of the person and multiply on the inside. Then, one bright day, the victim bursts and sends the nasty things flying in all directions, so they could infect others.”

Of course, it would be something like that. Then again, it made sense that Harlequin would send us on such a trial. A creature of that nature was bound to earn high marks. Although it seemed a bit too convenient that the information was so easy to come by.

Both of us knew that was an obvious lie, yet I had no idea what he was hiding behind it.

I bet you do.

Little more was said. I convinced him to give me some coins in exchange for the wolf skins, then set off to find the nearest temple.

Unlike the city in the previous trial, the ones here were maintained quite well. Dozens of people were present at the one I went to, all of them praying to the various deities to protect them from the worms.

Looking at the statues, there were only a few I recognized. Still, that didn’t stop me from checking what tasks each of them offered. The majority gave me pact offers, as one might expect, but there were a few that were open to other requests.

One asked that I bring a core shard from the fields and was willing to grant me a special in return. There was no indication what the special might be, but by the rules of the Grand Arcana, it had to be to my benefit.

Another was willing to grant me a booster sphere for every rhinoid fresh heart I brought to him. Once I found where such creatures resided, that could turn out to be a rather good deal.

Finally, a deity calling itself Breath of Moon required the antlers of a moonelk. The reward it was willing to offer in return was ‘information.’

Information, is it? I thought.

Apparently, the scavengers had started to make an appearance. There was no doubt that Breath was a minor deity, needing the antlers to increase its status among the divine ranks. Knowing it didn’t have anything worthwhile to offer, it had offered something that would only be useful in the trial and nowhere else.

“I accept your request,” I said.

Information came first. Everything else could wait until later.
