Gustav Merren drank water as if he wanted to wash down his own rage with it, but nothing came of it. Five soldiers in uniform stood before him. They all wore the same stupid faces and the same tense shoulders. All of them were silent.

“So,” Gustav said, setting the glass down on the table with a bang. “Repeat it again. So I can finally understand how exactly I got so lucky as to be saddled with a bunch of useless pieces of shit.”

No one answered. Gustav felt his left cheek start to twitch.

“I’m waiting.”

One of the soldiers, the youngest-looking of the five, swallowed and stepped half a boot forward.

“We searched the southern part of the forest, Lord Merren. And the old road. And two ravines to the east. But... we didn’t find the fugitives.”

Gustav stared at him for several seconds without blinking.

“Didn’t find them,” he repeated quietly.

“No, my lord.”

Gustav slammed his palm against the desk so hard the glass jumped and water splashed over the rim.

“Of course you didn’t find them!” he barked. “Because you’re not searching, you’re just stomping through the mud in your boots! I don’t care how many ravines you licked clean or how many hours you stank up the forest. Where is the result?”

The soldiers straightened even more, though it seemed impossible they could stand any straighter.

“A girl escaped with magic that should never have awakened,” Gustav went on, and his voice had become worse than a shout. “An obsidian creature was taken from my arena. Rumors are spreading through the city. And you come back to me empty-handed!”

No one moved.

“Do you understand what happens if we don’t find them? Do you understand that not only your pathetic service is on the line, but my reputation, my city, and all of Veyr, gods damn you all?”

He grabbed the glass again and took another swallow.

“If you don’t bring them to me within a day, I’ll strip the service marks off your uniforms and leave you to starve in the street,” he said more evenly. “No pay. And then you’ll learn very quickly what the life of trash looks like on your own skin.”

One of the soldiers went pale.

“Out,” Gustav snapped. “Before I think of something worse.”

They left almost at a run, trying not to meet his eyes. Gustav had just reached for a new document when there came another cautious knock at the door. He closed his eyes.

“What now?”

The door opened a crack, and his assistant slipped inside. A small, gaunt woman with a permanently frightened face.

“Lord Merren...” she began, already sounding guilty in advance. “The merchant has come for his reward.”

Gustav slowly raised his gaze to her.

“What merchant?”

“The one. The one who gave the tip about the girl from the ruins.”

Gustav’s face twisted.

“That persistent little man dares to bother me again?”

His assistant clearly wished she could vanish, but she had never been gifted with such a useful talent.

“He said it was urgent.”

“For him, maybe,” Gustav said through his teeth. Then he flicked his hand. “Bring him in.”

She disappeared almost at once. A minute later, the merchant himself entered the office. He was short, with a square face full of servility. Hunched, wearing a strained smile that could make anyone uneasy. He entered respectfully, bowing his head in greeting. Gustav watched him with a frown and did not offer him a seat.

“Speak.”

The merchant folded his hands in front of him.

“Lord Merren, my tip about the girl from the ruins turned out to be very... important.”

“I noticed.”

“And because of that, I believe it would only be fair to triple the reward you promised me.”

The office fell so quiet that he could hear water dripping from the edge of the desk. Gustav said nothing, and the merchant stayed silent too, though he did not retreat. He only hunched a little more and kept that careful smile on his face, the kind that made even Gustav want to look away. Several long seconds passed before Gustav laughed, and the sound filled the office without relaxing the merchant at all. On the contrary, he seemed to shrink in on himself even more.

“Amazing,” Gustav said, still smiling, though there was not the slightest warmth in it now. “The moment a real threat appears in the world, there is always someone who decides to profit from it first.”

The merchant did not back down.

“There is always a threat in this world, Lord Merren,” he said softly. “And money is needed to survive under that threat. You understand me.”

Gustav wanted to hit him for those words, but restrained himself. Mostly because touching that slimy little worm with his bare hand felt like a humiliation of its own.

“Did you come to me for mercy or for a lesson in economics?”

“For profit. For both of us,” the merchant replied at once. “If word spreads through the city that any information about the fugitives will be rewarded generously, people will be much more eager to look for them. Fear alone is not enough. Fear makes people hide. Money, though... money makes them step out.”

He lowered his head a little, openly flattering now, but doing it with intelligence.

“Start with me, Lord Merren. And in return, as a sign of special gratitude, I’ll make sure news of your generosity spreads quickly and in the right places.”

Gustav stared at him. The slimy worm was speaking sense. He had almost opened his mouth to order the man thrown out, but stopped. People really would move faster if coin started to scent the air. And if fear had not yet settled into every house, gold would reach those houses faster.

With disgust, Gustav reached into the desk drawer, took out a pouch of coins, and threw it across the desk. The merchant caught it surprisingly well for someone with such a hunched frame.

“Get out,” Gustav said. “And spread the word. Make sure not one drunken pig in the market doubts the gold is real.”

“Lord Merren, your foresight...”

“Out!”

“Of course. Naturally. Your name will more than once—”

“Out, before I change my mind!”

The merchant clutched the pouch to his chest, bowed once more, and slipped out of the office almost backward. The smell of cheap incense and sweat trailed behind him.

When the door shut, Gustav was finally alone. He watched the water slowly drip from the edge of the desk to the floor. And the arena rose before his eyes again. That girl. She had looked so fragile, small, and ridiculous at first glance that no one would have taken her for a threat. But she had managed to subdue an obsidian creature. Somehow, that pathetic little thing had inherited the magic of Crowlis.

Gustav wanted to catch the girl no matter what it took. He wanted to know how that magic worked. In the best case, she would end up in front of him alive and in chains. In the worst case, he would have to dissect her dead body to dig his way to the truth.

Because no ordinary magic worked that way. No whips, no seals, no pain runes, no chains could turn obsidian creatures into obedient weapons. Those monsters were too vicious, too unstable, too alien to tame. That was exactly why they were so dangerous... and so valuable. For many years, people had tried to use those monsters as weapons, but the weapon almost always turned on the hand holding it.

Gustav understood all too well what tamed obsidian creatures could become in the hands of the right people.

That thought was interrupted by another knock. Gustav did not even look up.

“What now?”

His assistant cracked the door open, cautious as a mouse at an altar. She knew very well who she was dealing with.

“Lord Merren, it’s time for your scheduled meeting.”

“What meeting? Explain properly!”

“With the Sacred Council, my lord.”

Gustav snorted, slowly rose from his chair, and wiped his forehead with a handkerchief.

“Have someone wipe up the water in here while I talk to those gray crows.”

He left the office without looking back. In another part of the building, the council chamber was waiting for him. Large, with a high ceiling, heavy curtains, and a round blackwood table that always irritated Gustav with its ceremonious uselessness. Five old men in white robes were already seated around it. The Sacred Council. Their faces were different, but their expressions were always the same: the patient, cold confidence of men far too accustomed to power.

Gustav entered without bowing.

“Gentlemen.”

One of the elders, the tallest and thinnest of them, laced his fingers together.

“Gustav Merren. We speak to you in the king’s name.”

“That is usually a bad beginning,” Gustav replied.

“As are your recent actions.”

Another elder, bald and heavy-lidded, spoke more sharply.

“How did you allow a forbidden power to manifest in the arena under state supervision, and how did its bearer walk away alive?”

Gustav did not sit with any grace. He dropped heavily into a chair.

“I have already thrown every available force into finding the fugitives. My men are sweeping the area. The city is locked down. We will bring them back.”

“‘Bring them back’ is weak wording,” a third man observed. “The Council would have expected greater certainty.”

Gustav clenched his jaw, but kept his voice even.

“Then allow me to be clearer. The girl and the creature will be in my hands again. Soon.”

“Those words are not enough,” said the tall elder. “We need radical action.”

Gustav narrowed his eyes.

“Radical?”

“You understand what we mean.”

Gustav stared at them in silence for several seconds. Then he spoke slowly.

“You can’t mean you want to release him.”

Not one of the elders smiled, but the answer was there in their silence.

“That is far too risky,” Gustav said. “He may not be ready. And if he loses control...”

“If you fail to recover the fugitives quickly, the consequences will be far worse,” the bald elder cut in. “The magic of Crowlis must not remain uncontrolled.”

“We are not asking for your approval, Lord Merren,” added the first. “We are informing you of the king’s will.”

Gustav very badly wanted to tell them all to go to a deep, stinking abyss. But the Council was not the sort of body one dismissed without consequences.

He exhaled slowly.

“Fine,” he said at last. “I will carry out the order.”

The elders said nothing. For them, the matter was settled.

For Gustav, it had only just begun.

Some time later, he was already descending with a torch in hand. Two soldiers and a warden with a ring of keys at his belt followed behind him. The staircase seemed endless. Damp air drifted up from below, the walls breathed cold, and the steps became more and more inconvenient. Gustav grunted as he descended, feeling his shirt cling unpleasantly to his skin. Sweat ran down his temples, and he kept wiping his face with his handkerchief.

“They couldn’t build it any lower? What fools,” he threw irritably into the dark.

No one answered. At last, the stairs ended. They emerged into underground corridors with low vaults and heavy iron doors. Behind each of those doors was something no one in decent company preferred to mention. They walked to the very end of the corridor. At the last door, Gustav stopped.

“Open it.”

The soldiers stepped forward at once. One removed the locks. Another drew back the bolts. The third turned the keys. The door was secured as if it held not a man, but a natural disaster. When the final bolt finally slid free, they opened it. The room inside was dark. The torchlight slid over stone walls, chains, and the floor. Then it reached him.

A man.

Massive and muscular. He hung from chains, bound in several places at once. His skin was marked by scars, but not ordinary ones. Patterned scars. As if someone had spent years cutting signs, lines, and seals into his flesh, turning his body into a living map of pain.

Gustav did not approach closely. He stopped right at the threshold, holding the torch a little higher.

“I have a task for you, Faerd.”
