CHAPTER 5

"Day One. The sky is violet. My phone is dead. My D&D character sheet is in my back pocket and it might be the most useful document I own. I'm going to need a moment with that." - The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr, London

For a long time after the message disappeared, nobody spoke.

Arthur was still gripping the bin lid, his fingers had locked around the rim and were refusing instructions to let go. He recognised what this was, they called it Grip lock, involuntary muscle seizure that happens when the body dumps enough adrenaline to override fine motor control. He’d seen it in workers who’d narrowly avoided a fall. The body grabbed on to something safe and won’t release until the nervous system accepts that the danger has passed.

His nervous system was not yet convinced.

He forced his fingers open, one at a time, and set the lid down against the wall. The clang of metal on brick sounded obscenely loud in the quiet alley. He stared down at the dented lid. Fifteen years in structural engineering and the most important piece of impact protection he'd ever tested was a bin lid.

Donna crouched beside the creature’s body gripping the multitool like a talisman. Something to hold on to while reality rearranged itself around her.

Steve stood three metres back, holding the pipe at his side and staring at the corpse with an expression Arthur had never seen on his face before. Awe, maybe. The stunned, unblinking look of a man trying to fit the corpse into reality.

***

“So,” Steve said. His voice was careful. “Are we going to discuss what just appeared in our vision?”

Donna looked up from the body. “The blue text.”

“The blue text,” Steve confirmed, exhaling in relief. “Thank you. Good. At least I’m not experiencing a solitary psychotic break. At least we're calibrating to the same hallucination.”

Arthur took a step back and leaned against the alley wall. His injured ribs sent a spike of pain through his side that was, in its own grim way, reassuring. Pain meant the body was still reporting accurately and that the world still had at least some rules, even if those rules had just expanded to include floating text and eight-legged predators.

“It said ‘threat neutralised’ and then a number. Twelve of forty-two.” Arthur said, slowly.

Steve nodded. “Ether absorbed, plus twelve. Integration: twelve of forty-two.” He’d memorised it. Of course he had. “That’s a progress metric. A threshold. Twelve units of something absorbed out of forty-two required.”

"Progress to what?" Donna asked.

Steve studied the creature’s body, assembling the pieces into a conclusion Arthur already suspected. The format had snagged something in the back of his mind the moment the text appeared. He'd built a hundred encounter tables like this but he didn't want to say what this resembled.

"Steve. Just say it."

Steve took a breath. "Experience gain." He paused, as though giving his own brain one last chance to find an alternative answer. It didn't. "We just received experience points for killing it."

Donna turned from the corpse to stare at Steve. Arthur said nothing, he’d been hoping Steve would find a different answer.

"I am—listen, I’m fully aware," Steve said, raising both hands, "of how mad that sounds. I'm standing in an alley next to a dead monster, covered in its blood, telling you that we just received experience points for killing it. I understand that this is insane. I need you to know that I understand that."

“But—" Donna said.

“But the data fits,” Steve said, his voice moving into the rapid, information-dense cadence that Arthur recognised as Steve’s brain outrunning his social awareness. “Think about it. We received a notification after a combat encounter. The notification included a quantified reward, twelve units of a resource called Ether, and a progress bar showing how far we are towards a threshold of forty-two. That’s a levelling system. That’s RPG mechanics. Kill the monster, gain experience, fill the bar.”

“This is not a game, Steve,” Donna said.

“No, of course it's not. People are dead. Not NPCs, real people. In the pub, on this street, probably across the entire city. This is the farthest thing from a game I can imagine.” He agreed. “But whatever is happening to the world, whatever that calibration event was, it’s using a framework our brains recognise. It’s presenting itself as a system with rules. And if it has rules, it can be understood.”

Donna stood slowly. “And if it can be understood, it can be survived.”

Steve pointed at her. “Quite.”

Arthur looked at the gash on his side. It had slowed to a seep and the edges, he noticed with a chill, appeared to be closing. They were pulling together with a barely visible faint blue luminescence. His body was changing. All their bodies were, and a progress bar implied that when it reached forty-two, something else would happen.

“Why forty-two though?” Steve murmured, half to himself. “Why not fifty? Why not a hundred? Forty-two is a rather odd threshold unless the number itself is significant.”

“Steve, we can discuss the numerology of the apocalypse later.” Donna said.

Her voice had cracked, just a fraction. When you hold to absolute composure at all times, even a small catch in the throat is like screaming.

***

Donna pulled her phone from her pocket and held the power button for three seconds, then four, then five. The screen remained black. She turned it over, pressed the button again and held it against her thigh. When body heat failed to revive it, she smacked it against her thighs in frustration.

Arthur had known Donna for four years. He’d seen her face down aggressive clients, navigate hostile buildings in the dark, and deliver security assessments that made grown men reconsider their careers. He had never once seen her repeat a failed action out of desperation.

“Donna.” Arthur called, his voice calm.

She lowered the phone. Her jaw was set hard enough to show the muscle.

“Emma,” she said.

“Oh,” Steve said, excitement draining from his face.

"She's— she's working tonight," Donna faltered. Her voice was even but her eyes were doing calculations Arthur could guess. Distance, route, threat density, time. “The restaurant on Fenchurch. Three streets from here, if whatever happened in the pub happened everywhere—"

She didn’t finish the sentence. The arithmetic was simple and brutal, if one in four people in the pub had dropped, then one in four people in Emma’s restaurant could have dropped too. Emma was either standing or she wasn’t, and there was no way to know that from this alley. Their phones were dead, and the streets had creatures that tore people open with hooked limbs.

Having made her decision, Donna turned towards the mouth of the alley and Arthur stepped into her path. He hadn’t moved to block her. He was just there, the way a retaining wall is there, something solid for the load to push against without collapsing.

“Three streets. We go together,” he said and picked the bin lid back up.

Gratitude showed in her eyes for a fraction of a second, then she blinked and it was gone.

“Fine. Then move.” She said.

“Standing in monster alley was starting to feel like a poor strategic decision anyway.” Steve said, picking the pipe back up.

They stepped out of the alley and back onto Lime Street, and the scale of it hit Arthur again like a shockwave he’d been bracing against without knowing it. The pub had been contained. It was one room, three bodies, and a handful of survivors in various stages of shock, something his mind could frame as an incident, bounded by walls and a door. The street had no walls and that meant the entire tableau of a world gone wrong was visible all at once.

More cars had drifted to a stop since they’d gone into the alley. A delivery van had rolled into a row of parked scooters, scattering them across the pavement. The bus at the intersection sat at a dead angle, its rear doors open, passengers still stood around it with the dazed, blinking gait of people surfacing from a disaster they hadn’t expected and couldn’t understand.

People knelt beside the bodies on the wet pavement, performing the same useless rituals Arthur had performed in the pub, checking pulses, shaking shoulders, calling names into faces that would not answer.

A man stumbled past them clutching his head, repeating a question to no one.

Arthur thought of the sirens again. The absence was the worst part. Worse than the sky, worse than the dead phones, worse even than the creature in the alley since it meant the system that caught people when they fell had itself fallen. There was nothing between the survivors and the ground except whatever they could build with their own hands.

Three streets to Emma. Then they’d figure out the rest.

Donna moved through the street in that measured but hurried pace only security personnel can manage, her eyes sweeping ahead. Arthur fell in beside her. Steve followed, the pipe held low, turning his head back every few seconds to check behind them.

They moved through the broken city in a tight formation that none of them had agreed on and all of them understood instinctively. Arthur at the front. Donna flanking with her hand on the multitool and Steve three paces behind, watching the angles.

It was, Arthur realised, the same positions they'd adopted in the alley fight. The same positions they used in the game every Tuesday night.

Tank. Striker. Range.
