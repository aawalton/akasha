“You're Ji Ning, right? The one who took an alchemist from Alchemy Peak as his master?”

“Yes, that's me.” Ning nodded.

The young man's expression immediately soured. “Then why are you here?”

Ning glanced around the table before answering, “…Because I was invited?”

“No. I mean, you have no right to sit here with us,” the man said bluntly.

Ning gave him another look. He appeared younger than most of the people gathered here, though judging a cultivator's age by appearance was rarely reliable. Still, interrupting a perfectly pleasant gathering just to announce that someone didn't belong certainly gave Ning an impression of him.

The conversation around the table quickly died down, and Sui Feng was the first to speak.

“Brother Lao San, what are you talking about? Junior Brother Ning is a spiritual farmer. He already has his Spiritual Farmer Certificate, and I heard he even used spiritual farming techniques during the outer sect competition.”

His normally boisterous voice had become noticeably firmer. Ning was his guest, after all, and Sui Feng had personally brought him here. Lao San declaring that Ning had no right to attend was also questioning Sui Feng's decision to invite him.

At this point, it was a matter of face.

“I know about his certificate,” Lao San replied. “That's exactly why I'm saying this. Elder Luo Hong praised him personally, and everyone knows he has talent in spiritual farming. He could have properly followed our path, yet when the time came to choose a master, he apprenticed himself to an alchemist.”

Lao San looked directly at Ning. “He chose Alchemy Peak. Why should we still consider him one of us?”

Ah. So that was the problem.

Ning had expected there to be some actual grievance behind the sudden hostility, but apparently Lao San was upset because he'd chosen an alchemist as his master.

“I see.” Ning considered his words for a moment. “So, because my master is an alchemist, you think I'm not a spiritual farmer anymore?”

“That's right,” Lao San replied with a nod.

“But I still farm. You can be two things at once, you know.”

Lao San paused, seemingly unsure how to respond to that.

“I have my own spirit fields, I grow spiritual crops, I know farming techniques, and I passed the same Spiritual Farmer certification as everyone else.” Ning gestured toward the jade slip he'd collected earlier. “I literally came here today to learn another farming technique from the Spiritual Farming Hall, so what exactly am I missing?”

Lao San opened his mouth, but Ning continued before he could answer.

“And what lineage are you talking about? Spiritual farming is one of the sect's professions. My master didn't teach me any of it. I learned by working the fields, reading the same books, and taking the same assessments as everyone else here.”

Ning glanced around the table before continuing, “Besides, since when did choosing a master mean you had to abandon everything else you've learned? Does becoming an alchemist somehow make me forget how to grow spiritual crops?”

He looked back at Lao San.

“If that's how it works, then I should probably check whether my fields have disappeared too.”

That earned a few chuckles from the surrounding disciples.

Lao San frowned. “That isn't the point. You had the opportunity to properly pursue spiritual farming, and you chose alchemy instead.”

“I chose both.”

Lao San fell silent for a moment.

Ning really didn't understand why this had to be so complicated. There was nothing stopping him from learning alchemy while continuing to farm, and the two professions worked quite well together anyway.

“Actually, wouldn't having an alchemist here be useful?” Ning asked, drawing several curious looks from around the table. “You grow spiritual herbs, and I'm learning how to turn those herbs into pills. That seems like a pretty convenient arrangement to me.”

Someone farther down the table stroked his chin thoughtfully.

“And if any of you need pills in the future, I can probably help once I'm good enough,” Ning added. “At least I won't charge my neighbors an outrageous price.”

Now that got their attention.

“Junior Brother Ning makes a good point.”

“Especially if we can provide the ingredients ourselves.”

Ning took another sip of plum wine, satisfied with how the conversation was developing.

Cultivators needed pills, spiritual farmers grew the ingredients, and Ning intended to become an alchemist. Everybody benefited, and considering he hadn't even started selling pills yet, a little early advertising certainly wouldn't hurt.

Lao San looked around as the atmosphere shifted against him. “You people…”

“Brother Lao San, let it go,” someone said.

Sui Feng nodded in agreement. “Exactly. He farms, he has the certificate, and he was invited by me. What more do you want?”

Lao San's face darkened as he turned back toward Ning.

“You…” He pointed at him. “Fine! If you claim to be a spiritual farmer, then do you dare compete with me? If I win, you leave. Do you have the guts?”

“No.”

The answer came so quickly that Lao San froze.

Ning looked at him as though the problem should have been obvious. “Brother, you're at the late stage of Qi Condensation, and you've probably been practicing spiritual farming much longer than I have. Why would I agree to that?”

“You're afraid?”

“Of course I am. You're stronger than me.”

Ning took another sip of wine before continuing, “If I challenged a new outer sect disciple to a farming competition tomorrow and demanded that he leave if he lost, wouldn't everyone say I was bullying him?”

“Exactly!” Sui Feng slapped the table. “Lao San, don't be shameless. If you want to compete with someone, find someone at your own level.”

Lao San's face reddened. “Y-you…”

Ning had no intention of accepting a challenge just because someone questioned his courage. Lao San was stronger, more experienced, and wanted Ning to leave if he lost.

And what did Ning get if he won?

Nothing.

It was a terrible deal.

“Enough.”

Old Fang finally spoke from his seat. He had remained quiet throughout the argument, but the moment he opened his mouth, the others settled down.

“This is a gathering between neighbors, not a place for settling grudges. Lao San, Ji Ning was invited here by Sui Feng, and he holds a Spiritual Farmer Certificate issued by the hall. That's enough.”

Lao San looked unwilling, but he didn't argue with Old Fang. After a moment, he turned away with a cold expression, and Ning simply returned to his food.

Unfortunately, the atmosphere never quite recovered after that. People continued talking for a while, but the conversations were less lively, and the gathering gradually came to an end as disciples began returning to their respective courtyards.

As Ning was preparing to leave, Sui Feng approached him.

“Junior Brother, don't think too much about what happened today. Our gatherings aren't normally like this.” He hesitated before adding, “Lao San is just… rather passionate about these things.”

“Passionate?” Ning repeated. “Why does he care so much about me choosing an alchemist as my master?”

Sui Feng glanced around before lowering his voice.

“Lao San follows Elder Lin Yue, one of the foremost geniuses of our Spiritual Farming Hall. He's basically one of his followers.”

That explained part of it, though Ning still didn't see why Elder Lin Yue would care about him.

Sui Feng continued, “You were praised by Elder Luo Hong, and there were already people saying your talent in spiritual farming might eventually rival Elder Lin Yue's. Then you turned around and chose another profession. Lao San probably took that personally.”

Ning's expression became strange.

Why would he take my career choices personally?

Cultivators truly had too much free time.

“There may be another reason as well,” Sui Feng added. “Your master and Elder Lin Yue are both late-stage Foundation Building cultivators.”

“Oh?”

“They're both trying to reach the next stage, and the resources they need aren't exactly unlimited. The strongest core disciples compete over those things all the time, and naturally, the people following them get involved too.”

Sui Feng leaned a little closer before continuing in a quieter voice.

“Most of it stays beneath the surface, so don't go around repeating what I told you. Lao San might simply dislike your decision, or he might have been trying to cause trouble for your master through you. I can't say for certain.”

That made much more sense.

Ning nodded. “Thank you, Brother Sui Feng. I'll keep it to myself.”

It was useful information. Ning probably would have learned about the inner sect factions eventually, but finding out before accidentally stepping on someone's toes was certainly preferable.

He had only recently become an inner disciple, yet there were already factions, resource disputes, and people getting offended over which profession he practiced.

Ning sighed. “Troublesome.”

...

“So that’s what happened, Senior Sister.” Ning finished recounting the gathering to Ling Yinran. “Will there be any trouble for Master?”

Ling Yinran simply shook her head. “You don’t have to worry about such things, Junior Brother. You’re still only at the mid stage of Qi Condensation, so focus on your cultivation first.” She paused, then added with certainty, “That doesn’t mean you should just stand there and get bullied, of course. If anything happens, call me. I’ll settle it.”

Her eyes were cold as she spoke. Strictly speaking, the trouble Ning had encountered had originated because of her aunt. While it was a disciple’s duty to protect their master’s honor and assist in their progress, it was not yet time for her junior brother to step forward for such matters, not while she was still around.

“I see.” Ning nodded.

That would certainly be useful. What he dreaded most was encountering those brain-dead cultivators with powerful backgrounds who caused trouble for others simply because they could. His senior sister’s offer was, therefore, greatly appreciated.

“Thank you, Senior Sister.”

He then turned his attention toward the open field and the puppet standing at its center.

It was constructed from hundred-tempered iron with inscription lines running along both arms like veins beneath the surface. Its proportions roughly resembled a human’s, except for the joints, which were elongated and segmented.

It looked quite bizarre, but after so many years in this world, Ning’s tolerance for strange things had reached a very high level.

“Alright.” Ning looked toward Ling Yinran. “Shall we proceed to the next experiment?”

Ling Yinran sighed, though the curiosity beneath it was difficult to conceal. “I still don’t know where you get these ideas.”

That was fair. At first, she had simply been humoring her junior brother, but now she was genuinely intrigued.

Their first experiment had involved using a storage bag to retrieve an opponent’s weapon. The premise was simple: in the middle of a battle, could someone use a storage bag to snatch a weapon directly out of their opponent’s grip?

The answer, as it turned out, was mostly no.

Any weapon of meaningful quality was usually blood-contracted or spiritually bound to its owner, making it impossible for someone else’s storage bag to absorb it.

Clothing, however, had produced different results.

Spiritual garments below a certain tier did not require blood identification or formal binding, which meant that, in principle, one could use a storage bag to steal someone’s clothes in the middle of a battle.

The practical implications of this discovery had given Ling Yinran pause.

On one hand: useful in almost any situation. An attack practically guaranteed to cause the social death of an opponent.

On the other hand: stripping someone mid-fight would probably create a lifelong enemy out of sheer humiliation, which might be worse than simply losing the fight.

She had spent approximately ten minutes trying to decide whether her junior brother was a tactical genius or a menace. Eventually, she concluded that he was probably both, which was either reassuring or deeply concerning depending on how one looked at it.

This experiment, at least, appeared to have a more straightforward and legitimate application.

“Today’s experiment,” Ning said, turning toward the puppet, “is to determine whether a storage bag can absorb an incoming attack at the precise moment of impact, essentially using it as a single-use survival tool.”

Ling Yinran considered the idea. “It would certainly destroy the bag, but the question is whether the absorption would neutralize the attack entirely or only partially.” She tilted her head. “If it works even partially, a storage bag could become a one-time emergency defense against an otherwise fatal blow.”

“Exactly. At the Qi Condensation stage, where trump cards are both scarce and essential, even a single-use survival method has value.”

Ling Yinran nodded thoughtfully. For most people, sacrificing a storage bag, an expensive and practical tool, just to absorb a single attack would seem absurd. But the logic held. A trump card just needed to work once, at the right moment.

“Alright.” She extended her spiritual threads toward the puppet. “I’ll handle the timing and bag deployment through the puppet. You provide the attack.”

The threads connected, thin and luminous, disappearing into the puppet’s iron frame. Its head turned slightly, a small movement that was enough to confirm that Ling Yinran’s consciousness was now controlling it.

Ning regarded the puppet with interest.

A puppet master.

Of all the hundred professions, it was quite rare. The ceiling was extraordinarily high; a skilled puppet master at the middle to late stages could function as a one-person army, deploying multiple constructs simultaneously while remaining personally protected.

The entry requirements were equally demanding: precise control of spiritual qi, a strong and flexible consciousness, creativity in both construction and combat, and enough resources to make Ning’s cultivation expenses look modest by comparison.

What surprised him most was Ling Yinran’s choice. She had an aunt who was a master alchemist, so the path of alchemy had practically been laid out for her.

Instead, she had chosen puppetry.

Ning had noticed a pattern. Su Fan, despite carrying a talisman inheritance and coming from the prestigious Su Talisman Family, had turned to alchemy. Now Ling Yinran had done something similar.

It seemed to be a growing trend.

“Ready whenever you are,” Ling Yinran said, her voice monotone and heavy as it came through the puppet.

“Understood.”

Ning turned toward Fenghuang, who was perched nearby in his usual posture of relaxed superiority.

“Flame Spiral Arrow.”

Fenghuang tilted his head before opening his beak.

White flame flowed steadily from his mouth, gathering around the tip of Ning’s prepared arrow. Precise flame control was as natural to a White Flame Raven as breathing, and attaching it to an external object required little effort. The flame clung to the arrowhead and gradually formed a tight, spiraling coil.

The Flame Spiral Arrow was Ning’s own creation, a variation of the standard Spiral Arrow enhanced with Fenghuang’s white flame. The original technique relied on rapid rotation to increase penetration, while the addition of white flame allowed the heat to follow the same spiral and concentrate around the point of impact.

Together, a mid-stage archer and his contracted beast could produce an attack comparable to a strike from a late-stage Qi Condensation cultivator.

Ning drew the bow, adjusted his aim, and released.

The arrow crossed the field with a sharp hiss, white flame trailing behind it.

The puppet moved.

Ling Yinran’s timing was precise. At the last possible moment, the storage bag opened toward the incoming arrow, and the formation inside activated as soon as the attack reached it.

A dull crack rang across the field as the formation strained against something it had never been designed to contain, followed almost immediately by a sharp pop.

The storage bag collapsed inward.

A moment later, the arrow fell back out, its tip slightly charred.

The storage bag itself was gone, reduced to fragments of formation material and the faint smell of something burnt, but the attack had been successfully stopped.

Ning lowered his bow.

Ling Yinran withdrew her consciousness from the puppet and examined what remained.

“Successful absorption.” She crouched beside the fragments. “The formation overloaded instantly instead of gradually, which is why the bag was completely destroyed. But the attack was fully contained.” She looked up at Ning. “If a cultivator had been holding that bag and activated it at the right moment, they would have survived.”

“With restrictions,” Ning said.

“With restrictions,” she agreed. “The timing window is extremely narrow. You also lose the storage bag and everything inside it.” She paused. “And this likely only works up to a certain threshold. Against a Foundation Establishment attack, I doubt an ordinary storage bag would survive long enough to complete the absorption.”

“Within Qi Condensation, though, this is a legitimate trump card.” Ning was quite satisfied with the result.

“Yes.” Ling Yinran nodded, unconcerned by the loss of the storage bag.

For her, this level of expense was manageable.

Ning was already recording the results in his notebook. “I think that’s sufficient for today.”

“Yes.” Ling Yinran dusted off her hands and glanced at him. “I’ll admit, when you first brought these ideas to me, I expected them to go nowhere.”

“Most of them did go nowhere,” Ning pointed out.

“The clothing one went somewhere very strange.”

"...Still could be useful though," Ning mumbled.

Ling Yinran laughed. “Alright, I won’t argue with you. Anyway, I have somewhere to be. Goodbye, Junior Brother. Call me if you run into trouble.”

“Yes, thank you.” Ning smiled and nodded.

As a late-stage cultivator in the inner sect, Ling Yinran’s schedule was naturally quite busy, whether she was cultivating or honing her skills as a puppet master.

As for why she had agreed to spend her valuable time helping Ning with these experiments, he suspected it had something to do with the guilt she still felt over attacking him indiscriminately when they first met. Ning had brushed the incident off long ago, but he could tell she still felt somewhat ashamed about it.

So he used these opportunities to ease some of that awkwardness while also satisfying his own curiosity.

A win-win situation.

Ning paused.

“…Now that I think about it from another perspective, doesn’t this look like I’m using her guilt to achieve my goals?”

He considered the matter for a moment, then nodded to himself.

“It must just be my imagination.”

There was no way he would manipulate people to get what he wanted. That was the behavior of final bosses and the like.

He was, at most, a background character.

There was no way he would somehow end up becoming some hidden mastermind behind the entire world…

Right?
