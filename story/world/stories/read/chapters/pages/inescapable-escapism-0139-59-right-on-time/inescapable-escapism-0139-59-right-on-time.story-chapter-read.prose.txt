At first, I couldn’t quite wrap my head around the thing I was staring at. My thoughts seemed to stumble to a halt as I looked at the map on the wall next to the elevator, and my brain refused to process it. It couldn’t. The map made no sense.

It looked like a cruise ship. That was my first thought. I’d never been on one of those before, but Phoebe had. She went on one with her mom a couple of years ago. They offered to take me too, but my mom refused. She thought the offer was out of pity, that Phoebe’s mom was only suggesting it because she thought we couldn’t afford to go by ourselves, and that made her hate Phoebe and her mom even more.

That wasn’t why she’d offered to take me, though. I knew that. Regardless of what my mom said about her, she was a kind person, and surely, if she was offering because she thought we couldn’t afford it, that proved how nice she was? I tried to say that to my mom, but it wasn’t worth it. She seemed determined to hate her.

Phoebe sent me countless videos of the ship they were on, though. She walked the length of every single floor to show me everything, and it was incredible to see. Each floor seemed to stretch on for far too long. There were so many rooms, so many restaurants, shops, swimming pools, and even a roller rink.

It was awesome, and it seemed huge, much bigger than anything I’d ever been on before. She sent me a picture from the outside too, once they got off the ship. I remember being so shocked that a thing that big could even float. I had no clue how they did it, how they stopped it from sinking straight to the bottom of the ocean, but the thing we were on was somehow even bigger.

There were more rooms than the ship Phoebe had been on too. My eyes skimmed over the map, trying to soak it all in and make sense of it, but they seemed to get stuck on the upper layer. I glanced at my guardians, checking to make sure they hadn’t noticed my fascination with the map before looking back at it.

The ship didn’t have a deck. The one Phoebe had taken had a deck. There was a pool and some restaurants on it. She said that was where most people spent their days, and in the evenings, sometimes they screened films out there. It seemed like a crucial part of the experience, but it was missing from the deck. In its place, there was an observation platform.

My mind seemed to spin. Why would there not be a deck on a ship? Surely, that was needed, wasn’t it? Unless the ship was entirely enclosed on top, but I’d never seen any that looked like that. I’d not spent much time around the shores, though. Perhaps boats that looked like that existed, and I’d just never seen one before.

Or maybe, in the new world I’d just entered, it was necessary. I knew nothing of the place, nothing of what had happened there. I couldn’t access any of those memories yet, and perhaps it wasn’t safe for us to go out onto the deck. We had to be shielded from the world outside at all times.

That would make sense, I realised as the ding came from the speaker above the elevator, and the doors opened. Immediately, I moved to the side. It was instinctual, something I’d always been trained to do, and the guardians did the same.

“Thank you,” the man said as he stepped out of the lift, the smile audible in his voice. “Ah, on the way to your annual medical appointment, Daffodil?”

My voice abandoned me. I had to swallow multiple times before I gained the ability to speak again.

“Yes, sir.”

It was more of a squeak than anything else, but the man’s smile widened slightly, and the skin around his eyes creased. He looked kind. Something about the way he was looking at me made it seem like he actually cared about what I had to say, and that made my stomach tighten in a combination of excitement and fear.

I wished I wasn’t so scared. I was too nervous, too terrified, and that made it impossible to say anything of note. I had nothing to tell the man, and I wasn’t sure why I cared so much about that, but I did.

“Excellent. It’s a big one this year, isn’t it?”

“Yes, sir,” was all I could say.

“It’s her sixteenth,” one of the guardians added for me.

The man before us nodded wisely.

“Of course,” he said. “Well, best of luck with it, Daffodil. I’m sure you’ll be very happy with your results.”

His reassurance was somehow both reassuring and even more anxiety-provoking.

“Thank you,” I told him before adding, “I hope you have a good day.”

The second the words left my mouth, I wanted to cringe. It was such an awkward thing to say, and he’d already started to move past us. However, at that, he paused and looked back at me. His eyes found my face, and he was silent for a moment. I genuinely had no clue how he was going to react, but to my relief, he smiled again.

“You too, Daffodil,” he said, nodding to my guardians before turning away once more.

My mind swam as we entered the lift. I felt as though I’d just done something terrifying, as though I’d jumped out of a plane for the first time or fired a gun or something. My heart was beating far too fast, and my palms were slick.

“It’s so wonderful that the governor remembers you, isn’t it, Daf?” one of my guardians asked. “He even remembered your birthday!”

“He truly is a good man, isn’t he?” the other added, their tone fond.

“Mmm,” I muttered.

Why did he remember me? There weren’t that many kids on board. I couldn’t recall how many there were exactly, but the number was small. There weren’t too many names to keep track of, but the fact that he remembered my birthday too did feel important, I thought as the elevator rose steadily. Maybe there was a reason for that. Perhaps…

A thought seemed to flit through my mind, the movements fleeting. It seemed foolish to consider it, but I knew that every other kid on board the ship dreamt about it sometimes. They all wondered whether the governor could be their bio, and I was no different. I’d also spent countless nights trying to convince myself that he could be my father.

There was no way of knowing, though. Even if he was one of my biological parents, I would never find out. The decision was made a little while ago to keep that from the new generations. Only the doctors and the medical team had access to that information, and that was because it was necessary when it came to producing offspring. They had to make sure that the risk of genetic abnormalities was as low as possible, which was a concern in a small population like ours.

But maybe the guardians knew too, a small voice in the back of my head whispered. They spent all their time looking after us, so maybe it was necessary for them to be informed too. For safety reasons. Perhaps they knew, and that was why they commented on the governor remembering me. They could have been trying to tell me who he was.

I was grasping at straws. I knew I was, but the hope had already taken root in my heart. If I was the daughter of the governor, surely that meant I’d be okay. My appointment had to go well, and they’d give me a good assignment. After all, people must expect good things from the offspring of a man so well-suited to leadership.

My stomach started to sink. But was that a good thing? Would I do well in a position of power like him? I wasn’t sure. I wasn’t a natural leader. I didn’t enjoy the responsibility of pressure that came with that, and surely the governor would be disappointed to hear that. He probably already knew, had read all of my school reports and with each one, his expectations for me sunk lower and lower.

What if I was a disappointment to him? It was nothing more than a thought. There was no truth to it, no certainty, but I felt my heart begin to break. He didn’t seem disappointed when he’d spoken to me, I tried to tell myself. He’d smiled when I spoke and hadn’t really hurried away, so perhaps he didn’t view me as a letdown.

Or perhaps I was just nothing to him. No relation. Just another random kid on the ship.

I took a deep breath and shook my head slightly, trying to clear it. That wasn’t helping. I was getting distracted and sidetracked, and it was a bad idea. I needed to stay focused, to remain as calm and under control as possible for what was about to come. There was no point in thinking about the governor any more. That would just make me more nervous, and I didn’t need it. Instead, I should have been thinking about…

My gaze roamed the elevator, searching for a distraction. There had to be something, anything that would occupy my mind as we rose through what felt like hundreds of levels of the ship. We’d already been going up for so long, and it seemed as though there were infinitely more floors to go. The illuminated light indicating where we were heading was so close to the top of the ship. It was only a few floors below the observation platform.

Would I be able to go out there after my appointment? The question came to me suddenly, but the moment it did, excitement began to grow within me. I hoped so. I wanted to see the world outside the ship. I still couldn’t recall anything about it, and that would help. It would give me some answers anyway.

Perhaps the air was no longer breathable. That could explain why we couldn’t go out onto the deck. Or maybe the ozone layer had been burnt away, and if we even set foot outside, we could burst into flames.

But surely, if that happened, the oceans would evaporate. The sun’s rays would turn it to steam, and then we’d have nothing to float in. That would make no sense. It had to be something else, something that made it unsafe for us to exist outside of the ship. There must have been a reason why I’d never left.

Confusion washed over me, but there was an edge of excitement to it. I was remembering things, I realised. I was starting to be able to access my memories in that world, and I was so eager to learn more, but nothing else came to me. All I knew was that I’d never left the ship.

That made sense. If the world outside was so terrible, they’d probably want to keep us inside all the time rather than risk losing us out there. Plus, the ship had everything we needed on it. I’d noticed that earlier. There was a medical centre, school, waste and water processing plants, and even a farm. It was fully equipped with everything we could ever need, so there was no reason to go outside.

But how long had the ship been in use? How long had people been living on it? It must have been a while. I’d been on there my whole life, and I was almost certain the guardians beside me had too. They would have mentioned it otherwise. So it must have been at least… I glanced across at the people on either side of me, trying to work out their ages.

They both looked younger than either of my parents, but it was hard to pinpoint exactly how old they were. The woman’s hair was flecked with greys, but the man had none. There were slight wrinkles around their eyes, but none on the foreheads. They seemed old, older than me, but maybe in their… forties?

That didn’t feel right, though. Something in the back of my mind told me that my calculations were off, that people had been living on the ship for generations. Hundreds of years had passed since the journey began, perhaps even longer.

Was that even possible, though? Could a ship really roam the seas for hundreds of years? Even if the world outside had come to a halt, if everyone else had died, surely something would have happened to put a stop to it before then? Either we would have run out of seas to cross, or the boat would have broken. There was no way any ship could be strong enough to withstand the waves for that long.

The elevator fell still, and there was a pause before the doors opened. In that silence, that moment of stillness, I noticed something. I hadn’t been expecting it. My body had been braced, waiting to feel them, but I did not. The ship was not moving. There were no waves.

No. It was moving. I could feel the constant vibration of the engines that had been running for as long as I could remember. The soft hum was present, just as it always was. The ship was moving. There were just no waves.

What could that mean, I asked myself as we stepped out of the elevator into another corridor. It was almost identical to the one on the lower floors, but the lights were slightly brighter, and the hallway was empty. There was no one else there.

Either the sea was completely calm and still, which didn’t seem right, or the ship was so big that it didn’t get buffeted by the waves. Perhaps they just broke against the sides without moving it at all. That didn’t feel right, though. Surely, it would still be moved a little bit. I would have been able to feel something, but I couldn’t.

Unless… maybe the ship wasn’t actually moving at all. Maybe it wasn’t even at sea. The entire thing could have been nothing more than a cruel trick, but why? Why would anyone do that? Why would they lock people on board a ship for years, for decades, and make them believe they were at sea?

We rounded another corner, and my eyes found a camera mounted to the wall above us. It was small, unobtrusive. If I hadn’t been looking for it, I wasn’t sure whether I would have even noticed it, but I did.

Someone was watching us. It may have just been a safety feature, something to make sure nothing bad was happening or that no one needed help, but it could have also been the people outside. The ones who had locked us there.

It was a cruel thing to do. I knew that. It was wrong to make people believe the earth was so unsafe and that the only remaining humans were the ones onboard with us. I wanted to believe that no one would ever do that, but I couldn’t. I’d been to cruel worlds before. Many of them, in fact. That meant I no longer trusted people. I knew just how evil they could be.

But why? That was the question that kept coming back to me as the guardians walked me along the corridor towards the door at the end. Why would anyone do that? What could they possibly have to gain from it?

Was it an experiment? A test to discover what happened to people when they were trapped inside for so long? Or was there something more sinister at play? That would have made sense too.

Perhaps it was a punishment of sorts. Maybe the people on board the ship had been selected to be there, chosen for some reason. They could have been criminals, violent and evil people who would have threatened the safety of every other person if they were free to roam the earth. Or maybe they were banished, locked up for breaking some inane rule. I’d seen that happen before.

My mind floated back to the other world I’d been to as we passed through a thick metal door. The one where I was Clea and had been arrested over nothing more than a misunderstanding. They’d assumed I’d done something malicious and with intent, but that wasn’t what happened. I didn’t know what I was doing, and maybe the people on board the ship were in the exact same situation I had been in. Perhaps they hadn’t known what they were doing either, and it had destroyed everything for them.

No, I thought, immediately dismissing that thought. I knew in my heart that it was not true. The other people on board were not being held against their will. They were not there to be punished, and their lives had not been destroyed. They had been saved. If their ancestors had stayed on earth, they all would have died.

My heart leapt, but I forced my thoughts to remain quiet, not wanting to scare away the scrap of information that had slipped into my mind. We were lucky to be there. It was the only way for humanity to continue on once the earth had been destroyed. We were the only hope. Without us, there was nothing.

I couldn’t work out if that scared me. It should have, I think. It was a lot of pressure to know that we were the last humans alive in the universe, but it also filled me with… purpose? I had a goal in life. Everyone on the ship did. We all had something we were working towards.

We needed to ensure that humanity would persevere, and that wasn’t always easy, but there was no other option. If we stopped, everything would be over, and we couldn’t let that happen. We would not just give up and wait for the end to find us.

That was why we were on the ship; I knew it. I could feel it in my heart. We were there because we refused to accept death. Every single one of our ancestors had turned away from those who said it was inevitable. They had boarded the ship to find a place where their children and the ones who followed could grow old in safety.

But could such a place even exist, I couldn’t help but ask myself. It didn’t feel possible. Surely if something had happened that had forced our ancestors onto the ship, it must have been catastrophic. It must have affected the whole planet, rendered the entire place inhabitable. Nowhere would have been spared from whatever disaster had occurred, so was our search in vain?

“Scan your hand, Daf,” one of my guardians instructed gently, tearing me out of my spiralling thoughts.

I glanced at the woman, my mind still racing. Which hand was I meant to hold out? And why? I looked away quickly, trying to figure it out before they caught on that I had no clue what was going on. I couldn’t let that happen. Not on such a big day.

My eyes landed on the many armed machine that had come to a stop before me. It was large yet sleek, and there was something strangely human about it. Perhaps it was the smiley face that looked down upon me from the screen as it waited to take note of my attendance.

I’d done it before, I realised. Part of me had been to the medical centre many times throughout my childhood on board the ship. I attended an appointment every year at least, and every time, I held my hand out to the medical assistance robot so that it could scan the chip inside and let the system know I was there.

Left hand.

The moment I remembered which one the chip was embedded inside, I thrust it out towards the robot. The smile on its screen widened, and one of the arms gently took my hand. The metal was reassuringly warm. It was just a degree or two cooler than my own skin, but it felt almost as though another person was holding my hand, not a machine.

“You’re right on time, Daffodil,” the robot told me, its voice warm. “Doctor Tulip is just supervising the disinfection of the room, but they shouldn’t be more than a few minutes. Can I get you a drink whilst you wait?”

“Um, no, thank you,” I replied.

“That’s quite alright,” it said before turning towards each of my guardians in turn. “Would you care for a drink, Rowan? Or Eucalyptus?”

“I’d love some water, if it’s not too much hassle,” one of them said.

Rowan. That was her name.

“It is not. Please take a seat in the usual waiting area, and I shall be back shortly,” the robot said before turning and moving away from us.

“Hopefully, we won’t be waiting for too long,” Cal, the other guardian, said. “The doctor was running so late for Ash’s appointment a couple of months ago. I think we waited almost half an hour!”

“We did not,” Rowan chuckled as we started to make our way through the medical centre. “It was fifteen minutes, if that!”

Cal laughed.

“Fifteen minutes, thirty. They’re basically the same,” he argued jokingly.

Rowan caught my eye and shook her head, but her lips were stretched up into a grin.

“Sure they are,” she said before her smile softened. “How are you feeling, Daf?”
