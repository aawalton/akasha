Interlude

“The Lattice requires maintenance. Maintenance requires tools. Tools require replacement. Do not confuse the tool’s usefulness with the tool’s consent.” - Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

The knock came at six. It was the same every morning.

Sarah had been dressed since five, sitting on the edge of the cot with her hands in her lap. If she was ready when the knock came, she could pretend she’d chosen to carry on with her duty.

“Doctor.” Enzo’s voice through the door. Always polite. “When you’re ready.”

She followed him through the compound. It was a converted fishing warehouse on the southern coast, three buildings around a courtyard with stone walls built for holding ice and fish, now holding people and ammunition. The corridor smelled of diesel and coffee. The Baron had good taste in real estate. Defensible, with sight lines to the plateau where the temples sat. Sarah noticed these things now.

The fighters she passed were making their way toward the goat tracks. She scanned them without meaning to. Favoured left knee on the tall one. The woman with the short hair was dehydrated; reduced skin turgor on the dorsal hand. The man they called Toni had gashes on his forearm hidden under his sleeve, and hidden meant they weren’t from a creature.

Enzo knocked twice at the south building’s end room and left.

The Baron opened the door. Solidly built and dark-eyed, with the kind of face that could be forty or fifty depending on whether he was smiling. Today he was not smiling. His right sleeve was already rolled to the elbow, the linen wrapping half-unwound. Underneath, the forearm was visibly thicker than the left, the skin taut across tissue that no longer matched the arm it had grown from.

“Come in, Doctor.”

She pulled a stool close and went to take his forearm in both hands. Her fingers stopped a centimetre short. It was unintentional but it happened every time. Three weeks of healing on command had left that in her. She breathed through it and made contact.

The Ether responded, her awareness flooding through the contact point like contrast dye through an IV line. The topography of the damage rendered in sensation, not image. She could feel bone density beneath the swelling, the thermal gradient where his blood ran warmer past the mutation boundary. Direct and precise.

The forearm was worse than three days ago.

Three days ago the mutation boundary had sat at the midpoint of the radius and ulna. Today it had crept two centimetres toward the elbow. The tissue beyond the line was dense, the bone thickened. The muscle fibres had been rewritten into something that contracted harder but no longer responded to the same neural signals.

The hardened ridge along the outer edge had deepened. She could feel the inflammation at the boundary. Two biological templates in the same limb, neither willing to concede. His Nadi Matrix was burning Reserve energy constantly trying to reconcile them.

“It’s spread,” she said.

“I know.” He flexed his hand. The fingers closed into a fist with visible effort. “How far?”

“Two centimetres since Tuesday. The rate is increasing.”

A calculation behind his eyes. “Can you slow it?”

“I can manage the inflammation. The spread is the core’s pattern asserting itself. I can’t tell it where to stop.”

She began to work, Ether flowing from her palms into the inflamed tissue, soothing the nerve disruption, calming the immune response where his body was still trying to reject what it had been forced to accept. The lines around his eyes softened by a degree. He’d never admit the pain was severe. She could feel exactly how severe it was.

“Two of the others are asking about a second core,” he said conversationally. “Kris. And the woman from the Mosta group.”

Kris was mid-twenties and had absorbed a low-grade core into his left hand six days ago. The mutation had thickened his knuckles and darkened the skin, but it was functional. He was also sleeping two hours a night and picking fights he didn’t remember starting.

“Kris shouldn’t absorb a second core until the first stabilises.”

“That could take weeks.”

“Yes.”

“We don’t have weeks, Doctor. The things on the plateau are getting bigger.”

She said nothing and focused on the boundary tissue. Eighteen to twenty-four hours of relief. Then the inflammation would build again.

“The arithmetic works, Doctor,” the Baron said. Flexing his right hand, testing. The hand was much stronger, stronger than any human hand had a right to be. “Kris can carry a shield hit that would snap a normal man’s wrist. My people come back alive because they’re stronger than what’s trying to kill them.”

She withdrew her hands after fifteen minutes. Her Reserve was low, and she had eleven more patients today. The mathematics of exhaustion was a calculation she ran constantly.

“The arithmetic works until someone absorbs too much and you lose them entirely,” she said. “But you don’t count those, do you?”

She left before he could answer. That was the one liberty she allowed herself; she decided when the session was over. He let her have it because it cost him nothing and made her believe she held some measure of control.

Her training let her diagnose that illusion as easily as a fracture.

* * *

The window was on the eastern wall of the storage building. It had a rusted latch and was unsealed, a minor oversight. She’d found it eleven days ago and spent three mornings working the latch loose a quarter-turn at a time.

Between six-forty-five and seven, the eastern perimeter had a gap. Night watch heading in, day watch not yet positioned. She would give herself twelve minutes.

She went at six-forty-two and the latch gave without sound.

The gap between the buildings was narrow and cool, limestone still holding the night’s chill. She turned sideways and moved.

The perimeter wall was chest height and she pulled herself over. Muscle memory from South Sudan when the shelling had started. The absolute clarity of a body that has decided to leave.

Morning air hit her face. Salt and limestone, with dry scrub underneath. Above, the cliffs rose pale and scarred, and at the top she could see the edge of the plateau where the five-thousand-year-old megaliths stood, older than the pyramids, older than everything except the thing that had put them there.

She started up the goat tracks and made it forty metres.

Renzo was sitting on a rock beside the track as though he’d been there for hours. One of the ones who’d absorbed a core. He was eating an orange, peeling it with hands that were too large for the fruit.

He looked at her but said nothing. Hands peeling the fruit and his eyes on her.

Sarah turned around and walked back up to the compound. She didn’t look back at the plateau. She knew she wasn’t going to reach it.

* * *

The Baron found her in the kitchen an hour later. She was eating bread and oil not because she tasted anything but because eating was a professional requirement.

He sat across from her. Sarah noticed that he’d re-wrapped the arm.

“I’m disappointed, Doctor.”

She looked at his face. Pupils equal and reactive. The jaw tension minimal. She could read his face the same way she could read his inflammation. The disappointment was real, settled into the muscles around his eyes, not performed for her benefit.

“I thought we had an understanding,” he said.

“We have an arrangement,” Sarah said. “Those aren’t the same thing.”

His smile was brief and functional. He’d already won the negotiation; her line had been a courtesy he could afford.

“Renzo will be on that path every morning from now on. You’ve cost me a sentry, Doctor.” He left.

Sarah sat with her bread. She thought about Renzo’s hands, the ridge growing since she’d last treated him. She thought about the Baron’s arm, the boundary creeping toward his elbow. She thought about Kris’s knuckles and the fights he didn’t remember starting.

She thought about her grandmother’s hands. Yiayia, who pressed her palms against Sarah’s forehead when she was feverish and spoke about the body’s rivers as though she could see them through the skin. Turns out she was right.

Sarah’s hands could do more than her grandmother ever dreamed. She could feel a fracture through three centimetres of swollen tissue. She could map the mutation’s edge with her fingertips. From a medical perspective, it was extraordinary.

She hated that she found it extraordinary.

The first patient she saw that day was a woman with a creature bite that had gone septic because nobody had cleaned it before bringing her to the Doctor. Why would they? The Doctor could fix anything. The Doctor didn’t get days off.

She put her hands on the wound. The hesitation was there. She healed her anyway.

* * *

She stood on the roof that night, looking at a world she could see but not reach.

The sea was black under the violet sky. The compound was quiet below. The Baron in his room with his maps and his arm and his arithmetic. The fighters playing cards or sleeping in the bunkhouse around him. Patrols, joking amongst themselves.

Night air filled her lungs with the smell of salt and limestone as she clenched her fists in quiet desperation.

She didn’t know that three people were standing in a cave in southern France at that moment, on a stone circle older than language, about to step into something that would fold the distance between Marseille and here into a single held breath.

She didn’t know that one of them carried a shield and thought in structures, or that another carried an axe and read rooms with the precision Sarah read wounds, or that a third carried a stolen guidebook and couldn’t stop chasing patterns even when the patterns were trying to kill him.

She looked at the sea, and the sea told her nothing, and she went back inside.
