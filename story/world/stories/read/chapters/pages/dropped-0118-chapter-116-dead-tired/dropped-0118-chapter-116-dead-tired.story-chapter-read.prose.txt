Chapter 116: Dead Tired

Back in England, if you talked about arms dealing, the image conjured tends to involve men wearing tactical gear and balaclavas, standing guard over crates transported in the dead of night, shipped off to parts unknown. To be fair, there was a degree of this, at least when the trade involved one or more parties subject to sanctions which effectively blocked any legal transactions, or goods subject to a high degree of control. To get around this, a degree of subterfuge was necessary, using false identities, shell companies and false flagged vessels to get the goods to their destination. There was quite a lot of more involved in this, from a personal perspective. From a national viewpoint? It was peanuts. The big leagues played in boardrooms and ballrooms, signing deals worth billions between meals of lobster and hundred year old wine.

The reality in this new world, it turned out, was somewhere in between. Arms controls were lax, owing to the persistent need for self-defence against monster spawns, so there was no need to hide most goods. It still wasn’t a good idea to work in the open, however, as that meant being subject to inspections, audits, and worst of all: taxation. In the name of avoiding a hefty tariff, some degree of subterfuge was still required, like finding a front business to launder our transactions. Fortunately, this was one area in which a recent monster attack actually played to our benefit.

”Why do you want a recently dead shopkeeper?” Ralph asked, somewhat bemused as he led our small party through an intricate network of back alleys towards the morgue. “I thought you weren’t hungry.”

“We’re not going to eat him,” Harvey chuckled, amused by the alley cat’s one track mind; a small bird perched on his shoulder, eyes gleaming as it channeled a form of Beastspeak, a Druid specialty, if I wasn’t mistaken. “I just need enough of him to form a contract; that way, we get a front man to keep the appearance of business as usual, while we make the real money away from prying eyes.”

Identity theft was much easier with the dead, as they were in no position to complain, something that evidently held true across both worlds. Granted, it was at least something the locals had attempted to address, with the various temples offering secured burial or cremation on holy ground, with a permanent guard posted and the entire process overseen by Clerics who were more than capable of detecting and invalidating any tampering. Of course, that cost a fair bit of money, enough to keep it within the middle and upper class; for the invalids, the destitute and those whose funds were tied up elsewhere, a cold slab in a neglected old graveyard was the best they could hope for. At least it wasn’t a landfill, because while I had no moral objection to dumpster diving, the smell was another matter entirely.

To be fair, when we eventually arrived, the graveyard was in better condition than I’d been expecting. The rows of gravestones were lined up orderly and mostly upright, while the burial mounds themselves were arranged neatly, with little to no dirt coming loose and making its way onto the cobblestone path between rows. There was probably even a guard at the front entrance, albeit not one alert enough to catch our approach, Ralph having led us in from behind a supply shed, employing his local knowledge of all the hidden places in the neighbourhood.

“Here we are,” Ralph declared, stopping at a grave so fresh I could still smell the moisture in the packed dirt cover. “Have fun, whatever it is you’re doing, I’m gonna go grab some lunch. There’s some fat, tasty rats hiding here, I can already taste them.”

Without further ado, the gluttonous alley cat scampered off into the distance, moving surprisingly quickly where food was concerned. It was rather strange, to see full sapience and sentience for what would have been household pets back on Earth. I’d initially thought that Pumpkin was a special case, marked by the System for greatness, but it seemed closer to the norm, at least where cats were concerned. That brought up questions for the wider ecosystem, particularly the meals I ate, ones that I resolved not to look too deeply into. I’d watched a few vegan shockumentaries before, the kind that did deep dives into factory farms and slaughterhouses, back when the plant-based diet was really taking off across the corporate world, and they made for grim viewing; not enough to give up meat, of course, but sufficient to make me look askance at my burger for a few days afterwards.

That might change if I learned that the local cows could talk, so it was best to keep such an eventuality out of sight and out of mind. While I was ruminating on such things, Harvey was already hard at work, or his summons were, a pair of workmen brought out with shovels in hand, unearthing the fresh plot with practiced efficiency. I took a few steps back, feeling a chill in the air that had nothing to do with the climate, as magic began to seep from the grimoire in his hands, of the kind that brought an instinctive revulsion to the forefront of my mind.

Humans were conditioned by evolution to detest rot and decay, I remembered reading at some point; one of many defences against food poisoning in the time before modern medicine, with it was more often than not a matter of life and death, not just an unpleasant evening on the ivory throne. I understood this, intellectually, but that didn’t mean I enjoyed looking at the unearthed body, already showing significant signs of decomposition.

”Ew,” Pumpkin remarked, scrunching her nose in distaste. “I prefer them fresh.”

”So do I, Pumpkin. So do I.”

Harvey dropped to one knee, reaching his free hand towards the grave in what looked like the world’s creepiest proposal, chanting in a language that sounded vaguely Scandinavian, but which I had no basis for understanding. I knew he was a necromancer of sorts, and could commune with the dead, but even so, I still nearly jumped with the corpse reached a hand out in return.
