Caleb woke up to a warm fire with meat skewered on a spit. Despite the presence of heat and meat, the meat didn’t seem to be cooked at all.

“Bog?” Caleb asked before his memories caught up to him. “Grisim?!”

He bolted upright and saw he was in a sheltered thicket, his brother on the far side of the fire and Bog between them protectively.

“Can you tell your stupid salamander I’m not going to hurt you,” Grisim said.

“His name is Bog,” Caleb said to Grisim, and then turned to Bog. “This is my brother Grisim.”

With the new word “brother,” Caleb sent emotions of family, trust, and mild irritation through the bond.

Bog understood finally and relaxed, allowing Grisim to get close enough to tend the meat.

“Craters, it’s still raw,” Grisim muttered.

“It’s the cold aspect,” Caleb guessed. “It absorbs a lot of extra heat before it will cook. Drop it into the coals.”

Grisim obliged, and then turned back to Caleb.

“Where in the world have you been?! Kelia is worried sick about you! Dad too, but he won’t admit it. We all thought you were dead! I found your spear wrapped up and abandoned. Are you mad?!”

Grisim went on a little longer before finally running out of steam. When his brother had finished, Caleb spoke, still mulling over his words.

“I…” he began, not sure where to start or how much to tell. In the end, he decided that if he couldn’t trust his brother, who could he trust?

Well, Kelia, but I don’t want to burden her alone with this secret.

“It’s a long story,” Caleb said, and then recounted the last few days.

“So, let me get this straight,” Grisim said, and Caleb braced for the expected dig. “Iron salamanders are dragons and we’ve just been wasting the initialization potential by drowning them?”

“That’s what you don’t understand?”

Caleb had worried Grisim was going to want to talk about his bloodline and the implications regarding their mother, but thankfully Grisim wanted to avoid that painful topic as much as he did. He’d told his brother about the bloodline and received the appropriate jibes about being a twig, but no more than that had been discussed.

“The rest makes sense,” Grisim explained. “I have no problem believing you would lose track of time working in a cave. The whole Dragon Rider thing is the least believable part, and that hinges on Bog over there being a dragon.”

“I didn’t ‘lose track of time’! I was looking for a way out!”

“It sounds like you just left the way you came in!”

“I only did that because I was about to be assaulted by an angry tribe of troglodytes whose home I destroyed!”

They argued for a while longer until Bog got up, walked between them and poked at the meat in the fire. He let out an irritated chuff and sat down, body curled around the fire to build an oven and heat the meat sooner.

“Fine, fine,” Grisim conceded, lifting his hands. “I was only messing with you. I mean, all of it is hard to believe. You of all people have a rare class. A Dragon Rider class. AND you killed multiple dragons and a basilisk.”

“Most of those dragons were basically babies and I’m pretty sure the basilisk was small for their species,” Caleb said.

“Stop being modest,” Grisim scolded his younger brother. In a high-pitched voice he continued, “You always do this. ‘Oh, yeah, it’s no big deal, I just rigged a mechanism that let me forge metal with the strength of an initiated,’ or ‘Oh, here’s a master-quality knife I made, you can have it, I have three.’”

“I don’t sound like that,” Caleb said, not arguing with the sentiment.

“You do, but that’s not the point. You always downplay what you do.”

“I don’t.”

“You do, and you’ll prove it for me,” Grisim said, pointing to the breastplate, helmet, and spear he had removed from Caleb after he’d passed out. “Where did you get that stuff?”

“I… uh… made it—well, not the breastplate, I just repaired that.” Caleb added the last part quickly lest his brother think the shoddy craftsmanship was his own work.

“And is there anything special about any of that equipment?” Grisim asked leadingly.

“How did you know?” Caleb asked, suddenly worried.

He had vague plans of making equipment in secret, hiding his new ability so word wouldn’t get out and catch the ear of the empire. They didn’t limit themselves to only kidnapping crafters with the classes to back them up, and Caleb was under no illusions that he’d be safe.

“I didn’t for certain. But I had a hunch. You threw me your master’s hammer like it was a dirty rag. You used to revere this stupid thing.” Grisim was holding the hammer out to Caleb handle first.

“It’s not a dirty rag… it’s just…” Caleb looked at the ground and sighed. “Not very well-made.”

“Hah! I knew it. So on top of getting a rare class, you figured out how to make magic equipment?!”

“Yeah…” Caleb admitted. “But it’s—”

This time Grisim didn’t interrupt him.

Through his wind sense, he felt the approach of something through the air. He turned just in time to catch the darting figure of a falcon swooping down at him. The falcon grabbed the hammer out of Grisim’s still-outstretched hand and continued on into the sky.

“Oh Impietor damn me,” Caleb said, “she’s going to be pissed.”

The “she” in question was Kelia, Caleb’s longtime—and if you asked his brother, out-of-his-league—girlfriend. Kelia had a falcon named Avis that she’d raised from her egg. The falcon went everywhere with them and, to Caleb’s dismay, strongly disliked him.

Caleb was fairly certain the dislike was due to the fact that he always smelled liked burnt coal, but Grisim was equally certain it was because the falcon was “a better judge of character than Kelia.” Kelia, for what it was worth, thought the falcon didn’t like that Caleb spent so much time with her, keeping her away from the forest.

The two brothers sat around nervously waiting for Kelia to arrive, which she did several minutes later, walking into their firelit clearing with Avis on her shoulder and a hammer in her hand.

Kelia was tall for a woman, though shorter than Caleb and Grisim. She had hair so blonde it was nearly white and had an athletic build. A bow was strapped over her back, sticking up opposite the side the bird perched on.

“Caleb!” she cried out in what Caleb wasn’t sure was joy, rage, or tears.

Hoping it was the first or last, he ran out to meet her with an embrace. She hugged him back, but when she brought her hands around his back, she struck him with the hammer.

A bit of all three, I guess, he thought.

“I’m sorry!” he said, holding her close.

“I forgive you, but next time you disappear, you better actually be dead.”

Caleb sent a strong reprimand through the bond and shifted around to hug Kelia from the side.

“Kelia,” he said, nervously. “I’d like you to meet my… uh… dragon. His name’s Bog.”
