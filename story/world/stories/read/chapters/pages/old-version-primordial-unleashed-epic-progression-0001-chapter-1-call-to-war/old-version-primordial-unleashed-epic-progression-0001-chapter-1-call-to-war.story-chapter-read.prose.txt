It had always been there, a quiet ember awaiting the tinder. Heat swept through Skippii’s lungs, bellowing the coals of an old, abandoned forge. He pressed his trembling hand to the earth and felt it radiate through his heart. Shutting his eyes, he held his breath and searched the silence between each heartbeat for a divine voice–a heavenly command. But none came.

A trumpet blared; the call to war. Skippii withdrew his hand from the earth. A cold draft whispered after its absence. But he pushed it from his mind, resolute in performing his duties. The Gods did not speak to the likes of him.

“Forward, legionnaires,” their Primus bellowed.

Hefting his shield, Skippii ran forward to survey the valley. All seemed peaceful at a glance, but the shadows of the treeline were treacherous. The Ürkün prowled within, garbed in animal hides like spirits of the wild. Each hour that they evaded the legion’s spears worsened his nerves. He longed for the heretic to crash upon their phalanx–to finally bloody his spear. To prove himself a true legionnaire.

“Form up!” A trumpet blast punctuated their Primus’ command. “Second Cohort, to the front.”

Skippii strode in line with the seven other legionaries of his companeight. As they reached the valley’s crest, he and the five hundred legionnaires of Cohort II laid down their shields and spears in the sun-dappled undergrowth. Each of the Imperium’s finest was mantled with a red cloak, repainting the untamed forest in legion colours. The same cloak now hung heavily from Skippii’s shoulders.

As he settled in the tall grass, the cohort’s standard caught the sun’s rays and stole his breath. Its proud insignia had guided him to this very moment. All his life, he had basked in the wondrous tales of cyclops-fighting, city-conquering, heretic-defying legionnaires of the Imperium Auctoritas. The danger and thrill of facing the enemy–the pride in performing his duty–all things he had craved for so long… Now he was making his mark, even if it was just a simple supply run.

“Have you sight of the enemy?” Tenoris asked.

“No,” Skippii said, scanning the valley. “But they’re out there.”

“I should like to meet him,” Tenoris growled.

A smile crept across Skippii’s lips. They were both nineteen years of age, but Tenoris was taller and more muscular. He raised a thick labourer’s hand to his brow and peered down at the valley. The pale sun shimmered in the deep bronze of his helmet and softly upon his white leather thorax armour.

“They can’t have gone far since last night,” Skippii said.

“What horrid luck.” Tenoris shook his head. “Two men dead, ever before their oaths could rise upon renown and reach the heavenly gates.”

“Novice mistake,” Skippii grimaced. “Don’t venture outside the palisade at night. It’s not safe in the highlands, not even with our auxiliaries about.”

“What, not even for women?” Kaesii leaned in. “You know, the fornicaria? I heard that’s why they went out.” The plump legionnaire sat atop his round shield with his back to the valley, tearing up clumps of grass. He was sweating, and the light tan of his cheeks had taken on a red sheen. He stared hungrily at his meagre harvest, then tossed it into the wind.

“Our enemy set a lustrous trap?” Tenoris scowled. “I would rather they line up and face our phalanx like men, rather than send harlots to do their evil deeds.”

“Cowards,” Kaesii grunted.

“Who knows what got them?” Skippii said. “Could have been the Ürkün. Could have been wolves, beasts…”

“Or monsters,” Kaesii said.

“Maybe the heretic,” Skippii whispered.

Tenoris shifted uncomfortably and hissed through his teeth. “I found our training somewhat lacking in that regard.”

Kaesii snorted. “What’s there to train? If we came up against one of them, they’d probably order us to run and wait for the coven to come rescue us.”

“You don’t sound too pleased about that,” Skippii said.

Kaesii huffed and ground his jaw. “Retreat is a dishonour, even against a superior foe. Especially against one.”

“Your bravery precedes you, Kaesii,” Tenoris beamed. “Should the bane of legionnaires reveal themselves, I hope that you will pardon me for following orders and retreating as we should. Let our Gods do battle with theirs, for in such a contest, our victory is assured.”

“But the Fifth Legion…” Skippii fell silent. He should not have brought up their ruin at Nerithon. Besides, rumours were always more morbid than the truth. They would find out soon enough for themselves what had happened to Legion V.

Skippii cleared his throat. “Do you see any cattle in the fields? I hope they have something left after winter. It’s not normal for us to be marching this much in spring.”

“How do you know?” Kaesii said.

“Because it is before the harvest,” Tenoris said. “But our quest is urgent.”

“Gods I’m hungry.” Skippii took a deep breath, as though the floral air could fill his stomach. He scanned the valley, assessing what supplies they could sequester from the locals. Below, a rushing stream cut through fields of crops. A dirt path wound alongside it to a quaint stone bridge, and a three-story, thatched-roof farmstead stood alone at the head of the valley.

“Think they’ll give us a fight?” a voice rumbled behind him. Skippii turned to see Drusilla standing over him. Broad, with boulder-like biceps, Drusilla carried his shield as easily as if it were a feather. Skippii glanced enviously at his physique, hard-wrought in the renowned gymnasiums of Summitus. A tattoo resembling the grain of marble cast his spear arm in stone.

“I wouldn’t just give up my food if I were them,” Kaesii said.

“They won’t fight us,” Skippii said. “They’re just farmers. And they’re not in league with the Ürkün.”

Kaesii snorted. “That farmhouse could be a nest of vipers… of heretic magi, and I’d still fight them all for a shank of beef. I’ve eaten nothing but oats for weeks.”

“We had rabbit yesterday,” Drusilla said dryly as he knelt in the grass beside them.

“Hardly,” Kaesii whined. “Split eight ways in a stew, I could barely taste it at all. It just made me want more.”

“I bet it did.” Drusilla’s thin moustache twitched into a grin, defiant of the legion’s strict shaving regulations.

“I am sure we will find them amenable,” Tenoris mused. “For it is the liberation of their lands that beckoned us to these shores.”

Behind him, their Primus barked orders. With a rustle of armour, the legionnaires around him rose and prepared to advance. Untying his travel sack, Skippii pressed one hand into the earth and clutched his brooch–a gift from his mother before he’d left her for the legion.

“One day, you’ll understand,” he whispered. “I’ll return with wealth, and renown, and stories to tell.”

Just come home alive. His mother’s words echoed in his memory.

Suddenly, the earth blossomed beneath him like a spring flower opening its face to the sun. Pressing his hand into the wet soil, he sought after the hidden flames. The raw energy fizzled between his fingers, rising within his arm. The faster his heartbeat, the stronger it pulsed within him. He grew dizzy with the feeling of rushing tides. Something powerful awaited him beneath the earth–familiar, yet unfathomable.

A shadow eclipsed the sun. He opened his eyes to see Cur standing before him. The old legionnaire glowered beneath the rim of his helmet. Wrinkles stretched at the corners of his eyes, creasing his brow into a permanent scowl.

“Strumping velvets,” Cur said. “Not getting nervous now, are you?”

“No.” Skippii wiped a bead of sweat from his brow and glared back. “What’s velvets? You keep saying that?”

“Velvet cloaks,” Cur said. “Clean cloaks.”

Tenoris laughed heartily. “So what if our cloaks are clean?”

Cur wrinkled his nose and spat. “Two of us are already curled up with stomach cramps. Barely weaned before the battlefield. This companeight’s the sorriest bunch of legionnaires I’ve ever met.”

“Hey, take it easy, Cur. Save some for the enemy.” Orsin approached from further down the line. His thorax armour creased around his lean shoulders like a second skin, tailored for a narrower fit. His green eyes assessed Skippii and the other recruits of their companeight with what appeared to be uncertainty.

“Get up lads, we’re heading out.” Orsin flexed the stiff shoulder of his spear arm, and two bronze bracelets tinkled against his bronze vambraces. Then, he lowered his voice so that their superiors would not overhear. “We’re two-down because of soured oats, but let’s not make a scene. It’s a simple job, but there’s no point in them getting worn out. We’ve still got a march ahead of us. So Arius and Fulmin will stay behind and look after our packs.”

“Glory to them,” Cur said sarcastically.

“The six of us, plus the slaves and mule, will make it to the farmstead.” He scanned their group, reduced to just six legionnaires. “Sound good?”

Cur shrugged. “Tomorrow, maybe I’ll get ill, and you can all carry me aloft like an emperor.”

With a snort, Orsin led them on a path that wound into the valley. Two more companeights flanked their unit, and the small force of twenty-two legionnaires descended upon the farmstead. The rest of Cohort II remained atop the valley’s ridge in reserve. As the distance between them broadened, a bead of sweat broke out beneath Skippii’s helmet. He clutched his spear, searching the shadows of the encroaching forest. Having grown up amongst Legion III, he knew the tactical purpose of their foraying force: if the enemy were indeed nearby, it would be his job to smoke them out. And if the Ürkün ambushed them, at least the whole cohort would not be caught off-guard. Only they, alone against a horde.

Something flashed in the upper window of the farmhouse. A glint of metal. Then the shutters closed.

“Did you see that?” Skippii said.

“Um-hm,” Orsin said. “They know we’re coming. It’s for the best.”

The muddy hoof-printed path that led to the farmhouse was patrolled by a flock of chickens and guarded by one curious cow. Not much of a battlefield or foe. But this quest cemented his place within the brick and mortar of Legion IX. His stiff leather thorax armour dug into his shoulders, and his bronze greaves chafed against his shins. But finally, they were his. His cloak, his weaponry. His oaths.

“No workers in the fields,” Skippii said. “And in spring. Do you think they’re hiding?”

“Perhaps their eyes do not work so well,” Tenoris replied. “Once they see the red of our cloaks, they shall sally forth with all manner of breads, meat and cheeses.”

“We’ll take what they can spare,” Orsin replied over his shoulder. “We’re liberators, not conquerors, remember.”

At the rear of their companeight, Drusilla snorted and glared at the peaceful cow with murderous eyes. “What’s the difference?”

“Who wants to explain?” Orsin said.

“Isn’t it obvious?” Kaesii was quick to answer, adopting a lofty lecturer’s tone. “Philoxenia is our ancient ally. We have come to free them, and re-unite their lands under the glorious banner of the Imperium Auctoritas.”

“So what?” Drusilla’s mountainous accent rolled the words like stones. “Still need subjugating, don’t they?”

“Only if they give us trouble,” Orsin said.

“They can spare a few chickens at least,” said Kaesii.

“That they can,” Orsin agreed.

Treading down the garden path, Orsin knocked on the farmstead’s door with the haft of his spear. Skippii took one look at the foundation’s brickwork, and the upper floors’ overhanging wooden beams, and knew he could climb all the way to its roof. Farmsteads had provided the best playgrounds for mischief as a child. Whether chasing livestock or, as he grew older, spying on the girls his age working in the fields, some of his fondest memories hailed from valleys such as this.

But he was a vagabond no more. He represented the legion now, and his actions bore consequences.

Beside him, Cur tossed his shield down and slouched on a bench. The leathery man had somehow avoided both death and promotion during his long career as a legionnaire, choosing to serve as a lowly foot soldier in his ripe forties. Skippii wondered what tied him to the army; was it a passion for war, or financial debtors at home?

“Hurry up,” Cur murmured. “My arse is going numb. The longer I sit here, the less likely I’m gonna get up.”

Definitely the debtors.

A latch scraped, and the door opened. An old man peered out, his rigid Philoxenian nose coming first like a boat’s prow. Wispy grey hair settled atop his sun-pocked scalp, mirrored in a goat-like beard. He gripped the doorframe limply, shoulders slouched, cheeks a little red. He did not seem surprised by the legionnaires; rather, he seemed reserved. Not quite fear… Skippii couldn’t put his finger on it, but something felt off.

“Yah.” Orsin greeted them in the local tongue and relayed the legion’s demands. The Philoxenian language shared a lot of similarities with Auctorian, and Skippii picked out a few words: “Grain,” “Ninth Legion,” and “Cooperate.” Basically, give us what we came here for, and we won’t have to take it by force.

The old man nodded and spoke tersely to his wife behind the door. Then, slipping on a pair of sandals, he squeezed past Orsin, shutting the door behind him. They followed him behind the house, but Skippii hesitated.

“I think I’ll check inside,” he said.

Orsin turned, a question on his face.

“Come,” the old farmer said irritably. “Come entháde. Take…Pollè eourish.”

Skippii ignored him. “Could be something in the kitchen.”

“Silverware?” Drusilla piped, taking a step towards the farmhouse.

Orsin held out the haft of his spear to stop him, like a shepherd controlling his cattle. “No silverware. No plunder. Just supplies.”

“Why?” Drusilla protested. “They don’t need it.”

“Neither do you,” the old Cur grumbled. “You want to carry a candlestick halfway across the world? Sweet Virelisus, who are these recruits you have sent me? Magpies and thieves. Pack mules. Trinket mules.”

Drusilla scowled, but despite all his muscle and brawn, Cur’s barbed tongue had whipped him into silence.

“Not silver,” Skippii said. “Salt. Maybe some pepper and luger root for seasoning. A little goes a long way.”

Orsin raised an eyebrow and nodded slowly. “Alright, good call, Skip. You and Tenoris take a look inside.”

He and Tenoris bowed through the doorframe and shut the door behind them. Tenoris’ helmet grazed the ceiling as he ducked into the farmhouse’s cloakroom, and into the kitchen beyond. An old woman eyed them from across a large table, hands crossed nervously beneath her chest. Cupboards and racks lined the walls, but he saw no food on display. A dog hobbled up to him and sniffed his leg; a large breed of a healthy age, yet timid.

“Hey boy.” Skippii let the dog sniff his hand. “Greetings,” he said to the farm lady, keeping his tone light. “Salt. You have salt?”

The old woman nodded abruptly and hurried across her kitchen. Frowning, he watched as she fumbled through the cupboards with shaky hands. Philoxenian farmers were often cautious of the legion, but fear was unwarranted. The Imperium and Philoxenia were ancient allies, who shared many cultural roots. Their pantheon was the same, and he knew of no animosity between the peoples. But the invading Ürkün had severed those bonds. Now the legions sought to reforge them. In just forty years of campaigning, the Imperium had freed two major Philoxenian cities from the barbarian’s grasp, not to conquer, but to liberate them and reinstate their rightful senate.

However, the campaign was stalled at Nerithon, where the Fifth Legion suffered a devastating loss to the city’s garrison, championed by a despot of heresy. And now, the darkness of their vile gods leaked across the land. Perhaps the raiding Ürkün had put the old lady on edge. Perhaps she didn’t know the difference between the barbarians and the noble Imperium Auctoritas. Young men with spears were rarely a good omen, despite what banners they flew.

“We are Legion Nine,” Skippii said gently. He set his spear aside and motioned for Tenoris to do the same.

“Legion,” he repeated, patting his chest. “Imperium Auctoritas. Friend…” He searched for a translation. “Phile.”

“Philia,” the old woman repeated, pouring salt into a pouch and tying it closed.

“Philia,” Skippii repeated, matching her accent with care. “Pepper? Luger root?”

She nodded and began rummaging again. Skippii rounded the table and looked out of the kitchen window. He spotted the red cloaks of his companeight between the orchard trees, moving slowly beyond his sight.

“I hope they find plenty,” Tenoris said, his low voice reverberating around the small kitchen. “I have missed the flavour of fruit.”

“They could bring out a sack-full and we’d be lucky to see any in our porridge tomorrow morning.”

Tenoris’ expression fell. “There are many mouths to feed.”

“Five hundred.”

“Oh, what I would do for a handful of raisins or the skin of a lemon.”

“We’ll see yet.”

Flowers climbed the farmhouse’s wall, curling around the window frame and poking their white and purple heads through the slats. His mother had picked flowers like them–a mild relaxant; their sweet smell mingled with the kitchen’s cosy wood-smoke. A glimmer of peace and normalcy amidst the uncertainty of war.

The floorboards creaked above Skippii’s head. The old woman froze with her hands raised to the pantry. She did not turn; did not give a sound.

“Family?” he asked. The woman ignored him. “Philia?”

A silence dragged between them, taught as a bowstring. He drew his eyes to the ceiling, a mere handspan above his head, and a sense of wrongness hollowed his insides. Then he lowered them to Tenoris. The brow of Tenoris’ helmet darkened his eyes, but the glint of danger shone from within. Skippii nodded once, then slowly paced to the old woman’s side.

“Who is here?”

The look she gave him spoke a thousand words. No difficulty in language could contain the wave of anxiety which spread from her. Her hands shook; her lips quivered. Then with a jolt, she thrust a pouch of spices into his chest.

“Go,” she said. “Shoo.”

“Has our welcome expired so soon?” Tenoris said.

“I don’t think so.” Dropping his shield, Skippii took the woman gently by her shoulders.

Her eyes sparkled with tears. She shook her head. “Go.”

“Who is upstairs?” Skippii asked, pointing.

The old woman’s lip quivered. “Kleresá.” She clenched her jaw. Tears streamed down her face freely.

Skippii’s heart pounded. He glanced at Tenoris, who now seemed to grasp the strangeness of the situation. The big legionnaire snatched his spear, then reached for the front door.

“Wait,” Skippii said in a hushed voice. “If they’re here… the Ürkün, upstairs… and they have a girl…” His mind raced, organising the scenario before him like pieces of a puzzle. His training told him to alert the others outside so that they could face the enemy at full strength, but it wasn’t that simple. This wasn’t a field battle, nor an arena duel. This was someone’s home.

“It has to be us.”

“What?” Tenoris blurted. “But why?”

“They have a prisoner,” Skippii said, motioning to the old woman’s distress. “Maybe a few. If we cause a fuss, they’ll slit their throats and make a run for it.”

“If the enemy are here-”

“Then we’ll deal with them,” Skippii said. “Quickly and quietly. You and I.”

Tenoris looked at him shocked. During their two weeks of travelling together, they had shared a few conversations by the campfire; idle talk about their homelands and ambitions. But all that chatter was smoke in the wind when it came time for a man to raise his spear and make true his oaths, and Skippii hoped desperately that Tenoris’ muscle was not merely an outfit for someone less brave.

Tenoris’ nostrils flared as he took a deep breath, eyes wild like a bull’s. His shoulders swelled, and a flash of anger shone in his eyes. With a jolt, Skippii feared that the big man would strike him down.

“I see, brother, that you are fearless,” Tenoris growled, hefting his spear and raising his shield in the cramped kitchen. “Chrysaetos be our witness. Let us make quick butchery of these villains.”

Invigorated, Skippii took up his spear and strode from the kitchen towards the stairs. His first test had arrived sooner than he expected. What kind of legionnaire would he make before the day was up? He hoped not a dead one.
