Clive woke up to the morning light streaming through the window. He lay still for a moment, watching the mist press against the glass. It was beautiful.

But he was alone.

He'd known he would be. Jill had taken the room next door. What would he need to do for her to trust him again. He lay there and stared at the ceiling and thought about that, and then thought about it some more, until he decided thinking about it wasn't getting him anywhere.

He got dressed, splashed water on his face, and went downstairs.

Azura was already at the table with a bowl of meat.

"Jill?" he said.

"Out." Azura tipped her bowl. "Early."

He stood in the doorway and looked at the street. The mist softened everything — the market stalls setting up along the lane, the people moving between them, the shapes of buildings going gray and indistinct at a distance. It was still quiet.

His thoughts wandered to Jill again. He needed to do something. He wasn't good at waiting, and he was worse at talking, and the space between them right now felt like a seam he didn't know how to follow. But flowers. Jill had always liked flowers. Back home she'd kept a small pot of something on the windowsill that he'd never learned the name of, and she'd watered it every morning without fail even when she forgot to eat breakfast.

It wasn't much. He knew it wasn't much. But it was a place to start.

He didn't know if Ashveil had a florist. He told himself he was exploring — getting a sense of the village layout, useful to know — and turned down the lane that ran along the east side of the market.

The first stall he passed sold root vegetables. The second sold candles. The third was a bunny woman arranging bundles of dried herbs on a wooden frame, and beside her, in a clay pot on the ground, a loose arrangement of blue, small-petaled flowers, growing in clusters.

He stopped.

"What are those?" he asked.

The woman looked at the pot, then at him. "Millwort," she said. "Grows in the bogs. People buy it for the smell, mostly. Keeps the shade-lings calm."

Clive looked at the flowers. Small and blue, clustered close together, nothing like anything he'd seen back home. He tried to imagine Jill's reaction, whether she'd find them charming or strange, whether she'd even know what they were.

"Do you have roses?" he asked.

The woman looked at him blankly. "Roses."

He pulled out his sketchbook and roughed one out quickly — the layered petals, the curved stem, the thorns.

She leaned over and studied it. "I've seen something like that," she said. "Wild ones, to the east of the village. Past the millstream." She handed the sketchbook back. "Not sure anyone grows them deliberately."

He considered that for a moment. He could manifest one himself, the shape was simple enough, well within what he could hold in his mind. It would take minutes.

Then he thought about Jill asking where he'd found it. I painted it didn't have the same weight as I walked across the village at dawn looking for something you might like. Magic was easy. Effort meant something different.

"I'll take a bundle of the millwort as well," he said. Jill had been in Ashveil long enough to know the local flowers, maybe long enough to have grown fond of them. The millwort might mean more to her than to me.

She cut him a small bunch and wrapped the stems in cloth. He tucked it under his arm and stood there a moment, looking at the bundle. It wasn't much on its own. But the woman had mentioned wild roses to the east, and there might be other things growing out there, something with color, that could fill out a proper bouquet. Let Jill pick what she liked best.

He headed for the east gate.

Azura was already there, leaning against the post, arms crossed, watching him approach with a stern expression.

"Were you thinking of adventuring without me?"

"I'm picking flowers," Clive said. "It's not adventuring."

"You were going to leave without telling me."

"I was going to be back in an hour."

"That's not a denial." She fell into step beside him as he passed through the gate, not waiting for an invitation. "I always know when you're leaving, you realize. I feel it. You can’t abandon me."

He glanced at her. Right, the bond. [Bonded Instinct], the system had called it. He'd half-forgotten, though he supposed he shouldn't have. It wasn't the kind of thing that switched off when he found it inconvenient.

"I wasn't trying to lose you," he said.

"No," Azura agreed. "You were just hoping I wouldn't notice in time to follow." She looked at the bundle under his arm. "Are those for Jill?"

"Maybe."

She was quiet for a few steps. "What kind of flowers does she like?"

"I'm trying to figure that out."

Azura nodded, as if this were a perfectly reasonable answer, and said nothing more about it. She walked with him out into the mist.

The mist thinned as they moved east, the village behind them giving way to open ground of low grass, scattered stones and the sound of water somewhere ahead that had to be the millstream. Clive had his eyes on the ground, half-watching for anything that might be worth adding to the bouquet.

A low growling sound caught his attention. He swirled his head, searching for the source.

Three shapes resolved out of the mist to the left. Dark fur, low to the ground, not fully solid, shadow bleeding into the outline of each one like ink in wet paper. Shadowhounds. Clive was only too familiar with them now, having fought them many times in Marblehaven.

His hand went to his sword instinctively.

"I wouldn't do that," said a third voice.

A man came around the bend in the road, leading a two-wheeled cart piled with covered goods. He was broad through the shoulders, with a clay pipe tucked behind one ear and a strip of dried meat in his other hand, which he was tearing pieces off as he walked.

The shadowolves immediately lost interest in Clive.

They converged on the merchant with the enthusiasm of animals reuniting with an old friend. He made a sound low in his throat and tossed the remaining strip of meat into the air. All three of them watched it arc and land, and then the smallest one had it and the other two were jostling for position and the merchant was laughing, scratching the nearest one behind the ears.

"Shadowhounds," he said, without looking up. "People out here call them that. 'Shadowolf' if you're from the city and want to sound educated about it." He glanced at Clive's hand, still on the hilt. "You can let go of that. They're not interested in you. Well —" he looked at the millwort under Clive's arm, "— they might be interested in whatever you're carrying, but they won't take it."

Clive released the sword.

"They den in the eastern bogs," the merchant continued, straightening up. The smallest hound had finished the meat and was now sitting on his foot. He didn't seem to notice. "Come into the valley when the mist rolls heavy. People feed them, they remember it. Feed one twice and it'll follow you for a week."

He pulled the pipe from behind his ear and pointed it at Clive. "You got a look about you. First time seeing them?"

"Not really.” Clive shook his head. “But aren't they usually more aggressive?"

He tucked the pipe back before laughing. “Maybe if you attack them. But they’re harmless really. In fact, they're good company on the road. Bandits tend to avoid them. I've been using them as an informal escort for years. Don't have to pay them much.” He tossed them another slab of meat. “Just remember them, and show them some gratitude."

Clive still had a look of skepticism on his face.

"Don't believe me, come on." The merchant waved him forward. "They won't bite. Well… they might bite, but not you. Not right now."

That wasn't as reassuring as it was probably meant to be. Clive approached slowly, hand still on the hilt. He didn't draw. But he was ready.

The nearest one stood and closed the distance between them without any particular hurry. It pressed its nose against his hand, the one still resting on the sword.

Then it licked him.

He looked down at it. It looked back up at him with pale eyes and thumped its tail twice against the ground.

The other two drifted in. One of them sniffed the air near arm and sneezed. The third simply sat down beside the first and waited, as if it had all the time there was.

The nearest one pushed its head against his palm and Clive found himself scratching behind its ears before he'd consciously decided to. The fur was cold and dense under his fingers, real enough, but like touching a shadow that had learned to hold its shape.

"He likes you," the merchant said.

Clive smiled as he looked down at the hound, which was still leaning into his hand. Whatever they were made of, they were just like dogs. He knew exactly how to reward them.

He reached for his sketchbook. The hound watched with sudden sharp attention, tracking the movement the way dogs tracked the opening of a kitchen drawer. Clive almost laughed. He flipped to a clean page and his pencil was already moving. A cut of meat, substantial, the good kind. He'd learned what that looked like yesterday. Knew the grain of it, the fat cap intact along the edge, the clean separation at the joint. His hand moved without hesitating.

The shape resolved on the page, and then off it — solid and real in his palm, the smell of it hitting the air before he'd fully closed the sketchbook.

All three shadowhounds stared at him with pleading eyes.

He broke it into three pieces.

They ate from his hands with the politeness of well-trained dogs at a dinner table. No snapping, no jostling. Just careful chewing and then three sets of pale eyes watching him afterward to see if there was more.

The merchant watched this with a smile. "You're good with animals."

"I had dogs," Clive said. "Back home."

Had. He thought about Yuki, his Japanese Spitz, white as a cloud. He'd had her for eleven years. When she died he'd told himself he just needed time before getting another one, and then time had passed and he'd kept not getting one, because the specific shape of that loss hadn't gone anywhere.

He looked down at the nearest shadowhound, which was still watching his hands.

"Just the one," he said. "A long time ago."

The merchant nodded, as if that explained something. He clicked his tongue once and the three hounds peeled away from Clive and drifted back toward the cart, resuming their loose orbit around it. He looked past them both, east, toward the mist and the sound of water that was just beginning to be audible beneath the quiet.

"Where are you headed? Not much east of here except the millstream and the bog."

"Someone told me there were wild roses. Past the millstream."

The merchant raised his eyebrows. "There are. Follow the stream north about a quarter mile until you see the old stone wall. They grow along the base of it — red ones mostly, couple of white. Been there longer than anyone in the village can remember." He glanced at the millwort again. "Bouquet?"

"Trying to put one together."

The merchant nodded slowly. "Millwort and wild roses. That'll do. Stream's about ten minutes on. You'll hear it before you see it in this mist."

He tipped his head and moved on down the road, cart wheels turning slow and quiet in the damp grass, shadowhounds drifting alongside him.

Clive watched them go.

"Good company on the road," he said.

"He wasn't wrong," Azura said, still watching the largest hound until it disappeared into the mist. Then she looked at him. "The stream's ten minutes. Are we going?"

He tucked the millwort more securely under his arm and kept walking east.

The painter could make anything from nothing. He chose to walk.
