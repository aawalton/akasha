Her morning began with numbers.

Elynara leaned against a mile-stone, staff tucked under one arm, watching the System panel hover faintly before her eyes.

The totals were satisfying. The steady climb of her mana capacity most of all. Though she could feel the sharper control on her fire spells and mana manipulation growing – greater than what spelled itself out neatly in blue-white light would suggest. Her grin caught before she could help it.

Progress. Honest and earned!

Only one line drew a frown. Water magic. Still pitiful beside her fire. And below that, Mana Sense, stubbornly clinging to its low level no matter how many quiet evenings she had spent cycling energy until her vision swam.

But Elynara figured it was just like with Meditation. She just needed the right training regime, the right foci, and to advance steadily.

“Right,” she said, pushing away from the stone. “Time to fix that.”

Starpuff floated into view, lazy and smug, a slow loop of green light circling her shoulder. It hummed once, which she decided to interpret as agreement.

“Don’t look so pleased,” she said, squinting at it. “You’re part of this plan too.”

The puff spun a half-circle and buzzed, pretending innocence.

“Exactly,” Elynara muttered.

She raised her staff and stretched the stiffness out of her arm. The air was clear and sharp, thin clouds scattering above the distant hills. A faint line of pale trees marked where the road dipped toward the east, and beyond them, the world thinned into flat fields where the horizon met white haze.

A long walk was still ahead. But Elynara didn’t really mind.

The list came to her naturally as she started forward, her voice keeping time with her boots.

“First– bring water magic up to par.”

Starpuff bobbed once in front of her.

“Second– finally practice Mana Sense properly.”

Another bob.

“Third– design that body-warming spell. I’m tired of pretending to enjoy freezing nights.”

The puff tilted in agreement.

“And last–” she paused, grinning to herself, “invent Life Magic. Or re-invent?”

It trilled uncertainly, and she could almost hear the judgment in the tone.

“Don’t start,” she said. “It doesn’t have to be grand. Just... practical. Comfort, healing, small repairs. Little miracles worth having! And the warming spell could be the beginning!”

She tapped her staff lightly against the dirt as she walked.

“If the old world had its share of impossible magic, maybe the new one can afford something useful. A spell to ease cold hands, to clean water, to make a sick friend breathe easier.”

The road curved gently downhill, sunlight catching in the fields below. Her shadow stretched long beside her, the steady rhythm of her steps blending with the wind’s slow hum.

“...After all, even a small warmth helps when all else fails,” she murmured.

Starpuff tilted again, quieter now. Its faint glow brushed her cheek before it drifted ahead, weaving lazy circles that left thin trails of light in her mana vision.

She tried to match the motion, reaching outward through her senses. The invisible threads of energy responded, faint but clear – the usual whisper of life flowing through soil and air. She steadied her breath and focused.

The ground felt different this far east: softer, older, with a subtle drag, as if mana here moved through thicker water.

Her sight flickered at the edges. Then steadied.

“Better,” she said under her breath. “Finally getting easier.”

The puff chirped approval, only to swoop down and peck at her hair.

“Hey!”

It darted away, buzzing gleefully, and she had to laugh.

“Fine, fine. Training break.”

Elynara let her arm fall and watched it loop overhead, its glow warm and erratic. Then she shaped some loops and mana trails for it to follow and fly through. To its obvious delight.

The air smelled faintly of damp soil, and far in the distance a few lone birds crossed the horizon.

The morning was alive in its own quiet way.

Her steps quickened again once the puff lost interest and floated back to her side, no mana constructs left in Starpuff’s wake.

The road ahead ran straight and pale under the sun. It would carry her through open plain before turning toward the far ridges – the path Alicia had traced on the map.

A good stretch for thinking, and for planning.

Fire she had mastered through instinct, and she was close to even greater heights in it. Water would take patience. Mana Sense would take precision. Mana Manipulation was improving naturally the more she practiced. And that new spell – something that layered warmth without burning – would take time to understand.

She smiled faintly.

The time she had on the road.

The wind picked up, tugging at her cloak. The faint hum of her mana flared in answer, instinctively reinforcing the heat she still held from the morning cycle. It worked better now – her control over the body’s flow steadier, more natural.

It wasn’t perfection, but it was progress.

“See?” she said to Starpuff. “We’re already improving.”

The puff trilled, drifting upside down before righting itself again.

Elynara watched it, amused.

Then her gaze lifted once more toward the east. The faint white haze that marked the Deadzone shimmered there, a distant boundary between what lived and what lingered. Even from here, it felt immense stretching across the horizon.

She inhaled, let the breath settle deep, and squared her shoulders.

“Alright,” she said, voice low but firm. “We’ll reach the edge, finish the map, and learn something worth bringing back.”

The words felt right.

The puff looped around her staff once, humming bright and steady.

Elynara’s grin returned.

“Good. Then let’s move.”

The mile-stone faded behind them as they walked, the air warming with the climb of the sun. Ahead, the plains stretched quiet and pale, waiting.

A new list, a clear sky, and miles of road to practice on. Enough to keep her busy – and enough to make her laugh softly when the puff tried to steal the warming mana spell from within her hood again.

“Fine, you rascal!” she said. “You can be in charge of morale. I’ll handle the magic.”

And with that compromise, the road carried them east, toward the horizon where the world began to thin.

Elynara stopped at the ridge while the sun still held the sky. The land below stretched pale and uneven, streaked with thin grass that bowed beneath the wind. It was far too open to be comfortable, but she liked the view.

It gave her distance enough to think.

She picked a spot between two stones and set her pack down. The warmth in the air had already faded, the wind carrying the kind of chill that slipped through sleeves and refused to leave.

She rubbed her hands together, then decided there was no reason to suffer through it.

“Alright,” she told the puff hovering near her ear, “Another test run.”

Starpuff trilled, skeptical but curious.

Elynara drew in a slow breath, letting her mana flow steady and light beneath her skin. She pictured the motion – threads sliding through the body’s channels, weaving into a thin net of warmth. The first attempt flickered bright and vanished before it could spread.

She frowned.

“Too shallow.”

The second took hold. Gentle heat pulsed through her arms and settled just over the surface of her skin. The chill in her shoulders eased. A quiet smile tugged at her lips.

“Better.”

The puff buzzed once, approving, and circled her head before dropping to nudge her cheek.

She leaned back, satisfied.

“You see? We’re improving.”

The ridge caught the last light, and she built her fire with practiced ease.

Well, not quite ease, but it was certainly much better than her first ever try!

And Marcus has given her some tips on how to camp better too. He might have had a bit of a mean streak, but the man was very helpful and eager to teach.

It was respectable.

And as smoke curled into the air, carrying the sharp scent of dry grass, she changed her opinion: it was very respectable!

Her dinner was simple: smoked meat, a heel of bread, and conjured water drawn clean through her mana. She shaped the liquid between her palms, pulling it into a smooth spiral.

It wobbled, broke apart, and splashed cold across her lap.

Elynara blinked, then laughed under her breath.

“Very graceful.”

Starpuff hummed with what she decided was unhelpful amusement.

She gathered the water again, slower this time. The spiral formed tighter, steadier, until it hung for three heartbeats before she let it collapse into mist. Her smile lingered even as her trousers soaked through.

Progress was progress.

The fire burned lower as she worked. Each attempt left the motion a little smoother, the mana a little clearer. She didn’t need the System to tell her she was improving; her body knew. Her focus had grown cleaner, her rhythm stronger.

And when the wind shifted and the chill crept close again, she refreshed the comfort spell. The warmth answered more easily this time, spreading without much effort. It felt different from her other work – less like control, more like listening. The magic wanted to be gentle, and she let it.

By the time she leaned back against her pack, the fire had dwindled to red coals. Clouds still covered most of the sky, thick and low.

Their edges glowed faintly where the hidden moons pressed light through.

Elynara pulled her cloak around her and watched the thin breaks forming in the gray. A small comfort, knowing even the sky eventually cleared.

Starpuff had taken to floating slow circles above the coals, the reflected glow painting its small form in shifting orange. Every turn left faint trails in her mana sight – loops and curls that seemed very deliberate now, no longer random. And far too smooth for what Starpuff usually displayed

The puff dipped and wobbled, pretending innocence.

She laughed quietly and stretched her legs, boots tapping against the packed earth.

When the first gap opened fully, the light of the nearest moon poured through – bright and silver, rounding the world in soft edges. The second followed, its faint violet hue turning the ridge to shadow and glass. And finally, far above, the broken one appeared, its scattered fragments glimmering in dusted arcs.

She forgot the cold for a moment. The sight filled her chest with quiet awe.

The third moon had fascinated and unsettled her a little from that very first night – its shape fractured, its orbit uneven – but there was a strange beauty in it tonight. The shards caught the starlight, holding together through nothing but motion and memory.

She lay back and let her gaze travel across the sky.

“You’re still hanging on, huh?” she whispered. “Even shattered.”

The words carried farther than she meant, slipping into the empty air.

Starpuff landed beside her shoulder and pulsed faintly green. It didn’t hum this time, only drifted close enough that she could feel the warmth of its glow.

She smiled.

“Maybe there is something to learn from that.”

Her fingers traced idle circles against the dirt. She thought of the world she had walked these past weeks – the villages with half-living fields, the forests fading at their roots, the glass plains ahead that swallowed light, mana, and breath. The moons above had endured worse and still found ways to shine.

If they could, so could she.

The wind pressed across the ridge, tugging lightly at her braid. The warmth spell pulsed steady beneath her skin, stronger now, anchored by thought and repetition. She tightened the cloak around her neck and let her eyes close for a moment.

The night held a fragile kind of peace – the sort that only existed between exhaustion and purpose.

And when she opened her eyes again, Starpuff had drifted higher, tracing wide loops against the violet light. Its glow left faint echoes in the air, small and bright, marking rhythm above the dying fire.

“Show-off,” she whispered, smiling.

The puff twirled once and dove back toward her, settling at the crook of her arm. Its hum returned, low and content.

Elynara tilted her head toward the moons one last time. Their light mixed across the clouds – silver, violet, and dusted white. She thought of their broken companion and the way even ruin could still catch light if it moved just right.

The fire’s last ember cracked softly. The warmth through her cloak held.

She breathed out and let the quiet settle. Tomorrow would bring the plains, and beyond them, the edge of everything. For now, the night was enough.

And under three moons, both whole and broken, she slept.

The road stretched thin under her boots, a ribbon of pale dust winding through open plain. Morning light rolled off the grass in dull waves, fading quick into dry soil where the wind never rested.

Elynara kept mostly to the open ground. The forested edges that once softened the horizon had fallen back into gray silhouettes, their leaves curled and weary. The world here looked drained, caught between seasons that had forgotten how to change.

She still marked her map with care. Every ruin of past villages or lonely mound of stone earned a note in neat handwriting: Collapsed wall. Broken well. No mana flow. The list grew shorter with each page.

There was less to name, and less still possible to fix.

Starpuff, on the other hand, thrived in the emptiness.

The puff zipped ahead and back again, trying to steal the strap from her satchel or dive-bombing her hood until she flinched.

When that failed to amuse, it floated proudly above her staff, turning lazy circles like a lantern too pleased with its own glow.

She stopped pretending to scold.

“Fine,” she told it one afternoon, brushing dust from her sleeve. “At least one of us has energy.”

It chirped triumphantly and zipped a victorious loop around her head.

The silence never pressed long when she worked. Training filled the hours and kept her focus steady. Water magic, once clumsy and slow, finally began to obey her call. She could draw it into a thin ribbon, bright and trembling, and hold it whole for as long as she wanted without it falling apart in her palms again.

Each attempt felt smoother, her control surer.

Her Mana Sense had widened too, as she took to the training from way back in the forest.

Threads of power pulsed beneath her steps – faint, nearly lost, but still there. When she closed her eyes and reached down with her awareness, she could sense the tiny lights of buried mana clumps flicker against the dark soil. Each one felt fragile, but alive.

Proof that the world, even here, hadn’t stopped breathing yet.

The discovery never failed to pull a smile out of her.

By midday the sun burned white above the haze, and she used her new favorite spell. The warmth spread through her cloak, slow and steady, until the wind’s bite dulled. It wasn’t perfect, but it wrapped her shoulders with gentle heat.

“Life Magic, Elynara edition,” she muttered, fastening her hood against the breeze.

Starpuff tilted its glow in mock solemnity.

“Yes, yes, laugh it up,” she said. “I’m sure this will save the world one cozy shoulder at a time.”

Her grin lingered as she walked. The magic hummed soft under her skin, her breathing even and light.

The road curved through empty fields that once must have been farms – she could still see the faint outlines of tilled furrows now filled with weeds. A broken plow half-buried in dirt marked one boundary. A few rusted nails glinted in the dust, all that remained of fences long gone.

Elynara didn’t stop.

She had already learned that looking too long at the remnants could make her heart heavy, and she needed her mind clear. The best way to honor what was lost was to move forward with eyes open and hands ready to mend what could still be saved.

Looking ahead, and planning for the future was the best way forward.

By the fourth day, the land grew flatter. The horizon pressed closer, pale and thin. Her sense of mana trembled each time she cast, tugging against her control. She welcomed the challenge.

In the afternoons she practiced her balance spells, coaxing faint patterns from her staff to keep her focus sharp. By evening she tried again with water, shaping smooth arcs that cut clean through the air before dissolving into mist.

When the last bit of light faded, she would breathe warmth back into her cloak, a slow rhythm that felt almost meditative now.

The nights were colder but calmer, her mana flow steadier than ever before.

Her only detour came near the end of that stretch – a village so small she would have almost missed it if not for Alicia’s reminder.

It was just a few houses huddled beside a shallow stream, smoke curling weakly above them. Goats grazed between dry reeds, watched by a child perched on a broken fence.

The people there looked surprised to see her, then quietly relieved. They asked for help with small things – a fence mended, traps cleared near the woodline, a leak patched in their storehouse roof, and a patrol sweep for a few stragglers.

Elynara did each gladly, losing herself in the rhythm of work.

The traps yielded three mana-starved vermin, their eyes dim and glassy. Elynara cleared them quickly, and set new ones up.

A Bonefang had wandered in from somewhere, its eyes finding her before she even noticed it there. Those monsters really were too keenly drawn to mana.

Not that it helped it any. It would be rations for the village now.

The villagers offered her some food in thanks – soft cheese, smoked roots, and a small jar of sweet syrup that tasted faintly of clover. They laughed at a few stories she recounted of her adventures so far.

It felt good to hear laughter again. Honest laughter.

She spent that evening mapping by the stream, the paper spread across her knees while the last of the light bent gold through the water. Her lines traced the road eastward, the path thinning to one long stretch that disappeared toward the horizon.

The villagers told her it would be a lonely walk from here – brittle fields, no trees, no rivers, just the last settlement far out, hidden at the edge of the forest and where the land began to crumble into dust.

“That suits me,” she said. “It means I will have more time to practice.”

They didn’t argue. They only wished her luck.

The next morning, she left before dawn. The warming spell hummed faintly beneath her cloak, chasing the chill from her arms. The stream glimmered silver behind her while the plain ahead brightened to pale gold.

Starpuff fluttered beside her, singing small hums that caught the wind.

Her steps felt lighter than they had in weeks. Every spell came smoother, every motion truer. The world might have been thinning, but her focus only sharpened against it. For every patch of silence she crossed, her mana thrummed stronger, clearer.

It wasn’t balance yet. But it was close.

By the fifth evening since the milestone marker, the road fell away beneath her feet and the world ended.

Elynara stood at the crest of a low ridge, breath slowing as the view opened before her.

Even Starpuff, the forever-happy puff of joy and mischief… was now hiding in her coat, shivering.

The plain beyond was colorless, with absolutely nothing but glints of glass to break the monotony.

There were no fields, no brush, no wind stirring through grass. No grass.

Just a vast sheet of pale-white dust and glass, stretching from one horizon to the next. The light of the setting sun skimmed across it, bright enough to sting her eyes.

She lifted a hand to shield them and stared until the shock settled into silence.

Nothing moved. Even the air seemed hollow. The wind came across the ridge, reached the border of the white expanse, and stopped – as if the world itself had forgotten how to breathe at the edges of this… thing.

Deadzone. A truly fitting moniker. One she have never quite believed would be so bad.

Not even the worst disasters her old memories hinted at looked like this.

She patted her shivering companion, feeding it some mana.

And once it calmed down, Elynara walked down until the last strip of living soil crumbled into the glassy plain.

Her boots scuffed to a stop at the line where color ended. The surface beyond looked smooth but uneven, fused and rippled by heat long spent. She crouched and brushed her fingertips along the edge. The texture bit coldly through her glove.

Stealing away heat… and pulling at her mana.

She exhaled, reached through her staff, and let a thread of mana drift forward. It vanished.

Just vanished.

The loss hit quick and sharp, like a door closing on her senses. She frowned and tried again, slower. The energy unraveled the instant it crossed the threshold, dissipating into the air without echo or trace.

“The mana, it is completely…” she murmured. “Gone.”

The sound of her voice died as soon as it left her lips.

No echo, no carry. Just dead quiet.

She drew her hand back, rubbing her palm where the flow had broken. Even the faint warmth of her own magic faltered for a breath before returning. The ground ahead did not just lack mana.

It consumed it.

She rose and studied the boundary. It wasn’t sharp – more a gentle fade, a slow surrender of life. The soil at her feet still held the faint hum of the world, but a step forward and that hum fell away to nothing.

Starpuff hovered beside her shoulder, its usual glow dulled to a pale green. It didn’t sing or dart ahead. It only drifted, uneasy, shifting with small nervous flicks.

“It’s alright,” she said quietly. “We won’t stay long.”

The puff turned toward her, then toward the horizon along the Deadzone again, light pulsing in uncertain rhythm.

Elynara followed its gaze.

On her left was the Deadzone, on her right the still living remnants of the world trying to eke out an existence along this wretched land.

And far in the distance, the plain darkened – a faint smudge breaking the uniform white. The line was uneven, jagged at its top. Forest, or what remained of one.

“The ruins should be near there. Past the village.” Her words sounded too small in the still air.

She rolled her shoulders, forcing the weight of quiet from them.

The world might have been broken, but it was still the world, and she had work to do!

Drawing a measured breath, she spread her senses again, testing the edge. The mana here didn’t resist. It didn’t fight her. It simply vanished without response. It was the first time she had felt the world fail to answer her entirely.

The loss left a hollow under her ribs, disbelief spreading through her body.

What could have done this? What could have carved out a piece of the world so completely that even air forgot how to carry sound?

The thought made her chest tighten, but her curiosity burned sharper.

Elynara planted her staff at the border, marking it with a small scratch of wood against glass. “Boundary,” she said under her breath, writing it into memory. “Zero flow. Consumption effect consistent.”

Starpuff floated lower, brushing the mark with its faint light before retreating again.

She knelt and touched the ground once more. The surface reflected the dying sun in thin bands of gold, fractured by tiny imperfections. Beneath the top layer, the glass turned milky, holding the faint shimmer of frozen motion. For all its stillness, the plain looked caught in the middle of something that had never finished ending.

When she stood again, her reflection wavered faintly at her feet—blurred by dust but recognizable.

That small echo steadied her.

“Terrifying,” she admitted softly, “but beautiful.”

The puff chirped once, low and unsure.

She smiled.

“I’ll take that as agreement.”

The wind picked up behind her, tugging at her braid before dying at the boundary. It felt like crossing into a held breath that would never release.

Elynara tapped her staff lightly against the stone again, grounding herself in the sound.

“Alright. No stepping forward until I understand this.”

Her curiosity itched, but caution had kept her alive this long. She reached instead for her notebook, flipped it open, and began sketching quick lines – the ridge, the slope, the white plain stretching flat, and that faint dark smear of forest far ahead.

She wrote beneath it:

The world’s skin, peeled and glassed. Mana devoured at touch.

Her handwriting wavered once before she caught it steady. The weight of the moment pressed down again. The awareness that she was standing at the edge of something unspeakably atrocious.

When she closed the book, the sun had slipped low enough to turn the glass gold.

The reflection reached out in ripples that shimmered faintly across the plain. She couldn’t decide if it looked alive or haunted, but either way, it drew her forward.

“We will continue tomorrow,” she said. “We go closer in daylight.”

The puff trilled, a weak but determined sound.

She smiled and turned back up the ridge. The warmth spell spread through her cloak again, faint but steady, fighting off the creeping chill.

Behind her, the glass plain shone under the sinking sun – silent, endless, and waiting.

Whatever had burned this place hollow still left its mark, and she meant to find what it was. Even if the answer would take her a long time to find.

Perhaps knowing would help her fix it, eventually.

But for now, that quiet promise was enough.

She had other things to work on yet, ones she could actually do something about.

Elynara glanced once more toward the distant line of forest, where the edge of the world met what little remained of life.

“Ruins or not,” she whispered, “I’ll see it through.”

Starpuff glowed faintly in agreement, its light small against the coming dark.

By late afternoon the next day, the horizon had stopped pretending to change. The white expanse to the side swallowed everything – the hills, the faint treelines, even the color of the sky. Elynara walked its side until her steps slowed without thinking, her eyes drawn to where the world broke apart.

At first, the Deadzone was only a blur against the light. But every mile made it larger, more absolute. The land no longer softened into curves or shadow. It simply ended, clean and cruel, a sea of pale dust that drank the sun instead of reflecting it.

The scale of it made her chest tighten.

Behind her stretched the last threads of living land, yellowing grass and scattered shrubs bowing under the wind. Ahead was stillness. No movement. Or depth.

Her mana sense fluttered and faltered. The air itself felt drained – emptier with each step. It wasn’t just weaker. It was unsteady, trembling near collapse. The energy in her own body pulled against the loss, strained toward something that wasn’t there.

When she got close and tried to shape a spark, the resistance hit instantly. The flow thinned, broke, and slipped away before her hand could finish the motion.

“Rude,” she muttered, shaking her fingers.

She tried again, stubborn, forcing the rhythm of her usual cycle. It worked this time, though the spark hung fragile and pale before flickering out. Her grin returned all the same.

“Still works,” she said.

The effort left a faint ache under her ribs. She ignored it. If the world wanted to test her, she would answer.

Elynara continued ahead, Starpuff far too quiet this stretch of the journey.

But she understood. The place affected her too.

Why did the villagers choose to settle here, she didn’t know.

But she might have come here all the same, regardless.

Just to see this haunting sight.

And perhaps try to fix it.

Then… A thought struck her mid-step – quiet, almost amused. She had spent weeks worrying about conserving mana, about it disappearing from the world, and yet herself ‘wasting’ it on frivolities and training.

Counting every cast like it was some rare coin to be hoarded. And here the world itself was bleeding it away every moment, pouring it into the Deadzone without pause or thought.

In amounts she would have no hope of matching. Every minute. Every day.

She snorted and shook her head.

“Guess I’ll stop feeling guilty about a few fire tricks, huh?”

Starpuff trilled, looping around her head in quick, approving circles before landing on her shoulder. Its glow had dimmed to a faint green hue in the washed-out light, but its hum still carried warmth.

“Glad you agree,” Elynara said, giving it a light tap.

The road turned to hard clay under her boots.

A few scraggly stalks of grass clung between cracks, then even those vanished. The path tilted slightly downward before flattening again, and ahead she spotted roofs – thin and gray against the pale backdrop.

Elynara slowed her steps, following the narrow dirt track that wound through scrub and broken roots. The map Alicia had drawn still matched. The final mark. The last place before the ruins.

She had reached the last village.

It was hardly a village at all. Six cottages, maybe seven, pressed close to the line of a dying forest. The trees behind them stood thin and hollowed, bark splitting under years of wind. The smell of smoke drifted from a single chimney, sharp and faint. No sound carried except the scrape of a shovel and the call of one distant crow.

Starpuff hovered above her shoulder, its glow dim in the pale air.

Even it seemed quiet, turning slow circles as they approached.

The people looked up when she entered the edge of the clearing. Forest folk, mostly – slight builds, faint points to their fur-covered ears, their eyes shadowed by fatigue. A few humans moved among them, wordless but steady in their work. When she raised a hand in greeting, none ran or frowned. They only watched, careful, waiting.

“I’m Elynara, on Guild’s business.” she said, holding up the guild badge. “Sent from Oltenar.”

Relief flickered across their faces, quickly tempered.

One of them, a tall woman with dark hair streaked gray, set down the bundle she carried and stepped forward.

“Then you’ve come a long way,” she said. “We weren’t sure if anyone still took requests from this far out.”

“Requests travel slower than footsteps,” Elynara replied, smiling faintly. “But I’m here.”

The woman’s mouth twitched at that.

“You’ll want to speak with the elder. He’s by the well.”

The elder turned out to be older than she expected, his shoulders bowed deeply and his frame frail, but his eyes remained sharp. When she explained why she had come, his gaze drifted toward the east.

“The ruins,” he said quietly. “You’ll find them, yes. They’re waiting, undisturbed. We didn’t bother to poke in.”

He said nothing more about them, and she didn’t press.

They offered food, and a place to rest – a shed near the fence line, half-covered by creeping moss. Its roof leaned badly to one side, but the hay inside was clean and dry.

Elynara ran a hand through it, nodded, and spread her cloak. The warmth spell still hummed beneath the fabric, enough to make the small space feel almost comfortable.

When she stepped outside again, the quiet struck her more sharply. No laughter. No children. Even the livestock chewed without sound.

And by evening, she had plenty of errands to attend to.

A snapped fence near the goat pens came first. She reset the post with a bit of help from little fire explosions – who knew setting a stone on top of a fence post and then blasting it from above would drive the wood into the earth so easily?

Another useful spell application, achieved!

Finally, the lumber for the storage hut. The villagers worked beside her, each task performed without talk but with quiet gratitude.

By dusk her arms ached, but it was a good ache that came from motion and small purposes achieved.

Those tasks, they were hardly even requests. But as Elynara had come to understand, work was the best currency in this world.

A helpful hand more useful than shiny trinkets ever could be.

And a common courtesy to help in exchange for food and shelter.

It felt fair. And fitting.

Elynara quite liked that.

When she asked about spirits, though, that quiet deepened. The elder stopped his slow repair of a rope and looked to the horizon again.

“We hoped you’d ask,” he said. “We hoped you’d find one. For we failed to do so for years.”

“How long has it been since you settled here?”

“Will be three years now. Never heard so much as a whisper.”

Elynara closed her eyes, and pressed her palms to the soil to ground herself. Then she reached inward, her senses expanding as far as she could make them.

Her mana spread through the village in soft waves, searching for a thread, a flicker, any pulse of spirit’s presence. But nothing answered her. Not even the faintest of hums.

She moved through the fields, touching stones, tree roots, the edge of the fence. Still nothing.

The air itself felt hollow, stretched thin, carrying no rhythm of its own.

By the time the sky turned red, she knew what she would tell them.

When she returned, the villagers didn’t ask what she had found. They already knew. The elder met her gaze and nodded once, in quiet disappointment. He understood, yet hope still lingered, against all reason.

“Thank you for looking,” he said.

“I’ll keep trying tomorrow,” she offered. “There’s still a chance near the woods, or in the forest itself.”

He smiled faintly.

“Then may the Goddess favor your stubbornness.”

The fire they lit that night was small, fed by brittle wood that smoked more than it burned. Elynara sat close, her staff across her knees. Starpuff hovered near the flame, turning slow loops. Its reflection shimmered faintly in the coals.

A few of the villagers shared stew from a single pot – it was thin but warm. The taste was earthy, touched with salt. It filled her chest with comfort more than her stomach with food.

When the first stars appeared, one of the hunters came to stand across the fire. Forest-born, tall, his movements measured.

His eyes carried that hardy far-off look she had noticed in many of those who spent their lives at the frontier villages, at the edges of things.

“I was the one who sent word to the guild,” he said. “About the ruins.”

Elynara looked up, interested.

“You’ve seen them then?”

“From afar,” he said. His gaze slid toward the horizon, where the last light still caught on the pale expanse.

“Close enough to make sure, but not to scout. I didn’t stay long. Too many things out there that might end the unprepared. Poking my nose where it does not belong is one such thing.”

She studied the tension in his stance – the way his hands never quite relaxed.

“You’ve lived with that view for years, haven’t you?”

He gave a short laugh, dry but not unkind.

“You’ll understand soon enough. The world doesn’t end out there. But it feels like it wants to.”

Elynara turned her eyes eastward. The faint glow of the Deadzone reached even here, bleeding into the night. She felt the pull of it under her skin, the whisper of mana bending wrong, thinning and disappearing.

“I’ll find out what’s waiting in there,” she said quietly.

The hunter nodded.

“Then I hope you return to tell us.”

When he left, only the fire remained. Elynara fed it a final twig and leaned back, letting the warmth seep into her cloak. Starpuff floated to her shoulder and settled there, humming faintly against her neck.

The air smelled of ash and smoke. The village slept behind her, fragile but enduring. Ahead, the world waited – shattered and silent, yet still full of mystery.

She smiled, small but certain.

“Tomorrow,” she said. “We go.”

The puff glowed once in answer, soft and steady.

And in that glow, the night no longer felt entirely still.
