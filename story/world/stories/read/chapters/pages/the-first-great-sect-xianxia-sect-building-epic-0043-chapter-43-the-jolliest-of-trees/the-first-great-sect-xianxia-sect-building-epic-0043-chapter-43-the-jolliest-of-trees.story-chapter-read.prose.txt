Her cousin dropped his flute, looking where Hua still pointed. It was distant, and Hua could only barely see it herself.

“Someone call the healer,” Weiji barked, rising to his feet. He gestured at Captain Yao. “Escort them to the Jolly Ranger. If he’s a Cultivator, he might still be alive.”

“Surely not,” said the mortal with the many lesions, his voice breaking as Hua’s soldiers set off.

They had risen in their shock and turned their backs on Hua. Another mortal, this a woman of some youth, took his arm and held it close. “The Rangers can’t die. They won’t. Heroes never die,” she said steady as the earth right before it was torn asunder.

Heroes are simply martyrs before they have died. But for this mortal, a wandering band of Cultivators was the closest he would get to witnessing a legend. Well, Hua as well, though she was at the start of her legend. The great story that would end only when the palaces of heaven were broken and the heads of every god were impaled upon the peaks of Kunlun Shen.

“Cultivators can survive grievous wounds,” Weiji reassured the horrified mortals which seemed a waste of breath.

“I have seen them endure what would kill us a dozen times over,” Liu Xin added.

For once, Weiji did not resume his ongoing battle with her spy. He simply nodded. “Our healer has performed miracles. If your Ranger’s heart still beats, this Liao Weiji promises he will be seen to with all care we can afford.”

Hua rose from her bench. If there was going to be such a commotion, she could stand to see it firsthand. Weiji fell in step with her as she made her way to the distant tree.

“You’re making promises on this Lord’s behalf,” she said calmly.

“It is Liao that stands sentinel and Liao that is guardian over these lands. We hold a responsibility to those who call you liege lord.”

“That isn’t our goal. We have a greater responsibility to peace and stability.”

His brows furrowed. “You’re making a distinction without a difference.”

“Am I?”

“Yes.”

The Jolly Ranger had been impaled with arms outstretched, pointing towards the sky. Through the wrists, another spike impaling him through the torso, and a final one through his feet. Soldiers were sawing through the wooden spike keeping the Ranger bound to the tree whilst the healer was checking the torso wound.

“How long did you see him?” Weiji asked her lowly. “Even I can see him now. With your eyes, you would have seen him two li ago.”

“Don’t make assumptions. I wasn’t paying attention to everything ahead of us, not when our scouts were there to do that for me. It was just a patch of orange.” It was autumn and they were near wheat fields. Shades of yellow, brown, and orange were far too common. “If I had seen him, don’t you think I would have had a reason to approach, if only for my own amusement?”

What better way to indulge her need for entertainment than an impaled man? There was no reason for her not to approach such an intriguing sight. He still watched her carefully and she became aware that others were observing them even if they could not hear the words they spoke.

They had managed to bring the Jolly Ranger down by the time Hua had reached the group. The Ranger’s blood had stained the tree’s bark with three sunsets, one below the other. One for each point of impalement. Young Ren the healer had two assistants aiding him as he worked on the Ranger.

The wooden spike was still in the Ranger’s body. Young Ren had pressed a mass of pungent herbs around the wound, a paste that Hua was familiar with from the healing pavilion in the clan compound. Her collarbone ached.

In the present, far from the memory of pain, she watched as one assistant held the Ranger’s arm out whilst the other was cutting through an arrow struck through the Ranger’s shoulder, attempting to remove the arrow’s fletching. They were planning on pushing it through and out the other side.

“What is the likelihood of survival?”

Young Ren was quiet, focused on the Ranger. Hua gave him the time he needed to think the question through as he worked to stitch the wounds through the Ranger’s wrists. Unlike Old Ren, he hadn’t yet worked up the confidence to curse out her existence, only to ignore her whenever he pleased. He got away with it by being a decent healer and a passable Alchemist.

“Decently high,” he said after ten minutes had passed. Relieved gasps rang out behind her. The mortals had followed them. “These wounds weren’t meant to kill her—the arrow in the shoulder is the most lethal one. Dehydration or starvation was a more likely cause of death. But look, up in the tree.”

A bucket of some kind had been carved and hung from a branch. They would have had to crane their neck to taste the sweet relief of water and when they didn’t, the steady drip, drip, drip on their head would be torture.

“Whoever did this wanted the Ranger to die slow. It’s a torture method.”

“Kang. This is Kang’s work.” The mortal spat the name like a curse. Maybe it was one. “This is his brutality. He’s a monster.”

Hua thought him efficient in his brutality. A smart person would turn around and avoid the chance of this happening to them. And for the mortals who hired the Rangers, they would know how futile a prospect seeking aid would be. Hua leaned towards the local Justice’s letters being intercepted rather than corruption. Someone this brutal, who could train and organise a dispatched force of bandits without direct leadership, that could lay still for hours, waiting for any passers-by to ambush, and who fought with incredible discipline against a pair of Cultivators, was a man who could easily organise a means to identify and kill any messengers sent to Liaojiangkou for aid.

Hua's fear of a boring trip was rapidly vanishing.

Surely, Kang could have used any of the other Rangers, she thought, staring at bloody depressions left in the tree trunk. So why make this one a centerpiece of your torture? What did they do to irritate you?

Weiji knelt down and displaced one of the struggling assistants. He was strong enough to push the arrow through easily. The Ranger’s flush deepened, and they moaned in their unconscious haze. Hua felt a deep discomfort in her gut hearing that sound. After the wound was cleaned and bandaged, Weiji laid his hand atop the Ranger’s stomach. Weiji’s Qi washed over Ranger who flinched in his sleep.

“They’re at the second stage of Qi Condensation.”

Hua’s attention heightened, focusing her golden gaze on the Ranger. That was a notable level of power. “Can you tell what method or Scripture?”

“I can’t work miracles. Something physical, I think. Either way, they were powerful, and they were left like this.”

“We’ll have the perimeter guard doubled for now. Captain Yao, see to it.” He nodded grimly. The Ranger was more powerful than he. Neither he nor his men would stand any chance against whoever did this. “We slaughtered three, maybe four times our number even from a disadvantaged position. We’ll make anyone who attacks our camp bleed for their temerity.”

“As my lord says.”

“This lord will venture out and secure our northern approach. Liu Xin, attend me. Mortals, this lord will speak to you later. Do not attempt to leave the area without permission. Cousin, you and the imperial scribe can question these mortals. I want a full accounting of this area’s history.”

Liu Xin proved himself a decent rider and a filthy liar who was not at all afraid of horses.

The scouts tended to only roam five li ahead. Hua could see that far. They took to finding a high vantage, one of the many hills along the undulating landscape. They left their horses behind to graze and made their way up the hill. Out west, in the distance, Hua could see the shadow of mountains that marked the furthest extent of Liao territory. Before them was the endless sea of amber scarred by fire and a world that betrayed them.

“What is your opinion of this situation? You are the closest I have to a mortal adviser.”

“Difficult to understand fully. Bits and pieces of a story, but I feel I am missing too much of it to make an accurate assessment. A corrupt Justice isn’t unheard of. Bandit armies are unfortunately common, especially in the northern provinces. But both, at once? So close to a Cultivation Clan? From what I gleaned speaking to the villagers, this has been going on for years with varying intensity.”

“Years and they never once made the journey to Liaojiangkou. Resources arrived from it, so if they were truly so desperate, why not hitch a ride on a barge? Mortals confuse me. This willingness to just lie down and die bewilders me.”

“Do you intend to help these people?”

“Perhaps. Perhaps not. It depends on how greatly this story of mortals continues to intrigue me.”

“Not Kang?”

“Powerful. Ruthless. Conniving. Those are descriptors I would expect from a bandit. He’s interesting as far as the threat he poses is concerned. No, it is the mortals that intrigue me.”

“I never thought I would witness the day you found mortals intriguing.

“Where exactly are they getting their supplies from? If they’ve been harried for months by a bandit group and they’ve been journeying away from their farmlands, how exactly are they sustaining themselves?”

“They could have taken their winter harvests before fleeing.”

“Did they look like they ate well? Off-colour skin. Bones you could see through their clothes. No, they didn’t have much food with them, and if they did, they left it behind with the convoy? Which suggests they’re the ones in the best condition, most able to make the trip. Do we think they all took months worth of food or is it more likely they had a way to resupply?”

“They could be living off the land. Hunters could find game meat. This was a bountiful summer. I know because meat was cheap. Beyond that, they could forage fruits and vegetables, even steal some crops. There are ways to sustain yourself in the wilderness.”

“Maybe. Perhaps. But I don’t find myself believing that so easily. Or perhaps I just don’t want to believe in so boring a reality.”

She turned south. If she squinted and focused, she could make out a twinkle that might have been the Liao River or it might have been a heat haze. Be it falsehood or truth, the thought of those banks she had grown up beside still filled her with calm. In winter, the placid river sometimes froze over and they would skate along the surface. Sometimes, during long summer evenings when the air had cooled and the muggy air didn’t feel impossible to breathe, she would dance along the banks with Qing. Feel her warmth and know something like peace.

Hua sat and patted the grass. It was mildly damp but compared to the rainfall and mud of the earlier days, that was nothing.

“Sit with me.”

Liu Xin did, curiosity writ plain across his features. He was young despite the greying at his temples. Stress, she thought it was. Something that happened to mortals. He had been a spy and at war with other scholars. It gave reason for the grey hairs even as his skin was clear except for light battle scars, his face filled with the vitality of a dedicated martial fighter.

“Breathe. Just breathe as I do.”

“Why?”

“If you cannot breathe correctly, your dantian will never face outward, and you will never learn to be a Cultivator. Was that not your goal in following me?”

His eyes grew wide. “My lord, I—”

“There is no guarantee that you can learn to Cultivate. It may be that your dantian has been improperly formed or that the cultivation methods I know will not work for you. But I think you have a chance of succeeding. I’ve met… I knew someone who reached Qi Condensation alone, with no Scripture or family legacy of Cultivation. He was more talented than I and only luck determined his fate. You might be older, but your body is tempered and so, there is a chance.”

In her robes was a bundle of pills. Their true purpose was to cleanse the spiritual channels of impurities. She retrieved them and presented them to Liu Xin.

He shook his head. “You’ve paid me enough. You saved my life and gave me silver in turn. I can’t accept this.”

“You can and you will. If Kang is powerful enough to kill Cultivators, then I will hedge my bets with another.”

Under her determined gaze, he took a pill and swallowed it whole. Then she directed him to breathe with her just as her father had made her breathe as a child too young to understand why it mattered.

It was just a first step. One of many. It might take weeks or years for any effect to show. But Hua was willing to place a bet. Fortune was merely the daughter of luck and foresight.

Hua would see if she could make her own luck.
