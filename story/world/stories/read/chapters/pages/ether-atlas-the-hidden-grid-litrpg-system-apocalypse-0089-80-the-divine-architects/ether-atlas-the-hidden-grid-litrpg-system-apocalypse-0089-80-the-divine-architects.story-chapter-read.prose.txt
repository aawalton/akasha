Chapter 80

“An uncontrolled deluge often floods the irrigation channels. The Grid is the mechanism. The nodes are both condensers and distributors.” — Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

“I don’t want to, because it’s exactly the kind of leap I’d have failed a student for.” Steve set the pencil down for good. “But you once told me everything wants to ring at a certain frequency. I think your example was a bridge.”

“Yes. Resonance,” Arthur said. “Same principle where an opera singer can shatter a wine glass by holding the internal frequency of the glass. The frequency keeps building on itself while she holds the tone, and eventually the glass will shatter.”

“Resonance.” Steve nodded, slow. “Now give me a number that lines up with nothing. That never falls into step, never doubles back, never lets anything build. Has no resonance.”

“A conduit. The monuments. The Grid.” Arthur straightened. “Archie, are the nodes conduits for Ether?”

“The nodes are cut for tolerance. The proportion is selected. It is not incidental. Your theory on Resonance is sound.”

“So Phi is the ratio you follow to build things that will not resonate. No force can pile up, because no two parts of the structure are ever quite in tune with each other,” Arthur mused.

“And the only force that needed grounding today is the Ether,” Steve added.

“Correct. The nodes are a grounding mechanism. Ether enters and is channelled into the Grid, which distributes it across the surface of the world at an efficiency your engineering will not match for some millennia. If your world survives, that is.”

The facets turned, slowly.

“Because the Ether does not pool, it does not mutate the node that carries it. A node built on Phi remains a node. This proportion is what lets it carry Ether without corrupting. You have, as you put it, deduced this.”

A longer pause.

“It is, on the whole, a reasonable inference. Given the cranial capacity involved, I would call it a good one.”

“And Luca Pacioli never knew any of that,” Steve said.

“Phi is far older than him. It came down to him the way everything comes down, hand to hand, generation to generation. The proportion, and the fact that it mattered. And somewhere along that line the reason for it wore away, and what was left was the word. Divine.”

He sat forward.

“He kept the rule perfectly. He lost the reason for it completely. And where the reason used to sit, he wrote God, because that is what people do with an instruction they no longer understand.”

“That’s the whole trick of it. Copy a thing carefully enough, for long enough, and the why drops out the bottom. You’re left holding the how.” He looked up at them. “And you call it holy.”

“It is an ongoing tendency of your species. Knowledge you do not understand, you kneel to instead. The pattern is reliable enough to plan around. We did,” Archie said.

Steve let out a long breath. For a moment he looked like a man who had waited twenty years to be told he was either right or to finally get rid of the question, and had just been handed neither.

“You don’t understand what this does to me,” he said.

“There’s a shelf of people. Real scholars. They took the old temples and megaliths, drew lines between and on the sites, and swore these were laid out to this exact proportion, on purpose, by people who knew the number.” He looked at the triangle. “Doxiadis. Richer. The whole field files them under cranks. I’ve stood at the front of a lecture hall and filed them there myself, out loud, more than once.”

He took a long pause.

“And I never could make myself believe them all the way down. I wanted to. I could never say so, because wanting a thing to be true is exactly how you talk yourself into seeing it.”

“A world built on a number because that proportion keeps the Grid working,” Sarah said. “I cannot even begin to imagine the amount of organisation and power it would have taken.”

“Okay, it is what it is, and we need to get a move on,” Donna said finally, breaking the quiet. “We have a triangle built to the golden proportion. Two riddles that we need to solve. And a third corner that has nothing on it.”

“And we can’t do any of that here,” Steve said. “The figure’s only a proportion until I can lay it over real ground, and I can’t do that until I know where we are.” He looked round at them all. “Which is the thing none of us has said out loud. We don’t actually know where we are.”

“Right. So we need to get out and figure that out first,” Arthur said, getting up.

“Wait. We will do it differently this time,” Donna said. “In both Marseille and Malta we walked straight up out of the ground into open air and had company inside ten minutes.” She rested the axe across her knees.

“We keep climbing out of a hole and saying hello before we’ve worked out who we’re saying hello to. After what happened in Malta, we have to be smarter.”

“You’ve got a better idea,” Arthur said.

“Get the lay of the land. We find out where we are before anyone finds out we’re here. And we decide beforehand what we’re telling people about us.” She nodded at the exit curving upwards.

“The last two times we have strolled up and announced ourselves. This time, I’d like to stroll up and tell people we got lost in a cellar. No portals, no node knowledge, no nothing until we know exactly what is happening up there.”

“Fair enough. Agreed.” Arthur nodded. The customs house was fresh in his memory.

“What do we tell them?” Sarah asked.

“As little as we can get away with,” Donna replied. “We can share what we know if we find people we can trust. Or we keep the information as a bargaining chip for later.”

She shrugged. “The rest we build once we’ve seen the ground. A story has to fit the place you tell it in, or it falls over the first time somebody leans on it.”

“We get to be Nobody,” Steve said excitedly.

“We are mostly nobody, Steve. Let’s not buy into our own hype,” Donna said.

“That’s not what I meant. In the Odyssey, Odysseus blinds the Cyclops and tells him his name is Nobody, so when the other giants ask who hurt him, the answer is ‘Nobody did.’ And when he finally gets home he doesn’t announce himself either. He walks in disguised as a beat-up Cretan and spins whoever he meets a different past, feeling out who he can trust before he picks up a bow.”

“Where do you keep all that?” Sarah asked with a smile.

While Steve tapped the side of his forehead, Donna cut in, “Cover stories. Normal people call it cover stories.”

A laugh went round the little circle of them, low and tired and real.

“We are decided, then. I’ll go and scout the surface,” Donna said. “And I’m going alone.”

Arthur opened his mouth to protest.

“You are too big and too lumbering to be innocuous, Arthur. I will not engage in anything. Just a quick look, and I’ll be back.”

“Ten minutes,” she said and vanished into the exit.

Steve waited like he always did, muttering over the ledger, redrawing the square and the swing to be sure he had it right. Happy in his thoughts.

Sarah came and sat on the dais beside Arthur, close enough that her shoulder came to rest against his. “How’s your shoulder holding up?”

“I thought you could see my Nadi-matrix and my internal state after the last breakthrough.”

“True.” Sarah smiled and said nothing else.

Donna was back in less than ten minutes. They heard unhurried steps coming down the exit, which told Arthur most of what he needed to know. She stepped out of the passage into the light with a glint in her eyes.

“Well, Sarah,” Donna said, looking at her, “I’ve got good news for you.”

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

Underground. Still don’t know where.

THE MAP WE ALREADY DREW

For twenty years I drew maps of where ancient people put their most important things, and argued about why they chose those spots. Water. Trade. High ground you could defend. A clean sightline to the solstice. All true, all real, and all of it, I now think, beside the point.

Because they weren’t choosing. Not the way I always wrote it.

If Archie is right, and the wall agrees with him, a node is a condenser. It gathers Ether and feeds it into the Grid, and the Grid spreads it across the surface of the world, all of it cut to a ratio that will not ring, so the carrying never shakes the carrier apart.

Fine. I can hold that much in my head. But here is the thing I keep walking into. Before the System there was no Ether. None. Nothing in the ground to feel, nothing in the air to dowse, no current running under anyone’s feet. So the old story I have told my whole career, that holy ground is ground people hold as sacred.

What if the reason behind this belief was engineered?

Someone who could see the finished machine, who knew exactly where each condenser had to stand for the Grid to balance when the flood finally arrived, put the marks down first.

And we built on the marks. Cathedrals built on temples, which themselves were built on older temples, which in turn stood on a buried thing nobody remembers laying. We didn’t find the sacred sites. We were handed the coordinates and told they were sacred, and we believed it.

Which means the discipline already owns the map. We have had it for two centuries. Stonehenge. The pyramids, both in Egypt and the ones across the Atlantic no one is allowed to mention in the same paper. Angkor. Teotihuacan. Göbekli Tepe, buried on purpose eleven thousand years ago by people who did not want it found and left no note saying why. I drew lines between those sites for a living and named the pattern trade, or migration, or borrowed myth.

And there is a measurement I dismissed that I owe an apology to. We have known for years that certain sites read strange. Higher background readings at the standing stones than the field beside them. Survey teams blamed the instruments and moved on, and so did I.

But if the standing stones are condensers, then of course the node reads hotter than the meadow. It is doing exactly what it was built to do, and we filed the proof under equipment fault because we had no box to put it in.

✎ I spent a career charting the pipework of an engine that had not been built to run yet, and calling it religion. We didn’t read the land and find God in it. We were given the map, told it was holy, and we never once asked who drew it, or what it would do the day the power came on.
