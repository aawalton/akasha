For the first time in a good while, sweat beaded on Vir's forehead.

Undoubtedly, he’d be asked to display his proficiency in magic. As he walked the halls of the Academy alongside the mysterious prana manipulation professor, he thought of all the ways he could fake his skills, all the tricks he could possibly come up with with the limited amount of Ash prana he had to regenerate after his display at the entrance exam.

But ultimately, anything he did would be seen through by her. And moreover, being a professor, it seemed possible that, wittingly or not, this woman acted as eyes and ears for the Fateweavers.

“Say, Professor,” Vir said, still not knowing the woman’s name. “How was it that you found me anyway? I thought you would be in attendance at the entrance exam.”

The elderly lady raised an eyebrow. “Could I not say the same for you?”

“Well,” Vir replied, looking away.

“I have attended that ceremony more times than I can remember,” she said with a small chuckle. “What there is to celebrate now, in these dark times, I cannot say. No, I just happened to be wandering as old folk like to do when my friend the librarian told me of quite the interesting new student who’d asked for books on prana conversion and regeneration.”

Vir nearly stopped in his tracks. The librarian. He hadn’t considered that. Was she an agent of the Fateweavers?

He shook his head, clearing away the thought. While it was true he needed to be cautious, there was a fine line between caution and paranoia. If he couldn’t trust anyone in the entire city with even the most basic of information, then he would get nowhere fast.

The whole point of infiltrating the Academy was to learn how to master prana such that he could scour its archives and halls for information on the whereabouts of the Vimana. All so that they could hasten their meeting with Janak and their return home.

It was not lost upon Vir that every minute spent and every hour wasted in this universe meant lives lost in theirs. Without him and Ashani to stem the surging tide of Ash in their realm, he could only imagine how much wanton destruction must be happening at this very moment. How many lives had been lost because he had left? Because he’d abandoned his vital duty?

No, he would have to take risks. For their sakes, he’d have to take every risk he could without dooming this universe or theirs.

Where exactly that nebulous line lay and how to identify it was another matter entirely.

“Here we are,” the professor said, approaching a wooden door with an inset Utility orb, pressing her fingers against it.

What Vir would give to have Prana Vision back. One look at how she manipulated her magic would tell him all he needed to know.

The mechanism opened with a click, and they entered a rather luxurious office decorated with all sorts of magical curios, devices, books, and apparatuses, as well as paintings of beautiful scenery ranging from cities to forests. It was full of the rich scent of ancient things.

“Is it so strange to long for all we’ve lost?” the professor asked, seeing his wandering eyes. “To remember and wish for what once was?”

Vir shook his head. “Not at all.”

Hadn’t he had the very same thought in relation to the heights the Prime Imperium had soared to? Rather, he understood the pain every soul in this forsaken place felt more than most.

“Well, professor,” he said, having finally decided upon his course of action. When all else failed, honesty was often the best course of action, and in this case, made him look normal and uninteresting.

Which, if he was a student, would be a tragic waste of an opportunity. As he was, however, it was perfect.

“I understand you’ve taken an interest in me, but I’m afraid I will have to disappoint. I barely passed that exam by the skin of my teeth.”

“Oh?” the professor asked, seeming more curious than angry for having wasted her time.

In another time and another place, he might’ve even trusted this woman. Something about her said they’d get along quite well. Here, though, that trust could very well come back to bite him. As he’d come to learn over the years, most of their agents didn’t even know they were.

It was what made them so terrifying. Even still, as much as he could gain from confiding in her, that wasn’t to say he couldn’t work this to his advantage.

“That’s why I was so desperately searching for a way to grow stronger,” he said.

“And tell me, child, what have you learned?”

Vir found her choice of words rather amusing, though he supposed she’d likely have called him the same, even if she was made aware of his age.

“That in this world, under the new rules of prana, it can no longer be absorbed from the environment and channeled as we have always known it to be. It must instead be developed within the body. Generated wholly from within.”

“Well, well,” the professor said with widened eyes. “I must admit I am surprised that you’ve come to this conclusion without any tutelage. Not even most mejai are aware of that secret, for it is one we hold tightly to our chest.”

“I’m aware,” Vir said, pressing his lips together. “Those books contained almost nothing useful. I found several books dancing about the topic, but none that actually mentioned how to generate prana from within the body.”

“Nor would you,” the professor laughed. “It wouldn’t do to sell our greatest secret.”

But that only made Vir frown. “I can’t imagine why not. Under normal circumstances, I understand why the Altani might wish to use this to their advantage, but you—the—universe itself is at stake,” Vir said, barely catching himself from saying “your universe.” “Shouldn’t you be enlisting the help of everyone you can get? Shouldn’t you publicly announce these secrets to everyone who might help fight the Ash?”

“Alas, there are voices within the Academy who wish nothing more than to do so. Other voices—powerful voices—believe that doing so would cause chaos within the city if people attempted to learn without guidance. Having seen how disastrous the outcomes can be, I am inclined to agree,” she said, gazing wistfully into the distance.

“I see,” Vir replied.

Even under the old rules, it wasn’t as though learning prana manipulation was a safe thing. If improperly altered, the pathways within the body could easily cripple a person. Vir had nearly done that to himself once, fumbling around in the dark.

Still, something felt off about that. Wouldn’t it be worth it from the Altani’s perspective if it even got them a few more valuable mejai?

Vir cleared his throat. “Back to the topic at hand, I’m afraid I have nothing to show you. My prana regeneration rate is abysmal.”

“As I imagine it would be,” she replied, giving him an appraising look. “I am not usually this generous—I can’t usually afford to be this charitable. But you have the drive.”

She walked over to a bookshelf and pulled out a tome that shined and sparkled with gold inlay. From the scent alone, Vir had no doubt it had been freshly bound.

“Try seeing what you can make of this,” she said. “I doubt anything will come of it, for it is quite the advanced text. But it will point you in the right direction should you grasp its contents. If you do manage it, come and see me. Then we’ll talk,” she said with a glint in her eyes.

Perhaps she expected to induct Vir as an apprentice or some such?

Unfortunately for her, Vir planned to spend not a moment longer in this universe than he absolutely had to. But for appearance’s sake, he made his eyes bulge.

“You’re giving this to me?” he said, putting on a performance that would’ve made even Riyan proud.

“Loaning,” she corrected. “I will be expecting that back. And I don’t need to tell you what would happen should that book find its way into others’ hands, yes?”

“Of course,” Vir said, stammering and stumbling over his words with just enough awkwardness to make it sound believable. “You need not worry about that, ma’am. Thank you! Thank you so much. You’ve given me a chance.”

There was no lie in his words. If this book truly did contain even the faintest morsel of a hint, Vir would find it, and with his vast understanding and experience in the world of prana manipulation, he was confident he could craft his own theories and find his own way.

Perhaps not all coincidences were bad after all. And if this encounter was indeed manufactured by the Fateweavers, then it proved that they weren’t all-powerful. He found it difficult to believe any entity could truly be omniscient.

But maybe they didn’t have to be.

A darker thought occurred to him just then.

Even if they lacked any conscious knowledge of these events, what if, in a universe where they had such thorough control, events simply tended to go their way?

A form of luck, as it were. Or rather, the guiding of the threads of Fate. Perhaps this, to them, was like breathing to humans—something that occurred subconsciously and without thought at all.

That was a more terrifying thought than any conscious plan on their part. For it meant that simply existing in this universe would skew the odds in their favor.

All the more reason to leave this place as quickly as possible.

He suppressed the shiver that ran down his spine, thanked the professor once again, and swiftly turned on his heel. He was halfway out of the Academy when he realized he hadn’t asked for the professor’s name. Nor, curiously, had she asked him his.

It was no matter. Perhaps even good this way, for he had more pressing matters to attend to. Like getting started understanding this tome.

Maiya arrived a few hours later, just as the sky began to darken. The light from the sun did not pierce the clouds in this realm. Not with how much Ash continuously fell. But it was still there, and as such, night would soon come.

“Well,” she said, closing the sorry excuse of a door to their shack, dusting the soot off her robes. “You didn’t miss anything. The entrance exam was as pompous and boring as you’d expected it to be.”

“I see,” Vir said distractedly, poring over and concentrating on the book.

“Looks like you at least had a productive time. What have you got yourself there?” she asked, crouching beside Vir.

Vir set down the book and filled Maiya in on all that had happened.

“Quite the productive afternoon, huh?” Maiya said, tapping her chin. “All the more reason to vacate, then. This is no environment to be studying, let alone practicing and experimenting with prana.”

Vir tilted his head. “You’ve got something in mind?”

“Well, we’re Academy students now,” she said, flashing her badge. “We might have had some trouble finding housing before, but not anymore.”

“Doesn’t the Academy provide student housing for their students?” Vir said, recalling what he knew of the Academy’s perks. Though it was more a detriment in their case. “If it’s a choice between dorms and here, I would rather stay here where there are fewer eyes.”

Maiya shook her head. “Ordinarily, yes, but have you seen how crowded the city is and how many people were admitted this year? The Academy is stretched far beyond its capacity, and they’re actually encouraging people to find their own housing. Begging, more like. Besides, do you think the Sawai brats would sully their hands with university housing?”

Vir chuckled, trying to imagine Vayren bunking with roommates in a tiny space. Tried, and failed. “No, I suppose not. I take it you’ve arranged something, then?”

Maiya grinned, putting her hands on her hips. “But of course. Why do you think it took me so long to get here? It’s nothing fancy, and it did cost an obscene amount. But I think you’ll find it adequate. It’s got two rooms and a kitchen. The wolves will have to stay out, but I doubt they'll mind that. More importantly, it’s in a much nicer neighborhood than this. We won’t need to worry about leaving Ashani there. Or anyone snooping on us, should we wish to practice magic.”

Vir shook his head, smiling ruefully. Maiya had gone and solved a problem he hadn’t even thought about. “Did I ever tell you how lucky I was to have you?”

Maiya brought a finger up to her cheek, tilting her head as though in thought. “Maybe once or twice, but you could say it more.”

“Well, I am lucky to have you, Mai,” he said. “Seriously. I don’t know what I would do without you.”

“Well, lucky for you, I’m all yours. Now, I suggest you continue poring over that tome while I find us some rags to hide Ashani in. We should migrate when night falls. I, for one, am not looking to spend a night in this dump.”

Vir nodded.

For the first time since coming into this universe, he felt something other than horror and worry. For the first time, he felt hope. Or the first traces of it, anyway.

Whether it was a fool’s hope, only time would tell.
