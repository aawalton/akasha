“And so he has returned to face trial and tribulation in the Court of Codex,” I murmured, my voice threading through the vaulted dark like ink poured into water. “Of course he has. He only comes when cornered. He is able to return at any time—has been for months—but the only moments he does are when he is in peril… or when these halls themselves tremble.”

The whisper left a bitter taste on my tongue. I hadn’t meant for it to sound as weary as it did. I rarely allow myself the indulgence of regret, but the word carried a weight that even I could not disguise.

The air stirred faintly around the balcony where I stood. Below me, the endless stacks of the Codex stretched out in a labyrinth of light and parchment. Billions of books—some bound in hide, others in song—murmured in their sleep. The words within them breathed softly, restless with expectation. They knew when someone returned. Especially him.

Yallas was, predictably, immediate in her rebuttal. “Part of that is your own blame. Another part of that is which Librarian you had assigned to him.”

The click of her pen punctuated her accusation. Always so sharp, so precise, so sure that truth could be written neatly into margins.

“It is not my fault,” I replied, the word cracking against the shelves like a struck bell. The books along the nearest wall flinched at the tone, their pages fluttering as though disturbed by wind. “That his uncle is a sociopath who thought thrusting that scroll upon a boy so boundless in curiosity and hunger was wisdom.”

I stood from my chair, the light bending around me in obedient fear. Every lantern within twenty meters dimmed slightly, as if acknowledging hierarchy.

“It is also not my fault that I cannot overwrite the laws of the Grandis. If I could, I would have brought him to the Sanctum immediately—made him a Speaker here, in these Courts, where he belongs.”

My shadow stretched toward the nearest table, dark fingers crawling across open volumes. The ink within them shifted uneasily. “But I cannot. His version breaks the Pote’s Paradox. You know that.”

Yallas’s expression didn’t change. The light from her desk lantern glimmered against her copper hair and the edge of her monocle, the lenses etched with glyphs that measured meaning itself. She had that irritating calm of someone who already knew the answer and asked the question only to confirm your guilt.

“He’s hurting,” she said, quiet but unwavering. “All he wants is agency.”

“Agency?” I repeated, the syllables dragging against my tongue like a stone. “You think I don’t know that?”

I turned on her fully. The parchment walls rippled at the movement, responding to my irritation like a tide drawn to gravity. “I’d be impressed if two things were not true: first, that your observation wasn’t the most obvious one in existence—and second, that you weren’t holding a copy of The Paper Walker in your hand while saying it.”

Her knuckles tightened on the book’s spine. I gestured to it, and the tome shuddered, half-alive, recognizing my authority. “You read from him, Yallas. You study him. And yet you forget the nature of stories—they aren’t made by wanting. They are made by doing. He doesn’t seek agency. He defines it.”

I stepped closer, lowering my tone until it was barely above the whisper of a quill across vellum. “Do you know why his path is so dangerous? Because it is unwritten. Every Walker follows a trail left behind by others—rituals, contracts, decrees. Alexander writes as he walks. That is why the Grandis cannot categorize him.”

Yallas hesitated, then set the book down. The title pulsed faintly, as if it still listened. “Then perhaps the Grandis is afraid,” she said.

I allowed myself a faint, humorless smile. “The Grandis does not fear. It observes. It records. It is I who fears.”

That admission hung between us like a physical object. The lamps flickered once, startled by the honesty of it.

“I fear what happens if he continues to breach the border between fiction and flesh. He has already tasted the ink of dreams. He will one day learn to drink it. And when he does, every library—mine included—will be nothing more than a page inside him.”

I turned away before her eyes could search mine. The shelves to my right opened, revealing the scrying pool of reflection—a mirror of condensed script that showed the present in abstract form. Glyphs rippled across its surface, rearranging themselves into a shifting mosaic of light.

Alexander stood within it, trembling, eyes bloodshot but burning with defiance. The Queen’s influence still flickered around him like ash in water.

“He reminds you of yourself,” Yallas said softly.

“Don’t be absurd.”

“He does. The way he argues with the text. The way he refuses to stay confined to a single narrative. He even uses your own sigil without permission.”

That drew a genuine, if reluctant, laugh from me. “A child drawing weapons from the armory without knowing the cost of steel,” I mused. “Yes. He is reckless. He is arrogant. He believes himself cursed by destiny, when in truth he is only noticed by it.”

I let my gaze linger on his image. “But you are right about one thing, Yallas. He wants agency. He believes freedom is something that can be taken. That is his flaw. Freedom is not stolen—it is granted, piece by piece, through understanding. The more you know of the world, the more it owns you.”

The scrying pool trembled. For a moment, the image fragmented into text—rows of letters forming and collapsing, spelling fragments of his name before dissolving.

“The present,” I continued, quieter now, “is a gift because it isn’t yet known. It is neither history nor prophecy. It simply is. Ephemeral. A sunset and a sunrise, giving way to both dusk and dawn. The tragedy of all mortals is that they confuse the horizon for a destination.”

Yallas was silent. The soft scratching of her pen had stopped. She was listening now, properly.

Outside the chamber, I could feel the subtle shift—the ripple of his entrance into the upper tiers of the archive. The Codex recognized him, even if it would not yet claim him.

“He’s climbing,” Yallas said finally.

“Yes.” I folded my hands behind my back. “And the staircase will test every version of him that has ever existed. Let us see which one reaches the top.”

The mirror brightened again. The staircase materialized within its surface—a column of rotating shelves, each step composed of stacked tomes, every volume bearing a different name he had once used, a different face he had worn.

As he climbed, those names peeled away from him, whispering as they fell into the void below. Alexander Duarte. The Paper Walker. The Boy of Ink and Ash. The One Who Barters With Silence. Each identity flickered and burned out, only for another to surface.

“The Archive tests through reflection,” I said, almost to myself. “It does not judge. It simply shows him the cost of his own narrative. Every lie he told himself becomes a rung on that ladder.”

Yallas watched the mirror in uneasy fascination. “And if he falls?”

“Then he will become text.”

A pause. “And if he reaches the top?”

“Then he will stop being readable.”

The thought sat between us, enormous.

The shelves nearby began to hum softly, resonating with his ascent. Pages rustled without wind, scrolls uncoiled, parchments whispered. He was rewriting proximity by existing. The Codex didn’t like uncertainty—it catalogued, it sorted. He was the first visitor it could not file.

“He’s merging,” Yallas whispered. “His essence is crossing the margins.”

“Of course it is,” I said. “Every step he takes up that stair is another story collapsing beneath him. He consumes narrative structure as oxygen.”

“And you allowed this?”

“I didn’t allow it. I witnessed it.” My gaze hardened. “There’s a difference.”

The mirror showed him stumbling near the upper tiers. His hand brushed a sigil I recognized—the door to the Librarian’s Gallery. The script around it flared in warning, but the door still opened.

He stepped through.

And the moment he did, the Court itself shuddered. A tremor rippled through the walls, strong enough to shake dust from the vaulted rafters. Several books fell open at once, their pages screaming soundlessly.

Yallas caught herself against the desk. “He’s inside the Gallery—he’s not permitted—”

“He’s already rewritten the permissions,” I said, watching the chaos with grim admiration. “You forget, Yallas, he was trained by Morres. He understands systems.”

“But this is the Codex! You can’t just walk through our structure—”

“Oh, he isn’t walking through it.” I smiled thinly. “He’s walking as it.”

The tremor passed. The lights steadied. When the mirror cleared, he stood in the center of the Librarian’s Gallery, surrounded by mirrors of bound script and hanging runes. His reflection looked older there—less boy, more silhouette.

The Queen’s remnants stirred again, like oil on the surface of a pool. Her shape formed behind him, whispering false promises. I could feel her pressure even from here—her attempt to nest herself into the Archive’s logic.

Yallas swore under her breath. “She’s trying to embed herself in the index.”

“She can’t,” I said, though my voice betrayed uncertainty. “Not fully. The Codex is not a hive. It will reject her in time.”

Alexander turned sharply, muttering something I couldn’t hear. Then the mirror flared white. The Queen’s form screamed, tearing like wet paper—and vanished.

I exhaled, the faintest tremor escaping me. “He’s learned. Good.”

Yallas looked to me, wide-eyed. “What did you do?”

“Nothing.” I smiled faintly. “He simply remembered his contract. The Library still recognizes him, even if the Grandis does not.”

The mirror dimmed again, leaving only faint ripples of ink in its wake.

“You see?” I said softly. “He’s not bound by the same cause-and-effect that chains the rest of us. His actions are self-referential. He acts, and reality retroactively justifies the act. The Grandis calls this heresy. I call it artistry.”

“You almost sound proud,” Yallas murmured.

“Perhaps I am.”

For a time, we both watched the dark mirror, each lost in our own thoughts.

Finally, I spoke again. “He is rewriting his meaning faster than the Codex can archive it. Soon the narrative will reject its own foundation. He will become a variable in the equation of the world.”

“That sounds… catastrophic,” Yallas said quietly.

“It would be,” I replied. “Unless he learns moderation. Unless he learns why I built these walls—to contain stories that grew too large for their tellers.”

The silence stretched. The ink in the air settled again, breathing in rhythm with the lights.

When I finally turned back toward Yallas, my tone was quieter, almost intimate. “Do you know what I envy most about him?”

She shook her head.

“He still believes that words can save him.”

I let that thought linger, my eyes returning to the pool, to the boy who had somehow outgrown it.

The mirror flickered one last time—his form breaking apart, fragmenting between image and intention. Letters swam across his body like scales. Every heartbeat turned him less into a person and more into an idea.

The shelves around us groaned, translating his existence into new equations the Codex could not solve. My assistant backed away, shielding her eyes from the distortion.

I remained still. I had seen this before, long ago.

“Pray, Yallas, that he does not find the version that writes in my tongue. If he does…”

I trailed off, watching as the scrying mirror flickered one last time—Alexander’s form fragmenting between boy and story, dreamer and ink.

“…then even the Grandis will have to rewrite its laws.”
