Chapter 35Memento

Rafflesia’s Memento. His gaze was glued to it. There was no way it was the same one, but it wouldn’t be far-fetched for Enthusia to create a faithful reproduction. But why, was all he thought. For such a kind goddess to torture him with a memento, what was the point?

He looked over to Lei-rei, fearing he might have spaced out and ignored her, but he found a question in her eye, instead.

Someone like her would be able to track his gaze at a glance. He knew she knew what he’d been staring at. He looked down.

“I’ll tell you if you ask,” he said, but even if it was Lei-rei, it wasn’t less nerve-wracking—but also because it was her, he was willing to summon the courage.

Lei-rei looked at his panel. “What’s with that number of bows?”

He found himself relieved. Maybe he shouldn't have felt that way. He didn't know what was right—I'll just accept the consideration, then.

He stared at the list of completed bows. The ones listed near the top had names like “Long-Range Club”—what was he even thinking? Well, the ones after that were simply numbered. In all, there were 3,122 numbered bows and 25 named ones, coming to a total of 3,157 completed bows.

This was pocket change compared to the number of bows he’d actually tried making in Enthusia’s domain. When he’d been starting out, it had taken one to three days to take a bow from stave to strung, and after almost three-hundred years at Enty’s place, that pace should have resulted in at least thirty-thousand bows.

Failure, alas, was the witch mother of mastery, and she demanded the first until the ninth children. The tenth was in his inventory.

Did he have something for this situation, though? Although he was confident Lei-rei would be able to handle any bow he gave her, the core was a tricky target. It flew too fast, and only the fastest bow would match it. The question now was whether his fastest was fast enough.

[Terminal Velocity (takedown, asymmetric, trebuchet)]

It stared him in the face…and he raised an eyebrow. I don’t remember making a trebuchet—it wasn’t even a bow in the first place!… It sounded like it’d hit the core no matter what, though, so he took it out anyway.

Two counter-rotating disks of light appeared in the air before him. Each disk was the size of a dinner plate, and they slowly drew apart, revealing the bow in their wake like a sheathe in two directions.

Once he saw enough of the bow’s riser, he realized it was twice as thick as it should be.

The ‘riser’ was what most people would identify as the handle. Although it did usually serve as one, it was also a structural component, as it held the bow’s limbs and generally kept them from flying off when set loose.

The odd riser that Craft was staring at consisted of a handle, carved for an ergonomic fit, but also a thick brace over where the archer’s fingers went. The brace was too thick to grasp, hence a handle was made separate.

The reason for the odd design became apparent as the glowing disks drew apart a little more. The limbs were thick like the paddle of an oar, and as the disks drew apart even further, Craft finally remembered when—and importantly why—he’d made such a stupid thing.

He caught it with both hands and an “Oof,” crouching to soften the blow and finally laying the thing on the ground. It must have weighed as much as an antimateriel rifle. It was five meters long, and the bowstring was a cable made of lead-steel alloy, keeping it soft and pliable. The lower limb, even if shorter than the upper, would still scrape the ground if someone where to hold it by the handle. There was no way anyone could use the whole thing without standing on a platform.

Lei-rei crouched near it. She poked it, and she looked up at Craft with the most incredulous expression. “What is this?”

“This is the fastest bow I have,” he replied.

“You’re joking.”

“So, the reasoning behind this one’s a bit crazy.”

“Sure. A bit.”

He inched closer, gesturing with his hands like a conspiracy theorist. “The way this is supposed to be used…” He pointed at the bottom limb. The bowstring wasn’t attached to the very tip, but a little before it. At the tip, instead, was a flat nub.

“The lower limb’s shorter so you can stand a chance of resting that end on the ground,” Craft continued. He pointed upwards. “And you aim the whole thing skywards.”

Lei-rei followed his finger up, then looked at him with narrowed eyes. “An anti-dragon bow?…”

“I mean, the draw weight’s about a metric ton, if I remember correctly, but”—he shook his head—“can you even use it?”

“Draw weight isn’t an issue”—“Wow”—“but forget about using it. If I need to stand on a platform, I can’t possibly chase the core.” Lei-rei frowned. “Don’t you have a normal one?”

He sighed and bowed his head, chuckling. “Well, it was worth a try.”

“You wanted me to use it?”

“Hey, don’t make it out like you weren’t interested.”

Lei-rei clicked her tongue. “Shush.”

Craft laughed. Lei-rei stood up with a little more relaxed expression. “Well? The bow, please.”

“Yep, coming up.”

Craft wasn’t insane enough to turn down a sane request. He pulled out [Bow #1000 (recurve, 50 lbs.)]. It was already strung when it plopped down into his hands. The handle was wrapped in snakeskin, and there was that characteristic curve towards the tips. When he was satisfied with its condition, he passed it to her.

She drew the string back a short distance, getting a feel for it. “It’s…remarkably normal.”

“I hope that’s a compliment.”

“Wait for me here,” she said, and she took off into the bushes.

Craft was about to call after her about the arrows, but thinking back to last time, she had produced an arrow out of nowhere. Maybe she had a hidden quiver, just like he had a [Bow Shed].

As soon as she was out of sight, the relief he felt was like he'd been holding his breath. Was he…glad he could hide the Memento from her? No, that couldn't be… But, Raffie's memories were just too precious. It felt like no one else would be able to understand just how precious they were.

What point was there for nostalgia now? He realized he was genuinely by himself for the first time in this place. Without anyone around, feelings he normally kept down began to rise to the surface. Like the shame of always knowing just one more ounce of courage could have put him in a better place—it eroded him.

Water drops fell on the ground, and it took him a second to realize they had come from him. Time passed just like this, and he didn’t care how much of it did.

The rustling of bushes had him panic. He quickly wiped his eyes with his sleeves, getting into a crouch, prepared to greet friend or foe.

“Craaaft!” It was Lei-rei giving up on the world. Craft let out a nervous chuckle and sat down again, She sauntered over with an uninspired gait and slumped shoulders. “Craaaft! You”—she noticed something about him and paused, keeping quiet.

She paused, then continued ’til before the five-meter bow on the ground. She stared at it. “It was armored,” she muttered. “I don’t think even a halberd’s pick could pierce it, but I did see”—she paused, seeming to hesitate, but whatever. “It had a small hole on top.”

Craft chuckled. He reached down and patted the gigantic bow, slapping it a little harder than he normally would to try and shake the residual feelings from his system. “You’re in luck. I conceptualized this thing as indirect fire artillery. Sending the arrow up is the first stage, but the arrow itself is supposed to be equipped with boosters that fire up as it comes back down.” He shrugged. “Theoretically. No idea how to aim it, though.”

Lei-rei furrowed her brows, and with the way she frowned, she was concentrating with the gusto of a maestro. “ ‘Terminal Velocity,’ after all,” she muttered. She looked up at the canopy—“I do have magic for the acceleration part”—then around—“Aiming will be a problem… No, it won’t.”

She crouched down and scooped up the bow by its riser, standing up, letting its shorter end rest on the ground, and the riser to rest on her shoulder.

The longer end tapped against a branch. She looked back and up. “This is ridiculous.”

Craft snorted. “Sounds like you’ve got a plan.”

She looked at him. “Come closer.”

He raised an eyebrow, but complied nonetheless. Once he was close enough, Lei-rei put her hand on his head and started whispering oddly cultish-sounding things.

“Hey—er—I’m going to be fine, right?”

“Shush. Oculus sancti…”

That took a long ten seconds, and when it was over and Lei-rei had taken off her hand, Craft felt…nothing different. “Hey, what was that?”

The system answered:

[Lei-rei is requesting Shared Eyes permissions. Accept? (y/n)]

He jerked back, but things clicked for him just as fast. So I’m playing artillery observer, huh? He accepted.

“Still, that’s a whole ritual you did there,” he remarked.

“It’s not widely known”—she cleared her throat—“and I hope it stays that way.”

He laughed. “Sure, sure.”

“Alright, let me just test it for a moment.” She closed one eye, opened it again, then closed it again. “Why is it blurred?” she muttered—and looked at him.

He was wiping his eyes.

“Craft?”

Her voice made him look, and only then did he realize what he was doing. “Sorry, I”—I should've just hidden it better.

Lei-rei frowned. “I didn’t want to ask, but you might need someone to do so.”

Had he been hoping for that? He hadn’t wanted to hope for it. He didn’t want to depend on her for this.

“Yeah, I might need it,” he said.

Terminal Velocity hit the ground with a thud. He chuckled. “Be careful with that.”

She approached him. “It has a spine of nearly unbendable metal. I doubt it’d break so easily.” She put her hand on his shoulder and slowly sat down, suggesting him to do the same. And it was only when they’d sat down when Craft finally sniffled.

“I don’t know what’s come over me,” he said.

“It’s about that ‘memento,’ isn’t it?”

He nodded meekly. “I didn’t expect it at all. I mean, well…it’s a long story.”

“We’ve got a long time.”

He nodded. “Don’t we?” He summoned Rafflesia’s Memento, and it landed across both his lap and Lei-rei’s. He could smell the petrichor of that day. There were brownish stains and streaks up and down. Is that my blood? He couldn’t help but touch it.

She saw the brown streaks too. “Is this…”

“It’s both mine and hers.”

Her gaze snapped to him.

“It was my last mission to kill her. I didn’t even realize my own feelings towards her until then.”

“You…loved her?” She academically tilted her lead, like it was the first time she'd heard the word. Well, he couldn't blame her. It didn't seem like a word that matched him.

“That’s what it was, I think.” He paused. “I’m jealous of you sometimes.” He already knew she would ask why, and he already knew the answer.

Lei-rei suppressed a frown. “Why is that?”

“You’re happy.” He tucked his lips. Should he have said that?

His heart was still weak, and he avoided her gaze. He didn’t want to know what she thought of his fragility, but just as hard as it was for him to begin speaking, it was hard for him to stop.

“When I see how much you smile over Nightshade, it reminds me of how happy I’d been just thinking about Raffie. I felt like I knew why I was alive. When they gave me that mission, I thought it’d be better if we died together instead. I thought we’d see each other again in some next life, but”—he rocked the bow on his lap like a baby in a crib—“where is she?”

Being surrounded by happiness made those who felt sadness feel worse, and those who possessed happiness would see theirs quelled the moment they are confronted by sadness, and so arose the unspoken conflict: should the happy ones hide their happiness in consideration of the sad, or should the sad ones hide their sadness in consideration of the happy?

Maybe he'd said too much. Craft looked at her, then away. “Sorry.”

One sad person—two sad people. Lei-rei had long learned to join in someone’s sadness, then pull them back up by however much was possible. It didn't matter how much, as she'd found out some time ago, because all that really mattered was that one wasn't made to feel alone.

She took his hand into both of hers. “There’s no wall between us.”

Craft nodded. She took it as a go-ahead.

"I know how it is to have nothing to be proud of and have nothing to your name—no achievements, no legacy, and no one who even knows you. Past happiness seemed meaningless. Good things kept passing me by, and I believed Enthusia had abandoned me until someone pointed out I wasn't trying to keep hold of them." She guided his hand to grip the bow, and he didn't resist.

“I can hear it in the words you say. You don’t want to let go. Don’t let go.”

It was the second time he'd cried.
