Chapter 122

They came at the sanctuary doors with rams and with fire, certain that anything built by hands could be broken by them. The doors held, and not for their strength. They were never built to answer strength, and the men outside had brought the wrong question.— Codex of the Long Watch, Watcher’s Commentary, 12th Century, Thessaly

Arthur had the shield across his back and the maul through the loop at his hip. Their weight had stopped registering as weight some weeks back. You carried forty pounds of iron long enough and your body quietly rewrote what empty should feel like, so that the morning you set it all down you walked lighter but felt wrong.

The market by the container yard was waking before the sun was fully up. Trestles came down off a cart. A woman roped a tarpaulin back from a stack of bruised marrows. Nobody looked twice at four people walking through with weapons. Edged iron was commonplace now, no stranger than the marrows.

A cart came up the lane against them. Two men pulling and another pushing from behind. What looked like four Council guards walked beside it, their eyes on the crowd. The sacks were stacked to the height of a man and roped twice.

“That is a lot of sacks for one cart,” Arthur said. “Where is it coming from?”

“Thriasio,” Petros said. “The plain out past Elefsina, one hour that way. Every second morning now, and always the guards with it, because the Council wants the whole port to see whose hand the bread comes out of.” He watched it pass with something close to fondness. “My cousin farms out there. He says the wheat has gone wrong, re. The stalk comes up thick like a finger and it stands in weather that should lay it flat. Same ground, same seed, no fertiliser. He already cut three times what he cut last year.”

“Three times,” Steve said.

“Three. And he is not a man who makes a story bigger. He counts the sacks twice and then complains about the number.” Petros shrugged at the cart. “Po po po. It is like Triptolemus himself is out there on the plough.”

Steve stopped walking.

“Say that again.”

“It is a saying. My grandmother said it about a good year,” Petros said. “The boy from Elefsina who got the grain from the goddess and took it round the world. Every child here learns it.”

“Every child here learns it, and it is wrong by about eight thousand years.” Steve started walking again. “That is the part nobody says out loud. The Eleusinian sanctuary is Mycenaean at the earliest, so call it fifteen hundred BCE. People were farming wheat and barley in the Fertile Crescent by ten thousand. There was barley at Mehrgarh by seven. The Greeks turn up eight millennia late holding a story about how their plains taught agriculture to the human race, and they defended that story with the death penalty for two thousand years.”

“So they got it wrong,” Donna said. “People do. Even you.”

“That is not my point. People get things wrong and then quietly drop them. Nobody guards a mistake that hard.” Steve had the notebook out. “You do not build a mystery cult, run it for two millennia, and execute people for describing it, in order to protect a claim your neighbours could disprove by pointing at their own fields. The subject is not agriculture. The grain was standing in for something.”

“There is a little room in the middle of it.” Sarah had her eyes on the hill. “At Elefsina. They took us there on a school trip. It is mostly rubble, with a cement works next to it now. The big hall is the Telesterion, and I remember the guide telling us that they rebuilt it four separate times over a thousand years and bigger each time. Every time they rebuilt and made the site bigger, they kept one small square room at the centre exactly where it was. Same floor, same angle. Everything around was changed except that one small room.”

“The anaktoron,” Steve said, nodding.

“He might have used that word. I was eleven, and what I took home was that they had to build around it.”

Arthur turned that over for a few paces.

“They had to build around it,” he said. “You do not preserve one chamber to an inch around it for four rebuilds and a thousand years unless something in that room could not be moved at all, and every generation that inherited the site worked out that they had to design their hall around the fact. That is not devotion. That is a constraint.”

“And only one man went in there.” Steve was writing without looking down, another of his superpowers. “One hierophant, appointed for life, out of one family, generation after generation. I have always taught that as priestly conservatism. With all we have seen, it could be a succession plan. The smallest number of hands you can pass a thing or knowledge through per century.”

He held up a finger before Donna could get there. “Most of what I am about to say is a tea towel. But there is a hostile third-century account from Hippolytus, a Christian with every reason to make it sound stupid, and he says what the hierophant showed them on the last night was an ear of grain, reaped in silence.”

Donna waited. “That is it?”

“That is it. Two thousand years of one of the best-kept secrets in the ancient world, and the answer is a piece of wheat.” Steve looked at the cart, sacks of the same thing rolling past them under guard. “What if they were handing something forwards. Writing gets translated, abridged, burned and thrown out by whoever stops understanding it. A rite just gets performed. It survives incomprehension. So you put a custodian in place to show the initiates new growth out of a dead world, and you hope it lands on the century where somebody can finally read it.”

“And by custodian you are obviously referring to the Curators,” Arthur added.

“And you meant that the central chamber that was never moved is Seeder built,” Steve said.

Arthur nodded. “Archie, any inputs?”

Steven’s model is mechanically sound, Archie said. A text requires a custodian who still values it. A rite requires only participants who continue to perform it, and performance of rites tolerates the total loss of meaning without any loss of content. The Athirathram is conducted in southern India and is approximately four thousand years old. It runs twelve days, it is built around offerings to a fire deity, and it has been carried through four millennia by voice, syllable by syllable, within families who transmit it to their sons. Sections of it were preserved with sufficient fidelity that the men performing them stopped understanding what they were saying and went on reproducing them exactly. It is not unusual on your planet. Until recently it was the only storage method you possessed that did not degrade.

Nobody said anything for a moment. The cart went on down towards the market with its guards.

“Fascinating as this is, this morning we are going into a hole in the ground,” Arthur said. “Write it up tonight.”

“Oh, I intend to write it up tonight at considerable length.”

“I know.” Arthur held the pace. “That is why I said tonight.”

Petros took them through the yard by a route he could have run blind. His eyes kept climbing the stacked containers, checking the tops, and he did not say much. He stopped just before the city exit.

“This is me,” he said. “I will go back now. You be careful.”

“That is the plan,” Sarah said.

“Plans.” Petros gave Sarah a quick hug. “Ela. Go.”

The stone told them where they were. It came out of the hillside in rows, pale and close-fitted and square-cut. Ancient in every line and new as last week in its condition. They had seen these, weeks back, on the day they first understood what the hill was. It had grown since. More of the stone was here now, the rows running further up the slope than before, as though the hill were remembering a building that used to be here. Between two rows of stone, where the masonry ran back into the hill, the opening waited, dark against the pale stone.

They went in with weapons up and the daylight from the mouth dying behind them inside twenty paces. The air changed first. It was cool first and then it was cold with the smell of quarried stone. The air had a fullness to it, a feeling of denseness.

The density here exceeds the projection for a formation of this age, Archie said, into the four of them. Your planet has carried the System for a matter of months. A saturation of this order is too early.

Steve opened his mouth to go further into it but stopped. Things moved in the dark ahead of them.

The first came out of a gap in the half-built wall at a flat sprint, a wolf the size of the container-yard alpha, its grey coat going to a dirty white along the spine. Arthur triggered Counterforce Plant and took it square on Breakwater. The impact ran up his arm and stored itself in glowing lines.

But the first was not alone. These hunted as a pack, not in a mass but in a rolling sequence, one drawing the eye while the others moved on the flank. Donna was moving before Arthur called it. She triggered Reflex Action and met the second as it came off the wall, Ravener taking it across the throat. The blade bit deep into muscle, she wrenched it free, dropped Reflex Action and hit the same line again, and the wolf went down.

Steve held the lane behind them. He sent a gout of fire across the gap the wolves were funnelling through, and when the fire broke their stride he followed up with a concussion wave behind it. A crack of pressure threw three of them off their feet and into the wall. The rod tracked the nearer one up off the floor without Steve seeming to aim, and the next concussion caught it square in the face and it stopped moving.

The next moment Donna was on the other two, her axe whirling before they had time to recover from the concussive wave. She was the flickering afterimage of Threshold Drive and she tore through the two downed wolves.

It was the sixth that got through.

It did not come at the shield. It came wide along the base of the far wall and went for the gap between Arthur’s flank and the others. Arthur saw it and could not turn in time. Donna was three strides too far. The thing was inside their formation aimed at Sarah.

Sarah, instead of stepping back, turned into it. She took the weight of the wolf on her body but managed to avoid its jaw, and her left hand touched it under the neck as it was moving past her. The wolf’s legs buckled as it came down and it lay twitching on the stone.

“Clear it. It is not dead.”

Steve made it dead.

The first wolf, the big one, came back at Arthur for its second pass, and Arthur answered back with the force he had been holding. He brought the maul down and emptied Counterforce Plant into the blow. The impact lifted the wolf straight off the ground and crashed it against a stone pillar. Arthur took three strides forward and brought the maul down on its head, ending the fight.

Then the chamber held nothing but four people breathing hard in the cold. The fight had run perhaps forty seconds. It had cost a shallow rake across Arthur’s shoulder where a claw had found the gap above the shield, and it had cost Sarah the wind out of her. Other than that the fight was almost anti-climactic.

“Are we over levelled for this?” Steve said, surveying the carnage.

“Steve, don’t you dare.” Donna glared.

“I’m just saying.”

“I’ll take over levelled rather than fighting for our lives any day of the week and twice on dungeon days,” Arthur said.

Sarah went around checking the bodies for cores and came up with three cores off the six. She held them out to Steve who dropped them into his pouch without ceremony. “Three off six,” he said. “Not too shabby.”

They found the den where the chamber narrowed and the half-built wall opened onto a low space that would have been a room once. Arthur smelled it from about ten metres out, the close animal reek of a place things slept in. He expected bones. He expected the leavings of a pack that ate what it caught, cracked and scattered and gnawed, the honest filth of predators.

What he found were things laid out. A pair of boots stood side by side against the stone, their heels against the wall. A cooking pot sat beside them, a coat had been spread flat. Further in lay a belt, a tin cup, a woman’s headscarf with a patch of blood folded into a square and a child’s shoe.

Arthur crouched by the boots. Nothing random set a heel square to a wall. Nothing hungry spread a coat out. This was kept.

“These were collected,” he said, then added after a pause, “From the dead.”

Steve crouched beside Arthur without touching any of the objects. His voice was quiet. “Wolf eats man, becomes more man. We did not know what that would look like.” He nodded at the boots, the cookpot, the single shoe. “It looks like this. A wolf does not keep. A wolf does not arrange. Keeping is ours. Arranging is ours. Something down here has started doing both.”

Nobody answered that for a moment. Then Donna, who had stood the whole time with her back to them and her eyes on the dark ahead while the rest of them looked at dead people’s belongings, spoke without turning round.

“Then further down is where we are going,” she said. “Standing here will not unarrange it.”

The passage past the den ran down at a descending gradient. The rough outer stone changed to finer masonry. Courses fitted without a blade’s width between them. Then the passage ended at a closed door.

The door was solid stone. A head taller than Arthur and half again as wide, two slabs of pale dressed rock set flush into the finished wall. It had the quality Arthur’s trade had taught him to trust least, which was that it looked simple. There was no handle. There was no hinge he could see, no pintle, no jamb, nothing to declare which way the thing was meant to swing or whether it was meant to swing at all.

Arthur put his hands on it. The stone was cold and dry and he could not feel any Ether. He ran them down the centre joint first, then out along the lines where each slab met its frame, and found nothing. The gap between the door and the frame was even the whole way and too fine to take a fingernail, let alone a bar.

“Talk to me,” Donna said.

“There is nothing to talk about yet.” He set his shoulder to it and pushed, his legs and weight behind it. The door did not notice him. He tried pushing the left leaf alone, then the right, then both together. Neither of them moved a hair against the other. He took Donna’s prybar and tried working the point along the edges and found no fault.

Steve had made a circuit around the room and had found nothing.

“It is a door,” Steve said, half to himself. “Somebody built a door here on purpose, and building a door means there is something behind it. Doors are made to open. You do not seal a sacred precinct with a thing shaped like a door and then give it no reason.”

Steve crouched down to the floor. “There should be a lever or a plate to stand on or something similar. It’s the oldest dungeon trope in the book.”

“Be careful. Weight plates that fire arrows are also the oldest dungeon trope in the book.” Arthur said.

Donna went over the floor with Steve in front of the door looking for a plate, a trip, a stone that gave under weight. She went over the walls for a hidden lever. They found nothing a person could stand on, pull on or turn.

“Right,” Donna said, looking at the three of them. “Anyone?”
