True to its name, the Outer Line of Defense wasn't a singular location, but a long stretch of earthworks protruding from the flat wastes. The walls were erected along thin fractures, cobbled into a uniform line as they snaked into the distance in a seemingly random, zigzagging pattern like a reverse crack in the earth, but always stretching in the general direction of north and south. They were molded from dirt and mud, four feet tall at most, and were slanted from the inside so that defenders would use it as cover before it came to a vertical drop on the outside. Where the angle allowed, Kalsin saw small sections had been reinforced with wood and plates of spiky metal.

They didn't get and didn't expect to get a welcome committee. Instead, they had a single rider approach their group with an unhurried trot across the red plains.

"What month is it?" the rider asked, pulling on the reigns as his chestnut horse peppered the recruits in a liberal dosage of dust.

"Nagy of Sommer," Fals called out. For some indecipherable reason—and to the amusement of the group—she was speaking with an accent. "Whot's the naaeme of that beaute you're roiding, chief? Me loike her fuur. Shoiny."

The man gripped Brownweather's arm with a grin, who returned the gesture with a smile of his own. "Skif. It's been a short while," Brownweather said.

"That it has, liout," Skif said. "A couple of months if I had to wager, but you know how it is. Or have you forgotten already? How easy it is to lose count when you sleep and wake by the horn?"

Even as Brownweather shook his head bemusedly, Skif straightened in his saddle and searched the crowd until he found Fals. "Shiny fur on her, and good eyes on you, ey? Good eyes, always be on the lookout for those with a pair of good eyes. I like that. I like you." He turned to back Brownweather and asked with a low voice, though Kalsin was still close enough to hear him. "Please tell me it's not another Fals?"

He didn't catch Brownweather's answer, but the momentary flicker of despair passing through Skif's face spoke loud enough. He then surveyed the crowd as if counting their numbers, and clicked his tongue to spur his grazing horse back in action. "I'll take the recruits from here, good men. Thank you."

Throughout the next few minutes, the soldiers that had accompanied them for their journey handed each recruit their full load of equipment and split off, making their exit with a relieved air of those glad to be going home, likely back toward Lostpoint Station. Kalsin's eyes followed them until they were small dots in the distance. He saw another couple of men lead their horses away, down south along the earthen barrier.

He wasn't the only one with questions on his face, because—after having dismounted and handed off his own horse—Skif gave them an explanation. "They'll be taken to the nearest stable, and they'll be cared for. Animals would only cause trouble during defense, and runners have an easier time if they know exactly where they can mount up. But come now. There’s never an idle second in this place."

The recruits, now moving in a formless mass, followed Skif and Brownweather along the inside of the continuous earthwork.

What seemed like a deserted stretch of land at first quickly turned out to be inhabited, albeit in a scattered manner with groups of fifty to sixty men stationed every mile or so. Countless clay huts had been built and were used as housing, with a few brick structures giving the expanse a barren sort of flavor to human civilization.

As for the outer side of the wall, lookout spots were erected at intervals, far enough into the Eastern Expanse that they appeared smaller than Kalsin's thumb. The earth was dark and bare in a large radius around them with not a single patch of red grass. If Kalsin counted correctly, there had to be a single lookout spot manned about every mile or two.

Skif kept talking as they went.

"...quite bleak, but you get used to it. There's plenty to do to keep yourself occupied, and when a steady routine meets unchanging surroundings, time tends to turn and turn like a bad case of digestive noncompliance."

"The wall, why's it so uneven?" asked Cyanfish.

"A'yes, an oldy question, that one. Classic. Why not just build a straight line? Look there, around the bend," Skif pointed. Kalsin couldn't see from where he stood. "We kept the wall generally going south to north, but made it criss and cross and built it along the natural fractures that split the earth nearby. There, we piled up the earth right at the edge, then widened the lip of the fractures, and the result? Ditches! The sort of ditches that have the tendency to snap ankles with lips of bottomless cracks."

"Fractures would not be common enough to cover the entire wall," Dweller said. "What stops the Veiled from simply avoiding those spots and climbing the wall elsewhere?"

Kalsin heard the grin in Skif’s voice. "Can't avoid them if you can't see them. Where no fracture borders the wall, we dug the same ditch, paved it with the same rocky earth, and filled it with the same water. Fake and real fractures connected, it all looks like a singular and continuous line of water. The jumbled pattern is by design, to give our guests a nice little confuzzle. You know, like an impromptu game of 'find the ball'? Except here, you gotta avoid it, or pay the price of a magnitude higher chance of drownage."

"To be fair," Brownweather added with a mutter. "Patterns of attacks have deviated towards locations without fractures. The easterners learn, and they remember some, much as we don't want them to."

"Heh, and then we shift men from the fractures to places without, figuring, why bother defending those spots if they don't attack there? Which results in an attack there. Say what you want about them, but fucking comical, the Veiled are."

There was a question Kalsin didn't catch, but Skif's answers always came loud and addressed to all of them. "Not many, true. We have to cover a large stretch of land with numbers we simply don't have. We're stretched thin."

"Isn't that sort of a problem?" Kalsin asked. “A lack of manpower?"

"Not for the Republic at large, it isn't. There's a common misconception about the Old, and the sooner you accept it, the better off you'll be; we're not defenders. We're no rugged band of wickedly dangerous fighters." He paused, voice solemn and a touch too dramatic in Kalsin's opinion. "We're a safety net. And the Veiled, they're rabid sharks constantly prodding and pushing, tearing out little pieces with each bite. The net holds them back for the most part. But if enough sharks come at once, the pitiful thing inevitably will tear against the overwhelming force.

"The Old, ey? The Hold would be a more fitting name. One horn means a Veiled has been spotted, be on your toes! Two horns, the Veiled are attacking, prepare for battle. And when three horns are sounded, that’s when our lookouts are signaling an approaching number of foes that we cannot hope to win against. Then, a runner is sent back to the Inner Line. You know what we do after that? We hold. Ha! We hold these lumps of dirt some people drunkenly named walls, knowing full well we're likely going to be dead by the time reinforcement arrives, but we hold all the same."

Weirdly enough, it was Brownweather who ended the heavy silence with a crooked grin. "I told you worthless lot already, haven't I? You're all fucked lucked, you really are, but take solace in the fact that the Republic could never defend the border without us. There's too much ground to cover, and having a designated line of men who know exactly where the enemy has breached makes the Republic as safe as it could be. It's a smart system."

"Vital little cogs," Kalsin murmured, "That keep an empire made of cogs above the clouds."

Shutter gave him a questioning look. Kalsin shrugged. The walk continued.

They kept going for one, then the shorter end of two hours. Though the scenery remained mostly uniform, red-and-white grass and dark earth, and a flat horizon that already promised several crimson sunsets, it was the human element that provided a semblance of variety. Storage buildings. Tiny stables. Odd, flat roofed barracks with training posts situated nearby, dark wood chipped from frequent usage and splinters trampled into the earth all around.

Wind lashed against the recruits and Kalsin breathed in a scent fresh and salty, the essence of the oceans taking him by surprise. The Kel Waters laid to the south of them, not close, but apparently still close enough to catch an occasional whiff. He looked out toward the expanse again, and noted with some relief the lookout spots had thin roofs over them. Getting soaked is not as pleasant as some would assume.

Activity along the Outer Line of Defense was constant, but it was more reminiscent of a flowing river rather than a buzzing anthill. No matter how far you walked, one could always observe groups of people doing what Kalsin figured they were supposed to be doing. Not many people, never crowded, but always there. Men low on the ground, bent over spent scraps of metal and scrubbing off rust. Men ringing their shields and swords against one another. Men huddled around thin fractures with sieves—sink, swirl, wash, repeat. And the idle ones. Adjust the sheath for the hundredth time, fiddle with that loose thread in the shirt, and keep looking East, over the earthworks. Watching, anticipating.

Skif nodded toward the side. There was a camp of lavish tents stretching a good distance inward, back enough from the wall that no danger threatened them. "Your Clenn counterparts. Looks like they know how to make themselves feel right at home."

Kalsin kept his eyes westward as they walked, watching the Clenn batch of recruits loiter among their considerably more luxurious makeshift camp. Their tents were a great deal taller than theirs, barely buffeted by the strong winds except for the colored strips of decorative fabric at the top that separated them based on surge. He was too far, but Kalsin liked to think he saw Jack and Cinder somewhere among the figures. They were there, he knew, but knowing and seeing are different brews for different ailments.

"Is there a reason why we’re stationed here, in particular?" Dweller asked.

"All this," Skif spread one arm like the showman in a circus, sweeping it across the general area. Kalsin noticed a set of claw-like scars peeking from his collarbone. "All this, give or take a sackful of miles, has been our busiest stretch to defend for the past few months. So what we do, then, is we plop the Clenn recruits that come every few months right at the center of our hot zones. Why, you ask? Because they're our strongest assets. Even greener than a frog's rubbery arse, a single overworld Clenn can save a hundred good men."

Youthere snorted. "They bitch about it, no?"

"To the contrary. You think the Clenn enjoy wasting their precious days in this sooted end of the world? With thousands of Scarrd as company? Psth. They'll be eager to join the first skirmish that happens at the walls, and then, after the killing's wrapped up, they'll be up and back to the Inner Line like fart on the wind. Gone, just like that. Then a few months from now, new recruits arrive, a Clenn squad included. The Scarrd stay and become older boys and die. The Clenn go back home. And rinse and repeat."

Kalsin's mates didn't like that. Silent, they shot cold glances at the far tents. Kalsin didn't like it much better, either, but he never felt the particular urge to give a public demonstration of his inner workings at all times. Instead, he followed Shutter's gaze, who was frowning toward the opposite direction and out into the Eastern Expanse.

Kalsin found himself in mute agreement with him. Now there, that carpet of red grass—swished and swayed by the wind like waves upon a dark sea. The things that lurked beyond. That was something to think about.

Skif said, "Not much farther now."

His statement turned out to be true enough, though their place of respite looked no different from the rest of the Old. The wall was a bit less steep here, sloping up gently enough for a person to comfortably stand their ground above the sudden, outward drop. No more than two dozen feet back inward, a series of huts had been built into the side of a rocky outcrop twice the height of a man. The whole picture resembled a miniature, artificial gorge. Or a valley between two mountains, or maybe a particularly wide and deserted alleyway.

Kalsin rolled his pen between his fingers. Not the worst place to be stationed at. Stuck between hard place and a hard place, but at least it had some character.

What remained of the day, the recruits spent settling in as Warmlight slowly dipped below the skyline. The vivid sunset was a hell of a sight, visible if you walked a hundred feet in either direction until the outcrop slanted and merged with the flatlands, and so it came as no surprise that many of them took the chance to see it for the first time. A thin, gentle strum of strings carried on the wind, courtesy of Fals perched on the slanted roof of a hut.

"No colors?" Crawl asked from behind.

Kalsin adjusted the sketchbook in his lap, frowned up at the sunset for a moment, then continued drawing. "Nope."

"That should be a criminal offense."

Kalsin said nothing. He was aware of things happening around him—a gathering of his mates sprawled on the ground, a few older boys coming and going along the wall, the crackling of an early campfire—but he paid them no mind.

"A sunset without colors is like dry soup. I don't see the point."

Crawl sat, carefully balancing two steaming bowls. The spicy scent of the meaty stew prickled Kalsin's nose when Crawl placed one of them on the ground in front of him. "Rescued a portion for you."

Kalsin gave a distracted hum as he drew. Then he looked at the stew and his pen stilled for a moment.

He blinked, and slowly closed his notebook. "Uh. Thank you, but I already got some food in me," he lied. He was hungry, but a sudden wave of nausea born of fear made him nearly retch, and the smell didn't help one bit. "I'm sure Littleguy would be happy to have it."

"Accurate enough," Crawl said with a laugh. He moved the bowl back to his side, and Kalsin's knotted stomach eased up a bit.

He did not see the red-haired ghost again, but… but could he trust the delusions of his own mind? Damn it.

Paranoia was an unhealthy medicine, one that prevented untimely deaths, but also ruined precious moments with its sour taste. It whispered ugliness. Posed uncertainties like, 'is there poison in my food?', or, 'watch out, surely there's a dagger behind the friendly handshake!'. Turned good things into bad things, all in the name of avoiding the worst thing.

Kalsin was aware of this, and yet he couldn't stop himself from casting a nervous glance around. No, there was no sight of the redheaded ghost. Part of him was distressed, because what sane person would expect to see ghosts?

And then another part of him, it was relieved. The part that had constructed a mad rationale; that maybe this ghost could only be seen when death was nearby. He had seen her first when his fate was being decided in the prison, right? The second time, with the poisoned thorns in his tent. So maybe she was a guardian angel, of sorts.

Only there was one simple issue with this line of thinking. Kalsin had been in dangerous situations his whole life, and he'd never seen the bloody girl before those events.

Fuck. He wanted not to be crazy so, so much.

Did the lack of the ghost appearing mean he was safe from poison? Was Crawl's gesture of goodwill truly that, and nothing more? Hell if he knew. But the seeds had been planted, and he couldn't help but see his mates differently now, filtering everyday acts of kindness through a lens of doubt. His fist curled in agitation. All this, because someone rich and mighty in the Civil House wanted him gone. All this, because they feared the possibility of Scarrd becoming Clenn.

"You heard about Quiescent yet?"

Kalsin uncurled his hand, smoothing out his trousers. He shook his head. "Not much to hear about, I don't think."

"Brrr. Right, I swear that man's body eats sounds for breakfast. Gives me the shivers." Crawl raised a spoon to his mouth, winced, and blew on the steaming mush before taking the bite. "Anyway, he and Leather used to be tight when they arrived. Only, they had a falling out yesterday. I barely caught the back end of the argument, but I heard the slur 'mythos' being thrown around." He stirred his bowl. Shrugged. "Wonder what's that about."

Kalsin snorted. Slur, right. A mythos hiding among the recruits would've killed at least a dozen of their numbers already, no matter how hard they tried blending in. It was in their nature. "I wouldn't read too much into it."

"You wouldn't? I thought you might. Since, 'yknow... I mean, it's not difficult to tell."

"Hm?"

"That you were a hunter, once. How long have you been doing that?"

Kalsin's idle thumb raked through the pages of his sketchbook. The sun had finished its retreat, but orange highlights still lingered to illuminate the distant clouds. "A while," he said. "Been a while."

There was a lull in the strings, then Fals' voice filled the air as she sang from the top of her hut.

I've seen the she red beaut',

On many times a day,

Brought an ode of things to come and play—and things to bring away.

I've d'mired the she red beaut',

And I've got much to say,

But never to her face because—because she'd turned away.

I've loved the she red beaut',

She trapper, me the prey,

To flaunt herself in front' my eyes—then take it all away.

I've reached for-the she red beaut',

Been rightly led astray,

Because she was a burning red—an'a million miles away.

The she red beaut' was never mine—and never she would stay.

Kalsin sat and listened among men and women, watching the sunset on the frontiers of the world with Crawl next to him, and Joe and Uggo on their backs not a stone's throw away, and Yesfellow, Crux, Shoreline and Ringtail and many others besides. Kalsin would've given a great volume of things to peer into the minds around him during those minutes. To know if the serene moment gifted them peace. To note, catalog the disparity between thoughts of past's nostalgia and future's hope. To see if the sets and tangles of the minds around him were similar, or entirely different from his own, or maybe weaving plans, still, to put a certain silver-eyed man in his early grave.

"Damn you," Crawl said, voice quiet, choked, and almost too low for Kalsin to hear. "Part of me relished it. So... ugly and sweltering. And I would do it again."

Kalsin didn't say anything, pretending not to hear the words that clearly weren't meant for him. If there were things to say, they would come out eventually, and all else would remain unsaid.

Kalsin preferred things unsaid. Better to let things lie rather than being forced into a corner to lie about them.
