They walked out of Azzopardi’s camp at first light.

Arthur carried the Ironhead in his right hand, twirling it appreciatively. He’d tested it on a dead cypress at the camp’s edge, and the trunk had split lengthwise in a single pop.

Steve was looking at the limestone underfoot.

“There’s a frequency in the rock,” he said, after fifty metres. He said it casually, like another person might mention the weather. “I can hear it through the soles.”

“What kind of frequency?” Arthur asked.

“Low and slow. Below what I’d have noticed yesterday.” Steve put his free palm flat to a flagstone they passed.

Donna did not turn her head. “Boys.”

“I’m walking. I’m walking and talking. I’m doing both.”

“Talking less. Walking more.”

They talked less and kept walking.

* * *

Ħaġar Qim came up through the dawn as a silhouette too symmetrical to be geological. The temple sat on the plateau in muted gold under a sky still the colour of bruised plum.

Arthur felt the node pull strengthen as they passed through the outer wall. The Ether pressure behind his eyes sharpened. Something inside the complex was waiting, and it knew he was here.

Steve led them through the trilithon entrance and into the connected chambers. He had spent the previous evening with the guidebook, cross-referencing the riddle against photographs and floor plans. He moved with the certainty of a scholar who had solved the academic problem and needed the physical site to confirm his theory.

The aperture was in the rearmost apse. A rectangle cut through a single megalith, thirty centimetres wide and twenty high, the limestone around it weathered to the colour of old bone. Through the opening, the sky was brightening toward violet.

“The window of bone,” Steve said. “Solstice sunrise enters through here and falls on one of the altar stones. But the solstice is months away.”

Steve looked across at Archie.

Archie’s geometry rotated toward the aperture. “Astronomical alignment was the original testing protocol. The Ether density required for node interface was only achievable at peak solar influx through the calibrated aperture and only for a fraction of a second.” A pause. “Current ambient Ether density exceeds the original design threshold by a factor of thirteen.”

Steve translated before Arthur could ask. “The temple was built and tested on the solstice because that was the only time enough energy concentrated to trigger the node. Now there’s so much ambient Ether that the candidate can force it.”

“Force it how?” Arthur said.

“‘Press the Root-palm to the pitted-heart stone.’ Fourth line of the riddle.” Steve walked to the altar in the central chamber. A broad slab of globigerina, its surface covered in erosion pits, thousands of small holes where rain had dissolved the softer limestone over millennia. The surface looked like honeycomb. “Pitted-heart stone. This is the altar. Root-palm is your hand.”

“Great, here we go touching stuff again. Get ready, something would try to eat our face in two minutes,” Donna said.

Arthur looked at the altar and then at his hand. The Resonance pull was concentrated here, dense and directional, pointing straight down through the stone into whatever infrastructure sat beneath the temple.

He pressed his palm to the pitted surface.

The stone warmed beneath his hand. Symbols resolved along the altar’s edge in blue-white light, Seeder notation spiralling outward from his palm. The Ether pressure spiked and his ears popped. The air in the chamber thickened until breathing felt like swallowing water.

The floor shuddered.

Donna had the axe up before the tremor finished. Steve was already backing toward the aperture apse, focus rod raised. Arthur pulled his hand from the altar and reached for the Ironhold, but the shuddering wasn’t stopping. It was deepening. The stones beneath his feet were vibrating at a pitch he could feel in his teeth.

Then the outer wall moved.

The fifty-seven-tonne megalith pulled free of its setting. Limestone dust cascaded from joints that had been sealed for five thousand years. The block beside it tilted, then slid, then lifted. Blocks from the apse walls detached and drifted toward the central chamber with the grinding patience of a glacier calving.

Limestone was assembling itself.

The megalith became a torso. Smaller blocks locked into place as limbs. The configuration was unmistakable. Arthur had seen the proportions in Steve’s photographs. The obese female form, the rounded masses of breast and hip, the vestigial arms, the featureless dome where a face should have been.

A Venus figurine the size of a building, assembled from the temple’s own architecture. She stood and the ground cracked under her weight. She filled the central chamber, dust raining from every surface. Dawn light through the aperture caught her flank in a band of gold and the Ether emanating from her was so dense Arthur’s Resonance read it as a physical force.

“Node guardian,” Archie said. “Threat assessment: Level twelve. Engagement is mandatory for activation completion.”

“Yes, Archie,” Donna said. “We can see that.”

* * *

The Mother’s arm swept the chamber.

Arthur took it on the Ironhold. The impact was nothing like a creature. This was architecture in motion, a stone beam swinging through a load-bearing arc, and the force drove him sideways. His boots carved trenches in the beaten earth floor. His shield arm screamed from shoulder to wrist and the Ironhold flared bright enough to throw shadows across the chamber. He held.

At STR 25, getting smacked had a new feeling to it. At STR 24, the same impact would have lifted him off the floor. At twenty-five, the force was absorbed by his whole body even though the impact was localized.

Donna went in, and her Reflex Acceleration fired. Her eyes sharpened into that terrible clarity and she read the guardian’s body - joints, gaps, weaknesses. The blocks were fitted, not fused. Seams ran between every stone. She found the widest seam at the shoulder and drove the axe into it.

The blade bit deep. A clean cut that would have severed any tendon in any creature she had ever met. The limestone closed around it.

The stone flowed like cold clay, sealing over the axe head, swallowing the blade into the guardian’s shoulder. Donna pulled, the axe didn’t move. She braced her foot and pulled harder. The limestone tightened.

“It’s eating my axe.” Her voice had the flat edge that on Donna meant fury.

She abandoned the weapon and drew the Ironspine. The guardian didn’t care. Its arm swept back for another pass and Donna dodged easily. Three steps clear before she realised that her body had moved.

Steve’s concussive blast struck the guardian’s torso. The fifty-seven-tonne megalith absorbed the impact like a cliff face taking a wave. The stone surface dented, then flowed back to its original shape.

“It’s reforming,” Steve called. “The damage isn’t holding.”

Arthur swung the hammer into the guardian’s knee joint. The impact drove a crater into the limestone. Before he could swing again, the crater filled. Limestone flowing back into the depression like water finding a level.

“We can’t out-damage its healing rate,” Steve called from the aperture apse.

“Working on it,” Arthur said, catching another arm sweep on the shield. His shoulder was losing feeling.

Donna went in again. Reflex Acceleration burst. The world slowed and she drove the Ironspine into the hip joint where smaller blocks met the leg assembly. The bar sank deep and the limestone immediately began to close. She ripped the bar free before it could be swallowed and rolled clear, the technique headache now a throb behind her eyes.

The guardian turned toward Steve. The vast faceless head oriented on the man with light building between his palms. Arthur slammed the shield against its leg to pull the aggro back. The limestone rang at a pitch Arthur could feel through his shield arm. The Mother turned.

Through the fading clarity of the second burst, Donna saw a gap. The shoulder seam where the axe was still embedded. If she could reach it, drive the Ironspine pry bar in beside the axe and lever, the keystone block would move and the whole arm assembly would collapse.

She triggered the third burst.

Then she went, and the world crystallized again as she crossed the chamber under the Mother’s swinging arm, low and fast, her body carrying her past two impacts that arrived where she had been. She drove the Ironspine into the seam beside the embedded axe and braced her boot against the guardian’s leg and levered.

The keystone moved about four centimetres. Then the limestone closed and held it.

The shoulder didn’t collapse.

Arthur swung his hammer to bring attention back to him and Donna rolled clear as the next sweep arrived. Now the headache was a knife behind her eyes.

She needed one more opening. The keystone had moved. If she could go in once more, drive the bar deeper, she might have enough leverage.

She triggered the fourth burst and nothing happened.

Her brain sent the signal. Her nervous system received it. And the technique did not engage. Three consecutive bursts without recovery - the neural wall. Her body had reached the limit of what accelerated perception demanded from human biology, and it had shut the door.

Worse was the pain that came along with the failed technique.

She saw the arm coming at normal speed. Normal, human, inadequate speed. A block the size of a washing machine, swinging in a lateral arc that her Dexterity could have outrun if her body was not racked with pain.

The impact caught her left leg below the knee. She heard the bone before she felt the second wave of pain. A sound that Arthur, twenty feet away, recognised from buildings: the sound of a beam exceeding its load capacity.

Arthur put himself between Donna and the guardian, shield raised, hammer up and triggered Structural Reinforcement. His body locked and the ground cracked as the guardian’s next swing hit the Ironhold like a demolition ball. The force drove through his locked joints and into the limestone beneath his feet. He held.

“Donna!” Steve’s voice cracked on the second syllable. He nearly lost the thermal spell building between his palms.

Donna was on the ground, leg bent wrong below the knee, her face white, reaching for the Ironspine with a hand that shook. Arthur was tanking a geological event. And Steve hadn’t solved the problem.

He looked at the guardian. Limestone. Calcium carbonate. It absorbed impact through deformation. It reformed because the crystal structure was intact. Every hammer blow rearranged the surface but the underlying lattice rebuilt from undamaged material below.

The lattice. The crystal structure. That was the target.

“Arthur. Keep it on you. Don’t let it turn,” Steve shouted.

Arthur struck the shield and the guardian locked on the sound.

Steve aimed the thermal spell at the knee joint. A focused, sustained stream of heat, channelled through the focus rod to a point the size of a coin. The limestone surface darkened, then whitened and then began to powder.

Eight hundred and forty degrees. Calcium carbonate decomposing into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide. The crystal lattice breaking apart at the molecular level. The joint that had been flowing limestone thirty seconds ago was now chalk.

While he held the stream, something low and steady pressed against his hearing. Below the thermal hiss. Below the cracking limestone. A tone that came up through the floor from the south, faint, sustained, more felt than heard. He could not look away from the focus rod. He filed the tone for later.

“The knee. Now.”

Arthur swung the hammer into the powdered joint. The calcium oxide exploded into dust. The guardian’s left leg collapsed. Half its mass hit the ground and did not reform. Calcium oxide didn’t become limestone again without water and geological time.

Steve moved the thermal stream to the hip joint. The focus rod trembled in his hands. Sustaining the temperature was draining his Reserve in visible increments, but the limestone was already whitening, the surface powdering beneath the heat. “Now.”

Arthur shoved with the shield and swung. The hip exploded into dust and the guardian’s pelvis separated from its leg. It staggered, balance gone, the vast torso tilting and trying to recalculate balance around the missing joint.

Faster than it should have been able to move with half its base gone, it reached for Steve, over Arthur's head. Arthur pushed upwards with his shield, deflecting the arm away from Steve. The guardian was learning.

“The shoulder!” Donna called from the ground. “My axe is still in the shoulder!”

Steve aimed. The limestone around the embedded axe head whitened, cracked and crumbled. The Conduit-Edge dropped free and rang against the floor and Donna dragged herself toward it.

The guardian’s arm fell. Without the shoulder joint, the block assembly that formed the limb had nothing to grip. Five tonnes of megalithic stone crashed to the temple floor as debris.

Steve was already burning the other shoulder. Arthur circled, hammer raised, watching the joint powder. The guardian was smaller now. Diminished. One leg, one arm, the faceless head turning to track them with Resonance that was weakening with every joint they destroyed.

Arthur broke the second shoulder and Steve burned the remaining knee. The torso toppled, the fifty-seven-tonne megalith hitting the temple floor with an impact that Arthur felt through his boots and up into his jaw. Dust rose in a cloud that turned the dawn light amber.

Steve was looking at the calcium oxide still settling across the temple floor.

“I just destroyed a five-thousand-year-old Neolithic religious monument,” he said.

“You destroyed a creature that was trying to kill us,” Arthur said.

“Yes.” Steve looked at his blistered hands, then at the powder coating everything within six metres. “Both things are true. I’m noting it for the record.”

The core crystallised from the last section as it failed. High-grade, dense with Ether, the colour of old gold.

“Mnajdra,” Steve said. He was standing in the aperture, looking south. “The sister temple. It’s responding.”

The tone held for ten seconds, then faded into the limestone.

“Then help me up.”

“You’re not walking on that leg.”

He picked her up.

She didn’t make a sound. Her jaw was locked, her fingers white on the Ironspine, her eyes wet but she didn’t make a sound.

They were halfway across the plateau when Azzopardi met them with two of his soldiers and a stretcher improvised from tent poles and canvas. “We heard the noise from the temple,” he said. “Didn’t know what was happening in there, but it sounded like a bad time, my friend.” He took one look at Donna’s leg and sent a runner south toward the compound.

“What are you doing?” Arthur said.

“Getting the doctor,” Azzopardi said. “Sarah. The Baron’s doctor. She’s the only healer on the island.”

The runner disappeared over the ridge. The Mnajdra tone had faded. But Arthur could feel it beneath his feet as they carried Donna toward the camp, a vibration so low it felt more like memory than sensation.

The sister temple could wait. Donna’s leg could not.

THE MOTHER

The Curators chose limestone for the Maltese temples. I have been treating that fact as quarry economics: soft stone, local supply. I have been thinking about it wrong. The material was not decoration. The material was the security architecture.

The guardian’s invulnerability was paired to limestone’s chemical structure, and the same calcium carbonate that built the temple was the substance the candidate had to dismantle to activate it. London and Marseille asked the candidate to break smaller things.

Malta asked us to grind the temple itself into chalk. Validate the activation by destroying the artefact. The Curators built sacrifice into the activation protocol. The destruction is the test. The figurines bother me more after today than before.

Five thousand five hundred years for the temples; twenty-five thousand for the figurine distribution. The architecture is local. The iconography is older than the architecture by a factor of four.  Either the Curators were operating long before they were built, or the figurines arrived through a channel that was running before the temples existed. I prefer the second hypothesis.

It implies the network predates the visible infrastructure, which would explain why we are finding nodes under sites that were sacred long before anyone wrote down what made them sacred.

✎ They did not build a religion. They built a sequence of sacrifices wearing religion’s clothes. Every site we activate, we burn. Every burn is the test.
