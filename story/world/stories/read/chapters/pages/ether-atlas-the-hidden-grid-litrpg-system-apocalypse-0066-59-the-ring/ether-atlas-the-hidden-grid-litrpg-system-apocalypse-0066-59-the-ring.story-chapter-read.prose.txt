Chapter 59

When the instruments cease to resonate, examine the hand that strikes them. The fault is rarely in the stone - Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

“Is it supposed to do that?” Sarah asked.

She was standing at the chamber’s edge, the Reacher held in front of her like a fence post between herself and the inscription. The blue-white letters were still brightening in the limestone, the glow pulsing with a rhythm that matched the vibration in Arthur’s boots.

“Yeah,” Arthur said. “We’ve seen it so many times that we forget how strange this must look to someone new.”

“Words appearing on a Neolithic floor?”

“In London it was a wall. In Marseille it was a cave. At Ħaġar Qim it was the altar stone.” He stepped off the threshold and the letters stayed. “The mechanism is consistent. When I touch the activation point, the inscription appears.”

“The mechanism,” Sarah repeated. “You say that like it’s plumbing.”

“In a crude sense it is plumbing,” Arthur said. “Alien plumbing. But plumbing.”

Steve was on his knees beside the inscription, pen moving, copying the lines into the ledger. His handwriting was getting worse and the pages were getting fuller and at some point the parish ledger Father Matthieu had given him was going to contain more fringe archaeology than church records.

“Scarred earth is the ruts,” he said, half to himself. “That’s clear. The cart ruts are conduit markers. We confirmed that on the walk down. Ringing with your hand is percussive activation. Acoustic, not contact. Different from the pitted-heart stone.” He tapped the pen against his teeth. “The question is ‘what answers.’ That implies call and response. You make a sound and something replies.”

He stopped tapping and looked up. “Arthur. When you activated Ħaġar Qim. What happened after the guardian fight?”

“The tone,” Donna said. She was leaning against the temple’s inner wall, arms crossed, her weight on both legs for the first time in weeks. “From down the hill. After the activation pulse, something answered from Mnajdra. You called it a sympathetic response.”

“Cross-site acoustic coupling.” Steve was on his feet now, turning between the inscription and the south-facing aperture, the pen tracing circles in the air. “Ħaġar Qim rang, Mnajdra answered. Now we’re at Mnajdra, and the riddle says to ring the scarred earth and follow what answers.” He looked south through the aperture, toward the scrubland and the haze beyond it. “Something further down the network is supposed to answer. Another node we haven’t found yet.”

“The ruts converge here,” Arthur said. He crouched at the threshold stone where the two channels met. The inscription glowed beside his boots. He put his palm flat on the limestone between the ruts. The vibration was steady, a low frequency running through the bedrock like current through a wire. “This is a junction point. If the conduit network carries vibration, this is where I’d put the signal.”

He pulled the hammer from his belt.

“You’re going to hit it?” Donna asked.

“The riddle says ring the scarred earth with your hand. The ruts are the scarred earth. This is where they meet.” He set the hammer head against the junction point. “If I’m wrong, all I’ve done is make a noise.”

“If you’re right,” Donna said, “everything in earshot just heard you knock.”

Arthur struck the stone.

• •

What came back through the limestone wasn’t the sound of iron on stone. It started as a ring, clean and bright, the note a tuning fork makes when it finds its frequency. Then the stone carried it. Arthur felt the vibration leave the hammer and enter the ground, felt it run through the ruts like current through a circuit. The ring travelled outward from the temple in every direction, following channels in the bedrock that the cart ruts had been marking for millennia.

The ring faded and nothing happened for a few long seconds. Then, from the south, something answered.

A low pulse rolled through the plateau like distant thunder trapped underground. Arthur felt it arrive through the soles of his boots, climb his legs and settle in his chest. Resonance. Something south of them, somewhere beneath the ground, had heard the hammer and sent a reply.

“The ground just answered you,” Steve said. His voice had gone quiet, the voice he used when something rewired his understanding of the world. “Arthur, you rang a bell and something out there rang back. Call and response through a subsurface conduit network that’s been sitting under these islands since before anyone built a temple on top of them.” He turned in a full circle, arms out. “They built a telephone out of limestone.”

“So what, we just head south?” Sarah asked

“The conduit response originated from a bearing of approximately one hundred and fifty-two degrees,” Archie said. “Distance cannot be determined from a single pulse. Triangulation would require additional strikes from different positions.”

“Or we walk south and keep striking until it gets louder,” Arthur said.

“That methodology is less precise but operationally functional.”

“Something’s coming,” Donna said. She was facing east, and Arthur followed her sightline to movement in the scrubland. Low shapes covering ground fast in the diagonal sprint of pack hunters cutting off prey. Goat-derived mutations. Bodies compact, skulls reinforced, forelegs built for charging. Four of them in a loose crescent, and they were steadily adjusting angles. Pack coordination.

“The activation pulse drew them,” Archie said. “Four contacts. Level eight to ten. Cranial plating consistent with secondary bone deposition. Standard impact weapons will be partially deflected by the skull reinforcement.”

“Sarah, behind us.” Arthur didn’t look back. He heard her feet on the limestone, moving without argument. Donna had the axe up. Steve’s hands were building light.

The first creature hit the temple’s outer wall at full sprint and launched itself over the megalith stones. Arthur caught it on the Ironhold and the impact drove him back two steps on the smooth floor. These were built for collision. He swung the hammer at the skull as it bounced off the shield. The reinforced bone turned the blow. The hammer skipped off plating that shouldn’t exist on something that had been a goat three weeks ago.

The creature scrambled upright. Arthur read its body and found the seam. The skull was armoured. The neck joint where plating met muscle was not. He caught the second charge on the shield, let the impact travel through his arm, and brought the hammer down on the neck joint. Bone cracked. The creature dropped.

The second creature came over the eastern wall. Donna triggered Reflex Acceleration. Her eyes snapped into focus and the world slowed around her. The creature’s trajectory was a line she could see before it completed, the arc of the leap mapped in her visual field like a blueprint. She met it at the apex with the axe, blade angled to catch the gap beneath the skull plate. The edge bit deep. The creature hit the ground and didn’t rise.

The third and fourth came together through the southern aperture. Steve’s concussive burst hit the lead creature square and staggered it backward, but the reinforced skull took the force. It stumbled, shook itself, and kept coming.

“Secondary bone deposition is localised to the cranial structure,” Archie said. “The remainder of the skeleton retains baseline density.”

“The head’s armoured but the body isn’t,” Steve translated and adjusted. The second burst hit lower, catching the creature’s chest.

Ribs cracked audibly and it went down thrashing. Arthur stepped forward and finished it with the hammer.

The fourth had gone wide when Steve had used the concussive burst and had looped behind the temple wall.

Arthur heard Sarah’s breath catch behind him. The creature was coming from the western wall, angling toward the one person who wasn’t holding a weapon like she knew how to use it.

Arthur turned and sprinted but the distance was too much. Twenty metres of open temple floor between him and the creature, and Sarah was closer to it than he was.

Donna threw.

It wasn't a perfect throw. This was more to buy time. The axe turned once and buried itself in the creature’s flank. It screamed and stumbled sideways. The charge was broken. Sarah backpedalled with the Reacher up, holding it across her body with white knuckles.

He was still too far away. He brought the hammer down on the shield’s face. He’d meant it as a warning, a distraction. A noise to pull the creature’s attention while Donna retrieved her axe. The ring that followed was nothing like a warning.

The Ironhold’s geometric surface caught the hammer’s note and amplified it, channelled it outward through the conduit network beneath the temple. Arthur felt the sound leave the shield and travel south. The same conduit response as when he’d struck the threshold stone. From the south, the answer came again.

The creature's head jerked up towards Arthur and it changed its angle. Donna reached it from behind and pulled the axe free. Steve’s concussive burst hit it a half-second later. As it staggered again, Donna buried the axe in its neck. Between the two impacts, the creature stopped moving.

“Arthur.” Steve said, staring at the shield. “You hit the Ironhold and the conduit responded. Same pulse as when you struck the threshold stone. Same direction.”

“And the creature came to him instead of going for Sarah,” Donna said. She was pulling the axe free, her breathing controlled despite the headache pulsing behind her eyes. “You made yourself the loudest thing around.”

“Two functions.” Steve was writing fast in the ledger. “Navigation and aggro pull. The shield-strike sends a signal down the conduit and draws creatures toward the sound. You hit the shield, the ground tells you which direction the next node is, and anything nearby comes to you instead of finding someone who can’t take the hit.”

Arthur looked at the Ironhold. The geometric patterns on its surface were still faintly luminous from the strike, the Seeder design catching light in ways that had nothing to do with reflection.

“Archie.” Steve looked up from the ledger. “The shield is coupling with the conduit network. A weapon Arthur forged from a manhole cover and an alien core is resonating with infrastructure that’s been underground for tens of thousands of years. How?”

“The Ironhold’s force-dissipation architecture distributes impact energy across its entire surface. The conduit network carries vibrational energy across distances.” A pause that lasted precisely long enough to mean something. “The candidate has discovered that these two systems are compatible.”

Sarah came forward across the temple floor and knelt beside Arthur, and this time the micro-hesitation between intention and contact was barely there. Her palms were flat against his left shoulder before he’d finished turning.

“Strained rotator cuff,” she said. “Partial tear in the supraspinatus. You swung heavy at a bad angle.”

“Yeah the hammer turned. The skull plating deflected the first swing.”

Her hands worked, the heat sinking into the joint and the burning ebbing. “What are the injury patterns usually like?”

“Blunt force from impacts,” Arthur said. “Lacerations from teeth and claws. Concussive feedback from Steve’s near misses.”

“Do they happen often?” Sarah smiled at Steve

“I prefer ‘Controlled adjacency’. The term ‘near-miss’ implies inaccuracy,” Steve said. “‘Controlled adjacency’ is more precise.”

“So soft tissue damage, fractures from the heavier creatures, internal bleeding if something gets past the shield.” Sarah withdrew her hands. The shoulder rolled freely. She looked at the four dead creatures and her jaw set. “I can work with that.”

Then she paused, blinked, and her eyes went briefly unfocused.

“I just got a notification,” she said. “Ether. A lot of it.”

“How much?” Steve asked.

“Three hundred and twelve. My Integration jumped from three twelve to six twenty-four.”

Steve’s pen stopped on the page. “That’s more than we got. We picked up about sixty each per kill. You got roughly eighty per kill.”

“Ether distribution during party-adjacent combat is weighted by Integration deficit,” Archie said. “Lower-level participants receive a proportionally larger share to accelerate convergence.”

“He’s saying the system gives you more because you’re behind,” Steve translated. “Catch-up scaling. The curve is steeper at the bottom. You’ll level faster than us for a while.”

Sarah looked south through the aperture. Every shield-strike would draw another fight. Every fight would close the gap.

“That’s how I catch up,” she said.

• •

The bearing was clear. The distance was unknown.

Donna was moving through axe guards, testing the repaired leg under combat loads. She’d pivoted on it twice during the fight and it hadn’t failed her. Her eyes were already on the southern terrain, reading it for approach vectors.

“Every time you ring the bell, they’ll come,” she said. “Strike, fight, walk. Strike, fight, walk. All the way to wherever this ends.”

“The alternative is walking blind,” Arthur said. “The conduit tells us when we drift off the path.”

“So we fight our way there,” Sarah said. She’d picked up the Reacher. Her hands were steady. “Strike, fight, heal.”

Arthur looked at her and ran the math. Level 7 in a party scaled for Level 12.

“Can you keep up?”

“I kept forty fighters alive on eighteen-hour shifts for three weeks.” She met his eyes. “I can keep four people alive until we get there.”

Arthur nodded and raised the hammer.

The first strike of the Walk carried south across the scarred earth, and everything that heard it started moving.
