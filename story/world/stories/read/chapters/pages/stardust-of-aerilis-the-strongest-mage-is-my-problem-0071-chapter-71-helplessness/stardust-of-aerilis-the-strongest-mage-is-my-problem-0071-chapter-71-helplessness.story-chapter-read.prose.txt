For a long while, no one in the meeting room spoke. The cloud-light through the portholes shifted by slow degrees, pale grey deepening into a quiet morning gold. Pelka had set fresh water in the cups without anyone asking, and now stood near the wall with her clipboard pressed against her chest, trying very hard to make herself shaped like furniture.

Lyra was the first to break the silence.

“I cannot stay much longer,” she said. Her hands had unfolded from her lap, and now rested flat on the polished oak. “I did not even mean to come this far.”

Aiven looked up.

“What do you mean?”

“I felt it,” Lyra said. “The seal breaking. I was the one who placed it, so I am the one who feels it the loudest when it tears. The moment the Loom-Breaker shattered, I knew. It was like a string snapping inside my chest.”

She lifted her hand, palm up, and turned it over slowly, as if examining it for some invisible thread.

“I used a teleportation artifact to get here. The kind that I’m not allowed to use repeatedly. I had... saved that one. For an emergency.”

Her eyes flicked toward Aiven, soft and apologetic.

“You were the emergency.”

Aiven swallowed.

“Does Lucien know you’re here?”

“I hope not.”

She said it without flinching, but her jaw tightened a little around the word.

“He told me about you. He told me he had come to you in the Fold and made his offer. He instructed me very clearly that I was not to interfere with your decision. He said you needed to choose, and that any voice in your ear would taint the choice.”

She let out a small breath that wasn’t quite a laugh.

“And yet here I am. Sitting at your table. Telling you to refuse him.”

Aiven’s hand tightened around his cup.

“Why?” he asked. “If he told you not to come, and if coming costs you... whatever it costs you... why are you so insistent? Why now, why this, why is it so important to you that I say no?”

Lyra was quiet for a moment.

“He told you about the world below, didn’t he?” she said. “The origin of the islands. The rotting continents. The dungeon cores eating the earth.”

“Yes,” Aiven said. His voice felt small in his own ears. “He told me all of it. The God of the Skies, the abandonment, the cores. He said he was the only one who could fix it.”

He hesitated.

“Was he lying?”

Lyra looked down at her hands.

“I cannot prove it to you,” she said softly. “I have not seen the bottom of the world with my own eyes. No one I know has, except him. But I have read the things he reads. The pieces fit. His theories are correct, as far as I can tell.”

She raised her eyes again.

“So I will not stand here and tell you he is wrong about the world.”

She paused.

“But let me ask you something instead, Aiven. Do you believe what he is doing is the right thing?”

Aiven did not answer right away.

He looked down at the cup in his hands. The water inside had gone perfectly still. He could see his own face in it, faintly. Tired eyes, a smudge of dust on his jaw he hadn’t noticed before. He thought about the question with the same slow, methodical attention he used to bring to a mismatched cargo manifest.

Was Lucien doing the right thing?

Honestly... he didn’t know. And worse than that, he wasn’t sure he cared.

He took a breath.

“I think... in the grand scheme of things,” he began carefully, “to some people, what he is doing probably is the right thing. He is technically saving the world. From an eventual rotting that nobody else even knows is happening. If he fails, everyone dies. Measured like that, his side of the ledger probably balances out heavier than mine.”

He set the cup down.

“But right and wrong... they’re different things, depending on where you stand. To the government, what they are doing is the right thing too. They exploit the dungeons. They use the cores for resources. They have built almost everything in Aerilis on top of that exploitation. The mana-rovers, the airships, the prosthetics, the lanterns in this room, all of it. To the Council, that is not greed. That is progress. That is keeping the lights on. That is the right thing.”

Lyra watched him without interrupting.

“So no. I don’t know what the right thing is. Maybe Lucien is. Maybe the Council is. Maybe nobody is.”

He looked up at her.

“For me, I just want to lead a good life.”

His voice cracked very slightly on the word good.

“When Hearthport went down, I... I learned something I never want to unlearn. That everything can be taken from you in a single afternoon. The buildings. The people. The plans you had for next week. All of it, gone, just like that. And I spent a long time after that not living. Just sitting in my apartment. Filing through what I should have done differently.”

He swallowed.

“I don’t want to live like that anymore. I don’t want to spend the rest of whatever time I have being someone’s tool, being somebody else’s plan, being a piece on a board because a man decided I was an interesting variable. I want to live without regret.”

Lyra tilted her head slightly.

“What does that mean to you?” she asked. “Living without regret.”

Aiven managed a small, tired smile.

“It means I want to see the world. The actual world. The one above the clouds, even if there’s a rotten one underneath. I want to take quests. I want to walk into a village I’ve never heard of and get told there’s a monster in the well, and I want to go fight that monster, and I want to be paid in copper coins and a hot bowl of stew. I want to be a hero in the small way. The way that fits inside a single afternoon. Not the way that requires me to swear loyalty to a man who treats lives like rounding errors.”

He looked at her.

“I don’t want to be tethered to a problem that won’t arrive for decades. Maybe centuries. I just want to live the life that I have, my way, while I have it.”

For a long moment, Lyra simply looked at him.

Something in her face changed. The tension around her eyes eased, just a little, and the smallest possible smile touched the corner of her mouth.

“It is good,” she said quietly, “to see you not being pessimistic for once.”

Aiven let out a breath that was almost a laugh.

“That’s because of you.”

Her smile faltered, became something softer and sadder.

“We still haven’t been able to go on adventures together,” he said. “We were supposed to. I keep thinking about that. I still hope we get to do it. Someday. The whole thing. The bad inn rooms. The map you can’t fold properly. All of it.”

Beside him, Virelle did not move.

Her eyes had not lifted from the copper spiral on the table for a long time now. Her hands, folded in her lap, had quietly migrated until her fingers were curled into the fabric of her skirt. Tightly. The translucent constellation-cloth bunched in her grip, and she did not seem to notice she was doing it.

Something was pressing in her chest.

It was not jealousy. She knew jealousy. Jealousy was hot and bright and easy to use. You picked it up like a knife and you waved it at whichever rival was foolish enough to stand near your Master, and the sensation gave you a clean, satisfying purpose.

This was not that.

This was a cold, quiet ache that had no name and no target. It was the feeling of standing in a room that was getting smaller, very slowly, in a way no one else could see. It was the feeling of being remembered last. It was the feeling of watching a story she had never been a part of be picked back up where it had left off.

She tightened her grip on her skirt.

She did not say a word.

Lyra drew in a breath and set her shoulders.

“I hope for that too,” she said. “I really do. But we need to deal with what is in front of us first.”

Her voice cooled a little.

“Lucien may be right about the world. He may even be right that someone has to do something about the cores. But his methods are extreme, Aiven, and he does not negotiate. If you join him, there is no room left for disagreement. There is no room for you to wake up one morning and decide you have changed your mind. Once you are inside his alignment, the only way out is the way he allows.”

Aiven nodded slowly.

“That is exactly why I won’t join him.”

Lyra closed her eyes for a moment, as if she had been holding her breath waiting for those words.

“Good,” she said.

She opened her eyes again, and now there was something harder in them.

“And there is something else you should know. Something he did not tell you.”

She leaned forward.

“Lucien is hiding something. The world below, the rotting cores, the calamity, all of that is real, but it is not the whole of his motivation. He has a... god-complex. He gives people chances. He measures them. He wants people to revere him. He wants to be the one whose hand they kiss when they are saved. He does not just want to fix the world, Aiven. He wants to be the one who is owed for fixing it.”

From her place against the wall, Pelka made a tiny sound.

It was somewhere between a squeak and an inhale. Her clipboard had risen a little higher in front of her face. She looked, for a moment, like she was deeply considering whether being in this room was a job-related necessity, or whether she should probably be outside.

Aiven’s mouth went dry.

“So you’re saying he plans to be the god of the new world.”

“Something like that,” Lyra said. “The past month with him has shown me things, Aiven. Things I cannot even bring myself to describe at this table. And the worst part is that I cannot truly leave his side. Not because I do not want to. Because of—”

Her body jerked.

It was sudden and small and wrong, like a puppet string had been pulled and immediately released. Her shoulders snapped forward. Her breath caught. A noise escaped her that was not quite a scream, more of a sharp, swallowed gasp, and then her arms gave out beneath her and she slumped forward onto the table.

“Lyra!”

Aiven was up before the cup he knocked had finished rolling.

He was beside her in two strides, one hand catching her shoulder, the other reaching for her face, lifting her gently. Her skin was cold. Her teeth were clenched so hard he could see the tendons standing out in her jaw.

BOOM.

The sound came from outside.

Not a thunder boom. Something heavier. A compressed, downward pressure that shook the floor of the meeting room and rattled the cups. Pelka let out a small shriek and ducked, her clipboard clattering. Vane was already moving toward the door, his hand on the hilt of the short blade at his belt.

Lyra clutched at Aiven’s sleeve.

“He’s here,” she gasped. Her voice was thin and ragged. “Time’s up. We have to go outside. Now. Quickly.”

“What’s happening to you,” Aiven said, his voice tight. “What is—”

“Outside, Aiven,” she breathed. “Please.”

He helped her up.

Her weight came against his side, lighter than he expected, like a person who had not been eating quite enough. He braced her with his right arm and let her lean into him. As they moved toward the door, he glanced over his shoulder.

“Pelka. Vane. Stay in the ship.”

Pelka was already nodding before he finished the sentence, her bangs flapping with the speed of it. Vane hesitated, his lion’s brow furrowing, then gave a slow, reluctant bow of acknowledgment.

Virelle rose from her seat without a word.

Her feet did not quite touch the floor as she drifted after him, her prismatic orb floating beside her shoulder, its pulse very low and very controlled. She did not say anything. She did not ask anything. She simply followed.

The morning air outside the ship’s landing ramp was cool and salt-tinged.

And in the middle of the clearing, a small crater had appeared in the grass.

Three meters across, smooth-edged, the dirt at its rim still settling in soft clods. In the center of it stood a single figure in a long, dark robe. The hood was pulled back. His golden eyes caught the morning light, and his hair was neatly in place, as though he had not just descended from the sky but simply stepped through a door.

Lucien.

Beside Aiven, Lyra’s breathing began to steady. The shudder eased out of her shoulders, the cold left her skin. Whatever had been wracking her in the meeting room had quieted now that she was within sight of the man who had caused it.

She did not look relieved.

Lucien smiled gently.

“Hello, Aiven,” he said. His voice carried easily across the clearing without seeming to be raised. “I hope I am not disturbing your morning. I have come to retrieve Lyra. If you would, please return her to me.”

Aiven’s jaw tightened.

He drew a breath to speak, and Lyra’s hand found his wrist before any sound could leave him. She squeezed once. Lightly. Stop.

He looked down at her.

“Do not,” she whispered. “Not now. Aiven, please. Just let me go.”

His mouth opened. Closed. He wanted to ask why. He wanted to ask a great many things. But her eyes met his and he understood, in the way he sometimes used to understand her in the back of her parents’ shop without either of them having to speak, that this was not a matter open to argument.

He swallowed it down.

“Lyra,” Lucien said again, and this time there was the smallest weight at the edge of his patience. “Come.”

She gasped. Pain lanced through her. Clean and immediate, like a blade pressed flat against the inside of her chest — not stabbing, just pressing, steady and merciless,

Her body buckled, her knees giving way. Aiven caught her against him for a fraction of a second before her hand patted at his chest, gently, telling him to release her. He let her go. He hated that he let her go.

Lyra looked up at him once. It was a long look. It was the kind of look people gave through train windows.

Then she turned and walked, with a stiffness in her steps, across the grass and into the crater. She came to a stop at Lucien’s side. She did not look at him. She kept her eyes lowered.

Lucien rested a hand briefly on her shoulder. Almost paternal. Then it dropped away.

He turned his attention to Aiven.

“I had intended to come to you in a few more days,” he said. “I thought it only courteous to give you time to consider. But something unexpected happened.”

His gaze touched Lyra and slid away again, gentle as a finger brushing dust from a shelf.

“So I came earlier. Now that I am here, I would like to ask, Aiven. Have you given my proposition the careful thought it deserves?”

Aiven looked at him.

He looked at the man with the gilded eyes who had described an apocalypse over a cup of tea and called it a partnership. He looked at the hand that had rested briefly on Lyra’s shoulder, and at the small, almost imperceptible flinch she had not quite been able to suppress.

“Yes,” he said. “I have.”

“And?”

“I will not join you.”

The clearing went very still.

Lucien’s expression did not change, but his head tilted, slowly, by a measured fraction.

“May I ask why,” he said softly. “Or is the answer simply that Lyra has planted some thoughts in your mind?”

“No,” Aiven said. His voice was quieter than he expected, but it did not shake. “Before she ever set foot on this ship, I had already made my choice. To put it simply, I do not really care about the fate of the world. Not the way you do. And I care even less about it when caring would mean losing my freedom.”

Lucien closed his eyes for a moment.

When he opened them again, there was something in them that had not been there during their tea in the Fold. Something cool and disappointed.

“If you are truly that certain,” he said, “then this is a great pity. You are a spark in the dust, Aiven. A rare talent. The kind of thing the world produces perhaps once in a generation.”

He let out a soft breath.

“And yet you have such a shallow mind.”

The air changed.

It was not a sound. It was not a movement. It was a pressure, a kind of attention, focusing toward a single point in the clearing. The temperature dropped a half-degree, the way it does in the moment before a spell unfolds. Virelle felt it before anyone else did.

Her hand was already rising. Lavender mana was already gathering at her fingertips, sharp enough to whistle, aimed squarely at the man in the crater.

“Master, no!”

It was Lyra who spoke.

She had taken half a step toward Lucien, her hand half-raised toward his sleeve, the urgency in her voice rough with fear. “Not here. Not now.”

Lucien stilled.

“He has unsealed his power with the Loom-Breaker,” Lyra said quickly. “And the Starweave is with him. A confrontation now would risk our plan. The timing is not right.”

Lucien turned his head.

Slowly.

He looked down at the woman who had just used his title, and his eyes were no longer warm at all.

“Is that not interesting,” he said quietly. “My Lyra, telling me when to act. While in fact, it is you who are foiling our plan with your mere presence here.”

Lyra dropped to her knees in the grass.

Her head bowed. Her hands rested flat on her thighs.

“Forgive me, Master,” she said. “I overstepped.”

Something inside Aiven cracked.

It was not the loud, dangerous kind of cracking, the kind that the Loom-Breaker had unmade. It was a quieter, meaner kind. A clerk’s kind. The kind that came from watching an injustice be filed and stamped and approved while you stood at the counter unable to do anything about it.

Lyra was on her knees in the grass.

Lyra, who had laughed in the back of her parents’ shop. Lyra, who had spent the many mornings trying to convince him to chase a dream. Lyra, who had reached across a table half an hour ago and rested her hand near his, palm up, an offering.

She was kneeling. In the grass. Apologizing.

His mana stirred.

He felt it move through the new throttle Lyra had set, felt the white roar of it pressing against the regulator from the inside. He could open it. He could open it right now. He didn’t know what would happen, didn’t know if he would be able to control it, didn’t know if he would be standing afterward.

He didn’t care.

A cool hand closed around his right wrist.

He looked down.

Virelle had not said anything. She did not need to. Her grip was light, almost weightless, and her eyes held his with a steadiness he had never seen in her before. She gave the smallest possible shake of her head. Just one, very slow, very firm.

Not now.

Aiven let out a breath he hadn’t known he was holding.

She was right. Of course she was right. Using his power now, half-trained, with no control, against a man Lyra herself was begging him not to fight, while Lyra was on her knees in the grass... it would be the worst possible thing he could do for her. It would be unwise. It would be reckless. And worse than that, Lyra would carry the consequences of it long after the dust settled. Whatever Lucien held over her, she had not yet been able to escape. Acting rashly here would only tighten the leash.

He unclenched his fist.

His mana settled, sullenly, back behind the regulator.

Lucien looked down at the kneeling figure beside him.

“Lyra,” he said. “Do you understand what is in store for you, when we return.”

“Yes, Master,” she said. Her voice was very small.

Lucien sighed.

It was a small, almost regretful sound. He turned his attention back to Aiven.

“I will find you again in one week,” he said. “I hope, by then, you will have changed your mind. I would still prefer this partnership to be voluntary, if at all possible. It is a better arrangement for both of us.”

He did not wait for an answer.

He raised his hand and snapped his fingers, once, with the casual motion of a man calling for the next round at a cafe. A jagged seam of violet light opened in the air behind him. Lyra rose from the grass without looking up, and stepped through it. Lucien followed.

The seam closed.

The clearing was empty.

Aiven stood there for a long time.

The morning wind moved across the grass. Somewhere on the ship’s deck, a turbine cycled down with a low, dropping hum. The crater Lucien had made was already beginning to feel ordinary, as if it were a thing that had always been part of the landscape.

He could not move.

It was Hearthport again. It was being a clerk in a delivery line who had looked up to find the world ending in front of him, and who had been able to do nothing, nothing at all, except stand there and feel frustration.

His hands were shaking.

Lyra was gone. Again. Just like that.

Behind him, Virelle stood very still.

Her hand had fallen away from his wrist some time ago, and now hung at her side, fingers half-curled. Her prismatic orb hovered close to her shoulder, dim and silent, its usual mischievous spin gone. She was watching her Master, watching the slope of his shoulders, watching the small, helpless tremble in his hands.

Inside her chest, something was tearing.

She knew the first part of it. That part was simple. Her Master was hurting, and she could not fix it. He was standing in the grass with his shoulders bowed, and there was no monster she could blast into cinders, no enemy she could humiliate, no clever cruelty she could perform that would put the smile back on his face. The girl was gone. Lucien had taken her. And for all of Virelle's celestial weave and theatrical violence, she could not undo that. She, who had erased boss-tier monsters with a flick of her wrist, was useless to the one person whose pain she actually felt.

That part was sadness. That part was frustration. Those were honest, and she could carry them.

It was the second part that horrified her.

Because somewhere underneath the sadness, somewhere quieter and meaner, there was a thought.

She was glad Lyra was gone.

Not glad in a clean, victorious way. Not glad in the way she might have been glad to chase off some other woman who looked at her Master too long. It was a smaller, uglier glad. A relieved kind. The kind that whispered: now the seat at the table is mine again. Now I will not have to sit beside her while she rests her hand near his palm-up. Now I will not have to watch him talk about going on adventures with someone who is not me. Lucien took her, and a quiet, wicked corner of Virelle had wanted him to.

She felt her own thought as if it had come from someone else.

It made her want to claw it out of her chest with her bare fingers.

She knew what her Master would think of that thought, if he could hear it. He would not be angry. He would be hurt. The kind of hurt that did not get put down again, the kind that lived behind his eyes whenever he looked at her after. And the idea of him looking at her like that was unbearable.

She was supposed to want what he wanted.

And he wanted Lyra back.

The two things sat inside her at the same time, refusing to resolve. The wanting and the not-wanting. The grief on his behalf and the small, secret relief on her own. They pressed against each other like two opposing tides, and the seam where they met was somewhere deep behind her ribs, and it ached.

She tightened her hand into a fist at her side.

She did not let any of it show.

She was, after all, the Master's mage. And the Master needed someone steady, right now, more than he needed anything else. The wickedness in her could wait. The sorting of it could wait. Whatever name this feeling eventually wore could wait.

For now, she simply moved one slow step closer to him, until her shoulder almost touched his arm, and she stayed there. Not speaking. Not floating. Just close enough that, if he reached for her, his hand would find her without having to look.
