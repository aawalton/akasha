Ning eventually reached Sector Three. Contrary to his expectations, it was neither a courtyard nor a cluster of buildings, but an entire valley. At a glance, he could see rows of farmland stretching across acres, with sect buildings tucked neatly along the slopes.

So this is the cliché of expansion. I really need to start adjusting to the size of things.

Ning added another task to his growing list of priorities: learn the geography of his surroundings.

The original Ji Ning had been nothing more than a countryside bumpkin. And in a sense, so was the current him. All he truly knew was that the sect he'd joined was called the Pure Qi Sect, and that its strongest cultivator was a Golden Core Patriarch.

The sect's territory supposedly spanned hundreds, perhaps even thousands of miles... yet that was considered small on the scale of the entire continent. And compared to the world at large, it was little more than a speck.

They say everything is bigger in a cultivation world. Turns out that wasn't a lie.

Ning didn't even want to begin unraveling the absurdities of physics in this plane of existence. Valleys stretched for miles, and mountains scraped the clouds without collapsing under their own weight.

He could already guess the answer to it all.

Qi. The source of everything supernatural.

Shaking his head, Ning soon arrived at an administrative building built into the hillside. It looked modest enough, though it still featured the distinctive pagoda-style architecture he'd come to associate with the sect.

Just like before, there was a line of people, so Ning quietly waited his turn. He deliberately kept his eyes down, careful not to stare too long at the paintings or calligraphy displayed near the entrance.

He had already learned that lesson.

"Next," the clerk muttered without looking up.

"Greetings, Senior Brother. I'm a new disciple assigned here." Ning stepped forward and slid the pristine jade slip he'd received from the elder onto the counter.

The clerk grabbed the slip, swiped it across the glowing formation on his desk with a practiced motion, and waited for the array to light up. Satisfied, he reached into a crate beneath the counter and tossed a small bundle of items toward Ning.

"Newcomer kit for Sector Three," the clerk rattled off, his voice carrying the sheer exhaustion of a fast-food worker during peak hours. "Contains the Small Cloud Rain Technique. That spell is your bread and butter for spiritual farming. The sect rules are recorded in the jade slips. Read them carefully, or you'll end up breaking a rule and getting expelled. Next."

Ning caught the items, blinking at the rapid-fire delivery. "Excuse me, Senior Brother, what do I need to look out for during spiritual farm—"

"Everything is in the rules. Read the jade slips, earn spirit stones, and visit the General Affairs Pavilion if necessary. There's also Old Zhou, your neighbor. He'll be responsible for teaching you what you need to know," the clerk interrupted, finally looking up with a dead-eyed stare.

He slapped a cheap, glowing paper talisman onto the counter.

"This is a directional guide. Follow the blue light down the western ridge path to Plot Forty-Two. Don't step off the main path, or the sector's defensive array will vaporize your legs. Next!"

Ning wisely took the hint, grabbed his starter pack and the blinking paper talisman, and bowed his way out of the office.

Well, that was a wonderful introduction to my new profession, Ning thought dryly as he stepped outside.

The talisman pulsed with a faint, rhythmic blue light, pointing him down a winding dirt path into the valley. He continued alone, passing several disciples who looked exhausted from a hard day's farming.

The solitary walk actually suited him. It gave him a moment to take in his surroundings. He watched distant senior disciples casting massive, shimmering arrays over fields of glowing grain, realizing just how insignificant a First Stage Qi Condensation newbie truly was in the grand workings of the Pure Qi Sect.

But hey, baby steps...

He followed the fading light until he arrived at a modest wooden hut nestled between two rolling hills.

An elderly man sat outside on a chair, a faint ripple of qi surrounding his fingers as he meticulously inspected a basket of dried herbs. His eyes lifted lazily as Ning's boots crunched against the gravel.

"A newcomer," the old man remarked in a thoroughly laid-back drawl. "And you navigated the ridge paths without triggering a single defensive array or getting lost. Not many manage that kind of basic competence."

Ning inclined his head politely, presenting his newly stamped registration slip. "Junior Ji Ning reports for duty, Senior. The registry clerk assigned me to this plot."

The old man took the slip, glanced at it, and let out a soft grunt. "The name's Zhou. Just call me Old Zhou. I manage this hillside, which means I'm stuck guiding you until you can keep your crops alive."

Old Zhou stood up, stretching his back as the faint ripples of qi around his fingers faded. He looked at Ning, then down at the bundle of starter items in the youth's hands.

"You look hungry. Go inside, wash those herbs on the counter, and chop the spirit greens. We'll eat first, then talk."

"Understood, Old Zhou," Ning said, immediately moving to help.

He had already realized that his current job involved being something of a yes-man.

Before long, the simple meal was ready, and Ning's cooking skills even earned an approving nod from Old Zhou. They set up a small wooden table and two chairs beneath a massive tree outside.

As the sun dipped below the valley walls, Old Zhou lit a small spiritual lamp and cracked open a sealed clay wine jar, allowing a rich, fruity aroma to spill into the cool evening air.

Old Zhou poured himself a generous cup and pushed the jar toward Ning. "Drink?"

"I'll pass for tonight, Old Zhou," Ning said with a polite smile, sliding his own cup aside.

He still had no idea what his body's alcohol tolerance was like in this life, and he wasn't about to compromise his safety on his very first night.

Old Zhou raised an eyebrow but didn't push. He took a deep sip, exhaling a breath of warm air.

"Smart. A cautious mind lasts longer on this road. Cultivation is a brutal business, brat. Even inside a recognized sect, you're constantly walking on thin ice. For the next five years, your main focus must be reaching the middle stage of Qi Condensation. If you fail, the sect will deem you useless, and you'll remain a peripheral laborer forever."

"I'll keep that in mind." Ning nodded seriously before leaning forward slightly. "What's the real deal with spiritual farming? The clerk who directed me here wasn't exactly talkative."

"You mean Clerk Shi? He's just upset about getting assigned to the spiritual farming sector, so he's being a bitch about it. He doesn't exactly take pride in being the clerk of this section," Old Zhou replied without the slightest restraint.

"Pride?" Ning asked.

Old Zhou let out a dry, mocking laugh. "Spiritual farming is one of the hundred professions of the cultivation world. It's necessary for everyone, but there's absolutely no glamour in it. For people like us with ordinary spiritual roots, it's widely considered one of the worst assignments in the outer sect."

Ning frowned slightly. "Is it really that bad?"

"Worse." Old Zhou's tone darkened. "Seventy-five percent of your total annual crop yield goes directly into the sect's granaries. You'll curse the heavens every harvest season, but you'll still hand it over down to the last grain."

"Seventy-five percent?" Ning's eyes widened. This time, his shock was genuine.

That's dark. No wonder no one lines up for this job.

"Don't look so miserable," Old Zhou grunted, noticing Ning's expression. "The sect provides the protected spiritual land, premium seeds, defensive formations, and even the spells required to cultivate them. Compared to independent cultivators outside, who pay eighty-five percent and only lease their fields, you've got the better deal."

Ning exhaled slowly, calming down.

He'd seen internships like this in his previous life: plenty of work, no pay, and a boss who claimed "experience" was the real salary. Compared to that, this arrangement wasn't entirely unfamiliar.

After all, the sect wasn't family. The sect was an employer.

"I know." Ning sighed, then said with a grin. "As the saying goes, no sword gleams without countless strikes at the whetstone. I just need to keep at it."

I'm glad I spent the past week writing down quotes. Surely, they're giving me a +10 bonus to every speech. Ning mused.

Old Zhou paused, his wine cup halfway to his mouth. He studied Ning for a moment before a genuine glint of approval appeared in his eyes.

"No sword gleams without the whetstone... Ha! Well said, brat. You've got surprisingly strong mental fortitude."

The old man chuckled, his gruff manner softening as he took another drink and pointed a finger at Ning.

"And look on the bright side. As a spiritual farmer, you're exempt from the sect's mandatory combat and scouting missions for an entire year. That time is yours to use as you please. Master your techniques, improve your fields, and build your foundation in peace."

Ning nodded slowly, letting the information sink in.

A whole year without interruptions... Ah! Yes, just the silver lining I needed.
