The question came from Jax.

He stood near the center of the obsidian platform with one hand on his weapon and the other lifted in a loose, sarcastic gesture at the void, the drifting frames, the parchment moons, the entire architectural nervous breakdown surrounding them.

“So,” Jax said, “aren’t you supposed to be the master of this dungeon?”

A few heads turned toward Kage.

Zherdev looked genuinely interested in the answer. Vami looked entertained. Zara looked like she already regretted being in a raid with both of them.

Kage gave Jax nothing. No look. No reply. He walked to the edge of the platform and watched a staircase decide it wanted to become a ramp for the third time in under a minute.

The geometry shifted again. Letters flowed together in the air to form a bridge. A patch of shadow slid across the structure, and half the glyphs rearranged themselves into a different sentence. The bridge sagged, corrected, then lost two planks altogether.

That part mattered more than Jax.

He crouched at the edge of the obsidian and watched the timing. The dungeon was already talking. Raids always talked. The good ones never shut up. Players just died because they kept trying to brute-force it.

“This room has an answer,” he said.

That got everyone’s attention faster than if he had snapped.

He kept his eyes on the shifting paths. “Raids frontload mechanics. Especially hidden ones. First room teaches theme, movement, pressure, or interaction. If we miss it here, it kills us later.”

Zara folded her arms. “Agreed.”

Finn stepped closer to the edge and peered into the dark below. “So the solution is somewhere on the platform.”

“Or on us.” Kage stood and turned back to the group. “The only things this place gave us directly were the marks.”

That landed immediately.

Everyone looked down at their hands.

The signatures still stood out against the strange light of the Gallery. The common-mark holders wore their chalk-gray smudges across knuckles and wrists, matte and dry, like somebody had sketched over their skin and decided to leave it there. Julie’s black geometric lines remained ruler-straight and crisp, elegant angles wrapped around her hands with the severe cleanliness of drafted architecture. Vami, Ren, and Zherdev carried luminous wet paint to the wrist, color pooling and curling over itself in slow currents. Kage’s own script floated above his skin in detached black letters that revolved around his forearm like obedient debris.

Every mark belonged to the place. Every mark had a purpose.

“Spread out, but stay on the platform,” Kage said. “Nobody tests anything alone near an edge. Call out any reaction. Movement, heat, sound, pull, visual change. Anything.”

Zherdev raised his marked hand and stared at it with theatrical suspicion. “My hand goo is still deeply concerning.”

Vami barked a laugh.

Kage ignored all of it and started organizing by mark type because chaos with ten people became comedy for exactly four seconds and then became a wipe.

“Common marks together first,” he said. “Lily, Zara, Finn, Jax, Valdrias. Move around the edges. Slow. Rare marks. Julie and I last.”

Jax pointed at himself. “You’re putting me in the disposable category already?”

“You volunteered the moment you started talking.”

That earned a short snort from Finn. Even Zara’s mouth twitched.

They moved.

Lily went left, careful and light-footed, gaze flicking between the platform edge and the hanging structures beyond it. Zara headed right with the kind of measured pace that suggested she was mentally building a chart already. Finn drifted after her, while Valdrias took the near side with his shield ready and Jax took the far side while muttering something under his breath about hidden art majors.

Kage turned to the three Rare holders.

Vami already had his hand held out at chest height, studying the luminous drops hanging from his knuckles. They were beautiful in an aggressive way. Liquid white with threads of pale gold, almost too bright to look at directly.

“When we got these,” Vami said, “the drips fell down.”

He tilted his wrist. A thick bead gathered at his index finger, stretched away from his skin, and hung suspended. It leaned past vertical.

“Now they’re falling somewhere else.”

Kage stepped closer.

The droplet extended in a clean line toward open air, not toward the platform below. It looked almost like a compass needle made of wet light.

Vami turned his torso ten degrees. The droplet swung with the motion, then corrected, pulling back toward the same point in space.

Ren was already repeating the test without being asked. His deep cobalt paint gathered at the edges of his fingers and drew itself into thin descending filaments. He rotated his arm through several angles, patient and precise. Each filament adjusted and settled toward the same distant vector.

“It holds a fixed orientation relative to the environment,” Ren said quietly. “Not to the body.”

Kage nodded once. “Zherdev.”

Zherdev extended his hand with an expression usually reserved for venomous wildlife. Crimson and amber paint swirled over his skin in luxurious, expensive-looking currents. A heavy droplet formed at his thumb, pulled sideways, and pointed into the void.

“Oh, that is deeply cursed,” Zherdev declared.

Kage stepped between the three Rare holders and looked outward along the line their marks indicated. The drips all angled toward the same general sector of the void. Not straight forward. Slightly up and left relative to the current platform.

Interesting.

Not enough on its own. Good raids layered mechanics. Direction without footing just meant the game wanted them to appreciate gravity on the way down.

He turned. “Rare marks point. Keep tracking the vector.”

“Point toward what?” Finn called.

“Later,” Kage said.

“Encouraging,” Jax muttered from the far edge.

A frame drifted past below platform level, carrying the image of a city street painted in broad wet strokes. One building on the canvas had never been finished. Bare linen showed through in a white block where a wall should have been. As the frame passed beneath them, its false sunlight cast a new angle of shadow through the void. A staircase to their right twisted into existence in response, each step emerging from darkness as if developed in a chemical bath.

Lily stopped.

“Wait,” she said.

Everyone turned toward her.

She stood near the text bridge on the left side of the platform, one foot a pace from the edge. The shifting letters nearest her had slowed. Kage saw it immediately. The script that had been slipping, rewriting, and reordering every few seconds had settled into place around her.

The nearest plank held its shape.

Then the next.

Lily raised her marked hand. The pale gray charcoal on her fingers had deepened, the dry smudge brightening at the edges with a faint chalk-white flare.

“My mark’s doing something,” she said.

The whispering sound of the bridge changed. Before, the letters had hissed over one another like paper rubbed against paper. Around Lily, the noise flattened into a dry stillness.

Kage crossed the platform at once.

“Don’t move yet.”

He came up beside her and looked down. The bridge extended six meters from the obsidian edge before dissolving into shifting script again. The section nearest Lily stood firm, each letter fused into hard black strokes with pale dust gathered in the seams.

“What changed?” he asked.

“I just got close,” Lily said. “It warmed up. Then the bridge stopped... doing that.”

Zara approached from the side, eyes narrowed. “Can you step back one pace?”

Lily did.

The effect faded almost instantly. The letters shivered. One of the planks blurred at the edges and began to break apart into individual glyphs.

Kage lifted a hand. “Forward again.”

Lily returned to her previous spot. The plank steadied. The letters set. Gray dust rose from her mark in a soft dry curl and sank into the bridge.

There it was.

Finn leaned in, interest overtaking caution. “Radius-based stabilization.”

“Looks like it,” Zara said.

Jax came over, skepticism still intact but losing ground. “Or the dungeon just likes healers.”

“Go stand there and prove your personality matters,” Zara said.

Jax opened his mouth, thought better of it, and walked to Lily’s place when she stepped back.

The bridge destabilized during the transition. Glyphs peeled loose and spun once in the dark. Then Jax’s chalk mark flared over his hand and wrist. The letters nearest him froze into place.

His eyebrows rose. “Huh.”

Valdrias took the next turn. Same result. The section nearest him locked solid, broad enough for a careful footing. Finn followed. Then Zara. Every common-mark holder produced the same effect. The exact dust patterns varied, the flare looked a little different on each hand, but the result held.

Kage watched each test with the same clipped focus he brought to combat. The room had finally given up one of its teeth.

“Common marks stabilize unstable geometry,” he said.

Nobody argued. The evidence sat in front of them as a bridge that kept deciding whether it believed in itself.

Zherdev brightened. “We’re useful.”

Julie gave him a sidelong look. “You’ve been useful for minutes now. Try not to make it weird.”

“I make everything weird. It’s branding.”

Kage ignored that and moved to the newly manifested staircase on the right side of the platform. It was in the middle of another identity crisis, the steps flattening into a slick incline and then dividing again. He gestured to Valdrias.

“Test this one.”

Valdrias stepped forward, shield on his arm, posture steady. The chalk-gray mark on his hand flashed. The section of stair nearest him locked into proper steps at once. Clean risers. Hard edges. Solid stone.

He put weight on the first one. It held.

Then he climbed three steps. The staircase stabilized around him in a moving bubble of certainty, each step freezing into place a moment before his boot touched it while the section above him kept trying on new shapes.

Zara watched closely. “Localized effect. Structure remains unstable outside the radius.”

“Means we can create a path as we move,” Finn said.

“And lose it when we leave it,” Jax added.

Kage filed that away. Important. The room wanted active participation, not one-time activation. Good. Annoying, but good. That kind of rule tended to scale across the raid.

He looked back toward the Rare holders. Vami still had his hand extended, paint dripping toward the same distant line through the void. Ren’s mark pointed there too. Zherdev’s followed, though his hand shook slightly because he was trying very hard not to stand too close to any edge.

“Where?” Kage asked.

Vami angled his chin toward the left-front quadrant of the Gallery. “Still the same. Slightly higher than our platform. Maybe through that chain of frames.”

Ren checked again and nodded. “Consistent.”

Kage turned to Julie.

“What about yours?”

She had been studying her hands rather than the environment, brows drawn in concentration. Up close, the black lines of her mark looked impossibly exact. No smear. No bleed. Just clean geometric precision laid over skin like the world’s strictest sketch.

Julie flexed her fingers slowly. “Nothing obvious.”

“Feel anything?” Zara asked.

Julie considered, then shjook her head.

She lifted one hand toward the shifting bridge. The black lines on her wrist stayed perfectly still. The bridge continued whispering and reordering itself beyond the stabilized section.

“Join the club,” Kage said.

Everyone looked at him.

He glanced down at the archaic letters orbiting his forearm. His own mark had been active from the moment they entered. That much was obvious. The script moved constantly, slow and detached, circling him as though his arm were the spine of a tiny black storm. It reacted to the environment in ways too slippery to pin down. When he faced the bridge, a few letters drifted toward the shifting text. When he looked at the staircase, some of the characters pulled toward its seams. The response felt broad. Conceptual. Useful later. Meaningless right now.

He stepped to the edge of the bridge and held his marked hand out over the changing script beyond Lily’s stabilized section.

The letters around his arm lifted.

Several broke orbit and stretched toward the bridge in thin black trails, as if trying to read something written inside the structure itself. For half a second the shifting glyphs beneath them sharpened. He caught a fragment of archaic text. Three words. Then the bridge rewrote itself, the meaning slipped, and his own orbiting letters returned to his arm.

That was all. No floor. No doorway. Just a hint. Irritatingly on-brand.

“Well?” Jax asked.

Kage looked at the bridge. “Later.”

Jax spread his hands. “You keep saying that like it’s a plan.”

“It is.”

Zara stepped in before the conversation could degrade into something louder. “We have enough to move. That’s the priority.”

She was right.

Kage took another long look at the room and fit the pieces together.

Common marks created footing. Rare marks provided direction. One group told them where they were allowed to stand. The other suggested where the raid wanted them to go. That solved the immediate problem. It also screamed design philosophy. The dungeon had divided the raid into functional roles before the first pull. Foundation. Orientation. Something else for Julie. Something else for him.

The place was a machine made of unfinished ideas, and it had already started sorting them into parts.

Fine, he thought. We can work with that.

He spoke louder so everybody could hear him clearly.

“Here’s what we know. Common marks stabilize shifting structures in a local radius. Rare marks point toward a consistent direction. We use both. Common holders create the floor. Rare holders keep us aimed.”

Julie looked from the bridge to the floating frames and back. “What order?”

Kage answered immediately, then stopped.

“Va—No. If we stack the Common marks at the front, the bridge dissolves under the back half.” He pointed along the span. “The gray marks stabilize a local radius. We go interleaved. Anchor, passenger, anchor, passenger.”

Kage continued. “Valdrias first. He’s the tank, and if the bridge reacts I want the shield eating it. Vami second. Lily third. Ren fourth. Zara fifth. Julie sixth. Finn seventh. Zherdev eighth. Jax ninth. Common holders keep the structure stabilized the whole length. Rare marks keep calling direction.”

“Hey,” Zherdev said. “I can fight.”

“Yes,” Kage said. “Somewhere in the middle between two stable sections.”

Lily actually smiled at that one.

“And you?” Zara asked.

Kage looked once more at his floating script.

“Rear,” he said. “If anything gives way, I want the back end.”

Jax pointed toward him. “That sounds made up.”

“It’s a raid plan,” Kage said. “It's always made up until it works.”

That, annoyingly, shut him up.

They gathered at the left edge where the text bridge formed and reformed itself over the void. Up close, the thing felt stranger. Each plank consisted of compressed archaic lettering woven into a surface just broad enough for a boot. Some of the words were incomplete. Some had too many strokes. Others looked like the same symbol copied by someone whose memory had started to fray. Beyond the nearest stabilization radius, the railings rippled and the bridge surface flowed like language trying to remember the shape of wood.

The drop beneath it vanished into dark distance. Frames drifted below like severed windows, each containing some separate unfinished scene. A shoreline. A ballroom. A field under a green sky. A library with its back wall missing.

Zherdev looked down once and made a face. “I hate heights.”

“Keep your eyes up,” Julie said.

He obeyed instantly.

Valdrias lifted his marked hand. The chalk-gray smudge across his fingers brightened, and the first span of bridge fixed into place with a dry hiss. Stone would have felt more reassuring. Language would have to do.

He glanced back at Kage. “Ready?”

Kage looked past him to the direction the Rare marks indicated.

Vami’s paint leaned toward a broken corridor of frames drifting in the far dark. Ren’s matched it exactly. Zherdev’s droplet stretched the same way, wobbling with his breathing. Three compasses. One answer.

He still didn’t know what his own mark did here. He still didn’t know what Julie’s did either. He had a very strong suspicion both would become everybody’s problem later.

For now, the dungeon had given them a floor and a direction. That was enough.

“Move slow,” he said. “Call every change. If the vector shifts, speak. If the bridge changes, speak faster.”

Kage stepped to the side and gave Valdrias the lane.

He placed his boot on the first stabilized plank.

The bridge held.

A second step. Another flare of pale dust from his mark. The script ahead of him dried into solidity.

Vami followed.

Then Lily.

Then Ren.

The bridge wrote itself firm beneath their feet, one safe section at a time, while beyond them the rest of the Gallery turned and revised and waited.
