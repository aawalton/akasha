During my few years in this strange world, I had fought dragons, gods, demons, kings, and whatever the hell Bagha Hamid counted as. Somehow, the most annoying enemy so far was Goryeo's land registry.

The branch was nearly done. Workers carried the last planks through the front gate while a tile-layer argued with his apprentice about alignment. Two Goryeo earth mages spent the morning reinforcing the rear foundation. A fire-aspected craftsman treated the upper beams against rot, muttering every time someone got sawdust in his sealwork.

The building looked like exactly what it was. A converted noble's manor in the worst district of Hwangcheon, repaired with too much skill and, somehow, not enough paint. The old manor no longer looked abandoned, but it had not yet begun to look safe.

I drove a nail into the doorframe. With the hammer, not my thumb.

The lone-star sigil of the Heavenly Demon Divine Cult would hang over this door in a day or two. I was planning a food office on the ground floor, a clinic in the rear, and a training hall upstairs. First recruitment would begin once Senna sent a small advance team through the Bifrost.

I was not exactly making a martial training place. Goryeo needed more than that, and as the ruler of Nevaramis, I had more. Be it food or medicine, or training for people who had spent their whole lives believing that power belonged somewhere else.

The idea of this particular branch of the Heavenly Demon Divine Cult here was to make people dream of a future where they were not the side characters. And then make that dream come true.

That idea of mine had not changed even after Ha-Yun's report yesterday.

Lord Fang Yizhen or whoever the Emperor was sending did not carry enough danger for me to stop hammering the nails.

Lilian sat cross-legged atop the lumber pile that had become her observation post. She was chewing roasted chestnuts and watching the street with the focused casualness of someone who could hear a heartbeat change from across the road.

"Have you noticed? The workers aren't scared anymore," she said, wearing a smug little smile that showed one fang.

I looked past her at a mason carrying mortar with both hands and no tremor in either wrist. "That's good news."

"Mm, I'm not sure. Don't you know what that means?" Lilian flicked a chestnut shell between two fingers. "It means either you're becoming far less scary and threatening, or this district has terrible survival instincts."

"At this point, it's probably both."

She threw a chestnut shell at my head. It bounced off my ear. Why was she trying to annoy me? I ignored her, set the hammer down, and leaned against the frame.

Tomorrow, Fang Yizhen would arrive.

A goodwill envoy from the Baolian Dominion, carrying the weight of an empire that had erased kingdoms for lesser provocations than a cult branch in a neighbor's capital.

I was not panicking. Panicking was for people who had not already survived an Arcane King ripping their heart out of their chest. I was waiting, patiently, to test my current limit.

More than this envoy, I was worried about something else. My mind wandered sideways.

To Valeria Nocturne, the Whisper That Burns. The Pishachini demoness who had died for Lilian and came back wrapped in a blanket in Nevaramis had become mine. Even though she had served the Demon King previously, she was currently under my roof back in Nevaramis, eating my food and most likely annoying my vampire fiancee.

The biggest problem right now was Emperor Shengzong, no denying that, but I still had to keep my eyes and ears open for others. The Demon King did not have an Arcane Crown, but there was a reason he was considered such a danger across all continents.

His shadow had not vanished because I decided Valeria belonged with us. She had been his for so many decades, while I had known her for weeks. That math was not flattering, and pretending it did not matter would be stupid.

What I had done was put my hand over the Demon King's claim and dared him to pull. Courageous of me, for sure, but perhaps arrogant too. I had not reached the very end of the game, [Arcane Crown], but the narrative had hinted that the Demon King was the final villain. If that was the case, he was not to be underestimated.

On that topic, there was something else to consider. The new Arcane King, or "Arcane Queen" as she liked to call herself. My old friend Rhydar Sylvenel's mother, the Elven Queen of Sylvanielle.

Rhydar and I were not best friends, but he had indeed gifted me the valuable Elven Spirit Pendant that allowed me to bond with Vyrn. So if I could make use of our connection to land a meeting with the Elven Queen, I might earn her support as well.

There were a lot of shadows here. From having played the game, I knew about the great hatred the Elven Queen carried toward the Demon King. That meant she probably hated me too, as I was the inconveniently titled "Heavenly Demon," but if I could just team up with her, the Demon King would become less of an immediate worry.

As crazy as it might sound, the Elven Queen would most certainly die if she challenged the Demon King now. Yes, even with the Arcane Crown.

That's such a messy situation.

I sighed, returning my focus to Goryeo. I could not wait until a direct fight with Emperor Shengzong before addressing the rest of the world. As the Maharani instructed, I should start by befriending the Valtherians of the Volcanic Islands.

That was the plan after finishing this construction.

Shelter. Huh, shelter was a nice word. So was protection. It reminded me of Ha-Yun's complicated expression. People liked nice words because they made heavy things easier to carry.

But Goryeo's ministers saw no shelter. They saw a foreign sect leader hammering nails into their capital while an Arcane Queen's communication orb sat in their Monarch's pocket.

Which, annoyingly, was not an unfair thing to see.

"Why are you role-playing as a statue? Are you brooding over a plank?"

I blinked myself awake and looked at Lilian. "It's, uh... the front door of our cult branch. People are going to talk shit behind our backs if I don't think properly about it."

Lilian stared at me for a second, then tossed another chestnut into her mouth. "You're such a weird guy. Anyway, are you done? The tile guy has been looking at you for two minutes. I think he wants to report on the tiles or something."

He was, in fact, shivering as he hesitated reporting something. His apprentice had misaligned three rows.

I went to fix it. Easier than fixing the rest.

While Iskandaar Romani was having the time of his life with his former maid, enjoying a fun morning hammering wood, the same could not be said for the ruling class of Goryeo.

Especially the Monarch and the Crown Princess.

Jin Ha-Yun stopped at the door to her father's study.

Her eyes fell on the table. There were bottles of cheongju lined up. Her father was holding the cup.

Monarch Jin Ha-Im drank at banquets and after victories. He drank when foreign envoys needed polite lies and Goryeo needed everyone smiling through them. He did not drink before noon with petitions still unopened beside him.

Jin Ha-Yun, the Crown Princess of Goryeo and Avatar of the Nine-Tailed Fox, had seen him face assassins during the Jade Rebellion. She had seen him read the casualty reports after the Xianli border collapse. She had watched him sit through the night her mother took ill and the royal physicians went silent one by one.

He had not drunk during the day for any of those.

That realization made Ha-Yun freeze. The report she carried suddenly felt heavier. She was here to tell him about guard rotations, Fang Yizhen's expected route from the harbor, and the seating arrangement for the reception banquet. Normal and necessary things. No matter the situation, a kingdom still failed if these things were done poorly.

She had come to speak of danger.

And yet she found her father drinking, eyes dark.

Ha-Yun cleared her throat and stepped inside, closing the door. She set the papers on the edge of the table, far from the cheongju, and sat in the chair across from him without waiting for permission. A breach of protocol. She did not care.

Her father noticed her eyes on the cup. "Do not start, Ha-Yun. It is one cup."

"It is still daylight, Father."

"Yes. I noticed."

He took a small sip and set the cup down. His golden aura, the Heavenly Mandate that had hummed through the palace stones for four centuries of Jin rule, lay quiet around him. That was worse than if it had been flaring.

A king whose power rested easy was a king at peace with his body. A king whose power went silent was a king whose mind was somewhere else. At times such as these, that was terrible. So she gave her report directly, in the hope of bringing his attention sharp.

Among other things, she also shared something she would have considered trivial if she had not seen him drink. She told him about Minister Zhou's proposed speech of welcome, which was three pages too long because Minister Zhou believed formality was a weapon.

Her father listened. Asked two practical questions about the harbor route, corrected one detail about the banquet hall's sight lines because he remembered a renovation that the steward had forgotten.

He was still the Monarch. She needed to remember that.

Then he set his cup down and looked at her. "What does Iskandaar Romani want after Fang Yizhen leaves?"

The word "leaves" carried a lot of possibilities. Either Fang Yizhen would leave without issue, or he would leave defeated. Regardless, Ha-Yun answered before she had finished choosing a gentler answer. "You know, he wants to help."

The words came out clean and confident and she believed them. She had believed them since the night Iskandaar had sat in her gazebo with a sea map showing some volcanic islands, that day when he was waiting for her as if showing up uninvited was what friends did.

Her father let the answer sit between them.

"I think," he said, slower now, "that you may be right. Maybe he does want to help us."

Ha-Yun's grip loosened around the edge of the report. Only for a second.

He added, "That is the part I dislike."

She stared at him.

"Take a moment to consider this, Ha-Yun.” He said, which confused her. “If Iskandaar Romani were lying, I would at least know what to do with him." Her father turned the cup between his fingers. The rich, golden aura around him stirred once, then settled. "Lies leave handles. But a sincere man is harder. You saw it, when the Maharani of Aryavarta answered his call and backed him up. He's someone with a demigod's power and an Arcane Queen behind him. Believe me, I trust him when he says he wants to save us from Shengzong."

Ha-Yun lowered her eyes. "Then?" she asked, despite already reaching the answer. "Well I... I know what you're implying, but he is not like Shengzong."

"No." Her father's voice gentled, which was unfair of him. "He is not. Shengzong would never build a clinic in our poorest district."

He paused.

"But kingdoms do not survive on intentions, Ha-Yun. They survive pressure. Like who applies it and who bends. Who thanks the hand that pushed them because the other hand held a sword." He touched the rim of the cup. "Iskandaar Romani may be kind. He is still heavy. So we cannot count on him to be kind forever."

Heavy was an inelegant word. She wanted to argue. She knew what to say, but the words simply did not feel true enough to say.

Because she had seen it herself. The branch rising in the eastern district. The Maharani's orb in her father's pocket. Palace attendants whispering about the golden-eyed foreigner who worked shirtless among commoners and somehow made charity look like conquest. Minister Zhou's face when Heavenly Demon appeared in a draft memorandum beside Goryeo.

Every noble house in the kingdom would calculate. They were already calculating.

"This is what I'm saying. Let's assume... that he succeeds," her father said. "Shengzong is maybe defeated somehow, and that's a big maybe. More likely is that he turns his attention elsewhere. Fang Yizhen drinks our tea, smiles, and simply leaves with every man he brought. Without shedding blood." He looked at the report she had carried in. "What remains after that?"

"Goryeo," Ha-Yun said too quickly.

He looked at the cup. "...Right," he said. "Yes, you're right. But what kind of Goryeo will that be?”

“....”

“One that survived because its Monarch held the line, or one that survived because a foreign world criminal stood in front of us?"

Ha-Yun looked at the old table, at the cup her father had set down, and at the report written in her own hand. For the first time that morning, the report looked small.

Ha-Yun had no answer.

She trusted Iskandaar. She really did. That trust had not come from Lady Yueling's blessing, or from some court calculation she could defend in a council chamber. Her father would get angry if she talked about her gut in a matter of state, but it was true. In her gut, she did not believe Iskandaar wanted to harm Goryeo.

This gut feeling had come from a boy in a gazebo holding a map showing volcanic islands. That day, before the meeting with the minister and her father, Iskandaar and she spent half an hour in that gazebo. He was laughing, telling her about his plans, sharing national secrets with her in such a light manner as if they were old friends… as if showing up to save her in her direst times was a perfectly normal thing to do.

That was personal trust.

Goryeo could not put that into a treaty. Her father sighed and reached for the cup again.

Ha-Yun leaned forward and took it from his hand. As his daughter first, not as the Crown Princess.

He looked at her.

She set the cup on the far side of the table, out of reach, and placed the guard rotation report in front of him instead.

"You can drink later, father," she said. "Fang Yizhen arrives tomorrow. Even if we're hoping the Heavenly Demon will take care of him if a battle breaks out, at the very least we must fulfill the political sides. We are the monarchs ruling Goryeo, you know?”

He stared into her eyes. Then a sound left him that was almost a laugh. "You know, your mother used to move the bottle first too."

"I always knew she had sense."

They worked until the light moved across the table and left the cheongju untouched.

Ha-Yun realized sometimes. Her father was scared Iskandaar was going to ruin Goryeo. That he'd live up to his demonic title and devour this nation. She didn't blame her father for being worried about such a thing, but she knew the truth.

Iskandaar Romani was a devourer, death for his enemies. But for those that he cared about, he could be the tallest wall in the world. And Ha-Yun was sure he cared about her.

The next day, the Third Flower stirred before my ears heard anything.

I was inside the upper frame of the branch, fitting the last window shutter into its groove, when a thread of intent brushed the edge of my perception. It came from the harbor road, precise and measured. The Demonic Sphere bloomed wide, confirming what the threads already showed.

A formation of people was approaching. Twenty bodies in disciplined spacing, most of them martial artists, armed under their robes.

One presence sat at the center, making the Sphere hum a warning against my ribs.

[Fang Yizhen, Lord of the Sunlit Edict, Level 208]

9th Ascension.

Aha. So that was the level of goodwill Baolian preferred.

I set the shutter into the groove, pushed it flush, and felt the frame catch cleanly. At least one thing today knew how to line up. Lilian was already standing with one hand loose at her side, the other still holding a half-eaten rice cake. Her ears had gone flat under the cloth wrap.

"Young master, he's not going to the palace first."

"I noticed."

The delegation was supposed to go to the palace. That was protocol. A foreign envoy arrived at the harbor, received an escort from the royal guards led by Goryeo’s First Sword, and proceeded to the palace for a formal reception. A neat and predictable procedure, which was probably why nobody involved was interested in doing it.

Fang Yizhen took a detour. His route shifted three blocks east, toward the market slope. Then toward the worst district. Now they were walking toward the building that did not yet have its sign, but that every spy in Hwangcheon had already reported to their masters.

Toward me.

The shift was so small that a courtier could call it natural if paid by the syllable. A scenic route, the Third Flower’s thread ‘heard’ them say. The envoy wished to see local architecture.

No one sane would believe it. But such diplomacy did not require belief. It only required an excuse that everyone could survive repeating.

So I climbed down the ladder and stood in the unfinished doorway.

That was a choice too. If I walked out to meet him in the street, this became a formal greeting. If I kept working, it would become an insult. Standing in the doorway was the annoying middle path, which meant it was diplomacy. Probably… Diplomacy had never been my strong suit, so I couldn’t be sure.

Fang Yizhen appeared at the end of the street with twenty men arranged in two rows behind him, led by the flustered Goryeo royal guards.

I looked at the man who carried the text [Fang Yizhen] above his head. I don’t know why I was expecting him to look like a conqueror. He did not look like one.

Fang Yizhen looked like a senior clerk who had outlived six ministers, and intentionally. His robes were Baolian formal, dark gold with the sun crest embroidered at the collar, neat without begging for attention. He had grey hair in a simple binding, a clean-shaven face, etched with deep lines around the mouth.

A man built out of ledgers and protocol. He looked like a patient man. But his aura told a different story. A 9th Ascension presence sat inside that administrative body like a sword hidden in a walking cane. He did not leak power like most of the other demigods I’d met.

He stopped outside the branch.

I stayed inside.

For a second, the whole street understood the picture without anyone saying it. I stood in the unfinished frame with sawdust on my arms, nails scattered on the floor, and a hammer hanging from my belt. He stood outside in formal robes with twenty armed fighters at his back.

The workers had stopped acting like they weren’t watching. I didn’t say anything at first, just meeting his eyes. He looked at the building. Then at me.

"Good afternoon," Fang Yizhen said, speaking clear Middle Chinese. His voice was clear and polite, like a man asking after the weather. "Forgive the intrusion. I noticed the work from the main road and was impressed. You’re building a clinic, I assume? Or a cultural office?"

"...Both, if the roof behaves," I replied in the same language.

"How civic-minded of you, carpenter."

"Goryeo is a civilized place, after all," I said.

"Huh, so you say." His eyes moved across the frame, the unfinished walls, the tools, the lumber. Reading. "Better craftsmanship than I expected in this district. Your workers?"

"What is mine, what is theirs? All things in this land are free; nobody belongs to anybody, and everyone holds a free universe in their heart. So they’re not my workers, but yes, they’re working with me on this project," I said, hoping to irritate him.

"So I see." His gaze returned to me and stayed there. I could see him actively gauging my Qi, and I didn’t bother to hide it. What would he do? Attack me? Funny. "Has anybody ever told you… You are quite strong for a carpenter."

Behind me, Lilian smirked and slouched just a little as if waiting for permission to pounce. I did not look back.

I held the man's eyes. "Good nails," I said. "Good timber. Shame to waste them."

Fang Yizhen studied me.

Those polite eyes did not warm when his mouth did. I had seen that separation before, many times. The first time was a long time ago, when I met Nebula's mother in Lockdarn, and she held my neck and calculated whether to kill me or use me. Most recently was with the Maharani as she was deciding how much of my existence to tolerate.

Fang Yizhen had the same patience.

He looked to the side, at the street the way tax offices looked at empty fields. Then he adjusted his sleeves. "I should not keep His Majesty waiting. He has been gracious in receiving us on short notice."

"Then I won't keep you."

Fang turned to leave. His men pivoted with him, smooth and simultaneous, a single organism pretending to be protocol. He took three steps.

Then he paused.

"Carpenter."

I waited.

He did not turn fully. Just enough for his voice to carry over his shoulder. "I have visited many kingdoms," he said. "Some understood friendship quickly. Some needed more time."

He let that sit.

"I do hope Goryeo prospers."

Then he walked on. The group moved behind him, and the street started moving again in pieces. First the tile apprentice lowered the tile he had been holding like a shield. Then a woman across the road started sweeping again, very carefully, as if broomwork had become a matter of state.

Lilian stepped up beside me, still watching the bend in the road where Fang had disappeared. "He was looking at the workers too, not just at you," she said.

"Yes, that was getting to my nerves."

The way her claws flexed once said she agreed with my sentiment. "He was counting what he could hurt," she said. “Fox-like bastard. Can I have this fight?”

I stared at the empty street.

“Sorry, Lilian,” I said. “But this one’s mine.”

The branch was almost finished. The frame stood solid while the shutters fitted clean. Tomorrow, the sign would go up. The day after, the first Goryeo citizens would walk through that door, be it porters with ruined knees, widows who had learned to bargain harder than merchants, and children pretending not to be curious at the sect banner.

They would learn cultivation breathing from instructors Senna had personally trained. That was the future I wanted to build here.

That future would come to fruition. Even if somewhere inside the palace, a man who had erased kingdoms with paperwork was being served tea right now.

Because Goryeo had chosen to fly the banner of the Heavenly Demon Divine Cult.
