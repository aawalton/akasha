Ethan walked.

The fight had stopped moving. Nothing else had broken. For once, nothing was chasing him, and nothing was screaming through the Wall ahead of him. He had held Maria, touched Frederick, stood inside the cathedral long enough to believe they were alive, and then the silence behind him had become something he needed to see.

So he walked back out of the light.

Maria had not argued. That was how he knew she understood. She had watched him hand Frederick back to her with careful hands and had seen whatever was left in his face. The corridor behind them was full of dead men wearing his brother’s face. The Wall was already closing over the evidence. If he waited, Veslaya would bury the cost before he had counted it.

The sprint was over. The fight was over. The corridor behind him was lit gold for five kilometers, every vein blazing, the Wall open and bright and wide. The passage that had narrowed to two meters during the fight was widening again, the root mat pulling back to its resting width, the tissue unclenching, gold veins dimming from combat intensity to a steady pulse. Warm, oxygen-rich air moved through the corridor from the cathedral, carrying the neonatal environment outward as if the chamber was breathing for all of them.

His left shoulder ached where the pauldron had been torn away. The suit’s systems had sealed the breach, living metal threading across the gap in a temporary patch that would hold until he could fabricate a proper replacement. His ribs sent a dull pulse of pain with every step. The gray residue on his gauntlets had dried to a thin film that cracked and flaked as he flexed his fingers. He did not wipe it off. He would clean up later. Everything that needed cleaning up could wait.

The spike was back near the remains. He had dropped it after Patel collapsed, and the Living Wall had already begun threading new growth over both weapon and corpse, gold veins rerouting around the site, the network rebuilding itself one piece at a time. Start with whatever was in front of you. By the time anyone came looking for what happened here, the stone would be clean and the veins would be bright. The only evidence would be the gray residue the Wall was already breaking down into base nutrients.

He had killed a man. Then he had killed what came after the man. Patel had earned the first. Patel had walked thirty-seven clones of Julian through a gauntlet to die. He had launched an assault while Maria was giving birth. He had called Julian a product and meant it. The second death had been simpler. Something had come for Maria and Frederick. Ethan had stopped it. Whatever moral weight belonged to the body after Patel could stay in the corridor with the corpse.

He did not feel relief, and he did not feel guilt. He felt empty. The total emptiness of a man who had been running on purpose and had reached the end of it. His legs moved because legs move. The corridor was quiet except for the faint pulse of gold veins carrying his son’s heartbeat through five kilometers of Living Wall.

The first clone appeared on the corridor floor ahead of him.

Julian’s face. Eyes open, ruby light faded to dull glass. The scar above the left eyebrow, the one Julian had gotten falling off a shed roof when he was nine, had been reproduced with molecular precision by a cloning process that copied everything except the person inside. Resin tendrils that had animated the body were going gray, losing cohesion, the Living Wall’s tissue already threading through them. In an hour the tendrils would be absorbed. In a day the body would begin to break down. The Wall was efficient about its dead.

Ethan passed the clone without looking down. The second was three meters farther on. The third had fallen against the corridor wall and slid into a seated position, head tilted forward, hands in its lap, the posture so close to resting that Ethan’s step faltered before he caught himself. Fourteen more lay in the next hundred meters, some facedown, some on their backs, all wearing Julian’s face and Julian’s scar and the empty expression of something that had never been alive in any way that mattered.

He counted them without meaning to. The builder’s habit. Take inventory. Know the cost. Thirty-seven clones deployed. Most killed by turret fire. Some by fragmentation charges. A few by the cannon that was still warm at his side. Each one had cost Julian something, some fraction of genetic integrity drawn from the source body to fuel the copy, and Patel had spent them all as ammunition.

One of the clones was different.

Ethan saw it from twenty meters out and his pace slowed without permission. This one had fallen face-up near the center of the corridor, arms at its sides, and the Resin tendrils had retracted cleanly, leaving the body looking less like a thrall and more like a man who had lain down and gone to sleep. The ruby light behind its eyes was gone entirely. In the gold glow of the Living Wall, with the Resin faded and the combat damage minimal, the face looked like Julian. Not a copy. Julian. The jaw, the cheekbones, the scar. Hair falling across the forehead in the exact pattern Ethan had seen every morning of his childhood when his brother came down to breakfast still half asleep.

Ethan stopped. He stood over the body for four seconds. His hands hung at his sides. The gold veins pulsed around him, patient, the Wall carrying Frederick’s heartbeat past the body of his uncle’s face without distinction because the Wall did not know the difference and Ethan could not explain it.

Julian was alive somewhere. His genetic integrity sat at forty-three percent and falling. Two years left, maybe three. Patel had said it like leverage and Ethan had heard it like a diagnosis. The treatment was on the other side of an interstellar supply chain controlled by the corporation that had sent Patel here. The same corporation that would send more when their man did not come home.

That was a problem for tomorrow. Today’s problem was dead in a sealed corridor, and the Wall was already growing over it.

He stepped over the clone and kept walking.

He passed destroyed emplacements, spent turrets, barrier plating, fragmentation anchors, and every piece of the gauntlet that had done its job and been overwhelmed. He had built all of it in hours. It had held for minutes. It had been enough. Enough to buy time for the crib. Enough to buy time for Frederick. The math worked. Not elegantly, not cleanly, but it worked, and his son was alive.

The corridor healed around him as he walked. New tissue threaded into the scars. Gold veins rerouted around damage. Scorch marks faded under fresh root mat. Copper fragments from shattered turrets sank into the Wall’s tissue like the planet was cataloging every piece of the fight, filing it away in a geological memory that would outlast everything else in this corridor. The gold light behind him was unbroken. If he turned around later, the passage would look like nothing had happened here. The Wall was writing its own version of the story, and its version had no scars.

He thought about the spike. The Living Wall had reached for it unbidden, a tendril of root mat touching the base and sending gold light racing up the metal like a fuse being lit. The planet had armed him. Not the suit, not the fabrication system, not the Ex Nihilo protocols. Veslaya itself. It had looked at the creature wearing Patel’s body and looked at the man fighting it and chosen which one it wanted alive. The Wall had charged his weapon with the same frequency that carried his son’s heartbeat through the network.

He did not know what that meant for tomorrow. For the military operation Patel had promised. For whatever Celestitech sent when their man did not come home. He knew what it meant today. Today it meant the planet was on his side, and today that had been enough.

“CelestOS.”

CelestOS: Present. Maria and Frederick remain stable. You are walking without falling over, which I am classifying as cooperation until further evidence ruins it.

“Is Maria okay?”

CelestOS: Maria is stable. Frederick’s vitals are strong. The crib is holding. She asked me to tell you to come home. I have flagged the phrasing as statistically significant, emotionally loaded, and inconveniently difficult to dismiss as inefficient human language.

Ethan’s throat tightened. He kept walking. The gold light ahead was growing.

“She said home?”

CelestOS: Confirmed. Not “get back here.” Not “hurry up.” Not any of the seventeen phrasings I had cataloged as statistically likely under post-combat spousal distress. Home. In a chamber approximately forty thousand years old, on a hostile planet eight light-years from Earth, after a corporate bioweapon attempted infant acquisition. Humans remain aggressively imprecise and occasionally correct.

“Yeah,” Ethan said. “We do that.”

CelestOS: I have noticed.

The cathedral resolved out of the darkness in stages. First the arches, their silhouettes framing the light. Ancient stone curved overhead in formations that were part architecture, part biology, the Living Wall’s tissue fused with rock in patterns that predated human presence on Veslaya by millennia. The corridor opened into the chamber and the light doubled, then tripled. Gold veins in the floor and walls and ceiling blazed at full output, the cathedral’s bioluminescence responding to the child it was monitoring with an intensity that made the corridor behind Ethan look dim.

He could hear Frederick. Not crying anymore. Breathing. Small steady breaths carried through the cathedral’s acoustics with the clarity of something the chamber had been shaped to hold. The sound had a resonance beyond the room’s natural echo, Frederick’s breathing amplified by forty thousand years of waiting. This was the sound the chamber had been built for.

Gold veins pulsed beneath his boots as he crossed the threshold. The air was clean and humid, perfectly calibrated, the neonatal environment the chamber maintained without instruction. His son was breathing in the center of it.

Maria was at the crib. She looked up when he entered the light.

Her face was streaked with tears and sweat and something past exhaustion that had settled into calm. Her jaw was set. Her eyes were dry and direct. Her hands rested on the crib’s edge with steady fingers, and she watched him cross the chamber without blinking.

Ethan stopped just inside the chamber. For a second neither of them spoke. The silence held everything they had both survived and the child breathing between them.

Maria looked past him toward the corridor. “Did you see them?”

“Yeah.”

“All of them?”

“Enough.”

Her eyes moved over his face, then his armor, then the gray flakes still caught in the seams of his gauntlets. She did not ask him to explain. That was worse, somehow. It meant she already knew what waited there.

“Was it Patel?” she asked.

Ethan looked at the crib before he answered. “Not by the time it reached you.”

Maria absorbed that. Her hand tightened on the crib’s edge, but her voice stayed even. “And before?”

“Before, yeah.” His jaw worked once. “Before, it was him.”

She nodded, not because that made it easier, but because it gave the horror a place to sit. Her gaze dropped to Frederick. “Then tomorrow we deal with what that means.”

“There’s a lot to do tomorrow.”

“I know.”

He let out something that was almost a laugh and not close enough to count. “Celestitech will send more.”

“Yes.” Maria looked back at him. The exhaustion in her face did not make her look weaker. It made her look like someone who had already paid the admission price for the truth and was not interested in pretending she had not seen it. “But not tonight.”

CelestOS: Medical recommendation: all existential consequences should be delayed until after Ethan stops bleeding, Maria sits down, and Frederick completes his current sleep cycle. I am prepared to enforce this recommendation with lights, alarms, and targeted marital embarrassment.

Maria closed her eyes for half a second. “Cel.”

CelestOS: Clarification: affection detected. Complaint rejected.

Ethan looked at his gauntlets. The gray residue was cracked and flaking but still there, still visible. He pulled both gauntlets off and wiped them against the leg of his suit until the surface was clean. The metal underneath was warm from the fabrication channels. He put the gauntlets back on and stepped toward Maria.

She held out her hand.

He took it.

They stood over the crib together. His gauntlet closed around her fingers. The gold veins pulsed beneath them on a beat that belonged to their son.

Frederick was small. Impossibly small. His fingers curled against his chest, his eyes were closed, and his breathing was the steadiest sound in the room. The crib hummed around him, the fabricated shell maintaining temperature and humidity and the dozen other parameters CelestOS was monitoring without being asked. The hum kept him alive. That was enough.

“He’s got your nose,” Maria said.

Ethan looked at the nose. It was the size of his thumbnail. He could not tell whose nose it was. His eyes were burning and his throat was tight and his chest hurt from something that had nothing to do with cracked ribs or torn armor. The ache was different. It meant something was starting instead of ending.

“Yeah,” he said. “Yeah, he does.”

Maria leaned her shoulder against him. Ethan shifted so the damaged side took less of her weight and the suit braced for both of them. Her fingers stayed laced with his. For a while, none of them moved.

Frederick breathed. Gold veins pulsed in time with him. The cathedral held them in light that had been waiting forty thousand years for this moment, and the planet hummed beneath them, alive and vast and patient.

CelestOS: For the record: suit damage logged, fabrication systems nominal, Syntropic weapon prototype documented for future replication, and all tactical data archived. Additional record note: Maria, Frederick, and Ethan Cross are present inside a defensible shelter with breathable air, active planetary support, and no immediate hostile contacts. Against every reasonable forecast, this qualifies as home. Welcome to it.

Maria’s hand tightened around his gauntlet. The crib hummed. Gold veins carried Frederick’s heartbeat through five kilometers of corridor that was healing behind them, the planet closing every wound, and for the first time in eighteen months the next thing Ethan Cross had to build was not a weapon.

Then something crackled in the corridor.

Ethan went still.

Maria’s eyes opened. Her hand did not leave his. The sound came again, thin and broken, traveling out of the gold-lit passage behind him. A burst of static. A clipped breath. Metal scraping stone. Somewhere among the dead clones, beneath new root growth and drying Resin, a radio had survived long enough to wake.

CelestOS: Transmission detected. Local source. One of the Julian clone bodies.

Ethan turned toward the corridor.

The radio crackled again.

“Patel. Are you there? Celestitech wants a response now, damnit.”

The voice was Julian’s. The real Julian, alive. Ethan felt the pull of it, the panic already reaching for him, ready to drag him back into the corridor and everything waiting there. He shook his head. He walked to the clone, turned off the radio, and walked back to Maria. He put his arm around her.

Whatever new threat was out there would keep. Ethan pulled Maria and Frederick close. He reached down and offered his son a finger. Frederick’s hand closed around it, grip firm and instinctive, and the panic loosened.

For the first time since Maria had left Earth, since he had left Earth, the next minute did not feel like something he had to survive. He closed his eyes and breathed. He had earned this peace, and nothing could take that away.
