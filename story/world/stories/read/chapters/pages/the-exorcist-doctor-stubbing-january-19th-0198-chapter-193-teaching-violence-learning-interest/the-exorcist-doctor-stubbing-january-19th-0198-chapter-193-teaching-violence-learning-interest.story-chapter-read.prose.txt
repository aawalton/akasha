Jin had expected teaching children how to fight would be easy, but that assumption lasted approximately twelve minutes before he decided to retract it. By the time afternoon came, he’d already thrown Rebus over his shoulder sixteen times, kicked Bran’s makeshift club out of his grip fourteen times, and physically pried three other boys from his back once they realized attacking one at a time was for idiots. They were in Bharncair, and he was an Exorcist after all. There was simply no reason to fight someone alone when he could fight them with a partner.

Still, Wraithpier children were… remarkably resilient.

“Again,” he ordered, rolling one shoulder while the dozen boys scattered around him dragged themselves upright. “And stop coming at me in a straight line. You’re still making it too easy.”

Rebus wiped blood from beneath his nose and grinned. “Easy? Yer breathin’ pretty hard, Mister Jin.”

“I’m breathing normally.”

“And sweatin’ enough t’fill a bait bucket,” Bran added.

Jin glared at him. Bran stopped smiling quite so widely. Truthfully, the boy wasn’t wrong. Jin had to remove his coat almost an hour ago and rolled his sleeves past his elbows, yet sweat still clung beneath his collar while his shirt stuck unpleasantly against his back. He hadn’t used any of his stronger mutations or his gauntlet—there wouldn’t be much point teaching children how to defend themselves if he simply flattened them with system-enhanced abilities—but even with his system-enhanced body, keeping track of twelve Wraithpier boys at once was considerably more exhausting than it had any right to be.

They weren’t trained fighters. That much was obvious. Their stances were ugly. Their punches came too wide. Half of them telegraphed attacks so badly Jin could see them coming a few seconds beforehand, and whenever they took up makeshift knives or clubs, most immediately forgot whatever lesson he’d taught them five minutes earlier and started swinging as though they were trying to beat fish to death on a dock.

But they were tough. Ridiculously tough. A boy Jin had flipped onto his back hard enough to knock the air from a normal adult would roll over, cough twice, and come charging back thirty seconds later. Another had taken an accidental elbow to the jaw earlier, spat out a loose tooth, tucked it into his pocket for reasons Jin didn’t want explained, and resumed fighting.

What the hell did these people feed their children?

… Fish, probably. Everything in Wraithpier somehow came back to fish.

“Pairs this time,” Jin said, gesturing two fingers at the boys. “One distracts me. One attacks. Don’t both swing at my face at once unless you actually want to hit each other.”

The boys immediately started whispering among themselves. Then Rebus and Bran came first. Rebus charged from the front with both fists raised too high, which meant distraction. Jin stepped inside his reach, caught him under one arm, and used the boy’s momentum to flip him sideways. Bran came from behind exactly when expected, swinging a padded wooden club towards Jin’s ribs.

Better.

Jin pivoted, caught Bran’s wrist, twisted, and sent the club clattering across the roof. Before Bran could recover, Jin swept his ankle and deposited him beside Rebus.

The next pair did slightly better. The pair after that almost clipped his shoulder. By the time all twelve boys had learned enough about coordination to ignore his instructions entirely and attack him together, Jin actually had to work for his life. Three rushed low. Two came from behind. Someone threw a fishing net. Jin ducked the net, grabbed the first boy by his coat, swung him sideways into two others without enough force to hurt them badly, then stepped around a club thrust and caught its wielder beneath the elbow. Another child latched onto his waist. Someone climbed onto his back. Bran somehow recovered the discarded net and tried wrapping it around Jin’s legs.

For several seconds, Jin disappeared beneath a heap of limbs, wet rags, shouted Wraithpier curses, and entirely too much enthusiasm. Then he cracked his neck, clicked his tongue, and threw everyone off—with four children still barely hanging to his back.

“Get off.”

“Nah!” Rebus shouted from somewhere behind his shoulder. “We nearly got ye!”

“You really didn’t.”

He grabbed Rebus’ sleeve before the child could jump away and tossed him gently into a pile of old sailcloth by the chimney. Soon enough, everyone else followed, and when the last boy landed flat on his back with a groan, Jin stood among twelve defeated children and took several heavy breaths through his nose.

That was closer than he’d ever admit to anyone. Wraithpier locals were insanely troublesome fighters. No wonder Fergal and the Saint’s Hands wanted to recruit them when they were young.

“Take five,” he muttered, walking away to retrieve the cup of water he’d left beside his coat. “And somebody fix that net before you use it again. Half the knots are coming loose.”

The boys needed no encouragement to collapse. Within moments, the roof transformed from a training ground into a collection of exhausted children sprawled across shingles and patched boards. Rebus dropped beside one chimney with both arms behind his head. Bran stole another boy’s water cup and immediately got punched in the shoulder for it. Two others started arguing over whether Jin’s shoulder throw counted as cheating because he was bigger than them. For his part, Jin ignored them and drank his cold water, wiping the sweat from his temple.

As he did, he glanced down through one of the larger holes in the roof of the clinic.

Vivienne was still teaching the girls how to shoot, and she walked behind their firing line with considering more gentleness than Jin had, correcting one girl’s shoulders before tapping another girl’s elbow lower. The rifles and harpoon guns stayed pointed towards the painted mannequins across the atrium, but nobody had shot through the ceiling yet. Which was good. He’d been slightly worried about leaving the girls to Vivienne alone, since leaving a group of Wraithpier children with rifles and harpoon guns seemed like the kind of idea Gael would like, which meant he hadn’t liked the idea—but Vivienne seemed to have them all under control.

Three months ago, he still probably would’ve asked to oversee the girls’ shooting alongside her, but now…

Jin looked away when he felt eyes on his back, and he turned to see Rebus staring at him. So was Bran.

Actually, almost every boy on the roof had gone strangely quiet as they all stared at him.

“What?” he asked.

Rebus’ grin widened. “Nothin’.”

“Then stop looking at me.”

“Just noticed somethin’, is all.” Rebus shuffled closer to where Jin sat to nudge him with an elbow, grinning. “You’ve been eyein’ Miss Vivi all afternoon.”

Several boys snickered as they shuffled closer as well, but Jin simply took another drink, nonplussed. “I’m only checking to see if any of the girls are at risk of shooting themselves. That happened once in a while during Exorcist training.”

Rebus ignored him, elbows on his knees and his grin became thoroughly smug. “So ye fancy her?”

“I don’t.”

His answer came easily enough. Not that anyone believed him, anyways. Bran made an exaggerated doubtful noise. Someone behind him whispered something about Vivienne being really pretty. Another boy argued that Jin clearly liked tall women because she was tall by Wraithpier standards, which was possibly the stupidest argument Jin had heard all week, so he simply chose to tune them out as they kept prodding and teasing him about it.

… Still, Rebus’ question remained irritatingly lodged in his head as he kept drinking and glancing down at the girls.

Did he like Vivienne?

Well, he definitely didn’t dislike her anymore. That much was obvious. Nine months ago, she still couldn’t hold her own in a fight, and she was still entirely too hesitant about drawing blood. If Maeve had been right that night she dragged him out for noodles in Bleakhearth—and he’d really been the one chaining Vivienne down and preventing her from getting stronger—she wasn’t right now. Vivienne had broken through those chains herself. She no longer froze whenever an ugly Myrmur screamed in her face, and when Rebus and Bran and Nixy tried to pickpocket them months ago, she’d raised her rifle at them before Jin even thought about chasing them.

As an Exorcist Host, she was now everything he could ask of a partner. And now that they were eating Myrmurs again, gaining more points, unlocking more mutations, and catching up to Gael and Maeve quickly—their new wings had only recently started developing—Vivienne was only going to get more and more self-sufficient. If she kept progressing at this pace, eventually they might not even need to hunt together anymore. For maximum Myrmur exorcising efficiency, they could detach their bloodshackle, go their separate ways to fight separate Myrmurs, and regroup only when he needed to drain his excess toxin.

In a way, he actually felt a little proud of her. Maybe that was a stupid thing to feel, considering he didn’t contribute anything to her getting stronger, but… still, it was fun watching her become stronger and more confident. He’d like to see just how much stronger she could still get.

So he paused mid-drink, his brows furrowing slightly.

… Wait.

So I really am interested in her?

As he tried to load the thoughts in his head, wondering what that really meant for him, a particularly hearty laugh from one of the boys pulled his attention back to them. While the boys had already forgotten all about him, and were simply joking and laughing amongst themselves, he found the sight of them playing and teasing each other… strangely familiar.

Gael, Fergal, and Liorin argued constantly too, but their dynamic was the very definition of ‘functionally dysfunctional’. Gael treated Liorin somewhere between a younger brother, an experimental subject, a clinic employee, and a stray animal he had accidentally adopted. Fergal always acted like the responsible adult until Gael provoked him into violence, while Liorin always bounced between both older men depending on who looked more entertaining to annoy. It had to be the age gap. The gap between Liorin and Gael was about the same as the gap between Gael and Fergal, which meant there had to be some sort of disconnect there that these boys of Miss Umbrage’s clinic didn’t have.

These boys were all close enough to each other in age that seeing them banter and argue reminded Jin of him and his brothers. They used to argue a lot about pottery and architecture, didn’t they?

That was a long time ago, though.

He looked away from the boys, and his eyes went across the crooked roofs, canals, and distant towers and bridges until he found Vharnveil, still floating above the center of the city like the lump of rock it really only was.

Somewhere up there, amidst those golden spires, was home.

What were his elder brothers doing now? What was his mother doing now? Had they given up on him entirely, and had they already moved on from their father’s murder?

He wasn’t sure whether thinking about them angered him or relieved him, but if he were to ever see them again, it’d be after he’d taken out the Breeder.

“Message!” a raven suddenly cawed above him. “Message! Message!”

Two more harsh caws broke overhead, and everybody on the roof looked up, seeing three giant ravens circling against the blue-misted sky. Jin recognized them immediately. They were Gael’s ravens. Balthazar, Marlowe, and… something else. He still couldn’t believe Gael actually named them.

Quickly, the ravens folded their wings and swooped down. One dropped a folded paper directly into Jin’s arms, and another released several messages over the rest of the boys. The last dove straight through one of the holes in the roof, releasing rolled-up letters to the girls in the atrium.

Jin unfolded his message and read Gael’s shabby, nigh-indecipherable handwriting quickly.

“... About time, huh?” he muttered.

He glanced down at the atrium, seeing Vivienne had also read Gael’s message, already looking back up at him.

Then she gave him a firm nod, and he felt his heart skip a beat once.

He frowned faintly at himself.

Hm?

What was that?
