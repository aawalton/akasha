Andrew talked to the officers, and they began their march north. Rows of soldiers—ex-soldiers, since they no longer had weapons—walked in formation. The journey took an entire day. James had assumed it would only take a few hours, but he had forgotten that he and Kouki traveled on mounts, while these soldiers were low level and on foot.

The researchers struggled the most. They barely exercised in their daily lives, let alone had any real stats. Some were already out of breath after just a few hours of walking.

“Greetings, travelers from afar. I am Maruk. Welcome. You have quite the company. I apologized in advance, but our town can host only six hundred people at most. We simply do not have enough food to last until the next tide,” Maruk, the orc chief, said.

“Yes. Understood. We will separate ourselves,” Andrew nodded. Before leaving, he turned to James and Kouki. “James, Kouki—could I leave these men in your care?”

“Hell no. Every man fends for himself. I’m not risking my butt to save them. I can guard maybe five guys, but are you seriously asking me to guard five hundred civilians? Are you nuts?”

“No. I’m not asking you to protect them. I’m asking you to lead them.”

“That’s the same thing. Most of them are single-digit level. Any monster encounter means risking their lives even with weapons. They’re no different from the civilians in the previous dungeon,” James said.

“…Well. In that case, just answer their questions to the best of your abilities. Their captains will handle the rest,” Andrew said. “Kouki, Kaina—will you stay here, or will you join us?”

“I’m staying.”

“Understood.” Andrew left, departing Maruk’s town with half the group, heading toward another city owned by Goliath.

A line formed in front of the census bureau. James watched them, his brows furrowing. This was too deliberate. It was as if the dungeon had predicted this would happen. And when James thought about it, the scene was inevitable. Even if someone had food-producing skills, like Anderson, the coins required to feed a thousand people would be far too costly.

Dungeons didn’t outright kill their victims. The chance of survival was never 100%, but it was never 0 either. Even level-ones could technically clear a dungeon. They just needed to level up. If something happened, like in Andrew’s case, the dungeon would boot him out.

So the dungeon had to give them food to give them a fighting chance. And the only way to obtain food and water here was to register in one of the two cities. But they couldn’t all register in a single city, so they had to split themselves—half and half.

“So deliberate.” James suspected the dungeon’s machinations. If they didn’t register, that meant they would have to go without food for seven days.

“Aren’t you going to register, James?” Kouki asked from the line, but James refused.

“Nah. I’m not registering. I’ll be fine even without eating for a week. And I have water,” James said. Doing this would lock him out of most city quests, preventing him from earning coins, but James was suspicious.

Maruk said the moon would cast its glare on the soil below for three days. That meant James had to survive on his biomass alone. Water was a non-issue since he had his Water Core, but could he survive with just his Chimera skill?

[Would you like to spend 100 biomass for sustenance?]

James clicked yes, and soon the gnawing hunger in his stomach faded. It was a strange feeling. He felt nothing in his mouth or stomach, yet he felt invigorated—and even sleepy—like he had just eaten a big meal.

“Damn it, I guess no Chimera Level 4 for me,” James muttered. He had 1,400 biomass left. That meant he could last for fourteen days. Two weeks. After that, he would have to go out and hunt animals to absorb into his chimera to sustain himself.

James chuckled at how his body had changed. He no longer needed to eat, and he could store as much biomass as he wanted.

Three days and 300 biomass later, James had become the equivalent of a homeless person in this city, while the soldiers and researchers took on odd jobs here and there to earn their keep. All he did every day was stare at the sky from the city wall.

Kouki and Kaina had told him that each job finished earned gold coins in addition to system coins. With this money, they could buy weapons and harvesting tools. James asked whether they were system weapons, but Kouki shook his head.

Then, one hour before the timer went to zero, a translucent barrier enveloped the city. James also saw another one on the horizon to the east, where Andrew was supposed to be. James knocked on the barrier, and it rebounded, sending ripples across the entire surface. It might have been the same color, but it was not the usual invincible purple barrier he knew.

Four soldiers ran toward James in panic. At that moment, James understood that he had done something wrong.

“Touching or attacking a city barrier is forbidden. Since this is your first offense, we will let it slide. But the next time you do it, we will put a bounty on your head,” the soldier said.

“Maruk said I was free to go even when the tide came,” James said. “But this barrier seems to isolate us. Did he lie?”

“Are you crazy? Do you have a death wish? Going out during a tide will kill you. It’s only reserved for corpses and death-row inmates.”

“I was just asking. I never said I would go outside. Sorry. It won’t happen again,” James said, bowing.

“If you have a death wish, go to the gate. There’s a tunnel there where people who wish to end their lives can go outside.” The soldiers scoffed. Clearly, this was not the first time they had seen people commit suicide.

“What about death row? What crime did they commit?”

“Dunno. The judge has various reasons. Murder, stealing food, or euthanasia. Don’t let us catch you attacking the barrier again or you’ll join them soon. We have no mercy for outsiders who attack our barrier.”

“I just knocked on it. Was this barrier so fragile that it could break from a mere knock?”

“I’ve warned you.” The soldiers left, refusing to elaborate.

During their exchange, the crimson moon cast its eerie light over the earth. The barrier rippled as if rain had struck the surface of water.

“Yo, I see you were giving them trouble this time, James. Stop being stubborn and register already. You’re the only one left. You’re missing out on a lot of coins. I bet you could become a captain in no time. You are the strongest, after all.” Kouki and Kaina met James on the wall. Several researchers and soldiers also followed, wanting to see the moon work its magic.

“No,” James answered shortly. He was stubborn, and he knew it.

The ground trembled as blades of grass sprouted before their very eyes. James gaped at the scenery. Worms came out of the ground, devouring the grass and transforming into huge armadillos. Water burst from the earth, forming a lake as fish and lizards were born from it. Then came the age of dinosaurs. The lizards and fish flopped onto land, growing feet and roaming about, eating one another.

It was all because the moon rained blood—nourishment for this wasteland. For the next three days, James watched how the animals lived outside the barrier. They kept changing, transforming, flourishing. They lived and thrived. Some died and were eaten by the red rain.

Some flew in the sky. Some prowled the land. Some swam in the river. All manners of life lived and prospered. A monk gave a dutiful prayer atop the gate, worshiping the moon as it blessed the land with life.

Then, the death row inmates came. Three people were to be executed that day. They were chained like dogs as soldiers dragged them to the gate. James didn’t know when it had been built, but there was indeed a tunnel that stretched beyond the barrier.

The difference between the two sides was uncanny. The outside was weathered, with moss and shrubbery growing over it, never reaching the barrier, while the inside was still solid stone.

Together with Kouki, James watched the execution inside the gate. The soldiers read the criminals’ crimes, then pushed the small carriage outside. The condemned cried out in anguish. Even though they were still inside the tunnel, their bodies were drenched in blood from above, meaning the light of the crimson moon could not be blocked by a canopy or cover the way James had done with the purple moon in the previous dungeon.

Just as Maruk said, the criminals transformed into unrecognizable monsters, similar to the animals outside. Their cries for help went unanswered as they staggered beyond the barrier, ready to be devoured by the creatures of the ever-changing world.

“James, the system says they’re lifting the barrier tomorrow. Let’s go hunt something,” Kouki said.

“Understood,” James nodded.

It ended as abruptly as it had started. The crimson moon ceased its glare. The blood rain that nourished the land stopped, and the animals stopped transforming.

The rippling on the barrier faded. Two hours later, the translucent wall that had protected them for the last three days vanished. Soldiers and farmers rushed to the gate, eager to gather the first harvest of the month.

James followed soon after.
