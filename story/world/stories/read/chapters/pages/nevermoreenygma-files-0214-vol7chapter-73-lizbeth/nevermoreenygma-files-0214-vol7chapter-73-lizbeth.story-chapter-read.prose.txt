Chapter 73

Lizbeth

The days and weeks that followed had no shape.

Time inside the facility—whatever its real name was—became a precise blend of clinical routine and mental erosion. They didn’t torture Mai, not in the classical sense. But every procedure, every repeated question, every sterilized needle descending toward her skin built a kind of silent siege. A prolonged encirclement. A war of attrition against her patience and her memory.

The army’s mages and esper mind-readers were the first.

They arrived with their scanning devices, their questions, and that faint ozone smell that clung to places where institutional magic was used too often. They tried to measure the “distortion” Azusa had left inside her, but all they found were impossible readings: energy spikes that matched no known pattern, flow lines that broke off as if a conceptual hole existed within her, blank zones in the logs that their instruments refused to acknowledge.

Mai watched everything from her metal table—tired, silent—neither cooperating nor resisting. She had nothing to hide. In the end, all she repeated was the truth: the entity was older than the fractus, deeper, and its logic didn’t belong to this world or any other they could catalogue. But they could not rule out that Azusa had erased parts of Mai’s mind. As far as their abilities reached, Mai wasn’t lying. There was no hidden-world conspiracy behind her actions.

Then came the military.

More severe, more methodical, more aware of political risk. They took notes without blinking. Asked for precise dates of the events prior to the collapse, angles, distances, the very texture of the air at the moment the bow appeared in her hand. They wanted to know why her, how her, what set her apart from the rest.

There were no satisfactory answers.

Not even Mai knew. When she asked Azusa why her, the entity simply answered that Mai had been the most suitable.

And that was the part that unsettled them the most.

What made her different?

The feys and aeon envoys from the Tripartite Treaty arrived after, escorted, with guarded faces and eyes that revealed fear and distrust alike. They didn’t look at her as a threat, but as an anomaly—someone who had opened a link with something that surpassed even them. They didn’t treat her badly, but they kept a distance, as if a single brush of contact might awaken something that even their ancient knowledge couldn’t contain. Only Travis Cornwall treated her kindly, even telling her that Nevermore and Wishper were doing everything in their power to have her released at last.

That brief conversation almost made her cry.

At least they hadn’t forgotten her.

The interrogations repeated endlessly, identical each time. Faces changed, badges changed, rooms changed… but the questions echoed each other perfectly. They tried to catch her in contradictions, to find cracks, to test the consistency of her story as though Mai’s mind were a minefield.

She answered the same thing, over and over.

Without embellishment or speculation.

Azusa was an entity from a higher dimension. She didn’t control it, but she could summon it.

It didn’t speak much. It didn’t care about the politics of civilization.

It had helped her because Mai was there, in that exact instant, and because for Azusa, Mai was the only logical choice at the right moment—even if that choice had cost millions of lives. All Mai could say was that the moment of her death on that snowy field was the exact moment when her shy organ could fuse with Azusa’s core in a different dimension. That, and that she could withstand the radiation produced by that summoning. The fact that archery had been something she’d practiced in the past was nothing more than a coincidence, since she was the one who’d told Azusa to take that form. In the state she was in at that moment, it was the first thing that had come to mind, along with the visions of her past—practicing with her father and then in Miho’s backyard.

The radiation issue was also something the scientists needed to consider, but aside from that, there was nothing else unusual about her. She had been just another soldier who, by chance, happened to be the most suitable person to do what she had done.

Through her, they tried to interrogate Azusa, but sometimes she answered and other times she didn’t. They had only managed to determine that she had been observing since the beginning of the war, learning, and had decided to play on one side while watching the soldiers’ efforts. It wasn’t true, but it was enough.

The teams of magic specialists were almost certain that Azusa was a young elemental who had formed at the start of the war. Through earth magic, via Ley Lines and some distortion that had combined some kind of Fractus with a Dark Event.

The hardest part wasn’t the repetition.

It was when she asked for the casualty records.

The officers hesitated, as if opening that file were akin to opening a grave still warm. But they complied. They showed her the lists. The dates. The coordinates. The medical confirmations.

Sasha.Eun-ji.Jiho.Soo-ah.Ludovic.

They were all there.

Present on paper, though absent in the world.

The letters were cold, but they carried more warmth than any of the voices interrogating her.

And though she didn’t cry, something inside her shifted with a slow, inevitable pain: the certainty that at least they had not been erased as she had. Their names existed. Their sacrifice existed.

She, on the other hand, did not.

Officially, Mai Izumi was never in Siberia.

She never wielded an impossible bow. Never shattered a dimensional sphere.

Never survived alone where an entire battalion died.

They wiped her from the conflict as if her shadow had been a clerical error.

A stain on the record. A convenient ghost.

But as days became weeks, one thing remained intact: the sense that her anonymity was not a shield—but a prelude.

The world had won a terrible battle, and without knowing it, it had already begun laying the foundations of the next.

And Mai, lying on her metal cot with Azusa breathing silently inside the earring, understood that her existence—her mere presence—had become the core of a conflict brewing between soldiers, mages, feys, and whatever hid between dimensions.

The war was over. What came next was less visible, but just as lethal.

And she might be the first piece to move.

Months passed, and March arrived. After four long months, she finally felt fresh air against her face.

Azusa insisted she could have freed her sooner, but Mai had refused. That freedom had come thanks to certain members of the Tripartite Treaty—Nevermore, Gehirn, and Whisper.

She was released under the condition that she would remain under Nevermore’s custody.

She had never been the visible face of that heroic sacrifice, in truth, Nevermore had taken careful steps to ensure very few knew the truth about what had happened during those final days. The weapon she had received from that entity named Azusa—an artifact only she could wield—could not be allowed to fall into the wrong hands. Its power was immeasurable, and equally dangerous. The earring that contained Azusa’s compressed form was a mystery even to the finest experts in magic and the Treaty technology.

The danger of someone wanting to use her as a test subject was always present, so Nevermore implemented a very specific protection system for her. She would be safe in their sanctuary on Siren Island, surrounded by agents who ensured her well-being and privacy. Though sectors of the Tripartite Treaty between humans, feys, and aeons promised to study and contain the power of the artifact within her, Leon and Rein—assisted by Gehirn, Jinat, Travis, and Lorena—had moved the necessary pieces to make sure she would be left alone.

After all, if anything happened to Mai, there was no guarantee Azusa wouldn’t unleash a disaster—bring the Fractus back, for instance. It wasn’t true, but the mere suggestion had terrified the human sector of the Treaty and the Joint Forces. Observation in an isolated place like Siren seemed safer, and in any case, Mai could always hand the earring to the Council if that put them at ease.

It was a convenient agreement for the three factions, but a lie about the true worth of what she had achieved.

The world was shifting toward a new order, an uneasy peace, but Mai knew she could not become part of that narrative.

She had seen too many friends fall, she had lost the family she’d built over the last years. And now she was trapped in an existence where she had to hide. Mai Izumi was part of Nevermore, but she had to keep a low profile.

But at least she would be with her other family, the friends she had made before that conflict. In that sense, yes, she had returned to one of her homes.

When she arrived on the island, Rein, Noki, Leon, Oxy, and many of her friends welcomed her with hugs, trying to comfort her. She allowed herself to break a little, to cry, and later, in the solitude of her room, she cried even more. Azusa remained silent at her side, and in some way, Mai was grateful for that silence. Like everyone else, she had nightmares. She woke in the middle of the night at the slightest sound, reaching for a weapon or expecting a call from her team.

She made sure the others were safe. She spoke with Nikolai about what had happened to Soo-ah. As she had suspected, he already knew everything and had gone through his grief. Even Natalya came to visit—she had now joined Veria as a special agent to continue the investigation of the Dark Events.

Others were still returning home—Miria and Nerea back to Tokyo, Milena and Sari, now on Siren Island, drawn to the place and considering it as a potential new home. Jinat was still somewhere in Africa, on assignment.

Mai wondered if she could go to Japan, see her home, visit the graves of Miho, Aya, and her father. She truly wanted to, but suspected she wouldn’t have that freedom for a long time.

Rumors were already spreading between few through the island—that Mai had been responsible for ending the war. But she, like many others, had no desire to celebrate. She had seen too much during those years. Not even decades working on Dark Events had prepared her for that carnage.

The image of those who had once been comrades, torn apart on the battlefield, was etched into her mind like a permanent scar. The screams, the thud of falling bodies, the desperation and terror—everything had been burned into her retina, and there was no erasing that horror. It was not easy to return from that abyss.

But Mai knew, despite everything, that her story was not unique. It was just one among the hundreds of thousands lived at the front. She had come close to dying so many times she had lost count.

While the world celebrated heroic deeds, Mai never felt like a hero. The society-crafted image of a “hero”—strong, unbreakable, serene—was nothing like what she lived in her own skin. What no one saw, what the public never imagined, were the nights she woke soaked in cold sweat, the nausea that drove her to crawl to the bathroom to vomit.

The heroine who fought invisible monsters on the battlefield was utterly unprepared for the demons that attacked her within her own mind. Not even her body behaved as it should. The erratic disruption of her menstrual cycle, lasting for months, became a constant reminder of the horrors she had witnessed.

Every time she saw blood, all she could picture were the corpses of soldiers, of fallen comrades lying on the snow—their faces frozen into the same expression: terror.

This had even given rise to new mental disorders, such as a fear of geometric shapes. In Japan, a doctor named Ikuna had coined the term “kikaphobia” to describe the phobia of geometric shapes related to fractals and associated with pareidolia, while cases of trypophobia had also increased in certain areas.

Meanwhile, she had tried to keep her mind occupied by helping Rein and the others. She forced herself to stay active so they wouldn’t worry, so they wouldn’t look at her with that constant need to make sure she was all right.

But even though Mai kept to the shadows, she never stopped being a witness—a silent outline watching the world move forward, fully aware of the cracks beneath the lie that held it together. The tensions were still there, even after the war. Now that the celebrations had faded, the world had to continue on its path, learning to live again under a new routine.

There were small skirmishes on the borders, frictions between races, and rumors about the future of the Tripartite Treaty and the Age of Singularity. The war had ended, yes, but the true struggle to rebuild the world—or rather, to build the new world—had only just begun. Mai knew that at some point she would have to make decisions that would once again alter the course of her future.

Eventually, when some distortion appeared, she would have to use Azusa. A double-edged weapon. Because now it could protect her, but the moment anything happened, she would be responsible for wielding that bow again. It seemed like a small price to pay, all things considered.

Days passed, and while politicians, diplomats, and generals delivered their speeches about victory, Mai sank into the deceptive calm of her sanctuary. The memories of battle still burned inside her, and the power of Azusa within her was like a shadow that never stopped watching, waiting to be released again.

The cameras—now turned off—no longer followed her. No one monitored her closely, but she could still feel the constant presence of those who wanted to take advantage of her power. The Tripartite Treaty had sent supervisors to quietly track her movements on Siren Island. They couldn’t let her go, but they couldn’t get too close either.

Mai was the secret heroine, the one who hadn’t been invited to the celebration, yet whose presence was essential for the future of all.

And so following days unfolded in an almost feverish haze.

Siren Island was too bright, too alive, as if the world were desperate to forget that it had died countless times. For Mai, however, every light felt like a needle reminding her she didn’t quite belong. She had survived, yes—but she still didn’t know who she was in this absurd pause they insisted on calling peace.

She moved noiselessly through the halls and workshops of the base, faithful to her habit of being invisible. She never stayed too long in any one place. Never spoke more than necessary. The tests, the endless interrogations, the clinical scrutiny of every corner of her mind still clung to her bones like a metallic scent.

She found some ease only when she wandered into natural spaces alone or with her friends. The dinosaurs and other creatures roaming the island soothed her—she liked watching them move through the jungle without a single care about war or something like that.

“I wish I was a dinosaur,” she thought foolishly one day as she looked the five Titanosaurus on the island from afar.

Alexia, Noki, Rein, Leon, Nikolai, Naomi, Raven, Oxy, Cleo—a golden dragon—all tried to cheer her up, and in a way they succeeded. She was grateful for that and didn't want to worry them.

It was hard to join conversations though, and when she did, her smiles were faint and short-lived.

But that was better than the testing rooms. She was between friends. She told herself she should feel lucky—she was alive, she had endured horrors, but she was still breathing. Even with the nightmares, she was here. Melancholy washed over her in waves, but that was normal. Everyone had it, the memories of war were all still too raw.

But that afternoon, in the crowded dining hall, something made her stop.

And for the first time, all the dark thoughts were erased for a moment.

A pair of eyes.

Yellow, like lemons cut open by sunlight.

Seeing them was like taking a cold shock to the back of her neck.

The face they belonged to felt familiar even before she understood why. That woman was sitting with Rein and Noki, eating hamburgers and chatting.

Half a second later, the sluggish gears of memory clicked into place: she had seen that face in the pages of Rein’s and Noki’s albums, in the picture frames at their house.

The old photographs, the name underlined countless times.

The mother.

The wandering adventurer.

Lizbeth Londonderry.

For the first time in a very long while, Mai felt heat rush to her cheeks.

She almost looked away immediately.

She didn’t know what to do with the sudden jolt in her chest, as if something invisible had tightened around her ribs. She couldn’t talk to her. She shouldn’t. She could barely keep the tray from trembling in her hands.

And yet, she felt Lizbeth watching her.

The woman had frozen mid-bite, mouth slightly open, crumbs clinging to her left cheek.

Mai knew—knew—that Lizbeth had recognized something in her, even if only a faint whisper of it. And that realization stole her breath.

Mortified by her own childish reaction, Mai dipped her head in a clumsy greeting—almost a plea—and turned toward a different conversation, pretending everything was normal. Her cheeks were burning, and a warm pulse throbbed in her chest. She cursed her body for its betrayal. What was she doing? Why did this matter?

It felt as if every pore in her skin had opened like flowers in spring.

She felt a soft tingling where her clothes brushed against her breasts.

She tried to tell herself it was just nerves. Exhaustion.

But it wasn’t.

It was… something else.

Hours later, when she thought she’d calmed down, she ran into her again.

The hallway was nearly empty, bathed in gentle sunlight filtering through the hanging plants. Lizbeth appeared around the corner with a quiet step, and for a brief instant—just one—Mai thought the sight was too perfect to be real: the long blonde hair catching the light, the posture weary yet steady, the depth of her gaze. She wore simple pants and a shirt with the top buttons undone. Mai’s eyes lingered, tracing every detail. The long legs. The wide hips, the waist, the breasts. The long neck and the lips.

She was beautiful.

Too beautiful.

Beautiful enough to hurt.

She didn’t know why, but her heart was pounding as if trying to break free from her ribs. She felt as though she could see that body naked beneath the clothes, every line, every shadow. She remembered the lonely nights when she had touched herself thinking of that face from the photographs.

Of the man called Shin, and even Mimi.

But this was different.

It was like seeing an image she was meant to recognize from somewhere else—almost like a famous painting whose first encounter she couldn’t recall.

And Mai hated feeling it.

Hated not having control over it.

Who was this woman, truly?

Had she been in love with her since she saw her in the photos with Shin? She couldn't deny it. Even during the war, she had thought about her and asked Rein about her. Rein had told her that she had joined Nevermore and was on the front lines. But Mai hadn't expected to meet her.

Lizbeth stopped in front of her. She didn’t speak.

Mai felt her senses tighten like strings about to snap. She almost felt her hidden antennae vibrate involuntarily beneath her silver hair.

The woman looked at her with a hint of surprise. Her lips slightly parted, those long eyelashes giving her an almost sensual air.

Mai swallowed hard, terrified she might start drooling at any moment.

Introducing herself first was the correct thing to do. It was what anyone trying to build a bridge—even a small, fragile one—was expected to do. And for some unknown reason, she wanted that bridge to exist.

She extended her hand.

She didn’t know if she was trembling or simply cold.

“Mai,” she whispered. “Izumi Mai.”

The name fell between them like a fragile note.

Lizbeth held it in silence, as if she understood something Mai had not said.

“Lizbeth…” she replied. “Lizbeth Londonderry.”

The touch was brief.

But enough.

There was no revealed destiny, no magical glow, no promises.

Only a suspended moment on an island trying to forget the war, while two women—too exhausted to pretend they were whole—allowed a thin, almost invisible thread to stretch between them.

Mai lowered her hand slowly.

Her heart was beating far too loudly for her liking, but she didn’t step back.

For the first time since her return, she felt something she didn’t know how to name.

It wasn’t fear. It wasn’t pain.

It was… attraction.

Or something like it.

Love?

And though she would never admit it aloud, the world felt a little less cold in that moment.
