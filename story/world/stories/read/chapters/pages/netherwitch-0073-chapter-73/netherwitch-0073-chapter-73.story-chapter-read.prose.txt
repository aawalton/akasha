“As we commit these souls to the deep, we ask that the Heavenly Will grants unto them peace and tranquility. Let the currents of chaos carry their bodies to the far shores, so they might find resurrection in the Kingdom of Heaven.”

As Sylvester Swift spoke, he tossed lamps from the Ouranó Fídi’s stern one by one. The cages of wrought iron bobbed in the void, their glass orbs filled with spectral fire. Souls drifted in the dark, a chain of celestial beauty. Slowly, they dispersed into the currents, their distant lights blending into the sea of stars.

“Amen.”

Solemnly, he drew a cross over his chest. This wasn’t just a religious gesture. His every motion released a bundle of runes. A sequence formed, one hundred and sixty-three sigils long. Fated request was cast carrying with it the intent: ‘may these souls wash ashore in Heaven.’

Surely, there had never been a captain of a pirate ship kinder than him.

With a wild grin, Sylvester faced his audience. A crowd of fifty pirates stood on deck, all wearing an expression of abject horror.

“Gentlemen, we are only three days from Tartarus,” Sylvester said, addressing the group. The five-foot-six man strolled down the line, his cane thumping on the deck. “Which means every single one of you needs to make a decision.”

In the past week and a half, Sylvester had emptied the Ouranó Fídi’s resurrection pool, reviving a score of sailors along with two enormous squids. These pirates had been chosen on Gerwen’s recommendation, cross-checked by Sylvester’s divinations. He had confirmed the loyalty of everyone using causality spells along with Phương’s Resonance.

The pirates here were above the board. Well, they were as above the board as Sylvester could expect. This was a pirate ship and he, a thieving captain. Mutiny was practically expected. Which was why he had the squid ready and waiting to murder anyone who stepped out of line.

“When we make port, you will be given a choice,” he continued. “First, you may depart this ship, never to return. Second, you may remain on the Ouranó Fídi. Those who choose the latter will not only receive fair compensation and a clean slate, they will be granted an opportunity beyond anything they can imagine.”

The warlock’s jasper eyes darkened.

“But make no mistake, those who turn against me will suffer a calamity no less than your former captain, Kabir. I will see you cast into the dark. Then, I’ll taint your path with foul wishes ensuring that when you awake, it will be in the hands of the enemy you fear most.”

Sylvester’s lips curled into a vile smile. “Now, Gerwen, I leave it to you.”

“You heard the Captain, full speed ahead,” the brown-haired dwarf roared. “We make port at Mystikipoli! And before we do, I don’t want to see any mistakes, or I’ll cast your sorry soul into the void myself!”

Sailors scrambled as Gerwen barked.

The Ouranó Fídi was not the Utrecht. The ship required at least ten men to sail, and twenty to navigate the starry void smoothly and without issue. If the weapon systems were included, this massive ship demanded two dozen more. This was a problem Sylvester would have to consider deeply when he brought the Ouranó Fídi home with him to the Cloud Island Wilderness.

But it was not an issue he needed to deal with now. Instead, the silver-haired man turned his crimson eyes to the pair of squids swimming alongside them. These giants were a rare astral species known as taara vidroop, or star squid as most preferred. They could be found nesting in lakes and oceans, where the rivers provided a clear and broad path to the void.

For Sylvester, their soul stones had great worth. All the great powers had methods to replicate powerful phantasmal beasts. Some would synthesize titanic phantasms to be used in war. For the Cloud Island Wilderness in particular, the taara vidroop was incredibly valuable. If the cores were consumed when creating a dungeon, they’d have an endless stream of dangerous beasts.

Of course, Sylvester had no intent to settle for the creature as is. While the taara vidroop was strong, it wasn’t suitable for war. The mollusc’s size, strength, and toughness were fine. Its ink was even better, as the substance absorbed magic weakly, defeating most divinations. The beast, however, had two fatal flaws. First, the squid was too slow. At most, it could swim through the void at 80 kilometers per hour. Second, the taara vidroop lacked an effective means to pierce the side walls of a fully armored military ship.

Which meant the behemoths were too weak to threaten Heaven’s fleets. And that wouldn’t do. It wouldn’t do at all. Thumbing through a catalog of nether codes, he considered how the creature might be crossed. When Heaven reached the Cloud Island Wilderness, he’d greet them with a nasty surprise.

The first day of Men-Pulvia dawned over the Asphodel Meadows. The sun, as usual, was hidden behind the ashen haze, the sky a curtain of gray. Crimson clouds lingered on the horizon, but no rain fell here in the Chaménos.

The Chaménos was a mountain range and a smuggler’s paradise. After the Blood Crusade, one of the Demon Kings had the bright idea of filling the mountains with illusions and anti-divination enchantments. The notion was that these mountains would serve as a base of operations should Heaven launch a second invasion. The invasion never came.

A few centuries after Ragnarok, the government had tried to clean up the mess. By then, the effort had been hopeless. Illusions, once spawned by spells, were now part of the plane’s world logic. For every enchantment extracted, the mountains grew two more. Eventually, the government gave up on the project, declaring all of Chaménos a nature preserve.

It was over these mountains that the Ouranó Fídi flew. The skyship had quietly slipped over the edge of the plane deep in the night. Though the Chaménos was difficult to navigate, the pirate crew had followed this path many times, and they were deeply familiar with the surrounding peaks.

“We should be coming up on the entrance in just a few minutes, Captain Swift,” the dwarf, Gerwen, informed.

From his place near the bow, Sylvester offered an absent nod.

Rather than the terrain, his attention was taken by the churning of fate. He could hear the murmur of intent in the air. A hidden war was being waged. Spells wrapped the plane and the starry void surrounding it. Divinations and magics of fate reverberated in the air, seeking knowledge of ‘Sylvia Swallows’, the ‘Thirteenth Piece,’ and the ‘Bluelight’.

But this was just a fraction of the storm, only detectable because the intent resonated with Sylvester’s name. Tens of thousands of mages and oracles were casting wishes and twisting fate to fulfill their desires.

When he had come to Tartarus last, he’d noticed none of it. He’d been too weak. Now, his combined level had risen from 850 to 1540. In addition, he had gained the trait ‘mystic attunement’ that not only sharpened his magical senses, but made distinct the intents behind magic, separating the spells from the seething chaos of nature. If that weren’t enough, he had used a skill book Web of Secrets to enhance his understanding of divination.

“Can the Shadow Baron be trusted?” Sylvester questioned, turning to face the dwarf.

Wind whistled over the deck, stirring the squat man’s beard. Skyships came in two types: air dominant and astral dominant. The Ouranó Fídi was designed first and foremost for the starry void. Compared to the Cumulus, it was sluggish with a handling so poor the flyt could run circles around the Fídi.

In fact, it was impressive that a ship so large could levitate at all. The Ouranó Fídi must’ve been incredibly expensive.

“The Shadow Baron is the scum of the earth,” Gerwen answered without hesitation. “He’s a conniving bastard who will screw you for everything you’re worth. But his rules are fair and strict. If not, no one would dock at Mystikipoli. More than anyone else, it’s us pirates who need a trustworthy port.”

“Then let us hope you are correct, because if I lose my new ship, I won’t be happy in the least,” Sylvester noted.

His crimson eyes turned back to the world beyond the bow. Ahead, barely seen through the dense fog, was a lake.

Mystikipoli was found in the diakenon, the intervoid which existed between Tartarus’s layers. Void, as defined in the netherworld, came in many flavors. The starry void, or the astral world, existed between the planes. The shimmering lights seen in the dark weren’t stars, but rather ripples where the light of Unus Mundus breached the dark curtain.

If an immortal in the starry void or a standard plane flew sufficiently high, they’d eventually reach the empty void, a place of pure darkness where the ether grew so thin it was non-existent. There, no demon could thrive, for the energy of life would slowly drain away to nothing. If the same individual went higher still, eventually the laws and logic of the world itself would peel away, demanding that the traveler bring a spark of reality with them.

Then there was the frozen void. This was the emptiness mortals knew best, because it was nothing less than what the denizens of the netherworld called outer space of the material universe.

A diakenon, by contrast, was the distinct void found within the scope of a plane. On most worlds, the diakenon could be reached by digging several kilometers straight down into the bones of the earth while far from the coasts. Tartarus provided a much more expedient way of reaching the diakenon. All one had to do was find a broken tunnel. If that didn’t satisfy, a traveler could head into any cavern, find its most distant reaches, then dig until the earth gave way to nothingness.

“You won’t be the only one displeased if you lose the ship, Captain Swift. I’ve been serving on the Ouranó Fídi for a century now,” Gerwen stated. The dwarf patted the wooden rail affectionately. “Now, if your lordship doesn’t mind, I need to check on the others and make sure they don’t do anything stupid.”

“Do as you wish,” Sylvester dismissed.

The dwarf’s shouts rang over the deck. Sailors moved, adjusting the fins and engines as instructed. The Ouranó Fídi tilted, bow pointing toward the surface of the lake. The ship plunged. Illusionary waters parted. Moments later, they found themselves in a giant, decaying tunnel. The crumbling stone walls revealed the dark void beyond.

Except, to Sylvester, the diakenon wasn’t dark at all.

Instead, with his pure starlight eyes, he glimpsed a world of a magnificent beauty. Crystalline spindles stretched out from the edge of existence, threads of space carrying energies primordial. When the strings were close, they spun around themselves, forming a funnel. In rare cases, these funnels merged into colossal, crystalline pillars connecting the layers of Tartarus. In others, the gathered strands were left loose, creating complicated tufts of tunnels, the type Belkis and her party would’ve explored.

It was an awe-inspiring image. Shk, shk, shk. Sylvester captured the scene with his System camera, though it may be months before he could post the pictures on the forums. But when he did, his fellow gamers would assuredly ooh and aah all over them.

If anything, the static depiction undersold the beauty. The world around the silver-haired asteri was alive. Though the diakenon was said to be similar to the empty void, this understated the complexity. The layers of Tartarus breathed, ether flowing in and out of the spindles like water rising and falling with the tides. As this energy flowed, the threads shrank, swelled, and shifted. Some grew longer. Others decayed.

It was in this twisted, complex void they found the city Mystikipoli.

Mystikipoli was tiny, by Tartarus’s standards. Its population wasn’t much bigger than that of Orasul Lunii, which made it around twice as large as Viridian City. The foundation of the Secret City was built on a crude astralship of sorts. Every five to ten years, it’d detach from a broken cavern stub then sail through the diakenon to attach to a new one. This process assured that only invited persons could find their way to the port while also placing the city at a point where ether was relatively abundant.

“Captain Swift,” Gerwen greeted, finding a moment to free himself from the ship’s labors. “Remember that the Shadow Baron will demand that you sign a soul contract.”

“You informed me of this when we discussed ports,” Sylvester replied.

“I just wanted to make sure your lordship was aware,” Gerwen said with a bow. “You are a person of great power, Mr. Swift, but do not think you can challenge the Shadow Baron. Mystikipoli is backed by powers far greater than you can imagine.”

Of this, Sylvester had little doubt. As the saying went, two men could keep a secret if one of them was dead. For Mystikipoli to stand so long demanded powerful support. There were many noble demons who would benefit from smugglers and a black market, such as Baron Naopte, who wished to transport slaves. At the very least, a great demon was protecting this city. Quite possibly, the true backer was an archdemon or an archduke.

Sylvester’s lips quirked. He could easily imagine that bandit, Lord Azazel, squeezing Mystikipoli for spare cash.

…

Then his expression tightened. The idea was uncomfortably plausible. Sylvester hoped he wasn’t sailing into Lord Azazel’s hidden domain.

Slowly, the Ouranó Fídi pulled into the city’s broad wharves. A dozen vessels were already tied to the docks. As the mooring lines were thrown overboard, other sailors moved to unload the Ouranó Fídi’s cargo. The Unmyeong Naru wasn’t the only ship Captain Kabir had targeted. It was merely the latest on his list. Having no wish to deal in souls, Sylvester had dumped that part of the load into Captain Lugh’s hands. The rest of the ill-gotten gains would be sold in Mystikipoli while the skyship underwent repairs.

Letting his minions work, he scanned the city with eyes of jasper. Mystikipoli sported no less than twelve cannons, most pointed at the wharves. At one hundred and fifty meters from bow to stern, the Ouranó Fídi was the largest vessel present by half. Indeed, most of the crafts found here were airships. As a general rule, airships were smaller than their void-faring cousins.

A pair of men, followed by a dozen guards, walked down the wharf. Sylvester floated down to greet them.

Two men separated from the crowd. The first was a stern looking man with a lion’s mane. It took a second to place his bloodline. He was a beast-kin, one of the rare few who had Awakened. Beside the lionman was a corpse-like figure, a wight.

“Where is Captain Kabir?” the lionman demanded.

“Drifting in the void,” Sylvester answered. He gave a small bow. “I am Sylvester Swift, the new owner of this vessel. It is my pleasure to make your acquaintance. From hence forth, all matters regarding the Ouranó Fídi will go through me.”

The wight spoke, his voice a deathly rattle. “We don’t let just anyone dock here in this city.”

“I am not just anyone, boy,” Sylvester said, his tone full of threat. The warlock’s crimson eyes glowed. The world thumped, five drum beats falling in tight sequence. “And I believe I have already given my name. Isn’t it polite to give me yours?”

The guards shifted. The lionman tensed. Sylvester’s glare was fearless and steady. Unlike gamers, demons didn’t have the luxury of the System. They judged an opponent’s strength by feeling the distortion imposed upon the world.

And Sylvester’s distortion was large. He was already C-VI/High, strong enough that the world logic began to warp around him. Most demons would never encounter someone of his power. It’d be rarer still for them to meet his better. If pressure alone didn’t make his strength clear, then the patter of his soul cores would resolve all doubts. Five beats meant five spiritual hearts, marking him as fey of the fourth consolidation.

It took no more than a second for the lionman’s expression to shift.

“Officer Valentino,” he greeted, suddenly looking very friendly. “And this is my companion, Officer Walenty. My apologies for our aggressive approach, but Mystikipoli is invitation only. Which is why the two of us will be happy to escort you to where you can receive your invitation.”

“By all means, Mr. Valentino,” Sylvester accepted. He waved his cane toward the ship. “Your men can keep an eye on my vessel. Should any of my sailors cause trouble, you need only inform me. I’ll make sure the fools regret whatever they’ve done.”

The warlock’s lips twisted into an evil smile.

With painted politeness, the officers brought him to a cushy room. Sylvester took a seat in a comfortable, leather chair. The room was quite nice, with paintings on the wall and a polished coffee table in front of him. He considered kicking up his heels for a moment, but decided that would be ungentlemanly for him.

Fifteen minutes later, Sylvester was graced by the Shadow Baron himself.

The baron was a tall man with golden fur on the back of his hands. A thin tail waved behind him, the tip a hairy tuft. The Shadow Baron was from the rare bloodline: Ma’at Thoth Aani. Prior to Awakening, such demons would have a baboon’s body and a dog’s head.

Unsurprisingly, the baron looked far more human than that. As Sylvester had when facing Officers Valentino and Walenty, the man made his cultivation clear. Four cores thumped, hard and heavy. The silver-haired warlock didn’t need his System to judge the baron at C-V/High.

A not-so-subtle warning. Though Sylvester was stronger, Mystikipoli had enough muscle to hold him in check.

“Mr. Swift.” The Shadow Baron’s voice was dangerous. “Not only did you steal the Ouranó Fídi, you dared to enter my city unannounced.”

“I prefer the word, ‘acquired,’” Sylvester responded. He leaned back in his chair, fingers tented like a devilish villain. “And I believe it is customary to introduce yourself. Unless, of course, Baron is your actual name.”

“I liked Captain Kabir,” the Shadow Baron continued. He rose – Bang! – slamming a heavy palm onto the coffee table. Leaning forward, he met the warlock’s jasper eyes. “I don’t like you.”

“That’s truly unfortunate,” Sylvester said blandly. “Perhaps you and Kabir will be lucky enough to meet again. If you do, remind him to pick his targets with more care.”

“Do you think so highly of yourself as to play games in my city, warlock?” the aani spat.

“What I think is that you are a man of business,” Sylvester returned. His crimson eyes were like blood-soaked steel. “I was told I would receive an invitation here. Is that not the case?”

The Shadow Baron stared. Then he sat, casually tossing a metal cylinder onto the table. The gray, metal contract shell clattered on the wood.

“Invitations are given to guests,” the Shadow Baron said. “Stowaways sign a contract. And don’t even think about leaving this building before you do. I might struggle to stop you, but it’d be easy to reduce your ship to tinder.”

Ah, a reasonable threat. Fortunately for the Shadow Baron, Sylvester was a reasonable man. Leaning back, the warlock set his left ankle on his right knee. “Show me this contract of yours, then.”

The aani studied the silver-haired gentleman for a moment longer. Then he unrolled a length of parchment. Sylvester’s fingers twitched. A whirl of wind plucked the leathery page from the table. When it floated close enough, he snagged it out of the air, his crimson eyes skimming over the terms.

In the silence, the baron’s fingers drummed on the arm of his chair. Minutes passed. Finally, the aani couldn’t hold his silence. “Stop wasting time and sign.”

“I will take as long as I need,” Sylvester replied, his words perfectly enunciated. Then he lifted his cane, using the handle to push the metal tube back across the table. “But if you want to resolve this quickly, replace this hunk of junk. That garbage won’t be going into my soul. Find something suitable for a man of my station.”

A soul contract consisted of two components: the ‘contract’ and the ‘shell’. The contract was the highly enchanted leather on which the abiding rules were written. The shell contained the contract and acted as the enforcer. The shell was, spiritually, the larger of the two. It not only had to serve as a bomb, it had to resist extraction, read the bearer’s actions, and evaluate them against the contract’s terms.

It was also the more expensive and substitutable of the two components.

“This isn’t a negotiation,” the Shadow Baron growled.

Sylvester smirked. Lies, deceit, and pressure tactics. No fourth consolidation fey would willingly sacrifice twenty droms of their carrying capacity for the next fifteen years. Nor would they consider signing a soul contract without discussing terms. If he were to fold, the Shadow Baron would suspect that Sylvester’s cultivation had been faked.

“Everything is subject to negotiation,” the warlock returned while he continued to skim the contract. “Especially for men of power, like us. There is no doubt you could cause me a fair bit of trouble. But I can cause you a great deal of trouble, too.”

He, of course, had no intentions of complying with this contract. Sylvester’s realm ring array was Hidden Hut, which had the False Compartment effect. This isolated a portion of his soul, shielding him from cursed tools, including soul contracts. To create an item like that said a lot about Yvonne Taubert’s conniving character.

How admirable. That crone’s heart was full of evil tricks, just as any good witch’s should be.

Sylvester’s reliance, however, was not his realm ring array. The mages who made soul contracts weren’t fools. They, too, had a bag of tricks ready to catch deceivers. It’d be quite unpleasant if the contract saw through Hidden Hut’s False Compartment and detonated instantly. No, it’d be far better to disarm the contract right at the start.

His scheme was for time. Asking for a replacement shell was one of those tricks. With sufficient time, Sylvester could analyze the scroll’s runic code, code he had fed into Runeworks Studio. Creating a counter spell like this verged on fluent magic, the calling card of a master magister. There were less than a hundred such wizards in all the netherworld.

Which was why the Shadow Baron would never suspect what came next.

“Since you insist,” the aani relented. He waved a hand in the air. The wight approached. “Bring us higher quality contract shells. Mr. Swift can look over the parchment while we wait.”

The warlock almost smirked. There was another reason he wanted a new shell. Expensive contract shells were custom work. As a result, their designs were rarely tested. A high price didn’t mean high quality. Quite often, expensive shells had serious security flaws.

Yet another means to slip the noose.

“Tut-tut-tut.” A minute later, Sylvester clicked with his tongue. He placed the parchment on the table then shoved it toward the aani. “You must think me a fool.”

Making a deceptive contract was hard. Magic could only process information in the form of runes or intent. Only a halfwit would sign a contract written in runes, which meant every contract had a psychic imprint. Thus, the signer was bound to the contract’s spirit and not its word.

But that did not mean deception was impossible.

“What excuse are you reaching for now?” the Shadow Baron questioned. “If you continue to test me, this will end poorly.”

“Excuse?” Sylvester sounded. With an angry expression, he tapped a finger on the parchment. “Don’t play games. I can see through your trick. Take this clause right here which says I’m not allowed to steal from any person residing in Mystikipoli.”

“A fair rule to which we hold everyone in this city,” the baron replied smoothly.

Sylvester’s lips turned up. What a magnificent bastard.

“Oh, really,” he sneered. He tapped another clause without missing a beat. “Here you define the word ‘steal’ as the taking of ‘property’. Then, on this page you define ‘property’ as whatever a ‘guest’ has in their possession when they enter the city.”

This was the game the Shadow Baron had played with his contract. He had defined each term in a precise manner, forcing the reader to jump back and forth across several pages. While the intent and the spirit were clear, this shuffling created enough confusion to insert a logic trap.

“I fail to see the problem, Mr. Swift,” the Shadow Baron retorted.

“You don’t see the problem?” Sylvester scoffed. “Then let me ask you this, am I a guest? Just minutes ago you called me a stowaway. And if I only become a guest when I sign this contract, is the Ouranó Fídi in my possession? Let me make this clear, the ship belongs to me, Mr. Baron. You will not take it.”

The Shadow Baron’s eyes narrowed. “I think it’s reasonable we consider the ship in your possession.”

“Spare me the bauernfrühstück,” Sylvester spat. His tongue tingled, the doctored tool correcting what would’ve been a cutesy curse. “You will fix the language directly, making clear that all the things I had when entering Mystikipoli belong to me, not just the things I possess right now.”

“You are quite the arrogant twit, aren’t you?” the aani spat. “Do you think Mystikipoli will cater to your every whim?”

“No, but I think those who back you would be very unhappy if all the ships in your harbor suddenly had their main beam snap,” Sylvester said in retort, his jasper eyes glowing with crimson light. “This is business, Mr. Baron, and we are gentlemen. Don’t try and pull the wool over my eyes again.”

The aani glared. Then he clawed the parchment to his side of the table. With swift scratches of his quill, he amended the contract to correct the ‘error’. Finally, the Shadow Baron shoved it back toward the silver-haired warlock.

“Changed as you asked,” he rumbled. “But you best remember who has the power here. And what the consequences will be if you cross the line.”

“So long as we both behave like reasonable men, I think we’ll get along swimmingly,” Sylvester returned, his righteous rage already banished.

He lifted the parchment with a smile, his fingers giving a subtle wiggle. A near invisible chain of runes fluttered over the contract, carrying with them a touch of void and law. Not a single word on the parchment changed, but its understanding of time had been brutally twisted. In the eyes of this paper, one year lasted for a single second.

Which meant, fifteen seconds after placing the contract in his soul, the terms would already have lapsed.

The door opened. Officer Walenty walked in, then set a series of six tubes on the table. Sylvester sat up. With a serious expression, he studied each, blatantly using Observe Item. Finally, he picked a brass tube laden with sapphires.

“I’ll go with this one,” he declared.

“Just sign the damn papers,” the aani growled.

Completing the soul contract, paying the docking fees, and dealing with the additional headaches took an hour. It also cost him 550 glints, which amounted to 300 to 330 thousand soli. By far, the largest chunk of the bill was the repairs to the Ouranó Fídi. Given time, much of the hull could regenerate. The more complicated parts, however, required a specialist.

The Tilaka targeting system, sadly, would have to wait. No one in Mystikipoli could fix it.

Everything in the Secret City was priced in glints. Typically in the netherworld, large transactions were finalized using banknotes. But criminals didn’t dare leave their expenses on record. Soli were too heavy. Small exchanges of a few hundred were fine. Paying out tens of thousands of soli would leave a soul sagging under their weight. The glint was thus the coin of choice.

Gold was the color of the underworld.

Freed from the drudgeries of criminal bureaucracy, Sylvester explored the city. Mystikipoli was a mess of unregulated construction and random, eclectic shops. Smugglers brought in goods from all over the netherworld. Many would try to sell their wares directly. Professional merchants added to the chaos, sweeping in to pluck various treasures from the cargo holds before filling their shops.

The result was a raucous marketplace. Fey, demons, and the occasional celestial shouted to the streets. In the awning-covered stalls lining the dark streets one could find wild deals, shady items, and outright scams. Of course, when one happened upon a truly valuable artifact, there was no way to know if it was too hot to touch.

A single glance revealed the chaos. Down the street, in an open stall, a salaman was selling black soul lamps. Across from him, a cyclops offered two lightning cannons so large their spiky ends jutted out through holes cut into the ceiling. Weirdly, there were no other siege weapons to be found. Instead, the cyclops had a warehouse of swords, spears, and various armors.

Just down the street, was a formal merchant whose store sported glass windows. Proudly displayed were slave collars of dark iron, copper, and platinum. Another window showed a different set of vile instruments like Ki Restriction Shackles and Mana Taps. On the sign was written: Ask about our falsified credentials! Sylvester’s stomach churned at the sight.

Slavery was a disgusting practice.

He turned the corner, finding another enterprise named: Solely Souls. A naga argued loudly with the owner, a score of glowing lamps in his possession. If a pirate group could spare the karma, they often caged the souls of the sailors and passengers before selling them to stores like this. Illegal slave rings would then take the unfortunate fellows to distant planes where falsified records were unlikely to be questioned.

It was souls that’d drawn the Ouranó Fídi to the Unmyeong Naru. Their aim had been the freshly coded demons born in Jeolsung. Such individuals had no memories and no history and, thus, were more easily transacted. Better yet, as most were doomed to slavery regardless, making the karmic hit that much smaller.

Capitalists always found loopholes, Heavenly Will be damned.

A minute later, Sylvester found a stall that caught his interest.

A hogmin rubbed greedy hands together as the warlock approached. Sylvester peered at the demon with interest. The bloodline Typhon Aplistia Choiros had long since been bred to reduce its potential. For this reason, hogmin were a favored slave race. It was very rare for any of their species to enter the second consolidation, much less complete their grand mutation.

Yet, this hogmin was Awakened. His blue eyes, rounded face, and pug nose didn’t detract from the obvious humanity. If it weren’t for the tusks, Sylvester might not have realized the shopkeeper was a hogmin at all.

“Finally, someone with a discerning eye. What catches your interest, sir? Is it the talismans or…,” the hogmin’s eyes gleamed. His voice turned low. “The Hellfire?”

Crimson eyes turned from the demon to the shop’s display. To stare any longer would be rude, and Sylvester was a gentleman.

“You have quite the collection here,” he noted. Sylvester picked up a red jewel, studying it carefully.

Talismans were stabilized potions. Just as a brew captured a spell, a talisman provided a similar service. The distinction was one of permanence. To be considered a talisman, the product had to endure for at least one month outside any container. The good ones lasted for years.

Which meant they could be stockpiled.

“A meteor talisman,” he observed after a moment. “Armaments of this quality are restricted goods. Where did you get them?”

“Show enough silver to the right coven, and who knows what they’ll supply,” the hogmin answered slyly. “The talismans cost twenty glints. Why not buy a dozen and drown your foe in a sea of fire?”

Sylvester let out a snort. The hogmin was overselling his product. Like everything in the netherworld, talismans had spiritual weight. Then there was a second obstacle. The jewel had been engraved with runes, so the user could merge their mana with the contained spell, thus imbuing their intent. Which was a valuable feature, as the caster would be able to aim the meteor blast once it was released. Sadly, that same feature restricted the rate at which the talisman could be triggered.

To activate a dozen would take a full second. Still, the hogmin wasn’t wrong to say it’d produce a sea of fire.

“A fair price,” Sylvester accepted. He grabbed a topaz jewel then a piece of parchment right after. “I’ll take this lightning talisman and the shielding talisman too.”

He set three viginiti on the table. With a thick hand, the hogmin scooped up the gold. His blue eyes showed a spark of understanding.

“If you’re trying to crack the formula, then you might want to buy five of each,” the shopkeeper enticed. He grinned, tapping a bottle of roiling black fire. “Or you can take a shot at a real puzzle. Hellfire was Hell’s most prized strategic weapon. The origin of the flame was humble. It came from the lowly shambler, a Class I phantasmal beast. An enterprising noble could buy shamblers by the legion. Of course, true Hellfire was a doctored formula derived from the shambler’s natural magic. Like the original, it was sticky, stubborn, and chewed through all defenses. Hellfire, however, devoured mana as easily as the shambler’s flame did ki, blood, and flesh.

And it was far more intense.

Hellfire was split into three grades: accursed, profane, and infernal. Even the weakest of the three was ten times stronger than a shambler’s blaze. What’s more, Hellfire was effective against titans. With just twenty bottles, Sylvester could burn the Ouranó Fídi to ash. A profane flame could threaten arch-level beings like Remiel and Asmodeus. Better yet, the blaze could eat the edges of a domain, making it a fine deterrence even if the individuals were never touched.

But there was one small problem.

“Heaven and the Fey Federation have been trying to crack Hellfire for four thousand years,” Sylvester scoffed. “Even a warlock with my brilliance has no chance of solving this puzzle.”

True Hellfire was produced by a transformation realm art. The art itself had long since been leaked, but that was irrelevant. The art couldn’t be practiced without a seed organ manufactured on Naraka. The grade of the flame was decided by the strength of the demon who produced it. Devils generated an accursed flame, great demons a profane fire, and archdemons an infernal blaze.

And that was only the first step. Hellfire was a potion. Bottling it required mixing a brew. And therein lay the most serious issue. The only stable alchemical base was a state secret. Worse, the recipe required a special essence only found on Helheim. Without it, the flame would endure for a few days at best.

Which was why only Hell had Hellfire. The other great powers had tried their best, but they had one hundred thousand bottles at most. Hell’s imperial government had billions.

“I know that look in your eye. You’re a scholar. Who’s to say what you’ll learn if you give it a try?” the hogmin tempted. “Hellfire is rare. Miss this chance and you might not see it again for centuries.”

Sylvester was enticed. He was enticed sorely. Unfortunately, he had business on Tartarus. Carrying a volatile and highly illegal flame in his briefcase was the kind of thing poor life decisions were made of. Thus, regretfully, he was forced to step back.

Talismans in hand, he continued his exploration of the city. It was a full hour before he found something that caught his interest.

“How much for the cloak and mask?” he asked.

Sylvester was browsing a dark shop lit by two dim lamps. Within, he’d found a pile of equipment fit for thieves. One of the fascinating tools he’d found here was a soul screw. This item, which looked like a torture device, was used to very gently push through the soul’s outer membrane. Once pierced, a dead demon’s equipment could be stolen.

“Seventy-five glints,” a deathly voice whispered.

The proprietor was a wraith, Nox Mors Larua. Wraiths were ghostly figures famed for their intangible forms and their ability to steal energy from others. Among demons, there was a great debate as to which bloodline was the worst one to be born with. Usually, hogmin won that ignoble honor. The larua, however, were a stiff competitor on that list. Until Awakening, the poor creatures lost their ability to touch, feel, or taste.

Incidentally, after Awakening all wraiths gained the ability to build a disposable physical body called a ‘lich’.

Sylvester ran his fingers over the charcoal gray cloth. The cloak was forged and enchanted to resist any attempt to see the person hiding behind it.

Then his jasper eyes narrowed. “I think 60 glints is a more reasonable price, don’t you?”

An intense round of haggling followed.

Assuming Lord Potami hadn’t leaked her identity to the top figures in the cult.

Lifespans in the Netherworld

While often considered immortal, every demon, fey, and celestial eventually faces true death. True death is the product of ennui and the accumulation of entropy in the soul. Eventually, this combination of forces results in an individual who lacks the will, desire, or integrity to return from Unus Mundus.

As a general rule, life spans can be calculated as follows:

Lifespan: (500+250*Mutations) * (1+consolidations)

Just as with the human lifespan, there is a great deal of variance among souls. For the unconsolidated, the range is roughly 400 to 600 years. As a demon’s life span increases, the lower end of the range tends to grow wider. Thus, seventh consolidation beings typically live for 4,000 to 12,000 years instead of the 8,000 to 12,000 expected. There is much debate as to why the deviation grows with strength, but the most common belief is that older demons eventually give up on life. Others insist that the lost lifespan results, instead, from suboptimal consolidations.

If true death is somehow avoided, eventually soul rot will set in. Outwardly, this results in the warping of the outer body through grotesque tumors and mutations. Mentally, soul rot produces intense discomfort, dementia, and eventually, total intellectual collapse.

Black Hand

Runes: 452

Mana: 750 to 6,000; 25%

Attack: 3250 to 19000

Penetration 150 + Mana / 5 pierce, 25% to 50% DRR, 75% multiplier

Max Boost: 200% to 400%

Max Range: 10km * Mysticism

Velocity: 15 * Mysticism m/s

Duration: 10 seconds

Black hand is a high magic of the abyss element. The spell creates a powerful hand able to crush or grind anyone caught in its grip. The hand starts at 20 meters measured from the bottom of the palm to its fingertips. If enough mana is supplied, the hand can reach 60 meters in length. The spell can squeeze, slap, punch, or crush, but it can only do so once. While striking at its target, black hand will exude a moderate gravitational pull which requires a Strength: Force of 300% to 1200% to resist.

Unlike most spells, black hand inflicts greater damage when attacking large scale constructions. The calculation is as such:

Actual Boost: MIN(Max Boost, (1/(1-SR))

Scale Adjust: Attack * (Actual Boost)^(2) * (1-SR)

Thus, black hand when cast at minimum power deals increased damage until scale resistance reaches 50%, where that damage is doubled. At a scale resistance of 75%, however, power is identical to that at a scale resistance of 0%. Finally, scaling beyond this point actually reduces the destructiveness of the spell.

If black hand were instead cast at max power, these break points would be 75% and 94%.

As a final note, black hand was designed to be used with rune gate. The large range allows the spell to sustain itself even after being teleported. What’s more, black hand incurs no increased mana cost when used in conjunction with rune gate. Base overhead, however, remains.
