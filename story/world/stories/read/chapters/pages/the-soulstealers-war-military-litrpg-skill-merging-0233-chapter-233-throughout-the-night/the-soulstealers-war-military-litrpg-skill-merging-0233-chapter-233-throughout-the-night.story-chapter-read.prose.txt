I changed into a simple tunic and donned a worker’s hood. It was too warm to wear a cloak, but a common head protection for workers in Gate was a detached hood made of woven reed stems. It shielded the backs of our heads from the sun and light debris, and more importantly, it was excellent at hiding my identity from prying eyes.

After making sure that Burr was safe and tucked away in the stable, I rushed out to the under-construction wall. A team of mortal engineers and masons was leading the project. They had escaped from a Domain to the north of here, having heard of the opportunities in Hyrdford, and they planned to work. With very few masons in Hyrdford, we needed their help to finish the wall in a timely fashion.

They assigned me a job, and I set to work.

The wall had begun construction almost as soon as we’d broken the cultists’ siege back in the spring. Now, most of it was fifteen feet high. They’d built the majority of it with large bricks gathered from a quarry down the valley, then sent barges down the river to deposit the bricks here.

I couldn’t say exactly what kind of stone it was. Gray, plain, but heavy. It would take days for me to punch through with my bare hands. Maybe even a week, given how tough the standing stones were to punch through.

I hauled carts of rock from the river to the last section of the wall that needed to be finished. There was no back gate—we only wanted one point of weakness—so the gap on the eastern side needed to be patched.

First, we loaded them from the barges onto a wagon. They were too heavy for most people to carry, and we didn’t have enough horses or oxen to pull all the carts. That was where the Dupes came in.

Though I could probably carry one of the bathtub-sized chunks of stone in my hands, given my enhanced strength, it was far more efficient to load them onto carts and carry three at a time. I navigated them up the shore, through the city, and out to the section of the wall that we were working on.

We’d dug a trench, which we then lined with bricks on the edges and filled with debris in the middle, as was relatively standard. If anyone tried to dig through, they’d have to deal with a waterfall of loose debris before they made it to the other side.

It was the best we could do for now, so we had to make it count.

~ ~ ~

After an afternoon of working as a transport mule, I took a short break. We planned to build through the night, and I could afford to spend a few night shifts helping out. But I couldn’t work constantly without taking a few breaks now and then. My hood was soaked in sweat, and my lungs were heaving. Though I’d eaten lunch, I was starving.

Supposedly, Path-walkers and people like us, who could use magic, could go for years without eating. Maybe you just got used to the hunger, or maybe there was something to do with maegen—and thus, it wasn’t available to regular Dupes. Whatever it was, I felt hungry, and the workers were happy enough to let me share the pottage they’d been cooking by the wall.

As the sun set and Welkinmere turned into a glowing lilac crescent in the sky, I took my bowl and climbed up to the top of the wall. The ramparts were wide—we could fit at least four rows of soldiers on it, all the way around. Towers dotted the walls, providing lookouts and bastions for archers to rain arrows down, and we’d mounted a few trebuchets atop them. We only had five, and all of them were facing east.

It wasn’t nearly as sturdy as Oschester’s walls, but it was better than nothing.

The merlons were utilitarian, but the wall had a slight overhang, making it difficult to get siege ladders up to it. As far as I knew, the scouts hadn’t reported any siege towers, but if there were, the slope of the wall’s base would make it difficult to roll them all the way up to the wall.

As I looked out over the surrounding valley, a steaming bowl of soup in-hand, Cuthward stepped up beside me.

“Lord Ceonwin told me you were back,” the old seer said. “He said you would be out here.”

“Anything to report?” I asked.

“Aside from another imminent attack?” he asked. “We’re just happy to know that you’re back.”

I gave him a nod of acknowledgement. Not many people knew I was still alive in our inner circles, and anyone who was had sworn a soul oath of secrecy. Even Cuthward.

In fact, more soldiers probably knew I was alive than nobles and aristocrats. It was probably better that way. Sure, the soldiers would swear an oath of secrecy as well, but I trusted them far more than a thegn or a portreeve.

“You’ve certainly made life interesting here,” Cuthward continued.

“I’m sorry.”

“While some are grieving, the deaths have been far fewer than they could have been,” Cuthward replied. “For now.”

“Let’s hope it stays that way. How is Sir Kenelm faring?”

“None the wiser. The last time he visited was a month ago, to collect his stipend, and then he left. He knows what the Warlord is up to, and he’s ready to escape when the final orc push begins.”

“There are only five thousand orcs out there.”

Five thousand. It didn’t feel like an ‘only’ when I said it like that, but compared to our numbers, they didn’t have good odds of winning a siege. During our strategy training, Moonhand had insisted that to win a siege, you needed ten times the number of the defender.

The orcs either had a backup plan, were confident enough in the Hangman, or didn’t know our numbers. Plus, we had a week to prepare.

But expecting things to go smoothly never worked well.

Besides, there were far more orcs scattered across the line, all ready to keep pushing across our territory at the same time and destroy the last few holdouts.

“Perhaps they lose,” Cuthward said. “I don’t expect them to stop, even if they do. They will keep trying.” He paused, then added, “Hardrick could use a meeting with you. He has been despairing ever since news of your death reached him.”

“I’ll meet with him tomorrow. He’s still been operating the guild?”

“Enough to pay for the wall and your army. You’re running a tight budget.”

“As long as we’re holding things together.”

“People are trying to carry on what they believe you left for them. No matter how short your reign was in their eyes. But that won’t last forever. If the orcs surround the city…”

I shook my head. “Their offensive will break the moment we rescue the 294th. Once we can restore that corridor, we should be able to push them back.”

“If you say so. Strategy is not my strong suit. However, you are facing another problem: the echoes of Lord Hinlaw’s public campaigns against you still remain in people’s minds. Although the Warlord has been comfortable enough to not continue smearing your name through the mud, the civilians in the core of the Domain remember you as a disloyal usurper who tried to dishonour a princess.”

“You think Hardrick could help with that?”

“Perhaps.”

“Tomorrow morning, then. I took a large sip from my bowl, ignoring the heat that would’ve burnt a mortal man’s mouth, then said, “I should get back to work.”

~ ~ ~

I worked all throughout the night, taking sporadic breaks, until finally, the sun rose again. I was expecting to feel light-headed, or at least have an unbreakable streak of yawns, but nothing came. My mind felt normal—for now. Maybe that was just the Focus talking, though.

In the morning, I set off and hunted for Hardric. As usual, he spent most of his days around the guild hall now, managing recruits and sales.

I wished I could have told him that I was alive sooner, but being a mortal man, he couldn’t swear a soul oath. It wasn’t safe. Since the incident at Steelshore, Subtle Men had visited many times, and he’d been one of their main interrogation subjects.

Sure, most of them probably believed I had died in Steelshore’s harbour, but there was enough suspicion. They had also asked about the rest of my squad, who were wanted fugitives now.

Heaping all that on Hardrick before things calmed down was just unfair.

Now that things had calmed down, it was probably safe to talk to him again.

I waited outside the guild hall early in the morning, and when he finally arrived, dressed in his best tunic and cloak, I whispered, “Hardrick.”

He glanced at me. “Is something wrong, Dupe? Am I needed at the hall?”

Pulling back my hood a few inches, I glanced at him. “We need to talk. Your office.”

“Y—yes.” His eyes lit with recognition. “Sir—”

“Not here.”

“Yes, sir.”

I guess my face was just that different from the other Dupes now. I pulled the hood back into place and followed him through the guild hall, then climbed up to the second level, where his office sat at the very end.

Once we were inside, I shut the door, then explained everything that had happened since I’d been gone. I apologized for not revealing myself sooner, then said, “I need a favour from you.”

“Yes, sir— My lord, I mean.”

“Pick guild members who you trust. We are going to send them throughout the Domain. We need ones who are good speakers, and who can spread the word—”

“About your survival?”

“Not yet. Not even they can know. But they need to begin planting the idea that most of what Hinlaw said was a lie, and that the Warlord is trying to sell the ground from beneath people’s feet. I need you to pick people who can change minds. People whose lives have gotten better over the past year, and can show, by example, how far we’ve come.”

“You think this will help?”

“If I’m going to take control of the domain, it’s a necessity. I can’t do it without the general support of the population, especially if I want them to fight a war and rise against the Warlord for me. Do you think you can do that?”

“I know who I can trust,” he said with a nod. “It may take some time, still, my lord.”

“I understand. We still have some time.”

“Very good, my lord.” He reached into a drawer in his desk and pulled out a stack of pages. “Would you…like to check over our finances? We’ve been doing some complex movements of money to get this wall built in a timely fashion.”

“I don’t think I’d understand it, even if it was given to me,” I replied. “Abacus looked these over?”

“He was responsible for most of the ideas, sir.”

“Then I trust his work. Carry on.”
