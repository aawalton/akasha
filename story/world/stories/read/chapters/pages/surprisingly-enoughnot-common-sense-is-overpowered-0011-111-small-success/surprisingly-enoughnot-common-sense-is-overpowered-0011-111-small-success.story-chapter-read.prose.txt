It was early morning, and Ning sat cross-legged beside the Fushan River, with a wooden fishing rod propped between two stones in case any fish decided to bite.

Since this was the period when yang energy was at its strongest, cultivating was ill-advised, so he naturally turned to the next best thing. After all, it would be a shame to waste precious hours that could otherwise be spent improving himself.

He took out one of the leaves he had prepared, pressed it against his forehead, and closed his eyes. The leaf trembled, slid half an inch, then stuck in place as he guided a faint thread of qi upward.

Stable.

Ning maintained his concentration, carefully regulating the flow to prevent the leaf from slipping. He'd been practicing this for days.

What exactly was he doing?

Control drills, a basic exercise to sharpen his control over spiritual energy.

From everything he'd read and watched back on Earth, Ning knew that raw strength was useful, but refined control could be terrifying. A master didn't waste power; they could cut mountains with a drop of water or shatter stone with a single breath. Precision made strength matter, so he wanted to improve his own control.

At first, Ning had assumed the sect would teach exercises like these from the beginning. Surely, an organization this large would have some structured methods for helping disciples improve their control.

However, when he'd asked Old Zhou, the old man had merely chuckled and said that control would "come naturally" with time and martial practice.

That answer hadn't satisfied him.

"Impossible. A sect this big, with qi at the core of everything, and not a single structured control method? Either I'm too low-ranking to access it, or they keep it locked away."

It wasn't impossible. Knowledge was valuable in the cultivation world, and the sect certainly wasn't handing out its entire collection of techniques to newcomers. Then again, perhaps most cultivators simply learned control through years of practice, as Old Zhou had suggested.

To Ning, it felt absurdly familiar, like stumbling into an RPG where entire regions of the map were greyed out until you reached a sufficient level.

So, Ning turned to the next best thing: drawing inspiration from the fictional works he'd encountered in his previous life and seeing whether he could apply those ideas to improve his control.

After all, this world itself would've been considered fiction back on Earth, so it wasn't such a ridiculous idea. Besides, it was only common sense to make use of everything at his disposal.

Hence, the leaf-sticking exercise.

In a certain gutsy ninja anime, it was one of the methods used to train chakra control, alongside the famous tree-climbing and water-walking exercises.

The principle was simple: use energy to hold a leaf in place without letting it fall. Maintain the flow long enough, and controlling that energy would gradually become easier.

Ning picked up another leaf and pressed it beside the first. Both quivered, threatening to slide free whenever his concentration wavered. Sweat gathered at his temple as he carefully adjusted the qi supporting them.

Of course, leaves weren't his only training tools. He had experimented with several other methods as well.

One involved balancing pebbles on his fingertips, each supported by a thin stream of qi. The challenge was to keep them steady without allowing the flow to fluctuate.

Another involved holding a cup of water between his palms while using qi to keep the surface perfectly still. At first, ripples appeared within seconds, but now he could maintain the balance for several minutes.

Then there was walking with a bowl of water balanced on his head, using qi to cushion each step.

Another classic straight out of a Chinese martial arts movie.

Martial artists used it to train posture and balance. Ning had adapted the exercise to practice controlling his qi while moving, though his record was still only five steps before disaster.

None of these exercises was especially exciting, but Ning could feel the difference. His qi had gradually become easier to manipulate, allowing him to guide smaller amounts with greater precision.

The best part? They were cheap.

For someone with his current financial situation, that alone made them worth practicing.

While Ning was concentrating on the leaves, a sudden tug on his fishing line caught his attention.

A bite!

He quickly set the leaves aside and grabbed the rod. With his current physique, reeling in the fish wasn't difficult.

Before long, a yellow carp emerged from the water, struggling against the hook.

Ning examined his catch and tried sensing the spiritual qi within it. Unfortunately, although the fish contained faint traces of spiritual energy, there wasn't nearly enough for it to qualify as a true spiritual fish.

At best, it could be called a half-step spiritual fish.

"Eh, better than nothing."

He quickly brushed aside his disappointment.

Thanks to the farming formation, many fish in the river had absorbed traces of spiritual energy. True spiritual fish, however, remained rare. In gacha terms, they were the super-rare pulls.

"You've already caught one, Ning?"

An old man approached with his own fishing equipment. Who else could it be but Old Zhou?

Fishing had become something of a daily ritual for the old man, and Ning had recently started joining him whenever he had the time.

The sect didn't provide meat as part of the peripheral disciples' regular rations. Outer disciples, on the other hand, were said to receive a pound of spiritual beast meat every week.

For someone who enjoyed good food as much as Ning did, the lack of protein was genuinely upsetting. Fortunately, he'd discovered that following Old Zhou's example and catching fish was a decent solution.

"Just an ordinary yellow carp," Ning replied, unable to hide a little disappointment.

Old Zhou settled onto his usual rock, resting his fishing rod against his knee. After glancing at the catch, he chuckled knowingly. "Not a spiritual fish, eh? You've been spoiled ever since that one time."

Ning smiled.

He remembered that fish quite clearly. Its scales had carried a faint glow, and the spiritual qi contained within its flesh was far denser than anything he'd caught since.

Unfortunately, it had been too valuable to justify eating.

He'd traded it to Old Zhou for a pouch of powdered pesticide, which could prove invaluable if pests ever threatened his crops.

It had seemed like the sensible choice at the time. Of course, the pouch was still sitting unused in his storage.

Old Zhou adjusted his fishing line before turning back toward Ning.

"You'll catch another one someday. Until then, stop looking so mournful. With your cooking skills, even an ordinary fish should make a fine dish."

"Old Zhou, you're flattering me," Ning replied with a chuckle.

"You really should've become a spiritual cook," Old Zhou continued with mock seriousness. "Those heirloom recipes of yours are quite unique. It would be a shame not to make something of them."

Ning shook his head in amusement.

Two months ago, he'd been the Congenital Holy Farming Body. Now the old man wanted him to become a spiritual cook.

At this rate, who knew what profession he'd be suited for next?

Still, the mention of cooking gave him an idea.

"I'll bring some dishes over later, then. That is, if you're willing to part with some of that spiritual wine of yours."

Old Zhou's eyes immediately gleamed.

"Ah, so you finally admit it!" He burst into laughter, his beard trembling. "You've been eyeing my wine all along! Always pretending you weren't interested, hm? Do you know how many times I've caught you sneaking glances at my gourd during dinner?"

Ning merely smiled, neither confirming nor denying the accusation.

He hadn't touched the spiritual wine during their first dinner together, mostly because he hadn't known how well his new body could handle alcohol.

Who could've guessed that Old Zhou was such a skilled brewer? His wine tasted excellent, and it even helped with cultivation. Since then, Ning had become quite interested in it.

A few peaceful minutes passed as the river flowed gently beside them and the morning sky grew brighter.

Just as Ning was preparing to leave, Old Zhou spoke again, his tone suddenly turning peculiar.

"By the way, Ning, you should take better care of yourself."

Ning blinked. "Uh... what?"

His body tensed slightly. Was something wrong? Had the old man noticed some hidden danger?

"I've seen your lights burning late into the night," Old Zhou continued, stroking his beard with a knowing grin. "Sometimes, I even hear movement coming from your courtyard. You're still young, so don't overstrain yourself."

Ning frowned, completely confused. "I was just practicing."

"Right, right. 'Practicing.'" Old Zhou nodded with exaggerated solemnity. "Just remember not to overdo those... training sessions."

Realization finally dawned, and Ning's eyes widened.

"Y-You dirty old man!"

Old Zhou erupted into laughter, nearly toppling off his rock as his booming cackle echoed along the riverside.

...

Upon returning to his farm, Ning calmly put down his fishing equipment, his earlier indignation already forgotten.

By now, he had a fairly good understanding of Old Zhou's personality.

The old man had spent decades cultivating without much hope of advancing further. Although he was getting older, the vitality granted by cultivation meant that his interest in worldly pleasures hadn't diminished. If anything, he'd become even more enthusiastic about them.

"Alas! Old Zhou really looked like a wise elder when I first met him."

Ning shook his head.

Have I somehow stumbled into the perverted old man cliché?

Putting that thought aside, he turned his attention back to cultivation.

For the past two months, Ning had maintained a strict routine, carefully planning his days to make the most of every available hour. Even his sleep schedule had been reduced to the minimum he could comfortably manage.

At his current realm, cultivating continuously was impossible. His meridians needed time to recover, and forcing himself to continue beyond his limits would only risk injury or qi deviation.

He had to balance cultivation with rest, farming, martial arts practice, and the other activities necessary to keep himself functioning.

It was tedious work, but Ning had discovered that he genuinely enjoyed watching his progress accumulate.

After all, who didn't like watching numbers go brr?

Speaking of numbers...

"Panel."

[Name: Ji Ning

Spiritual Root: Mid-grade Spiritual Root

Attributes: Five Elemental Attributes

Talent: Extreme Space-Time Sensitivity

Realm: First Stage Qi Condensation (42/100)

Cultivation Method: Pure Qi Sutra (First Floor: 21/100)

Martial Arts: Bone Forging Fist (Minor Accomplishment: 12/200), Shadow Steps (Minor Accomplishment: 3/200)

Spells: Small Cloud Rain Technique (Starting: 99/100)]

His progress over the past two months had been quite satisfying.

His cultivation had advanced steadily, while his martial arts had both reached Minor Accomplishment. The Small Cloud Rain Technique, however, had shown the most dramatic improvement.

Through constant practice, Ning had also begun to understand the practical differences between martial arts and spells.

Martial arts were especially useful during the early stages of cultivation.

They required no lengthy incantations or complicated hand seals. Once the movements became familiar, a cultivator could execute them quickly, channeling qi through the body or a weapon with relatively little preparation.

This made martial arts useful in close combat, where even a moment of hesitation could prove fatal.

Spells, meanwhile, offered a much wider range of possibilities, though beginners often struggled to cast them quickly.

Every attempt required carefully controlled qi circulation, precise hand seals, and sometimes an incantation to stabilize the technique. Against an experienced martial artist, a novice spellcaster might be struck down before finishing the first few movements.

Take the Small Cloud Rain Technique, for example.

When Ning had first learned it, every cast had felt clumsy. The cloud took ages to form, his concentration couldn't waver for even a moment, and after all that effort, the rain barely covered a patch of land three meters across.

Now, however, things were different.

The technique had reached the very brink of Minor Accomplishment.

Old Zhou had spent years cultivating the spell before finally reaching Great Accomplishment, while Ning had nearly reached Minor Accomplishment in only two months.

There were several reasons for his progress.

His spiritual root was certainly one advantage. Since he possessed all five attributes, he could cultivate water-based techniques without the additional difficulty faced by someone who lacked the water attribute entirely.

Old Zhou, for instance, possessed only the earth attribute. Although he had managed to master the Small Cloud Rain Technique through years of practice, it had taken him much longer.

Ning also had an advantage when it came to comprehension.

He wasn't some heaven-defying genius who could understand everything at a glance, but he was good at recognizing patterns and figuring out the principles behind what he was learning. Once he understood how something worked, finding ways to improve became much easier.

His control exercises had helped as well.

After weeks of practice, he could manipulate smaller amounts of spiritual qi with far greater precision. This made it easier to regulate the flow of qi and maintain a spell without wasting unnecessary energy.

Of course, there was one final reason.

Ning had shamelessly min-maxed.

A single glance at his panel was enough to prove it.

While Bone Forging Fist and Shadow Steps had improved slightly, nearly every spare moment of the past two months had been devoted to practicing the Small Cloud Rain Technique.

Thousands of repetitions had gradually pushed its proficiency toward the next threshold.

"So..." Ning rolled up his sleeves. "One more."

He refused to leave that final point unfinished.

Seeing a progress bar stuck at ninety-nine percent was a special kind of torture.

Ning formed the familiar hand seals and guided his qi through the pathways he had practiced countless times.

A small cloud condensed overhead, and spiritual rain began falling across the field.

Once the spell ended, he rested briefly before casting it again.

The hours passed as he repeated the process, carefully managing his qi and allowing himself time to recover whenever necessary.

Every section of his farmland received its share of spiritual rain, though his attention remained fixed on the technique itself.

Then, midway through another cast, something clicked. The familiar hand seals suddenly felt more natural, and the qi circulating through his meridians flowed with an ease he hadn't experienced before.

Even maintaining the cloud required less concentration.

The panel flickered.

[Small Cloud Rain Technique: Starting → Minor Accomplishment (1/200)]

Ning's lips curled into a satisfied smile.

He adjusted the flow of qi almost instinctively, increasing the amount of spiritual energy supplied to the cloud.

Above him, the cloud expanded rapidly, growing from a few meters across to more than thirty meters before stabilizing.

Spiritual rain began falling over a much larger section of the field.

Ning watched the rain for a moment, unable to suppress his grin.

Finally.

He could move on to the next step of his plan.
