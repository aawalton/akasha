Chapter 80

The Vault

He took a step back, raising an arm to ensure the girls’ safety, but nothing threatening emerged from that opening. The stone door opened slowly, its deep rumble echoing throughout the chamber, as though the stone had been waiting centuries for the chance to move.

The air that emerged from the other side was dry and still, carrying the smell of a place that had remained sealed for eons. The dust made them cough, and they waited a little before moving forward.

As they approached and stepped inside, Mai raised the light and turned around. She noticed more words engraved along the inside of the upper arch, written in several strange languages. One of them was Latin, once again similar to the inscription on the other side.

“Hic ossa tua relinques,” Mai read quietly.

“Here you will leave your bones,” Shin translated.

It was hardly an encouraging warning. But there were no carvings like those on the other side. The moment they crossed the threshold, the lamp revealed the truth.

They opened their eyes, trying to make sense of what they were seeing.

The entrance extended about ten meters into the chamber, but that was where it ended, just as the stone bridge over the abyss had before. Unlike that place, however, there was no stone floor beyond it. The end of the walkway was marked by a kind of pedestal standing in the center.

Bones lay scattered across the entrance floor. Bones of different sizes, skulls with all kinds of protrusions, hands with elongated fingers, femurs as long as a human leg, and even enormous skulls. Shin had already been forced to duck in several places throughout the pyramid, but now he wondered what kind of creatures had made it this far, and how large they must have been.

Skeletons lay piled on top of one another, some complete, others fragmented, as though they had fallen over each other in their final moments. Some rested against the walls. Others… seemed to have tried to move forward. There were even remains of creatures they could only associate with enormous vipers or humanoid serpents, along with the bones of animals.

“What is this?” Shin took another step into the chamber. The echo sounded strange, lingering longer than it should have.

Mai illuminated the interior more thoroughly.

There was too much to take in, too much that left them bewildered. The first thing was the sheer size.

The chamber was enormous. So enormous that they began to doubt its dimensions, or wonder whether their eyes were deceiving them. Where was the ceiling? The wall behind the entrance seemed to continue upward and curve inward, carrying on until they finally glimpsed the ceiling in the faint light and understood. That did nothing to make them feel better.

The chamber was spherical, and Shin estimated that its circumference was probably around two hundred meters. If that was even the right word for it, they were standing at the sphere’s equator.

They approached the edge of the walkway, stepping around bones as they shone their light downward. Sure enough, the curve continued below them. But there was something unusual. Barely visible at first, a series of pedestals could be seen along the wall at the lower pole. Unlike the ones where they stood, these followed the sphere’s circumference but had no platforms beneath them. They were simply embedded in the wall.

The walls lacked the intricate reliefs found elsewhere in the pyramid. There were only a series of channels and small ledges that followed the curvature as well, giving the impression of a spherical labyrinth.

That was when they began to wonder whether they were hallucinating.

Slowly, a dim light spread through the chamber, bright enough to let them see everything more clearly. It seemed to emanate from the very section of the spherical wall where they stood. A band of light, fading gradually in intensity, ran all the way around the sphere’s equator, while the poles remained dark, though they were now somewhat more visible. With the improved lighting, they could see that there really were several pedestals embedded in the walls around the middle and at both poles. They were more numerous in the lower half, their numbers decreasing toward the top.

At the center of the sphere floated a circular stone structure with no visible supports. It simply hung in the air.

A pedestal stood on that structure, and atop it rested a sphere of dark stone that seemed to be slowly turning red. Despite the darkness, the central structure gave off a reddish glow that helped them make out its overall shape.

Lizbeth stopped dead, her hand tightening around Mai’s arm.

“No…” she whispered.

Shin looked at her. “What’s wrong?”

Lizbeth pointed toward a distant spot on the ceiling. Shin narrowed his eyes, but couldn’t quite make out what she meant. His attention had been distracted by the floating staircases scattered throughout the chamber.

The chamber was filled with stone staircases of all shapes and sizes.

That was the second thing that caught their attention, and perhaps the most distracting feature of an already bizarre place.

Mai stood there with her mouth hanging open, staring at the spectacle. The size of the chamber, the reddish glow, and the structure at its center had already stunned them, but something else made them wonder whether they were hallucinating again because of those spores that could induce visions.

There were no visible supports or anything of the sort. Some staircases had only a few steps, while others had many more. Many connected to one another, while others were separated by gaps of several centimeters or even meters.

They moved slowly. Although their arrangement seemed random, many followed the curvature of the sphere. Perhaps stepping onto one staircase and crossing to another would have been relatively easy, if not for the fact that everything curved and changed direftion around them.

There was something unsettling about some of the floating shapes. It seemed that, even though they were stone structures, some had undergone some sort of distortion or deformation, almost as if the stone had been as malleable as clay and someone had decided to twist the shape before it had hardened.

The three exchanged glances, but Lizbeth seemed more interested in something else. She looked toward the ceiling, and Shin finally realized what had caught her attention. Far away, barely distinguishable, he spotted one of those circular covers she disliked so much. Could it be some kind of decoration?

“No… good…”

Mai swallowed. “Is this a trap?”

Shin did not answer immediately.

He studied the chamber, the skeletons, and their arrangement. There were plenty of them where they stood, but none could be seen on the central floating structure.

None had made it out.

Shin thought of the chambers elsewhere in the pyramid, where they had seen staircases carved into ceilings and other impossible angles.

Gravity follows the mind.

Then the other inscription.

Here you will leave your bones.

[You have already left them many times,] the voice in his head said.

Shin took another cautious step forward, approaching the pedestal.

Nothing happened. But when he raised his eyes, something changed.

A faint pressure at the back of his neck again.

As though something were trying to push his thoughts in a particular direction.

[Forward.]

What the hell is all this?

They were standing on little more than an entrance platform, but they knew they would have to cross the chamber to get anywhere. They needed to reach something, find a way out. Yet apart from those pedestals, there was no sign of another platform like the one they stood on.

At the center floated the circular platform.

It was not suspended by columns or chains.

It was simply there.

There were staircases leading toward the central platform, although none seemed to reach it completely. They all stopped short, as if something had interrupted their construction. They floated as though gravity itself had decided this was a good time to take a vacation.

Shin took a few steps into the chamber.

The echo of his footsteps spread throughout the sphere as he approached the pedestl. It was devoid of any decoration except for the square marble slab on top, on which something had been carved.

The engraving was clear: a pyramid composed of three triangles. Beneath it was a square slot. Beside the carving was an arrangement of twenty-two interconnected dots.

Shin looked at the pedestals embedded in the sphere. Three were near the upper pole, seven between the equator and the upper pole, and the remaining eleven below the equator. Shin thought about it. Which one was missing? He looked at the pedestal, then at those along the walls. As far as he could tell, they were all identical. Could the place where they stood count as well?

Was that possible?

He turned around and approached Lizbeth.

“Let me borrow that,” he said, taking the bronze head she was carrying. He searched inside, where only a pinecone remained, but also found what he was looking for.

The small pyramidal stone they had found after fighting the serpents at the first bridge, where they had met Lizbeth.

He examined it for a moment, then walked back into the chamber.

He hesitated for a second before placing it in the slot on the pedestal.

It fit perfectly. As though it had been made specifically for that spot.

The entire sphere trembled.

A deep rumble rolled through the walls, like the growl of a gigantic mechanism awakening after centuries of silence. Several sections of the sphere shifted and reconfigured as it shook. The surface now had deeper channels and was no longer as smooth as before. Some sections had receded while others had risen, making the chamber look even more like a non-Euclidean labyrinth.

They were so busy looking around that by the time they turned back, it was too late.

The door they had entered through slammed shut behind them. Lizbeth flinched. Mai swung the lamp toward the entrance, but there was no longer any door. Only stone. The mechanism had closed so abruptly that it startled both girls.

Mai walked towards the door, but stopped before she reached it and said nothing. She just sighed, and she and Lizbeth both looked at him. Shin didn’t know why, but he was certain that they, too, were feeling that tingling sensation at the nape of their necks.

Even so… there was something in both their eyes that struck him as strange.

A chill ran down Shin’s spine.

The first riddle had been solved.

And now there was no turning back.

He walked toward the entrance to make sure, but it was indeed sealed tight.

Mai was speaking to Lizbeth. All Shin could hear was a ringing in his ears.

[Move. You can only go forward. If you want to keep them safe, you have to keep moving.]

The trembling continued for a few more seconds. Then something strange happened.

The world seemed to tilt around them as a sudden lurch twisted their stomachs.

Mai took a step backward to keep her balance and dropped the bronze head. It rolled across the floor, completely empty. The water had run out hours ago.

“Did you feel that?”

Shin was already looking around.

The staircases that had previously floated motionlessly, including those descending toward the center, now seemed to point in different directions. Their sense of up and down had shifted.

As he studied the staircases, Shin noticed something he had missed before. Along their undersides, if that was even the right word for them, faint straight lines glimmered in geometric patterns. The lines were red, with white segments at intervals.

[Magic.]

Was that what kept everything floating?

Then he remembered the inscription they had read earlier.

Gravitas mentem sequitur.

Gravity follows the mind.

[Keep moving.]

At first, it was only a suspicion. Then he decided to test it. To make sure, he removed the pyramid from the pedestal.

Nothing happened. He had expected some kind of change, but there was none.

Shin began walking toward a nearby wall, recalling the inscriptions in the chambers they had passed through. The images of people walking along staircases at impossible angles.

“What are you going to do?” Mai asked.

Shin raised one leg toward the wall and e xtended it at a vertical angle.

He took a breath and pressed his lips together. It felt no different from taking an ordinary step.

The sensation in the pit of his stomach returned, and he instinctively spread his arms for balance as he crouched into his new spatial orientation. It was disconcerting, but he did his best to adjust to the view.

The wall had become the floor beneath his feet.

A nearby staircase drifted toward him and descended until its steps were within reach.

Mai held her breath as she watched him walk across a surface that, from her perspective, was completely vertical. Then she watched him step onto the staircase, where he should have fallen.

“Shin…” she whispered.

He did not answer immediately. He was concentrating.

Every step was steady, but his expression was less so. He felt that pressure again, similar to what he had experienced in the previous chamber, but subtler now. As though the sphere were watching his thoughts, waiting for him to hesitate.

“Don’t look into the void…” he finally murmured to himself. “Decide where the floor is… and don’t change your mind.”

Carefully, he made his way along the steps to the end. Another staircase continued the path, but it was separated from the first by about thirty centimeters and positioned at a different angle.

Shin studied it, keeping his concentration. He tried to lean sideways and look underneath what had become the floor from his perspective. The red and white lines on the opposite side glowed more intensely.

[Circuit.]

The pyramid had activated this place. To get out, they had to solve something, he thought as he straightened up and decided to retrace his steps carefully.

Mai and Lizbeth were waiting for him when he returned, both expecting an explanation. He led them to the pedestal and showed them the engraving.

“This place uses magic to keep everything we see here suspended, but this is a test. We have to travel around the entire sphere. There must be something we need to do at each pedestal. Maybe we have to use this pyramid.”

Mai looked at the pyramid Shin was holding.

“How is that possible? You and Liz found that when you defeated those serpents. What does it have to do with this place?”

“Maybe defeating the creatures in the pyramid is the key to activating something. Maybe even the exit.”

Mai and Lizbeth surveyed the sphere once more. They could see no platform resembling the one they were standing on.

“I don’t see any way out.”

“At this point, we have nothing to lose.”

Mai swallowed, and Lizbeth took her hand. She seemed more determined now.

“Let’s do it.”

The next two hours passed quickly as Shin helped Mai practice.

The first challenge was the now-sealed entrance. Mai had the most trouble accepting the idea that gravity followed the mind. She simply couldn’t wrap her head around it. She tried placing one leg against the wall, then the other, but it didn’t work at all. She couldn’t bring herself to believe she could walk on a vertical surface. Shin caught her whenever she showed signs of falling. She was frustrated, but he didn’t blame her. The idea seemed ridiculous to him as well. And yet, at the same time, something about it felt natural. As though changing his perspective wasn’t particularly difficult for him.

Lizbeth managed it too after barely twenty minutes of practice. In fact, it came almost naturally to her. Unlike Shin, who had proceeded cautiously, Lizbeth walked with far more confidence. Shin and Mai shuddered when they watched her move about ten meters above them. They called for her to come back, and she did, but before reaching the floor, she jumped from a height of three meters onto the platform and rolled across it.

She really was like a cat.

“How did you do that?” Mai asked. “Is there some trick to it?”

Lizbeth seemed to think for a moment.

“D-don’t think… too much.”

No. No. No, Shin thought. He found that difficult to imagine. He had even hesitated himself. But he considered it more carefully.

Lizbeth had lived there for a long time, surrounded by darkness and shadows, with only the occasional dim light from certain chambers. Perhaps spending so much time trapped there, sometimes swimming through the water, had altered her sense of spatial orientation. Maybe that helped her.

Shin then looked at the lamp in Mai’s hands. An idea occurred to him, but he would have to test it first.

He gave Lizbeth instructions to help him.

He walked along the wall again and moved several meters away. Then he opened the hand holding the pyramid.

It didn’t fall.

So the same priinciple applied to objects?

“Watch closely,” he told Lizbeth.

He tossed it a couple of times. The pyramid drifted only a few centimeters before falling back into his hands. On the third attempt, he threw it a little farther.

Suddenly, the pyramid’s angular momentum changed, and it fell downward.

Lizbeth moved quickly and caught it.

So there was a limit. Around forty centimeters.

“Now throw me the pinecone.”

Lizbeth tossed the last resin-coated pinecone toward Shin with pinpoint accuracy. He caught it and repeated the experiment. The pinecone stayed in his hand unless it moved roughly forty or fifty centimeters away from him. They repeated the test several times, and the result was always the same. They even tried one of the bronze heads. The outcome was identical. Objects followed the gravity of whoever was carrying them, provided they stayed within a certain distance.

Shin finally returned to the platform. Black particles covered his hand, and he ignited the pinecone. He walked along the walls and watched the direction of the flame.

Fire obeyed the same rule.

He tested it a couple more times before finally asking Mai to hand him the lamp. He was concerned about what would happen to its light, but it worked too.

He returned to the platform and explained his findings.

Mai always felt calmer when she had the light. They could have her carry the lamp, then. That way, she could focus on the light and her feet without paying too much attention to the chamber’s changing orientation. She simply had to think of the floor as being beneath her feet, and nothing else.

Mai genuinely wondered whether it would work.

It took three hours of trial and error, but it did.

During the first hour, she failed spectacularly, with Shin and Lizbeth catching her whenever she stumbled forward and nearly landed on her back. As the minutes passed, she gradually calmed down. At one point, she managed to take two steps almost without realizing it. The moment she became aware of what she was doing, she fell, but they caught her.

The next hour of attempts brought her a little farther. Eventually, she managed to move a couple of meters with hesitant steps. Then three meters, and finally four, before making it back.

At last, she tried with the lamp.

Her steps were slow, but she had done it. She only had to concentrate and imagine she was walking across a normal surface.

Then all three tried moving together. With each attempt, it began to feel more natural, and they gradually grew accustomed to it.

Even so, they lost track of time.

“I-I’m thirsty.”

Shin looked at Lizbeth. His own throat was dry, and Mai ran her tongue over her parched lips.

They had gone at least twenty-four hours without food and more than twelve without drinking. They regretted not having gathered some moss, if only to eat it, even if they weren’t insects.

Water was a different matter. The thirst, made worse by their physical exertion, forced them to resort to something Shin had suggested on the day he and Mai had first entered the pyramid. By that point, they had spent so many days naked together that Mai barely blushed when it happened. It was necessary if they wanted to survive and keep moving.

Shin suspected that drinking their own body fluids could make their situation worse, but they had no better option at that moment. The water they had carried had gradually dwindled as they walked, and now they couldn’t go back. That was a desperate measure, one they could only afford to try once. Still, none of them could explain why they felt they were close to discovering something important, as though the path itself had led them there.

Even so, it was embarrassing.

“Thank you…” Shin said, his cheeks flushing as he got up from the floor and wiped a few drops away with the back of his hand. It was better not to think about it. It really did feel warm.

Mai closed the lips, stepped away, and looked aside, her ears bright red.

“Don’t mention it…”

Lizbeth knelt and looked at Shin. Mai followed her example. Shin could only manage a nervous sigh.

Lizbeth moved closer, took hold of him, and placed the tip in her mouth. Mai jolted.

“You don’t have to do that,” Shin said, feeling her lips against him.

Lizbeth looked at him. Although her cheeks were flushed, her expression was serious. “W-we c-can’t waste it.”

She was right.

Mai nodded. “Y-you drink first. Then I will.” When it was Mai’s turn, she took hold of him and gave him a slightly annoyed look. “Don’t get hard. And why the hell is it so big?”

“It’s not my fault… It’s a reflex.”

Once they had quenched their thirst, it was time to see whether they could finally carry out their plan. They examined the surface of the sphere and studied the routes leading to the pedestals.

Shin decided to head for the nearest one and inspect it. After five minutes, during which he changed orientation thirteen times, he finally reached it.

The pedestal was identical to the one at the entrance, except for one detail. A small cube rested in the center of its upper base, with a hole in each of its faces. One corner had a small protrusion.

Shin picked it up and examined it. The entire surface seemed to repeat a pattern, and the engraving on the pedestal’s base also depicted a square.

If his theory was correct, the protrusion might indicate a direction pointing toward the center, meaning that particular corner had to be oriented inward.

They really did have to assemble a larger piece.

He considered the possibility of visiting all the pedestals himself.

It makes the most sense for me to do it alone.

He was already studying the other pedestals when he felt that sensation of being watched again. He didn’t know why, but he suspected this trial required all three of them to solve the puzzle. If he completed it alone, perhaps it would count for him and him alone.

The girls’ calls pulled him out of his thoughts, and he returned to them. Yes, it would be better if all three took part.

Shin suggested that he handle the higher pedestals. They were the most dangerous and the farthest away. The ones along the equator were tricky because the sphere had changed in several places, forcing them to shift their orientation frequently. The same was true of those below the equator, which were even more dangerous. It would be easy to trip over the bones scattered there, forming a petrified pit of calcium, sharp as needles.

With Lizbeth carrying the bronze woman’s head they set off. They left the man’s head behind. They would take only one in case they needed it, but carrying extra weight in their situation was unwise. Mai would carry the lamp, and Lizbeth would stay with her.

Mai swallowed and looked at the staircase where Lizbeth awaited her. And towards where Shin was – he was now standing on another one, but upside down from the perspective of the two of them.

Then Mai looked at the “floor” beneath her feet. They didn’t agree.

“This is crazy…”

Lizbeth got close and tightened her grip on Mai’s arm.

“No… fall…”

Shin stopped on the staircase and looked up. Technically, from Mai’s perspective, he must have appeared to be staring down at them upside down, like a bat hanging from the ceiling.

Mai took a deep breath and looked at Lizbeth.

Lizbeth was still holding on to her, but her breathing had grown steadier. As though there was nothing left to lose at this point.

She closed her eyes for a moment. Then they moved toward the wall, just as Shin had done.

For two long minutes, they struggled to make themselves do it. Finally, they took each other’s hands and exchanged encouraging looks.

“You can do this,” Shin said.

“Don’t distract me! You’re upside down right now!”

“All right…” Lizbeth murmured. “The floor… is there…”

They both sighed and raised one leg toward the wall. They took a step. Their bodies tilted.

For an instant, it seemed they were about to fall onto the floor, and Shin pressed his lips together. But they didn’t.

Gravity shifted, and the wall received them as though it had always been the floor.

Mai’s eyes flew open.

“…It worked.”

Lizbeth smiled.

“Let’s go,” Mai said, tightening her grip on Lizbeth’s hand.

Lizbeth looked at her. Then at the void. Then back at Mai.

And they took the step.

The transition was more abrupt this time.

Their bodies trembled, but they managed to steady themselves. Slowly, they made their way to the staircase and reached Shin.

Now all three stood on the same “surface.”

They looked up, which was now down. The entrance lay some distance away, inverted from their new perspective.

And the skeletons were still there. Some hung at impossible angles, as though they had tried to move forward without understanding the rule.

“They… didn’t try, did they?” Mai murmured.

Shin nodded. “Or they hesitated. Some may simply have stayed where they were until they died.”

He looked toward the central platform, still far away. The staircases didn’t quite connect.

“It’s not just about walking,” he said. “We have to choose carefully.”

Mai frowned. “Choose what?”

Shin didn’t answer immediately. He studied the staircases with their directions and angles.

The way everything seemed to change depending on the point of view.

“The path,” he finally said. “And believing it’s the right one.”

Lizbeth took his hand and squeezed it.

“D-Don’t… think… fall…”

Shin looked at her lemon eyes and nodded, a faint smile tugging at his facial muscles.

“Exactly.”

Before them, the floating platform seemed to be waiting.

Silent and unreachable… unless they could figure out how to get there.
