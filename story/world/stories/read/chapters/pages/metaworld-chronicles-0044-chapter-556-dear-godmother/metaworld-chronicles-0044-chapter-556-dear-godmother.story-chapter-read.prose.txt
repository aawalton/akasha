Like a hallucination from a midautumn fever dream, Shalkar’s horizon-spanning canopy smouldered amber from one grey edge of the Pocket Plane to the other, burning so vividly that its Regent momentarily forgot the woes of a goddess.

Her Harpies, colourful as rainbows, their feminine faces brimming with ardour, went up in a single sheet of noise. The chicks ascended, while the old hens from Phaelera’s brood drifted in lazy spirals, scattering into the higher branches and then down again in viridian spirals, filling the space with birdsong.

Richard sat with both hands on the trunk, trying to feel the tree’s movement, his grin growing stiff.

“Gwen,” he gingerly asked via a Message between them. “Is this one of the things you were afraid of?”

“Shh…” was the reply her cousin received instead. “And don’t let me fall.”

Her body might be beside her cousin, but her mind was already diving deep down, down and down into her Astral Body.

Within her proverbial third eye, the Sky Garden didn’t so much fade as stop being interesting in the physical sense. As a liminal pocket attached to the World Tree, she could see the multi-coloured strands of elemental mana that made it so hospitable for the Harpies. She stood as witness to the entire tree’s conduit systems, cascading like water, down through the Geofront, down into the galleries, the Dyar Morkk station, down to the Fifth Vel portal, and into Terra itself, where her eyes could not reach.

Every strand, woven like a fine braid, was living tissue. Every fibre, representative of what had germinated in place of nothing, tethered to the Axis Mundi, its mana, and the golden Faith of Shalkar’s inhabitants.

And there, at the heart of it, in the place where there was a tiny scale embedded…

A vaguely feminine shape made itself known to her.

Known, but not formed. It was what Ayxin had germinated in her womb through Jun’s hope, and the Yinglong’s sacrifice. It was Sufina if the Dryad had not begun its life on an island in the Indonesian archipelago, but as the fruit of a divine tree that spanned the Elemental Planes.

Sufina of Shalkar would not be a woman made of moss and patience, a guardian to the tomb of a man who had irrevocably changed the place humanity held in the world—she would be something more, but what?

Gwen didn’t know. She didn’t even know what Neo Sufina should call herself.

She reached out with her Essence, weaving the golden strands around her arm so that they would touch, like God to Adam on the Sistine Chapel.

The shape answered, reaching out with Essence of its own. Where Gwen’s was more Faith, less World Tree, and some of Almudj, the Dryad’s was mostly tree, some Faith, and a smidgen of the Rainbow Serpent’s Dreaming.

Almudj…

She could feel the Land God, so vividly, somewhere in the deep dark of the roots, on another continent and yet, close enough to touch.

Sufina laid herself on Gwen’s back, metaphysically speaking, and a shiver ran through incalculable tonnes of living timber and out along every root that had married itself into the Dyar Morkk.

A hundred kilometres away, in a transit tunnel under the Sawahi, she could feel a Dwarven signalman look up at the golden glow of the roots and swear an oath to the Sju Dorfran that he would take greater care when tapping mana from the living wood.

She withdrew. In time, Sufina might manifest into the Prime Material, as Ariel or Caliban, but not today.

Her mind withdrew.

“She’s not awake,” Gwen breathed out.

Tryfan

The Grove did not have weather, which was a stark difference to Shalkar, where the weather did indeed penetrate the planar distortion and bless the Geofront with both wet and dry seasons, as well as winter and summer.

That said, Tryfan did have a climate—which was perfect by any measure—and humidity, which was also perfect for Gwen—and its beauty was like the World Tree at Shalkar at an unimaginable scale.

She arrived via Sanari, as was tradition. The Druid’s aid was both an invitation and an acceptance of her presence.

This time, they did not arrive at the trunk, but directly at the top of the tree, at the Pocket Plane within the Pocket Plane that was the tree’s crown, The Bloom in White’s nested greenhouse.

The place hadn’t changed since she was here almost five years ago—which should come as no surprise. One could see the changes in Sanari—clothing, style, the floral scent of the changing seasons—but The Bloom was timeless in a sense that was too uncanny for comfort.

Gwen did not curtsy, as Sanari did, but bowed her head a little, from one Guardian to another.

“Regent.” The golden-eyed Elf bowed while barely moving her head; her gossamer dress was caressed by a wind that was not there. The Bloom in White looked as she did in their first meeting, delicate as a flower, supple as a willow, long-limbed, pale, and golden.

It was a radiance they now shared.

Ageless Elves.

Godmother.

A compound word. The second half was a job. The first half was a category.

“Gwen,” said Gunther. “You’ll be the Godmother, right?”

“With all my life.” Gwen got her face back under control. “Unreservedly. Yours will be a blessed, bonny babe, Gunther.”

A kid with a mana signature in the womb…

“She’ll hold you to it.” Gunther’s smile was warm, and his presence was radiant, and he loomed. “Now, go see to your girlfriend. I bet she’s got an offer for you too…”

Ahaha… Gwen felt her insides crinkle.

“Will you stay for dinner?” Gwen asked anyway.

Gunther shrugged. “The amount of work I have still… Waiting for you was a good break. I am serious.”

Then he gave her a pat on the back. “Don’t forget, if you need my help—we’ll come to your side, anytime, for any reason.”

They hugged once more, and Gwen felt more grounded, even as she added a stray thought to Gunther’s promise—that if she did have her way, their sibling bond wouldn’t just be for anytime—but for all of time…

Elvia found her at the rail an hour later, after Gwen gave her summons.

The sheer act of it felt so strange, and yet, going back to the VIP waiting area to pull Elvia out by the hand had felt equally awkward.

Her Evee looked different from how she remembered her; that much was certain. The puppy fat was gone, the rounded cheeks were no longer so pinchable. Elvia was a young woman now, mid-twenties, like herself, and though her skin was flawless, her vitality was exuberant; the Elvia she had met in Sydney a decade ago had long expired.

What stood in front of her now was a youthful-looking woman, ripe with adulthood, and possessed of sad eyes that were too mature.

Comparatively, her own reflection, captured in Elvia’s eyes, bore more resemblance to her late teen self than to what she had looked like in her mid-twenties, in another world, more so thanks to the uncontrollable glow.

“It’s a beautiful city,” Elvia said. “The tree makes it hard to be miserable. It’s almost like heaven, in a sense.”

“It’s Edenic,” Gwen said.

“That’s the word,” Elvia said carefully, like a girl stepping around broken glass. “You always were the best with words, Gwennie.”

Gwen laughed. In the past, she would have hugged Elvia already and pressed her cheeks against her cheeks.

“You’re not going to pitch me?” She said, her voice controlled and measured.

“I was instructed to pitch you.” Elvia stood closer. Her friend did not bow or kneel, and Gwen was grateful for the show of spine. “The Ordos would like me to instruct you in the sorcery of Faith. The Crown believes an untaught Demi-deity is a hazard. The Factions, every Faction, believe in you more than the Church, and I fear they are right.” Elvia turned her head. God, Evee looks so pretty still… pretty and pitiable.  “So I am asking. Will you let me teach you Faith Magic?”

“No.”

“No,” Elvia agreed, lowering her head.

“This is what I’ve chosen.” Gwen felt her chest tighten, though she was genuine in her thoughts. “What good is a veil that covers the Faith? It’s sophistry. Nothing will change what I have become. And there is nothing to hide and nothing to be ashamed of.”

“Yes.” Elvia was quiet for a moment. “Then I’ll spare you the rest of the excuses I had for the Regent. Gwennie. Can I say my own thoughts now?”

“You may.”

“I would like to leave the Church,” Elvia said, “and come to Shalkar, and run a chapel, devoted to you.””

The wind off the desert moved the amber above them. Somewhere far below, a shift-bell rang in the Dwarven Geofront. It was lunchtime, and a great mass of bodies began to move in the city below.

“Evee…”

“I have thought about it for a long time. Since before you went to Teithuacán.” Elvia’s voice was perfectly steady, which made Gwen’s heart sore. “Back in London, you said I had to wait. Well, I’ve waited, and I’ve gone to places, cured countless people. I’ve been to Che’ell-Cressen, where I gave my hand to the Mistress of the Suffocating Dark. I’ve murdered and healed, healed and murdered, until Ruxin negotiated my release and the Ordo paid its pound of flesh. I cost Mathias his dreams… I’ve done it all…”

Elvia looked at her now, and her eyes were melting pools of hope. “Is that enough?”

And there it was. Gwen’s jaws tightened.

Solana’s curse.

Elvia would live a long, long life. But Elvia would be thirty in a flash, then forty, then sixty, a hundred, a hundred and fifty. With the Yinglong’s blessing, and with Sen-sen, she could live a very, very long time.

But compared to Solana? To a common Hvítálfar?

She would need the Golden Mead, as Henry had gifted Sobel, forever, so that Elvia would be the High Priestess of her Goddess and live in Shalkar forever, in a city built out of a World Tree’s Essence, eating bread grown by Essence and time, healed by Golden blood, under a canopy that turned amber when Gwen felt the mood was right.

What kind of relationship was that?

Or, she could carpe diem. Love her Elvia at forty, and sixty, and eighty, and then stand at a graveside in three hundred years’ time with her face unchanged, and Evee would be but one body in the great procession of pale Princes and Princesses of the Regent’s reign—then the Pale Goddess of Shalkar would go back to her paperwork.

“Gwen,” Elvia said. “You are crying.”

Gwen dabbed her face with the heel of her hand, and it came away with gold in it. There was Faith-Essence even in her tears.

“So… no then?” Elvia said.

It becomes husbandry.

Gwen said nothing. If Solana had no answer, how could she, who had yet to live the years?

“I… don’t know,” Gwen confessed. “I can tell you I am scared shitless, though. If that helps.”

Elvia laughed, just a little. Then she leaned in, and she was silent for a long moment against her shoulder.

Then she said, very quietly:

“Gwennie, I am scared.”

“I know.”

“I don’t think you do.” Elvia pulled back enough to look at her, and her eyes were wet but steady. “Do you remember what I told you, back in London? That I was not afraid of God…”

“But you’re afraid of me…”

“And I remain afraid.” Elvia’s hands were still on her arms. “I am not afraid of a world that needs Gwen Song. I have seen that world; I have worked in it; it is a better world for what I can do to help.” Her fingers tightened. “I am afraid of a Gwen who no longer needs the world.”

The amber canopy moved above them. Far down in the Core, something warm stretched in its sleep, and every root in the Dyar Morkk stretched with it.

Gwen agreed with one thing.

She should not have so haphazardly gone to see Solana.

Richard came up the terrace at nineteen minutes past six, in casual business attire, looking relaxed, with a data slate and an expression she had seen before.

“Regent.” He did not look at Elvia. “We need your eyes on this.”

“What is it?” Gwen asked, still lingering in the limbo of Solana’s liminality.

“The encirclement of Amazonia is complete.” He turned the slate around. The map on it was not a map of the surface; it was six radial arteries and a hundred and forty nodes and a low-way road that ran, uninterrupted, from Deepholm to the Andes, Bunker to Bunker, Geofront to Geofront. “Strun’s last Himsegg Vjeit tied off at the eastern margin nine hours ago. Deepholm confirms the foundry is at full draw—they’ve cold-staged fabricator engines at every node on the Arica web, and Hanmoul’s cut the first eight thousand rations into the Mead stock. Shield Golems are seeded across every node.” A fractional pause. “And there’s something else as well. Cuzco reports that there are disturbances in Amazonia. I am not sure if it’s related to us—but there are reports of a growing beast tide. It will overwhelm the southern Suyu. Prince Inti has requested aid.”

Elvia moved from her shoulder, but the weight on them had only increased.

She owed the Dwarves this. They had built her an empire and put her likeness next to the Bringer of the Lumen, and now, this was what she owed Hilda’s people.

And Inti was a good and trustworthy friend. She had given them trade, shelter and a way to connect to the rest of the Human world—as well as bringing woes to an otherwise isolated theocracy.

Phyr Quar-Tath, first Daughter of Quar-Tath, would be the muppet responsible. She was the one sheltering the Sinneslukare Dwarves.

Last time, she had visited the realm of Midnight as a tourist. This time…

Gwen stood and paced, recollecting her train of thought.

Richard waited. He was very patient with her, as always.

It becomes husbandry.

Elvia sat where she had left her, white against the amber, hands folded, waiting to be told to go.

Looking at the friend she had wanted to spend eternity beside, she suddenly crossed the terrace, took Elvia’s surprised face—and kissed her on the forehead.

She felt the warmth going out of her and into the other woman, unbidden, unasked-for, and she glowed golden for a moment, cut off from all the trouble of the Prime Material.

Elvia reached out, but the Regent was gone. Gwen had gone to her cousin, her Majordomo, and she was ready for the next chapter of the Pale Goddess’ wrath.

“Assemble the Rat-kin legions from Central America,” she said. “Make ready the Tower for the southern Suyu.”

“Regent.” Richard snapped to attention.

The light that radiated from her halo was no longer so warm. It was sharp and stabbing and stung the eyes. “Get the Tower in range. We tame the tide—then, whatever the outcome, we shall revisit Che’ell-Cressen.”
