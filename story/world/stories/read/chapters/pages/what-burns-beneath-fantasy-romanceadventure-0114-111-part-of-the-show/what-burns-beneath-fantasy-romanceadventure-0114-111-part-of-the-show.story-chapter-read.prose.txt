I sank into my chair as malicious glee spread across Embriel’s face. He slouched forwards, fingers drumming on iron, watching Velidan with something eerily like triumph.

“Allow me to introduce my unhappy mortal,” he chuckled before tilting towards me, gaze glittering with the jade glow of water and glass. “Poor Brin has endured much since meeting me. Her hands were cut to ribbons, she nearly bled to death in bed, she was badly beaten-”

“We will have words again.” Alora’s voice was ice. “We will have blood, too.”

“-and just the other night,” he continued as if she hadn’t spoken, “a hag took her prisoner. The creature held her captive, preyed upon her loved ones, and… how did you phrase it, Brin? Nearly strangled you to death?”

I sat frozen, eyes flitting from face to face. Iron dug into my spine as the weight of far too much attention pressed in.

“Answer.” His fingers threaded through my hair and brushed the nape of my neck, making my pulse jump.

“Y-yes,” I rasped, fighting not to flinch away. One hand lifted absently, clasping at my throat. “Yes, she nearly killed me. My lord.”

“Much of that happened in the mortal realm, where whatever distant guardian shares its magic should have been able to intervene. And she was bled power every time, but it was never enough to save her.” Velidan fixed me with a mocking sneer. “Tell us, mortal; when the hag held you at death’s door, did some shining force of good fall upon her?”

I pictured the black ripping of the air, the gleam of starlight through smoke and the rush of fury as he’d torn Baltha away. I met his eyes, perhaps more brazenly than I was supposed to, and answered firmly, “No, my lord.”

“No. I fell upon her.” He turned to our audience. “Yet she’s a runekeeper. Something that should be defended, and clearly is not.”

Thoughtful murmuring slithered through the air. Far too many gazes stayed on me, and the malice in some made my stomach churn.

Embriel’s wolfish expression only sharpened. "There you have it, friends. Our only true enemies are silent."

I eyed Velidan. That was a show of some kind, wasn’t it? Velidan wanted to speak up in front of everyone and… offer support? Prove how awful he is? But he’s been quiet this whole time, so why act like he’s choosing a side now?

And there are sides, aren’t there? Literal ones. I hadn’t realized it at first, but there was a tangible divide in the Fae. Alora’s small end of the table had looked consistently upset throughout this discussion. Those in the middle seemed curious, and the ones near us had listened to Embriel with either enthusiasm or chilling calm. Because some of them already knew. They’re not surprised by any of this. He’s made sure some of the others are on his side.

The Fae began talking amongst themselves, some of them still staring at me, while I considered the numbers at the table. Embriel hasn’t said anything about going to Sedgewick Vale tonight, and clearly a few of the others would be outraged. So he’s only told some of them. Saints, if I just stand up and say he’s planning something, would that be enough? Maybe I’d get my head ripped off, but could Alora and a few of the others stop him?

My heart sank as I followed the trail of reasoning. Her side of the table was small. None seemed as outraged as her. They didn’t have a way to kill him, so even if they stopped whatever was happening tonight, wouldn’t that only delay his plans? And then he’d be more careful; the next attack could come with no warning.

“So this explains your silence.” Alora’s hiss reclaimed my attention. She was leaning forwards, face bone-white and eyes simmering. The expression on her beautiful visage was one of such restrained wrath that I nearly whimpered, though it was directed to my side. “A moon ago you would have been the first to denounce this folly. And now… what? You harmed an innocent and received no consequence, so now you’ve a taste for blood?”

“More a taste for reason, as Embriel called it.” Velidan sipped his drink, then examined his host over the rim. “I have questions about the practical side, though. You spoke of portals at the last gathering. A permanent one, specifically.”

My brows twitched together. He pressed about that last night, too. That’s what he’s trying to get more information on.

Embriel’s tone turned sly. “Excellent question, Vel. We can hardly unite two realms when simply getting to theirs takes a day of chanting and a river of blood.”

Velidan leaned forward. “Explain your solution, then.”

“I’ve been toying with this idea for years, friends, and a few moons ago, I finally solved the final piece; Garruk and I figured out how to tear a doorway open and hold it open. Not for a handful of breaths; forever. For anything to walk through.”

Mutterings of ‘impossible’ and ‘madness’ came again.

“How?” The figure beside me rumbled above the din.

Embriel tented his fingers. “By getting creative. It’s doable, and can be done again. Think of it; a permanent, stationary door. A link between the realms. A way to transport goods, animals, plants, a safe passage for Fae who want to return here during the day or humans who’d like to explore our realm.”

“Who’d be ripped apart as soon as they entered.” Sybion rubbed his temples. “Alora’s right. Maybe it could work eventually, but too many things here would go mad with so much fresh meat. It’d be a slaughter.”

“Genocide,” she snarled again.

Any answers were cut off by the loud, clanging drag of metal against stone. It echoed from down the hallway, followed by a raspy voice that sharpened to a high-pitched squeal of pain. Then a bone-rattling crack and chilling silence.

Several Fae at the table rose. Alora swore and moved towards the archway as a shape appeared within. Ripples of blue illuminated a tall, broad figure with unkempt dark hair framing bloodshot eyes and bared teeth. He was dangling one of the gray-skinned little Fae by its ankle. The creature was limp.

“Kallian,” Embriel’s voice oozed through the quiet like sticky, sweet syrup. “Ah… good help is hard to find, you know.”

“Was,” the Fae snarled, then dropped his victim. It thumped unmoving to the glass as he stalked past.

Alora, lightning-quick, backed to a position behind Bernard’s chair. She watched the newcomer as if he was a rabid dog.

“What are we meeting so secretly about?” Dark eyes flicked along, regarding the scene with palpable malice. His thin upper lip curled as the expression settled on me. “And who left a stain in my seat?”
