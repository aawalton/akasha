Chapter 38

”Acuity twenty-five. The threshold. Everything before this was foundation. Everything after is the building.”- The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr, Marseille

Maryam met them at the compound gate with a canvas bag and an expression that suggested she’d been expecting worse.

“Sit,” she said.

Arthur sat, and Maryam took his left forearm in both hands and turned it. Pain spiked from wrist to shoulder. He locked his jaw.

“Fracture,” she said. “Ulna. Already stabilising, which should be impossible without surgery.” She looked at him over her glasses. “How?”

“I think it’s the Constitution. The attribute. The higher it gets, the faster things heal.”

“Putain.” She said it under her breath. “You’re telling me you have a stat that fixes bones.”

“That’s exactly what I’m telling you.”

Maryam absorbed this, splinting his arm with the calm economy of field medicine, her hands quick and sure and something else. Warm.

The warmth wasn’t body heat but a radiance Arthur could feel through the bruised skin, sinking into the bone like sunlight. The pain didn’t vanish, but it did recede a tiny bit, pushed back by something.

Maryam paused and looked at her hands, flexing her fingers as though they belonged to someone else.

“That’s been happening,” she said. “Since Tuesday.”

“Your hands?” Steve asked, sitting with his bandaged palms up.

“They get warm when I treat people. The bad injuries, mostly.” She moved to Donna. “Ribs. Show me.”

Donna lifted her shirt, and Maryam’s fingers walked the intercostals. Donna drew one sharp inhale, then grit her teeth.

“Cracked. Two, possibly three.” Maryam bound them tight, her hands glowing faintly against Donna’s side.

“You’re channelling Ether,” Steve said. “The system is giving you a healing technique. You’re doing it without knowing.”

“I’m a GP from Montpellier,” Maryam said, not looking up. “I don’t channel things.”

“With respect, your hands are glowing.”

“With respect, your hands are burned. Palms up and don’t move.”

Steve submitted, and Maryam cleaned the blisters, applied a salve that stung, and wrapped both his hands.

“Eight hours. No gripping. No fists.”

“What about eating?”

“Someone will feed you.”

“I’m thirty-three years old.”

“Then you should know better than to set yourself on fire.”

Donna made a sound that might have been a laugh if her ribs hadn’t vetoed it.

Maryam sat back against the low wall, and the clinical mask slipped. Grey circles under her eyes. A tremor in her hands that was the cost of a week without enough sleep.

“I lost four people on Day Zero,” she said with a faraway look. “In this compound. Standing and talking and then not. The light came and they fell. Cédric’s daughter. Nadia’s deputy. Two dockworkers I hadn’t learned the names of yet.”

The quay was quiet around them, as if mourning her loss with her.

“One in four. We got word from some other areas. The same ratio everywhere. I was not trained for this. Not for death without explanation.” She looked at her hands. “And now the ones who survived are healing faster than they should, and there are crystals forming inside the animals we butcher, and my hands do something I can’t explain. I keep going. Because the queue doesn’t get shorter when you stop.”

Donna held her gaze a long second, a professional acknowledging the impossible odds of another.

“Sleep,” Maryam said. “All of you. You might have magic numbers but your body is still muscle and bones.” And then she added quietly, “At least for now.”

They slept longer than they had meant to.

Morning came grey and smelling of salt. Arthur woke to the ache of a body rebuilt twice in twenty-four hours.

Nadia arrived with coffee, real coffee, drawn from the stockpile Cédric guarded like ammunition.

“You look better than yesterday,” she said. “Can’t say the same for your equipment.”

Arthur looked down at the Bullguard. The dent from the alpha’s tusk was structural, a deformation that changed how force would distribute. A compromised shield lied to you about what it could take.

“You just gave me an idea,” he said, “for how to say thanks.”

He set the alpha’s core on the crate. Egg-sized, dark, dense. “Have your people seen anything like this?”

Cédric took one look, spoke rapid French, and two minutes later Marcel appeared, weathered and salt-stained and unhurried. He reached into his pocket and produced a crystal the size of a hazelnut, dull and cloudy but unmistakably a core.

“From a boar,” Cédric said. “Yesterday’s butchering.”

Steve was on his feet. “It’s starting. Ether density is climbing, more saturation in the creatures, more cores forming. Your standard kills will start dropping these.” He looked at Nadia. “Keep every single one.”

Arthur picked up the alpha core. “Give us an hour and then I’ll show you what you can do with them.”

The room caught morning light through its grimy windows. Three crates, three people, six points each.

Steve went first, because he had already decided. Four into Acuity. Two into Resonance.

“Resonance?” Arthur asked.

Steve held up his gauze-wrapped hands. “I think these burned because I pushed more Ether than my body could channel cleanly. Acuity is throughput, how much I can push. If I’m right, Resonance is efficiency, how cleanly it arrives. Without both, I’m a hose with no nozzle.”

The first Acuity point took him from twenty-three to twenty-four. Painful but familiar, the neural pathways widening, the Ether flow increasing. Twenty-five broke the pattern.

Steve gasped and doubled forward on the crate, his body spasming. The Ether flowing through his Nadi Matrix didn’t just increase; it also changed direction. Pathways that had been inward-facing, channelling Ether through his own body, opened outward. He could feel the air in front of his hands, not with his skin but with something else that read temperature and density and molecular motion the way his eyes read light.

The world was full of energy. It always had been; he just hadn’t been able to reach it.

“Steve?” Arthur was starting to panic.

Finally he managed to say, “I’m fine,” and sat back up.

He wasn’t fine. His body was rewriting itself from the inside, his hands were shaking, and the grin on his face terrified him. “Something just opened. The Ether isn’t just inside me anymore. I can feel it out there.”

The third and fourth Acuity points deepened it. Twenty-six, twenty-seven. Each one hurt less but carried more weight, the new pathways filling and the external perception sharpening. By twenty-seven he could feel the Ether density gradient between the warehouse wall and the open window. He could feel Donna’s Nadi Matrix three metres away, and Arthur’s like a banked furnace.

The two Resonance points arrived last and quieter. If Acuity was the fire hose, Resonance was the nozzle. The flow tightened.

Steve looked at his hands. Warm, but not burning anymore. “The Reacher is backup from now on. For when nothing else works.” A grin broke across his grey face. “I’m throwing fireballs now.”

Donna snorted from the next crate. “You set yourself on fire and collapsed. That’s not a fireball. That’s a cry for help.”

“It was a prototype.”

Arthur went next and took it slowly. Three into Strength, three into Constitution, one point at a time, testing how each shift affected his balance.

The strength points came in the familiar way, muscle fibres reweaving and denser. Constitution was deeper, his fractured ulna warming as the knitting intensified. He flexed the splinted arm. It was still broken but the gap was closing.

There was no drama in it, no phase transition, just the steady work of a man building a wall one brick at a time.

Donna went last, jaw set. Two into Strength, the axe arm gaining density. “Creatures are getting stronger. I need more behind the blade.”

Two into Constitution for insurance. Her hands trembled when the second point took hold, and she gripped the crate’s edge to kill the vibration.

“Two into Dexterity.”

Twenty-four held to the familiar pattern, the speed she’d been building since Level 2, narrowing the gap between thought and movement by one more fraction. Twenty-five broke her.

Donna’s body locked against the crate. Every muscle went rigid simultaneously, nothing like the controlled lock of Arthur’s Structural Reinforcement but a seizure. Her eyes snapped wide. She pitched sideways off the crate and hit the floor, limbs rigid, jaw clenched so hard Arthur heard her teeth.

Arthur looked at Steve.

“Don’t touch her. She is going through the same thing I did. It has to finish. Though Dexterity threshold seems a lot more painful than Acuity.”

Arthur knelt anyway, his hands hovering. The blue lattice flickered beneath Donna’s skin, brighter than he’d ever seen it, tracing lines from her spine outward along every limb. Her Nadi Matrix was rewiring itself, every motor neuron pathway rewritten simultaneously.

After another minute or so Donna’s body unlocked, and she drew a breath that sounded like surfacing from deep water. Her eyes refocused on the ceiling, then on Archie.

“What the hell, Archie.”

“The restructuring event-“

“You warn people before their body shuts down! What if I’d done that in a dungeon? What if I was mid-fight?”

“The restructuring requires conscious allocation. It cannot occur involuntarily during combat.”

“That’s not the bloody point-“

“The point you are making is that I should have predicted the response. I could not. This is the first time I have observed a member of your species reach this value in Dexterity. I did not have a model for your biology’s reaction.”

Steve’s head came up slowly.

“A member of our species,” he said.

Archie’s geometry went still, the stillness of a wall.

“Archie,” Steve said. “You had a model for other species. Just not ours.”

Silence.

“You just confirmed that aliens are real.”

More silence, the kind that filled rooms.

Donna was still on the floor. She flexed her hands, and the micro-adjustments were gone. The tiny corrections every human body makes between intent and execution. The tremors and recalibrations the eye doesn’t notice until they aren’t there.

“Well Steve is right. It sure is different,” she said. “The lag is gone. Between wanting to move and moving. Just gone.”

Donna moved her hands through the air and watching them obey with a precision that didn’t have a human word yet. Nothing wasted between rest and readiness.

Steve watched Donna move, then looked down at his own hands and back to Arthur.

“Mine was external projection, hers was neural rewiring, but the same kind of event. A phase transition. At twenty-five.”

“Every stat,” Donna said. She’d arrived there faster than Steve. “Every stat has a gate.”

They both looked at Archie.

“The principle applies across all five attributes,” Archie said. Nothing more.

“And you didn’t think to mention this before we started putting points in,” Donna said.

“You did not ask.”

“I didn’t know there was something to ask about.”

“That is a recurring limitation of your species’ information-gathering methodology.”

Donna stared up at him. “Are you saying humans are bad at asking questions?”

“I observed that your species frequently fails to inquire about systems it has not yet conceptualised. This is not a criticism. It is a structural constraint of linear cognition.”

“That’s a criticism,” Steve said.

“It is a diagnosis.”

Steve was pacing now, his gauze-wrapped hands gesturing despite Maryam’s instructions. “Archie. We got our first technique at Level 5. When’s the next one?”

“The existing technique’s application range expands at class formalisation. Additional applications may emerge from subsequent behavioural data.”

“May,” Donna said. “Not will.”

“Is there a skill tree?” Steve asked. “Branching paths? Something we can plan toward?”

“There is no selection interface. The Nadi Matrix evaluates behavioural data and formalises the patterns it observes. Your behaviour is the selection process.”

“So no skill tree,” Steve said. “The system watches what we do and decides what we become. It’s not a menu. It’s a learning algorithm.” He looked at Donna. “We’re the training data.”

Arthur stood up from his crate and asked with a smirk, “So when does an adventurer get a class?”

Steve laughed, and it was an actual laugh, the first real one any of them had managed since the boar fight. “Oh, that’s perfect. Archie, when does the system assign a class?”

“At sufficient data accumulation. Approximately ten developmental thresholds.”

“Level 10,” Steve translated. “We get a class at Level 10. One more level.”

“The term class is a cognitive translation. The Matrix identifies your dominant operational pattern: how you fight, how you allocate, how you respond under threat. It formalises that pattern. Your existing technique evolves. You do not receive a title. You receive a recognition of what you already are or are trying to be.”

“But if we change what we do,” Steve said slowly, “if we deliberately train something new, the system updates. We can influence the diagnosis.”

“Behavioural data is behavioural data. The Matrix does not distinguish between combat and practice.”

Donna flexed her hands again, with the eerie precision of a mechanism freshly recalibrated. “Shield bashes,” she said to Arthur.

“What?”

“You went offensive with the shield in the last fight. If the system is watching, you’ve told it you’re not purely defensive. Keep doing that.”

Arthur filed the thought away. Good tactical thinking, Donna’s natural habitat.

Steve was writing things down, his fingers moving gingerly. The threshold principle. The alien confirmation. The cognitive domain theory, sketched in notation that was half archaeological shorthand and half chicken scratch.

Somewhere above her, a floating geometric shape that had just accidentally confirmed the existence of extraterrestrial intelligence maintained a silence that could have filled a galaxy.
