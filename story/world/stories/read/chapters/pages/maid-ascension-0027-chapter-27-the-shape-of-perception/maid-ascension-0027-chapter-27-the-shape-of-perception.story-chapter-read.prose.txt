Now that the first spell was complete, Lira considered what to create next. Since the spell she wanted to acquire, the Book Absorption one, focused on knowledge acquisition, she figured she should create something that directly supported that goal. The general idea was obvious in hindsight, but defining precisely what it meant was another matter altogether.

“Pika. Selan. Tureth,” she cast.

With the clarity of mind that came with meditation, her thoughts settled into a more structured flow. Instead of chasing ideas, she examined them one at a time, turning them over and refining them.

The most basic form of acquiring knowledge was not reading or memorizing, but observation. Before something could be understood, it needed to be perceived first.

A spell that enhanced perception would not only help her learn faster, but it would also improve every other aspect of her magic. Better awareness meant better understanding, and better understanding meant even stronger spells.

She let the meditation state fade slowly before reaching for her father’s ledger.

She flipped through the pages with a clearer sense of purpose, no longer searching blindly but looking for words that fit the defined concept.

Pika remained the core of the spell, as the mind was the base for it.

The next word took longer. She needed something that captured awareness, not just sight, but the act of noticing. Her finger paused on Velar, meaning clarity. The word carried a sense of sharpening, of bringing things into focus rather than simply seeing them.

The last piece was harder still. Observation alone was not enough. Information without understanding was useless.

She was about to settle for Cognar when she found Syneth, meaning interpretation.

That should work well as a bridge between perception and knowledge.

Pika. Velar. Syneth.

The structure felt correct. Clean, direct, and purposeful.

She pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward her and began constructing the primer. This time, the process was more streamlined, having already done it multiple times.

She began with one of the governess books, the one focused on raising ladies. One section emphasized observing social cues, noticing the subtle details people revealed without realizing it. Posture, tone, hesitation, and the way someone avoided eye contact. It described how meaning often lay beneath what was spoken, hidden in patterns and inconsistencies.

She copied parts of it, then expanded it with her own words, focusing on the act of paying attention rather than the conclusions drawn from it. She had dealt with scammers and thieves; she was not new to the art of observing body language.

Next, she moved to a scout’s manual. It approached observation from a different angle, one grounded in survival. Tracking, reading disturbances in the environment, and identifying what did not belong. A broken branch, a shifted stone, or a faint imprint on the ground. It emphasized that the world always left signs to those who knew how to read them.

Again, she wrote, blending the ideas with her own understanding.

Finally, she turned inward, drawing from her own experience. Calligraphy had taught her observation differently. When copying another’s work, she could not rely on general shapes. She had to notice the smallest details, the angle of a stroke, the pressure applied, and the slight variations that made one handwriting distinct from another. Forgery was not about imitation at a glance; it was about finding repeating patterns.

Observation, in that sense, was the foundation of replication.

Between each section, she wrote the incantation carefully.

Pika. Velar. Syneth.

She spaced it evenly, allowing it to settle into the text. By the time she finished, the page felt complete in the same way her previous primer had.

Without delay, she prepared to test it.

She sat cross-legged once more, placing the paper in front of her. Instead of forcing herself into a trance, she reached for the cantrip she had just created.

“Pika. Selan. Tureth.”

The transition came quickly this time. The stillness settled over her with far less resistance, her thoughts aligning without effort.

Once the state stabilized, she brought her focus to the new concept. She let the meaning of the primer rise in her mind.

A few minutes of quiet reflection passed, her awareness narrowing and sharpening.

Then it clicked.

“Pika. Velar. Syneth.”

The change was immediate.

The world did not shift in a dramatic way, yet everything felt more defined. Details that had always been present rose to the forefront of her awareness, layering over one another faster than she could consciously process. Information flooded in; she would have been overwhelmed if not for the lingering effects of meditation allowing her to filter and sort through it all, quieting the noise and focusing on what mattered.

Her gaze drifted across the alcove.

This had been a hiding place, not just a reading corner. The positioning of the chairs suggested intimacy. Two seats, placed close enough for shared reading, angled away from the main space. This was where the old dowager and the former head librarian had come when they wanted to be alone, where they could speak without interruption.

Her eyes shifted to the shelves.

The books here were different. Many bore markings of sponsorship, names, and dedications written in careful script. This section had been curated intentionally. The old woman had wanted to give something back, as a thank you to the living authors who enriched her library.

Her attention moved again.

Outside the alcove, the state of the library told a longer story. The decline had not been sudden. There were signs of multiple attempts at organization, each system layered over the last, none fully replacing what came before. Different hands, different methods, none maintained long enough to stabilize. Each effort had added complexity instead of order.

The information kept coming, connections forming faster than she could follow.

She felt the strain begin to build.

Without the meditation spell, this would have been overwhelming from the start. Even now, she could feel the pressure rising, the weight of too many observations competing for attention.

That was enough.

She released both spells before they could push her further.

The world dulled slightly, the sharpness fading as her mind returned to its normal state.

She exhaled slowly, grounding herself.

“Wow, that was something,” she muttered under her breath. “Better use it in small doses.”

Even so, a faint smile formed on her lips.

Used together, the two spells complemented each other perfectly. One created the state of mind, the other filled it. Control and input, balance and clarity.

Two cantrips completed. One more to go.
