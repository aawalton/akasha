I was separated from the group.

Strangely, that alone didn’t scare me—not at first. I was used to Alex being pulled away from us by strange forces, strange rules, stranger people. He had a way of being singled out by the universe like it had a personal grudge or an unhealthy fascination. If anyone was going to be isolated, it was always going to be him.

But this time, it wasn’t just him.

I knew, instinctively, that Cordelia wouldn’t have willingly stepped away. Ten wouldn’t either—she was reckless, impulsive, loud, but never when it came to protecting what was hers. And V? V especially wouldn’t leave. He wore indifference like armor and sarcasm like a shield, but underneath all that salt and swagger was a heart that bled far too easily. He liked to pretend he was just a trapper. That all he was good for was mines, wires, and retreat paths.

That was a lie.

I’d seen him in a real fight. Seen him plant his feet and swing like the world had personally offended him. Sure, most of those instances had been fueled by alcohol and questionable decisions, but even then—especially then—his instincts were sharp. His fists were faster than his mouth. And when it came down to it, V always stood between danger and the people he cared about, whether he admitted that to himself or not.

So I wasn’t worried about Cordelia. I wasn’t worried about Ten. And I wasn’t even especially worried about V.

I was worried about Fallias.

Fallias was brave—too brave sometimes—but she was also honest in a way that could get her hurt. She didn’t hide her fear well. She didn’t mask her emotions behind snark or logic or cruelty. What she felt showed on her face, in her posture, in the quiet tension of her hands. And right now, being cut off from Alex—being separated in an unknown realm ruled by an absent Dominus and an over-polished herald—was exactly the kind of situation that could unravel her.

She loved him openly. Fiercely. Without irony.

That made her strong.

That also made her vulnerable.

The forest around me pulsed softly with that eerie, living light—every leaf glowing, every root faintly radiant, like the whole realm was breathing through color instead of air. It should have been beautiful.

Instead, it felt like standing inside the throat of something very large and very patient.

I drew my arms around myself, suddenly acutely aware of how quiet it was. No birds. No insects. No rustling animals in the undergrowth. Just the low hum of the Nexus beneath my feet and the soft glow of a world that watched without blinking.

“Alex…” I whispered under my breath, not even sure if he could hear me.

I was thinking about the people I loved.

The forest went silent in the worst possible way.

Not the gentle, respectful quiet of a sleeping grove—but the hollow kind. The kind that meant something had just noticed me.

My breath fogged faintly in the bioluminescent haze as I slowed my steps, every instinct screaming. The glow of the leaves above dimmed a fraction, as if the forest itself were holding its breath. I slid one dagger into my palm without looking—the familiar weight grounding me.

“Okay,” I murmured softly. “That’s never a good sign.”

Then the ground behind me cracked.

Not snapped.

Not rustled.

Cracked—like timber splitting under sudden pressure.

I twisted just as a shape tore free of the forest floor.

A wooden figure hauled itself up from the roots—humanoid, rough-hewn, its joints bound with living vines that pulsed faintly with green light. Its face was a mask carved into something almost familiar.

Too familiar.

It looked like V.

Not him—not really—but a grotesque puppet mockery built from bark, knotted roots, and glowing sap eyes.

“Not funny,” I hissed.

The simulacrum lunged.

I vanished.

My illusion snapped into place as my real body twisted sideways, its wooden claws tearing straight through a false image of me that burst into prismatic smoke. I rolled through glowing ferns and came up behind it, both daggers flashing.

Steel met wood—

—and rang like metal.

I blinked.

“What—?”

The bark had crystallized mid-strike.

Bismuth.

My own power answered my shock without conscious thought—veins of iridescent mineral spiderwebbed across the creature’s arm where my blade struck, freezing the motion in a violent rainbow shimmer. I pivoted, slammed my second dagger into the crystallized joint, and shattered the limb in a burst of spectral fragments.

The simulacrum didn’t scream.

It kept coming with one arm.

“Fantastic. It doesn’t feel pain either.”

I backflipped, scattering three illusionary doubles across the clearing. The wooden V snapped its head between targets in jerking, unnatural movements—then chose wrong.

It lunged for an illusion.

I flicked my wrist.

A ribbon of molten bismuth snapped from my palm like liquid lightning, coiling around its legs and flash-crystallizing into jagged, prismatic shackles. I yanked hard.

The simulacrum hit the dirt.

I didn’t hesitate.

Both daggers plunged into its chest—directly into the glowing core beneath the bark.

The light inside it flared—

—and went out.

The body collapsed into lifeless timber.

The forest didn’t relax.

Instead—

clap.

Slow. Polite.

I spun.

Another figure stood between the glowing trunks.

This one was Ten.

Same posture. Same loose confidence. Same too-wide grin carved into wooden flesh.

“Don’t,” I whispered.

It moved faster than the first.

Ten always did.

The simulacrum blurred forward, movements boosted by unnatural torque, wooden limbs bending where bone never could. I barely got my daggers up before its kick launched me through a curtain of glowing vines.

Pain detonated through my ribs.

I skidded across wet roots, rolled, vanished into illusion—

—and reappeared in its blind spot.

I slashed.

It parried.

With a bismuth-coated forearm.

My own power again—copied.

A chill crept down my spine.

“Oh. You learn.”

Good to know.

The Ten-simulacrum split in two—no, not split—duplicated. Two wooden Tens charged from opposite sides, one throwing a glowing bismuth spear, the other slamming down with a hammerfist like a falling tree.

“Rude.”

I dropped flat.

The spear passed overhead, crystallized midair as I forcibly inverted its molecular alignment, and exploded into harmless shards. The hammerfist cratered the ground where my skull had been a breath ago.

I rolled between its legs, raked both daggers up the inner calf.

Bismuth surged.

The leg froze solid.

Momentum did the rest.

The simulacrum tore itself in half as it tried to keep charging.

The second one tackled me from behind.

We hit the dirt hard.

Wooden hands wrapped around my throat.

Pressure mounted fast.

Stars burst in my vision.

I drove my forehead into its face.

Bark cracked.

Sap sprayed.

Then I forced bismuth directly from my lungs into its chest.

The crystallization detonated outward.

The Ten-simulacrum shattered above me in a storm of rainbow shards and dead wood.

I lay there gasping, heart slamming against my ribs.

The forest shifted.

Footsteps echoed.

Slow.

Measured.

Too calm.

A third figure emerged.

Fallias.

My stomach dropped.

This one didn’t rush.

It tilted its head the same way she did when she was analyzing a battlefield.

“Don’t,” I said again, firmer this time.

It exhaled steam.

The air temperature spiked.

Fire mana condensed around its wooden claws, burning without consuming the body.

Oh.

So they weren’t just copying us.

They were copying how we fought.

The Fallias-simulacrum vanished in a burst of heated mist—

—and reappeared beside me.

I barely blocked the claw strike.

The impact flung me sideways.

My daggers skidded through glowing moss.

I switched tactics instantly.

Seven Fractals exploded into being around the clearing—every one of them real-looking, every one armed.

The simulacrum hesitated.

Wrong move.

All seven threw.

Bismuth knives streaked through the air.

The first six passed straight through illusion.

The seventh struck true.

The knife embedded in its spine.

I clenched my fist.

The bismuth expanded violently, branching through its entire body in a fraction of a second.

The creature froze mid-step—

—and then fractured into a thousand glittering fragments.

Silence crashed back into the clearing.

I stood there shaking, chest heaving, daggers re-forming in my hands from condensed mineral light as my illusions peeled away one by one.

Three down.

My instincts screamed that meant nothing.

Because the forest was still watching.

And somewhere deeper in that glowing, suffocating green—

Something had learned how I killed.

And was already building the next one.

I turned slowly, blades up, breathing fire and crystal and defiance into my stance.

“Yeah,” I whispered into the glowing dark. “…send the next.”

I was alone.

That, in itself, was not unusual. I had spent years roaming the great library in solitude, drifting between aisles of forgotten wisdom and shattered histories while the rest of the Keepers passed in and out like uncertain dreams. Their presence had been intermittent at best. Most nights, it had just been me and the dead voices trapped in paper and song. Even now, in this breath-heavy forest, that old familiarity clung to me. Solitude was not new.

But this was different.

The somber wind that threaded its way through the towering trees carried something like memory in its breath. Each passing gust seemed to hum with broken melodies, like a choir that had forgotten the shape of its own song. The bioluminescent glow along the bark pulsed faintly like buried heartbeats, lighting the underbrush in trembling shades of teal and gold. Shadows layered upon shadows. Nothing stood still for long.

When I looked between the trees, I saw no monster. No ambush. No enemy waiting to strike.

Only my own silhouette, stretched and distorted across bark and glowing moss.

It should have comforted me. It should have reminded me that I was real, that I existed, that I was still here. Instead, it unsettled me in a way I could not explain. Seeing only myself reflected back in endless repetition made the world feel hollow. As though this forest, for all its impossible life, had no one else in it at all.

It was terrifying.

It was beautiful.

It was lonely.

And loneliness had a way of pulling old thoughts back to the surface.

I found myself thinking of him again. Of Alexander. The way he frowned when he was thinking too hard. The way his hands always hesitated for just a fraction of a second before reaching for someone, as if he feared he might be taking something he did not deserve. The way his presence filled a space even when he was silent.

The distance between us felt wrong. It pressed against my ribs with every breath. I could not sense him clearly through the tangled mana of this place, and the absence gnawed at me far more than any threat ever could.

I missed him.

So much more than I wanted to admit.

The wind shifted.

The forest answered with a low, aching sigh.

And without realizing it at first, I began to speak aloud, my voice threading into the murmuring dark like a confession meant for no one and everyone at once.

“The last light, flickers tonight. The last flight, soars in the morn. The last fight, never arrives. The last, always delivers.”

The words drifted away from me, carried into the glowing canopy where they dissolved among the leaves and drifting spores. An old verse. One of the forgotten hymns from the lowest stacks of the library. A poem about endings that never quite ended. About hope that never stopped arriving, even when it was unbearable to wait for it.

I wrapped my arms around myself, feeling the heat in my scales pulse faintly against the cool, damp air. My fire felt muted here. Not weakened. Merely… listened to. As if the forest itself was aware of what I was and was quietly deciding how it felt about that.

“I don’t like this place,” I whispered at last. “It listens too well.”

The glow between the trees pulsed in response.

Somewhere far beyond my senses, beyond layered mana and stranger laws, I knew the others were still fighting, still moving, still surviving. I trusted them. I trusted Fractal with her impossible blades and dazzling lies of light. I trusted Cordelia with her steady mind and unyielding will. I trusted V to defy death with traps and stubborn laughter.

And I trusted Alexander.

But trust did not make the waiting easier.

I took a slow breath and started forward again, one careful step at a time, letting my senses stretch outward into the forest. The loneliness remained, but it no longer felt empty. It felt… watched. Not in the threatening way. Not yet. More like the feeling of being observed by something ancient and curious, something that was not certain whether I was prey, guest, or intrusion.

My shadow moved with me.

And for a moment, just a moment, it almost felt like I was not walking alone after all.
