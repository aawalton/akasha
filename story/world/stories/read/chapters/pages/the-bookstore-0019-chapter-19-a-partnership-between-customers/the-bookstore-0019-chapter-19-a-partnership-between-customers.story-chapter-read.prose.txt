Nora traced the flower pattern through the piece of golden pine with slow, careful cuts, paying attention to depth, line, and texture all at once.

It was the kind of work that demanded such complete attention that breathing itself became a distraction. Golden pine was not a material that passed through her hands often. In truth, it had only happened a few times in her life. When the mayor’s wife had brought the commission, Nora had agreed before she had even decided what price to ask. If necessary, she would have done it nearly at cost just for the chance to work such fine essence wood.

Fortunately, the elegant elf woman had not been stingy.

The knife continued its dance through the polished grain, shaving away thin curls that smelled faintly sweet and resinous, until the whole thing was ruined by a violent slam from upstairs.

Nora froze.

Then she set the knife down with enormous care, because if her hand slipped now and scarred the carving, she truly might kill someone.

“Rick!” she shouted, already marching toward the stairs. “You dare go smashing doors in my house? Have you lost your senses? Do you know how much concentration this kind of work needs? Come out here and let me teach you some manners!”

The reply came immediately, though the door stayed shut.

“Mom, sorry! I just—”

He cut himself off, breathed, and tried again in a tone that was far more cautious.

“I’m truly sorry, Mom. Did I ruin anything? I’ll come down and clean it up. If I damaged materials, I’ll pay for it.”

That cooled her temper faster than she cared to admit.

Nora stopped outside the door instead of pounding on it.

“No, it’s fine,” she said, though she was still annoyed enough to mean for now. “What happened to get you this angry? You’re not usually the sort to smash things.”

Inside, Rick let out the sound of a man discovering he might survive the next few minutes after all.

The door opened.

“It’s that crazy mayor, Mom,” he said. “This morning I went to the market to place an order for equipment, and he’s forbidden the merchants from bringing anything in from Riverend. They can only order from the capital now. If anyone ignores him, he says he’ll confiscate permits.” Rick threw up one hand in disbelief. “How is that fair?”

“Son,” Nora said automatically, “you know better than to call the mayor that.”

Then she frowned.

“What is the real problem? Buying from the capital costs a little more, but it’s not worth fighting the mayor over a few extra coins.”

“Silver, Mom,” Rick snapped, then forced himself to lower his voice. “Not a few coins. Silver. Money that should stay in my pocket instead of being wasted because he can’t stand a richer town existing nearby. And the capital’s essence metal work costs more too.”

This time Nora said nothing immediately. That showed Rick how serious his own tone had been.

The recent boar hunt had driven the lesson into him hard enough that he doubted he would ever forget it. His spear had looked like a toy against that beast. If not for the herb extract and the guidance from the beastiarium, he might have died with a broken shaft in his hands and his bones scattered under the roots of some forest tree. Now, for the first time in his life, he had enough coin to improve his equipment.

And naturally, that was when the town decided to make the process more difficult.

Mountainend had a blacksmith, but no metalmancer. For true hunting gear—good essence metal heads, better balance, real reinforcement—he had to look elsewhere. Riverend was the obvious choice. Strangely enough, for all its reputation as the richer and more expensive town, only common goods and daily consumables were worse there. Anything tied to crafting, services, or competition had been dropping in price.

Rick barely understood why. A merchant acquaintance had explained it to him after the market argument. Too many crafters, too many adventurers, too many sellers chasing too many buyers. When options multiplied, prices fell. According to the man, Rick could save up to ten silver if he bought everything in Riverend instead of placing the order through the capital.

Ten silver.

Rick had nearly laughed when he first heard the number, not because it was small, but because it was not.

“I don’t understand why the mayor won’t just take advantage of it,” he muttered. “If Riverend’s work is cheaper right now, shouldn’t we be using that? Wouldn’t that help Mountainend too?”

Nora gave him the sort of look mothers perfected over decades.

“Because you are thinking like a man who wants someting,” she said, “and not like a politician.”

Rick made an irritated sound. That was exactly the problem.

If the roads were safe, he would have gone himself and ignored the whole farce. But they were not. With so many people pouring toward the borderlands, crime on the roads had swelled badly. Bandits, thieves, desperate drifters, opportunists—too many kinds of trouble for one hunter carrying coin. That was why merchants traveled with escorts now, even in places as small as Mountainend.

The local mercenary company was not exactly famous for battlefield glory, but it served its purpose. Criminals generally preferred easier targets to provoking the whole town at once.

Then came a knock at the front door.

The sound interrupted the argument. Nora adjusted her dress and passed a quick hand over her hair before going down to answer it. Rick stayed where he was for a moment, still angry enough that his chest felt tight, but curiosity eventually pulled him after her.

The visitor was Dorgh. The town asset manager stood at the doorway with the look of a man performing an errand slightly beneath his dignity but not unpleasantly so.

“Mr. Rick,” the dwarf said after the necessary greetings, “Miss Anne asked me to deliver a message. She would like you to come to the bookstore. Apparently there is a business opportunity.”

Rick blinked.

“A business opportunity?”

“That is what I was told.”

Dorgh’s expression suggested he knew more than he was saying, which in his case usually meant he found the withheld information amusing.

Rick’s irritation faltered.

Anything described that way by the bookstore owner herself was worth hearing.

When Rick arrived, Anne was already waiting.

Not alone, either.

The trio from before—Cyrus, Iris, and Hakon—were there as well. They stood in the bookstore with varying degrees of composure, which was to say Iris looked determined, Hakon looked unreadable, and Cyrus looked as though he might say three foolish things before anyone managed to stop him.

Anne had arranged them around one of the central tables, not too formally, but not by accident either. Tea had already been served.

Rick noticed that first. The woman never missed a chance to make a conversation more favorable before it truly began.

“There you are, Mr. Rick,” Anne said, sounding pleasantly welcoming and not at all like someone who had deliberately summoned him into the middle of a carefully prepared scene. “Please, join us.”

Rick did.

He was still a little wary, but far less than he had been during his first visits. The bookstore had earned that much. Also, he had recently carried several hundred kilograms of boar through half the surrounding region. Something about that made it easier to enter a room full of adventurers without feeling immediately smaller than he ought.

“Hi, I’m Rick,” he said, because that still seemed the simplest way to begin.

“I’m Iris. Nice to meet you,” the girl replied at once, formal enough to sound prepared.

“Hi, I’m Cyrus. I’m a warrior. I heard that you—”

“Hakon,” the foxkin said, cutting across him with calm efficiency.

The contrast nearly made Rick laugh.

A month ago, he might have been too nervous to do more than nod stupidly at them. Adventurers had belonged to a different category in his mind then—people from elsewhere, with guilds, gold, and futures that did not begin and end in Mountainend. But things had changed too quickly for old habits to keep up. He had hunted deeper into the forbidden forest than before, studied real books, made maps, killed a essence beast, and sold the result for more coin than he had ever handled at once.

That did not make him equal to them in every way. But it did make blind awe feel harder to maintain.

The trio, on the other hand, seemed unsure what to do with him.

They knew what a hunter was, obviously, but knowing of a profession was not the same as dealing with one closely. Hunters were supposed to be solitary people, weren’t they? Quiet, practical, a little rough, and not terribly connected to adventurer business. At least, that seemed to be the reason for their uncertainty.

Cyrus was the worst at hiding it. He kept looking at Rick with bright, badly contained curiosity, as if bronze-tier beasts and successful forest runs were subjects far too interesting to leave alone. Hakon, by contrast, had clearly decided that silence and observation were the wiser path. Iris seemed to be doing the real work of negotiation for the group, which did not surprise Rick in the least.

Anne watched all of it over her tea.

She looked calm, of course. She always did when business might grow from other people’s discomfort.

“Why don’t we sit properly,” she said, stepping in before the awkwardness could harden into something more difficult. “It is easier to discuss cooperation when no one is standing around pretending not to evaluate everyone else.”

That earned the smallest reaction from Hakon, which Anne almost certainly noticed and stored away for later.

They sat.

For a moment no one spoke. Then Iris took the lead, exactly as Rick had expected she would.

“We heard about your hunt,” she said. “Not just that you brought something back. We heard it was a real essence beast, and that you had been moving through the forbidden forest.” She paused, studying his face. “We wanted to ask if those rumors are true.”

Rick glanced once at Anne.

The bookstore owner did not even try to hide her interest. In fact, she seemed almost pleased by the directness.

He understood why quickly enough.

This was not merely a conversation. It was one of Anne’s arrangements. She had taken one customer’s success and another group’s need, set them across from each other, and waited to see what profitable shape might emerge.

Rick, unexpectedly, did not resent that.

“Well,” he said slowly, “the rumors are true enough.”

Cyrus leaned in immediately.

“So you really hunted that boar alone?”

“Yes.”

“How?”

Iris closed her eyes for a heartbeat as if begging the gods for patience.

Rick almost smiled.

“With preparation,” he said. “And with knowledge.”

That answer pleased Anne so visibly that Rick suspected she might have repeated it later in private simply because it sounded good.

He did not explained everything. Not the Mapping Book. Not the exact method of the kill. Not the parts of Anne’s help that felt too valuable, or too personal to the agreement between them, to spread carelessly. Instead, he explained it in general. He had spent time studying. He had mapped a workable section of the forest. He knew the local beasts there better now. He had shelters, routes, and a decent sense of what moved where.

The adventurers listened carefully. That alone separated them from many older people Rick had known. They did not interrupt every other line to declare what was impossible.

“We’re thinking about doing an expedition here too,” Iris said once he finished. “Not deep at first. Just enough to see whether Mountainend’s side offers real opportunities.”

Cyrus shifted in his seat.

“What she means,” he said, trying and failing to sound casual, “is that we’d like to know if there’s actually something worth doing here instead of crawling back to Riverend for every chance at coin.”

That, Rick understood. Too well, perhaps.

They feared he might want the area to himself. He could see it in the way Iris phrased things and in the care with which Hakon still watched him. If Rick had found a profitable ground, why would he share it with a full party of armed strangers who might simply take the advantage and push him out?

Under different circumstances, that would have been a fair concern. But Rick had already moved beyond that way of thinking.

The mapped area near the mountain was useful, but it was not where his ambitions ended anymore. Ever since the forest routes had begun making sense in his head, ever since Anne had pushed him toward note-taking, mapping, and real observation, something larger had taken shape behind the day-to-day work of hunting.

The ground he knew now was only the beginning.

Beyond it lay harder terrain, more dangerous beasts, and eventually the other side of the mountain itself. Rick had not forgotten that possibility for a single day. If anything, the little success near the Wall had made it harder to stop thinking about it.

And for that, a lone hunter had limits. Limits that a proper party did not share.

He looked at the three of them again. Perhaps Anne had done it again. Perhaps she had placed the right people in the same room on purpose and waited for them to discover the usefulness of one another.

Rick leaned back slightly.

“I mapped a large area,” he said. “I know the shelters, some good observation points, and most of the beasts that roam there. If all you want is to test whether Mountainend’s side is worth something, then yes, it is.”

The three adventurers straightened almost at once.

Rick let the moment hang before continuing.

“But I’m not interested in staying there forever.”

That caught them. Even Anne’s eyes narrowed.

Rick did not mind. For once, he was almost enjoying being the one who knew something others did not expect.

“The place I mapped is only a start,” he said. “What I really want is farther in. Beyond the mountain, if possible.”

Cyrus stared.

Iris forgot to hide her surprise.

Hakon’s ears shifted slightly, which Rick was beginning to suspect meant more than the foxkin would ever admit aloud.

And Anne—

Anne lowered her teacup with the small, satisfied calm of a woman watching one good investment recognize another.
