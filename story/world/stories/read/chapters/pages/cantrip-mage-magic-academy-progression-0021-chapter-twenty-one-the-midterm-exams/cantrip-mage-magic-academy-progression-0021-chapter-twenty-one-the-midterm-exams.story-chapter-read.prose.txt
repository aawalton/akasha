Eola woke up with a bad feeling in her gut.

She was used to those feelings, and did what she usually did—make the trip to breakfast and work on a bowl of porridge until the feeling went away. Only, this time, it didn’t go away after a few bites. “I could have been studying,” she moaned to no one.

The bowl sat, mostly uneaten, on the table, and after twenty minutes, the smell of apples faded, and the last warmth went away. Eola cleared her place and made a longer, and much colder, journey. Then she sat down in the Ideograms classroom for the first time in a very long while.

“Your exams are being passed out now,” Instructor Tarik said, waving his hand. Twenty-five small sheafs of parchment flew across the room, landing on the tables in front of each student. “Begin.”

Eola stared at the test for a moment, the feeling in her stomach finally letting up a bit. The test wanted her to draw, from memory, the First Order rune for Smith’s Breath. Eola had first drawn that rune when she was twelve.

She sketched the two shapes—the anvil and the bellows, both stylized in Old Alemic runes—and then ringed them with Forsooth Script. The modifying marks in Smith’s Breath were clever enough that Eola hadn’t tried to locate them yet, but that was fine. Instructor Tarik wasn’t asking for that.

Then she flipped the page over.

Three minutes later, she did it again. Then again. When she got to the third page, a request for a Second Order spell confronted her. She’d never seen that spell before. She hadn’t seen the one on the back of the third piece of parchment, either; it was a Second Order spell called Light Lance.

The right thing to do was turn in the pages she had. Instructor Tarik couldn’t expect any of his first-years to know Second Order spells by heart at the midterms. But…

But there was another option. Light Lance sounded like a more advanced Bright Ball. If Eola used the principles she’d learned, it shouldn’t be impossible to string together the modifying marks she knew and create something that, just maybe, imitated Light Lance.

So, for the next thirty minutes, she lightly sketched different modifying marks in different places, experimenting as student after student finished their exams and brought them up. At last, she was the only one left, and Instructor Tarik cleared his throat. “Light Lance, Miss Lemiene?”

“I’m close,” she said.

“Prove it.”

“I won’t be able to. Too much Mana. You’ll just need to trust me.” Eola brought her exam to the professor’s desk, then spent two minutes walking him through what her spell-chain would do.

And, at the end, he shook his head. “It’s a solid attempt, but I’m not convinced this would replicate Light Lance.”

Eola deflated. Her shoulders slumped, and she turned, leaving the test on the desk.

“Wait a moment.”

Eola froze in place and turned as Tarik shuffled through the other tests. It took him almost two minutes to nod slowly. “Only four other students made any attempt at either of the Second Order ideograms. Of those, only one other student produced something acceptable—and even then, her work has flaws that would be dangerous if she were to add Mana to it. Your spell wouldn’t replicate Light Lance, but it would likely be safe to cast. Congratulations.”

“Thank you, sir,” Eola said. She fled the classroom, hurrying to her next class. She had a new feeling in her stomach, and not even the Mana Studies exam could possibly change that.

“This examination will consist of two steps. In the first, you will use the Heavy Haul spell, without modifying marks, to test your Mana lines and soul’s attuned Mana efficiency,” Madame Reyanna said. Eola hid her rolling eyes; the Mana Studies professor was already testing the good mood she’d left the Ideograms midterm with. “As you cast Heavy Haul, remember that, while it is Child’s Magic, it can—and should—be used with your attuned Mana. For this test, we are testing your attunement. All students will take this examination at the same time.

“The second part, in accordance with the best teachings of Ordered Magic and Varin’s Academy’s policies, will be to measure the depths of your soul.”

Madame Reyanna pointed to the fifteen chromite bars on her classroom’s wall. Each matched the other, with the exception of the massive fifteenth bar. Eola still didn’t know what that last one was for, but she had a feeling she’d find out before the rest of her classmates. “The test of bars is an ancient form of Mana measurement, but one that is still considered accurate. Magic reacts interestingly when poured into a structure of silver cores and chromite shells, with silver’s pro-magic and chromite’s anti-magic siphoning Mana from the caster at an increasingly rapid rate.”

“So all we have to do is cast at them?” Catrine asked.

The professor nodded from her podium. “Use Mana Mark. It’s a harmless spell, but one that maintains itself like Heavy Haul. It’s also Child’s Magic, and the purpose of the test is not to measure attuned Mana. Now, prepare yourselves for the first test.”

She waved a hand, and an hourglass appeared mid-air. “Begin.”

Eola did nothing. She simply watched as the other students sketched their anvils and filled them in. This part of the Mana Studies exam was guaranteed to be a disaster, and it was better to fail on her own terms than humiliate herself. She had no attuned Mana, and she’d been ignoring Mana lines the whole term. The professor’s eyes passed over her. She flushed almost as red as Madame Reyanna, but kept her eyes locked on the gnome. “I’m finished.”

“You’re finished? Three seconds? Less? That’s almost impressively poor, Miss Lemiene.”

There wasn’t a good response to that, so Eola just waited as the other students dropped out, one by one. Over the next fifteen minutes, all twenty-four of them gradually ground to a halt. When Garreth finally stopped channeling his Heavy Haul, Madame Reyanna clapped her hands once. “We will now take fifteen minutes to recover our Mana. Food generally helps, as does the academy itself. Please eat.”

She pulled a tapestry away, revealing a nook—a nook filled with sweets. Eola wasn’t first to it, but she was close. The mostly-untouched porridge felt like days ago, and she snatched a pair of cookies and a square of a smooth, dark brown cake, then returned to her spot.

The cookies were delicious, but the square? It was creamy and rich, and it almost seemed to melt in Eola’s mouth. Even better, it reminded her of something she’d eaten back in Greenarbor, before she’d started living with her uncle. Something her father had made. She ate half of the tiny square before she even realized it, then spent the next ten minutes slowly working on the rest, unwilling to finish it and let the nostalgic taste fade.

Fifteen minutes later, on the dot, Madame Reyanna looked up from her notebook. “The second test will now begin. Even a child can fill a single bar. Therefore, the line to achieve a score of two on this exam is three full bars. Journeymages should be able to fill the first four, and I expect some of you may reach five or even six. Mr. Tremory, you’re up.”

Colin grabbed his spellbook and walked to the front of the class, flipping through the dog-eared pages as he tracked down Mana Mark. Eola crossed her fingers under her desk. He was her best friend, and she knew from watching him that he’d underperformed on the first test. Not as badly as she had, but even so…he’d tried, and she hadn’t. He drew the simple symbol—a single spiral of Old Alemic—and added Mana.

Then he lit up like an aurora in the northern sky. Mana poured from his body as the rest of the class started murmuring and gasping. Colin didn’t react, though, and Madame Reyanna started to talk. “The reaction between Mana Mark and the chromite and silver alloy creates an illusion facing outward, and a second one facing inward. He’s not perceiving the same thing we are, and to the best of our knowledge, none of us is seeing what’s really happening. But we on the outside do get to see the results.”

A single bar lit up, purple, green, and red flames running across its surface. Then a second. The third took longer, and by the time the flames were halfway around the fourth, they were guttering out. Eola’s fingers were still crossed, and she closed her eyes to silently offer a quick prayer to Y’aer: let her friend finish four, please.

When she opened her eyes, Colin was on the ground, panting and sucking in air—but the fourth bar was ablaze with Mana. The chromite rectangles guttered out one by one, the first going empty before the others, and Madame Reyanna cleared her throat. “Four. An acceptable score, Mr. Tremory. Get another cookie. Lady Andrese, you’re next.”

Catrine managed to fill five bars, then push to the sixth. It lit up, and she stepped away, still standing but pale and shivering. It took eight more students to reach the same number—and one of those barely scraped by with three bars of Mana. Then two students hit six, and the second one, a noble boy, was halfway up the seventh bar when he stalled out. “Full bars only, Lord Olethe,” Madame Reyanna said.

And then it was Eola’s turn.

She cast the spell.

The entire world went dark except for the single spiral in the very center of the first bar. A thin, almost thread-like strand of Mana reached from her finger and touched the bottom of the rectangle, burning bright white. That alone was a relief—and a mark of how far Eola had come in controlling her Mana. As her Mana touched the first chromite-and-silver bar, it started to glow with the same brilliant white color. It filled the first bar, then the second and third.

When the fourth filled and the fifth began, she had to open the champagne bottle in her soul a little further. The seventh bar offered even more resistance, and Eola opened her soul completely. The thread turned into a rope, then a stream of Mana, and the seventh filled.

She started the eighth. Surely, she couldn’t fill the eighth. Her soul felt empty, and her stomach rumbled loudly enough that the entire class had to have heard it. But the eighth bar lit up brightly.

The ninth was too much, though. Eola dropped to a knee, and the Mana Mark symbol faded. The white lights around each bar faded slowly, and as they did, the classroom swam back into focus.

It was silent except for the pulsing blood in her temples and the protests of her empty-feeling stomach.

Then, Madame Reyanna’s stammering broke the silence. “Ridiculous. How did you do that? An eighth-bar Child Mage? Why haven’t you hit Journeymage or Truemage yet, Miss Lemiene? And why intentionally fail the first half of the test?”

Eola had no answers to any of the scarlet-faced professor’s questions. She walked shakily to the table with the food, grabbed a double handful of the smooth, creamy chocolate squares, and returned to her seat, leaving the professor sputtering until she called the next student up.

Eight was the same number of bars Eddarn had claimed.

The Introduction to Monsters test was a nightmare.

Eight pages of parchment, with descriptions—of varying quality—of different monsters. Some were easy, like the trogs and goblins. Others could have been four or even five different creatures, and only minor details that were often missing could have narrowed it down. Eola did her best, but when she placed her sheaf of answers in the basket Instructor Clearance had set out for finished tests, she was pretty sure she’d messed up more than she’d gotten right.

Then it was time for the dance. Maestro Yarrowbloom had—apparently—arranged for a four-hour block with every first-year student in Varin’s Academy, and the gymnasium was packed with a hundred sweaty eighteen-year-olds. Eola spotted a few full elves and a handful of brightly-colored half-tall gnomes like Madame Reyanna and Ursula among the sea of people. The Maestro, Instructor Tarik, and a severe-looking woman with her hair in a tight braid and armor over her robes split the students into groups of four efficiently.

Eola ended up with one of the elves named Irisbreeze, a dark-haired young man who did nothing but scowl at everyone, and a girl who’d arrived with her own practice gear—gear ensorcelled with silver runes. Eola stared at the padded practice armor and silvered helmet jealously. Whoever this was, she came from a family with real money.

Her group fought third. The bout with the elf was close, but in the end, the Taron-Li was his domain, and he got the fifth hit in before she could land her fourth and extend the duel. They unsummoned their various shields and spells, and the angry boy and rich girl fought. To Eola’s surprise, neither used a single spell, and the girl—Miss Jicene Wenderly—beat Roth Gerr two to five. Then Wenderly and Irisbreeze fought a shockingly close duel, settled when the girl finally used magic—a First Order spell that transfigured Irisbreeze’s armor and clothes into wood, locking him in place.

Eola had her next two fights in a row, first with Miss Wenderly and then with Roth. Her first fight was fast, furious, and completely out of control. As the two girls traded blows and Child’s Magic, Eola felt her form slipping, and she forced herself to maintain the fundamentals of the Taron-Li, even as the noble girl landed blow after blow. In the end, she lost, five to three. That same spell—the wood-cloth one—had decided not one, but two points.

The rich girl pulled her helmet off after the fight and stuck out a gloved hand. “You’ve got the right idea, you just need better tutors.”

Eola nodded, clenching her teeth beneath her own helmet. “Thanks.” She shook the rich girl’s hand, trying not to think about everything her mother had taught her. It wasn’t Miss Wenderly’s fault. She didn’t know. But even so, by the time Roth Gerr took his position on the pitch, Eola’s fingers gripped her practice sword like she was trying to strangle it.

“Begin,” Maestro Yarrowbloom said, and Eola lunged.

Her sword tapped Roth’s chest before he could even react, and Eola smiled under her mask. His counterattack landed right in a Safe Shield as Eola expertly—in her mind—summoned the Xen-Da and danced behind it, then landed another hit to the side of Roth’s helmet. The bout turned one-sided after that; Roth Gerr still refused to cast a spell, and without magic, Eola’s fully-realized Taron-Li was too much for him. She ended the fight in a win, five points to one.

All in all, it wasn’t the result she’d hoped for, and Eola left the gymnasium as quickly as she could.

Eola collapsed on her bed, exhausted from the exams, and stroked Atta’s back. “What a day.”

The blue cat purred but didn’t say anything, and after a moment, her yellow eyes drifted to the door. Patrice stood there, leaning against the frame, a sheepish grin on her face. “I failed everything. How was it for you?”

“It was fine,” Eola said, staring at the ceiling.

“Doesn’t sound fine.” Patrice took Eola’s chair. “I was in the first group, though, so I didn’t get to see your fights. We won’t have our marks tomorrow—my brother said they never give them back on Y’aer’s Day. “

“So we need to wait before we find out our fates?”

“Don’t be so down about it. You did better than me. I’ve got a plan to keep our minds off it, though. Let’s grab Colin tomorrow and spend the day in Varin’s Town, celebrating and mourning.”
