The recruits gathered outside the fortress on a dusty road. A large hovering platform stood in the middle of the road.It hummed slightly, and a pale blue light shone from the bottom of it. Upon the platform was a large box covered in a black thick canvas.

Cale was hugging an old gray jacket tighter around him, pressing his pink hoverboard against his side with an elbow. The biting wind outside wasn’t just annoying, it was painful. He had to periodically swipe his face to remove the ticklish red particles, like fine ruby sand.

“Did you figure out what this stuff is?” Cale asked.

“Yes,” Aura said decisively. “Not pleasant, but not dangerous to you. These particles contain sigils... Very intricate, very alien. These are not the language my creators used. This is... a perversion.”

“What do the sigils say?”

“They are programming,” Aura said. “They will infiltrate your cells and start reproducing, utilizing your tissue.”

Cale’s heart dropped to his stomach. He had thought the particles were like second-hand smoking or something.

“You said this was not dangerous!” Cale hissed under his breath.

“Stop pissing your pants for a second and listen,” Aura said. “It is not dangerous to you, because I am a truly magnificent creation. Please do not debase yourself by throwing yourself at your feet, but feel free to thank me for my magnanimous protection.”

“You are truly a goddess among men,” Cale muttered and put a hand over his hammering heart. The alarm and urgency were settling, and Cale thought he might survive this moment without a blood vessel bursting.

The Sullied assigned to them approached. In his meaty hand he was carrying a round glass bottle. It had twenty or so small white beads.

“Detoxification pills. This one suggests you eat one immediately, and then one every four to six hours,” DR-12 said. “It keeps the Ruby Death away.”

Cale took the bottle and pocketed it. The tailor-fitting gray suit pants he wore had really nice large pockets. The Sullied was about to turn, but Cale stopped him.

“Hey, uhh... Why are you called DR-12?”

A flash of emotion passed on the Sullied’s face, but it was gone as soon as it came. Dr-12 shot a shifty look at Cale, but kept his tone demure.

“This one does not want to bother...” DR-12 muttered.

Fiara came up next to them. “Tell him,” she said.

DR-12 muttered something and wrung his hands.

“It was not a suggestion,” Fiara said icily. “Tell him.”

The Sullied nodded and spoke quietly. “This one is called DR-12 because he was the twelfth Sullied captured and created by Darius Roas.”

Fiara smiled brightly at DR-12. “Good boy.”

The nonchalance of it all punched through Cale’s gut like a lead weight. Created? The word stuck in his mind, thick and heavy, somehow stripping the Sullied of their humanity. These weren’t just debt slaves—they were Darius’s property. People stripped of even their past sins, reduced to numbers. And Fiara barely blinked at it.

Did they really deserve it all?

Cale wasn’t sure, but he would refuse to make a judgement call yet. Making assumptions in a world like this was not wise. Rolling with the punches was what he did best.

Still, it bothers me…

Fiara turned to Cale completely oblivious to any moral struggles Cale might be having. She smiled and looked at Cale’s hand. “Oh you got your pills, good. They’ll be deducted from your contribution credits, but it’s not like they’re optional...”

“Right...” Cale said and nodded slowly. “How many points?”

“Two hundred for twenty pills,” Fiara said. “But I don’t think you’ll use them all.”

“And how much do I get for killing a murderbot?”

“You mean a Praetorian?” Fiara said and shot him an exhausted look. “You get twenty for a tier two bot. You should easily cover the pills once we are done.”

“Have you done one of these before?” Cale asked.

“No,” Fiara said, trying to sound more carefree than she looked. “But it’s a standard procedure. Even though Core Crystallization advancement is rare, so is something going wrong during the operation.”

“What about enemy sabotage?” Cale asked.

Fiara shrugged. “It happens. But if it does, I’ll let Darius know immediately, and the syndicate will remember. And the other factions know that.”

“Slim consolation if we’re all dead by then.”

Fiara gave him a sly smile. “Finally a chink in the armor.”

“You’d notice them more often if you weren’t busy playing games with me,” Cale said.

“I don’t play games,” Fiara said immediately.

“You didn’t tell me why you participated in Darius’s challenge,” Cale said.

“I did,” Fiara said and glared at him. “Because I wanted to see how far I’d get.”

“You never told me why.”

“You never asked.”

“Will the two of you please shut up for a second,” Madia said as she fastened a cinch on the hovering platform. “This sucks enough without you two constantly yammering.”

“It is good for us all that we build rapport,” Artan said quietly. “The more we know each other and can trust each other, the higher our chance to succeed.”

“Trust is earned,” Risto said in a lazy drawl. “Through action, not chit-chat.”

“But chit-chat is a good start, darling,” Cassidy said as she passed Risto and gently bumped her hip against his. She smiled over her shoulder. “I’m sure we can get to the action later, after a little warm-up…”

Risto flinched and muttered something. This was the first time Cale saw him lose his cool aloofness.

“Let’s go already!” Ragnak said, standing on top of the payload, watching at the red and black wasteland before them, raising both hands in the air. “Talk is boring. I haven’t had a good fight in days.”

After they made sure they had their gear and supplies, the six recruits, Cale, Fiara and DR-12 set off towards the wilderness of machine and mana.

The wind had teeth. Not metaphorical ones either—Cale was pretty sure the gusts carried actual shards of corrupted dust, slicing against his cheek like tiny razors. They left no mark, because of Cale’s improved body, but they would cut a mortal to bits. The land was hostile.

Cale tugged the collar of his coat higher. He breathed out a soft mist. It was cold to boot.

The land stretched out in uneven, jagged shapes, as if the earth itself had tried to scream and then froze mid-contortion. In the distance, a withered husk of a tree twisted upon itself, its branches knotted into gnarled, agonized limbs. Some of the blackened bark peeled back like curling fingers, revealing pulsing veins of sickly red light underneath.

That’s not how trees work.

But it wasn’t a tree. It hadn’t been a tree for a long time. Now it was just a stray form resembling a tree in the great vast uniform mass that enveloped the world here.

Cale took a step forward, and the ground beneath him cracked—not the normal kind of crack from dried soil, but a hollow sound, as if something was moving beneath the surface.

He glanced at Risto, who stood at a lazy slouch a few feet away, watching something black skitter between the red and black plated rocks.

"Is the ground supposed to be... breathing?" Cale asked.

Risto lazily flicked a pebble at the nearest cracked patch. It hit the ground, bounced once—then disappeared. The earth swallowed it whole.

“Huh,” Risto murmured, mildly interested.

Great. The ground eats things now. Just great.

The rest of the team was scattered in a loose formation around the payload, making their way through the ruined landscape toward the spire. Fiara led them with sharp, purposeful strides, eyes flicking between her map and the terrain ahead. Ragnak, for some reason, was still standing on top of the payload. He had removed his jacket and shirt and the red particles peppered his body constantly.

“What are you doing up there?” Sintra asked him.

“The spiritual pressure test taught me something!” Ragnak yelled, his white messy hair flapping in the wind. “I am not hard enough, not tough enough! So I’ll harden myself!”

Galvon gave Ragnak a look and shook his head. “Idiot,” he muttered loud enough to carry.

Cale kept walking, kicking a chunk of something brittle and white out of his path. It took him a second to realize it was shaped like a spine protruding in a curve through the earth. The bone was black, with glowing lines of red pulsing along the ridges.

He exhaled through his nose. Perfectly normal place. Lovely ambiance.

Up ahead, the remains of a drained pond stretched out like a gaping wound in the earth. The cracked bed was lined with In the very center of the pond, a metal-like lattice had begun forming—thin, spindly strands of wire weaving themselves into a geometric shape. It looked vaguely like a sigil.

Something small scuttled along the exposed dark mud of the pond, shifting the silt as it moved.

Cale narrowed his eyes. The thing was barely the size of his hand, an insectile construct of polished black metal with spindly legs that never quite touched the ground. The longer he looked, the more he realized there were dozens of them.

They weren’t building anything. They weren’t attacking anything. They were just... working.

Maintaining.

Spreading.

"...What the hell are those?" Sintra asked, stepping up beside him.

Fiara didn’t even look up from her map. "Non-combat drones. Maintenance units."

"Maintenance for what?" Cale asked, watching as one of the drones spun a thin filament of wire from its own body and wove it into the air. The strand didn’t fall—it just... hung there.

"This," Fiara said, gesturing at the pond, the cracked land, the unnatural geometry creeping into the world.

“Why aren’t we destroying them?” Cale asked.

“That’s the primary job of the Sullied. They destroy the drones and remove the corruption. It keeps things from getting completely out of control.”

“That suggests things are somewhat out of control.”

“Fiara shrugged. “It does spread. But the spread is slow.”

Cale exhaled slowly, watching the drones restring the landscape into something incomprehensible.

Cale realized something. Omnic Manifest wasn’t some angry AI hurling waves of combat units at them. It was something that didn’t acknowledge them at all. It wasn’t a hostile AI. It was indifferent.

It was simply reshaping the world in its image.

And that was somehow worse.

Cale looked back at the tree with its exposed, glowing veins, the spiraling strands of metal in the pond, the breathing cracks in the ground.

If left unchecked, would the whole world end up like this? It harrowed Cale how seemingly little the cultivators seemed to care about the spread. They weren’t blind or ignorant. They just… chose not to care.

I sure hope that’s the right call…

It still felt wrong. What if someday this cascaded out of control? If the Nevani had left great treasures buried in the capital, why didn’t the cultivator factions wage war against this cancer?

He clenched his jaw. Cale knew the answer.

Because they can’t cooperate. They’re worried that if they try to do something good for a change, the other factions will pounce. It creates a deadlock that will never yield.

And so they do nothing…

Did that mean that the world was doomed? Cale decisively did not like that thought.

Was I made to stop this? Is that what the failsafe means?

He wasn’t sure how he felt about that yet.

Cale exhaled, slow and measured, his fingers tightening around the bottle of Ruby Death pills in his pocket. He looked back at the tree with its glowing veins, the drones weaving their silent threads, the pulsing cracks in the ground.

If nothing changed, would this be the world one day?

Total annihilation.

Cale’s grip hardened. He didn’t want that to happen. But what a laughable idea that he could stop it as he was. How? Talk to Darius about it? He would laugh Cale out of the room. No.

Cale wasn’t sure if he was the failsafe, or what it meant. Or how strong his resolve to stop the spread of this cancer was. But one thing was for sure: If he did not get strong, really strong…

He wouldn’t even have the option.
