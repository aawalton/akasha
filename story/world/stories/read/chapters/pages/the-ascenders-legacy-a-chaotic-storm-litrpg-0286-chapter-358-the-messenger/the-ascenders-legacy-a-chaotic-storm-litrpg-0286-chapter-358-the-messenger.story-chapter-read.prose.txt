With their plan having failed, the enemy alliance had to find a different approach.

Malverin, for her part, was deeply relieved it had. She had hated the plan from the start and had only gone along with it because she was tired of always being the voice of opposition.

But to exploit a child's trauma—to weaponize his guilt and manipulate his grief just to force the transmigrants out—was beyond ruthless. It was monstrous. And she couldn't express just how glad she was that Mordred had refused to be part of it.

Even so, she knew it would have worked splendidly had he agreed.

Abused children never truly escaped their abusers. They spent their whole lives either running or proving they were strong enough to fight back. Darien was no different. Put Mordred in danger, and he would have been unable to stay away. He would have come out. And the transmigrants would have followed.

But that plan was ash now, and only one viable alternative remained.

The transmigrants.

Malverin hated it. She always had.

Unlike the other sects, the Illusion Sect had only one transmigrant among its ranks. If that transmigrant died, they would become obsolete—stripped of what little power and political standing they still held in Gordon's Assembly.

They would become no different from the voiceless sects, whose affinities were too small and inconsequential for anything they did to matter.

And beyond even that was the shrinking of their Origin Plane.

She had watched it happen to the Blood and Earth sects after the death of Kael Varros and Volgrim Kaizen. How their Origin Planes had shrunk—how in their bid to restore balance, they had simply caused more imbalance.

If the Illusion Sect released its transmigrant and she died, their plane would shrink too. Only they wouldn't have two more transmigrants to fall back on.

They had only the one.

And so when Aspwick proposed using the transmigrants again, Malverin had stood firmly against it.

But it had been twelve hours since then and no other alternative had presented itself.

Exhaling slowly, she glanced around the room, at the other members of the alliance. There were six of them. If they each put forward a single transmigrant, they would be a match for the storm sect who now also had six transmigrants, including the two the Space Sect had contributed.

The war would resume. And if everything went well, balance would be restored.

If.

Malverin sighed again.

This could be the mistake of a lifetime, but at this point, that was a risk she would just have to take. She glanced back up at the six other elders, all of whom wore a different shade of the same expression—waiting for her input. For her agreement.

She regarded them for a long moment. Then, very hesitantly, she nodded. “Fine. I’m in.”

“Finally.” Helion Varkas frowned. “My transmigrants are itching for this battle. I had to conduct a trial among them just to know who to pick.”

But only Varkas seemed excited about the turn of events. And who could blame him. He hadn’t seen the world burn or felt the earth groan from its deepest depths. He hadn’t seen the catastrophe that had claimed the lives of tens of thousands.

He hadn’t seen just how brutal this war was compared to any other Balance War.

The others, however, had experienced it firsthand and their expression remained completely sober as they pushed the names of their chosen transmigrants forward.

“This is it,” Elder Mournwind spoke, his voice almost mournful. “Let’s hope we don’t regret it.”

Malverin glanced at the name she had pushed forward, and a slight knot of dread formed in her core.

“Let’s hope.”

Back in the Storm Sect, beneath a sky of roiling gray clouds and flashing lightning, a certain transmigrant floated atop a platform of crackling electricity, his frost-white hair whipping in the wind.

Before he’d arrived, the storm had raged—lightning arcing, and thunder booming—but in his presence, it had grown subtly subdued, like a child settling in the company of its father.

It recognized authority. It recognized Aodhán.

Even if, at the moment, Aodhán seemed entirely unaware of it.

He sat silently just beneath the roiling clouds, not really thinking—just staring down at the sect beneath him, not quite sure how to go on after everything he had learned today.

All his life, he'd thought his mother dead. He'd never imagined the possibility of her being alive. But that was no longer the case.

Now there was a real possibility that she was. That somewhere out there in the vastness of space, she was waiting for him. Thinking of him as she fought in an eternal war she hated.

The thought weighed heavy on his heart. But even heavier was the irony of it all.

Wasn't it funny that his mother had fled AEFLYM to Earth for a chance at a normal life, while he had done the exact opposite—practically jumping at the opportunity of becoming anything but normal?

He was running toward what she had run from.

And as funny as the irony was, it was frightening too. Because his mother had reached the peak of cultivation—had touched immortality—and had been too willing to give it all up for a chance at mundanity.

What did that say about cultivation? About the Collective? What had she learned to make her flee?

He thought about that for a long moment before concluding it was ultimately futile. His mother had risked her life to give him a normal life, but fate had simply brought him back anyway.

It made him wonder—if he ran after his own ascension, would he just be perpetuating the cycle?

He suspected so. But then again, he had little interest in mundanity. He didn't crave normalcy. He never had. He loved cultivation. He loved his abilities.

He simply would have loved it more if it hadn't come with all the wars and bloodshed.

Barely a year in this world and he'd already lived through three. The war between Ragnarok and Sunstone. The war between Lutia and the Children of the Watch. And now the Balance War.

It was too much. And the idea of an eternal war waiting on the other side of ascension wasn't helping matters one bit.

He sighed, feeling a pulse of warmth move through his spirit as Varéc tried to comfort him. A nice attempt, but ultimately futile—his heart was too heavy to be swayed.

He appreciated it nonetheless, sending a pulse of gratitude back. Varéc growled, sending an image of himself smiling, and despite everything, Aodhán couldn't help but chuckle.

"You look demented." He said between laughs, and Varéc replied with another image—this time of both of them smiling.

Aodhán laughed again, but it was different this time. Sadder. Heavier. It came with tears.

Varéc didn't wait to be summoned. He rushed out of Aodhán's spirit, midnight-black scales stark against the gray sky.

"Aodhán," he rumbled—still too much growl to be an actual word. Aodhán couldn't understand it. And so Varéc settled for pressing his massive head gently against Aodhán's, a quiet rumble emanating from his throat.

Aodhán pressed back, feeling his throat close around a sob as his emotions rose again. He let them flow, wanting to be rid of the weight in his chest.

He let himself feel the pain. The regret. He let himself feel angry at his mother. He let himself cry for Seamus. He let himself feel the one thing he hadn't allowed himself in the past three days.

He let himself fall apart.

His eyes burned with tears. His heart, with pain. His entire body shook with grief.

But Varéc was there to hold him up—the warmth of his wings more comforting than any hug, the scent of ozone on his scales more grounding than anything else in the world.

For a long while they remained like that, unmoving, until Aodhán finally pulled back, eyes red but dry.

"Thank you, buddy." He smiled at Varéc. "Now go soar the skies."

Varéc growled—are you okay to be alone?

"Don't worry about me." Aodhán caressed his snout. "I'll be fine. Besides, I won't be alone for long."

It was true. A familiar core was already approaching.

Varéc glanced in the core's direction, and with an amused growl, he turned and flew up into the clouds—leaving Aodhán alone with the approaching presence.

Nymera's core.

She arrived a few seconds later, frost-white hair whipping in the wind, concern plain on her face. Seeing his tear-stained cheeks, she sat down beside him—close enough to touch, far enough that he didn't feel crowded.

She stayed silent for a long moment, giving him a chance to speak. Anything.

When he didn't, she asked quietly. "Did you get the answers you were looking for?"

Aodhán glanced at her, then back at the roiling clouds. "Yes. I did."

She regarded him a moment, her smile a little sad. "Were they answers you liked?"

Aodhán pursed his lips and exhaled. "Not really. I don't hate them. I just… don't know how to deal with them yet."

Nymera nodded. "So it's true then. Your mother is an Ascendant."

Aodhán turned to look at her. "I thought you didn't believe me."

"It was hard to believe," she said matter-of-factly, her gaze narrowing. "You have to at least admit that."

Aodhán chuckled. "I'm not admitting anything. You should have believed me."

"Fine." Nymera sighed. "Next time, if there's a next time, I'll believe you. No matter how insane it seems."

Aodhán smiled. "That's a big promise."

"It is." Nymera smiled back. "I'm already regretting it."

Aodhán laughed, and for the next few minutes they sat in comfortable silence until he spoke again.

"You know, my birthday is in five days."

"Really?"

Aodhán nodded. “Really.”

"That's amazing." Nymera's smile widened. "We have to throw you a huge party. War or not, you deserve one."

Aodhán smiled widely at her enthusiasm. He did, didn't he? After everything the past year had put him through, he deserved a party. And ascendant mother or not, war or not, he was going to throw himself one.

Because just like Nymera said—he deserved it.

Dravion Ashscar, on the other hand, did not feel deserving of a party.

If anything, what he deserved was a burial. Because by all accounts, he should be dead—his throat crushed to pieces in Volgrim's grip. That was the fate he had earned. He should have been the one to die. Not Seamus.

Had he died that day, Elder Ghaltris would never have signed off on Darien and Seamus entering the battlefield.

But he had survived. And now Darien was unconscious. Seamus was dead.

And to make things worse, he felt responsible for all of it.

For the first few hours after Seamus's death, he'd tried to convince himself he wasn't to blame. That none of it was his fault. But he had failed to make himself believe it. And in that moment, he had known what he needed to do.

He had to fix his path. He had to make Seamus's death worth something.

But it had been three days since then—three long days of sitting in his cultivation room under the ruse of a bloodline evolution—meditating, discarding, and ripping apart every idea on how to rebuild his path before the next battle arrived and someone else died for his weakness.

He couldn't afford to enter the next battlefield like this. Weak. Useless. Too easily countered. If he was paired against any manipulator of sound or silence, he was finished—and this time Nymera most likely wouldn't reach him in time.

He needed the storm to still answer when his voice failed. He needed to herald it without needing his throat.

But altering an already-formed path was no simple matter, as the past three days had quickly proven. It was like trying to redirect a river that had carved its course through stone for centuries. The water would fight you every step of the way.

It would be far easier to abandon it entirely and carve a new one.

But Ashscar didn't want a new path.

He wanted to be a Herald. He loved being a herald. He loved the feeling of the storm answering his call, of thunder rolling at his command, of reality bending to words spoken with absolute certainty of their fulfillment. He loved speaking things into existence.

His near-death at Volgrim's hands had simply shown him he couldn't always rely on his voice to do it.

"You're tired," Giselle said from her corner, avian eyes watching him steadily. "You need to rest."

"What I need is a solution," Dravion replied. "What I need is to not be so easily countered."

"Dravion—"

“No! Giselle. I am a liability like this. The enemies already know my weakness. They will exploit it. I need a solution. And I need it now.”

Giselle clicked her beak in a sigh. "Maybe if you rest, it'll come to you."

"I'm not resting. I need to meditate."

She mantled her wings in exasperation. "You never listen, Dravion. You're going to run yourself ragged. Rest—now!"

"No!" Dravion snapped, truly angry. "You don't get to tell me what to do, Giselle. You're my familiar. You're not the boss of me."

"No, the storm is!" Giselle squawked, fury lighting in her avian gaze. "But I'm not trying to be the boss of you. I simply care—"

"The storm is not the boss of me," Dravion cut her off coldly. "I am its herald."

The moment the words left his mouth, a repulsion echoed through his spirit. A dissonance—sharp and unmistakable—between his path and what he had just said.

His body went still.

His eyes widened slowly.

And in that moment, Dravion realized the problem.

He looked up at Giselle. “I didn’t mean that.”

But that was a lie.

He had meant it. Somewhere between the creation of his path and now, he had quietly stopped seeing the storm as something above him. As something to serve. He had forgotten the core meaning of a herald. He had forgotten he was just a messenger. A servant of the storm.

He had become a proclaimer rather than a herald.

Speaking things into existence. Bending the storm to his will with his words. That wasn't the duty of a herald. It wasn't his job to call the storm or pronounce judgment.

He was a herald. A servant to the storm.

The storm wasn’t supposed to hear him. He was supposed to hear the storm. . To listen to its wishes and carry them out. To announce what the storm already intended.

A herald. A harbinger. Not a proclaimer.

And for as long as he could remember, he had gotten it entirely wrong.

He had created a path. But he had never truly walked it.

For a long moment, Dravion sat perfectly still, unable to believe how wrong he'd gotten it. Then he looked down at his own hands.

"I am a usurper," he whispered. Then he repeated it, louder this time.

He was a usurper. But no more. He understood his path now—truly understood it. He was a messenger. And messengers listened.

It was long past time he did the same.

The storm essence roiling around him stilled immediately. The lightning dancing through the air slowed. The winds circling his body grew calm—almost reverent.

Dravion inhaled slowly.

For the first time since he had created his path, he did not reach out to command the storm. He did not speak anything into existence.

Instead, he listened.

At first, there was nothing.

Then—

A whisper. Not sound. Not truly. Something deeper. Something older. A pulse of meaning carried through the currents of storm essence.

The air trembled.

Lightning flickered. Once. Twice.

And then the world fell away—dissolving like mist in the wind—to reveal the one place Dravion had been craving for three days.

The Origin Plane of Storm.

It thundered all around him—an endless expanse of roiling clouds, pouring rain, arcing lightning, and rolling thunder. The winds buffeted him harder than the last time he'd been invited in. The thunder boomed louder. The lightning blazed more blindingly.

And he floated at the center of it all.

It should have been terrifying. It was terrifying. But as the all-encompassing consciousness of the Origin Plane loomed over him, Dravion couldn't help but smile.

"Master," he whispered—barely audible against the crash of clashing thunder.

The consciousness heard him.

Dravion's smile widened, and without hesitation, he bowed his head and lowered himself to one knee—reverently, fully, without reservation.

For the first time since walking his path, he did not try to speak over the storm. Did not try to tell it what he wanted.

Instead, he waited.

He listened.

And when the consciousness spoke, the joy that erupted within him nearly split his mind in two.

He was a messenger.

And he had just received his first message.

Even the seal burning itself into his chest was an afterthought to the joy blazing within him.
