This world is a Cradle. A mere speck of existence amongst a million others—each one churning out soldiers for the great army of light. The great army of the Collective.

—Zarxan Al’ruhn.

Gordon, 2276.

The Collective was not a hidden secret.

It was not even a secret at all. It was simply a truth revealed only to those who had fulfilled the requirements of its knowledge. Zarxan himself had only learned of it after his evolution into the Calamity class.

It had come as a notification. As a title.

Congratulations! You have become a candidate of the Army of the Collective.

Ascend from this world to join its immortal ranks.

That was all.

The system had given him nothing else.

But even that little revealed more to Zarxan about the Collective than years of study ever had about AEFLYM—about this world they were trapped in.

It was a farm. A farm filled with magic and superintended by a system created for the sole purpose of producing powerful cultivators for another war—a cosmic war that seemed to have been ongoing since the beginning of time.

It was no wonder many delayed their ascension. After fighting centuries of war in AEFLYM, ascendancy felt less like an end and more like the beginning of another—only this time, an immortal one. Perhaps even an eternal war.

And so Aodhán’s mother had run, choosing to live her life on a world outside the Collective. Outside magic. Outside cultivation.

It made complete sense now, this story Aodhán couldn’t put together.

And unfortunately, it was a sad one.

Aodhán wanted answers, but what he really needed was understanding. And to understand the Collective, Zarxan needed to find out what he already knew first.

So, with a shuddering breath—aware of the magnitude of what he was about to do—Zarxan asked, “Tell me, Aodhán. What do you know of the Collective?”

The question seemed to hit Aodhán out of nowhere, and for a moment confusion painted his expression.

“I…” he began, his frown deepening. “I know very little. I’ve heard of them before though. From you, from the Watchers of the children back in Lutia, and even from Az’marthon. But I don’t know much about them.”

“What do you know?” Zarxan pressed, forcing Aodhán to think back to every single conversation he’d had concerning the matter. Every time he’d heard the term, it had been in discussions about worlds.

Az’marthon had mentioned it when he’d asked if he was still on Earth during his second awakening vision. And Zarxan had also mentioned it when they were discussing his transmigration and how worlds were millions of light-years apart.

The conclusion was simple.

“The Collective is a collection of worlds living close to each other.”

His definition seemed lacking, yet Zarxan nodded. “That’s the basics of it, yes. The Collective is a collection of worlds under a unified rule against something else. I don’t know what. But I know it is a great army filled with ascendants.”

He let out a deep breath before continuing. “I believe the Collective is a cosmic rule above our own. And when Calamities ascend, they join that rule. They join its ranks. Perhaps we can advance more. Evolve further—I don’t know. But I suspect your mother had no interest in joining another war after all the battles she had fought here on ÆFLYM.”

Slowly, very slowly, understanding dawned on Aodhán, and his eyes widened. “She fled to Earth.”

Zarxan nodded. “She fled to a world outside the Collective. But she didn’t just go there to leave a legacy, as many ascendants do to establish an anchor on unintegrated worlds. She went there to live. And for what I suspect were dozens of years, she lived in peace. Without access to essence, her power, or even her screen, she must have lived no differently from any human.”

Zarxan’s words brought tears to Aodhán’s eyes, and this time he let them fall, memories of his mother playing out in his mind. He could see it now—the freedom in her eyes, the happiness of mundanity—his mother had loved it on Earth.

She had lived. She had loved. She had created a family.

A memory of Christmas flashed in his mind—the lights, the music, the food, the warmth, the people—his mother had had it all. She’d been happy. She’d been content.

Until Az’marthon arrived.

He looked up at Zarxan and saw sympathy reflected there.

Zarxan sighed heavily, “Az’marthon ascended years after your mother. Decades, even. And that proves one thing—Az’marthon isn’t your father. But he loved your mother, and somehow he found a way to locate her across the vastness of space. Rather than join the army of the Collective, he must have fled to find her. And considering his strength and power, the Collective pursued him.”

Pain twisted within Aodhán like a burning dagger as Zarxan shed light on hazy memories.

“He must have found her,” Zarxan whispered, runic irises gleaming with sympathy. “He found you. And the agents of the Collective… they found you too.”

“They were the attackers,” Aodhán whispered, his voice heavy with pain.

Zarxan nodded. “I suspect they were. They wouldn’t have begun with violence—I doubt that. But things must have escalated somehow. Violence broke out, and your mother asked you to run.” A beat of silence passed before he added, “The rest you know.”

Aodhán stared down at his feet, his heart heavy. Tears poured down his cheeks, forming a small pool at his feet, but he didn’t care to hide his vulnerability from Zarxan. The man had taken him in as a son—the least he could do was be open.

But being open was hard. So hard that, despite all he had just learned—or perhaps because of it—Aodhán couldn’t bring himself to ask for the comfort he so desperately needed.

Still… there was one small mercy.

Az’marthon wasn’t his father.

Aodhán wasn’t sure how he would be handling all this if that had been the case. One ascendant parent was mind-blowing already. He couldn’t handle two.

For several minutes, he simply sat there, staring at the polished floor as his tears splattered against it. He remained until his chest stopped heaving. Until his tears finally dried.

Then he looked up, eyes clear. “Thank you, High Elder Zarxan, for telling me this. Thank you for shedding light.”

Zarxan studied him briefly. “I’m glad I could.”

Aodhán stood, bowed, and turned toward the exit without another word. But Zarxan couldn’t just let him go.

“Do you understand what this means, Aodhán?” he asked quietly.

Aodhán paused and turned, but said nothing.

“Do you understand, Aodhán,” Zarxan pressed, “that somewhere within the vastness of space, your mother could be alive? Breathing. And most likely waiting for you. Do you understand that?”

Aodhán looked up at him, quiet determination in his eyes.

“I do. I understand.”

The Sect of Entropy was a sect of decay and disorder.

Located within a wasteland where the world itself seemed to be dying, the sect stood—a perfect monument to slow, inevitable ruin.

Cracked pillars and weathered statues filled every corner of the mountain-sized temple. Cold winds carried dust through the ruins, and carrion beasts patrolled gray skies.

Entropy was the inevitable end of all things. And when that end came—as it seemed to be doing for the crumbling sect—its cultivators were more than happy to let it run its course rather than preserve or repair.

It was a ghastly sight for Clearfall, whose entire path revolved around order, beauty, and the symmetry of glass. But Clearfall hadn’t come here to admire the Entropy Sect or its crumbling façade.

He had come to see a man who could tilt this entire war against the Storm Sect in their favor—and the acrid scent of decay in the air couldn’t stop him.

“This is it,” he said to Aspwick, who stood silently beside him. “The moment we reach those weathered gates, there’s no going back.”

Aspwick stared at the Entropy Sect for a moment, then sighed. “We are here already. Let’s do this.”

Clearfall nodded, and with determined strides, both elders began moving toward the gates of the sect, knowing full well that its weathered appearance did not mean it was weak or defenseless.

In the last decade alone, the Sect of Entropy had fought seven Balance Wars, and though their numbers had been severely reduced, they were still a force to be reckoned with.

They reached the broken gate a few minutes later, and with a deep breath, Clearfall called out, “Evening to the Sect of Entropy. We seek an audience with one of your own. We come in peace.”

Silence.

For a moment, no one responded. Then a cold wind blew, carrying with it the stench of decay and death.

A voice echoed behind them. “Good evening, strangers.”

Of course.

Clearfall grimaced as he and Aspwick turned to see a beautiful woman with skin as pale as chalk and hair as dark as midnight. The woman wore a robe that seemed to be constantly unraveling at the seams, and for a moment—a fraction of a second—Clearfall found himself hoping that it would.

The thought passed almost as quickly as it came, and he cleared his throat. “Good evening. I am Severance Clearfall. My partner here is Herod—”

“—Aspwick,” the woman finished, a frigid gleam entering her gaze. She stared at him for a moment, then sneered. “We know who he is.”

Clearfall glanced at Aspwick, confused, and Aspwick sighed. “I have not come here for trouble.”

“And yet, trouble finds all things. It is inevitable.”

“I’d rather it doesn’t find me now,” Aspwick said, his gaze glinting dangerously. “I’m not who I once was. I no longer hold up the teachings of your sect.”

“You never held them up,” the woman challenged, a pulse of decay erupting from her. “Even as a child, you were always rebellious. And now you return as if the blood you spilled on your way out could be forgotten.”

Aspwick let out a frustrated breath. “I’m not doing this with you, Signoria. That is not why I’m here.”

“Why, then, are you here?”

“We are here to see Mordred Velkaris,” Clearfall cut in before the argument could escalate further. “We request an audience with him.”

Signoria paused, her arms stilling at her sides.

“Mordred Velkaris,” she repeated, as though she hadn’t quite heard him the first time.

Clearfall nodded. “Yes. There is an urgent matter we wish to speak to him about.”

Another gust of cold wind blew, and another voice echoed from behind them. “What do you need to speak to my dad about?”

Clearfall turned to see a boy roughly Darien’s age, though much rougher around the edges. He had midnight-black hair and pale features—but while Signoria looked beautiful, he looked more like a corpse.

His gaze landed on Aspwick, and his eyes narrowed. “I… I know you. You’re Herod—”

“Your brother,” Aspwick cut him off with a scowl. “We need to speak to your father about your brother.”

The boy’s eyes widened.

“Darien…” he breathed, then his expression twisted into a scowl. “He’s still alive?”

“Yes,” Aspwick replied patiently. “And he’s become a problem. Now lead us to him.” He glanced at Signoria. “Either of you.”

“No.” Signoria shook her head. “Rhuben, get your father. He will meet them here. I will not allow a murderer to walk into our sect.”

Aspwick’s eyes flashed, and though he said nothing, Clearfall sensed an overwhelming surge of bloodlust beneath the surface.

Herod Aspwick was angry.

And when blood cultivators got angry, death was never far behind.

***

Over an hour passed before Mordred finally arrived—a beast of a man who resembled some towering war-giant more than anything that could reasonably be called human.

He was huge, nearly eight feet tall and built like a siege engine, his frame packed with thick, corded muscles that strained against the dark fabric of his robes. His skin was light brown, shimmering faintly gold in the evening sun, and his hair was a wild tangle of gray and black that refused any attempt at discipline.

Contrary to expectation, his eyes looked kind—and perhaps many who didn’t know him well might have agreed—but Aspwick knew better. He was a friend to his eldest brother, after all, and he had seen a side of him hidden from the rest of the world.

A nastier side.

“Evening, visitors,” he called as he reached the gate, his gaze sliding over Aspwick to Clearfall before suddenly jerking back, lighting with recognition.

“Herod Aspwick!” His eyes widened, then narrowed. “What are you doing here? How dare you show your face in this sect? Have you come to grovel?”

Aspwick fought to keep his expression passive. “No, Mordred. I’m not here to grovel. I do not regret the blood I shed that day.”

“Murderer!” Signoria spat from beside the gate. “You killed your entire family and feel not even a shred of remorse. The bloodlust has truly taken you.”

But Aspwick’s gaze didn’t change. No remorse glinted in his eyes.

His family had nearly killed him for awakening a different affinity from entropy. They had blamed it on his rebellion—on his preference for order over chaos.

And perhaps they were right.

The creation of sects had helped foster the awakening of specific affinities. There was a ninety percent chance that a cultivator born into the Blood Sect would awaken the blood affinity.

But ninety percent wasn’t certainty.

There were outliers. He had been one of them.

And after months of abuse from his parents, he had packed his bags, killed his family in their sleep, and simply left.

He did not regret it.

And that certainty shone brightly in his eyes as he stared back at Mordred and Signoria—the former a family friend, the latter a childhood friend.

He regretted nothing.

Mordred studied him briefly, then sneered. “What do you want?”

Clearfall decided it was a good time to step in. “We want to talk to you about your second son, Darien Velkaris.”

Mordred stilled, his eyes narrowing. “What about him?”

Clearfall exchanged a glance with Aspwick before continuing. “We don’t know if you’re aware, but he has become a double inheritor since leaving the Entropy Sect and is now a member of the Storm Sect.”

“What?” Rhuben exclaimed, all of this clearly news to him. His father, however, didn’t seem nearly as surprised.

He had been keeping tabs on Darien.

His jaw worked silently for a moment, the scowl on his face deepening as he spoke. “I know. What about it?”

“What?” Rhuben exclaimed again, shock plain on his features. “Father, you said—”

“Quiet, Rhuben,” Mordred snapped, his voice like a whip. “I know what I said.” He glanced back at Clearfall, his scowl deepening further. “Now, what do you want?”

“We are fighting a Balance War against the Storm Sect,” Aspwick took over, his voice gaining a dangerous edge. “But they’ve pulled their transmigrants from the front lines. We want to force them out—and we thought you could help.”

Mordred sneered. “You want me to pretend I am captive so you can kill my son.”

“He’s not your son, father,” Rhuben spat, anger flashing in his eyes. “You disowned—”

A crack split the air, and an instant later, Rhuben was sent flying before he could finish. The boy slammed against a weathered pillar, and another snap echoed.

A bone had cracked.

Aspwick felt anger boil up within him at the sight, but he forced himself to keep his gaze on Mordred. Forced himself to look unaffected.

He didn’t quite succeed.

“Will you be helping us or not?” he asked, his voice thick with restrained fury.

Mordred smiled. “No. I will not help you kill my son. You have wasted your time here. Now return to whence you came.”

He spat at Aspwick’s feet and, without another word, turned and headed back into the sect.

Aspwick watched him go, hatred and resentment boiling within him.

“Let’s go, Aspwick,” Clearfall said quietly, his gaze still on Mordred’s retreating back. “It didn’t work. We’ll find another way.”

Aspwick didn’t respond. He simply turned and began walking away. However, they had barely taken more than a few steps when an agonized voice cried out.

“Take me.”

They turned. Signoria turned. Mordred turned. But Rhuben had already made up his mind, his eyes burning with white-hot fury.

“Take me,” he repeated, his voice a growl as he stumbled to his feet. “I will help you kill my brother.”

“You will not dare!” Mordred thundered, eyes blazing with rage. “If you step out of that gate, you will no longer be my son.”

Rhuben stared at his father, and for a moment Aspwick thought he might stay.

Then his gaze hardened. A sneer touched his lips.

“Being your son has been hell.” He walked out of the gate, his head high. “I’ll take my chances with the outside world.”

“No, you will not.” Mordred sneered, a loud CRACK echoing as the earth beneath his feet exploded in an overwhelming wave of entropy.

“You will not betray your brother.”

The words hung in the air like a death sentence, and Aspwick sighed.

Slowly, reluctantly, he shifted away from Rhuben. On the other side, Clearfall did the same.

Rhuben froze, staring at them in shock, betrayal written plainly across his face.

“No… don’t leave me,” he whispered, his eyes wide as they abandoned him to the mercy of his father.

But as much as Aspwick hated it, he knew there was no other way.

They couldn’t fight Mordred here, in the heart of his Sect.. It would be the two of them against the entire Entropy Sect—and that was not a battle they could win. Besides, the plan had already failed.

Mordred would undoubtedly warn the Storm Sect of their visit, of their intentions. Taking Rhuben with them was no longer of any use.

Aspwick closed his eyes for a brief moment. The decision tasted like ash—but it was the only one left.

“We’re leaving,” Aspwick said, voice flat. He turned to Clearfall. “Now.”

Slowly, they backed away from Rhuben just as his father approached, blazing fury in his eyes.

He would beat him—he would beat him terribly. But Aspwick knew he couldn’t save him.

Only the boy could save himself.
