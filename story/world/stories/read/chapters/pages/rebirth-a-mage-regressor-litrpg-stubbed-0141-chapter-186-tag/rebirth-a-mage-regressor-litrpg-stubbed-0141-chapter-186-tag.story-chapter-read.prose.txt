The tap landed on Peregrine’s shoulder, and Adom was three streets gone before the word finished leaving his mouth.

This was the part he was good at. He'd been the Ghost of Arkhos for years now, and the rooftops of the old quarter knew his feet. He went up a drainpipe, over a gable, along a spine of slate with the city opening gold and blue below him, the last sun on the western towers and the lamps coming up in the streets one by one.

He gave it twenty seconds, then looked back to see how his protector was managing.

Peregrine was right behind him.

Not on the roofs. On the street, flat out, running the ground as fast as he ran the sky.

Faster than a horse over a short distance, she'd said, and she hadn't been bragging. She kept level with a man going over the rooftops, and showed no sign it cost her anything.

Fine. Adom grinned and opened up.

He cut inland, into the knot of the old district where the alleys turn back on themselves and a stranger loses herself in three corners. He took them at a sprint, doubled once, dropped through a covered passage he knew and out the far end into a quiet square with a fountain running, certain he'd shaken her.

She was sitting on the fountain's edge.

So Adom stopped.

There'd been no way to get ahead of him. He'd taken the only fast line and she'd been behind it. Yet here she was in front, and not even breathing hard. Then he saw how. She stood, stepped to the long shadow the fountain threw across the cobbles, and went into it, sinking out of the world between one stride and the next, and gone.

She came up out of the dark under the archway across the square, twenty feet on, already moving.

Oh, Adom thought. That's not fair at all.

It was a spell, and a cruel one. She dropped out of one shadow and rose from another, and at dusk, with the sun dying and the whole city throwing long blue dark off every wall and eave and lamp-post, there was no end of shadow for her to use.

At this rate, she would undoubtedly catch up.

"Whoa!"

He suddenly felt the air move where her hand had closed.

It had been a long time since anything in this city came that near to laying hands on him, and the old, old part of him underneath did not enjoy the reminder that the world still kept things quicker than he was.

And she was getting quicker. Every time she surfaced she was half a stride closer than the last, and the half-strides were running out.

So Adom hunted for the seam, because everything had one.

He found it on the next pass. She erupted from the shadow of a chimney dead ahead — where he'd have been, had he held his line — and closed on empty air, because at the last instant he'd cut the other way. She landed, turned, found him again.

But he had it now. She came out where he was going, not where he was. She couldn't see while she traveled the dark. She had to guess his line before she went under, and rise where the guess sent her.

Which meant the answer wasn't to be faster.

It was to be somewhere she hadn't guessed.

Huh, that was useful information, Adom supposed. If that was her best card, then he decided he was done running.

He stopped in the middle of the next square and turned to face her. Peregrine rose out of a doorway's dark not ten feet off, already set to take him.

Adom smiled and raised a hand. "Catch me if you can!"

Then he wove the smoke: a single thick grey billow that boiled up and swallowed him whole. A magician's flourish, the kind that ends on an empty stage and a gasp. Poof. Gone.

Which was the trick, and the trick was old as the trade. The smoke was what she'd watch and where she'd strike. And while every sense she owned stayed nailed to the spot where Adom had so grandly stopped existing, the latter was already a street away and walking, out of an alley she'd had no reason to mind.

Misdirection. The oldest trick a street conjuror owned, and it worked on Shadows the same as on anyone.

He gave it a few minutes before she'd tear through the last of the haze and grasp she'd been guarding a cloud. And about twenty minutes for her to search for him all over the city. Time and to spare.

Adom went down toward the Dregs.

Valiant kept about twenty hideouts spread through the quarter and moved between them on a schedule that, from the outside, looked like no schedule at all.

There was good reason for it. Not every gang had gone straight, and a spymaster makes enemies whether he wants to or not. Valiant himself was relatively safe; everyone down here knew he was affiliated with Adom, and nobody sane wanted a magus knocking on their door over him.

So he was mostly left alone. Old grudges still floated around: the last of the Children of the Moon, and a few others who'd hated his late uncle Cisco, and people who carry a grudge that long don't always stop to check whose door they're kicking in. So, twenty houses, and a schedule only two people knew the pattern of, and Adom was one of them.

He'd gone three streets when a hand caught his sleeve.

Adom turned fast, weight already shifting, the old reflex reaching up out of habit — here it comes, the purse or the teeth.

He stopped it a hand's width from the man's nose.

"Whoa! Whoa, whoa—" The man rocked back, both palms up, a fan of paper flapping in one of them, grinning even as he flinched. "Shit! Okay, that's on me, that's fair, I grabbed you, I shouldn't grab people like that, my bad, completely my bad." He laughed and stuck out the hand that wasn't full of paper. "Luis. I'm Luis. Sorry. Hi."

Adom lowered the fist, feeling like an idiot. "...Hi."

"Here," Luis peeled a sheet off his stack and pressed it into Adom's hand. A flyer, the ink still a little smudged. "Everybody's getting one. It’s for my inn. The Copper Pot, end of next month, big opening, food and drink and all of it. Whole street's invited. You should come!"

"Oh, uh, thank you."

Then he looked at Adom properly, and something lit up all over his face. "Wait. Wait — you're him. The Ghost. You're the one who cleaned all this up."

"I just—"

He suddenly seized Adom's hand and shook it in both of his, flyers and all. "Okay, now you gotta come. You especially. First one in my whole damn family to own an inn, you know that? First to own anything that wasn't stolen off somebody first."

He laughed, delighted with himself. "Second one not to be a crook, too. And trust me, in my family that's a low bar. So, big month coming. Big month!"

Adom couldn't help smiling. "That's really something. Your parents must be proud."

"Oh, they're dead."

He said it lightly, still beaming.

"I'm sorry to hear that," Adom said.

"Eh." Luis waved the flyers. "My old man, he was the criminal; real nasty piece of work. Killed my mother right in front of me when I was small, like this high—" a cheerful hand at hip height "—and then he goes and gets himself killed too, later, some gang thing, cut him up good. So." A little shrug. "Just me now. But it's good, you know? You keep going. And now I got an inn."

Adom had stopped thinking about the inn entirely.

"Are..." He searched the beaming face for some crack that wasn't there. "Are you okay?"

"Me? Oh, yeah, yeah." Another wave of the stack. "Long time ago. So, end of the month, yeah? First drink's free for everybody. Second one's free for you. Bring your friends. You got friends, right?"

"...Some."

"Bring 'em!" He clapped Adom on the shoulder, already turning to the next person down the row with a flyer out. "The Copper Pot! You come, yeah?"

"I'll come," Adom said, and found he meant it.

"Good man. Good man." And off he went, pressing paper on a startled fishwife, whistling between sentences.

Adom looked down at the smudged flyer in his hand.

Then he walked on, turning it over.

Today's house was a tall, narrow place wedged between a cobbler's and a shuttered bakehouse, with a green door that badly wanted paint.

Adom knocked; and not just any knock. He rapped out the opening of "The Cooper's Daughter," that brainless little tavern tune half the city had been unable to stop humming for two summers running, da-da-da-DA-da, and finished on the two hard beats at the end, the ones the whole alehouse bellows her name on.

It was a stupid knock. Valiant had invented it himself, years back, and made Adom swear to use that one and only that one, so he'd always know it was him at the door.

A dog started barking inside. Then a voice came through the wood, pitched low and gravelly, working very hard to sound like something you would not want to meet in an alley.

"...Who's there?"

Adom looked at the door.

"Valiant. You made me knock a special way so you'd know it was me. " He waited. "So who do you think it is?"

A pause.

"Oh." The voice dropped the whole act and snapped back to its usual quick chirp. "Right. Yeah. I forgot I did that. Hang on!"

Then all he could hear were locks being opened. Several of them, more than a door that size had any business carrying. Then it swung open and a dog shot out of the gap.

"Otis!"

Adom dropped to one knee and caught the wriggling, wagging, full-body mess of him — a big brindle mutt, all elbows and tongue. The last time Adom had seen this dog, he'd been a sorry thing: every rib showing, one ear chewed half off, flinching at any hand that moved too fast. None of that now. Now he was fat, glossy and certain the whole world had turned up at this door specifically to love him.

"Look at you," Adom said, scrubbing both hands through the thick fur. "Look at you. You look incredible. Who's eating well, huh? Who's the most handsome dog in the Dregs?"

Otis had no idea, but was thrilled to hear it.

"He likes you better than me," Valiant said from the doorway. "I feed him, I scratch him, I tell him every day he's handsome, and you show up once a month and he loses his entire mind."

Adom looked up, and the grin slipped a little.

"...You don't look so good."

Valiant was a small mouse, white all over, and most days he gave off the sense that there was twice as much of him as the size allowed, all of it going at once. Tonight it had run down. The fur under his eyes was dull, and the constant motion of him had slowed to almost nothing.

He waved a small paw. "Eh. I'm fine. I'm — okay, I'm not fine."

He rubbed his whole face with both paws. "It's like — okay. There's a sleep in you, right? One specific sleep, the one you actually need. And every night you lie down and you sleep — you really do, you sleep fine — but it's never that sleep. It's some other, lesser sleep. And you wake up and the real one's still sitting in there. Unslept. Just... waiting."

He flapped both paws in the air. "Every single night. It's driving me out of my tiny mind. You get it? Tell me you get it."

Adom opened his mouth to say no.

"...Actually," he said. "Yeah. Yeah, I get it."

And that bothered him, a little.

He scratched Otis behind the ear and decided not to think about it.

Still, Valiant lit up as little forks of current crackled to life along his whiskers, standing them straight out from his face the way they always did when he got going.

"You get it," he said. "You actually get it? I knew you would! Everybody else, I tell them and they look at me like I've finally cracked, but you, see, you get it, this is exactly why—"

"Can I come in?"

"Oh. Yeah. Yeah, come in."

He stood aside, current still fizzing off his whiskers, and Adom ducked under the low frame into a cramped front room that smelled of dog and lamp oil and old paper. Otis padded in after them and claimed the spot by the cold hearth.

Adom didn't sit. "Castle White," he said. "Where are we with it?"

Valiant blinked at the change of gear, then waved him toward the one good chair. "Okay, so, funny thing, I actually think that's why I haven't had The Sleep, right, because every time I shut my eyes my brain just starts running the staff rotations, who covers which wing, who I trust, who I half-trust, and there's this one laundry maid I cannot get a clean read on, she's either nothing or she's the whole game, and that kind of thing it just sits up there and spins and spins and spins until—"

"Valiant. Please. Focus."

"Right. Sorry." He gave himself a shake, whiskers throwing a faint spark. "I mean. We're in. Got someone onto the staff weeks ago, clean placement, nobody blinked. So we're inside. We're just still working it. Mapping the rounds, the timings and all that. It's delicate. We're being careful."

"What's the soonest it can be done?" Adom kept his voice level. "Say it's urgent. Say we have to move as fast as it possibly can be. Give me the closest date."

Valiant chewed on it. "Tomorrow, I guess? If I had to. We're in tight enough for it. I just don't love it, you know? I like a clean job. No loose ends."

He scratched behind one ear. "Uncle Cisco always told me..." He dropped his voice down into a rumble far close to Cisco’s, doing the old mouse surprisingly well: "Lemme tell you somethin', kid. The job that kills you, eh? It's never the one you didn't plan for. It's the loose end. The one you saw. You looked right at it, and you thought, ehh. Leave it."

He chuckled, sliding back up to his own register. "Said it to me a hundred times. One of the last things he ever told me, more or less. Before, you know." There was a pause. "Aah. I miss him."

He sat with that a moment, gone somewhere else, and Adom let him have it.

When Valiant was done, Adom asked, "And you're sure? Tomorrow, if it came to it?"

"Yeah?" Valiant said. "I think so. Yeah."

"Good. Because we've got a problem." Adom sat at last, forward, elbows on his knees. "And I think it means we go this week. Next at the latest. Not later than that."

Valiant's whiskers went still. "What kind of problem?"

"A shadow one."
