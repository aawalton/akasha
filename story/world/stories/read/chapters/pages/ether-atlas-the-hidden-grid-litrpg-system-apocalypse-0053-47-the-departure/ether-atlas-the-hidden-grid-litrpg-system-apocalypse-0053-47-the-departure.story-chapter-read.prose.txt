Chapter 47

“The party that enters is never the party that leaves. Marseille took three people with a shared hobby and returned three people with a shared purpose. The difference is load-bearing.” - The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr, Marseille

They told Nadia about their plans to leave that evening.

Nadia listened with her arms folded, leaned over the compound’s main table, the same posture she’d held when they’d first walked in off the quay. Arthur laid it out: the transit chamber beneath Cosquer, the passage to a new destination, the Grid pulling south across the Mediterranean. They were leaving in the morning.

Nadia nodded once. “I knew you were passing through, you said as much the afternoon you came to the docks.”

“We didn’t know how we were passing through,” Arthur said. “Now we do.”

He reached into his pack and set one of the beetle cores on the table. Small and dark, the chitinous shell catching the lamplight. The armour properties were visible in the grain: dense, overlapping structures that echoed the beetles’ plating.

“One core,” he said. “You’ve seen how binding works. Vessel, core, intent. Rémy’s hatchet, Cédric’s knife, whatever you think needs the upgrade most.”

Nadia picked up the core and turned it in her fingers, weighing it like she’d weighed everything else they’d brought her.

Nadia set the core down carefully. “You’ve left us the knowledge. That’s worth more than one crystal.”

* * *

The Gardiens packed them for the road with remarkable generosity.

Maryam contributed a medical kit. Bandages, compression tapes and a suture kit in a canvas roll. She put the kit in Steve’s hands and held on for a moment longer than necessary. “Take care of your palms. The burns are healed but the skin is new. It will tear.”

Cédric produced food: dried boar and fish, tinned vegetables and salt. “Four days,” he said. “If you’re not somewhere with food in four days, you’ve got bigger problems than my provisions.”

Rémy gave them rope. Thirty metres of dock line, braided and rated for heavy load. He didn’t explain why. Arthur took it because Rémy was a légionnaire and légionnaires didn’t give you rope without reason.

Steve chuckled from behind. “Never go dungeon diving without rope.” Rémy glared at him.

Léonie came as well to bid them farewell.

“The tunnel creatures will repopulate,” she said to Arthur. “Within a week. Maybe less. If anyone else needs to reach that cave, they’ll have to fight through again.”

“They won’t need to,” Arthur said. “The transit chamber has a surface exit. The cliff face at Morgiou.”

Léonie filed this. “I’ll mark it.”

* * *

They ate on the quay that night. Just the three of them.

The violet had deepened over the days they’d been in Marseille. It was richer, more layered, with threads of colour he didn’t have names for woven through the familiar bruise. The stars were still invisible behind the Fence. He missed stars.

Cédric’s dried boar was tough but better than protein bars. Donna had found a bottle of red in one of the warehouse crates, Bandol, 2019, the kind of wine that wouldn’t impress anyone but wouldn’t disappoint either. They passed it between them.

Steve was writing in the ledger Father Matthieu had given him. The pencil moved in the firelight, steady and precise. He’d filled nine pages since the basilica. Field notes, creature observations, technique analyses, the Ether-forging discovery, the underwater combat framework. A record of everything they’d learned.

Arthur watched the harbour. The water carried its faint Ether glow, lapping against stone that had been a dock for two thousand years. Somewhere beneath them, Roman foundations held the weight of everything that had been built since. He thought about that. About things built to last longer than the people who built them.

He looked across the fire at Steve and Donna.

“Maybe it’s too late to ask,” he said. “But why did the two of you come?”

Steve looked up from the ledger. “What do you mean? I didn’t decide anything. I was teleported. One moment I was in a chamber under Temple Church, the next I was in a box in Marseille.”

“I don’t mean Marseille. I mean with me. To wherever this is leading.” Arthur turned the wine bottle in his hands. “You could have found safety. Barricaded somewhere. Found a group. Steve, you could have stayed with Mrs Hadley and read books until the world sorted itself out.” Arthur looked at Donna. “You could have gone back for Emma and stayed with her. Protected her. Built a wall and waited.”

The fire cracked, and the harbour was quiet beyond it.

Steve closed the ledger and set the pencil in the spine. When he spoke, the manic energy was gone. Just Steve, talking to the two people he trusted most in a world that had stopped making sense.

“I’m a scientist,” he said. “And this is the biggest thing to happen in the history of our species. Not in my lifetime. In the history of the world. An alien intelligence rewrote human biology, embedded a progression system in our cells, and left a trail of infrastructure across the planet for someone to activate. There is no version of me that sits in a library and reads about this secondhand.”

He picked up the wine bottle and drank. “Besides, you two are the kind of people it makes sense to be around during the apocalypse. Arthur builds things that don’t fall down. Donna makes sure the things trying to knock them down don’t get close. I explain why both of those things are happening. We’re a functioning system.”

Arthur looked at Donna.

She was leaning back against a bollard, the Conduit-Edge Axe across her knees, the firelight catching the Refined edge. She’d been quiet since they sat down. Donna was always quiet when the conversation turned inward; not because she had nothing to say, but because the things she had to say cost her something she budgeted carefully.

“I’ve never been the one to sit still,” she said. “Yes, I could have gone back for Emma. I could have found her, barricaded, waited for someone else to figure it out.” She turned the axe on her knees. “But I need to know what’s happening. I need to understand how to stop it. Sitting behind a wall while the world changes around me isn’t protection. It’s a slower way to lose.”

She looked at Arthur. “And if I stayed back, who would protect your arses?”

Arthur laughed.

Steve picked up the wine. “There’s also the fundamental tactical principle.”

“Which is?” Arthur said.

“You never split the party.” Steve drank. Passed the bottle to Donna. “That is D&D 101. The moment the party splits, the encounter math falls apart. ‘Maybe we should split the party’ is almost always followed by a total party kill.”

Donna took the bottle and drank. Arthur caught the faintest trace of a smile, the kind she rationed like ammunition.

“…and you never split the party,” she said.

Arthur looked at the two of them. The archaeologist with his ledger and his patterns and his Constitution of 10. The soldier with her axe and her predictions and her refusal to sit still while the world burned. Two people he’d known for years around a table rolling dice, and two people he knew would have his back now that the dice were reality.

He took the bottle back and drank the last of it.

“You never split the party,” he said.

* * *

Father Matthieu found them packing in the morning. He pressed a hand to each of their foreheads. He said something in French that Steve translated later as a benediction for travellers, though Steve admitted his liturgical French was approximate at best.

“The witnesses go where the testimony leads,” the priest said. He was looking at Steve’s ledger. “Write what you see, Monsieur Parr.”

“I will, Père.”

Rémy shook Arthur’s hand at the compound gate. The handshake lasted three seconds, one soldier recognising another. No words. None were needed.

Nadia walked them to the harbour’s edge through a still morning. Fishing boats sat motionless on water that carried its Ether luminescence even in daylight.

“The binding,” she said. “We’ll use it. Cédric has the process documented in triplicate.”

“I’m not even a little bit surprised,” Steve said.

Arthur looked at the harbourmaster on a dock she’d run for longer than any of them had been alive, holding a community together with coffee, pragmatism, and a ledger that tracked everything from ammunition to alien crystals.

“You’ll be alright,” he said.

“We were alright before you arrived. We’ll be alright after you leave.” She paused. “But the cores will help.”

“Find us, when you are done. You will always be welcomed here.”

* * *

The walk to Cap Morgiou took two hours.

They followed the coast road south, packs heavy with Cédric’s provisions, Maryam’s medical kit, and Rémy’s rope that Arthur suspected would save someone’s life before too long. The Calanques rose on their left, majestic white limestone cliffs carved over millennia.

“The erosion patterns on the eastern face are consistent with sustained wave action over approximately nine thousand years,” Archie said, unprompted. “The limestone has held on adequately.”

“High praise,” Steve said.

“It was not praise. It was a geological observation. Your species’ persistent interpretation of quantitative statements as qualitative judgements remains a processing anomaly I have not resolved.”

“He likes the cliffs,” Donna translated.

Arthur found the cave entrance and led them through worked stone into the transit chamber beneath Cosquer Cave.

The platform waited at the centre of the chamber, its concentric rings cut into dark translucent stone. The same architecture that had taken them from Temple Church to Marseille.

Arthur looked at the chamber one last time. The stairs leading up to the painted hall. The Seeder notation on the walls. Three nodes activated. A city of survivors who now had cores and knowledge and a woman who would make sure both were used.

Marseille had been good to them.

The party stepped on to the humming platform and then the light took them.
