Chapter 118

He rode out at first light.

Riley had wanted the report before the second night and Thorne had not argued the timing. He took one of the faster horses out of the stable and one day's rations in the saddlebag and a light spear across the back of the saddle, and he rode out through the inner gate alone.

He held the pace steady through the woods.

The forest canopy held the light off the path for the first hour and he moved through without pushing the horse. Travelling to Hoshin was familiar to him, it let his mind wander about the recent events he shared with Zelgra. He thought he might have made a mistake not sharing his true feelings about Zelgra’s potential with Riley, but the moment didn’t seem right nor did the audience. If Riley did not agree then saying that in front of Zelgra wouldn’t be wise. She was making meaningful contributions to the community and Throne wanted that to continue.

He reached the Hoshin village by mid-morning. He did not enter the ruins; he didn’t see a point – this was the past now. He turned the horse east, off the Hoshin path, onto the line of country the prisoners had given.

The distance from Hoshin was long enough that it was work for the horse and would be real work on foot. A column of foot soldiers would need a good part of a day, moving in order, and the tree line along the road would offer cover to stop and camp inside the trees if the country ahead did not allow it.

He filed that.

He rode on until the road bent again and the ground began to open. Then he slowed the horse to a walk. He rounded the bend. The depot stood in the distance where the road came out of the trees onto an open flat. He backed the horse before he was seen. After tying off the horse in several steps into the tree line Thorne continued on foot. He moved slowly.

The tree line ran alongside the road at a distance and then closed toward it as the road curved back toward the depot from the east. Where the road passed in front of the south gate, the tree line stood a good stone's throw back from the road on the opposite side. That was the cover he would use. He walked in under the trees at a pace that did not disturb the ground and brought himself to a place inside the tree line where he could see the main gate of the depot clearly.

He crouched and waited.

The depot have four walls with the main gate on the south facing wall. Four guard towers stood at the four corners, each of them a tall wooden platform on a set of squared posts with a shallow roof and a rail around it. The walls between the towers were of stockade timber, the height of two men laid end to end, and along the top of the wall ran a walkway low enough that a man crouched down could hide his head behind the wall for cover. The road came in from the west, ran past the south gate at a distance of forty paces, and continued off to the north-east until it bent north somewhere out of sight.

The tree line held cover in two places. It held cover in front of the south gate where he now was, a good throw back from the road, and it held cover along the east side of the depot where the road came in from that direction. On the east side the tree line hugged the road closely, close enough that a man on foot could reach the road out of the trees in three strides.

He watched the guards.

There was one guard on each of the four walls, walking the length between the towers at an unhurried pace, and there was one archer visible in each of the four towers. He could see the four archers because their heads and shoulders showed above the rail against the sky. He could not see behind the walls from where he crouched. The gate was solid.

He looked at the guard walking the south wall. The man was thick through the chest and heavy in the shoulder, and his gait was steady. His helmet sat properly on his head. His weapon rode on his back, not slung loose the way a raider slung a weapon. His movement was the movement of a soldier who had been drilled to walk this wall a hundred times and would walk it a hundred more without changing the length of his stride.

Thorne's vision flickered.

Level Four. Not a raider. Not the loose men Riley's forces had cut through. These Clawborn were drilled and standing straight in dry armour on a wall in the middle of the country they controlled. It would take more to put one of these down than it took to put down a raider.

He counted again.

Four towers. One archer each. Four walking guards, one to a wall. Eight men outside where he could see them. He tried to estimated the yard, without being able to see into it. Storehouses had to hold men to work them. A gate this size had to have men to open it. Rotation of the guard would need a reserve. He put the whole garrison somewhere in the neighbourhood of thirty, give or take.

He filed thirty.

He looked at the gate itself.

The gate was made of solid planks squeezed together so he could not see through it. The wood of the gate faced south. The south face of a piece of timber was the face the sun beat on longest through the day and through the year, and this timber had been on this gate long enough that the sun had done its work. It had gone grey. The grain had opened. The face of it looked dry.

Dry wood burned.

There was no water source he could see. No stream ran past the depot on the visible sides. No pond sat outside the walls. Whatever water they used they would be drawing from a well inside the yard, and a well fed one bucket at a time and buckets did not put out a burning wall. If he set this gate alight and the walls after it, the men inside would have a real problem. He wondered what their fire drill looked like. He wondered whether they had one at all.

The depot was not heavily guarded. It should have been heavier. A supply depot on the road between the Clawborn kingdom and the country south of it should have carried two companies at least, not one, and it should have carried a mounted patrol running the road outside the walls at a proper interval. It carried neither. The Clawborn were leaning on the rule of the north and on the reputation of what they did to people who tried them, and they were letting the fear do the work a second company would have done. That was a choice.

He was still working through what he had when the sound came down the road from the east.

He heard the wagon before he saw it.

It came at the slow steady pace of a heavy wagon on hard road, the wheels running clean on the packed dirt without the rattle of a lighter cart, the hooves of the draft team coming in a four-beat. He held where he was. He did not shift his weight forward for a better look. He waited for the wagon to come to him.

It came around the last bend and into the open.

It was a large wagon.

Two soldiers rode up front on the seat. One held the reins. One sat with a short bow across his knees, an arrow already nocked and held low. On either side of the wagon a mounted soldier rode with the wheels, one to each side, both with bows out and arrows held ready across the pommel of the saddle. The wagon was long and heavy and roofed in dark canvas, and its sides showed the scarring of long roads and heavy loads. There was no arrow strike anywhere on it. No axe. This wagon had never been fought. It had run this road so many times without challenge that whoever loaded it had stopped expecting a challenge.

He read the four riders.

❖ Four Clawborn Archers Level 4

Four Level Fours on the escort. Two of them archers on the seat. Two of them archers to the flank.

The wagon came in toward the south gate.

Now the archers in the towers came up.

They had been sitting low against the rail until this point. Now the head and shoulders of three of them came up in each of the four towers, twelve archers in all, bows drawn or nocked, arrows trained on the wagon.

Twelve.

He had counted four. There were twelve.

A voice called from the wall above the gate. It was a short call, then the gate opened.

The wagon creaked and came in slowly. Two men on the inside working the hinges, he judged, from the pace of it. As it opened, eight infantry came out through the gate in a loose fan, four to a side, weapons drawn, and they took up positions around the wagon in a formation they had clearly done before. They did not talk. They did not look at each other for their positions. They knew them.

One of them dropped to a knee.

He put his cheek near the ground and looked underneath the wagon along its full length. He did not find whatever he was looking for. He stood.

He called something short to the wall and a moment later the wagon moved.

It rolled forward through the gate at the slow walk, and the infantry moved with it, the four on either side keeping station on the wheels. As the wagon passed the arch Thorne saw for the first time into the yard.

He saw two long storehouses on the north side of the yard, timber and shingle, larger than the store buildings at the settlement. He saw a smaller hut set against the west wall, low-roofed, plain, the sort of building that would be guard quarters. He saw ground the colour of the packed dirt outside the gate, worn smooth where feet had crossed it many times.

The wagon moved towards the gate and the infantry began to draw back through the gate with it. They did not turn as they came back through. They walked backwards through the gate, holding the fan they had come out in, weapons still up and still facing outward, until the last of them cleared the arch. Then the gate began to close. It closed at the same pace it had opened. When it was closed, the extra archers in the towers went back down behind the rail, and only one head to a tower showed again above the wood.

He watched to see if anything changed once the gate had shut. The whole operation went back to the way it had been before the wagon had arrived.

He rocked back onto his heels and processed what he had saw.

There were trained guards on a half day rotation. There was cover in two places. In front of the south gate at a stone's throw. Along the east side of the road where the trees came in close. There was open field to the north and west.

The gate was dry with wood that would burn easily. Once the wood caught the wall would follow. A garrison of thirty on a bucket line would not put out wild fire.

He wondered what their fire drill looked like and whether they had ever had to run one.

He rose slowly out of the crouch, and he moved back through the trees at the same pace he had come from. He walked without breaking a stick. He came to the birch where the horse stood and untied him and mounted.

He turned the horse west and disappeared further into the trees.
