Sunlight filters through the canopy overhead, staining the forest in the soft, fading hues of dusk.

Gold spills across broad leaves and tangled roots, catching on drifting motes of pollen and dust. The air is warm and still, carrying the earthy scent of moss and sap, the forest breathing out after a long day.

Somewhere far off, something chirps. Somewhere closer, something rustles, then settles again. It’s the kind of quiet that feels earned, the kind that invites you to slow down, to exist in it for a moment.

I take a breath.

Then something tries to stab me in the spine.

I dodge by less than a finger’s width.

I feel the air tear open behind me—feel the exact moment where my back was supposed to stop being a long-term investment—and my body twists on reflex, boots skidding through leaf-choked dirt as claws rake nothing but space.

Before I can even consider counterattacking, it’s gone, vanishing back into the canopy like it was never there.

…Rude.

It reappears above me, landing sideways on a tree trunk without a sound.

That’s the first red flag.

No snap of bark. No rustle of leaves. No displaced debris. Just presence, sudden and profoundly incorrect, like the forest collectively forgot to file a noise complaint.

The creature clings to the trunk at an angle that makes my spine itch in sympathy. Too many joints. Not in a body-horror way. Worse. In a designed-by-an-engineer way. Every limb bends exactly as far as it needs to, no more, no less, like comfort was listed under “optional features” and then immediately removed.

It’s roughly wolf-sized, maybe a bit longer, but far leaner, with tight muscles that look coiled rather than bulky. Its body is flattened slightly, chest narrow, abdomen tapered, built for slipping through space instead of forcing it open.

Its hide is bark rather than fur.

Not rough or cracked, but instead smooth and dark, layered in overlapping plates that resemble living wood polished by centuries of wind. Thin veins of muted green pulse faintly beneath the surface, syncing with its breathing in a way that makes me uncomfortable.

Its head tilts.

The face is wrong.

Too narrow. Too still. No visible mouth. Right up until it opens one, splitting vertically down the center of its face, lined with pale, hook-shaped teeth that don’t look designed for chewing so much as latching onto mistakes.

And its eyes—

Small and deep set, almost invisible on first glance. And they are very much not watching me.

They’re watching my movement.

Every shift of weight. Every breath. Every unfinished step.

The tail uncoils behind it—long, segmented, ending in a thin, leaf-bladed fin that sways once, twice—

Not impatient.

Measuring.

The forest doesn’t react to it.

No birds scatter. No insects flee. Even the leaves around it are still.

Like everything nearby has learned the correct response is to not be noticed.

My tail flicks, equal parts irritation and professional offense.

“…Of course you live up there.”

The creature doesn’t respond.

It simply lets go.

It drops.

I pivot instantly, blade already moving to greet it where it should land—

—and it isn’t there.

Something brushes past my shoulder instead, close enough that my hair stirs, claws slicing air behind me like it’s correcting a typo I made.

…Excuse you?

I skid to a stop, heart kicking up a notch as bark chips rain down from the trunk it actually lands on, a full step farther than physics says is polite.

Okay.

So it’s not falling to attack.

It’s falling to reposition.

Annoying.

Inspect fires.

Deepwood Canopy Stalker (Level 12 | Tier 1)

Species: Deepwood Flora

Aspect: Tempo

A carnivorous plant predator native to the Deepwood. Utilizes exceptional speed and stealth to ambush prey from the canopy.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

I pause.

…A plant monster?

Huh.

So my long-held assumption that plant monsters exist exclusively to jump-scare inattentive wanderers was, apparently, optimistic.

Honestly?

Good.

That’s way more interesting.

I look back up at the bark-covered attitude problem clinging to the tree above me and narrow my eyes.

Alright then.

What should I do now?

I already know the answer.

Stab it.

Repeatedly.

The real question is how.

I’m faster than it. With gravity and a little enthusiasm, I could absolutely chase it through the canopy like a flaming fox-shaped missile powered by pure spite and questionable decision-making.

But that would violate my completely reasonable, entirely self-imposed challenge of “Minimal star powers until my swordplay skill improves.”

A challenge I just made up fifteen seconds ago.

Unacceptable.

As the universe’s greatest—and most humble—prodigy, even contemplating failure feels like a personal attack on my dignity.

…

My ear flicks impatiently.

The monster still hasn’t moved.

Of course.

Here I am, openly standing beneath it, mid–internal monologue, practically begging to be attacked, and it refuses to take the hint and try to rip my throat out.

Honestly.

Some predators have no sense of showmanship at all.

I stare at it.

It stares at me.

Still unmoving.

I sigh, loudly, then turn on my heel and start walking away.

And—of course—that’s when it attacks.

A grin snaps onto my face as I spin back around, blade whispering through the air toward where it should be—

—and it isn’t.

My gravity sense screams a warning and I Astral Step sideways a heartbeat before claws rake through the space my shoulder occupied moments ago.

Close.

I Dash after it immediately, refusing to let it escape back into the canopy again. My sword flashes toward its neck—

—but it slips aside by the barest margin, rewarding me with nothing but a chunk of bark flaking free.

Its tail whips around in a wide, gleaming arc toward my throat. I glide backward just enough to avoid it—

—which, in hindsight, is a mistake.

The creature uses the space instantly, springing back into the air and latching onto another trunk with effortless precision.

And just like that—

—we’re back where we started.

I click my tongue.

I very briefly consider burning the entire forest down in retaliation.

But no.

I refuse to let my pride fall that far.

The forest survives.

For now.

I’ll file mass arson under “future problem-solving strategies.”

…

Which means I actually need to deal with this.

The offensive style I used against the sentient tree with delusions of grandeur clearly isn’t going to work here. Whatever skill it’s using keeps nudging me half a beat off, and I don’t feel like spending the next hour aggressively trimming bark.

So I pause.

Think.

Then shift my stance.

My sword lifts, angled toward the sky. My body turns sideways, weight settling instead of coiling.

Defensive it is.

And just like that, the staring contest resumes.

Alright.

Let’s see who has more patience.

Seconds pass.

Then a minute.

Then two.

At the three-minute mark—

—the monster finally moves.

And I don’t.

It drops from the trunk without warning, not fast, not slow, precise. Too precise.

I feel the shift in air pressure before I actually see it, the forest inhaling sharply like it knows what’s about to happen and would really prefer not to be involved.

Claws scythe toward my torso again.

I don’t dodge.

I don’t Dash.

I don’t even think about Astral Step.

I just… turn.

Not a full pivot. Just a subtle roll of my shoulder, a quiet shift of weight from heel to toe. My blade comes up a fraction late—late enough that instinct starts screaming about my continued survival prospects—

—and the claws miss.

Barely.

Close enough that I feel the drag of air along my back. Close enough that a few strands of my hair get caught on its claws.

Hair that, apparently, refuses to break even in the face of predatory horticulture.

Good to know.

The monster sails past, momentum carrying it farther than it clearly intended. It scrapes against the bark of the next tree, claws biting in hard as it arrests itself with an irritated snap of wood.

We freeze again.

Me, still standing exactly where I was, sword half-raised, trying not to process how close that was.

It, clinging to the tree like it needs a quiet moment to reconsider several recent life choices.

My heart thuds once. Hard.

…Okay.

That worked.

I don’t know why, but it definitely worked.

Now I just need to figure out the part where I actually stab it—

—preferably without unlearning whatever that was.

It doesn’t attack again right away.

The Canopy Stalker shifts along the trunk instead, claws scraping softly as it repositions—sideways, then down, then up again—never fully committing, never dropping. Its tail sways once, twice, the leaf-bladed fin slicing lazy arcs through the air.

Testing.

I don’t move.

This is, frankly, excruciating.

My legs want to adjust. My shoulders want to square. My sword wants to do literally anything useful. I ignore all of them and just… stand there, weight settled, blade angled, breathing slow.

The monster creeps closer along the bark, head tilting, eyes tracking the tiniest things, my chest rising, my fingers flexing, the faint shift of moss under my boots.

Then it strikes.

A shallow slash. Quick. Careful.

I lean.

Just enough.

The claws scrape past my chest, close enough that I feel the chill of displaced air, but they don’t bite. The Stalker recoils instantly, springing back up the trunk like it touched something hot.

We reset.

Again.

It tries another angle, dropping partway, aborting mid-fall. I don’t chase. It circles. I don’t turn fully to follow.

Silence stretches.

The forest feels… tense.

Like it’s waiting for one of us to blink.

The monster finally stills, crouched low on the bark, tail twitching in tight, irritated motions.

Huh.

It’s hesitating.

That’s… unfortunate for it.

And also deeply inconvenient for my understanding of what I’m doing right now.

It crouches.

Low. Tense. Coiled.

Not testing anymore.

Committing.

The Canopy Stalker drops again, this time faster, heavier, claws angled not to graze or probe but to finish. Its timing is perfect. Its line is clean. This is the attack it’s been saving.

I feel it coming.

And for the first time since this fight started, I don’t react immediately.

My sword shifts.

I draw the hilt back to my left shoulder.

A simple motion. Almost lazy.

The monster closes the distance in a heartbeat, claws reaching for where my chest should still be—

—and I step forward.

Not away, or even to the side.

Into it.

The world narrows to a single line.

I thrust.

No flourish. No starfire. No dramatic declaration.

My blade slides cleanly through bark and coiled muscle alike, the point punching out the creature’s back in a burst of splintered wood and dull green ichor.

The Canopy Stalker goes rigid.

Momentum carries it another half-step before gravity remembers it exists. Its claws scrape uselessly at my shoulder as its weight collapses forward, then down.

Dead before it hits the ground.

I hold the position for a second longer than necessary, sword still extended, just in case reality decides to argue.

It doesn’t.

A notification blooms in the corner of my vision.

I ignore it and exhale.

Slowly.

Huh.

That was… quieter than expected.

I pull my blade free and watch the body settle into the undergrowth, already starting to look like just another piece of fallen forest debris.

My stance relaxes on its own.

No ache. No strain. No spike of exhaustion.

That also felt… right.

I frown.

I don’t understand.

The fused form I forced together during the wolf pack fight felt wrong. Messy and overextended, like I was wearing two coats at once. But this?

This, and the pure offensive state I used against the Rootspine, both felt clean. Natural.

Compatible.

I stare down at my sword, then at the cooling bark-and-flesh mess at my feet.

…Great.

Now I have a mystery.

As well as an uncomfortable suspicion that I’ve been approaching this whole “style” thing from entirely the wrong direction.

I sigh.

I briefly question my infinite genius.

Then I keep moving through the forest.

Because if there’s any reliable way to figure out where I’m going wrong, it’s in the middle of a life-and-death battle with something aggressively homicidal.

Plant, animal, concept, maybe the System itself.

I’m not picky.

My sword is rated E for everything.
