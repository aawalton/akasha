Chapter 129

I cleared the blocked drain beneath the archive as instructed. The blockage, it turned out, had been holding the water below the floor for decades. The master commended the drainage and reprimanded the flooding. I did not explain that they were the same action.  — Codex of the Long Watch, Watcher's Commentary

Donna had gone ahead to check the first stretch of the path. She came back up the slope with the unhurried stride of someone who had found nothing worth reporting.

“Clear to the junction. Decent footing.”

The morning was cool and overcast. Arthur held the gate open with one hand and counted the followers through. The knot by the altar had not moved. He had offered twice, through Sarah, and twice they had refused. The old woman had not looked up.

Steve came through the gate last. He carried Harrow tucked within the leather journal as a sort of bookmark. The leather pouch was tucked under his coat where it could not catch the light. He stopped beside Arthur and looked back at the compound.

“Some of them won’t come,” Steve said. “Not today.”

“I know.”

“The ones who are coming are going to talk. Every one of them saw what happened. There will be an awful lot of questions when we get back.”

Arthur nodded. “We tried our best.”

Arthur walked at half his usual pace so the column could keep up, and made himself stop imagining the ways in which the road could turn on them.

Kastella church stood above the old theatre, and the road the followers had climbed to reach it ran back down towards the port in a long easy grade a wounded man could manage. Dressed stone at the top gave over to cracked tarmac lower down.

Sarah walked the length of the column, moving up and down the file and checking on the wounded with the efficient manner of someone who had done this in worse places. She stopped long enough to look, touch and keep going.

Steve kept up a low commentary the whole way down, half to Arthur and half to a boy walking beside him who did not know a word of English. Themistocles had walled this port before there was a Parthenon to defend, he said, which made the hill under their boots older than most of the ideas people had ever killed each other over. People had been walking down it to that same waterline for two and a half thousand years, and every one of them had been sure their own disaster was the end of the world.

Archie, who would normally have had something to add about humanity’s relationship with crisis, or the poverty of the roofline they were walking under, said nothing and Arthur was glad for the quiet.

Donna ranged wide on the seaward side with Ravener slung and her hands loose. She was not watching the followers. She watched the empty windows, the mouths of the side streets, the flat roofs, the places a thing would come from if a thing was going to come.

The inhabited zone announced itself by smell before it showed itself through the trees. Woodsmoke from cooking fires rather than wreckage. Then the smell of bread, or something close to bread. The temperature climbed as the road widened and the scrub gave way to walls, roof gardens and the first evidence of daily life behind shuttered windows. A dog barked from a yard below.

Donna fell back from the front and walked beside Arthur. She motioned with her head and said, in a low voice, “Picket ahead, two hundred metres. Half a dozen. They’ve clocked us.”

“Armed?”

“Spears, short blades and a crossbow.”

Arthur looked at the column behind him. Twenty-three civilians and three children walking behind four foreigners carrying weapons that had no business being in this city. He considered how it looked from the other end.

“We invoke Antoniou,” he said. “If they want to hold us and send a runner first, we let them.”

“Agreed.” Donna nodded and moved a few steps behind.

• •

Someone, probably the council, had closed the street. A barricade of dead vehicles was pushed together with a gap in the centre wide enough for two men abreast. Two figures sat up on a flatbed roof with spears across their knees. Behind the cars, three more came up off their haunches when the column filled the road, blades in their hands and one crossbow among them.

A man stepped into the gap with his spear levelled, and called out in Greek. Arthur did not need it translated. Stop there. Who are you? Or something similar.

Sarah was already moving towards the front, hands open at her sides, talking before she reached the gap.

“We are coming down from Kastella,” she said in Greek, “from the church on the hill. These people lived up there. They couldn’t return because of the wolves. We are escorting them back to the city, they have nowhere else to be.”

The guard looked past her at the column, at the children, at the dried blood on the four foreigners, and brought his eyes back to Sarah. “Kastella? How did you survive the wolves?”

“We were sent by Christos Antoniou on the Civil Council with a task. You can confirm with him. My name is Sarah Vassilou. These are the survivors and we need to report to Antoniou directly.”

The names did the work, both hers and Antoniou’s. A man from behind the cars asked the obvious question. “Vassilou? Doctor from the container yard? Petro’s sister?”

“Yes,” Sarah said with a relieved smile.

“You wait,” the man said.

“One more thing, please,” she said. “Could one of you get word to Petros at the boatyard on the eastern quay? Tell him his sister is back from Kastella and heading to the Civil Council. He will want to know.”

The guard nodded once and passed it down.

The runner came back in under ten minutes with a man in a clean coat. He looked at the column of people and then at the blood on the four leading them.

“Follow me,” he said and turned.

Two of the picket men fell in around the column, not quite guards and not quite an escort, and walked them through the gap and into the city.

The Council still held the old municipal square at the top of the rise, the same place they had come to bargain for the crossing. Three storeys of ugly concrete and steel-framed glass at the north end, the ground-floor windows bricked halfway and entrance narrowed to a single reinforced door.

The council people took the followers in hand and Arthur sighed in relief, then felt a sudden pang of guilt for feeling relief. A woman with an armband counted heads while water and bread came out of the building. Steve counted the three children twice to be sure of them and told the armband woman, in his terrible Greek, to keep them together.

Last time they had waited an hour in the sun for Antoniou to decide he had time for them. This time the reinforced door opened before they reached it and the man himself came out onto the step. He was as Arthur remembered, broad and going soft with the years, grey moustache, reading glasses shoved up into his hair as though he had forgotten they were there.

A guard on the door started to say something about their weapons. Antoniou lifted two fingers off his hip and the guard stopped talking.

“You are alive,” Antoniou said, pleased, the glasses catching the light as he came down the step. “Po po po. And you have brought me a congregation.”

“We brought you the ones who would come,” Sarah said.

He looked past them at the square, at the people milling about. “Ela. Inside. You will tell me what this is and who these people are.”

He took them down the corridor to the room they had made the deal in, the long table still buried under paper. The hand-drawn map of the coast, weighted at one corner with the same flat stone. He sent the clerk for coffee without being asked.

“Sit,” Antoniou said. “Tell me what this is.”

“Before we go through all of it,” Arthur said, “there is someone who should hear it with you. The archbishop.”

Antoniou did not answer at once. He unhurriedly drew a chair out from the table and sat down.

“Kastella was much more than a dungeon and innocents were caught in its horror. Someone has to decide what happens to them, and it is not us, and it is not only you,” Sarah said.

“You went to the hill on Civil Council business. Why are you suggesting I invite Nikephoros?”

“I am suggesting that what happened on that hill is going to land on his desk whether we put it there or not,” Arthur said. “Better it arrives with context.”

“And we would rather tell this once,” Donna said, flatly, “to everyone who is going to act on it, than to have it repeated by someone else and let the two tellings drift apart.”

Antoniou took his time with that. He had spent thirty years in this building reading what people wanted underneath what they asked for, and it showed in the pause.

“You want a witness,” he said. It was not really a question. “Or you want him in the room when you say whatever it is that you saw at Kastella. I had heard Nikephoros had gone to the Vassilou household.”

None of them answered him.

Antoniou sat with a pen between his fingers and looked at the four of them across the table. Arthur could see the gears turning. The task he had sent them on had turned out to be larger than what he had anticipated. He had sent four foreign fighters to clear a nest of wolves. They had brought back twenty-three mouths to feed and a problem.

He was quiet a moment longer. Then he raised his voice towards the door. He told whoever waited outside to send a runner up to the Metropolitan’s house, with his compliments. The archbishop was to be asked whether he might spare the Council an hour on a matter that touched the Church.

“Ela, we have some time. At least tell me this, is the threat over?”

“For now, yes.” Arthur nodded. “But like any anomaly, it will resaturate. But this time I don’t think it will be werewolves.”

“What you mean?”

“The anomaly was being fed,” Arthur said. “Misguided humans and that bit is why we wait for the archbishop.”
