The battle did not last much longer after that. Once the five plate-armored freelancers finally broke through the mercenary formation and reached the opposing banner, the outcome became almost immediate.

Even without my spyglass, I could see Edmund, marked by his own banner, drive his warpick directly into the chest of the opposing standard bearer. The poor man folded backward from the impact, and the Fearless March banner tilted violently before collapsing into the dirt.

What surprised me most was how little resistance followed. I expected a heroic final stand, an officer to start screaming for the men to hold the line, but no, there was no second volunteer raising a replacement banner.

Instead, a strange hesitation passed through the mercenary ranks. It was as if the entire battlefield was collectively anticipating what would come next now that the symbolic center of their formation had fallen.

Then, almost all at once, the hesitation turned into something much more practical, and the mercenaries began disengaging.

At first, it happened in smaller pockets. A few men backed away while still holding formation, shields raised as though they feared pursuit. Then others followed more frantically when they were left alone, facing the freelancers. Soon enough, the entire center started peeling apart as mercenaries moved sideways and backward away from the freelancers with increasing urgency, their survival instincts suddenly remembered.

“That is it?” I asked, taking my spyglass once more, after I noticed that Katar had lowered it.

“The banner has fallen,” He said, as though that explained everything.

“That feels strangely anticlimactic.”

“That is intentional,” Raik said. “If armies continued fighting even after there was no one to rally them, most battles would end in massacres.”

I suppose that made sense.

While the mercenaries still vastly outnumbered the Twenty Men Army, at least in raw numbers, did that really matter when the Twenty Men were able to break the military formation in half? More importantly, their banner fell in a matter of minutes.

No, morale mattered far more than simple arithmetic. That’s why, the moment the banner hit the dirt, whatever willingness they had to continue fighting evaporated.

Still, the battle had not fully ended everywhere.

Near the center of the field, where the mercenary command structure had collapsed, Widmore was still engaged in a duel with Captain Walfang. Around them, the remaining freelancers had formed a loose circle, effectively ensuring nobody interfered.

A few mercenaries attempted exactly that and were immediately repelled. One man rushed toward the duel only for Lanka to casually introduce the flat side of her axe to his ribs hard enough to send him tumbling several meters backward, and another was kicked away by one of the scale-armored freelancers, a dark-skinned man who chose to fight without a weapon even though he was in the middle of a battlefield.

The message was clear. The duel would remain uninterrupted.

Inside the circle, Captain Walfang looked significantly less composed than he had during tea.

His body had partially transformed. His jaw had lengthened slightly, exposing sharper teeth that now resembled fangs, and his muscles visibly bulged beneath his armor, stretching the metal in ways armor wasn’t meant to stretch. His shoulders had also broadened enough to make him look almost distorted.

He looked less like a knight and more like a man stuck halfway through a werewolf transformation.

“Kindred, I assume?" I asked.

“Definitely,” Raik said, “some sort of beast transformation.”

That made sense. It also explained what I was seeing next: some of the wounds he had received earlier were visibly closing. It wasn’t happening instantly by any means, but it was still fast enough to be visible. Most of those regenerating wounds were on spots the armor had failed, like near his armpit or the knee.

“That is a good power to have,” Ja’a said. “It is almost worth having that ugly face.”

“He is either from a well-established clan," Kan added,” or he must have eaten a lot of troll meat.”

“Definitely the former,” nodded Calr.

I was surprised by how casually we were talking while people were fighting for their lives.

Another clank of metal turned my attention back to the duel as a strike from Widmore hit Walfang on the shoulder, forcing the plate armor open like a clamshell. I noticed that most of the previous strikes had been aimed at places where armor usually had clasps.

It was impressive, especially considering that Widmore was not using any active magic.

He had no glowing aura around him or any visible mana manifestations. I initially thought it was because he was kindred, but even then, he did not display any obvious supernatural strength or speed either. He simply fought with sword and shield, but somehow every movement was perfect.

“I need to fight him,” Katar said. “His combat skill is absurd.”

“I could see a divine blessing in his soul,” Ja’a said. “He is not like you, scorning all paths to power other than weapon skill.”

“Pity,” Katar grimaced.

Watching them fight almost made me admire the display. One fought with dramatic strikes while the other used minimal movement to counter him. Still, one side glance at the field was enough to remind me of the bloodshed, which made me feel guilty for watching people kill each other like a Roman in the Colosseum.

I handed my spyglass to Katar again, and he immediately raised it, watching the battle with glee.

After the armor fell off, Widmore started making his opponent bleed.

A series of cuts to the torso, neck, and arms was clearly meant to exhaust Walfang’s regeneration. It was the same strategy we had used when fighting the troll, which made me feel even more uneasy.

“He is literally just beating him through resource management,” Calr muttered.

“That is one way to describe it,” Ja’a chuckled beside me.

The duel continued for several more exchanges, each one looking increasingly miserable for Captain Walfang.

Finally, after another overcommitted strike, Widmore shifted sideways by perhaps half a step and slammed his shield directly into Walfang’s face.

The mercenary captain staggered.

Widmore stepped in and struck him once with the pommel of his sword against the neck, then again across the temple, then a third time hard enough to send Walfang crashing to one knee.

Widmore placed his blade against the man’s throat.

There was a brief pause.

Then Walfang dropped his weapons in surrender.

A cheer erupted from the slime village, from the guards and a few of the villagers who had climbed the battlements to watch the fighting.

Even from this distance, I could hear it clearly. It was not especially elegant, just raw collective relief from people who had spent the past week under siege and had just watched twenty heroes dismantle an entire mercenary force.

Loumpa and his wife jumped over the wall and joined the Twenty Men Army in celebration.

“That ended much faster than I expected,” Ja’a said.

“Battles usually do,” Raik replied.

So much buildup and tension resolved in what could not have been more than twenty minutes of actual violence.

The thought of someone being born, living for twenty years, eating the equivalent of thousands of animals' worth of meat, only to die in a twenty-minute conflict for a few gold coins of pay.

I could never understand why anyone would choose this life. At least freelancers got to travel the land, explore unknown places, and help people along the way.

This... this was a life wasted.

My melancholic thoughts were interrupted by Vena as she rose to her feet.

She simply brushed sand from her robes and started walking downhill toward the battlefield.

“Where are you going?” I asked, despite already knowing the answer.

She gave me a brief look over her shoulder. “Healing.”

We all followed her, forming an honor guard around her, with Shingo and his intimidating two-and-a-half-meter-tall frame in the lead.

The first thing that hit me was the noise.

Groaning, crying, shouting for help, and scattered screaming rose from every direction in an ugly, chaotic chorus. Someone nearby kept asking whether his leg was still attached, his voice climbing higher in panic each time nobody answered him. Another man was calling for his mother between ragged breaths, which unsettled me more than the screaming. A third was openly sobbing while clutching what remained of his arm, rocking back and forth in the dirt, as blood soaked through the fabric wrapped around the stump.

The smell hit second. Blood, sweat, dirt, and excrement.

Vena ignored all of it, or rather, it did not let it interfere with her actual task.

She walked directly toward the initial clash zone, the exact area where Edmund and his team had broken through the mercenary center.

Most of the wounded who could still move had already dragged themselves away or had been dragged elsewhere by their friends, leaving behind only those too injured to stand.

It was a disturbing sight. Bodies were scattered everywhere, some moving, some not.

Vena knelt beside the first man she reached. He had a hatchet embedded in his stomach, the handle jutting upward at an unpleasant angle. She checked his neck for a pulse, her fingers pressing briefly against his throat. Then she shook her head and simply stepped over him, moving to the next casualty.

That one had a severe head wound, his helmet had split in half, and blood was pouring down one side of his face dramatically.

I initially assumed he was already dead, but Vena checked his vitals. Then she clasped both hands over her glass and sand totem and muttered a prayer.

Golden light burst outward, and a second later, the wounded man gasped violently and lurched upright as though surfacing from underwater.

He immediately tried to grab at Vena's hand, but thankfully Kan’s chain snapped around his wrist before he could touch her, stopping the movement midair.

“Do not touch the healer,” Kan said flatly.

The golden light attracted attention far faster than I would have liked. Within seconds, people started converging on us. A crowd formed almost immediately, half of them genuinely injured and the other half clearly panicking over injuries that could absolutely wait.

“Critical wounds first,” Vena ordered sharply.

Nobody listened. People started shouting over one another.

“My arm is broken!”

“He is bleeding!”

“I was stabbed!”

“My friend is dying!”

One particularly annoying man insisted his finger might be fractured while standing fully upright and looking otherwise completely functional.

Before the crowd could fully collapse into chaos, Katar unsheathed his blades with a slow metallic scrape. At the same time, Shingo banged his hammer against his shield while Raik casually ignited, flames crawled over his skin, outlining him in shimmering heat.

That stopped most of them in their tracks. The one exception was halted when a javelin and a hatchet landed directly in front of him, embedding themselves into the dirt hard enough to make him stumble backward.

I looked back up toward the tower, and the tower trio of ranged freelancers waved at us. Apparently, they were still providing support.

The message was immediate and universally understood: Do not mob the cleric.

I sighed in relief as the crowd stopped advancing. I did not want to fight a mob of desperate people.

Vena ignored all of that entirely and remained focused on the unconscious patients already on the ground.

So, apparently, battlefield triage had just become my responsibility.

“If you are standing, walking, and not leaking organs, move to the left!” I shouted.

“If you are conscious but need assistance walking, move right!” I added. “Anyone carrying an unconscious comrade, move forward!”

That worked even better than expected, as a surprising number of people obeyed immediately.

It was a good start.

A few wounded mercenaries looked offended by being ordered around by a stranger, or perhaps by a woman, but one glance at Shingo, Katar, and the burning Raik standing between me and them made compliance suddenly very appealing.

I started sorting people almost automatically.

One first aid class was the extent of my experience, but I tried my best anyway.

Those with visible heavy bleeding were moved first. Those pale enough to look concerning got checked for vitals.

I pressed fingers to necks and wrists, checking pulses rapidly.

Weak pulse? Priority.

Rapid, shallow breathing? Priority.

Visible abdominal wounds? Priority.

Minor cuts and dramatic whining? Not a priority.

Kan and Calr helped move people accordingly, dragging or carrying wounded toward Vena or redirecting them elsewhere.

A cluster of mercenaries had already begun setting up their own treatment area for lighter injuries. They were wrapping wounds, applying splints, and distributing bandages with such efficiency that I immediately concluded several of them must be battlefield medics.

That was unexpectedly professional and reassuring. I did not want to shoulder the burden of triage on my own. Deciding who gets to live and who gets to die was not something I was comfortable with, so whenever the medics sent someone my way, I knew it was urgent. Otherwise, they would have treated them themselves.

That realization almost made me smile in relief. So, of course, Fate would choose that exact moment to punish me…

The next pulse I checked felt like cold skin… No heartbeat, just a stiff neck beneath my fingertips.

My hand recoiled instantly. For a moment, I simply stared at the dead person.

The shock froze me in place for perhaps half a second before another scream snapped me back into motion.

Focus on the living. You cannot do anything for the dead.

Not everyone could reach Vena, so once the immediate surrounding casualties were stabilized, mercenaries began bringing additional wounded toward our increasingly improvised medical zone.

At some point, the Twenty Men Army support trio joined us as well.

The maid moved over to the mercenary medics and immediately started cleaning wounds.

She would brush them with her feather duster while praying to Kana, and blue light would burst forth, removing blood, dirt, and debris from open and bandaged wounds alike.

The battlefield maid had become a battlefield nurse; even if she did not have healing miracles, her prayers were still useful.

The bard stored away his trumpet and drums before pulling out a flute instead. A soft melody drifted across the area. It was a calm, gentle tune, unnervingly soothing given the surrounding misery.

The witch was somehow even stranger. She wandered through the waiting wounded, handing people small rolled objects.

“Is that a joint?” I narrowed my eyes.

The smell was unmistakable; it was absolutely cannabis. I stared at her in disbelief. I had not expected recreational drug use for pain management to be part of the healing strategy.

But honestly, though, it was not the worst idea.

A few freelancers from the Twenty Men Army also joined us, mostly ensuring their support members remained safe and helped maintain order.

At this point, any hostility from the mercenaries was completely gone. It was hard to remain hostile toward the people currently trying to stop your friends from bleeding out.

So the rest of my afternoon became triaging wounded people. Moving them closer to Vena as needed. Checking repeatedly on those waiting in case their vital signs deteriorated. And trying very hard not to think too deeply about what I was doing.

I helped Kan carry a man with a punctured abdomen. That was easily one of the worst sensory experiences of my life. The smell coming from exposed intestines was indescribably foul. Rotting meat mixed with poop. Still, compared to whatever the injured man was currently feeling, my discomfort was laughable.

He let out an absolutely gut-wrenching scream when we lowered him near Vena.

“Sorry,” I muttered, the apology felt deeply insufficient.

Time blurred after that.

At first, every injury felt personal, and every visible corpse made my body momentarily lock up. Then, gradually, something shifted. There were simply too many dead and wounded for my brain to emotionally process them individually. So eventually, my emotions muted themselves, and I slipped into autopilot.

Injuries stopped being personal tragedies and became categories.

Critical.

Urgent.

Can wait.

And, according to Kan:

“Put a bandage on it yourself and stop acting like a pussy.”

That final category was, unfortunately, a useful one.

Vena did not have infinite healing. She was mostly stabilizing people, not bringing them back to full strength the way she did for us in the middle of a fight.

That's also why she wasn’t doing group healing; she couldn't afford the loss of efficiency.

There were simply too many wounded.

By the time the sun started lowering, I was mechanically directing people away from Vena, who was sweating and visibly exhausted.

The worst of the wounded had been saved, and those beyond saving were gathered in a row, some with blankets covering them.

The final casualty count was significantly lower than I expected. At least, from the hilltop, I had assumed the death toll would be in the hundreds. Watching an army collapse under a group of elite freelancers gave the impression of absolute slaughter.

In reality, there had been twenty-one confirmed dead. Thirty-six more would have died without Vena’s intervention. Sixty wounded still required continued treatment, though their lives were no longer considered to be in danger. One hundred and eleven minor injuries remained, most treatable with bandages, rest, and time.

“That seems... low,” I admitted quietly while staring at the organized rows of corpses laid out nearby.

The dead had been placed side by side and covered with cloth where possible, though not everyone had been afforded that dignity yet.

Katar, who had been instructed by Raik to watch over me, simply shrugged.

“That is what happens when armies fight.”

“What does that mean?” I frowned.

“When people are injured, they disengage,” he explained. “Someone healthier rotates in and takes the next blow.”

He gestured vaguely toward the battlefield.

“You have seen the injuries yourself. How many of these people would be dead already if not for armor, luck, or someone dragging them away in time?”

He was right. A disturbing number of the wounded I had helped sort were only alive because the armor that had collapsed inward had absorbed most of the damage. I imagined that without defensive equipment and the natural hardiness of people in this world, things could have been much worse.

“Besides,” Katar continued, “those were mercenaries. They fight for money, and dead men do not get paid.”

“You keep saying that,” I muttered, not liking the way he disparaged the dead. “Are freelancers really that different?”

“Freelancers mostly fight monsters,” he gave me a flat look.

“That explains absolutely nothing.”

“Monsters do not stop. If a beast is trying to eat you, you keep fighting until it dies...” He paused. “Or you do.”

I had nothing to say to that. So I returned my gaze toward the dead.

Twenty-one dead. It was just a number. And yet each one had been a person.

Born to parents, lived through childhood, and grew into adulthood. Each one had relationships, habits, and preferences. I wondered if they used to stay awake at night thinking about embarrassing memories, or if they had expectations for the future, hopes, and dreams.

Did any of it matter now that they were reduced to cloth-covered shapes arranged in a line?

My thoughts were interrupted when someone called my name.

“Alice? Is that you?”

I turned.

Edmund was approaching, his armor still visibly scratched from battle. Though it looked pristinely clean, probably the maid’s doing.

“I am surprised to see you here,” he said.

“Sir Edmund.”

I tried to offer him a smile, but I could not. He studied me for a moment, then glanced toward the corpses.

“First battlefield?”

“Not exactly.” I sighed. “I have fought monsters before. I have fought people. I have even killed someone.”

The unpleasant memory of Luna’s abuser surfaced, but I pushed it away.

“But this...” I exhaled slowly. “This feels different.”

Edmund waited for me to continue.

“The waste of life,” I said finally. “The waste of potential. The sheer pointlessness of it.”

I gestured vaguely toward the battlefield.

“All this death over a siege contract and a few gold coins.”

He remained silent, but he did step closer and rest his shoulder against mine.

“How do you do it?” I asked. “How do you kill people like this and then just... move on?”

“They were the enemy,” Edmund answered simply. “And they were threatening a friend.”

That answer irritated me more than it should have.

“But they were weaker than you.”

My voice came out sharper than I intended, the bottled-up emotions from the day finally boiling over against my better judgment.

“Doesn’t it bother your sense of honor as a knight? Killing people who never really had a chance?”

I regretted the words the moment they left my mouth. I was lashing out at a man who had just spent the afternoon saving a village.

Edmund’s expression hardened slightly.

“You speak as though I slaughtered civilians.”

“Is there really a difference when they are weaker than you?”

“Those men lived by the sword,” he replied. “And they accepted the risks of that profession.”

His gaze shifted toward the rows of bodies.

“I assure you, had the situation been reversed, they would not be grieving over your corpse.”

“I know that,” I snapped, my voice came out louder than intended.

“See that man?”

I pointed toward one of the uncovered corpses. The body had a recently broken nose.

“He tried to ask me out in Verraden.”

“What?” Edmund blinked, caught off guard.

“He was pushy. Annoying. And he did not like taking no for an answer, so I broke his nose using a spear strike you taught me.”

That got a slight reaction from Katar beside me, though, as always, he said nothing.

“He was an asshole,” I continued. “But I still did not want him dead.”

The words came out more emotionally than intended.

Edmund followed my gaze toward the corpse.

“Well,” he said after a moment, “he wasted this life. He may have a better chance in the next one.”

“What?” I stared at him.

“In the next life.”

“What do you mean?”

“If he is mythic and follows a god, he may spend some time in the afterlife first,” Edmund explained casually, despite the topic. “But all souls are eventually bound for reincarnation.”

I blinked repeatedly.

“That is your religious belief,” I said cautiously.

Edmund gave me a strange look.

“No.” He shook his head. “That is just an established fact.”

“Even the Souldealers, godless as they are, have confirmed it centuries ago.”

I remembered reading about reincarnation in the Temple of the Holy, but I had assumed it was a mythic belief, not something confirmed across the Seven Realms.

I rubbed my forehead.

“It is not the same,” I finally said. “Are we our souls, our memories, our bodies, or a combination of everything?”

“Now you are getting philosophical,” Edmund said, sounding faintly amused.

“There are rituals that allow temporary access to fragments of past-life memories.”

He pointed toward one of his teammates, the witch who was currently smoking weed with the archers she had rendered obsolete.

“Cases have been documented repeatedly across multiple realms.” He shrugged. “Your soul is your true self, and your soul is immortal.”

“Reincarnation is not a matter of faith,” he continued. “It is simply how souls function.”

He looked back toward the dead.

“It is still a waste,” I insisted.

“For those who wasted their lives, perhaps a fresh start is preferable.”

His expression hardened slightly.

“Better that than spending another forty years extorting villages and harassing civilians.”

That was... not entirely unfair.

Still, I shook my head.

“I wish I could find that comforting.”

Edmund raised an eyebrow.

“Life still feels too precious,” I admitted. “Even if death is apparently less final than I was taught.”

No, that was the real issue, wasn’t it?

My Earth logic conflicted with what I was being told.

I was not an atheist. I believed in something, or perhaps it would be more accurate to say that I believed in everything, so there was always some degree of doubt when it came to what happened after death.

Here, apparently, death came with a waiting room and eventual reassignment.

I sighed.

“I am sorry,” I said after a moment. “You found me in a bad mood. I did not really mean to question your character.”

“Do not worry about it.” Edmund smiled faintly.

That somehow made me feel slightly worse.

I summoned my spear into my hand. The familiar weight was grounding.

Then I turned toward Katar.

“Tell Raik I will be back in the morning.”

He nodded without asking any questions. Good. I did not particularly feel like explaining myself.

I took in all my stored star mana and wished to teleport to the Sunless Reach.

The battlefield vanished, along with the smell, the crying, and the distant sounds of celebration.

I reappeared in the quiet solitude of my camp in the Sunless Reach, greeted only by cold air and a sky overflowing with stars.

I lay back on the ground, watching the heavens and trying very hard not to think.
