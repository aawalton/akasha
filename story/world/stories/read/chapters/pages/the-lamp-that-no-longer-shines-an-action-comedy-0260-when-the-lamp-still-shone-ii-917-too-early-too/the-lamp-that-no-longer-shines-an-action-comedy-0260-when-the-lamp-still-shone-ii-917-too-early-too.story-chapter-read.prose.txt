[Time]: Year 1981[Location]: The Umbral Cathedral

The Umbral Cathedral had been built by its mistress, and the interior proportions reflected this without apology. Its grand corridors stretched wide enough for armies, the vaulted ceilings climbing to heights the eye surrendered to rather than measured. Enormous chandeliers floated at intervals through the nave, casting long columns of warm light across the stone floors. Along the walls, floor-to-ceiling stained glass blazed with portraits of Greater Lantern Cats rendered at full scale, their painted eyes catching the chandelier glow with an unsettling fidelity.

Irene set her teacup down with a soft, precise click. "Tell me something, Ovelia," she said. "Do you know what an Aspect actually is?"

Ovelia, poured across the adjacent cushions, cracked one starry black eye open. "Cat," she said, slowly, from the bottom of an interrupted nap, "is the one who is supposed to be asking the pedagogically leading questions."

"An Aspect is a possibility," Irene said. "A stray thread of thought that captures a specific potential within a Witch. When that possibility is isolated from its source, it evolves independently, anchoring itself into a stable physical form."

Ovelia's ears gave a single pleased twitch. She looked up at the ceiling. "Not entirely dense."

"Then tell Cat this." Ovelia shifted on the sofa, and somehow it became a throne. "Every Witch who touches the absolute boundary line of an era is compelled to forge these Aspects. Why?"

Irene met the fathomless starry gaze. "To complete their Authority. To achieve—" A longing without a visible floor surfaced in her voice. "Omniscience. And omnipotence."

Irene looked away from Ovelia. Her gaze drifted to Avenka, napping on the Nine-Tailed Cat Stone, one tail draped over the edge in an elegant arc. The hypothesis that had been nagging at her for weeks finally resolved into certainty.

"Your Aspects remember the wrong things," Irene said. "A piece cut from a witch keeps that witch's memories, however far it leans to one side of her. Once, Avenka was half asleep with her ear in my hand, and she told me not to take the left passage again, because the last time I took it the two of us spent three days digging our way out." She looked back at Ovelia. "I have never been underground with Avenka in my life. Neither, I would wager, have you."

Ovelia's tail stopped moving. She looked at Avenka asleep on the stone for a long moment, and then she let out a breath.

"Other witches cut away a stray thought and grow it from their own stock, like a cutting in a pot," she said. "Naturally it only remembers the garden it came from." She trailed a finger through the air, as if drawing a line across water. "Cat found that tedious. Why raise one from a cutting, when somewhere it has already grown up?

"When Cat cut those threads loose, Cat cast them out like baited hooks, each one weighted with a particular inclination, and let them sink until each caught a Cat who had already lived that inclination all the way down. They are all Cat. What Avenka remembers is not a lie. It simply did not happen here."

Irene thought of a classroom door torn open by the wind, and Ovelia had said her name before anyone could introduce them, as though she had been saying it for years.

"Am I in every life you have ever lived?" she asked.

Ovelia looked away. "Stupid girl."

From behind a velvet drape in the far corner, something on Shaar's game console beeped. "You were in mine," she said. "I moved out."

Ovelia's ears went flat, and she did not turn round.

Irene let that one wait. "There are two of you I have never met," she said. "Why have I not been allowed to see them?"

Ovelia addressed a blank pillar. "The remaining two, Solvane and Aerthain, are special circumstances."

"Because the Authorities we hold, the possibilities we represent..."

It was a child's voice, and it was very old, and it had stopped caring about the cold a long time ago.

"...are, to be precise, the exact things the main body categorically required and would not have."

The deepest patch of shadow in the Cathedral rippled, and a figure stepped through it. She appeared even younger than Shaar: the underdeveloped, fragile frame of a sickly child. Her hands and forearms were wrapped tightly in layers of black gauze. A pristine white cloth was bound over her eyes. She moved with an old-world, aristocratic stillness, each footstep noiseless against the stone.

Irene's mana sense caught the critical detail first: she was a pure Human Witch. Black gauze at the wrists and white bound over the eyes made high mourning, and Irene had walked behind enough coffins to know it on sight. Her chest went tight, and then something uglier: in some other life, somebody had been loved enough to be mourned like this. For whom?

The child-witch turned her blindfolded face toward Irene. She took the hem of a dress that may or may not have existed in both hands, at two exactly mirrored points, and sank into a precise, antiquated curtsy with her heels together. The blindfold stayed level on Irene through the whole of the descent, and through the whole of the rise.

"I am [The Curser Who Hates Life]. Solvane." Her voice was a rasp pulled thin by exhaustion so old it had become geological. "Our first meeting."

She let the pause run, and behind the blindfold her gaze stayed on Irene's face.

"Irene." It came out stiff, like a word from a language she had long since stopped speaking. Then, as though correcting herself: "First Witch."

The moment the title was spoken, Ovelia's expression sharpened into irritation. "Too early," she said.

Solvane allowed her head to tilt, which was as much attention as another person's objection had drawn from her in a very long time. "Too late," she said, her voice losing what little animation it had retained. "Alas. I have already waited far too long."

Through the white cloth, Irene felt the dead, unfocused vacancy of the eyes beneath. Slowly, Solvane raised her gauze-wrapped hands and closed them around her own throat. For a moment the black fingers tightened. Then they loosened, as though she had remembered how little it would accomplish, and fell back to her sides. The skin of her throat was pale and perfect.

Irene's fingers curled into her palms until the nails bit. So it was for that one of me. The Irene of that life had been pathetic enough to die, and had left Ovelia to come down to this: a child in grave-cloth, too tired to finish raising her own hands.

"I am sorry," Irene said. "For making you wait so long."

Beneath the white cloth, on one side only, something flickered gold. Solvane's gauze-wrapped fingers twitched. "Hold me," she commanded.

Irene wrapped her arms around the small, black-clad figure. Solvane's body weighed almost nothing, the specific lightness of a child who had been slowly disappearing for a very long time. Two small hands came to rest against her back, and through the gauze of the left one something narrow and hard pressed into her spine: a band, on the fourth finger. The moment their bodies made contact, something closed over Irene's mind.

It was the weight of life.

The accumulated mass of every second of existence that had not yet resolved into silence: every living thing still breathing that had not yet been permitted to stop, every moment of continuity grinding forward against the grain of something that desperately wanted to end. It was exactly like drowning, beneath ice so thick it blocked even the memory of light. Her threads began to snap, one after another, and Irene tightened her arms.

A hand seized her collar and hauled, and her arms stayed shut. It took a second wrench, hard enough to rattle stone, to tear her loose.

Irene hit the Cathedral floor. She pressed her palms flat and breathed: first in small, inadequate gasps, then in longer, ragged pulls. She looked up.

Solvane stood exactly where she had been. The child's lips had curved into a faint smile, weary, patient, and on her childish face somehow deeply demonic. "Remember this sensation," she whispered, her voice fading like the last exhale of a dying star. "There will come a day when you require this mercy."

She stepped backward, and the shadows took her completely.

Ovelia stood over Irene. The reflexive irritation had drained away, replaced by something heavier. "As you have seen," she said quietly. "Aspects are meant to be born from threads of thought a Witch deliberately discards. That is the orthodox path."

Irene looked toward the place where Solvane had been. Allison, Kara, Avenka: the bright possibilities, nothing any witch would throw away on purpose. They were the anomalies.

Solvane was the orthodox product. A burden rightfully shed.

1939. The End War. The Doomsday Clock Tower. Thirteen deaths.

Too late for that one, Irene thought, and her hands closed at her sides. I am not dying first in this one. The rest of her stays whole, and whatever she cannot carry from here on, I will take.

Ovelia's brow furrowed, and she swept the Cathedral's mana currents with a frown. "Where," she muttered, "did that other idiot disappear to? Cat specifically ordered her to stop going out in public and humiliating the entire household."

Nothing answered. Ovelia flicked her wrist, and a line of Original Witch Script hung in the air between them; she prodded Irene's shoulder with the tip of her tail. "Read this aloud."

Irene read it, and her face twitched. It was a summoning invocation in the full form of a prayer, layered honorifics and a three-part ascending address, and witches had been refusing gods as a species since roughly the start of their recorded history. Saying it out loud was the sort of thing one did once and then spent a century hoping nobody had heard.

Ovelia had given her a direct command. Irene cleared her throat.

"Thunderer above the crowns that outlasted their kings —""Dragon of faith compressed past the point of return —""She who answers before the question completes itself —"

She did not reach the final line.

The nave went white as lightning came down through the vaulting in a single blue-white arc and struck the center of the floor, and the thunder caught up a beat later and rolled the length of the Cathedral and back.

The third line proves itself immediately, Irene noted, shielding her eyes against the afterimage.

The afterimage cleared. Standing in the center of the hall was a figure with Ovelia's raven-black hair and starry black eyes, and there the resemblance ended. Translucent dragon wings spread from her back, clear as glass, with the void folded dark inside the membranes. Horns of abyssal indigo curved from her temples, threaded with violet lightning, and a heavy, scaled tail swept the floor behind her. Lightning was still running across her shoulders, and the storm winds were doing something dramatic with her hair.

The Dragon Witch looked down the length of the nave, and when she spoke it was at the volume a sovereign uses in a hall built to carry it. "For what purpose is the Lord of Heaven and Thunder invoked?"

The question went up into the vaulting and came back smaller. Kara had sat up on the cat sofa with sleepy, irritable eyes. From behind the velvet drape in the far corner, Shaar had lowered her game console to one knee, watching.

Ovelia had gone quiet and incandescent, in the way of a woman who had watched this entrance one time too many. "Pack up your special effects," she said. "Are you trying to demolish Cat's house?"

"The vaulting would have held," the Dragon Witch said.

The wings folded regardless. The storm above her went out like a lamp under a hand, and the last of the lightning ran down her shoulders and off her fingers and ended there.

Ovelia took a slow, stabilizing breath and turned to Irene. "As you can see," Ovelia said, with the flat delivery of a woman who has accepted her own biography, "this is Aerthain. The Aspect who governs Mandate."

Irene looked at Aerthain. "Is she truly your Aspect?"

"Cat asks myself that every single day," Ovelia said, rubbing her temples. "Witches pray, even the ones who swear they never do, and all of it has to go somewhere. Had Cat swallowed it straight, there would be no Cat left, only a very loud shrine. So Cat made something to catch it first." She pointed an accusing finger at Aerthain. "And she has been soaking in worship since the day she was made, and it went to her head. She genuinely believes she is a God."

"I have never claimed to be a God," Aerthain said. "I am the King of the World."

Ovelia looked at Irene with an expression borrowed from someone who has just been asked to explain the weather pattern that destroyed their ancestral home. "Do you see? We operate under a parliamentary system now. And this relic is still making that claim."

Aerthain laughed, once. "Why maintain the fiction? They obey us precisely as they obeyed the throne. The only difference is that a throne could be held to account, and I am the—"

From the far corner, half-hidden behind a pillar, Daena made a small sound and pressed further into the shadow.

Aerthain stopped. She let the rest of it go, and looked at the pillar until the small sound behind it had settled.

They are opposites, Irene thought.

Daena was the [Unanswered Empress]: the Ovelia who had never answered her people's prayers, never taken the throne, and lived in the shadows instead.

Aerthain had answered, and taken the weight of it, and in her life the age that called for her had never ended.

"In summary," Ovelia said, turning briskly back to Irene, "nothing good ever comes of letting faith anywhere near your own head. If you ever end up with more devotion than you can use, make an Aspect like this one and use her as the septic tank."

Aerthain folded her arms and made a short, cold sound. She let it stand.

Ovelia ignored her and closed the distance to Irene, and the air came with her, as it did whenever she set down the pretense of being simply a very large and inconveniently powerful cat.

"Irene." The voice carried its full weight. "Have you ever considered abandoning your identity as a Human Witch to undergo a species conversion?"

Irene hesitated. Ovelia herself had gone from Human Witch to Dragon Witch to Lantern Cat Witch, and was a Ghost Witch besides.

"Would that be a mistake?" Irene asked.

"Catastrophically," Ovelia said.

Ovelia went still, the stillness she kept for things worth stopping everything else to say.

Aerthain took the opening. "The Mandate of the old era rests with me. The Milan'thirskaya dynasty, the epoch when Dragon Witches held the world outright, every throne that age raised and every throne it broke. I hold the whole sum of it, and the sum is complete."

Ovelia's gaze held Irene's without release, and her voice dropped into a register that existed somewhere between a lecture and something else entirely. "Only a Human Witch can claim a Mandate greater than all of that. Keep what you are, and you can surpass me."

Irene's heart kicked once, sharp and entirely without her permission. Ovelia was looking her in the eye and showing her the way past herself, in a room full of witnesses.

"What," Irene asked, and her voice was entirely level, "is a Man—"

"In the old eastern schools they called it Merit," Aerthain said. "The western occultists called it the Power of Rules. Both of them were describing the wall and looking at the shadow."

"Cat is teaching," Ovelia said, and turned the starry black eyes on Aerthain. Aerthain inclined her head and gave her the floor.

Ovelia turned back to Irene and let her see the predator: cold, and gloriously, unapologetically cruel. "Mandate is plunder. It is annihilation. Stripped to its final, irreducible form: purity." Her voice expanded to fill the entire Cathedral. "Why does the Mandate of the Orcs demand free our kind; claim our freedom? Why does the Human Mandate require peace across all ages; tribute from all peoples? Why does a nation's Mandate become drive out the invader; reclaim the heartland? Why does a mere village's Mandate come down to full harvests; fat herds?"

Ovelia spread her arms, and the shadows in the upper arches shifted to accommodate the gesture. "A Mandate is how the universe pays you, and it pays by how ruthlessly you plunder, how completely you destroy, and how violently you take every scrap of power for your own race, your own faction, your own ground."

Irene finished the rest herself: Human Witches were the overwhelming majority of their species, so the Mandate of the Witch race was, in practice, the Mandate of the Human Witch.

Something in Irene's spine cracked and widened, and her mana capacity rose past where it had any right to be: a byproduct of the lesson, and entirely real.

Irene looked up at her master. "The primary Mandate you currently bear," Irene asked quietly. "Is it that of the Witches, or the Lantern Cats?"

Something fractured behind Ovelia's starry black eyes. "It is that of the Witches," she said. "And it is that of the Lantern Cats."

The breath Irene had been holding slipped free, and with it the cold expectation of a war between species.

She looked at the divided sovereign and saw the flaw. Two peoples meant the plunder was split in two. Ovelia had built the ceiling, and because she would let go of neither side, she could never break through it herself.

"Mandate," Irene said softly, "is purity."

Ovelia's predatory edge dissolved into a quiet, bittersweet smile.

"Correct."

Aerthain came down the nave and stopped in front of Irene. "She has taught you taking," she said. "Take well and the universe pays. It is true." A pause. "Keeping is a separate craft. A throne stands on the number of people who would rather be wrong beside you than right against you, and that number is built in daylight, in front of the ones who will live under it."

"You will have that number. You will be correct so often that objecting to you will come to feel like poor manners. They will bring you flowers. They will applaud, and every one of them will mean it, and the ones who disagree will look around the room and applaud as well. The world entire has been held by no witch yet." She gave the last of it room, the way words are given room when they are meant to bind. "You will hold it."

Irene heard the prophecy out the way one hears one's own name read aloud. What held her attention was the woman delivering it: every throne of an age stacked on one back, and no witch yet who had held the world beside her. Two for two, Irene thought. One of me dies, and the other never turns up at all.

"You held it alone, then," Irene said. "How extraordinarily boring." Her smile widened by a degree. "I am here now."

Aerthain said nothing. Ovelia's chin came up, and her tail lashed once, close enough to brush Irene's sleeve. Irene's gaze stayed on Aerthain a moment longer; then she reached out and caught the tip of the tail.
