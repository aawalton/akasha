My breath caught in my chest, and I found myself staring at the doctor, unable to speak. Was I ready for my results? Part of me wanted to agree, to beg Doctor Tulip to just tell me everything, but my lips refused to move. I was too scared.

It shouldn’t have mattered to me. I knew that. It had no real impact on me; I’d only been in that world for a short amount of time. It still didn’t quite feel real to me, and I knew that should have meant I didn’t really care, but I did. I cared passionately.

Part of me had been waiting for that exact moment my entire life. Everything I’d ever done had been leading towards it. All of my schooling, every test, every evening and weekend spent on placement. It was all so that they could determine how my future should look, and I was about to find out whether it was worth it, whether I’d done enough to get the life I wanted.

A spear of doubt found its way into my heart. I wasn’t sure; that was the problem. I couldn’t recall whether I’d worked hard enough. Had I spent enough time studying? Had I given the right answer to every question, or was there something that I messed up? Was one bad response enough to mean I’d be destined to be unhappy and unfulfilled for the rest of my life? Surely, it couldn’t be. Right?

I swallowed, my throat suddenly dry. I knew that I needed to reply to Doctor Tulip, but I couldn’t get any words out. My mouth refused to produce enough saliva to moisten my parched throat, and I had to swallow again before I could speak.

“Yes.”

Doctor Tulip must have heard my anxiety. She looked away from her computer, her eyes finding my face. An emotion seemed to flit across her features, but it was too quick for me to figure out what it was. Sympathy, perhaps? I wasn’t sure, but it scared me.

She leant forward, placing one of her hands on top of mine, and I tried to relax my grip and not flinch away from her touch. It must have been obvious, but I didn’t want her to know just how petrified I was.

It was bad news, though. It had to be. There was no way she’d be acting like that if it was good news, my brain told me. I’d screwed up, made a mistake at some point, and now, the only responsibility they felt comfortable handing me was something minor like waste management. I had done a placement there, after all.

No. That was too much pressure for someone like me. Someone who was a failure. During my placement, they’d explained how important the role was, how crucial it was to the overall health of the ship. There had to be something else, some other assignment that was even less important.

Either that or death.

“You’ll be happy with the results, Daf,” Doctor Tulip promised.

I tried to reply, but no words came out. I was too scared. My jaw felt as if it had been locked shut, and that was probably a good thing. I was almost certain that if I opened my mouth, I’d just yell at the doctor to hurry up and tell me, and that would be a bad idea. It would be counted as further proof that I was unstable and poorly equipped to manage anything.

The best I could do was nod. My movement was jerky, but it was enough to make Doctor Tulip remove her hand from mine and sit back. Immediately, my grip tightened again. My nails bit into the skin on the back of my hands, but the faint burst of pain felt distant. It barely even bothered me.

“Okay, which would you prefer to start with?” Doctor Tulip asked, glancing at her computer screen. “Your assignment or reproduction?”

My lips parted, but no words escaped. I just stared at her blankly, unsure how to respond. I didn’t know. Both were important, obviously. They were what the testing had led to, and I wanted to know both results, but my head was spinning.

I wished there was a third option, that it was possible for her to tell me both at the same time. Maybe I could have asked for that. I could have requested for her to just blurt them both out, and then we’d discuss them. That would have been better than keeping me in such painful suspense, but I couldn’t do it. I couldn’t bring myself to ask. Instead, I just forced myself to swallow one more time and chose the one that scared me the most.

“Assignment,” I said, barely managing to get the word out. “Please.”

Doctor Tulip seemed to hear me just fine, though. She nodded, her lips pulling up into a slight smile as if she expected that response from me, but she didn’t reply straight away. She took her time, tapping on her computer screen before typing something quickly.

It took every ounce of self-restraint that I possessed not to lean forward and peer at whatever she was writing. Had I just made the wrong choice? Or was it the right one? Was that why she was smiling?

I shifted in my chair, my desperate curiosity getting the better of me, but it was useless. The computer screen had been angled away from me. The only way I’d be able to see anything was if I got up and went to stand behind Doctor Tulip, and I couldn’t do that. She’d never allow it, and it would be a terrible display of impulsivity and impatience.

“Fantastic,” Doctor Tulip said after what felt like a lifetime. “Well, as you might have expected… you’re going to be working right here!”

My brain turned blank, and all I could do was stare at her. My thoughts were a jumbled mess that made no sense. They flitted about constantly as I tried to make sense of her words.

“Right here?” I repeated. “But…”

Why would I work in Doctor Tulip’s office? Did she need an assistant? Was that what it was? She had the medical assistance robot, though. Meddy. The bot was good. It did everything that Doctor Tulip could have needed, and it was more efficient than I could ever be, than any human could ever be.

Or was I going to be an experiment? Was that what she meant? Perhaps my results were so bad that it was the only thing I was suitable for. They were planning to lock me in her office, to keep me in a barely alive state so they could run endless tests on my body.

That would use a lot of resources. It would take a lot of energy, and the room would be wasted. Doctor Tulip would have to move her examination room elsewhere too, but maybe it would be worth it. Perhaps they’d be able to use me to figure out the cure to some of the diseases that ravage our colony. That wouldn’t be too bad, I guessed. At least then, I’d help people.

“Well, not right here,” Doctor Tulip corrected herself. “Not right away, anyway. You’ll be working in the lab for a little bit as you train.”

“Oh.”

That was all I could say. My brain refused to come up with any other words.

“Then, you’ll start shadowing Doctor Orchid and myself,” she continued. “After that, once we both think you’re ready, you’ll be able to start seeing patients on your own.”

“On my own,” I repeated.

I was vaguely aware that I really needed to say something else. I needed to thank her for the opportunity that I was being handed or explain just how excited and grateful I was, but I couldn’t wrap my head around what she was saying. It didn’t make sense.

“It’ll be a slow transition, and we’ll be here to support you every step of the way,” Doctor Tulip reassured me. “You won’t be expected to rush into anything or take on that responsibility until we all feel you’re ready and can handle it, and even then, we’ll keep an eye on how things are going. Don’t worry, I’m not planning on retiring for at least another twenty years, and Doctor Orchid has even longer, so you won’t be expected to handle anything alone!”

She beamed at me, and I felt my lips twitch as I tried to return the expression, but I was too distracted. I fought desperately, trying to grapple with what I’d just been told and force my brain to process the information, but it kept rejecting it.

I was going to be one of the colony’s doctors. That was my assignment. I hadn’t failed anything. The opposite, actually. I’d done well. I must have somehow done well. Otherwise, there was no way they would have been so comfortable handing me such an important role with so much responsibility.

The health of the people on board the ship was my responsibility. I was the one who would oversee that going forward. Obviously, Doctor Tulip and Doctor Orchid would be there too, just like Doctor Tulip said, but I had just become a part of that team, and I wasn’t sure how to feel about that.

Overwhelmed. Thrilled, excited, and overwhelmed. That was how I felt. I wanted the role. Ever since my first placement in the medical centre years ago, it was the thing I’d been working towards. I’d even written it on my end of year report when I was twelve. Someone had laughed at me for that. Bass. He’d told me it was foolish, that there were only ever two doctors in the colony, and it was too soon for them to select another. He’d suggested I worked in the lab, that it would be a better and more realistic fit for me, but I’d ignored him and wrote it anyway.

I was going to be a doctor.

Tears burned behind my eyes, and I looked down, not wanting Doctor Tulip to see. I didn’t want her to know just how affected I was by the news. I should have had better control over my emotions, but I felt like I was about to burst into sobs.

Frantic energy was building up in my centre. I could feel it thrashing within me and begging to escape, but I pushed it aside and forced myself to take a deep breath as my grip on my hands became more painful. I wanted to stop, to loosen my fingers, but they refused to obey me.

Sucking in another slow breath, I tried to squash my dizzying blur of emotions down, but they fought back. Luckily, I was used to it, though. In both worlds, I was used to ignoring how I felt and hiding my feelings away behind a mask. In that world, the one where I was Daffodil, it was because negative emotions often impacted group cohesion. They made relationships volatile, and there was little space for that on board the ship, but in real life, it was because of my mom. I couldn’t let her see how much her words and actions affected me.

That felt dangerous for a number of reasons. Either it would set her off and make her become even crueller to me, or it would encourage her. She would see just how deeply she could wound me, and I knew she’d chase that, wanting to know how far she could push it. Or at least, that was what happened before when I’d slipped up and not been able to conceal my sadness from her.

“Thank you,” I said after a few seconds, finally able to get the words out. “I’m honoured that you, Doctor Orchid and the governor are trusting me with such an important assignment, and I’m very excited to show you all that you made the right decision.”

My voice broke on the last word, but I managed to get it out. That was good. It was the right thing to say; I was almost sure of it. I couldn’t recall the conversation exactly, but my guardians had warned us before that we’d be expected to thank the doctor when we got our results.

Normally, it didn’t need to be anything too big. We just had to make sure they knew we were grateful and happy about our assignments, but it was different for me. The doctors always had a role in the assignment decision-making. They assisted and advised the governor on that, but that was usually it. When choosing a new doctor, they were more involved. They had to put the name forth. They both had to agree that a person was the right decision for the role before they could even suggest their name to the governor. Cal had told me that when I asked a few years ago.

They both thought that I’d be a good doctor, I thought. The realisation almost caused me to burst into tears again. They must have discussed it, come to the conclusion that I was a good fit, despite what had happened last year.

My memories of the event were hazy, but that was enough to cause embarrassment and shame to wash over me. There was an illness back when I was assisting Doctor Orchid. Something had torn through the ship, and it was bad. Almost everyone caught it, and the medical centre was full. The doctors had to visit people in their homes, and Doctor Orchid brought me along to see someone. I was just meant to stand in the background and observe, to do whatever the doctor asked me to do, and I messed up.

Blood began to creep into my cheeks as I remembered Doctor Orchid snapping for me to bring an emesis bag for the patient to throw up into. They needed to send it to the lab to test for something, but I’d been too slow. The patient, Plum, had been reaching out towards the bag in my hands when they’d vomited, but I just wasn’t quick enough.

A shudder slipped down my spine in both worlds as the memory of it somehow finding its way around the protective mask I was wearing and hitting me in the face slammed into me. It was so bad, but it was my reaction that I was certain had ruined my chances of being assigned to work as a doctor, though. I’d dealt with some gross things since I started doing placements in the medical centre. I thought I’d become desensitised to it, but that was so disgusting. I couldn’t help tame the nausea that rose within me.

Tears burned in my eyes as I’d vomited, and I’d cried so hard as Doctor Orchid ordered me into the shower in Plum’s apartment. She must have been able to hear my tears as she waited outside the door for me to finish scrubbing my skin, and I thought that was it. I was sure she’d immediately tell Doctor Tulip and the governor that I’d be a horrible doctor, and my chances were ruined, but that hadn’t happened.

“It was an easy decision to make,” Doctor Tulip said, ripping me from my thoughts. “I probably shouldn’t tell you, but we had no concerns or hesitations to raise.”

My mouth dropped open. That was unheard of. Obviously, we had no idea what happened in the assignment process. Everything we knew about it had come from rumours or the guardians, but I wasn’t sure where their information came from. It would have made sense if they were brought into the process, though. They spent the most time with us out of any adult, so they must have had a pretty good insight into what we were like.

There were always hesitations. Every single person had done something dumb at some point in their lives, and it had to be discussed before it could be dismissed. They had to consider whether that stupid action was a one-off event or if it said something more about the person’s character.

Actually, a small voice at the back of my mind piped up, there weren’t always hesitations or concerns. It had happened before. Or, at least, there had been rumours about that. I wasn’t sure how true it was, but someone had told me that no hesitations or concerns had been raised during the governor’s assignment. Arum had said it was a unanimous decision, as it must be with the governor, but there was nothing anyone even felt the need to raise. He was so perfectly suited to the role, and everyone could see that.

I wasn’t sure if that was true or even where Arum had heard it, but I believed it without question. It must have been true. He’d been leading us for so long and had navigated so many potential problems on board the ship with wisdom and grace.

“Thank you,” I told Doctor Tulip again.

“That’s quite alright. I look forward to working with you.”

We were going to work together, I thought, a grin spreading on my lips. She was going to be my mentor, my colleague.

“I look forward to working with you!” I replied, the words coming out much louder than I expected.

But the doctor just laughed. She seemed genuinely happy, and I could barely stop myself from bouncing in my chair from the excitement coursing through me.

“Fantastic. Well, would you like to hear the rest of your results?” Doctor Tulip asked.

My smile froze, and I had to fight to stop it from slipping from my lips. I’d forgotten about the rest. The news of my assignment had pushed all other thoughts from my mind, but suddenly, my anxiety returned.

“Yes, please,” I said, trying and failing to sound just as enthusiastic as I had before.

“Great. Well, luckily, your reproductive health seems to be extremely good,” Doctor Tulip informed me. “Based on your numbers, we’re going to do a few cycles of egg retrieval, the first starting within the next nine months or so. How does that sound?”

She had to ask that. It was how things were done on the ship, and I knew that, but there was no real need. I only had one answer I could give her, regardless of how I truly felt. I didn’t mind too much, though. I always knew that I’d be expected to contribute to the genetic bank onboard the ship, that I would donate my eggs at some point, and they could be used to create offspring.

Everyone without a mutation was expected to do so. It was necessary to conserve genetic diversity and maintain a healthy population. Otherwise, the genetic pool could get too small, and that was dangerous. Illnesses, mutations and complications became even more common when that happened.

“That sounds great,” I replied after a slight pause.

“Wonderful! And we estimate that you’ll carry two children to term, but obviously, that might change,” Doctor Tulip said. “Despite the number of tests we run, everyone’s body reacts to pregnancy, so it might be that you’re only able to withstand one round, or perhaps you’re better suited to three or four. We’ll be able to assess that slightly better after your first pregnancy.”

I nodded mechanically, but my brain had turned blank once more. I knew it was coming. Just as contributing to the generic bank was expected of me, I’d always known that I’d most likely be expected to carry a child. It probably wouldn’t share my DNA; that wasn’t how things were done on the ship because that could cause a range of complications, but I would still carry it for close to nine months before giving birth.

It was one of my duties. Everyone on the ship who had the potential to carry a child and whose health allowed it was expected to do so. We had to maintain the ship’s numbers, to ensure that humanity did not die out, and I knew that, but it was still a shock to hear it. There was another question, though. Something else played on my mind and demanded answers.

“When?” I blurted out before quickly correcting myself. “I’m sorry. I meant… do you know when I’ll be expected to carry my first child?”

Luckily, Doctor Tulip didn’t seem offended by the bluntness of my question. She must have anticipated it because she simply nodded.

“It depends partially on how the egg retrieval goes. Obviously, we need to ensure your health is as good as possible for when we do that, but if all goes well, we estimate that your first pregnancy will begin in approximately eighteen months.”

“Eighteen months,” I repeated.

Time moved slowly in that world, but it was too soon. I was only sixteen, and I knew that some people got pregnant when they were my age—some people became pregnant when they were even younger, but it felt wrong for me. I wasn’t ready for it, and I knew that I wouldn’t have to raise the child once I’d given birth. The guardians did that, but I still had to be pregnant, and that terrified me.

“Yes. It’s on the younger end of the spectrum, but we felt it best for you to get that out of the way as soon as possible so that it doesn’t interfere with your assignment,” Doctor Tulip explained. “Ideally, you’ll complete both pregnancies whilst you’re still training so you’re as healthy as possible throughout, and you don’t have any patients of your own to look after. Does that make sense?”

It did. That was the problem. I understood her logic and where she was coming from, but I was starting to hyperventilate. I was too young. I wasn’t ready, but I couldn’t tell her that. There was nothing I could do about it. I just had to push my feelings aside again and deal with it, until—

A noise cut through the room. A siren started to blare, and my eyes flicked towards Doctor Tulip’s face, but she hadn’t reacted. She was still looking at me expectantly, as if she couldn’t even hear the alarm.

She couldn’t, I realised. Doctor Tulip was completely oblivious to the sound because it was coming from another world. My heart leapt, and I reached out towards the familiar dizziness, grasping it tightly. It wrapped itself around me and dragged me from the world.
