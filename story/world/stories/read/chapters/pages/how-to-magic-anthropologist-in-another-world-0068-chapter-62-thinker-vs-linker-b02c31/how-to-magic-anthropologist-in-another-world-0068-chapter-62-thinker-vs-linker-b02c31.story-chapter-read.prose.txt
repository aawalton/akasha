Calr.

White.

They were floating in white.

It was not light. It was not darkness. It was simply white.

They tried to breathe and found there was no air. They tried to move and discovered there was no body.

Then the white fractured.

A mosaic of images from their lives splintered across the void, overlapping and colliding.

Him playing with Louis and Mary.

Her hugging Obora for warmth.

Him digging through the trash behind a noble mansion for something he could sell.

Her searching through the refuse at a military camp for something she could eat.

Memory flooded the white space.

Not his. Not hers. Yet both of theirs all the same.

They were in a small room.

Children were packed shoulder to shoulder across the floor. The air was stale, and the smell of sweat and unwashed bodies was thick enough to taste. Someone was crying. Someone else was coughing.

And there was hunger.

It was a physical thing, a clawing thing inside their stomach.

This was her memory, not his. While he knew hunger, it had never been this sharp.

They were four years old... or maybe six.

Time blurred inside the memory.

Obora stood beside her. She was thinner than she remembered, with eyes that looked too big for her face.

NoName stood near the doorway, chewing on some bread.

She had real bread.

“How did you get that?” Obora asked.

NoName shrugged. “Kitchen.”

“Share,” they begged. “Please.”

NoName’s eyes hardened. “No.”

“Why?” they asked.

“Because I stole it. If you want to eat, you should be good enough to steal, too.”

A pause followed.

“Only the strong survive.”

The memory froze.

It was blurry, faded around the edges, and the emotion saturating it was too strong. Most of the children’s faces were indistinct.

That was not how he stored memories.

With his perfect recall and Analyze power, Calr normally stored events independently from the emotional state he had felt at the time. He did not like subjectivity in his memories. When he reviewed them later with a fresher perspective, he did not want lingering emotion to cloud his judgment.

So he tried applying Analyze to this memory, stripping it back into something closer to his standards.

The room expanded slightly.

The emotional saturation dimmed.

NoName grew thinner, just like the rest of them. She might have had a few successes, but she was also abused like the others.

The room resolved into something closer to a barracks. The children were not literally huddled shoulder to shoulder. Instead, they slept in bedrolls placed too close together.

The faces became clearer. One sharpened into Suu’s face. She had been there too.

His analysis skill began connecting the dots. He noticed how the children looked at each other, how the bedrolls were not all the same quality, and how some children were in worse condition than others.

This was an artificial competitive environment.

A structure deliberately designed to reward predation.

Children were trained to view each other as rivals for survival and resources.

She had felt inferior to NoName, which made the other girl appear stronger than she actually was.

Adult laughter bled faintly into the scene.

Cut.

The image shattered back into white.

Then it shifted again.

A mess hall appeared.

Long wooden tables stretched across the room. Rough men ate meat with grease shining on their fingers while they drank and spoke loudly.

Children stood off to the side while the adults ate, watching the men who seemed strong and frightening.

They moved with Obora.

The bread sat near the edge of the table.

A small hand reached.

The cook turned.

A slap landed.

Obora fell sideways, her small wing cushioning the impact.

A second slap followed.

Heat rose behind their eyes as they collapsed onto the rough ground.

Humiliation, shame, and the certainty that they would spend another night hungry.

Then silence spread through the hall like spilled ink.

Donas entered.

He was tall… abnormally tall.

The ceiling seemed lower around him.

The men straightened, almost like honorable knights greeting their liege.

The cook stepped back.

“What is happening?” Donas asked.

His voice was calm and deep.

The cook muttered something about stealing.

Donas looked down at the girls.

“Are you hungry?”

They nodded.

He lowered himself to their level.

“I am sorry,” he said gently. “There is not enough food for everyone.”

He lifted both girls, one under each arm.

Then he sat down and placed them on his lap.

He put bread in their hands.

“You can eat my share.”

In the memory, the man glowed.

Not literally.

But the light seemed to bend around him.

He smelled clean, strong, and safe.

The food tasted better than anything they had ever eaten.

Calr could not believe how wrong this memory was.

With an effort of will, the scene shifted.

The strong men at the table changed.

They transformed from imposing figures into something closer to bandits.

They were not surviving on rationed scraps.

They were overeating.

Half-finished food lay discarded.

Their bellies were thick with excess.

The cook was smiling and relaxed.

He seemed completely unworried despite being caught striking a child.

Then Donas changed as well.

The ceiling rose.

Donas shrank to a normal height.

The glow vanished.

He was simply a man wearing noble clothes while surrounded by people in rags and rough armor.

The hall lost its grandeur.

It became grimy and filthy.

The men were not bowing.

They continued eating in Donas’ presence.

The food had seemed extraordinary only because she had known nothing except scraps.

The focus shifted.

The kitchen was full.

Bags of grain rested in stacked piles. Deer hung from meat hooks. Barrels of wine lined the walls. Plates still held unfinished meat while men drank freely from pitchers that were constantly refilled.

There was a hierarchy to the room.

Donas sat at the head of the table.

He controlled the food allocation.

He allowed scarcity among the children.

He permitted physical discipline.

Then he interrupted selectively.

He created contrast.

He positioned himself as a rescuer within a system he himself governed.

She pushed back.

“He saved us.”

“He let you be slapped first.”

“He apologized.”

“For a system he controls.”

“He fed us.”

“He could feed all of you first. Yet he feeds you last.”

Silence followed.

The slap replayed, slower this time.

He focused on the cook’s face.

There was no fear in the man’s eyes. There was no concern about consequences.

Why would there be?

He had done nothing wrong.

He had followed orders.

Donas’ orders.

There was no punishment to be issued, and no structural change had followed that event.

The memory trembled.

Her gratitude flickered.

He was twisting her memory.

He did not argue.

Cut.

Back to white, then the scene shifted again.

A small kitchen appeared, lit by warm candlelight.

They were sitting beside Marca at a table while eating.

They had spent the entire day outside and had missed both lunch and dinner.

Their stomach still growled despite already finishing one serving.

Marca did not look at them.

She quietly emptied half her plate onto theirs.

She did not say anything.

There were no witnesses.

It was not a spectacle.

She waited for them to come home before eating, just in case they wanted a second serving.

The children always ate first before she did.

She was stern and strict, and they had never once seen her smile, yet she always did this.

They wanted to earn money so they would not be a burden on her. In the end, they had failed and returned empty-handed.

She did not chastise them for being late.

She simply ate the smaller portion without comment.

The memory held steady, as clear as the day he had witnessed it.

It was devoid of distortion, unlike her memories.

A perfect retelling, with no dramatic lighting and no exaggerated movement.

Yet she felt something unfamiliar.

He overlaid both memories.

Side by side.

One gave publicly and gained loyalty.

One gave privately and asked nothing in return.

She tried to push him away, but she could not.

The link held too strongly.

Information flowed in both directions.

He felt the hunger, the pain, and the humiliation.

She felt the warmth, the fullness, and the quiet security.

A memory surfaced of her fighting another child over scraps.

Another memory appeared of his sister Sara brushing crumbs from his face.

A memory of NoName calling her weak.

A memory of Sara calling him an idiot, affection woven through the insult.

She was training, fighting Obora.

He was playing house with Mary and Louis.

Their chest tightened.

That was not how it worked.

That was not how any of it should work.

He had always thought his life had been miserable growing up an orphan.

She saw his life as paradise.

They knew he was evil.

Deep down, they must have known.

Another memory surfaced.

A low male grunt.

A woman weeping.

“I hated when they stopped fighting back,” someone shouted.

They froze.

That sounded like Izair.

They had always been terrified of him.

They were holding a tray of food meant for the prisoners.

The man stepped out of a cell, rearranging his breeches.

The door remained open behind him.

They instinctively linked to his mind.

And they saw.

Vile things.

Evil things.

What he had done.

What he wanted to do.

What he planned to do.

Women who were slated to be sacrificed.

Women he had randomly met on the road.

Women who had once lived under Donas’ protection.

Women who had lost that protection for one reason or another.

Discarded, reclassified, and finally available.

“You are lucky you are off-limits,” he said with an evil grin. “For now.”

He shoulder-checked her as he passed.

The platter slipped from their hands.

Metal struck stone, and food scattered across the floor.

The memory was sharp and vivid.

It did not need his analysis.

It was already sharp enough.

That man had been her so-called father’s peer.

She could see him clearly for who he truly was.

So why had she never questioned Donas before now?

He knew the answer.

They shifted back to white.

Then another memory manifested.

This one had happened only last week.

They were traveling inside the cart.

Alice sat cross-legged in front of them with a book in her hands.

History of the Holy Faith, by Gray Crownerson.

“There is a word for that,” Alice had said. “Grooming.”

She continued speaking as she held the book open.

“Children under the age of twenty-five still have developing brains. Their minds are still maturing. They rely on adults to define reality for them. Abusing that trusted bond between adult and child is called grooming.”

“Having a power imbalance and subtly manipulating a young person’s worldview is grooming. Gaining unearned trust while someone is vulnerable is grooming.”

The words overlaid Donas’ actions.

The bread.

The apology.

The life she had lived.

“Relieving someone’s pain when you are the one who caused it, in order to gain loyalty, is grooming,” Alice continued in the memory. “It is about imbalance. You cannot refuse someone who controls every aspect of your life.”

The word echoed through the white space.

“Stop.”

Calr turned toward the voice. While he had no body, the sound finally gave him orientation. Kitame stood opposite him. She did not have a physical body either, but her presence still held the outline of herself.

“Stop what?” Calr asked calmly. “You know that I am not inside your head, right? You are inside mine.”

Kitame recoiled. “That’s not true.”

Calr did not raise his voice. “That is why I have more control than you do.”

He paused, analyzing even as he spoke.

“It seems that when you tried to read my mind, we formed a two-node hive mind,” Calr continued. “Two incomplete halves completing a circuit. This is probably what our ancestors experienced constantly. This sense of oneness.”

“I don’t like it,” Kitame said sharply. “The way you think is wrong. You separate emotion from fact as if they are different things.”

“They are,” Calr replied.

“They are not,” she snapped. “Every action I have taken was driven by my emotions. My love for my adoptive father. My loyalty. Those things are valid. They are real. You do not get to sweep in and shatter reality however you please.”

Calr tilted his head slightly.

“I did not ask for your help,” Kitame continued. “I did not want you digging through my memories. I was happier being blind to his faults. At least that meant I had people who loved me.”

Silence stretched between them.

“I am sorry,” Calr said.

She blinked.

“But I could not help it,” he continued. “I think this is my role within the hive mind. I am a Thinker. You are a Linker. That is likely how our ancestors functioned. You connect. I analyze.”

“You’re guessing,” Kitame said.

“I am inferring,” Calr corrected. “From observable patterns. Did you know that some of the ghosts here operate like us?”

He stepped closer through the white space.

“I cannot help analyzing the memories you pass to me. That is simply how I am structured. And believe it or not, you were the one granting access. Every memory that surfaced came from you. Deep down, you wanted to know the truth.”

“I did not,” she whispered.

Another memory surfaced before she could stop it.

They were ten years old.

They stood inside Donas’ office.

The room was rich with dark wood. Shelves heavy with books lined the walls, and a map of the Contested Realm hung prominently behind the desk.

He called them inside with a gentle tone.

“Come here, Kitame,” he said while holding a book.

They approached cautiously.

“Can you read this?” he asked, showing them a page written in a strange script.

They stared at it.

The letters meant nothing to them.

“I… can’t,” They admitted.

“That is the language of your ancestors,” Donas said fondly. “The ancient telepaths. I hoped that since you can read minds, you might also be able to read this.”

They lowered her head. “I’m sorry.”

He smiled.

“Do not apologize. You are still young.”

They hesitated before asking a question.

“Why can I read everyone’s mind except yours?”

He winked.

“That is a secret.”

They laughed softly.

Their eyes returned to the book. “It’s pretty.”

“It belonged to a great man,” Donas said. “His name was Taka Agame.”

Calr felt his attention sharpen immediately.

“He detonated himself, you know?” Donas continued. “He released all of his mana at once. The explosion killed two immortals. Great men who had lived for three thousand years. Lawshak Kahal and Yama, Son of the Colossus. Taka died protecting Hano from them during the last revolt.”

Calr felt the historical weight of those names.

Both had been hybrid children of Dravac and former patriarchs of noble families. Both had grown resentful of the world’s current order and had sought a return to the age of the Bloodline Catastrophe, when monsters ruled the realms.

As for Taka…

That was Raik’s father.

“I was the first person at the site of the explosion,” Donas continued. “I lost three friends that day. Only this book remained.”

“Three?” they asked.

“Yes. I was friends with all of them.”

“I thought you hated Hano nobles,” they said.

“I do,” Donas replied. “But that does not change the fact that I respected Taka and considered him a friend.”

He turned the page.

“This book contains writing in many different languages. Did you know it even contains information about you?”

“Me?” they asked.

“Yes.”

He tapped a passage.

“It says that there are different kinds of telepaths. Thinker, Linker, Worker, Watcher, And Blinker.”

Their eyes widened.

“The last type can teleport,” he added.

“I wonder if there are more of your kind somewhere in the world,” he said thoughtfully.

Then he added something else.

“Or perhaps we could breed a teleporter out of you.”

The last sentence was quieter.

Lower, almost absentminded.

Inside the white space, Kitame went completely still.

Calr did not speak immediately.

He simply allowed the words to replay in the silence.

“I wonder if we could breed a teleporter out of you.”

Kitame swallowed.

“He was joking,” she said weakly.

Calr looked at her evenly.

“Was he?” Calr asked. “Or was that the reason you were protected while the others were not?”

Silence followed.

“I can’t deal with this right now,” Kitame cried.

“You can leave whenever you want,” Calr said. “You only need to exit my head.”

The white space began dissolving around them.

Before the link fully collapsed, Calr could not help giving one last warning.

“Make sure you do not reveal my existence to anyone,” Calr called. “Otherwise Donas might decide that incest is the most efficient way to build his army of teleporters… cousin.”

The white fractured again.

But this time, Calr was pulled back to reality.
